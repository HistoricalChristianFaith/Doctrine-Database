Bede on Naaman — the Gentile leper, baptism, and the sevenfold Spirit

Dates: c. 710 (the Commentary on Luke [In Lucae evangelium expositio], on Luke 4:27; Bede b. c. 672/673, monk of Wearmouth–Jarrow, d. 735) · Wikipedia
Summary: Commenting on Jesus’ Nazareth reference to Naaman, Bede gathers the whole patristic typology and carries it into the medieval West: Naaman (“beauty”) is the Gentile people washed from the leprosy of unbelief in the sacrament of baptism, which alone saves because it regenerates by the sevenfold Spirit; his renunciation of foreign gods and his portion of holy earth prefigure the baptismal renunciation, confession, and communion.

Claims & evidence

“Purified… through the sacrament of baptism” — Naaman the Gentile, the sevenfold Spirit, the flesh of a child (Commentary on Luke)

Jesus, preaching in the synagogue at Nazareth, recalls that in Elisha’s day “none of them was cleansed, saving Naaman the Syrian” (Luke 4:27) — a Gentile healed while lepers in Israel were passed over. Bede reads the whole episode as a figure of the Church of the nations, and in a single sweep hands on the reading the Fathers had built up:

And thus Naaman the Syrian, who is interpreted as “beauty,” represents the people of the nations, once stained by the leprosy of unbelief and sins, but purified from all foulness of mind and body through the sacrament of baptism. Advised by a captured girl, which is the grace of divine inspiration, which the Jews could not keep but the Gentiles seized, he was urged to hope for salvation and to be washed seven times. Because obviously, the only type of baptism that regenerates by the Holy Spirit saves. Hence it is rightly remembered that his flesh appeared like the flesh of a small child after the washing… And so that you may know all the prefigured sacraments of baptism here, in which we are commanded to renounce Satan and to confess faith, Naaman declares that he will no longer make offerings to foreign gods, but will serve the Lord alone in all things. He also rejoices to take with him a portion of the Holy Land, because the baptized must also be confirmed by participation in the body of the Lord.

— Bede, Commentary on Luke, on Luke 4:27.1 Every element of the story is decoded: the leprosy is unbelief; the seven dippings in the Jordan are the one saving baptism, whose power is the sevenfold Spirit; the little captive maid who first names the prophet (2 Kings 5:2–3) is the grace of inspiration that Israel let slip and the nations grasped; and the restored flesh “like the flesh of a small child” (2 Kings 5:14) is the regeneration of the newly baptized. Naaman’s cure is thus not merely a healing but the whole rite of Christian initiation set out in advance.

Notes

  • A transmission node. Bede reads the entire typology off Jesus’ own Nazareth citation of Naaman (Luke 4:27) and passes the Fathers’ reading — especially the Gentile-leper figure and the sevenfold-Spirit motif drawn out earlier by Ambrose and Caesarius of Arles — into the Latin medieval schools. His commentaries became standard classroom texts, so through him the baptismal reading of Naaman becomes the common inheritance of the West.
  • Naaman’s own after-acts read sacramentally. Bede presses the figure further than most: he allegorizes not only the washing but Naaman’s later deeds. The renunciation of foreign gods becomes the baptismal renunciation of Satan; his vow to serve the Lord alone becomes the confession of faith; and his request for two mules’ burden of holy earth (2 Kings 5:17) becomes communion in the body of the Lord, by which the baptized are confirmed.
