Bede on Naaman — the Gentile leper, baptism, and the sevenfold Spirit

Dates: c. 710 (the Commentary on Luke [In Lucae evangelium expositio], on Luke 4:27; Bede b. c. 672/673, monk of Wearmouth–Jarrow, d. 735) · Wikipedia
Summary: Commenting on Jesus’ Nazareth reference to Naaman, Bede reads the episode as a figure of baptism: Naaman (“beauty”) is the Gentile people washed from the leprosy of unbelief in the sacrament of baptism, which alone saves because it “regenerates by the Holy Spirit” (in the rendering of Bede’s text quoted in Aquinas’s Catena Aurea, “that baptism saves which the seven-fold Spirit renews”); his renunciation of foreign gods and his portion of holy earth prefigure the baptismal renunciation, confession, and communion.

Claims & evidence

“Purified… through the sacrament of baptism” — Naaman the Gentile, the sevenfold Spirit, the flesh of a child (Commentary on Luke)

Jesus, preaching in the synagogue at Nazareth, recalls that in Elisha’s day “none of them was cleansed, saving Naaman the Syrian” (Luke 4:27) — a Gentile healed while lepers in Israel were passed over. Bede reads the whole episode as a figure of the Church of the nations:

And thus Naaman the Syrian, who is interpreted as “beauty,” represents the people of the nations, once stained by the leprosy of unbelief and sins, but purified from all foulness of mind and body through the sacrament of baptism. Advised by a captured girl, which is the grace of divine inspiration, which the Jews could not keep but the Gentiles seized, he was urged to hope for salvation and to be washed seven times. Because obviously, the only type of baptism that regenerates by the Holy Spirit saves. Hence it is rightly remembered that his flesh appeared like the flesh of a small child after the washing… And so that you may know all the prefigured sacraments of baptism here, in which we are commanded to renounce Satan and to confess faith, Naaman declares that he will no longer make offerings to foreign gods, but will serve the Lord alone in all things. He also rejoices to take with him a portion of the Holy Land, because the baptized must also be confirmed by participation in the body of the Lord.

— Bede, Commentary on Luke, on Luke 4:27.1 Every element of the story is decoded: the leprosy is unbelief; the seven dippings in the Jordan are the one saving baptism, which “regenerates by the Holy Spirit” (“which the seven-fold Spirit renews,” in the Catena Aurea rendering);2 the little captive maid who first names the prophet (2 Kings 5:2–3) is the grace of inspiration that Israel let slip and the nations grasped; and the restored flesh “like the flesh of a small child” (2 Kings 5:14) is read two ways: “Either because grace as a mother begets all baptized in Christ to a single infancy, or more likely he is to be understood as the child of whom it was said: A child is born to us, a son is given to us (Isaiah 9). By whose body through baptism the whole offspring of believers is united.”3 In Bede’s reading, Naaman’s cure sets out the rite of Christian initiation in advance.

Notes

  • Earlier and later use. Bede reads the typology off Jesus’ Nazareth citation of Naaman (Luke 4:27). The Gentile-leper figure appears earlier on this timeline in Ambrose and Caesarius of Arles, and the sevenfold-Spirit motif in Caesarius; in Bede’s text the motif appears in the Catena Aurea rendering, not in the translation quoted above. Bede’s commentaries were used as standard classroom texts in the medieval Latin schools ⚠ unverified.
  • Naaman’s own after-acts read sacramentally. Bede allegorizes not only the washing but Naaman’s later deeds. The renunciation of foreign gods becomes the baptismal renunciation of Satan; his vow to serve the Lord alone becomes the confession of faith; and his request for two mules’ burden of holy earth (2 Kings 5:17) becomes communion in the body of the Lord, by which the baptized are confirmed.
