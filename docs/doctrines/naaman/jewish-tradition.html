The Jewish traditions on Naaman

Dates: c. AD 93 (Josephus, Antiquities) – c. AD 1100 (Rashi) · a reception stratum, not a single author: the Second-Temple historian Josephus (Antiquities of the Jews 8, c. 93), the tannaitic–amoraic Babylonian Talmud (the baraita at b. Gittin 57b and b. Sanhedrin 96b, redacted c. 500–600), and the medieval Jewish commentators (the Aramaic Targum to Chronicles; Rashi, d. 1105) · Wikipedia
Summary: Jewish sources develop Naaman in more than one direction — one legal, one legendary, and one comparative. In law, the Talmud classes him with a single word: he was not a full proselyte (ger tzedek) but a ger toshav, a “resident” gentile who forswore idolatry and kept the Noahide commandments without taking on the whole Torah. In legend, a separate strand — already in Josephus and elaborated by the Targum, Rashi, and the midrash — makes Naaman the anonymous archer of 1 Kings 22:34 who “drew a bow at a venture” and killed King Ahab, so that the “deliverance” 2 Kings 5:1 says the LORD gave Aram “by him” was the death of Israel’s king. And in comparing the confession itself with others, the Mekhilta ranks it above the proselyte Jethro’s: where Jethro had called the LORD only “greater than all gods,” Naaman denied the rest outright — “there is no God in all the earth, but in Israel” — and so, the tradition says, “conceded more” than he.

Placement on the timeline

“The Jewish tradition” on Naaman is not one book but a stream running for a millennium. Its earliest witness is the historian Josephus (Antiquities of the Jews, written at Rome c. AD 93), who already knows the tradition that Naaman was the archer who killed Ahab — though the extant text of Josephus, which passes over most of the Elisha miracles, does not narrate the leprosy-cure of 2 Kings 5, so he carries only that legendary half. The legal classification comes later, in the Babylonian Talmud (the amoraic commentary redacted in Sasanian Babylonia by roughly AD 500–600, transmitting older tannaitic material): a terse baraita classes Naaman a ger toshav. The legend is then filled out by the medieval commentators — the Aramaic Targum to Chronicles and, above all, Rashi (Troyes, d. 1105), who welds the archer of 1 Kings 22 to the “deliverance” clause of 2 Kings 5. Because a saying first written on a later page may transmit a tradition centuries older, and because the whole reads as one cumulative Jewish portrait, this page is taken not as a single dated author but as a reception stratum. It is slotted on the timeline after the New Testament and before the Christian Fathers, since its earliest extant witness — Josephus, c. 93 — predates them, while its rabbinic core runs down into the Middle Ages.1

Claims & evidence

A “resident-convert,” not a full proselyte (b. Gittin 57b; b. Sanhedrin 96b)

Discussing which gentiles counted as converts, the Talmud classes Naaman — and pairs him with another foreign general. Nebuzaradan, the Babylonian general who burned the Temple, is held in the same passage to have become a full and righteous convert; Naaman is placed in the lesser category:

Naaman… was not a convert, as he did not accept all of the mitzvot, but rather he was a ger toshav, a gentile who resides in Eretz Israel and observes the seven Noahide mitzvot. Nebuzaradan, by contrast, was a convert, as explained previously.

— Babylonian Talmud, Gittin 57b; the same classification is given in baraita form (“The Sages taught”) at Sanhedrin 96b, “Naaman was a ger toshav… Nebuzaradan was a righteous convert [ger tzedek].”2 The passage, in the William Davidson translation, defines the category as “a gentile who resides in Eretz Israel and observes the seven Noahide mitzvot,” and gives the reason Naaman falls short of a convert: “he did not accept all of the mitzvot.” Naaman’s own confession in the biblical text is “there is no God in all the earth, but in Israel” (2 Kings 5:15); what the classification withholds is full proselyte status, the acceptance of all the commandments.

A separate item in the same passage. A few lines after the ger-toshav notice, the same passage lists converts’ descendants who “studied Torah in public.” The list names the descendants of Haman (who “studied Torah in Bnei Brak”), of Sisera (who “taught children Torah in Jerusalem”), and of Sennacherib (whose line “taught Torah in public… Shemaya and Avtalyon”). Naaman’s classification and that roll of teaching descendants are two distinct items standing side by side in the one sugya.3

The archer who felled Ahab (Josephus, Rashi, the Targum, and the midrash)

The second strand begins from a riddle in Naaman’s very first verse. Scripture introduces him with a clause the narrative does not explain: “Naaman… was a great man with his master… because by him the LORD had given deliverance unto Syria” (2 Kings 5:1). Deliverance from what, and by what feat? The Jewish tradition answers by identifying Naaman with the nameless bowman of the battle of Ramoth-gilead: “a certain man drew a bow at a venture, and smote the king of Israel between the joints of the harness” (1 Kings 22:34) — the shot that killed Ahab. On this reading the “deliverance” the LORD gave Aram “by” Naaman was the death of King Ahab.4

The identification is old — it stands already in Josephus, the earliest datable witness, who names the archer where Scripture leaves him anonymous:

there was a young nobleman belonging to king Benhadad, whose name was Naaman; he drew his bow against the enemy, and wounded the king through his breastplate, in his lungs.

— Josephus, Antiquities of the Jews 8.15.5.5 Rashi then ties the legend directly to the puzzling clause of 2 Kings 5:1, glossing “deliverance to Aram”:

He drew back his bow innocently and killed Achov [Ahab].

— Rashi on 2 Kings 5:1. The word Rashi renders “innocently” (le-tummo) is the same Hebrew word rendered “at a venture” in 1 Kings 22:34 — the verbal hook that lets him weld the two verses into one story. The Aramaic Targum to Chronicles makes the same identification at the death-scene itself, naming the archer whom Chronicles too leaves unnamed: “And Naaman, commander of the army of the king of Aram, drew the bow…” (Targum to 2 Chronicles 18:33).6 Louis Ginzberg, gathering the scattered midrash into a single narrative, gives the legend its miraculous point — that no ordinary bowshot could have done it:

Ahab was slain in a miraculous way by Naaman, at the time only a common soldier of the rank and file… God permitted Naaman’s missile to penetrate Ahab’s armor, though the latter was harder than the former.

— Louis Ginzberg, The Legends of the Jews, vol. IV.7 On this identification, the archer who brought Aram its deliverance at Ramoth-gilead is the same man later healed of leprosy who confesses the God of Israel.

Ranked above Jethro: “Naaman conceded more” (Mekhilta, Amalek 3; Deuteronomy Rabbah 2)

A third strand compares the confession itself with those of other Gentiles. The tannaitic Mekhilta, glossing the Midianite priest Jethro’s words “the LORD is greater than all gods” (Exodus 18:11) as the confession of a seeker who had tried every idolatry, sets a graded ladder of Gentile confessors of the God of Israel — and places Naaman a rung above Jethro:

There was no idolatry in the world that Yithro did not come across and did not worship, viz.: “than all the gods.” And Na‘aman was even more conceding in this regard than he, viz. (II Kings 5:15) “Now I know that there is no G–d in all the land, but only in Israel.” And thus, Rachav the harlot (Joshua 2:11) “for the L–rd your G–d is the G–d in the heavens above and on the earth below.”

— Mekhilta de-Rabbi Ishmael, Tractate Amalek 3. The measure is one of degree. Jethro’s “greater than all gods” is comparative — it ranks the LORD highest while still granting the other gods their reality — whereas Naaman’s “no God in all the earth but in Israel” denies them outright, and so “concedes more”; the harlot Rahab climbs higher still, owning the LORD in heaven and on earth. The homiletical Deuteronomy Rabbah preserves the same ascending ladder, though it reads Naaman’s as a partial acknowledgement (“no God… but in Israel” grants Him the earth but is silent on the heavens Rahab names) — a lower rung than Rahab’s, but still set above Jethro’s.8

In rabbinic tradition Jethro is a proselyte — in this same Mekhilta passage, the seeker who had tried every idol and left it — and the passage ranks the healed Syrian’s words above his. How Jethro’s own confession is read is set out on the Jethro timeline.

Notes

  • Strands of one man. The legal (ger toshav) and the legendary (Ahab’s archer) developments are independent, and each hangs off a detail of the biblical text: the first off Naaman’s confession and vow in 2 Kings 5:15–18, the second off the “deliverance to Syria” of 2 Kings 5:1, which the narrative does not explain. The comparative ranking above Jethro is a third strand, on the same confession of 5:15.
  • Where the Ahab-arrow identification is found. The identification’s datable homes are Josephus (Antiquities 8.15.5), the Targum to 2 Chronicles 18:33, Rashi on 2 Kings 5:1, and Midrash Tehillim (Shocher Tov) on Psalm 60 (the last cited here from the Jewish Encyclopedia’s survey rather than opened directly).
  • Why he was struck: two further midrashim on the leprosy. Beyond the strands above, the midrash also asks why Naaman was leprous, and answers two ways. Numbers Rabbah makes it a punishment for arrogance, reading the “great man” of 2 Kings 5:1 as a swollen one: “It is that he had an arrogance of spirit because he was a great warrior. It is due to this that he was afflicted with leprosy” (Bamidbar Rabbah 7:5). The Tanhuma gives a different cause — his seizure of the Israelite girl who would send him to the prophet: “For what reason was he a leper? Because he had taken a little girl captive from the Land of Israel” (Midrash Tanchuma, Tazria 11). Both are opened here firsthand on Sefaria; the standard reference map of the whole haggadah remains the “Naaman” article, Jewish Encyclopedia, which pairs the ger-toshav ruling with “Deuteronomy Rabbah 2”; that passage (Devarim Rabbah 2:57) is not the ger-toshav ruling but the confessors’ ladder quoted above, on which Naaman again outranks Jethro. The ger-toshav ruling’s home is the Talmud (Gittin 57b), as cited above.
  • Text and editions. The Talmud, Rashi, and the Targum are quoted from the William Davidson digital edition (Koren–Steinsaltz and the accompanying translations) on Sefaria; Josephus in William Whiston’s translation; Ginzberg from The Legends of the Jews (Philadelphia: Jewish Publication Society, 1909–1938). The standard reference map of the whole haggadah is the “Naaman” article in the Jewish Encyclopedia.
