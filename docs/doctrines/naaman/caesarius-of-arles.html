Caesarius of Arles on Naaman — the fullest figure of baptism

Dates: c. 520 (Sermon 129; Caesarius b. c. 470, bishop of Arles 502–542) · Wikipedia
Summary: Caesarius gives the most complete patristic reading of the episode as baptism: Elisha figures Christ, Naaman the Gentiles, the seven washings the sevenfold grace of the Spirit, and the flesh “like a little child” the rebirth of the baptized — cleansed in the very Jordan Christ would consecrate.

Claims & evidence

Elisha = Christ, Naaman = the Gentiles, sent to the Jordan Christ would consecrate (Sermon 129)

Where earlier witnesses had glanced at one strand or another, Caesarius draws the whole figure. The prophet who heals the foreign leper stands for Christ; the leper himself stands for the nations; and the detail that Elisha never touches Naaman becomes the mission of Christ through the apostles rather than in his own person:

This signified that before Christ was crucified, the Gentiles did not believe in Christ when he spoke in his own person, but afterwards they devoutly came to the sacrament of baptism after the preaching of the apostles. For this reason Elisha told Naaman to wash seven times in the Jordan. See, brothers: Elisha sent Naaman to the river Jordan because Christ was to send the Gentiles to baptism. Moreover, the fact that Elisha did not touch Naaman himself or baptize him showed that Christ did not come to the Gentiles himself but through his apostles… Notice further that Naaman, who prefigured the Gentiles, recovered his health in the same river that later Christ consecrated by his baptism.

— Caesarius of Arles, Sermon 129.4.1 The single river carries the whole weight of the type: the water that cleansed the Gentile leper is the water Christ himself would step into and hallow, so that Naaman’s cure and Christian baptism are one washing seen at two moments.

The seven washings, the sevenfold Spirit, and the flesh “like a little child” (Sermon 129)

Caesarius then presses the number and the outcome. The seven immersions answer to the sevenfold grace of the Holy Spirit that rested on Christ — the same Spirit that descended as a dove when Christ was baptized in this river — and the restored flesh “like the flesh of a little child” becomes the regeneration of every baptized believer, all of whom, old or young, are called infants:

“Wash seven times,” he said, because of the sevenfold grace of the Holy Spirit, which reposed in Christ our Lord. Moreover, when our Lord was baptized in this river, the Holy Spirit came on him in the form of a dove. When Naaman descended into the river as a figure of baptism, “his flesh became like the flesh of a little child.” Notice, beloved brothers, that this likeness was perfected in the Christian people, for you know that all who are baptized are still called infants, whether they are old or young… Therefore, just as Naaman, although he was an old man, became like a boy by washing seven times, so the Gentiles, although old by reason of their former sins and covered with the many spots of iniquity as with leprosy, are renewed by the grace of baptism in such a way that no leprosy of either original or actual sin remains in them.

— Caesarius of Arles, Sermon 129.5.2 The reading is complete and self-contained: the leper’s number is the Spirit’s fullness, his renewed skin is the new birth of the font, and the cleansing reaches original as well as actual sin. Naaman aged in years but young in flesh becomes the emblem of the convert reborn from the sins of a lifetime.

Notes

  • Every strand knotted into one reading. Caesarius gathers what the earlier witnesses had touched separately — the Gentile leper, the seven washings as the sevenfold Spirit, the Jordan of Christ’s own baptism, and the rebirth “like a little child” (regeneration) — and binds them into a single, sustained baptismal figure. It is the fullest development of the type in the fathers.
  • “All the baptized are called infants.” Caesarius’s move that everyone baptized, old or young, is called an infant ties the type to the doctrine that no age is exempt from the sacrament — a note that sits adjacent to the infant-baptism timeline.
