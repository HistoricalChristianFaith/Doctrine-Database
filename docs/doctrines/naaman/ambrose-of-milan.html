Ambrose of Milan on Naaman — the Jordan cleansing as the figure of baptism

Dates: c. 390 (the catechetical De mysteriis and De sacramentis, delivered to the newly baptized at Milan; Ambrose b. c. 339, bishop of Milan 374–397) · Wikipedia
Summary: In his mystagogical lectures Ambrose makes Naaman the standing type of the baptized Gentile: he was cleansed not by the water but by grace; only the water that has the grace of Christ heals; and the captive servant-maid who sent him to the prophet is the Church gathered from the nations.

Claims & evidence

“Not of the waters but of grace that a man is cleansed” (De mysteriis)

Expounding to the newly baptized the meaning of the rite they had just received, Ambrose reaches back to the reading from Kings and retells Naaman’s sevenfold dipping in the Jordan as the pattern of what has happened to them. The lesson he draws is that the cure lay never in the element itself but in the grace it carried:

Lastly, let the lessons lately gone through from the Kings teach you. Naaman was a Syrian, and suffered from leprosy, nor could he be cleansed by any. Then a maiden from among the captives said that there was a prophet in Israel, who could cleanse him from the defilement of the leprosy… And when he had come, he bade him dip himself seven times in the river Jordan. Then he began to reason with himself that he had better waters in his own country, in which he had often bathed and never been cleansed of his leprosy; and so remembering this, he did not obey the command of the prophet, yet on the advice and persuasion of his servants he yielded and dipped himself. And being forthwith cleansed, he understood that it is not of the waters but of grace that a man is cleansed.

— Ambrose, Concerning the Mysteries (De mysteriis) 3.16–17.1 Naaman’s hesitation — his own Syrian rivers were finer than the muddy Jordan — is exactly the doubt Ambrose expects in a catechumen who cannot see how plain water washes the soul. The point of the type is that the Jordan had no natural virtue the Abana and Pharpar lacked; what made it heal was the grace set upon it, precisely as the baptismal font heals not as water but as consecrated water.

The captive maid is the Church of the Gentiles (De mysteriis)

Ambrose then reads the minor figures of the story allegorically. The little captive servant-girl who first named the prophet of Israel is not a bystander but the type of the Church drawn out of the nations — herself once held in the captivity of sin, yet the one who directs the Gentile to his cleansing:

Understand now who is that young maid among the captives. She is the congregation gathered out of the Gentiles, that is, the Church of God held down of old by the captivity of sin, when as yet it possessed not the liberty of grace, by whose counsel that foolish people of the Gentiles heard the word of prophecy as to which it had before been in doubt. Afterwards, however, when they believed that it ought to be obeyed, they were washed from every defilement of sin. And he indeed doubted before he was healed; you are already healed, and therefore ought not to doubt.

— Ambrose, Concerning the Mysteries (De mysteriis) 3.18.2 The turn at the end is pastoral: Naaman doubted before his cure, but the newly baptized are already healed, and so have no ground left for doubt. The whole episode becomes a mirror of the Gentile congregation’s own passage from the captivity of sin to the liberty of grace — the leper Naaman, the counselling maid, and the washing all mapped onto the Church and the font.

“Only the water that has the grace of Christ can heal” — element and consecration (De sacramentis)

In the parallel course of the De sacramentis, Ambrose states the underlying principle bare. Water in itself does not save; it is the descent and consecration of the Holy Spirit that makes an ordinary element into a sacrament — the distinction on which his whole reading of Naaman turns:

So what does it mean? You saw water: but not all water heals; but the water that has the grace of Christ heals. One thing is the element, another is the consecration: one thing is the work, another is the operation. Water is the work, the operation is of the Holy Spirit. Water does not heal unless the Spirit descends and consecrates that water.

— Ambrose, On the Sacraments (De sacramentis) 1.5.3 This is the doctrinal core the Naaman narrative is enlisted to teach. “One thing is the element, another is the consecration” explains why the Jordan could cleanse where the rivers of Damascus could not, and why the font cleanses where a bath does not: not the substance but the Spirit’s operation upon it. Naaman’s cleansing “not of the waters but of grace” is the same claim told as a story.

Notes

  • This is mystagogy, not ornament. Ambrose is delivering these lectures to the just-baptized, explaining the meaning of the rite they have already received — so Naaman is not decorative illustration but a catechetical proof that the power in the water is the Spirit’s, not the element’s. The type does real work: it answers the new Christian’s natural doubt that plain water could wash the soul by showing, in a familiar Scripture reading, that the same water heals or fails to heal depending on the grace upon it.
  • Part of a whole theology of baptismal necessity. The same Ambrose grounds infant baptism from the circumcision-type elsewhere — see Ambrose on infant baptism — so his Naaman typology does not stand alone but sits inside a fully worked-out account of why the washing is needed and what it accomplishes.
