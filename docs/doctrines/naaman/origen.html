Origen on Naaman — the mystery of “our Jordan”

Dates: c. 233 (the Commentary on the Gospel of John, Book 6, written after his move to Caesarea; Origen b. c. 185 at Alexandria, d. c. 253 at Tyre) · Wikipedia
Summary: Origen reads the Jordan of Naaman as “our Jordan,” the singular river of grace that cleanses the leper — for as none is good but God the Father, so among rivers none is good but the Jordan; and the true cleansing of leprosy belongs to the Lord Jesus alone. The reading is Jordan-mystical: it points at Christian cleansing without yet naming the rite as such.

Claims & evidence

“None is good but the Jordan” — the river of grace where Christ cleanses the leper (Commentary on John, Book 6)

Origen turns to Naaman while expounding the Jordan of the Gospel — the river of Christ’s own baptism — and finds in the Syrian’s reluctant washing a figure of the one water that heals. The point is not the prophet, nor the act of dipping seven times, but the river: the Jordan itself is the cleansing agent, and the one who truly puts his hand on the leprosy is the Lord Jesus:

Then Naaman is angry; he does not see that our Jordan is the cleanser of those who are impure from leprosy, from that impurity, and their restorer to health; it is the Jordan that does this, and not the prophet; the office of the prophet is to direct to the healing agency. Naaman then says, not understanding the great mystery of the Jordan, “Behold, I said that he will certainly come out to me, and will call upon the name of the Lord his God, and lay his hand upon the place, and restore the leper.” For to put his hand on the leprosy and cleanse it is a work belonging to our Lord Jesus only… For as none is good but one, God the Father, so among rivers none is good but the Jordan, nor able to cleanse from his leprosy him who with faith washes his soul in Jesus.

— Origen, Commentary on the Gospel of John 6.242–245.1 The move is a deliberate transfer of Christ’s own words — “none is good but one, God” (Mark 10:18) — onto the rivers: as the Father alone is good among beings, so the Jordan alone is good among waters. The “great mystery of the Jordan” is the thing Naaman fails to grasp when he expects a mere gesture of the prophet; the river’s grace, not the man’s hand, restores him, and behind the river stands the only true cleanser of leprosy, the Lord Jesus.

Notes

  • Baptism-adjacent, not yet the rite. Origen’s accent falls on the Jordan itself as the river of grace and on Christ as the sole cleanser of leprosy — so his reading is baptism-adjacent (the mystery of the river of Christian cleansing) more than an explicit doctrine of the sacrament as such. What he sketches as the grace of “our Jordan” the later Latin catechists — Ambrose, Caesarius of Arles, Bede — would spell out as Naaman’s sevenfold washing prefiguring the water of baptism.
  • The nerve of the later typology. The phrase “washes his soul in Jesus with faith” already binds the water to faith and to Christ — the very nerve of the developed baptismal reading. Origen has the elements in hand (the singular river, the cleansing of the leper, the soul washed in Christ through faith); the step his successors take is only to name the rite that this river signifies.
