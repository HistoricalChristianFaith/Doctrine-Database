The Donation of Constantine: a forgery convicted out of its own Latin?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: sound — the interpretation holds and is near-consensus: the Constitutum Constantini is a later (eighth-century) fabrication, demonstrable on internal grounds — anachronistic Latin, institutions that did not yet exist, and a self-contradictory date — and no modern scholarship defends its authenticity. Two honest limits keep it from being a clean win for its most famous proponent: Valla’s 1440 tract is a politically-occasioned rhetorical showpiece, not a neutral audit, and unmasking the document removes one prop of the medieval papacy’s temporal claims without by itself refuting the others · advanced by Lorenzo Valla (and, independently and earlier, Nicholas of Cusa and Reginald Pecock)

Related arguments: sacred philology — see also The Comma Johanneum: did ad fontes criticism correct the text, or unsettle it?

The claim

O thou scoundrel, thou villain! … And you say that within the first days, immediately, the Senate, the nobles, the satraps, as though already Christians, with the Caesar passed decrees for the honoring of the Roman church! What! How do you want to have satraps come in here? Numskull, blockhead! Do the Caesars speak thus; are Roman decrees usually drafted thus? Whoever heard of satraps being mentioned in the councils of the Romans?

— Lorenzo Valla, The Discourse on the Forgery of the Alleged Donation of Constantine (1440), trans. Christopher B. Coleman.1 The Constitutum Constantini purported to record the emperor Constantine’s grant to Pope Sylvester and his successors of Rome, Italy, and the whole Western empire — the parchment on which the medieval papacy’s temporal claims most heavily leaned. The interpretation to weigh: is the Donation a later forgery, demonstrable on internal linguistic and historical grounds? — the paradigm case of humanist philology turned on an ecclesiastical document, and, with the Comma Johanneum, one of the two sharpest tests of what the ad fontes method could do to a text authority had leaned on.

Who advances it

The verdict was reached three times over in the fifteenth century, and by men who did not depend on one another. Nicholas of Cusa argued it first, in his conciliarist treatise De concordantia catholica III.2 (1433), on historical grounds — the Donation is mentioned in none of the church histories of its supposed age, and the record shows the popes acknowledging imperial authority in the West down to the eighth century; Cusa called the document a dictamen apocryphum, an apocryphal composition.2 Lorenzo Valla gave the case its decisive philological form in his 1440 Declamatio, convicting the charter out of its own Latin and chronology. And Reginald Pecock, Bishop of Chichester, reached the same conclusion independently in England in The Repressor of over much Blaming of the Clergy (c. 1450–57).3 The counter-position — that the Donation is genuine — had defenders among canonists and jurists who went on quoting it into the sixteenth century, but it has no living advocacy: even Cardinal Baronius, the great Catholic annalist, conceded the forgery (in the Annales Ecclesiastici, 1588–1607), after which the judgment became all but universal.4

The evidence for

The case is internal, and it is overdetermined — the text convicts itself on several independent counts. Anachronistic vocabulary. A fourth-century Roman chancery would not have written of satraps, a Persian title Valla could not recall ever meeting in a Roman decree, and the charter deploys later feudal language (a “fief”) centuries out of Constantine’s reach. Institutions that did not yet exist. The document names Constantinople among the great patriarchal sees — on the third day, it says, after Constantine’s baptism — when, as Valla put it, the city was “not yet a patriarchate, nor a see, nor a Christian city, nor named Constantinople, nor founded, nor planned” (Byzantium still occupied the site).1 A self-contradictory date. The charter dates itself by the fourth consulate of Constantine (315) together with the consulate of Gallicanus (317) — two datings that cannot name the same year, an impossibility on the document’s own face.5 And the positive picture that has replaced it is secure: modern diplomatics places the composition in the eighth century, in the orbit of the papal–Frankish alliance under Pope Stephen II and Pepin the Short (with Corbie Abbey among the proposed places of manufacture) — exactly the era whose language and institutions the text betrays.5 On this evidence the demonstration is not a clever conjecture but a settled result, and Valla’s tract is routinely named a founding moment of modern textual criticism.6

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The core finding — the Donation is a later forgery, provable from the text itself — is as close to consensus as any conclusion in this field, and it does not rest on any one prop. Strip out Valla’s weakest flourishes and the anachronistic vocabulary, the impossible institutions, the self-contradictory date, the silence of the early church histories, and the positive eighth-century provenance each still point the same way. That three scholars in three countries reached it independently, and that the document’s own defender-in-chief (Baronius) finally conceded it, is the mark of a genuinely overdetermined case.

What is overstated or should be flagged. Two things keep the verdict honest without touching its core. (1) Valla’s tract is a polemic, not a neutral audit. He wrote it in 1440 as secretary to Alfonso of Aragon, who was then at war with Pope Eugenius IV over the Kingdom of Naples — a kingdom the papacy claimed the right to dispose of partly on the strength of the Donation. The Declamatio is soaked in invective (“Numskull, blockhead!”) and aimed to strip the pope of a title-deed, and some of its arguments are rhetorical rather than probative. That political occasion does not make the philology wrong — the anachronisms are real whoever points them out, and Cusa and Pecock found them without Valla’s patron — but it means the tract should be read as advocacy that happened to be right, not as disinterested science. (This is flagged on Valla’s own page.) (2) Exposing the document is not the same as refuting everything it propped. Valla’s polemic slides from “the charter is forged” toward “the papacy has no temporal right at all”; the narrower conclusion is what the evidence establishes. The medieval papacy’s temporal power rested also on the real Donation of Pepin (754–756) and on centuries of actual possession, so unmasking the forgery removed one prop, not the whole edifice. The interpretation this page rates is the textual one — the Donation is a forgery — and on that question the wider political argument is a separate matter.

Assessment

Sound. On the interpretation it serves — that the Constitutum Constantini is a later forgery, demonstrable on internal linguistic and historical grounds — the argument holds and is near-consensus. The text betrays itself by anachronistic vocabulary, by naming institutions (a patriarchal Constantinople) that did not yet exist, and by a date it cannot make consistent; the early church histories are silent where they should be loud; and modern scholarship has replaced the forgery with a positive eighth-century provenance. What should be kept distinct from the finding, not folded into it, is the tract’s character and reach: Valla’s 1440 Declamatio is a politically-occasioned, invective-laden polemic that was right about the philology, and its exposure of the parchment does not by itself dissolve every temporal claim the parchment was used to support — those overreaches belong on the proponent’s page, not against the textual verdict, which stands. (For the sibling case of ad fontes criticism turned on a sacred text — the same method, the same “does it correct or corrode?” question — see the Comma Johanneum argument.)
