Is the Donation of Constantine Constantine’s own grant, and if not, when and where was it composed?

Disputed reading · on Christian Humanism (ad fontes / bonae litterae) · witness: Lorenzo Valla

Related readings: Is the Comma Johanneum (1 John 5:7–8) part of the epistle, or a later Latin gloss?

The passage

O thou scoundrel, thou villain! … And you say that within the first days, immediately, the Senate, the nobles, the satraps, as though already Christians, with the Caesar passed decrees for the honoring of the Roman church! What! How do you want to have satraps come in here? Numskull, blockhead! Do the Caesars speak thus; are Roman decrees usually drafted thus? Whoever heard of satraps being mentioned in the councils of the Romans?

— Lorenzo Valla, The Discourse on the Forgery of the Alleged Donation of Constantine (1440), trans. Christopher B. Coleman.1

The question

The Constitutum Constantini presents itself as the emperor Constantine’s grant to Pope Sylvester and his successors of honours, of the city of Rome, and of the provinces of the West. Popes and canonists in the Latin West, and canonists in the Greek East, cited it from the eleventh century; in the fifteenth Nicholas of Cusa, Lorenzo Valla and Reginald Pecock argued that it was not Constantine’s. What is disputed is whether the document is the emperor’s grant, and, among those who hold it a later composition, where and when it was written. Valla wrote in 1440 as secretary to Alfonso, king of Aragon, Sicily and Naples, then conducting a campaign against Pope Eugenius IV in furtherance of his claims to Italian territories.2 Johann Peter Kirsch (1909) reports that after Baronius the document’s falsity was “soon universally admitted”; no modern defender of its authenticity was located for this page.3

Constantine’s grant to Sylvester

In brief: the document records a real grant by Constantine to the Roman see, and stands in the canon law of both East and West.

Held by: Leo IX (Catholic), letter to Michael Cerularius (1054); Innocent III (Catholic), Sermo de sancto Silvestro; Theodore Balsamon (Orthodox canonist), canonical collection (1169 onward); Matthew Blastares (Orthodox canonist), Syntagma (c. 1335).3,4

Leo IX, the first pope Kirsch finds using the document in an official act, cited it in 1054 to show that the Holy See possessed both an earthly and a heavenly imperium, “the royal priesthood.” Anselm of Lucca and Cardinal Deusdedit placed it in their collections of canons; it was added to Gratian’s Decretum as a Palea; Urban II used it in 1091 for his claim to Corsica; and Innocent III, Gregory IX and Innocent IV took its authority for granted. On Kirsch’s account the medieval adversaries of the popes “never denied the validity of this appeal,” contesting only the legal deductions drawn from it, and its authenticity “was doubted by no one before the fifteenth century.”3

The document was equally at home in the East. It appears in Balsamon’s collection and was admitted by Blastares into his; Kirsch reports that it was “highly esteemed in the Greek East,” where it was read as granting to the clergy of New Rome the privileges it gave to the pope and the Roman clergy.3 Balsamon used it to justify Michael Cerularius’s dismissal of the papal legation in 1054.4 On this reading the Constitutum is a received imperial charter, transmitted in the canonical collections of both churches and used by each to set out the honours of its see.

A later composition, betrayed by its language and chronology

In brief: the text uses words, institutions and dates that cannot be Constantine’s; it was composed centuries later, at Rome in the eighth century or in the Frankish realm in the ninth.

Held by: Nicholas of Cusa (Catholic), De concordantia catholica III.2 (1433);5 Lorenzo Valla (Catholic), Declamatio (1440);1 Reginald Pecock (Catholic bishop), The Repressor of over much Blaming of the Clergy (c. 1450–57);6 Caesar Baronius (Catholic), Annales Ecclesiastici (1588–1607).3,7

Cusa, writing on historical grounds, found the grant in none of the church histories of its supposed age and the popes acknowledging imperial authority in the West into the eighth century, and called the document a dictamen apocryphum.5 Valla argued from within the text. No Roman decree, he held, spoke of satraps; and the charter names Constantinople among the patriarchal sees on the third day after Constantine’s baptism, “when it was not yet a patriarchate, nor a see, nor a Christian city, nor named Constantinople, nor founded, nor planned.” Coleman notes that satrap was applied to higher officials at Rome only in the mid-eighth century.1 The charter dates itself by Constantine’s fourth consulate (315) together with the consulate of Gallicanus (317), and uses terms such as “fief” drawn from a later age.8 Pecock reached a similar conclusion independently in England.6

Holders of this reading place the composition differently. Most of the writers Kirsch surveys locate it at Rome between 752 and 795, in connection with the popes’ dealings with Pepin (Döllinger, Hauck, Friedrich under Stephen II; Scheffer-Boichorst and Mayer under Paul I; Langen and Loening under Adrian I); Hergenröther and Grauert locate it in the Frankish realm, Grauert at St-Denis between 840 and 850, and Kirsch holds that view “rightly tenable” against the majority; Johannes Fried (2007) dates it to the ninth century and places it at Corbie.3,8 On every placement the document belongs to the age whose language, sources and politics it reflects, and Valla’s treatise is named a founding moment of textual criticism.9
