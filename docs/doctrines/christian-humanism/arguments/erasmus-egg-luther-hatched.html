Did Erasmus “lay the egg that Luther hatched”?

Disputed reading · on Christian Humanism (ad fontes / bonae litterae) · witness: Erasmus of Rotterdam

Related readings: Is Erasmus’s philosophia Christi a piety of grace and rebirth, or chiefly an ethic of imitation?

The passage

Erasmus laid the egg, and Luther hatched it.

— the saying current against Erasmus in the early Reformation, as reported in the Erasmus–Luther literature.1 Erasmus is reported to have answered: “I laid a hen’s egg; Luther hatched a bird of quite a different feather.”2

The question

Erasmus’s Greek New Testament, his annotations and his writings on inward, Scripture-centred piety preceded Luther’s public protest, and Luther used them; in 1524–25 the two divided over the freedom of the will. What is disputed is whether Erasmus’s humanist scholarship and reform prepared and made possible Luther’s Reformation, or whether the two were distinct programmes that stood together for a time against common opponents. Arnoud Visser (2017) writes that “Luther’s precise debt to Erasmus has remained an elusive problem.”3 A Catholic historian later than Sauer (1909) taking either reading was not located for this page.

Humanism prepared the Reformation

In brief: Luther’s movement took its Greek text, some of its key readings, its critique of ceremony and its lay audience from Erasmus’s work.

Held by: the theologians of Louvain (Catholic, early 1520s, as a charge against Erasmus);4 Joseph Sauer (Catholic), “Desiderius Erasmus,” Catholic Encyclopedia (1909);4 Preserved Smith, historian, Luther’s Correspondence (1913);5 Bernd Moeller, historian, “Die deutschen Humanisten und die Anfänge der Reformation” (1959), ⚠ unverified.6

The charge began among Erasmus’s Catholic critics, the theologians of Louvain foremost among them, who held him answerable for Luther. Sauer gives it a historian’s form: Erasmus’s works up to 1517 “made him the intellectual father of the Reformation,” for “what the Reformation destroyed in the organic life of the Church Erasmus had already openly or covertly subverted in a moral sense” in the Praise of Folly, the Adagia and the Colloquia, and “his bitter sarcasm had, indeed, done much to prepare the way for the Reformation.”4

Its holders name the debts: the New Testament Luther lectured on was Erasmus’s Novum Instrumentum of 1516.7 Erasmus’s note on Matthew 3:2 rendered metanoeite as resipiscite, an inward change of mind, against the Vulgate’s poenitentiam agite; Luther’s first thesis of 1517 reads repentance as the whole life of believers.8 In 1518 Luther told Staupitz that “by the favor of the learned men who taught me Hebrew and Greek” he learned that the Greek word is metanoia, “coming to one’s right mind, afterwards”; Smith notes that Luther “is apparently referring to the study of the New Testament in Greek edited by Erasmus” and that “in this letter he follows Erasmus’ note to Matthew iii. 2.”5 The Enchiridion (1503) and Paraclesis (1516) had already argued for an inward lay religion fed on Scripture.9 Moeller compressed the case into the dictum “Ohne Humanismus keine Reformation” (without humanism, no Reformation).6 On this reading humanism supplied the Reformation with its text, its tools and its readers.

Two programmes that met and parted

In brief: Erasmus sought a renewal of learning and piety within the existing church, Luther a theology of grace; they shared opponents for a few years, not a programme.

Held by: Erasmus, Spongia (1523) and De libero arbitrio (1524);10 Martin Luther (grants his debt to Erasmus’s Greek, 1518; rejects a shared theology), letters (1516–17) and De servo arbitrio (1525);11 Erika Rummel, historian, The Confessionalization of Humanism in Reformation Germany (2000), ⚠ unverified;12 David C. Fink (2015; he grants the saying’s ground in the metanoia reading).13

Erasmus spent his last two decades answering the accusation that he had inspired the Reformation. He refused to join Luther, defended himself in the Spongia against being enlisted in Luther’s cause, and in 1524 wrote De libero arbitrio in defence of the freedom of the will.10 His reported retort, a hen’s egg and “a bird of quite a different feather,” states the reading. Fink describes Erasmus as seeing himself “as calling the church to return to the sources of its own most profound insights; Luther he regarded as a dangerous radical.”13

Luther marked the difference from the start. In October 1516 he objected that Erasmus, interpreting Paul, understood the righteousness of works or of the law to mean only “ceremonial and figurative observances,” and “will not have the apostle speak of original sin, in Romans, chapter V”; in March 1517 he feared Erasmus “does not sufficiently advance the cause of Christ and God’s grace.”11 In 1525 he thanked Erasmus for attacking “the vital spot” of their dispute, the bondage of the will, rather than the papacy or indulgences.14 Rummel, as her reviewer Matthias Pohlig summarizes her, holds that the early identification of reformers with humanists rested on a misunderstanding of surface agreements, humanism being a cultural movement and the Reformation pursuing religious aims.12 On this reading humanist learning and Luther’s theology of grace ran side by side against the same scholastic opponents and then divided along a line present from the beginning.
