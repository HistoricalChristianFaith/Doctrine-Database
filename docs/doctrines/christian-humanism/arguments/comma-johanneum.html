Is the Comma Johanneum (1 John 5:7–8) part of the epistle, or a later Latin gloss?

Disputed reading · on Christian Humanism (ad fontes / bonae litterae) · witness: Erasmus of Rotterdam

Related readings: Is the Donation of Constantine Constantine’s own grant, and if not, when and where was it composed? · Why did Erasmus’s Greek New Testament, and not the Complutensian, become the base of the received text?

The passage

For there are three that bear record in heaven, the Father, the Word, and the Holy Ghost: and these three are one. And there are three that bear witness in earth, the Spirit, and the water, and the blood: and these three agree in one.

— 1 John 5:7–8 in the King James Version, the verse as the “received text” carried it. The words from “in heaven” to “in earth” are the Comma Johanneum, the words that modern critical editions of the Greek New Testament do not print.1

The question

Erasmus left the Comma out of his Greek New Testament in 1516 and 1519, finding it in none of the Greek manuscripts he consulted; Edward Lee and Diego López de Zúñiga (Stunica) attacked the omission, and in 1522 he printed the words from a single Greek manuscript, the “British codex” (Codex Montfortianus), with an annotation stating that he believed that copy had been altered to agree with the Vulgate.2 What is disputed is whether the words belong to John’s letter and were lost from the Greek copies, or arose as a Latin gloss and entered the text later; and whether manuscript evidence alone decides whether they are read as Scripture.

How the words entered the printed text is itself told two ways. Edward F. Hills (1956) recounts that Erasmus agreed to restore the Comma if a single Greek manuscript containing it were found, and included it in 1522 “bound by his promise.”3 H. J. de Jonge (1980) found no such promise in Erasmus’s works, traced the story no earlier than T. H. Horne (1818), and holds that Erasmus included the passage out of care for his good name and for the success of his New Testament.4

A genuine part of the epistle, lost from the Greek copies

In brief: the words belong to John’s letter; unfaithful translators and copyists dropped them, and the Latin church preserved them.

Held by: the Prologue to the Catholic Epistles ascribed to Jerome, in Codex Fuldensis (541–546);5 Edward Lee (Catholic), Annotationes (1520);6 the Orthodox Confession of Peter Mogila (Orthodox, 1643; cites the verse as Scripture only);7 Edward F. Hills (Protestant), The King James Version Defended (1956), as “at least possibly genuine.”3,8

The oldest Vulgate manuscript carries a prologue complaining that “some unfaithful translators have gone far astray from the truth of the faith,” giving only water, blood and spirit and omitting “the testimony of the Father, the Word and the Spirit, by which the Catholic faith is especially strengthened.”5 Lee quoted that prologue in 1520 to show the passage “adulterated by heretics,” asked whether “what is in the Greek manuscripts” should be treated “as possessing the status of an oracle,” and held the Comma the single most important passage in Scripture for the doctrine of the Trinity.6 The Complutensian Polyglot printed it in Greek, its editors stating that they took their reading from a Rhodian manuscript at Alcalá.8 The Orthodox Confession cites 1 John 5:7 as the Evangelist’s teaching that Father, Son and Holy Spirit are consubstantial.7

Hills traces the reading through the Latin church: the passage “seems to have been quoted at Carthage by Cyprian (c. 250)” (“And again concerning the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit it is written: and the Three are One”), and with Scrivener he holds it “surely safer and more candid” to admit that Cyprian read it;9 Priscillian and his accuser Idacius Clarus cite it in the fourth century, African writers against the Vandals in the fifth, and Old Latin witnesses carry it. He asks why, if the words grew from a trinitarian reading of verse 8, they do not use the usual formula “the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit.” On this reading the Comma is a reading that dropped out of the Greek text but was kept in the Latin through the usage of the Latin-speaking church, behind which Hills sees the guiding providence of God.3

A Latin gloss on the earthly witnesses that entered the text

In brief: the words began as a Latin reading of the Spirit, the water and the blood as a figure of the Trinity, and passed from the margin into Latin Bibles and from them into a few late Greek copies.

Held by: Erasmus (Catholic), Annotationes (1516–1535);10 Richard Simon (Catholic priest), Critical History of the Text of the New Testament (English, 1689) ⚠ unverified;11 Bruce M. Metzger (critical scholarship), A Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament (2nd ed., 1994);12 Basileios Antoniades (Orthodox), editor of the Patriarchal edition of the New Testament (1904), ⚠ unverified.13,14

Erasmus’s 1516 annotation reported that in the Greek he found only the threefold witness of spirit, water and blood. Answering Lee and Stunica, he pointed out that Cyril of Alexandria, citing 1 John 5 against the Arians, omits the Comma, which he would have used had he known it, and he reported two old manuscripts at St Donatian in Bruges that lacked it.10

Metzger sets out the case in full. The passage is absent from every known Greek manuscript except eight, late ones that appear to translate a late Latin recension; no Greek father quotes it in the Sabellian or Arian controversies; its first appearance in Greek is a Greek version of the acts of the Fourth Lateran Council (1215); every ancient version but the Latin lacks it; and it is absent from the early Old Latin (Tertullian, Cyprian, Augustine) and from the Vulgate as Jerome issued it (Codex Fuldensis, Codex Amiatinus). Its earliest citation as part of the epistle is the fourth-century Latin Liber Apologeticus of Priscillian or Instantius. On this account the gloss arose when the three witnesses were understood as a symbol of the Trinity, was perhaps written first in the margin, and spread through Old Latin and Vulgate manuscripts from the sixth century. No reason is found why copyists of hundreds of Greek manuscripts would have dropped it, and the words break the sense of the passage.12 Antoniades, as quoted by Maurice A. Robinson, found the passage “entirely unattested in church texts, in the fathers and teachers of the Eastern Church, in the ancient versions,” and printed it in small type.13

A question for the Church’s judgment, not for textual criticism alone

In brief: whatever the manuscripts show, whether the passage is read as Scripture in the Church is a judgment the Church’s authority makes.

Held by: the Holy Office (Catholic), decree of 13 January 1897;15 the Holy Synod of the Ecumenical Patriarchate (Orthodox), in retaining the passage in the 1904 Patriarchal edition, ⚠ unverified;13 Walter Drum (Catholic), “Epistles of Saint John,” Catholic Encyclopedia (1910; he grants that textual criticism tells against the passage).15

Drum reads the question through the Council of Trent, which received “the entire books with all their parts, as these have been wont to be read in the Catholic Church and are contained in the old Latin Vulgate.” Neither condition, he holds, has been verified with certainty for the Comma, and textual criticism “seems to indicate” that it was not everywhere read and is not in the original Vulgate. “However, the Catholic theologian must take into account more than textual criticism”: the Holy Office decreed on 13 January 1897, with Leo XIII’s approval two days later, that the authenticity of the passage “may not with safety (tuto) be denied or called into doubt,” and Drum holds that further discussion must be carried on “with due deference to this decree.”15 A further declaration of 2 June 1927 left the passage open to investigation by Catholic scholars who promised to accept the Church’s judgment.16 In the Orthodox Patriarchal edition of 1904, Antoniades reports, the passage “is retained [solely] upon the opinion of the Holy Synod.”13 On this reading the Church that received the text is the guardian of what it reads, and manuscripts inform that judgment without settling it.
