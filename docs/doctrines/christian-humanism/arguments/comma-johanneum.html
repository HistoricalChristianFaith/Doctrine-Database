The Comma Johanneum: did ad fontes criticism correct the text, or unsettle it?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: sound — on the textual question the humanist method got it right: the “three heavenly witnesses” of 1 John 5:7 are a late Latin interpolation, absent from every early Greek witness and from the Greek fathers, so removing them recovered the authentic reading rather than corrupting it. The “it corrodes the church’s authorized text” objection is answered, not ignored: correcting a demonstrable gloss is the opposite of corroding the text. Two popular embellishments — that Erasmus made a rash promise and that Codex Montfortianus was forged to trap him — are themselves later legends that do not bear on the verdict · at issue: Erasmus of Rotterdam

The claim

For there are three that bear record in heaven, the Father, the Word, and the Holy Ghost: and these three are one. And there are three that bear witness in earth, the Spirit, and the water, and the blood: and these three agree in one.

— 1 John 5:7–8 in the King James Version, the verse as the “received text” carried it. The italicized words — in heaven, the Father, the Word, and the Holy Ghost: and these three are one. And there are three that bear witness in earth — are the Comma Johanneum: the explicit trinitarian gloss that the modern critical text lacks, so that the authentic verse reads only “there are three that bear witness, the Spirit, and the water, and the blood.”1 When Erasmus left the Comma out of the first Greek New Testament in 1516 — because he found it in no Greek manuscript — his critics said the grammarians were unsettling the church’s authorized text and stripping the Trinity of a proof-text. The interpretation to weigh: did the humanist ad fontes method here correct the received text, recovering the true reading, or unsettle it, throwing doubt on an authorized verse? This is the sharpest single test case of the whole program.

Who advances it

The “it corrodes authority” charge was pressed in Erasmus’s own day by Edward Lee and by Diego López de Zúñiga (Stunica), who took the omission of a trinitarian verse as evidence that philology in lay hands endangered dogma; it survives today in the “King-James-only” defence of the Textus Receptus, which holds the Comma authentic and its removal a modern corruption. The opposite verdict — that the method corrected the text — is the settled position of textual scholarship from Richard Simon and Isaac Newton down to Bruce Metzger and Grantley McDonald: the Comma is a Latin interpolation that the return to the Greek rightly exposed.2 The two readings cannot both stand, and the manuscript evidence decides between them.

The evidence for

The case that the correction recovered the true text is as strong as any in New Testament criticism. The Greek is silent. Of roughly five hundred Greek manuscripts of 1 John, only four carry the Comma in the text — minuscule 629 (a fourteenth-century Greek–Latin diglot), Codex Montfortianus (minuscule 61, of Erasmus’s own era), and two very late copies (2318 and 2473) — with a handful more adding it as a still later margin-note; every one shows the marks of translation from the Latin, not an inherited Greek reading.3 The Greek fathers never cite it. Through the fourth-century trinitarian and Arian controversies, when an explicit “the Father, the Word, and the Holy Spirit… these three are one” would have been the decisive proof-text, no Greek father quotes it — not at Nicaea, not Athanasius, not the Cappadocians — which they could hardly have passed over had it stood in their copies. Its trail is Latin and late. The gloss most likely began as a marginal note reading the three earthly witnesses (Spirit, water, blood) as a figure of the Trinity; the earliest citation of it as part of the epistle’s text is in the Latin Liber Apologeticus of Priscillian (or his follower Instantius), c. 380; from the fifth century North-African and Italian Latin fathers quote it, and from the sixth it spreads through Old-Latin and Vulgate manuscripts — while the oldest and best Vulgate codices (Fuldensis, Amiatinus) still lack it.4 The steelman for the older reading — Cyprian, c. 250, writing “it is written of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit, ‘And these three are one’” — is the one real early trace, and it cuts the other way: Cyprian is best read as giving the allegorical sense of the earthly witnesses, the very interpretation that later hardened into the interpolated text, not as quoting a Greek Comma he could not have had.5 On this evidence Erasmus’s omission did not delete a verse of Scripture; it declined to print a Latin gloss that the Greek never knew.

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The textual verdict is near-consensus and load-bearing: the Comma is absent from every Greek witness before the fourteenth century, from the Greek fathers, and from the earliest Latin Bibles, and its diffusion is a documented Latin drift from margin to text. On the narrow question the lead poses — correct or unsettle? — the answer is correct: going back to the fountain-head recovered what the fathers and the best manuscripts actually attest, exactly the promise of the ad fontes program.

What is overstated or imprecise — on both sides. Three cautions keep the verdict honest. (1) The “corrodes authority” objection is not baseless in form, only in result. It is true that overturning a verse the church had read, sung, and dogmatized for a thousand years is a genuine disturbance of an authorized text — the critics were right that something authoritative was being unsettled. The answer is that what was unsettled was a later accretion, not the apostolic text; correcting a demonstrable interpolation restores the church’s Scripture rather than corroding it, and the doctrine of the Trinity never rested on this verse (it is taught across the New Testament read whole, as the fathers who lacked the Comma held it). The disturbance is real; the charge of corrosion fails. (2) The “Comma is authentic” counter-thesis is genuinely advocated but does not hold. The King-James-only defence must either dismiss the entire Greek and patristic record or credit a cluster of very late, Latin-dependent copies against it; that is special pleading, not a live textual case. (3) Two favourite details of the story are legends that should be dropped. The popular tale that Erasmus made a rash promise or wager to insert the Comma if shown one Greek manuscript, and then that Codex Montfortianus was forged to trap him, cannot be found in Erasmus’s own works or letters; H. J. de Jonge traced the “promise” no earlier than 1818, and Metzger himself later added a footnote conceding the point. Erasmus added the Comma in 1522 not to keep a vow but to spare himself the charge of unorthodoxy — and printed it with a long note doubting its authenticity.6 None of these touches the textual finding; they only clear away the melodrama around it.

Assessment

Sound. On the interpretation it serves — that the humanist return to the sources corrected the text here rather than corrupting it — the argument holds and is close to consensus. The Comma Johanneum is a late Latin interpolation: no early Greek manuscript has it, no Greek father cites it in the centuries when they most needed it, and its documented path runs from a fourth-century Latin allegory into the Vulgate and only thence, in Erasmus’s own age, into a few Latinizing Greek copies. Erasmus’s omission recovered the apostolic reading; the modern critical editions (Nestle–Aland, UBS) follow him, and the verse survives only in Bibles descended from the Textus Receptus. The “it unsettles the authorized text” objection names a real disturbance but mislabels it as corrosion: an interpolation removed is Scripture restored, not lost, and the Trinity was confessed by the very fathers whose text lacked the Comma. What should be flagged — on Erasmus’s own page as much as here — is not the textual judgment but the folklore that has grown around it: the “promise,” the “wager,” and the “forged manuscript” are storytelling, not documented history, and the honest case needs none of them. (On the separate, methodological point that a doctrine’s resting on a single passage does not make it “unbiblical,” see the ot-canon argument — a different question from whether this passage is textually genuine.)
