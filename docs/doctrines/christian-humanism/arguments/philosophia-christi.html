Is Erasmus’s philosophia Christi a piety of grace and rebirth, or chiefly an ethic of imitation?

Disputed reading · on Christian Humanism (ad fontes / bonae litterae) · witness: Erasmus of Rotterdam

Related readings: Did Erasmus “lay the egg that Luther hatched”?

The passage

This kind of philosophy consisteth rather in the affects of the mind, than in subtle reasons. It is a life rather than a disputation, an inspiration rather than a science, and rather a new transformation than a reasoning… What other thing is the philosophy of Christ, which he himself calleth the new birth, than the restoring or repairing of our nature which in his first creation was good?

— Erasmus, Paraclesis (1516), anonymous early English translation; Erasmus’s own word for the “new birth” is renascentia, rebirth.1

The question

Erasmus framed his ad fontes programme as a philosophia Christi, a “philosophy of Christ” open to every ploughman and weaver, in which religion is an inward change of mind rather than outward observance. What is disputed is whether that piety rests on grace, redemption and rebirth, or treats Christ chiefly as teacher and pattern, leaving sin and grace in the background; the answer bears on how Erasmus is placed between the Catholic and the Protestant theology of his century.

A Christ-centred piety of rebirth, perfected by grace

In brief: the philosophia Christi is life in Christ received by grace and drawn from Scripture, of which moral renewal is the fruit.

Held by: Erasmus, Enchiridion (1503), Paraclesis (1516) and letter to Jan Slechta (1519);2 O’Malley, introduction to Collected Works of Erasmus vol. 66, ⚠ unverified;3 Erika Rummel and Eric MacPhail, historians, “Desiderius Erasmus,” Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy (2017, rev. 2025).3,4

Erasmus grounds the philosophia Christi in renascentia, “the restoring or repairing of our nature,” and calls it “an inspiration rather than a science”: a work done in the believer.1 Its centre is Christ met in Scripture; the Enchiridion asks why a Christian would venerate Paul’s relics and not “the mind of Paul” in his writings.2 To Slechta in 1519 he summed it up: “The whole of Christian philosophy lies in this, our understanding that all our hope is placed in God, who freely gives us all things through Jesus his son, that we were redeemed by his death and engrafted through baptism with his body,” so that “we claim nothing for ourselves, but ascribe any good we do to God.”3

Rummel and MacPhail find three marks in Erasmus’s pietas: it is “an internal quality independent of the outward observance of rites; it is perfected through divine grace; and it is inclusive.” “The ability to fulfill one’s moral duty depends on divine grace,” they write, a point tied to the Catholic doctrine of free will, and Erasmus “is emphatic about this aspect in his definition of the philosophia Christi.” O’Malley calls the Erasmian concept of piety “principled rather than prescriptive.”3 On this reading Erasmus’s quarrel was with a piety buried under ceremony and scholastic subtlety, and his programme was a return to the Gospel’s own grace-borne life of faith and charity.

An ethic of imitation, light on sin and grace

In brief: Erasmus reads the Gospel above all as a pattern of moral life, and leaves original sin, grace and the bondage of the will at the margins.

Held by: Martin Luther, letters (1516–17) and De servo arbitrio (1525);5 Joseph Sauer (Catholic), “Desiderius Erasmus,” Catholic Encyclopedia (1909);6 Johan Huizinga, Erasmus (1924), ⚠ unverified.7

Luther read the 1516 Annotationes as they appeared. He objected that Erasmus took Paul’s “righteousness of works, or of the law” to mean only “ceremonial and figurative observances” and “will not have the apostle speak of original sin, in Romans, chapter V, though he admits that there is such a thing.” In March 1517: “I fear that he does not sufficiently advance the cause of Christ and God’s grace… for human considerations weigh with him more than divine.”5 In 1525 he pressed the point to its root: “If we believe that Christ redeemed men by his blood, we are compelled to confess that the whole man was lost; otherwise, we make Christ superfluous, or a Redeemer of the grossest part of man only.” He held that Christ must be received as gift before he can be followed as example.8

Sauer reaches the same reading from the Catholic side. Erasmus, he writes, held that the religious life was not furthered by discussion of the procession of the Holy Spirit or of the scholastic distinctions of grace, and that “of just as little consequence was the doctrine of original sin”; where Luther sought in the Bible “the certainty of justification by Christ,” Erasmus, “with an almost Pelagian definiteness, sought therein the model of a moral life.”6 On this reading the philosophia Christi places the weight on moral renewal and the imitation of Christ, and treats the doctrines of sin and grace as matters ordinary Christians may leave aside.
