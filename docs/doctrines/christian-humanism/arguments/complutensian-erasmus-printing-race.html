Why did Erasmus’s Greek New Testament, and not the Complutensian, become the base of the received text?

Disputed reading · on Christian Humanism (ad fontes / bonae litterae) · witness: Erasmus of Rotterdam; see also the Complutensian Polyglot

Related readings: Is the Comma Johanneum (1 John 5:7–8) part of the epistle, or a later Latin gloss?

The passage

Although the first printed Greek New Testament was the Complutensian Polyglot (1514), Erasmus’ was published first (1516).

— the standard statement of the sequence, “Novum Instrumentum omne,” Wikipedia.1

The question

The Complutensian New Testament carries a colophon of 10 January 1514, but Cardinal Cisneros held it back to issue it with the Old Testament; Erasmus’s Novum Instrumentum was printed at Basel between 2 October 1515 and 1 March 1516 under a four-year privilege, and Leo X authorized the release of the Polyglot by a brief of 22 March 1520. Erasmus’s text, revised across five editions (1516–1535), became the base of the printed “received text” behind the Reformation Bibles.1,2 That sequence is not disputed. What is disputed is what decided the outcome, and what the haste of Erasmus’s first edition implies for the authority of the text that descended from it. No distinct Catholic or Orthodox reading of the question was located for this page.

Priority, privilege and circumstance decided it

In brief: Erasmus’s printer raced to reach the market first, the privilege and the Complutensian’s own delays kept the Spanish edition back, and the hastier text took the field.

Held by: S. P. Tregelles, textual scholar, An Account of the Printed Text of the Greek New Testament (1854);3 Bruce M. Metzger and Bart D. Ehrman, The Text of the New Testament (4th ed., 2005);4 Grantley McDonald, historian, Raising the Ghost of Arius (2011).2

Tregelles reports that Froben “wished to anticipate the edition of the Greek Testament which was (as he heard) in preparation in Spain,” approached Erasmus on 17 April 1515, and “would leave no stone unturned to get his edition into the hands of the public before that which was already finished at Alcala”; by 1522 the first two editions, 3,300 copies between them, were in circulation.3 Erasmus himself called his first edition “praecipitatum verius quam editum,” “precipitated rather than edited”; Scrivener judged it “in that respect the most faulty book I know,” and Metzger notes that Erasmus relied on a few late Byzantine minuscules.4

McDonald traces the Spanish side. Cisneros died without applying for papal permission to publish, his executors disagreed over the price of the six hundred sets printed, and Leo X’s brief ordering their sale came “a mere three weeks after the expiry of Froben’s privilege,” which McDonald holds “had played a part in his timing.” He also reports Reeve and Screech’s finding that leaves of the Complutensian New Testament were reprinted during the delay to answer readings in Erasmus’s edition.2 Complutensian readings later entered the received text through Robert Estienne (1550), whose edition showed variants from it.5 On this reading the edition that shaped the printed Bible was the one that reached readers first and stayed in print.

Providential preservation through the printed editions

In brief: Erasmus’s haste is real, but the text he printed agrees in all essentials with the text the Greek church preserved, and its spread was God’s providence at work.

Held by: Edward F. Hills (Protestant), The King James Version Defended (1956).6

Hills grants the haste: Erasmus “performed his task in great haste in order to meet the deadline set by the printer,” and the first edition “contained a number of errors of a minor sort.” He reads the event otherwise: “In the first printing of the Greek New Testament we see God’s preserving providence working hiddenly and, to the outward eye, accidentally.” In all essentials, he holds, the text printed by Erasmus and later by Stephanus (1550) and Elzevir (1633) agrees with the Traditional Text preserved in the great majority of Greek manuscripts, so that its printing “was, after all, no accident but the work of God’s special providence.”6 Erasmus, on Hills’s account, was guided by the “common faith” of his day in the current text, more cautious in his text than in his notes, and the usage of the Latin church led him and his successors to follow the Vulgate in the few places where it kept the genuine reading. Stephanus’s later editions drew in readings from the Complutensian.6 On this reading the received text rests on the church’s long use of the text it printed, and the speed of its first printing does not touch that foundation.
