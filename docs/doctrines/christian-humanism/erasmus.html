Erasmus of Rotterdam — the Greek New Testament and the philosophy of Christ

Dates: c. 1466–1536; on this timeline slotted c. 1516, the year of the Novum Instrumentum · Wikipedia
Summary: Erasmus is the centre of gravity of Christian humanism. Where Valla proved that philology could correct a sacred text and Colet carried ad fontes into the pulpit, Erasmus realized the program on the New Testament itself — editing and printing the first published Greek New Testament (the Novum Instrumentum, 1516) with a fresh Latin translation and critical Annotationes, and framing the whole as a philosophia Christi: a plain philosophy of Christ, drawn from the cleared text and laid open to every ploughman and weaver. His work runs from the inward piety of the Enchiridion (1503) through the Greek New Testament to the fault line where humanism met the Reformation and he broke with Luther. This page treats his biblical ad fontes work and the philosophia Christi; for his program of classical education see his page on the liberal arts.

Why c. 1516? Erasmus’s load-bearing works span roughly 1503–1524; he is anchored here at 1516, the Novum Instrumentum, because that is the moment the ad fontes program was realized on the sacred text and the year around which the rest of his biblical work orbits.

Claims & evidence

Piety is an inward thing, not the outward ceremony — the Enchiridion (1503)

With great veneration you revere the ashes of Paul, which I do not condemn, if your religion is consistent with your devotion. If you venerate mute and dead ashes and ignore his living image still speaking and breathing, as it were, in his writings, is not your religion utterly absurd? You worship the bones of Paul preserved in a relic casket, but do not worship the mind of Paul hidden away in his writings? You make much of a piece of his body visible through a glass covering, and you do not marvel at the whole mind of Paul shining through his writings?

— Enchiridion militis Christiani (“Handbook of the Christian Soldier,” 1503), trans. Charles Fantazzi.1 The Enchiridion is Erasmus’s first full statement of the message he would carry the rest of his life: that true religion is an inward disposition of the mind, and that the ceremonies, relics, and observances a Christian can pile up count for nothing if the mind behind them is not turned to Christ. The argument is not that relics are wicked but that they are the lesser thing — the point drives straight at the Scriptures, where Paul’s living mind is to be found. It is the same instinct that would send him back to the Greek text a decade later: honour the mind, not the casket.

The philosophy of Christ, laid open to all — the Paraclesis (1516)

Christ would that his counsels and mysteries should be spread abroad as much as is possible… I would to God they were translated in to the tongues of all men, so that they might not only be read, and known, of the Scots and Irishmen, but also of the Turks and Saracens… I would to God, the plowman would sing a text of the scripture at his plowbeam, and that the weaver at his loom, with this would drive away the tediousness of time.

— Paraclesis, the exhortation prefixed to the Novum Instrumentum (1516), anonymous early English translation.2 The Paraclesis (“summons”) is the best short statement of the Erasmian program. The philosophia Christi — the plain philosophy of Christ — is not a discipline for trained theologians but a teaching open to anyone who brings “a godly and ready mind”; and because it is for everyone, Erasmus wants Scripture cleared, retranslated, and put in the vernacular so that the ploughman at the plough and the weaver at the loom can recite it. This is the “plowboy” wish that Tyndale would echo a decade later; it makes the whole editorial labor of the Greek New Testament a means to a pastoral end.

Back to the fountain-head: metanoeite is “come to your senses,” not “do penance” — the Annotationes (1516)

Meo iudicio commode verti poterat Resipiscite, sive Ad mentem redite.

[In my judgment it could suitably have been translated Resipiscite (“Come to your senses”), or Ad mentem redite (“Return to a right mind”).]

— Annotationes in Novum Testamentum, note on Matthew 3:2 (“metanoeite”), against the Vulgate’s poenitentiam agite, “do penance.”3 Here the ad fontes program does its sharpest work. For generations the Baptist’s and Christ’s opening cry had stood in Latin as poenitentiam agite — “do penance” — and had been read as one of the chief scriptural props of the sacrament of penance. Erasmus, going behind the Vulgate to the Greek, observed that metanoeite names an inward change of mind, not a ritual act, and proposed resipiscite, “recover your senses.” He did not rewrite Scripture; he corrected its translation — but the correction severed the philological link between the text and the penitential system built on it, and the young Martin Luther, studying the 1516 edition, seized on exactly this point. It is the ad fontes method of Valla (whose New Testament notes Erasmus had printed in 1505) turned on the one text that mattered most.

Notes

  • The Comma Johanneum (1 John 5:7). Erasmus’s first two editions (1516, 1519) omitted the “three heavenly witnesses” — the trinitarian gloss the Father, the Word, and the Holy Spirit — because he found it in no Greek manuscript, only the Latin. Under fierce attack for leaving out a proof-text of the Trinity, he added it to the third edition (1522) once a single late Greek manuscript (Codex Montfortianus, itself apparently rendered from the Latin) surfaced containing it, appending a note doubting its authenticity. The passage passed from there into the printed “received text” and the early vernacular Bibles. The popular story that Erasmus promised to insert it if shown one Greek manuscript, and that Montfortianus was forged to trap him, is repeated everywhere but is now doubted by textual scholars; what is not in doubt is that the verse lacks early Greek support.4 The episode is the double edge of ad fontes: the sources both corrected the text and unsettled a reading the church had leaned on.
  • Recovering the fathers. The same year as the Greek New Testament, Erasmus brought out with Froben at Basel a nine-volume edition of the works of Jerome — himself the great ad fontes translator — editing the letters and weeding out the spurious pieces that had accumulated under his name. Restoring the fathers whole, out of good manuscripts, was of a piece with restoring the New Testament.5
  • The debt to Valla. The pattern for the Greek New Testament was not Erasmus’s invention. In 1504 he found Lorenzo Valla’s Annotationes — the Italian philologist’s collation of the Vulgate against the Greek — and printed them at Paris in 1505, taking from that notebook the method he would carry to completion.6
  • The fault line with Luther. Erasmus did more than anyone to open the Greek New Testament, and Luther drew on his text and his metanoeite point; yet Erasmus refused to follow the reform he had helped set in motion, breaking with Luther in 1524 over the freedom of the will (his De libero arbitrio, answered by Luther’s De servo arbitrio). Whether “Erasmus laid the egg that Luther hatched” — whether Christian humanism was the mother of the Reformation or a third way it declined — is weighed on its own argument page (assessed contested). That later quarrel is treated on his page on predestination and the will.
  • Erasmus elsewhere on this wiki. He appears as a witness on the timelines of the liberal arts (learning as the making of a person), of divorce and remarriage (the “Erasmian” reading of the exceptive clause), and of predestination (the freedom of the will, against Luther).
