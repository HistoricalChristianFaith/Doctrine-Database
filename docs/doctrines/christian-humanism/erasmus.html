Erasmus of Rotterdam — the Greek New Testament and the philosophy of Christ

Dates: c. 1466–1536; on this timeline slotted c. 1516, the year of the Novum Instrumentum · Wikipedia
Summary: Erasmus applied the ad fontes program to the New Testament itself. After Valla had collated the Vulgate against the Greek and Colet had carried ad fontes into the pulpit, Erasmus worked on the New Testament — editing and printing the first published Greek New Testament (the Novum Instrumentum, 1516) with a fresh Latin translation and critical Annotationes, and framing the whole as a philosophia Christi: a plain philosophy of Christ, drawn from the cleared text and laid open to every ploughman and weaver. His work runs from the inward piety of the Enchiridion (1503) through the Greek New Testament to his break with Luther over the freedom of the will (1524–25). This page treats his biblical ad fontes work and the philosophia Christi; for his program of classical education see his page on the liberal arts.

Why c. 1516? Erasmus’s principal works span roughly 1503–1524; he is anchored here at 1516, the Novum Instrumentum, the year his Greek New Testament appeared, around which the rest of his biblical work is dated.

Claims & evidence

Inward piety and outward ceremony — the Enchiridion (1503)

With great veneration you revere the ashes of Paul, which I do not condemn, if your religion is consistent with your devotion. If you venerate mute and dead ashes and ignore his living image still speaking and breathing, as it were, in his writings, is not your religion utterly absurd? You worship the bones of Paul preserved in a relic casket, but do not worship the mind of Paul hidden away in his writings? You make much of a piece of his body visible through a glass covering, and you do not marvel at the whole mind of Paul shining through his writings?

— Enchiridion militis Christiani (“Handbook of the Christian Soldier,” 1503), trans. Charles Fantazzi.1 The Enchiridion is an early full statement of a theme found throughout Erasmus’s work: that true religion is an inward disposition of the mind, and that the ceremonies, relics, and observances a Christian can pile up count for nothing if the mind behind them is not turned to Christ. The argument is not that relics are wicked but that they are the lesser thing — the point drives straight at the Scriptures, where Paul’s living mind is to be found.

The philosophy of Christ, laid open to all — the Paraclesis (1516)

Christ would that his counsels and mysteries should be spread abroad as much as is possible… I would to God they were translated in to the tongues of all men, so that they might not only be read, and known, of the Scots and Irishmen, but also of the Turks and Saracens… I would to God, the plowman would sing a text of the scripture at his plowbeam, and that the weaver at his loom, with this would drive away the tediousness of time.

— Paraclesis, the exhortation prefixed to the Novum Instrumentum (1516), anonymous early English translation.2 The Paraclesis (“summons”) states the Erasmian program in brief. The philosophia Christi — the plain philosophy of Christ — is not a discipline for trained theologians but a teaching open to anyone who brings “a godly and ready mind”; and because it is for everyone, Erasmus wants Scripture cleared, retranslated, and put in the vernacular so that the ploughman at the plough and the weaver at the loom can recite it. William Tyndale used the same ploughboy image in the 1520s; in the Paraclesis Erasmus presents the editorial labour of the Greek New Testament as a means to a pastoral end.

Metanoeite rendered “come to your senses” — the Annotationes on Matthew 3:2 (1516)

Meo iudicio commode verti poterat Resipiscite, sive Ad mentem redite.

[In my judgment it could suitably have been translated Resipiscite (“Come to your senses”), or Ad mentem redite (“Return to a right mind”).]

— Annotationes in Novum Testamentum, note on Matthew 3:2 (“metanoeite”), against the Vulgate’s poenitentiam agite, “do penance.”3 For generations the Baptist’s and Christ’s opening cry had stood in Latin as poenitentiam agite — “do penance.” Erasmus, going behind the Vulgate to the Greek, proposed resipiscite, “recover your senses.” He presented the change as a correction of the translation, not of Scripture. Roland Bainton holds that the rendering removed any philological connection with the sacrament of penance, and reports that Luther made use of it; Erika Rummel and Eric MacPhail report that Erasmus’s critics saw in the change “a Lutheran slant.” The Council of Trent (1551) cites the Lord’s and Peter’s “do penance” for the penitence, “with a hatred of sin and a godly sorrow of mind,” that it holds necessary at all times, and holds that the Lord “principally instituted the sacrament of penance” when he breathed on his disciples (John 20:22–23). The Haydock Catholic Bible Commentary keeps “do penance” at Matthew 3:2, “especially since a true conversion comprehends not only a change of mind, and a new life, but also a sorrow for past offences, accompanied with self-denials, and some severities of a penitential life.” The note applies the method of Valla, whose New Testament notes Erasmus had printed in 1505.

Reception and debate

  • The Comma Johanneum (1 John 5:7). Erasmus’s first two editions (1516, 1519) omitted the “three heavenly witnesses” — the trinitarian words the Father, the Word, and the Holy Spirit — because he found them in no Greek manuscript, only the Latin. After attacks by Edward Lee and Diego López de Zúñiga (Stunica) for leaving out a proof-text of the Trinity, he added the passage to the third edition (1522) from a single Greek manuscript (Codex Montfortianus), appending a note doubting its authenticity. The passage passed from there into the printed “received text” and the early vernacular Bibles. The story that Erasmus promised to insert it if shown one Greek manuscript is told by Edward F. Hills (1956), among others; H. J. de Jonge (1980) found no such promise in Erasmus’s works.4 Whether the words belong to the epistle, and how they entered the printed text, are set out as a disputed reading.
  • Recovering the fathers. The same year as the Greek New Testament, Erasmus brought out with Froben at Basel a nine-volume edition of the works of Jerome, editing the letters and setting aside pieces that had accumulated under his name and that he judged spurious.5
  • Valla’s notes. In 1504 Erasmus found Lorenzo Valla’s Annotationes — the Italian philologist’s collation of the Vulgate against the Greek — and printed them at Paris in 1505; Jerry Bentley traces their influence on the Novum Instrumentum of 1516.6
  • Erasmus and Luther. Luther drew on Erasmus’s Greek text and his metanoeite note; Erasmus declined to join Luther’s reform and in 1524 wrote De libero arbitrio, answered by Luther’s De servo arbitrio (1525). Whether “Erasmus laid the egg that Luther hatched” is a disputed reading; the later quarrel is treated on his page on predestination and the will.
  • The philosophia Christi. Erasmus framed his program as a philosophia Christi — “a life rather than a disputation,” an inward change of mind open to ploughman and weaver. Luther (1516–1525) and the Catholic historian Joseph Sauer (1909) read it as an ethic of imitation light on sin and grace; Erasmus’s own letter to Jan Slechta (1519) and the historians Erika Rummel and Eric MacPhail read it as a piety of rebirth perfected by grace. Both readings are set out on its interpretation page.7
  • Erasmus elsewhere on this wiki. He appears as a witness on the timelines of the liberal arts (learning as the making of a person), of divorce and remarriage (the “Erasmian” reading of the exceptive clause), and of predestination (the freedom of the will, against Luther).
