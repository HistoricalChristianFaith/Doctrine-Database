Thomas More on Christian humanism

Dates: c. 1478–1535 (born London, 7 February 1478; Utopia printed at Louvain, 1516; Lord Chancellor, 1529–1532; beheaded 6 July 1535) · Wikipedia
Summary: More is the lay, civic face of Christian humanism — the London lawyer and courtier who turned the new learning on his own society. Utopia (1516), written in polished humanist Latin and seen through the press with Erasmus’s help, applies the humanist’s classical reading and moral imagination to a mordant critique of Tudor England and a picture of a commonwealth ordered by reason. He was the closest English friend of Erasmus — who wrote The Praise of Folly in More’s house and punned its Greek title on More’s name — and of John Colet, and he defended Greek studies at Oxford against the scholars who mocked them. Yet the same man served as Henry VIII’s Lord Chancellor, wrote at length against Luther and Tyndale, and went to the scaffold in 1535 a loyal Catholic — the pointed case of a humanist who stayed with Rome, and so a chief exhibit for reading humanism and the Reformation as two roads, not one.

Claims & evidence

Humanist social criticism: Utopia (1516)

More’s masterpiece is the studia humanitatis turned on the ills of a Christian commonwealth. In Book 1 the traveller Raphael Hythloday indicts the enclosure of English farmland for sheep-pasture — the engrossing of arable fields into wool-runs that drove tenants off the land and into vagrancy and the gallows — in the image for which the book is still quoted:

your sheep that were wont to be so meek and tame, and so small eaters, now, as I heard say, be become so great devourers and so wild, that they eat up, and swallow down the very men themselves. They consume, destroy, and devour whole fields, houses, and cities.

— Utopia, Book 1, trans. Ralph Robinson (1551).1 This is the humanist as social critic: classical learning and rhetorical force marshalled not against a corrupt manuscript but against a live injustice. The imagined island is the mirror held up to it. Closing his account of the Utopians’ propertyless commonwealth, Hythloday makes the contrast explicit:

Thus have I described to you, as particularly as I could, the Constitution of that commonwealth, which I do not only think the best in the world, but indeed the only commonwealth that truly deserves that name. In all other places it is visible that, while people talk of a commonwealth, every man only seeks his own wealth; but there, where no man has any property, all men zealously pursue the good of the public…

— Utopia, Book 2, trans. Gilbert Burnet (1684).2 And the one thing that keeps the world from copying Utopia, he says, is not the difficulty of the scheme but a vice — pride, the “infernal serpent” that measures its own happiness by the misery of others:

…[the world] would have drawn all the world over to the laws of the Utopians, if pride, that plague of human nature, that source of so much misery, did not hinder it; for this vice does not measure happiness so much by its own conveniences, as by the miseries of others… This is that infernal serpent that creeps into the breasts of mortals, and possesses them too much to be easily drawn out.

— Utopia, Book 2, trans. Burnet.3 The book is deliberately double-edged — the very name Utopia (Greek ou-topos, “no-place,” punning on eu-topos, “good place”), the unreliable narrator whose surname Hythloday means “dispenser of nonsense,” and More’s own demurral in the dialogue all warn against reading it as a policy blueprint. It is humanist moral criticism in the guise of a traveller’s tale: a rational pagan commonwealth set against a nominally Christian one to shame the latter.

The closest friend of Erasmus — and the pun on his name

More stood at the English end of the Erasmus circle. When Erasmus returned to England in 1509 he wrote The Praise of Folly (Moriae Encomium) while lodging in More’s house, and dedicated it to him — opening the book by turning More’s own name into its joke, the Greek mōria (“folly”) sounding almost like “More”:

The first thing was your surname of More, which comes so near the word Moriae (folly) as you are far from the thing… in the whole course of your life have played the part of a Democritus.

— Erasmus, dedicatory epistle of The Praise of Folly, trans. John Wilson (1668).4 The pun is a token of a real intimacy: the two men were, in Erasmus’s phrase, of one mind in two bodies, and it was through More and Colet that Erasmus’s philosophia Christi found its English home. That More could be the addressee of the age’s most famous satire on monks, theologians, and superstition — and share its laughter — marks how far the reforming, source-loving temper reached before the Reformation forced its friends to choose sides.

Defending Greek: the Letter to the University of Oxford (1518)

When a faction at Oxford who styled themselves “Trojans” began ridiculing the study of Greek — one of them preaching a Lenten sermon against the new learning — More wrote from the King’s court at Abingdon to the university to defend it. He names the faction’s real motive without flattery: not scruple but idleness and spite, a pack that had taken the enemies of Homer’s Greeks for their nickname:

Ego quum Londini essem, audivi iam nuper saepius, quosdam scholasticos Academiae vestrae, sive Graecarum odio litterarum, seu pravo quopiam aliarum studio; seu, quod opinor verius, improba ludendi nugandique libidine, de composito conspirasse inter sese, ut se Troianos appellent.

— “When I was in London I heard, not long ago and more than once, that certain scholars of your university — whether out of hatred of Greek letters, or from some warped zeal for other studies, or, as I think nearer the truth, out of a shameless appetite for joking and trifling — had by common agreement conspired among themselves to call themselves ‘Trojans.’”5 Greek, he then argues, is no ornament but the indispensable key to theology itself, which cannot be reached without the biblical tongues — and above all the key to the New Testament and the Greek fathers:

Taceo Novum Testamentum, totum fere primo scriptum Graece. Taceo vetustissimos quosque atque peritissimos sacrarum litterarum interpretes fuisse Graecos, Graeceque scripsisse…

— “I say nothing of the New Testament, nearly the whole of it first written in Greek; I say nothing that the oldest and most expert interpreters of the sacred writings were Greeks and wrote in Greek…” The letter names the fathers a theologian cannot read without the language — Jerome, Augustine, Ambrose, Cyprian, Chrysostom, Gregory, Basil — and insists that even the “secular” learning the preacher despised prepares the soul for virtue. To mock Greek from the university pulpit, More tells them, is ignorance dressed as piety and beneath the dignity of a place of learning — one of the sharpest humanist defences of the classical languages as the indispensable tools of a reformed theology, written by a layman to the scholars who should have needed no telling.5 The same year Erasmus’s enlarged Utopia and More’s Epigrams were printed together at Basel, confirming his standing as a European man of letters just as he entered royal service.

The humanist who stayed with Rome

More is the case the timeline most needs, because he shows that the road from ad fontes did not run only to Wittenberg. As Lord Chancellor (1529–1532) and as a controversialist he turned his learning against the Reformation: he answered Luther in the Responsio ad Lutherum (1523), and Tyndale — whose English New Testament he judged a corruption of the text, not a recovery of it — in A Dialogue Concerning Heresies (1529) and the vast Confutation of Tyndale’s Answer (1532–33). When Henry VIII broke with Rome, More resigned the chancellorship, refused the oath acknowledging the king as supreme head of the church, and was beheaded for treason on 6 July 1535, saying he died “the king’s good servant, and God’s first.”6 The same philology that armed Erasmus and Luther against the schoolmen armed More against Tyndale: for him the sources purified the church from within its unity, and to make them a warrant for schism was to abuse them. Humanism, on his witness, was no more the mother of the Reformation than its enemy — it was a tool that reformers and their opponents alike reached for.

Notes

  • Where he stands on the timeline. More is anchored at c. 1516, the year of Utopia, alongside the Erasmus he was closest to and just before the generation (Melanchthon) in which humanism folded into the Reformation. Where Erasmus carried ad fontes to the Greek New Testament and Colet into the pulpit, More carried it into civic and political criticism — and then, unlike either reforming heir, stood with Rome to the death.
  • Utopia is a criticism, not a programme. More the Catholic statesman did not propose to abolish private property or clerical marriage in England; the propertyless, divorce-permitting, priest-few island is a philosophical provocation — a rational commonwealth held up to shame a Christian one that fell short of reason, not a manifesto. Reading it as More’s political blueprint mistakes the genre; the framing devices (the punning place-names, the nonsense-named narrator, More’s own closing reservations) are built to prevent exactly that.
  • Editions. Utopia was written in Latin (first printed at Louvain, 1516; the definitive Basel edition, 1518). The Book 1 sheep passage above is quoted in Ralph Robinson’s first English translation of 1551; the Book 2 passages in Bishop Gilbert Burnet’s translation of 1684, which is the standard free full text online. The Oxford letter survives in Latin; the passages quoted above are from that original (letter 60 in the Center for Thomas More Studies’ e-text of the Latin correspondence, with English renderings from the Latin), and the letter is edited and translated in the Yale Complete Works of St. Thomas More, vol. 15, and in Elizabeth Frances Rogers, St. Thomas More: Selected Letters (Yale, 1961).
