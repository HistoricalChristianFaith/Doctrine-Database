Philip Melanchthon on Christian humanism

Dates: c. 1497–1560 (born Philipp Schwartzerdt at Bretten, 16 February 1497; professor of Greek at Wittenberg from 28 August 1518, opening with the inaugural oration De corrigendis adolescentiae studiis; Loci Communes, 1521; died Wittenberg, 19 April 1560) · Wikipedia
Summary: Melanchthon is the pivotal case of humanism folded into the Reformation — not broken with it, as Erasmus was, nor kept within Rome, as Thomas More was. A great-nephew and protégé of Johann Reuchlin — who Graecized the boy’s German name “Schwartzerdt” (“black earth”) into its Greek equivalent and won him the Greek chair at Wittenberg at twenty-one — he arrived a finished humanist and at once joined Luther’s cause. His 1518 inaugural made the recovery of Greek, Hebrew, and the liberal arts a necessity of theology itself; his Loci Communes (1521), the first systematic theology of the Reformation, turned the humanist ad fontes return to the sources of Romans into Protestant dogma; and his mature prefaces kept the pagan classics in the Christian school while subordinating philosophy to the Gospel. He is remembered as the Praeceptor Germaniae, the “teacher of Germany.” (His classical-education program is treated on the liberal-arts timeline; here the angle is ad fontes and the humanism–Reformation hinge, not pedagogy.)

Claims & evidence

The humanist prodigy Reuchlin renamed and Luther recruited

Melanchthon came out of the very quarrel that made Reuchlin a hero to the northern humanists. Reuchlin was his great-uncle (the brother of his mother’s grandmother), and it was Reuchlin who in 1509 turned the twelve-year-old’s German surname Schwartzerdt (“black earth”) into the Greek Melanchthon — the same humanist fashion by which Reuchlin himself wrote as “Capnion.” When Wittenberg wanted a professor of Greek in 1518, Reuchlin recommended his great-nephew to the Elector of Saxony in terms that placed him second only to the prince of humanists:

I know of no one among the Germans who is superior to him, save only Erasmus Roterodamus, and he is a Dutchman.

— Reuchlin to the Elector of Saxony, 1518.1 So the youth who carried Italian and German humanism to Wittenberg was the living link between the Hebrew-books controversy and the Reformation: Reuchlin’s heir by blood and by learning, he crossed almost at once into Luther’s camp — a defection the aging Reuchlin never forgave, dying estranged from the cause his great-nephew had joined.

The inaugural manifesto: ad fontes as a necessity of theology (1518)

Four days after arriving in Wittenberg the twenty-one-year-old delivered his inaugural oration De corrigendis adolescentiae studiis (“On correcting the studies of youth”) — a humanist manifesto against the schoolmen and for a return to the sources in their original tongues. Its core is the claim that theology cannot be received purely except through the languages it was written in:

… theology is partly Hebrew and partly Greek, still we drink of it from Latin banks, and the eternal languages are to be learned, and not be just mute actors when we deal with theology.

— De corrigendis adolescentiae studiis (Wittenberg inaugural oration, 1518), trans. Ralph Keen.2 This is the ad fontes program of Valla and Erasmus made the foundation of a reforming university: past the medieval glosses to the Greek New Testament and the Hebrew Old, and past scholastic disputation to grammar, rhetoric, and the disciplined reading of texts. The oration convinced Luther that Wittenberg had found the Greek teacher it needed, and set in motion the reform of studies Melanchthon would carry through the Lutheran world. (The educational program itself — the graded curriculum, the school ordinances, the textbooks — is treated on his liberal-arts detail page.)

Humanism folded into the Reformation: the Loci Communes (1521)

The clearest proof that Melanchthon carried humanism into the Reformation rather than away from it is the Loci Communes rerum theologicarum of 1521 — the first systematic theology of the Protestant movement, written by a twenty-four-year-old professor of Greek out of his lectures on Paul’s Epistle to the Romans. It is the humanist ad fontes method turned on doctrine itself: Scripture read continuously at the fountainhead, its “commonplaces” (loci) drawn from the text rather than from the Sentences of the schoolmen. And it opens by setting the reformer’s practical, salvation-centred theology against scholastic speculation on the divine natures — the same turn from ceremony and syllogism to the plain sense of the Gospel that Erasmus had called the philosophia Christi:

But as for one who is ignorant of the fundamentals—namely “the power of sin,” “the law” and “grace”—I do not see how I can call him a Christian. For from these things Christ is known, since to know Christ means to know his benefits and not, as they teach, to reflect upon his natures and the modes of his incarnation. For unless you know why Christ put on flesh and was nailed to the cross, what good will it do you to know merely the history about him?

— Loci Communes (1521), trans. Wilhelm Pauck.3 Luther prized the book above all his own systematic efforts — “next to Holy Scripture, there is no better book” — and never wrote a dogmatics of his own. Where Erasmus’s philosophia Christi stopped short of Luther’s doctrines of sin and grace, and broke with him over the freedom of the will, Melanchthon’s humanism ran straight on into them: the same tools, aimed the same way, but pressed all the way to the Reformation’s conclusions.

The classics kept, but subordinated to the Gospel: the preface to Cicero’s De Officiis (1534)

Folding humanism into the Reformation did not mean surrendering the pagan classics — a temptation the early Reformation felt strongly, with some of Luther’s followers concluding that learning was superfluous to Christians and Erasmus jibing that “wherever Lutheranism reigns, knowledge perishes.” Against that Melanchthon spent his life defending the reading of Cicero and Homer in Christian schools. But he did it on the Reformation’s own terms — keeping the classics for civic morality while denying them any part in salvation, which belongs to the Gospel alone. In his 1534 preface to Cicero’s De Officiis he drew the line exactly:

… listeners need to be warned to banish the error which has already occupied their minds, namely that the writings of the pagans are unworthy of being read by Christian people and that Christians should give a wide berth to philosophy. Let me therefore expound my opinion on this matter. Philosophy does not provide confirmation of the will of God, nor does it instruct in the fear of God and the trust in Him; that pertains properly to the Gospel. However, apart from these things, precepts for civic life are necessary, which teach how men may live peacefully with each other. These are expounded in [pagan] philosophy and their causes are put in relation and perceived by excellent men. … Since Christians should cherish and support civil society, this teaching of civic morals and duties has to be known by them.

— preface to Cicero’s De Officiis (1534), trans. Sachiko Kusukawa.4 This is the precise shape of humanism within the Reformation: ad fontes retained in full for grammar, rhetoric, and the moral wisdom of the ancients, but the philosophy of Christ narrowed to the Gospel of grace and no longer blurred, as it arguably was in Erasmus, with the ethics of the philosophers. The road from the sources ran on to Wittenberg, but it left pagan philosophy at the door of the church.

Notes

  • Where he stands on the timeline. Melanchthon is anchored at c. 1518, the year of the Wittenberg inaugural, last on the timeline as it stands — the generation in which humanism and the Reformation met. He is the hinge the timeline turns on: Valla, Colet, and Reuchlin opened the sources; Erasmus carried them to the Greek New Testament and then refused to follow the reform; More carried them into civic criticism and stayed with Rome; and Melanchthon carried them into the Reformation itself.
  • The mediator who kept the humanist peace. Melanchthon’s moderate temper let him keep friendships across the widening divide — with the Catholic Erasmus and, later, with the Reformed Calvin — and made him the Reformation’s chief negotiator (the Augsburg Confession of 1530 is his drafting). He summed up his own vocation as the humanist’s: “I apply myself solely to one thing — the defense of letters. By our example, we must excite youth to the admiration of learning and induce them to love it for its own sake, and not for the advantage that may be derived from it.”
  • Sourcing. The inaugural is quoted in Ralph Keen’s translation (A Melanchthon Reader, 1988, from the Corpus Reformatorum critical text) as reproduced in an accessible modern essay; the Loci in Wilhelm Pauck’s standard translation (Library of Christian Classics); the De Officiis preface in Sachiko Kusukawa’s. The wording within quotation marks is Melanchthon’s own in each translator’s rendering.
