Lorenzo Valla on Christian humanism

Dates: c. 1407–1457 (the load-bearing works: the Declamation exposing the Donation of Constantine, 1440; the Elegantiae linguae Latinae; and the Collatio / Annotationes on the New Testament, which Erasmus printed in 1505) · Wikipedia
Summary: Valla is the fountainhead of the ad fontes method: he turned the humanist philologist’s tools — grammar, style, the history of a language — on the church’s own authoritative documents. He proved the Donation of Constantine a forgery by its Latin alone, made the restoration of classical Latinity a program, and collated the Vulgate against the Greek New Testament — the notebook that, printed by Erasmus, seeded the whole northern project of biblical humanism. He is the earliest witness on this timeline, and the one who first showed what the new learning could do to a sacred text.

Claims & evidence

Philology can unmask a forged charter — the Donation of Constantine

The Constitutum Constantini purported to record the emperor Constantine’s grant to Pope Sylvester and his successors of Rome, Italy, and the whole Western empire — the parchment on which the medieval papacy’s temporal claims most heavily leaned. In 1440 Valla wrote a Declamation arguing it was an anachronistic fabrication, and he opened by measuring the danger of what he was about to do:

I have published many books, a great many, in almost every branch of learning. Inasmuch as there are those who are shocked that in these I disagree with certain great writers already approved by long usage, and charge me with rashness and sacrilege, what must we suppose some of them will do now! How they will rage against me, and if opportunity is afforded how eagerly and how quickly they will drag me to punishment! For I am writing against not only the dead, but the living also, not this man or that, but a host, not merely private individuals, but the authorities. And what authorities! Even the supreme pontiff…

— The Discourse of Lorenzo Valla on the Forgery of the Alleged Donation of Constantine, trans. Christopher B. Coleman.1 The point is not merely that a churchman may err but that a document the church leaned on can be tried — and convicted — at the bar of grammar and history. That is the humanist wager in its sharpest form.

The forgery convicts itself out of its own Latin and chronology

Valla’s method is internal: the text uses words and describes institutions that did not yet exist in Constantine’s day, so the fourth-century emperor cannot have written it. He fastens, for instance, on the word satraps — a Persian title no Roman chancery would have used:

O thou scoundrel, thou villain! … And you say that within the first days, immediately, the Senate, the nobles, the satraps, as though already Christians, with the Caesar passed decrees for the honoring of the Roman church! What! How do you want to have satraps come in here? Numskull, blockhead! Do the Caesars speak thus; are Roman decrees usually drafted thus? Whoever heard of satraps being mentioned in the councils of the Romans? I do not remember ever to have read of any Roman satrap being mentioned, or even of a satrap in any of the Roman provinces. But this fellow speaks of the Emperor’s satraps…

— Valla, Discourse, trans. Coleman.2 The chronological blunders are as fatal as the verbal ones. The charter names Constantinople as one of the great patriarchal sees — on the third day, it says, after Constantine’s baptism — when the city did not yet exist:

How in the world—this is much more absurd, and impossible in the nature of things—could one speak of Constantinople as one of the patriarchal sees, when it was not yet a patriarchate, nor a see, nor a Christian city, nor named Constantinople, nor founded, nor planned! For the ‘privilege’ was granted, so it says, the third day after Constantine became a Christian; when as yet Byzantium, not Constantinople, occupied that site. I am a liar if this fool does not confess as much himself.

— Valla, Discourse, trans. Coleman.3 The verdict has held: the Donation is universally reckoned a forgery (an eighth-century one), and Valla’s demonstration is routinely named a founding moment of modern textual criticism — the paradigm case of humanist philology unsettling a text authority had leaned on.4

Restoring the sources begins with restoring the language: the Elegantiae

The tool that convicted the Donation was a mastery of what classical Latin actually was, over against the “barbarous” Latin of the medieval schools. Valla made that mastery a program in the Elegantiae linguae Latinae, and in its most famous preface he cast the Latin language itself as the true, surviving body of Rome — a thing to be guarded with almost sacramental reverence:

it is the great sacrament, indeed, the great divinity of the Latin language that in a holy and religious fashion has been defended among pilgrims, barbarians, and enemies for so many centuries, to such a point that we Romans should not lament but rather rejoice and indeed, with the whole world hearkening, take pride. We have lost Rome, we have lost our kingdom, we have lost power – but this is not our fault but that of the times. And yet we rule over most of the world through this more illustrious power… For wherever the Roman language dominates, it is there that one finds Roman power.

— Elegantiae linguae Latinae, preface to Book IV, trans. Christopher S. Celenza.5 This is ad fontes as a discipline: the fountainhead of learning is the language read exactly, and once the language is restored the sources can be read as their authors meant them — the classics, the fathers, and, most consequentially, Scripture.

Turning grammar on the Vulgate: the Collatio / Annotationes

Valla carried the same tools into the New Testament, collating the Latin Vulgate verse by verse against Greek manuscripts and noting where the translator had rendered the Greek loosely or wrongly. Against the objection that a mere grammarian has no business touching Holy Writ, he drew the crucial distinction — he corrects not Scripture but its translation:

if I am correcting anything, I am not correcting Sacred Scripture, but rather its translation, and in doing so I am not being insolent toward scripture but rather pious, and I am doing nothing more than translating better than the earlier translator, so that it is my translation—should it be correct—that ought to be called Sacred Scripture, not his.

— Valla, preface to the Annotationes, trans. Christopher S. Celenza.6 The move is the whole northern program in miniature: the Vulgate is not identical with the Word of God but is one scholar’s Latin rendering of the Greek, and a better Latin is possible — and so the received text may be criticized, corrected, and restored.

The load-bearing link: Erasmus prints Valla’s notes, 1505

That is exactly the lesson the young Erasmus drew. In 1504 he found a manuscript of Valla’s New Testament notes in the library of the Abbey of Parc near Louvain, and in 1505 he had it printed in Paris by Josse Bade under the title he coined for it, Annotationes. Valla’s verse-by-verse collation showed Erasmus, in a historian’s summary, “how an editor might criticize, correct, and restore a text even as revered as the Vulgate,” and set the pattern that led to Erasmus’s own Greek New Testament, the Novum Instrumentum of 1516.7 Here the Italian philology of the fifteenth century passes directly into the biblical humanism of the sixteenth: the method that had unmasked a forged charter became the method that would re-edit the New Testament.

Notes

  • Reach and limit of the collation. Valla’s notes did not flag 1 John 5:7, the “Johannine Comma.” When Erasmus later omitted that verse from his Greek New Testament for want of any Greek manuscript, critics such as Edward Lee and Diego López de Zúñiga rebuked him partly on the ground that even Valla had let it stand — a reminder that Valla’s collation was a beginning, not the finished critical apparatus later scholarship would build.8
  • A polemic that happened to be right — and not the first. Valla wrote the Declamation in 1440 as secretary to Alfonso of Aragon, who was then at war with Pope Eugenius IV over the Kingdom of Naples — a realm the papacy claimed the right to dispose of partly on the strength of the Donation. The tract is advocacy, soaked in invective (“Numskull, blockhead!”), and it slides from “the charter is forged” toward “the papacy has no temporal right at all” — a wider claim than the philology establishes. Nor was Valla first to the verdict: Nicholas of Cusa had argued it on historical grounds in De concordantia catholica (1433), and Reginald Pecock reached it independently in England soon after. None of this unsettles the textual finding — the anachronisms are real whoever names them — but the finding and its polemical reach are weighed apart on the argument page.
  • Why he leads this timeline. Colet, Reuchlin, Erasmus, and More all inherit the wager Valla first made good: that a return to the sources, pursued with real philology, purifies rather than corrodes. The argument this doctrine weighs — whether that wager was vindicated or whether it “laid the egg that Luther hatched” — begins with the man who showed the tools worked.
