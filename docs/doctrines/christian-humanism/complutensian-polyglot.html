The Complutensian Polyglot — the institutional face of ad fontes

Dates: Greek New Testament printed at Alcalá de Henares (Complutum) 10 January 1514; the trilingual Old Testament (Hebrew, Latin Vulgate, and Greek Septuagint, with the Aramaic Targum added under the Pentateuch) printed by 10 July 1517; the six volumes published c. 1522 · commissioned and financed by Cardinal Francisco Jiménez de Cisneros, called Ximénez (c. 1436–1517), Archbishop of Toledo and founder of the University of Alcalá · on this timeline slotted c. 1514 · Wikipedia
Summary: Where Reuchlin opened the Hebrew fountain with a lone grammar and Erasmus printed the Greek New Testament (1516) almost single-handed, the Complutensian Polyglot is ad fontes made an institution: a cardinal’s new university, a trilingual college, and a standing team of Greek, Hebrew, Latin, and Aramaic scholars, producing the first printed Greek New Testament and the editio princeps of the Septuagint. It is the collaborative, church-sponsored face of the sources-program; its prologue explains why Jerome’s Latin stands between the Hebrew and the Greek. This page treats the Polyglot as a monument of humanist ad fontes scholarship; its bearing on the Old Testament canon — the preface that set the deuterocanon “outside the canon” in Jerome’s grade — is treated on its Old Testament canon page.

Why c. 1514? The whole enterprise was commissioned around 1502 and not published until c. 1522, but the moment that belongs on this timeline is the printing of the Greek New Testament (colophon 10 January 1514) and, with it, the first appearance in print of the Greek Bible in parallel with its originals — the sources laid open on the page. It slots between Reuchlin (the Hebrew grammar, c. 1510) and Erasmus (the Novum Instrumentum, 1516).

Claims & evidence

Ad fontes made an institution: the trilingual College of San Ildefonso

Everything the northern humanists did as individuals, Cisneros built into a standing institution. Archbishop of Toledo, Grand Inquisitor, and twice regent of Castile, he founded the University of Alcalá (chartered 1499, opened 1508) and within it the Colegio Triligüe — a college for the three scriptural tongues, Latin, Greek, and Hebrew — and staffed the Polyglot from its scholars. Around the project he gathered a team: Diego López de Zúñiga (Stunica) and Hernán Núñez (El Pinciano) on the Latin and Greek, the Cretan Demetrius Ducas for the Greek, and the Jewish converts Alfonso de Zamora, Pablo Coronel, and Alfonso de Alcalá for the Hebrew and Aramaic; Cisneros borrowed and bought Greek and Hebrew manuscripts from Venice and the Vatican to give them a text to work from. The result, printed before the whole Bible was ready, was the first printed Greek New Testament and the editio princeps of the Greek Septuagint.1 This is the same ad fontes conviction that drove Valla, Reuchlin, and Erasmus — back past the glosses to the original tongues — carried out not by one scholar and a printer but by a cardinal’s university.

Nebrija and the correction of the Vulgate

Cisneros first put the Spanish humanist Antonio de Nebrija in charge of the Vulgate column, and Nebrija proposed to correct it from the originals: wherever the Latin manuscripts disagreed, go back to the Greek; wherever Greek and Latin disagreed in the Old Testament, go back to the Hebrew. He urged the cardinal to

Light up once more the two unlit torches of our religion, the Hebrew and the Greek languages. Reward those who devote themselves to this task.

— Antonio de Nebrija to Cisneros, as reported in his own defence of the work.2 But Cisneros would not have the Church’s authorized Latin substantially altered against the originals, and Nebrija — unwilling to lend his name to a Vulgate he was not free to correct — withdrew from the project. The same question, whether recovering the sources may alter the received text or only stand beside it, was raised over Erasmus’s edition of 1516. At Alcalá Cisneros kept the Vulgate, and Nebrija left the project.

“Like Christ between two thieves”: the prologue on the page layout

The prologue gives Cisneros’s own account of the page layout. The three Old Testament columns set the Hebrew on the outer edge and the Greek on the inner, with Jerome’s Latin Vulgate running down the middle — and the preface explains the arrangement as a theology in typography:

We have placed the Latin translation of the blessed Jerome between that of the Synagogue [the Hebrew] and that of the Oriental Church [the Greek], just as the thieves were hung, one on each side of Jesus, who represents the Roman, or Latin, Church.

— prologue to the Complutensian Polyglot (Cisneros).3 In the prologue’s figure the Hebrew and Greek columns stand as the two thieves and the Vulgate in the place of Jesus. The Polyglot printed no Spanish column. A saying attributed to Cisneros, that “the Word of God had to be enveloped in discreet mysteries far from the reach of the common man,” is current in popular accounts of the Polyglot but has not been traced here to a primary or scholarly source (⚠ unverified).3

Printed first, published later: the Complutensian and Erasmus

The Greek New Testament volume carries a colophon of 10 January 1514 — two years before Erasmus’s — but Cisneros held it back to publish the New Testament together with the still-unfinished Old Testament as one complete Bible. In the gap, word of the Spanish project reached Erasmus, who brought out his own Greek New Testament (the Novum Instrumentum) through Froben’s Basel press in 1516, secured a four-year exclusive printing privilege from Pope Leo X and the Emperor Maximilian, and reached the market first. Cisneros died in November 1517, months after the Old Testament was printed and years before the work was released; the Polyglot did not circulate until about 1522.4 Why Erasmus’s text, and not the Complutensian’s, became the base of the received text is a disputed reading.

Placement and related pages

  • Placement on the timeline. Between Reuchlin’s Hebrew grammar (c. 1510) and Erasmus’s Novum Instrumentum (1516): a collaborative, church-sponsored edition between two editions made by individual scholars.
  • The canon dimension is treated separately. The Polyglot is also a witness on the Old Testament canon: its second preface set the deuterocanonical books “outside the canon,” received “for the edification of the people” rather than for confirming doctrine, in Jerome’s graded sense. That question, and the reading of Leo X’s approval of the work, are treated on its Old Testament canon page; this page does not restate it.
  • Cisneros the man. Francisco Jiménez de Cisneros (c. 1436–1517) financed the whole Polyglot from his own archiepiscopal revenues and founded the university that housed it, but died before it was published — so the “cardinal’s Bible” was compiled under his direction and released after his death.
  • Provenance. The English of the Nebrija plea and of the “two thieves” prologue is given from the standard scholarly accounts and library editions cited below; the printing dates are from the volumes’ own colophons as reported in those accounts.
