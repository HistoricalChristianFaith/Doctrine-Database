The Complutensian Polyglot — the institutional face of ad fontes

Dates: Greek New Testament printed at Alcalá de Henares (Complutum) 10 January 1514; the trilingual Old Testament (Hebrew, Latin Vulgate, and Greek Septuagint, with the Aramaic Targum added under the Pentateuch) printed by 10 July 1517; the six volumes published c. 1522 · commissioned and financed by Cardinal Francisco Jiménez de Cisneros, called Ximénez (c. 1436–1517), Archbishop of Toledo and founder of the University of Alcalá · on this timeline slotted c. 1514 · Wikipedia
Summary: Where Reuchlin opened the Hebrew fountain with a lone grammar and Erasmus would print the Greek New Testament almost single-handed, the Complutensian Polyglot is ad fontes made an institution: a cardinal’s new university, a trilingual college, and a standing team of Greek, Hebrew, Latin, and Aramaic scholars, producing the first printed Greek New Testament and the editio princeps of the Septuagint. It is the collaborative, church-sponsored face of the sources-program — and, in Cisneros’s own conservative vision for it, the sharpest possible contrast to Erasmus’s plowman-and-weaver openness. This page treats the Polyglot as a monument of humanist ad fontes scholarship; its bearing on the Old Testament canon — the preface that set the deuterocanon “outside the canon” in Jerome’s grade — is treated on its Old Testament canon page.

Why c. 1514? The whole enterprise was commissioned around 1502 and not published until c. 1522, but the moment that belongs on this timeline is the printing of the Greek New Testament (colophon 10 January 1514) and, with it, the first appearance in print of the Greek Bible in parallel with its originals — the sources laid open on the page. It slots between Reuchlin (the Hebrew grammar, c. 1510) and Erasmus (the Novum Instrumentum, 1516), which is exactly where the contrast it draws is sharpest.

Claims & evidence

Ad fontes made an institution: the trilingual College of San Ildefonso

Everything the northern humanists did as individuals, Cisneros built into a standing institution. Archbishop of Toledo, Grand Inquisitor, and twice regent of Castile, he founded the University of Alcalá (chartered 1499, opened 1508) and within it the Colegio Triligüe — a college for the three scriptural tongues, Latin, Greek, and Hebrew — and staffed the Polyglot from its scholars. Around the project he gathered a team no lone editor could match: Diego López de Zúñiga (Stunica) and Hernán Núñez (El Pinciano) on the Latin and Greek, the Cretan Demetrius Ducas for the Greek, and the Jewish converts Alfonso de Zamora, Pablo Coronel, and Alfonso de Alcalá for the Hebrew and Aramaic; Cisneros borrowed and bought Greek and Hebrew manuscripts from Venice and the Vatican to give them a text to work from. The result, printed before either the whole Bible or the world was ready for it, was the first printed Greek New Testament and the editio princeps of the Greek Septuagint — the sources of Scripture set in type, in their own alphabets, for the first time.1 This is the same ad fontes conviction that drove Valla, Reuchlin, and Erasmus — back past the glosses to the original tongues — carried out not by one scholar and a printer but by a cardinal’s university.

How far may philology correct the Vulgate? Nebrija walks out

The fault line that ran through all of Christian humanism ran through Alcalá too. Cisneros first put the great Spanish humanist Antonio de Nebrija in charge of the Vulgate column, and Nebrija pressed the full ad fontes logic: wherever the Latin manuscripts disagreed, go back to the Greek; wherever Greek and Latin disagreed in the Old Testament, go back to the Hebrew. He urged the cardinal to

Light up once more the two unlit torches of our religion, the Hebrew and the Greek languages. Reward those who devote themselves to this task.

— Antonio de Nebrija to Cisneros, as reported in his own defence of the work.2 But Cisneros would not have the Church’s authorized Latin substantially altered against the originals, and Nebrija — unwilling to lend his name to a Vulgate he was not free to correct — withdrew from the project. It is the Alcalá version of the quarrel Erasmus would provoke openly two years later: whether recovering the sources is allowed to touch the received text, or only to sit alongside it. At Alcalá the conservative answer won, and the boldest philologist left.

“Like Christ between two thieves”: the sources recovered, then enthroned below the Vulgate

The most revealing sentence in the whole Polyglot is Cisneros’s own account of the page layout. The three Old Testament columns set the Hebrew on the outer edge and the Greek on the inner, with Jerome’s Latin Vulgate running down the middle — and the preface explains the arrangement as a theology in typography:

We have placed the Latin translation of the blessed Jerome between that of the Synagogue [the Hebrew] and that of the Oriental Church [the Greek], just as the thieves were hung, one on each side of Jesus, who represents the Roman, or Latin, Church.

— prologue to the Complutensian Polyglot (Cisneros).3 The Hebrew and the Greek — the very fountains the humanists prized — are the two thieves; the Vulgate is Christ. The Polyglot recovers the sources magnificently and then subordinates them: it prints the Greek and Hebrew so the Latin can be checked and defended, not dethroned. Of a piece with this, Cisneros printed no Spanish column and wanted none — he held that “the Word of God had to be enveloped in discreet mysteries far from the reach of the common man.” Set beside Erasmus’s wish, published two years later, that the plowman sing Scripture at the plow and the weaver at the loom, the contrast is total: two scholars going back to the same fountains, and drawing opposite conclusions about who is allowed to drink. The Complutensian is the humanist recovery of the sources in the service of the old order, not against it.

Printed first, published second: the race with Erasmus

Alcalá won the printing and lost the market. The Greek New Testament volume carries a colophon of 10 January 1514 — two years before Erasmus’s — but Cisneros held it back to publish the New Testament together with the still-unfinished Old Testament as one complete Bible. In the gap, word of the Spanish project reached Erasmus, who rushed his own Greek New Testament (the Novum Instrumentum) through Froben’s Basel press in 1516, secured a four-year exclusive printing privilege from Pope Leo X and the Emperor Maximilian, and reached the market first. Cisneros died in November 1517, months after the Old Testament was printed and years before the work was released; the Polyglot did not circulate until about 1522.4 Whether that priority dispute shows scholarship losing to speed and commercial privilege — and what it says about humanist print culture — is weighed on its own argument page (assessed sound).

Notes

  • Where it sits on the timeline. Between Reuchlin’s Hebrew grammar (c. 1510) and Erasmus’s Novum Instrumentum (1516) — the collaborative, church-sponsored monument standing between the two lone scholars who bracket it, and the foil against which Erasmus’s one-man, plowman-facing edition reads most sharply.
  • The canon dimension is treated separately. The Polyglot is also a load-bearing witness on the Old Testament canon: its second preface set the deuterocanonical books “outside the canon,” received “for the edification of the people” rather than for confirming doctrine, in Jerome’s graded sense — a Catholic cardinal’s Bible voicing the shorter-canon distinction a generation before Trent. That question, and the careful limits on the “Pope Leo X approved the shorter canon” slogan, are weighed on its Old Testament canon page; this page does not restate it.
  • Cisneros the man. Francisco Jiménez de Cisneros (c. 1436–1517) financed the whole Polyglot from his own archiepiscopal revenues and founded the university that housed it, but died before it was published — so the “cardinal’s Bible” was compiled under his direction and released after his death. The recovery of the sources was, for him, a work of the Church’s learned elite, kept within the Church.
  • Provenance. The English of the Nebrija plea and of the “two thieves” prologue is given from the standard scholarly accounts and library editions cited below; the printing dates are from the volumes’ own colophons as reported in those accounts.
