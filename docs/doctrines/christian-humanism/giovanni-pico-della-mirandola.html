Giovanni Pico della Mirandola on Christian humanism

Dates: 1463–1494 (the Oration on the Dignity of Man and the 900 theses, Rome, December 1486; the papal condemnation and Apologia, 1487; absolution, 1493) · Wikipedia
Summary: Pico is the Platonist wing of Christian humanism, and its boldest wager on the question this timeline exists to weigh. Where Valla and the northern circle took ad fontes back to the Greek and Hebrew of Scripture, Pico — the young prodigy of Marsilio Ficino’s Florentine Academy — carried the return-to-the-sources instinct back to Plato, Plotinus, the Hermetica, Zoroaster, Orpheus, Pythagoras, and above all the Hebrew Kabbalah, in pursuit of a prisca theologia: an “ancient theology” he held to run beneath all the sages and to converge on the truth of Christ. It is the most venturesome answer the movement gave to its own central question — that a return to pagan and Jewish wisdom does not corrode Christian faith but confirms it — and it is the first to be condemned.

Claims & evidence

The dignity and freedom of man — the Oration (c. 1486)

Pico wrote the Oration on the Dignity of Man as the opening speech for a public disputation of 900 theses he planned to hold at Rome. It has become the best-known philosophical text of the fifteenth century, and it opens not on Scripture but on a pagan sage — Hermes Trismegistus — set beside an Arab, to announce man as the wonder of the world:

I have read in the records of the Arabians, reverend Fathers, that Abdala the Saracen, when questioned as to what on this stage of the world, as it were, could be seen most worthy of wonder, replied: “There is nothing to be seen more wonderful than man.” In agreement with this opinion is the saying of Hermes Trismegistus: “A great miracle, Asclepius, is man.”

— Oration on the Dignity of Man, trans. Elizabeth Livermore Forbes.1 The reason man is that wonder, Pico says, is that he alone has no fixed nature. In the Oration’s famous set-piece God addresses the newly made Adam and gives him, uniquely among creatures, the power to make himself into whatever he chooses:

Neither a fixed abode nor a form that is thine alone nor any function peculiar to thyself have we given thee, Adam, to the end that according to thy longing and according to thy judgment thou mayest have and possess what abode, what form, and what functions thou thyself shalt desire. The nature of all other beings is limited and constrained within the bounds of laws prescribed by Us. Thou, constrained by no limits, in accordance with thine own free will, in whose hand We have placed thee, shalt ordain for thyself the limits of thy nature. We have set thee at the world’s center that thou mayest from thence more easily observe whatever is in the world. We have made thee neither of heaven nor of earth, neither mortal nor immortal, so that with freedom of choice and with honor, as though the maker and molder of thyself, thou mayest fashion thyself in whatever shape thou shalt prefer. Thou shalt have the power to degenerate into the lower forms of life, which are brutish. Thou shalt have the power, out of thy soul’s judgment, to be reborn into the higher forms, which are divine.

— Pico, Oration, trans. Forbes.2 This is ad fontes read as an anthropology: the return to the sources is possible because man is the one creature free to ascend the whole chain of being, drawing on any wisdom to fashion himself upward toward the divine. The speech is Christian humanism’s highest claim for what the disciplined study of all the sources could do to a human soul.

The concord of the sages — prisca theologia and the one truth

Pico refused to bind himself to any one school. The second half of the Oration defends the sheer number and variety of his theses on the ground that truth is single and scattered piecemeal through every tradition, so that the way to it is to range across them all:

I, on the other hand, have so prepared myself that, pledged to the doctrines of no man, I have ranged through all the masters of philosophy, investigated all books, and come to know all schools.

— Pico, Oration, trans. Forbes.3 To investigate the Latin schoolmen only, he argues, would be to shut out the fountainhead, “since all wisdom has flowed from the East to the Greeks and from the Greeks to us.”4 This is the doctrine the humanists called prisca theologia — an “ancient theology” older than the schools, half-hidden in the pagan and Hebrew sages, and secretly agreeing with the Gospel. Pico names its witnesses and stakes his theses on their concord:

For this reason I have not been content to add to the tenets held in common many teachings taken from the ancient theology of Hermes Trismegistus, many from the doctrines of the Chaldaeans and of Pythagoras, and many from the occult mysteries of the Hebrews. I have proposed also as subjects for discussion several theses in natural philosophy and in divinity, discovered and studied by me. I have proposed, first of all, a harmony between Plato and Aristotle, believed to exist by many ere this but adequately proved by none.

— Pico, Oration, trans. Forbes.5 Where Valla’s philology cleared errors out of the sacred text, Pico’s syncretism reached to draw the whole pagan inheritance in — on the wager that the same truth speaks, veiled, in Moses and in Plato alike.

Natural magic and the Kabbalah — the boldest test of the question

Among the 900 theses were conclusions on magic and on the Kabbalah, and it is here that Pico’s answer to “purify or corrode?” reached its furthest edge. He is careful to split magic in two: the “former,” which works through demons, he condemns; the “latter,” natural magic, he defends as the crown of the study of nature:

The other, when it is rightly pursued, is nothing else than the utter perfection of natural philosophy. While the Greeks make mention of both of them, they call the former γοητεια, in no wise honoring it with the name of magic; the latter they call by the characteristic and fitting name of μαγεια, as if it were a perfect and most high wisdom.

— Pico, Oration, trans. Forbes.6 Then he turns to the Hebrew Kabbalah — books he had bought and mastered at great cost — and cites them expressly “for the confirmation of the inviolable Catholic faith.”7 His claim is startling: read rightly, the Jewish esoteric tradition proves the specifically Christian mysteries the rabbis themselves deny:

When I had purchased these books at no small cost to myself, when I had read them through with the greatest diligence and with unwearying toil, I saw in them (as God is my witness) not so much the Mosaic as the Christian religion. There is the mystery of the Trinity, there the Incarnation of the Word, there the divinity of the Messiah; there I have read about original sin, its expiation through Christ, the heavenly Jerusalem, the fall of the devils, the orders of the angels, purgatory, and the punishments of hell, the same things we read daily in Paul and Dionysius, in Jerome and Augustine.

— Pico, Oration, trans. Forbes.8 He compressed the same conviction into one of his most notorious theses: “There is no science that assures us more of the divinity of Christ than magic and the Kabbalah.” With that sentence Christian Kabbalah is born as a scholarly project — and its first utterance already carries the charge that a Christian layman was claiming for pagan and Jewish esoterica what philosophy and the fathers alone could not do.9

The condemnation — when the sources-quarrel first drew blood

Pico published the theses at Rome in December 1486 and offered to pay the travel of any scholar who would come to dispute them at Epiphany 1487. The disputation never took place. Pope Innocent VIII suspended it and appointed a commission, which condemned thirteen of the theses as heretical or suspect; when Pico answered with a defiant Apologia defending the incriminated conclusions, the pope condemned the whole nine hundred — in language that names exactly the humanists’ feared “corrosion”:

In part heretical, in part the flower of heresy; several are scandalous and offensive to pious ears; most do nothing but reproduce the errors of pagan philosophers.

— the condemnation of Innocent VIII (1487), as quoted in the modern accounts.10 Pico fled toward France, was briefly arrested at the pope’s demand and held at Vincennes, and was released only through the intercession of Italian princes; he was not cleared of the censures until 1493, under the new pope Alexander VI. This is the sharpest single episode on the timeline: the same “back to the sources” energy that in Valla unmasked a forged charter and in the northern humanists re-edited the New Testament, pressed to its widest source in Pico, met the church’s first formal “this corrodes.”11

Notes

  • Where he stands on the timeline. Pico is the Platonist counterweight to the philological wing. Valla, Colet, Reuchlin, the Complutensian team, and Erasmus all read ad fontes as textual criticism — recover the original words of Scripture and the fathers. Pico reads it as metaphysics: recover the one ancient truth scattered through all the sources, pagan and Jewish included. He therefore tests the timeline’s central question at its outer limit — not “does the Greek Testament unsettle the Vulgate?” but “does the whole inheritance of Hermes, Zoroaster, and the Kabbalah purify the faith or paganize it?” — and he is the first witness here to be formally condemned for the answer.
  • The Florentine Academy and Ficino. Pico drew the prisca theologia from Marsilio Ficino, whose Academy under Medici patronage had translated Plato, Plotinus, and the Hermetica into Latin and argued that a “pious philosophy” ran from the ancient sages down to Christ. Ficino is the natural next witness for this wing; Pico is slotted first as the sharper and more controversial representative.
  • The line to Reuchlin. Pico’s Christian Kabbalah is the direct ancestor of Johann Reuchlin’s, later on this timeline: Reuchlin took up Pico’s thesis that “no science assures us more of the divinity of Christ than magic and the Kabbalah,” built the first Christian textbooks of Hebrew and cabalistic study, and inherited the same charge — that Christian resort to Jewish books corrodes the faith — that had felled Pico a generation earlier.
  • An overreach the movement itself could not carry. Even by the humanists’ own lights Pico’s boldest claim overshoots: that magic and the Kabbalah give surer proof of Christ’s divinity than any other science is a conjecture far beyond what the evidence bears, and it rests in part on texts (the Hermetica above all) that later scholarship would date long after Moses rather than before him. The church’s condemnation was not only about authority; the syncretist wager genuinely strained toward the paganism its critics feared. The wiki weighs the underlying reading — that pagan wisdom confirms the Gospel — where it belongs, on its own argument page rather than in this witness’s advocacy.
  • His later self-correction. Pico did not hold every occult enthusiasm to the end. In his last years, drawn to the reforming preacher Savonarola, he wrote the Disputationes adversus astrologiam divinatricem — a massive attack on divinatory astrology as fraudulent and impious — pulling back from part of the very occult program the 1486 theses had embraced. He died in 1494, aged thirty-one, the same day Charles VIII of France entered Florence.
  • Editions. The verbatim above is from Elizabeth Livermore Forbes’s translation of the Oration in The Renaissance Philosophy of Man (Cassirer, Kristeller, and Randall, eds., University of Chicago Press, 1948), with Paul Oskar Kristeller’s introduction; the Greek words (γοητεια, μαγεια) are Pico’s own, kept in the Greek by Forbes. The 900 theses are edited and translated in S. A. Farmer, Syncretism in the West: Pico’s 900 Theses (1486) (1998).
