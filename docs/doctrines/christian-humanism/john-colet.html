John Colet on Christian humanism

Dates: c. 1467–1519 (the Oxford lectures on St Paul’s Epistles, from about 1497; the founding of St Paul’s School, c. 1509–1512; the Convocation sermon, 6 February 1512) · Wikipedia
Summary: Colet is the man who carried ad fontes from the Italian study into the English pulpit and lecture-hall. Reading St Paul at Oxford, he set aside the scholastic four-fold sense and expounded the epistles as real letters written by a real man to a real church in a datable historical moment — the humanist grammarian’s method turned on Scripture. He became the hub of the English humanist circle (the friend of Erasmus and Thomas More, and the man Erasmus credited with turning him toward theology), founded St Paul’s School on humanist lines under lay governors, and preached a scorching reform sermon to Convocation in 1512 — humanist reform from within the church, a decade before the Reformation broke.

Claims & evidence

Reading Paul as a letter to a real church — the Oxford lectures (from c. 1497)

Returning from study in Italy, Colet lectured freely at Oxford on St Paul’s epistles without a divinity degree or a chair. The break with the schools was in the method: where the scholastic quaestio broke a verse into its four senses (literal, allegorical, moral, anagogical) and hung syllogisms on it, Colet read the letter continuously, as one man’s words to a particular congregation at a particular date. Expounding Romans he pauses to fix the epistle in real time:

This Epistle to the Romans was written during the reign of Claudius, at the close of his reign, about the twentieth year of St. Paul’s ministry. At which time also, as I gather from the histories and from the letters of St. Paul himself, both Epistles to the Corinthians were written, as well as that to the Galatians: but this one to the Romans after them, not long before St. Paul’s last journey to Jerusalem.

— An Exposition of St. Paul’s Epistle to the Romans, trans. J. H. Lupton.1 Then, reading Paul’s counsel of submission to the authorities (Romans 13) not as a timeless maxim but as tactful advice shaped by a live political danger, he reconstructs the occasion out of Suetonius — the historian’s method, not the schoolman’s:

I mention this, that St. Paul’s great thoughtfulness and prudence may be remarked. For being aware that Claudius Cæsar had succeeded to the throne; a man of changeable disposition, and bad principles, and sudden purposes; a man too who, as Suetonius writes in his Life, banished the Jews from Rome, as they were in constant insurrection at the instigation of Chrestus (on account of which insurrection I suppose St. Paul to have written this Epistle…)

— Colet, Exposition of Romans, trans. Lupton.2 This is bonae litterae applied to the sacred page: the epistle is a historical document, its meaning recovered by grammar, chronology, and the classical sources, and its plain sense — not a fourfold allegory — is what the preacher expounds.

Humanist reform from within: the Convocation sermon of 1512

When Archbishop Warham summoned Convocation in February 1512, Colet, now Dean of St Paul’s, was appointed to preach the opening sermon — and used it to indict the clergy to their faces. He took his text from Paul, and read the apostle’s “be not conformed” as aimed first of all at priests and bishops:

For he, writing unto the Romans, and under their name unto you, saith, “Be you not conformed to this world, but be you reformed in the newness of your understanding, that ye may prove what is the good will of God, well pleasing and perfect.” This did the Apostle write to all Christian men, but most chiefly unto priests and bishops.

— The Convocation Sermon, modernized text ed. Katherine Stearns.3 The sermon’s sharpest stroke reverses the age’s alarm about Lollard heresy: the corrupt life of the clergy, Colet says (following Bernard), is itself a graver heresy than any doctrinal error:

We are also nowadays grieved of heretics, men mad with marvelous foolishness. But the heresies of them are not so pestilent and pernicious unto us and the people as the evil and wicked life of priests, the which, if we believe Saint Bernard, is a certain kind of heresy, and chief of all and most perilous.

— Colet, Convocation Sermon, ed. Stearns.4 And the remedy he urges is not new legislation but the recovery of what already exists — the same “back to the sources” instinct, turned on canon law and clerical discipline:

The way whereby the Church may be reformed into better fashion is not for to make new laws. For there be laws many enough and out of number… Therefore it is no need that new laws and constitutions be made, but that those that are made already be kept.

— Colet, Convocation Sermon, ed. Stearns.5 The sermon is the clearest sixteenth-century statement that humanist reform meant reform inside the church — a purifying of clergy and text from within, not a break with Rome. Colet died a loyal Catholic; the reforming energy he voiced would within a decade be claimed by men who did break.

The hub of the English humanist circle — Erasmus’s testimony

Colet’s importance is magnified by the friends he drew. Erasmus, who came to England in 1499, heard Colet lecture at Oxford and never forgot it; in his memorial letter to Justus Jonas (1521) he recalled the crowds Colet’s free lectures drew:

Soon after his return from Italy he left his father’s house, as he preferred to reside at Oxford, and there he publicly and gratuitously expounded all St. Paul’s Epistles… Though he had neither obtained nor sought for any degree in Divinity, yet there was no Doctor there, either of Divinity or Law, no Abbat, or other dignitary, but came to hear him, and brought his text-books with him as well.

— Erasmus, The Lives of Jehan Vitrier and John Colet, trans. J. H. Lupton.6 It was Colet who pressed the younger Erasmus to leave the arts of eloquence for the exposition of Scripture. In a letter of 1499 Erasmus, half-flattered and half-alarmed, records Colet’s insistence that he take up the Old Testament as Colet had taken up Paul:

For you urge, nay, you almost reproachfully insist, that, as you are doing with St. Paul, so I should expound the ancient Moses, or the eloquent Isaiah, and thus, as you express it, try to lend warmth to the studies of this University in the chilliness of these winter months.

— Erasmus to Colet, 1499, in Lupton, A Life of John Colet.7 Erasmus declined the invitation then — pleading that he was too poorly equipped in Greek — but the redirection stuck: the Greek that Colet’s challenge exposed as lacking he went off to master, and the philosophia Christi and the Greek New Testament of 1516 grew from the soil Colet turned. Thomas More too belonged to this circle, calling Colet the director of his life. The England–Rotterdam axis of the northern Renaissance runs through Colet’s Oxford lecture-room.

St Paul’s School, founded on humanist lines

Colet put his humanism into an institution. About 1509–1512 he spent his large inherited fortune re-founding St Paul’s School beside the cathedral for 153 boys, taught the new Latin (and Greek) learning free of charge. Most tellingly, he refused to entrust it to churchmen, handing its governance to the lay Mercers’ Company — a choice Erasmus reports in Colet’s own reasoning:

Over the revenues and the entire management of his school he placed neither priests, nor the Bishop, nor the Chapter (as they call it), nor noblemen, but some married citizens of established reputation. And when asked the reason, he said that, while there was nothing certain in human affairs, he yet found the least corruption in these.

— Erasmus, Lives of Vitrier and Colet, trans. Lupton.8 The school made bonae litterae the staple of an English education and outlasted its founder by centuries — the humanist program embodied in bricks and a curriculum, not just argued in a preface.

Notes

  • Where he stands on the timeline. Colet inherits Valla’s wager — that real philology purifies rather than corrodes — and carries it north into biblical exposition and church reform. He is the English hinge between the Italian philologists and the great biblical humanist of the next generation, Erasmus, whom he turned toward theology.
  • A loyal reformer, not a Protestant. Colet’s attacks on clerical corruption, image-cult, and pilgrimage abuses led some later Protestants to claim him as a forerunner, and drew a charge of heresy from his own bishop (Fitzjames) that Archbishop Warham dismissed. But Colet died in communion with Rome; his reform was the humanist “keep the laws we have,” not a new church. Whether that road-not-taken could have held is part of what this timeline exists to weigh.
  • Editions. Colet’s Oxford lectures survive only in the Latin manuscripts J. H. Lupton edited and translated in the 1870s; the verbatim above is from Lupton’s Exposition of St. Paul’s Epistle to the Romans (1873). The Convocation sermon is quoted from the modern critical-modernized text edited by Katherine Stearns (Center for Thomas More Studies, 2020); the original early-modern English version, and the Latin, are printed by Lupton in his Life of John Colet (2nd ed., 1909).
