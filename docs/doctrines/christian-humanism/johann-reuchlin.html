Johann Reuchlin on Christian humanism

Dates: c. 1455–1522 (born Pforzheim, 29 January 1455; the Hebrew grammar De rudimentis hebraicis, 1506; the Recommendation on the Jewish books, dated Stuttgart 6 October 1510, published in the Augenspiegel, 1511; the heresy trial and the Speyer acquittal, 1513–1514; the papal condemnation of the Augenspiegel, 23 June 1520; died Stuttgart, 30 June 1522) · Wikipedia
Summary: Reuchlin carried ad fontes to the Hebrew Old Testament and its rabbinic apparatus. His De rudimentis hebraicis (1506) was an early substantial Hebrew grammar written by a Christian for Christians, opening the language of the Old Testament to Christian scholars. When the convert Johannes Pfefferkorn and the Cologne Dominicans won an imperial mandate to confiscate and burn Jewish books, Reuchlin — alone among the experts consulted — argued for their preservation, on scholarly and on legal grounds, and was prosecuted for heresy in a trial that split the German learned world. His humanist backers rallied to him in the satirical Letters of Obscure Men, a satire of the schoolmen.

Claims & evidence

Ad fontes to the Hebrew source — De rudimentis hebraicis (1506)

Reuchlin had learned Hebrew from the emperor’s Jewish physician Jacob ben Jehiel Loans and paid dearly for the lessons. His De rudimentis hebraicis of 1506 — an in-folio of some 600 pages combining an alphabet, a Hebrew dictionary arranged by root, and a grammar drawn from the medieval Jewish grammarians (above all David Kimhi) — was an early workable Hebrew grammar by a Christian. From its appearance, in a phrase quoted in the scholarship (⚠ unverified: its author is not named in the sources cited here), “it was possible to learn Hebrew.” Reuchlin closed the volume with a line from Horace:

Exegi monumentum aere perennius. Nonis Martiis Anno M.D.VI.

— colophon to De rudimentis hebraicis (7 March 1506): “I have raised a monument more lasting than bronze.”1 Horace had meant his poetry would outlast bronze; Reuchlin applies the line to a Hebrew grammar.

The Hebrew-books controversy — the Recommendation of 1510

In 1509 the convert Johannes Pfefferkorn, backed by the Dominicans of Cologne, obtained a mandate from Emperor Maximilian to confiscate and destroy Jewish books alleged to slander Christianity. When princes and prelates protested, the emperor referred the question to a commission of experts. Reuchlin, a Hebraist, delivered his opinion (the Recommendation whether to Confiscate, Destroy, and Burn All Jewish Books, dated Stuttgart, 6 October 1510) against the burning. His first argument was that one may not condemn what one has not read.

How then can the Christians justify the condemnation of the Talmud, a work which they themselves do not even understand?

— Reuchlin, Recommendation (1510), trans. Peter Wortsman.2 And the rabbinic commentaries the campaign wanted burned are, he argues, exactly what Christian scholarship of the Old Testament cannot do without — the living channel of the Hebrew itself:

The Christian Church can and may not cast aside such commentaries, for they keep alive the original Hebrew, a vital element which the Holy Scripture… cannot do without.

— Reuchlin, Recommendation, trans. Wortsman.3 On Reuchlin’s argument, burning the rabbinic commentaries would leave Christian study of the Old Testament without the Hebrew they keep alive.

Jews as fellow-citizens — the legal defense

Reuchlin was not only a Hebraist but a trained jurist and an imperial judge, and he grounded the second half of his case in law rather than philology. Against the long tradition (Duns Scotus among others) that treated Jews as chattel-slaves of the empire with no property rights, he argued that Jews resident in the empire were concives — fellow-citizens — whose goods, their books included, the state could not simply seize:

We and they are fellow citizens of one and the same Roman Empire.

— Reuchlin, Recommendation (1510).4 He was careful that this did not claim equal standing in religion — he still hoped for Jewish conversion — but as a matter of imperial and canon law (he cited the Sicut Judaeis constitution) their property was protected. The German leader of Jewry, Josel of Rosheim, would later call Reuchlin’s intervention a “miracle within a miracle.” The opinion led to a heresy trial.

The Reuchlin affair and the Letters of Obscure Men

Pfefferkorn answered with a pamphlet (the Handspiegel) hawked at the 1511 Frankfurt fair; Reuchlin replied by publishing his opinion within the Augenspiegel (“Eye-glass,” 1511), turning a confidential report into a public cause. The Cologne inquisitor Jacob van Hoogstraaten charged the book with heresy; on 7 October 1512 the university and inquisitor won an imperial order to confiscate it. Reuchlin was summoned before the Inquisition, but his case was moved to the bishop’s court at Speyer, which in 1514 acquitted him and taxed the costs to Hoogstraaten. Cologne appealed to Rome; a papal commission largely favored Reuchlin, but in July 1516 the trial was simply suspended, and on 23 June 1520 — the same season Leo X moved against Luther — the pope at last condemned the Augenspiegel and silenced Reuchlin, ordering him to pay the costs.5

By then the affair had become the open war of the humanists against the schoolmen. When Reuchlin printed a volume of supportive letters from the learned (his Letters of Illustrious Men, 1514), his younger partisans — Crotus Rubeanus and Ulrich von Hutten among them — answered with a parody: the Letters of Obscure Men (1515–1519), fictional letters in barbarous dog-Latin from imaginary Cologne theologians who expose their own ignorance, gluttony, and bigotry as they fret over Reuchlin and the Jews. One “obscure man” writes to the real Cologne master Ortuin Gratius in a panic of misplaced scruple, having tipped his hat at the Frankfurt fair to two dignified figures in gowns — who turned out to be Jews:

Then the bachelor slapped me on the back, and said: “For the love of God, what are you doing? They are Jews, and you have taken off your hat to them!” At this such a fright seized me as if I had seen the devil, and I answered: “Sir Baccalaureus, God have mercy upon me. I have done it in ignorance; so what do you think; is that a grievous sin?”

— Letters of Obscure Men, trans. Merrick Whitcomb.6 The satire’s standing joke is that Reuchlin’s enemies cannot even read the languages they wish to burn: the “obscure men” gravely report that Pfefferkorn “could not possibly be the author of the book, for he has never learned a word of Latin,” then explain that he simply writes down whatever he overhears.7 The satire set the “good letters” against the “barbarous learning” of the schools.

Placement and editions

  • Placement on the timeline. Reuchlin is slotted just after Colet on the anchor of the controversy (1506–1520), though the two are near-contemporaries. Where Valla worked on the Latin text and Colet on the Pauline epistles, Reuchlin worked on the Hebrew source.
  • Editions. The verbatim above is from: the 1506 first edition of De rudimentis hebraicis (Pforzheim: Thomas Anshelm) for the colophon; Peter Wortsman’s translation of the Recommendation for the two book-controversy passages; and Merrick Whitcomb’s translation of the Letters of Obscure Men. The concives line is cited from David H. Price’s standard study, which quotes the Recommendation. Francis Griffin Stokes’s 1909 edition (Latin and English) remains the fullest scholarly text of the Letters.
