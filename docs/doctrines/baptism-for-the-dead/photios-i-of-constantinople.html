Photius of Constantinople — the reductio, and Paul’s third person as tact

Dates: c. 875 (his exegetical fragments on 1 Corinthians, preserved in the Greek catena on Paul; Photius was patriarch of Constantinople 858–867 and 877–886); Photius c. 810–893 · Wikipedia
Summary: The great ninth-century patriarch and scholar reads “baptized for the dead” with the settled Greek non-proxy sense — baptism looks to the resurrection of the dead, and denying that resurrection empties it — but sharpens it into a pointed reductio against the resurrection-deniers, and adds an observation none of his predecessors made: that Paul frames the clause in the third person (“those who are baptized”) not because the baptized are other people, but as deliberate rhetorical tact — rebuking the deniers “under other persons” so as not to drive them to despair. He appears on this wiki also as a witness on the filioque timeline.

The two fragments on 15:29

Photius’s comment survives as two consecutive fragments in the Greek catena on 1 Corinthians, the first rubricated Φωτίου (“of Photius”) and the second Τοῦ αὐτοῦ (“of the same”), printed side by side with Chrysostom’s and Theodoret’s notes on the verse:

Φωτίου. «Ἐπεὶ τί ποιήσουσι,» φησὶν, «οἱ βαπτιζόμενοι,» ὥστε μὴ εἶναι διὰ παντὸς νεκροὶ καὶ ἐν τῷ Ἅιδῃ κατέχεσθαι; οἷον· τῆς ἀναστάσεως, φησὶν, μὴ οὔσης, οἱ ἐπὶ τοιαύταις ἐλπίσι βαπτιζόμενοι, ὥστε μετὰ θάνατον ἀναστῆναι, τί ποιήσουσιν;»

Τοῦ αὐτοῦ. «Εἰ ὅλως νεκροὶ οὐκ ἐγείρονται, τί βαπτίζονται ἵνα ἀναστῶσιν; αὐτὸς δὲ ἐπιπληκτικώτερον ὑπὲρ τῶν νεκρῶν φησιν, οἷον, οὓς ὑμεῖς διὰ τῆς ἰδίας δόξης ἀϊδίους νεκροὺς ἀπεφήνατε· νεκροὺς δὲ καὶ τοὺς ἄλλους καὶ ἑαυτοὺς, καὶ νεκροὺς αἰωνίους ἀποφηνάμενοι, τί βαπτίζεσθε ὑπὲρ ὑμῶν τῶν μηκέτι καθ’ ὑμᾶς ἀναστησομένων ἀλλ’ ἀεὶ νεκρῶν ἐσομένων; ἐπὶ ἄλλων δὲ προσώπων προσάγει τὸν λόγον, καὶ οὐχὶ ἐπὶ αὐτῶν ἐκείνων, ἵνα μὴ πλέον καθαψάμενος εἰς ἀπόνοιαν ἐκκαλέσηται.»

— that is: [Photius] “‘For what will they do,’ he says, ‘those who are baptized,’ so as not to be dead forever and held fast in Hades? That is: if there is no resurrection, he says, those who are baptized on such hopes — namely, to rise again after death — what will they do?” [Of the same] “If the dead are not raised at all, why are they baptized in order to rise? And he speaks the more cuttingly on behalf of the dead — as if to say: those whom you, by your own doctrine, have pronounced everlastingly dead — having pronounced the others too, and yourselves, dead, and eternally dead — why are you baptized on behalf of your own who, on your view, will never rise but remain forever dead? But he directs the argument under other persons, and not against those very men, so that he might not, by pressing them too hard, provoke them to despair.”1

The reading: a reductio, not a rite

Photius takes the verse wholly of ordinary baptism, in the same non-proxy line as Chrysostom and Theodoret: the baptized are “baptized for the dead” in that they are baptized in hope of the resurrection of the dead, so that a denial of the resurrection turns the whole rite into an absurdity — on the deniers’ own premise, the baptized are washed only “to remain forever dead and held in Hades.” He presses this as a formal reductio ad absurdum: grant that “the dead are not raised at all,” and the practice the Corinthians themselves keep makes no sense. This is the settled Greek reading given a debater’s edge.

Paul’s third person: tact, not distance

The distinctive contribution is the second fragment’s closing sentence. Photius notices what modern advocates of the vicarious reading count their strongest card — that Paul writes in the third person (“what shall they do… why are they baptized”), never “you” or “we” — and gives it the opposite construction. Where the vicarious reading takes the distancing “they” as Paul holding a rite he does not share at arm’s length, Photius reads it as rhetorical tact: Paul “directs the argument under other persons” (ἐπὶ ἄλλων δὲ προσώπων), addressing the resurrection-deniers obliquely rather than accusing them to their faces, “so that he might not, by pressing them too hard, provoke them to despair” (εἰς ἀπόνοιαν … ἐκκαλέσηται). On this reading the very grammar the vicarious camp leans on is a pastoral softening, not a marker of someone else’s rite. It is the one ancient reply that meets the grammatical argument head-on rather than working around it — and it is weighed, with its limits, in the adversarial assessment of the non-vicarious readings and of the vicarious reading.

His place in the tradition — and a note on “Oecumenius”

Photius stands with Theodoret (c. 435) and Cosmas Indicopleustes (c. 550) in the Greek non-proxy line, and just ahead of Theophylact of Ohrid (c. 1075), who a couple of centuries later closes the medieval Greek tradition by reproducing Chrysostom’s homily almost verbatim. Meyer, cataloguing the ancient readings, files “Chrysostom… Pelagius, Oecumenius, Photius, Theophylact” together under the creedal-confession sense.2 The commentary that travels under the name of Oecumenius adds nothing independent on this verse: its comment on 15:29 simply weaves together Chrysostom’s note and Photius’s fragments, and its own remarks in this stretch attach to the previous verse (the Son’s subjection, 15:28) — so there is no separate “Oecumenian” witness to slot here.3
