How has “baptized for the dead” (1 Corinthians 15:29) been read?

Disputed reading · on Baptized for the Dead (1 Corinthians 15:29) · witnesses: Tertullian, Epiphanius of Salamis, John Chrysostom, Theodoret of Cyrus and Photius of Constantinople

Related readings: What was the character and origin of the Corinthian “baptism for the dead”?

The passage

“Else what shall they do which are baptized for the dead, if the dead rise not at all? why are they then baptized for the dead?”

— 1 Corinthians 15:29 (KJV), source.1

Ἐπεὶ τί ποιήσουσιν οἱ βαπτιζόμενοι ὑπὲρ τῶν νεκρῶν; εἰ ὅλως νεκροὶ οὐκ ἐγείρονται, τί καὶ βαπτίζονται ὑπὲρ αὐτῶν;

— 1 Corinthians 15:29, Greek, as printed by Matthew Michael Connor (2010), following the Nestle–Aland text.2

The question

Paul cites the practice of those “baptized for the dead” within his argument for the resurrection and does not describe it further. Interpreters differ over whom “the dead” denotes, what sense the preposition ὑπέρ bears, what “baptized” refers to, how the verse is punctuated, and whether Paul wrote it; the answer bears on whether the verse is the earliest extant reference to a baptism received for the dead. Hans Conzelmann (1975) counted over two hundred interpretations; Michael F. Hull (2005) finds “about forty general hypotheses.”3

A baptism received on behalf of dead persons

In brief: some at Corinth were being baptized on behalf of particular dead people, and Paul cites their practice to those who deny the resurrection.

Held by: Tertullian (On the Resurrection of the Flesh 48, c. 200); Ambrosiaster (c. 380); C. K. Barrett (commentary, 1968); Hans Conzelmann (Hermeneia commentary, 1975).4

The reading takes the words in what Conzelmann calls their “normal” sense: ὑπέρ with the genitive as “on behalf of,” and “the dead” as dead persons, the same dead whose rising the chapter argues for.5 Barrett writes that vicarious baptism is the idea “most naturally suggested by the words used” and reads the verse as an ad hominem: Paul presses the logic of what some Corinthians do, while the view that “the immersion of a living body can secure benefits to a dead man” “was not Paul's view.”6 Holders differ over Paul's attitude to the practice: Barrett reads the citation as ad hominem, while the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, which quotes the verse in Doctrine and Covenants 128 (1842), reads it as a reference to an ordinance of the early church.7 Ancient writers describe such rites. Tertullian says their practitioners supposed “that the vicarious baptism… would be beneficial to the flesh of another in anticipation of the resurrection”; Epiphanius heard that among the Cerinthians of Asia and Galatia “others would be baptized for them in their names”; Ambrosiaster writes that “some people were at that time being baptized for the dead”; and Chrysostom and Didymus describe the rite among the Marcionites.8

Baptism “for the body”: the baptized person's own mortal body and its resurrection

In brief: the phrase describes ordinary Christian baptism, received in the hope that the baptized person's own dead body will rise.

Held by: Tertullian (Against Marcion 5.10, c. 200); John Chrysostom (Homilies on 1 Corinthians 40, c. 395); Theodoret of Cyrus (c. 435); J. C. O'Neill (1980, reading an elided “bodies”).9

Tertullian sets the proxy practice aside, appeals to Paul's “but one baptism” (Ephesians 4:5), and concludes: “To be ‘baptized for the dead’ therefore means, in fact, to be baptized for the body; for, as we have shown, it is the body which becomes dead.”10 Chrysostom reads the verse as an argument from what Paul's hearers themselves do, “when a man brings forward the gainsayers themselves as witnessing by their own actions what he affirms.” What every candidate does is confess the resurrection at the font: “bidding them say, ‘I believe in the resurrection of the dead,’ and upon this faith we are baptized,” so that “the dead” are “the dead bodies.”11 Theodoret grounds the same sense in the rite as burial with Christ (Romans 6:4): “the one being baptized is buried together with the Master… but if the body is dead and does not rise, why on earth is he even baptized?”12 Photius meets the third person directly: Paul “directs the argument under other persons, and not against those very men, so that he might not, by pressing them too hard, provoke them to despair.”13 O'Neill supplies an elided σωμάτων and renders, “Otherwise what do those hope to achieve who are baptized for their dying bodies?”14

The baptism of the dying, “looked upon as already dead”

In brief: those “baptized for the dead” are catechumens baptized when near death, in hope of the resurrection.

Held by: the interpreters whose explanation Epiphanius reports and calls satisfactory (Panarion 28.6, c. 375); John Calvin (Reformed, Commentary on the Epistles to the Corinthians).15

Epiphanius gives the explanation that “as long as they are catechumens, the dying are allowed baptism before they die because of this hope, showing that the person who has died will also rise, and therefore needs the forgiveness of his sins through baptism.”16 Calvin starts from Paul's wording: “he does not speak of all when he says, What shall they do, who are baptized? etc.”, so that Paul “points out something peculiar.” He reads: “those are baptized for dead, who are looked upon as already dead, and who have altogether despaired of life; and in this way the particle ὑπέρ will have the force of the Latin pro.” He grounds it in a known custom: catechumens who had “fallen into disease, if their life was manifestly in danger, were accustomed to ask baptism, that they might not leave this world before they had made a profession of Christianity.” Calvin weighs the reading against the letter as a whole: Paul reproves lesser faults at Corinth and has “inveighed” against their handling of the Supper, so Calvin understands him “to speak here of the right use of baptism.” On this reading “Paul, however, here simply mentions a custom that was sacred, and in accordance with the Divine institution.”17

Baptism for “dead works”: the washing away of sins

In brief: “the dead” are sins, and to be baptized for them is to be baptized for their forgiveness.

Held by: Thomas Aquinas (Catholic, Commentary on 1 Corinthians, c. 1270).18

Aquinas takes “dead” as he finds it in Hebrews 9:14: “by ‘dead’ the works of sin are understood. They are dead, because they lack the life of grace and lead to death: ‘The blood of Christ will purify your conscience from dead works.’” On this sense, he writes, “the words are plain. What do people mean by being baptized on behalf of the dead? i.e., for washing away their sins, if they are not to have the life of grace?” The second half of the verse then runs: “if the dead are not raised at all, why are people baptized on their behalf, i.e., for their sins, since they are not forgiven.”19 On this sense the verse speaks of ordinary baptism for the forgiveness of sins, whose purpose fails if the dead do not rise.

Baptism over the graves of the dead

In brief: ὑπέρ has its local sense, “over,” and the Corinthians were baptized at the graves of the dead.

Held by: Martin Luther ⚠ unverified (Lutheran).20

White reports that Luther “wished to understand υπέρ in a local sense, postulating that baptisms in Corinth were performed over the graves of the dead”; Connor, that he “proposed that ὐπέρ be viewed in a locative and not an attributive sense, and thus the Corinthians were being baptized literally ‘over the dead’ in graveyards.”21 The lexical ground is the preposition's first sense with the genitive in Liddell and Scott, “I. of Place; over, above.”22 On this reading the rite is ordinary baptism, given at the resting-place of believers who had died, and Paul's question is why the living should be baptized over the graves of the dead if those dead do not rise. Baptism, “the dead” and the resurrection all keep their ordinary sense, and the preposition alone carries the place.

Repunctuated as clipped rhetorical questions

In brief: re-divided, the verse is a string of short taunting questions, and no baptism for the dead is described.

Held by: P. Dürselen ⚠ unverified; Bernard M. Foschini (Franciscan, Catholic Biblical Quarterly 1950–51; book 1951).23

The earliest Greek manuscripts were written without punctuation or word-division, so the punctuation of printed texts is editorial.24 Foschini places a question mark after οἱ βαπτιζόμενοι and another after βαπτίζονται, and translates: “Otherwise what shall they do who are baptized? for the dead? (that is, are they baptized to belong to, to be numbered among the dead, who are never to rise again)? Indeed, if the dead do not rise again at all, why are people baptized? For them? that is, are they baptized to be numbered among the dead who are never to rise again?” He judges this rendering “more simple and more probable than any other.”25 The verse stands at the head of a run of rhetorical questions (vv. 29–34), and the repunctuated questions keep that tone. Foschini's conclusion is that “no such ‘Baptism for the dead’ existed, and that it was a complete absurdity which he [Paul] fabricated,” a figure of the deniers' position: baptism into a company of the dead who never rise.26

Baptized “for the sake of” the dead, to be reunited with them

In brief: unbelievers were being baptized so as to be united with their baptized dead relatives at the resurrection.

Held by: Maria Raeder (Zeitschrift für die neutestamentliche Wissenschaft, 1955); Joachim Jeremias; Rudolf Schnackenburg; F. F. Bruce.27

Raeder takes the preposition as “das υπέρ der Abzweckung,” the ὑπέρ of purpose, and renders the phrase “die sich um der Toten willen taufen lassen” (that is, those who have themselves baptized for the sake of the dead). White summarises her reading: “it was not, in her view, the Christians in Corinth who were being baptized on behalf of unbelievers; rather unbelievers were submitting to Christian baptism for the purpose of being united with their deceased baptized relatives in the resurrection.”28 “The dead” keep their ordinary sense as dead persons, here dead believers, and baptism its ordinary sense as Christian baptism; the third person names a particular group, those brought to baptism by their dead. Paul's question then turns on hope: if the dead do not rise, there is no reunion for which to be baptized.

A Corinthian slogan about the apostles, taken up by Paul

In brief: the verse quotes a derogatory Corinthian phrase about the apostles, “those being destroyed,” which Paul turns to his own argument.

Held by: Jerome Murphy-O'Connor (Revue Biblique, 1981).29

Murphy-O'Connor starts from the context. As White quotes him: “If we accept that 1 Cor 15:29 refers to vicarious baptism, we are obliged to postulate a complete break between vv. 28 and 29, and another between vv. 29 and 30-34.” He finds instead “some intrinsic relationship between verses 29 and 31-32,” where Paul speaks of his own dangers and daily dying, and “a relationship between verse 29 and the antecedent context.” βαπτιζόμενοι bears its classical metaphorical sense, “those being destroyed,” and refers to the apostles; “the dead” are those “who for the spirituals [i.e., the πνευματικοί in Corinth] were not worth bothering about.” His translation: “What will they do who are being destroyed [i.e., the apostles] on account of (the resurrection of) the dead?”30 Connor paraphrases the proposal as a “Corinthian slogan denigrating Paul's work which Paul used to further his own argument.”31 The verse thus belongs to the paragraph about apostolic hardship that follows it.

Baptized on account of the apostles, “the dead” figuratively

In brief: Corinthians were baptized because of the apostles who brought them to faith, men Paul describes as dying daily.

Held by: Joel R. White (Journal of Biblical Literature, 1997).32

White offers as “the correct interpretation of 1 Cor 15:29: ‘Otherwise what will those do who are being baptized on account of the dead (that is, the dead, figuratively speaking; that is, the apostles)? For if truly dead persons are not raised, why at all are people being baptized on account of them (that is, the apostles)?’” He reads ὑπέρ causally, a use he finds in extrabiblical sources of Paul's time, and finds support in vv. 30–31, where Paul's “I die daily” is said of his apostolic ministry. The setting is the factions of 1 Corinthians 1–4: “Certain groups in Corinth were brought to faith and baptized ‘on account of’ some of the apostles, especially Paul and Apollos, to whom they subsequently and quite naturally felt an affinity.” Paul's own phrase in 4:6, “on the account of one (υπέρ του ενός, i.e., Paul),” uses the same construction. If there is no resurrection, “the Corinthians' allegiances to the apostles under whose ministries, respectively, they were converted is all the more ludicrous since the apostles, figuratively speaking, are already dead.”33

A later interpolation, not written by Paul

In brief: 1 Corinthians 15:29–34 was added to the letter by a later hand, perhaps to give apostolic warrant to a proxy baptism.

Held by: William O. Walker Jr. (Catholic Biblical Quarterly, 2007).34

Walker holds that 15:29–34 is an “interpolation, neither composed by Paul, nor included by him in his Corinthian letter.” Connor reports that he argues from the verses' lack of context, from their vocabulary (a number of New Testament hapax legomena and uncommon phrases in the section) and from their content. Walker reasons: “(1) The text speaks, without disapproval, of vicarious baptism on behalf of the dead. (2) It is highly unlikely, however, that Paul would have approved of such a practice. Therefore, (3) the text is most likely non-Pauline in origin.” Since Marcion's version of the letter included 15:29–34, and a Marcionite proxy baptism is reported later, he suggests that “1 Cor. 15:29 may have been inserted into Paul's Corinthian letter by a Marcionite or proto-Marcionite interpolator in order to provide apostolic warrant for the practice [of vicarious baptism].”35 On this reading the verse records a practice of the interpolator's own circle, and the Marcionite rite described by Chrysostom is the practice that lies behind it.
