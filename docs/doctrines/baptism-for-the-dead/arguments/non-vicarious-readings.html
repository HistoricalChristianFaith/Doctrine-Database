Do the words of 1 Corinthians 15:29 mean ordinary Christian baptism, not a proxy rite? — the non-vicarious readings

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: plausible — the reading that keeps the verse within ordinary Christian baptism (baptized professing “the resurrection of the dead”) is coherent, makes Paul’s argument-from-a-shared-practice work, and needs no aberrant Corinthian rite; but it pays a real grammatical price and is the less natural reading of the words, so it stands short of the vicarious sense · advanced by the Greek fathers (Chrysostom, Cosmas, Theophylact) and most of the orthodox tradition.

Related arguments: reading “baptized for the dead” — see also Does 1 Corinthians 15:29 attest a vicarious “baptism for the dead” at Corinth? (the vicarious reading)

The claim

“Else what shall they do which are baptized for the dead, if the dead rise not at all? why are they then baptized for the dead?”

— 1 Corinthians 15:29 (KJV). The claim weighed here is the mirror-image of the vicarious reading: that the phrase does not point to anyone undergoing baptism on behalf of a corpse, but describes the ordinary baptism of living believers — so that no proxy rite, orthodox or heretical, need be supposed at Corinth at all. This is a family of construals; the one advocated here in its strongest form is the creedal reading, the Greek East’s standard exegesis: to be “baptized for the dead” is to be baptized with a view to the resurrection of the dead that one confesses at the font, so that if the dead do not rise the whole baptismal profession is emptied. The kindred non-proxy readings are folded in below as less-probable variants of the same interpretation.

Who advances it

This is the reading the orthodox tradition reached for once it had declined to own the proxy rite. Its classic form is Chrysostom’s (c. 395): after ridiculing the Marcionite ruse he unfolds his own sense — the candidate, “bidding them say, ‘I believe in the resurrection of the dead,’ and upon this faith we are baptized,” is “baptized for the dead,” i.e. “the dead bodies… the resurrection of thy dead body.” Cosmas Indicopleustes (c. 550) repeats it — “we are baptized mystically for our dead bodies… from the hope and promise of the resurrection” — and Theophylact of Ohrid (c. 1075) reproduces Chrysostom’s homily almost word for word. A modern grammatical variant (“because of the dead”) is defended by Maria Raeder and others surveyed by Thiselton, and Calvin gives a further non-proxy reading (the dying catechumen “as good as dead”).1

The evidence for

Paul argues from a practice he shares. The rhetorical engine of the whole paragraph is that Paul points to something his hearers do and shows it presupposes the resurrection they deny — “a man brings forward the gainsayers themselves as witnessing by their own actions what he affirms” (Chrysostom). Ordinary baptism, with its confession of “the resurrection of the dead,” is exactly such a universally-held practice: to be baptized on that confession while denying its object is to sign a bond and then repudiate it. On this reading Paul needs no fringe custom — he appeals to the one rite every Corinthian Christian has undergone.2

ὑπέρ need not mean “in the place of” a person. With the genitive ὑπέρ ranges over “on behalf of,” “for the sake of,” “because of,” and (approaching περί) “with reference to, concerning.” The creedal reading takes it in the final/referential sense — baptized with a view to the raising of the dead — and the “because of” variant takes it causally (baptized on account of dead believers: converted through their witness, or in hope of reunion with them). Neither requires the substitutionary “in the stead of” that the proxy reading loads onto the preposition; both keep ὑπέρ inside its ordinary lexical range.3

It saves Paul from citing a rite he neither shares nor rebukes. Every clear ancient attestation of a literal “baptism for the dead” locates it among heretics — Marcionites, Cerinthians, Valentinians — and the mainstream church legislated against corpse-baptism (Carthage, 419). The non-vicarious reading spares the apostle the awkwardness the vicarious reading concedes: that he would appeal, without a word of reproof, to a “magical abuse” of the sacrament (Calvin’s objection). If the verse is about ordinary baptism, that awkwardness never arises.

The confession is real and early. The baptismal profession of bodily resurrection that Chrysostom invokes is not invented for the occasion — the interrogatory creed at the font, with its clause on “the resurrection of the flesh/the dead,” is attested across the early rites, so the creedal reading rests on a genuine liturgical fact, not a convenient supposition.4

The variants, strongest first

(1) The creedal reading (advocated here). “The dead” is the baptizand’s own mortal body, and “for the dead” is “with a view to [the resurrection of] the dead body” professed at baptism (Chrysostom, Cosmas, Theophylact). Its strength is the tight logical fit with Paul’s argument-from-baptism; its cost is that it must read τῶν νεκρῶν (“the dead”) not as the dead persons the chapter is otherwise about but as a shorthand for the doctrine of their rising, or as the baptizand’s own corpse-to-be.

(2) “Because of the dead.” ὑπέρ is causal/final and “the dead” stays actual dead people: some were baptized on account of the dead — moved to conversion by a dying or deceased Christian’s witness, or in hope of reunion with dead loved ones at the resurrection (Raeder). This variant pays the smallest grammatical price and, unlike the creedal reading, comfortably explains Paul’s third-person “they” (it names a subset — converts prompted by the dead — not the whole church). Its cost is rhetorical: the logic (“why be converted in hope of reunion if there is no rising?”) is looser than the creedal reading’s.

(3) “In view of one’s own death” / the dying catechumen. Either ὑπέρ = “with a view to” one’s own coming death and hoped-for resurrection, or (Calvin) “the dead” are those “looked upon as already dead,” the mortally-ill catechumen who asks baptism before he dies. Both keep the rite ordinary, but both strain τῶν νεκρῶν: the plural “the dead” does not naturally mean “one’s own single future corpse,” and Calvin’s “reckoned as good as dead” is a supplement the words do not carry.

Adversarial assessment

What holds. As an account of Paul’s argument, the creedal reading is strong: it gives him a premise his opponents actually grant — their own baptism, professing resurrection — and turns it against them, which is precisely the move the surrounding verses (“why stand we in jeopardy every hour?”, v. 30) make with other shared practices. It needs no lost or heretical rite, it coheres with a real baptismal confession, and it kept the field in the Greek church for a millennium. It is a coherent, well-pedigreed reading that is not ruled out.

What is strained. Two costs keep it below the vicarious sense. (1) The word “the dead.” Throughout 1 Corinthians 15 “the dead” (οἱ νεκροί) are dead persons; the creedal reading must make this one occurrence mean either the abstract doctrine of resurrection or the baptizand’s own not-yet-dead body — a shift the text does not signal. (2) Paul’s third person. The vicarious reading’s strongest card is that Paul asks what “they” do, not “we” or “you” — distancing himself from the practitioners two verses before he says “why stand we in jeopardy.” If the verse describes the ordinary baptism Paul and every Corinthian shares, the third person is awkward: one would expect “why are we baptized?” The creedal reading has no clean answer here; only variant (2), which restricts the “they” to a subset moved by the dead, absorbs the third person without strain — but it buys that at the price of the tight logic that made the creedal reading attractive. The family cannot have both advantages at once.

Symmetry with the rival. The honest picture is that this is the less probable reading of the words and the more comfortable reading of Paul’s theology. The proponents’ confidence overreaches in the other direction: Chrysostom dismisses the opposing reading as the folly of “madmen” and “fools” when in fact the grammar he is fighting is the plainer one — that is rhetoric, not exegesis, and it is flagged as such on his page. Conversely, the vicarious reading’s own weakness — that it must attribute to Paul an unrebuked, elsewhere-heretical rite — is exactly what keeps the non-vicarious family alive rather than refuted.

Assessment

Plausible — coherent, anciently and widely advocated, and free of the vicarious reading’s embarrassment about an unrebuked rite; but the less-probable reading of the Greek, since it must bend “the dead” away from the dead persons the chapter is about and (in its flagship creedal form) cannot easily account for Paul’s distancing third person. It is best judged not as better grammar than the vicarious reading but as the reading that costs less theologically while costing more philologically — a genuine and live alternative, short of probable.
