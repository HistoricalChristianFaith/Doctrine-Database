What was the character and origin of the Corinthian “baptism for the dead”?

Disputed reading · on Baptized for the Dead (1 Corinthians 15:29) · witnesses: Tertullian and Epiphanius of Salamis

Related readings: How has “baptized for the dead” (1 Corinthians 15:29) been read?

The passage

“Else what shall they do which are baptized for the dead, if the dead rise not at all? why are they then baptized for the dead?”

— 1 Corinthians 15:29 (KJV), source.1

The question

Interpreters who read the verse as describing a baptism received on behalf of the dead find no account in the letter of why the Corinthians practised it. What is disputed is whether such a rite took its meaning from Christian hope for the resurrection of the unbaptized dead or from the Greco-Roman care of the dead in Roman Corinth, and whether any rite of the kind lies behind the verse at all; the answer bears on how the proxy baptisms reported from the second to the fourth centuries relate to Paul's words. The wider question of how the phrase should be read is set out on the related page.

A rite of Christian hope for the resurrection of the unbaptized dead

In brief: those baptized for the dead were Christians anxious that the unbaptized dead would not rise, or would rise only to be condemned.

Held by: Tertullian (On the Resurrection of the Flesh 48, c. 200); the tradition Epiphanius heard in Asia and Galatia (Panarion 28.6, c. 375); Ambrosiaster (c. 380); Thomas Aquinas (Catholic, Commentary on 1 Corinthians, c. 1270), of a baptism for dead relatives.2

The writers who give the practice a motive give it one tied to the resurrection. Tertullian says its practitioners adopted it “with such a presumption as made them suppose that the vicarious baptism (in question) would be beneficial to the flesh of another in anticipation of the resurrection; for unless it were a bodily resurrection, there would be no pledge secured by this process of a corporeal baptism.”3 Epiphanius heard that when some of the Cerinthians “died too soon, without baptism, others would be baptized for them in their names, so that they would not be punished for rising unbaptized at the resurrection and become the subjects of the authority that made the world.”4 Ambrosiaster writes that some “were at that time being baptized for the dead because they were afraid that someone who was not baptized would either not rise at all or else rise merely in order to be condemned.”5 Aquinas describes people “baptized again for some dead relative, so that he too would be freed from sins after death,” and finds them to be commended “in the fact that they seemed to have faith in the resurrection.”6 On this reading the motive and the setting of the verse agree: Paul cites the practice in an argument for the resurrection because the practice itself looks to the dead rising.

No baptism for the dead lay behind the verse; the later proxy rites came from it

In brief: Paul's words do not describe a proxy rite, and the proxy baptisms reported in later centuries were drawn from readings of those words.

Held by: Bernard M. Foschini (Catholic Biblical Quarterly 1950–51; book 1951); Joel R. White (Journal of Biblical Literature, 1997).7

Both read the phrase so that it describes no baptism on behalf of the dead: Foschini by repunctuating the verse into taunting questions, White by reading “baptized on account of the dead” as baptized on account of the apostles (see the related page). Foschini concludes that “no such ‘Baptism for the dead’ existed.”8 White sets this beside the record of ancient funerary practice: “a great deal is known about Greco-Roman funerary practices, in Corinth and elsewhere, but there is no evidence anywhere in the ancient world for anything like baptism for the dead.” Of the customs designed to influence the fate of the dead, he writes, “none of them involved baptism; they differ, in other words, precisely at the crucial point.” The proxy baptisms of the fathers he places downstream of the letter: “Later references to baptisms for the dead in Marcionite or Gnostic circles are irrelevant; they are all influenced by our text.” He holds that a custom rooted in the colony's culture would have left more than one obscure verse, and asks how such a custom “could vanish, excepting one reference in Paul whose obscurity even DeMaris is forced to concede (p. 661), without the merest trace.”9 On this reading the origin of the proxy rite lies in the reception of 1 Corinthians, among the groups the fathers name, rather than in Corinth.

A Christian form of the Greco-Roman care of the dead

In brief: Corinthian Christians extended baptism to their dead to help the newly dead pass into the world of the dead, as their neighbours' rites did.

Held by: Richard E. DeMaris (Journal of Biblical Literature, 1995; The New Testament in its Ritual World, 2008).10

DeMaris begins from the verse's brevity, “There is, in fact, not much in the text to go on,” and turns to the archaeology of the Corinthia, which “may help explain why the Christians of early Roman Corinth extended the rite of baptism to their dead.” Greek and Roman societies cared for the dead “primarily because the living were thought to be obligated to help the deceased become integrated into the realm of the dead.” At Corinth, “the rise of the Palaimon cult at Isthmia and the orientation of Demeter devotion in the Roman period point unequivocally to a development transcending cult boundaries: the emergence during the middle of the first century CE of a religious outlook focused intensely on the dead and the world of the dead.” He specifies the rite: “it is likely that those who had themselves baptized were kin of the dead,” and baptism “was more likely undertaken for the recently deceased than for the long departed.” Read as a rite of passage, it would help the dead “pass through the transition.”11 He argues in 2008 that the rite falls “within the typical range of ritual variation.”12 On his reading the Corinthians saw no contradiction in practising the rite while doubting the resurrection, because “a ritual marking the transition from life to death says little if anything about what lies beyond death and nothing at all about bringing the dead back to life.” Paul, DeMaris holds, “saw the matter differently”: for him “a ritual concerning the dead necessarily involved the issue of resurrection,” while for the Corinthians the practice confirmed the movement of the deceased into the world of the dead and let the surviving community begin anew.13
