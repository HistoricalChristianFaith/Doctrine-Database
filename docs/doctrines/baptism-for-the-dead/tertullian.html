Tertullian — a reported rite, pressed for the resurrection of the body

Dates: c. 200 (On the Resurrection of the Flesh and Against Marcion both belong to his Carthaginian maturity); Tertullian c. 155–220 · Wikipedia
Summary: Tertullian is the earliest orthodox writer to engage 1 Corinthians 15:29, and he does with it exactly what the verse's whole later history would repeat: he acknowledges that a vicarious “baptism for the dead” was practised by some, uses it as proof that the resurrection is bodily, and — in his anti-Marcionite mode — keeps the practice at arm's length while re-reading the phrase to his own ends. He does not tell us whose rite it was, and in the two passages he handles it two different ways.

The resurrection is of the flesh (On the Resurrection of the Flesh 48)

Arguing that the body, not the soul only, will rise, Tertullian reaches for the practice as evidence: a baptism undertaken for the dead only makes sense if the dead body is what will be raised.

But inasmuch as “some are also baptized for the dead,” we will see whether there be a good reason for this. Now it is certain that they adopted this (practice) with such a presumption as made them suppose that the vicarious baptism (in question) would be beneficial to the flesh of another in anticipation of the resurrection; for unless it were a bodily resurrection, there would be no pledge secured by this process of a corporeal baptism. “Why are they then baptized for the dead,” he asks, unless the bodies rise again which are thus baptized?

— Tertullian, On the Resurrection of the Flesh 48.1 He takes the rite as a real one, grants that its practitioners meant it to profit “the flesh of another,” and turns that intention into an argument: whoever is baptized for the dead is by that very act confessing the resurrection of the body. He passes no verdict here on whether the practice is licit — only on what it presupposes.

“Never mind that practice, whatever it may have been” (Against Marcion 5.10)

Refuting Marcion, who denied the resurrection of the flesh, Tertullian handles the same verse more warily — because Marcion could exploit an obscure rite as easily as he could. So he brackets the practice and re-reads the phrase:

“What,” asks he, “shall they do who are baptized for the dead, if the dead rise not?” Now, never mind that practice, (whatever it may have been.) The Februarian lustrations will perhaps answer him (quite as well), by praying for the dead… that he might all the more firmly insist upon the resurrection of the body, in proportion as they who were vainly baptized for the dead resorted to the practice from their belief of such a resurrection. We have the apostle in another passage defining “but one baptism.” To be “baptized for the dead” therefore means, in fact, to be baptized for the body; for, as we have shown, it is the body which becomes dead. What, then, shall they do who are baptized for the body, if the body rises not again?

— Tertullian, Against Marcion 5.10.2 Here he does three things at once. He refuses to defend the rite (“never mind that practice”), leaning instead on Paul's own rule of “one baptism” (Ephesians 4:5) to bar any second, proxy washing. He offers a gloss that would echo for a millennium — “to be baptized for the dead… means, in fact, to be baptized for the body” — making “the dead” the mortal body of the baptizand rather than a deceased third party. And he still keeps the argumentative point of chapter 48: even those “vainly baptized for the dead” did it “from their belief of such a resurrection.”

Why Tertullian sets the pattern

Everything the later tradition will do with 1 Corinthians 15:29 is already latent in these two passages. The rite is reported but never owned; it is credited to unnamed others (“they,” not “we”); it is turned into a proof of the bodily resurrection; and the phrase is re-read so that “the dead” need not mean deceased persons at all. Chrysostom's creedal reading and Cosmas's “baptized for our dead bodies” are downstream of Tertullian's “baptized for the body”; the later refusal to grant a genuine vicarious rite (Calvin) sharpens his wariness into outright denial. What Tertullian does not do is name the practitioners — that the fathers who come after him will supply, pinning the rite on the Marcionites, Cerinthians, and Valentinians.
