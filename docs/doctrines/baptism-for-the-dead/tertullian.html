Tertullian — a reported rite, pressed for the resurrection of the body

Dates: c. 200 (On the Resurrection of the Flesh and Against Marcion both belong to his Carthaginian maturity); Tertullian c. 155–220 · Wikipedia
Summary: Tertullian discusses 1 Corinthians 15:29 in two works. He acknowledges that a vicarious “baptism for the dead” was practised by some, uses it as evidence that the resurrection is bodily, and — writing against Marcion — sets the practice aside and reads the phrase as “baptized for the body.” He does not tell us whose rite it was, and in the two passages he handles it two different ways.

A rite cited for the bodily resurrection (On the Resurrection of the Flesh 48)

Arguing that the body, not the soul only, will rise, Tertullian reaches for the practice as evidence: a baptism undertaken for the dead only makes sense if the dead body is what will be raised.

But inasmuch as “some are also baptized for the dead,” we will see whether there be a good reason for this. Now it is certain that they adopted this (practice) with such a presumption as made them suppose that the vicarious baptism (in question) would be beneficial to the flesh of another in anticipation of the resurrection; for unless it were a bodily resurrection, there would be no pledge secured by this process of a corporeal baptism. “Why are they then baptized for the dead,” he asks, unless the bodies rise again which are thus baptized?

— Tertullian, On the Resurrection of the Flesh 48.1 He takes the rite as a real one, grants that its practitioners meant it to profit “the flesh of another,” and turns that intention into an argument: whoever is baptized for the dead is by that very act confessing the resurrection of the body. He does not say here whether the practice is licit, only what it presupposes.

“Never mind that practice, whatever it may have been” (Against Marcion 5.10)

Refuting Marcion, who denied the resurrection of the flesh, Tertullian handles the same verse differently: he sets the practice aside and reads the phrase another way:

“What,” asks he, “shall they do who are baptized for the dead, if the dead rise not?” Now, never mind that practice, (whatever it may have been.) The Februarian lustrations will perhaps answer him (quite as well), by praying for the dead… that he might all the more firmly insist upon the resurrection of the body, in proportion as they who were vainly baptized for the dead resorted to the practice from their belief of such a resurrection. We have the apostle in another passage defining “but one baptism.” To be “baptized for the dead” therefore means, in fact, to be baptized for the body; for, as we have shown, it is the body which becomes dead. What, then, shall they do who are baptized for the body, if the body rises not again?

— Tertullian, Against Marcion 5.10.2 Here he does three things at once. He declines to defend the rite (“never mind that practice”), leaning instead on Paul's own rule of “one baptism” (Ephesians 4:5) to bar any second, proxy washing. He offers a gloss — “to be baptized for the dead… means, in fact, to be baptized for the body” — making “the dead” the mortal body of the baptizand rather than a deceased third party. And he still keeps the argumentative point of chapter 48: even those “vainly baptized for the dead” did it “from their belief of such a resurrection.”

The two passages compared

In both passages the rite is reported, not described as his own church's practice; it is credited to unnamed others (“they,” not “we”); and it is used as evidence for the bodily resurrection. In Against Marcion he adds a reading of the phrase in which “the dead” is the mortal body of the person baptized. He does not name the practitioners. Theodotus the Valentinian (c. 170), earlier on the timeline, had already read the verse of a proxy baptism; later writers report proxy baptisms among the Marcionites and Cerinthians, and Chrysostom, Theodoret and the later Greek commentators read “the dead” as the baptized person's own body (see the interpretation page).
