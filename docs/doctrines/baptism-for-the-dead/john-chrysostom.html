John Chrysostom — the Marcionite ruse and the creedal re-reading

Dates: c. 395 (the Homilies on 1 Corinthians, preached at Antioch before his elevation to Constantinople in 398); Chrysostom c. 347–407 · Wikipedia
Summary: Chrysostom gives the fullest and most memorable ancient account of an actual vicarious baptism — the Marcionite ritual of hiding a living man under the dead catechumen's bier to answer for him — and then, having ridiculed it, offers the reading that became standard in the Greek East: to be “baptized for the dead” is to be baptized in the confession of the resurrection, “the dead” being one's own mortal body.

What the Marcionites did

Chrysostom warns his hearers that the Marcionite heretics appeal to this verse, and describes the practice with open scorn:

viz., when any Catechumen departs among them, having concealed the living man under the couch of the dead, they approach the corpse and talk with him, and ask him if he wishes to receive baptism; then when he makes no answer, he that is concealed underneath saith in his stead that of course he should wish to be baptized; and so they baptize him instead of the departed, like men jesting upon the stage. So great power hath the devil over the souls of careless sinners. Then being called to account, they allege this expression, saying that even the Apostle hath said, “They who are baptized for the dead.”

— Chrysostom, Homilies on 1 Corinthians 40.1 He answers the Marcionite appeal on its own terms first: if Paul really meant a proxy baptism, then no one need ever go unbaptized (someone can always be baptized for him afterward), and Christ's warnings that a man must himself eat the flesh and be born of water (John 6:53; 3:5) would be idle — for “what hinders both Greeks and Jews thus to become believers, other men after their decease doing these things in their stead?”

What Paul meant: the confession at baptism

Having disposed of the heretics, Chrysostom — guarding the words from the uninitiated, “as through a veil” — unfolds his own reading. At baptism the candidate recites the creed:

this also we add at the end when we are about to baptize, bidding them say, “I believe in the resurrection of the dead,” and upon this faith we are baptized… This therefore Paul recalling to their minds said, “if there be no resurrection, why art thou then baptized for the dead?” i.e., the dead bodies. For in fact with a view to this art thou baptized, the resurrection of thy dead body, believing that it no longer remains dead.

— Chrysostom, Homilies on 1 Corinthians 40.2 On this reading “the dead” are not deceased third parties at all but the baptizand's own mortal body, and “baptized for the dead” means baptized in the hope of its resurrection — a hope the rite itself enacts, since “the being baptized and immersed and then emerging, is a symbol of the descent into Hades and return thence,” which is why Paul elsewhere calls baptism a burial (Romans 6:4). To deny the resurrection, then, is to make one's own baptismal confession an empty form.

His place in the tradition

Chrysostom's homily is the hinge of the Greek reception. His report of the Marcionite rite is the vivid companion to Epiphanius's Cerinthian one, and together they are the evidence modern scholars cite that literal proxy baptisms were really practised. His creedal re-reading, meanwhile, becomes the settled Eastern exegesis: Cosmas Indicopleustes repeats it, and Theophylact of Ohrid reproduces this very homily almost word for word seven centuries later. The pattern Tertullian began — report the rite, refuse to own it, re-read the phrase — reaches its classic form here.
