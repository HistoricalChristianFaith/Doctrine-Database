Epiphanius of Salamis — a proxy baptism reported among the Cerinthians

Dates: c. 375 (the Panarion, his catalogue of heresies, was written c. 375–377); Epiphanius c. 315–403 · Wikipedia
Summary: Epiphanius, bishop of Salamis on Cyprus and the great heresy-hunter of the fourth century, supplies the most circumstantial ancient report that a literal vicarious baptism was actually practised — a “tradition” he says he heard current in Asia and Galatia. He ties 1 Corinthians 15:29 to it, but then carefully offers the orthodox alternative reading beside it, so that his witness cuts both ways: it is the strongest patristic evidence that the rite existed, and a witness to the church's discomfort with reading Paul as endorsing it.

The context: refuting Cerinthus (Panarion 28)

Epiphanius is refuting the Cerinthians, who (he says) held that Christ “has suffered and been crucified but has not risen yet, but he will rise when the general resurrection of the dead comes.” Against them he marshals Paul's resurrection chapter. It is in this setting — the deniers of a present resurrection — that he introduces the report of a baptism performed for the dead:

For their school reached its height in this country, I mean Asia, and in Galatia as well. And in these countries I also heard of a tradition which said that when some of their people died too soon, without baptism, others would be baptized for them in their names, so that they would not be punished for rising unbaptized at the resurrection and become the subjects of the authority that made the world.

And the tradition I heard of says that this is why the same holy apostle said, ‘If the dead rise not at all, why are they baptized for them?’ But others explain the text satisfactorily by saying that, as long as they are catechumens, the dying are allowed baptism before they die because of this hope, showing that the person who has died will also rise, and therefore needs the forgiveness of his sins through baptism.

— Epiphanius, Panarion 28.6, trans. Frank Williams.1

Two readings, laid side by side

Notice what Epiphanius does. He first gives the “tradition” that the verse describes a genuine proxy baptism: the living stood in for the unbaptized dead, being baptized “in their names” so the deceased would not rise unbaptized and fall under the power of “the authority that made the world” — the demiurge of the Cerinthian and broader Gnostic myth. This is the plainest sense of Paul's words, and Epiphanius reports it as a live custom in the very regions where the sect flourished. But he does not leave it there. “Others,” he adds, “explain the text satisfactorily” another way: the “dead” are dying catechumens, granted baptism on their deathbeds “because of this hope” of the resurrection. That second reading — baptism for those about to join the dead, not baptism on behalf of the already dead — is the one the orthodox tradition (Chrysostom, Cosmas, Calvin) would prefer.

The value of his testimony

Epiphanius matters precisely because he does not tidy the evidence. He is a hostile witness to the heretics, yet he transmits their practice in concrete detail — the deceased baptized by proxy, in his name, to secure his standing at the resurrection — and he does so twice over, first as report and then as the occasion of Paul's remark. Modern readers who take 1 Corinthians 15:29 to describe a real vicarious baptism lean heavily on this passage (with Chrysostom's) as showing such rites were not merely imaginable but attested. At the same time, Epiphanius's care to append “but others explain the text satisfactorily” registers the church's reluctance to let the plain reading stand unqualified — the same reluctance already visible in Tertullian and hardened later into Calvin's flat denial.

Notes

  • Scope: Epiphanius reports the practice as something he “heard of” (a “tradition,” paradosis) among the Cerinthians of Asia and Galatia; he is not an eyewitness to the rite, and he sets a rival reading beside it. His testimony is strong evidence that such baptisms were done and remembered, weaker evidence for their exact form. See the summary timeline for the parallel report in John Chrysostom (the Marcionite version).
  • The demiurgic note — the unbaptized dead falling to “the authority that made the world” — is the Gnostic frame in which the rite made sense: baptism sealed the soul against the world-making powers, so a proxy baptism extended that seal to one who had died without it.
