Theodoret of Cyrus — baptism as burial with Christ

Dates: c. 435 (the Interpretation of the Fourteen Epistles of Paul — the earliest complete Greek commentary on the whole Pauline corpus — composed while he was bishop of Cyrrhus, c. 433–448); Theodoret c. 393–458/466 · Wikipedia
Summary: The leading Antiochene exegete of the generation after Chrysostom reads “baptized for the dead” of ordinary baptism — but gives it a turn of his own. Where Chrysostom grounded the phrase in the baptismal creed (“I believe in the resurrection of the dead”), Theodoret grounds it in the rite itself as a burial with Christ: one is baptized to be buried and to rise with the Master, so “the dead” is the mortal body, and if the body does not rise the whole act is pointless.

The comment on 15:29

In his verse-by-verse commentary on 1 Corinthians Theodoret glosses the clause tersely, reading it wholly of the believer’s own baptism:

ὁ βαπτιζόμενος, φησι, τῷ δεσπότῃ συνθάπτεται, ἵνα τοῦ θανάτου κοινωνήσας καὶ τῆς ἀναστάσεως γένηται κοινωνός· εἰ δὲ νεκρόν ἐστι τὸ σῶμα, καὶ οὐκ ἀνίσταται, τί δήποτε καὶ βαπτίζεται.

— that is: “The one being baptized, he says, is buried together with the Master, so that having shared in His death he may become a partaker also of the resurrection; but if the body is dead and does not rise, why on earth is he even baptized?”1 The engine is Romans 6:4–5: baptism is a being-buried-with-Christ in order to rise with Him. “For the dead” therefore concerns the resurrection of the dead body — deny that the body rises, and the buried-and-raised symbolism of the font is emptied of point.

A different turn from Chrysostom

Theodoret arrives at the same destination as his fellow-Antiochene — “the dead” is the baptizand’s own mortal body, not a deceased third party — but by a different road. Chrysostom anchors the phrase in the words spoken at the font, the candidate’s confession “I believe in the resurrection of the dead”; Theodoret anchors it in the enacted shape of the rite, the immersion as burial and the emergence as rising with Christ. Meyer, cataloguing the ancient readings, files Chrysostom, Pelagius, Oecumenius, Photius, and Theophylact together under the creedal-confession sense, and notes that Theodoret “gives it a different turn, but likewise imports a meaning, making the reference to be to the dead body.”2 Both readings belong to the non-vicarious family, and both share its characteristic strength and its characteristic cost: they spare Paul any appeal to a proxy rite, but they must bend “the dead” — dead persons everywhere else in the chapter — into the baptizand’s own not-yet-dead body.

His place in the tradition

Theodoret’s commentary is the earliest complete Greek exposition of the whole Pauline corpus, and on this verse he is the Antiochene link between Chrysostom (c. 395) and Cosmas Indicopleustes (c. 550), who reads the verse the same enacted-death-and-rising way. The non-proxy reading then holds the Greek field for a millennium, and Theophylact of Ohrid (c. 1075) closes it by reproducing Chrysostom’s homily almost word for word. Between them the ninth-century Photius of Constantinople (c. 875) presses the same non-proxy sense as a pointed reductio and reads Paul’s third-person “they” as rhetorical tact rather than distance from a rite. The whole non-proxy construal is weighed on its merits, Theodoret’s burial-with-Christ form included, under the non-vicarious readings.
