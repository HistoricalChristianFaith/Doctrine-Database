Gregory of Nazianzus addressing the holy dead

Dates: c. 379–381 (the relevant orations: Oration 18 on his father, c. 374; Oration 24 on Cyprian, c. 379; Oration 43 on Basil, c. 381); Gregory of Nazianzus c. 329–390 · Wikipedia
Summary: Several of Gregory's orations close by turning to address the departed in the second person and asking them to act for the living — to "gaze upon us from above," to stay an affliction by their prayers, to guide the flock, to shepherd the congregation. Two of these are funeral orations for intimates (his father, his friend Basil). The third, Oration 24, is addressed to the martyr Cyprian, whom Gregory did not know, on the saint's feast day, and asks for the Church's protection.

Oration 43: the closing address to Basil (c. 381)

Gregory's panegyric on Basil — delivered at Nazianzus around 381, probably on the anniversary of Basil's death (1 January 379) — says, before it turns to address him, that Basil is now

in heaven, where, if I mistake not, he is offering sacrifices for us, and praying for the people, for though he has left us, he [has] not entirely left us.

The oration then closes with an address to Basil:

Yet mayest thou gaze upon us from above, thou divine and sacred person; either stay by thy entreaties our thorn in the flesh, given to us by God for o[u]r discipline, or prevail upon us to bear it boldly, and guide all our life towards that which is most for our profit. And if we be translated, do thou receive us there also in thine own tabernacle.

The closing address contains concrete requests — stay the thorn in the flesh by thy entreaties, guide all our life, and receive us in thine own tabernacle — following the preceding section's statement that Basil is "praying for the people… offering sacrifices for us."1

Oration 18: the closing address to his father (c. 374)

The earliest of the three, the funeral oration on Gregory the Elder, says that the departed's intercession is now more powerful than his earthly ministry was —

his intercession is of more avail now than was his instruction in former days, since he is closer to God, now that he has shaken off his bodily fetters.

and closes by asking the dead father to act for the living:

Make known to us where thou art in glory, and the light which encircles thee, and receive into the same abode thy partner soon to follow thee, and the children whom thou hadst laid to rest before thee, and me also… and mayest thou guide and free from peril, as I earnestly entreat, the whole flock and all the clergy, whose father thou art said to be.

With "as I earnestly entreat," Gregory asks his dead father to guide and free from peril the whole flock. The setting is a son's funeral oration for his father, and §6 of the same oration states that "his intercession is of more avail now."2

Oration 24: the address to the martyr Cyprian (c. 379)

The panegyric on Cyprian was preached in Constantinople around early October 379. Cyprian is a martyr Gregory did not know (he opens by half-joking that he had nearly forgotten him), the occasion is the saint's feast, and the petition is for the Church's protection. The same oration reports the martyr's relics performing exorcisms and healings. In the closing address Gregory turns to Cyprian in the second person and prays the dead martyr to watch over and shepherd the living congregation — in the Greek, optatives of request (epopteuois… poimainois, ἤ sympoimainois): "supervising… and shepherding, or rather co-shepherding, the holy sheep of this congregation," and driving off the "predatory wolves" of heresy.3

A near-identical close ends Oration 21 on Athanasius (§37): Gregory asks the dead Athanasius to "cast upon us from above a propitious glance" and to "conduct this people" in its worship of the Trinity. Across 374–381 Gregory's addresses to the holy dead carry requests — receive me, guide the flock, stay the thorn, shepherd the congregation. Lefteratou notes the shared "co-shepherd my congregation" request in Oration 21 and Oration 24.4

Placement on the timeline

On the distinction set out under Is invoking a saint the same act as believing the saints intercede?, Gregory's closing addresses are in the second person and contain imperatives and optatives of request. His older contemporary Basil addresses the Forty Martyrs in the vocative and urges the living to bring petitions to them. Gregory's addresses occur in panegyrics and funeral orations; Orations 18 and 43 are spoken over his father and his friend, while Oration 24 is addressed to a martyr he did not know, on a feast, with a request for the Church. Together with Gregory of Nyssa's encomium on Theodore, these orations are discussed under the question Is the invocation of saints a fourth-century development or an older practice?

Related pages

  • The orations bear on two disputed readings of this doctrine: Is invoking a saint the same act as believing the saints intercede? and Is the invocation of saints a fourth-century development or an older practice?
  • The nearest entries on the timeline are Basil of Caesarea (the homily on the Forty Martyrs) and Gregory of Nyssa (the encomium on Theodore), Gregory's friend and the third of the Cappadocians.
  • Oration 24 blends into one figure the hagiographies of Cyprian of Carthage (martyred 258) and the legendary Cyprian "the magician" of Antioch; this bears on the identity of the saint addressed, not on the form of the address.
