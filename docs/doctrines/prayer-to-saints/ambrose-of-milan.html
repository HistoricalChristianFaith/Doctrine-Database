Ambrose of Milan on entreating the angels and martyrs

Dates: c. 377 (De viduis / Concerning Widows, written at Milan soon after De virginibus); Ambrose of Milan c. 339–397 · Wikipedia
Summary: Ambrose, bishop of Milan, is a Latin witness of the same decade as Basil’s homily on the Forty Martyrs and the Cappadocian encomia. Expounding the healing of Peter's mother-in-law to the widows of his church, he exhorts the faithful that "the angels must be entreated for us… the martyrs must be entreated," giving as grounds that the martyrs' patronage attaches to the relics the church possesses and that the sinner, too weak to pray for herself, should "make use of others to pray for her to the physician." The grammatical addressees of the passage are the living congregation.

The exhortation in De viduis

In his treatise Concerning Widows (c. 377), Ambrose holds up the Gospel scene in which Christ heals Simon Peter's mother-in-law — "they besought him for her" (Luke 4:38) — as a pattern for the widow who feels herself alone. The point he draws is that one need not approach God unaided: the apostles and martyrs are "near," and they pray for those who cultivate their friendship.

"She was taken," it is said, "with a great fever, and they besought him for her." You too have those near you to entreat for you. You have the Apostles near, you have the Martyrs near; if associated with the Martyrs in devotion, you draw near them also by works of mercy… Cherish, then, the nearness of Peter and the affinity of Andrew, that they may pray for you and your lusts give way… You, widows, have then assistance, if you choose such sons-in-law for yourselves, such patrons and friends for your posterity.

Having said that the soul "bound by great sin" is least able to pray for itself, Ambrose states his conclusion with the Latin gerundive obsecrandi sunt, "they must be entreated":

So Peter and Andrew prayed for the widow. Would that there were some one who could so quickly pray for us, or better still, they who prayed for the mother-in-law, Peter and Andrew his brother. Then they could pray for one related to them, now they are able to pray for us and for all. For you see that one bound by great sin is less fit to pray for herself, certainly less likely to obtain for herself. Let her then make use of others to pray for her to the physician. For the sick, unless the physician be called to them by the prayers of others, cannot pray for themselves… The angels must be entreated for us, who have been to us as guards; the martyrs must be entreated, whose patronage we seem to claim for ourselves by the pledge as it were of their bodily remains. They can entreat for our sins, who, if they had any sins, washed them in their own blood; for they are the martyrs of God, our leaders, the beholders of our life and of our actions. Let us not be ashamed to take them as intercessors for our weakness, for they themselves knew the weaknesses of the body, even when they overcame.

Three features of the passage figure in discussion of it. It is phrased as an obligation: the gerundive says the angels and martyrs are to be entreated. It ties the martyrs' "patronage" to their relics, "the pledge as it were of their bodily remains," the link between relic and patronage that Peter Brown's The Cult of the Saints (1981) studies in the fourth-century cult. And it sits within an account of mediated prayer: the sinner, too sick to reach the physician unaided, secures the prayers of others, among whom Ambrose names the angels and martyrs.1

Grammatical form of the passage

The page Is invoking a saint the same act as believing the saints intercede? sets out the distinction between believing that the saints intercede and addressing a saint in the second person. Ambrose's text does more than state that the saints pray for the living: it tells the faithful to entreat them, with reasons. Its grammatical addressees, however, are the living throughout ("you" the widow, "us" the church); the passage does not record Ambrose himself turning to address an apostle or martyr. In this it resembles Basil's homily, whose one imperative ("may your requests be with the martyrs") is likewise addressed to the congregation, while the encomium of Gregory of Nyssa on Theodore addresses the martyr directly.2

Placement on the timeline

The passage is a Latin text of c. 377, from the same decade as Basil's homily (c. 373) and the Gregories' encomia (c. 379–381). It is discussed under the question Is the invocation of saints a fourth-century development or an older practice?

Dating and authenticity

De viduis (Concerning Widows) is a genuine work of Ambrose, written at Milan shortly after his De virginibus of 377 and conventionally dated to the same year; its authenticity is not in dispute. The passage above is §§54–55 in the standard division (NPNF). Ambrose addresses the departed elsewhere as well — his funeral orations on the emperors Valentinian II and Theodosius (De obitu Valentiniani, De obitu Theodosii, both early 390s) turn to speak to the dead — but those are funeral panegyrics for named rulers, whereas De viduis speaks of the entreaty of the saints in general.3
