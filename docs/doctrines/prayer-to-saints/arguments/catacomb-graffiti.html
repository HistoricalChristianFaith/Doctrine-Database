Do the San Sebastiano graffiti to Peter and Paul attest third-century invocation of the saints?

Disputed reading · on Prayer to the Saints · witness: the graffiti of the Memoria Apostolorum on the Via Appia (c. 250–320)

Related readings: Is invoking a saint the same act as believing the saints intercede? · Is the invocation of saints a fourth-century development or an older practice? · Does the closing of the Council of Constantinople’s letter to Theodosius (381) attest invocation of the saints?

The passage

Paule et Petre petite pro Victore

Petre et Paule in mente habete nos

in mente habete … in orationibus vestris

Petre et Paule in mente abete Ursinum in refrigerium

— Graffiti on the plaster of the triclia at the Memoria Apostolorum ad Catacumbas (now under San Sebastiano), c. 250/260 – c. 320: “Paul and Peter, intercede for Victor”; “Peter and Paul, keep us in mind”; “keep [us] in mind … in your prayers”; “Peter and Paul, keep Ursinus in mind, in [the eternal] refreshment.” Antonio Ferrua’s edition (ICUR n.s. V, 1971) counted some 640 graffiti; Antonio Felle’s re-edition fixes the published total at 538.1

The question

The graffiti are dated by the archaeological sequence: the triclia, a porticoed court for funeral meals, was built over a filled-in cemetery around the middle of the third century and was covered when Constantine’s Basilica Apostolorum was raised over the site.2 Whether their addresses to Peter and Paul are prayers asking the apostles’ intercession, of the kind later called invocation of the saints, or the commemorative formulae of a funerary banquet continuous with the Roman cult of the dead, is disputed; the answer bears on how far back second-person petitions to the saints can be traced. No confessional Protestant or Orthodox reading of the graffiti themselves was located.

Prayers asking the apostles’ intercession

In brief: the visitors addressed Peter and Paul as heavenly intercessors and asked their prayers for the living and the dead.

Held by: Carlo Carletti (epigraphist, “Rozzi ‘viatores’ per Pietro e Paolo,” L’Osservatore Romano, 2010);3 the Oxford Cult of Saints in Late Antiquity project (record E05087).4

Carletti sorts the formulae by what they ask: “remember” (in mente habete), “protect”, “pray for” (petite, orate, rogate pro), “help” (subvenite, adiutate), on behalf both of the living and of dead relatives, and renders the commonest verb as “intercede”: “Pietro e Paolo intercedete (petite) per Vittore”; “Paolo e Pietro intercedete (petite) e pregate (rogate) per Eros.” The apostles are “directly called upon by name” more than two hundred times, and behind the stereotyped formulae he finds “la spontanea e quasi confidenziale consuetudine di un rapporto interpersonale con i due apostoli” (“the spontaneous and almost confidential habit of an interpersonal relationship with the two apostles”).3 The formula “in orationibus vestris” asks in so many words to be kept in the apostles’ prayers. Where the graffiti name the refrigerium, Carletti reads “Ursinum in refrigerium” as the eternal refreshment of the hereafter (“quello eterno dell’aldilà”), with the apostles asked to lead the dead into it.3 He describes the whole as a devotion of public reach arising “from below,” officially recognized and monumentalized only some fifty years later, under Constantine and then Damasus.3 The Depositio Martyrum independently calendars a 29 June cult of Peter in catacumbas, fixing organised veneration at the site.5 The Oxford project catalogues the site as one of funerary feasts “at which the apostolic martyrs were evidently invoked.”4 On this reading the wall preserves second-person petitions for the apostles’ prayers, written by ordinary Roman Christians from the third century onward.

Formulae of the funerary banquet and the cult of the dead

In brief: the graffiti belong to the refrigerium, the Roman meal for the dead, which Christians kept and extended to the apostles; they show a popular Christianity continuous with ancestral rites.

Held by: Graydon F. Snyder (Ante Pacem, rev. ed. 2003), ⚠ unverified on these graffiti;6 Ramsay MacMullen (The Second Church: Popular Christianity A.D. 200–400, 2009; “Christian Ancestor Worship in Rome,” 2010), ⚠ unverified on these graffiti.7

The triclia was built for the funeral meal, and its walls record that meal: “Tomius Coelius refrigerium fecit” (“Tomius Coelius held a banquet”), “Dalmatius / votum eis … promisit / refrigerium,” and the dated “XIIII kal(endas) apriles refrigeravi Parthenius.”8 Snyder and MacMullen are cited for reading the whole complex as a popular Christianity continuous with the Greco-Roman cult of the dead, the apostles drawn into an inherited rite (⚠ unverified: their treatments of the graffiti were not opened here).6 MacMullen’s survey of Rome describes the wider material record of that rite, as summarized by his reviewer in BMCR: Christian epitaphs opening with the pagan D(is) M(anibus), stone coffins at Isola Sacra with holes for libations, Constantine’s cemetery basilicas filled with burials and used chiefly for refrigeria (with cooking vessels found in and around them), and a mensa over Paul’s sarcophagus equipped with a libation hole.7 MacMullen’s 2010 article is titled “Christian Ancestor Worship in Rome.” On this reading the address to Peter and Paul is the voice of the banquet at their memorial, the honoured dead remembered and asked to remember, in the popular Christianity of third-century Rome.
