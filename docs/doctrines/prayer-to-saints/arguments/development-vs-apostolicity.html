Is the invocation of saints a fourth-century development or an older practice?

Disputed reading · on Prayer to the Saints · witnesses: Hippolytus of Rome · Origen · Gregory of Nyssa · Augustine

Related readings: Is invoking a saint the same act as believing the saints intercede? · Do the San Sebastiano graffiti to Peter and Paul attest third-century invocation of the saints? · Does the closing of the Council of Constantinople’s letter to Theodosius (381) attest invocation of the saints?

The passage

Tell me, you three boys, remember me, I entreat you, that I also may obtain the same lot of martyrdom with you

— Hippolytus of Rome, Commentary on Daniel 2.30 (c. 204), addressing the three youths of the fiery furnace, source.1

leave what you are doing up there for a little while … Intercede on behalf of your homeland with our common King

— Gregory of Nyssa, Encomium on the Great Martyr Theodore (c. 380s), addressing the martyr, source.2

The question

Second-person petitions to the saints survive in a homiletic address of c. 204, in Roman graffiti of c. 250–320, and in homilies of the 370s and 380s, after which they are found in Greek and Latin writers alike; no earlier address to a departed saint is extant. Whether that record shows a practice received from the earliest church, a post-apostolic addition, or a legitimate development of earlier teaching is disputed. The question concerns the address to the saints, not the belief that they pray for the living (on that distinction see the related reading).

A post-apostolic addition, absent from the earliest writers

In brief: the earliest Christian writers do not invoke the saints; the practice forms gradually and becomes general in the fourth century.

Held by: Philip Melanchthon, Apology of the Augsburg Confession art. XXI (Lutheran, 1531);3 Peter Brown (historian, The Cult of the Saints, 1981), on the rise of the cult of saints as a whole;4 Gavin Ortlund (Reformed Baptist historical theologian, 2021);5 and James Clark (Anglican, 2024), with E. H. Browne as he quotes him.6

Melanchthon reads the patristic record as silent before Gregory the Great: “Neither have the rest of the ancient writers before Gregory made mention of invocation,” and “although the old prayers make mention of the saints, yet they do not invoke the saints.”3 The Anglican exposition Clark follows dates the change earlier: “nothing of the sort was permitted or endured in the first four centuries.”6 Brown writes that “Christianity … was born without such a cult; it took rise and that rise needs chronicling,” and places the rise of shrines, patronage and praesentia in the fourth century, as a movement that engaged Ambrose, Augustine and the Gregories.4 Ortlund reads the patristic sources as a practice in formation: “the ambivalence/unfamiliarity of Origen, and the strictures/warnings of later figures like Augustine on certain forms of the practice, and the generally mild nature of a lot of earliest examples of the practice, fits with my proposal of development. It genuinely looks like its forming, coming to be, in the mid to late patristic era.”5 Origen reserves prayer “in its full meaning” for “the God and Father of All,” routing every petition “through the High Priest”;7 Augustine writes that the martyrs “are not invoked by the sacrificing priest” and that the dead do not by nature know earthly affairs (“if so great Patriarchs were ignorant what was doing towards the People …”).8 Homiletic addresses to named martyrs in public worship come from Basil, Gregory of Nazianzus and Gregory of Nyssa in the 370s and 380s.2 The Sub Tuum Praesidium’s papyrus was dated by its editor C. H. Roberts to the later fourth century and by Hans Förster to the sixth or seventh, and the invocations once credited to Methodius and Ephrem are assigned by their editors to later hands.9 On this reading the fourth-century invocations mark the arrival of a new form of devotion that grew out of the martyr-cult.

A practice received from the primitive church

In brief: the church has called on the saints since it first had martyrs; the early writers’ silence is non-treatment of an existing practice, and third-century evidence attests it.

Held by: the Council of Trent (Catholic, Session 25, 1563);10 J. P. Kirsch (Catholic historian, The Doctrine of the Communion of Saints in the Ancient Church, Eng. tr. 1910), for the third century;11 and Lawrence Farley (Orthodox archpriest), in the Orthodox Church in America’s Essential Orthodox Christian Beliefs (2023).12

Trent instructs bishops to teach “the intercession and invocation of saints” “agreeably to the usage of the Catholic and Apostolic Church, received from the primitive times of the Christian religion, and agreeably to the consent of the holy Fathers.”10 Farley dates it to the age of the martyrs: “The intercession of the saints has been sought and invoked in the Church from almost the very beginning of its history—i.e., from the time that the Church began to produce martyrs, which happened very early indeed,” and “the Church has always invoked the prayers of its martyrs.”12 Kirsch finds the practice of “asking the dead to intercede before God” in “clear evidence … in the third century in Africa and Rome,” and holds that the third-century inscriptions “prove beyond doubt that the faithful besought the souls of the just for their intercessions.” He reads the writers’ silence as one of subject: “In their writings on prayer, the teachers of the Church in the third century do not specially deal with these invocations,” and “it is easy to understand that no special mention was made of these invocations when the writers were dealing with prayer in the strict sense.” For the earliest Christian ages he finds “no evidence for the direct invocation of the martyrs in the literature,” yet holds that “the earliest form of invocation, which regards the apostles and martyrs as the protectors and patrons of the faithful with Christ the Lord and Judge, has its roots in ideas prevalent in the earliest Christian times.”11 Further evidence adduced for this reading includes13 Hippolytus’s address to the three youths c. 204;1 the graffiti at the apostles’ memorial on the Appian Way, c. 250–320, “Paule et Petre petite pro nobis omnibus” (“Paul and Peter, intercede for us all”);14 the Sub Tuum Praesidium, “Beneath thy compassion we take refuge … do not despise our petitions,” on Edgar Lobel’s dating of its hand “not later than the third century”;9 and the synodal letter of the Council of Constantinople (381), which asks that God act “by the prayers of the Saints.”15 On this reading the fourth-century homilies give public voice to a usage the church had kept since the martyrs.

A legitimate development of the doctrine of mediation

In brief: invocation is a development, but one that unfolds what the faith held from the start about Christ, his mediation and the glory of his saints.

Held by: John Henry Newman (Catholic, An Essay on the Development of Christian Doctrine, 1845; rev. 1878).16

Newman lists invocation among “the existing developments of doctrine” and derives it from the doctrine of the Incarnation: “From the doctrine of Mediation follow the Atonement, the Mass, the merits of Martyrs and Saints, their invocation and cultus.” The developments hang together: “the veneration of Saints and their relics are parts of one; their intercessory power and the Purgatorial State, and again the Mass and that State are correlative.” His principle is that “Christianity being from heaven, all that is necessarily involved in it, and is evolved from it, is from heaven.”16 He finds the ground of the practice in Athanasius’s teaching that, through union with Christ’s body, “we too have become God’s temple,” which he calls “a doctrine which both interprets and accounts for the invocation of Saints, the cultus of relics, and the religious veneration in which even the living have sometimes been held.”17 Newman treats the public honours given to saints after the conversion of the Empire as part of “the development of the Catholic ritual,” and meets the charge of paganism with the steadiness of doctrine through the same period: “If what is called in reproach ‘Saint-worship’ resembled the polytheism which it supplanted, or was a corruption, how did Dogmatism survive?”18 He notes the counter-reading of the patristic texts—“when Roman Catholics urge St. Gregory’s invocations, they are told that these are but rhetorical”—and sets the texts within that larger pattern of development.19 On this reading the lateness of the explicit practice and its legitimacy belong together: invocation is the gradual unfolding of what the doctrines of the Incarnation and mediation already contained.
