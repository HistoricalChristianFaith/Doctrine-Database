Is invoking a saint the same act as believing the saints intercede?

Disputed reading · on Prayer to the Saints · witnesses: Origen · Augustine

Related readings: Is the invocation of saints a fourth-century development or an older practice? · Do the San Sebastiano graffiti to Peter and Paul attest third-century invocation of the saints? · Does the closing of the Council of Constantinople’s letter to Theodosius (381) attest invocation of the saints?

The passage

Now request and intercession and thanksgiving, it is not out of place to offer even to men—the two latter, intercession and thanksgiving, not only to saintly men but also to others. But request to saints alone, should some Paul or Peter appear, to benefit us by making us worthy to obtain the authority which has been given to them to forgive sins

But if we accept prayer in its full meaning, we may not ever pray to any begotten being, not even to Christ himself, but only to the God and Father of All

— Origen, On Prayer XIV–XV (c. 233), trans. William A. Curtis, source.1

they are not invoked by the sacrificing priest. For it is to God, not to them, he sacrifices, though he sacrifices at their monument

— Augustine, City of God XXII.10 (c. 426), on the martyrs, source.2

The question

Writers on every side of the debate distinguish the belief that the saints in heaven pray for the living from the act of addressing a saint and asking for those prayers. Whether asking a saint to pray is the same kind of request Christians make of one another, or a further act that needs a warrant of its own, is disputed; the answer governs how each early witness on the timeline is sorted, and both sides read passages such as Origen’s and Augustine’s above as drawing their own line. On Origen’s clause, J. P. Kirsch (Catholic historian, Eng. tr. 1910) holds that by “saints” Origen “seems clearly to mean only the saints in the primitive Christian sense of the word,” that is, “living believers, for he goes on to say, one is only to address prayers for assistance to a saint, if one comes across a Paul or a Peter”; Gavin Ortlund (2021) cites “the ambivalence/unfamiliarity of Origen” among the evidence for his account of the practice as developing.3

One communion of prayer: asking the saints is request, not worship

In brief: asking a saint to pray belongs with asking a fellow Christian to pray; the line that matters runs between the worship owed to God alone and the request made of members of Christ’s body.

Held by: Thomas Aquinas (Catholic, Summa Theologiae II-II q.83 a.4, c. 1272);4 the Council of Trent (Catholic, Session 25, 1563);5 Georges Florovsky (Orthodox, “On the Veneration of the Saints,” Collected Works III, 1976);6 and the Catechism of the Catholic Church §§2679, 2683 (Catholic, 1992), with further holders in the note.7

Aquinas sets out the distinction this reading works with: prayer is offered to a person “first, as to be fulfilled by him, secondly, as to be obtained through him.” Prayer of the first kind goes to God alone; “in the second way we pray to the saints, whether angels or men, not that God may through them know our petitions, but that our prayers may be effective through their prayers and merits.”4 Trent teaches that the saints “offer up their own prayers to God for men,” and that it is good “to have recourse to their prayers, aid, (and) help for obtaining benefits from God, through His Son, Jesus Christ our Lord, who is our alone Redeemer and Saviour.”5 The Catechism draws the two acts together: the saints’ intercession “is their most exalted service to God’s plan. We can and should ask them to intercede for us and for the whole world”; of Mary, “We can pray with and to her.”7 For Florovsky the address to the saints is the communion of the Church made audible: “it is not only to get help and intercession that the Holy Spirit teaches every believer to pray to the glorified saints but also because this calling on them, through communion in prayer, deepens the consciousness of the catholic unity of the Church.”6 Bulgakov describes the moment of canonization as the change from praying for a person to addressing him, “asking his intercession for us before God by his prayers,” since “the relations of love with the saints already glorified by God are not interrupted by death.”8 On this reading the invocation of a saint is the intercession-belief spoken in the second person, as one asks a friend on earth, and it stays on the side of request: the line between legitimate and illegitimate prayer is the line between honour and the worship reserved to God.

A distinct act that needs a warrant of its own

In brief: granting that the saints pray for the church, addressing them is a further act, and these writers find it neither commanded nor exemplified in Scripture.

Held by: the Apology of the Augsburg Confession art. XXI (Lutheran, 1531);9 John Calvin (Reformed, Institutes III.20, 1559);10 the Thirty-Nine Articles, art. XXII (Anglican, 1563);11 and Gavin Ortlund (Reformed Baptist historical theologian, 2021), with further holders in the note.12

The Apology grants the intercession-belief: “concerning the saints we concede that, just as, when alive, they pray for the Church universal in general, so in heaven they pray for the Church in general.” It then separates the further act: “even supposing that the saints pray for the Church ever so much, yet it does not follow that they are to be invoked.” Prayer “ought to be made from faith,” and faith rests on a word of God; for invocation “neither a command, nor a promise, nor an example can be produced from the Scriptures,” and “Whence do we know without the testimony of Scripture that the saints perceive the prayers of each one?”9 Calvin grounds the same separation in Christ’s office as sole intercessor: “since the Scripture calls us away from all others to Christ alone … it were the extreme of stupidity, not to say madness, to attempt to obtain access by means of others.”10 Ortlund grants that the saints in heaven may be aware of us and pray for us, and trains his case on the act of directly addressing them.12 Within the Anglican tradition James Clark (2024) states the positive form of the reading: Christians “can rightfully avail ourselves of their prayers, not by asking the saints directly to pray for us, but by praying to God that He would bless us through their intercessions,” the practice E. J. Bicknell called “comprecation,” “asking God Himself for a share in the prayers of the saints.”13 On this reading the communion of saints is honoured by prayer addressed to God that names the saints’ intercession, and the second-person address to a saint is a separate act whose warrant must be shown on its own terms.
