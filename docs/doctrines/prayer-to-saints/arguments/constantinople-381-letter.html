Does the closing of the Council of Constantinople’s letter to Theodosius (381) attest invocation of the saints?

Disputed reading · on Prayer to the Saints · witness: the First Council of Constantinople (381), letter to the Emperor Theodosius

Related readings: Is invoking a saint the same act as believing the saints intercede? · Is the invocation of saints a fourth-century development or an older practice? · Do the San Sebastiano graffiti to Peter and Paul attest third-century invocation of the saints?

The passage

May the Lord establish your empire in peace and righteousness, and prolong it from generation to generation; and may he add unto your earthly power the fruition of the heavenly kingdom also. May God by the prayers (εὐχαῖς τῶν ἁγίων) of the Saints, show favour to the world, that you may be strong and eminent in all good things as an Emperor most truly pious and beloved of God.

— Letter of the Holy Synod to the Most Pious Emperor Theodosius the Great (First Council of Constantinople, 381), closing benediction, trans. H. R. Percival, source.1

The question

The letter by which the council of 381 forwarded its decrees to Theodosius for ratification closes by asking that God show favour “by the prayers of the Saints”; the council met May–July 381, and Theodosius confirmed its acts by edict on 30 July 381.1 Whether the line is of a piece with the invocation of the saints practised today, or a prayer addressed to God that names the saints’ intercession without addressing them, is disputed; it bears on whether an ecumenical council witnesses to invocation by 381. The first reading is held here by popular writers and its academic literature was not located; no proponent of the second reading was located who discusses this letter.

The bishops’ appeal to the saints’ prayers, of a piece with invocation

In brief: the assembled bishops called on the saints’ prayers in an official document, in a refrain like the ones churches use when they invoke the saints today.

Held by: Christopher Orr (Orthodox writer, “On the Invocation of the Saints,” 2010).2

Orr places the letter in a florilegium of patristic witnesses to the invocation of the saints, between second-person addresses to the martyrs and Cyril of Jerusalem’s liturgy.2 The letter is also offered as a conciliar witness, from a council rather than a private devotion.3 The benediction is spoken in the name of the whole synod.1 On this reading the 150 bishops, writing to the emperor, called on the saints’ prayers as the churches still do, and the council stands among the witnesses to that practice.

A prayer to God that names the saints’ intercession

In brief: the sentence asks God to act, naming the saints’ prayers as the means; it witnesses to belief in their intercession, in the form of prayer addressed to God.

Held by: ⚠ unverified — no named proponent located yet.

The petition is “May God … show favour,” with the saints’ prayers named as the means: “by the prayers of the Saints,” Greek εὐχαῖς τῶν ἁγίων, an instrumental dative. Read so, the line expresses the belief that the saints pray for the world and that God acts through those prayers, the belief the related reading distinguishes from the address “O Saint N., pray for us.” Writers who make that distinction describe the older liturgical prayers in the same terms: Melanchthon in 1531 held that “although the old prayers make mention of the saints, yet they do not invoke the saints,”4 and the Anglican E. J. Bicknell called the asking of God “for a share in the prayers of the saints” comprecation, “a truly primitive and Catholic practice, found in the ancient Liturgies.”5 Neither writes of this letter. On this reading the letter shows that belief in the saints’ intercession could stand, in 381, in a synodal letter to the emperor, voiced as prayer to God.
