Vigilantius of Calagurris on the cult of the saints

Dates: c. 406 (the lost tract attacking relics, vigils, and prayer to the dead, answered by Jerome the same year); Vigilantius b. c. 370 at Calagurris in Aquitaine, a presbyter in Gaul · Wikipedia
Summary: Vigilantius, a Gallic presbyter, is the earliest extant named objector to the practice on this timeline. Around 406 he wrote against the reverence paid to relics, the night-vigils at the martyrs' shrines, the lighting of tapers, and the belief that the dead saints can hear or help the living, asking whether "the souls of the martyrs" "hover round" their tombs to hear petitioners. His tract is lost; his words survive only as quoted by his opponent, Jerome, in Contra Vigilantium.

What Vigilantius argued, as Jerome quotes him

Vigilantius had stayed with Jerome at Bethlehem in the 390s before returning to Gaul, where (with the support of his bishop, Exsuperius of Toulouse) his views spread. The reverend presbyters Riparius and Desiderius sent his tract to Jerome, who quotes lines from it in order to answer them. On relics and the honour paid to the martyrs' remains:

What need is there for you not only to pay such honour, not to say adoration, to the thing, whatever it may be, which you carry about in a little vessel and worship? … Why do you kiss and adore a bit of powder wrapped up in a cloth?

Vigilantius also denied that the saints can be present to hear those who pray to them, and denied post-mortem intercession outright:

Is it the case that the souls of the martyrs love their ashes, and hover round them, and are always present, lest haply if any one come to pray and they were absent, they could not hear?

… so long as we are alive we can pray for one another; but once we die, the prayer of no person for another can be heard…

In these quotations Vigilantius rejects the cult of relics, the belief that the saints in glory pray for the living, and the belief that they perceive and hear those who address them. Jerome's reply runs to a full treatise.1

The source: Jerome's Contra Vigilantium

No work of Vigilantius survives. Jerome had met him and mentions him earlier in his correspondence (Letter 61, a rebuke); his theology is recoverable only from Jerome's treatise. The NPNF editors judge that "this treatise has less of reason and more of abuse than any other which Jerome wrote": Jerome puns the man's name into Dormitantius ("Sleepy"), calls him a drunken tavern-keeper "animated by an unclean spirit," and assigns him to the heresy of "Eunomius" and the long-dead "heresy of Cain." W. S. Gilly's Vigilantius and His Times (1844) reconstructs his life and views from Jerome's report. The quotations Jerome gives in his opponent's voice are the only direct record of Vigilantius's position.2

Placement on the timeline

Vigilantius writes in the same decades as the Cappadocians (Basil, the two Gregories), Ambrose, Chrysostom, and Jerome, whose writings promote the cult of the saints, and his objection is the one recorded dissent among them on this timeline. Jerome's near-contemporary Augustine honours the martyrs and affirms their intercession while barring sacrifice to them and holding that for the departed "by their proper nature" to be interested in the affairs of the living is "impossible".3

Reception and debate

  • Vigilantius's protest figures in Is the invocation of saints a fourth-century development or an older practice? It records an objection to the practice in 406; Jerome, for his part, sets against him "the bishops of the whole world."
  • On the distinction between addressing a saint and believing that the saints intercede, see Is invoking a saint the same act as believing the saints intercede? Vigilantius's question whether the martyrs can "hear" bears on the first; his statement that after death "the prayer of no person for another can be heard" bears on the second.
  • A parallel figure a half-century earlier. Aerius of Sebaste (c. 355) denied prayer and offering for the dead and was answered by Epiphanius, as Vigilantius, denying the hearing of prayer by the dead and the veneration of relics, was answered by Jerome. Each was a provincial presbyter (Aerius in Pontus, Vigilantius in Gaul), each survives only in the quotation of the churchman who answered him, and each has been claimed as a "proto-Protestant" forerunner (⚠ unverified: not yet sourced).
