John Calvin (and the Reformation confessions) on the invocation of the saints

Dates: c. 1559 — the argument against invocation in the Institutes of the Christian Religion III.20.20–27 (final Latin edition, 1559; the Reformed critique had stood in substance from the 1536 first edition); John Calvin 1509–1564, Geneva. With the Lutheran corollary already in the Augsburg Confession, art. 21 (1530) · Wikipedia
Summary: Calvin rejects the invocation of the saints, on the ground that Scripture directs prayer to God through the one Mediator Christ, and that the office of intercession ascribed to the departed saints has, in his words, "not a word" of support in Scripture. His argument stands in the final edition of the Institutes (1559); the Lutheran Augsburg Confession (1530) had already stated that "the Scripture teaches not the invocation of saints." The earlier objector on this timeline is Vigilantius (c. 406), answered by Jerome; the Council of Trent issued its decree on the invocation of saints in 1563.

Claims & evidence

Calvin: Scripture calls the faithful to Christ alone

Wherefore, since the Scripture calls us away from all others to Christ alone, since our heavenly Father is pleased to gather together all things in him, it were the extreme of stupidity, not to say madness, to attempt to obtain access by means of others, so as to be drawn away from him without whom access cannot be obtained.

— Institutes of the Christian Religion III.20.21, trans. Henry Beveridge, source. Calvin's doctrine of prayer (III.20) is that the faithful approach God through the one Mediator, Christ, "in whose name" alone prayer is heard. His objection in this section is to directing petition to the saints to gain access by their means, which he holds displaces the sole access won by Christ.1

Calvin: no scriptural warrant for invoking the saints as intercessors

Then what angel or devil ever announced one syllable to any human being concerning that fancied intercession of theirs? There is not a word on the subject in Scripture.

— Institutes III.20.21, source. Calvin's objection is that no command to invoke the saints, and no promise that they will hear, is given in Scripture, so that in his view the practice rests on human invention, not divine warrant. In the same section he presses that to make the saints intercessors-to-be-invoked is "to transfer to them that office of sole intercession which we have above claimed for Christ." His concluding section (III.20.27) gathers the grounds: the practice "robs God of his glory," "destroys the intercession of Christ," "is repugnant to the word of God," is "without approved example," and "springs from distrust."2

The Augsburg Confession (1530): honour the saints; Scripture "teaches not the invocation of saints"

Of the Worship of Saints they teach that the memory of saints may be set before us, that we may follow their faith and good works, according to our calling … But the Scripture teaches not the invocation of saints or to ask help of saints, since it sets before us the one Christ as the Mediator, Propitiation, High Priest, and Intercessor. He is to be prayed to, and has promised that He will hear our prayer.

— The Augsburg Confession (1530), article 21, "Of the Worship of the Saints," trans. Triglot Concordia, source. Melanchthon's article, presented before Charles V at the Diet of Augsburg on 25 June 1530, three decades before Calvin's final edition, states from the Lutheran side that the saints are to be honoured and imitated, but not invoked, because Scripture presents Christ as the one to be prayed to. Both the Lutheran and the Reformed texts deny invocation while affirming that the saints may be honoured.3

Placement on the timeline

Calvin is the second objector to invocation on this timeline, eleven centuries after Vigilantius (c. 406), whose protest survives only in Jerome's quotation. Calvin's position was set out in the Institutes of the Reformed churches, and the Augsburg Confession states the Lutheran position. The preceding entries on the timeline are Nicaea II (787) and Aquinas (c. 1272); the following one is the Council of Trent (1563). Calvin's argument is directed at invocation, the second-person address he holds to be without scriptural warrant; it bears on Is invoking a saint the same act as believing the saints intercede? and Is the invocation of saints a fourth-century development or an older practice?

Reception and debate

  • Calvin also appears as a witness on the infant-baptism timeline (Institutes IV.16) and on the perpetual virginity timeline.
  • The texts quoted here object to the living addressing the saints, on the ground that Scripture does not authorise it. The Council of Trent, Session 25 (1563), defined that to invoke the saints "is good and useful" and condemned as impious those who "assert … that the invocation of them … is idolatry."
