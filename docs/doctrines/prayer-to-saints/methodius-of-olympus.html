Methodius of Olympus on prayer to the saints

Dates: Methodius of Olympus c. 250–311 (his undisputed works c. 290) · Wikipedia
Summary: Methodius is listed — for instance by a commenter styled "a Protestant inquiring into the Ancient Church," in the online exchange around Gavin Ortlund's critique of praying to the saints — among the fathers in whom the invocation of the saints appears. The text in question is the Oration on Simeon and Anna, whose closing section addresses the Virgin in the second person ("Hail to thee for ever, thou virgin mother of God …") and asks both Mary and Simeon to remember and advocate for the living. The oration's authorship is questioned: the editors of the Ante-Nicene Fathers note that its Theotokos-language is "hardly appropriate to the period preceding Cyril of Alexandria," and the Clavis Patrum Graecorum lists it among the spurious works (CPG 1827), with R. Caro (1972) dating it to the fifth or sixth century, as Roger Pearse reports. Methodius's one complete Greek work, the Symposium, contains no second-person address to a saint.

The passage that names him: the invocation in the Oration on Simeon and Anna

The Oration on Simeon and Anna — a homily on the Presentation (Luke 2) transmitted under Methodius's name — closes, in its fourteenth section, with a direct address to the Virgin and then to Simeon, in the second person:

Hail to thee for ever, thou virgin mother of God, our unceasing joy, for unto thee do I again return. … Wherefore, we pray thee, the most excellent among women, who boastest in the confidence of thy maternal honours, that thou wouldest unceasingly keep us in remembrance. O holy mother of God, remember us, I say, who make our boast in thee …

The same breath turns to Simeon and asks his patronage:

And do thou also, O honoured and venerable Simeon, thou earliest host of our holy religion, and teacher of the resurrection of the faithful, be our patron and advocate with that Saviour God, whom thou wast deemed worthy to receive into thine arms.

If dated to Methodius's lifetime (he died c. 311), the passage would be a pre-Nicene second-person petition to a named saint and to the Mother of God, decades before the Cappadocian homilies; its date therefore depends on the question of authorship.1

The authorship of the oration

Several works travel under Methodius's name, among them the seventh-century Apocalypse of Pseudo-Methodius. Three points are raised against this homily's authenticity in the sources cited here:

  • The Marian title. The address turns on calling Mary Theotokos ("God-bearer") as a settled, celebrated title; the title was at the centre of the Nestorian controversy and the Council of Ephesus (431). The editors of the Ante-Nicene Fathers note at the words "thou virgin mother of God": "[He again apostrophizes the Blessed Theotocos, but in language hardly appropriate to the period preceding Cyril of Alexandria.]"
  • Signs of working-over. At "thou art its middle and end" the same editors add: "[Not so, for he ends with a noble strain of worship to the Son of God. This expression suggests interpolation.]"
  • Catalogue entry. Roger Pearse (2011, 2023) reports that "[a]ccording to the Clavis Patrum Graecorum, the 'Sermo de Simeone et Anna' (CPG 1827) is not genuine," and that R. Caro, La homiletica Mariana griega en el siglo v (1972), "gave a date of 5-6th century"; Pearse adds, "Subsequent writers, I find, seem to repeat his conclusion." Wikipedia's article on Methodius likewise calls the work "not genuine."

If the oration is of the fifth or sixth century, the homily is evidence for invocation of Mary and the saints in the fifth or sixth century, not c. 290.2

Methodius's undisputed works

His one work surviving complete in Greek is the Symposium (the Banquet of the Ten Virgins), a Platonic dialogue in which ten virgins in the garden of Arete each praise chastity, closing with Thekla's hymn to Christ the Bridegroom of the Church. Its concern is virginity and the soul's restoration; it contains no second-person petition to a saint or to Mary. The surviving fragments and the other works attributed to him without dispute (e.g. On the Resurrection, On Free Will) are likewise silent on invocation; they do not show what Methodius held about the cult of the saints.3

Bearing on the dating question

The oration's date bears on Is the invocation of saints a fourth-century development or an older practice? The commenter in the exchange around Gavin Ortlund's video opens a list of witnesses with "asking the saints for their intercession is in Methodius …". If the oration is dated to the fifth or sixth century, as the sources cited above date it, it stands beside the post-Ephesus Marian material rather than beside the pre-Nicene items discussed on this timeline (Hippolytus's address to the three youths, c. 204; the San Sebastiano graffiti, from c. 250; the Sub Tuum Praesidium, whose papyrus is variously dated).4

Reception and debate

  • The claim that Methodius invokes the saints rests on the Oration on Simeon and Anna, whose address is quoted above; the authorship of that work is discussed in the section above.
  • Other pre-Nicene material on the timeline: Hippolytus, the catacomb graffiti, the Sub Tuum Praesidium, and Origen's reservation of prayer in the full sense to God; the readings of this material are set out under Is the invocation of saints a fourth-century development or an older practice?
