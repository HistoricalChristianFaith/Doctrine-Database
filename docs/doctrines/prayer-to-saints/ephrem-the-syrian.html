Ephrem the Syrian and the Marian hymns

Dates: c. 363–373 (the Syriac madrāšē — teaching-hymns — of his last decade at Nisibis and Edessa); Ephrem c. 306–373 · Wikipedia
Summary: Ephrem is a fourth-century Syriac witness, writing in a tradition distinct from the Greek and Latin texts elsewhere on this timeline. He is often named among early witnesses to the invocation of the saints, above all for Marian prayer, and the texts in question fall into two groups. His Syriac hymns praise Mary as "the pure Mary," without "blemish," the new Eve whose honour reverses Eve's reproach; that praise is sung about Mary, with refrains addressed to Christ, and the hymns quoted here do not petition her. The second-person prayers to Mary transmitted under his name — "O Virgin Lady, Mother of God… intercede for me… be my advocate at the hour of judgment" — belong to the Greek corpus known as Ephraem Graecus, which the syri.ac guide calls "sporadically authentic"; Pearse, following Manousakas, dates one of them (the Threnos) c. 1400. Compare the oration once credited to Methodius.

The Syriac hymns: praise of Mary, prayer to Christ

Ephrem's Syriac works are verse — the madrāšē sung by the choirs of Nisibis and Edessa — and Mary is a frequent subject of them. In the Hymns on the Nativity he calls her, again and again, "the pure Virgin" and "the pure Mary," and frames her as the new Eve whose glory undoes the first Eve's shame:

Let women praise Her, the pure Mary,—that as in Eve their mother,—great was their reproach,—lo! in Mary their sister,—greatly magnified was their honour. R., Blessed is He Who sprang from women!

Elsewhere Ephrem speaks of Mary's sinlessness, addressing Christ: "You alone and your Mother are more beautiful than any others, for there is no blemish in you nor any stains upon your Mother" (Nisibene Hymns 27). The praise in the Nativity hymn is in the third person, "let women praise Her," and the hymn's recurring response — "Blessed is He Who…" — is doxology directed to Christ. Where Ephrem gives Mary a voice, it is Mary who addresses her Son:

Again, I am Thy Mother because of Thy Conception, and Thy Bride am I because of Thy sanctification, Thy handmaid and Thy daughter, from the Blood and Water wherewith Thou hast purchased me and baptised me.

On the distinction set out under Is invoking a saint the same act as believing the saints intercede?, these Syriac hymns express honour of Mary as praise and typology; they contain no second-person request such as "O Mary, pray for me."1

The Greek "prayers to Mary": pseudo-Ephrem

The sustained second-person prayers begging Mary's intercession belong to Ephraem Graecus ("Ephrem the Greek"), the large body of Greek material transmitted under his name. The syri.ac guide calls the corpus "sporadically authentic": some pieces are Greek renderings of Syriac works of Ephrem, while others are later adaptations or independent compositions in his manner, each to be compared against the critical Syriac edition (Beck's, in the Corpus Scriptorum Christianorum Orientalium).2

Two of the most-quoted "Ephrem" Marian prayers belong to this Greek corpus:

  • The Threnos or "Lamentation of the most holy Mother of God," read on Good Friday (CPG 4085), long printed as Ephrem's, is identified by Roger Pearse, following M. I. Manousakas, as a translation of an anonymous Greek lament on the crucifixion that — resting on fourteenth-century Good-Friday troparia and the later recension of the Acts of Pilate — was composed around the year 1400. The same Greek pseudo-Ephrem material, Pearse reports, supplies the widely circulated "Prayer of St. Ephrem" that invokes Mary directly — "Holy and immaculate Virgin… be my intercessor and advocate at the hour of my death and judgment."
  • The lines often quoted in later devotion — "Queen of all after the Trinity, Consoler after the Paraclete, Mediatrix of the whole world after the Mediator" — come from an Oratio ad Dei Matrem ("Prayer to the Mother of God"), part of the same Greek corpus transmitted under Ephrem's name rather than the Syriac corpus edited by Beck.

Pearse discusses the use of these prayers, on the strength of Ephrem's association with the time of Nicaea, to date developed Marian invocation (and Marian sinlessness) to c. 325.3

Placement on the timeline

Ephrem's Syriac hymns bear on the question Is invoking a saint the same act as believing the saints intercede?: they praise Mary's purity and dignity, and address their petitions to God; second-person petitions to Mary appear in the Greek corpus under his name.

They are also discussed under the question Is the invocation of saints a fourth-century development or an older practice?, as evidence of Marian devotion by c. 370 in a Syriac tradition distinct from the Greek and Latin churches. The prayers to Mary that would bear on the dating of invocation belong to the Greek corpus discussed above, as does the oration credited to Methodius on the Greek side.

Attribution and dating

The c. 363–373 window is for the Syriac hymns of Ephrem's last decade (Nisibis until 363, then Edessa to his death in 373). Which "Ephrem" a given Greek piece represents is settled item by item. The benchmark is Beck's critical Syriac edition and S. P. Brock's guide to the corpus; the Greek pseudo-Ephrem material is catalogued separately (Phrantzolas's seven-volume Greek edition; the Clavis Patrum Graecorum numbers).4
