Gregory the Dialogist on seeking the martyrs and praying to St. Peter

Dates: c. 604 — the Dialogues (593–594), the Forty Gospel Homilies (590–592), and the Register of letters (590–604); Pope Gregory I c. 540–604 · Wikipedia
Summary: Gregory the Great — "the Dialogist" — bishop of Rome 590–604, writes of the saints' patronage in his Dialogues, homilies and letters. In the Dialogues he states that the martyrs "be there present to hear our prayers" of those who "seek them with a pure mind," even where their bodies do not lie, and he recounts, approvingly, a paralytic maid who "prayed to St. Peter" for healing and was answered by the apostle in a vision, calling Peter "our pastor and patron." As pope he closes an official letter to a queen with a benediction that God prosper her "through the intercession of the blessed Peter, Prince of the apostles, to whom you commend them." His texts include both a narrated prayer addressed to a saint and a prayer to God made through a saint's intercession.

The martyrs "be there present to hear our prayers" — Dialogues II

In the second book of the Dialogues — the Life of St. Benedict — Gregory's interlocutor Peter raises the puzzle that martyrs seem to work greater miracles at shrines that hold only their relics than where their bodies actually rest. Gregory answers that the saints hear the prayers directed to them wherever those prayers are made:

Where the holy martyrs lie in their bodies, there is no doubt, Peter, but that they are able to work many miracles, yea and also do work infinite, to such as seek them with a pure mind. But for as much as simple people might have some doubt whether they be present, and do in those places hear their prayers where their bodies be not, necessary it is that they should in those places shew greater miracles, where weak souls may most doubt of their presence. But he whose mind is fixed in God, hath so much the greater merit of his faith in that he both knoweth that they rest not there in body, and yet be there present to hear our prayers.

Gregory says that the faithful seek the martyrs and direct prayers to them, and that the martyrs — though absent in body — "be there present to hear our prayers." He presents knowing this as the greater merit of the one "whose mind is fixed in God," and the greater miracles at relic-shrines as a help to "weak souls."1

A paralytic "prayed to St. Peter" and was answered — Dialogues III

The third book narrates such a prayer. Telling of Abundius, a keeper of the church of St. Peter, Gregory recounts a young woman crippled by palsy who had long prayed to the apostle himself for healing, and whom St. Peter answered by appearing to her in the night:

For a certain young maid, that frequented his church, was so pitifully sick of the palsy, that she crept upon her hands, and, for very weakness, drew her body upon the ground. Long time had she prayed to St. Peter for help of this her infirmity: who upon a night in a vision, stood by her and spake thus: "Go unto Abundius, and desire his help, and he shall restore thee to thine health." … Then quoth she: "Our pastor and patron, blessed St. Peter the Apostle, hath sent me, that you should help me of this my disease."

In the story the maid prays to St. Peter directly, over a long time, for her own healing, and calls him "our pastor and patron." Gregory relates it as evidence that "the blessed Apostle St. Peter did by miracle declare" his favour toward the keeper Abundius. As with Chrysostom's emperor "begging the saints to be his advocates," the petition is described in a story rather than spoken by Gregory in the first person; his statement in Book II, that the martyrs hear the prayers of those who seek them, concerns the same kind of prayer.2

"Through the intercession of the blessed Peter" — the papal Register

Closing a letter to Brunichild, Queen of the Franks, Gregory asks God to fulfil the queen's desires through the apostle's intercession:

May Almighty God ever keep your Excellency in His fear, and so fulfil your desires and those of our sons the most excellent kings your grandsons, through the intercession of the blessed Peter, Prince of the apostles, to whom you commend them, as to grant you to have stable joy in their continual welfare, as you desire.

Here the petition is addressed to God, "through the intercession of the blessed Peter," with no saint spoken to in the second person; compare the closing of the Council of Constantinople's 381 letter. It is written by the bishop of Rome in a diplomatic letter to a reigning queen, and Gregory adds that the queen "commends" her family to Peter. Gregory also sent correspondents "keys of the blessed Peter" containing filings from the apostle's chains, as a blessing.3

Placement on the timeline

Gregory dies in 604, nearly two centuries after Augustine (d. 430) and the fourth-century texts of the Cappadocians, Ambrose, and Chrysostom. Augustine had honoured the martyrs while barring sacrifice to them and holding that the dead do not by nature perceive the living; Gregory writes that the martyrs "be there present to hear our prayers." His texts bear on the question Is invoking a saint the same act as believing the saints intercede?, since the Dialogues describe the saints being sought and heard and the papal letter asks God to act through a saint's intercession, and they are discussed under the question Is the invocation of saints a fourth-century development or an older practice?4

Related pages

  • Gregory also appears on two neighbouring timelines in the same family (the living and the dead): the intermediate state (Abraham's bosom as the "secret rest of the fathers"; the soul's survival; immediate particular judgment) and purgatory (the cleansing fire before judgment for lighter faults).
  • The saint addressed in the maid's prayer and named in the papal benediction is St. Peter, who is also addressed in the third-century San Sebastiano graffiti ("Paule et Petre petite pro nobis") and by Chrysostom's emperor. In Gregory's texts the cult of Peter appears both in popular devotion (the paralytic at his church) and in papal correspondence (the chain-relic "keys," the letters commending petitioners to him).
