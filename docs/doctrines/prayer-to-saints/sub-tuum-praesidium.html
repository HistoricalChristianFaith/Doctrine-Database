The Sub Tuum Praesidium (Papyrus Rylands Greek 470)

Dates: the papyrus c. 250 – c. 700 (dated variously; see the dating debate below) · Wikipedia
Summary: An anonymous Greek prayer on an Egyptian papyrus fragment in the John Rylands Library, Manchester, described in the library's record as the earliest Greek copy of the Sub tuum praesidium and the earliest surviving evidence of Christians addressing Mary as Theotokos ("God-bearer"). It is a petition addressed to Mary in the second person ("Beneath thy compassion we take refuge, O Theotokos … rescue us"). Its date is disputed: palaeographers have placed the hand from the third century (Lobel) to the sixth–seventh century (Förster), and the Trismegistos database records c. AD 700–900.

The prayer

The fragment preserves, in a fragmentary state with letters supplied by its editor where the papyrus is damaged, the prayer known from its later Latin incipit as the Sub Tuum Praesidium. The reconstructed Greek reads:

Ὑπὸ τὴν σὴν εὐσπλαγχνίαν
καταφεύγομεν, Θεοτόκε·
τὰς ἡμῶν ἱκεσίας μὴ παρίδῃς ἐν περιστάσει,
ἀλλ᾽ ἐκ κινδύνων ῥῦσαι ἡμᾶς,
μόνη ἁγνή, μόνη εὐλογημένη.

In English:

Beneath thy compassion we take refuge, O Theotokos [God-bearer]: do not despise our petitions in time of trouble, but rescue us from dangers, thou alone pure, thou alone blessed.

— text and translation as given for Papyrus Rylands Greek 470; the later Byzantine liturgical recension expands and smooths the wording (e.g. λύτρωσαι for the papyrus's ῥῦσαι, and a closing "ever-virgin"), but the core petition is already here.1 The grammatical form figures in the debate: Θεοτόκε and μόνη ἁγνή are vocatives, and ῥῦσαι ἡμᾶς ("rescue us") is a direct imperative. The prayer addresses Mary and asks her to act, rather than stating that she intercedes.

The manuscript

The fragment is Papyrus Rylands Greek 470 (P.Ryl. III 470), a small leaf of papyrus from Egypt, purchased by the John Rylands Library in Manchester in 1917 and first published by the papyrologist C. H. Roberts in 1938 in the third volume of the library's Catalogue of the Greek and Latin Papyri. Roberts identified the text as an early copy of the Sub Tuum Praesidium and translated the surviving lines. Arne Effenberger judges the physical object an amulet — a prayer carried for personal protection — rather than a leaf from a liturgical book, which bears on both its function and its script.2

The dating debate

The papyrus carries no date; its dating rests on palaeography — the reading of the handwriting against dated comparanda — and the specialists' dates span nearly five centuries. An early date would make the fragment both the earliest extant Marian petition and the earliest extant use of Theotokos.

  • Edgar Lobel (early): "not later than the third century." Roberts's colleague Lobel, the Oxyrhynchus papyrologist, judged on the hand alone that the fragment could hardly be later than the third century — most probably written c. 250–280.
  • C. H. Roberts (the editor): the later fourth century. Roberts noted Lobel's palaeographical judgement but, on theological grounds, called it "almost incredible that a prayer addressed so directly to the Virgin in these terms could be written in the third century," and so proposed "not before the second half of the 4th century."
  • Otto Stegmüller (1952): the script cannot decide. Stegmüller noted that the formal "chancery" hand stayed essentially the same for centuries, so palaeography could fix only the earliest possible point (the beginning of the fourth century) and "is unable to decide whether the script was written in the 4th, 5th or 6th century."
  • Hans Förster (1995, 2005): the sixth–seventh century, perhaps later. Förster reopened the question with a detailed analysis of the letter-forms (the narrow tall letters, the alpha with a broken crossbar, the distinctive mu, the dot-like thickening at stroke-ends) and the brown ink, comparing them to securely-dated later papyri and to Coptic scribal habits of the eighth–ninth century. He concluded that a third- or fourth-century date is "very improbable" and placed the fragment between the sixth and seventh centuries. Theodore de Bruyn and Arne Effenberger endorsed the reassessment; the Trismegistos database now records the fragment as c. AD 700–900.3

G. Giamberardini defends a third-century date on the ground that nothing literary or theological forbids it, and C. Römer judges Förster's redating unconvincing. Roger Pearse's survey of the scholarship (2023) concludes that "Lobel's brief paleographical remarks … cannot remain the principal basis for assigning the papyrus an early date."4

The date of the papyrus and the date of the prayer are separate questions: a late copy can carry an older composition, and an early copy would not by itself fix the later liturgical wording. Independent of the papyrus, Piotr Towarek (2021) places the prayer in liturgical use by the fifth century through the Georgian Iadgari, the chant-book of the Jerusalem liturgy, while dating its earliest secure textual witness to the sixth–seventh century.5

The title Theotokos

The fragment also carries the word Θεοτόκε / Θεοτόκος ("God-bearer," "Mother of God"). That title was at the centre of the Nestorian controversy and was affirmed at the Council of Ephesus (431). On a third-century date the papyrus would be the earliest known use of the term, before the conciliar debate; on a sixth-century or later date it would follow Ephesus. The same palaeographical question thus bears both on the earliest extant Marian petition and on the earliest extant use of the title.6

Reception and debate

  • This witness figures in two of the questions in this doctrine's Disputed readings section: Is invoking a saint the same act as believing the saints intercede? (the prayer is a second-person petition, not a statement that the saints pray for us) and Is the invocation of saints a fourth-century development or an older practice?, where its date is discussed.
  • Because the fragment is anonymous and undated, it is not slotted on the timeline at a fixed year; this page records the full range of proposed dates.
