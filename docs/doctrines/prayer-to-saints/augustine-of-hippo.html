Augustine of Hippo — the martyrs honoured, latria reserved to God

Dates: c. 426 (the witnesses span Reply to Faustus c. 400, Confessions c. 397–401, On Care to Be Had for the Dead c. 421–424, and The City of God c. 413–426); Augustine 354–430 · Wikipedia
Summary: Augustine, bishop of Hippo, writes about the cult of the martyrs across a quarter-century of works. He affirms that the martyrs in glory intercede for the living ("the martyrs do these wonders, or rather God does them while they pray and assist") and that the dead are aided by the prayers and the Eucharistic offering of the church. He also sets limits on the cult: "we do not build temples, and ordain priests, rites, and sacrifices for these same martyrs; for they are not our gods, but their God is our God." Sacrifice and the worship the Greeks call latria belong to God alone; at the altar over a martyr's tomb the offering is made "to the God of martyrs," and the martyr "is named in their own place and rank … but [is] not invoked by the sacrificing priest." In On Care to Be Had for the Dead he writes that for the departed "by their proper nature" to be interested in the affairs of the living is "impossible": the martyrs aid the living by a divine power, and exactly how, he says, surpasses the strength of his understanding.

"They are not our gods, but their God is our God" — The City of God VIII.27

Closing his long argument against the Platonists' veneration of demons and deified men, Augustine turns to the obvious objection — do not Christians do the same with their martyrs? — and answers by sharply distinguishing honour from worship:

But, nevertheless, we do not build temples, and ordain priests, rites, and sacrifices for these same martyrs; for they are not our gods, but their God is our God. Certainly we honor their reliquaries, as the memorials of holy men of God who strove for the truth even to the death of their bodies… But who ever heard a priest of the faithful, standing at an altar built for the honor and worship of God over the holy body of some martyr, say in the prayers, I offer to thee a sacrifice, O Peter, or O Paul, or O Cyprian? for it is to God that sacrifices are offered at their tombs… Therefore, whatever honors the religions may pay in the places of the martyrs, they are but honors rendered to their memory, not sacred rites or sacrifices offered to dead men as to gods.

Augustine grants the martyrs honour — their relics, their tombs, their memory, altars raised over their bodies — and he expects that honour to "stir ourselves up to imitate them … calling to our help that same God on whom they called." What he denies is that any of this is worship: no temples, no priesthood, no sacrifice directed to the saint. His liturgical test is that no celebrant ever says "I offer to thee a sacrifice, O Peter."1

"They pray and assist" — yet "not invoked by the sacrificing priest" (City of God XXII.10; Reply to Faustus XX.21)

Defending the martyrs' miracles against those claimed for the pagan gods, Augustine affirms that the martyrs intercede, and in the same passage says that they are not invoked in the sacrifice:

…but the martyrs do these wonders, or rather God does them while they pray and assist… to our martyrs we build, not temples as if they were gods, but monuments as to dead men whose spirits live with God. Neither do we erect altars at these monuments that we may sacrifice to the martyrs, but to the one God of the martyrs and of ourselves; and in this sacrifice they are named in their own place and rank as men of God who conquered the world by confessing Him, but they are not invoked by the sacrificing priest. For it is to God, not to them, he sacrifices, though he sacrifices at their monument.

Augustine had drawn the same line a quarter-century earlier in Reply to Faustus the Manichaean, answering the Manichee charge that Christians had merely swapped idols for martyrs. There he names latria, the worship he reserves to God alone:

It is true that Christians pay religious honor to the memory of the martyrs, both to excite us to imitate them and to obtain a share in their merits, and the assistance of their prayers. But we build altars not to any martyr, but to the God of martyrs… What is properly divine worship, which the Greeks call latria, and for which there is no word in Latin, both in doctrine and in practice, we give only to God. To this worship belongs the offering of sacrifices… Accordingly we never offer, or require any one to offer, sacrifice to a martyr, or to a holy soul, or to any angel.

In the same passage Augustine names "the assistance of their prayers" among the purposes of honouring the martyrs. His distinction concerns the kind of honour: sacrificial worship (latria) is given only to God, while the martyrs receive religious honour paid to their memory. Later Western theology names the honour owed to the saints dulia; the passage here uses only the term latria.2

"Impossible … by their proper nature" — the caution of On Care to Be Had for the Dead

Asked by Paulinus of Nola whether burial near a saint's tomb benefits the dead, Augustine wrote De cura pro mortuis gerenda (c. 421–424) and there took up whether the dead know and attend to the affairs of the living. Citing Isaiah's "Abraham hath not known us, and Israel is not cognizant of us," he reasons that even the patriarchs were "ignorant what was doing towards the People" descended from them — so "how are the dead mixed up with affairs and doings of the living, either for cognizance or help?" His answer about the martyrs:

We are not to think then, that to be interested in the affairs of the living is in the power of any departed who please, only because to some men's healing or help the Martyrs be present: but rather we are to understand that it must needs be by a Divine power that the Martyrs are interested in affairs of the living, from the very fact that for the departed to be by their proper nature interested in affairs of the living is impossible.

And as to how the martyrs help those who pray at their shrines, he confesses the limit of his knowledge — weighing whether the martyrs are themselves present, or whether God hears their prayers and acts through angels (just "like as we pray for the dead, to whom however we are not present, nor know where they be"):

Howbeit it is a question which surpasses the strength of my understanding, after what manner the Martyrs aid them who by them, it is certain, are helped… A matter is this, too high that I should have power to attain unto it, too abstruse that I should be able to search it out.

Augustine holds that the martyrs aid the living — "it is certain" they do — and that the dead do not, of their own nature, perceive the living; whatever knowledge or help they extend is, on his account, God's gift, and its workings hidden.3

A neighbouring practice: prayer for the dead (Confessions IX)

Prayer for the dead is a neighbouring practice to prayer to the holy dead, and Augustine is an early witness to it. In Confessions IX his mother Monica, dying at Ostia c. 387, makes her one request:

Lay this body anywhere, let not the care for it trouble you at all. This only I ask, that you will remember me at the Lord's altar, wherever you be.

Augustine then offers such a prayer for her soul, asking God to forgive her sins — the Eucharistic intercession for the dead that he elsewhere defends as profiting the departed:

Forgive her, O Lord, forgive her, I beseech You; enter not into judgment with her. Let Your mercy be exalted above Your justice.

The passage records the living interceding for the dead at the altar; the cult of the martyrs, which Augustine describes elsewhere with the qualifications above, has the holy dead interceding for the living.4

Placement on the timeline

Augustine follows Jerome and the Vigilantius controversy (c. 406); Gregory the Great (c. 604) is the next entry. Jerome closes his eulogy of Paula by addressing her directly — "aid with your prayers the old age of your votary." In the works surveyed here Augustine affirms the martyrs' intercession and prayer for the dead, reserves sacrificial worship to God, says the saints are "not invoked by the sacrificing priest," and holds that the dead do not by nature perceive the living. His texts are discussed under the question Is the invocation of saints a fourth-century development or an older practice?, together with the relic-cult, altars over tombs, miracles at shrines and the martyrs' assisting prayers that he describes.5
