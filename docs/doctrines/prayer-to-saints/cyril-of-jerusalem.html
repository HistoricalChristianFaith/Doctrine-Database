Cyril of Jerusalem on the saints in the liturgy

Dates: c. 350 (the Mystagogical Catecheses, traditionally placed near Cyril's accession; see the authorship note below on a possible date c. 390); Cyril of Jerusalem c. 313–386 · Wikipedia
Summary: Describing the Eucharistic prayer step by step for the newly baptized, the Mystagogical Catecheses report that the church commemorates the patriarchs, prophets, apostles and martyrs "that at their prayers and intercessions God would receive our petition." The passage places the saints' intercession within the Eucharistic liturgy of fourth-century Jerusalem. The prayer is addressed to God, and the saints' intercessions are named as the ground on which God is asked to act.

The commemoration of the saints in the Eucharistic prayer

The last of Cyril's catechetical lectures — the fifth of the Mystagogical Catecheses, delivered to the newly baptized in the week after Easter — walks through the liturgy of the faithful in order: the washing of hands, the kiss of peace, the Sursum corda ("Lift up your hearts"), the Sanctus, the invocation of the Spirit upon the gifts, and then the great intercessions. Having described the church praying "for the common peace of the Churches, for the welfare of the world … for all who stand in need of succour," Cyril turns to the commemoration of the holy dead:

Then we commemorate also those who have fallen asleep before us, first Patriarchs, Prophets, Apostles, Martyrs, that at their prayers and intercessions God would receive our petition. Then on behalf also of the Holy Fathers and Bishops who have fallen asleep before us, and in a word of all who in past years have fallen asleep among us, believing that it will be a very great benefit to the souls, for whom the supplication is put up, while that holy and most awful sacrifice is set forth.

Two practices sit side by side here. The first is the commemoration of the glorified saints — patriarchs, prophets, apostles, martyrs — so that "at their prayers and intercessions God would receive our petition." The second is the church's prayer for the other departed (bishops, fathers, all the dead), the practice treated under prayer for the dead. This page records the first, which Cyril describes as a fixed part of the Eucharistic prayer.1

Grammatical form of the commemoration

The page Is invoking a saint the same act as believing the saints intercede? sets out the distinction between believing that the saints intercede and addressing a petition to a saint in the second person. The commemoration in Cyril is spoken aloud at the altar during the Eucharistic prayer. Grammatically it is directed to God: God is asked to "receive our petition," and the saints' "prayers and intercessions" are the ground on which that request is made. The saints are spoken of in the third person; the congregation does not, in this text, address them. Compare Origen, who affirms that the saints pray with the living, and the Sub Tuum Praesidium, which addresses Mary in the second person.2

The passage is discussed under the question Is the invocation of saints a fourth-century development or an older practice?, as a mid-fourth-century liturgical text naming the saints' intercession, from the same generation as the Cappadocian homilies that address the saints directly.

Dating and authorship

The eighteen Catechetical Lectures proper are ascribed to Cyril and usually dated to around 350. The five Mystagogical Catecheses (Lectures 19–23), from which this passage comes, are ascribed to Cyril in some manuscripts and to his successor John of Jerusalem (bishop 387–417) in others, and a redaction of the 380s or 390s, possibly John's, is discussed in the literature cited in note 3. If the text is Cyril's, it dates from the mid-century; if it is John's, from the last decades of the century. On either ascription it describes the Jerusalem liturgy of the second half of the fourth century. The page dates it c. 350, following the traditional ascription to Cyril, and notes the later date.3
