Irenaeus of Lyons on prayer to the saints

Dates: c. 202 (Against Heresies) — Irenaeus of Lyons c. 130–202 · Wikipedia
Summary: Irenaeus is the earliest figure on this timeline. In the passages surveyed here he does not address a departed saint, and he gives no explicit statement of the belief that the saints intercede. His one account of how the church prays describes her as "directing her prayers to the Lord, who made all things … and calling upon the name of our Lord Jesus Christ," working her miracles "in His name" and not "by means of angelic invocations." Whether that last clause bears on the invocation question was disputed by his editors: Grabe read it as excluding any invocation of angels in the primitive church; Massuet restricted it to the conjuring of evil spirits.

Claims & evidence

The church prays to the Lord and works miracles in Christ's name — not "by means of angelic invocations"

In Against Heresies II.32 Irenaeus is answering the Gnostics' claim to work wonders like Christ's. His reply distinguishes the true church's miracles — exorcisms, healings, even raisings — from the magicians' phantasms by their source and their manner. The church's power is exercised freely, for benefit not deception, and above all it is invoked in one Name only:

Wherefore, also, those who are in truth His disciples, receiving grace from Him, do in His name perform [miracles], so as to promote the welfare of other men, according to the gift which each one has received from Him. For some do certainly and truly drive out devils, so that those who have thus been cleansed from evil spirits frequently both believe [in Christ], and join themselves to the Church. … Others still, heal the sick by laying their hands upon them, and they are made whole. Yea, moreover, as I have said, the dead even have been raised up, and remained among us for many years. …

Nor does she perform anything by means of angelic invocations, or by incantations, or by any other wicked curious art; but, directing her prayers to the Lord, who made all things, in a pure, sincere, and straightforward spirit, and calling upon the name of our Lord Jesus Christ, she has been accustomed to work miracles for the advantage of mankind, and not to lead them into error.

— Against Heresies II.32.4–5, trans. Ante-Nicene Fathers vol. 1, newadvent.org; also historicalchristian.faith. In this passage the church's prayer is "directed to the Lord" and offered by "calling upon the name of our Lord Jesus Christ"; no other addressee is named. The scope of the negative clause, "nor … by means of angelic invocations," is discussed in the next section.1

What "angelic invocations" means here: Grabe and Massuet

The clause "nor does she perform anything by means of angelic invocations" stands in a passage about magic: Irenaeus is contrasting the church with Simon Magus, Menander, and Carpocrates, whose "curious arts," "incantations," and conjured phantasms he has just described. The two early-modern editors of the Latin text differed on how far it reaches, and the Ante-Nicene Fathers preserves the disagreement in a note:

Grabe contends that these words imply that no invocations of angels, good or bad, were practised in the primitive Church. Massuet, on the other hand, maintains that the words of Irenaeus are plainly to be restricted to evil spirits, and have no bearing on the general question of angelic invocation.

— editorial note to Against Heresies II.32.5, Ante-Nicene Fathers vol. 1, newadvent.org. Grabe's edition appeared at Oxford in 1702, Massuet's at Paris in 1710.2

The intermediate state: the souls of the disciples await the resurrection

Against those who taught that the soul flies "above the heavens" at death, he insists the souls of the disciples "go away into the invisible place allotted to them by God, and there remain until the resurrection, awaiting that event" — following the Master, who did not ascend at death but "tarried" three days among the dead:

the souls of His disciples also, upon whose account the Lord underwent these things, shall go away into the invisible place allotted to them by God, and there remain until the resurrection, awaiting that event; then receiving their bodies, and rising in their entirety, that is bodily, just as the Lord arose, they shall come thus into the presence of God.

— Against Heresies V.31.2, historicalchristian.faith (fuller treatment on his intermediate-state page). In this account the righteous wait in an appointed interim place until the bodily resurrection. Irenaeus does not, in the passages surveyed here, say whether the waiting souls pray for the church. Cyprian, half a century later, holds both that the dead are in paradise and that they go on praying for the living.3

Reception and debate

  • The material here is of two kinds: an account of the church's prayer as directed to the Lord in Christ's name (II.32.5), and an eschatology in which the righteous await the resurrection (V.31). The passages surveyed contain neither an address to a saint nor a statement that the saints intercede.
  • The "angelic invocations" of II.32.5 refers to angels, not to departed human saints; its scope is the point on which Grabe and Massuet differed (above).
  • In the Latin of Against Heresies V.19.1 Mary becomes the advocata of the virgin Eve, and J. Armitage Robinson's translation of the parallel in the Demonstration §33 reads "that a virgin should be a virgin's intercessor"; the Ante-Nicene Fathers note at V.19.1 calls the word "ambiguous" and suggests a Greek original meaning one "called in to help," so that "Mary was thus the counterpart or balance of Eve." Whether the word names intercession or a typological counterpart is disputed; see Does Irenaeus's advocata make Mary the advocate (intercessor) of Eve?4
  • He is the earliest witness bearing on two questions in this doctrine's Disputed readings section: Is invoking a saint the same act as believing the saints intercede? and Is the invocation of saints a fourth-century development or an older practice?, where the silence of the early writers is discussed. The other pre-Nicene witnesses on the timeline include Origen (c. 233), who reserves prayer "in the full sense" to God, and Cyprian (c. 252), who holds that the dead pray for the living.
  • Irenaeus speaks of invocation also in his Eucharistic theology, where the bread receives "the invocation of God" and becomes the body of Christ.
