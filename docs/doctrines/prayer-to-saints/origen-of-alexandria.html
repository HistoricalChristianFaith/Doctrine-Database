Origen of Alexandria on prayer to the saints

Dates: c. 233–234 (On Prayer / Peri Euchēs) and c. 248 (Against Celsus / Contra Celsum); Origen of Alexandria c. 184–253 · Wikipedia
Summary: Origen holds that the saints already at rest and the angels pray with and for the living, and he allows that one may make a request ("should some Paul or Peter appear … to benefit us") to a saint. He also draws a line: prayer "in its full meaning" (proseuchē) is to be offered only "to the God and Father of All," through Christ the High Priest — "we may not ever pray to any begotten being, not even to Christ himself," as addressed apart from the Father — and in Against Celsus he declines to invoke the angels. Gavin Ortlund cites "the ambivalence/unfamiliarity of Origen" among the evidence for his view that the invocation of saints developed in the mid-to-late patristic era.

The four kinds of prayer, and to whom each is owed

In On Prayer, written at Caesarea c. 233–234, Origen takes up Paul's fourfold list in 1 Timothy 2:1 — "requests (deēseis), prayers (proseuchai), intercessions (enteuxeis), thanksgivings (eucharistiai)" — and asks, of each kind in turn, to whom it may rightly be addressed. His answer is graded. Request, intercession, and thanksgiving may be offered even to fellow human beings; but prayer in the strict sense may be offered to God alone:

Now request and intercession and thanksgiving, it is not out of place to offer even to men—the two latter, intercession and thanksgiving, not only to saintly men but also to others. But request to saints alone, should some Paul or Peter appear, to benefit us by making us worthy to obtain the authority which has been given to them to forgive sins …

— so a request may be directed to a saint such as Peter or Paul. Of prayer proper he writes:

But if we accept prayer in its full meaning, we may not ever pray to any begotten being, not even to Christ himself, but only to the God and Father of All to whom our Savior both prayed himself … and teaches us to pray.

The reasoning is christological and exegetical: when the disciples asked to be taught to pray, Jesus "does not teach men to pray to Himself but to the Father," saying "Our Father in heaven." Origen runs through the logical options — pray to the Son and not the Father, to both, or to the Father alone — and concludes that only the last is right:

It remains, accordingly, to pray to God alone, the Father of All, not however apart from the High Priest who has been appointed by the Father with swearing of an oath … so we are not to offer any prayer to the Father apart from Him.

For Origen, prayer in the full sense runs to the Father through the Son.1

The saints and angels pray with the living

The same treatise holds that the blessed dead and the angels are active on the living's behalf. Earlier in On Prayer Origen argues that "these pray along with those who genuinely pray — not only the high priest but also the angels … and also the souls of the saints already at rest," adducing Raphael, who "present[s] the prayers of saints" before God (Tobit), and the appearance of "God's prophet Jeremiah," who "prays much for the people and the sacred city" (2 Maccabees 15). Because love of neighbour is "possessed in a far higher degree by saints already at rest than by those who are in human weakness," they continue to care for the church below. And of the angels he writes:

… we may surmise that the presence of the angels who exercise oversight and ministry for God is sometimes brought into conjunction with a particular suppliant in order that they may join in breathing his petitions. Nay more, beholding ever the face of the Father in heaven … the angel of each man, even of "little ones" within the church, both prays with us, and acts with us where possible, for the objects of our prayer.

Origen thus affirms that the saints and angels "pray with" and "for" the living, while reserving prayer to in the full sense for God; on the distinction, see Is invoking a saint the same act as believing the saints intercede? He is quoted by writers on both sides of the dating question.2

Against Celsus: the angels honoured but not invoked

About fifteen years later, in Against Celsus (c. 248), the restriction sharpens into an explicit refusal to invoke angels — written against the pagan Celsus, who taxed the Christians both with worshipping a man and with inconsistency for not also worshipping God's other ministers. Origen grants that the angels "ascend, bearing the supplications of men" and "come down … conveying to each one … something enjoined by God," yet denies that this makes them recipients of prayer:

… but not so that we are commanded to honour and worship in place of God those who minister to us, and bear to us His blessings. For every prayer, and supplication, and intercession, and thanksgiving, is to be sent up to the Supreme God through the High Priest, who is above all the angels, the living Word and God.

In the next chapter he states the principle directly — knowledge of the angels' true nature "will not permit us to pray with confidence to any other than to the Supreme God" — while still affirming the angels' help:

For to invoke angels without having obtained a knowledge of their nature greater than is possessed by men, would be contrary to reason. … And it is enough to secure that the holy angels of God be propitious to us, and that they do all things on our behalf, that our disposition of mind towards God should imitate … the example of these holy angels …

The pattern recurs across Book 8: Christians "worship with all our power the one God, and His only Son … by prayers and supplications," declining to "worship and serve" ministers of God (VIII.13); the guardian angel "offers up his prayers through the one High Priest to the God of all, and also joins his own prayers with those of the man who is committed to his keeping" (VIII.36); Christians are those "who pray to God alone through Jesus" (VIII.37). In these passages the angels assist, accompany, and carry up prayers, and are not themselves addressed in prayer.3

Origen in the dating debate

Origen's theology of prayer, written out by the mid-third century, holds together several points: the saints and angels intercede; a saint may be the object of a request; prayer in the full sense is reserved to the Father through Christ; and invoking angels without knowledge of their nature is "contrary to reason." Gavin Ortlund (Protestant) names "the ambivalence/unfamiliarity of Origen," with Augustine's strictures and the mild character of the earliest examples, as fitting his "proposal of development."4 J. P. Kirsch (Catholic historian, Eng. tr. 1910) reads the "saints" of Origen's clause as "living believers," and holds that the third-century writers on prayer "do not specially deal with these invocations," which he finds attested in the inscriptions of the period.5 The catacomb petitions to Peter and Paul at San Sebastiano are dated from c. 250, the decade of Against Celsus.
