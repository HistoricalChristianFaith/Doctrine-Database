Basil of Caesarea on the Forty Martyrs of Sebaste

Dates: c. 373 (the Homily on the Forty Martyrs of Sebaste, preached at the martyrium in Caesarea on the martyrs' feast); Basil of Caesarea c. 330–379 · Wikipedia
Summary: Basil's homily on the Forty Martyrs, preached at their shrine in Caesarea, is the earliest datable Cappadocian text on the cult of the martyrs gathered here. It calls the martyrs "common guardians of the human race," "helpers in prayer," "most powerful intercessors," and says the afflicted "take refuge" in them. Its address to the martyrs ("O common guardians of the human race!") is in the vocative; the homily's one imperative of request, "may your requests be with the martyrs," is spoken to the living congregation.

The apostrophe to the Forty Martyrs

Basil preached the Homily on the Forty Martyrs of Sebaste at the shrine of the Forty during their festival, to a crowd gathered at the relics. The Forty were soldiers of the Legio XII Fulminata frozen to death on a lake at Sebaste in Armenia under Licinius (c. 320); their cult was among the most popular in Anatolia, and the Oxford Cult of Saints project calls Basil's homily its earliest datable testimony. Having narrated the martyrdom, Basil turns to the martyrs' present power for the living:

What would you not do, to find one somewhere to entreat the Lord for you? There are forty, sending up a harmonious prayer. Where two or three are gathered in the name of the Lord, there he is in their midst. But where there are forty, who doubts the presence of God? The one who is afflicted takes refuge in the forty, the one who rejoices runs to them… Here a pious woman is found praying for her children, asking for the return of her husband who is abroad, for the salvation of one who is sick. May your requests be with the martyrs.

He then addresses the martyrs themselves:

O holy chorus! O sacred company! O unbreakable shield-wall! O common guardians of the human race! Good sharers of our cares, helpers in prayer, most powerful intercessors, stars of the world, flowers of the Churches. The earth did not hide you, but heaven received you; the gates of paradise were opened to you.

The Greek behind the key phrases — koinoi phylakes ("common guardians/keepers"), deēseōs synergoi ("fellow-workers of [our] supplication"), presbeutai dynatōtatoi ("most powerful ambassadors/intercessors") — ascribe to the Forty an ongoing intercessory office: they guard "the human race," they assist the prayers of the living, they are "most powerful" envoys before God. In Basil's account the afflicted "take refuge in the forty," and he commends bringing one's petitions into the martyrs' company.1

Grammatical form of the homily

The page Is invoking a saint the same act as believing the saints intercede? sets out the distinction between believing that the saints intercede and addressing a petition to a saint in the second person. Basil's homily attests a public cult of the martyrs — a shrine, a feast, a crowd, relics, and the conviction that the Forty guard and assist the living.

Its address "O holy chorus!… O common guardians… helpers in prayer, most powerful intercessors" is in the vocative and contains no imperative verb. The one sentence that carries an imperative of request — "may your requests be with the martyrs" — is addressed in the second person to the living congregation, urging the people to bring their petitions into the martyrs' company.2

The homily is discussed under the question Is the invocation of saints a fourth-century development or an older practice? It is dated c. 373, a few years before the addresses to saints in Gregory of Nazianzus (the oration to Cyprian) and Gregory of Nyssa (the encomium on Theodore).

Dating

The c. 373 date is a scholarly inference rather than a fixed datum: it rests on the homily's festival setting at the Caesarean martyrium and on Basil's episcopate (370–379), and the homily is grouped with his other martyr homilies (on Gordius, Mamas, Julitta), all apparently delivered in the 370s. The Oxford Cult of Saints in Late Antiquity project calls it "the earliest datable testimony to the rise of one of the most popular cults of Anatolia." Basil does not name the place of martyrdom and stresses the martyrs' heavenly citizenship; several Cappadocian and Pontic sites claimed individual members of the Forty. The authenticity of the homily (CPG 2863) is not in dispute.3
