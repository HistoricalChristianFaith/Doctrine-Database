Cyprian of Carthage on prayer to the saints

Dates: c. 252 (Epistle 56 to Cornelius; Oxford/Hartel Ep. 60) — Cyprian of Carthage c. 200–258 · Wikipedia
Summary: Cyprian, bishop of Carthage, writes that the faithful who die pass into the Lord's presence and go on praying for the brethren they left behind. Writing to the exiled Cornelius of Rome, he hopes that whichever of the two dies first will "continue" his love and his "prayers for our brethren and sisters" before God; in On the Mortality he pictures the dead in paradise as "still solicitous for our salvation." Both passages speak of the departed praying for the living; in the texts surveyed here Cyprian does not address a prayer to a departed saint. His texts figure in the question Is invoking a saint the same act as believing the saints intercede?

"Let our prayers for our brethren and sisters not cease in the presence of the Father's mercy"

In the summer of 252 Cyprian wrote to Cornelius, bishop of Rome, then banished for confessing Christ. The letter closes with a mutual exhortation to pray for one another through the coming persecution — and then extends that prayer past death. Whichever of the two "shall go hence the first," Cyprian hopes, will keep interceding for the survivors from the Lord's presence:

Let us remember one another in concord and unanimity. Let us on both sides always pray for one another. Let us relieve burdens and afflictions by mutual love, that if any one of us, by the swiftness of divine condescension, shall go hence the first, our love may continue in the presence of the Lord, and our prayers for our brethren and sisters not cease in the presence of the Father's mercy.

In the letter the departed do not fall silent but "continue" their love and their prayers for the living "in the presence of the Lord." Cyprian states it as an expectation, without arguing for it. The act described is the dead praying for the living: Cyprian asks Cornelius to keep praying for him after death, and the letter does not speak of those on earth addressing the departed.1

The dead in paradise, "still solicitous for our salvation"

The same picture recurs in On the Mortality (De Mortalitate, c. 252), Cyprian's pastoral treatise written as plague swept Carthage. Consoling Christians afraid to die, he describes death as a homecoming to a paradise already crowded with those we have loved, and the blessed dead there as concerned for the salvation of the living still behind them:

There a great number of our dear ones is awaiting us, and a dense crowd of parents, brothers, children, is longing for us, already assured of their own safety, and still solicitous for our salvation. To attain to their presence and their embrace, what a gladness both for them and for us in common! … There the glorious company of the apostles — there the host of the rejoicing prophets — there the innumerable multitude of martyrs, crowned for the victory of their struggle and passion …

The dead are "assured of their own safety" yet "still solicitous for our salvation." Cyprian bids the living hasten toward the departed and long to be with them; the treatise does not have the living petition them.2

Placement on the timeline

Cyprian's letters (some eighty survive) and treatises are much concerned with the martyrs, the dead, and prayer. He honours the martyrs, offers the Eucharist for the departed, and expects the dead to pray for him; in the passages surveyed here he does not address a petition to a saint. His contemporary Origen reserves prayer "in its full meaning" to God. In the same decade (c. 250) the graffiti at the Roman memorial of Peter and Paul begin, with petitions such as "Paule et Petre petite pro nobis" scratched into the plaster. All three are discussed under the question Is the invocation of saints a fourth-century development or an older practice?3
