The Council of Trent on the invocation of the saints

Dates: c. 1563 — Session 25, the twenty-fifth and final session of the Council of Trent (3–4 December 1563), the "Decree on the Invocation, Veneration, and Relics of Saints, and on Sacred Images" · Wikipedia
Summary: In its last session the Council of Trent, which the Catholic Church counts as ecumenical, decrees that the saints reigning with Christ pray to God for men, that "it is good and useful suppliantly to invoke them," and condemns as impious those who deny that the saints are to be invoked or who call such invocation idolatry or contrary to the honour of the one mediator. The decree came four years after Calvin's final Institutes and frames invocation as obtaining benefits from God "through His Son, Jesus Christ our Lord, who is our alone Redeemer and Saviour."

The decree

The saints pray for us, and it is good and useful to invoke them

the saints, who reign together with Christ, offer up their own prayers to God for men; that it is good and useful suppliantly to invoke them, and to have recourse to their prayers, aid, (and) help for obtaining benefits from God, through His Son, Jesus Christ our Lord, who is our alone Redeemer and Saviour.

— Decree on the Invocation, Veneration, and Relics of Saints, and on Sacred Images, Session 25 (1563), trans. J. Waterworth, The Canons and Decrees of the Council of Trent (1848), source. The decree states both that the saints intercede ("offer up their own prayers to God for men") and that the living may invoke them ("suppliantly to invoke them, and to have recourse to their prayers"), and it places the whole "through His Son, Jesus Christ our Lord, who is our alone Redeemer and Saviour."1

Condemnation of those who deny invocation or call it idolatry

they think impiously, who deny that the saints, who enjoy eternal happiness in heaven, are to be invocated; or who assert either that they do not pray for men; or, that the invocation of them to pray for each of us even in particular, is idolatry; or, that it is repugnant to the word of God; and is opposed to the honour of the one mediator of God and men, Christ Jesus; or, that it is foolish to supplicate, vocally, or mentally, those who reign in heaven.

— ibid., source. Calvin had written that invocation "robs God of his glory," "destroys the intercession of Christ," and "is repugnant to the word of God," and the Augsburg Confession (1530) that Scripture "teaches not the invocation of saints." The decree's clause names the positions it condemns — that invocation is idolatry, repugnant to the word of God, opposed to the honour of the one mediator, or foolish — and says those who hold them "think impiously." For the Catholic Church the decree is a dogmatic definition.2

Placement on the timeline

Trent legislates in 1563, four years after Calvin's final Institutes (1559) and thirty-three after the Augsburg Confession (1530). The decree states both that the saints intercede and that they may be invoked, and it routes all benefit "through His Son, Jesus Christ our Lord, who is our alone Redeemer and Saviour." It bears on the question Is invoking a saint the same act as believing the saints intercede?, since its grammar distinguishes the two; on the question Is the invocation of saints a fourth-century development or an older practice? it is a sixteenth-century definition, not an early witness.

Related texts

  • Compare Trent's clause — benefits obtained from God "through His Son, Jesus Christ our Lord, who is our alone Redeemer and Saviour" — with Aquinas's distinction (worship to God as "the Author of our goods," the saints invoked "as our advocates") and with the Nicene proskynesis/latreia distinction of 787.
  • The same decree treats the veneration of relics and of sacred images alongside invocation and, like Nicaea II, holds that the honour paid to images "is referred to the prototypes which they represent," reserving worship to God.
