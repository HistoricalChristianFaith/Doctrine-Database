Epiphanius of Salamis against the Collyridians

Dates: c. 377 (the refutation of the Arabian Collyridians written into Book III of his catalogue of heresies, the Panarion or “Medicine Chest,” as Panarion 79; the work was finished about 377); Epiphanius, bishop of Constantia in Cyprus, c. 315–403 · Wikipedia
Summary: In Panarion 79 Epiphanius distinguishes in so many words between the honour paid a saint (later termed dulia) and the worship owed God alone (latria). Refuting the Collyridians, a group of women who, on his report, offered a cake to Mary, he calls the rite idolatry while insisting that the Virgin be honoured: “Mary should be honoured, but the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit should be worshipped; no one should worship Mary.” He applies the same rule to Elijah, John, Thecla, “nor any of the saints.” The chapter also reports a fourth-century Marian rite that Epiphanius judged to be worship.

The rite he condemned

The Collyridians (from Greek kollyris, a small loaf or cake) were, on Epiphanius’ report, women who had brought a devotion from Thrace and Scythia into Arabia: on a set day of the year they prepared a decorated seat, laid out bread, offered it in Mary’s name, and then shared it out. Epiphanius pairs them with the Antidicomarians of the neighbouring chapter, who had denied Mary’s perpetual virginity, and calls the two equal and opposite errors, one belittling the Virgin, the other glorifying her to excess.

For as, long ago, those who, from an insolent attitude towards Mary, have seen fit to suspect these things were sowing damaging suspicions in people’s minds, so these persons who lean in the other direction are guilty of doing the worst sort of harm… For the harm done by both of these sects is equal, since one belittles the holy Virgin while the other, in its turn, glorifies her to excess.

For certain women decorate a barber’s chair or a square seat, spread a cloth on it, set out bread and offer it in Mary’s name on a certain day of the year, and all partake of the bread… so as to grub out the roots of this idolatrous sect.

— Epiphanius, Panarion 79 (Against Collyridians) 1.4–7.1 By c. 377, on Epiphanius’ report, the honour of Mary had in some quarters taken a form he judged to be worship.

Honour the Virgin, worship God alone

The refutation turns on a distinction between honour and worship: the Virgin is to be honoured, even honoured above the other saints, but not worshipped, because worship belongs to God alone:

Yes, of course Mary’s body was holy, but she was not God. Yes, the Virgin was indeed a virgin and honored as such, but she was not given us to worship; she worships Him who, though born of her flesh, has come from heaven, from the bosom of his Father.

Mary should be honored, but the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit should be worshiped; no one should worship Mary. There is no commandment to < offer > the Eucharist even to a man, < as though > to God, let alone to a woman; not even angels are allowed such glory.

…even though Mary is all fair, and is holy and held in honor, she is not to be worshiped.

— Epiphanius, Panarion 79.4.6; 79.7.5; 79.7.7.2 Compare the Definition of the Second Council of Nicaea (787), which distinguishes the reverence (τιμητικὴ προσκύνησις) paid the saints from the adoration (λατρεία) “which pertains alone to the divine nature,” and the treatment of prayer by Thomas Aquinas (c. 1272).

Elijah, John, Thecla and Mary

Epiphanius does not confine the rule to Mary. He compares her with Elijah, who did not die, with the beloved disciple John, and with the virgin-martyr Thecla, honours her above them, and applies to all of them the same negative: none is to be worshipped.

…she is like Elijah, who was virgin from his mother’s womb, always remained so, and was taken up and has not seen death. She is like John who leaned on the Lord’s breast… She is like St. Thecla; and Mary is still more honored than she, because of the providence vouchsafed her. But Elijah is not to be worshiped, even though he is alive. And John is not to be worshiped… But neither is Thecla worshiped, nor any of the saints.

— Epiphanius, Panarion 79.5.2–3.3 He reinforces it a fortiori: “If it is not his will that angels be worshiped, how much more the woman born of Ann” (79.5.4). On the distinction set out under Is invoking a saint the same act as believing the saints intercede?, the chapter concerns worship: the passages quoted here neither petition a departed saint nor discuss asking a saint’s prayers. The line Epiphanius draws is between the honour due the saints and the adoration due God.

Placement on the timeline

Epiphanius writes c. 377, in the same years as Basil (c. 373), who addresses the Forty Martyrs as “most powerful intercessors,” and Ambrose (c. 377), who enjoins that “the martyrs must be entreated.” Like Origen before him, who reserved prayer “in its full meaning” to God, and Augustine after him, who barred any sacrifice offered to a saint, he distinguishes the honour paid to saints from the worship due to God.

The chapter also records a Marian devotion that, on his report, existed in Arabia c. 377 and that he condemned. It is discussed alongside the early silence on the Assumption and the Six Books apocryphon.

Related pages and reception

  • The companion chapter. Panarion 79 is the twin of Panarion 78 against the Antidicomarians; Epiphanius wrote both as a single pastoral letter to Arabia and folded them into the Panarion. His Mariology is set out more fully on his perpetual-virginity detail page and his new-Eve witness.
  • Worship and invocation. The rule of Panarion 79 concerns worship (the offering made in Mary’s name); the passages quoted here do not address asking a saint’s prayers. On that distinction see Is invoking a saint the same act as believing the saints intercede?
  • The sect. Stephen Shoemaker reads Epiphanius’ anti-Collyridian polemic as evidence of an organized fourth-century Marian cult in Arabia and Palestine — the same milieu as the earliest Dormition traditions — rather than a scattered aberration.4
