Epiphanius of Salamis against the Collyridians

Dates: c. 377 (the refutation of the Arabian Collyridians written into Book III of his catalogue of heresies, the Panarion or “Medicine Chest,” as Panarion 79; the work was finished about 377); Epiphanius, bishop of Constantia in Cyprus, c. 315–403 · Wikipedia
Summary: Epiphanius is the earliest father to draw in as many words the line the whole later defence of invoking the saints rests on — between the honour paid a saint (later termed dulia) and the worship owed God alone (latria). Refuting the Collyridians, a group of women who offered a cake to Mary as to a goddess, he brands the rite idolatry while insisting the Virgin be honoured, and states the rule flatly: “Mary should be honoured, but the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit should be worshipped; no one should worship Mary.” The same rule bars worship of any creature — Elijah, John, Thecla, “nor any of the saints” — so his witness sits on the timeline as a brake, alongside Origen and Augustine: he honours the saints and grants their intercession, but rules the adoration due God strictly off-limits to them. The datum cuts both ways, for the very sect he attacks is fourth-century evidence that Marian devotion had already, in some quarters, crossed into cult.

The rite he condemned

The Collyridians (from Greek kollyris, a small loaf or cake) were, on Epiphanius’ report, women who had brought a devotion from Thrace and Scythia into Arabia: on a set day of the year they prepared a decorated seat, laid out bread, offered it in Mary’s name, and then shared it out. Epiphanius pairs them deliberately with the Antidicomarians of the neighbouring chapter, who had denied Mary’s perpetual virginity: the two are equal and opposite errors, one degrading the Virgin, the other deifying her.

For as, long ago, those who, from an insolent attitude towards Mary, have seen fit to suspect these things were sowing damaging suspicions in people’s minds, so these persons who lean in the other direction are guilty of doing the worst sort of harm… For the harm done by both of these sects is equal, since one belittles the holy Virgin while the other, in its turn, glorifies her to excess.

For certain women decorate a barber’s chair or a square seat, spread a cloth on it, set out bread and offer it in Mary’s name on a certain day of the year, and all partake of the bread… so as to grub out the roots of this idolatrous sect.

— Epiphanius, Panarion 79 (Against Collyridians) 1.4–7.1 Modern scholarship reads the sect more soberly than Epiphanius’ polemic does — the ritual may have been closer to an ordinary saint’s offering than to the goddess-cult he paints — but the datum stands either way: by c. 377 the honour of Mary had, in some quarters, taken a form Epiphanius judged to have overshot into worship.

Honour the Virgin, worship God alone — the dulia/latria line

The whole refutation turns on a distinction Epiphanius states more explicitly than any earlier father: the Virgin is to be honoured, even honoured above the other saints, but not worshipped, because worship belongs to God alone. He grants her the highest place among creatures and denies her the one thing reserved to the Creator:

Yes, of course Mary’s body was holy, but she was not God. Yes, the Virgin was indeed a virgin and honored as such, but she was not given us to worship; she worships Him who, though born of her flesh, has come from heaven, from the bosom of his Father.

Mary should be honored, but the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit should be worshiped; no one should worship Mary. There is no commandment to < offer > the Eucharist even to a man, < as though > to God, let alone to a woman; not even angels are allowed such glory.

…even though Mary is all fair, and is holy and held in honor, she is not to be worshiped.

— Epiphanius, Panarion 79.4.6; 79.7.5; 79.7.7.2 This is the exact axis the later defence of invocation runs on — the reverence (τιμητικὴ προσκύνησις) paid the saints over against the adoration (λατρεία) “which pertains alone to the divine nature,” as the Second Council of Nicaea would fix it four centuries later, and as Thomas Aquinas would systematize it. Epiphanius is the earliest witness to name that boundary in so many words.

No saint is to be worshipped — not Elijah, John, Thecla, nor Mary

The point directly bearing on this timeline is that Epiphanius does not confine the rule to Mary. He ranks her explicitly among the greatest of the saints — the ever-virgin Elijah who never died, the beloved disciple John, the virgin-martyr Thecla — and honours her above them; then applies to the whole class the same negative: none of them is to be worshipped.

…she is like Elijah, who was virgin from his mother’s womb, always remained so, and was taken up and has not seen death. She is like John who leaned on the Lord’s breast… She is like St. Thecla; and Mary is still more honored than she, because of the providence vouchsafed her. But Elijah is not to be worshiped, even though he is alive. And John is not to be worshiped… But neither is Thecla worshiped, nor any of the saints.

— Epiphanius, Panarion 79.5.2–3.3 He reinforces it a fortiori: “If it is not his will that angels be worshiped, how much more the woman born of Ann” (79.5.4). Read through the lens the whole timeline must be read through, Epiphanius is emphatically not an early witness to strict invocation — he nowhere turns and petitions a departed saint. What he attests is the boundary: the settled fourth-century conviction that the saints are to be honoured, and their prayers valued, but that the second-person address which is worship is reserved to God. He affirms the saints’ honour and their intercession while ruling their adoration out of bounds — the very brake the developed practice would later have to argue past.

Where he sits on the timeline

Epiphanius belongs with the timeline’s brakes rather than its proponents. Like Origen before him, who reserved prayer “in its full meaning” to God alone, and like Augustine after him, who affirmed the martyrs’ intercession yet barred any sacrifice offered to a saint, Epiphanius holds the two halves apart: honour and intercession, yes; worship, no. His near neighbours on the timeline pull the other way — Basil (c. 373) apostrophizes the Forty Martyrs as “most powerful intercessors,” and Ambrose (c. 377) enjoins that “the martyrs must be entreated” — so Epiphanius stands among them as the same generation’s check on the developing cult, the boundary-marker beside the cult-builders.

The datum cuts both ways, and both edges matter. Against the antiquity of Marian worship, Epiphanius is a hostile fourth-century witness who calls the offering to Mary flat idolatry. For the antiquity of Marian devotion, the very existence of the Collyridians shows that popular cult had, by his day, already run ahead of what a learned bishop would permit — the same tension the early silence on the Assumption and the Six Books apocryphon turn on.

Notes

  • The companion chapter. Panarion 79 is the twin of Panarion 78 against the Antidicomarians; Epiphanius wrote both as a single pastoral letter to Arabia and folded them into the Panarion. His positive Mariology — the honour that stops short of worship — is set out more fully on his perpetual-virginity detail page and his new-Eve witness.
  • Not invocation. Epiphanius is sometimes miscited as an early opponent of invoking the saints as such; what he actually rules out is worshipping them. He says nothing against asking a saint’s prayers, and his own tradition prizes the saints’ intercession — the line he draws is between honour/intercession and adoration, not between intercession-belief and invocation. On that distinction see the invocation / intercession-belief argument.
  • The sect’s reality. Stephen Shoemaker reads Epiphanius’ anti-Collyridian polemic as evidence of an organized fourth-century Marian cult in Arabia and Palestine — the same milieu that produced the earliest Dormition traditions — rather than a scattered aberration; so the chapter is a genuine window on how far, and how early, Marian devotion had developed.4
