Hippolytus of Rome on prayer to the saints

Dates: c. 204 (the Commentary on Daniel; Hippolytus c. 170–235) · Wikipedia
Summary: In the Commentary on Daniel, the oldest surviving Christian commentary on a book of Scripture, Hippolytus breaks off his exposition of Daniel 3 to address the three youths of the fiery furnace directly: "remember me, I entreat you, that I also may obtain the same lot of martyrdom with you." The clause is a second-person petition to the three, set within an extended homiletic apostrophe (the same sentence asks the youths to "describe to us" the form of the fourth man in the furnace). It is adduced in the debate over when the invocation of saints arose; see Is the invocation of saints a fourth-century development or an older practice?

The passage

Hippolytus is expounding the canticle the three sing in the furnace (the Song of the Three Children, inserted at Daniel 3:24–90 in the Greek of Theodotion). Having walked through their enumeration of all creation, he quotes their closing self-naming — "Bless the Lord, Ananiah, Azariah, and Misahl … because he took us from Hades and saved us from the hand of death" — and then, at the head of the next section, turns from third-person exposition to address the three themselves:

Tell me, you three boys, remember me, I entreat you, that I also may obtain the same lot of martyrdom with you, who was the fourth person with you who was walking in the midst of the furnace and who was hymning to God with you as from one mouth? Describe to us his form and beauty so that we also, seeing him in the flesh, may recognize him. Who was he who in this way orderly described all creation through your mouth, so that you omitted nothing of which is and has been?

— Commentary on Daniel 2.30.1–2, in T. C. Schmidt's translation. The address continues in the same key, telling the youths that "the Word was with you and was speaking through you," before Hippolytus returns to third-person commentary.1 The petition proper is the clause remember me, I entreat you, that I also may obtain the same lot of martyrdom with you: Hippolytus asks the three to keep him in mind so that he too may be granted martyrdom — a request addressed to them, in the second person, in the optative of his own desire.

The setting: a martyr's commentary under persecution

The Commentary on Daniel is the earliest surviving running commentary on a book of the Bible by a Christian author, composed c. 202–211 (conventionally c. 204) while the Roman church suffered under the persecution of Septimius Severus. Hippolytus, a presbyter and theologian of the early-third-century Roman church, himself later reckoned a martyr, wrote to steady a frightened congregation, holding up Daniel, Susanna, and above all the three youths as models of those who do not flinch before the fire. He casts the three as proto-martyrs and "athletes" contending against the state, and earlier in the work has them "about to be crowned" with the victor's wreath.2 The apostrophe at 2.30 comes at the climax of that exhortation: a writer who longs for martyrdom turns to the furnace's survivors and asks to share their lot. Tradition records that Hippolytus died in exile on Sardinia c. 235. The address stands within a pastoral commentary.3

Features of the passage cited in the debate

The grammar. The clause is in the second person: Hippolytus writes remember me, I entreat you, addressing the three and asking them to keep him in mind. On the distinction between addressing a saint and believing that the saints intercede, see Is invoking a saint the same act as believing the saints intercede? The work is dated c. 204, about half a century before the catacomb graffiti and nearly two centuries before the Cappadocian homilies.

The setting. The petition sits inside an apostrophe, a turn from exposition to address the three: in the same sentence Hippolytus asks them to "describe to us" the appearance of the fourth man in the furnace and to say "who was he who … described all creation through your mouth." The address comes at the climax of a martyrdom-exhortation, and its addressees are the three youths of Daniel 3.

How these features bear on the date at which invocation arose is disputed; the readings are set out under Is the invocation of saints a fourth-century development or an older practice?4

A note on the canticle

A separate fragment of Hippolytus, "On the Song of the Three Children," opens with the line "O Ananias, Azarias, and Misael, bless ye the Lord; O ye apostles, prophets, and martyrs of the Lord, bless ye the Lord." That looks like a second address to the youths; the line echoes and expands the canticle itself, the Benedicite, in which the three call on every order of creation, and finally on themselves, to bless God (Daniel 3:88 / Song 65: "O Ananias, Azarias, and Misael, bless ye the Lord"), here glossed with the church's orders of saints. The second-person petition discussed above is the one at 2.30, not this canticle-quotation.5

Reception and debate

  • This witness figures in two of the questions in this doctrine's Disputed readings section: Is invoking a saint the same act as believing the saints intercede? (the passage is in the second person) and Is the invocation of saints a fourth-century development or an older practice?, where its date and its homiletic setting are both discussed.
  • Related early material on the timeline: the third-century catacomb petitions to Peter and Paul, which share the "remember us"/"keep us in mind" form and come from the memorial of the apostles on the Via Appia, and the Sub Tuum Praesidium, a petitionary prayer to Mary whose papyrus is variously dated.
