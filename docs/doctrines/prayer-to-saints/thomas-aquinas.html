Thomas Aquinas on whether we ought to pray to the saints

Dates: c. 1272 — the Secunda Secundae of the Summa Theologiae (the treatise on prayer, II-II q.83), composed c. 1271–1272; Thomas Aquinas c. 1225–1274 · Wikipedia
Summary: Aquinas asks "whether we ought to pray to God alone?" and answers with a distinction: prayer can be offered to a person in two ways — to be fulfilled by him, or to be obtained through him — and in the first way prayer is offered to God alone, but in the second way "we pray to the saints … not that God may through them know our petitions, but that our prayers may be effective through their prayers and merits." For Aquinas the saints are addressed as advocates, not as the source of the goods sought, and religious worship is offered to God alone. He grounds the practice on the teaching (q.83 a.11) that the blessed in heaven, the greater their charity and the closer their union with God, the more they pray for the living and the more efficacious their prayers.

Claims & evidence

Aquinas: two ways of praying, to God to fulfil, to the saints that our prayers be effective through theirs

Prayer is offered to a person in two ways: first, as to be fulfilled by him, secondly, as to be obtained through him. In the first way we offer prayer to God alone, since all our prayers ought to be directed to the acquisition of grace and glory, which God alone gives … But in the second way we pray to the saints, whether angels or men, not that God may through them know our petitions, but that our prayers may be effective through their prayers and merits. Hence it is written (Apoc. 8:4) that "the smoke of the incense," namely "the prayers of the saints ascended up before God." This is also clear from the very style employed by the Church in praying: since we beseech the Blessed Trinity "to have mercy on us," while we ask any of the saints "to pray for us."

— Summa Theologiae II-II q.83 a.4 ("Whether we ought to pray to God alone?"), respondeo, trans. Fathers of the English Dominican Province, source. The sed contra of the same article grounds the answer in Scripture — "Call … if there be any that will answer thee, and turn to some of the saints" (Job 5:1) — and the article is Aquinas's principal statement on invocation. For Aquinas, praying to the saints is a different act from the prayer offered to God, who alone is asked to fulfil the petition.1

Aquinas: worship to God alone; the saints invoked as advocates

To Him alone do we offer religious worship when praying, from Whom we seek to obtain what we pray for, because by so doing we confess that He is the Author of our goods: but not to those whom we call upon as our advocates in God's presence.

— Summa Theologiae II-II q.83 a.4, reply to objection 1, source. Here Aquinas answers the objection that invocation is an act of religious worship directed to a creature: the latria of prayer belongs to God as "the Author of our goods"; the saints are not worshipped but "call[ed] upon … as our advocates." The Second Council of Nicaea (787) had distinguished proskynesis from latreia; the Council of Trent issued its decree on invoking the saints in 1563.2

Aquinas: the saints in heaven pray for the living, the closer to God the more efficaciously

As Jerome says (Cont. Vigilant. 6), the error of Vigilantius consisted in saying that "while we live, we can pray one for another; but that after we are dead, none of our prayers for others can be heard" … But this is absolutely false, because, since prayers offered for others proceed from charity … the greater the charity of the saints in heaven, the more they pray for wayfarers, since the latter can be helped by prayers: and the more closely they are united to God, the more are their prayers efficacious.

— Summa Theologiae II-II q.83 a.11 ("Whether the saints in heaven pray for us?"), respondeo, source. In this article Aquinas defends the belief that the saints pray for the living, on which he grounds the invocation of a.4, and he does so by quoting Jerome's answer to Vigilantius (c. 406). Aquinas addresses the objection of how the dead know our petitions by following Gregory the Great: whatever it is fitting the blessed should know "is made known to them in the Word," so "the petitions which we raise to them are known to them through Divine manifestation" (a.4 ad 2).3

Placement on the timeline

Aquinas writes c. 1272, the next entry on this timeline after Gregory the Great (604) and Nicaea II (787). In q.83 the question he poses is how prayer to the saints is to be stated without derogating from Christ, and his answer gives two ways of praying, worship to God and advocacy to the saints, and the saints' impetration "through their previous merits and through God's acceptance." The Tridentine decree of 1563 states that invoking the saints "is good and useful" and condemns the view that it "is opposed to the honour of the one mediator." His text bears chiefly on Is invoking a saint the same act as believing the saints intercede?, since he distinguishes the two and grounds the first on the second; see also Is the invocation of saints a fourth-century development or an older practice?

Reception and debate

The one-mediator objection is answered in the Council of Trent's decree and pressed by Calvin.
  • Aquinas quotes Jerome's Against Vigilantius (c. 406) and names Vigilantius's denial "the error of Vigilantius"; see Vigilantius.
