Jerome on the intercession of the saints: Against Vigilantius and the address to Paula

Dates: c. 406 (Against Vigilantius / Contra Vigilantium, written in a single night at Bethlehem; with the Epitaph on Saint Paula, Letter 108, c. 404); Jerome c. 347–420 · Wikipedia
Summary: Jerome is the earliest extant witness on this timeline to defend recourse to the saints in a treatise against a named opponent. When the Gallic presbyter Vigilantius denied that the martyrs can hear or help the living — "once we die, the prayer of no person for another can be heard" — Jerome answered in Contra Vigilantium (c. 406) that "if Apostles and martyrs while still in the body can pray for others … how much more must they do so when once they have won their crowns." The treatise defends the saints' intercession (that the holy dead pray for the living) and the cult of relics. Two years earlier, closing his eulogy of his friend Paula, Jerome addresses her directly in the second person: "And now, Paula, farewell, and aid with your prayers the old age of your votary."

"How much more … when once they have won their crowns" — Contra Vigilantium

Around 406 the presbyter Vigilantius of Calagurris, in Aquitaine, circulated a tract attacking the reverence paid to relics, the night-vigils at the martyrs' shrines, the burning of tapers, and — at the root — the very idea that the dead saints could pray for the living. The reverend presbyters Riparius and Desiderius sent the tract to Jerome at Bethlehem, who dashed off his reply, by his own account, in a single night. Jerome quotes his opponent's denial squarely:

You say, in your pamphlet, that so long as we are alive we can pray for one another; but once we die, the prayer of no person for another can be heard…

Against this Jerome mounts the argument from the lesser to the greater:

If Apostles and martyrs while still in the body can pray for others, when they ought still to be anxious for themselves, how much more must they do so when once they have won their crowns, overcome, and triumphed? A single man, Moses, oft wins pardon from God for six hundred thousand armed men; and Stephen, the follower of his Lord and the first Christian martyr, entreats pardon for his persecutors; and when once they have entered on their life with Christ, shall they have less power than before?

The claim Jerome defends here is that the saints in glory pray for those still on earth, and do so the more powerfully for being crowned. He grounds it in the saints being "not… dead, but… asleep" and in the Lamb's omnipresence ("If the Lamb is present everywhere, the same must be believed respecting those who are with the Lamb"), so that they are not, as Vigilantius held, confined to their tombs. The treatise's wider burden is the cult of relics: Jerome ridicules Vigilantius for calling the martyrs' bones "a bit of powder wrapped up in a cloth," and points to emperors translating relics and bishops building altars over the tombs of Peter and Paul as proof that the whole Church honours them. In the treatise Jerome does not himself address a saint in the second person; its argument is that the saints can and do pray for the living. It also preserves, in Jerome's quotation, the words of Vigilantius, the earliest extant objection to the practice on this timeline.1

"Aid with your prayers the old age of your votary" — Jerome's address to the departed Paula

A second-person petition addressed to a departed saint appears in Jerome's own voice about two years before the Vigilantius treatise. Paula, the noble Roman widow who had followed Jerome to the Holy Land and founded the monasteries at Bethlehem, died early in 404; Jerome's long memorial letter to her daughter Eustochium (Letter 108, the Epitaphium Sanctae Paulae) ends by turning from narration to direct address of the dead woman:

And now, Paula, farewell, and aid with your prayers the old age of your votary. Your faith and your works unite you to Christ; thus standing in His presence you will the more readily gain what you ask.

Here Jerome speaks not about Paula but to her ("aid… your votary"), asking her to help him by her prayers, on the stated ground that she now stands in Christ's presence and "will the more readily gain what you ask." The words close Jerome's memorial letter for Paula. Taken with Contra Vigilantium, Jerome's writings contain both the claim that the saints pray for the living and a request addressed to one of them, within the years 404–406.2

Placement on the timeline

Jerome writes a little after the Cappadocians (Basil, c. 373; Gregory of Nazianzus and Gregory of Nyssa, c. 379–381), Ambrose (c. 377), and John Chrysostom (c. 390). Unlike their homilies, Contra Vigilantium is a polemical treatise answering an opponent who rejected the practice, and the same exchange preserves that opponent's objections (Vigilantius, known only through Jerome's quotations). Jerome writes shortly before Augustine, who affirms the martyrs' intercession, bars sacrifice to the saints, and holds that for the departed "by their proper nature" to be interested in the affairs of the living is "impossible".3

Reception and debate

  • This witness figures in two of the questions in this doctrine's Disputed readings section. On Is invoking a saint the same act as believing the saints intercede?, Contra Vigilantium defends the belief that the saints pray for the living, while Letter 108 asks a departed saint to pray. On Is the invocation of saints a fourth-century development or an older practice?, the treatise records an objection to the practice in 406, and Jerome calls Vigilantius a "monster" and sets against him "the bishops of the whole world," who enter the martyrs' basilicas.
  • The opponent Jerome answers, Vigilantius, has his own entry on this timeline; his words survive only as Jerome quotes them.
