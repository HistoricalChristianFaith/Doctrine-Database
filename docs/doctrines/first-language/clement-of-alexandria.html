Clement of Alexandria on the first language

Dates: c. 200 (Stromata, c. 198–203) · Wikipedia
Summary: Clement does not narrate Babel or name a primeval tongue, but two threads of the Stromata feed the first-language debate. He fixes the number of tongues the one speech divided into — "seventy-two generic dialects, as our Scriptures hand down" — and, in his running case that the "barbarians" (non-Greeks) are older and wiser than the Greeks, he holds that "the Phoenicians and the Syrians first invented letters" and that the Jewish institutions are of "far higher antiquity than the philosophy of the Greeks." The threads lend antiquity to Semitic letters and to Moses, the backdrop the Hebrew-primacy case would lean on, but Clement himself names no surviving tongue.

Claims & evidence

The one speech divided into seventy-two dialects

Euphorus and many other historians say that there are seventy-five nations and tongues, in consequence of hearing the statement made by Moses: "All the souls that sprang from Jacob, which went down into Egypt, were seventy-five." According to the true reckoning, there appear to be seventy-two generic dialects, as our Scriptures hand down. The rest of the vulgar tongues are formed by the blending of two, or three, or more dialects.

— Stromata I.21, newadvent.org / historicalchristian.faith. Clement reads the count of tongues off the table of nations: the seventy-two "generic" dialects are the primary languages the single pre-Babel speech split into, every later "vulgar tongue" a blend of them. It presupposes the one-into-many division without staging the Babel scene or naming what came first.

The barbarians invented letters and wisdom before the Greeks

And Cadmus, the inventor of letters among the Greeks, as Euphorus says, was a Phoenician; whence also Herodotus writes that they were called Phoenician letters. And they say that the Phoenicians and the Syrians first invented letters …

— Stromata I.16 ("That the Inventors of Other Arts Were Mostly Barbarians"), newadvent.org. The chapter is one long argument that "barbarians were inventors not only of philosophy, but almost of every art" — letters among them — so that the Greeks are the borrowers and the Semitic East the source. It is the priority-of-the-barbarians thesis that makes the antiquity of Hebrew and its neighbours a live premise for the later primacy debate.

Moses and the Hebrews older than Greek philosophy

The seventy-two-dialect note sits inside the chapter Clement titles "The Jewish Institutions and Laws of Far Higher Antiquity Than the Philosophy of the Greeks" (Stromata I.21), the long chronological argument running through Book I that "Moses" and the Hebrew Scriptures antedate the Greek sages. Clement's interest is apologetic — to show Greek wisdom derivative and the barbarian (here, Hebrew) source the elder — and it supplies the Hebrew-primacy reading its background claim of Hebrew antiquity, though Clement never turns it into a claim about the first tongue.

Notes

  • Clement is best read, on this question, as a witness to the shared framework — a fixed division of the one speech into many tongues (here numbered, from Scripture, at seventy-two) — and to the antiquity of Semitic letters, not as a partisan for Hebrew or Syriac primacy. He names no surviving Adamic tongue.
  • The "seventy-two" (or seventy) tongues became the standard patristic and medieval count of the nations of Babel, later systematized by Isidore in the Etymologies.
  • Clement appears across this wiki on many timelines — among them the canon, the origin of the Septuagint, the Nephilim, and astrology — several of them, like this one, drawing on the chronological apologetic of Stromata Book I.
