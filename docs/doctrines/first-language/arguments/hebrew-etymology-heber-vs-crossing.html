Is the name "Hebrew" taken from the patriarch Heber, or from "crossing over"?

Disputed reading · on The First Language of Mankind · witnesses: Augustine of Hippo, Theodoret of Cyrus

The passage

from him, as tradition says, the Hebrews derived their name, though the other etymology which derives the name from Abraham (as if Abrahews) may possibly be correct. But there can be little doubt that the former is the right etymology, and that they were called after Heber, Heberews, and then, dropping a letter, Hebrews; and so was their language called Hebrew.

— Augustine, The City of God 16.3, trans. Marcus Dods, newadvent.org.1

εἰ δὲ ἐκ τοῦ Ἕβερ ἑβραῖοι καλοῦνται, οὐ μόνους ἐχρῆν τούτους οὕτω προσαγορεύεσθαι· πολλὰ γὰρ ἔθνη ἐκ τοῦ Ἕβερ κατάγει τὸ γένος.

— "But if they are called Hebrews from Heber, these ought not to be the only ones so named; for many nations trace their descent from Heber." Theodoret of Cyrus, Questions on Genesis §62, Greek in Patrologia Graeca 80.2

The question

Genesis calls Abram "the Hebrew" (hāʿibrî, Gen 14:13) and Shem "the father of all the children of Eber" (Gen 10:21); the name belongs to the root ʿ-b-r, "to cross, pass over," whose preposition ʿēber means "beyond."3 Interpreters have derived "Hebrew" from the patriarch Heber (Eber), from Abraham's crossing of the Euphrates, or from a regional name, "those from beyond." The question bears on the history of the first-language debate: Augustine ties the name to the house of Heber, in which, he holds, the primitive language survived Babel (City of God 16.11), while Theodoret pairs the "crossing" derivation with his case that Syriac is the older tongue.

Named for the patriarch Eber

"Hebrew" is a patronymic: the people, and with them their language, are called after their ancestor Heber.

Held by: Josephus (Jewish, Antiquities I.6.4, c. 93); Augustine (City of God 16.3 and 16.11, c. 420); Jacob of Edessa (Syriac bishop, Letter XIII, c. 700); McClintock & Strong (Cyclopaedia, s.v. "Hebrew," 19th c.).4

The genealogy itself names the people from Eber. Shem is "the father of all the children of Eber" (Gen 10:21), Balaam's oracle calls Israel "Eber" (Num 24:24), and Eber's son Peleg is named because "in his days was the earth divided" (Gen 10:25), which Josephus reads as "the dispersion of the nations" and Jerome as the division of languages at Babel. Augustine notes that Heber is named first among Shem's sons although he stood five generations from Shem, and draws the name from his house: "they were called after Heber, Heberews, and then, dropping a letter, Hebrews." Eusebius, too, derives "the very name which is the appellation of the whole race" from Heber, whose own name he glosses as "the man that passes over."

McClintock & Strong argue from the grammar: ʿibrî is the regular patronymic form from ʿēber, on the pattern of "Moabite" and "Danite," and in Genesis 14:13 "Abram the Hebrew" stands beside "Mamre the Amorite," "the former of these … as evidently a patronymic as the latter." They hold that a patronymic fits the usage better than a regional name, since ʿēber alone is never used for the land beyond a river (the phrase is ʿēber hannāhār), and a regional name would be expected to stay with "such as continued to dwell there, not … a race descended from one who had left that region." That one line among Eber's descendants should bear his name they explain by analogy: "Why might not one race of the descendants of Eber call themselves by pre-eminence sons of Eber, just as one race of the descendants of Abraham called themselves by preeminence sons of Abraham." They report Ibn Ezra's suggestion that Abraham's descendants kept the name because they alone kept Eber's faith; Augustine and Jacob of Edessa give a linguistic reason, that Eber's line alone kept the first language.5

Named for Abraham, who crossed the Euphrates

"Hebrew" marks Abraham as the one who crossed over, or came from beyond, the Euphrates, and passed from him to his descendants.

Held by: Theodore of Mopsuestia (Antiochene, c. 400); Theodoret of Cyrus (Questions on Genesis §62, c. 453); Bleek (Einleitung ins A. T.).6

Scripture first applies the name to one man, Abram, at Genesis 14:13, and the Greek translators render hāʿibrî there as ὁ περάτης, "the one who crossed over," with Aquila following suit, the derivation McClintock & Strong describe as "sanctioned by the Greek translators." Joshua 24:2–3 places Abraham's fathers "on the other side of the flood," beyond the River, and has God take Abraham "from the other side." Theodoret draws the name from Syriac: "hebra in the tongue of the Syrians is called a crossing" (ἑβρα γὰρ τῇ σύρων φωνῇ διάβασις ὀνομάζεται). Abraham came out of the land of the Chaldeans into Palestine across the Euphrates, and Scripture calls him "the crosser" (τῷ περάτῃ). A name taken from that crossing belongs to Abraham's line alone, where descent from Eber is shared by many nations: Theodoret lists the Ishmaelites, the sons of Keturah, the Edomites, Amalekites, Moabites, Ammonites and the people of Carrhae. The root's own sense, "to cross, pass over," supplies the meaning, so the name records an event rather than a pedigree. In the form McClintock & Strong report from Bleek and others, the Canaanites called the newcomer "the man from the region beyond," "just as Europeans might call an American 'a transatlantic,'" and the name stayed with his descendants. Al-Mas‘ūdī (c. 956) reports the same account: "it is said" that the first to speak Hebrew was Abraham, after he "crossed the Euphrates," the name being taken from that passage.7

A regional name, "those from beyond," which the genealogy personifies as Eber

"Hebrew" first named a population from across a river, and the ancestor Eber in the genealogies is that regional name made into a person.

Held by: Emil G. Hirsch and John P. Peters (Jewish Encyclopedia, s.v. "Hebrew," 1901–1906); Wellhausen and Stade, as reported there.8

Hirsch and Peters derive ʿibrî as "a gentilic noun, formed by adding the suffix 'i' to the word 'eber,'" the common preposition "beyond" or "across," which in Hebrew designates the region across a sea or river, above all the lands beyond the Jordan or the Euphrates. Joshua 24:2, in their reading, preserves "an early Israelite tradition" that understood "Hebrew" as "the people whose ancestors had dwelt in the land beyond the River," and the phrase ʿēber hannāhār has counterparts in Assyrian ʿebir nari and Minaean ʿIbr-naharan. The same interpretation, they hold, "lies also behind the treatment of the eponymous Eber in the Priestly Code," and Genesis Rabbah and Rashi took it up. The usage fits a name given from outside: in the earlier period, they write, the Israelites "were known to other peoples by the name 'Hebrews,'" and used it themselves "in contrasting themselves with other nations." Wellhausen and Stade read it as "the people from beyond Jordan," originally a general term for the peoples east of the Jordan, which would account for Shem being "father of all the children of Eber" (Gen 10:21); the Israelites, on this view, "finally became the Hebrews par excellence." The article reports a further possibility that the Habiri of the El-Amarna tablets "may have been 'Hebrews.'"9
