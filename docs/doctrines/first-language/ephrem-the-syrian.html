Ephrem the Syrian on the first language

Dates: c. 363 (Commentary on Genesis; Ephrem c. 306–373) · Wikipedia
Summary: Ephrem holds that mankind once shared a single primeval speech and that one tongue alone survived the confusion of Babel — but in the works Yuliya Minets treats as authentic he never names that surviving language, neither Hebrew nor Syriac. Yuliya Minets and Sergey Minov place the Syriac-primacy claim popularly attached to his name in the Cave of Treasures, a sixth-/seventh-century narrative ascribed to him.

Claims & evidence

One primeval tongue, and one survivor at Babel — left unnamed

Commenting on Genesis 11, Ephrem takes the pre-Babel world to have shared "a single language," and reads the confusion as the loss of that original speech by every nation save one — without ever saying which nation kept it:

It is likely that they lost their common language when they received these new languages. For if their original language had not perished their first deed would not have come to nothing. It was when they lost their original language, which was lost by all the nations, with one exception, that their first building came to nought.

— Ephrem, Commentary on Genesis VIII (on Gen 11), trans. Edward G. Mathews & Joseph P. Amar, St. Ephrem the Syrian: Selected Prose Works (Fathers of the Church 91), historicalchristian.faith. The "one exception" is the point at issue: Ephrem affirms a surviving primeval tongue but leaves it anonymous. Yuliya Minets, surveying the writings she treats as authentic, concludes that "his story's primordial language remains nameless" — his genuine works are "surprisingly equivocal concerning the pre-Babel language."

Notes

  • The Cave of Treasures. The assertion that Syriac was the original tongue, older than Hebrew, comes from the Cave of Treasures — a history dated to the mid-sixth or early seventh century and ascribed to Ephrem, which Minov treats as pseudepigraphal. Sergey Minov (Memory and Identity in the Syriac Cave of Treasures, Leiden: Brill, 2021) situates that text in Syrian Orthodox circles of the period, and he and Minets locate there the popular claim that Ephrem made Syriac the first tongue. Minets finds that in Ephrem's own works "his story's primordial language remains nameless."
  • Placement on the timeline. Ephrem shares the common premise of the whole discussion — one original speech, broken at Babel — with both camps, while leaving the surviving tongue unnamed. He is therefore placed as a witness to the shared framework, with neither the Hebrew-primacy writers (Augustine, Isidore) nor the Aramaic-primacy writers (Theodore of Mopsuestia, Theodoret of Cyrus).
  • Source. Yuliya Minets, "The Primordial Language in Ephrem the Syrian," Hugoye: Journal of Syriac Studies 25:2 (2022), 313–363 (open-access copy); the passage quoted is Commentary on Genesis §8.3–8.4.
