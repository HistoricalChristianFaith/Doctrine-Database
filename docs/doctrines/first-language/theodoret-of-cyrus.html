Theodoret of Cyrus on the first language

Dates: c. 453 (Questions on the Octateuch; Theodoret c. 393–458/466) · Wikipedia
Summary: A native Syrian bishop who read Syriac, Theodoret gives Camp B its most developed patristic form: Syriac (Aramaic) is more ancient than Hebrew — Hebrew is the sacred tongue, but not the oldest. He rejects the derivation of "Hebrew" from the patriarch Heber, and derives the name instead from a word for "crossing over," making Abraham "the Hebrew" the crosser of the Euphrates. He is heir to Theodore of Mopsuestia and stands squarely against the Hebrew-primacy line of Augustine and Isidore.

Claims & evidence

Syriac/Aramaic is the more ancient tongue — Hebrew a later, sacred language given through Moses

In the Questions on Genesis Theodoret puts the antiquity question outright — Ποία γλῶσσα ἀρχαιοτέρα; ("Which language is the more ancient?") — and answers it from the primeval names of Genesis, which he reads as Aramaic: Adam, Cain, Abel and Noah, he says, belong "to the tongue of the Syrians" (τῆς Σύρων … γλώττης), "Adam" being from the Syriac word for red earth. The oldest names are Aramaic, so Aramaic is the older speech (§60). Hebrew comes later, and by gift: he holds it "a sacred tongue" (ἱερὰν … φωνήν) that the God of all "gave through Moses," a language "taught, not innate" (διδακτὴν οὖσαν, οὐ φυσικήν) — Hebrew children, he notes, do not speak it from birth but learn its letters as youths (§61). Holy, then, but not the primeval mother-language. That Theodoret worked from the Syriac and the Greek versions and knew no Hebrew is well established, and fits a reading that placed Syriac at the root. The Greek is edited in Patrologia Graeca 80, freely available online (Documenta Catholica Omnia); the copyrighted modern English (Hill & Petruccione, 2007) renders the same passage.

"Hebrew" is not from Heber — the Hebrews were not Eber's only descendants

Theodoret weighs and rejects the derivation of "Hebrew" from the patriarch Heber (Eber):

εἰ δὲ ἐκ τοῦ Ἕβερ ἑβραῖοι καλοῦνται, οὐ μόνους ἐχρῆν τούτους οὕτω προσαγορεύεσθαι· πολλὰ γὰρ ἔθνη ἐκ τοῦ Ἕβερ κατάγει τὸ γένος.

— "But if they are called Hebrews from Heber, these ought not to be the only ones so named; for many nations trace their descent from Heber" (§62; Greek in PG 80, Documenta Catholica Omnia) — and he lists them: the Ishmaelites, the sons of Keturah, the Edomites, Amalekites, Moabites, Ammonites, and the people of Carrhae (Harran). The same objection is summarized in the standard reference literature: "Theodoret (Quaest. in Genesis 61) urges against it that the Hebrews were not the only descendants of Eber, and, therefore, could not appropriate his name" (McClintock & Strong, Cyclopaedia of Biblical, Theological, and Ecclesiastical Literature, s.v. "Hebrew," biblicalcyclopedia.com). Where Camp A makes the patriarch Heber the eponym of the whole race and its tongue, Theodoret cuts the etymological hinge: many peoples descend from Eber, so his name cannot single out the Hebrews. This particular objection, though, over-argues: peoples are routinely named for one ancestor among many descendants — Israel was one line of Eber's with brothers to spare, and Ishmael and Esau no less than Israel descend from Abraham — so shared descent does not by itself void an eponym. The rival etymologies are weighed on their own argument page, which lands the question at contested: the Heber derivation is disputed, but so is Theodoret's "crossing over," and neither settles which tongue was primeval.

The name comes from "crossing over" — Abraham the Hebrew who crossed the Euphrates

In place of the Heber derivation Theodoret takes "Hebrew" from a word for crossing: "for hebra in the tongue of the Syrians is called a crossing" (ἑβρα γὰρ τῇ σύρων φωνῇ διάβασις ὀνομάζεται). The name is Abraham's — the patriarch who came from the land of the Chaldeans into Palestine "crossing the river Euphrates," whom Scripture calls "the crosser" (τῷ περάτῃ, Gen 14:13, LXX) — so "Hebrew" marks the crossing, not descent from Heber (Questions on Genesis §62). The same "one who passes over" sense that Eusebius had attached to Heber, Theodoret redirects to Abraham, detaching name and tongue from the Hebrew-primacy line. (The rival etymologies — Heber vs. "crosser" — are weighed on their own argument page.)

Notes

  • Text. Theodoret's Questions on Genesis §§60–62 are quoted here from the Greek of Patrologia Graeca 80, freely available online (Documenta Catholica Omnia); the modern critical English (Hill & Petruccione, 2007) is under copyright but renders the same passage. Editions differ slightly in numbering — the antiquity-of-language answer, the origin of Hebrew, and the "crossing" etymology fall in questions 60, 61, and 62 respectively.
  • Sacred, not oldest. Theodoret does not disparage Hebrew — he grants it is the holy tongue of Scripture. His point is chronological: Aramaic came first. This is the careful, sociolinguistic version of the thesis Theodore states more baldly.
