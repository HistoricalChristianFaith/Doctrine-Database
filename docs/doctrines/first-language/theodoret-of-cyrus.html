Theodoret of Cyrus on the first language

Dates: c. 453 (Questions on the Octateuch; Theodoret c. 393–458/466) · Wikipedia
Summary: A native Syrian bishop who read Syriac, Theodoret gives the fullest statement of Camp B on this timeline that survives in its author's own words: Syriac (Aramaic) is more ancient than Hebrew — Hebrew is the sacred tongue, but not the oldest. He rejects the derivation of "Hebrew" from the patriarch Heber, and derives the name instead from a word for "crossing over," making Abraham "the Hebrew" the crosser of the Euphrates. He shares the position of Theodore of Mopsuestia and stands against the Hebrew-primacy line of Augustine and Isidore.

Claims & evidence

Syriac/Aramaic is the more ancient tongue — Hebrew a later, sacred language given through Moses

In the Questions on Genesis Theodoret puts the antiquity question outright — Ποία γλῶσσα ἀρχαιοτέρα; ("Which language is the more ancient?") — and answers it from the primeval names of Genesis, which he reads as Aramaic: Adam, Cain, Abel and Noah, he says, belong "to the tongue of the Syrians" (τῆς Σύρων … γλώττης), "Adam" being from the Syriac word for red earth. The oldest names are Aramaic, so Aramaic is the older speech (§60). Hebrew comes later, and by gift: he holds it "a sacred tongue" (ἱερὰν … φωνήν) that the God of all "gave through Moses," a language "taught, not innate" (διδακτὴν οὖσαν, οὐ φυσικήν) — Hebrew children, he notes, do not speak it from birth but learn its letters as youths (§61). Holy, then, but not the primeval mother-language. Theodoret worked from the Syriac and the Greek versions and did not read Hebrew ⚠ unverified. The Greek is edited in Patrologia Graeca 80, freely available online (Documenta Catholica Omnia); the copyrighted modern English (Hill & Petruccione, 2007) renders the same passage.

"Hebrew" is not from Heber — the Hebrews were not Eber's only descendants

Theodoret rejects the derivation of "Hebrew" from the patriarch Heber (Eber):

εἰ δὲ ἐκ τοῦ Ἕβερ ἑβραῖοι καλοῦνται, οὐ μόνους ἐχρῆν τούτους οὕτω προσαγορεύεσθαι· πολλὰ γὰρ ἔθνη ἐκ τοῦ Ἕβερ κατάγει τὸ γένος.

— "But if they are called Hebrews from Heber, these ought not to be the only ones so named; for many nations trace their descent from Heber" (§62; Greek in PG 80, Documenta Catholica Omnia) — and he lists them: the Ishmaelites, the sons of Keturah, the Edomites, Amalekites, Moabites, Ammonites, and the people of Carrhae (Harran). McClintock & Strong summarize the same objection: "Theodoret (Quaest. in Genesis 61) urges against it that the Hebrews were not the only descendants of Eber, and, therefore, could not appropriate his name" (McClintock & Strong, Cyclopaedia of Biblical, Theological, and Ecclesiastical Literature, s.v. "Hebrew," biblicalcyclopedia.com). Where Camp A makes the patriarch Heber the eponym of the whole race and its tongue, Theodoret argues that many peoples descend from Eber, so his name cannot single out the Hebrews. McClintock & Strong answer the objection that one line may take an ancestor's name by pre-eminence: "Why might not one race of the descendants of Eber call themselves by pre-eminence sons of Eber, just as one race of the descendants of Abraham called themselves by preeminence sons of Abraham." The name comes from "crossing over" — Abraham the Hebrew who crossed the Euphrates

In place of the Heber derivation Theodoret takes "Hebrew" from a word for crossing: "for hebra in the tongue of the Syrians is called a crossing" (ἑβρα γὰρ τῇ σύρων φωνῇ διάβασις ὀνομάζεται). The name is Abraham's — the patriarch who came from the land of the Chaldeans into Palestine "crossing the river Euphrates," whom Scripture calls "the crosser" (τῷ περάτῃ, Gen 14:13, LXX) — so "Hebrew" marks the crossing, not descent from Heber (Questions on Genesis §62). Eusebius had attached the sense "one who passes over" to Heber; Theodoret attaches it to Abraham. (The rival derivations, Heber and "crosser," are set out on the disputed-reading page.)

Notes

  • Text. Theodoret's Questions on Genesis §§60–62 are quoted here from the Greek of Patrologia Graeca 80, freely available online (Documenta Catholica Omnia); the modern critical English (Hill & Petruccione, 2007) is under copyright but renders the same passage. Editions differ slightly in numbering — the antiquity-of-language answer, the origin of Hebrew, and the "crossing" etymology fall in questions 60, 61, and 62 respectively.
  • Sacred, not oldest. Theodoret calls Hebrew the holy tongue of Scripture; his claim is chronological, that Aramaic came first. Theodore's reported version makes Aramaic the speech God used with Adam.
  • Theodoret is also a witness on the Perpetual Virginity of Mary timeline.
