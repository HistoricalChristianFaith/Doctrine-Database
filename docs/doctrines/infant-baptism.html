Infant Baptism (paedobaptism)

Should the infant children of Christians be baptized, or does baptism belong only to those old enough to profess faith for themselves? The two axes on which views divide are who is a fit subject for baptism (a professing believer only, or a household including its children) and when it should be given (as early as possible, or deferred until a person can ask for it). Baby dedication — presenting an unbaptized child for blessing — is a subset of the same question. The witnesses below run earliest first.

Timeline

The Didache (The Teaching of the Twelve Apostles, c. 100)

The Didache, the earliest surviving Christian church order, preserves the oldest extant baptismal rubric, and it describes the rite without raising the age of its subjects. Coming to baptism only after the “Two Ways” catechesis (chapters 1–6), it directs: “concerning baptism, thus baptize ye: Having first said all these things, baptize into the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit, in living water… But before the baptism let the baptizer fast, and the baptized… one or two days before.”1 The rite it describes fits an instructed, professing candidate, one who has been taught and who can fast. Kurt Aland pressed such pre-200 descriptions of professing candidates against Joachim Jeremias as the original norm; Philip Schaff held that the Didache’s silence about infants “cannot be fairly used as an argument against” infant baptism; see How far back does the earliest evidence trace infant baptism? It also allows pouring “thrice upon the head” where immersion-water is lacking, a provision cited in later debate over the mode of baptism.2

Justin Martyr (the First Apology & Dialogue with Trypho, c. 155)

Justin gives the fullest surviving pre-Tertullian account of Christian baptism, and, like the Didache before him, he is cited by both sides. His set-piece description of the rite portrays instructed adults: those who “are persuaded and believe that what we teach… is true” are, after fasting and prayer for the remission of past sins, “brought by us where there is water, and are regenerated” — a description Kurt Aland pressed, with the Didache’s, against Joachim Jeremias (see the Didache entry above).3 Jeremias adduced Justin’s mention of believers “who have been Christ’s disciples from childhood” and remain pure at sixty or seventy (First Apology 15) as an early hint of children among the baptized; Aland read it as describing Christians raised in the faith who professed and were baptized when able. Justin also frames baptism as the “spiritual circumcision” that replaces the carnal one (Dialogue 43), the earliest extant equation of baptism with circumcision. He does not say whether believers’ children were brought to the rite.4

Irenaeus of Lyons (c. 180)

Irenaeus never discusses the baptism of infants directly, but in a famous sentence of Against Heresies II.22.4 he says Christ came to save "all… who through Him are born again to God — infants, and children, and boys, and youths, and old men."5 Because Irenaeus elsewhere defines baptism as "the new birth unto God,"6 Joachim Jeremias read the sentence as evidence for the practice, which would make Irenaeus the earliest extant, if implicit, witness to infant baptism, a generation before Tertullian; Kurt Aland judged it not decisive, and Everett Ferguson, who allows that it “could be the earliest reference to infant baptism,” stresses its setting in Irenaeus's argument for recapitulation (Christ sanctifying every age by living through it). Whether it documents the practice or states that the incarnation sanctifies infancy is disputed; see Does Irenaeus's "born again to God" attest infant baptism?7

Tertullian (c. 203)

Tertullian’s is the earliest extant explicit discussion of the baptism of little children — and he opposes it, holding that baptism is better delayed: weighing "the circumstances and disposition, and even age" of each person, he judges "the delay of baptism… preferable; principally, however, in the case of little children."8 His reasons are the risk to the sponsors who make the promises on the child's behalf, and his sense that the "innocent period of life" need not "hasten to the remission of sins" — better to let children "become Christians when they have become able to know Christ."9 His objection is also the earliest extant evidence that infants were being brought for baptism: he argues against a custom already practised in North Africa by c. 200. How much older that custom is, and whether Tertullian was resisting an inherited practice or a spreading one, is disputed; see How far back does the earliest evidence trace infant baptism?10

Hippolytus of Rome (the Apostolic Tradition, c. 215)

Where Tertullian argues against the custom, the Apostolic Tradition — the church order traditionally ascribed to Hippolytus — prescribes it. Setting out the order of the paschal baptism, §21 directs: "first baptize the little ones; if they can speak for themselves, they shall do so; if not, their parents or other relatives shall speak for them," and only then the men and the women.11 Taken at face value this is the earliest extant descriptive witness to infant baptism — infants baptized first, with sponsors answering the credal questions on their behalf — set out as part of the order of the rite, a decade after Tertullian had opposed the practice. The church historian David F. Wright notes that this earliest attestation of infant baptism as the normal practice takes “the form of an instruction how to baptize—first those who can answer for themselves and then those who cannot.”12 The document’s date and authorship are disputed: its Greek original is lost, it survives mainly in a fourth-century Latin version, and Paul Bradshaw, Maxwell Johnson and L. Edward Phillips treat it as a composite church order layered over generations rather than one author's work of c. 215, so that on their reading the passage cannot be dated to a single year.13

Origen (c. 248)

Origen is the earliest extant writer to call infant baptism a custom the Church received from the apostles. Commenting on Romans 6:6, he asks why the Law commands a sin offering for a newborn (Leviticus 12) — how can an infant have sinned? — and answers: “the church has received a tradition from the apostles to baptize even infants,” because the apostles “knew that there are real stains of sin in everyone which must be cleansed by water and the Spirit.”14 He refers to the practice elsewhere too: baptism “is given even to infants” by “the usage of the Church” (Homilies on Leviticus 8.3), and in Homilies on Luke 14 he reasons from the custom — if little children are baptized “for the remission of sins,” the “stains of birth” must be what the rite puts aside.15 Two points bear on the much-cited sentence: “5.9” is the commentary's section, not Romans 5:9, and that commentary survives for these books only in the Latin of Rufinus, who says he abridged as he translated; the separately transmitted homilies on Leviticus and Luke also attest the practice.16

Cyprian of Carthage (c. 253)

Cyprian supplies the earliest conciliar witness. A bishop named Fidus had proposed that a newborn wait until the eighth day for baptism, on the analogy of Old-Testament circumcision; Cyprian, writing for a council of sixty-six African bishops, reports that they rejected the delay unanimously — “in this course which you thought was to be taken, no one agreed; but we all rather judge that the mercy and grace of God is not to be refused to any one born of man.”17 The letter does not dispute whether infants are baptized (Fidus assumes it too), only when. The eighth-day type is answered as a shadow fulfilled in Christ, and the newborn — who “has not sinned” but has “contracted the contagion of the ancient death” from Adam — is to be baptized without delay, so that “how much rather ought we to shrink from hindering an infant.”18 In this African synod of c. 253, a generation after Origen and about fifty years after Tertullian argued for delay, the timing of infant baptism was the question in dispute.

Cyril of Jerusalem (the Catechetical Lectures, c. 350) — the adult catechumenate

Cyril’s Jerusalem catechesis gives the fullest surviving picture of a fourth-century baptismal preparation — and, like the Didache and Justin before him, he is cited by both sides. Every stage he describes presupposes a candidate old enough to answer for himself: enrollment, exorcism, a forty-day Lenten repentance — “thou hast forty days for repentance… But if thou persist in an evil purpose… the water will receive, but the Spirit will not accept thee” — and a personal profession at the font.19 He puts faith and repentance before the water: “Make ready the vessels of the soul, cleansed by unfeigned faith… Begin at once to wash your robes in repentance,” so that baptism works “in order that, the soul having been born again by faith, the body also might by the water partake of the grace.”20 He nowhere discusses infants; the witness is to what the adult road to the font looked like in mid-century Jerusalem. On how such descriptions of professing candidates are read, see the Didache entry above.

Basil of Caesarea (the Great, On the Holy Spirit & the Exhortation to Holy Baptism, c. 375) — baptized as an adult

Basil — eldest of the three Cappadocian Fathers and Gregory of Nyssa’s brother — was born into a devout Christian family yet himself baptized only as an adult (c. 357, on his return from Athens), an instance of the fourth-century deferral of baptism, and he speaks of that baptism as “the beginning of life, and that day of regeneration the first of days.”21 His baptismal theology puts confession before baptism: “Faith and baptism are two kindred and inseparable ways of salvation… first comes the confession, introducing us to salvation, and baptism follows, setting the seal upon our assent.”22 Yet his Exhortation to Holy Baptism is aimed not at infant baptism but at adult procrastination — grown catechumens, some “instructed in the divine word from… infancy” yet still unbaptized in old age: “when will you become a Christian?” he presses, for “the whole period of man’s life is the time for baptism.”23 He nowhere argues against the baptism of children; the delay he attacks is the adult’s, and the witness is to the fourth-century adult road to the font. Kurt Aland read such deferrals as showing that infant baptism had not yet become the norm; Joachim Jeremias, as a temporary decline from an earlier custom; see Do the spread of infant baptism and the patristic delay of baptism show whether the practice was apostolic?

Gregory of Nazianzus (c. 381)

Gregory, preaching On Holy Baptism at Constantinople, is a fourth-century Eastern witness that child baptism was accepted, with the discussion, as at Carthage, turning on timing. He tells parents not to fear consecrating even an infant: “let him be sanctified from his childhood; from his very tenderest age let him be consecrated by the Spirit… O what a small-souled mother, and of how little faith!”24 Facing the objection directly — “those who are still children… are we to baptize them too?” — he answers “Certainly, if any danger presses,” better “unconsciously sanctified than… unsealed and uninitiated”; and for the ordinary case (no danger) he counsels waiting “till the end of the third year, or a little more or less,” when a child can “answer something about the Sacrament.”25 The Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers editor reads these directions as showing that “S. Gregory did not reject infant Baptism.”26 David F. Wright cites the same counsel for the baptism of young children able to “answer something about the sacrament”; how the fourth-century counsels of delay bear on the practice’s origin is disputed (see the interpretation page).27

Gregory of Nyssa (On the Baptism of Christ, c. 383) — the adult catechumenate

Basil’s younger brother, the third Cappadocian, preaching on the Feast of Lights to a congregation of the baptized and the “uninitiated” catechumens still awaiting the font, frames baptism as a conscious regeneration — and, like Cyril and Basil, describes the ordinary adult road to baptism. His imagery assumes a grown subject with a moral history to be cleansed: baptism brings “him who bears the scars of sin, and has grown old in evil habits, to the innocence of the babe.”28 The rite turns on a profession the candidate makes for himself — “not receiving the sacrament in silence, but while there are spoken over us the Names of the Three Sacred Persons on Whom we believed” — and its reality is proved by the change of life that follows, the mark of “the newly-baptized man, who remembers his own profession, which he made to God.”29 He nowhere discusses infants; where his fellow Cappadocian Nazianzus does address children directly (emergency infant baptism without hesitation, elective baptism about age three), Nyssa describes and exhorts the professing-adult catechumenate — silent on infants, like the Didache and Justin, and part of the fourth-century pattern of adult baptism discussed in Do the spread of infant baptism and the patristic delay of baptism show whether the practice was apostolic?

Ambrose of Milan (c. 387)

Ambrose, bishop of Milan — and the man who baptized Augustine at the Milan font at Easter 387 — is a Western witness, a few years before Augustine, to the doctrine that underlies the practice. In De Abraham he expounds the command to circumcise the eight-day-old infant (Genesis 17) as a type of baptism, reasoning that no age is exempt from the sacrament because no age is exempt from sin: neither the aged proselyte nor the native-born infant is excluded, since every age is liable to sin and therefore fit for the sacrament.30 He clinches it from John 3:5, refusing to write any exception into Christ’s universal rule: “Unless a man be born again… No one is excepted, not the infant, not the one prevented by some necessity.”31 The witness is chiefly to the necessity and fittingness of infant baptism, argued from the circumcision type Cyprian had used, rather than an explicit description of the rite; and on the infant who dies unbaptized Ambrose, unlike Augustine (see his entry), allows a possible exemption from punishment but professing not to know “whether they have the honor of the kingdom.” Ambrose grounds the rite in sin reaching into infancy; for the rationale Chrysostom gives a few years later, and the readings of it, see the next entry.31

John Chrysostom (c. 390)

Chrysostom, preaching to catechumens at Antioch, takes infant baptism entirely for granted and turns it into an argument about the richness of the sacrament: because many suppose baptism gives nothing but the remission of sins, he points to infants as proof that it gives much more. “It is on this account that we baptize even infants, although they are sinless, that they may be given the further gifts of sanctification, justice, filial adoption, and inheritance, that they may be brothers and members of Christ, and become dwelling places for the Spirit.”32 The custom appears incidentally, as a settled premise rather than the thing to be proved, placing infant baptism in the fourth-century East as at Cyprian’s Carthage or Gregory’s Cappadocia. His rationale is read in more than one way: he calls infants “sinless” and does not list the remission of sins among what their baptism confers, naming the gifts of grace instead. Augustine read “sinless” as referring to personal sins; Antoine Wenger saw in the omission “some foundation for the position that Chrysostom considered infants free from all sins, actual and original.”33 John Meyendorff (Orthodox, 1974) places Chrysostom in a Greek tradition that sees the inheritance from Adam as mortality rather than guilt, and sets the Eastern account of infant baptism, in which the Church baptizes children “in order to give them a new and immortal life,” against the Western framing of Cyprian’s “contagion of the ancient death” and Augustine’s argument from “the remission of sins.”34

The Council of Carthage (the foundling canon, September 401)

A generation before its more famous anti-Pelagian namesake, a North African council meeting at Carthage on 13 September 401 — convened chiefly to soften the church’s stance toward the Donatists — left an incidental witness on infant baptism from the pastoral side. Africa’s bishops regularly took in abandoned children ransomed from barbarian raiders, and could not always know whether such a child had ever been baptized; the synod’s seventh canon ruled that where no reliable witnesses could confirm it and the children were unable, “on account of their tender age,” to answer for themselves, “all such children should be baptized without scruple, lest a hesitation should deprive them of the cleansing of the sacraments.”35 The ruling never argues that infants ought to be baptized — it assumes it throughout: the children in view cannot “answer concerning the giving of the sacraments to them,” yet the whole anxiety is that a doubtful case might leave one unbaptized, the doubtful re-baptism reckoned the lesser danger. Like Cyprian’s council of c. 253 (which settled when) and unlike the later Council of Carthage of 418 (which fixed why), this one touches only an individual case’s certainty — the infant’s fitness for the font being the unquestioned premise beneath all three.36

Jerome (c. 403)

Jerome shifts the register from whether or when to baptize to the parental duty to do so. Writing to Laeta (Letter 107, A.D. 403) on how to raise her infant daughter for Christ, he warns that the guilt of a withheld baptism falls on the parents: “perhaps you imagine that, if they are not baptized, the children of Christians are liable for their own sins; and that no guilt attaches to parents who withhold from baptism those who by reason of their tender age can offer no objection to it. The truth is that, as baptism ensures the salvation of the child, this in turn brings advantage to the parents.”37 Like Origen and Augustine, he presupposes the practice — the infant is a fit subject without discussion, and the only live question is the parents’ faithfulness in bringing her, so the witness is to the settled custom more than an argument for it. A decade or so later, in his Dialogue Against the Pelagians (c. 415), he makes that presupposed practice bear doctrinal weight: pressed “why little children are baptized,” his speaker Atticus answers “that their sins may be forgiven them,” grounds it in inherited Adamic guilt (Romans 5:14 — “he that is an infant is released in baptism from the chain which bound his father”), and marshals Cyprian’s letter to Fidus and Augustine’s treatises on infant baptism in support — the Western original-sin frame, with Jerome now a contemporary ally of Augustine against Pelagius.38

Augustine of Hippo (c. 412)

Augustine sets out the practice’s theological rationale at length. Against the Donatists he treats infant baptism as the settled custom of the whole Church — “the firm tradition of the universal Church, in respect of the baptism of infants” — and lays down the rule that “what is held by the whole Church, and that not as instituted by Councils, but as a matter of invariable custom, is rightly held to have been handed down by apostolical authority.”39 Against the Pelagians he makes that universal practice the central argument for original sin: the Church everywhere baptizes infants for the remission of sins, yet infants commit no actual sins of their own, so — “inasmuch as infants are not held bound by any sins of their own actual life, it is the guilt of original sin which is healed in them by the grace of Him who saves them by the laver of regeneration.”40 In Letter 98 to Boniface he explains how a speechless infant is nonetheless truly baptized — a believer “through the sacrament of that faith,” his sponsors answering for him, delivered “from that condemnation which by one man entered into the world.”41 Augustine joins two arguments found in earlier witnesses, the antiquity and universality of the custom and its link to inherited sin (Cyprian’s “contagion of the ancient death”), and the practice, not itself in dispute between Augustine and the Pelagians, is the premise from which he argues the doctrine. Augustine grounds the rite in guilt to be washed away; on the different rationale in Chrysostom’s preaching, and the readings of it, see the Chrysostom entry above.40

Pelagius (c. 415)

Pelagius — Augustine’s principal opponent, the man who denied that Adam’s guilt is inherited — is a witness from the opposing side of the controversy: he conceded the practice outright and disputed its meaning. His own confession of faith grants it in the plainest terms — infants “ought to be baptized for the remission of sins, according to the rule of the Church universal, and according to the meaning of the Gospel, we confess.”42 What he would not grant is that the sin so remitted is original: his disciple Coelestius said openly that the concession “was not admitted… with the view of our seeming to affirm sin by transmission,” and the positive Pelagian reading — a “novel opinion” that scandalized Augustine at Carthage — was “that infants are not baptized for the purpose of receiving remission of sin, but that they may be sanctified in Christ.”43 He did not deny the rite on which Augustine built his argument; the controversy of the early fifth century turned on why infants were baptized, not whether. (The Encyclopedia of Ancient Christianity (2014) is cited for the view that Pelagius opposed the practice; G. F. Wiggers and Philip Schaff read his confession as retaining it while denying its original-sin rationale; see his detail page.)

The Council of Milevum (the anti-Pelagian synod of Numidia, 416)

Two years before its more famous sequel at Carthage, the North-African church condemned the Pelagian re-reading of infant baptism at a pair of provincial synods in the autumn of 416 — one at Carthage, and one at Milevum (Mileve, in Numidia, Augustine’s own province), where some sixty bishops under the primate Silvanus rejected the doctrines of Pelagius and Coelestius as contrary to the catholic faith.44 As at every stage of the controversy, the practice of baptizing infants was never the question — only its meaning: the synods, and the accompanying letter of five bishops (Augustine among them) to Pope Innocent I, set out the doctrine of original sin, infant baptism, and grace, and Innocent, replying on 27 January 417, confirmed their decisions, rejected the Pelagian theory of infant baptism, and excommunicated Pelagius and Coelestius — the condemnation the plenary Council of Carthage of 418 then made conciliar law (next entry). Milevum’s own act was this synodal letter, not a canon of its own: the familiar anathema on any who deny that “infants fresh from their mothers’ wombs” should be baptized — sometimes cited as “Milevum, canon 3” — is the second canon of the 418 Carthage synod, filed under Milevum’s name only because a later canon-collection grouped the two councils’ anti-Pelagian canons under one joint heading.45

The Council of Carthage (the anti-Pelagian canons, 418)

Where Cyprian’s council of 253 had settled the timing of infant baptism, the North African synods of the next century and a half fixed its rationale as binding doctrine — the conciliar counterpart to Augustine and the formal answer to Pelagius. A plenary council of more than two hundred African bishops, meeting at Carthage on 1 May 418 — the culmination of the anti-Pelagian councils of Carthage and Mileve (Milevum) in 416, whose synodal letters Pope Innocent I had confirmed — decreed in its second canon that “whosoever denies that infants newly from their mother’s wombs should be baptized, or says that baptism is for the remission of sins, but that they derive from Adam no original sin, which needs to be removed by the laver of regeneration… let him be anathema,” grounding the rule in Romans 5:12 as “the Catholic Church everywhere diffused has always understood it,” so that “even infants, who could have committed as yet no sin themselves, therefore are truly baptized for the remission of sins.”46 The canon condemns both errors Pelagius’s circle had floated — that the newborn need not be baptized at all, and (the live Pelagian claim) that they are baptized while carrying no inherited Adamic guilt — welding the practice, never itself in dispute, to the original-sin doctrine as a test of communion. It is Augustine’s argument from the baptismal liturgy made a conciliar canon; a further canon denying unbaptized infants a happy “middle place” outside the kingdom is printed in some editions and omitted in others, being absent from the early canon-collections of Dionysius and Isidore; nothing here rests on it.47

Peter of Bruys (the Petrobrusians, c. 1131) — a twelfth-century opponent

Some seven centuries after the North African councils, Peter of Bruys, an itinerant preacher in Provence and Languedoc, rejected the baptism of infants, the first of the “errors” his opponents catalogued. He left no writings; his teaching survives in Peter the Venerable’s Contra Petrobrusianos (c. 1139–1140), whose lead charge is that among the Petrobrusians “the baptism of persons before they have reached the years of discretion is invalid,” their believers’ baptism resting on Mark 16:16, with those baptized in infancy re-baptized on coming to faith.48 In N. A. Weber’s summary, baptism was for them “a necessary condition for salvation, but… preceded by personal faith, so that its administration to infants is worthless”: a faith-precedes-baptism case read off Mark 16:16, the verse Menno also cited (c. 1539). The Petrobrusians insisted that they did not re-baptize, the infant washing having been no true baptism, as the sixteenth-century Anabaptists also said of their own practice. It differs from that of the roughly contemporary dualist Cathars, who spurned water baptism as a thing of evil matter: Peter kept baptism and made it a believer’s rite. His is a recorded challenge to infant baptism four centuries before the Reformation.49

Petr Chelčický and the Unity of the Brethren (c. 1443) — dissent without rebaptism

A century before the Anabaptists, the Bohemian (Hussite) reformation produced a position in which faith should precede baptism without excluding infant baptism. The lay theologian Petr Chelčický made unforced personal faith central — “by the use of force no man is brought to faith in Christ” — and preferred that baptism follow it, yet he neither forbade infant baptism nor demanded rebaptism.50 His heirs, the Unity of the Brethren (Unitas Fratrum, organized at Kunvald in 1457), went further in practice: they “rebaptized converts… because they considered personal faith an essential condition of the baptismal covenant… But they did not on this account reject infant baptism. Children were baptized soon after their birth… then… rebaptized… when old enough to exercise personal faith” — a middle position (infant baptism and adult rebaptism on profession) that they “relinquished by a formal act of the General Synod of 1534,” substituting confirmation, partly to distance themselves from the Anabaptists.51 The position held that faith should precede the font; the Brethren half-institutionalized it and then gave it up at the moment the Swiss and Dutch radicals were raising adult baptism as their banner.

Andreas Bodenstein von Karlstadt (the abolition of infant baptism at Orlamünde, c. 1524) — a middle position

Before the Swiss radicals acted, Luther’s own early Wittenberg colleague had already moved — a step short of them. Karlstadt is the earliest of the magisterial-generation Reformers on record to reject infant baptism: at his parish of Orlamünde (1523–1524) he stopped baptizing infants, though “unlike the Anabaptists” he “spoke only incidentally” against the rite, classing it with circumcision as an “outward thing,” and holding “that it was better to postpone baptism until the candidate was sure of his faith.”52 His fullest statement, the Dialogus… vom Tauff der Kinder (Worms, 1527), argues from its very title that infant baptism rests on a fremder Glaube — an “alien faith,” the sponsors’ or the Church’s, and not the child’s own — the objection Luther answered in 1529 by grounding baptism in God’s Word rather than the recipient’s faith. Yet — distinctively — Karlstadt neither rebaptized adults christened as infants nor was rebaptized himself, resembling in this the magisterial Reformers who held their Roman baptism valid: a stance between Zwingli, who defended the paedobaptist practice, and the Swiss Brethren, who made adult rebaptism their banner. Conrad Grebel wrote to him in 1524 and Felix Manz helped print his eucharistic tracts, so his writings reached the men who rebaptized one another the following January — the reason a standard study is titled Karlstadt as the Father of the Baptist Movements, though he stopped short of founding one.53

Wilhelm Reublin (Röubli, a Zürich-area pastor preaching against infant baptism, c. 1524)

Before the founding act came the agitation that led to it. Reublin, a priest at Witikon in the villages around Zürich, was — on the record — the earliest of the Swiss radicals to move against the font: the reference tradition calls him “the first in the canton of Zürich… to preach against the baptism of infants,” and reports that he “began to call in question the Scriptural authority and the propriety of infant baptism.”54 It was acted on: “acting on his advice several parents withheld their infants from christening and incurred severe punishment therefor,” the refusal spreading from Witikon to Zollikon until the Zürich council intervened in August 1524 — fining the refusers, briefly imprisoning Reublin, and warning him to desist. He was an antipedobaptist speaker at the disputation of 17 January 1525, and was expelled from the canton with Conrad Grebel and Felix Manz four days before the Swiss Brethren’s first believers’ baptism, at which he was present.55 His witness is an act before it is a text — like the rebaptism a few months later, it enacts the credobaptist “No” (the infant no fit subject of the font) before any confession or treatise spelled it out — and it was he who carried believers’ baptism outward, baptizing Hubmaier and some sixty others at Waldshut about Easter 1525. He left no surviving tract of his own on baptism: an agitator and evangelist, not a systematic writer, he broke with Zwingli over the same refusal to let the council set the pace of reform.

The Swiss Brethren (Conrad Grebel, George Blaurock & Felix Manz — the believers’ baptism of 21 January 1525)

Between the confessions and treatises that argued the credobaptist case and the patristic paedobaptism they rejected stands the act that began the movement: the believers’ baptism from which Anabaptism dates its origin. When the Zürich council, after the disputation of 17 January 1525, ordered every unbaptized infant baptized within the week on pain of expulsion — a decree aimed at Zwingli’s former associates, who had refused to present their children (Conrad Grebel had left his newborn daughter Rachel unbaptized) — the radicals gathered on 21 January at the house of Felix Manz’s mother. The Chronicle of the Hutterian Brethren records what followed: after prayer, “George of the house of Jacob [George Blaurock] stood up and besought Conrad Grebel for God’s sake to baptize him with the true Christian baptism upon his faith and knowledge… Conrad baptized him, since at that time there was no ordained minister to perform such work,” and Blaurock in turn baptized the rest.56 The deed makes a believer’s own “faith and knowledge” the thing baptism answers to — which no infant can bring — and so enacts the exclusion that Schleitheim confessed in 1527 and that Hubmaier (1525), Menno (c. 1539) and Smyth (1609) argued at length. It did not come from nowhere: Wilhelm Reublin had been preaching against infant baptism in the villages around Zürich since about 1523, and baptized Hubmaier at Waldshut that Easter.57 Grebel (c. 1498–1526), Manz (c. 1498–1527, drowned in the Limmat, the first Anabaptist executed by a Protestant government), and Blaurock (c. 1491–1529, burned) all died within a few years — but the gathering of that January night stands at the head of the Swiss Brethren, and behind them the Mennonites, Hutterites, and Amish.

Huldrych Zwingli (Of Baptism, 1525) — the covenant sign

Zwingli wrote the earliest magisterial-Reformation defence of infant baptism against the emerging Anabaptists, and the earliest to ground it in the covenant. When his own former associates among the Zürich radicals began rebaptizing one another in January 1525, he answered them in Of Baptism (Von der Taufe, May 1525) — the booklet Hubmaier refuted that July — and more fully in his Refutation of the Tricks of the Catabaptists (1527), where he argues from covenant standing rather than any act of the child’s: “The children of Christians are no less sons of God than the parents, just as in the Old Testament. Hence, since they are sons of God, who will forbid their baptism?”58 The backbone is the circumcision parallel: “Circumcision among the ancients (so far as it was sacramental) was the same as baptism with us. As that was given to infants so ought baptism to be administered to infants” — baptism now doing what circumcision once did, so the believer’s infant is its natural subject.59 Against the Brethren’s demand for prior faith, Zwingli reverses the order of dependence: one belongs to the covenant body first and the sign is added to a membership already held, so an infant is “not excluded because they cannot hear or understand… being of the body precedes bearing the mark of the body.” The sign seals covenant membership; it does not create it — and so, he concludes, Scripture does not require the recipient’s prior profession.60

Balthasar Hubmaier (On the Christian Baptism of Believers, 1525)

Two years before Schleitheim put the credobaptist position into a confession, Hubmaier — the Waldshut pastor and doctor of theology — had already argued it at treatise length. His On the Christian Baptism of Believers (1525), written in five days against Zwingli and judged by the historian James Stayer the most effective Reformation-era defence of believers’ baptism, defines the rite so as to require a personal confession no infant can make: to baptize “is to pour outward water over the confessor of his sins… and to inscribe him in the number of sinners upon his own confession and acknowledgment.”61 Against Zwingli he reads off Scripture a fixed order — “the word, hearing, faith, baptism, works” — and concludes that “since infants cannot be taught, they should not be baptised”; to baptize a child as a future believer, he says, “is like hanging out a hoop as a sign of future wine.”62 Two credobaptist arguments that recur later on this timeline are already present here: the Great Commission read as a teach-then-baptize sequence that bars the un-teachable infant — the case Smyth put as a syllogism in 1609 — and the broader apostolic order of hearing and believing before the font. His emblem was “Truth is immortal.” At Zürich in 1525–26 he recanted, withdrew the recantation, and after imprisonment and the rack made a further submission before being expelled; at Vienna he sent King Ferdinand a statement of his beliefs (January 1528) that some of his biographers have called a recantation, though in it, Henry Vedder notes, he still held to his belief about the sacraments.63 He was burned at Vienna on 10 March 1528, his wife Elsbeth drowned in the Danube three days later.64

The Schleitheim Confession (Michael Sattler & the Swiss Brethren, 1527)

The Schleitheim Confession is the earliest confession on this timeline to reject infant baptism outright, treating as a question of whether a child is a subject of baptism what the patristic witnesses above had discussed as a question of when. Its opening article restricts baptism to those “who have learned repentance and amendment of life, and who believe truly that their sins are taken away by Christ… and who with this significance request it… and demand it for themselves. This excludes all infant baptism, the highest and chief abomination of the pope.”65 By making repentance, personal faith, and a conscious request — none of them possible for a newborn — constitutive of a valid baptism, the article denies that an infant can be baptized in any true sense, appealing to the apostolic pattern of preaching-then-belief (Matthew 28, Mark 16, Acts 2, 8, 16, 19).66 Adopted by a gathering of Swiss Brethren at Schleitheim on 24 February 1527 and now credited chiefly to the ex-Benedictine prior Michael Sattler — burned at the stake three months later — it is the earliest confessional statement on this timeline of the credobaptist (believers’-baptism) position, breaking with the Catholic Church and with the magisterial Reformers Luther, Zwingli, and Calvin, who all retained the infant baptism attested in the patristic witnesses above, as the Orthodox churches also did.67,68

Martin Luther (the Large Catechism, 1529)

Two years after Schleitheim, Luther answers the Anabaptist objection directly in the fourth part of the Large Catechism, defending infant baptism, the practice attested in the patristic witnesses above. He argues first from its fruits: “that the Baptism of infants is pleasing to Christ is sufficiently proved from His own work, namely, that God sanctifies many of them who have been thus baptized, and has given them the Holy Ghost” — naming Bernard, Gerson, and Hus as men of undeniable sanctity whose only baptism was in infancy, for God “can never be opposed to Himself… or for its promotion impart His grace and Spirit” to a rite He rejected.69 But his central move relocates the ground of a valid baptism away from the recipient’s faith and onto God’s Word: “everything depends upon the Word and command of God… Baptism is valid, even though faith be wanting. For my faith does not make Baptism, but receives it.”70 Where Schleitheim had made repentance, faith, and a personal request constitutive of baptism — and so excluded the newborn — Luther makes the Word constitutive and faith receptive, answering the objection that an infant without faith cannot be baptized. The rationale is neither the Western original-sin argument of Augustine nor the gifts of grace Chrysostom names, but a sacramental one about what makes a baptism.70

John Calvin (the Institutes, 1536) — the covenant seal

Calvin defends paedobaptism at length in the Institutes. He first answers the Anabaptist argument from silence: that no infant is named as baptized by the apostles settles nothing, he argues, for “if such kinds of argument were good, it would be necessary, in like manner, to interdict women from the Lord’s Supper, since we do not read that they were ever admitted to it in the days of the apostles” — and he reads the baptized households of Acts (16:15, 16:32) as including their children. The claim that paedobaptism went unknown for years after the resurrection he calls “a shameful falsehood, since there is no writer, however ancient, who does not trace its origin to the days of the apostles” — the same apostolic-antiquity appeal Origen and Augustine had made.71 His constructive ground, though, is neither antiquity nor (with Luther) the Word that makes the sacrament, but the covenant: baptism is “the impress of a seal” confirming the promise that the Lord will be “a God not to him only, but to his seed” (Genesis 17), so a believer’s child already stands within the covenant the sign seals and must not be denied it.72 This covenantal framing — baptism as the new-covenant successor to circumcision — is the ground Zwingli had given in 1525 (above).72

Menno Simons (the Foundation of Christian Doctrine, c. 1539)

Menno Simons — the ex-priest whose followers became the Mennonites — is the Dutch codifier of the believers’-baptism position Hubmaier had argued and Schleitheim had confessed before him. In the baptism section of his Foundation of Christian Doctrine he fixes a scriptural order in which faith always precedes the font — “he that believeth and is baptized, shall be saved… Faith is before baptism” — and rests it on three pillars: “First, because of the divine commandment of our beloved Lord Jesus Christ, which can never be broken. Secondly, because of the teaching of the holy apostles. Thirdly, because of the practice of these same apostles.”73 From that order he excludes infants directly: because a child “cannot be made to understand any thing visible,” still less can it be “taught and instructed in invisible, celestial matters of the Spirit,” so to baptize it is to invert Christ’s appointed sequence and pervert the ordinance.74 Where Schleitheim had made repentance, faith, and a personal request constitutive of baptism, Menno reaches the same exclusion through the un-teachability of infancy — the broad faith-precedes-baptism case, with the appeal to Christ’s commission (Mark 16); Smyth put the Great-Commission argument as a syllogism in 1609. His followers took the name Mennonites; on baptism his case follows the order Hubmaier and Schleitheim had already set out.75

John Smyth (The Character of the Beast, 1609)

Smyth carries the credobaptist case from Schleitheim’s confessional assertion to a formal argument from the Great Commission. An English Separatist minister exiled at Amsterdam — and, holding his own infant baptism void, the self-baptizing “Se-Baptist” who gathered what is generally reckoned the first English Baptist church — he reasons against infant baptism from Matthew 28:19: “They only are to be baptized that are made Disciples by teaching. Infants cannot be made Disciples by teaching. Ergo, Infants are not to be baptized.”76 He then presses it from probability into prohibition: “Every precept affirmative conteyneth a negative vnder it,” so the command to “make Disciples by teaching & baptise them” itself forbids baptizing “those that are not made Disciples by teaching… & so infants are not to be baptized.”77 Where Schleitheim had made repentance, faith, and a personal request constitutive of baptism, Smyth grounds the exclusion in the order of the commission — teaching must precede baptism, and an infant cannot be taught — so that the commission itself becomes a bar against the practice. His syllogism states in formal terms the reading that the order of Matthew 28:19 (make disciples, then baptize) excludes infants.

Thomas Helwys (A Declaration of Faith of English People Remaining at Amsterdam, 1611) — the Spitalfields congregation

Helwys plants Smyth’s believers’-baptism principle in English soil and fixes it in a confession. His 1611 Declaration of Faith, the earliest Baptist confession, makes the exclusion of infants a standing article: baptism is received “upon the Confession of their faith and sins… according to the primitive Institution (Matthew 28:19) and practice (Acts 2:41),” and, being “the outward manifestation of dieing unto sin, and walking in newness of life (Romans 6),… therefore in no wise appertains to infants.”78 A Nottinghamshire gentleman who had funded the congregation’s exile, Helwys broke with Smyth when Smyth repudiated his own self-baptism and sought union with the Waterlander Mennonites, led a remnant of about a dozen back to England, and c. 1611–1612 gathered at Spitalfields (London) what is generally reckoned the first Baptist church on English ground — a General (Arminian) Baptist congregation.79 Where Smyth had reasoned the exclusion from the order of the Great Commission, Helwys does not add a new syllogism so much as institutionalize the position — resting it on the same proof-texts (Matthew 28:19; the Pentecost pattern of Acts 2:41, where confession precedes the font — the faith-precedes-baptism case) and on the Romans 6 death-and-resurrection symbolism a newborn cannot enact. His Short Declaration of the Mistery of Iniquity (1612) then couples believers’ baptism to the earliest extant English plea for total liberty of conscience — a stand that cost him imprisonment and death in Newgate.

John Spilsbury (A Treatise Concerning the Lawfull Subject of Baptisme, 1643, and the First London Baptist Confession, 1644) — the Particular Baptists and the recovery of immersion

Where Smyth’s self-baptism (1609) began the Arminian General Baptists who baptized by affusion, Spilsbury marks a distinct node in the credobaptist “No”: the Calvinistic (Particular) strand and the recovery of believers’ baptism by immersion. A London cobbler who seceded from an independent congregation over infant baptism, he led what is generally reckoned the earliest Particular Baptist church (its immersion usually dated to about 1638), and in his A Treatise Concerning the Lawfull Subject of Baptisme (1643) answered the covenant case on its own ground — approving covenant theology, on James Renihan’s account, but arguing that under the new covenant its membership is spiritual: none are Abraham’s seed but as they believe as he believed, so the sign belongs to the believing and not to the infant by descent (the credobaptist reply to the covenant-circumcision argument).80 His name stands among the subscribers of the First London Baptist Confession (1644), whose baptism articles make the position confessional: baptism is “to be dispensed only upon persons professing faith, or that are Disciples, or taught, who upon a profession of faith, ought to be baptized” (Art. 39, the first edition reading “onely”) — the newborn, professing none, excluded.81 And its manner “the Scripture holds out to be dipping or plunging the whole body under water: it being a sign, must answer the thing signified” — the death, burial, and resurrection of Romans 6 — the earliest English creed to make immersion confessional; the article concerns how to baptize, where Article 39 concerns whom.82
