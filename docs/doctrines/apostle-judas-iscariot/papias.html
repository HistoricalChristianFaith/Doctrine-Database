Papias of Hierapolis on the death of Judas

Dates: c. 100–130 (Papias’s Exposition of the Oracles of the Lord, surviving only in fragments) · Wikipedia
Summary: Papias, bishop of Hierapolis, a hearer of the elders and a writer of the early second century, reports a third account of Judas’s end — a bodily swelling ending in his being crushed and bursting. It survives only as a quotation in later writers (chiefly Apollinaris of Laodicea).

Claims & evidence

Judas swollen, crushed, and burst

The fragment, preserved by Apollinaris of Laodicea and printed among the remains of Papias, reads:

Judas walked about in this world a sad example of impiety; for his body having swollen to such an extent that he could not pass where a chariot could pass easily, he was crushed by the chariot, so that his bowels gushed out.

— Papias, Fragments III (ANF vol. 1, tr. Roberts–Donaldson). The detail that Judas swelled until he “could not pass where a chariot could pass” and was then crushed is found only in Papias; it shares the “bowels gushed out” of Acts 1:18 and gives a cause — a swelling — that the New Testament does not give.

Theophylact’s further particulars, attributed to Papias

The ANF editors note that the Byzantine commentator Theophylact, quoting this passage, “adds other particulars, as if they were derived from Papias”: that Judas’s eyes swelled shut beyond a physician’s probing, that his body ran with worms, and that he died in a solitary spot so foul that “no one could pass the place without stopping up his nose with his hands.”

— editorial note to Papias, Fragments III (ANF vol. 1).

Reception and debate

  • A third manner of death. Papias reports a swelling and a crushing, a manner of death that neither Matthew (hanging) nor Acts (falling headlong) contains. The relation of his account to the two New Testament notices is set out on the New Testament page.
  • Transmission of the fragment. Papias wrote in the early second century; the death-fragment survives only through later quotation (Apollinaris, Theophylact, and the catenae), so its original wording and context are not fully recoverable. Eusebius, who knew Papias’s five books, wrote that Papias gave accounts from unwritten tradition including “some other more mythical things,” and, discussing his millenarian expectation, that “he appears to have been of very limited understanding” (Church History 3.39.11–13).
