The New Testament on Judas Iscariot

Dates: c. 30 (the betrayal), the Gospels and Acts written c. 50–95 · Wikipedia
Summary: Judas is named last in every list of the Twelve, always with the note that he betrayed Jesus. The New Testament gives him a surname (“Iscariot”), a father (Simon), a role (keeper of the common purse), an act (handing Jesus over for thirty pieces of silver), and two accounts of his end — Matthew’s hanging and the field bought by the priests, and Acts’ falling headlong in a field of his own.

Claims & evidence

The name: “Iscariot,” son of Simon

In all four lists of the Twelve Judas comes last, and always with the same tag — the betrayer. John twice gives his patronymic, “the son of Simon”:

He spake of Judas Iscariot the son of Simon: for he it was that should betray him, being one of the twelve.

— John 6:71 (cf. Matthew 10:4, Mark 3:19, Luke 6:16, each closing the list with Judas “who also was the traitor”). Proposed derivations of the surname Iskariōtēs include the Hebrew ’îsh-Qêriyôth, “man of Kerioth” — a town of southern Judah (Joshua 15:25) — on which Judas would be a Judean among a company of Galileans; the Latin sicarius, “dagger-man”; and an Aramaic root for “the false one.” ⚠ unverified — the proponents of each derivation are not yet cited here.

The role: keeper of the bag — and a thief

John alone reports that Judas carried the disciples’ money, and adds a charge against him. When Mary of Bethany anoints Jesus and Judas objects that the ointment might have been sold for the poor, the evangelist adds:

This he said, not that he cared for the poor; but because he was a thief, and had the bag, and bare what was put therein.

— John 12:6. The “bag” (glōssokomon) is the common purse of Jesus’ band; the charge of pilfering from it is made in John, not in the Synoptics.

The betrayal for thirty pieces of silver

Matthew fixes the price, in the terms of the prophecy he cites later in the passion narrative:

Then one of the twelve, called Judas Iscariot, went unto the chief priests, and said unto them, What will ye give me, and I will deliver him unto you? And they covenanted with him for thirty pieces of silver.

— Matthew 26:14–16. At the last supper John has Jesus mark the traitor with a morsel of bread: “And after the sop Satan entered into him. Then said Jesus unto him, That thou doest, do quickly” (John 13:26–27). In Gethsemane Judas identifies Jesus with a kiss.

The end, first account: Matthew — remorse, the temple, the hanging

Matthew narrates Judas’s death in a single scene of remorse, returned money, and suicide:

Then Judas, which had betrayed him, when he saw that he was condemned, repented himself, and brought again the thirty pieces of silver to the chief priests and elders, saying, I have sinned in that I have betrayed the innocent blood. And they said, What is that to us? see thou to that. And he cast down the pieces of silver in the temple, and departed, and went and hanged himself. And the chief priests took the silver pieces, and said, It is not lawful for to put them into the treasury, because it is the price of blood. And they took counsel, and bought with them the potter’s field, to bury strangers in. Wherefore that field was called, The field of blood, unto this day.

— Matthew 27:3–8. Matthew then binds the scene to prophecy: “Then was fulfilled that which was spoken by Jeremy the prophet… And they took the thirty pieces of silver… and gave them for the potter’s field” (Matthew 27:9–10) — a citation whose wording is mostly that of Zechariah 11:12–13. Here Judas hangs himself; and it is the priests who buy the potter’s field, called “the field of blood” because bought with the price of blood.

The end, second account: Acts — a field of his own, and falling headlong

Peter, proposing that Judas’s place be refilled, gives a second account of his end:

Now this man purchased a field with the reward of iniquity; and falling headlong, he burst asunder in the midst, and all his bowels gushed out. And it was known unto all the dwellers at Jerusalem; insomuch as that field is called in their proper tongue, Aceldama, that is to say, The field of blood.

— Acts 1:18–19. Peter too anchors the fate in Scripture, but to the Psalms: “Let his habitation be desolate… and his bishoprick let another take” (v. 20, quoting Psalms 69:25 and 109:8). Here Judas himself acquires the field; the death is described as falling headlong and bursting open; and the name “field of blood” is reported as following from the event becoming known to all Jerusalem. Chrysostom took the name to have been given “because of Judas.”1

Reception and debate

  • The two death-notices. Matthew and Acts both report that Judas died violently, that his betrayal-money went into a field, and that the field was afterwards called “the field of blood.” They are worded differently on how he died (hanging; falling headlong), who acquired the field (the priests; Judas), and why it bears its name (bought with the price of blood; the event becoming known). C. K. Barrett (1994) holds that no harmonization of the accounts is possible, and Bart Ehrman (2015) that they are “at odds with one” another.2 Darrell Bock (2007) argues against Barrett that the accounts can be combined. James Bejon (2021) reads Acts’ prēnēs genomenos as “to become headlong/prostrate,” a description of the body rather than of a fall, and notes that Luke uses the verb “acquire/possess” (ktaomai) of Judas and the field; Peter J. Williams holds that the Geneva Bible of 1560 “probably introduced ‘head long’” into English.3
  • “Man of Kerioth.” If “Iscariot” is ’îsh-Qêriyôth, Judas is the only member of the Twelve whose surname marks him a Judean rather than a Galilean. The epithet also distinguishes him from “Judas… not Iscariot” (John 14:22), whose identification with Thaddaeus/Jude is discussed on that page.
  • The prophecy citation. Matthew’s ascription of the thirty-pieces oracle to “Jeremy the prophet” was raised by Eusebius, who takes the words to be Zechariah’s, noted that the passage “is not found in the prophecy of Jeremiah” and asked whether it had been removed or miscopied.4
