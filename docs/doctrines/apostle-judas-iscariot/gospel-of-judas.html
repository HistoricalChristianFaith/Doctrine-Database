The Gospel of Judas on Judas Iscariot

Dates: composed c. 130–170 (Greek original, lost); surviving in a single Coptic copy of c. 300 (Codex Tchacos) · Wikipedia
Summary: A 2nd-century Gnostic text — named by Irenaeus about 180 and lost until a Coptic copy surfaced and was published in 2006 — in which Judas is the disciple who understands Jesus and receives secret knowledge. Whether its author honours or condemns Judas is read differently by its 2006 editors and by April DeConick; Thomas A. Wayment judges it “highly unlikely” to contain historically authentic material about Jesus or his disciples.

Claims & evidence

The earliest extant notice: Irenaeus on a “Gospel of Judas” (c. 180)

A century before our surviving copy was written, Irenaeus of Lyons knew and denounced a text of this name, ascribing it to a group who held that Cain “derived his being from the Power above” (labelled “Cainites” in the ANF chapter heading):

They declare that Judas the traitor was thoroughly acquainted with these things, and that he alone, knowing the truth as no others did, accomplished the mystery of the betrayal; by him all things, both earthly and heavenly, were thus thrown into confusion. They produce a fictitious history of this kind, which they style the Gospel of Judas.

— Irenaeus, Against Heresies 1.31.1 (ANF vol. 1). This attests a text of this name, with a Judas who knows the truth, by about 180. Whether the book Irenaeus describes is the Sethian text now extant is debated ⚠ unverified — the parties to that debate are not yet cited here.

The recovered text: Judas and the secret dialogue

The Coptic Gospel of Judas is a secret dialogue set in the days before the crucifixion. In it Judas alone is able to stand before Jesus and confess his true, heavenly origin — “I know who you are and where you have come from. You are from the immortal realm of Barbelo” — and to him alone Jesus discloses the Gnostic cosmology hidden from the other eleven: a transcendent God above the flawed creator of the material world, and salvation by knowledge (gnosis) for an elect few.1 On the reading published with the 2006 edition, Judas’s handing-over of Jesus is a service commanded by Jesus himself — freeing the divine spirit from its mortal body — rather than a crime.

DeConick’s reading: Judas as the thirteenth daimōn

April DeConick reads the Sethian author as condemning Judas: Jesus names Judas the “thirteenth daimōn” — in Sethian usage a title for the world-ruling archon — and tells the disciples they are not of the holy generation and will not ascend.2 Whether the text honours or condemns Judas is thus read differently by the 2006 editors and by DeConick.

Date and transmission

  • Date and relation to the canonical Gospels. Thomas A. Wayment (BYU Studies) dates the Greek original to the mid-second century, holds that it depends on the canonical Gospels, and judges it “highly unlikely that the text could contain historically authentic material about Jesus or his disciples.”3
  • Manuscript and composition. The single surviving copy — in the Coptic Codex Tchacos, found near El Minya in Egypt in the 1970s and radiocarbon-dated to between 220 and 340 — is centuries younger than the 2nd-century text it preserves. It was restored and published by the National Geographic Society in 2006.4
