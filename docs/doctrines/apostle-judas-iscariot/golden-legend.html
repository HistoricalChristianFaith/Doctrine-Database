The Golden Legend on Judas Iscariot

Dates: c. 1260 (Jacobus de Voragine’s Legenda Aurea); the underlying legend medieval · Wikipedia
Summary: By the high Middle Ages a life-story of Judas was in circulation — a tale, on the Oedipus pattern, of an exposed infant who unknowingly kills his father and marries his mother before coming to Christ. Jacobus de Voragine includes it in the Golden Legend as the preamble to the life of Matthias (the apostle chosen to replace Judas), introducing it as “named apocrypha” and closing it as “not authentic.” Dante’s Inferno places Judas at the centre of Hell.

Claims & evidence

The legend of Judas’s early life

The Golden Legend tells Judas’s early life before it reaches Matthias, and opens by naming the story’s source as apocryphal:

It is read in a history, though it be named apocrypha, that there was a man in Jerusalem named Reuben…

— Jacobus de Voragine, the Golden Legend, life of St. Matthias (tr. William Caxton). The tale runs: Judas’s mother, Cyborea, dreams she “had conceived a child that should destroy their people,” so the parents set the newborn adrift — “they put him to a little fiscelle or basket well pitched… and set it in the sea.” The waves carry him to “an island named Scarioth” (whence, the legend says, his surname), where the queen finds and rears him. Grown and having learned he is a foundling, “Judas slew the child that he had supposed to be his brother, and was son to the king,” then “fled anon and came into Jerusalem, and entered into the court of Pilate.” Sent by Pilate to steal fruit from an orchard, he quarrels with its owner — his own unrecognized father — and “Judas smote his father with a stone on the head that he slew him.” Pilate then seizes the dead man’s goods and “gave his wife to Judas in marriage” — so that Judas weds his own widowed mother. Learning the double crime of parricide and incest, Judas “went to Jesu Christ, which did so many miracles, and prayed him of mercy and forgiveness.”

Jacobus’s closing note on the legend

Voragine closes the legend with a note on its source:

Thus far it is read in the history which is not authentic.

— the Golden Legend, life of St. Matthias. He then turns to the betrayal and the election of Matthias.

Dante: Judas in the mouth of Satan (c. 1320)

In Dante’s Inferno the deepest zone of Hell — the ninth circle’s innermost ring — is named Giudecca, for traitors to their benefactors; and at the very centre Dante places Judas as the soul with “the greatest pain,” held head-first in the central mouth of the three-faced Satan, his back forever flayed:

“That soul up there which has the greatest pain,” the Master said, “is Judas Iscariot; with his head inside, he plies his legs without.”

— Dante, Inferno 34.61–63 (tr. Longfellow).

Reception and debate

  • The compiler’s own framing. The infancy-tale follows the pattern of the Oedipus story (exposed child, parricide, marriage to his mother); Voragine introduces and closes it with his own notes on its source (“named apocrypha”; “not authentic”). On the Golden Legend as a source for the apostles generally, see the apostles section.
  • The name. The legend derives “Iscariot” from the island “Scarioth”; other proposed derivations, including “man of Kerioth,” are set out on the New Testament page.
