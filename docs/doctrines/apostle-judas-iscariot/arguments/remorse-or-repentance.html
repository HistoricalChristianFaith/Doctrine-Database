Judas’s remorse: true repentance, or despair? — and could he have been forgiven?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: sound — that Judas’s metameleia (Matthew 27:3) was real remorse that stopped short of saving repentance, foundering on despair and suicide rather than on any exhaustion of God’s mercy, is the church’s near-unanimous reading and is well-grounded in the text: he confesses, returns the money, then hangs himself instead of turning to the Christ he betrayed. Two things are commonly overstated in the popular form of the argument, and the reading is stronger without them: the lexical prop — that the verb metamelomai “merely” means regret, not repentance — is overdrawn (the same verb is used of the son who repents and obeys, of Paul, and of God), and whether mercy in fact remained open to Judas is a point on which the fathers themselves divide, most saying it did.

The claim

Matthew did not use metanoeō, which means a genuine change of mind and will… but metamelomai, which merely connotes regret or sorrow. He did not experience spiritual penitence but only emotional remorse.

— a representative modern statement of the popular reading (John MacArthur, “Did Judas Repent?”).1 The interpretation weighed here is the one that statement serves: that when Matthew 27:3–5 says Judas “repented himself” (metamelētheis), brought back the thirty pieces, confessed “I have sinned in that I have betrayed the innocent blood,” and then went and hanged himself, it describes a genuine but arrested turning — remorse that ended in despair rather than in the restoration Peter found after his own denial (Matthew 26:75, “he went out, and wept bitterly”). The steelman of the reading rests on the shape of Judas’s end, not on the single word.

Who advances it

The popular modern form — that the Greek verb itself proves “remorse, not repentance” — runs through a good deal of preaching and study-Bible teaching (MacArthur; Rick Renner; countless sermons on “worldly sorrow”). It stands downstream of a much older and more careful reading in the fathers, who did not rest the point on the word (the Greek writers use “repent” of Judas freely) but on what he did with his sorrow. Theophylact puts the whole thesis in a sentence: “he repented, but it was not a good repentance.” The modern debate over how contrite Matthew’s Judas really is — Davies and Allison, Ulrich Luz, and John Nolland reading him as genuinely penitent, Craig Blomberg reading him as falling short — is the same question in critical dress.2

The evidence for

The end, not the word, carries it. Whatever metamelētheis means in the abstract, the narrative is unambiguous about where Judas’s sorrow went. He does not seek out Jesus, or the disciples, or mercy; he throws the money into the temple and hangs himself. Set beside Peter — who denies Christ with oaths one chapter earlier, weeps bitterly, and is restored — the contrast Matthew builds is not between two words but between two outcomes: restoration versus self-destruction. This is exactly how the church read it. John Chrysostom grants the remorse but locates the failure precisely: Judas repented “late, and slowly, and became self-condemned”; to throw down the silver “were all acceptable things; but to hang himself, this again was unpardonable, and a work of an evil spirit. For the devil led him out of his repentance too soon, so that he should reap no fruit from thence.”3

The fathers agree on the diagnosis: sorrow without hope is despair. Thomas Aquinas states it as a formula that gathers the whole tradition: “this repentance was not true repentance; yet it had something of repentance, because repentance ought to be a middle way between hope and fear; but Judas indeed had fear and sorrow… but he did not have hope. And such is the repentance of the wicked.” Jerome says the remorse was real but “availed him nothing, by which he could not correct his crime… he not only could not correct the wickedness of his betrayal, but also added the crime of his own homicide to the former sin.” Theophylact: “to pass judgement against oneself is good, but to hang oneself is of the devil… when he ought instead to have wept and reconciled himself to Him Whom he had betrayed.” The point on which they converge is not lexical but pastoral — a true turning would have run toward the offended Christ; Judas’s ran away from life.4

Even Origen, who reads the remorse most generously, ends in the same place. Origen goes further than any of them: Judas’s confession “belonged to repentance and to saving sorrow,” of the very kind Paul describes as “sorrow according to God” which “works repentance unto stable salvation” (2 Corinthians 7:10) — the reality of it, he argues, refutes the heretics who held Judas an irredeemably “evil nature,” for “how an evil nature could have repented.” Yet Origen too says the turning was cut off before it could save: Judas “did not repent also according to knowledge, as he ought,” took up instead a “more abundant sorrow sent under to him by the devil,” and so hanged himself — whereas had he “sought a place of repentance and awaited the time of repentance, perhaps he would even have found Him who said: as I live, I do not will the death of the sinner, but rather his repentance.” That last clause is the second half of the claim, and Origen affirms it: the mercy was there to be had; despair kept Judas from it.5

What is overstated or imprecise

The lexical prop is overdrawn. The popular argument makes the whole case turn on a supposed hard line between metamelomai (mere regret) and metanoeō (true repentance). That line will not bear the weight. Matthew himself uses metamelētheis — the same participle — of the son in the parable who first refuses his father and then “repented, and went” (Matthew 21:29): there the word names a change that issues in obedience, the good son of the pair. Paul uses the verb of his own change of feeling over a letter (2 Corinthians 7:8), and Scripture uses it of God, who “will not repent” (Hebrews 7:21, quoting Psalm 110). The verb simply does not mean “superficial” regret as opposed to real repentance; the NET Bible’s note is blunt — “there is little difference between it and the normal Greek word for ‘repent,’ metanoeō.” Nor does Matthew reserve metanoeō for the genuine article: he does not use it of Peter’s bitter weeping either. Davies and Allison accordingly “do not see as sharp a distinction between Judas’ remorse and Peter’s repentance,” and Luz insists the remorse “was not merely superficial” — “that can be seen… in the fact that he, an allegedly greedy person, returns the money, and… in the strong words with which he confesses his guilt.” The sound form of the argument reads Judas’s failure off his despair, not off his vocabulary.6

Whether he could have been forgiven is genuinely disputed — and the majority say yes. The reading is sometimes pressed into a harsher claim: that Judas was beyond mercy, the metamelomai proving him reprobate. The tradition does not line up so neatly. Chrysostom calls the suicide “unpardonable,” and Jerome darkly applies “let his prayer be turned into sin” (Psalm 109:7) — but Origen holds mercy was still available, and Leo the Great locates Judas’s ruin not in an exhausted mercy but in his own unbelief: “he believed not Jesus to be the Son of God… had he not thus denied His omnipotence, he would have obtained His mercy.” The dominant patristic verdict is that the door was open and Judas fled it, which is precisely why the suicide, not the remorse, is treated as the fatal act. Whether he would in fact have been received is a theological judgment the texts leave underdetermined; what the evidence supports is the narrower structural claim, not a confident sentence of damnation read out of a single verb.7

Assessment

Sound. That Judas felt real remorse but never reached saving repentance — because it collapsed into despair and self-murder instead of turning back to Christ — is the church’s near-unanimous reading (Chrysostom, Origen, Jerome, Theophylact, Leo, Aquinas) and squares with the text as it stands: a genuine confession, a returned bribe, and then a noose rather than a plea for mercy. It is confirmed by contrast, in the Judas/Peter foil Matthew plainly builds — two catastrophic failures, one ending in weeping and restoration, the other in the rope.

Two guards keep it honest. The interpretation does not rest on the popular claim that the verb metamelomai mechanically means “remorse, not repentance”: that distinction is overdrawn — Matthew uses the same word of the obedient son, and reserves neither verb for Peter — and Davies and Allison, Luz, and Nolland reasonably read Matthew’s Judas as genuinely contrite. Nor does the reading warrant a confident verdict that Judas was beyond mercy; on that the fathers divide, and the majority (with Origen and Leo) hold the opposite — the mercy was there, and despair, not any limit in God, kept him from it. Rate the interpretation sound in its structural form — remorse arrested by despair — and flag the lexical shortcut and the damnation-by-word overreach wherever they appear.
