Matthew’s repentant Judas and Acts’s silence: conflict, or omission?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: sound — Matthew reports that Judas “repented himself” and confessed; Acts’s notice of him says nothing of remorse and frames him only as one who “by transgression fell.” That is an omission, not a contradiction: Peter’s speech in Acts 1 is explaining why the twelfth apostolic seat must be refilled, not narrating Judas’s state of mind, and silence about the remorse denies it no more than it denies the thirty pieces of silver. It is the thinnest of the four divergences between the two death-notices, and the one no ancient reader even felt as a difficulty. What the harmonization does not settle — whether Matthew’s remorse scene is history or his own composition — is a separate question.

Related arguments: the two accounts of Judas’s end — see also Matthew’s hanging and Acts’ bursting: one death, told twice · Matthew’s priests and Acts’ Judas: one purchase, one name

The claim

[Matthew:] Then Judas… repented himself… saying, I have sinned in that I have betrayed the innocent blood… [Acts:] …this ministry and apostleship, from which Judas by transgression fell, that he might go to his own place.

— Matthew 27:3–4 and Acts 1:25. Alongside the three famous tensions between the two death-notices — the manner of death, who bought the field, and why it bears its name — a fourth is sometimes urged: Matthew shows a Judas stricken with remorse, while Acts’s account (Peter’s speech) gives no hint of it, calling him only one who “fell” from his ministry to “his own place,” whose field was bought with “the reward of iniquity.” The reading weighed here is that this is no contradiction at all: Acts is simply silent on Judas’s interior, and an argument from silence establishes nothing.1

What can be said for it

Acts never denies the remorse; it does not raise the subject. Peter’s speech has one errand: to show from the Psalms that Judas’s vacated office must be filled (“his bishoprick let another take,” Acts 1:20, quoting Psalm 69 and 109), and so to motivate the choice of Matthias. It is not a life of Judas. It compresses his whole end into a single relative clause, and it no more comments on whether he felt remorse than it comments on what he said to the priests, or on the temple, or on the rope. Where a text is silent, it contradicts nothing; a contradiction needs two assertions that cannot both hold, and Acts makes only one — that Judas fell and died. Even Bart Ehrman, cataloguing the divergences between the accounts, presses the manner of death and the naming of the field, not the remorse: the silence is not where the real difficulty lies.2

Acts’s negative frame and Matthew’s remorse describe one fallen man, not two. The apparent clash — a penitent Judas in Matthew, a reprobate Judas in Acts — dissolves on inspection, because Matthew’s Judas is every bit as fallen. In Matthew it is Judas himself who says “I have sinned” and “I have betrayed the innocent blood,” and it is Matthew who has him hang himself. Remorse in Matthew does not undo the transgression; it sits on top of it. Acts’s “by transgression fell” and “reward of iniquity” are judgments Matthew would not contest — they are, if anything, Judas’s own self-verdict in Matthew. The two texts weight the same man differently (Matthew lingering on the stricken conscience, Acts on the ruin), but they are describing the same fall, not two incompatible characters.3

No ancient reader felt this as a difficulty. The manner of death drove the fathers to elaborate harmonies — the snapped rope, the failed hanging, Papias’s swelling — but the remorse never did. The Apostolic Constitutions run the whole story together without a seam, Matthew’s repentance and Acts’s bursting in one breath: Judas “repented, and hanged himself, and burst asunder in the midst.” The commentators on Acts who quote Matthew’s scene — Chrysostom, Oecumenius, Theophylact — treat the remorse as simply part of the one narrative, a thing Acts happens not to repeat. That the difficulty is invisible to the tradition is a fair sign of how thin it is.4

What weighs against it

The serious version of the objection is not contradiction but independence. The sharp-eyed critic does not say Acts denies the remorse; he says the silence is a symptom — that Matthew and Acts carry two independent traditions of Judas’s end that never knew each other, and that Matthew’s remorse scene is Matthew’s own theological composition rather than remembered fact. The scene is stitched from Matthew’s signature material: the “innocent blood” motif that runs through his passion (Pilate washing his hands, “I am innocent of the blood of this just person,” Matthew 27:24; the crowd’s “his blood be on us,” 27:25), the thirty pieces and the potter’s field drawn from Zechariah and Jeremiah, and the pointed foil with Peter’s restoration. On this reading Acts is silent about the remorse because the remorse is not in the tradition Acts received — which is a claim about history and redaction, not a logical contradiction between the texts. It leaves the harmonization intact on its own terms (the two do not collide) while denying it the further prize (that the remorse is therefore historical).5

Assessment

Sound. Taken as a charge of contradiction — “Matthew says he repented, Acts says he did not” — the objection fails outright, because Acts does not say he did not. Peter’s speech names Judas’s fall in a single clause to explain a vacant office; it neither asserts nor denies anything about his state of mind, and its bleak language (“transgression,” “reward of iniquity”) is a verdict Matthew’s own scene shares, since in Matthew it is Judas who confesses the sin and Judas who hangs himself. This is the lightest of the four divergences between the death-notices, and the only one the ancient harmonizers passed over without effort. Rate the reconciling reading sound.

Two limits keep it honest. First, “sound” here means only that the texts do not contradict — not that Matthew’s remorse scene is thereby established as history; whether it is Matthew’s memory or Matthew’s composition is a live question that the silence of Acts, on the independent-traditions reading, may even tell against. Second, this argument is narrower than its neighbours: the real weight of the Matthew–Acts problem rests on the manner of death and the field, not here. The distinct theological question the remorse raises — whether Judas’s turning was saving repentance or despair, and whether he could have been forgiven — is weighed separately.6
