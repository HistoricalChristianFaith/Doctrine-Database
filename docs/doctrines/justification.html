Justification

Justification is the doctrine of how a sinner is set right with God. It was a principal point of dispute between the Lutheran, Reformed and Anglican confessions and the Council of Trent in the sixteenth century, over two questions: what justification consists in — God’s pardoning the sinner and accounting him righteous, or that together with the inward renewal of the person — and the role of works and the meaning of “faith alone” (sola fide), including how Paul’s “a man is justified by faith without the deeds of the law” (Rom 3:28) relates to James’s “by works a man is justified, and not by faith only” (Jas 2:24).1 The related question of merit is traced on the treasury of merit and indulgences timelines. This timeline records how the doctrine has been stated, earliest extant attestation first.

Related doctrines: Grace, faith, and justification — see also original sin & the Fall · predestination, grace & free will.

The positions in brief. The Lutheran and Reformed confessions teach that God justifies the sinner freely through faith, pardoning his sins and accounting him righteous for Christ’s sake, the Westminster Confession calling faith “the alone instrument of justification: yet is it not alone in the person justified, but is ever accompanied with all other saving graces”;2,3 the Anglican Thirty-Nine Articles call justification “by Faith only” “a most wholesome Doctrine.”4 A 1995 agreed statement of the international Lutheran–Orthodox Joint Commission, signed by its presidents Metropolitan Spyridon of Italy (Orthodox) and Bishop William Lazareth (Lutheran), describes the Lutheran position (§9): justification is “God’s gracious declaration of the forgiveness of sins for the sake of Jesus Christ, crucified and risen, and at the same time the free gift of new life in him,” a promise “received by faith alone (sola fide).”5

The Council of Trent defines justification as remission of sins together with the sanctification and renewal of the inward man; teaches that “we are therefore said to be justified freely, because that none of those things which precede justification—whether faith or works—merit the grace itself of justification”; and anathematizes justification by faith alone “in such wise as to mean, that nothing else is required to co-operate in order to the obtaining the grace of Justification,” or by the sole imputation of Christ’s justice “to the exclusion of the grace and the charity” poured into the heart.6 Benedict XVI, in a general audience of 19 November 2008, said that “Luther’s phrase: ‘faith alone’ is true, if it is not opposed to faith in charity, in love.”7

In the same 1995 agreed statement the Orthodox and Lutheran sides together describe justification (dikaiosis), in the language of the Ecumenical Councils and the Fathers, as “liberation from the dominion of the devil and the restoration of our communion with God” (§6). The statement describes the Orthodox position (§8): salvation is “a gratuitous gift of God offered in Jesus Christ to all human beings”, which they “must both freely choose” and “work for,” calling this synergia, in which Christ leads the faithful “from illumination/justification” to “glorification.”5 Kallistos Ware (Orthodox) writes that “when we Orthodox speak about salvation, we do not have in view any sharp differentiation between justification and sanctification” ⚠ unverified.8 The 1999 Joint Declaration and its reception are recorded in the last entry below.

Timeline

Clement of Rome — “justified… by faith” (c. 96)

Clement of Rome, writing to Corinth, says that “we, too, being called by His will in Christ Jesus, are not justified by ourselves, nor by our own wisdom, or understanding, or godliness, or works which we have wrought in holiness of heart; but by that faith through which, from the beginning, Almighty God has justified all men.” In the English translation cited, the chapter is headed “We are justified not by our own works, but by faith.” The next chapter continues: “What shall we do, then, brethren? Shall we become slothful in well-doing, and cease from the practice of love? God forbid that any such course should be followed by us! But rather let us hasten with all energy and readiness of mind to perform every good work.”9

John Chrysostom — faith opens the door “to the world” (c. 391)

John Chrysostom, preaching through Romans, comments on 3:28 (“a man is justified by faith without the deeds of the Law”): “he does not say, a Jew, or one under the Law, but after leading forth his discourse into a large room, and opening the doors of faith to the world, he says ‘a man,’ the name common to our race.” Paul then meets an objection, he continues: “For since it was likely that the Jews, upon hearing that faith justifies every man, would take it ill and feel offended, he goes on” to verse 29.10

Augustine against Pelagius — justified “freely by His grace” (c. 412–427)

Augustine, writing during the Pelagian controversy, holds that no one is justified by law or by his own will: “they are justified freely by His grace—not that it is wrought without our will; but our will is by the law shown to be weak, that grace may heal its infirmity” — and “in Him only is the ungodly man justified,” to whom “on believing… faith is reckoned for righteousness.”11 On Paul’s “the doers of the law shall be justified” he asks, “what else does the phrase ‘being justified’ signify than being made righteous,—by Him, of course, who justifies the ungodly man, that he may become a godly one instead?”, and offers a second sense: “Or else the term ‘They shall be justified’ is used in the sense of, They shall be deemed, or reckoned as just.”12 Of the saints’ reward he writes that “It is His own gifts that God crowns, not your merits,—if, at least, your merits are of your own self, not of Him. If, indeed, they are such, they are evil; and God does not crown them; but if they are good, they are God’s gifts.”13

The Augsburg Confession, Article IV (1530)

The Lutheran confession of 1530 states that men “cannot be justified before God by their own strength, merits, or works, but are freely justified for Christ’s sake, through faith, when they believe that they are received into favor, and that their sins are forgiven for Christ’s sake… This faith God imputes for righteousness in His sight.”2

The Council of Trent, Session VI (1547)

The Council of Trent’s Decree on Justification defines justification as “not remission of sins merely, but also the sanctification and renewal of the inward man, through the voluntary reception of the grace and of the gifts, whereby man of unjust becomes just” (Ch. VII). Chapter VIII explains Paul’s words that man is justified “by faith and freely”: “we are therefore said to be justified by faith, because faith is the beginning of human salvation, the foundation, and the root of all Justification”, and “we are therefore said to be justified freely, because that none of those things which precede justification—whether faith or works—merit the grace itself of justification.” Chapter XVI states that the justified may be accounted, “by those very works which have been done in God… to have truly merited eternal life, to be obtained also in its (due) time, if so be, however, that they depart in grace,” and adds: “God forbid that a Christian should either trust or glory in himself, and not in the Lord,” whose bounty is such “that He will have the things which are His own gifts be their merits” (Ch. XVI). Its canons anathematize anyone who says “that by faith alone the impious is justified; in such wise as to mean, that nothing else is required to co-operate in order to the obtaining the grace of Justification, and that it is not in any way necessary, that he be prepared and disposed by the movement of his own will” (can. 9), or that men are justified “by the sole imputation of the justice of Christ… to the exclusion of the grace and the charity” poured into the heart (can. 11), or that justifying faith is nothing else but “confidence in the divine mercy” (can. 12).6

The Thirty-Nine Articles and the Westminster Confession (1571–1646)

The Church of England’s Thirty-Nine Articles, Article XI, hold “we are accounted righteous before God, only for the merit of our Lord and Saviour Jesus Christ by Faith, and not for our own works or deservings… that we are justified by Faith only, is a most wholesome Doctrine.”4 The Westminster Confession (XI.1) states that God justifies “not by infusing righteousness into them, but by pardoning their sins, and by accounting and accepting their persons as righteous… by imputing the obedience and satisfaction of Christ unto them, they receiving and resting on him… by faith,” and (XI.2) that faith “is the alone instrument of justification: yet is it not alone in the person justified, but is ever accompanied with all other saving graces, and is no dead faith, but works by love.”3

The Joint Declaration on the Doctrine of Justification (1999)

On 31 October 1999 the Lutheran World Federation and the Catholic Church signed, at Augsburg, a Joint Declaration setting out “a consensus on basic truths of the doctrine of justification”: “By grace alone, in faith in Christ’s saving work and not because of any merit on our part, we are accepted by God and receive the Holy Spirit, who renews our hearts while equipping and calling us to good works” (§15). It states that “the corresponding doctrinal condemnations of the sixteenth century do not apply to today’s partner”1 and adds that “Nothing is thereby taken away from the seriousness of the condemnations related to the doctrine of justification” (§42).14 The Catholic Church’s official Response (1998) had found it difficult to see how, “in the current state of the presentation, given in the Joint Declaration,” the Lutheran doctrine of “simul iustus et peccator” “is not touched by the anathemas of the Tridentine decree on original sin and justification”;15 the Official Common Statement and its Annex, signed with the Declaration, refer to that Response and state that “the earlier mutual doctrinal condemnations do not apply to the teaching of the dialogue partners as presented in the Joint Declaration.”14 Ted Dorman (Reformed, 2001) reports that before the signing a “Position Statement” of over two hundred mostly German Lutheran theological teachers, among them Peter Stuhlmacher, Martin Hengel and Otto Betz, objected that the Declaration promulgates an essentially Catholic view of justification, while the Catholic theologian Avery Dulles concluded that at key points it appears to favour the Lutheran perspective.16 The World Methodist Council (2006), the Anglican Consultative Council (2016) and the World Communion of Reformed Churches (2017) later associated themselves with the Declaration.14
