Maximus the Confessor on the person of Christ

Dates: c. 640 (the Monothelite controversy; lifespan c. 580–662) · Wikipedia
Summary: Monk and theologian who opposed Monothelitism, the teaching of one will in the one incarnate Christ. Maximus argued that will belongs to nature, so that two natures entail two natural wills, the human will in harmony with the divine. Condemned and mutilated, he died in exile in 662; the Third Council of Constantinople (680–81) defined two natural wills in Christ.

Claims & evidence

Christ has a human natural will, shaped but not opposed by the divine

The Saviour therefore possesses as a human being a natural will, which is shaped, but not opposed, by his divine will.

— Opusculum 3, trans. A. Louth (Maximus the Confessor, Routledge), source. For Maximus the Savior’s human will is “shaped, but not opposed” by the divine. The sentence just before, in the same text, grounds this in the divine will that Son and Father share: “the Son’s will is by nature the same as the Father’s.”

Gethsemane: the human will in harmony with the divine

Clearly the negation here—Not what I will—absolutely precludes opposition and instead demonstrates harmony between the human will of the Savior and the divine will shared by him and his Father, given that the Logos assumed our nature in its entirety and deified his human will in the assumption.

— Opusculum 6, “On the Two Wills of Christ in the Agony of Gethsemane,” in On the Cosmic Mystery of Jesus Christ, p. 176, as quoted in the Paideia Center discussion guide to that volume. Maximus reads Christ’s prayer “not my will, but thine, be done” (Luke 22:42) as the prayer of a human will: “having become like us for our sake, he was calling on his God and Father in a human manner when he said, Let not what I will, but what you will prevail.”

Reception and debate

  • The Third Council of Constantinople (680–81), the Sixth Ecumenical Council in the reckoning of the churches that received it, defined that Christ has “two natural wills… not contrary the one to the other… but his human will follows, and that not as resisting and reluctant, but rather as subject to his divine and omnipotent will” (NPNF² vol. 14). Maximus had died in exile in 662, his tongue and right hand cut off.
  • He is remembered by the title “the Confessor.” His broader thought (on deification and the cosmic scope of the Incarnation) reaches beyond this timeline.
  • Maximus also appears as a witness on the Filioque timeline, for his Letter to Marinus (c. 645) on the Greek “through the Son” and the Latin “and the Son.”
