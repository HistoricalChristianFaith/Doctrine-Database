Maximus the Confessor on the person of Christ

Dates: c. 640 (the Monothelite controversy; lifespan c. 580–662) · Wikipedia
Summary: Monk and theologian who fought the last great Christological battle, against Monothelitism — the claim that the one Christ has a single (divine) will. Maximus argued that will belongs to nature, so two natures entail two natural wills, the human will freely conforming to the divine rather than being overridden. Condemned and mutilated, he died in exile; the Third Council of Constantinople (680–81) vindicated him.

Claims & evidence

Christ has a human natural will, shaped but not opposed by the divine

The Savior therefore possesses as a human being a natural will, which is shaped, but not opposed, by his divine will.

— Opusculum 6, trans. A. Louth (Maximus the Confessor, Routledge), source. Maximus saw Monothelitism as Apollinarianism returning at the level of the will: to deny Christ a human will is to leave the manhood incomplete in exactly the faculty that must be healed. Because “the Son’s will is by nature the same as the Father’s,” the divine will is not a second thing added; but the assumed manhood brings its own natural will, which in Christ is perfectly and freely aligned with God — not suppressed, not made idle.

Gethsemane: the human will freely conforming

Maximus read Christ’s prayer “not my will, but thine, be done” (Luke 22:42) as the decisive proof of a real human will — not a will in conflict with God, but a human will that chooses obedience. A single divine will would make the prayer a fiction; a human will merely overpowered would make the obedience unfree. The freedom of Christ’s human “yes” is, for Maximus, the pattern of human salvation: the healing of the very faculty by which Adam disobeyed.

Notes

  • The Third Council of Constantinople (680–81), the Sixth Ecumenical Council, vindicated Maximus (who had died in exile in 662, his tongue and right hand cut off). Its Definition confesses “two natural wills… not contrary the one to the other… but his human will follows, and that not as resisting and reluctant, but rather as subject to his divine and omnipotent will” (NPNF² vol. 14).
  • The two-wills settlement is the last refinement of Chalcedon’s logic: it extends “in two natures” to “two natural wills and two natural operations,” completing the confession of a whole, unabridged humanity in the one Christ. It is the direct heir of Leo’s “each form does what is proper to it.”
  • Maximus’s stand cost him everything and is why the church remembers him as “the Confessor” — one who suffered for the faith short of martyrdom. His broader thought (on deification and the cosmic scope of the Incarnation) reaches well beyond this timeline.
