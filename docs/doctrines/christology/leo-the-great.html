Leo the Great on the person of Christ

Dates: c. 449 (the Tome to Flavian; pope 440–461) · Wikipedia
Summary: Bishop of Rome, whose Tome to Flavian gave the Latin West its decisive two-natures statement. Written against Eutyches — who had pushed “one nature” until the manhood was swallowed in the Godhead — the Tome holds the two natures together in one person, each keeping its own properties and its own proper agency. Read aloud at Chalcedon (451), it was acclaimed as the voice of Peter and shaped the Council’s Definition.

Claims & evidence

The two natures come together in one person, each keeping its properties

Without detriment therefore to the properties of either nature and substance which then came together in one person, majesty took on humility, strength weakness, eternity mortality… For both natures retain their own proper character without loss: and as the form of God did not do away with the form of a slave, so the form of a slave did not impair the form of God.

— Letter 28 (the Tome to Flavian) III, trans. NPNF² vol. 12, source. Leo’s target is Eutyches’s confusion of the natures. Against it he insists that in the union nothing is lost from either side: “in the whole and perfect nature of true man was true God born, complete in what was His own, complete in what was ours.” The manhood is whole — the answer to Apollinaris and Eutyches alike.

Each nature performs what is proper to it

For each form does what is proper to it with the co-operation of the other; that is the Word performing what appertains to the Word, and the flesh carrying out what appertains to the flesh.

— Tome to Flavian IV, source. This is the Tome’s most quoted line, and the point Eastern critics found hardest: it seems to distribute Christ’s acts between the two natures as distinct agents (the Word works miracles, the flesh weeps and dies). Cyrilline theologians worried it verged on Nestorius — two subjects, not one; the neo-Chalcedonians of 553 would later insist it be read as one subject acting in and through both natures. Leo himself frames it as one Christ in whom “each form” acts “with the co-operation of the other,” not two persons.

True God and true man, without confusion and without lie

For He who is true God is also true man: and in this union there is no lie, since the humility of manhood and the loftiness of the Godhead both meet there.

— Tome to Flavian IV, source. Where Cyril begins from the “one” and works to preserve the distinction, Leo begins from the two and works to preserve the unity — the two traditions converging on the same confession from opposite directions. Chalcedon’s achievement was to hold both.

Notes

  • At the “Robber Synod” (Latrocinium) of Ephesus in 449, Dioscorus of Alexandria refused to let the Tome be read and rehabilitated Eutyches. Leo’s protest and the change of emperor led to Chalcedon two years later, where the assembled bishops are reported to have cried, “Peter has spoken through Leo” — after testing the Tome against Cyril to be sure the two agreed.
  • The Tome’s “each form does what is proper to it” is the western root of the later two-wills / two-operations question that Maximus the Confessor would settle: if each nature has its own operation, each nature has its own will.
