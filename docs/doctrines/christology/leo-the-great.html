Leo the Great on the person of Christ

Dates: c. 449 (the Tome to Flavian; pope 440–461) · Wikipedia
Summary: Bishop of Rome, whose Tome to Flavian, written against the monk Eutyches, sets out a two-natures teaching: the two natures come together in one person, each keeping its own properties and acting “with the co-operation of the other.” Read at Chalcedon (451), it was acclaimed and received in the Council’s Definition beside the synodical letters of Cyril.

Claims & evidence

The two natures come together in one person, each keeping its properties

Without detriment therefore to the properties of either nature and substance which then came together in one person, majesty took on humility, strength weakness, eternity mortality… For both natures retain their own proper character without loss: and as the form of God did not do away with the form of a slave, so the form of a slave did not impair the form of God.

— Letter 28 (the Tome to Flavian) III, trans. NPNF² vol. 12, source. Leo’s target is Eutyches’s confusion of the natures. Against it he insists that in the union nothing is lost from either side: “in the whole and perfect nature of true man was true God born, complete in what was His own, complete in what was ours.”

Each nature performs what is proper to it

For each form does what is proper to it with the co-operation of the other; that is the Word performing what appertains to the Word, and the flesh carrying out what appertains to the flesh.

— Tome to Flavian IV, source. The sentence has been read in different ways. Severus of Antioch read it as distributing Christ’s acts between the two natures and so dividing the one Christ. Pius XII (1951) reads the sentence within Leo’s attribution of both natures’ properties and activities “to the One Person of the Word,” and quotes it as an instance of the exchange of properties “which Cyril vindicated against Nestorius” (Sempiternus Rex Christus, §22). John Romanides (Greek Orthodox, 1965) writes that Leo “tended to separate or distinguish the acts of Christ in such a way that the two natures seem to be acting as separate subjects,” and holds the Tome nonetheless “adequately Orthodox, definitely not Nestorian” (Greek Orthodox Theological Review 10.2). V. C. Samuel (1977) writes that theologians of the Alexandrine tradition would take it to make the natures, rather than the one hypostasis, the agents of Christ’s words and deeds (The Council of Chalcedon Re-Examined, p. 193).

True God and true man, without confusion and without lie

For He who is true God is also true man: and in this union there is no lie, since the humility of manhood and the loftiness of the Godhead both meet there.

— Tome to Flavian IV, source.

Reception and debate

  • At the second Council of Ephesus (449), which Leo called a meeting “not of judges but of robbers” (Letter 95, to Pulcheria Augusta, trans. NPNF² vol. 12), Eutyches was restored and the Tome was not read. Leo, and the Roman legates at Chalcedon, charged Dioscorus of Alexandria with withholding it; V. C. Samuel writes that the charge “was raised on the whole by persons representing western interests” and examines the evidence (The Council of Chalcedon Re-Examined, pp. 56–57). At Chalcedon two years later the bishops cried, “Peter has spoken thus through Leo… Leo and Cyril taught the same thing” (Acts, Session II (continued), NPNF² vol. 14); John Romanides holds that the Tome was accepted only after it had been compared with Cyril’s Twelve Chapters (see the disputed reading on Chalcedon and Cyril).
  • The two-wills question was decided later, by the Third Council of Constantinople (680–81); see Maximus the Confessor.
