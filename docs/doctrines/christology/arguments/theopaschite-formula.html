“One of the Trinity suffered in the flesh” — the theopaschite formula

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: sound — because the union is real, the properties of the manhood may be truly predicated of the one divine subject, so “God suffered” and “one of the Trinity was crucified” are orthodox — provided the qualifier in the flesh stands and the divine nature is not made passible; drop that guard and the same words tip into theopaschism proper · advanced by Cyril of Alexandria, ratified at the Second Council of Constantinople (553)

The claim

If any one confess not that the Word of God suffered in the Flesh and hath been crucified in the Flesh and tasted death in the Flesh and hath been made First-born of the Dead, inasmuch as He is both Life and Life-giving as God, be he anathema.

— Cyril of Alexandria, the Twelve Anathemas (appended to the Third Letter to Nestorius), no. 12, as given in the introduction to the Five Tomes against Nestorius, trans. P. E. Pusey.1 The asserted reading: because the one Christ is the eternal Word, and the union of Godhead and manhood in him is real (a union “according to hypostasis,” not a partnership of two), the properties of the human nature belong to the one divine person, so it is true to say that God suffered, that the Lord of glory was crucified, and — the sixth-century sharpening of the same grammar — that one of the Holy Trinity suffered in the flesh. This is the communicatio idiomatum (the “interchange of properties”): the natures are not confused, but their attributes are jointly predicated of the single subject who owns both.

Who advances it

The formula’s father is Cyril of Alexandria, whose twelfth anathema (430) made “the Word suffered in the flesh” a binding test against Nestorius. Its most famous later carriers were the Scythian monks (led by John Maxentius) who at Constantinople c. 519 pressed the exact phrase “one of the Trinity suffered in the flesh” (unus ex Trinitate passus est carne) as a touchstone of Chalcedonian orthodoxy against a lingering Nestorianism. Resisted at first by the patriarch of Constantinople and (hesitantly) by Pope Hormisdas, the formula was championed by the Emperor Justinian and approved by Pope John II, and was dogmatized at the Second Council of Constantinople (553): “our Lord Jesus Christ who was crucified in the flesh is true God… and one of the Holy Trinity.”2 It is standard Chalcedonian, Catholic, and Orthodox doctrine, and (as the communicatio idiomatum) it passed whole into the Reformation confessions.

The evidence for

The engine is the identity of the subject. Scripture already predicates divine and human of the one Christ across the line: the “Lord of glory” was crucified (1 Cor. 2:8), God “purchased the church with his own blood” (Acts 20:28), and it was the Word who “became flesh” (John 1:14) and so could be “put to death in the flesh” (1 Pet. 3:18) — the very verse Cyril leans on. If the one who died is none other than the eternal Son, then “God died” is not a category mistake but the plain sense of the incarnation.

Cyril’s decisive move is to lodge the paradox in the qualifier, not in the divine nature. In That Christ is One he stages the Nestorian objection in the mouth of an interlocutor — “they say that it savours of the marvellous and inclines much to the incredible, that we should have to say that the Same both suffers and does not suffer… either surely He hath as God not suffered or if He is said to have suffered, how will He be God?” — and answers that Peter the apostle himself supplies the resolution by writing not that Christ simply suffered but that he suffered in the flesh: “even though He be said to have suffered in the flesh, freedom from suffering even thus is kept to Him in that He is conceived of as God… he hath allotted to Him impassibility in that He is conceived of as God, most skilfully adding, in the flesh, in respect to which suffering exists.”3 The Word truly makes the suffering flesh his own and so truly suffers as a man, while the divine nature, which grounds his identity, remains what it is by nature — impassible. Predication attaches to the person; passibility attaches to a nature. That is why “God suffered” and “the divine nature suffered” are not the same sentence, and only the first is confessed.

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The predication logic is sound and is the settled mind of the church. Once the union is “according to hypostasis” — one who, not one what alongside another what — the attributes of both natures must terminate on the single subject, or the incarnation is emptied: a Christ of whom you may not say “God was born” and “God died” is a Christ split into two sons, which is precisely the Nestorian failure the formula was built to exclude. Chalcedon confessed the same when it named Mary “Mother of God according to his manhood” and Leo’s Tome conceded that “the Son of God… is said to have been crucified and buried” — the birth-language and the death-language stand or fall together.

Where the line actually falls. The formula is orthodox only while two guards hold, and its critics were right to watch them. First, the qualifier in the flesh is load-bearing, not decorative: it names the nature in which the one subject suffers and thereby protects the impassibility of the Godhead. Drop it — say flatly “the divine nature suffered,” or attribute the passion to the deity as deity — and the sentence crosses from the communicatio idiomatum into theopaschism proper, the genuine heresy that God is by nature passible. The church marked this exact boundary in the Trisagion controversy: when Peter the Fuller, patriarch of Antioch (471–488), inserted “who was crucified for us” into a hymn his opponents read as addressed to the whole Trinity, the addition was condemned — not because “the crucified is God” is false, but because, aimed at the Trinity as such, it made the Father and the Spirit crucified too and suggested the divine nature itself was slain.4 The very words that are orthodox of the incarnate Son in his manhood become heresy when re-aimed at the divine nature or the undivided Trinity. That the two cases — the Scythian formula upheld, the Trisagion addition condemned — sit a few decades apart is not inconsistency but precision: the church affirmed the predication and refused the confusion.

The Antiochene worry, weighed. The Antiochene instinct to reserve suffering-language to the humanity — to say “the man Jesus suffered” and stop — is not simply wrong; it is guarding a real truth (the impassibility of God) by a clumsy means (a division of subjects). Its cost is fatal: a suffering that never reaches the person of the Word is a suffering of someone other than the Son, and then the cross saves no one, for it is not God who gave himself. Cyril’s formula keeps the Antiochene truth — the Godhead does not suffer in its nature — while paying its debt: the one who suffers is God, in the flesh he made his own.

Assessment

Sound. As a statement of the communicatio idiomatum the theopaschite formula holds and is near-consensus among the Chalcedonian churches: “God suffered,” “one of the Trinity was crucified in the flesh,” are true because the one who suffered is the eternal Son who made a passible manhood his own, not because the divine nature became passible. Its soundness is conditional in a way worth stating precisely rather than hiding: it stands on the qualifier in the flesh and the person/nature distinction, and the same words, stripped of that guard and aimed at the deity as such or at the Trinity as a whole, become theopaschism proper — which the church condemned in Peter the Fuller’s Trisagion even as it dogmatized the Scythian formula. The paradox “the Same both suffers and does not suffer” is therefore not a contradiction the formula smuggles past us but the exact thing it means: suffering predicated of the person through the humanity, impassibility retained by the nature that is Godhead.
