Was Chalcedon a betrayal of Cyril?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — Chalcedon’s terminology (“in two natures”) really did depart from Cyril’s watchword and rehabilitate figures his party had fought, so the charge is strong on the letter; but that its substance abandoned Cyril is heavily disputed — the neo-Chalcedonian reading and the modern Orthodox dialogues judge the two confess one faith in two idioms · advanced by Severus of Antioch

The claim

… we must anathematize those who confine the one Christ in two natures and say that each of the natures performs its own acts… as the words of Leo’s impious letter state… «each of the forms effects… that which belongs to itself»… distributing the properties to the two natures severally, as one who divides the one and only Christ into two natures.

— Severus of Antioch, Letter I, trans. E. W. Brooks (1915).1 The interpretation this argument serves: that Chalcedon’s Definition — confessing one Christ “in two natures” (en duo physesin) and canonizing Leo’s Tome — capitulated to Antiochene dyophysitism and abandoned Cyril’s “one incarnate nature of the Word” (mia physis), so that Chalcedon, not miaphysitism, is the deviation from the faith of Ephesus and Nicaea.

Who advances it

The whole non-Chalcedonian (“Oriental Orthodox”) communion — Coptic, Syriac, Armenian, Ethiopian, and the churches of India — still some sixty million Christians, who never received Chalcedon. Its classic theological statement is Severus of Antioch (c. 465–538), whose Philalethes (c. 508–511) answered the pro-Chalcedonian Florilegium Cyrillianum point by point, arguing that its Cyril-extracts were selective and that the real Cyril was no two-nature theologian.2 Behind Severus stand Dioscorus of Alexandria and Timothy Aelurus; the charge has been pressed continuously since 451.

The evidence for

The historical-terminological case is genuinely strong, and much of it is simply true. (1) Cyril’s own watchword was “one incarnate nature of God the Word”; he used it as the test of orthodoxy against Nestorius, and Chalcedon pointedly did not adopt it, choosing instead the formula “in two natures” — a phrase Cyril never used and which reads far closer to the Antiochenes he had fought. (2) Leo’s Tome, which Chalcedon endorsed as “the voice of Peter,” says “each form does what is proper to it” — language that, taken alone, distributes Christ’s acts between the two natures as if between two agents, exactly the partition Cyril’s twelfth anathema had condemned. (3) Chalcedon rehabilitated Theodoret of Cyrus and Ibas of Edessa — open critics of Cyril’s anathemas — whom the Cyrilline party had condemned; that a council could restore Cyril’s antagonists while dropping his signature formula was, to the miaphysites, proof of where its sympathies lay. (4) The council’s own defenders half-conceded the point: within a century the emperor Justinian convened the Second Council of Constantinople (553) precisely to re-read Chalcedon “through Cyril’s eyes” — condemning the “Three Chapters” (Theodoret’s and Ibas’ anti-Cyrilline writings) and dogmatizing the theopaschite “one of the Trinity suffered in the flesh.” That neo-Chalcedonian correction is itself the strongest admission that Chalcedon, as it stood in 451, leaned away from Cyril.3

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The letter of the charge stands: Chalcedon declined Cyril’s “one incarnate nature,” adopted the “in two natures” formula he avoided, and restored men his party had anathematized. A miaphysite who measures fidelity by Cyril’s vocabulary has real ground. And the wiki grants his own christology is no crude Eutychianism: Severus preserves the natures’ difference and their properties, rejecting mixture — so his “one nature” is a composite one, close in substance to what Chalcedon meant.

What is overstated. The leap from “different words” to “betrayal of the faith” does not follow. (a) Chalcedon expressly built on Cyril: it received his Second Letter to Nestorius and the Formula of Reunion, confessed the hypostatic union and Theotokos, and condemned Nestorius by name — it did not “introduce” Nestorianism but re-excluded it. (b) Cyril himself had already signed the 433 Formula’s “union of two natures,” conceding two-nature language when the unity was safeguarded — so “in two natures” is not per se anti-Cyrilline. (c) The read of Leo’s “each form” as splitting the subject is uncharitable: Leo confesses “one and the same” Christ and the communicatio idiomatum as firmly as Cyril, and “in partnership with the other” names one agent’s two modes of operation, not two agents. (d) Decisively for the modern verdict: the twentieth-century Eastern–Oriental Orthodox dialogues, having each side read the other’s meaning rather than its slogans, jointly declared that the two families “have always loyally guarded the same and authentic… Christological Orthodox faith,” the difference being one of terminology, and that rejecting both Eutyches and Nestorius, “the acceptance or non-acceptance of the Council of Chalcedon does not entail the acceptance of either heresy.”4 That agreement is fatal to “betrayal” construed as a real doctrinal abandonment, though it leaves the terminological complaint standing.

Assessment

Contested. This is a genuinely disputed reading with strength and weakness sorted by discipline. As a claim about terminology and church politics it is strong — Chalcedon did set aside Cyril’s formula and honor his opponents, and its own party’s neo-Chalcedonian “correction” in 553 concedes the tilt. As a claim about substance — that Chalcedon abandoned the faith of Cyril and is the true deviation — it does not hold on its best case: the Definition keeps Cyril’s hypostatic union, Theotokos, and anti-Nestorian edge, and the modern dialogues, reading meaning past vocabulary, find one christology under two idioms. Both verdicts are honest at once, which is what “contested” names; the miaphysite reading is coherent and not to be dismissed, but so is the judgment that the split was, at bottom, over the counting of physis—words, not the reality confessed. (Severus’ sharpest strokes — that Leo’s Tome is “impious” and that Chalcedon simply is Nestorius — overreach, and are flagged on his page.)
