Did the Council of Chalcedon (451) depart from Cyril of Alexandria’s Christology?

Disputed reading · on The Person of Christ (one hypostasis, two natures) · witnesses: the Council of Chalcedon; Severus of Antioch

The passage

… we must anathematize those who confine the one Christ in two natures and say that each of the natures performs its own acts… as the words of Leo’s impious letter state… «each of the forms effects… that which belongs to itself»… distributing the properties to the two natures severally, as one who divides the one and only Christ into two natures.

— Severus of Antioch, Letter I, trans. E. W. Brooks (1915).1

…it receives the synodical letters of the Blessed Cyril, Pastor of the Church of Alexandria, addressed to Nestorius and the Easterns, judging them suitable, for the refutation of the frenzied folly of Nestorius, and for the instruction of those who long with holy ardour for a knowledge of the saving symbol. And, for the confirmation of the orthodox doctrines, it has rightly added to these the letter of the President of the great and old Rome, the most blessed and holy Archbishop Leo, which was addressed to Archbishop Flavian of blessed memory, for the removal of the false doctrines of Eutyches…

— The Definition of Faith of the Council of Chalcedon (451), trans. NPNF² vol. 14.2

The question

Chalcedon’s Definition confessed one Christ “in two natures” and received Leo’s Tome beside Cyril’s synodical letters, without adopting Cyril’s formula “one incarnate nature of God the Word.” Whether the Council thereby kept Cyril’s Christology or set it aside is the question on which the churches that received the Council and those that did not (today the Oriental Orthodox) divided, and Eastern Orthodox, Oriental Orthodox, Catholic and Protestant scholars still read the Acts of 451 differently.

The Council received Leo as teaching what Cyril taught and confessed Cyril’s faith

On this reading the Council received Leo’s Tome as teaching what Cyril taught, and its Definition confesses Cyril’s faith.

Held by: John Romanides (Greek Orthodox, Greek Orthodox Theological Review, 1965); Richard Price (Catholic, The Acts of the Council of Chalcedon, with Michael Gaddis, 2005/2007); Donald Fairbairn (Protestant, Gordon-Conwell Theological Seminary, Journal of Analytic Theology, 2022).3

The case begins from the Council’s own words. When Leo’s letter was read the bishops cried, “Peter has spoken thus through Leo. So taught the Apostles. Piously and truly did Leo teach, so taught Cyril. Everlasting be the memory of Cyril. Leo and Cyril taught the same thing, anathema to him who does not so believe,” and the Definition receives Cyril’s synodical letters first and then adds Leo’s letter to them.4 Romanides reads the minutes as showing that the Tome passed only by Cyril’s measure: passages of it were challenged as it was read and defended “by the use of parallel passages from Cyril”; Atticus of Nicopolis moved that it be compared with Cyril’s Twelve Chapters; and Theodoret and Ibas “were restored to the episcopacy because they accepted Ephesus I and especially the Twelve Chapters.” For Romanides the Definition received the Tome “in the light of and in subordination to the letters of Cyril,” and “the terminology and faith of Cyril were fully accepted.”5 Fairbairn quotes Richard Price, translator of the Acts: “their criterion of orthodoxy remained agreement with Cyril,” and “It is to misconceive the mood of the council to think of the Definition as attempting a synthesis between Cyril’s theology and that of the Antiochene school.” Fairbairn argues that the councils “understood themselves as moving in a straight line of development from one to another,” so that the Second Council of Constantinople’s reading of Chalcedon through Cyril (553) states what Chalcedon itself meant.6 On this reading the Definition’s “one and the same” Son, Only-begotten and Word is Cyril’s single subject, and “in two natures” guards against Eutyches the manhood Cyril also confessed.

Leo’s letter was received as a rule of faith in concord with the earlier councils, and Chalcedon perfected Ephesus

On this reading Leo’s letter, presented by the Roman legates and acclaimed as Peter’s voice, taught the faith of Nicaea, Constantinople and Ephesus, and the Definition perfected Ephesus by giving Cyril’s terms an exact sense.

Held by: Paschasinus of Lilybaeum (Roman legate at Chalcedon, 451); Pius XII (Catholic, Sempiternus Rex Christus, 1951); Francis Schaefer (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, “Council of Chalcedon”, 1908).7

Pius XII sets out the case from the conduct of the Council. The papal legates “took the first places among the bishops” and “by virtue of their delegated authority, they confirmed or rejected the decisions of the others.” When the emperor’s representatives asked for a new formula, Paschasinus replied that none was needed, since the creeds in use and the documents the Church had approved, Leo’s letter the chief of them, covered the ground: “the writings composed by the holy and apostolic Leo, pope of the universal Church, against the heresies of Nestorius and Eutyches, have already shown what the true faith is.” At the reading of the letter the bishops acclaimed it with the words “Peter has spoken through Leo” (the acclamation quoted above), and “all unanimously agreed that the document of the bishop of Rome fully and perfectly concorded with the creeds of Nicea and Constantinople.”8 For Pius XII the letter’s doctrine “differs in no way from that of the council of Ephesus”: its attribution of both natures’ acts “to the One Person of the Word” is the exchange of properties “which Cyril vindicated against Nestorius.” Cyril used “One Incarnate nature of the Word God” “with a perfectly correct meaning, since he used the term ‘nature’ to signify ‘person’,” and the Fathers of Chalcedon, applying to the Incarnation the terms of Trinitarian theology, “totally removed what was ambiguous or liable to cause error in these expressions.” Chalcedon did not correct Ephesus: “Rather the one perfected the other.”9 On this reading Leo’s letter and the Definition state one faith with Ephesus and with Cyril, in exact terms.

Chalcedon’s “in two natures” and Leo’s Tome set Cyril’s confession aside

On this reading the Council, by adopting “in two natures” and approving the Tome, left the Cyrilline norm of Ephesus (431).

Held by: Severus of Antioch (anti-Chalcedonian patriarch of Antioch, venerated by the Oriental Orthodox, Letter I and Philalethes, c. 508–512); Adolf von Harnack (Protestant church historian, History of Dogma, vol. IV, English trans. c. 1900); V. C. Samuel (Indian Orthodox, The Council of Chalcedon Re-Examined, 1977).10

Severus states the case from Leo’s own sentence. “Each of the forms effects in partnership with the other that which belongs to itself” distributes the properties “to the two natures severally,” so the Tome divides the one Christ. Severus confesses the union in hypostasis as “one nature of God the Word incarnate himself, as the holy Cyril also says,” in which “the difference also is preserved”; he accepts “from two natures” and refuses “in two natures” after the union.11 His Philalethes answered the Florilegium Cyrillianum, a dossier of extracts compiled to show that Cyril had spoken of two natures after the union.12 Samuel reads the Acts the same way. The bishops’ draft “from two natures” was replaced by “in two natures” with, “judging from the minutes,” no recorded discussion, and “the eastern critics of the council saw in it a betrayal of the already established norm of the faith.” Read by Alexandrine theologians, the Tome made the natures, not the one hypostasis, the agents of Christ’s words and deeds, “a real discrepancy” with Cyril’s letters; and the Definition “went beyond the Alexandrine premises in insisting that Christ is to be ‘acknowledged in two natures’.” Severus and his party, in Samuel’s account, quoted the fathers from Ignatius to Cyril “to show that the council of Chalcedon renounced it in favour of a position which in substance was Nestorian.”13 Harnack held that the majority at Chalcedon “wished for nothing else beyond the ratification of the Creeds of Nicæa and Ephesus as understood by Cyril,” yet accepted a formula “which was that of strangers, of the Emperor and the Pope, and which did not correspond to their belief”; in 553 “the East dictated to the West how this formula was to be understood.”14 On this reading the Cyrilline confession of Ephesus was carried forward by the churches that did not receive the Council.

The Definition joined Alexandrian, Antiochene and Western emphases

On this reading Chalcedon neither restated Cyril alone nor abandoned him, but set his stress on the one subject beside the Antiochene and Western stress on two complete natures.

Held by: Philip Schaff (Protestant church historian, History of the Christian Church, vol. III, 1884); R. V. Sellers (The Council of Chalcedon, 1953); Aloys Grillmeier (Catholic, Jesuit, Christ in Christian Tradition, vol. 1, 1975); Jaroslav Pelikan (Lutheran, The Christian Tradition, vol. 1, 1971) ⚠ unverified.15

Schaff reads the Definition as a boundary rather than a system. It “strikes the true mean between Nestorianism and Eutychianism”; “It accepts dyophysitism; and so far it unquestionably favored and satisfied the moderate Antiochian party rather than the Egyptian,” while teaching “with equal distinctness, in opposition to consistent Nestorianism, the inseparable unity of the person of Christ.” Its merit, for him, is that it “seeks to do justice alike to the distinction of the natures and to the unity of the person,” and he quotes F. R. Hasse: “By the Creed of Chalcedon justice has been done to both the Alexandrian and the Antiochian Christology.” Leo’s letter, which “takes a step beyond this, towards a theological interpretation of the doctrine,” for that reason “cannot have the same binding and normative force as the symbol itself.”16 Fairbairn describes the twentieth-century form of this reading, which he calls “synthesis of emphases.” In his account Sellers held that the Definition’s climactic paragraph was “deliberately drawn from different sources—Antiochene, Alexandrian, and Western—in order to bring together the distinctive contributions of different groups”; Grillmeier traced the “two physeis” formula to the Antiochene Formula of Reunion (433) and to the Tome, while finding that the Definition describes the incarnation as an event that happened to the Logos “in strongly Alexandrian/Cyrillian fashion”; and Pelikan saw Chalcedon as “a compromise formula uniting the partisans of opposing theories and as a basis for continuing development.”17 On this reading the Council kept Cyril’s one Son and Theotokos, took its two-nature language from Antioch and Rome, and so produced a settlement drawn from all three traditions.

One Cyrilline faith confessed in two terminologies

On this reading the Chalcedonian “in two natures” and the non-Chalcedonian “one incarnate nature” express the same faith of Cyril, so that receiving or not receiving the Council entails neither Nestorianism nor Eutychianism.

Held by: the unofficial consultation of Eastern and Oriental Orthodox theologians at Aarhus (1964); the Joint Commission of the Theological Dialogue between the Orthodox Church and the Oriental Orthodox Churches (Chambésy, 1990); John Behr (Orthodox, St Nersess Theological Review, 1998); Bishop Youssef (Coptic Orthodox, St Nersess Theological Review, 1998).18

At Aarhus fifteen theologians of the two families reported that in their study of Chalcedon Cyril’s mia physis formula “was at the centre of our conversations”: “On the essence of the Christological dogma we found ourselves in full agreement. Through the different terminologies used by each side, we saw the same truth expressed. Since we agree in rejecting without reservation the teaching of Eutyches as well as of Nestorius, the acceptance or non-acceptance of the Council of Chalcedon does not entail the acceptance of either heresy.” Both sides “found themselves fundamentally following the Christological teaching of the one undivided Church as expressed by St. Cyril,” and Chalcedon is “best understood in the light of the later Council of Constantinople (553).”19 The official Joint Commission set the two formulas side by side: the Oriental Orthodox “will continue to maintain their traditional Cyrillian terminology of ‘one nature of the incarnate Logos’,” which “the Orthodox also use,” and they agree “that the Orthodox are justified in their use of the two-natures formula, since they acknowledge that the distinction is ‘in thought alone’,” a use that “Cyril interpreted correctly” in his letters to John of Antioch, Acacius, Eulogius and Succensus. Both families “have always loyally maintained the same authentic Orthodox Christological faith… though they have used Christological terms in different ways.”20 Behr places the agreement in a longer reassessment of Severus: Bolotov held that Severus rejected “in two natures” as a “one-sided, clumsy choice of dogmatic words” rather than as heresy, and Lebon called the Severan Christology “absolutely correct and complete”; the dialogues rested on “a careful differentiation between the Council itself and the faith that it proclaimed.” Bishop Youssef notes that the statements distinguished an early Western reading of Chalcedon from the Eastern Orthodox one, “primarily based on the Fifth Council,” whose teaching “depended on the teachings of St Cyril.”21 On this reading the Definition, read with Cyril’s own distinction “in thought alone,” and the “one incarnate nature” confess the same Christ.
