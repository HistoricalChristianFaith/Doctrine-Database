Cyril of Alexandria on the person of Christ

Dates: c. 430 (the Nestorian controversy; patriarch 412–444) · Wikipedia
Summary: Patriarch of Alexandria (412–444), who against Nestorius taught that the Word was united to the flesh hypostatically: for Cyril, the Word did not merely dwell alongside a man but became man, so that there is one Christ, one Son — whence Mary is Theotokos and the impassible Word “suffered in the flesh.” His Second Letter to Nestorius was approved at Ephesus (431), and later readers of Chalcedon on every side of the dispute over it have appealed to him.

Claims & evidence

The Word was united to the flesh hypostatically — one Christ, one Son

Rather do we claim that the Word in an unspeakable, inconceivable manner united to himself hypostatically flesh enlivened by a rational soul, and so became man and was called son of man… two different natures come together to form a unity, and from both arose one Christ, one Son.

— Second Letter to Nestorius (Ep. 4), source. This letter, read aloud and approved at the Council of Ephesus (431), is Cyril’s central statement. The union is “hypostatic” — at the level of the person, which Cyril set against a union of two who stay two — “not by God’s will alone or good pleasure, nor by the assumption of a person alone.” It is the exact word Nestorius’s “conjunction” had avoided.

Mary is truly the Mother of God (Theotokos)

So have they dared to call the holy virgin, mother of God, not as though the nature of the Word or his godhead received the origin of their being from the holy virgin, but because there was born from her his holy body rationally ensouled, with which the Word was hypostatically united and is said to have been begotten in the flesh.

— Second Letter to Nestorius, source. Cyril grounds the title in the Incarnation: the Godhead does not originate from Mary, but because the one born of her is the Word made flesh, to deny her “Mother of God” is, for Cyril, to deny that her child is God. He made this the first of his Twelve Anathemas. His fuller treatment of the title is on the Theotokos timeline.

The impassible Word truly suffered in the flesh

Therefore even though He be said to have suffered in the flesh, freedom from suffering even thus is kept to Him in that He is conceived of as God.

— That Christ is One, trans. P. E. Pusey (LFC 47), source. Because the union is real, the properties of each nature belong to the one Christ — the communicatio idiomatum. So “it is the Lord of glory Himself Which was crucified for us,” yet the Godhead does not suffer as Godhead. His Scholia on the Incarnation makes the same guard: “we say that He was both crucified and died, the flesh suffering this, not the Word apart and by Himself, for He is Impassible and Immortal.” In 553 the Second Council of Constantinople made the confession of Christ crucified in the flesh as “one of the Holy Trinity” an anathema.

One Christ out of two, the natures distinct but unconfused

We must not then sever the One Lord Jesus Christ into Man separately and into God separately, but we say that Jesus Christ is One and the Same, yet knowing the distinction of the Natures and keeping them unconfused with one another.

— Scholia on the Incarnation, source. Cyril holds “One and the Same” together with “the distinction of the Natures… unconfused.” He also used the formula “one Incarnate Nature of the Word,” which he took to be Athanasian; its origin is discussed on the Apollinaris page. Pius XII wrote in 1951 that Cyril used “the term ‘nature’ to signify ‘person’” in it (Sempiternus Rex Christus, §25); the Oriental Orthodox churches keep it as their “traditional Cyrillian terminology,” and the Eastern Orthodox stated in 1990 that they “also use this terminology” (Joint Commission, Second Agreed Statement, §7). How the Definition of Chalcedon stands to Cyril is a disputed reading: Did the Council of Chalcedon (451) depart from Cyril of Alexandria’s Christology?

Reception and debate

  • Two years after Ephesus, Cyril accepted the 433 Formula of Reunion with the Antiochenes — “a union of two natures… one Christ, one Son, one Lord.” Adolf Harnack reports that some in Cyril’s own party “had never forgiven him the union of 433 which had led Cyril to agree to the expression ‘δύο φύσεις’” (History of Dogma, vol. IV, §2); the 1990 Joint Commission statement holds that Cyril “interpreted correctly” the two-natures usage in his letter to John of Antioch and in later letters.
  • Cyril’s Twelve Anathemas (esp. the first, on Theotokos, and the twelfth, on “the Word of God suffered in the flesh… was crucified in the flesh”) were rejected by the Antiochene party; John Romanides cites Ibas of Edessa’s letter to Mari the Persian for the Antiochenes’ rejection of Ephesus on account of them (Greek Orthodox Theological Review 10.2, 1965). The Second Council of Constantinople (553) anathematized the writings of Theodoret and Ibas against Cyril.
  • The quarrel with Nestorius itself — the letters, the Twelve Anathemas, the rival councils at Ephesus, the gold spent at court, and the exile — is told as a relationship across time on Cyril of Alexandria and Nestorius: the road to Ephesus, where Cyril has a detail page telling the arc from his side.
