Cyril of Alexandria on the person of Christ

Dates: c. 430 (the Nestorian controversy; patriarch 412–444) · Wikipedia
Summary: Patriarch of Alexandria and the architect of the doctrine of the hypostatic union. Against Nestorius he insisted that the Word did not merely dwell alongside a man but personally became man, so that there is one Christ, one Son — whence Mary is truly Theotokos and the impassible Word truly “suffered in the flesh.” His theology was ratified at Ephesus (431) and became the lens through which the East read Chalcedon.

Claims & evidence

The Word was united to the flesh hypostatically — one Christ, one Son

Rather do we claim that the Word in an unspeakable, inconceivable manner united to himself hypostatically flesh enlivened by a rational soul, and so became man and was called son of man… two different natures come together to form a unity, and from both arose one Christ, one Son.

— Second Letter to Nestorius (Ep. 4), source. This letter, read aloud and approved at the Council of Ephesus (431), is Cyril’s central statement. The union is “hypostatic” — at the level of the person, not a partnership of two who stay two — “not by God’s will alone or good pleasure, nor by the assumption of a person alone.” It is the exact word Nestorius’s “conjunction” had avoided.

Mary is truly the Mother of God (Theotokos)

So have they dared to call the holy virgin, mother of God, not as though the nature of the Word or his godhead received the origin of their being from the holy virgin, but because there was born from her his holy body rationally ensouled, with which the Word was hypostatically united and is said to have been begotten in the flesh.

— Second Letter to Nestorius, source. Cyril grounds the title strictly in the Incarnation, guarding exactly the point Nestorius feared: the Godhead does not originate from Mary; but because the one born of her is the Word made flesh, to deny her “Mother of God” is to deny that her child is God. He made this the first of his Twelve Anathemas. His fuller treatment of the title is on the Theotokos timeline.

The impassible Word truly suffered in the flesh

Therefore even though He be said to have suffered in the flesh, freedom from suffering even thus is kept to Him in that He is conceived of as God.

— That Christ is One, trans. P. E. Pusey (LFC 47), source. Because the union is real, the properties of each nature belong to the one Christ — the communicatio idiomatum. So “it is the Lord of glory Himself Which was crucified for us,” yet the Godhead does not suffer as Godhead. His Scholia on the Incarnation makes the same guard: “we say that He was both crucified and died, the flesh suffering this, not the Word apart and by Himself, for He is Impassible and Immortal.” The formula would be dogmatized by the Second Council of Constantinople (553): “one of the Holy Trinity… crucified in the flesh.”

One Christ out of two, the natures distinct but unconfused

We must not then sever the One Lord Jesus Christ into Man separately and into God separately, but we say that Jesus Christ is One and the Same, yet knowing the distinction of the Natures and keeping them unconfused with one another.

— Scholia on the Incarnation, source. Cyril’s own balance holds “one” and “the distinction of the natures… unconfused” together — which is why the Chalcedonian Definition could claim him as much as it claimed Leo. His most contested phrase, however, is the mia physis formula: “one Incarnate Nature of the Word.” He believed it Athanasian and used it in good faith; in fact it came from Apollinaris. Read against his own “unconfused” language it means one subject, not one nature in Chalcedon’s sense — but the words let the miaphysite churches claim him against Chalcedon.

Notes

  • Two years after Ephesus, Cyril signed the 433 Formula of Reunion with the Antiochenes — “a union of two natures… one Christ, one Son, one Lord” — conceding “two natures” language he had resisted in the heat of the fight. It is the bridge from his own theology to Chalcedon.
  • Cyril’s Twelve Anathemas (esp. the first, on Theotokos, and the twelfth, on “the Word of God suffered in the flesh… was crucified in the flesh”) were his sharpest polemic. The Antiochenes found the twelfth close to Apollinarian passibility; the neo-Chalcedonian Second Council of Constantinople (553) later vindicated it.
  • The single ambiguity that outlived him — the Apollinarian mia physis formula — is the seam the church tore along after Chalcedon; the miaphysite (“one-nature”) churches of Egypt and Syria held it against the “in two natures” of the Definition.
