John of Damascus on the person of Christ

Dates: c. 743 (lifespan c. 675–749) · Wikipedia
Summary: Book 3 of his An Exact Exposition of the Orthodox Faith sets out, in technical form, the two-natures teaching of the councils from Chalcedon to the Third Council of Constantinople: one compound subsistence of two perfect natures “without change or confusion or alteration or difference or separation”; the manhood real yet subsisting in the Word’s own hypostasis (the enhypostasia the timeline associates with Leontius); the mutual exchange of properties (communicatio idiomatum) through the natures’ interpenetration; and, following Maximus and the Third Council of Constantinople, two natural wills and two natural energies.

Claims & evidence

One compound subsistence of two perfect natures

“And therefore we hold that there has been a union of two perfect natures, one divine and one human; not with disorder or confusion, or intermixture, or commingling, as is said by the God-accursed Dioscorus and by Eutyches and Severus, and all that impious company… but by synthesis; that is, in subsistence, without change or confusion or alteration or difference or separation, and we confess that in two perfect natures there is but one subsistence of the Son of God incarnate.”

— An Exact Exposition of the Orthodox Faith 3.3, trans. S. D. F. Salmond (NPNF Series 2, vol. 9), source (also at New Advent). The passage restates the Definition’s terms: one subsistence (hypostasis), two perfect natures, and the four Chalcedonian negations, and names those it opposes. The natures are “united to each other in one compound subsistence,” yet each keeps its essential difference: “the created remaineth created, and the uncreated, uncreated.”

The manhood subsists in the Word’s hypostasis (enhypostasia)

“For the flesh of God the Word did not subsist as an independent subsistence, nor did there arise another subsistence besides that of God the Word, but as it existed in that it became rather a subsistence which subsisted in another, than one which was an independent subsistence. Wherefore, neither does it lack subsistence altogether, nor yet is there thus introduced into the Trinity another subsistence.”

— An Exact Exposition of the Orthodox Faith 3.9 (“In reply to the question whether there is Nature that has no Subsistence”), trans. Salmond, source (also at New Advent). The passage answers the objection that a complete human nature ought to be its own person, so that “two natures” would mean two sons. John’s reply is that Christ’s humanity is neither anhypostatos (without any subsistence, a mere illusion) nor a fourth hypostasis, but enhypostatos, subsisting in the subsistence of the Word. The timeline associates the concept with Leontius of Byzantium.

The exchange of properties through the natures’ interpenetration (communicatio idiomatum)

“Moreover, the Word appropriates to Himself the attributes of humanity: for all that pertains to His holy flesh is His: and He imparts to the flesh His own attributes by way of communication in virtue of the interpenetration of the parts one with another, and the oneness according to subsistence… Hence it is that the Lord of Glory is said to have been crucified, although His divine nature never endured the Cross.”

— An Exact Exposition of the Orthodox Faith 3.3, trans. Salmond, source. Because the union is real, whatever is true of either nature may be predicated of the one subject: “either nature giving in exchange to the other its own properties through the identity of the subsistence and the interpenetration of the parts with one another” (3.4). On this basis John speaks of “the Lord of Glory… crucified” (1 Corinthians 2:8) while holding that the divine nature does not suffer: the Word suffers in the flesh he made his own, not in his Godhead. The Second Council of Constantinople’s confession (553) of the crucified Christ as “one of the Holy Trinity” is on the timeline.

Two natural wills and two natural energies

“Since, then, Christ has two natures, we hold that He has also two natural wills and two natural energies. But since His two natures have one subsistence, we hold that it is one and the same person who wills and energises naturally in both natures… For we hold that wills and energies are faculties belonging to nature, not to subsistence.”

— An Exact Exposition of the Orthodox Faith 3.14 (“Concerning the volitions and free-will of our Lord Jesus Christ”), trans. Salmond, source (also at New Advent). John follows Maximus and the Third Council of Constantinople (680–81): will and operation attach to nature, not to person, so two natures entail two wills and two energies, yet the one person wills in both, the human will freely conforming, “without involving any opposition in judgment.”

Reception and debate

  • The Exact Exposition draws on Cyril’s anathemas, Leo’s Tome, Chalcedon’s Definition, the enhypostasia associated with Leontius, and Maximus’ two wills. Its later use as a textbook of the faith in the East and, in Latin translation, in the medieval West and by Aquinas is reported here without a source (⚠ unverified).
  • He also appears on the Filioque timeline (for the same Exposition, Book 1, on the procession of the Spirit), and on the veneration of the Cross, Theotokos, the real presence, and others.
