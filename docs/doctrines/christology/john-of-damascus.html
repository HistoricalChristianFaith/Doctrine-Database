John of Damascus on the person of Christ (the Eastern synthesis)

Dates: c. 743 (lifespan c. 675–749) · Wikipedia
Summary: The last of the Greek fathers gathered the whole Chalcedonian result into one textbook. Book 3 of his An Exact Exposition of the Orthodox Faith states, in settled technical form, the entire two-natures grammar the councils had hammered out over three centuries: one compound subsistence of two perfect natures “without change or confusion or alteration or difference or separation”; the manhood real yet subsisting in the Word’s own hypostasis (the enhypostasia the summary credits to Leontius); the mutual exchange of properties (communicatio idiomatum) through the natures’ interpenetration; and, following Maximus and the Third Council of Constantinople, two natural wills and two natural energies. He adds no new doctrine — he systematizes, and it is his synthesis, not the councils’ scattered acts, that carried the confession forward as the standard of the East.

Claims & evidence

One compound subsistence of two perfect natures

“And therefore we hold that there has been a union of two perfect natures, one divine and one human; not with disorder or confusion, or intermixture, or commingling, as is said by the God-accursed Dioscorus and by Eutyches and Severus, and all that impious company… but by synthesis; that is, in subsistence, without change or confusion or alteration or difference or separation, and we confess that in two perfect natures there is but one subsistence of the Son of God incarnate.”

— An Exact Exposition of the Orthodox Faith 3.3, trans. S. D. F. Salmond (NPNF Series 2, vol. 9), source (also at New Advent). This is Chalcedon’s Definition rendered as a school-thesis: one subsistence (hypostasis), two perfect natures, and the four Chalcedonian negations — against Eutyches’ confusion on one flank and Nestorius’ separation on the other. The natures are “united to each other in one compound subsistence,” yet each keeps its essential difference: “the created remaineth created, and the uncreated, uncreated.”

The manhood subsists in the Word’s hypostasis (enhypostasia)

“For the flesh of God the Word did not subsist as an independent subsistence, nor did there arise another subsistence besides that of God the Word, but as it existed in that it became rather a subsistence which subsisted in another, than one which was an independent subsistence. Wherefore, neither does it lack subsistence altogether, nor yet is there thus introduced into the Trinity another subsistence.”

— An Exact Exposition of the Orthodox Faith 3.9 (“In reply to the question whether there is Nature that has no Subsistence”), trans. Salmond, source (also at New Advent). This is the technical answer to the sharpest anti-Chalcedonian objection — a complete human nature ought to be its own person, so “two natures” would mean two sons. John states the settled reply: Christ’s humanity is neither anhypostatos (without any subsistence, a mere illusion) nor a fourth hypostasis, but enhypostatos — real, whole, and subsisting in the subsistence of the Word. The concept the christology summary traces to Leontius of Byzantium is here fixed as the standard grammar of the union.

The exchange of properties through the natures’ interpenetration (communicatio idiomatum)

“Moreover, the Word appropriates to Himself the attributes of humanity: for all that pertains to His holy flesh is His: and He imparts to the flesh His own attributes by way of communication in virtue of the interpenetration of the parts one with another, and the oneness according to subsistence… Hence it is that the Lord of Glory is said to have been crucified, although His divine nature never endured the Cross.”

— An Exact Exposition of the Orthodox Faith 3.3, trans. Salmond, source. Because the union is real, whatever is true of either nature may be predicated of the one subject: “either nature giving in exchange to the other its own properties through the identity of the subsistence and the interpenetration of the parts with one another” (3.4). This licenses the boldest Gospel speech — “the Lord of Glory… crucified” (1 Corinthians 2:8) — while guarding the divine nature from suffering: the Word suffers in the flesh he made his own, not in his Godhead. It is the mechanism behind the theopaschite formula the timeline weighs separately.

Two natural wills and two natural energies

“Since, then, Christ has two natures, we hold that He has also two natural wills and two natural energies. But since His two natures have one subsistence, we hold that it is one and the same person who wills and energises naturally in both natures… For we hold that wills and energies are faculties belonging to nature, not to subsistence.”

— An Exact Exposition of the Orthodox Faith 3.14 (“Concerning the volitions and free-will of our Lord Jesus Christ”), trans. Salmond, source (also at New Advent). John locks in the verdict Maximus won at the Third Council of Constantinople (680–81): will and operation attach to nature, not to person, so two natures entail two wills and two energies — yet the one person wills in both, the human will freely conforming, “without involving any opposition in judgment.” The Gethsemane obedience is real human willing, not an overridden manhood.

Notes

  • John adds nothing new here; his contribution is the synthesis. Where the doctrine was won in scattered acts — Cyril’s anathemas, Leo’s Tome, Chalcedon’s Definition, Leontius’ enhypostasia, and Maximus’ two wills — John of Damascus set the whole result down as one connected exposition, which is why his Exact Exposition became the standard textbook of the faith for the East (and, through its Latin translation, was quarried by the medieval West and by Aquinas).
  • He appears on this wiki also as a witness on the Filioque timeline (where the same Exposition, Book 1, builds the Eastern wall against a double procession of the Spirit), and on the veneration of the Cross, Theotokos, the real presence, and others — the last great synthesizer touching nearly every doctrine the councils had settled.
