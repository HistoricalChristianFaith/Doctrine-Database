Apollinaris of Laodicea on the person of Christ

Dates: c. 375 (the mature Apollinarian teaching; lifespan c. 310–390) · Wikipedia
Summary: Bishop of Laodicea and earlier an ally of Athanasius against the Arians. He taught that in the incarnate Lord the divine Word took the place of the human rational soul or mind, so that Christ’s flesh is human but his governing mind is the Word. The first canon of the Council of Constantinople (381) anathematized the heresy “of the Apollinarians” (Canon 1, trans. NPNF² vol. 14).

Claims & evidence

The divine Word replaces Christ’s human mind

The Word himself has become flesh, not having taken upon himself a human mind that is changeable and enslaved to sordid ways of thinking, but a mind that remains divine, an unchangeable and heavenly mind.

— Apollinaris, fragment (ed. H. Lietzmann, Apollinaris von Laodicea und seine Schule, 1904), trans. Early Church Texts. Gregory of Nazianzus, answering him, reports the reasoning of Apollinaris’s party: “He could not contain Two perfect Natures”; “the Godhead took the place of the human intellect”; and “our mind is subject to condemnation” (Letter 101, to Cledonius, NPNF² vol. 7). On this account Christ’s flesh and animal soul are human, and his rational, governing mind is the Logos itself.

“One incarnate nature of the Word”

The formula μίαν φύσιν τοῦ Λόγου σεσαρκωμένην (“one incarnate nature of the Word”) expresses a single-subject Christ. Its origin is disputed. The Wikipedia survey of miaphysitism reports that Cyril and his successor Dioscorus held such terminology to be present in Athanasius, Julius I of Rome and Gregory Thaumaturgus; it reports that this is disputed and, citing Mark DelCogliano and Bradley K. Storin (“Apollinarius of Laodicea, Selected Letters,” The Cambridge Edition of Early Christian Writings, vol. 3, 2022, pp. 308–321, ⚠ unverified), that the formula originated in the writings of Apollinaris. A number of Apollinaris’s writings are, in the words of the Wikipedia article on him, “concealed under the names of orthodox Fathers.” Pius XII wrote in 1951 that Cyril of Alexandria took the expression “from St. Athanasius” and used it “with a perfectly correct meaning” (Sempiternus Rex Christus, §25). V. C. Samuel reports R. V. Sellers’s argument that the critics of Chalcedon relied on “Apollinarian forgeries as patristic excerpts,” and replies that in the fifth and sixth centuries “Both sides quoted such passages” (The Council of Chalcedon Re-Examined, 1977, p. 200). The formula is kept by the Oriental Orthodox churches as their “traditional Cyrillian terminology” (Joint Commission, Second Agreed Statement, 1990, §7).

Reception and debate

  • Gregory of Nazianzus answered Apollinaris in Letter 101 to Cledonius: “that which He has not assumed He has not healed; but that which is united to His Godhead is also saved.” For Gregory, if the Word did not assume a human mind, the human mind is not redeemed; a Christ “without a human mind… is really bereft of mind, and quite unworthy of salvation.”
  • Apollinaris’s own works survive almost entirely as fragments quoted by his opponents; the critical Greek text is Lietzmann’s 1904 edition.
  • He also appears on the Theotokos timeline, where Gregory of Nazianzus’s insistence that Christ was “at once divinely and humanly formed” in the Virgin is quoted.
