Apollinaris of Laodicea on the person of Christ

Dates: c. 375 (the mature Apollinarian teaching; lifespan c. 310–390) · Wikipedia
Summary: Bishop of Laodicea and once Athanasius’s ally against the Arians, Apollinaris made the first great Christological error. To secure the unity of Christ he taught that in the incarnate Lord the divine Word took the place of the human rational soul or mind — a Christ who is God clothed in human flesh but lacking a human mind. Condemned at Constantinople (381), his error set the agenda for the two-natures debates that followed.

Claims & evidence

The divine Word replaces Christ’s human mind

The Word himself has become flesh, not having taken upon himself a human mind that is changeable and enslaved to sordid ways of thinking, but a mind that remains divine, an unchangeable and heavenly mind.

— Apollinaris, fragment (ed. H. Lietzmann, Apollinaris von Laodicea und seine Schule, 1904), trans. Early Church Texts. Apollinaris reasoned that two complete natures — each with its own mind and will — could only be two persons; so to keep Christ one, the changeable human mind (the seat of sin) must be replaced by the immutable Word. Christ’s flesh and animal soul are human; his rational, governing mind is the Logos itself.

“One incarnate nature of the Word”

Apollinaris coined the formula μίαν φύσιν τοῦ Λόγου σεσαρκωμένην (“one incarnate nature of the Word”) to express this single-subject Christ. Because his followers circulated it under Athanasius’s name, it later passed to Cyril of Alexandria, who used it in good faith believing it Athanasian — and it became the rallying cry of the miaphysite churches that rejected Chalcedon. The formula’s tainted origin is a running theme of the later controversy.

Notes

  • The classic refutation is Gregory of Nazianzus, Letter 101 to Cledonius, whose axiom governs all later Christology: “that which He has not assumed He has not healed; but that which is united to His Godhead is also saved.” If the Word did not assume a human mind, then the human mind — the very faculty that fell — is not redeemed; a Christ “without a human mind… is really bereft of mind, and quite unworthy of salvation.”
  • Apollinaris’s own works survive almost entirely as fragments quoted (and refuted) by his opponents; the critical Greek text is Lietzmann’s 1904 edition. His error is the mirror-image of the one Nestorius would later be accused of: Apollinaris fuses the natures until the manhood is incomplete, Nestorius divides them until the one Christ seems two.
  • He appears on the wiki also on the Theotokos timeline as the foil for Gregory of Nazianzus’s anti-Apollinarian insistence that Christ was “at once divinely and humanly formed” in the Virgin.
