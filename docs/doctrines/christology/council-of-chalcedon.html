The Council of Chalcedon on the person of Christ

Dates: 451 (the Fourth Ecumenical Council) · Wikipedia
Summary: The Fourth Ecumenical Council, convened by the emperor Marcian, fused the Alexandrian “one” of Cyril and the Latin “two” of Leo into the church’s enduring formula: one and the same Christ, in two natures, without confusion, change, division, or separation. It is the high-water mark of the doctrine — and the rock on which the miaphysite churches of Egypt and Syria broke away.

Claims & evidence

One and the same Christ, in two natures, unconfused and undivided

This one and the same Jesus Christ, the only-begotten Son [of God] must be confessed to be in two natures, unconfusedly, immutably, indivisibly, inseparably [united], and that without the distinction of natures being taken away by such union, but rather the peculiar property of each nature being preserved and being united in one Person and subsistence, not separated or divided into two persons, but one and the same Son and only-begotten, God the Word, our Lord Jesus Christ.

— The Definition of Chalcedon, trans. NPNF² vol. 14 (The Seven Ecumenical Councils, ed. H. R. Percival), source. The four adverbs each fence off a wrong turn: unconfusedly and immutably against Eutyches and Apollinaris (the natures are not blended, the manhood not changed into Godhead); indivisibly and inseparably against the division charged to Nestorius (the one person is not split into two). “One Person and subsistence” (prosopon and hypostasis) names the single subject; “the peculiar property of each nature being preserved” is Leo’s point; “in two natures” is the phrase the miaphysites could not accept.

Born of the Virgin, the Mother of God according to his manhood

…born [into the world] of the Virgin Mary, the Mother of God according to his manhood.

— The Definition of Chalcedon, source. The Council ratified Theotokos — already dogmatized at Ephesus (431) — but with Chalcedon’s careful qualifier “according to his manhood,” guarding Nestorius’s legitimate concern (the Godhead does not take its origin from Mary) inside Cyril’s confession (the one she bore is God the Word). The title’s own timeline is on the Theotokos page.

Notes

  • Chalcedon defined the “grammar” but left a puzzle its opponents pressed: if the human nature is complete and real, is it not a second person? The answer — that the manhood subsists in the hypostasis of the Word (enhypostasia) — was supplied a century later by Leontius of Byzantium and built into the neo-Chalcedonian synthesis of the Second Council of Constantinople (553).
  • The miaphysite (“one-nature,” often called Monophysite by opponents) churches — the Coptic, Syriac, and Armenian — read “in two natures” as a betrayal of Cyril’s “one incarnate nature” and never received the Council; the schism endures to this day, though modern dialogue has narrowed it toward a shared meaning behind the rival words.
  • Whether Chalcedon was faithful to Cyril or a capitulation to the West — the very charge the miaphysites pressed — is a genuinely contested question in its own right, distinct from the plain sense of the Definition traced here.
