Severus of Antioch on the person of Christ (the miaphysite voice)

Dates: c. 515 (the Philalethes and the miaphysite patriarchate; patriarch of Antioch 512–518; lifespan c. 465–538) · Wikipedia
Summary: The great theologian of the churches that refused Chalcedon. Holding strictly to Cyril’s “one incarnate nature of God the Word” (mia physis), Severus taught that Christ is from two natures but is, after the union, one nature — and judged that Chalcedon’s “in two natures” (and Leo’s Tome) split the one Christ into a Nestorian duality. Yet, against Eutyches, he insisted the natures’ difference is preserved in Christ’s properties — a real, not a verbal, monophysitism only in name. He is the timeline’s witness for the non-Chalcedonian communion, still some sixty million Christians today.

Claims & evidence

The union yields “one incarnate nature of God the Word” — Cyril’s formula, held strictly

… since the union in hypostasis is acknowledged, it follows that those which were united are not separated from one another; but there is one Son, and one nature of God the Word incarnate himself, as the holy Cyril also says… Those therefore who confess one incarnate nature of God the Word, and do not confuse the elements of which he consists, recognise also the propriety of those that were joined in union…

— Letter I (to Oecumenius the Count, “About Properties and Operations,” c. 508–12), trans. E. W. Brooks (1915). For Severus the union is hypostatic and therefore real: what the Word and the flesh brought to it “are not separated from one another,” so after the union there is one composite subject — “one nature of God the Word incarnate.” He takes the slogan to be Cyril’s own (as Cyril himself believed), which is precisely the seam Chalcedon would decline to follow, since the phrase in fact reached Cyril from Apollinaris.

Yet the difference of the natures is preserved — this is no Eutychianism

Flesh does not renounce its existence as flesh, even if it has become God’s flesh, nor has the Word departed from his nature, even if he has been hypostatically united to flesh which possesses a rational and intelligent soul: but the difference also is preserved, and the propriety in the form of natural characteristics of the natures of which Emmanuel consists, since the flesh was not converted into the nature of the Word, nor was the Word changed into flesh.

— Letter I, trans. Brooks. This is the decisive line for reading Severus rightly: he confesses that Christ is “of two natures” and that each keeps its own natural properties without confusion — expressly repudiating the mixture or absorption of Eutyches, whom he never defended. His “one nature” is a composite one, not a swallowing of the manhood; modern scholarship (Lebon, engaged by Grillmeier) accordingly describes his position as a “verbal” monophysitism — one that differs from Chalcedon over vocabulary far more than over the reality confessed.

“In two natures” divides the one Christ — so he anathematizes it

… we must anathematize those who confine the one Christ in two natures and say that each of the natures performs its own acts.

— Letter I, trans. Brooks. The objection is not to distinguishing the natures but to counting them “after the union”: once you say Christ is “in two natures” and let “each nature perform its own acts,” Severus argues, you have cut the one Christ into a duality and reopened the door to Nestorius—so the one who healed the lame and the one who walked the earth become two agents, not one incarnate Word. He accepts “from two natures” but refuses “in two natures.”

Leo’s Tome — the charter of Chalcedon — is “impious”

… as the words of Leo’s impious letter state in what he said: «For each of the forms effects in partnership with the other that which belongs to itself, the Word doing that which belongs to the Word, and the body performing the things which belong to the body»… Again the Tome of Leo says: «For each of the natures preserves its own property without diminution», distributing the properties to the two natures severally, as one who divides the one and only Christ into two natures.

— Letter I, trans. Brooks. Here the “betrayal of Cyril” charge is explicit at its root. Leo’s celebrated sentence — “each form does what is proper to it” — which the timeline records as the West’s decisive contribution and which Chalcedon endorsed, Severus reads as a Nestorian partition of the one acting subject into two. Whether the reading is fair is weighed on the argument page below.

The Philalethes: Chalcedon’s Cyrilline dress is a selective misuse of Cyril — ⚠ unverified (secondary)

Severus’ first major treatise, the Philalethes (“Friend of Truth” — the truth-friend being Cyril himself; c. 508–511), was written against the anonymous pro-Chalcedonian Florilegium Cyrillianum, a dossier of Cyril extracts assembled to show that Cyril too had spoken of “two natures after the union.” Severus’ aim was to prove that, by cherry-picking, the Chalcedonians had made the great Alexandrian out to be a two-nature theologian he never was — that Chalcedon, not miaphysitism, was the deviation. The Philalethes (ed. R. Hespel, CSCO 133–134) is not yet freely available online in English; the account here follows Grillmeier and the standard scholarship. No public-domain primary translation located.

Notes

  • Severus was patriarch of Antioch 512–518, then spent his last twenty years in exile in Egypt after the emperor Justin I turned against the anti-Chalcedonians (518); he died in 538. He was anathematized by name at the Second Council of Constantinople (553)’s milieu and in the later Eastern tradition — yet is venerated as a father and doctor by the Oriental Orthodox churches to this day.
  • His interpretation of Chalcedon is adjudicated on its own page: “Was Chalcedon a betrayal of Cyril?” (assessment contested).
  • The wiki reads Severus as a “verbal” monophysite: he preserves the difference of the natures and rejects Eutyches, so the quarrel with Chalcedon turns largely on whether physis is counted before or after the union. The twentieth-century Eastern–Oriental Orthodox dialogues (the 1989/1990 Chambésy agreed statements) concluded on this basis that the two families “have always loyally guarded the same and authentic… Christological Orthodox faith,” the difference being one of formula — a modern verdict that bears directly on the “betrayal” charge.
  • He is the counter-voice to Cyril, Leo, and Chalcedon on this timeline — the sophisticated defender of the reading that the Definition’s winners ruled against.
