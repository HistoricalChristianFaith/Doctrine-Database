Severus of Antioch on the person of Christ (the miaphysite voice)

Dates: c. 515 (the Philalethes and the miaphysite patriarchate; patriarch of Antioch 512–518; lifespan c. 465–538) · Wikipedia
Summary: Patriarch of Antioch (512–518), revered as a father by the churches that did not receive Chalcedon, today the Oriental Orthodox. Holding to Cyril’s “one incarnate nature of God the Word” (mia physis), Severus taught that Christ is from two natures and, after the union, one incarnate nature, and he held that Chalcedon’s “in two natures” and Leo’s Tome divided the one Christ. Against Eutyches he held that the difference of the natures is preserved in their natural properties.

Claims & evidence

The union yields “one incarnate nature of God the Word” — Cyril’s formula, held strictly

… since the union in hypostasis is acknowledged, it follows that those which were united are not separated from one another; but there is one Son, and one nature of God the Word incarnate himself, as the holy Cyril also says… Those therefore who confess one incarnate nature of God the Word, and do not confuse the elements of which he consists, recognise also the propriety of those that were joined in union…

— Letter I (to Oecumenius the Count, “About Properties and Operations,” c. 508–12), trans. E. W. Brooks (1915). For Severus the union is hypostatic and therefore real: what the Word and the flesh brought to it “are not separated from one another,” so after the union there is one composite subject — “one nature of God the Word incarnate.” He takes the formula from Cyril; its earlier origin is discussed on the Apollinaris page.

The difference of the natures is preserved

Flesh does not renounce its existence as flesh, even if it has become God’s flesh, nor has the Word departed from his nature, even if he has been hypostatically united to flesh which possesses a rational and intelligent soul: but the difference also is preserved, and the propriety in the form of natural characteristics of the natures of which Emmanuel consists, since the flesh was not converted into the nature of the Word, nor was the Word changed into flesh.

— Letter I, trans. Brooks. Severus confesses that Christ is “of” two natures and that each keeps its own natural properties: “the flesh was not converted into the nature of the Word, nor was the Word changed into flesh.” John Behr reports that after 518 Severus also wrote against non-Chalcedonians whom he judged to have misunderstood Cyril’s mia physis Christology, Julian of Halicarnassus and Sergius, and that Joseph Lebon judged the Christology of the non-Chalcedonians represented by Severus “absolutely correct and complete” while continuing to call it “monophysitism” (“Severus of Antioch: Eastern and Oriental Orthodox Perspectives,” St. Nersess Theological Review 3, 1998). The description of his position as a “verbal” monophysitism, credited to Lebon in Grillmeier’s account, is ⚠ unverified here.

The anathema on “in two natures”

… we must anathematize those who confine the one Christ in two natures and say that each of the natures performs its own acts.

— Letter I, trans. Brooks. The objection is not to distinguishing the natures but to counting them “after the union”: once you say Christ is “in two natures” and let “each nature perform its own acts,” Severus argues, you have cut the one Christ into a duality and reopened the door to Nestorius. His own example in the letter: “to walk and travel in bodily form upon the earth is without contention human; but to bestow on those who are maimed in the feet and cannot walk upon the ground at all the power of walking like sound persons is God-befitting. Yet the one Word incarnate performed the latter and the former, and the one nature did not perform the one, and the other the other.” He accepts “from two natures” but refuses “in two natures.”

Severus on Leo’s Tome

… as the words of Leo’s impious letter state in what he said: «For each of the forms effects in partnership with the other that which belongs to itself, the Word doing that which belongs to the Word, and the body performing the things which belong to the body»… Again the Tome of Leo says: «For each of the natures preserves its own property without diminution», distributing the properties to the two natures severally, as one who divides the one and only Christ into two natures.

— Letter I, trans. Brooks. Severus reads Leo’s sentence “each form does what is proper to it,” received at Chalcedon with the Tome, as a partition of the one acting subject into two. Other readings of the sentence are given on the Leo page and in the disputed reading Did the Council of Chalcedon (451) depart from Cyril of Alexandria’s Christology?

The Philalethes and the Florilegium Cyrillianum

Severus’ first major treatise, the Philalethes (“Friend of Truth”; c. 508–511), was written, in John Behr’s description, “against the anonymous pro-Chalcedon Florilegium Cyrillianum, a list of extracts from the writings of Cyril attempting to demonstrate that Cyril had spoken of two natures after the union” (Behr, 1998). Severus’ aim, in the accounts of Grillmeier and of Allen and Hayward (not opened here), was to show that the collection had cited Cyril selectively ⚠ unverified. The Philalethes (ed. R. Hespel, CSCO 133–134) has no freely available English translation.

Reception and debate

  • Severus was patriarch of Antioch 512–518, then spent his last twenty years in exile in Egypt after the emperor Justin I turned against the anti-Chalcedonians (518); he died in 538. Behr reports that in the winter of 534/5 Severus attended Justinian’s conferences at Constantinople and that he and Anthimus “managed to come to an agreement on the substance of Christology,” before Anthimus resigned the patriarchal throne in 536 (Behr, 1998). Each side condemned teachers the other revered; the unofficial Eastern and Oriental Orthodox consultation at Geneva (1970) asked that such condemnations be discontinued “as in the case of Leo, Dioscurus, Severus, and others” (Geneva, 1970). He is revered as a father by the Oriental Orthodox churches.
  • His charge against Chalcedon is set out as a disputed reading: Did the Council of Chalcedon (451) depart from Cyril of Alexandria’s Christology?
  • The Aarhus consultation of Eastern and Oriental Orthodox theologians (1964) stated that “the acceptance or non-acceptance of the Council of Chalcedon does not entail the acceptance of either heresy” (Aarhus, 1964), and the Joint Commission (Chambésy, 1990) that both families “have always loyally maintained the same authentic Orthodox Christological faith, and the unbroken continuity of the apostolic tradition, though they have used Christological terms in different ways” (Second Agreed Statement, §9).
