Nestorius on the person of Christ

Dates: c. 429 (the outbreak of the controversy; lifespan c. 386–451) · Wikipedia
Summary: Patriarch of Constantinople, formed in the Antiochene tradition, whose ranking of the title Theotokos below Christotokos opened his controversy with Cyril of Alexandria. He taught that the two natures keep their own properties in an “unconfused conjunction” in the one person; his opponents charged him with dividing the one Christ into two sons, and in the Bazaar of Heracleides, written in exile, he denied teaching any such division.

Claims & evidence

“Christotokos” is preferable to “Theotokos”

In the case of the term Theotokos, I am not opposed to those who want to say it, unless it should advance to the confusion of natures in the manner of the madness of Apollinaris or Arius. Nonetheless, I have no doubt that the term Theotokos is inferior to the term Christotokos, as the latter is mentioned by the angels and the gospels.

— Letters to Celestine (the third letter), source. Nestorius feared that “God-bearer” either made the eternal Godhead take its origin from a creature or fused the natures the way Apollinaris had; “Christ-bearer,” naming the united person rather than a single nature, seemed to him to “keep the assertion of both parties to the proper limits.”

The two natures joined in an “unconfused conjunction” in one person

…each nature is adored through an unconfused conjunction of the highest sort in the single person of the only-begotten.

— Letters to Celestine (the second letter), source. The stress falls on keeping the natures unmixed: the “bodily passions” must not “pour over into the divinity of the only-begotten.” Where Cyril spoke of a hypostatic union — a becoming-one at the level of the person — Nestorius’s word was conjunction (Greek synápheia).

“We have not two Christs or two sons”

Christ in that [he is] Christ is not divided; for we have not two Christs or two sons, for there is not with us a first and a second Christ nor one and another, nor again one son and again another; but the son is double not by authority but by nature.

— Bazaar of Heracleides, fragment 292, source. Written in exile long after his condemnation, the Bazaar is Nestorius’s sustained self-defence. Fragment 312 repeats the point: “we know not two Christs or two sons… but one and the same, who is visible in the uncreated and the created nature.” Whether he was condemned for a Christology he did not hold is set out as a disputed reading on the Theotokos timeline: Was Nestorius condemned at Ephesus for a Christology he did not hold?.

Reception and debate

  • Nestorius’s reply-letter to Cyril (his “second letter,” the formal refusal of Theotokos) is not among the works surveyed here; it survives among the Ephesus council documents. The quotations above are from the letters to Pope Celestine and the Bazaar.
  • He also appears on the Theotokos timeline, where his ranking of the title is quoted, and the question of his condemnation is set out at Was Nestorius condemned at Ephesus for a Christology he did not hold?.
  • The quarrel with Cyril itself — the exchange of letters, his appeal to Rome, the council he refused to attend, his deposition and exile to the Egyptian desert, and the Bazaar of Heracleides he wrote there — is told as a relationship across time on Cyril of Alexandria and Nestorius: the road to Ephesus, where Nestorius has a detail page telling the arc from his side.
