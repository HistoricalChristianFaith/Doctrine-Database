Nestorius on the person of Christ

Dates: c. 429 (the outbreak of the controversy; lifespan c. 386–451) · Wikipedia
Summary: Antiochene patriarch of Constantinople whose objection to the title Theotokos ignited the crisis. He guarded the distinction of the two natures so strictly — each keeping its own properties in an “unconfused conjunction” — that his opponents charged him with splitting the one Christ into two sons. He protested to the end that he taught no such division; his own defence survives in the Bazaar of Heracleides.

Claims & evidence

“Christotokos” is preferable to “Theotokos”

In the case of the term Theotokos, I am not opposed to those who want to say it, unless it should advance to the confusion of natures in the manner of the madness of Apollinaris or Arius. Nonetheless, I have no doubt that the term Theotokos is inferior to the term Christotokos, as the latter is mentioned by the angels and the gospels.

— Letters to Celestine (the third letter), source. Nestorius feared that “God-bearer” either made the eternal Godhead take its origin from a creature or fused the natures the way Apollinaris had; “Christ-bearer,” naming the united person rather than a single nature, seemed to him to “keep the assertion of both parties to the proper limits.”

The two natures joined in an “unconfused conjunction” in one person

…each nature is adored through an unconfused conjunction of the highest sort in the single person of the only-begotten.

— Letters to Celestine (the second letter), source. The stress falls on keeping the natures unmixed: the “bodily passions” must not “pour over into the divinity of the only-begotten.” Where Cyril spoke of a hypostatic union — a becoming-one at the level of the person — Nestorius’s word was conjunction (Greek synápheia), which his critics heard as a mere joining of two who remain two.

“We have not two Christs or two sons”

Christ in that [he is] Christ is not divided; for we have not two Christs or two sons, for there is not with us a first and a second Christ nor one and another, nor again one son and again another; but the son is double not by authority but by nature.

— Bazaar of Heracleides, fragment 292, source. Written in exile long after his condemnation, the Bazaar is Nestorius’s sustained self-defence. Fragment 312 repeats the point: “we know not two Christs or two sons… but one and the same, who is visible in the uncreated and the created nature.” Modern scholarship is divided over whether Nestorius was in fact a “Nestorian” — whether his “conjunction” language really denied the personal union, or whether he was condemned for a Christology he did not hold. (The wiki weighs that question on the “Was Nestorius misrepresented?” argument page.)
