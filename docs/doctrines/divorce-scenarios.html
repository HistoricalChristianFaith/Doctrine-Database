Divorce and remarriage: the evidence under competing scenarios

The other pages of this doctrine either ask who believed what across time (the divorce timeline, Hermas to Amoris Laetitia) or weigh a single proof-text or crux on its own merits (the exception clause, the indissolubility thesis, the Pauline privilege, and the rest). This page does something different: it fixes a whole bundle of presuppositions — a “scenario” — and then reads the same body of evidence straight through under each, to see what that reading gets for free, what it must pay for, and what it simply cannot absorb. Three scenarios are weighed, the three historic answers the church actually gave: (A) Latin/Catholic indissolubility, (B) the Erasmian–Protestant dissolution reading, and (C) the Eastern Orthodox practice of pastoral oikonomia. The mainstream historical-critical reading of the texts is not a scored column but the neutral baseline the linked argument pages already carry, against which the three bundles are measured.

Granting a scenario is not proving it. The point is the opposite of apologetics: hold a bundle fixed, force it to account for all the evidence at once, and publish the bill — how many independent texts it must read against their plain grain, and what residue it leaves unexplained even on its own terms. A scenario can come out carrying more than it can pay, and a ledger may conclude against a bundle. Each item is scored under every scenario into one of four buckets, and the base facts and neutral ratings live on the linked pages — this page holds only the delta and the bill.

The scenarios

Each scenario is a spelled-out set of commitments with a bill — the specific texts it must read a certain way and what would break it — not a mood or a label. All three are normative church frameworks that read the one shared shelf of Gospel and Pauline texts differently; the note “Why these three columns” below says why the historical-critical reading is folded into the baseline rather than scored as a fourth.

Scenario A — Latin/Catholic indissolubility

Grants: the marriage of two baptized persons is an ontological sacramentum — a sacred, permanent sign of the union of Christ and the Church (Ephesians 5:31–32) — which, once ratified and consummated, “can be dissolved by no human power and by no cause, except death.”1 Matthew’s exception (19:9) licenses only separation from bed and board, never a new marriage while the first spouse lives; the three exceptionless witnesses (Mark 10:11–12, Luke 16:18, and Paul’s own relay of the Lord’s charge at 1 Corinthians 7:10–11) govern the reading; and the Pauline privilege (1 Corinthians 7:15) dissolves only non-sacramental unions in which one or both partners are unbaptized. It was fixed as dogma at Trent (1563) and restated in the sharpest terms by the 1983 Code of Canon Law.2 Bill: it must read Matthew’s twice-stated exception as licensing less than it appears to say; it must account for the innocent party — the wronged spouse of a proven adulterer, barred for life from remarriage though guilty of nothing (the case Origen called “something monstrous” and Erasmus pressed pastorally); and it must hold the entire ancient, continuous Eastern remarriage practice — which Trent’s canon 7 was deliberately reworded not to anathematize — a sustained departure from divine law.

Scenario B — the Erasmian–Protestant dissolution reading

Grants: adultery (porneia) dissolves the bond and frees the innocent party to marry again — Matthew’s exception governs the whole divorce-and-remarry conditional, not the dismissal alone (Erasmus, and D. A. Carson’s “govern the entire protasis”) — and desertion by an unbelieving spouse likewise dissolves it, the “Pauline privilege” of “is not under bondage” (1 Corinthians 7:15), so the innocent and the deserted may both remarry (the Westminster Confession 24.5–6). Underneath sits the covenant model of marriage: a relationship of sworn obligation which, like other covenants, treachery can break.3 Bill: it must subordinate the three exceptionless witnesses — Mark, Luke, and Paul’s direct relay of the Lord’s charge — to Matthew’s single clause; it must scope 1 Corinthians 7:11’s “let her remain unmarried” to a non-adultery case so it does not reach the innocent party; and it must hold the near-unanimous strict patristic reception mistaken for some fifteen centuries.4

Scenario C — the Eastern Orthodox oikonomia practice

Grants: at the level of the canon (akribeia, strictness) the bond is indissoluble and the sayings are exceptionless — C reads the text essentially as A does — but pastoral “economy” (oikonomia), condescending to human weakness, permits a penitent to remarry after a divorce (for adultery and, by extension, other grave causes), under a penitential rite and up to a limit. Its lineage runs from the bishops Origen reports tolerating remarriage, through Basil’s frankly-admitted “custom” and Epiphanius’ “concession to weakness,” to the graded penance of the Council in Trullo (692) and the modern Orthodox discipline.5 Bill: it must hold that economy can relax what its own akribeia calls the Lord’s standard without dispensing from divine law — the standing objection that economy reaches only ecclesiastical, not divine, law; its practice permits precisely what 1 Corinthians 7:11 forbids the separated; and it grew entangled with the swinging imperial civil law of Justinian, its own defenders conceding it became “accommodation with the powers-that-be.”6

Why these three columns, and where the critical reading lives

This page scores the three normative frameworks that actually reorganize the shared shelf — each reads the same Gospel and Pauline texts into a different total settlement of what a Christian may do. It does not carry a fourth, historical-critical column, because the mainstream reading of the texts — that the earliest tradition (Mark, Luke, Paul) transmits an absolute prohibition, that Matthew alone added the exception, and that Jesus may have entered the Shammai–Hillel quarrel — is a descriptive layer the linked argument pages already establish, not a rival rule for what the church should practice.7 That reading is the backdrop against which the three bundles are measured, and it does not sit neutrally between them: its “exceptionless original” is congenial to A and C (whose akribeia reads the sayings the same way), while its “Matthew added the clause” verdict cuts against B, whose founding text is that very clause. Folding the critical reading into the baseline keeps the comparison where the interest is — among the three total frameworks that read the same data differently — rather than re-litigating a source- critical verdict the linked pages settle on their own evidence.

The bill (per scenario)

The honesty floor: for each live bundle, what it still cannot explain (✗), and how many independent texts it must read against their plain grain (△) — whether those costs share one mechanism (correlated) or are a scatter of independent moves (ad hoc). The friendly bundle is shown beside its bill.

Scenario A — indissolubility

Unexplained (✗): two residues, both ecclesial rather than textual. The innocent-party problem — the wronged spouse of a proven adulterer, guilty of nothing, condemned either to lifelong celibacy or to the name of adulterer if they remarry (Origen’s “something monstrous”); and the whole Eastern remarriage practice, ancient and continuous from Origen’s tolerating bishops onward, which A must judge a sustained error even though Trent pointedly declined to condemn it. Texts it must read against the grain (△): essentially two, and largely correlated under one mechanism — the sacramentum governs and the three exceptionless witnesses control Matthew, so both Matthew’s twice-stated exception (read as separation-only) and the ambiguity of 19:9 resolve together. The one more ad hoc lever is the baptismal distinction that confines the Pauline privilege (1 Corinthians 7:15) to non-sacramental unions — a line the text itself does not draw — and the modern Amoris Laetitia development adds an internal-consistency strain. So A’s textual bill is small and mostly correlated; its exposure is the two residues, not a scatter of exegetical excuses.

Scenario B — Erasmian dissolution

Unexplained (✗): the near-unanimous strict patristic reception. B must hold that the church misread Matthew’s exception from Hermas to the Reformation, resting the correction on a thin Latin minority (Ambrosiaster, the Gallic councils) — a fifteen-century residue no reading of a single clause dissolves. Texts it must read against the grain (△): three exceptionless witnesses at once — Mark, Luke, and Paul’s direct relay of the Lord’s charge (1 Corinthians 7:10–11, including the scoping of “remain unmarried”). These are strikingly correlated: one mechanism absorbs them all — Matthew preserves the fuller (or assumed) qualification the others compressed. But that virtue has a sharp edge: the same mechanism sits in tension with the majority source-critical verdict that Matthew added the clause, which, if granted, removes its dominical authority — the exact opposite pressure. B’s bill is therefore a single, elegant, correlated claim that is nonetheless squeezed from two sides at once (the reception residue, and the added-clause verdict), which is its real exposure.

Scenario C — Orthodox oikonomia

Unexplained (✗): 1 Corinthians 7:11. C’s practice permits exactly what this Lord-attributed charge forbids the separated party; on C’s own akribeia — which grants the bar as the Lord’s standard — the remarriage-permission dispenses from what it calls divine, not merely ecclesiastical, law, and economy by definition cannot reach divine law. C absorbs the residue only through the contested “the bond can die” theology; without it, the practice contradicts the canon it affirms. Texts it must read against the grain (△): essentially none — C grants A’s strict exegesis wholesale, so its △ column on the texts is empty. Its cost is not textual violence but principle and history: the akribeia/economy gap (its practice overrides the canon it holds), and its entanglement with Justinian’s swinging civil law, its own defenders conceding it became “accommodation with the powers-that-be.” C is thus the cheapest on textual violence and the most exposed on principle — the mirror image of B.

The comparative verdict

What follows is a coherence-and-cost accounting, not a proof of any bundle. Reading a text the most naturally does not make a framework true, and carrying a large bill does not make one false — a scenario could be correct and still owe more than the present evidence lets it pay, and the texts are only one axis of a question that also has theological and pastoral dimensions this page does not weigh. What the ledger can say honestly is which framework the textual and historical evidence sits easiest under, which leaves the largest residue, and whether each one’s costs are a single correlated claim or a scatter of independent ones.

The three do not fail or hold in the same currency. Scenario A pays the smallest textual bill — two mostly-correlated readings governed by the sacramentum and the exceptionless witnesses, plus one more ad hoc baptismal line on the Pauline privilege — but carries two residues that are not textual at all: the innocent party barred though guiltless, and a whole ancient Eastern practice it must call error where Trent would not. Scenario B reads Matthew’s exception the most naturally and answers the innocent-party problem outright, but pays the heaviest textual bill — three independent exceptionless witnesses subordinated to one clause — and cannot absorb the fifteen-century strict reception; its bill’s structural elegance (one correlated mechanism) is also its exposure, squeezed between “the others abbreviated” and “Matthew added it.” Scenario C pays almost nothing in textual violence — it grants the strict exegesis entire — yet carries the sharpest principled residue of the three: it permits what its own canon, on its own account of that canon’s authority, forbids. None of this settles which bundle is true. That turns on the indissolubility question, on the relative weight of Matthew’s single sentence against the three exceptionless parallels (the remarriage-or-separation crux), and on the theology of the bond (the covenant-or-ontology model) — each weighed on its own page, and each deliberately left unadjudicated here. What the accounting delivers is the price tag on each total reading of the same texts, and the currency each pays it in, laid where it can be seen.
