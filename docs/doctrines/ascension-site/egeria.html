Egeria on the Imbomon (the place of the Ascension)

Dates: c. 384 (the Itinerarium Egeriae, c. 381–384) · Wikipedia
Summary: The Western nun whose diary gives the fullest firsthand account of 4th-century Holy-Land liturgy is the first source to name the Imbomon — “the place whence the Lord ascended into heaven” — and to show it as a fixed liturgical station, distinct from the Eleona lower down, and still a “place” rather than a church (the round church was built there only a few years later).

Claims & evidence

The Imbomon named — “the place whence the Lord ascended” (c. 384)

And when the ninth hour approaches they go up with hymns to the Imbomon, that is, to the place whence the Lord ascended into heaven, and there they sit down, for all the people are always bidden to sit when the bishop is present; the deacons alone always stand.

— The Pilgrimage of Egeria (the Palm Sunday afternoon procession, ch. 31), trans. M. L. McClure & C. L. Feltoe. The gloss — “the Imbomon, that is, the place whence the Lord ascended into heaven” — recurs verbatim each time she names it (Palm Sunday, Holy Thursday, the Easter octave, and Pentecost), a fixed liturgical formula by the 380s.

Distinguished from the Eleona; the Ascension Gospel read there at Pentecost

…both in the church which is on Eleona… and also in the Imbomon, that is, in the place whence the Lord ascended into heaven.

…they first enter the Imbomon, that is, the place whence the Lord ascended into heaven… The passage from the Gospel is also read where it speaks of the Lord's Ascension, also that from the Acts of the Apostles which tells of the Ascension of the Lord into heaven after His Resurrection.

— The Pilgrimage of Egeria, the Easter-octave and Whitsunday (Pentecost) offices (chs. 39 and 43), trans. McClure & Feltoe. Egeria keeps the two Olivet sites firmly apart — “the church which is on Eleona” (Constantine's teaching-cave church) and “the Imbomon” (the higher ascension-spot) — and it is at the Imbomon, on Pentecost, that the Ascension passages of Luke and Acts are read on the very ground.

What it attests — and what it does not

With Egeria the ascension-spot proper first steps out from the teaching-cave. Eusebius had run cave, teaching, and ascension together; Egeria names a distinct upper station, the Imbomon, and embeds it in a developed stational liturgy that reads each scene on its own ground. Two subtleties are worth keeping. First, she still calls it a “place,” never a church — consistent with the round Church of the Ascension being built there only around 384–390, by the pilgrim Poemenia, at or just after her visit. Second, and often missed: Egeria's observance on the fortieth day after Easter — the day the church would later keep as Ascension — is held not at the Imbomon but at Bethlehem; the Ascension narrative is read at the Imbomon on Pentecost. The site is fixed and richly used by the 380s; the Ascension feast as later Christians know it is not yet localized on the spot.
