Eusebius of Caesarea on the Ascension site (the Mount of Olives cave)

Dates: c. 314–339 (the Proof of the Gospel c. 314–318; the Life of Constantine c. 337–339) · Wikipedia
Summary: The bishop-historian of Caesarea gives the earliest extant attestation, among the sources on this timeline, of a shown Ascension site on the Mount of Olives: before the Constantinian churches he reports a cave “shewn there” where believers gather from all the world, tied to Zechariah 14:4; and he records Helena's church raised “on the very summit” in memory of the Ascension — the same church later sources call the Eleona.

Claims & evidence

The cave “shewn there” and the feet on Olivet — before any church (c. 314–318)

Which it is possible for us to see literally fulfilled in another way even to-day, since believers in Christ all congregate from all parts of the world, not as of old time because of the glory of Jerusalem, nor that they may worship in the ancient Temple at Jerusalem, but they rest there that they may learn both about the city being taken and devastated as the prophets foretold, and that they may worship at the Mount of Olives opposite to the city, whither the glory of the Lord migrated when it left the former city.

There stood in truth according to the common and received account the feet of our Lord and Saviour, Himself the Word of God, through that tabernacle of humanity He had borne up the Mount of Olives to the cave that is shewn there; there He prayed and delivered to His disciples on the summit of the Mount of Olives the mysteries of His end, and thence He made His Ascension into heaven, as Luke tells us in the Acts of the Apostles…

— The Proof of the Gospel (Demonstratio Evangelica), Book VI, ch. 18 (on Zechariah 14:4), trans. W. J. Ferrar (1920). Writing before the Constantinian building programme, Eusebius treats a Mount-of-Olives cave as the received place where Christ prayed, taught the disciples, and ascended, and ties it to Zechariah's prophecy that “his feet shall stand… upon the mount of Olives.” In this passage one cave holds the teaching, the prayer, and the Ascension together.

Helena's church “on the very summit,” in memory of the Ascension (c. 337–339)

In the one case, he rendered due honor to that which had been the scene of the first manifestation of our Saviour's divine presence, when he submitted to be born in mortal flesh; while in the case of the second cavern he hallowed the remembrance of his ascension to heaven from the mountain top.

And farther, the mother of the emperor raised a stately structure on the Mount of Olives also, in memory of his ascent to heaven who is the Saviour of mankind, erecting a sacred church and temple on the very summit of the mount. And indeed authentic history informs us that in this very cave the Saviour imparted his secret revelations to his disciples.

— Life of Constantine, Book III, chs. 41 and 43, trans. E. C. Richardson, NPNF2 vol. 1. Eusebius pairs the Bethlehem grotto and the Olivet cave as Helena's two foundations, and ties the second to “his ascent to heaven.” The building he describes over the cave where, he writes, “the Saviour imparted his secret revelations to his disciples” is the church the Bordeaux Pilgrim sees in 333 and Egeria calls the church “on Eleona.”

Placement on the timeline

Eusebius's two works record two things. First, before the Constantinian churches, a Mount-of-Olives cave shown as the place of the Ascension, which he gives “according to the common and received account” — the earliest extant datable attestation of the site among the sources on this timeline. Second, Helena's church on the summit over that cave. Later sources name three separate commemorations on Olivet: the teaching-church (Eleona), the ascension-rotunda (the Imbomon), and the footprint-relic.

Notes

  • Eusebius is also a witness on the timelines of the neighbouring Passion sites — the tomb of Jesus, the Garden of Gethsemane, and Bethany & Bethphage.
  • Egeria's account (c. 384) distinguishes the Eleona (teaching-cave church) from the Imbomon; Eusebius's names a single cave.
