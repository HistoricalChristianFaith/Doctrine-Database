Eusebius of Caesarea on the Ascension site (the Mount of Olives cave)

Dates: c. 315–338 (the Proof of the Gospel c. 314–318; the Life of Constantine c. 337–339) · Wikipedia
Summary: The bishop-historian of Caesarea is the earliest witness to a shown Ascension site on the Mount of Olives: before Constantine he already reports a cave “shewn there” where believers gather from all the world, tied to Zechariah 14:4; and he records Helena's church raised “on the very summit” in memory of the Ascension — the same church later sources call the Eleona.

Claims & evidence

The cave “shewn there” and the feet on Olivet — before any church (c. 314–318)

Which it is possible for us to see literally fulfilled in another way even to-day, since believers in Christ all congregate from all parts of the world, not as of old time because of the glory of Jerusalem, nor that they may worship in the ancient Temple at Jerusalem, but they rest there that they may learn both about the city being taken and devastated as the prophets foretold, and that they may worship at the Mount of Olives opposite to the city, whither the glory of the Lord migrated when it left the former city.

There stood in truth according to the common and received account the feet of our Lord and Saviour, Himself the Word of God, through that tabernacle of humanity He had borne up the Mount of Olives to the cave that is shewn there; there He prayed and delivered to His disciples on the summit of the Mount of Olives the mysteries of His end, and thence He made His Ascension into heaven, as Luke tells us in the Acts of the Apostles…

— The Proof of the Gospel (Demonstratio Evangelica), Book VI, ch. 18 (on Zechariah 14:4), trans. W. J. Ferrar (1920). Writing before the Constantinian building programme, Eusebius already treats a Mount-of-Olives cave as the received place where Christ prayed, taught the disciples, and ascended, and expressly hangs it on Zechariah's prophecy that “his feet shall stand… upon the mount of Olives.” Note the fusion: one cave holds the teaching, the prayer, and the Ascension together — the finer distinction between the teaching-cave (Eleona) lower down and the ascension-spot proper (the Imbomon, higher) is a later refinement.

Helena's church “on the very summit,” in memory of the Ascension (c. 337–339)

In the one case, he rendered due honor to that which had been the scene of the first manifestation of our Saviour's divine presence, when he submitted to be born in mortal flesh; while in the case of the second cavern he hallowed the remembrance of his ascension to heaven from the mountain top.

And farther, the mother of the emperor raised a stately structure on the Mount of Olives also, in memory of his ascent to heaven who is the Saviour of mankind, erecting a sacred church and temple on the very summit of the mount. And indeed authentic history informs us that in this very cave the Saviour imparted his secret revelations to his disciples.

— Life of Constantine, Book III, chs. 41 and 43, trans. E. C. Richardson, NPNF2 vol. 1. Eusebius pairs the Bethlehem grotto and the Olivet cave as Helena's two foundations, and explicitly ties the second to “his ascent to heaven.” The building he describes over the cave “where the Lord taught his disciples” is the church the Bordeaux Pilgrim sees in 333 and Egeria calls the church “on Eleona.”

What it attests — and what it does not

Two things enter the record with Eusebius. First, the antiquity of the memory: even before Constantine built anything, pilgrims were being shown a Mount-of-Olives cave as the place of the Ascension — the earliest datable attestation of the site, and one Eusebius already presents as “common and received.” Second, the Constantinian monumentalizing: Helena's church fixes that memory in stone on the summit. What Eusebius does not yet give is the later precision. His single cave does the work three later sites will divide between them — the teaching-church (Eleona), the ascension-rotunda (the Imbomon), and the footprint-relic. The tradition he witnesses is genuine and early, but it is a memory of the general summit, not a first-century survey of the exact step from which Christ rose.
