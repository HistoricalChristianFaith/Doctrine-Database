Arculf (via Adomnán) on the round Church of the Ascension

Dates: c. 680 (Arculf's visit c. 670; recorded by Adomnán of Iona c. 680) · Wikipedia
Summary: The Frankish bishop Arculf, whose eyewitness report Adomnán of Iona wrote up as De Locis Sanctis, gives the earliest detailed architectural description of the Ascension church on the Mount of Olives: a great round building deliberately left open to the sky over the spot, the footprints of the Lord still visible in the dust within, and lamps blazing at night toward Jerusalem.

Claims & evidence

A round church, roofless over the sacred spot (c. 680)

a great round church, having in its circuit three vaulted porticoes covered over above… The interior of the church, without roof or vault, lies open to heaven under the open air, having in its eastern side an altar protected under a narrow covering.

— Adomnán, De Locis Sanctis, Book I, ch. 23 (from the report of Arculf), trans. J. R. Macpherson, Palestine Pilgrims' Text Society, vol. 3 (1895). The building is hypaethral by design — deliberately unroofed above the place of the footprints, later writers explaining the open crown by an annual wind that swept away any covering.

The footprints in the dust, carried off yet undiminished

the mark of the dust that was trodden by the Lord is so lasting that the impression of the footsteps may be perceived… Although the faith of such as gather daily at the spot snatches away some of what was trodden by the Lord, yet the area perceives no loss, and the ground still retains that same appearance of being marked by the impress of footsteps.

— Adomnán, De Locis Sanctis I.23, trans. Macpherson. This is the same footprints-in-the-dust tradition Paulinus of Nola and Sulpicius Severus report c. 403 — the dust perpetually carried away by pilgrims yet never diminishing — now placed inside the built rotunda a quarter-millennium later.

The lamps shining toward Jerusalem

twice four windows have been formed high up with glazed shutters, and in these windows there burn as many lamps… The brightness of these lamps is so great that, as their light is copiously poured through the glass from the summit of the Mountain of Olivet… the lofty path which rises by steps up to the city of Jerusalem from the Valley of Josaphat, is clearly illuminated in a wonderful manner, even on dark nights.

— Adomnán, De Locis Sanctis I.23, trans. Macpherson. “Twice four” = the eight lamps that later writers (and Bede, who reworked Arculf's account) name outright.

What it attests — and what it does not

Arculf's value is that he fixes the fabric. Between Egeria's bare “place” (c. 384) and the surviving octagonal edicule of the Crusaders lie the Byzantine round churches — Poemenia's original, destroyed by the Persians in 614, and its rebuild — and Arculf walked into that rebuilt rotunda and described it: circular, three-portico, open to the sky, the footprints inside, the eight lamps. His report (through Adomnán, and then Bede) carried the shape of the Ascension church into the Latin West for centuries. What he attests is the continuity of the built site and its footprint-relic from the 4th century to the 7th; what he cannot attest — and does not claim — is that the dust he saw preserved a first-century impression rather than a devotional tradition already three centuries deep in his own day.

Notes

  • Adomnán's De Locis Sanctis, built from Arculf's eyewitness account, is one of the most important early-medieval descriptions of the Holy Land; Bede abridged it in his own De Locis Sanctis, spreading the picture of the roofless Ascension rotunda across the West.
