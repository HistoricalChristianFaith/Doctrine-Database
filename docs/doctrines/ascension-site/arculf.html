Arculf (via Adomnán) on the round Church of the Ascension

Dates: c. 680 (Arculf's visit c. 670; recorded by Adomnán of Iona c. 680) · Wikipedia
Summary: The Frankish bishop Arculf, whose eyewitness report Adomnán of Iona wrote up as De Locis Sanctis, describes the architecture of the Ascension church on the Mount of Olives: a great round building open to the sky over the spot, footprints shown in the dust within as those of the Lord, and lamps burning at night toward Jerusalem.

Claims & evidence

A round church, roofless over the sacred spot (c. 680)

a great round church, having in its circuit three vaulted porticoes covered over above… The interior of the church, without roof or vault, lies open to heaven under the open air, having in its eastern side an altar protected under a narrow covering.

— Adomnán, De Locis Sanctis, Book I, ch. 23 (from the report of Arculf), trans. J. R. Macpherson, Palestine Pilgrims' Text Society, vol. 3 (1895). Adomnán's chapter gives two explanations for the open crown: that from the place of the footprints “the way may be always open and free to the eyes of those who pray towards heaven,” and that on the anniversary of the Ascension “a most violent tempest of wind comes on regularly every year,” so that the part over the footprints “cannot be vaulted over.”

The footprints in the dust, reported undiminished

the mark of the dust that was trodden by the Lord is so lasting that the impression of the footsteps may be perceived… Although the faith of such as gather daily at the spot snatches away some of what was trodden by the Lord, yet the area perceives no loss, and the ground still retains that same appearance of being marked by the impress of footsteps.

— Adomnán, De Locis Sanctis I.23, trans. Macpherson. Paulinus of Nola and Sulpicius Severus report the footprints c. 403, and Sulpicius the same carried-away yet undiminished ground; Arculf describes them inside the rebuilt rotunda some 270 years later.

The lamps shining toward Jerusalem

twice four windows have been formed high up with glazed shutters, and in these windows there burn as many lamps… The brightness of these lamps is so great that, as their light is copiously poured through the glass from the summit of the Mountain of Olivet… the lofty path which rises by steps up to the city of Jerusalem from the Valley of Josaphat, is clearly illuminated in a wonderful manner, even on dark nights.

— Adomnán, De Locis Sanctis I.23, trans. Macpherson. “Twice four” = the eight lamps that later writers (and Bede, who reworked Arculf's account) name outright.

Placement on the timeline

Between Egeria's “place” (c. 384) and the surviving octagonal edicule of the Crusaders lie the Byzantine round churches — Poemenia's original, destroyed by the Persians in 614, and its rebuild. Arculf describes the rebuilt rotunda: circular, with three porticoes, open to the sky, the footprints inside, the eight lamps. Bede reworked his account through Adomnán's.

Notes

  • Adomnán's De Locis Sanctis, built from Arculf's eyewitness account, is an early-medieval description of the Holy Land; Bede abridged it in his own De Locis Sanctis.
