Does the summit chapel mark the actual place of the Ascension?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: weak — the Mount of Olives is securely the place of the Ascension, but that this summit point is the exact spot is a fourth-century commemorative fixing in tension with Luke's own Bethany-ward wording · advanced by the site-tradition from Eusebius and Egeria onward.

The claim

And when the ninth hour approaches they go up with hymns to the Imbomon, that is, to the place whence the Lord ascended into heaven…

— Egeria, The Pilgrimage of Egeria (c. 384), trans. McClure & Feltoe. The claim: the spot now marked by the Chapel of the Ascension near the summit of Olivet is the actual place from which Christ ascended — the “Imbomon” of the fourth-century liturgy, fixed on the ground and built over ever since.

Who advances it

The site-tradition itself, in an unbroken devotional and liturgical chain: Eusebius's cave “shewn there” (before 325), Helena's summit church, Egeria's named Imbomon (c. 384), Poemenia's rotunda built on it, and every later pilgrim, Crusader, and custodian who has treated the summit edicule as the place of the Ascension.

The evidence for

Two things are genuinely strong. First, the mountain is fixed by Scripture itself: Acts names “the mount called Olivet, which is from Jerusalem a sabbath day's journey,” and Zechariah 14:4 sets the Lord's feet “upon the mount of Olives, which is before Jerusalem on the east.”1 The Ascension-on-Olivet identification is therefore not a mere tradition but the plain sense of the text. Second, the memory is early and continuous: a shown site on Olivet is attested before Constantine and has been marked on the ridge without interruption for seventeen centuries — a pedigree matched among the Passion sites only by the Holy Sepulchre and the Lazarus-tomb. On the general question — was the Ascension on the Mount of Olives? — the answer is a confident yes.

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The Mount of Olives as the site of the Ascension is sound — scripturally fixed and continuously remembered. Nothing below disturbs that.

What is overstated. The precise-spot claim is another matter, and three things weigh against it. (1) The site drifted within the tradition's own first century of witnesses: Eusebius knows a single cave that holds teaching, prayer, and ascension together; by Egeria's day the ascension-spot has migrated uphill to a distinct higher station, the Imbomon, leaving the cave (Eleona) as merely where the Lord “taught.”2 A spot that moves uphill inside two generations is being chosen, not remembered. (2) There is no pinpoint before the fourth century, and the only physical anchor the site ever had — the footprint-rock — is a devotional relic that fails on its own argument page. (3) The tradition sits in unresolved tension with Luke's own geography: the Gospel has Jesus lead the disciples out “as far as to Bethany” before the parting — Bethany lies on the far, eastern slope, well over the ridge — whereas Acts's “sabbath day's journey” (about 2,000 cubits) fits the near summit where the church stands. The two Lucan notices pull in slightly different directions, and the summit chapel resolves the tension by quietly siding with Acts's distance against Luke's Bethany-ward wording.3 None of this is a naturalistic objection to the Ascension itself — the event is granted; the question is only whether this point on the hill can be located as the exact spot, and the evidence says it is a commemorative fix, not a survey.

Assessment

Weak — as a claim about the exact spot; the general location is sound. Keep the two levels distinct. That the Ascension was on the Mount of Olives is scripturally grounded and secure. That the summit Chapel of the Ascension marks the actual point is weak: the site demonstrably migrated uphill between Eusebius and Egeria, no source pinpoints it before the fourth century, its lone physical anchor is an unsupported footprint-relic, and it stands in tension with Luke's “as far as to Bethany.” The honest reading is that the church fixed a commemorative spot on the accessible ridge-top, harmonizing Acts's “Olivet” with a place pilgrims could reach — a devotional choice on the right mountain, not a located first-century footfall.
