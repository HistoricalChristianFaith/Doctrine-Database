The martyrdom-legend: the dog-headed Reprobus

Dates: Greek and Latin recensions; earliest written form undated, standing behind all later versions (by the sixth–ninth centuries)
Summary: The developed story of Christopher first reaches us in the passio — the martyrdom account — surviving in Greek and Latin. Here the saint is a giant named Reprebus (Rebrebus / Reprobus), a dog-headed cannibal from the edge of the world, conscripted into a Roman unit, baptized, renamed Christopher, and martyred. This is the layer that gives the East its dog-headed icon and gives modern scholarship its clues.

Claims & evidence

Reprebus the cynocephalus

In the earliest Greek passions the protagonist takes the name Christopher only at his baptism; before it he is Reprebus. The earliest Latin passion tells the same tale but spells the name Rebrebus; behind both stands the Latin Reprobus, “the reprobate” or “wicked one.” He is described as a cynocephalus — dog-headed — of enormous size, from a tribe of cannibals dwelling beyond the frontier. As David Woods notes, both name-forms “look very like corrupt readings of the Latin term Reprobus,” so that the texts “read suspiciously like a moralising tale rather than a factual report”: a wicked man, Reprobus, who becomes a bearer-of-Christ, Christophorus.

— David Woods, “St. Christopher, Bishop Peter of Attalia, and the Cohors Marmaritarum” (Vigiliae Christianae 48.2 [1994]: 170–186); cf. “Saint Christopher,” Wikipedia.

The numerus Marmaritarum

A striking, un-legendary detail survives in the account: after his capture in war the giant is forced to serve in a Roman unit named the numerus Marmaritarum, “the unit of the Marmaritae” — the Marmaritae being a Berber people of Marmarica, west of Egypt in the region of Cyrenaica. The land “of cannibals and dog-headed peoples” from which the saint is said to come is, on Woods’ reading, precisely that frontier tribe, cast in the stock ethnographic language the Greco-Roman world used for peoples at the edge of its map. The military detail is the sort of thing a legend does not usually invent, and it is the thread modern scholarship pulls to reach a historical core.

Baptism, name, and martyrdom

In the story the giant is catechized, baptized, and given the name Christopher; he then confesses Christ before a persecuting emperor — named as Decius in some copies, Diocletian (or Maximinus) in others — endures tortures, and is beheaded. The disagreement among the copies over which emperor, and over the place, is itself a mark of a legend still in motion: the frame is fixed (capture, conversion, martyrdom) while the historical coordinates drift.

Notes

  • The seed of the icon. Because the passio makes the saint literally dog-headed, the Eastern churches painted him so: the cynocephalic icon is this textual layer made visible, and it long outlived the Western rationalization of the giant into a mere man.
  • A moralising shape. That the pre-baptismal name means “reprobate” and the post-baptismal name means “Christ-bearer” makes the whole read as a conversion-parable in miniature — one reason the historicity of the narrative (as against the early cult) is weighed cautiously.
