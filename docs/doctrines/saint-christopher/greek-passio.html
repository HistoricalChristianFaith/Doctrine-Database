The martyrdom-legend: the dog-headed Reprobus

Dates: Greek and Latin recensions; earliest written form undated (the Greek legend “may belong to the sixth century,” Mershman, 1908)
Summary: The developed story of Christopher first reaches us in the passio — the martyrdom account — surviving in Greek and Latin. Here the saint is a giant named Reprebus (Rebrebus / Reprobus), a dog-headed cannibal from the edge of the world, conscripted into a Roman unit, baptized, renamed Christopher, and martyred. David Woods (1999) holds that the Greek tradition’s literal reading of this account produced the dog-headed icon, and builds his reconstruction of the saint on its details.

Claims & evidence

Reprebus the cynocephalus

In the earliest Greek passions the protagonist takes the name Christopher only at his baptism; before it he is Reprebus. The earliest Latin passion tells the same tale but spells the name Rebrebus; behind both, on David Woods’ reading, stands the Latin Reprobus, “the reprobate” or “wicked one.” He is described as a cynocephalus — dog-headed — of enormous size, from a tribe of cannibals dwelling beyond the frontier. Woods writes that both name-forms “look very like corrupt readings of the Latin term Reprobus,” so that the texts “read suspiciously like a moralising tale rather than a factual report”: a wicked man, Reprobus, who becomes a bearer-of-Christ, Christophorus.

— David Woods, “St. Christopher, Bishop Peter of Attalia, and the Cohors Marmaritarum” (Vigiliae Christianae 48.2 [1994]: 170–186); cf. “Saint Christopher,” Wikipedia.

The numerus Marmaritarum

The account includes a military detail: after his capture in war the giant is forced to serve in a Roman unit named the numerus Marmaritarum, “the unit of the Marmaritae” — the Marmaritae being a Berber people of Marmarica, west of Egypt in the region of Cyrenaica. The land “of cannibals and dog-headed peoples” from which the saint is said to come is, on Woods’ reading, precisely that frontier tribe, cast in the stock ethnographic language the Greco-Roman world used for peoples at the edge of its map. Woods takes the unit to be a real one and builds on it his identification of a historical soldier.

Baptism, name, and martyrdom

In the story the giant is catechized, baptized, and given the name Christopher; he then confesses Christ before a persecuting emperor — named as Decius in some copies, Diocletian (or Maximinus) in others — endures tortures, and is beheaded. The copies agree on the frame (capture, conversion, martyrdom) and disagree on the emperor and the place. Woods holds that tradition kept the regnal year, “the 4th year,” and lost the name of the emperor, so that the author of the original acts “was left to guess at the correct name of this persecuting emperor.”

— David Woods, The Origin of the Cult of St Christopher (web study, 1999).

Reception and debate

  • The passio and the icon. The passio describes the saint as dog-headed, and the Eastern churches painted him so; on Woods’ reading the Greek tradition took the description “absolutely literally, and this is why Byzantine icons often depicted St. Christopher with a dog’s head.” See the cynocephalic icon, and, for other readings of the dog’s head, its own page.
  • A moralising shape. The pre-baptismal name means “reprobate” and the post-baptismal name “Christ-bearer”; Woods reads the texts as “a moralising tale rather than a factual report.” Whether a historical martyr can be identified beneath the narrative, as distinct from the early cult, is set out on its own page.
