The dog-headed icon, and its regulation, in the East

Dates: earliest cynocephalic images 5th–6th c.; prohibited by the Russian Holy Synod (1722; a prohibition by the Great Moscow Council of 1667 is also reported ⚠ unverified); retained by the Old Believers, whose practices the Russian Church ceased to ban in 1971
Summary: While the Latin West came to depict Christopher as a giant in human form, the Byzantine and Slavic East painted him with a dog’s head, as the passio describes him. The image is early, became popular in Russia, was prohibited by the Russian Synod in 1722, and was kept by the Old Believers.

Claims & evidence

An image nearly as old as the cult

The cynocephalic Christopher appears in Eastern art from an early date — a terracotta icon of the fifth or sixth century from Vinica (North Macedonia) shows him dog-headed, close in time to the spread of the cult itself. In the earliest images the face is fierce, the teeth bared; over the centuries the expression is softened toward a “serene, meek and gentle face,” but the dog’s head remains. Dog-headed images became very popular in Russian icon painting from the second half of the sixteenth century, while in the West the Golden Legend’s giant had no dog’s head.

— “Understanding the Dog-Headed Icon of St Christopher,” Orthodox Arts Journal; “Saint Christopher,” Wikipedia.

The councils move against animal-headed saints

A Synod of the Russian Church held in 1722 banned the dog-head imagery, ordering that Christopher be shown in human form. An earlier condemnation of saints painted with animal heads by the Great Moscow Council of 1667 is also reported ⚠ unverified. The iconographer Jonathan Pageau (2013) writes that “because of all the difficulties it presents, the icon was proscribed in the 18th century by Moscow.” How the dog’s head arose, and what it means, is set out on its own page.

— “Saint Christopher,” Wikipedia; Jonathan Pageau, “Understanding the Dog-Headed Icon of St Christopher,” Orthodox Arts Journal (2013).

The Old Believers keep it

The traditionalist Old Believers, who rejected the seventeenth-century reforms, went on painting and venerating the dog-headed warrior saint in the following centuries, as did Orthodox churches not subject to the Russian Synod. The Russian Church lifted its ban on the Old Believers’ practices in 1971. The dog-headed Christopher thus remained in living use into the twentieth century.

— “Saint Christopher,” Wikipedia (“Iconography”).

Reception and debate

  • Readings of the icon. David Woods (1999) holds that the Greek tradition read the passio’s dog-headed homeland literally, “and this is why Byzantine icons often depicted St. Christopher with a dog’s head”; Jonathan Pageau (2013) reads the dog-headed saint and the Western giant as one image of the foreigner carried into the Church. Both readings, and others, are set out on the dog’s-head page.
