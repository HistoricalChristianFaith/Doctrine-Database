The Golden Legend’s Christ-bearer

Dates: Jacobus de Voragine, Legenda aurea, c. 1260 (Caxton’s English, 1483)
Summary: The version the Latin West remembers — Christopher the towering ferryman who bore the Christ-child across a swollen river — is the Golden Legend’s. Here there is no dog’s head: where the passiones have a cynocephalus, Voragine has a giant of the “lineage of the Canaanites,” a man of prodigious stature, and the story turns on his name, Christo-phoros, “Christ-bearer.”

Claims & evidence

Reprobus renamed, and the meaning drawn out

Voragine opens with an etymology of the name, and in doing so records the older name the tradition carried:

Christopher tofore his baptism was named Reprobus, but afterward he was named Christopher, which is as much to say as bearing Christ.

The pre-baptismal name Reprobus, “the reprobate,” is the passio’s; the dog’s head and the cannibalism are absent, and the change of name carries the meaning.

— Jacobus de Voragine, The Golden Legend, “The Life of S. Christopher,” tr. William Caxton: text at Christian Iconography.

The Canaanite giant

Here Christopher is a man of great size:

Christopher was of the lineage of the Canaanites, and he was of a right great stature… and he was twelve cubits of length.

The “Canaanite” label appears earlier in the Western tradition, in Walter of Speyer, beside the dog’s head. David Woods (1999) holds that it began when a Latin canineus (“dog-like”) was “amended to read ‘Canaanite’ (Cananeus)”; Jonathan Pageau (2013) writes that the giant Canaanite of Catholic images “signifies the same reality as Dog-Headed men.” Both readings are set out on the dog’s-head page. The giant seeks to serve only the mightiest king; a hermit sends him to a river to carry travellers across on his great strength.

— David Woods, The Origin of the Cult of St Christopher (web study, 1999); Jonathan Pageau, “Understanding the Dog-Headed Icon of St Christopher,” Orthodox Arts Journal (2013).

The child heavier than the world

The famous scene follows. Carrying a small child across the ford, Christopher finds him growing impossibly heavy:

the child was heavy as lead, and alway as he went farther the water increased and grew more and more: and the child was more and more heavy…

On the far bank the child reveals himself — Christ, whom Christopher has borne, “and all the world upon thy shoulders.” In the West the Christ-bearer is the patron of travellers; his statues stood at the entrances of churches and dwellings and at bridges, and (much later) his image was set in cars against sudden death.

— On the statues: Francis Mershman, “St. Christopher,” Catholic Encyclopedia vol. 3 (1908).

Reception and debate

  • Placement on the timeline. In the Golden Legend the dog’s head is absent while the “Canaanite” label and the great size are present. In the East the dog-headed icon remained in use for centuries.
