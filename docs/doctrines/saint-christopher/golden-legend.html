The Golden Legend rationalizes the monster into the Christ-bearer

Dates: Jacobus de Voragine, Legenda aurea, c. 1260 (Caxton’s English, 1483)
Summary: The version the Latin West remembers — Christopher the towering ferryman who bore the Christ-child across a swollen river — was fixed by the Golden Legend. Here the dog’s head has quietly vanished: the cynocephalus of the passiones has become a giant of the “lineage of the Canaanites,” a man of prodigious stature but a man, and the story turns on his name, Christo-phoros, “Christ-bearer.”

Claims & evidence

Reprobus renamed, and the meaning drawn out

Voragine opens by construing the name — his habitual etymologizing — and in doing so records the older name the tradition carried:

Christopher tofore his baptism was named Reprobus, but afterward he was named Christopher, which is as much to say as bearing Christ.

The saint of the passio is thus still visible under the surface — Reprobus, the “reprobate” — but the grotesque details are gone, and only the name-change survives to carry the meaning.

— Jacobus de Voragine, The Golden Legend, “The Life of S. Christopher,” tr. William Caxton: text at Christian Iconography.

The Canaanite giant

The dog-headed cannibal of the East has become simply a very large man:

Christopher was of the lineage of the Canaanites, and he was of a right great stature… and he was twelve cubits of length.

The “Canaanite” note is the fossil of the Western tradition already seen in Walter of Speyer — and, on the modern reconstruction, the residue of a Latin misreading of canineus (“dog-like”) as Cananeus (“Canaanite”), the very step argued below. The giant seeks to serve only the mightiest king; a hermit sends him to a river to carry travellers across on his great strength.

The child heavier than the world

The famous scene follows. Carrying a small child across the ford, Christopher finds him growing impossibly heavy:

the child was heavy as lead, and alway as he went farther the water increased and grew more and more: and the child was more and more heavy…

On the far bank the child reveals himself — Christ, whom Christopher has borne, “and all the world upon thy shoulders.” The dog-headed martyr of the East has become, in the West, the gentle patron of travellers, his image set at church doors and (much later) in cars against sudden death.

Notes

  • The rationalization step. The Golden Legend is where the legend’s oldest, strangest layer — the literal dog’s head — is smoothed away for a Western audience, leaving only the “Canaanite” and the great size as traces of it. The East, by contrast, kept the dog-headed icon for centuries more.
