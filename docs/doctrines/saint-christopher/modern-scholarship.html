Modern scholarship proposes a frontier soldier

Dates: David Woods, Vigiliae Christianae article 1994; web study 1999
Summary: Working back from details the passiones preserve — a specific Roman unit, a land of “dog-heads,” a baptizing bishop — the classicist David Woods (University College Cork) proposed a historical identification of the saint: a north-African soldier, captured in Diocletian’s campaign, baptized near Antioch, and martyred in 308, whom Woods identifies with the Egyptian martyr Menas. On his reading the dog’s head is a cliché for frontier peoples taken literally.

Claims & evidence

The reconstruction

Woods reconstructs the man behind the legend as a member of the north-African tribe of the Marmaritae, captured by Roman forces during the emperor Diocletian’s campaign against the Marmaritae in late 301 / early 302, transported for service in a garrison in or near Antioch in Syria, baptized there by the refugee bishop Peter of Alexandria, and martyred on (by his reckoning) 9 July 308, in the fourth year of Maximinus Daza. His starting point is the numerus Marmaritarum preserved in the passio, which he takes to be a real unit, the Cohors Tertia Valeria Marmaritarum, formed from the captives of a real frontier war.

— David Woods, “St. Christopher, Bishop Peter of Attalia, and the Cohors Marmaritarum: A Fresh Examination,” Vigiliae Christianae 48.2 (1994): 170–186 (also on Academia.edu); Woods, The Origin of the Cult of St Christopher (web study, 1999).

Bishop Peter, the relics, and St Menas

Woods’ title names Bishop Peter of Attalia. In the earliest Greek text a bishop Peter of Attalia pays a large ransom for the martyr’s body and returns with it to his city; the earliest Latin text gives the role to a bishop Athanasius “of Italy.” Woods holds that “it seems probable that bishop Peter of Attalia is identifiable with the ‘presbyter’ Peter who baptized Christopher, i.e. as bishop Peter of Alexandria” — the bishop of Alexandria (d. 311), a refugee near Antioch during the Great Persecution. On his reading Peter arranged the return of the corpse to the saint’s homeland, Marmarica, by way of Alexandria in 311, during the pause in persecution of that year. Because Egyptian sources did not know the martyr as Christopher, Woods looks for his cult between Marmarica and Alexandria and finds it at the shrine of the soldier-martyr Menas, south-west of Alexandria, whose earliest Greek martyrdom account he calls “complete fiction”; he concludes that Christopher “is really identifiable with the Egyptian martyr known as St. Menas.” The article’s three headline subjects are thus the baptizing and relic-bearing bishop, the unit that fixes Christopher’s origin, and the saint himself.

— Woods, The Origin of the Cult of St Christopher (web study, 1999), the source of the quotations; Woods, “St. Christopher, Bishop Peter of Attalia, and the Cohors Marmaritarum” (Vigiliae Christianae 48.2 [1994]: 170–186); cf. “Saint Christopher,” Wikipedia.

Woods on the dog’s head

Woods writes that the description “has absolutely nothing to do with the Egyptian cult of the jackal-headed god Anubis” — “the real explanation is rather more prosaic.” On his account the civilised inhabitants of the Greco-Roman world routinely described peoples living at or beyond their frontier as cannibals and “dog-headed” peoples. To say Christopher came “from the land of the dog-headed” was, in origin, to say he came from the periphery — and the Marmaritae inhabited such a borderland. Later readers took the description literally, and the Eastern churches painted the dog’s head they read on the page.

— Woods, The Origin of the Cult of St Christopher (web study, 1999).

From canineus to Cananeus

On Woods’ account the Latin tradition developed differently: some early Latin translations rendered the Greek kunokephalos, “dog-headed,” as canineus, “dog-like,” and “this was amended to read ‘Canaanite’ (Cananeus) as time progressed since it was obvious that he could not really have been ‘dog-like’” — whence the “land of the Chananei” of Walter of Speyer and the “lineage of the Canaanites” of the Golden Legend. On this account a frontier soldier described in stock monster-language became, by successive re-readings, first a literal dog-headed cannibal and then a Canaanite giant.

— Woods, The Origin of the Cult of St Christopher (web study, 1999).

Reception and debate

  • Other readings. Francis Mershman (1908) had written that “almost nothing certain is known about his life or death”; Woods’ identification is set beside that reading on the historical-martyr page. Jonathan Pageau (2013), citing Woods’ study, objects to “the usual ho-hum conclusions that Christian tradition develops basically as a series of misunderstandings, confusions and fantastical exaggerations” and reads the dog’s head symbolically; the readings of the dog’s head are set out on their own page.
