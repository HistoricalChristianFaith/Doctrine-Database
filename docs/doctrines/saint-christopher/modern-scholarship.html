Modern scholarship reconstructs the frontier soldier

Dates: David Woods, Vigiliae Christianae article 1994; web study 1999
Summary: Working back from the odd, un-legendary details the passiones preserve — a specific Roman unit, a land of “dog-heads,” a baptizing bishop — the classicist David Woods (University College Cork) proposed a plausible historical core beneath the myth: a real north-African soldier, captured, baptized near Antioch, and martyred under Diocletian. On his reading the dog’s head is not a memory of a monster but a cliché taken literally.

Claims & evidence

The reconstruction

Woods reconstructs the man behind the legend as a member of the north-African tribe of the Marmaritae, captured by Roman forces during the emperor Diocletian’s campaign against the Marmaritae in late 301 / early 302, transported for service in a garrison in or near Antioch in Syria, baptized there by the refugee bishop Peter of Alexandria, and martyred on (by his reckoning) 9 July 308. The load-bearing clue is the numerus Marmaritarum preserved in the passio: a genuine-looking military detail that points to a real unit and a real frontier war, not to a fabulous homeland.

— David Woods, “St. Christopher, Bishop Peter of Attalia, and the Cohors Marmaritarum: A Fresh Examination,” Vigiliae Christianae 48.2 (1994): 170–186 (also on Academia.edu); Woods, The Origin of the Cult of St Christopher (web study, 1999).

The dog’s head explained

Woods is explicit that the motif has nothing to do with the Egyptian jackal-headed Anubis, as older speculation held — “the real explanation is rather more prosaic.” The civilised inhabitants of the Greco-Roman world routinely described peoples living at or beyond their frontier as cannibals and “dog-headed” monsters. To say Christopher came “from the land of the dog-headed” was, in origin, simply to say he came from the periphery — and the Marmaritae inhabited exactly such a borderland. Later readers, no longer hearing the cliché, took the description literally, and the Eastern churches painted the dog’s head they read on the page.

From canineus to Cananeus

The Latin West received the tradition and, on Woods’ account, garbled it a step further: the Greek kynokephalos / “dog-like” description passed into Latin as canineus, which was in turn misread as Cananeus, “Canaanite” — whence the “land of the Chananei” of Walter of Speyer and the “lineage of the Canaanites” of the Golden Legend. A frontier soldier described in stock monster-language thus became, by successive re-readings, first a literal dog-headed cannibal and then a Canaanite giant.

Notes

  • Reconstruction, not documentation. The strength of Woods’ case is that it explains the passiones’ strangest details economically; its limit is that it builds from late and legendary texts, so the specific identification (this tribe, this bishop, this year) is a strong inference rather than an attested fact — the ground on which the historical-martyr and dog-head arguments are rated plausible.
