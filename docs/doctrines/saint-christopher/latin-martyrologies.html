The Latin West receives Christopher: the martyrologies

Dates: Hieronymian martyrology (5th–6th c.; Gaulish redaction c. 592) → Ado of Vienne and Usuard (mid-9th c.)
Summary: Between the Greek and Latin passiones and Walter of Speyer’s tenth-century verse life, the datable carriers of Christopher’s cult into Western liturgical memory are the Latin martyrologies. He enters as a bare name-and-place commemoration on 25 July in the fifth-/sixth-century Hieronymian martyrology — no legend at all — is absent from Bede (c. 725–731), and only in the ninth century, with the Carolingian “historical” martyrologists Ado and Usuard, does the passio’s outline (the persecuting king, the tortures, the converts) enter the notice. Crucially, at no stage does a martyrology entry carry the dog’s head or the “Canaanite” tag — those travel only in the fuller passiones and in Walter of Speyer.

Claims & evidence

First Western witness: a bare commemoration (Hieronymian martyrology, 5th–6th c.)

The oldest Latin liturgical notice of Christopher is in the Martyrologium Hieronymianum — the “Hieronymian” martyrology falsely ascribed to Jerome, compiled in the fifth–sixth century and given its surviving Gaulish redaction at Auxerre c. 592. Under 25 July (VIII Kal. Aug.) it records simply:

In Licia civitate Samo natale Christofori.

— “In Lycia, in the city of Samon, the feast of Christopher.” This is a bare commemoration: a name, a place (Samon in Lycia), a date — and nothing else. There is no emperor, no torture, no giant, no dog’s head, no “Canaanite.” As with the Chalcedon martyrium a century earlier in the Greek East, the cult reaches the Latin calendars before the developed legend does. The reading is that of the early manuscripts (Weissenburg 81, BnF lat. 10837) as given in the de Rossi–Duchesne edition. — De Rossi–Duchesne, Martyrologium Hieronymianum (Acta Sanctorum, Nov. II.1, 1894); text quoted in the Acta Sanctorum for 25 July and in the Oxford Cult of Saints in Late Antiquity database (record E04892).

Absent from Bede (c. 725–731)

Bede’s martyrology — the first of the “historical” martyrologies, which for the first time gave many saints a short narrative notice rather than a bare name — has no entry for Christopher, neither bare nor narrative. His July notices run through Rufina and Secunda (9 July), Phocas (14 July), and the Scillitan martyrs (17 July), but Christopher is not among the roughly 115 authentic notices reconstructed by Quentin. The Western cult was evidently not yet prominent enough, in Bede’s Northumbria, to draw a historical notice. — Henri Quentin, Les martyrologes historiques du moyen âge (Paris, 1908); the authentic text at Latin Wikisource (no Christopher); modern edition, Dubois–Renaud, Édition pratique des martyrologes de Bède, de l’Anonyme lyonnais et de Florus (Paris, 1976).

The passio enters the notice: Ado of Vienne (c. 855–860)

It is with the Carolingian historical martyrologists that the passio’s furniture first passes into the martyrological line. Ado of Vienne, under 25 July, summarizes the legend: Christopher is martyred in Lycia (the city of Samos) under the persecuting king Dagnus — the passio’s corruption of the emperor Decius — scourged and beheaded, together with the two women Niceta and Aquilina, sent to seduce him, who instead destroy the idols of Jupiter and Apollo and are themselves martyred. The Bollandists flag “Dagnus” as belonging to the “fabulous Acts” of Christopher — “Dagnus occurrit in Actis fabulosis S. Christophori… nec in ullis historiis notus est” — a name known to no real history. What Ado’s notice does not contain is any dog’s head or “Canaanite” descent. — Ado of Vienne, Martyrologium, 25 July; ed. Dubois–Renaud, Le martyrologe d’Adon (Paris, 1984), and Migne, Patrologia Latina 123; the “Dagnus” note in the Acta Sanctorum, 25 July.

Usuard abridges Ado — and feeds the Roman Martyrology (c. 865–877)

Usuard, whose martyrology became the most widely copied in the medieval West and the direct base of the printed Roman Martyrology, abridged Ado’s notice. In the terse Usuard-family form — preserved verbatim in the closely dependent martyrology of Auxerre, immediately after the entry for St James under VIII Kal. Aug. — it reads:

In Licia civitate Samon natale S. Christophori martyris.

— still just place, name, and the word “martyr.” When Baronius built the Roman Martyrology (1584) on Usuard and Ado, the fuller summary was restored under 25 July: Christopher martyred in Lycia under Decius, “virgis ferreis attritus, et a flammae aestuantis incendio superna Christi virtute servatus, ad ultimum sagittarum ictibus confossus, capitis obtruncatione martyrium complevit” — scourged with iron rods, saved from the fire by the power of Christ, transfixed with arrows, and at last beheaded. The tortures are in; the fullest passio-flourishes (the tens of thousands converted, the flowering staff) and, again, the dog’s head and the “Canaanite” are not — those stay in the Golden Legend and the fuller passiones. — Usuard, Martyrologium, 25 July; ed. J. Dubois, Le martyrologe d’Usuard (Brussels, 1965); the Usuard-family wording at the martyrology of Auxerre (Wikisource); the Baronian entry in the 1959 Roman Martyrology.

Down to today: the Roman Martyrology retains a bare notice

The line runs unbroken into the modern liturgical books. Christopher was dropped from the universal calendar in the 1969 reform, but he was kept in the Roman Martyrology; the current edition (editio typica altera, 2001/2004), having stripped the Baronian tortures back out, again gives only a bare notice under 25 July — “In Lycia, sancti Christophori, martyris” (“At Lycia… Saint Christopher, martyr”). The Western liturgical memory thus ends where it began fifteen centuries earlier in the Hieronymian martyrology: a name, a place, the word “martyr.” — Martyrologium Romanum (editio typica altera, 2004), 25 July, at GCatholic.

Notes

  • What the martyrologies bridge. They fill the centuries between the passiones and Walter of Speyer with datable Western witnesses, and they show the legend entering the calendars in stages: a bare name (5th–6th c.), silence in Bede (8th c.), then the passio’s human-martyrdom outline (9th c., Ado → Usuard). The liturgical tradition took up the martyrdom but never the monster.
  • Why this matters for the dog’s head. That the Latin martyrologies carry Lycia, Samos, Dagnus or Decius, and the tortures — but never the cynocephalus or the “Canaanite” — is a concrete datum for the transmission argument: the dog’s head and the canineus→Cananeus misreading travel in a separate channel (the fuller passiones, then Walter of Speyer and the icon tradition), not through the calendar-notices that carried the mainstream Western cult.
