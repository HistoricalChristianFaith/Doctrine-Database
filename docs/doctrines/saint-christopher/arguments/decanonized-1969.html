Did the 1969 reform of the Roman calendar de-canonize St Christopher?

Disputed reading · on Saint Christopher the dog-headed · witness: the Latin martyrologies and the Roman calendar

Related readings: Can a historical martyr be identified beneath the Christopher legend?

The passage

Memoria S. Christophori, anno circiter 1550 in Calendario romano ascripta, Calendariis particularibus relinquitur: quamvis Acta S. Christophori fabulosa sint, antiqua inveniuntur monumenta eius venerationis; attamen cultus huius Sancti non pertinet ad traditionem romanam

— Calendarium Romanum (1969), p. 131, as quoted in “General Roman Calendar of 1969,” Wikipedia. (“The memorial of St Christopher, entered in the Roman calendar about the year 1550, is left to particular calendars: although the Acts of St Christopher are legendary, ancient monuments of his veneration are found; yet the cult of this saint does not belong to the Roman tradition.”)1

The question

On 14 February 1969 Paul VI’s motu proprio Mysterii Paschalis approved a revised General Roman Calendar, published that year as the Calendarium Romanum; Christopher’s memorial was among some two hundred observances taken off the universal calendar. What is disputed is what that act did to his standing: whether it suppressed his feast and his recognition as a saint, or moved a feast to local calendars and left his status as it was.

The reform suppressed Christopher’s feast

On this reading the 1969 reform ended Christopher’s place in the Roman Church’s worship, on the ground that his story is legend.

Held by: Jonathan Pageau (Orthodox iconographer, Orthodox Arts Journal, 2013), on the feast; the wider popular form (“de-canonized,” “un-sainted”) ⚠ unverified — no named proponent located yet.2

Until 1969 Christopher had a fixed place in the Roman calendar: entered about 1550, commemorated on 25 July, and by 1954 commemorated at all Masses of the day. The Calendarium Romanum removed him from the General Roman Calendar, together with Valentine and about two hundred others, and gave its reasons in terms that bear on the saint himself: his Acts are fabulosa, legendary, and his cult “non pertinet ad traditionem romanam,” does not belong to the Roman tradition. The universal Roman Rite no longer keeps his day. Pageau, writing of the saint’s place in Catholic practice, puts it in these terms: “In the Roman Catholic Church, the feast of St-Christopher was suppressed entirely with Vatican II modernization, though he continues to be one of the most popular saints in Catholicism.” On this reading a saint whose feast the universal Church ceased to keep, for want of a credible history, was withdrawn from its common worship, whatever devotion continued in private and in local custom.

A feast moved to local calendars, his status unchanged

On this reading the reform changed only the rank of a feast in the universal calendar; Christopher remains a saint and may be honoured wherever a local calendar keeps him.

Held by: Philip Kosloski (Catholic, Aleteia, 2022).3

The decree’s operative words are “Calendariis particularibus relinquitur”: the memorial is left to particular calendars, not abolished, and the same sentence records that “antiqua inveniuntur monumenta eius venerationis.” The General Roman Calendar is a selection of the canonized saints for observance everywhere; the Roman Martyrology is the list to which all canonized saints are added, and the current Martyrologium Romanum keeps Christopher under 25 July: “In Lycia in present-day Türkiye, Saint Christopher, martyr.” On this reading the stated grounds were his late entry into the Roman calendar and the legendary character of his Acts, not a denial that the martyr existed. Kosloski writes that St Christopher “has a feast day on July 25, but is no longer on the Roman Calendar,” that he was “removed from the General Calendar due to doubts from historians regarding the historicity of the saint and his precise origins,” and that “he is still celebrated by many Eastern Christians on May 9.” Christopher belongs to the ancient saints venerated by immemorial cult rather than raised by a formal process, and there is no canonical act of “de-canonization” by which a saint’s status could be withdrawn ⚠ unverified.
