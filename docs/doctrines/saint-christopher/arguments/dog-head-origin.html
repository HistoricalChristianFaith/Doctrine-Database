Why is St Christopher described and painted as dog-headed?

Disputed reading · on Saint Christopher the dog-headed · witness: the Greek and Latin passiones

The passage

Now this Christopher was one of the Dogheads, a race that had the heads of dogs and ate human flesh.

— Irish Passion of St. Christopher §1 (two early fifteenth-century manuscripts), tr. J. Fraser, Revue Celtique 34 (1913), text at David Woods’ site.

Christopher was of the lineage of the Canaanites, and he was of a right great stature… and he was twelve cubits of length.

— Jacobus de Voragine, The Golden Legend (c. 1260), tr. William Caxton, text at Christian Iconography.1

The question

The early passiones make Christopher a cynocephalus from a land of cannibals and dog-headed peoples; Byzantine and Russian icons paint him with a dog’s head; the Latin West calls him a Canaanite giant. What is disputed is how the dog’s head entered the tradition and what it means, which bears on how the Eastern and Western images of the saint are related.

One of the dog-headed race

On this reading Christopher belonged to the dog-headed people at the world’s edge, and his conversion is the story of such a being made a Christian.

Held by: the earliest Latin Passio Christophori (Burchard Homiliary, late 8th c.); Ratramnus of Corbie (letter to Rimbert, before 865); Walter of Speyer (Vita et passio, c. 983–986); the Irish Passion (15th-c. manuscripts).2

The earliest written accounts, Greek and Latin, describe the saint’s homeland as a land of cannibals and dog-headed peoples and the saint himself as a cynocephalus. Ian Wood (2010) reports that Christopher “was known as being dog-headed in the early medieval West,” in what appears to be the earliest Latin passion, a manuscript of the last quarter of the eighth century. Ratramnus of Corbie, asked whether the dog-headed peoples of the north were men, cites Christopher as a dog-headed man who was baptized; Wood writes that for Ratramnus “the existence of a dog-headed Christopher was, like the some-time existence of Gygantes, a fact that was not debatable.” Walter of Speyer portrays a giant of a cynocephalic species from the land of the Chananei who ate human flesh and barked, and who receives a human appearance after baptism. The Irish Passion states it without qualification. On this reading the dog’s head belongs to the story itself: a member of the most remote and savage of peoples receives the gift of speech, confesses Christ, and dies a martyr.

An ethnographic metaphor read literally, with “Canaanite” a later Latin form

On this reading the original author said only that the saint came from the edge of the world; later readers took the description literally, and the Latin West turned “dog-like” into “Canaanite.”

Held by: David Woods (classicist, “St. Christopher, Bishop Peter of Attalia, and the Cohors Marmaritarum,” 1994; The Origin of the Cult of St Christopher, 1999); Ian Wood (medieval historian, “Categorising the cynocephali,” 2010), on the Latin step.3

Woods identifies the saint as one of the Marmaritae, a north-African people, captured in Diocletian’s campaign of 301/302 and enrolled in a Marmaritan unit. “The real explanation is rather more prosaic”: the inhabitants of the Greco-Roman world “had long been accustomed to describe those who lived on the edge of their world and beyond as the strange inhabitants of stranger lands, cannibals, dog-headed peoples and worse.” The author of the original account “was merely signifying that he came from the edge of the civilised world,” and “the Marmaritae did indeed inhabit such a peripheral region.” The Greek tradition “came to interpret this passage absolutely literally, and this is why Byzantine icons often depicted St. Christopher with a dog’s head.” In the West, Woods writes, some early Latin translations rendered kunokephalos as canineus, “dog-like,” and this “was amended to read ‘Canaanite’ (Cananeus) as time progressed since it was obvious that he could not really have been ‘dog-like’.” Ian Wood, on the Latin step, writes that “the switch from the word canineus to cananeus was only too easy,” and that the dog-headedness “either derived from or prompted his association with Canaan.” Walter of Speyer’s cynocephalus from the land of the Chananei carries both forms side by side.

An icon of the foreigner carried into the Church

On this reading the dog-headed and the Canaanite-giant images carry one meaning, the stranger at the edge of humanity who becomes a bearer of Christ, and the tradition’s images cohere with the Bible.

Held by: Jonathan Pageau (Orthodox iconographer, “Understanding the Dog-Headed Icon of St-Christopher,” Orthodox Arts Journal, 2013).4

Pageau reads the dog-headed men of icons and manuscripts (at Pentecost, in the apostles’ missions, beside St Mercurios) as “representing barbarian foreigners par excellence, those living on the edge of the world, the edge of humanity itself.” The Western giant, “though less visually shocking for having lost his monstrous face, signifies the same reality as Dog-Headed men.” He traces the dog as the figure of the outsider through Scripture (Goliath’s “Am I a dog that you come at me with sticks?”, the dogs and the crumbs of Matthew 15, Caleb the foreigner who crosses the Jordan beside Joshua) and reads the river-crossing as “an image of baptism” and “an image of the Church itself.” On his reading, “rather than simply being a series of accidents and exaggerations, the basic story and iconography of St-Christopher are perfectly coherent with Biblical narrative and tradition”: both strains point to “the foreigner being the vehicle for the advancing of the Church to the ends of the Earth.”

An image shaped by Egyptian animal-headed figures

On this reading the earliest dog-headed images of Christopher drew their form from Egyptian statues of animal-headed figures, above all the Graeco-Egyptian Hermanubis.

Held by: P. Saintyves (Saint Christophe: Successeur d’Anubis, d’Hermès et d’Héracles, 1936), as cited by David Woods ⚠ unverified; Danijela Stefanović (Historia 62.4, 2013) ⚠ unverified.5

The reading starts from the oldest surviving image. A terracotta icon of the fifth or sixth century from Vinicko Kale (Vinica, North Macedonia) shows Christopher and George as warrior saints spearing a serpent, and Christopher has the head of a dog: the type appears almost as early as the spread of the cult in the East. In a published summary of Stefanović’s study (n. 5), the dog-headed figure on the Vinica icon “shows the influence of Egyptian statues of people with animal heads and, in particular, the depictions of the Graeco-Egyptian God Hermanubis,” the syncretic figure of Hermes and Anubis. On this reading the visual model for the dog-headed saint lay ready to hand in the late-antique eastern Mediterranean, where a dog- or jackal-headed figure was a familiar image.
