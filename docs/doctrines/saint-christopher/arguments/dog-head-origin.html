Why is St Christopher dog-headed — a literalized cliché, or a genuine dog-man tradition?

Related arguments: the cynocephali — see also the dog-headed races on the Christopher timeline · could a dog-headed man be saved?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: plausible — the transmission account (a frontier soldier described in the stock language of “dog-headed” edge-of-the-world peoples, later read literally, with the Western “Canaanite” a misreading of canineus) is coherent, fits the texts, and is the leading scholarly reconstruction; but the decisive etymological step is a reasonable conjecture rather than a documented slip, so it stops short of sound · advanced by David Woods and the mainstream of critical hagiography.

The claim

The claim is that Christopher’s dog’s head is an artifact of transmission, not a report of a real monster. In its strong form it has two joints. First, the “dog-headed” description in the passiones is the Greco-Roman ethnographic cliché for peoples at the edge of the world — cannibals and cynocephali beyond the frontier — applied to a real frontier tribe (the Marmaritae) and only later taken literally, so that Eastern painters drew the dog’s head they found on the page. Second, the Western “Canaanite” label (Walter of Speyer’s Chananei, the Golden Legend’s “lineage of the Canaanites”) arose when Latin canineus, “dog-like,” was misread as Cananeus, “Canaanite.”

— David Woods, “St. Christopher, Bishop Peter of Attalia, and the Cohors Marmaritarum” (Vigiliae Christianae 48.2 [1994]: 170–186), and The Origin of the Cult of St Christopher (1999).

Who advances it

The reading is Woods’ and, in its broad shape, the consensus of critical scholarship on the legend: the dog’s head is a literary and iconographic accident, not evidence of a belief that the saint belonged to a race of dog-men. The rival positions are (a) that the medieval church genuinely held Christopher to be a member of a “monstrous race” of cynocephali — creatures the bestiary tradition and some missionary legends treated as real — and (b) the older derivation from the Egyptian jackal-headed god Anubis.

The evidence for

The transmission account is economical and text-anchored. The passiones really do describe the saint’s homeland in the exact borderland-monster idiom (“cannibals and dog-headed peoples”), and they really do attach him to a nameable Roman unit, the numerus Marmaritarum — so the “dog-headed land” and a real frontier tribe coincide, which is what the cliché-taken-literally reading predicts. The Western sources line up in the order the misreading requires: the dog’s head comes first (Greek/Eastern), then “dog-like,” then “Canaanite,” with Walter of Speyer preserving both the cynocephalus and the “Chananei” side by side — the transitional pairing a single garbled word would leave behind. Canineus and Cananeus differ by a hair in Latin minuscule, so the slip is graphically easy.1

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The ethnographic-cliché explanation is strong: casting frontier peoples as “dog-headed” cannibals is a well-attested ancient habit (Herodotus onward, through Pliny’s monstrous races and the Physiologus/bestiary tradition), and reading such a description literally is exactly the kind of thing that happens when a text outlives the idiom it was written in. Against the two rivals it wins cleanly: the Anubis derivation Woods rightly calls needless (there is no line of descent from an Egyptian god-cult to this soldier-martyr), and the “he really was thought a dog-man” reading gets the causation backwards — the belief in a dog-headed Christopher is the effect of literalizing the cliché, not an independent tradition feeding it.

What is conjecture. The one genuinely load-bearing step that is not documented is the canineus→Cananeus misreading itself. It is graphically plausible and it fits the sequence of the sources, but no manuscript is adduced actually caught in the act of the error; it is inferred from the outcome. That is a reasonable philological conjecture, not a proven event — and since the whole “Canaanite” leg of the argument rests on it, the argument earns plausible rather than sound. (The prior leg — the dog’s head as a literalized borderland cliché — is on its own stronger, near-consensus; it is the “Canaanite” etymology that carries the risk.)

Assessment

Plausible. That Christopher’s dog’s head is a transmission-artifact — a frontier-peoples cliché taken literally — is coherent, text-anchored, and the best available explanation, clearly preferable to the Anubis and “real dog-man” rivals. Its weakest joint is the specific canineus→Cananeus misreading behind the Western “Canaanite”: a good conjecture, but a conjecture, which keeps the composite claim short of sound. The core point — that the familiar dog-headed (and Canaanite-giant) Christopher is a product of how the legend was re-read, not a datum about a monster — stands.
