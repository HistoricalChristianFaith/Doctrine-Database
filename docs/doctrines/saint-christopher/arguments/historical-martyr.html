Can a historical martyr be identified beneath the Christopher legend?

Disputed reading · on Saint Christopher the dog-headed · witness: the martyrium near Chalcedon

Related readings: Why is St Christopher described and painted as dog-headed? · Did the 1969 reform of the Roman calendar de-canonize St Christopher?

The passage

The foundations of the martyrion of Saint Christophoros were laid with God in the third indiction, in the month of May, after the consulate of Protogenes and Asturius of clarissimus rank, under the emperor Theodosius and Eulalios, bishop of Chalkedon. It is built by the most dignified cubicularia, Euphemia. And the deposition (of the relics) took place in the 5th indiction just completed, on the 22nd day of the month of September, during the consulate of [Sporacius] of clarissimus rank.

— Building inscription of a martyr shrine found near Chalcedon (450–452), tr. in the Oxford Cult of Saints in Late Antiquity database (E00953), as quoted in “Saint Christopher,” Wikipedia.1

The question

The Chalcedon inscription is the earliest datable evidence for a cult of a martyr Christopher; the narratives about him come later and disagree with one another on his emperor, his place of death and his name. What is disputed is whether those narratives preserve enough to identify the man the cult honoured, or whether he is known only through the cult itself.

A martyr attested by his cult, his life unrecoverable

On this reading a real martyr Christopher was venerated from an early date, but the surviving narratives do not allow his life to be reconstructed.

Held by: Molanus (De picturis et imaginibus sacris, 1570) and Nicholas Serarius (Litaneutici, 1609) ⚠ unverified; Francis Mershman (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1908).2

Mershman calls Christopher “a martyr, probably of the third century,” and writes that “the existence of a martyr St. Christopher cannot be denied, as was sufficiently shown by” Serarius and Molanus. Mershman cites early evidence for the cult: the Chalcedon shrine of 450–452; the burial of St Remigius of Reims in a small church of the martyr Christopher in 532; a monastery of St Christopher mentioned by Gregory the Great (d. 604); an office in the Mozarabic books; and what he calls the oldest picture of the saint, at Mount Sinai, from the time of Justinian. “Almost nothing certain is known about his life or death,” Mershman writes; the Greek legend “may belong to the sixth century,” and “the simple form of the Greek and Latin passio soon gave way to more elaborate legends.” He suggests that the legend grew from the name and titles themselves: “The fact that he was frequently called a great martyr may have given rise to the story of his enormous size.” The notice in the 1969 Calendarium Romanum sets the two side by side in one sentence: “quamvis Acta S. Christophori fabulosa sint, antiqua inveniuntur monumenta eius venerationis” (although the Acts of St Christopher are legendary, ancient monuments of his veneration are found). On this reading the cult preserves the martyr, and the narratives preserve the Church’s meditation on his name.

A Marmaritan soldier martyred in 308

On this reading the earliest passiones preserve real details from which the martyr can be identified closely: a north-African captive soldier baptized at Antioch and martyred in 308.

Held by: David Woods (classicist, “St. Christopher, Bishop Peter of Attalia, and the Cohors Marmaritarum,” 1994; The Origin of the Cult of St Christopher, 1999).3

Woods starts from the earliest Greek (BHG 309–311) and Latin (BHL 1764) accounts, which make Christopher a soldier martyred at Antioch, and from a detail they preserve: his service in a Marmaritan unit, the numerus Marmaritarum. In his summary, “St. Christopher was a member of the north African tribe of the Marmaritae. He was captured by Roman forces during the emperor Diocletian’s campaign against the Marmaritae in late 301/early 302 and was transported for service in a Roman garrison in or near Antioch in Syria. He was baptised by the refugee bishop Peter of Alexandria and was martyred on 9 July 308.” The earliest Greek passion sets the events in the fourth year of Decius; Woods holds that tradition kept the regnal year and lost or garbled the emperor, and dates the martyrdom to the fourth year of Maximinus Daza. He holds that the bishop “Peter of Attalia” who, in the earliest Greek text, ransomed the body “is identifiable with the ‘presbyter’ Peter who baptized Christopher, i.e. as bishop Peter of Alexandria,” who returned the corpse to the saint’s homeland in 311. Because Egyptian sources did not know him by the name Christopher, Woods looks for his cult between Marmarica and Alexandria and finds it at the shrine of St Menas: “He is really identifiable with the Egyptian martyr known as St. Menas.” On Woods’ reading the saint’s own name was lost early, and “Christopher,” “Bearer-of-Christ,” was an honorific title.
