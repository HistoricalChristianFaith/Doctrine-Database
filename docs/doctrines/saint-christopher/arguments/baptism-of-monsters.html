Did patristic and Carolingian writers count the dog-headed peoples as human?

Disputed reading · on Saint Christopher the dog-headed · witness: Augustine of Hippo and Ratramnus of Corbie

Related readings: Why is St Christopher described and painted as dog-headed?

The passage

What shall I say of the Cynocephali, whose dog-like head and barking proclaim them beasts rather than men? But we are not bound to believe all we hear of these monstrosities. But whoever is anywhere born a man, that is, a rational, mortal animal, no matter what unusual appearance he presents in color, movement, sound, nor how peculiar he is in some power, part, or quality of his nature, no Christian can doubt that he springs from that one protoplast.

— Augustine, The City of God 16.8, text (Book 16).1

The question

Ancient ethnography placed dog-headed peoples, the cynocephali, at the edge of the world, and the Christopher legend made one of them a baptized martyr. What is disputed is how Christian writers classed such beings: as men descended from Adam, and so within the reach of mission and baptism, or as beasts. Ian Wood (2010) reads the surviving texts as “a debate about the categorisation of beings, and hence about the definition of a frontier.”2

Human only if rational, and the dog-heads doubtful

On this reading the test of humanity is reason, not bodily form, and whether the dog-headed peoples pass it is left in doubt.

Held by: Augustine of Hippo (City of God 16.8; d. 430).3

Augustine catalogues the races of secular report: the one-eyed, the backward-footed, the mouthless, the Pygmies, the shade-footed Skiopodes, and the Cynocephali. Form does not settle the question. A man born with crescent-shaped two-fingered hands at Hippo-Diarrhytus is still descended from the first man, and so are “all the races which are reported to have diverged in bodily appearance from the usual course which nature generally or almost universally preserves, if they are embraced in that definition of man as rational and mortal animals.” The condition is reason. The dog-heads’ head and barking “proclaim them beasts rather than men,” and the reports themselves may be false. He ends “cautiously and guardedly”: “either these things which have been told of some races have no existence at all; or if they do exist, they are not human races; or if they are human, they are descended from Adam.” Wood reads Augustine as concentrating on “whether such monstrous beings were human, id est animal rationale,” and writes that “unlike some subsequent writers Augustine doubted whether they could be so categorised”; he “implicitly came down in opposition to the notion of the cynocephalus being an animal rationale.” On this reading Augustine fixes the rule that later writers apply: a rational creature of any shape is a son of Adam.

A people at the outer margin, unclean and beast-like

On this reading the dog-headed peoples are classed with the beasts, or set as far from the human as a people can be.

Held by: the Cosmographia of “Aethicus Ister” (8th c.); unnamed “most theologians” of Ratramnus’s day.4

The eighth-century Cosmographia attributed to Aethicus Ister describes the cynocephali in detail: their appearance, dress, diet, housing and herding. Wood reads its author as deploying “the categories of Leviticus to portray them as being as marginal as possible, while still describing them by the term gens”; the Cosmographer’s description draws on the Abominations of Leviticus, and he associates the dog-headed beings with Canaan. The classification had a practical edge. Rimbert, missionary in Scandinavia and later Archbishop of Hamburg-Bremen, wrote to ask, in Wood’s paraphrase, whether the dog-heads were “humans, descendents of Adam, and thus the potential object of mission, or were they animals, which could as a result be killed with impunity?” Augustine had written that their head and barking proclaim them beasts. Ratramnus, answering, reports that most theologians were “inclined to consider the cynocephali as animals rather than men,” in the New Schaff-Herzog’s summary of his letter. On this reading the dog-headed belong with the monstrous and unclean at the world’s end, at or beyond the boundary of the human.

Rational, descended from Adam, and to be evangelized

On this reading the dog-headed peoples reported by the missionaries reason, keep laws and know shame, and so are men to whom the Gospel is to be preached.

Held by: Ratramnus of Corbie (Epistola de cynocephalis to Rimbert, before 865); the apocryphal Acts of Andrew and Bartholomew, in narrative.5

Ratramnus works through Rimbert’s report point by point. The dog-heads live in villages under common law; they plough and reap; they cover their genitals; they wear woven cloth as well as skins. Law implies common consent, agriculture implies skill, clothing implies a rational soul, modesty implies knowledge of what is honourable, and none of these exists without the judgement of reason. They keep domestic animals, which they could not do, Ratramnus argues, if they had bestial rather than rational souls, since Genesis subjects the animals to man. Reason distinguishes man from the beasts, the point of Augustine’s animal rationale; therefore the cynocephali are human and descended from Adam. Ratramnus adds St Christopher, a cynocephalus who “was regarded as being a man and merited baptism,” in Wood’s summary. English renderings of the letter have him say he cannot see how they could subdue animals “if they had an animal and not a rational soul,” that “there cannot be any law, which common descent has not decreed,” and that modesty requires “a soul which can distinguish between what is shameful and what is honourable,” and that to deny Rimbert’s testimony “would be stubbornness rather than prudence” ⚠ unverified. The apocryphal Acts tell the same story: a dog-headed cannibal, named “Abominable,” is baptized, loses his animal nature, receives human speech and joins the apostles’ mission.
