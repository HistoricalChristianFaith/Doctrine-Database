The earliest trace of a cult: the martyrium near Chalcedon

Dates: built May 450, consecrated 22 September 452
Summary: The earliest datable evidence for a Saint Christopher is a dedicatory inscription from a martyrium (a martyr’s shrine) near Chalcedon, opposite Constantinople. It attests a cult of a martyr named Christopher in Asia Minor by the mid-fifth century. It carries none of the legend found in later sources: no dog’s head, no river, no Christ-child.

Claims & evidence

The inscription

A stone inscription recovered from the ruins of a church near Chalcedon (Kadıköy, on the Asian shore across from Constantinople) records the building of “the martyrion of Saint Christophoros.” By its own dating the work ran from May 450 to the consecration on 22 September 452. This is the earliest datable evidence that a martyr called Christopher was already venerated, with a shrine of his own, in the Greek-speaking East by the middle of the fifth century — roughly 150–200 years after the persecutions (Decius or Diocletian) in which the passiones place his death.

— See the references gathered in “Saint Christopher,” Wikipedia, and David Woods, “St. Christopher, Bishop Peter of Attalia, and the Cohors Marmaritarum: A Fresh Examination,” Vigiliae Christianae 48.2 (1994): 170–186.

A cult without the legend

The inscription contains no dog’s head, no giant, no ferrying of travellers, no Christ-child — only a shrine to a martyr. The story now attached to the name survives in later sources, the Greek and Latin passiones. Francis Mershman (1908) writes that “originally, St. Christopher was only a martyr, and as such is recorded in the old martyrologies,” and that “the simple form of the Greek and Latin passio soon gave way to more elaborate legends.” David Woods (1999) adds that the saint’s real name, on his reading, “must have been lost at a very early date,” given the inscription of 452.

— Francis Mershman, “St. Christopher,” Catholic Encyclopedia vol. 3 (1908); David Woods, The Origin of the Cult of St Christopher (web study, 1999).
