The Dating of Noah's Flood

This timeline gathers the datable sources that have been used to fix, or to dispute, a date for Noah's Flood: the begettings of Genesis 5 and 11, the chronographers who reckoned from them, a Babylonian list of kings before the Flood, and two geological horizons proposed as the flood of tradition. The historicity of Noah's Flood records who has held the Flood to be a real event across the centuries; this page records the dates proposed for it and the sources they rest on.

The biblical figures are themselves disputed. The genealogies of Genesis 5 and 11 survive in three text-traditions whose numbers disagree — the Masoretic Hebrew, the Septuagint Greek, and the Samaritan Pentateuch — so the sources below yield not one interval from Creation to the Flood but three (see the entry on the genealogies). Eusebius and the Alexandrian chronographers reckoned on the Greek figures, Augustine and Ussher on the Hebrew, and the question of which text keeps the earlier numbers reaches into the Septuagint's origin and the transmission of the Hebrew text.

A note on ordering: the timeline entries below are datable evidence-sources — a stratum's deposition, a genealogy's composition, a chronographer's table — arranged by the age of the evidence, not by the date of the Flood each bears on. The order is a shelf of sources, not a claimed chronology.

Related doctrines: Primeval history (Genesis 6–9) — see also the sons of God and the Nephilim (the belief-history of the giants) · the historicity of Noah's Flood (the belief-history of the event whose date is recorded here).

Timeline

Datable evidence-sources bearing on the date of the Flood, earliest evidence-date first (see the ordering note above). Each yields, or disputes, a candidate date; the disputed readings are gathered in the "Disputed readings" section below.

The Black Sea shelf-drowning stratum (deposition c. 5600 BC)

The oldest date-bearing source here is a physical one: the drowned shoreline, wave-cut terraces, and the freshwater-to-marine mollusc change on the Black Sea shelf, which William Ryan and Walter Pitman proposed in 1997–98 as a catastrophic early-Holocene inundation — the event their book titled Noah's Flood. The horizon they identified lies near 5600 BC. Ali Aksu, Richard Hiscott and colleagues (2002) argued instead for a persistent Holocene outflow of Black Sea water into the Mediterranean, which by their title "Contradicts Noah's Flood Hypothesis."1

The Mesopotamian alluvial silt bands (deposits dated c. 3500–2800 BC)

Leonard Woolley struck a clean silt band at Ur in 1929, below the Royal Cemetery. Of the first test pit he cautioned that "one could scarcely argue for the Deluge on the strength of a pit a yard square"; in his later accounts of the find he wrote that he was "quite convinced of what it all meant," and identified the deposit with the Flood. David MacDonald, who surveys it with the comparable deposits at Kish and Shuruppak, dates the Ur deposit to about 3500 BC.2 Reasoning from the Kish dynasty's reign-lengths, Max Mallowan placed the flood stratum at Shuruppak and Kish near 2900–2800 BC and concluded the flood was local, not global.3 Mallowan's date falls some five centuries before Ussher's Masoretic 2348 BC (below).

The genealogies of Genesis 5 and 11 (the chronological schemes)

The begettings and lifespans of Genesis 5 (Adam to Noah) and Genesis 11 (Shem to Abraham) yield a reckonable interval from Creation to the Flood, but the three surviving text-traditions do not agree on the numbers.4 Summed, the Masoretic figures give 1656 years from Creation to the Flood, the Samaritan Pentateuch 1307, and the Septuagint about 2242 — a spread of nearly a thousand years, driven mostly by whether each patriarch's begetting-age carries an added hundred years. Which tradition keeps the earlier figures is disputed by the chronographers below and on the interpretation page in the "Disputed readings" section; the question connects to the Septuagint's origin and the transmission of the Hebrew text.

Berossus' Babyloniaca: ten antediluvian kings, 432,000 years (composed c. 278 BC)

The Babylonian priest Berossus, writing in Greek for Antiochus I, recorded ten kings reigning before the Flood for 120 saroi — 120 × 3,600 = 432,000 years — a span preserved through Eusebius and George Syncellus after Berossus' own text was lost.5 Every antediluvian span in the list is a whole multiple of the šár (3,600 years), the 432,000 an even 120 of them.

Josephus' Antiquities: the reckoning to the Flood, and a text in crux (composed c. AD 93)

Flavius Josephus, retelling the primeval history in Greek for a Roman readership, closes the antediluvian genealogy with an explicit total — the Flood came, he writes, "two thousand six hundred and fifty-six years from Adam the first man" (Antiquities 1.82).6 The transmitted 2656 varies across manuscripts (2262, 1656, 1056), and Josephus' own summed begetting-ages add instead to 2256 — the reading Isaac Vossius adopted in 1659 to harmonize him with the Septuagint against the received text. His individual patriarch-ages carry the same centenary additions as the Septuagint's, so his interval runs roughly six centuries longer than the Hebrew's 1,656 and fourteen years from the Septuagint's 2,242; Felix Schlichter sets out the arithmetic and the early-modern controversy it fuelled. His figures are cited in the dispute over whether the Hebrew was later shortened (on the transmission-of-the-text page). He agrees with the Hebrew and Samaritan against the Greek in omitting the second Cainan of Genesis 11 (his Arphaxad begets Sala directly). He appears also as a witness on several other timelines, gathered on his detail page.

Eusebius' Chronicon: the three reckonings tabulated (c. 320 AD)

Eusebius of Caesarea, in his Chronicon, set the Hebrew, Septuagint, and Samaritan totals of Genesis 5–11 side by side and chose among them.7 He preferred the longer Septuagint reckoning against the Hebrew, and held that the Hebrew begetting-ages had been shortened; his case is set out on his entry on the transmission-of-the-text timeline.

The Alexandrian world-era chronographers: the Flood at Anno Mundi 2242 (Panodorus and Annianus, c. 400–412)

The Alexandrian monks Panodorus (fl. c. 400) and Annianus (fl. c. 400–412, under Patriarch Theophilus of Alexandria) built the Alexandrian world era on the Septuagint figures of Genesis 5–11. Reckoning the Greek begetting-ages, they placed the Flood at Anno Mundi 2242 (the total George Syncellus preferred, on Eusebius' authority, over Africanus' 2262), about 3250 BC, with Creation fixed at 25 March 5492 BC (Annianus) / c. 5493 BC (Panodorus).8 Their scheme counts exactly 5,500 years from Creation to the Incarnation, with Creation, Annunciation, and Resurrection all on 25 March; Syncellus, through whom their work survives, states his aim as confirming "the incarnation of Christ at the completion of the 5500th year." The Byzantine world era (Creation 1 September 5509 BC) and the Chronicon Paschale (5507 BC, c. 630) both keep the Flood at Anno Mundi 2242. They appear with the fuller reckoning on their detail page.

Augustine's City of God XV: the chronology dispute, decided for the Hebrew (c. 420)

Augustine, writing in Roman Africa, took up the same discrepancy. In City of God XV.10–14 he lays out the uniform hundred-year offset between the Hebrew manuscripts and his own Latin's Septuagint parent. Taking up the Methuselah crux, where the Greek numbers leave the patriarch alive fourteen years after the Flood he did not board, he judges the Hebrew reading true, assigns the Greek's added centuries to a single copyist's deliberate scheme (not to Jewish tampering, and not to the Seventy), and lays down the rule that where the versions diverge one should prefer "that language out of which the translation was made."9 He reconstructs the copyist's motive as padding each begetting-age so that the antediluvian "years" would look short. Augustine elsewhere defends the authority of the Septuagint (City of God XVIII.42–43); here he does not convert either reckoning into a calendar date for the Flood. The fuller treatment is on his detail page.

Ussher's Annals of the World: the Flood at 2348 BC (1650)

James Ussher's Annales veteris testamenti fixed Creation at 4004 BC and the Flood at 2348 BC, reckoning on the Masoretic figures (1656 years from Creation to the Flood).10 Ussher noted that the interval depends on the text chosen — Hebrew 1656, Samaritan 1307, Septuagint/Ethiopic longer — and adopted the Hebrew.
