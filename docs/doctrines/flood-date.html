The Dating of Noah's Flood

The other Genesis 6–9 timelines on this wiki ask different questions. The historicity of Noah's Flood is a belief-history: it tracks who held the Flood to be a real event across the centuries. The Flood and the Nephilim in the material record asks whether the datable ground — tablets, strata, king-lists, megaliths — corroborates a Flood at all ("is this silt band a deluge deposit?"). This page asks the next question down, and a distinct one: granting that a Flood happened, when? It walks the candidate dates — the ones the texts yield and the ones the ground yields — and rates whether any of them cohere. Corroboration and dating are separate sub-claims: a stratum can be a real catastrophe (a question for the material-record page) and still give a date that fits no biblical reckoning (the question here). So where a horizon has already been weighed for corroboration, this page points at that page rather than re-litigating it, and asks only what date it yields.

Granting the premise is not assuming the conclusion. Each candidate date is weighed on its own merits, and several are expected to come out weak — possibility is not proof, and a page's job is sometimes to report that the dates do not converge. This page also carries an axis the material-record page structurally lacks: the biblical chronology is itself disputed. The genealogies of Genesis 5 and 11 survive in three text-traditions whose numbers disagree — the Masoretic "short" chronology (on which Ussher fixed the Flood at 2348 BC), the Septuagint "long" chronology (pushing Creation and the Flood roughly a millennium earlier), and the Samaritan Pentateuch, shorter still. So "the biblical date of the Flood" is not one number but a contested range, and that dispute reaches into the Septuagint's origin and the transmission of the Hebrew text. The two argument groups below take the two axes in turn: the textual dispute (which reckoning?) and the dispute against the ground (which horizon, if any, fits?).

A note on ordering, as on the material-record page: the timeline "witnesses" below are datable evidence-sources — a stratum's deposition, a genealogy's composition, a chronographer's table — arranged by the age of the evidence, not by the age of the Flood it bears on (which is exactly what this page leaves open and tests). The order is a shelf of sources, not a claimed chronology.

Timeline

Datable evidence-sources bearing on the date of the Flood, earliest evidence-date first (see the ordering note above). Each yields, or disputes, a candidate date; the arguments that weigh them are gathered in the "Arguments" section below.

The Black Sea shelf-drowning stratum (deposition c. 5600 BC)

The oldest date-bearing witness on the shelf is a physical one: the drowned shoreline, wave-cut terraces, and the freshwater-to-marine mollusc change on the Black Sea shelf, which William Ryan and Walter Pitman proposed in 1997–98 as a catastrophic early-Holocene inundation — the event their book titled Noah's Flood.1 Its bearing here is only the date it yields. Even granting the breach was catastrophic and granting it seeded a flood memory — both contested on the material-record page — the horizon sits near 5600 BC, with no textual chain running from it to the Mesopotamian flood poems four millennia later. It therefore supplies a candidate date only for those willing to sever the Flood from the biblical genealogies entirely, and nothing that could fix a date on the biblical side.

The Mesopotamian alluvial silt bands (deposition c. 2900 BC)

The clean silt band Leonard Woolley struck at Ur in 1929, with the comparable deposits at Kish and Shuruppak, is the horizon most often equated with the flood of the Sumerian King List and, through it, with Noah's.2 Reasoning from the Kish dynasty's reign-lengths, Max Mallowan placed the deposit near 2900–2800 BC and concluded the flood was local, not global.3 Its date-bearing value is that it sits on the short end of the range — within a few centuries of Ussher's Masoretic 2348 BC — which is why short-chronology defenders reach for it; whether it is a Flood deposit at all is weighed separately on the material-record page.

The genealogies of Genesis 5 and 11 (the chronological schemes)

The begettings and lifespans of Genesis 5 (Adam to Noah) and Genesis 11 (Shem to Abraham) are the only witness that yields a reckonable interval from Creation to the Flood — but the three surviving text-traditions do not agree on the numbers.4 Summed, the Masoretic figures give 1656 years from Creation to the Flood, the Samaritan Pentateuch 1307, and the Septuagint about 2242 — a spread of nearly a thousand years driven mostly by whether each patriarch's begetting-age carries an added hundred years. This divergence is not a rounding error but the crux of the whole dating question, and it is developed as its own argument (below) rather than resolved here; it connects directly to the Septuagint's origin and the transmission of the Hebrew text.

Berossus' Babyloniaca: ten antediluvian kings, 432,000 years (composed c. 278 BC)

The Babylonian priest Berossus, writing in Greek for Antiochus I, recorded ten kings reigning before the Flood for 120 saroi — 120 × 3,600 = 432,000 years — a span preserved through Eusebius and George Syncellus after Berossus' own text was lost.5 The ten antediluvian kings map onto the ten antediluvian patriarchs of Genesis 5, and both descend from the same Mesopotamian matrix as the Sumerian King List — but the numbers are transparently sexagesimal artifice, not a rival measured chronology. Berossus stands on the shelf as the datum a biblical dating has to out-explain: the same ten-generation frame, inflated to a scale no reckoning of Genesis approaches.

Eusebius' Chronicon: the three reckonings tabulated (c. 320 AD)

Eusebius of Caesarea, building the first comparative Christian chronology, set the Hebrew, Septuagint, and Samaritan totals of Genesis 5–11 side by side and had to choose among them — the earliest surviving witness to the very dispute this page turns on.6 His Chronicon is the anchor the textual arguments below reuse rather than reinvent; he already lays out that the choice of text-tradition, not any single number, is what fixes the interval — and his own preference for the longer Septuagint reckoning against the Hebrew is itself part of the case that the shorter Hebrew figures were later shortened.

Ussher's Annals of the World: the Flood at 2348 BC (1650)

James Ussher's Annales veteris testamenti fixed Creation at 4004 BC and the Flood at 2348 BC, reckoning on the Masoretic figures (1656 years from Creation to the Flood) — the canonical statement of the short chronology, and the number most readers mean by "the biblical date of the Flood."7 Ussher himself noted that the interval depends on the text chosen — Hebrew 1656, Samaritan 1307, Septuagint/Ethiopic longer — and simply preferred the Hebrew. His date is the fixed point the "short vs. long" argument below is organized around.

Arguments

Sub-claims marshaled for (or against) a date, each weighed adversarially on its own page with an assessment of the interpretation it serves. Two groups take the two axes in turn: which biblical reckoning fixes the interval, and whether any material horizon fits a datable Flood. Where a line has not yet been developed, the question is stated without a verdict rather than pre-judged.

Related arguments — the biblical chronologies

The genealogies of Genesis 5 and 11 survive in three disagreeing text-traditions, so "the biblical date of the Flood" is a contested interval, not a single number. These arguments weigh the rival reckonings against each other — the Masoretic "short" chronology (Flood c. 2348 BC), the Septuagint "long" chronology (Flood in the fourth millennium or earlier), and the Samaritan Pentateuch, shorter still (1,307 years to the Flood) — asking which better preserves the original figures and which coheres better with the datable record. They are best read as a set; each is developed as its own page in turn.

Does the Masoretic short chronology date the Flood to c. 2348 BC? (assessment: weak)

A clean reckoning that fails as a date — summing the begetting-ages of the received Hebrew text gives 1,656 years from Creation to the Flood, which Ussher chained to 2348 BC, the number most study-Bible margins still carry. The computation is unimpeachable and the claim that the Masoretic figures are the more original (against the Septuagint's inflated ones) is a genuinely live text-critical position. But the interpretation it serves — that this fixes a real Flood date — does not hold: the "original figures" premise is one side of an unsettled dispute, summing the ages assumes closed genealogies that the lists themselves show to be open (the Cainan the Greek Genesis and Luke carry but the Hebrew omits), and a literal global deluge at 2348 BC collides with the continuous, unbroken record of Egypt and Mesopotamia straddling that date. Weak — scoped to the young single-date global-Flood model, not to the Hebrew text or to a flood tradition with a real kernel.8

Does the Septuagint long chronology date the Flood to the fourth millennium BC? (assessment: weak)

The better-defended reckoning that still fails as a date — read the same genealogies in the Greek and most patriarchs beget a hundred years later, stretching the interval to about 2,240–2,260 years from Creation to the Flood and pushing the event back roughly a millennium, to c. 3300–3000 BC (Creation c. 5554 BC on the leading modern reconstruction). This is the older Christian reckoning — Eusebius' Chronicon, the Byzantine Anno Mundi, the "world will last six thousand years" scheme — and the claim that the Greek figures are the original ones, deflated later in the Hebrew, is a genuinely live text-critical position, arguably the stronger of the two reckonings: it also relieves the impossibly compressed post-Flood interval and lands the Flood before the literate king-lists the short chronology collides with. But the interpretation it serves — that this fixes a real Flood date — still does not hold: the "original figures" premise is one contested side of the dispute (and the long chronology may itself be inflated to seat Christ at Anno Mundi 5500), the transmitted Greek numbers are unstable (Methuselah outlives the Flood in the standard printed text), the genealogies are open (the Greek text's own extra Cainan, which Luke repeats), and even c. 3200 BC the continuous Naqada-Egyptian and Uruk-Mesopotamian sequences run across the horizon with no rupture. Weak — the milder and more instructive failure of the two, scoped to the young single-date global-Flood model, not to the Greek text or to a flood with a real kernel.9

Does the Samaritan Pentateuch preserve the original chronology of Genesis 5 and 11? (assessment: weak)

The third witness, edited in the act — the Samaritan Pentateuch reads Genesis 5 almost exactly like the Hebrew but cuts three begetting-ages (Jared 162→62, Methuselah 187→67, Lamech 182→53), summing to 1,307 years from Creation to the Flood, the shortest of the three reckonings. A real minority — Bertheau (1878), Jepsen (1929), Larsson (1973), and a modern prime-number argument — has advocated these as the original figures from which the Hebrew derived, so the claim clears the "is it advocated?" bar and earns a rated page. But it fails on its best case: those three cuts are precisely the amounts needed to make Jared, Methuselah, and Lamech all die in the Flood year (AM 1,307) — a transparent schematic edit inherited from the Book of Jubilees, and the cleanest single proof that at least one tradition was reworked on purpose. No advocate even converts 1,307 into a Flood date; it functions not as a rival date but as a text-critical witness — a thin priority claim, and, because it is pre-rabbinic, a genuine datum in the deflation debate. Weak — scoped to the "Samaritan-is-original" thesis, not to the Samaritan text's value as a witness.10

Related arguments — dating against the ground

Granting a Flood, these arguments test whether any datable material horizon actually yields a date a biblical reckoning can accept — a young single-date deluge against the strata, and the unbroken continuity of civilizations that a recent global Flood would have had to interrupt. They inherit the material-record page's corroboration verdicts and ask the narrower dating question on top of them; each is developed as its own page in turn.

Does a datable horizon converge with a biblical Flood date closely enough to be the same event? (assessment: weak)

A date-match that is not an identity — set the shelf of firmly dated flood horizons beside the candidate biblical dates and two fits are real, one of them striking: the Liangzhu flood-collapse (uranium–thorium dated to c. 2395–2374 BC) falls within a few decades of Ussher's Masoretic 2348 BC, and the Septuagint's fourth-millennium window sits squarely among the Mesopotamian silts, with Ur just above it and Shuruppak — the tradition's own named city — just below. But a shared date is not a shared event. The tightest fit of all, Liangzhu, is a monsoon flood of the Yangtze delta that no text remembers, which shows date-coincidence proving nothing; the biblical target is a thousand years wide (the three text-traditions disagree) against a shelf of a dozen Holocene floods, so a near-hit is a selection effect rather than a discovery; and every horizon that lands near a biblical number is a non-synchronous local flood that civilizations occupied straight through, with no globally correlated stratum and, on the relevant side, no memory-chain. Weak — scoped to the global-deluge identification; the one instructive residue, the Septuagint window at Shuruppak, points at the longer reckoning and at a regional Mesopotamian flood behind the texts, the smaller kernel claim weighed on the material-record page.11

Does the continuous record of Egypt and Mesopotamia across c. 2350 BC falsify a young global Flood date? (assessment: weak)

The prediction that fails at the horizon — the classic and strongest empirical objection to the short-Flood date, run as the young single-date global model's own risky prediction. A worldwide eight-survivor deluge at c. 2348 BC must leave a universal, synchronous rupture — depopulation, broken king-lists, ceramic and linguistic discontinuity, a restart from eight people — at that horizon. It is not there: at 2348 BC Egypt is mid-Sixth-Dynasty, building and administering an unbroken Old Kingdom that runs into the brief, documented First Intermediate Period and out again; Mesopotamia is at the close of Early Dynastic III on the cusp of Sargon of Akkad, its cities continuously occupied through Akkad and Ur III — and the Sumerian King List itself places its Flood before this whole sequence. The creationist repair, compressing Egyptian chronology by a millennium, is answered not by the disputed king-lists but by radiocarbon, which anchors the third-millennium sequence to the calendar independently and confirms rather than compresses it; to escape that the model must reject an independent physical method exactly where it converges with the record. Weak — scoped to the young single-date global model; a regional Mesopotamian flood (which the tradition places before this record) and the historicity of a flood memory with a real kernel both stand untouched.12
