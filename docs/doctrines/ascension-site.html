The site of the Ascension: the Imbomon and the Chapel of the Ascension

The New Testament fixes Christ's Ascension on the Mount of Olives — Luke has him lead the disciples out “as far as to Bethany” and be “carried up into heaven,” and Acts places their return “from the mount called Olivet, which is from Jerusalem a sabbath day's journey” — but it never marks a spot. This page traces how the church answered the question the text leaves open: which point on the long ridge of Olivet became the shown place of the Ascension, how it was built, roofed (and pointedly left un-roofed), lost, and rebuilt, and how a single footprint-bearing rock came to stand at its centre.

It is a reception-history timeline: not a survey of who believed a doctrine, but a chronicle of how one location-tradition grew and was tested. Its “witnesses” are datable sources and buildings — a pre-Constantinian apologist's aside, an emperor's biographer, the oldest travel diary, a nun's liturgical road-log, a Roman noblewoman's rotunda, two Latin writers on a miracle of footprints, a Frankish bishop's architectural sketch, a Crusader edicule, and a mosque's dome — ordered earliest-source-first and each footnoted. The shape that recurs across the Passion sites appears here in a sharpened form: the mountain is certain and the memory continuous, but the exact spot drifted — from a teaching-cave to a higher summit rotunda — and its one physical anchor is not a tomb or a wall but a footprint-relic that is itself a fourth-century miracle-tradition. Two questions — whether that footprint is Christ's, and whether the summit chapel marks the actual place of the Ascension against Luke's Bethany-ward wording — are weighed on their own argument pages.

Timeline

The Gospels place the Ascension on Olivet (1st century)

Two Lucan texts carry the whole geography. The Gospel ends with a parting on the road toward Bethany: “he led them out as far as to Bethany, and he lifted up his hands, and blessed them. And it came to pass, while he blessed them, he was parted from them, and carried up into heaven.”1 Acts sets the scene on the mountain and gives a distance: “a cloud received him out of their sight… Then returned they unto Jerusalem from the mount called Olivet, which is from Jerusalem a sabbath day's journey.”2 Behind both stands Zechariah's oracle that the Lord's “feet shall stand… upon the mount of Olives, which is before Jerusalem on the east” — the prophecy every later writer reads onto the site.3 Note the seam the tradition will have to manage: Luke's “as far as to Bethany” points over the eastern slope, while Acts's “sabbath day's journey” fits the near summit — and it is the summit that later gets the church.

Eusebius: the cave “shewn there” and Helena's church on the summit (c. 315–338)

The earliest datable notice of a shown Ascension site is in Eusebius, and it predates any church. In the Proof of the Gospel, expounding Zechariah 14:4, he reports that believers “congregate from all parts of the world… that they may worship at the Mount of Olives,” where the Lord “had borne up the Mount of Olives to the cave that is shewn there; there He prayed and delivered to His disciples… the mysteries of His end, and thence He made His Ascension into heaven.”4 A single cave here holds the teaching, the prayer, and the Ascension together. A generation later his Life of Constantine records Helena raising “a sacred church and temple on the very summit of the mount… in memory of his ascent to heaven” — the church over the teaching-cave that later sources call the Eleona.5 The memory is early and continuous; the precision — separating the teaching-cave from the ascension-spot proper — comes later.

The Bordeaux Pilgrim marks Constantine's basilica on Olivet (333)

The oldest surviving Christian travel diary, written the year of the journey, catches the Constantinian building in place: “you go up into the Mount of Olives, where the Lord taught his apostles before his passion; there a basilica was made on Constantine's command.”6 This is independent confirmation, from a different pen the same decade as Eusebius, of the church on the teaching-cave by 333. Two cautions the timeline will honour: the pilgrim ties the basilica to where the Lord taught, not explicitly to the Ascension, and the “little hill” he mentions nearby is the Transfiguration-tradition knoll, not the ascension-spot — the finer localizing is still a half-century off.

Egeria: the Imbomon, “the place whence the Lord ascended” (c. 384)

By the 380s the ascension-spot proper has stepped out from the teaching-cave and acquired a name. The pilgrim Egeria, describing the Palm Sunday afternoon procession, has the people “go up with hymns to the Imbomon, that is, to the place whence the Lord ascended into heaven,” and repeats the identical gloss at each visit — Holy Thursday, the Easter octave, and Pentecost, when “the passage from the Gospel… where it speaks of the Lord's Ascension, also that from the Acts of the Apostles… is read” on the very ground.7 She keeps the two Olivet sites firmly apart — the church “on Eleona” below, the “Imbomon” above — but still calls the Imbomon a place, not a church: the round Church of the Ascension was built there only in her own decade, or just after. (A telling detail: Egeria's fortieth-day-after-Easter observance falls not here but at Bethlehem; the Ascension feast is not yet localized on the spot.)

Poemenia builds the round church around the footprints (c. 384–390)

The first church on the Imbomon was raised, a few years after Egeria's visit, by Poemenia, a wealthy Roman aristocrat of the imperial circle, who financed it “around Christ's last footprints.” It was built as a rotunda open to the sky — a ring of porticoes and arches with the sacred spot beneath the open crown — the form all the early descriptions share.8 Poemenia's name reaches us through the biographical tradition around Palladius and the Life of Peter the Iberian rather than through a building inscription, so the attribution is a matter of scholarly reconstruction; what is firm is that a round, open-topped church stood on the Imbomon by about 390, deliberately unroofed above the footprint-relic at its heart.

Paulinus and Sulpicius Severus: the footprints the pavement would not cover (c. 403)

Two Latin writers, corresponding friends, fix the footprint-tradition in the record within a few years of the church's building. Paulinus of Nola writes that in the basilica of the Ascension is “the place from which [Jesus] was taken up to heaven in a cloud,” and that “this one place — and no other — was sanctified by the divine footprints to such an extent that it has never been covered with marble or any pavement.”9 His correspondent Sulpicius Severus tells it as a standing miracle: the spot “could not be joined by a pavement with the remaining part of the street. For the earth… rejected all the appliances laid upon it, and often threw back the blocks of marble in the faces of those who were seeking to place them… the footprints are still to be seen; and although the faith of those who daily flock to that place… carry away what had been trodden by the feet of the Lord, yet the sand of the place suffers no injury.”10 The open crown of Poemenia's rotunda and the footprint-relic beneath it are, from the start, explained by the same miracle-story: the ground will not be covered, and the earth is inexhaustible.

The Persians destroy the rotunda; it is rebuilt (614–c. 630)

Poemenia's church fell in the Persian sack of Jerusalem under Khosrow II in 614, which levelled much of the Christian building on Olivet, and was rebuilt in the following years (tradition assigns the restoration of the holy places to Modestus, later patriarch of Jerusalem).11 It is this rebuilt rotunda — still round, still open to the sky — that the next great witness walks into.

Arculf: the roofless round church, the footprints, the eight lamps (c. 680)

The Frankish bishop Arculf, whose eyewitness report Adomnán of Iona wrote up as De Locis Sanctis, gives the earliest detailed description of the fabric: “a great round church, having in its circuit three vaulted porticoes… The interior of the church, without roof or vault, lies open to heaven under the open air.” Inside, “the mark of the dust that was trodden by the Lord is so lasting that the impression of the footsteps may be perceived,” and though pilgrims daily “snatch away some of what was trodden by the Lord, yet the area perceives no loss” — the same inexhaustible dust Sulpicius described. High in the walls “twice four windows” hold “as many lamps” whose light pours down the mountain toward Jerusalem.12 Adomnán's account, abridged by Bede, carried the picture of the open-crowned Ascension rotunda and its eight lamps across the medieval West.

The Crusader octagon and Saladin's waqf (12th century–1187)

The Crusaders replaced the ruined rotunda with the small octagonal edicule that survives — an octagonal outer wall enclosing an inner octagonal shrine over the footprint-rock, an open-air martyrium in the old tradition.13 After Saladin took Jerusalem in 1187 the site passed into Muslim hands and became an Islamic endowment (waqf): a mihrab was set in the shrine, and — reversing eight centuries of deliberate openness — the octagon was at last roofed, capped with the stone dome and drum it still carries. The Ascension is honoured in Islam too, which is why the shrine was kept rather than razed.

The site today: the footprint rock, the shared feast, the summit rivals

The edicule stands under the authority of the Islamic waqf and is open to visitors of all faiths for a small fee; by long custom Christians of several communions are permitted to set up altars and celebrate the Ascension there each year. At its centre is the “Ascension Rock,” shown as bearing the imprint of Christ's right foot — the stone said to have held the left having been carried off to the al-Aqsa Mosque in the Middle Ages.14 The summit now carries other Ascension claimants as well — most visibly the Russian Orthodox convent with its tall “Russian Candle” bell-tower — a reminder that the exact point of the Ascension, unlike a tomb or a wall, has never been anchored to a single fixed feature. Whether this summit spot, or the footprint at its heart, can bear the weight the tradition puts on them is taken up below.

Arguments

Is the venerated rock the footprint of Christ? — weak

The footprint in the “Ascension Rock” is a genuine and ancient object of devotion: the tradition of footprints preserved in the dust is attested from the very building of the church — Paulinus and Sulpicius Severus c. 403, Arculf c. 680 — and has been continuously venerated since. But the claim that the mark is the physical imprint of Christ's foot is weak: the earliest witnesses describe footprints in dust that pilgrims carried away yet that never diminished — a standing miracle, not a stable mineralogical impression — and the shaped stone shown in the edicule today is a medieval relic, its “right foot” and vanished “left foot” the furniture of later devotion. No source earlier than the fourth-century cult, and nothing in the rock itself, connects the mark to the first century. Authentic as a 1,600-year-old devotional focus; without basis as a bodily trace.

Does the summit chapel mark the actual place of the Ascension? — weak

A two-level verdict, kept distinct. That the Ascension happened on the Mount of Olives is well grounded — Acts names “the mount called Olivet” explicitly, at “a sabbath day's journey” from the city — so the general location is secure. But that this summit point is the actual spot is weak: the site demonstrably drifted in antiquity (Eusebius's single cave, then Egeria's higher Imbomon), no source pinpoints it before the fourth century, its only anchor is the footprint-relic assessed above, and it sits in unresolved tension with Luke's own wording — “he led them out as far as to Bethany,” over the eastern slope, farther than Acts's sabbath-day distance to the summit. The summit chapel is best read as a commemorative fixing that harmonizes Acts's “Olivet” with the accessible ridge-top, not as a located first-century spot. The mountain is certain; the step is a devotional choice.
