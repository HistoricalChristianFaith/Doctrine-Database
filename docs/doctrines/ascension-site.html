The site of the Ascension: the Imbomon and the Chapel of the Ascension

The New Testament fixes Christ's Ascension on the Mount of Olives — Luke has him lead the disciples out “as far as to Bethany” and be “carried up into heaven,” and Acts places their return “from the mount called Olivet, which is from Jerusalem a sabbath day's journey” — but it never names a spot on the ridge. This page traces, source by source, which point on Olivet was shown as the place of the Ascension, how it was built on, left open to the sky, destroyed, rebuilt and finally roofed, and how a footprint-bearing rock came to stand at its centre.

It is a reception-history timeline: not a survey of who believed a doctrine, but a chronicle of one location-tradition. Its “witnesses” are datable sources and buildings — a pre-Constantinian apologist's aside, an emperor's biographer, the oldest travel diary, a pilgrim's liturgical road-log, a rotunda attributed to a Roman noblewoman, two Latin writers on a miracle of footprints, a Frankish bishop's architectural sketch, a Crusader edicule, and a mosque's dome — ordered earliest-source-first and each footnoted. The sources move from a single teaching-cave (Eusebius) to a separate upper station, the Imbomon (Egeria), and from the late fourth century they report footprints of Christ shown at that station.

Timeline

The Gospels place the Ascension on Olivet (1st century)

Two Lucan texts give the New Testament's geography of the Ascension. The Gospel ends with a parting on the road toward Bethany: “he led them out as far as to Bethany, and he lifted up his hands, and blessed them. And it came to pass, while he blessed them, he was parted from them, and carried up into heaven.”1 Acts sets the scene on the mountain and gives a distance: “a cloud received him out of their sight… Then returned they unto Jerusalem from the mount called Olivet, which is from Jerusalem a sabbath day's journey.”2 An Old Testament text that later writers cite, Zechariah's oracle, sets the Lord's “feet” “upon the mount of Olives, which is before Jerusalem on the east.”3 The Gospel's parting is placed “as far as to Bethany,” on the eastern slope; Acts measures the return to the city as “a sabbath day's journey.”

Eusebius: the cave “shewn there” and Helena's church on the summit (c. 314–339)

The earliest extant datable notice of a shown Ascension site on this timeline is Eusebius's, written before the Constantinian churches. In the Proof of the Gospel, expounding Zechariah 14:4, he reports that believers “congregate from all parts of the world… that they may worship at the Mount of Olives,” where the Lord “had borne up the Mount of Olives to the cave that is shewn there; there He prayed and delivered to His disciples… the mysteries of His end, and thence He made His Ascension into heaven.”4 In this passage a single cave holds the teaching, the prayer, and the Ascension together. Some two decades later his Life of Constantine records Helena raising “a sacred church and temple on the very summit of the mount… in memory of his ascent to heaven” — the church over the teaching-cave that later sources call the Eleona.5

The Bordeaux Pilgrim marks Constantine's basilica on Olivet (333)

The oldest surviving Christian travel diary, written the year of the journey, catches the Constantinian building in place: “you go up into the Mount of Olives, where the Lord taught his apostles before his passion; there a basilica was made on Constantine's command.”6 The pilgrim ties the basilica to the place where the Lord taught and does not mention the Ascension; the “little hill” he names nearby is the place where, he writes, the Lord went up to pray and Moses and Elijah appeared.

Egeria: the Imbomon, “the place whence the Lord ascended” (c. 384)

The pilgrim Egeria, in the 380s, names the place of the Ascension separately from the teaching-cave. She, describing the Palm Sunday afternoon procession, has the people “go up with hymns to the Imbomon, that is, to the place whence the Lord ascended into heaven,” and repeats the identical gloss at each visit — Holy Thursday, the Easter octave, and Pentecost, when “the passage from the Gospel… where it speaks of the Lord's Ascension, also that from the Acts of the Apostles… is read” there.7 She distinguishes the two Olivet sites — the church “on Eleona” below, the “Imbomon” above — and calls the Imbomon a place, naming no church there; the round church on the Imbomon is dated c. 384–390 (next entry). Her vigil for the fortieth day after Easter is kept at Bethlehem, and the Ascension readings at the Imbomon fall on Pentecost.

Poemenia's round church on the Imbomon (c. 384–390)

The first church on the Imbomon was raised, at or just after the time of Egeria's visit, by Poemenia, a wealthy Roman aristocrat of the imperial circle, who financed it “around Christ's last footprints.” It was built as a rotunda open to the sky — a ring of porticoes and arches with the sacred spot beneath the open crown.8 Poemenia's name is attested through the biographical tradition around Palladius, the Coptic tradition and the Life of Peter the Iberian rather than through a building inscription; the attribution to her is reconstructed from those sources.

Paulinus and Sulpicius Severus on the uncovered footprints (c. 403)

Two Latin writers, corresponding friends, report the footprints c. 403, a decade or more after the church's building. Paulinus of Nola writes that in the basilica of the Ascension is “the place from which [Jesus] was taken up to heaven in a cloud,” and that “this one place — and no other — was sanctified by the divine footprints to such an extent that it has never been covered with marble or any pavement.”9 His correspondent Sulpicius Severus tells it as a standing miracle: the spot “could not be joined by a pavement with the remaining part of the street. For the earth… rejected all the appliances laid upon it, and often threw back the blocks of marble in the faces of those who were seeking to place them… the footprints are still to be seen; and although the faith of those who daily flock to that place… carry away what had been trodden by the feet of the Lord, yet the sand of the place suffers no injury.”10 Both writers connect the uncovered ground with the footprints, and Sulpicius reports the sand as undiminished by the pilgrims who carry it away.

The Persians destroy the rotunda; it is rebuilt (614–c. 630)

Poemenia's church fell in the Persian sack of Jerusalem under Khosrow II in 614, which levelled much of the Christian building on Olivet, and was rebuilt in the following years (the restoration of the holy places is credited to Modestus, later patriarch of Jerusalem).11

Arculf: the roofless round church, the footprints, the eight lamps (c. 680)

The Frankish bishop Arculf, whose eyewitness report Adomnán of Iona wrote up as De Locis Sanctis, describes the rebuilt fabric in detail: “a great round church, having in its circuit three vaulted porticoes… The interior of the church, without roof or vault, lies open to heaven under the open air.” Inside, “the mark of the dust that was trodden by the Lord is so lasting that the impression of the footsteps may be perceived,” and though pilgrims daily “snatch away some of what was trodden by the Lord, yet the area perceives no loss” — as Sulpicius had reported of the sand c. 403. High in the walls “twice four windows” hold “as many lamps” whose light pours down the mountain toward Jerusalem.12 Bede abridged Adomnán's account in his own De Locis Sanctis.

The Crusader octagon and Saladin's waqf (12th century–1187)

The Crusaders replaced the earlier rotunda with the small octagonal edicule that survives — an octagonal outer wall enclosing an inner octagonal shrine over the footprint-rock, open to the sky like the rotundas before it.13 After Saladin took Jerusalem in 1187 the site passed into Muslim hands and became an Islamic endowment (waqf): a mihrab was set in the shrine, and the octagon was roofed with the stone dome and drum it still carries. The shrine was kept rather than razed.

The site today: the footprint rock, the shared feast, the summit rivals

The edicule stands under the authority of the Islamic waqf and is open to visitors of all faiths for a small fee; by long custom Christians of several communions are permitted to set up altars and celebrate the Ascension there each year. At its centre is the “Ascension Rock,” shown as bearing the imprint of Christ's right foot — the stone said to have held the left having been carried off to the al-Aqsa Mosque in the Middle Ages.14 The summit also carries the Russian Orthodox Convent of the Ascension, built in 1870 about 200 metres north-east of the chapel, with its tall “Russian Candle” bell-tower (⚠ unverified).
