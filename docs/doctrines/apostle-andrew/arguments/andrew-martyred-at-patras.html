Was Andrew crucified at Patras in Achaia?

Disputed reading · on Andrew · witness: The Acts of Andrew

Related readings: On what shape of cross was Andrew crucified? · Did Andrew found the church of Byzantium and ordain Stachys its first bishop?

The passage

Accordingly the proconsul Ægeates, having come into the city of Patras, began to compel those believing in Christ to worship the idols… And they having come up, lifted him on the cross…

— Acts and Martyrdom of the Holy Apostle Andrew, ANF vol. 8.1

The question

The New Testament records nothing of Andrew's death. The second-century Acts of Andrew places it at Patras in Achaia, under the proconsul Aegeates, while the earliest extant notice of his mission field — in a passage Eusebius ascribes to Origen — assigns him Scythia. The question is whether the Patras account preserves where and how Andrew died.

A crucifixion at Patras, held in a fixed tradition

In this reading Andrew ended his mission in Achaia and was crucified at Patras under the proconsul Aegeates, as the Acts of Andrew and the later tradition report.

Held by: the Acts of Andrew (second century); Joseph MacRory (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1907); Sean McDowell (The Fate of the Apostles, 2015).2

From the Acts of Andrew onward the accounts of Andrew's death name a single city (Patras), a single province (Achaia), a single magistrate (Aegeates) and a single manner (crucifixion, bound rather than nailed). On this reading the tradition carries the marks of a settled memory: a named place and official, a geography in which Achaia had an early Christian presence, and no rival city claiming his death or his grave. Jerome (395) places Andrew's ministry “in Achaia,” and the Achaian church kept his memory and his relics until their translation to Constantinople. MacRory gathers the mission notices — Scythia (Eusebius, following Origen), Epirus (Gregory of Nazianzus), Achaia (Jerome), Hellas (Theodoret) — and judges that “probably these various accounts are correct,” following Nicephorus's itinerary from Cappadocia and the Scythian deserts through Byzantium and Thrace to Achaia; he writes that “it is generally agreed that he was crucified by order of the Roman Governor, Aegeas or Aegeates, at Patrae in Achaia,” and that the relics “were translated from Patrae to Constantinople” about 357. Scythia and Achaia are, on this reading, successive stages of one mission. McDowell, surveying the evidence for each apostle's fate, grades Andrew's martyrdom “more plausible than not”: the tradition is early, consistent and without a rival account.3

An Achaian tradition later than the Black Sea mission

In this reading the oldest notice of Andrew's mission places him on the Black Sea, and his activity and death in Achaia rest on a later tradition that descends from the Acts of Andrew.

Held by: the editor's notes to Eusebius, Church History 3.1, in the English translation.4

The editor writes that “the traditions respecting Andrew are very uncertain and contradictory, though … the original form, represented here, assigned as his field the region in the neighborhood of the Black Sea,” and that Andrew “is also called the patron saint of Greece, where he is reported to have been crucified; but his activity there rests upon a late tradition.” The editor's notes say no more than this; the rest of the case below is the reading's own, with no named holder located. The earliest datable notice, the “Scythia to Andrew” that Eusebius ascribes to Origen, is on this reading the oldest layer, and it names no martyrdom. The narrative of the death at Patras is taken to come from a single stream, the apocryphal Acts of Andrew, a romance with a long speech from the cross, a dialogue on its mystery and a revolt of the crowd, and the proconsul Aegeates is named only in the Andrew legend (⚠ unverified). The Achaian martyrdom is, on this reading, a tradition attached to Andrew after the Black Sea notice, carried by the Acts and the texts derived from them.5
