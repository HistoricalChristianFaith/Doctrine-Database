On what shape of cross was Andrew crucified?

Disputed reading · on Andrew · witness: The Acts of Andrew

Related readings: Was Andrew crucified at Patras in Achaia? · Did Andrew found the church of Byzantium and ordain Stachys its first bishop?

The passage

And they having come up, lifted him on the cross; and having stretched his body across with ropes, they only bound his feet, but did not sever his joints, having received this order from the proconsul: for he wished him to be in distress while hanging…

— Acts and Martyrdom of the Holy Apostle Andrew, ANF vol. 8.1

The cross on which he suffered is commonly held to have been the decussate cross, now known as St. Andrew's, though the evidence for this view seems to be no older than the fourteenth century.

— Joseph MacRory, “St. Andrew,” Catholic Encyclopedia (1907), New Advent.2

The question

The Greek Acts and Martyrdom has Andrew “lifted on the cross” and bound by ropes, without describing the cross's form. Wikipedia's article on Andrew reports that the Acts of Andrew known to Gregory of Tours (sixth century) describe a Latin cross.3 The Golden Legend (c. 1260) has Andrew “bounden by the feet and hands unto the cross” without naming its shape. In art, according to Réau (1958), the X-shaped cross appears from the tenth century and becomes the standard image of his martyrdom only in the seventeenth; Judith Calvert (1984) found no sculpture of Andrew on the saltire earlier than an early-twelfth-century capital from Quercy; both are reported by Wikipedia.4 The diagonal cross (the crux decussata or saltire) is now his emblem in art, heraldry and the flag of Scotland. The question is which shape the tradition of his martyrdom remembers. Neither reading was located here with a modern scholarly proponent arguing it as history.

An upright cross of the kind on which Christ died

In this reading Andrew was bound, not nailed, to an ordinary upright cross, the cross of the Passion.

Held by: the Acts of Andrew as known to Gregory of Tours (sixth century), as reported by Wikipedia.5

Wikipedia's article reports that the Acts of Andrew, in the form known to Gregory of Tours, describe Andrew as bound “not nailed, to a Latin cross of the kind on which Jesus is said to have been crucified.” On this reading that is the earliest description of the cross's form. The Greek Acts and Martyrdom speaks of “the cross” and the ropes. On this reading the cross of the early narratives is the cross of the Passion, the one whose “mystery” Andrew expounds to the proconsul before he dies.6

The saltire

In this reading Andrew died on an X-shaped cross; one strand of the tradition adds that he asked for it, deeming himself unworthy to die on a cross like Christ's.

Held by: the developed tradition of the saltire (the humility motive as one strand of it); attesting the shape only: the Scottish legend of King Óengus's vision and the seal of the Guardians of Scotland (1286, ⚠ unverified).7

On this reading the saltire is a remembered form of the cross. One strand of the developed tradition holds that Andrew asked to be crucified on a differently shaped cross, out of the same humility the tradition attributes to his brother Peter, who asked to be crucified head-downward. The Greek Acts and Martyrdom, which describes the binding and the ropes but not the form of the cross, leaves its shape open, on this reading, for the later tradition to supply. MacRory (1907) reports that the cross “is commonly held to have been the decussate cross.” By 1286, it is reported, the seal of the Guardians of Scotland showed him on it (⚠ unverified). In the Scottish legend King Óengus, before battle, saw white clouds form an X in the sky, read them as “the crux decussata upon which Andrew was believed to have been crucified,” and after his victory made Andrew the patron of Scotland. The saltire has since stood for Andrew in the flags of Scotland, Tenerife and the Russian Navy, and in his iconography across the West.8
