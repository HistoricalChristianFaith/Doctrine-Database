Did Andrew found the church of Byzantium and ordain Stachys its first bishop?

Disputed reading · on Andrew · witness: the Greek apostle-lists of Pseudo-Epiphanius and Pseudo-Dorotheus of Tyre

Related readings: Was Andrew crucified at Patras in Achaia? · On what shape of cross was Andrew crucified?

The passage

Stachys, whom the Apostle also mentions in the epistle to the Romans; whom also Andrew the apostle, traversing the sea of Pontus, in Argyropolis of Thrace appointed bishop of Byzantium

— List of the Apostles and Disciples ascribed to Dorotheus of Tyre, NASSCAL.1

The question

The Greek apostle-lists ascribed to Pseudo-Epiphanius and Pseudo-Dorotheus of Tyre, and the related Story of Andrew, make Andrew the founder of the church of Byzantium — the city Constantine refounded as Constantinople — and the ordainer of Stachys, the man Paul greets in Romans 16:9, as its first bishop. Philostorgius records the translation of Andrew's relics to Constantinople in 357, and Gregory of Tours' epitome of the Acts carries him through Byzantium on his journey to Patras; neither tells of a foundation. The question is whether the foundation account preserves the apostolic origin of the see or took shape with the lists themselves.

An apostolic foundation by Andrew, with Stachys as first bishop

In this reading Andrew, travelling from his Black Sea mission toward Achaia, planted the church of Byzantium and ordained Stachys its first bishop.

Held by: the lists of Pseudo-Epiphanius and Pseudo-Dorotheus of Tyre (eighth–ninth centuries); Nicephorus Callistus (Ecclesiastical History II.39); Joseph MacRory (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1907).2

The oldest “division of the world” places Andrew on the Black Sea (Origen's Scythia), and Byzantium stands at that sea's threshold: an apostle bound for Scythia, or returning to Achaia, would pass the Bosphorus. The older Acts of Andrew, in Gregory of Tours' Latin epitome (c. 593), already carries him “from Pontus by way of Amasia, Sinope, Nicaea, Nicomedia, Byzantium, Thrace” and on to Patras. The city had an early Andrean cult: his relics were deposited in the church of the Apostles there about 357, according to MacRory, and Dvornik notes a church built in his honour by Arcadia, daughter of the emperor Arcadius. The lists name as first bishop a man Paul greets, “Stachys my beloved,” and give the place of his ordination, Argyropolis. MacRory gathers the notices of Andrew's fields and concludes that “probably these various accounts are correct, for Nicephorus …, relying upon early writers, states that Andrew preached in Cappadocia, Galatia, and Bithynia, then in the land of the anthropophagi and the Scythian deserts, afterwards in Byzantium itself, where he appointed St. Stachys as its first bishop.” On this reading Nicephorus and the lists drew on earlier writers and preserve the see's own memory of its apostle, the brother of Peter and the first-called.3

A foundation account formed at the end of the eighth century

In this reading the older tradition knew of Andrew passing through Byzantium, and the account of his founding the see and ordaining Stachys took shape with the apostle-lists of the late eighth century.

Held by: Francis Dvornik (The Idea of Apostolicity in Byzantium and the Legend of the Apostle Andrew, 1958).4

Dvornik dates the composition of the Pseudo-Dorotheus lists “at the earliest, around the very end of the eighth century,” and notes that the monk Epiphanius, writing his Life of Andrew after 815, did not know them. The Chronicon Paschale, written in the first half of the seventh century under Heraclius, lists Metrophanes as the first bishop of Constantinople; it “mentions Stachys, but says nothing of Andrew’s supposed connection with the foundation of the bishopric of Byzantium,” while it speaks of Rome, Antioch and Alexandria as apostolic foundations. Gregory of Tours' epitome, on this reading, attests the older form: a journey through the city, with no see founded. Dvornik traces another source of Constantinople's apostolic standing, its position as “heir of Ephesus and of the Apostle John”; he finds that Photius “nowhere claimed an apostolic character for his see” in the dispute with Pope Nicholas I, and that the anti-Roman opuscule built on the Andrew tradition is “falsely attributed to Photius.” He places the “Spread of the Andrew and Stachys Legend in the tenth century,” and reads the lists themselves as Byzantine imports from Syria, made to revive interest in “the fate of Christ’s disciples.” On this reading the foundation account belongs to the eighth- and ninth-century literature of the apostles, and Stachys entered the see's list from Paul's greeting in Romans.5
