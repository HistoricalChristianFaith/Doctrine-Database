The Acts of Andrew

Dates: c. 150–210 (the apocryphal romance; the surviving Greek Acts and Martyrdom is a later reworking) · Wikipedia
Summary: The apocryphal romance that is the earliest extant account of Andrew's end: his mission in Achaia, his clash with the proconsul Aegeates at Patras, and his death bound — not nailed — to a cross so as to prolong his suffering.

Claims & evidence

Achaia, Patras, and the proconsul Aegeates

Accordingly the proconsul Ægeates, having come into the city of Patras, began to compel those believing in Christ to worship the idols…

— Acts and Martyrdom of the Holy Apostle Andrew (ANF vol. 8). The scene is set in Achaia, in the Peloponnesian city of Patras; the whole central section is a long dialogue between Andrew and Aegeates on the “mystery of the cross,” ending in the sentence of crucifixion.

Bound, not nailed, to prolong the death

And they having come up, lifted him on the cross; and having stretched his body across with ropes, they only bound his feet, but did not sever his joints, having received this order from the proconsul: for he wished him to be in distress while hanging…

— ibid. Andrew is tied to the cross rather than nailed, so that he will die slowly; the text then has him hang and preach for days before he dies. The death-narrative does not describe the cross's shape; the apostle is “lifted on the cross.” The X-shaped saltire later associated with him is discussed at On what shape of cross was Andrew crucified?

Reception and debate

  • Among the apocryphal Acts. Wikipedia's article reports that the Acts of Andrew was traditionally credited to “the ‘Leucius Charinus’ who is credited with all the 2nd-century romances” (the Acts of Peter, John, and Thomas among them, ⚠ unverified), and that “it is now considered encratite.” It attests the veneration of Andrew in the second century and is the earliest extant account of the Patras–crucifixion; whether later accounts derive from it is set out at Was Andrew crucified at Patras in Achaia? On the apocryphal Acts as a class, see the apostles hub.
  • The surviving texts. The original Acts of Andrew survives only in fragments and epitomes (among them Gregory of Tours's sixth-century Latin Book of the Miracles of the blessed Andrew); the connected Greek Acts and Martyrdom quoted here, and the related Epistle of the presbyters and deacons of Achaia, are later reworkings of the martyrdom.
  • The Patras account as history. Joseph MacRory writes that “it is generally agreed that he was crucified by order of the Roman Governor, Aegeas or Aegeates, at Patrae in Achaia” (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1907); Sean McDowell (The Fate of the Apostles, 2015) grades the martyrdom “more plausible than not”; the editor of the English translation of Eusebius writes that Andrew's activity in Greece “rests upon a late tradition” (Church History 3.1, n. 4). The readings are set out at Was Andrew crucified at Patras in Achaia?
