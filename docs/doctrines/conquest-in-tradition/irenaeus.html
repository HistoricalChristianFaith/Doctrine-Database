Irenaeus on the Conquest of Canaan

Dates: c. 180 (Against Heresies, Book IV; the Demonstration of the Apostolic Preaching is a later work; Irenaeus of Lyons, c. 130–c. 202) · Wikipedia
Summary: Irenaeus tells the conquest as a step in the history of salvation. In the Demonstration, his short summary of the faith, Joshua "divided the Jordan," "overthrew and destroyed the seven races," and settled the people in the land. In the same work the peoples of Canaan are the posterity of Ham, "cut off by God, being delivered up to judgment." He tells Jericho's fall "at the sounding of the seven trumpets" as a real event and reads Rahab's spies as a figure of the Trinity. His main opponents are Marcion and the Gnostics, who set the punishing God of the Old Testament against the Father of Jesus. Irenaeus does not single out the Canaanite wars in reply. He answers the whole class of objection at once: one God judges in both Testaments, "in the one case indeed typically, temporarily, and more moderately; but in the other, really, enduringly, and more rigidly." He passes on a presbyter's rule that deeds Scripture records without censure are not to be accused but searched for a type. The contrast between Moses, who led the people out, and Joshua, who led them in, runs through his typology.

Claims & evidence

Joshua "divided the Jordan" and "destroyed the seven races"

And, when Moses had finished his course, it was said to him by God: Get thee up into the mountain, and die: for thou shalt not bring in my people into the land. So he died according to the word of the Lord; and Jesus the son of Nun succeeded him. He divided the Jordan and made the people to pass over into the land; and, when he had overthrown and destroyed the seven races that dwelt therein, he assigned to the people the temporal Jerusalem, wherein David was king, and Solomon his son, who builded the temple to the name of God, according to the likeness of the tabernacle which had been made by Moses after the pattern of the heavenly and spiritual things.

— Demonstration of the Apostolic Preaching 29. The Demonstration is a catechetical summary, and this chapter is its whole account of Joshua: the crossing of the Jordan, the destruction of the seven nations, and the settlement, set in a plain sequence that runs on to David and Solomon. No figure is drawn and nothing is qualified. The conquest is one link in a chain of events between the death of Moses and the building of the temple. The two chapters before it tell the spies and the forty years in the same manner. Moses "changed the name of Oshea the son of Nun … and named him Jesus: and so he sent them forth with the power of the Name"; ten spies reported that "the cities were exceeding great and walled, and the sons of the giants dwelt therein"; and of the adults only "Jesus the son of Nun and Caleb the son of Jephunneh" entered the land (27). In chapter 28 Moses assembles the people "over against Jericho" to give Deuteronomy.1

The Canaanites "delivered up to judgment" as the posterity of Ham

Now the curse of Ham, wherewith his father Noah cursed him, is this: Cursed be Ham the child; a servant shall he be unto his brethren. This having come upon his race, he begat many descendants upon the earth, (even) for fourteen generations, growing up in a wild condition; and then his race was cut off by God, being delivered up to judgment. For the Canaanites and Hittites and Peresites and Hivites and Amorites and Jebusites and Gergasites and Sodomites, the Arabians also and the dwellers in Phoenicia, all the Egyptians and the Libyans, are of the posterity of Ham, who have fallen under the curse; for the curse is of long duration over the ungodly.

— Demonstration of the Apostolic Preaching 20. This is Irenaeus's account of why the nations of Canaan were destroyed. The list begins with the seven peoples of Deuteronomy 7:1 and adds Sodom, Egypt, and others. They are Ham's descendants, they "in sins … increased and multiplied," and after fourteen generations God "cut off" the race "being delivered up to judgment." The conquest is the curse coming due on "the ungodly." Irenaeus read a Septuagint text in which the curse falls on Ham himself, not on Canaan, as the translator J. Armitage Robinson notes. The peoples of Canaan still head his list. He does not argue the point. He states it as the reason behind events.2

Jericho fell "at the sounding of the seven trumpets," and Rahab was saved

Thus also did Rahab the harlot, while condemning herself, inasmuch as she was a Gentile, guilty of all sins, nevertheless receive the three spies, who were spying out all the land, and hid them at her home; [which three were] doubtless [a type of] the Father and the Son, together with the Holy Spirit. And when the entire city in which she lived fell to ruins at the sounding of the seven trumpets, Rahab the harlot was preserved, when all was over [in ultimis], together with all her house, through faith of the scarlet sign.

— Against Heresies IV.20.12. Irenaeus gives the destruction of Jericho in a single clause: "the entire city … fell to ruins at the sounding of the seven trumpets." He tells it as a fact and draws no figure from the fall itself. The figures attach to Rahab's side of the story. The spies (two in Joshua 2:1, three in his telling) are a type of the Trinity. The harlot who receives them stands for the Gentile Church. The scarlet sign "meant the passover." The siege is the setting in which the one Gentile household is saved, as it had been for Clement.3

One God judges in both Testaments: the answer to the Marcionites

Inasmuch, then, as in both Testaments there is the same righteousness of God [displayed] when God takes vengeance, in the one case indeed typically, temporarily, and more moderately; but in the other, really, enduringly, and more rigidly: for the fire is eternal, and the wrath of God which shall be revealed from heaven from the face of our Lord … entails a heavier punishment on those who incur it,-the ciders pointed out that those men are devoid of sense, who, [arguing] from what happened to those who formerly did not obey God, do endeavour to bring in another Father, setting over against [these punishments] what great things the Lord had done at His coming to save those who received Him, taking compassion upon them; while they keep silence with regard to His judgment.

— Against Heresies IV.28.1 ("ciders" is a misprint for "elders" in the ANF text). Irenaeus is answering the Marcionite argument that the punishments of the Old Testament prove a second, lower God. His reply turns the argument around. The New Testament's judgment is not milder but harsher, since its fire is eternal. The earlier punishments were "typically, temporarily, and more moderately" inflicted. The word "typically" does not mean that they did not happen. It means that the temporal judgment foreshadowed the final one. The examples he names are the Egyptians, the wilderness generation, and Sodom; he does not name the Canaanites. He says those who received the inheritance were those who believed, "as did Caleb [the son] of Jephunneh and Joshua [the son] of Nun, and innocent children" (IV.28.3). In IV.29–30 he takes up two particular Marcionite charges, the hardening of Pharaoh and the spoiling of the Egyptians. He defends the second as just wages, since "the Egyptians were debtors to the [Jewish] people, not alone as to property, but as their very lives." These are the nearest he comes to the moral case against the conquest.4

The presbyter's rule: deeds Scripture does not censure are searched for a type

When recounting certain matters of this kind respecting them of old time, the presbyter [before mentioned] was in the habit of instructing us, and saying: "With respect to those misdeeds for which the Scriptures themselves blame the patriarchs and prophets, we ought not to inveigh against them, nor become like Ham, who ridiculed the shame of his father, and so fell under a curse; but we should [rather] give thanks to God in their behalf, inasmuch as their sins have been forgiven them through the advent of our Lord; … With respect to those actions, again, on which the Scriptures pass no censure, but which are simply set down [as having occurred], we ought not to become the accusers [of those who committed them], for we are not more exact than God, nor can we be superior to our Master; but we should search for a type [in them]. For not one of those things which have been set down in Scripture without being condemned is without significance."

— Against Heresies IV.31.1. Irenaeus attributes the rule to a presbyter "who had heard it from those who had seen the apostles" (IV.27.1). It has two parts. The sins that Scripture itself condemns are forgiven and not to be mocked. The deeds it records without blame are "simply set down [as having occurred]" and are to be read for a type, not put on trial. His own example is Lot and his daughters. The rule is general, and Irenaeus does not apply it to Joshua's wars. It is the principle later readers bring to the ban. It treats the deed as a deed and looks for its meaning; it does not deny it or explain it away.5

Moses led the people out, "Jesus" leads them into the inheritance

Then He set forth in types beforehand that which was to be; now in very truth He has brought us out from the cruel service of the Gentiles, and a stream of water in the desert has He made to flow forth in abundance from a rock; and that rock is Himself; and has given twelve fountains, that is, the teaching of the twelve apostles. And the obstinate unbelievers He brought to an end and consumed in the wilderness; but those who believed on Him, and in malice were children, He made to enter into the inheritance of the fathers; whom not Moses, but Jesus puts in possession of the heritage: who also delivers us from Amalek by the expansion of His hands, and brings us to the kingdom of the Father.

— Demonstration of the Apostolic Preaching 46. The pairing is explicit: "then" in types, "now in very truth." The Word did the first deliverance and the entry into the land, and the same Word does the second. "Not Moses, but Jesus" plays on the shared name, so that Joshua's leading the people in and Christ's are one sentence. Against Heresies IV.30.4 makes the same contrast: the Church is led "into His own inheritance, which Moses the servant of God did not [bestow], but which Jesus the Son of God shall give for an inheritance." A short fragment on Numbers 27:18, printed among his fragments, puts it most simply: "it was proper that Moses should lead the people out of Egypt, but that Jesus (Joshua) should lead them into the inheritance," and that "Moses should give manna as food to the fathers, but Joshua wheat." The type is the event read forward. It needs a real Joshua and a real entry.6

Notes

  • History told plainly; the answer to Marcion made general. Irenaeus never questions that the conquest happened as told. The Demonstration recites it as history, and Against Heresies mentions its episodes in passing as facts: Jericho's walls, the "five kings of the Amorites, whom Joshua the son of Nun shut up in a cave" (II.24.4). His reply to the Marcionite charge of a cruel Creator answers all the Old Testament judgments together. One God judges then "temporarily" and later "enduringly." He does not argue the ban case by case. For the objection and the later answers of Origen and Augustine, see the moral problem of the ḥerem.7
  • Rahab. The three-spies passage is treated on its own on Irenaeus on Rahab; see also the scarlet-cord typology and the Rahab timeline.
  • Ham and Canaan. For the wider tradition of the curse that Irenaeus uses to explain the Canaanites' fate, see the curse of Ham timeline. Justin had already tied the conquest to Noah's curse.
  • The material record. What the ground shows of the campaigns Irenaeus recites is on the conquest in the material record; for Jericho, see the Jericho dating argument.
