The New Testament on the Conquest of Canaan

Dates: c. AD 50–90 (apostolic era) · verse pages: Acts 7:45 · Acts 13:19 · Heb 11:30–31 · Heb 4:8 · Jas 2:25 · Matt 1:5
Summary: The New Testament never narrates the conquest, but it mentions it in several places. It appears as a past event in Israel's history, recited in sermons (Stephen in Acts 7, Paul at Pisidian Antioch in Acts 13) as one link in the chain from Egypt to David. Two of its figures carry most of the weight. Jericho's fall and Rahab's rescue are exempla of faith (Hebrews 11) and of works (James 2), and Rahab herself stands in the Messiah's genealogy (Matthew 1). And in Hebrews 4:8 the Greek name the two men share (Iēsous: Joshua, Jesus) opens the Joshua–Jesus name-typology found in later Christian writers. Joshua "gave them" a rest in the land, but not the final rest.

Claims & evidence

The conquest recited as sacred history (Acts 7:45; 13:19)

Both of the long historical speeches in Acts run through the conquest without a pause, as one more fixed point in the story of God's dealings with Israel. Stephen, tracing the tabernacle from the wilderness to David, says the fathers brought it into the land "with Jesus", that is, with Joshua (Greek Iēsous), when God drove the nations out:

Which also our fathers that came after brought in with Jesus into the possession of the Gentiles, whom God drave out before the face of our fathers, unto the days of David; (Acts 7:45, KJV)

The agent is God ("whom God drave out"), and the occasion is a narrative of the tabernacle's journey.1

Paul's synagogue sermon at Pisidian Antioch gives the same sequence in compressed form (exodus, forty years, conquest, allotment, judges, Samuel, Saul, David) and names the conquest in the language of Deuteronomy 7:1's "seven nations":

And when he had destroyed seven nations in the land of Chanaan, he divided their land to them by lot. And after that he gave unto them judges about the space of four hundred and fifty years, until Samuel the prophet. (Acts 13:19–20, KJV)

Once more God is the subject of the verb: "he had destroyed seven nations." The destruction is reported as an act of God. Paul's sermon is also the New Testament's one piece of conquest chronology. In the Greek text of the oldest manuscripts (as rendered in the ESV, note 2), the "about four hundred and fifty years" belongs to verses 17–19 (sojourn, wilderness, and conquest) and not to the judges, as it does in the KJV.2

Jericho and Rahab in the roll-call of faith (Hebrews 11:30–31)

Hebrews 11 lists the heroes of faith in historical order (Abel, Enoch, Noah, Abraham, Moses), and the conquest comes next. Joshua is not named; the faith belongs to the people and to one Canaanite woman:

By faith the walls of Jericho fell down, after they were compassed about seven days. By faith the harlot Rahab perished not with them that believed not, when she had received the spies with peace. (Hebrews 11:30–31, KJV)

The phrase "them that believed not" describes the citizens of Jericho who died as unbelievers, and the one Canaanite who believed is spared. Verse 32 then moves on to Judges: "Gedeon, and… Barak, and… Samson, and… Jephthae."3

"If Jesus had given them rest": Joshua as a type of Christ (Hebrews 4:8)

The New Testament's only explicit typology of Joshua turns on his name. Commenting on Psalm 95's "they shall not enter into my rest", Hebrews argues that the rest Joshua won in Canaan could not have been the final one, since David, long after Joshua, still speaks of "another day":

For if Jesus had given them rest, then would he not afterward have spoken of another day. There remaineth therefore a rest to the people of God. (Hebrews 4:8–9, KJV)

The KJV's "Jesus" here is Joshua. The Greek has Iēsous, the Septuagint's form of the Hebrew Yehoshuaʿ, and modern translations render it "Joshua." A few verses later the same name returns for "Jesus the Son of God" who has "passed into the heavens" (4:14). The contrast is between a Joshua who brought the people into an earthly rest and a Jesus who brings them into a heavenly one. Jean Daniélou calls this the New Testament's single "hint" of a Joshua typology and notes that the shared name is "a main reason why the Fathers see in Joshua a type of Jesus."4

Rahab justified by works (James 2:25)

James draws on the same Jericho episode as Hebrews for the opposite half of his argument: faith that acts.

Likewise also was not Rahab the harlot justified by works, when she had received the messengers, and had sent them out another way? (James 2:25, KJV)

Rahab is paired with Abraham (2:21–23): she received the messengers and sent them out. James keeps the label "harlot," as Hebrews does; Josephus's retelling calls her an innkeeper.5

Rahab in the Messiah's genealogy (Matthew 1:5)

Matthew's genealogy names four women before Mary, and one of them is the Canaanite of Jericho:

And Salmon begat Booz of Rachab; and Booz begat Obed of Ruth; and Obed begat Jesse; (Matthew 1:5, KJV)

Nothing in the Old Testament makes Rahab the wife of Salmon. The identification of Matthew's "Rachab" with the Rahab of Joshua 2 is the one followed in the later tradition collected on the Rahab timeline; on that reading the rescued Canaanite family enters the line of David and of Christ. Joshua 6:25 says "she dwelleth in Israel even unto this day."6

Uses of the conquest across the New Testament texts

  • Four uses. The New Testament texts use the conquest as a step in the sacred chronology (Acts), as an exemplum (Hebrews 11, James 2), as a genealogical link (Matthew 1), and as the earthly rest that Hebrews 4 contrasts with another rest. Origen's homilies on Joshua, on his page, read the wars as spiritual warfare.
  • The ḥerem. The New Testament texts give the destruction of the Canaanites to God ("whom God drave out", "he had destroyed seven nations") and, in Hebrews 11:31, describe Jericho's dead as "them that believed not." The moral question about the ban does not appear in these texts; the Marcionite objection to the Old Testament conquest, dated to the 140s, is reported on Tertullian's page.
  • Rahab. The New Testament's three Rahab texts (Hebrews 11:31, James 2:25, Matthew 1:5) are collected with later treatments on the Rahab timeline.
  • Placement on the timeline. The New Testament layer precedes Josephus (c. AD 93–94), whose Antiquities gives the earliest extant full post-biblical retelling of Joshua, and Clement of Rome (c. 96), the earliest extant Christian writer outside the canon to take up Rahab. « Back to the Conquest in Christian Tradition timeline
