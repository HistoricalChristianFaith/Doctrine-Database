John Chrysostom on the Conquest of Canaan

Dates: c. 395 (homilies preached at Antioch, 386–397, and at Constantinople, 398–404; the Homilies on Hebrews are from his last years in the capital; Chrysostom b. c. 349, d. 407) · Wikipedia
Summary: John Chrysostom, the great preacher of Antioch and later bishop of Constantinople, wrote no commentary on Joshua, but its episodes run all through his homilies. He uses them as the Antiochene school did, as plain history with a moral or a type drawn from them. Jericho's walls fell "not by natural sequence, nor yet by any law of nature," but by faith. The sun stood still at Joshua's word, a greater miracle than the parting of the sea. In a parenthesis he turns the long day against "they who assert that the heaven is a sphere." He gives a moral reason for the land left unconquered: "had they chosen to obey God, they might have taken all the cities, without either arms or array." Achan, "stoned with his whole family," shows that God's punishments "are not threats and words." Noah's curse on Canaan "came to pass in the Gibeonites." Unlike Ambrose, who praised Joshua for keeping the Gibeonite oath, Chrysostom cites it as a warning, one of the oaths that are "a snare of Satan." He never raises the moral problem of the ban.

Claims & evidence

Jericho and the sun: miracles "contrary to expectation"

"By faith, the walls of Jericho fell down, after they had been compassed about for seven days." For assuredly the sound of trumpets is not able to throw down stones, though one blow for ten thousand years; but Faith can do all things. Seest thou that in all cases it is not by natural sequence, nor yet by any law of nature that it was changed, but all is done contrary to expectation?

Consider of how great value is the righteous man. Joshua the son of Nun said, "Let the sun stand still at Gibeon, the moon at the valley of Elom" (Josh. x. 12), and it was so. Let then the whole world come, or rather two or three, or four, or ten, or twenty worlds, and let them say and do this; yet shall they not be able. But the friend of God commanded the creatures of his Friend, or rather he besought his Friend, and the servants yielded, and he below gave command to those above. Seest thou that these things are for service fulfilling their appointed course? This was greater than the [miracles] of Moses. Why (I ask)? Because it is not a like thing to command the sea and the heavenly [bodies]. … The name of Joshua [Jesus], was a type. For this reason then, and because of the very name, the creation reverenced him. What then! Was no other person called Jesus? [Yes]; but this man was on this account so called in type; for he used to be called Hoshea. Therefore the name was changed: for it was a prediction and a prophecy. He brought in the people into the promised land, as Jesus [does] into heaven; not the Law; since neither did Moses [bring them in], but remained without. The Law has not power to bring in, but grace.

— Homilies on Hebrews 27.3, 27.6, trans. Frederic Gardiner.1 Preaching on Hebrews 11, Chrysostom insists that the fall of Jericho had no natural cause: trumpets cannot bring down stones, "though one blow for ten thousand years." The miracle of the sun he ranks above the parting of the Red Sea, because it is greater to command the heavens than the sea. The figure comes only after the event: the creation obeyed Joshua because he bore the name of Jesus, and his leading the people into the land, where Moses could not, is the type of grace bringing believers into heaven. The renaming of Hoshea, he says, "was a prediction and a prophecy." It is the same reading as Justin's and Origen's, and in Chrysostom it rests on a miracle he takes to have happened.

The long day and the shape of the heavens

Perhaps each of you might wish to be such as to be able to command the sun and moon. (At this point what would they say who assert that the heaven is a sphere? For why did he not [merely] say, "Let the sun stand still," but added "Let the sun stand still at the valley of Elom," that is, he will make the day longer? This was done also in the time of Hezekiah. The sun went back. This again is more wonderful than the other, to go the contrary way, not having yet gone round his course.)

— Homilies on Hebrews 27.7, trans. Gardiner.2 The aside is brief and its argument is not spelled out. Chrysostom, like other Antiochenes, rejected the Greek picture of a spherical heaven turning round the earth. Earlier in the same series, on the tabernacle "which the Lord pitched," he asks, "Where are they who say that the heaven whirls around? where are they who declare that it is spherical?" (Homilies on Hebrews 14). Here he seems to take the place-names in Joshua's command (Gibeon, the valley of Aijalon) as fixing the sun over a particular spot, which he thinks a sphere cannot explain. What is clear is that he reads the long day as a literal lengthening of the day, and the sun's going back for Hezekiah as a still greater wonder. Elsewhere he uses the event against sun-worship: God "hath also bidden His servants, that were but men, command them," so that the sun and moon are seen to be "fellow-servants," and so "Joshua, the son of Nave, said, 'Let the sun stand still in Gibeon'" (Homilies on the Statues 10). On Matthew he places the sun's standing still and its going back among the rare miracles of Israel's later history, "few and at intervals," like those the Church saw in his own day under Julian (Homilies on Matthew 4).

Why the land was not all taken

For they indeed toiled even after they had entered into the land of promise;—rather, they toiled not, for had they chosen to obey God, they might have taken all the cities, without either arms or array. Jericho, we know, they overturned, more after the fashion of dancers than of warriors. We however have no warfare after we have entered into the land of promise, that is, into Heaven, but only so long as we are in the wilderness, that is, in the present life.

— Homilies on Ephesians 23, trans. Gross Alexander.3 Chrysostom contrasts Israel's long wars in Canaan with the Christian's rest in heaven. In doing so he answers, in a sentence, the question of the land left unconquered (Joshua 13:1; Judges 1). The wars dragged on because Israel did not obey. Had it obeyed, every city would have fallen as Jericho did, to marching and trumpets. This is the conditional reading of the promise that Theodoret would give at length: the land was promised on terms, and Israel broke them. A homily on Acts makes the same point from the other side: "Joshua the son of Nun went out to war, and alone achieved all; the rest were of no use" (Homilies on Acts 8). Victory came from one righteous man, not from numbers.

Achan: "stoned with his whole family"

For if you will not believe by the punishments that are to come, at least believe by those that have happened already. For what have happened, and have come forth into reality, surely are not threats and words. Who then was it that flooded the whole world, and affected that baleful wreck, and the utter destruction of our whole race! … Shall I mention also those that were punished individually! Cain, who was given up to a continual vengeance? (the son of) Charmi, who was stoned with his whole family?

— Homilies on Romans 25, trans. J. Walker, J. Sheppard and H. Browne, revised by George B. Stevens.

One single man, a common soldier, stole the sacred property, and all were smitten. Ye know, doubtless, the history I mean? I am speaking of Acham the son of Carmi, the man who stole the consecrated spoil.

— Homilies on Ephesians 6, trans. Alexander.4 Achan ("the son of Charmi") appears in Chrysostom as proof that God's punishments are real. In the homily on Romans he is answering hearers who say hell is "but mere threats," and he lists judgments that "have come forth into reality": the Flood, Sodom, Egypt, Korah, and Achan "stoned with his whole family." In the homily on Ephesians the point is that one man's theft brought defeat on the whole army at Ai. Chrysostom does not stop over the children stoned with their father, which Augustine and Jerome would treat as a hard case. For him the story's force depends on its having happened.

The Gibeonites: Noah's curse fulfilled, and an oath to regret

This is a law of prophecy, that in many cases much that is spoken of one set of persons is fulfilled in another; of which kind is that which is said touching Simeon and Levi, "I will divide them," saith He, "in Jacob, and scatter them in Israel" And yet not in themselves did this come to pass, but in their descendants; and Noah's saying again about Canaan, came to pass in the Gibeonites, Canaan's descendants.

— Homilies on Matthew 8.5, trans. George Prevost, revised by M. B. Riddle.

Think what the tribes had to suffer for their oath in the matter of the tribe of Benjamin (Judges xxi. 5-10): what Saul had to suffer for his oath (1 Sam. xiv. 24, etc.). For Saul indeed perjured himself, but Herod did what was even worse than perjury, he committed murder. Joshua again—you know how it fared with him, for his oath in the matter of the Gibeonites. (Joshua, ch. ix.) For it is indeed a snare of Satan, this swearing.

— Homilies on Acts 13, trans. J. Walker, J. Sheppard and H. Browne, revised by George B. Stevens.5 The Gibeonites appear twice. On Matthew 2:15 ("Out of Egypt have I called my Son") Chrysostom needs a prophecy fulfilled in a person's descendants, and he gives Noah's curse on Canaan, "let Canaan be his servant," fulfilled when the Gibeonites, "Canaan's descendants," became woodcutters and water-carriers for Israel (Joshua 9:21–27). Ephrem had found the same curse fulfilled in the conquest as a whole. In the homily on Acts the oath Joshua swore to the Gibeonites, without asking counsel of the Lord (Joshua 9:14), is one of a series of binding oaths that led their swearers into trouble. Chrysostom preached often against swearing. Ambrose had used the same oath to praise Joshua for keeping faith even with deceivers. Chrysostom does not say whether Joshua should have kept the oath once it was sworn. His point is that swearing it brought him trouble.

The Sabbath at Jericho, and Rahab

Twice Chrysostom notes that Jericho fell on a Sabbath. On Matthew 12 he lists the cases in which the Sabbath "hath been violated, and that often," "both by circumcision, and by many other works; and at Jericho too one may see the same to have happened" (Homilies on Matthew 39). On John 5 he asks why Christ, defending a healing on the Sabbath, "did not mention what took place at Jericho" (Homilies on John 38). Rahab he treats at length elsewhere. She is the believer who "heard what the men related, and forthwith believed" (Homilies on Hebrews 27.3), and in the homilies on repentance the figure of the Church, whose lie to the king's men he calls "this good lie." Notes

  • An Antiochene reading. Chrysostom's use of Joshua is that of the Antiochene school: the events are history first, the type and the moral are drawn from them, and he does not allegorize the wars. Compare his fellow Antiochene Theodoret, who answered questions on Joshua half a century later, and the Latin Ambrose, his older contemporary.
  • The moral question. Chrysostom never raises Marcion's charge against the ban. He uses the conquest's punishments (Achan's household, the cities that would not obey) as proofs of God's justice, and he does not address the killing of the Canaanites. The patristic answers are weighed at Do the patristic defenses answer the moral problem of the ḥerem?
