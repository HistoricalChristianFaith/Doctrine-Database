Eusebius of Caesarea on the Conquest of Canaan

Dates: c. 325 (the Onomasticon, finished before c. 330; the Chronicle, first issued c. 311 and later revised; the Proof of the Gospel, c. 314–320; the Church History, c. 311–325; the Theophany, c. 337; Eusebius b. c. 260, bishop of Caesarea from c. 313, d. 339) · Wikipedia
Summary: Eusebius, bishop of Caesarea in Palestine and heir to Origen's library there, handles the conquest in three registers. As a geographer he treats Joshua's campaign as something that happened in the country he lived in. His Onomasticon, a gazetteer of biblical place-names, records which city "Josue razed," which king he killed, and where the site is "pointed out" in his own day: Jericho and the remains of its earlier cities, the twelve stones at Gilgal, the ruins of Ai, the cave at Makkedah, Joshua's own tomb. As a chronographer he fixes Joshua in world history. He gives him twenty-seven years, sets his rule beside the kings of Argos and Athens, and argues over how many years run from the Exodus to Solomon. As a theologian he reads the shared name Jesus (Iēsous) as a providential sign of Christ. He sees the commander who met Joshua at Jericho as the divine Word, coming to fight beside Israel. He explains the destruction of the Amorites as God's sentence, delayed until "their sins were fulfilled." The typology sits on top of the history. Nowhere does he set the history aside.

Claims & evidence

Joshua's sites are real places in fourth-century Palestine

'Ierichō. 530 City which Josue razed (after crossing the Jordan) and captured (killed) its king. Afterwards Ozan from Bethel, from the tribe of Ephraim restored it. Here our Lord Jesus the Christ (our Lord and Savior) came and revealed himself as worthy of honor. It was also destroyed at the siege of Jerusalem (by the Romans) because of the faithlessness (perfidy) of the inhabitants. It was rebuilt a third time and the city is now pointed out (which city remains today). Of the two former cities there is even now some evidence remaining. (Remains of both older cities are pointed out up to the present day.)

— Onomasticon (Concerning the Place Names in Sacred Scripture), §530, trans. C. Umhau Wolf.1 The Onomasticon lists the places named in Scripture in Greek alphabetical order, book by book, and says where each one lies "now." The Jericho entry gives the site a continuous history. Joshua destroyed it. A man of Bethel rebuilt it (1 Kings 16:34). Christ visited it. The Romans destroyed it again. It now stands as a third city, and the remains of the first two are still shown. Wolf's English combines the Greek with Jerome's Latin version of about 390. Words in parentheses are Jerome's variants, so the last sentence is Jerome's restatement of the Greek sentence before it. The Greek is Eusebius's own: Joshua's Jericho is one of the "two former cities" of which "there is even now some evidence remaining."

The same treatment runs through the Joshua sections of the book. Gilgal is where the people camped and the Jordan stones were set up, and the place is still revered:

Galgala. 311 This is the above mentioned Golgol, "to the east" of old Jericho going toward the Jordan. There Josue circled with the people and made "the Passover" using wheat for the first time after the cessation of the manna. (In this place) He set up stones from (carried from the bed of) "the Jordan." The tent of witness remained there. Given by lot to the tribe of Juda. The deserted place is pointed out [two miles from Jericho which the people] today worship as holy (some men of that region hold in holy awe).

Under the "hill of foreskins" (Joshua 5:3) he adds that the stones themselves can be seen: "At the second mile from Jericho is pointed out even now (today) the stones which were brought up out of the Jordan (as scripture records)" (§211).2 Wolf's notes add that the sixth-century Madaba mosaic map labels Gilgal dōdekalithon, "twelve stones."

Most of the Joshua entries take the same form: the event, the tribe that received the place, and where it is now.

  • Ai (Gai): "Josue attacked it killing its king. It is now deserted." In the Genesis section it lies west of Bethel, and "there are shown only ruins" (§§312, 3).
  • Gibeon: "From whence came the Gabaonites in order to make supplication to Josue," once "a great metropolis and capital … of the Hevites," now "a village called the same" four miles west of Bethel (§313).
  • Beth-horon: "To where the (enemy) kings were pursued by Josue," now two villages twelve miles from Jerusalem on the road to Nicopolis (§212).
  • Makkedah: "Where Josue shut up five kings in the cave, which kings he also killed along with the king of Makeda," now eight miles east of Eleutheropolis (§668).
  • Hazor: "City of King Jabin which Josue burned because it was alone 'chief of all the kingdoms …'" (§51).
  • The valley of Achor: "where Achar (Achan) the condemned thief was stoned," "even now pointed out near Jericho beside (not far from) Galgala" (§413).
  • Bethel, Gezer, Debir, Eglon: each entry records that Joshua killed its king ("Josue attacked it killing its king," of Gezer), then gives the present village and its distance from a Roman city (§§192, 315, 375, 414).
  • Timnath-serah: "City of Josue son of Nun … in which even now there sepulchre of Josue is pointed out" (§503).

He also records where the conquest stopped short. At Gezer, "(Quite obvious) the tribe of Ephraim did not capture it from the heathen" (§315).3 None of these entries reads the conquest as a figure. The book was written for readers who wanted to find the places on the ground, and it was used that way. Jerome translated it, and Paula's pilgrimage in 385–386 follows its sites (see Jerome).

Joshua has a place in world chronology: twenty-seven years, "according to the tradition of the Hebrews"

That is what the Apostle says. According to him, there were 534 years after Joshua. As well as the 450 years, which he assigns to the judges until Samuel, there must be added 40 years for Saul, another 40 years for David, and the four years of Solomon's reign before the building of the temple, which makes a total of 534 years from Joshua the successor of Moses until Solomon. If you add the 40 years of Moses in the wilderness, and the 27 years of Joshua the son of Nun, then the total for the whole period will be 600 years, according to the Apostle.

— Chronicle, Book 1 (the Chronography), trans. Andrew Smith.4 The first book of the Chronicle works out how long the period from Moses to Solomon lasted. Paul's sermon at Pisidian Antioch, which he quotes ("Joshua destroyed seven nations in the land of Canaan," Acts 13:19), and the judge-by-judge totals of Judges give about 600 years. First Kings 6:1 gives 480 in the Hebrew and 440 in the Septuagint. Eusebius chooses 480. His argument is a historian's. Five generations run from Nahshon, who died in the wilderness, to David. Six hundred years would mean each man fathered his heir at about 120, "which is wholly incredible." So the periods of foreign oppression must be counted inside the judges' years, not added to them. On Joshua himself he is careful about what the text does and does not say:

About Joshua, the book which bears his name tells us nothing more than that he died at the age of 110 years. But the Hebrews say that he was leader for 27 years; and so he was 43 years old when Moses went out of Egypt.

The second book, the Canons, sets this out in parallel columns year by year. It survives in Jerome's Latin translation. Under the year after "Death of Moses" it reads: "Of the Hebrews, after Moses Joshua is appointed leader for 27 years. The five books of Moses, according to the translation of the LXX elders, contain 3,730 years until this point." A few lines later: "Joshua the successor of Moses distributes the land of the Palestinians to the Jewish nation by lot." Then: "After the death of Joshua, strangers held the Hebrews subject for 8 years, which are included in the date-range of Gothoniel, according to the traditions of the Jews." In the parallel columns for the same years stand Danaus, king of Argos, Erichthonius building "the first four horse chariot team in Greece," and Phoenix and Cadmus, who "reigned in Tyre and Sidon."5 The conquest is a datable event, synchronised with Greek history. (The online edition's modern column puts Joshua's first year at 1471 BC. That figure is the editors' conversion, not Eusebius's.)

The shared name: Moses "gives the name of Jesus to him whom he is about to constitute the successor"

MOSES was also the first to use the Name Jesus, when he changed the name of his successor and altered it to Jesus. … when no one of those before him had ever used the name Jesus, he first of all, impelled by the Holy Spirit, gives the name of Jesus to him whom he is about to constitute the successor of his rule over the people, changing the other name he had used before. He did not consider the name of his forefather given him when he was born sufficient (for his parents called him Nauses). But being the prophet of God he changed the name received by birth, and called the man Jesus at the bidding of the Holy Spirit; that he might lead the whole people after his own death, (with the knowledge that) when the law laid down by Moses some day should be changed and have an end, and should pass away like Moses himself, that no one else but Jesus the Christ of God would lead that other polity, which would be better than the former.

— Proof of the Gospel (Demonstratio Evangelica) IV.17, trans. W. J. Ferrar.6 The Septuagint calls Joshua Iēsous, the same Greek name as Jesus. Eusebius builds his typology on Numbers 13:16, where Moses renames Hoshea (Greek Auses, "Nauses" in Ferrar's text). Moses gave "both the names of our Saviour" to the two leaders of his people: "Christ" to Aaron the anointed priest, "Jesus" to his successor. The Church History repeats the argument at its opening. There the name was "a gift of honor, far greater than any kingly diadem," and Joshua "bore a resemblance to our Saviour in the fact that he alone, after Moses and after the completion of the symbolical worship which had been transmitted by him, succeeded to the government of the true and pure religion" (I.3.3–5).7 The type lies in the succession: Moses' law gives way to a leader called Jesus. Eusebius does not draw the type from the battles. He also traces it back to the Hebrew. The chapter closes by noting that "the son of Nave is called by the Hebrews Joshua," a name meaning the salvation of God, so that "wherever the Salvation of God is named in the Greek versions, you are to understand that nothing but Jesus is meant."

The commander at Jericho was the Word of God, come to fight beside Israel

And as Joshua, the successor of Moses, was about to fight against the former possessors of Palestine his enemies, foreign and most ungodly races, He rightly appears to him with a sword drawn and pointed against the enemy, shewing by the vision that He Himself is about to attack the ungodly with an unseen sword and with divine power, the fellow-soldier and the fellow-combatant of His people. Wherefore He gives Himself the name of Chief and Captain of the Lord to suit the occasion.

— Proof of the Gospel V.19, on Joshua 5:13–15.8 The "captain of the host of the Lord" whom Joshua met near Jericho is, for Eusebius, the pre-incarnate Word. The command to remove his sandals repeats the command given to Moses at the bush, which shows "that the God Who answered on both occasions was one and the same." He uses the passage the same way in Church History I.2.11–13. The theophany is a christological proof-text. It also takes the war as real and fought with God's support. The Word appears "with a sword drawn and pointed against the enemy," because the campaign about to start is one He will fight in "with an unseen sword and with divine power." The Canaanites are "the former possessors of Palestine," described as "foreign and most ungodly races."

The ban as judgment: the Amorites destroyed when "their sins were fulfilled"

For many times of old the long-suffering of God had borne with their transgressions before the Saviour came, as is shewn by the prophet's words: but just as in the case of the ancient foreign inhabitants of the land of promise it was said to Abraham, "The sins of the Amorites are not yet fulfilled," and if they were not yet fulfilled they could not yet be driven from their native land, but when they were fulfilled, they were then destroyed by Joshua, the successor of Moses: so also you will understand in the case of the before-mentioned people.

— Proof of the Gospel VIII.2, on Daniel 9:24.9 This is the closest Eusebius comes to the moral problem of the conquest. He does not argue it as a separate question. He uses it as a settled precedent. Genesis 15:16 had said the Amorites' iniquity was "not yet full." God waited, and when it was full "they were then destroyed by Joshua." He then applies the same pattern of delayed judgment to Jerusalem's fall to Rome. The destruction of the Canaanites is taken as a historical act of divine justice that came only after long patience. It is not allegorised and it is not called a problem.

Thirty kings expelled, and the cities that were "not extirpated"

The Jews therefore, immediately after the egress from Egypt by Moses, when they had come into Palestine, expelled the Kings, thirty in number, from their cities. Those however, who were not extirpated, remained and availed themselves of their population, local residences, and kings: those (I say), who resided apart in Gaza, Ashkelon, Joppa, and Azotus, again rebelled among themselves.

— Theophany II.66, trans. Samuel Lee (from the Syriac).10 Here the conquest serves as evidence in an argument about the ancient world. Before Augustus, Eusebius argues, every city had its own king, and the nations were always at war. The conquest is one of his examples. Joshua 12 lists thirty-one kings in the Hebrew and twenty-nine in the Septuagint. Eusebius rounds the number to thirty. He also notes, as Joshua and Judges do, that the coastal cities were not taken. He reads the conquest as Joshua and Judges tell it: real and partial.

Notes

  • Typology and history together. Eusebius was trained in Origen's school at Caesarea. He shares Origen's reading of the name: Joshua's renaming is a prophecy of Jesus, and the succession from Moses to Joshua figures the passing of the law to the Gospel. Compare Origen of Alexandria on the Conquest of Canaan. He does not share Origen's homiletic project of reading the battles as the soul's war. In the gazetteer, the chronicle and the apologetic works the conquest is an event with a place, a date and a moral cause.
  • The moral question. Eusebius's one explicit word on the slaughter is the Genesis 15:16 argument above. The Canaanites were judged when their sins were complete, and the Word fought on Israel's side. It is a retributive answer, close to Augustine's "righteous retribution." The patristic answers are weighed at Do the patristic defenses answer the moral problem of the ḥerem?
  • The sites today. Eusebius identifies Ai with a ruin near Bethel, and Gilgal with a revered place about two miles from Roman Jericho. Wolf's notes suggest the mound now called Tell es-Sultan was shown as Gilgal, not Jericho, in his day. For the modern debate, see the identification of Ai, Jericho's destruction and the dating of Jericho. Eusebius's choice of 480 years from the Exodus to the temple bears on the early date of the conquest. His wider chronology belongs to the history of biblical chronology.
  • Eusebius's Onomasticon also appears on this wiki in Eusebius of Caesarea on Bethany and Bethphage and on the birthplace of Christ. His Deuteronomy-32 reading of the nations is in Eusebius of Caesarea on the Divine Council, and his treatment of the giants in Eusebius of Caesarea on the Nephilim.
  • Jerome translated both the Onomasticon and the Chronicle into Latin and followed the gazetteer on the ground. See Jerome on the Conquest of Canaan. For the historian Eusebius quotes most often, see Josephus on the Conquest of Canaan.
