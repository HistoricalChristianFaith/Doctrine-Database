Josephus on the Conquest of Canaan

Dates: c. AD 93–94 (the Antiquities of the Jews, Books IV–V; Josephus b. c. AD 37, d. c. 100) · Wikipedia
Summary: Flavius Josephus, a Jewish priest and historian writing at Rome, gives the earliest full retelling of Joshua outside the Bible, in Book V of the Antiquities. For him the conquest is military and political history. There are spies, sieges, ambushes, a five-year campaign, thirty-one defeated kings, surveyors who measure the land, and a general who dies at 110 after leading the people for twenty-five years. He keeps the ban on the Canaanites unsoftened ("they all perished, even to the women and the children"). He keeps the miracles too, but tells them in measured terms. The sun at Gibeon becomes "the day was lengthened," vouched for by "the Books laid up in the temple." Rahab becomes the keeper of an inn. There is no typology. He is not a Christian witness, but he was the history of the conquest that Christian readers inherited alongside Joshua itself.

Claims & evidence

The conquest as a dated military campaign

Josephus heads the chapter as a war history: "How Joshua, the commander of the Hebrews, made war with the Canaanites, and overcame them, and destroyed them; and divided their land by lot to the tribes of Israel." The narrative that follows is the Joshua of a Greco-Roman historian, with reconnaissance and logistics added. The spies at Jericho "saw which parts of the walls were strong, and which parts were otherwise," and the campaign has a measurable length:

The fifth year was now past; and there was not one of the Canaanites remained any longer; excepting some that had retired to places of great strength.

At the assembly at Shiloh Joshua tallies the result, "that thirty one of those Kings that ventured to give them battel were overcome." The land is then surveyed by "some geometricians, who could not easily fail of knowing the truth" before it is allotted. Joshua dies with a career that can be counted:

So Joshua, when he had thus discoursed to them, died; having lived an hundred and ten years: forty of which he lived with Moses, in order to learn what might be for his advantage afterwards. He also became their commander after his death for twenty five years.

Josephus also fixes the conquest inside a continuous chronology. Solomon's temple, he says elsewhere, was begun "Five hundred and ninety and two years after the Exodus out of Egypt". This figure is longer than the 480 years of 1 Kings 6:1.1

The ban told without softening

Josephus's version of Moses' law of war already holds the exception for Canaan. Israel is to "do nothing that is cruel," to spare fruit trees, and to take tribute from the defeated:

When you have beaten your enemies in battel, slay those that have fought against you; but preserve the others alive, that they may pay you tribute: excepting the nation of the Canaanites; for as to that people you must intirely destroy them.

Jericho's fall is then told in full:

So they were slain, and their throats cut; some in the ways, and others as caught in their houses. Nothing afforded them assistance; but they all perished, even to the women and the children: and the city was filled with dead bodies, and not one person escaped.

The Gibeonites resort to their ruse because they expect "little mercy from him, who made war that he might intirely destroy the nation of the Canaanites." Josephus offers no apology, no moral theory, and no allegory of the slaughter. The extermination is part of the record, commanded and carried out. The moral question that Marcion would press half a century later does not arise for him.2

The miracles kept, in measured terms: Jordan, Jericho, and the lengthened day

Josephus does not remove the wonders, but he tells them carefully. At the Jordan, "God promised so to dispose of the river, that they might pass over it; and that by taking away the main part of its waters." At Jericho the walls fall with no human cause:

…when they had gone round it seven times, and had stood still a little, the wall fell down: while no instruments of war, nor any other force was applied to it by the Hebrews.

At Beth-horon he keeps the thunder and the great hail. For the climax of Joshua 10:12–14, though, he names neither sun nor moon and gives no command from Joshua to the heavens:

Moreover it happened that the day was lengthened; that the night might not come on too soon, and be an obstruction to the zeal of the Hebrews in pursuing their enemies. Insomuch that Joshua took the Kings, which were hidden in a certain cave at Makkedah, and put them to death. Now that the day was lengthened at this time, and was longer than ordinary, is expressed in the Books laid up in the temple.

Joshua's "Sun, stand thou still upon Gibeon" becomes an impersonal event ("it happened that"), and its authority becomes a documentary one. In place of Joshua 10:13's "Is not this written in the book of Jasher?" Josephus appeals to the temple archive. The miracle is affirmed as fact and given a source, as a historian would handle it, with the wonder held at a distance.3

Rahab the innkeeper

The spies lodge "in the inn kept by Rahab." Josephus's Rahab knows the city is doomed: she asks the spies to save her "when they should take the city, and destroy all its inhabitants: as they had decreed to do", having been "assured by those divine miracles of which she had been informed". She is told to "hang out scarlet threads before her doors." After the fall Joshua "gave her certain lands immediately; and had her in great esteem ever afterwards." Josephus never calls her a harlot. The scarlet threads are only a recognition signal, with none of the typology that Clement would find in them a few years later.4

Joshua as the ideal commander

Josephus's Joshua is a model statesman, and his eulogy at the general's death is Josephus's own addition:

He was a man that wanted not wisdom, nor eloquence, to declare his intentions to the people; but very eminent on both accounts. He was of great courage and magnanimity, in action and in dangers: and very sagacious in procuring the peace of the people, and of great virtue at all proper seasons.

Louis Feldman's study of the portrait finds that, combining this eulogy with the earlier one at Antiquities 3.49, Josephus "stressed in Joshua the qualities of wisdom, eloquence, courage, endurance, flexibility, and piety." These are the virtues of a Hellenistic general and lawgiver's successor. Josephus answers the question of what to make of Joshua by making him a great man in history, not a figure of anyone else.5

Notes

  • History without typology. Josephus and the New Testament are near-contemporaries and share the name Iēsous for Joshua, but nothing in the Antiquities points beyond the events. The Christian readers who follow on this timeline (Clement, Justin, Irenaeus) take the same history for granted and add a figurative layer above it.
  • The ḥerem. Josephus's unflinching account of Jericho, and his law that spares every enemy "excepting the nation of the Canaanites," is the plain-history baseline against which the later Christian answers to the moral problem are measured. See the moral problem of the ḥerem and the patristic answers.
  • The material record. Several points in Josephus's retelling are tested on the archaeology pages: the fallen wall of Jericho (Jericho's dating), Ai "situate above Jericho" (Ai's identification), and the altar on Mount Ebal on which the curses were "engraven" (the Mount Ebal altar). His 592 years from the exodus to the temple bears on the early date. See also the conquest in the material record.
  • Rahab. Josephus's innkeeper is treated at length on Josephus on Rahab and the harlot-or-innkeeper argument.
