Augustine of Hippo on the Conquest of Canaan

Dates: c. 400 (the Reply to Faustus the Manichaean, c. 400; the Questions on the Heptateuch, Book VI, on Joshua, c. 419; the City of God, 413–426; life 354–430) · Wikipedia
Summary: Augustine keeps Origen's figure of Joshua as Jesus, and he reads the walls of Jericho and the scarlet cord as signs of Christ and the Church. He sets that figure on a history he treats as plain fact. In the City of God Joshua leads Israel into the land, divides it among the tribes and rules for twenty-seven years. Augustine dates his death against the kings of Assyria, Argos and Athens, and counts the years from the entry into Canaan to the founding of Rome. The Jordan crossing, the fall of Jericho and the sun standing still are real miracles, which he cites against pagan sceptics. On the morality of the wars he argues the point directly. Against the Manichaean Faustus he defends war waged at God's command as "not ferocity but obedience" and "righteous retribution." In his questions on Joshua he goes through the hard cases one by one: the ambush at Ai, the cities where "nothing that breathed" was left alive, the stoning of Achan's children, and the oath sworn to the Gibeonites. His question on the ambush gave the Western just-war tradition its standard definition of a just war. He also checks the book's sweeping formulas against the later history. Unlike Origen, he resolves them historically and does not turn them into allegory.

Claims & evidence

The conquest as a stage of sacred history, and the first fulfilment of the promise to Abraham

On the death of Moses, Joshua the son of Nun ruled the people, and led them into the land of promise, and divided it among them. By these two wonderful leaders wars were also carried on most prosperously and wonderfully, God calling to witness that they had got these victories not so much on account of the merit of the Hebrew people as on account of the sins of the nations they subdued. After these leaders there were judges, when the people were settled in the land of promise, so that, in the meantime, the first promise made to Abraham began to be fulfilled about the one nation, that is, the Hebrew, and about the land of Canaan; but not as yet the promise about all nations, and the whole wide world, for that was to be fulfilled, not by the observances of the old law, but by the advent of Christ in the flesh, and by the faith of the gospel. And it was to prefigure this that it was not Moses, who received the law for the people on Mount Sinai, that led the people into the land of promise, but Joshua, whose name also was changed at God's command, so that he was called Jesus.

— City of God 16.43.1 This is Augustine's whole view of the conquest in a few sentences. The entry, the division of the land and the wars are told in the narrative's own order, between Moses and the judges. The moral reason is Deuteronomy's: the victories were given for "the sins of the nations," not for Israel's merit (Deuteronomy 9:4–5). The conquest is the first, earthly fulfilment of God's promise to Abraham. The figure comes last, with "and it was to prefigure this." It is drawn from a fact of the history, that Joshua and not Moses led the people in. The next book adds a historical limit to the fulfilment. Neither under "the illustrious leader Jesus the son of Nun, who led that people into the land of promise, and, after driving out the nations, divided it among the twelve tribes," nor under the judges, did Israel hold the land "from some river of Egypt even to the great river Euphrates." That came only under David and Solomon (17.2).2

A datable link in world chronology

Joshua the son of Nun succeeded Moses, and settled in the land of promise the people he had brought in, having by divine authority conquered the people by whom it was formerly possessed. He also died, after ruling the people twenty-seven years after the death of Moses, when Amyntas reigned in Assyria as the eighteenth king, Coracos as the sixteenth in Sicyon, Danaos as the tenth in Argos, Ericthonius as the fourth in Athens.

— City of God 18.11.3 Book 18 runs the history of the two cities side by side, using the tables of Eusebius and Jerome. Here the conquest is a fixed point in that history. It is "by divine authority," and it falls in a stated reign of Assyria, Sicyon, Argos and Athens. The next chapter dates the Greek cults and fables invented "from Israel's exodus from Egypt down to the death of Joshua the son of Nun" (18.12). Later in the book Augustine adds up the years: "At the time when Rome was founded, then, the people of Israel had been in the land of promise seven hundred and eighteen years. Of these years twenty-seven belong to Joshua the Son of Nun, and after that three hundred and twenty-nine to the period of the judges" (18.22). The conquest is counted in years like any other period of the past.

The miracles of the conquest: real portents, cited against the sceptics

But we read in the divine books that even the sun itself stood still when a holy man, Joshua the son of Nun, had begged this from God until victory should finish the battle he had begun; and that it even went back, that the promise of fifteen years added to the life of king Hezekiah might be sealed by this additional prodigy. But these miracles, which were vouchsafed to the merits of holy men, even when our adversaries believe them, they attribute to magical arts; … For in our sacred books we read that this also happened, that a river "turned backward," was stayed above while the lower part flowed on, when the people passed over under the above-mentioned leader, Joshua the son of Nun.

— City of God 21.8.4 The chapter defends the possibility of miracles, so Augustine needs cases he holds to be real. He starts from a portent that Varro, a pagan author, recorded: the planet Venus once changing its colour, size and course. He then sets the sun's standing still at Gibeon and the halting of the Jordan beside it. His principle is that "a portent, therefore, happens not contrary to nature, but contrary to what we know as nature." Book 10 treats the Jordan crossing and the fall of Jericho the same way, as "miraculous testimonies to the law." The river "stopped in its course"; Jericho's walls "suddenly fell down, though assaulted by no hand, struck by no battering-ram" (10.17). In the Confessions the sun's standing still is evidence in his account of time: "when at the prayer of one the sun stood still in order that he might achieve his victorious battle, the sun stood still, but time went on" (11.23.30). In the Tractates on John the crossing and the stopping of the sun are among the works "which no other man has done" (91.2).5 He does not offer a natural explanation for any of them.

The figure laid on the history: Joshua's name, Rahab's cord, Jericho's walls

He will see Jesus leading the people into the land of promise; for this name was given to the leader of Israel, not at first, or by chance, but on account of the work to which he was called. He will see the cluster from the land of promise hanging from a wooden pole. He will see in Jericho, as in this perishing world, an harlot, one of those of whom the Lord says that they go before the proud into the kingdom of heaven, putting out of her window a scarlet line symbolical of blood, as confession is made with the mouth for the remission of sins. He will see the walls of Jericho, like the frail defences of the world, fall when compassed seven times by the ark of the covenant.

— Reply to Faustus the Manichaean 12.31.6 Faustus had denied that the Hebrew scriptures foretell Christ. Book 12 answers with a run of figures, and this passage brings in Joshua, Rahab and Jericho. The figure works the way Clement's and Origen's did. Rahab's scarlet line is "symbolical of blood," and the fall of the walls points to the world's defences giving way. Book 16 makes the name the strongest proof: Moses renamed Hoshea "Jesus" when he sent him into the land, and "no event or action could have a more distinctly prophetical character than this, where the very name is a prediction." The same passage keeps the man and the figure apart. The Jew is asked to "recognize in this dead Jesus, who led the people into the land of mortality, a figure of the true living Jesus" (16.19–20). A letter of the same years reads the walls of Jericho, a name "said to signify 'moon,'" as the defences of this mortal life, which fall "not by violent assault, but of themselves" (Letter 55.6.10). In each case the sign is drawn from something Augustine takes to have happened.

Faustus's charge, and the defence of war at God's command

Book 22 of the Reply answers Faustus's attack on the morals of the patriarchs and prophets. Faustus lists the charges against them, and the one against the lawgiver reads:

… or how Moses committed murder, and plundered Egypt, and waged wars, and commanded, or himself perpetrated, many cruelties. And he too was not content with one wife.

— Faustus, in Reply to Faustus 22.5.7 Faustus names Moses, not Joshua. Augustine answers for "the wars of Moses," but he states the answer as a rule for any war "carried on by divine command":

Now, if this explanation suffices … of the vast difference between the indulgence of passion and presumption on the part of men, and obedience to the command of God, who knows what to permit or to order, and also the time and the persons, and the due action or suffering in each case, the account of the wars of Moses will not excite surprise or abhorrence, for in wars carried on by divine command, he showed not ferocity but obedience; and God in giving the command, acted not in cruelty, but in righteous retribution, giving to all what they deserved, and warning those who needed warning. What is the evil in war? Is it the death of some who will soon die in any case, that others may live in peaceful subjection? This is mere cowardly dislike, not any religious feeling. The real evils in war are love of violence, revengeful cruelty, fierce and implacable enmity, wild resistance, and the lust of power, and such like.

— Reply to Faustus 22.74.8 The argument has three steps. The first is the principle of 22.73: the same act differs with "the act, the agent, and the authority," as Abraham's sacrifice of Isaac would be "shocking madness" on his own initiative and obedience at God's command. The second is that the evil of war is a disposition of the heart, not bodily death. The third is that God "can never require what is wrong," so a war waged "on the authority of God" is blameless (22.75). He meets the Gospel objection, "resist not evil," with a distinction of times. Under the Old Testament "the patriarchs and prophets, then, have a kingdom in this world, to show that these kingdoms, too, are given and taken away by God." Under the New, "the apostles and martyrs had no kingdom here" (22.76). In his summary the old ministers served by "putting sinners to death," the new by "being put to death by sinners," and "the service in both cases is rendered to one God" (22.79).9 At 22.92 he adds that the wars of Moses also carry "prophetic and symbolic" meaning. That is a second layer, set on a defence that does not need it.

The ambush at Ai, and the definition of a just war

Inasmuch as God ordered Joshua to plant an ambush in their rear, that is, to plant warriors in hiding to ambush the enemy, we can learn that such treachery is not unjustly carried out by those who wage a just war. Thus, a just man, if he wishes to undertake a just war, ought to think chiefly in these matters about nothing else than whether it is right for him to do so, for it is not lawful for everyone to wage war. However, once he has undertaken a just war, it makes no difference to the justice of the war whether he wins in open warfare or by treachery. However, those just wars ought to be defined as those which avenge injuries, if the tribe or state which is about to be sought in war, either neglected to punish a crime improperly committed by its own countrymen or neglected to repay what had been lost through those injuries. Moreover, without doubt that type of war is just which God commands, since there is no iniquity in him and he knows what ought to be done to each person. In this type of war the general of the army or the people themselves are not to be deemed so much the instigator of the war as much as its agent.

— Questions on the Heptateuch VI (Questions on Joshua) 10, on Joshua 8:2.10 Twenty years after the Reply, Augustine applies the principle to Joshua by name. The Latin of the central sentence is iusta autem bella ea definiri solent, quae ulciscuntur iniurias, "just wars are usually defined as those that avenge injuries." This is the definition Gratian copied into canon law (Decretum C. 23 q. 2 c. 2) and Thomas Aquinas cited in his article on war (Summa theologiae II-II q. 40 a. 1, with the ambush sentence in a. 3). Augustine adds that a war God commands is just "without doubt," and that its general is God's "agent" (minister), not its author. The next question asks whether the feigned flight at Ai was a lie, and whether a lie told to one "who should be deceived" can be just. If not, "we must still relate what transpired with the ambush to the truth with some other meaning" (q. 11). That is, he would read the ruse as a figure before he would call it a lie. He does not doubt that the ambush "transpired." Jericho falls under the same rules. The closing of its gates was itself an act of war, "for the inhabitants of Jericho did not send ambassadors to ask for terms of peace" (q. 26).11

"Nothing that breathed": the ban defended as God's command, not cruelty

One should not at all think it a horrible cruelty that Joshua did not leave anyone alive in those cities that fell to him, for God himself had ordered this. However, whoever for this reason thinks that God himself must be cruel and does not wish to believe then that the true God was the author of the Old Testament judges as perversely about the works of God as he does about the sins of human beings. Such people do not know what each person ought to suffer. Consequently, they think it a great evil when that which is about to fall is thrown down and when mortals die.

— Questions on Joshua 16, on Joshua 11:14–15.12 The Latin title of the question states its thesis: Praeceptum Domini, etiam si crudele videatur, sine crudelitate perficitur, "the Lord's command, even if it seems cruel, is carried out without cruelty." The people he answers are those who refuse to believe "that the true God was the author of the Old Testament," which is the Manichaean position he had met in Faustus. His reply restates the Reply (22.74). The killing is not in doubt. What he denies is that it was cruel, since God commanded it and death comes to mortals in any case.

Achan's children: God's hidden justice and the limits on human judges

The hardest case Augustine takes up is Achan, stoned "with all his" for taking from the devoted spoil of Jericho (Joshua 7). The Questions on Joshua survive in no public-domain English version, so the Latin is given here with a translation made for this page:

quaeri solet quomodo iuste pro alterius peccatis in alios vindicetur; maxime quia in Lege Dominus dixit, nec patres pro filiorum, nec filios pro patrum peccatis esse puniendos? An illud iudicantibus hominibus est praeceptum, ne quemquam pro altero puniant; Dei autem iudicia non sunt eiusmodi, qui alto et invisibili consilio suo novit quatenus extendat etiam temporalem hominum poenam salubremque terrorem? Non enim aliquid dirum, quantum attinet ad universi mundi administrationem, contingit mortalibus, cum moriuntur quandoque morituri.

"It is often asked how it can be just for punishment to fall on some for the sins of another, especially since the Lord said in the Law that fathers are not to be punished for the sins of their sons, nor sons for the sins of their fathers. Or was that commanded to men when they judge, that they should punish no one for another, while the judgments of God are not of that kind, since by his deep and invisible counsel he knows how far to extend even the temporal punishment of men and a salutary terror? For, as regards the governance of the whole world, nothing dreadful befalls mortals when they die who would die at some time."

— Questions on Joshua 8.13 The question sets a firm limit on human justice. Had Achan been brought before Joshua as a judge, "it is by no means to be thought" that a human judge would have punished anyone else with him. Only God, "who is able even after death … either to free or to destroy," may do so. The next question faces the children directly. Joshua had them stoned "not by human judgment, but by a prophetic spirit" (non tamen hoc iudicio humano, sed prophetico spiritu, q. 9.3). Augustine then denies that they were damned:

Nec ideo sane credendum est eos pro peccato patris, a quo innocentes erant, etiam post mortem supplicio inferni ignis incensos. … Quo ergo iudicio Dei, vel misericordia, fuerit irrogata sive filiis huius, sive illis triginta sex viris, cum omnes ab eius peccato alieni fuerint, latet apud eum apud quem non est iniquitas.

"Nor, surely, is it to be believed that for the sin of their father, of which they were innocent, they were also burned after death in the punishment of the fire of hell. … By what judgment of God, then, or by what mercy, it was inflicted, whether on this man's sons or on those thirty-six men, since all of them were strangers to his sin, lies hidden with him with whom there is no iniquity" (q. 9.4). He calls the children innocent. He grants that their death was a temporal penalty only, and he leaves its reason to God's hidden judgment. In the same place he suggests that death, which must come to all, "is useful to some when it is hastened." He does not deny that the stoning took place.

Mercy and oaths inside the ban: the Gibeonites and the spared Canaanites

Augustine reads the ban as a command that Israel partly failed to keep, and he weighs each failure. The Gibeonites won their oath by a lie, and Israel spared them when it "was necessary according to God's command to subdue all whom they had found." God approved:

Deus autem hoc approbavit, nec parcentibus succensuit … Et quoniam iuratio sic servata est, quamvis in hominibus qui mentiti sunt, ut ad clementiam sententia flecteretur, non displicuit Deo.

"But God approved this, and was not angry with those who spared them. … And since the oath was kept in such a way, though to men who had lied, that the sentence was bent toward clemency, it did not displease God" (q. 13). His proof is that God later avenged the Gibeonites on the house of Saul (2 Samuel 21). He adds that an oath to kill would not bind in the same way, as David's oath against Nabal did not. On Joshua 11:20, "it was of the Lord to harden their hearts," he argues that the Canaanites came out to fight so that Israel would not be moved to mercy against the command, "since God had ordered that none of the Canaanites were to be spared" (q. 18). At the close of the book he notes that Joshua's farewell does not rebuke Israel for sparing the remnant. His explanation is that their failure came "not from malice, nor from pride or contempt of the Lord's command, but from weakness of soul" (non de malitia, neque de superbia vel contemptu praecepti dominici, sed de animi infirmitate), and God chose not to count it against them (q. 30.2).14

The book's formulas checked against the later history

It is asked how this can be true, since the Hebrews were not altogether able to capture all the cities of those seven nations either in the times after the judges or in the times of the kings. But one must understand it to mean that Joshua never approached any city with hostile intent that he did not capture. Or it may mean that no city remained uncaptured except for those which were in the regions mentioned above.

— Questions on Joshua 17, on Joshua 11:19.15 Origen met the same sweeping formulas ("Jesus took all the land," "the land rested from wars") by denying that they were literally true of Joshua and reading them of Christ. Augustine asks the same question, "how can this be true?", and answers from the history. On Joshua 21:43–45, "the Lord gave unto Israel all the land," he sets out the seven nations of Exodus and Deuteronomy, the eleven of Genesis 15, and the reach of Solomon's kingdom "from the river" (1 Kings 4:21). He concludes that "God nevertheless fulfilled the faith of his promise in the times of Solomon" (Deus tamen temporibus Salomonis fidem suae promissionis implevit). The land still unconquered under Joshua had been "given" in another sense, left for Israel's exercise (q. 21.3–4). He handles other details the same way:

  • the Jordan "full over all its banks … at the time of harvest" (Joshua 3:15): "This seems incredible in our regions; but there, as those who know report, the wheat harvest is at the beginning of spring, and at that time that river fills more than through the winter" (q. 3);
  • the timetable of the "three days" before the crossing, which he reconciles with the spies' days in hiding (q. 2);
  • the notes on Pharaoh's capture of Gezer (Joshua 16:10) and on the rebuilding of Jericho under its curse (Joshua 6:26). These are in the Septuagint but not in "the translation from the Hebrew." He will not read them as prophecy, since "this history is believed to have been written down in those times when those deeds were recent" (cum haec historia illis temporibus conscripta credatur, quibus erant gesta illa recentia). He judges them additions by the Seventy, "who knew it had been done" (q. 19).

He keeps the figurative option for a few details only. For the "hornets" sent before Israel (Joshua 24:12), which no narrative records, he suggests "the sharp stings of fear" or evil spirits. He also allows that "not everything which took place has been written down," so that "real wasps" might be meant (q. 27). The stone that "heard" Joshua's covenant (24:27) cannot literally hear, so it stands for Christ, though it was "visibly set up" (quamquam visibiliter statutum, q. 30.1).

The spoil of Jericho as a precedent in his own day

When, however, the spoils of these places are applied to the benefit of the community or devoted to the service of God, they are dealt with in the same manner as the men themselves when they are turned from impiety and sacrilege to the true religion. We understand this to be the will of God from the examples quoted by yourself: the grove of the false gods from which He commanded wood to be taken [by Gideon] for the burnt-offering; and Jericho, of which all the gold, silver, and brass was to be brought into the Lord's treasury.

— Letter 47.3, to Publicola (398).16 Publicola had asked whether Christians might use goods from pagan temples pulled down "by permission from the authorities." Augustine takes the rule of Jericho's devoted spoil (Joshua 6:19) as a working precedent. Nothing may go to private use, "so that it may be manifest that in overthrowing these we are influenced, not by greed, but by piety," but it may go to God's service. This is the ban applied to objects, not to people. Elsewhere Augustine denies that a Christian may use the war-law of the conquest against persons (Reply to Faustus 22.76–79, above).

Notes

  • Typology kept, history argued. Augustine keeps the figure of Joshua as Jesus that Justin made central and Origen worked out at length. He sets it on a history he states, dates and defends, and he argues the ethics of the wars on the literal sense. Where Origen called the totality formulas literally untrue and read them of Christ, Augustine reconciles them with the later history of the judges and kings.
  • The moral question. Whether Augustine's divine-command and just-war answer, or Origen's spiritual reading, meets the moral problem of the ban is weighed at Do the patristic defenses answer the moral problem of the ḥerem? (assessment: contested). The Questions on Joshua above (qq. 8–9, 13, 16, 18) show him dealing with the innocent dead, Achan's children, by name.
  • Jericho and Ai. Augustine's Ai is the biblical Gai of the Septuagint and Old Latin. The material record of the two cities is discussed at the Jericho dating argument and the identification of Ai; see also The Conquest of Canaan in the Material Record.
  • Rahab. Besides the scarlet line of Reply to Faustus 12.31, Augustine discusses Rahab's lie to the king's men in Against Lying 31–34. God rewarded "not their deceit, but their benevolence," and her hospitality is "meet to be imitated." See the Rahab timeline and the scarlet-cord typology.
  • Chronology. His dating of the conquest belongs to his wider reckoning of world history; see Biblical chronology and Augustine on the date of the Flood. On his view of the Septuagint translators' authority, which q. 19 uses, see Augustine on the Septuagint's origin.
  • Later reception. The definition in q. 10 passed through Gratian to Thomas Aquinas. Augustine's handling of the same book can be compared with his contemporaries Jerome and, a generation later, Theodoret of Cyrus.
