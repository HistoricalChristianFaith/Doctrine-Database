John Calvin on the Conquest of Canaan

Dates: c. 1564 (the Commentary on Joshua, dictated 1563–64 and his last work; the Institutes, final Latin edition 1559; life 1509–1564) · Wikipedia
Summary: Calvin's Commentary on Joshua was the last book he wrote, dictated in the months before his death in May 1564. He reads Joshua as straight history: the record of how God kept his promise of the land, "truly" dividing it by lot, in spite of a people who stopped short of taking all of it. The miracles are real events. The Jordan stood "gathered up into a heap," which was "certainly neither a natural nor fortuitous event"; Jericho's walls "fell of their own accord"; at Gibeon "the day was continued through the whole night." He suspends judgment on who wrote the book and reads its sweeping formulas with care: "it was far from being true that Joshua had actually acquired the whole land." He meets the moral problem of the ban head-on, with no allegory. The slaughter "might seem an inhuman massacre, had it not been executed by the command of God." The Canaanites' iniquity had been borne "four hundred years, until … complete," God hardened them so that they fought, and "the judgment-seat of heaven is not subject to our laws." He holds Rahab's lie a fault, forgiven and not imputed. He draws no typology from the shared name of Joshua and Jesus. His Christ in the book is the "captain of the Lord's host," whom he takes to be the pre-incarnate Son, and the promise left half-kept, which pointed believers forward to Christ.

Claims & evidence

The book is history: the record of God keeping his promise of the land

Although the people had already gained signal victories, and become the occupants of a commodious and tolerably fertile tract of country, the Divine promise as to the land of Canaan still remained suspended. Nay, the leading article in the Covenant was unaccomplished, as if God, after cooping up his people in a corner, had left his work in a shapeless and mutilated form. This Book, then, shows how, when the intolerable impiety of the people had interrupted the course of deliverance, God, while inflicting punishment, so tempered the severity of justice as ultimately to perform what he had promised concerning the inheritance of Canaan.

— Commentary on Joshua, "Argument of the Book of Joshua."1 The "Argument" that opens the commentary runs through the book in order, and every step is told as an event. The Jordan crossing repeats "the same miracle" as the Red Sea. Circumcision is renewed at Gilgal. "The walls of Jericho fell of their own accord, shaken merely by the sound of trumpets." The nations "were not completely routed by a single battle, nor in one short campaign, but were gradually worn out and destroyed by many laborious contests." The allotment was not "elusory or fallacious," since "the inheritance, in possession of which God had placed them by His own hand, was truly and distinctly divided by His orders." The same passage is cautious about authorship. "As to the Author of this Book, it is better to suspend our judgment," Calvin writes, because "Joshua died before the taking of Hebron and Debir, and yet an account of it is given in the 15th chapter." He guesses that "a summary of events was framed by the high priest Eleazar." None of this touches the essential point, "that the doctrine herein contained was dictated by the Holy Spirit for our use."

The miracles happened: the Jordan, the walls, the sun at Gibeon

First, Joshua explains the nature of the miracle for the purpose of removing doubt, and preventing profane men from denying the divine interposition by a subtle searching for other causes. It is not, indeed, impossible that the flowing of the water might have been restrained for a short time, and that some portion of the channel might thus have appeared dry, or that the course might have changed and taken some other direction. But it was certainly neither a natural nor fortuitous event, when the waters stood gathered up into a heap.

— on Joshua 3:15–16.2 Calvin names the natural explanations of the Jordan crossing and sets them aside. He adds that the stones set up at the crossing were ordered "as a perpetual memorial, that this distinguished mercy might be celebrated by posterity in all ages." On Gibeon (10:12–14) he takes the sun's standing still as a literal event, an "incredible miracle unlike any that had previously taken place," in which Joshua told "the sun and the moon to deviate from the perpetual law of nature":

I do not give myself any great anxiety as to the number of the hours; because it is enough for me that the day was continued through the whole night. Were histories of that period extant, they would doubtless celebrate this great miracle; lest its credibility, however, should be questioned, the writer of this book mentions that an account of it was given elsewhere, though the work which he quotes has been lost, and expounders are not well agreed as to the term Jazar.

The "book of Jasher" is to Calvin a lost written record cited to back the event. He prefers to read its title as referring to "either God or Israel, rather than the author of a history." He sets the miracle beside Hezekiah's sign (Isaiah 38:5–8), so it "is extolled as singular" without being the only one of its kind. The Institutes use the same event as a proof of providence: "when we read, that at the prayer of Joshua the sun was stayed in its course (Josh. 10:13) … by these miracles God declared that the sun does not daily rise and set by a blind instinct of nature, but is governed by Him in its course."3

The ban: "might seem an inhuman massacre, had it not been executed by the command of God"

The indiscriminate and promiscuous slaughter, making no distinction of age or sex, but including alike women and children, the aged and decrepit, might seem an inhuman massacre, had it not been executed by the command of God. But as he, in whose hands are life and death, had justly doomed those nations to destruction, this puts an end to all discussion. We may add, that they had been borne with for four hundred years, until their iniquity was complete. Who will now presume to complain of excessive rigor, after God had so long delayed to execute judgment? If any one object that children, at least, were still free from fault, it is easy to answer, that they perished justly, as the race was accursed and reprobated.

— on Joshua 6:21.4 Calvin does not soften the text or move it into allegory. He grants that the act, taken by itself, would be atrocious: "it would have been barbarous and atrocious cruelty had the Israelites gratified their own lust and rage, in slaughtering mothers and their children." What changes it is the command. The Israelites are "justly praised for their active piety and holy zeal, in executing the command of God." The "four hundred years" is the delay of Genesis 15:16, where the Amorites' iniquity is "not yet full." He returns to the point at 10:40, in stronger terms:

Had he proceeded of his own accord to commit an indiscriminate massacre of women and children, no excuse could have exculpated him from the guilt of detestable cruelty, cruelty surpassing anything of which we read as having been perpetrated by savage tribes scarcely raised above the level of the brutes. But that at which all would otherwise be justly horrified, it becomes them to embrace with reverence, as proceeding from God. … Though, in our judgment at least, the children and many of the women also were without blame, let us remember that the judgment-seat of heaven is not subject to our laws.

— on Joshua 10:40.5 He ends the passage with Romans 9: "the potter will nevertheless have absolute power over his own vessels, or rather over his own clay." At 11:19–20 he takes up the objection that the Canaanites could not have sued for peace, since Israel was forbidden to grant it. His answer is that the war was still just, because the Canaanites attacked first: "the Israelites, though they were forbidden to show them any mercy, were met in a hostile manner, in order that the war might be just." The hardening of their hearts was God's own work, "who thus precipitates those whom he means to destroy." He rejects any reading of the verse that gives God only "a permissive power." The Institutes cite the same verse for the same doctrine: "Moses says of the inhabitants of the land of Canaan, that they went forth to battle because the Lord had hardened their hearts (Josh. 11:20)."6

Rahab: faith commended, her lie still a fault

As to the falsehood, we must admit that though it was done for a good purpose, it was not free from fault. For those who hold what is called a dutiful lie to be altogether excusable, do not sufficiently consider how precious truth is in the sight of God. … Rahab also does wrong when she falsely declares that the messengers were gone, and yet the principal action was agreeable to God, because the bad mixed up with the good was not imputed. On the whole, it was the will of God that the spies should be delivered, but he did not approve of saving their life by falsehood.

— on Joshua 2:4.7 Calvin asks two questions about Rahab: was her betrayal of her city excusable, and was her lie? He clears her of treachery. "It was therefore only the knowledge communicated to her mind by God which exempted her from fault," and in passing "by faith to a new people, she behooved to renounce her countrymen." The lie he does not clear. He compares it to Rebekah's deception of Isaac: a holy aim spoiled by a crooked means, the fault "suppressed and not taken into account." He also rejects the reading of Rahab as an innkeeper. "Why some try to avoid the name harlot, and interpret zvnh [zonah] as meaning one who keeps an inn, I see not," he writes, and he puts it down to rabbinic efforts to save the nation's honour. At 6:22 he explains why her family could be spared from a city under the ban. Rahab "had bargained for her impunity" only "by the suggestion of the Spirit," and so she "was exempted by special privilege."

The Gibeonite oath bound Israel, though it should never have been sworn

In regard to the general position, the obligation of an oath ought to be held in the greatest sacredness, so that we may not, under the pretext of error, resile from pactions, even from those in which we have been deceived, since the sacred name of God is more precious than the wealth of a whole world.

— on Joshua 9:16–19.8 Calvin treats the treaty with Gibeon (Joshua 9) as a real case of conscience and gives a two-sided answer. As a general rule an oath binds even when it was obtained by fraud. But here "a special reason left the Israelites at liberty to recede from the deceitful compact," because the leaders had "improperly departed from the command of God, with which it was not lawful to interfere in the smallest iota." By keeping the oath "they twice profaned the name of God." He still praises "the integrity of the age" because the people kept their leaders' word. He contrasts Rome's repudiation of the Caudine peace. He reads the Gibeonites' sentence to servitude (9:20–27) as "a kind of middle course, so as not to make the oath altogether void."

"Joshua took the whole land": the sweeping formula read with care

Although it was far from being true that Joshua had actually acquired the whole land, yet he is truly said to have obtained it as God had declared to Moses, the latter clause restricting the meaning of the general sentence. For it had been expressly added that the conquest which God had promised would be made gradually, lest it should afterwards become necessary to war with the ferocious wild beasts of the woods, if they pressed forward into a desert waste.

— on Joshua 11:23.9 Calvin does not take "Joshua took the whole land" at face value. He reads it against the book's own account, in which the lots included "lands … which were not actually subdued by the people till Joshua was dead, nay, till many ages after." He resolves the tension inside the history. The promise was to be fulfilled "gradually" (Deuteronomy 7:22), and Joshua received "a certain specimen of the promise." In the "Argument" he blames the shortfall on Israel, who "turned backwards to indulge in sloth and cowardice" and spared nations they had been ordered to drive out. Those nations then "would prove to them thorns and stings."

Jericho's ruins as a "trophy," and Hiel's rebuilding

He wished, therefore, that the ruins and devastation should exist for ever as a kind of trophy; because the rebuilding of it would have been equivalent to an erasure effacing the miracle.

— on Joshua 6:26.10 To Calvin, Joshua's curse on whoever rebuilt Jericho was meant to keep the visible ruin as a lasting memorial of "the divine power." He links it forward in the sacred history: "In the time of Ahab (1 Kings 16:34) arose Hiel, a citizen of Bethel," who rebuilt the city, and the denunciation "did not fail of its effect; for Hiel founded the new Jericho in Abiram his first-born, and set up its gates in his younger son Segub." He uses the conquest as one link in a continuous history running from Joshua to the kings.

Christ in the book: the captain of the Lord's host, and a promise left unfinished

We have said that in the books of Moses the name of Jehovah is often attributed to the presiding Angel, who was undoubtedly the only-begotten Son of God. He is indeed very God, and yet in the person of Mediator by dispensation, he is inferior to God. I willingly receive what ancient writers teach on this subject, -- that when Christ anciently appeared in human form, it was a prelude to the mystery which was afterwards exhibited when God was manifested in the flesh.

— on Joshua 5:14.11 Calvin's reading of Joshua is Christ-centred, but not in the way the Fathers' was. He makes nothing of the fact that Joshua and Jesus share one name (Iēsous in Greek), the root of the patristic typology. The figure he identifies with Christ is the "captain of the Lord's host" who meets Joshua before Jericho. This is an appearance of the eternal Son, with a caution: "we must beware, however, of imagining that Christ at that time became incarnate." His other route to Christ runs through the history itself. In the "Argument," the land promised to Abraham "from the river of Egypt to the great river Euphrates" was never fully possessed, and "the apparent failure reminded the children of God that they were to look forward to a more excellent state … Hence their thoughts were raised to Christ." The Institutes state the principle in general form. God "promised them the land of Canaan for an inheritance, not that it might be the limit of their hopes, but that the view of it might train and confirm them in the hope of that true inheritance, which, as yet, appeared not."12 The type is built on the land actually given. The history stays in place under it.

Notes

  • The edition quoted. All quotations are from the Calvin Translation Society version by Henry Beveridge (Edinburgh, 1854), made from the Latin and collated with the French. That edition also carries its editor's own notes, marked "Ed." Several are often quoted as if they were Calvin's: the remark that Calvin seems "a stranger to the Copernican system," the list of rabbis who explained the Gibeon miracle away, and the argument against De Wette on the date of the book of Jasher. These are Beveridge's, not Calvin's, and nothing on this page rests on them.
  • Luther on the sun at Gibeon. The one remark of Martin Luther's on Joshua that is widely quoted comes from his table talk of 4 June 1539. It concerns "a new astrologer" who held "that the earth moves and not the heavens." Anton Lauterbach's notes, the earliest record of the conversation, give Luther's words as: "sicut ille facit, qui totam astrologiam invertere vult. Etiam illa confusa tamen ego credo sacrae scripturae, nam Iosua iussit solem stare, non terram" ("as that man does who wants to turn the whole of astronomy upside down. Even in these things that are thrown into confusion, I believe the Holy Scripture, for Joshua commanded the sun to stand still, not the earth").13 The sharper wording usually quoted, "Der Narr will die ganze Kunst Astronomiae umkehren" ("the fool wants to turn the whole art of astronomy upside down"), is from Johann Aurifaber's printed edition of the table talk (1566), which recast Lauterbach's notes into German. Both versions read Joshua 10:12–13 as a literal event that settles a question of physics.
  • The moral problem. Calvin's answer to the ban is the divine-command answer at its starkest: God's sentence, the Canaanites' completed iniquity, and God's hardening of their hearts, with no appeal to allegory. It stands in the line of Augustine and against Origen's spiritualizing. The two patristic strategies are weighed at Do the patristic defenses answer the moral problem of the ḥerem? (assessment: contested). See also Augustine of Hippo and Origen of Alexandria on this timeline, and Thomas Aquinas, the last witness before Calvin.
  • Rahab. The Rahab tradition is collected on the Rahab timeline. Calvin's rejection of the "innkeeper" reading bears on Was Rahab a harlot or an innkeeper?
  • The material record. What the ground shows of Jericho, Ai, and Hazor is set out on the conquest in the material record; see especially the Jericho dating argument.
