Tertullian on the Conquest of Canaan

Dates: c. 207–208 (Against Marcion, in its final edition; also An Answer to the Jews, On Idolatry, Scorpiace, and On Fasting; Tertullian of Carthage, c. 155–c. 220) · Wikipedia
Summary: Tertullian wrote the longest early reply to Marcion, who had set the Creator of the Old Testament against the Father of Jesus. He treats the conquest throughout as fact. Jericho's walls fell on the seventh day "just as was predicted," and the city was "made a prey by the edge of the sword." The Jordan "wholly ceased to run at the bidding of Joshua." The sun stood still at Gibeon. Joshua's "mighty deeds … you yourselves read." Over this history he lays Justin's typology: Hoshea renamed Jesus, the land of promise as eternal life, and the knife of stone as the circumcision of Christ. The Marcionite objection he reports about Jericho is about the Sabbath, not about bloodshed. Against the wider charge that the Creator is cruel he argues that God's severity is justice, "good, because just." He will not let Joshua's wars serve as a warrant for Christians to fight: "if it pleases you to sport with the subject." The one surviving Marcionite antithesis aimed at Joshua's conquest comes from a later report, the Dialogue of Adamantius. It sets the sun held still "that he might kill more" against Paul's "let not the sun go down upon your wrath."

Claims & evidence

The objection: Marcion's antitheses, as later reported

Ὁ προφήτης τοῦ θεοῦ τῆς γενέσεως, ἵνα πολεμῶν πλείονας ἀνέλῃ, ἔστησε τὸν ἥλιον τοῦ μὴ δῦσαι μέχρι συντελέσῃ ἀναιρῶν τοὺς πολεμοῦντας πρὸς τὸν λαόν· ὁ δὲ κύριος, ἀγαθὸς ὤν, λέγει· ὁ ἥλιος μὴ ἐπιδυέτω ἐπὶ τῷ παροργισμῷ ὑμῶν.

— Dialogue of Adamantius I.13 (Greek text as edited by van de Sande Bakhuyzen), spoken by the Marcionite Megethius. Rendered literally here: "The prophet of the God of creation, so that by fighting he might kill more, stopped the sun from setting until he had finished killing those who were fighting against the people. But the Lord, being good, says: 'Let not the sun go down upon your wrath.'" Marcion's own Antitheses (c. 144) are lost. Tertullian describes the work as "contrary statements set in opposition … compiled with a view to such a severance of the law from the gospel as should divide the Deity into two." The Joshua item survives only in this dialogue, written around 300 by an anonymous author against later Marcionites. A neighbouring antithesis (I.11) sets Moses' hands, stretched out on the mountain so that he might kill many in the war (ἵνα πολλοὺς τῷ πολέμῳ ἀνέλῃ), against Christ's hands, stretched out "not to destroy men, but to save" (Mahar's English). In reply the orthodox speaker says that killing those who make war unjustly has already been shown to be just. The charge assumes the history as much as the reply does. Neither side doubts that Joshua stopped the sun and that the Canaanites died. They dispute what kind of God would do it. Tertullian does not quote this antithesis, and no Joshua antithesis appears in his five books.1

The Marcionite charge about Jericho: the Sabbath broken by God's command

Similarly on other points also, you reproach Him with fickleness and instability for contradictions in His commandments, such as that He forbade work to be done on Sabbath-days, and yet at the siege of Jericho ordered the ark to be carried round the walls during eight days; in other words, of course, actually on a Sabbath. You do not, however, consider the law of the Sabbath: they are human works, not divine, which it prohibits. … They, however, who on the Sabbath carried the ark round Jericho, did it with impunity. For it was not their own work, but God's, which they executed, and that too, from His express commandment.

— Against Marcion II.21. The one charge about the conquest that Tertullian reports from the Marcionites is about consistency, not slaughter. The Creator forbade work on the Sabbath and then ordered work on one. Tertullian's answer accepts the siege as told. The circuit of Jericho was "a rare and a sacred work," a divine work done at God's command, and so outside the ban on human labour. In IV.12 he turns the same siege back on Marcion's Christ, who healed on the Sabbath. If Christ broke the Sabbath, "He simply acted after the Creator's example," since at Jericho the ark went round the walls "for eight days running." It was "really God's work, which He commanded Himself, and which He had ordered for the sake of the lives of His servants when exposed to the perils of war." In both places Tertullian has "eight days," where Joshua 6 has seven days of circuits and An Answer to the Jews 4 has seven.2

Jericho's walls fell "just as was predicted," and the city was taken by the sword

In short, so true is it that it is not in the exemption from work of the sabbath-that is, of the seventh day-that the celebration of this solemnity is to consist, that Joshua the son of Nun, at the time that he was reducing the city Jericho by war. stated that he had received from God a precept to order the People that priests should carry the ark of the testament of God seven days, making the circuit of the city; and thus, when the seventh day's circuit had been performed, the walls of the city would spontaneously fall. Which was so done; and when the space of the seventh day was finished, just as was predicted, down fell the walls of the city. Whence it is manifestly shown, that in the number of the seven days there intervened a sabbath-day. For seven days, whencesoever they may have commenced, must necessarily include within them a sabbath-day; on which day not only must the priests have worked, but the city must have been made a prey by the edge of the sword by all the people of Israel. Nor is it doubtful that they "wrought servile work," when, in obedience to God's precept, they drave the preys of war.

— An Answer to the Jews 4. Here Tertullian argues that the Sabbath was temporary, and he does it by reasoning from the calendar of the siege. Seven days must include a Sabbath. So on a Sabbath the priests worked, the walls fell, and the city was put to the sword and plundered. The argument works only if the siege happened on real days. The fall of the walls is told as a fulfilled prediction ("Which was so done"). The putting of the city to the sword is mentioned without comment. Tertullian's interest is in the day it happened, not in its morality.3

Joshua, renamed Jesus, a figure of Christ: "which mighty deeds Joshua the son of Nun did, and you yourselves read"

In the course of the appointing of a successor to Moses, Oshea the son of Nun is certainly transferred from his pristine name, and begins to be called Jesus. Certainly, you say. This we first assert to have been a figure of the future. For, because Jesus Christ was to introduce the second people (which is composed of us nations, lingering deserted in the world aforetime) into the land of promise, "flowing with milk and honey" (that is, into the possession of eternal life, than which nought is sweeter); and this had to come about, not through Moses (that is, not through the Law's discipline), but through Joshua (that is, through the new law's grace), after our circumcision with "a knife of rock" (that is, with Christ's precepts, for Christ is in many ways and figures predicted as a rock ); therefore the man who was being prepared to act as images of this sacrament was inaugurated under the figure of the Lord's name, even so as to be named Jesus. … For Joshua was to introduce the people into the land of promise, not Moses. Now He called him an "angel," on account of the magnitude of the mighty deeds which he was to achieve (which mighty deeds Joshua the son of Nun did, and you yourselves read), and on account of his office of prophet announcing (to wit) the divine will.

— An Answer to the Jews 9. Against Marcion III.16 repeats the passage almost word for word against Marcion, under the heading "Joshua a Type of Him." The argument is Justin's: the renaming, the entry into the land, the knife of stone, and the angel of Exodus 23:20–21 who bears God's name. Jean Daniélou judges that Tertullian's "dependence on St Justin is obvious." The parenthesis "which mighty deeds Joshua the son of Nun did, and you yourselves read" puts the history on common ground with the Jewish reader. Each parenthesis glosses one item of the history ("the land of promise," "a knife of rock") with its fulfilment ("eternal life," "Christ's precepts"). The figure reads the deed. It does not take its place.4

The Jordan stopped "at the bidding of Joshua": the miracles set against Marcion's Christ

Read, too, how that the Jordan was as a sword, to hinder the emigrant nation in their passage across its stream; how that its waters from above stood still, and its current below wholly ceased to run at the bidding of Joshua, when his priests began to pass over! What will you say to this? If it be your Christ that is meant above, he will not be more potent than the servants of the Creator.

— Against Marcion IV.20, on Christ's stilling of the storm (Luke 8:22–25). The Marcionites took the miracle as proof that a new God had arrived. Tertullian's answer is that the elements had always obeyed the Creator's servants: Moses at the Red Sea, and Joshua at the Jordan. The crossing is told in the terms of Joshua 3:16. It is set beside the gospel miracle as the same kind of event. Its force as a precedent depends on its having happened. In IV.13 the twelve apostles are prefigured by, among other things, "the twelve stones appointed by Joshua to be taken out of the Jordan, and set up for the ark of the covenant." These are "stones massive in their faith, which the true Joshua took out of the layer of the Jordan."5

The sun stood still at Gibeon: a precedent for a fast

Think we that Joshua the son of Nun, when warring down the Amorites, had breakfasted on that day on which he ordered the very elements to keep a Station? The sun "stood" in Gibeon, and the moon in Ajalon; the sun and the moon "stood in station until the People was avenged of his enemies, and the sun stood in the mid heaven." When, moreover, (the sun) did draw toward his setting and the end of the one day, there was no such day beforetime and in the latest time (of course, (no day) so long), "that God," says (the writer), "should hear a man"-(a man,) to be sure, the sun's peer, so long persistent in his duty-a Station longer even than late.

— On Fasting 10, a work of Tertullian's Montanist period defending the "Stations," fasts kept until mid-afternoon or later. He cites Moses praying against Amalek "till sunset," Saul's fast in battle, and this. Joshua, "warring down the Amorites," kept the longest day in history without breakfast, a "Station longer even than late." Tertullian is joking, and the joke assumes the event. The day was real, the battle was real, and the sun stood. He quotes Joshua 10:13–14 and stresses that it was one day, and the longest there has been.6

God's severity is justice: the general answer to the charge of cruelty

In this case, you must decide that man, although the wilful contemner of the divine law, unjustly bore the doom which he would like to have escaped; that the wickedness of those days was unjustly smitten by the deluge, afterwards by the fire (of Sodom); that Egypt, although most depraved and superstitious, and, worse still, the harasser of its guest-population, was unjustly stricken with the chastisement of its ten plagues.

— Against Marcion II.14. Tertullian's reply to the Marcionite God of wrath is a distinction between two kinds of evil. The devil is the author of the evils of sin (mala culpae). God is the author of penal evils (mala poenae), which "are, no doubt, evil to those by whom they are endured, but still on their own account good, as being just." The Marcionite must therefore show that the punishment was unjust, not merely that it was severe. "Even His severity then is good, because just: when the judge is good, that is just" (II.16). His list of just judgments takes in the Fall, the Flood, Sodom, the plagues of Egypt, the bears sent against the boys who mocked Elisha, and God's punishment of Israel. The Canaanites are not on it. Tertullian never applies the argument to the ban by name. It is the frame within which he would place it.7

"If it pleases you to sport with the subject": Joshua's wars no warrant for Christians

There is no agreement between the divine and the human sacrament, the standard of Christ and the standard of the devil, the camp of light and the camp of darkness. One soul cannot be due to two masters-God and Caesar. And yet Moses carried a rod, and Aaron wore a buckle, and John (Baptist) is girt with leather and Joshua the son of Nun leads a line of march; and the People warred: if it pleases you to sport with the subject. But how will a Christian man war, nay, how will he serve even in peace, without a sword, which the Lord has taken away? For albeit soldiers had come unto John, and had received the formula of their rule; albeit, likewise, a centurion had believed; still the Lord afterward, in disarming Peter, unbelted every soldier.

— On Idolatry 19, on whether a believer may serve as a soldier. Someone has pointed to Joshua: he "leads a line of march; and the People warred." Tertullian does not deny that Israel fought. He denies that this licenses a Christian to fight, and he treats the appeal as hardly serious. The sword is one "which the Lord has taken away." Israel really warred. The Church may not. The same line between then and now runs through Origen's "we shall not fight as the ancients fought."8

Judges as history: the nations Joshua left, and Israel punished by war

After the death of Joshua the son of Nave they forsake the God of their fathers, and serve idols, Baalim and Ashtaroth; and the Lord in anger delivered them up to the hands of spoilers, and they continued to be spoiled by them, and to be sold to their adversaries, and could not at all stand before their enemies. … "Since, indeed," He says, "this nation have transgressed my covenant which I established with their fathers, and have not hearkened to my voice, I also will give no heed to remove from before them a man of the nations which Joshua left at his death." And thus, throughout almost all the annals of the judges and of the kings who succeeded them, while the strength of the surrounding nations was preserved, He meted wrath out to Israel by war and captivity and a foreign yoke, as often as they turned aside from Him, especially to idolatry.

— Scorpiace 3, a treatise against Gnostics who disparaged martyrdom. Tertullian retells Judges 2 as part of a sequence of Israel's idolatries and their punishments, beginning with the golden calf and Baal-peor. The incomplete conquest ("the nations which Joshua left at his death") is a fact that explains the period after it. The same God who drove the nations out let some remain to punish Israel for idolatry. Tertullian calls the whole span "the annals of the judges and of the kings," and treats it as one continuous history.9

Notes

  • History throughout, typology layered on it. Tertullian cites the conquest as fact in every kind of argument. It is a calendar proof against the Jews, a precedent against Marcion's Christ, a precedent for a fast, and a counter-example on military service. He repeats Justin's Joshua/Jesus typology without changing its basis. He never treats an episode of Joshua as allegory alone.
  • The ban. Tertullian answers the Marcionite charge of a cruel Creator at length, but in general terms: penal evil is justice, and severity is good because just. He never applies this to the Canaanites by name. The moral case against the conquest is made explicit in the later Marcionite antithesis on Joshua's sun and is taken up by Origen and Augustine; see the moral problem of the ḥerem.
  • Christians and war. On Idolatry 19 is the first place on this timeline where Joshua's wars are raised as a possible model for Christians and refused. The refusal does not deny the wars; it confines them to Israel.
  • The material record. What the ground shows of Jericho and the other cities Tertullian names is on the conquest in the material record and the Jericho dating argument.
  • Where this stands on the timeline. Tertullian follows Justin Martyr, whose renaming argument he repeats, and Irenaeus, who had answered the Marcionites in briefer terms. A generation later Origen preached the whole book of Joshua. « Back to the Conquest in Christian Tradition timeline
