Origen of Alexandria on the Conquest of Canaan

Dates: c. 249–251 (the Homilies on Joshua, preached at Caesarea; Against Celsus c. 248; d. c. 253) · Wikipedia
Summary: Origen gave the Church its first full reading of Joshua: twenty-six homilies in which Joshua son of Nun, who in Greek bears the same name as Jesus (Iēsous), becomes a figure of Christ, and the conquest of Canaan becomes the believer's war against sin and the demons. He is often taken to have dissolved the conquest into allegory. The homilies show something else. He sets out the history first and calls it history: the Amorite coalition is "the history of the deeds done," which "has plainly been fulfilled"; the sun's standing still is among the "miracles of divine power, wrought indeed according to the history"; Christians no longer fight "as the ancients fought." He builds the figure on top of the history. He does three things to the letter. He denies that it is enough for a Christian reader. He denies that it gives the Church any warrant for war. And he denies the literal truth of particular details, chiefly the book's sweeping formulas: "all the land" taken, the land "at rest from wars," Ai "uninhabitable forever," the Jebusites in Jerusalem "to this day." These he reads as signals that point to the spiritual sense. On the slaughter itself he never says it did not happen. He does refuse, more sharply than on any other point, to let it stand as an example for Christians.

Claims & evidence

The book is "the mysteries of my Lord Jesus" — "not so much" Joshua's deeds, rather than not at all

To what end, then, do all these things look for us? Surely to this, that this book does not so much set forth for us the deeds of the son of Nave as it depicts for us the mysteries of my Lord Jesus. For it is he who after the death of Moses took up the command, it is he who leads the army and "fights against Amalek"; and what was there foreshadowed on the mountain with hands stretched out, "he fastens to his cross, triumphing over the principalities and powers in himself."

— Homilies on Joshua 1.3.1 This sentence governs the whole series. Its pivot is the shared name: the Septuagint calls Joshua "Jesus son of Nave," and Origen preaches on the assumption that the coincidence was providential. The construction is comparative ("not so much … as"), the Latin non tam … quam. The book's deeds are not denied; they are ranked below what they depict. The homilies survive only in the Latin translation Rufinus made around 400, so the exact wording is his. The method, though, runs through all twenty-six homilies.

The history is stated first, then the figure is built on it

"Every place," he says, "whatsoever you shall put under the treading of your feet, shall be yours." He had said this to those who then existed, concerning the regions of the "Canaanites," the "Perizzites," the "Jebusites," and the other nations, whose regions, when the unworthy inhabitants had been driven out, they themselves took as an inheritance; but let us see what is promised to us in these words.

— Homilies on Joshua 1.6.2 The pattern recurs throughout the series. The promise was made "to those who then existed." The Canaanites were "driven out." Israel "took" the land "as an inheritance." Only after that does the sermon move on to "what is promised to us." The same sequence opens Homily 3, on the two and a half tribes: "This indeed the text of the narrative sets forth; but since we 'have now turned to the Lord' … let us attempt to ascend from the letter to the spirit." Homily 9 frames the Gerizim–Ebal ceremony the same way: "These things indeed the histories of the ancients relate as deeds done; but how shall we refer this narration of the history to a mystical understanding?" The narrative is called history, and the figure is built on top of it.

The miracles of the conquest were "wrought indeed according to the history"

But then Jesus, seeing the divine right hand doing battle alongside him and all things proceeding with prosperous success, lifts up to heaven a certain new and wondrous prayer, saying: "Let the sun stand still over Gibeon, and the moon over the valley of Aijalon, until I overpower this people. And the sun and the moon stood still in their place, until the Lord utterly vanquished their enemies. And the sun stood still in the midst of heaven, and did not advance toward its setting for the space of one whole day. There was no day like this before this, nor did God so hearken to a man, because the Lord fought for Israel. And those five kings fled, and hid themselves in a cave in Makkedah." These miracles of divine power, wrought indeed according to the history, proclaim themselves to all the ages and need no interpretation from outside, in which the light of the deeds flashes forth. Yet let us inquire what spiritual understanding these things may contain.

— Homilies on Joshua 11.1, on the battle at Gibeon.3 This is Origen's plainest statement on the book's most astonishing event. The hailstones and the sun's standing still were done "according to the history," and they are so evident that they "need no interpretation from outside." The spiritual reading, in which the day is lengthened for the Church's mission, comes after this, introduced by "yet." Homily 1 had already set the two side by side: "my Jesus made 'the sun stand still' not only then, but much more so now in his coming" (1.5). Homily 4 treats the parting of the Jordan the same way. It tells the congregation not to "be astonished when these things are reported to you as having been done for the former people," and adds: "do not suppose that these things were done among the men of old, but that in you … nothing of the kind is done" (4.1). The objection Origen argues against is that the miracle happened then and means nothing now. He never argues against the claim that it happened.

The wars were real, "plainly fulfilled," and are no longer the Church's to fight

The history of the deeds done is clear and needs no explanation, since it has plainly been fulfilled; yet from these visible things which have been done, let us contemplate the wars and triumphs which our Lord Jesus the Savior has waged, although we also see these fulfilled visibly in him himself. For the kings of the earth gathered together, the senate, and the people, and the Roman princes, to make war against the name of Jesus and Israel together.

— Homilies on Joshua 9.10, on the Amorite coalition of Joshua 9:1–2.4 The conquest is "visible things which have been done." Its counterpart in his own day, the Roman authorities ranged against the Church, is equally a matter of history. Homily 12, which exists to argue that "the wars which Jesus waged ought to be understood spiritually," states the history before reading it: "Jesus came into that land with the army of the Lord and the leaders of Israel; he storms them all, destroys them, takes possession, and by the merit of his victory receives the kingdoms of those whom he conquered." It then glosses Paul's "our struggle is not against flesh and blood" as "we shall not fight as the ancients fought, nor are battles to be waged by us on earth against men" (12.1). The contrast only makes sense if the ancients did fight on earth against men. The famous opening of Homily 15 assumes the same thing:

Unless these carnal wars bore the figure of spiritual wars, never, I think, would the books of the Jewish histories have been handed down by the Apostles to be read in the churches by the disciples of Christ, who came to teach peace.

— Homilies on Joshua 15.1.5 "These carnal wars" (bella ista carnalia; Bruce renders it "those physical wars") are the subject of the sentence, not something it denies. The question it settles is why a church that preaches peace still reads a book of wars aloud, not whether the wars took place. The answer Origen gives is about edification. Without the figure, the history would have no place in Christian worship. That does not make the history false.

Even the slaughter is a thing "done" — read as signifying, not denied

But meanwhile Jesus slew the enemies, not teaching cruelty thereby, as the heretics suppose, but designating in the things that are done the mysteries that are to come, so that, when he has slain those kings who hold the kingdom of sin in us, we may be able to fulfill that which the Apostle said: "as we presented our members to serve iniquity unto iniquity, so now let us present our members to serve righteousness unto sanctification."

— Homilies on Joshua 11.6, on the five kings of Joshua 10.6 The heretics he has in mind are the Marcionites, who took the conquest as proof that the God of the Old Testament was a lower and crueler god. Origen's reply is not that the killing never happened. He says that "Jesus slew the enemies," and that what he did signifies something. The "things that are done" carry "the mysteries that are to come." Homily 13 puts it the same way. The Lord Jesus now accomplishes "in truth … through each and every soul of believers what was first foreshadowed by the son of Nave through each and every city" (13.1). On this model the event is the shadow and its spiritual meaning the substance, and a shadow needs an event to cast it. Origen calls the spiritual reading the "better" way to understand the Israelite wars (13.3), not the only true one.

Where the letter "cannot be true": the totality and perpetuity formulas

But it is said in what follows that "Jesus took all the land, as the Lord commanded Moses." That "Jesus," the son of Nave, I do not see that he "took all the land." For how much "land did he take," who held Judaea alone? But our Lord Jesus himself truly "took all the land" in this, that from "all the earth" and from all the nations a multitude of believers ran together to him. It is also added after these things, after "Jesus took all the land": "and the land rested from wars," it says. How will this seem to be truly said of the son of Nave, that "the land ceased from wars," which almost never in his times "ceased from wars"?

— Homilies on Joshua 15.7.7 This is where Origen does reject the letter, and it matters which letter he rejects. He denies that the summary formula "took all the land" is literally true of Joshua. His reason is a historical one: Joshua "held Judaea alone," and his times "almost never" knew rest from war. He uses the history to correct the hyperbole. The other places where he says a statement "cannot be true" literally are of the same kind. They are formulas of totality or perpetuity, or physical details he finds impracticable:

  • the Jebusites dwelling with Judah in Jerusalem "down to the present day," which "cannot be true," Origen argues, since in his day "not even the 'sons of Judah' themselves 'dwell in Jerusalem'" (21.1; compare Rahab joined to Israel "unto this present day," 7.5);
  • Ai burned and made "a wasteland, that it should not be inhabited forever," which "pertain[s] more to the truth of the mystery than of the history" (8.5);
  • Joshua writing the whole of Deuteronomy on the altar stones while all Israel stood by: "How is the truth of the history demonstrated in this?" (9.4). Even here he grants, a section earlier, "however the son of Nave was then able to inscribe 'the law upon the stones of the altar'" (9.3);
  • the horses hamstrung after "none was left who breathed," a detail he finds pointless "to those who wish these things to be understood only according to the letter" (15.2);
  • "great Sidon." Origen reports that, "since I have several times lingered in the regions of Sidon," he has "never found that there are two Sidons, one great and another small, as far as pertains to the earthly place" (14.2). He checks the text's geography against what he has seen for himself before turning to the meaning of the name.

In every case the argument works on a detail, not on the campaign. These are what On First Principles calls the "stumbling-blocks" the Spirit placed in the narrative to send the reader toward its deeper sense (see below).8

The hardest case: the ban at Ai, and "We left not anything alive"

But let us see what is also written in what follows: "and they struck them," he says, "with the edge of the sword, until there was not left of them one who might be saved, nor who might escape." When the Jews read these things, they become cruel and thirst for human blood, thinking that the saints too struck those who inhabited Ai, so that "there should not be left of them one who might be saved, nor who might escape," not understanding that in these words mysteries are foreshadowed, and that this is rather indicated to us, that of these whose dwelling is chaos and who reigned in the abyss, of the demons, we ought to leave none at all, but to slay them all.

— Homilies on Joshua 8.7.9 This passage comes closest to setting aside the literal extermination. The fault Origen finds in his Jewish contemporaries is that they think "the saints too struck" Ai's people in this way, and that the reading makes them "cruel." The point he presses is what the reader takes from the text. The ban at Ai is read as a model for present killing, and the remedy is a reading that aims the sword at the demons. He does not add that no one died at Ai. Earlier in the same homily he tells the battle without reservation: the feigned flight, the ambush, the city "left empty," "those who were last and these who were first slay all the enemy enclosed in the middle" (8.1). His hearers' complaint, "what does it profit me if I come to know that those who inhabited Ai were conquered?" (8.2), assumes that there is something to know. Against Celsus goes furthest. Answering Celsus's taunt that the Jews were promised they "should slay their enemies," Origen writes:

As to the promise made to the Jews that they should slay their enemies, it may be answered that any one who examines carefully into the meaning of this passage will find himself unable to interpret it literally. … And there are many similar expressions to be found in the law, as this, for example: "We left not anything alive."

— Against Celsus 7.19, trans. Frederick Crombie (ANF 4), quoting Deuteronomy 2:34.10 The context is the law's promises: riches, dominion, victory, which Celsus reads as plain material rewards and Origen as figures of spiritual goods. The extermination formula of the wars against Sihon and Og is put among them. Origen is denying that the promise is only literal. He does not say the Transjordan campaign never happened, but here he goes furthest in reading a ban-formula as a figure.

The ancient wars were a real, and now superseded, dispensation

For Christians could not slay their enemies, or condemn to be burned or stoned, as Moses commands, those who had broken the law, and were therefore condemned as deserving of these punishments; since the Jews themselves, however desirous of carrying out their law, are not able to inflict these. punishments. But in the case of the ancient Jews, who had a land and a form of government of their own, to take from them the right of making war upon their enemies, of fighting for their country, of putting to death or otherwise punishing adulterers, murderers, or others who were guilty of similar crimes, would be to subject them to sudden and utter destruction whenever the enemy fell upon them; for their very laws would in that case restrain them, and prevent them from resisting the enemy.

— Against Celsus 7.26.11 This passage holds the two halves of Origen's position together. Israel's wars belong to a real past: a nation with "a land and a form of government of their own" needed "the right of making war upon their enemies," and providence granted it. The Gospel has ended that dispensation, and the same providence "has destroyed their city and their temple." What Origen rejects is the war-ethic as a pattern for Christians. He does not reject the record of the wars. Read with the homilies, this is the likeliest sense of "these carnal wars" in 15.1. They were real wars in a real state, and they are kept in the churches only for what they figure.

The rule behind it: most of Scripture's history is literally true, and no Joshua event is listed as unhistorical

But that no one may suppose that we assert respecting the whole that no history is real because a certain one is not; and that no law is to be literally observed, because a certain one, (understood) according to the letter, is absurd or impossible; or that the statements regarding the Saviour are not true in a manner perceptible to the senses; or that no commandment and precept of His ought to be obeyed;—we have to answer that, with regard to certain things, it is perfectly clear to us that the historical account is true; as that Abraham was buried in the double cave at Hebron, as also Isaac and Jacob, and the wives of each of them; and that Shechem was given as a portion to Joseph; and that Jerusalem is the metropolis of Judea, in which the temple of God was built by Solomon; and innumerable other statements. For the passages that are true in their historical meaning are much more numerous than those which are interspersed with a purely spiritual signification.

— On First Principles (De Principiis) IV.1.19, from the Greek preserved in the Philocalia (IV.3.4 in the Koetschau/Butterworth numbering).12 This is Origen's own theory of the "stumbling-blocks." Where the history could not carry the spiritual sense, Scripture "interwove in the history (the account of) some event that did not take place, sometimes what could not have happened" (IV.1.15). Origen then lists his examples: the first three days without a sun, God planting a tree of life "visible and palpable," God walking in the garden, Cain going out from God's presence, the devil showing Jesus all the kingdoms of the world from one mountain, and laws such as the goat-stag and the Sabbath rest that cannot be kept (IV.1.16–17). No event of the conquest is on the list. Joshua appears in On First Principles IV only as a type of the Saviour and as the book that catalogues the cities of Judah. The homilies' "cannot be true" verdicts fit the theory exactly. They fall on details and formulas, the "few words … interpolated which are not true in their literal acceptation," never on the crossing, the fall of Jericho, the battle at Gibeon, or the wars as a whole.

Notes

  • On whether Origen's allegory denied the conquest's history: the evidence above favours a qualified "no." He presupposes the conquest throughout. He explicitly affirms the miracles (11.1), the Amorite war (9.10), the driving-out of the Canaanites (1.6) and the ancient right of war (Against Celsus 7.26). He denies three things: that the letter is sufficient, that it is normative, and that certain formulas and details are literally true. The evidence is not all one way. Hom. 8.7 and Against Celsus 7.19 come close to setting aside the literal extermination-formula, and a modern reader of the homilies has fairly observed that Origen "seems to say different things in different places about whether the violence depicted in that text actually happened or not."13
  • The moral question the conquest raises (whether Origen's spiritualizing, or Augustine's divine-command defense, answers the problem of the ḥerem) is weighed separately at Do the patristic defenses answer the moral problem of the ḥerem? (assessment: contested). On the evidence above, Origen's answer leaves the history standing. What he declines is to let the slaughter serve the Church as a lesson in killing.
  • The same pattern holds for the Flood. In Origen on the Historicity of Noah's Flood the arch-allegorist defends the ark as a real vessel against Celsus and builds his allegory on that history. See also Was patristic allegory of the Flood a denial of its history?
  • Augustine's handling of the same book is set out in Augustine of Hippo on the Conquest of Canaan. He keeps the typology but argues the history and the ethics of the wars head-on.
  • The homilies reached the medieval West in Rufinus's Latin, re-preached and cut down. Caesarius of Arles built three of his four sermons on Joshua from them, often sentence for sentence, and added a literal answer to the moral objection that Origen did not give. Isidore of Seville made them the backbone of the Joshua chapters of his Questions on the Old Testament, and Cassiodorus passed them on to his monks at Vivarium.
