Bede on the Conquest of Canaan

Dates: c. 725 (The Reckoning of Time, 725, whose chapter 66 is his world chronicle; the Commentary on Acts, c. 709, and its Retractation, after 725; the allegorical commentary on 1 Samuel, Books 1–3 finished before 716; Bede b. c. 673, monk of Wearmouth–Jarrow, d. 735) · Wikipedia
Summary: Bede, the Northumbrian monk who wrote the Ecclesiastical History of the English People, wrote no commentary on Joshua, but he handled the conquest in two ways that seldom meet in one writer. As a chronologer he placed it in the years of the world. In his world chronicle Joshua leads Israel over "the bed of the Jordan laid open" in the year of the world 2493 and rules twenty-six years. Bede notes that "holy Scripture is silent" on the length of Joshua's rule. He weighs Josephus against Eusebius on it, and he corrects his own earlier figure, because nothing must make the 480 years of 1 Kings 6:1 come out wrong: "it is not to be supposed that there was an error in the writings of Holy Scripture." As an exegete he reads the same events as figures of Christ, in the line of Origen and Augustine. Joshua circumcising with knives of stone is grace completing the law, and the rebuilding of Jericho stands for a monk going back to his sins. He grants that Rahab lied, and he holds that she "could not fulfill the merit of justification" without the lie. He never raises the moral problem of the ban. Where Scripture commands that infants be killed (1 Samuel 15:3), he reads the infants as the first stirrings of sin.

Claims & evidence

The conquest in the years of the world: "holy Scripture is silent"

Joshua rules the people of Israel 26 years, as Josephus teaches; for holy Scripture is silent on how many the years of his leadership were. Why Eusebius put 27 in his chronicles we shall say below. In the first year of his leadership, in the first month, on the tenth day of the month, Joshua led the people into the land of promise, the bed of the Jordan having been laid open. In that year, as we have found in the chronicles of the aforesaid Eusebius, the fifty-first jubilee according to the Hebrews was beginning, that is, 2,500 years from the beginning of the world were complete, each order of jubilees being reckoned at fifty years. But our inquiry has been quite unable to find that this is the sum of this period. For it is agreed that there were 1,656 years up to the Flood; from there 292 to Abraham, who was 75 years old when he received the promise of God; the years of the promise, 430; of the leadership of Moses, 40. This number makes, not 2,500, but seven fewer, that is, 2,493 years, as we noted above.

— The Reckoning of Time (De temporum ratione) 66, entry for the year of the world 2519, translated here from Mommsen's Latin.1 Chapter 66 of Bede's handbook on the calendar is a chronicle of the six ages of the world, with each entry set against the years since Creation. Joshua's entry is dated by the year of his death, and its account begins with the crossing. Bede dates the crossing to the day given in Joshua 4:19. He tells the miracle in a subordinate clause, "the bed of the Jordan having been laid open" (patefacto Iordanis alveo), as a fact that needs no argument. What he argues over is the arithmetic. Eusebius had put the entry in the 2,500th year of the world, at the start of a jubilee. Bede adds up the figures of Genesis and Exodus himself and gets seven years fewer. A later entry in the same chronicle explains the 27 years. The Septuagint leaves out the judge Elon and his ten years, so Eusebius, to keep the total of 480 years "which Scripture proclaims," gave "Joshua son of Nun, Samuel and Saul, whose years Scripture does not state, more years than he read in Josephus." The figure of Joshua as Jesus does not enter the chronicle at all.

Weighing Josephus, Eusebius and the 480 years: "not to be supposed that there was an error"

Therefore, recount the times of the Judges, and you will find that Othniel ruled the people for forty years, Ehud for eighty, Deborah for forty, Gideon for forty, Abimelech for three, Tola for twenty-three, Jair for twenty-two, Jephthah for six, Ibzan for seven, Elon for ten, who is not found in the Septuagint: Abdon for eight, Samson for twenty, Eli for forty, which together make three hundred thirty-nine years. To these, add forty years of the journey from Egypt, the same for David's reign, four for Solomon's, making four hundred twenty-three years: evidently fifty-seven years less than four hundred eighty, which must be accounted for in the times of Joshua, Samuel, and Saul; but how we should divide them, the Scripture does not teach. … Therefore, to open some way for inquiry, let us turn to the writings of Josephus, who is easily found more learned in such matters than anyone else besides the divine scriptures. And we will find in his history of Antiquities that Joshua ruled for twenty-six years, Samuel for twelve, and Saul for twenty over the people; these make fifty-eight years together. Nor should we be troubled by the one year that exceeds the four hundred eighty. For either Scripture, following its usual manner, placing the full sum, did not care to add the one year that exceeded; or, what is more believable, the writing of Josephus, through the carelessness of long age, added one year somewhere, as often happens. For it is not to be supposed that there was an error in the writings of Holy Scripture … Why should we not be allowed to subtract at least one year from that count, especially since we are concluded by similar necessity, and give twenty-five years to Joshua, and thirty-two to Samuel and Saul? Lest by believing historians more than proper, we seem to violate Scripture: which absolutely asserts the four hundred and eightieth year of the exodus from Egypt to be the fourth year of Solomon.

— Allegorical Exposition of Samuel (In primam partem Samuhelis), preface to Book 2, in the English made from Migne's Latin given at historicalchristian.faith.2 Bede says at the start of this commentary that he means to read Samuel allegorically, yet he opens its second book with a page of plain chronology. The years of Joshua, Samuel and Saul are a real quantity to be solved for. Josephus is the best witness "besides the divine scriptures," and the chronicles are faulted for neglecting him. Scripture sets the limit, and a historian must give way to it: Bede will cut a year from Josephus rather than "violate Scripture." Here he gives Joshua twenty-five years. In The Reckoning of Time he gave him Josephus's twenty-six. In his Retractation on Acts he went back to the question and explained why Eusebius had departed from Josephus:

But also, having more carefully reread the books of Chronicles, I noticed why Eusebius did not want to follow the authority of Josephus in this place, just as he did not in the years of Joshua, to whom Josephus attributes twenty-six years, while he thought it better to annotate twenty-seven. This, therefore, was the reason: because if he followed him, he could by no means have the prescribed number of four hundred and eighty years from the exodus of Israel from Egypt until the temple began to be built, but would find in his codices ten years less of the same age. This he endured because, following the Septuagint interpreters, he neglected to place Ahialon the judge, who ruled the people for ten years after Abessa, in his Chronicles. … Thus, he adds one year to the times of Joshua, eight to the times of Samuel and Saul, and to complete the tenth number, he includes the fourth year of Solomon's reign, in which the temple began to be built: which whoever follows the Hebrew truth finds should not be done.

— Retractation on the Acts of the Apostles, on Acts 13:21.3 Eusebius's twenty-seven years had passed into the Latin tradition through Jerome's translation of the Chronicle and through Augustine, whose City of God gives Joshua the same figure. Bede traced it to its source, a scribal gap in the Greek text of Judges, and preferred the Hebrew.

Five years of war, then the lot

He distributed their land to them by lot, as it were after four hundred and fifty years. God had said to Abraham that his seed would be a stranger in a foreign land for four hundred years. And again he said: "In Isaac shall your seed be called." From the birth of the seed until the exodus of Israel from Egypt, there were (as Exodus writes) four hundred and five years. Add to these the forty years in the desert, and five in the land of Canaan, during which it seems that the land rested from battles, and the lot was cast, and you will find four hundred and fifty years.

— Commentary on the Acts of the Apostles, on Acts 13:19.4 Paul's sermon at Pisidian Antioch says that God "destroyed seven nations in the land of Chanaan" and "divided their land to them by lot." To make Paul's 450 years come out, Bede counts five years in Canaan before the land "rested from battles" and the lots were cast. He does not say where the five years come from. Josephus had the war over in "the fifth year," and Theodoret worked out seven from Caleb's age. When Bede returned to the verse in his Retractation, he noted that the Greek text joins the 450 years to the judges instead. He confessed that how this agrees with 1 Kings "is not within our capacity to explain," unless Paul "followed the popular saying in speaking."

The figure laid on the history: Joshua's knives of stone, "not done in vain"

But because the discussion is about circumcision, it is pleasing to inquire why Moses himself … did not wish anyone to be circumcised during the entire time of his leadership, except only one, his own son … and left all who were born in the wilderness to be circumcised by Joshua; evidently, a divinely commanded custom, observed for four hundred and six years and handed down by ancestral succession, he discontinued for forty continuous years. I would not believe this was done in vain, but rather with great mystery. About this, preserving the understanding of the elders, I will briefly say what I think. Moses preaches circumcision, but Joshua fulfills it, because the law was given through Moses, but grace and truth through Jesus Christ (John 1). … But Joshua, having led the people into the promised land, cuts off with stone knives the foreskin that had grown while Moses was alive. Because where sin abounded, grace abounded much more (Rom. 5). For anyone who reads understands why Joshua uses stone knives for circumcision, because the rock was Christ (1 Cor. 10) …

— Commentary on Luke, on Luke 2:21.5 The order of Bede's reasoning is the order the Fathers had set. First come the facts: a custom kept for 406 years and then suspended for forty. Then comes the question of why, and the answer is a mystery, "not done in vain." The mystery is Origen's and Augustine's. Moses is the law, Joshua is Jesus, and the stone is Christ. He treats the end of the manna (Joshua 5:10–12) in the same way, on Luke 22:15:

For, when Moses died, Joshua restored the people whom he had provided with manna for a time across the Jordan … Thereafter, he crossed the Jordan, circumcised with knives made of stone … In fact, Joshua was ordained leader when Moses died because Christ was incarnated when the law had been corrupted by the traditions of the Pharisees. Joshua fed with and was fed by manna across the Jordan because, until the time of his baptism, the Lord observed the ceremonies of the law and wanted them to be observed by everyone else.

— Commentary on Luke 6, on Luke 22:15.6 On Ezra the shared name carries the figure: "the son of Nun clearly signifies the Lord Christ Jesus. Hence each led the people into the promised land, that one from a long stay in the wilderness, this one from the longer captivity, in a figure of the true Jesus." The two Joshuas are history, and Christ is the figure they share.

Jericho's curse fulfilled to the letter, then read of the backsliding monk

It is said of the time when Ahab reigned, "In his days Hiel of Bethel built Jericho; he laid its foundations in Abiram, his firstborn; and he set up its gates in Segub, his youngest son." The apparent sense is that when the above-mentioned city's builder began to lay its foundations, his firstborn, named Abiram, died; and that after the city had been built, when he tried to fortify its gates, he lost his youngest son, named Segub. Joshua predicted that this would happen when, after Jericho's destruction he made it anathema by cursing it … Because Hiel is translated as "living for God" and Bethel as "house of God," Hiel of Bethel restores Jericho's walls (which Joshua had destroyed and cursed) whenever any who have taken up the religious life in the church resume doing the evil deeds for which the Lord Jesus forgave them on the day of [their] baptism and whenever they who have renounced the devil's pomp return to it by wanton living or prefer false doctrines or Gentile fables to the church's truth in which they were instructed.

— Thirty Questions on the Book of Kings 16, trans. W. Trent Foley and Arthur G. Holder.7 Bede wrote the Questions for Nothelm, a priest of London, who had sent him puzzles about the books of Kings. His first answer is the plain one: Joshua's curse (Joshua 6:26) came true in Ahab's reign, son by son. Only then comes the figure. Jericho is the life of sin that a Christian renounced at baptism, and to rebuild its walls is to go back to it. In On the Tabernacle he makes a similar move with Shiloh. When Joshua brought the tabernacle "into the land of promise" and set it up there, it had the Mediterranean to its west, as at Sinai it had had the Red Sea, "mystically, therefore," a sign that the saints look out on the passing sea of this world.

The land given, and the enemies who were never cleared

This was also to illustrate that the land of Canaan was promised to Abraham and his seed in such a way that it signified more the inheritance of the heavenly fatherland … Therefore, the land of Canaan is promised to Abraham and his seed, and after the long toil of Egyptian servitude, it is restored by Joshua as a leader, to mystically suggest that the heavenly fatherland promised to us will be restored through Jesus Christ our Lord after the affliction of present exile. But even in that land of Canaan, the children of the promise never ceased laboring or fighting against enemies, so they would understand, as we have said, that they should seek another fatherland beyond this one, where they will truly enjoy heavenly blessing and eternal rest.

— Commentary on Genesis (Hexaemeron), on Genesis 12:10.8 Bede never takes up the book's formulas that Joshua "took all the land," and he never asks why Canaanites were still in the land under the judges. Here he makes a lesson of the unfinished settlement. Israel was always fighting in Canaan, and the endless war showed that Canaan was not the true homeland. It is the reading of Hebrews 4:8–9, the rest Joshua gave that was not the final rest. He takes the land's later history as fact. Noah's curse that Canaan should serve Japheth (Genesis 9:27) was fulfilled "when the Greeks or Romans … possessed the kingdoms of Asia … and among other things made the Canaanites tributary to themselves." On Genesis 15:18 he reads the gift of Canaan "to possess forever" as a covenant with conditions: Abraham's offspring would serve God "with a faithful heart; and the Lord in turn would grant him and his descendants the land."

Rahab's lie: "she could not fulfill the merit of justification" without it

But if anyone desires to know about the necessity of lies or the constancy of a just man possibly requiring such a necessity, let him read the book of Saint Augustine on the eight kinds of lies, wonderfully and healthily moderated; and also the conferences of the Fathers, where Joseph, a distinguished figure amongst the Fathers, disputed excellently about not defining anything, also fully addressing this minor question. I have proposed only that this is enough to say briefly for the present. While truth should always be preferred to a lie without any doubt, sometimes, due to the circumstances, a lie might be usefully resorted to for a time, and the truth healthily hidden. Finally, did not Rahab the harlot, justified by works, receive the messengers and send them out another way? But she could not fulfill the merit of justification without temporarily assuming the aid of a lie.

— Allegorical Exposition of Samuel 3, on 1 Samuel 21:2.9 The question is David's story to the priest at Nob, which Bede first defends as truth "concealed … in more cautious words." He then takes up lying in general, and Rahab is his test case. Rahab's lie to the king of Jericho (Joshua 2:4–5) was a lie, and Bede does not explain it away. He goes further than Augustine, whom he cites. Augustine praised Rahab's kindness but not her lie. Bede makes the lie the means without which her justifying work could not have been done, and he adds that such a lie may be "usefully resorted to for a time." He also points to the Conferences of John Cassian, where Abba Joseph had allowed a lie in necessity. Calvin, nine centuries later, would call the same lie a fault "not imputed." In Bede's Commentary on the Catholic Epistles, on James 2:25, Rahab is the foreign sinner justified by the works of mercy and hospitality, "even at the risk of her life."

The ban read as a war on vices: "the infant and the suckling"

Do not spare them, but kill, etc. From man to woman, from the perfected work of sin to carnal thought, namely the filthy mother and nurse of wicked offspring. The infant and the suckling, the very beginnings and purpose of nefarious action, which a recent evil thought desires to nourish even worse; understand, the ox, the sheep, the camel, and the donkey, symbolize dullness of folly, laziness of sloth, the baseness of pride, and the wantonness of luxury.

— Allegorical Exposition of Samuel 2, on 1 Samuel 15:3.10 The war on Amalek is not Joshua's, but it is the plainest command of the ban in Scripture: "slay both man and woman, infant and suckling." Bede does not comment on its justice. He reads each class of victim as a degree of sin, from the finished deed to the first thought. On 1 Samuel 15:29 he takes the history as given. Saul is "the less excusable … in preserving the anathema" because his victory came "from heaven," and "the war against Amalek is the Lord's alone." Then he turns the point inward: "we endanger ourselves the more by sparing our enemies, that is, by indulging in vices." The ban at Jericho itself appears only once in the works surveyed here, as a point of grammar. In On the Temple he notes that "the children of Israel have transgressed … and have taken of the accursed thing" (Joshua 7:1) puts "the plural for the singular," "since Achan alone, and not several of the children of Israel, did this." He does not discuss the stoning of Achan's household.

The miracles: told as events, never argued

Bede never defends the miracles of the conquest; he assumes them. The parted Jordan is a clause in a dated chronicle entry. On Mark 2:27 Joshua's march is a precedent in law: "the Sabbath is commanded to be observed in such a way that if there is necessity, one is not guilty of breaking the Sabbath … For Joshua also circled the walls of Jericho with his army for seven days." On Ezra he counts "the book of Jashar" (Joshua 10:13), the source Joshua cites for the long day, among the real books of Israel "of which now only the memory of the name remains." He nowhere discusses the sun's standing still at Gibeon in the works surveyed here. His commentary on the song of Habakkuk reads "the moon stood in its place" (Habakkuk 3:11) of the Church made steady in faith, with no word of Joshua.11

Notes

  • Chronologer and allegorist. Of the witnesses on this timeline, Bede argues longest over the length of Joshua's rule, setting Josephus against Eusebius and the Septuagint against the Hebrew. Eusebius had synchronized Joshua with the kings of Argos and Athens and given him twenty-seven years, and Augustine kept the figure. Bede took twenty-six from Josephus in The Reckoning of Time, and twenty-five in the Samuel commentary to keep 1 Kings 6:1 exact. Compare Eusebius of Caesarea, Augustine of Hippo and Josephus. The same Bede reads Joshua as Jesus and Jericho as the life of sin, after Origen and Augustine. In his works the two readings never compete. The figure needs the event, and the chronicle records the event without the figure.
  • The moral question. Bede does not answer the objection to the ban, because he never raises it. Where he meets the ban, he takes the history as God's command ("the war against Amalek is the Lord's alone") and gives the moral sense to the war against vice. This is Ambrose's and Origen's line, without Caesarius's literal defence. The patristic answers are weighed at Do the patristic defenses answer the moral problem of the ḥerem?
  • Rahab. Bede's view of Rahab's lie is unusually lenient for the Latin tradition. See the Rahab timeline and Bede on Rahab, and compare Calvin.
  • Translations. The Reckoning of Time 66 is translated here from Mommsen's critical Latin; Faith Wallis's English is the standard published translation. The Questions on Kings and the Luke 22 passage are quoted in published translations (Foley and Holder; the Ancient Christian Commentary). The other commentaries are quoted in the English made from Migne's Latin given at historicalchristian.faith. The key sentences on Joshua's years, the five years of war and Rahab's lie are checked against Migne's Latin (PL 91–92).
  • Bede appears on this wiki also as a witness on the Rahab, curse of Ham, Flood, Nephilim, Goliath's height and Naaman timelines, among others.
