Nicholas of Lyra on the Conquest of Canaan

Dates: c. 1330 (the Postilla litteralis on the whole Bible, c. 1322/23–1331; the Postilla moralis, 1333–1339; Nicholas b. c. 1270, Franciscan master of theology at Paris, d. 1349) · Wikipedia
Summary: Nicholas of Lyra, the Franciscan whose Literal Postill became the standard literal commentary of the later Middle Ages, read Joshua as the history its author left. "Joshua wrote this book," near the end of his life. He kept the figure of Christ for a separate Moral Postill. He draws steadily on the rabbis, chiefly Rashi ("Rabbi Solomon"). From them he takes Rahab the "innkeeper," the spies who played deaf, and the identification of the "book of the just" with Genesis. He also weighs them, and rejects one of their stories as "made up." He treats the miracles as real and "against nature." He holds that Joshua's long day was the longest ever, "because it is good to uphold the words of Scripture as far as possible." Paul of Burgos, a converted rabbi who annotated him a century later, disagreed with him over Hezekiah's day, which Joshua's long day was measured against. Lyra reads the sweeping formulas as "a common way of speaking," hyperbole for "the greater part." He gives three reasons why God left part of the land unconquered. He defends the ban outright. The Canaanites were to be destroyed "by God's just judgment, for their iniquities," and so that they would not draw Israel into idolatry. Peace could be offered to cities outside the land, never inside it. Here he follows Rashi, and the rabbinic tradition that Joshua offered peace before he attacked does not appear. But he holds that the sentence fell on the "obstinate," so the Gibeonites, who came "from faith in the one true God," "earned clemency." The oath Israel swore to them had to be kept, because "God is readier to show mercy than to condemn."

Claims & evidence

"Joshua wrote this book": the literal sense and the moral

Dicunt quod hoc refertur ad tempus in quo Iosue scripsit librum istum, quia post victoriam praedictam vixit longo tempore et librum istum scripsit circa finem vitae suae.

— Postilla litteralis on Joshua 10:14, "there was no day so long before or after" (Venice 1603, II, col. 80; the printer's abbreviations are expanded in all the Latin on this page). In English: "They say that this refers to the time in which Joshua wrote this book, for after the victory he lived a long time, and wrote this book near the end of his life."1 Lyra treats the book as written by Joshua and takes the problems that follow from that seriously. At Joshua 7:2 the text calls a place Beth-aven, "house of idols," and Lyra notes the difficulty: "Joshua wrote this book," but the name seems to come from Jeroboam's calves, some five hundred years later. One answer is that "Joshua foresaw it in the spirit, and so named it in this book by anticipation." Lyra calls that "a very great anticipation" and prefers another explanation, that the name came from the idols of the Canaanites. Where a historical reading is available, he takes it before he takes a prophetic one.

The figure of Christ is kept, but in a separate commentary. The Postilla moralis, written after the literal postill was finished, opens Joshua with Jerome:

Allegorice per Iosue Christus designatur, quia sicut dicit Hieronymus in epistola ad Paulinum de omnibus sacrae scripturae libris: Iosue non solum in gestis, sed etiam in nomine figuram gerit salvatoris.

— Postilla moralis on Joshua 1:1 (II, col. 9). In English: "Allegorically, Christ is signified by Joshua; for, as Jerome says in the letter to Paulinus on all the books of sacred Scripture, Joshua bears the figure of the Saviour not only in his deeds but also in his name." The two senses are kept apart: the literal postill gives the history first, and the moral postill reads that history as a figure afterwards. At Gibeon, for example, the sun's help "signifies the aid of Christ" and the moon's "the aid of the Virgin Mary." The figure is built on the history and is not used to explain it away.

The miracles: "against nature"

Quod autem hic interponitur de inundatione Iordanis, hoc fuit ad declarationem maioris miraculi in siccatione alvei Iordanis… Steterunt aquae descendentes: Contra naturam, cum sint fluidae.

— on Joshua 3:15–16 (II, col. 27). In English: "What is put in here about the flooding of the Jordan was to show the greater miracle in the drying of the Jordan's bed… 'The waters coming down stood': against nature, since they are fluid."2 Like Comestor before him, Lyra takes the flood season as a reason the crossing was more of a miracle, not less. On Jericho he writes that the city "had been overthrown by a miracle," and that Joshua cursed whoever would rebuild it "for the memory of this deed and the glory of God." "The Hebrews say" the seventh day of the march was a sabbath. Israel "did not sin," he says, "because they did this at the command of God, who is lord of the sabbath."

The long day gets the most attention. Joshua had just seen hail from "the airy heaven," and so "trusting in this, he asked a second sign from the starry heaven." Sun and moon were both above the horizon, "as often happens at the full moon," and Joshua asked for the day "so that he would have time to pursue the enemy." A formal question follows: which day was longer, Joshua's or Hezekiah's, when the shadow went back on the dial of Ahaz (2 Kings 20)? Lyra works through artificial and natural hours and cites Dionysius's letter to Polycarp, and then decides by the text:

Sed quia bonum est sustinere dicta sacrae scripturae quantum est possibile, quae absolute dicit hic, quod non fuit tam longa dies ante neque post, ideo … potest dici quod dies sub Iosue fuit longior…

— question on Joshua 10:14 (II, cols. 79–80). In English: "But because it is good to uphold the words of sacred Scripture as far as possible, and here it says absolutely that there was no day so long before or after, therefore … it can be said that the day under Joshua was longer…" A century later the 1603 edition's annotators carried the argument on. Paul of Burgos, a rabbi who became bishop of Burgos, held in his Additio that "in the literal sense it can probably be said, saving the authority of the saints," that Hezekiah's day "was neither longer nor shorter than usual, and so the whole dispute of the Postillator in this chapter falls away." The Saxon Franciscan Matthias Döring answered in his Replica that Burgos "imagines strange things without reason, against the authority of the saints." No one in the exchange doubts that the sun stood still at Gibeon. What they dispute is the astronomy of Hezekiah's sign, and whether the text's "no day like it" holds against it.

The Hebrews' readings: Rashi brought in and weighed

Lyra read Hebrew and knew Rashi, whom he cites as "Rabbi Solomon," and he brings rabbinic exegesis into the Christian commentary on Joshua verse by verse. The clearest case is the "book of the just" (liber iustorum, the Book of Jasher) cited at Joshua 10:13. He sets aside the view of "some of our doctors" that the book never reached the Latins. If that were so, it would still exist among the Hebrews, "which their expositors do not say." He also sets aside the view that it is the Twelve Prophets, because Habakkuk 3 ("sun and moon stood still") was written "after the transmigration to Babylon," more than eight hundred years after Joshua, "as will be plain if anyone cares to reckon the times of the judges and kings as they are contained in sacred Scripture and Josephus." Then:

Et ideo aliter dicunt Hebraei, quod iste liber est liber Genesis, quia ibi scribuntur facta Abraham, Isaac, et Iacob, qui fuerunt excellentes iusti… Dicunt igitur quod hoc scriptum est Genes. 48 … de Ephraim dicitur: Et semen eius crescet in gentes. Hebraei dicunt: Et semen eius congregabit gentes…

— on Joshua 10:13 (II, cols. 73–74). In English: "And therefore the Hebrews say otherwise, that this book is the book of Genesis, for there are written the deeds of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, who were outstanding just men… They say, then, that this is written in Genesis 48 … where it is said of Ephraim, 'And his seed shall grow into nations'; the Hebrews read, 'And his seed shall gather the nations'…" Joshua, of Ephraim's line, fulfilled it when the nations were gathered to hear him command the sun. This is Rashi's explanation, which goes back to the Talmud (Avodah Zarah 25a).3

There are other borrowings. "The Hebrews say" the spies passed through Jericho pretending to be deaf and dumb. "Rabbi Solomon" says they were sent before the thirty days of mourning for Moses had ended. The Jebusites whom Judah could not drive out (Joshua 15:63) were, "the Hebrews" say, descendants of Abimelech of Gerar, protected by Abraham's oath until its term ran out and David could take the city. Lyra does not take the Hebrews on trust, though. At Joshua 7:24 "the Hebrews say" Achan's sons were brought out only to watch their father punished. "Our doctors say they were brought out to die with their father, and this seems more reasonable." At 7:14 he reports Rashi's story that the guilty tribe was found because its stone dimmed on the high priest's breastplate. He rejects it as "made up" (fictitium), because the text says the lot was cast.

Rahab: an "innkeeper," and a helpful lie

Et ideo translatio Chaldaica ubi nos habemus Mulieris meretricis, habet Mulieris hospitalariae, et sic dicunt et Hebraei. Nomen enim Hebraicum Zonah … aequivocum est, ad designandum meretricem, et venditricem victualium, quod est proprie hospitalarium.

— on Joshua 2:1 (II, col. 18). In English: "And therefore the Chaldean translation, where we have 'of a harlot woman,' has 'of an innkeeper woman,' and so too the Hebrews say. For the Hebrew word zonah … is ambiguous, signifying a harlot and a seller of provisions, which is properly an innkeeper."4 His reason is that she later married Salmon, and "it is not likely that he would have taken to wife a woman who was, or had been, of ill fame." The Targum's "innkeeper" thus enters the Latin commentary. Rahab's own timeline traces it (Rahab of Jericho). On her lie to the king's men, Lyra gives the judgment of Augustine and the schools in one line:

Cumque porta clauderetur. In hoc mentita est, fuit tamen mendacium officiosum, videlicet propter salutem suam et exploratorum.

— on Joshua 2:5 (II, col. 20). In English: "'And when the gate was being shut.' In this she lied; but it was an officious lie, namely for her own safety and that of the spies." An "officious" lie is one told to help, and the schools counted it a lesser sin. When the city falls, he explains why she was spared despite the command of Numbers 33 that all the inhabitants be destroyed. "With Rahab and those joined to her a dispensation was made as to life and living among them, because of the special deed of her faith and virtue." He then sets a limit: "in the precepts of the Decalogue, which are of the dictate of natural law, no such dispensation falls." God could release Israel from the ban, a positive command, but not from the natural law.

The ban: "by God's just judgment, for their iniquities"

Nullum omnino permittes vivere. Quia ex iusto Dei iudicio propter suas iniquitates erant omnes omnino delendi et interficiendi, et ad hoc filii Israel mittebantur… Additur etiam alia ratio, scilicet ne si remanerent aliqui eorum cum filiis Israel, pertraherent eos ad idololatriam.

— on Deuteronomy 20:16 (Venice 1603, I, col. 1576). In English: "'You shall let nothing at all live.' Because by the just judgment of God, for their iniquities, they were all to be utterly destroyed and slain, and for this the sons of Israel were sent… Another reason too is added, namely, that if any of them remained with the sons of Israel, they would draw them into idolatry."5 This is the answer of Augustine and Aquinas. Israel was the executor of a sentence passed for guilt, and the danger of idolatry gave a second reason. On Joshua 6 he explains the word: "In the Hebrew it is ḥerem, which has a double meaning, for it means destruction and consecration." Jericho was the first city taken, and by a special miracle. So Joshua "fittingly put it under the ban, destroying some things utterly in sign of divine justice, and consecrating others to the Lord as first-fruits of the spoil." Achan's theft is the occasion for a principle: "sometimes the whole community is punished, and justly, and above all by God, to whom all are debtors of death."

Lyra also holds that there was to be no peace inside the land. On the law that a city must first be offered peace (Deuteronomy 20:10), he writes: "It speaks of the cities that were outside the land of promise, for with those that were within, it was forbidden them to make peace with them in any way." The same rule explains why Moses offered peace to Sihon (Deuteronomy 2:26): "when they were going to fight other nations, they had first to offer peace." Here Lyra follows Rashi, who says the verse "is speaking of a war which is not obligatory." The other rabbinic reading, which Nahmanides argued against Rashi from the Jerusalem Talmud, was that Joshua sent a proclamation before every campaign: "he who wishes to make peace, let him make peace; he who wishes to leave, let him leave." That reading does not appear in Lyra. So when Joshua 11:19 says that no city made peace except the Hivites of Gibeon, Lyra reads it as a statement about the cities that surrendered, not about an offer refused:

Praeter Heveum. Hoc dicitur ad ostendendum quod civitates quae tradiderunt se ei, hoc fecerunt virtute armorum coactae, et ideo non meruerunt clementiam aliquam, sicut meruerunt Gabaonitae…

— on Joshua 11:19 (II, col. 87). In English: "'Except the Hivite.' This is said to show that the cities which gave themselves up to him did so compelled by force of arms, and therefore did not earn any clemency, as the Gibeonites did…" The Gibeonites "went out of their own land to the sons of Israel to seek peace and mercy from them." Surrender under arms earned no mercy. Coming to Israel before the attack did.

The Gibeonite oath: "God is readier to show mercy than to condemn"

Lyra's longest discussion of the conquest's ethics is a formal question on Joshua 9:19–20: "whether Joshua and the princes were bound to keep the oath." He states the case against it first. The oath went against the Lord's command; a conditional oath does not bind when its condition fails; and "fraud and deceit ought to protect no one." He answers that the oath had to be kept, for three reasons, one from each party. The first concerns the Gibeonites:

…praeceptum domini de interfectione habitatorum terrae non videbatur contra eos currere, quod erat datum propter malitiam habitantium in terra et obstinationem eorum in malis: unde infra 11 cap. dicitur de obstinatis: Domini enim sententia fuerat ut indurarentur corda eorum… Ex quo patet quod Gabaonitae meruerunt clementiam, nec currebat contra eos sententia domini.

— question on Joshua 9:19–20 (II, cols. 68–70). In English: "…the Lord's command about killing the inhabitants of the land did not seem to run against them, for it had been given on account of the malice of the inhabitants of the land and their obstinacy in evil; hence below, in chapter 11, it is said of the obstinate, 'For it was the Lord's sentence that their hearts should be hardened'… From which it is plain that the Gibeonites earned clemency, and the Lord's sentence did not run against them."6 This is the moral centre of Lyra's reading. The ban was a sentence on the obstinate, not a decree against a people as such, and those who turned did not fall under it. The Gibeonites came "not only led by fear, but also by faith in the one true God … and for this they are to be commended, though not for the pretence of a falsehood." The second reason concerns Israel. Breaking the oath "would have been spread through the lands round about," Israel would have been thought faithless, "and in consequence it would have turned to irreverence or blasphemy of God." The third concerns God:

Deus pronior est ad miserandum quam ad condemnandum. Et ideo credendum est quod Iosue ex familiari consilio Dei mortem eorum corporalem mutavit in civilem, ut sic de suis mendaciis punirentur, et iuramentum servaretur.

— In English: "God is readier to show mercy than to condemn. And therefore it is to be believed that Joshua, by God's private counsel, changed their bodily death into a civil death, so that they were punished for their lies and the oath was kept." Their servitude as hewers of wood stood in for the death they had escaped. God's approval, Lyra adds, is shown in 2 Samuel 21, where Saul's breach of the oath brought famine on Israel. To the objection from fraud he answers with a distinction: "fraud and deceit, properly, are lies in word or deed ordered to a bad end," but the Gibeonites' lies, "though bad in themselves, were ordered by them to a good end, namely their bodily and spiritual salvation."

The ambush at Ai and the death of Achan

…quaeritur hic utrum in bello liceat insidiis et cautelis uti… Possunt autem hostes dupliciter decipi … uno modo quia dicitur eis falsum, vel non servatur semper promissum, et hoc est semper illicitum, quia in bellis sunt quaedam iura semper observanda etiam cum adversariis, secundum quod dicit Ambrosius lib. de officiis, sicut observatio treugarum… Alio modo decipiuntur hostes per hoc quod adversarii occultant eis verbis vel factis suam intentionem, et hoc est licitum…

— question on Joshua 8:2 (II, cols. 57–58). In English: "…it is asked here whether in war it is lawful to use ambushes and stratagems… Enemies can be deceived in two ways … in one way because something false is told them, or a promise is not kept, and this is always unlawful, for in wars there are certain rights to be observed always, even with adversaries, as Ambrose says in the book On Duties, such as the observance of truces… In another way enemies are deceived when their adversaries hide their intention from them by words or deeds, and this is lawful…"7 He first lays down that if a war is unjust, no means of fighting it is lawful. He then quotes Augustine as given in the Gloss: "if anyone has undertaken a just war, whether he wins by open battle or by ambush makes no difference to justice." Such ambushes "belong not to injustice but to military prudence." The question follows Aquinas's article on stratagems (Summa II-II q. 40 a. 3), which rests on the same passage of Augustine. With Lyra it moved from the theologians' Summa into the commentary on Joshua itself. On Achan, Lyra holds that "it is not likely that he was damned," because "his temporal death would be an expiation for him." Like Augustine, he leaves the eternal sentence open.

"A common way of speaking": the sweeping formulas

Non est intelligendum quod omnes fuerunt ibi mortui vel vulnerati … sed omnes fuerunt ibi devicti, et pro magna parte interfecti, et est communis modus loquendi, sicut dicitur: Omnes de civitate vadunt ad tale spectaculum, licet multi remaneant.

— on Joshua 11:8 (II, col. 84). In English: "It is not to be understood that all were killed or wounded there … but that all were defeated there, and for the most part killed; and it is a common way of speaking, as it is said, 'Everyone in the city is going to such-and-such a show,' though many stay behind."8 He reads the other summary statements the same way. "Joshua took all that land" (11:16) means "Joshua held the land for the greater part, and as much as was enough at that time for the dwelling of the sons of Israel, for many cities and villages remained that were not acquired in Joshua's time, as is said below in chapter 13." The phrase "he took them all in battle" (11:19) "is a hyperbolic way of speaking." When "the land rested from war" (11:23), it rested "from wars waged in common," because battles were later fought within the tribes' own lots (chapter 15; Judges 1). Origen had resolved these formulas by the spiritual sense, and Comestor by the history of the fortresses. Lyra explains them as ordinary speech. He then gives three reasons why God left part of the land unconquered (Joshua 13:1). Israel was not yet numerous enough to fill it, and the wild beasts would have multiplied (Exodus 23:29). Second, "so that the sons of Israel, having adversaries near them, would be exercised in battle, and not become slack, as men are wont to become in a long peace." Third, so that they would take care to keep God's commands, since their neighbours afflicted them whenever they broke them (Judges 3).

Notes

  • The text. No critical edition or English translation of Lyra on Joshua exists. The quotations follow the great printed Bible of Venice, 1603 (Bibliorum sacrorum cum glossa ordinaria… et Postilla Nicolai Lyrani), which prints the biblical text, the Glossa ordinaria, Lyra's literal and moral postills, Paul of Burgos's Additiones and Matthias Döring's Replicae together on each page. Volume and column numbers are those of that edition, and abbreviations are expanded.
  • Josephus. In the Joshua postill Lyra names Josephus only as a source for chronology, at Joshua 10:13. The other extracts from Josephus on the same pages belong to the editors' marginal additions, not to Lyra.
  • Where he stands among the witnesses. On the first question that divides the witnesses, Lyra keeps the literal and spiritual senses in separate commentaries, with the literal first. On the second, he reads the sweeping formulas as ordinary hyperbole and says plainly that much of the land "remained." On the third, he gives the retributive and anti-idolatry answer of Augustine and Aquinas. He narrows it in one respect: the sentence fell on the "obstinate," and those who came to Israel in faith before the attack, as the Gibeonites did, could earn mercy. The fullest weighing of these answers is the argument on the morality of the ḥerem.
  • Afterlife. The Postilla was the first Bible commentary printed (Rome, 1471–72). It was reprinted with the Gloss into the seventeenth century, and through it Rashi's readings reached Reformation-era readers of Joshua. The saying "if Lyra had not piped, Luther would not have danced" (si Lyra non lyrasset, Lutherus non saltasset) is later and proverbial.
