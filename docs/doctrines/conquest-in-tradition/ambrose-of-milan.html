Ambrose of Milan on the Conquest of Canaan

Dates: c. 390 (the Exposition of the Christian Faith, 378–380; On the Duties of the Clergy, c. 388–390; the Exposition of the Gospel of Luke, c. 390; the Explanation of Twelve Psalms, preached over many years and broken off in Psalm 43 at his death; Ambrose b. c. 339, bishop of Milan from 374, d. 397) · Wikipedia
Summary: Ambrose, bishop of Milan and the man who baptized Augustine, wrote no commentary on Joshua, but he returns to the book often, in three ways. In On the Duties of the Clergy, his Christian answer to Cicero's On Duties, Joshua's campaign is a store of moral examples: fortitude in war, which "without justice is the source of wickedness"; the love of money that undid Achan; and, above all, the oath to the Gibeonites, which Joshua kept even after he learned it had been won by fraud. In his sermons and commentaries the same events are read as figures. Joshua bears the name of Jesus, Jericho is "the figure of this world," and Rahab's house is the Church. He states the order of the two readings in a sentence: "This is the simple history of truth: which, if considered deeply, signifies wondrous mysteries." In the last psalm he ever dictated he draws the line Origen drew: the Canaanites, Hittites and Amorites are now "the weaknesses of human passions," because Israel "fought in figure" and the Church fights "in spirit." He never treats the ban as a moral problem. Jericho's total destruction is a "full miracle," the Canaanites are Ham's heirs "subdued by the righteous people," and the Amorites were enemies "whom it is not a crime to kill."

Claims & evidence

Joshua's wars as moral examples: "fortitude without justice is the source of wickedness"

But the thought of warlike matters seems to be foreign to the duty of our office, for we have our thoughts fixed more on the duty of the soul than on that of the body; nor is it our business to look to arms, but rather to the affairs of peace. Our fathers, however, as Joshua, the son of Nun, Jerubbaal, Samson, and David, gained great glory also in war. 176. Fortitude, therefore, is a loftier virtue than the rest, but it is also one that never stands alone. For it never depends on itself alone. Moreover, fortitude without justice is the source of wickedness. For the stronger it is, the more ready is it to crush the weaker, whilst in matters of war one ought to see whether the war is just or unjust.

— On the Duties of the Clergy (De officiis) 1.35.175–176, trans. H. de Romestin.1 Ambrose wrote On Duties for his clergy, and he says at the outset that he writes "as Cicero wrote for the instruction of his son" (1.7.24). He follows Cicero's scheme of the four virtues and replaces Cicero's Roman examples with biblical ones. So Joshua appears here as a war hero, alongside Gideon (Jerubbaal), Samson and David. Ambrose concedes that war is not the clergy's business, but he does not treat Joshua's wars as an embarrassment. He sets a condition on them instead: courage in war is a virtue only when it is joined to justice, and "one ought to see whether the war is just or unjust." He does not spell out the test, but he asks the question that Augustine, his convert, would answer with the classic definition drawn from the ambush at Ai.

The same book lists the miracles of the campaign as plain deeds of courage:

But how brave was Joshua the son of Nun, who in one battle laid low five kings together with their people! Again, when he fought against the Gibeonites and feared that night might stop him from gaining the victory, he called out with deep faith and high spirit: "Let the sun stand still;" and it stood still until the victory was complete.

— On Duties 1.40.205.2 The English "he fought against the Gibeonites" is a slip of the translation. The Latin (cum adversum Gabaonitas surgeret proelium) says the battle "rose against the Gibeonites": the five kings were attacking Gibeon, and Joshua fought on its side, as Joshua 10 tells it. Book 2 makes Joshua a model for young clerics, since he became great by keeping close to Moses, and it ranks the two men: "The one ruled the sea, the other heaven." Joshua was "worthy surely … to stand forth as a man who might stay the course of the river, and who might say: 'Sun, stand still,'" "great in the wonders he wrought by faith, great in his triumphs" (2.20.98–99).

Achan, Caleb and the uses of the history

When the city of Jericho fell at the sound of the priests' trumpets, and Joshua the son of Nun gained the victory, he knew that the valour of the people was weakened through love of money and desire for gold. For when Achan had taken a garment of gold and two hundred shekels of silver and a golden ingot from the spoils of the ruined city, he was brought before the Lord, and could not deny the theft, but owned it. … Overcome by love of money Achan led to destruction all the people of the fathers. So Joshua the son of Nun, who could stay the sun from setting, could not stay the love of money in man from creeping on. At the sound of his voice the sun stood still, but love of money stayed not.

— On Duties 2.26.129–130.3 Achan's theft from the devoted spoil of Jericho (Joshua 7) is, for Ambrose, a lesson against avarice. He uses it to warn a clergy that honors the rich. He does not take up the hard part of the chapter, the stoning of Achan's household with him, which Augustine and Jerome would face by name. In Book 3 the spies' report on the land shows that God approves "those who put what is virtuous before what is useful." Joshua and Caleb urged the people on, and they "entered the land of promise together with those who were innocent by reason of age or sex," while the spies who counselled safety "died forthwith of a great plague" (3.8.53–56, on Numbers 13–14 and Joshua 14). The point drawn is moral, but the examples work only as things that happened.

Justice with enemies: the Gibeonite oath kept

When the report had gone forth among the various peoples that the sea was dried up at the crossing of the Hebrews; that water had flowed from the rock; that food was supplied daily from heaven in quantities large enough for so many thousands of the people; that the walls of Jericho had fallen at the sound of the holy trumpets, being overthrown by the noise of the shouts of the people; also, that the king of Ai was conquered and had been hung on a tree until the evening; then the Gibeonites, fearing his strong hand, came with guile … But when he came to their country and the deceit was found out,—for though they lived quite close they pretended to be strangers,—the people of our fathers began to be angry at having been deceived. Joshua, however, thought the peace they had made could not be broken (for it had been confirmed by an oath), for fear that, in punishing the treachery of others, he should be breaking his own pledge. He made them pay the penalty, however, by forcing them to undertake the lowest kind of work. The judgment was mild indeed, but it was a lasting one, for in their duties there abides the punishment of their ancient cunning, handed down to this day in their hereditary service.

— On Duties 3.10.67–69.4 This is Ambrose's fullest treatment of any episode in Joshua. The chapter heading states the point: Scripture as well as civil law teaches us "to avoid fraud in every agreement, as is clear from the example of Joshua and the Gibeonites." He offers it as "the ancient judgment of the patriarchs on deceit," older than any rule of the Roman lawyers. He takes the run of events in Joshua 2–9 as a single, known history: the sea, the rock, the manna, Jericho, the king of Ai hanged "until the evening." He also excuses Joshua for not consulting God (Joshua 9:14): "We must not blame his readiness to believe, but should rather praise his goodness" (3.10.68). Joshua keeps his oath to a people that deceived him, and the penalty he lays on them is "mild." Book 1 had already set the Gibeonites against the Midianites as two cases of justice in war:

It must even be preserved in all dealings with enemies. … But a deeper vengeance is taken on fiercer foes, and on those that are false as well as on those who have done greater wrongs, as was the case with the Midianites. For they had made many of the Jewish people to sin through their women; for which reason the anger of the Lord was poured out upon the people of our fathers. Thus it came about that Moses when victorious allowed none of them to live. On the other hand, Joshua did not attack the Gibeonites, who had tried the people of our fathers with guile rather than with war, but punished them by laying on them a law of bondage.

— On Duties 1.29.139, under the heading "Justice should be observed even in war and with enemies." The chapter ends: "faith and justice should be observed even in war; and … it could not but be a disgraceful thing if faith were violated" (1.29.140). This is as near as Ambrose comes to a theory of the ban. Total killing, as at Midian, is a "deeper vengeance" for a graver wrong. Joshua's lighter penalty on Gibeon shows that justice binds even the war leader of God's people. He does not apply the measure to the Canaanite cities that Joshua destroyed, and he does not ask whether they deserved it.

History first, then the mystery: Jericho and Rahab

71. In order to better understand the place that has been proposed to us, let us revisit the ancient history of the city of Jericho. We remember that Jericho, as we read in the book called Joshua, was a great city surrounded by walls that could not be penetrated by iron or battering ram (Joshua 6:1). In that city, Rahab the prostitute lived. She received and provided shelter for the spies sent by Joshua, gave them instructions, and when the citizens asked about them, she hid them on her roof. In order to save herself and her people from the destruction of the city, she tied a scarlet cord in the window (Joshua 2:4ff). However, the invincible walls of the city itself fell down at the sound of the seven priestly trumpets and the shout of the jubilant people (Joshua 6:20).

See how each person keeps their own duty: the scouts guard, the prostitute has secrets, the conqueror has trust, the priest has religion. … Now, who would not think that it is a full miracle that no one from the whole city was saved, except for the one whom the prostitute freed?

This is the simple history of truth: which, if considered deeply, signifies wondrous mysteries. For Jericho is the figure of this world, into which Adam, expelled from paradise, that is, from celestial Jerusalem, descended through the transgression of his disobedience …

— Exposition of the Gospel of Luke 7.71–73, on Luke 10:30 (English from the Migne Latin, as given at historicalchristian.faith).5 To explain the road "from Jerusalem to Jericho" in the parable of the Good Samaritan, Ambrose first retells the fall of Jericho as history, and then reads it. The sentence that joins the two is exact in the Latin: haec simplicis historia veritatis est. quae si altius consideretur, admiranda signat mysteria ("this is the history of the plain truth; if it is considered more deeply, it signifies wonderful mysteries"). The history comes first and stays true; the mystery is found by looking deeper into it. The retelling is also the place where he comes nearest to the ban, and he treats it as a wonder. That "no one from the whole city was saved, except for the one whom the prostitute freed" is "a full miracle" (plenum miraculi). He praises Joshua for keeping faith with Rahab before the city fell, "anxious to preserve trust more than to conquer" (fidem magis servare quam vincere).

The figure itself is Clement's and Justin's. Joshua's victory and Rahab's thread are two signs of the one Christ:

126. And did Joshua, the son of Nun, err in recognizing the leader of the heavenly host? But after he believed, he forthwith conquered, being found worthy to triumph in the battle of faith. Again, he did not lead forth his armed ranks into the fight, nor did he overthrow the ramparts of the enemy's walls, with battering rams or other engines of war, but with the sound of the seven trumpets of the priests. Thus the blare of the trumpet and the badge of the priest brought a cruel war to an end. 127. A harlot saw this; and she who in the destruction of the city lost all hope of any means of safety, because her faith had conquered, bound a scarlet thread in her window, and thus uplifted a sign of her faith and the banner of the Lord's Passion; so that the semblance of the mystic blood, which should redeem the world, might be in memory. So, without, the name of Joshua was a sign of victory to those who fought; within, the semblance of the Lord's Passion was a sign of salvation to those in danger.

— Exposition of the Christian Faith (De fide) 5.10.126–127, trans. de Romestin.6 The argument is against the Arians: Christ's testimony is true, and those who believed it were not deceived. Joshua is one of them, because the "leader of the heavenly host" he met before Jericho (Joshua 5:14) was the Son. The war is "a cruel war," and it was really ended, but by trumpets and priests, not siege-engines. The Explanation of Psalm 39 sets the same figure in a list of Old Testament leaders in whom Christ "was expected." Joshua "received both his power and his name," dried the river, and "set the sun still, until he might fully complete victory in power. But he was still a figure, not the reality" (sed adhuc figura erat illa, non veritas). The type here is the person: Joshua, who bore the name, is the figure, and Christ is the reality. The events are not made less real by it.

The Church's conquest: "they fought in figure, we fight in spirit"

(Verse 3.) Your hand has scattered the nations and planted them: You have afflicted peoples and driven them out. Indeed, we know that the Lord has uprooted and overthrown many nations, so that the people of the Jews might find possession for themselves, the lands of which the Lord declared He would give to the descendants of Abraham. But when this psalm announces the Gospel of the Lord and the times of His coming, it does not seem to me to recount what has been done by the Jews, but rather to indicate what will happen in the future, as the people of the nations would believe. Therefore, it confirms the trust of the Church before it proclaims, and it enumerates the victories of its piety: which did not expel nations with its arm or with its sword, and did not scatter hostile troops in battle, but peacefully and faithfully possessed the lands of its enemies without any bloodshed; for it is faith alone that fought. … So let us understand which peoples the Church has conquered. I speak of ancient names, but new mysteries. There are the Canaanites, the Khetites, and the Amorites, the Perizzites … However, these are not only the names of peoples; they also represent the weaknesses of human passions, the motives of sinful actions, and the disgrace of sins. … Therefore, the people of the Church did not fight with military weapons and iron weapons, as the people of the Jews fought. The former fought in form, we fight in spirit: the former fought against foreigners, we ourselves have war within us; and therefore, we must first conquer the passions of our own bodies.

— Explanation of Psalm 43 (Hebrew 44) 9, English from the Migne Latin, as given at historicalchristian.faith.7 The psalm recalls the conquest: "Thy hand destroyed the nations, and thou plantedst them" (Psalm 43:3 Vulgate). Ambrose grants the history in his first sentence ("we know that the Lord has uprooted and overthrown many nations") and then reads the verse as prophecy of the Church, which takes its enemies' lands "without any bloodshed." The nations of Canaan become the passions, and he goes on to give each name a vice. Paul's "law in my flesh fighting against the law of my mind" is the war "against the Canaanite nation"; the Amorites are "the cunning disputations … which through philosophy deceive some with falsehood"; the Kenites are those "possessed by their wealth" (43.10). The key sentence in the critical Latin is ille in figura pugnavit, nos pugnamus in spiritu; ille adversum alienigenas pugnavit, nos ipsi bellum habemus in nobis: Israel "fought in figure, we fight in spirit; it fought against foreigners, we have the war within ourselves." (The English "in form" renders in figura.) It is Origen's contrast: Christians "shall not fight as the ancients fought." Ambrose makes the point Origen made, that the conquest is no pattern for the Church's own conduct. The contrast assumes that Israel did fight with iron weapons against foreigners.

The next verse, "they got not the land in possession by their own sword," gives him the history again, with grace in place of merit:

For it was not in his own confidence that Joshua said, 'Sun, stand still over Gibeon' (Joshua 10:12), but because he presumed in Christ, whom he recognized as the leader of the heavenly army, and he worshipped him humbly. Therefore, he deserved to eradicate wild nations and to lead the people of the Fathers into the promised land; because he attributed nothing to his own works … For it was not Joshua, who brought them in, nor he who planted them; but God, who gave increase to the people, has the principality of glory.

— Explanation of Psalm 43 12. Joshua "deserved to eradicate wild nations" (gentes feras eradicare promeruit), and the credit for it is God's. On verse 10 ("thou goest not forth with our armies") he adds that when Joshua worshipped the captain of the heavenly host, "without iron he conquered and without any siege destroyed the city of Jericho, which had very strong walls" (sine ferro vicit et sine ulla obsidione destruxit urbem Iericho, 43.31). This commentary was the last thing Ambrose worked on. His secretary and biographer Paulinus says that a few days before he took to his bed, "he was dictating the 43rd Psalm," and that "he could not finish that psalm" (Life of Ambrose 42).

The Canaanites: Ham's heirs, and enemies "whom it is not a crime to kill"

But Ham was the father of Canaan … It also signifies that from him the Canaanites arose, who after many generations were subdued by the righteous people and gave up their possession to them. Therefore, it is clear that Canaan was the originator of the Canaanites, and he was the son of this Ham, who was impious towards his father.

— On Noah 105, on Genesis 9:18–19 (English from the Migne Latin, as given at historicalchristian.faith).8 Like Justin and Irenaeus, Ambrose connects the conquest with Noah's curse on Canaan. Genesis names Ham's son "to heap up his guilt," and the line it begins is the people later "subdued by the righteous people." He goes straight on to a moral reading of the names ("Cham is heat, Chanaan is their disturbance"), but the historical sentence stands first. In On Tobit the Amorites appear in a law case. Deuteronomy 23:20 lets Israel charge interest to "a stranger," and Ambrose asks who the stranger was:

Who were the foreigners at that time, if not the Amalekites, if not the Amorites, if not the enemies? Charge interest there, it says. To whom you wish to harm rightly, to whom weapons are properly directed, to him interest is legitimately imposed. Whom you cannot easily defeat in war, from this one you can quickly take revenge. Demand interest from one who it is not a crime to kill. He who demands interest fights without a sword; he who was an exacting usurious enemy avenges himself without a sword. Therefore, where there is the right of war, there is also the right of interest.

— On Tobit 15.51. The Latin of the last sentences is ab hoc usuram exige quem non sit crimen occidere … ergo ubi ius belli, ibi etiam ius usurae. Ambrose's purpose is to forbid usury between Christians, who are all "brothers," and the passage became a standard text in medieval debates on lending. Along the way it takes for granted that Israel's war on the Amorites was lawful, and that killing them was "not a crime." It is a casual remark, and it shows how far the ban was from being a problem for him.

Where the letter is hard: the cities of refuge

In this passage the literal interpretation causes difficulty. First, the period of flight is limited by chance rather than by any consideration of fairness; further, in like cases the result is unlike. For it could happen that the high priest might die on the day after the homicide took refuge. However, what is the meaning beneath the uncertainty? And so, because the letter causes difficulty, let us search for spiritual meanings. Who is that high priest but the Son of God, the Word of God?

— Flight from the World (De fuga saeculi) 2.13, on Joshua 20:6, trans. Michael P. McHugh.9 Ambrose does have Origen's rule that a hard letter sends the reader to the spirit, and he applies it to Joshua once. The case is the law of the cities of refuge, where a manslayer's exile ends at the high priest's death, which seems to him to make the term a matter of chance and so unfair. He does not apply the rule to the wars. The difficulty he finds is in a law's fairness, not in the campaign's bloodshed.

Notes

  • Between Origen and Augustine. Ambrose read the Greek exegetes closely, and his figure of the conquest is Origen's: the nations as vices, the war as the soul's, and the contrast "they fought in figure, we in spirit." Compare Origen of Alexandria on the Conquest of Canaan. His moral use of the history in On Duties looks the other way, toward Augustine, whom he baptized at Milan in 387: he asks "whether the war is just or unjust," and he uses the Gibeonite oath to show that faith binds even in war. Augustine's Questions on Joshua also take up the Gibeonite oath. See Augustine of Hippo on the Conquest of Canaan.
  • The moral question. Ambrose never answers Marcion's charge against the ban, because he never raises it. Where he comes near it (the "full miracle" of Jericho, the Canaanites "subdued by the righteous people," the Amorites "whom it is not a crime to kill") he treats the destruction as a just act of God or of lawful war. What he contributes is a limit on its use: the Church conquers "without any bloodshed," and its war is "within us." The patristic answers are weighed at Do the patristic defenses answer the moral problem of the ḥerem?
