Thomas Aquinas on the Conquest of Canaan

Dates: c. 1270 (the Summa Theologiae, 1265–1273, with the Summa contra Gentiles and the lectures on Matthew and Hebrews; Thomas b. c. 1225, d. 1274) · Wikipedia
Summary: Thomas Aquinas wrote no commentary on Joshua. He uses the book throughout his theology, and always as history. His rule of interpretation is that every spiritual sense "is based on the literal, and presupposes it," and that only the literal sense can ground an argument. His lectures on Hebrews follow that order: the walls of Jericho fell "at God's command," and only then does a moral reading follow. He counts the sun's standing still at Gibeon among miracles of the highest rank, wrought by God "obeying the voice of a man." On the ban he is plain. In the cities of the promised land "all were ordered to be slain, on account of their former crimes," with Israel acting as "executor of Divine justice." This rests on a principle he states elsewhere: God is "Lord of life and death," and one who carries out his sentence "will be no murderer." Through Augustine's questions on Joshua, the conquest also supplies his theory of war with two texts: the definition of a just cause, and the lawfulness of the ambush at Ai.

Claims & evidence

"All the senses are founded on one — the literal"

Therefore that first signification whereby words signify things belongs to the first sense, the historical or literal. That signification whereby things signified by words have themselves also a signification is called the spiritual sense, which is based on the literal, and presupposes it.

Thus in Holy Writ no confusion results, for all the senses are founded on one---the literal---from which alone can any argument be drawn, and not from those intended in allegory, as Augustine says (Epis. 48). Nevertheless, nothing of Holy Scripture perishes on account of this, since nothing necessary to faith is contained under the spiritual sense which is not elsewhere put forward by the Scripture in its literal sense.

— Summa Theologiae I q.1 a.10, body and reply to objection 1.1 This article sets the terms for everything Aquinas says about Joshua. God signifies "not by words only … but also by things themselves." So the events of the Old Testament can themselves be signs of the New Testament (the allegorical sense), of how Christians should live (the moral sense), and of glory (the anagogical sense). The events have to have happened for that to work. The things signified by the words are the things that carry the further meaning. On this model a figure cannot replace the history it rests on. Augustine had said that only the literal sense can ground an argument, and Aquinas makes that the rule of the schools.

Jericho's walls: first what happened, then the moral sense

This is mentioned in Jos. (6), where it is stated that at God’s command the priests for seven days should go around the first city beyond the Jordan, namely, Jericho, with the ark of the covenant, and on the seventh day the walls fell down. Here was something on the part of men, namely, that at the Lord’s command they went round, believing that God’s command would be fulfilled, and something on God’s part, namely, the walls fell down by their going round.

Morally, Jericho is interpreted moon or defect, and signifies this world. Its walls are the obstacles by which some are held fast in the world. By the trumpets, which the Levites and priests sounded, the voice of preachers is signified.

— Lectures on Hebrews 11:30, nos. 624–625.2 The lecture applies the rule of I q.1 a.10. The fall of the walls is told first, as an event with a human part (the march in faith) and a divine part (the collapse). The moral reading is then added as a second layer, marked "morally." The figure does not compete with the event. Commenting on Hebrews 4:8, Aquinas treats the land in the same way. "If Jesus Nave, i.e., Joshua, had given the children of Israel final rest," David would not have spoken of another day. "Hence, it is clear that that rest was a sign of spiritual rest." The rest in Canaan was real, and it was not the last rest.

The sun at Gibeon: a miracle of the highest rank

In either case, however, God is the principal worker, for He uses instrumentally either man's inward movement, or his speech, or some outward action, or again the bodily contact of even a dead body. Thus when Josue had said as though authoritatively (Josh. 10:12): "Move not, O sun, toward Gabaon," it is said afterwards (Josh. 10:14): "There was not before or after so long a day, the Lord obeying the voice of a man."

— Summa Theologiae II-II q.178 a.1 ad 1.3 Aquinas uses Joshua's command to the sun as his example of a miracle worked "by authority" rather than by prayer. God is the cause, and Joshua's word is his instrument. In the Summa contra Gentiles he ranks miracles. The highest are those "in which something is done by God which nature never could do," and his examples include "that the sun reverse its course, or stand still," which points to Joshua 10 and Hezekiah's sign. In ST II-II q.174 a.4 an objection argues that Joshua, "who made the sun and moon to stand still," worked greater miracles than Moses. Aquinas grants the point: "These signs of the prophets mentioned were greater as to the substance of the thing done; yet the miracles of Moses were greater as regards the way in which they were done, since they were wrought on a whole people." The Supplement to the Summa, compiled after his death from his commentary on the Sentences, lists "the sun standing still at the time of Josue" with Hezekiah's sign and the eclipse at the Passion as changes in "the course which is appointed for all cases." He does not discuss astronomy or how the miracle happened. He takes the event as fact and classifies it.

The ban: "all were ordered to be slain, on account of their former crimes"

A distinction was observed with regard to hostile cities. For some of them were far distant, and were not among those which had been promised to them. When they had taken these cities, they killed all the men who had fought against God's people; whereas the women and children were spared. But in the neighboring cities which had been promised to them, all were ordered to be slain, on account of their former crimes, to punish which God sent the Israelites as executor of Divine justice: for it is written (Dt. 9:5) "because they have done wickedly, they are destroyed at thy coming in." The fruit-trees were commanded to be left untouched, for the use of the people themselves, to whom the city with its territory was destined to be subjected.

— Summa Theologiae I-II q.105 a.3 ad 4.4 The objection was that the Law cared more for trees than for people, since it commanded that "all the inhabitants of a captured hostile city" be slain but the fruit trees spared (Deuteronomy 20:13–19). Aquinas answers with the distinction Deuteronomy 20 itself makes. Distant cities lost only their fighting men. The cities of the promised land lost all. The reason he gives is judicial: their "former crimes," with Israel as the instrument of God's justice. He does not soften the command or read it as a figure. The body of the article praises the Law's "moderation in pursuing the advantage of victory, by sparing women and children." The reply shows that this sparing applied to the distant cities, not to Canaan. The Supplement gives the same reason when it explains why the Law forbade marriage with "inhabitants of the land of Canaan": "the Lord had commanded them to be slain on account of their obstinacy," and they might draw Israel into idolatry.

God, "Lord of life and death": the executor is no murderer

Likewise when Abraham consented to slay his son, he did not consent to murder, because his son was due to be slain by the command of God, Who is Lord of life and death: for He it is Who inflicts the punishment of death on all men, both godly and ungodly, on account of the sin of our first parent, and if a man be the executor of that sentence by Divine authority, he will be no murderer any more than God would be.

— Summa Theologiae I-II q.100 a.8 ad 3.5 Aquinas's examples here are Abraham, the spoiling of the Egyptians, and Hosea's marriage, not the conquest. The principle, though, is the one that stands behind I-II q.105 a.3 ad 4. The commandment against killing forbids killing that is "undue." Since all die by God's sentence on original sin, a death God commands is not undue. The article on natural law says the same: "by the command of God, death can be inflicted on any man, guilty or innocent, without any injustice whatever" (I-II q.94 a.5 ad 2). That sentence covers the hardest case the ban raises, the death of those not personally guilty. Aquinas answers it with God's universal sentence of death, not with the guilt of each person.

The conquest in the theory of just war

On the contrary, Augustine says (QQ. in Hept. qu. x super Jos): "Provided the war be just, it is no concern of justice whether it be carried on openly or by ambushes": and he proves this by the authority of the Lord, Who commanded Joshua to lay ambushes for the city of Hai (Joshua 8:2).

— Summa Theologiae II-II q.40 a.3.6 Question 40, Aquinas's short treatise on war, uses the book of Joshua three times. All three come through Augustine's Questions on the Heptateuch. His definition of a just cause in article 1 is Augustine's comment on Joshua (QHept 6.10): "A just war is wont to be described as one that avenges wrongs, when a nation or state has to be punished, for refusing to make amends for the wrongs inflicted by its subjects, or to restore what it has seized unjustly." In article 2 the priests who blew the trumpets at Jericho (Joshua 6:4) are his precedent for clergy giving "spiritual help to those who fight justly" without bearing arms. In article 3 the ambush at Ai, commanded by God, shows that concealing one's plans from an enemy is lawful. Aquinas adds a limit. Telling the enemy "something false, or … the breaking of a promise … is always unlawful." An ambush is only "concealment" of a plan. The conquest supplies precedent for the general theory of war.

Achan: why all Israel suffered, and why human judges may not copy it

Thirdly, in order to mark the unity of human fellowship, whereby one man is bound to be solicitous for another, lest he sin; and in order to inculcate horror of sin, seeing that the punishment of one affects all, as though all were one body, as Augustine says in speaking of the sin of Achan (QQ. sup. Josue viii).

But human judgment cannot be conformed to God's hidden judgments, whereby He punishes certain persons in temporal matters without any fault of theirs, since man is unable to grasp the reasons of these judgments so as to know what is expedient for each individual. Wherefore according to human judgment a man should never be condemned without fault of his own to an inflictive punishment, such as death, mutilation or flogging.

— Summa Theologiae II-II q.108 a.4, replies to objections 1 and 2.7 The objection cited Joshua 7: Israel "delivered into the hands of their enemies for the sin of Achan." Aquinas accepts that the whole people suffered for one man's theft. He explains it, again from Augustine's questions on Joshua, as a temporal penalty that taught a common responsibility. He then draws a firm line. What God may do by his hidden judgments, a human court may not do. No one may be put to death for another's fault. Elsewhere he cites Joshua's words to Achan, "give glory to the Lord God of Israel, and confess" (Joshua 7:19), to show that an accused person may not deny the truth to escape condemnation (II-II q.69 a.1).

Rahab: the spies' host, and Salmon's wife

For when Joshua had sent spies to explore Jericho, they escaped with the aid of that woman who is called a harlot, i.e., an idolater. Or she was literally a harlot, with whom they stayed, not to sin but to hide. … She did not perish with the unbelievers, who perished corporally, because the spies had sworn to free her and everyone of her father’s house; which they did.

— Lectures on Hebrews 11:31, no. 626.8 Aquinas asks the practical questions of a reader who takes the story as fact. Why her house? Because it was by the wall, and because the spies could hide more easily with a woman who received everyone. In his lectures on Matthew he follows the same order. He first gives the figure: Rahab means the Church, and "the thread in the window is our confession of the passion of Christ." Then he asks "according to the letter" how a harlot came to marry Salmon, "a chief in the tribe of Judah." His answer is that "Rahab did something great, in as much as she scorned her own people and her paternal rite and chose the worship of the God of Israel." He does not take up Rahab's lie to the king's men. His article on whether every lie is a sin (II-II q.110 a.3) discusses the Hebrew midwives of Exodus 1. They "were rewarded, not for their lie, but for their fear of God."

Notes

  • Augustine's heir. Nearly every use Aquinas makes of Joshua passes through Augustine's Questions on the Heptateuch: the just cause, the ambush at Ai, Achan's sin. He shares Augustine's literal, divine-command reading of the ban and gives it a fuller rationale (God as Lord of life and death, Israel as executor of justice). Compare the Antiochene literalism of Theodoret, and the figurative emphasis of Origen, whose spiritual senses Aquinas keeps only as resting on the letter. The Reformation reading follows with John Calvin.
