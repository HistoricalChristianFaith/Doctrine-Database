Peter Comestor on the Conquest of Canaan

Dates: c. 1170 (the Historia scholastica, composed c. 1169–1173; Peter Comestor d. 1178, chancellor of the cathedral school of Notre-Dame, Paris) · Wikipedia
Summary: Comestor's Historia scholastica retells the Bible as one continuous history. It became the standard Bible-history of the medieval schools, and in it the conquest is told in the literal sense alone. Its "History of the book of Joshua" opens by saying that whatever place the book holds in the canon, "the history does not change." It then retells the crossing, Jericho, Ai, Gibeon and the division of the land, adding Josephus, "the Hebrews," Augustine, Eusebius and Bede as it goes. The miracles are told as events: the Jordan stood "like a mountain," the walls "fell flat," and at Gibeon "neither before nor after was there so long a day." He notes that Joshua and Jesus "are the same name" and draws no figure from it. Most of his effort goes into reconciling the text. He sets 60,000 armed men against Chronicles' 50,000, reconciles the two numbers at Ai, and settles how much of the land Israel held by separating land "granted" from land "handed over" by lot. He states the ban without defending it. Joshua laid the north waste "sparing no age or sex," because "by the Lord's command he was sparing none." The one moral judgment he passes falls on Israel for sparing too many: the tribes "preferred to keep them as tributaries rather than destroy them, transgressing the Lord's command." He closes Joshua's history with the gentile events of the same years, from Cadmus to Danaus, set alongside it as one chronology.

Claims & evidence

"The history does not change": the book read as history

Liber Josue a nomine auctoris censetur, qui et Jesus dictus est. Nam Josue et Jesus idem est nomen. … In quocunque autem ordine sit liber iste, historia non mutatur.

— Historia scholastica, "Historia libri Josue," preface (PL 198, cols. 1259C, 1260C). In English: "The book of Joshua is named for its author, who was also called Jesus; for Joshua and Jesus are the same name. … In whatever order this book is placed, the history does not change."1 The preface names Joshua as the author and tells him apart from Jesus son of Sirach. It then sets out the two ways of ordering the canon: the Hebrews, who put Joshua first among the Prophets, and Origen, who joined it to the Pentateuch to make a "Heptateuch." The closing sentence is the rule for the whole section. Where the book sits in the canon is a question for the librarian, and it does not touch what happened. Comestor notes that Joshua and Jesus share a name, but he draws no figure of Christ from it, as Origen, Isidore and Bede had. No allegory of the conquest appears in the section. The nearest thing to a moral reading is one sentence on Joshua 5:15, where the angel bids Joshua loose his shoe. There "the bareness of the feet" in holy places "can signify innocence" (potest notari innocentia), "for the first parents had it, naked." He also gives two etymologies of Gilgal. The Historia was written as the literal course of the schools, to be read before the spiritual senses, and the Joshua section keeps to that.

The miracles: told as events

Accedentes autem ad Jordanem, repererunt eum immeabilem. Plus enim solito tempore triticeae messis excreverat, tum pro solutione nivium facta in montibus, tum propter imbrem serotinum noviter datum. Cumque intrassent sacerdotes aquam, steterunt aquae ascendentes, et instar montis intumescentes apparebant procul ab urbe quae vocatur Adom…

— "Historia libri Josue," the crossing of the Jordan (PL 198, col. 1262B–C). In English: "Coming to the Jordan, they found it impassable. For at the time of the wheat harvest it had risen higher than usual, both from the melting of the snows in the mountains and from the late rain newly given. And when the priests had entered the water, the waters stood, rising, and swelling like a mountain they appeared far off, from the city called Adam…"2 The natural causes he gives are for the flood, not for the parting. They explain why the river was "impassable," and so why the crossing was a miracle. Jericho falls in the words of Joshua 6:20, "the walls fell flat" (muri funditus corruerunt), and the way to take the city is one that "the angel taught him." At Gibeon, he tells the hailstorm and the long day as one battle:

Et Dominus misit grandinem super eos, et plures occidit grando, quam gladius. Videns autem Josue solem descendentem ad occasum et lunam ascendentem, timens ne beneficio noctis evaderent hostes, clamavit ad Dominum… Steteruntque sol, et luna spatio unius diei, nec antea, nec postea fuit tam longa dies.

— Gibeon (PL 198, cols. 1266D–1267A). In English: "And the Lord sent hail upon them, and the hail killed more than the sword. But Joshua, seeing the sun going down to its setting and the moon rising, and fearing that the enemy would escape by favour of the night, cried to the Lord… And the sun and the moon stood still for the space of one day; and neither before nor after was there so long a day." Joshua's motive is a soldier's: night would let the enemy get away. Comestor offers no explanation of how the day was lengthened and does not mention the Book of Jasher here. (At 2 Samuel 1:18 he says the "book of the just" is not extant, and supposes it was burned by the Babylonians.)

The text reconciled with itself: numbers, days and the land

Most of the section's notes reconcile the text. Joshua orders the people to be ready to cross "after the third day" (Joshua 1:11), but they did not cross until the seventh. Comestor answers that "Joshua said this by human counsel" (Hoc de humano consilio dixit Josue), and that "the Lord allowed Joshua to err, as Augustine says, so that he would attempt nothing of the kind again without divine counsel." The spies stayed out three days, and "after their return the people waited three days for the waters to go down."3 The Transjordan tribes had "sixty thousand armed men; in Chronicles only fifty are read, in which much has been corrupted by the fault of scribes." At Ai the spies said three thousand would be enough, but "Josephus says thirty thousand." Joshua 8 puts the ambush at thirty thousand (8:3) and at five thousand (8:12). Comestor makes them one force: "he sent ahead thirty thousand men … who, having left five thousand in the place of ambush, returned to the army." Where the sweeping formula says that Joshua took all the land, he notes the fortresses that held out:

Fecerant enim sibi Chananaei plures munitiones praevalidas a die qua audierant egressum Israel ab Aegypto adversum se, nec eas nisi longo tempore et gravi labore obtinuit Israel.

— the northern campaign (PL 198, col. 1267C). In English: "For the Canaanites had built themselves many very strong fortresses from the day they heard that Israel had come out of Egypt against them, and Israel did not take them except after a long time and with heavy labour." Here the formula is narrowed by the fortresses, not by a figure. When the land is divided, he finds that the writers seem to disagree over who held what, and he settles it with a distinction: "If, then, the authors seem to contradict one another on this, it must be determined according to land granted, or land handed over" (secundum terram concessam, vel traditam). Joshua "granted" Judah the hill country to the south and Ephraim the hill country to the north, "but not yet by lot," and the lot "handed it over" later. So when Joshua urges only seven tribes to go and survey the land (Joshua 18), it is because "two tribes and a half possessed land granted, as if handed over."

Rahab: "lying," and honoured

Qui cum misisset ad Raab, ut educeret eos, absconderat eos in stipula lini, quam siccabat supra murum, mentiens eos egressos, cum clauderetur porta.

— the spies at Jericho (PL 198, col. 1261D). In English: "When he had sent to Rahab to bring them out, she had hidden them under the stalks of flax that she was drying on the wall, lying that they had gone out when the gate was being shut."4 He calls it lying (mentiens) and passes no judgment on it. Augustine, Jerome and Bede all weighed it, and Comestor says nothing either way. At the fall of the city, "Rahab, honoured with gifts, with all her house, they received into Israel for ever; whom in course of time Salmon, a prince in the tribe of Judah, took to wife." That marriage is what puts her in the line of David and in the genealogy of Matthew 1:5.

The ban: stated, not defended

Comestor defines the ban twice, both times in the language of the text. The spoil of Jericho is anathema, "that is, set apart from human use," and the city itself is "anathema, as it were devoted to death" (quasi devotata in mortem). He tells its execution in Scripture's words, and adds of the north:

Vastavit tamen universam circa regionem, nulli aetati hominum parcens aut sexui, ditatusque est supra modum ex hostium praeda.

— the northern campaign (PL 198, col. 1267C–D). In English: "Yet he laid waste the whole region round about, sparing no age or sex of men, and was enriched beyond measure from the spoil of the enemy."5 The only reason he gives is the command. The Gibeonites resorted to their trick because they heard "that by the Lord's command he was sparing none of the inhabitants of the land of promise, nor making friendships with them" (quod ex mandato Domini nulli parceret). He does not mention the Canaanites' sins, or the "iniquity of the Amorites" of Genesis 15:16. He does not mention Shem's right to the land, or any offer of peace before the war. Where the Historia does report the law of offering peace first, at Abel in 2 Samuel 20, it is not applied to Canaan. His retelling of the law of war in Deuteronomy says the same thing more sharply:

Addidit quoque, ne cum gentibus terrae promissionis foedus inirent, sed penitus exstirparentur (Deut. XX): in quo post graviter peccaverunt, cum finitimis autem gentibus pacifici, quantum possent, et foederati essent.

— "Historia libri Deuteronomii," "That they should make no covenant with the nations" (PL 198, col. 1254C). In English: "He added also that they should not enter into a covenant with the nations of the land of promise, but that these should be utterly rooted out (Deut. 20), in which they afterwards sinned gravely; but with the neighbouring nations they should be peaceable as far as they could, and allied." Comestor finds the sin where the text finds it, in sparing the Canaanites. His history of Judges says it again: the tribes, "once they had grown strong, preferred to have them as tributaries rather than destroy them, transgressing the Lord's command, and turned themselves to farming." The moral problem the Fathers had answered since Marcion does not come up.

The Gibeonite oath and the death of Achan

Tunc acclamavit populus non esse standum juramento, quod per subreptionem factum erat, et praesertim cum Dominus inhibuisset talibus confoederari. Majores vero qui juraverant, pro religione jurisjurandi servanda et murmuri populi sedando, sic dispensaverunt, ut non haberent eos socios, sed servos aquarios, et lignarios…

— "Of the deceit of the Gibeonites, and their penalty" (PL 198, col. 1266C). In English: "Then the people cried out that the oath must not stand, since it had been made by deceit, and especially since the Lord had forbidden alliance with such people. But the elders who had sworn, to keep the sacredness of the oath and to quiet the murmuring of the people, so arranged it that they would have them not as allies but as servants, carriers of water and cutters of wood…"6 The people's case is put fairly: the oath was won by fraud and broke a divine prohibition. The elders keep it anyway, for the sake of the oath, and find a middle course. The Gibeonites are spared but not made allies. This is the case that Ambrose and Augustine had made a lesson in keeping faith.

On Achan he deals with a textual problem in the punishment. The Lord commanded that Achan be burned (Joshua 7:15), and the people stoned him. "Nor is it to be thought that the people neglected the Lord's command … for in sacred Scripture fire is often taken for a severe punishment." Then he gives two other answers. "The Hebrews think that the Lord commanded him to be stoned," and the things that stones could not touch to be burned. Augustine's answer is that Joshua acted "from a certain consideration": "either Achan repented, and so was not worthy of the fire of Gehenna … or, if he did not repent and was worthy of eternal fire, he reserved the punishment of fire for the Lord." Both of Augustine's alternatives look to Achan's eternal state, and they leave the earthly sentence to God's judgment.

The conquest in the years of the world: Eusebius, Bede and the "incidentia"

Et nota quod sic legitur in Chronicis Eusebii. Secundum Hebraeos, ille annus erat jubilaeus, et erat quinquagesimus primus inter jubilaeos… Secundum LXX vero, longe plures anni fluxerant ab initio mundi. Beda quoque in Chronicis suis ponens numerum annorum, secundum Hebraicam veritatem, probat de praedicta summa annorum septem annos defluxisse.

— the camp at Gilgal (PL 198, cols. 1262D–1263A). In English: "And note that it is read thus in the Chronicle of Eusebius: according to the Hebrews that year was a jubilee, and it was the fifty-first among the jubilees… But according to the Seventy, far more years had passed since the beginning of the world. Bede too, in his Chronicle, setting out the number of years according to the Hebrew truth, proves that seven years are lacking from the aforesaid sum."7 This is Bede's correction in The Reckoning of Time, carried into the schoolbook four and a half centuries later. Comestor dates events within Joshua's rule ("the fifth year had now passed"; "when the fourteenth year had now passed"), and he works out its length from Joshua's age. Joshua "was forty-four years old when he came to Moses, and served him forty years in the desert; from which it is gathered that he ruled the people across the Jordan twenty-six years." At the end of the book he adds an Incidentia, the events among the gentiles in the same years:

In diebus Josue Erictonius primus junxit quadrigam in Graecia, erat quippe apud alias nationes. Busiris tyrannidem exercuit in hospites. Phoenix et Cadmus de Thebis Aegyptiorum in Syriam profecti, in regione Tyri et Sidonis regnaverunt…

— "Incidentia" (PL 198, col. 1272B). In English: "In the days of Joshua, Erichthonius first yoked the four-horse chariot in Greece (it already existed among other nations). Busiris practised tyranny against strangers. Phoenix and Cadmus, setting out from Thebes of the Egyptians into Syria, reigned in the region of Tyre and Sidon…" The list goes on to Europa and to Danaus's fifty daughters. These are figures of Greek myth, placed in the same column of years as the conquest, following the chronicles of Eusebius and Jerome. For Comestor the conquest was one period of the world's single history, and its date could be set beside events in Greece and Phoenicia.

Notes

  • The text. The quotations follow Migne's Patrologia Latina 198, which reprints the Madrid edition of 1699. No critical edition of the Joshua section exists. Agneta Sylwan's CCCM 191 (2005) covers Genesis only, and Mark Clark has argued that the text as printed carries revisions by Stephen Langton, Comestor's successor in the Paris schools. The column references follow Migne's markers.
  • His sources in Joshua. Josephus is named at least six times (the weight of Achan's gold, the numbers at Ai, the blessings written on the altar, Shechem given to Joshua, the inscription on the Transjordan altar), and Augustine three times. "The Hebrews" are cited four times, Eusebius and Bede once each, and Jerome once, for the tombs of the eleven patriarchs he said he saw at Shechem. Comestor was the main channel by which Josephus reached the medieval Latin West, as on the pillars of Seth. Neither Isidore nor the Gloss is named, though the Historia was taught beside the Gloss.
  • Where he stands among the witnesses. On the three questions that divide the witnesses, Comestor answers the first by keeping to the letter alone. On the second, he narrows the sweeping formulas with the history of the fortresses and the distinction of land "granted" from land "handed over." The third, the moral problem of the ban, does not come up at all, and the only fault he finds is that Israel spared the Canaanites. A century later Thomas Aquinas took up the same literal sense and defended it as theology: "God is Lord of life and death." Comestor only tells the history and reconciles the text.
  • The Historia scholastica was read in the schools and turned into the vernacular "historiated Bibles" (the French Bible historiale of Guyart des Moulins, 1290s). Many lay readers in the later Middle Ages knew Joshua mainly in this form.
