Caesarius of Arles on the Conquest of Canaan

Dates: c. 542 (the sermons on Joshua, Sermons 114–116A in Morin's numbering, preached at Arles during his episcopate; Caesarius b. c. 470, bishop of Arles 502–542, d. 542) · Wikipedia
Summary: Caesarius, bishop of Arles in southern Gaul for forty years, preached on the lessons of the Old Testament as they were read in church, in plain Latin for a mixed congregation. Four of his sermons follow Joshua from the death of Moses to the fall of Jericho. Three of them re-preach Origen's Homilies on Joshua, often sentence for sentence: Joshua is "the true Josue or Jesus," the Jordan is baptism, Jericho is the world, its walls fall to the preaching of priests, Rahab is the Church, and the Canaanites are the vices within. The fourth, Sermon 114, does what Origen never did. It answers the moral objection on the level of the history. "Pagans and especially the most wicked Manichaeans" asked what justice there was in driving out the people of Canaan. Caesarius replies "according to the letter": the land had been Shem's, the Canaanites had seized it by force, and they had filled up the measure of their sins, so Israel "merely recovered their own country." Only then does he read the figure, and he says that all this "is, indeed, recorded as accomplished literally by the just judgment of God." He keeps Origen's history, and he adds a defence of it that Origen did not give.

Claims & evidence

Caesarius's sermons are cited here by the numbers of Germain Morin's critical edition and quoted in the English of Mary Magdeleine Mueller. Morin printed in full-size type the sermons he judged to be almost wholly Caesarius's own work; in reduced type those that show only a minimum of his work, such as an opening or a closing; and with a dagger those borrowed from other sources but reworked in his own style and thought. The four Joshua sermons are all in the first class. The Benedictine editors of Augustine had printed two of them, Sermons 114 and 115, among the sermons falsely attributed to Augustine (Appendix, Sermons 34 and 35 in Migne's text), and their Latin is checked below against that printing.1

The Manichaean objection answered "according to the letter"

(1) In the lessons which were read to us on Tuesday, we heard that, at the death of Moses, Josue received the rule. After crossing the Jordan, the people of Chanaan were driven out or killed, and their lands given into the possession of the children of Israel. As often as these lessons of the Old Testament are read, dearly beloved, pagans and especially the most wicked Manichaeans are wont to blaspheme and say with impious lips: What was the justice of God in removing the children of Israel from the land of Egypt by force, and afterwards driving out the people of Chanaan, in order to give their lands into the possession of Israel? So lest this wicked murmuring perchance deceive some simple or ignorant souls, with God's help, I wish as well as I can to briefly show your holiness the most true reason which has been handed down from the ancient fathers. I want to prove that our God is not only merciful but also just, and although His judgments are often hidden, they are never unjust.

(2) Our ancestors tell us that when the sons of Noe divided the whole earth among themselves, at this division of the world, the land which is called Chanaan came into the possession of the elder son of Noe, Sem, and he kept it for a considerable time. However, later the people of Chanaan who were from that son who had been cursed by his father, came to those regions and drove out by force their brethren who were the sons of Sem, holding their land like barbarians. Since blessed Abraham is read to have been of the race of Sem, his sons or the Israelites did not reach a strange land under the guidance of the Lord, but they merely recovered their own country which their fathers had lost through violence. Now the Chanaanites, besides that sin whereby they had attacked the possessions of another, had committed many indescribable and unheard of crimes, even to such an extent that when the Lord destroyed Sodom and Gomorrha we read that He said: 'The wickedness of the Amorrites is not yet complete.' Then the Lord waited for them to be converted and do penance or, if they refused to be converted, adding sin upon sin and filling the measure of sinning, at least that they should suffer the exceedingly just sentence of God. So the divine censure found them doubly guilty for invading the lands of another and afterwards committing sins and unheard of crimes, and, therefore, they are punished by a just judgment when the possessions of their ancient fathers are restored to the Jewish people. We thought these things should be explained to your charity according to the letter, as we find them written in the books of the saints. Here they are in a plain and simple sermon which everyone can understand, in order that you may have something to reply to the foul and wicked Manichaeans.

— Sermon 114.1–2, "That the Chanaanites were Driven from the Promised Land through the Just Judgment of God," trans. Mueller.2 This is the one place in the series where Caesarius takes up the moral problem of the conquest, and he takes it up directly. The objectors are named: pagans and Manichaeans, who, like the Marcionites before them, set the God of the Old Testament against the God of the Gospel. He states their question fairly and grants the facts it rests on: the Canaanites "were driven out or killed" (expulsis vel interfectis). He does not reply with a figure. He gives two reasons "according to the letter" (secundum litteram). The first is a property claim. Canaan fell to Shem at Noah's division of the earth, the Canaanites took it "by force" and held it "like barbarians" (more barbarico), and Israel, as Shem's heirs through Abraham, took back what was theirs. The second is guilt. The Canaanites added "unheard of crimes" to the theft, and God, as Genesis 15:16 shows, waited for their repentance until the measure of their sins was full. They were "doubly guilty." He frames the whole as a defence of God's justice: his judgments "are often hidden, they are never unjust" (plerumque sunt occulta, nunquam tamen injusta).

The first reason is not Origen's. His Homilies on Joshua do not use it. Caesarius says he has it "from the ancient fathers," and the story is older than Christian exegesis. The book of Jubilees tells how Canaan "saw the land of Lebanon to the river of Egypt, that it was very good," and settled there against the warning of his father and brothers: "Dwell not in the dwelling of Shem; for to Shem and to his sons did it come by their lot" (Jubilees 10:29–31). Epiphanius of Salamis used the same story in the 370s against the same opponents. He quotes Mani's charge that the God of the Law "expelled the Amorites, Girgashites and other nations, and gave their land to the children of Israel," and answers that "they had taken their own land back which had been seized from them," so that in Joshua's day "the sons of Shem took their own land with no wrong involved, but a putting to rights" (Panarion 66.83–84).3 The second reason, the Amorites' iniquity "not yet complete," is the one Eusebius, Augustine and Theodoret also give. Caesarius says nothing about the women and children of the Canaanite cities, or about whether all the dead shared the guilt. The children of Israel, he ends, "did not take what belonged to another as though by injustice."

"Recorded as accomplished literally," then the figure

(3) All this is, indeed, recorded as accomplished literally by the just judgment of God. However, since as the Apostle says: 'All these things happened to them as a type, and they are written for us, upon whom the final age of the world has come,' if you notice carefully, as is your custom under God's inspiration, you will also perceive very clearly what they signify spiritually. Before Adam sinned, we were the promised land which the Lord extols so many times as flowing with milk and honey, for then there was nothing in us except what the mercy of the Creator had bestowed. However, after we all sinned in Adam … through the transgression of the first man, the Chanaanites began to take possession of the promised land. … When through God's grace the possession of our soul is again restored to the virtues, we will be seen to recover our own land, rather than seizing what belongs to another.

— Sermon 114.3.4 The Latin of the opening sentence is Et haec quidem secundum litteram justo Dei judicio gesta referuntur: these things "are related as done, according to the letter, by the just judgment of God." The spiritual sense is built on the literal one and copies its logic. The Canaanites took Shem's land, and the vices took the soul after Adam's sin. Israel recovered its own country, and the virtues recover their own ground, "rather than seizing what belongs to another." The figure does not replace the property argument; it repeats it at another level. He goes on to read the conquest as grace. The land was restored "not through Moses, that is, the law of the Old Testament, but through Josue, the successor of Moses," and "the land of promise was not recovered before the River Jordan was crossed," because "a pure conscience, is not reached except through the sacrament of Baptism" (114.4–5).

Origen's sentence re-preached: "not so much … the exploits of the son of Nun"

(1) The lesson which was read to us just now, dearly beloved, does not so much preach to us the exploits of the son of Nun, as depict the mysteries of our Lord Jesus Christ, for He it is who received the rule after the death of Moses. Therefore, Moses died and Josue governed; the old law ceased, and the true Josue or Jesus ruled. Thus, indeed, the evangelist testifies: 'Until John came, there were the Law and the Prophets.' Moreover, that Moses is considered as the law we read in the Gospel: 'They have Moses and the Prophets,' that is, the law and the prophets …

— Sermon 116.1, "On Rahab the Harlot and the Two Messengers."5 This is Origen's governing sentence on the book, taken over with its proofs. In Rufinus's Latin, Origen's first homily on Joshua says that the book "does not so much set forth for us the deeds of the son of Nave as it depicts for us the mysteries of my Lord Jesus," and goes on: "'Moses died,' therefore; for the law ceased, since 'the law and the prophets were until John,'" proving from the Gospel's "They have Moses and the prophets" that "Moses is called the law" (Origen, Homilies on Joshua 1.3). Caesarius keeps the comparative "not so much … as." The exploits are ranked below the mysteries, not denied. The same sermon then compares the two crossings. Under Moses the people came through "salty waters" with "confusion or violence," and the older generation died in the desert. Under Joshua "the priests walked ahead and the ark of the testament was carried on their shoulders," and the people entered "the Jordan which was sweet and pleasant water," "as through the sacrament of Baptism."

The Jordan and Jericho: wonders that happened, read as baptism and preaching

(6) Now at the trumpets of the priests, the walls of Jericho which contained a sinful people fell to the ground. No army struck them, no military machine destroyed them, but remarkably enough the sound of priests and dread of a trumpet overcame them. Walls which had stood impregnable against weapons collapsed at the sacred sound of trumpets. Who would not be amazed at the fact that rocks were shattered by a sound, foundations shaken by the noise of irrational things? So complete was the destruction that, although the victors damaged nothing with their hands, there remained nothing standing among the adversary. Even though no one touched those walls, still the dwellings of the sinners inside were destroyed by the sound of the just outside; thus by the just judgment of God, a path was opened for the just, but defense was denied the wicked. Doubtless, all these things happened figuratively, for what else do we think the priestly trumpets of that time signified, but the preaching of priests in our day?

— Sermon 115.6, "Josue Bids the People be Ready to Cross the Jordan; also Something Concerning Rahab the Harlot and the Destruction of Jericho."6 Caesarius tells the fall of Jericho as a wonder that happened. The walls fell with no ram and no siege engine, and "who would not be amazed" that a sound broke stone? The city "contained a sinful people," and its fall was "the just judgment of God." Mueller's "all these things happened figuratively" renders haec omnia in figura facta esse, non dubium est: "there is no doubt that all these things were done as a figure." The phrase echoes 1 Corinthians 10:11, which he had quoted in Sermon 114. It says that the events were done so as to signify something. It does not say they were not done. The figure is Origen's: "Jericho holds the figure of the present age," whose strength and defences are destroyed "by the priestly trumpets" (Origen, Homilies on Joshua 7.1). Caesarius turns it to his own purpose, a long charge to his clergy not to be silent: "the trumpet is necessary for sinners." The Jordan crossing is handled the same way. "After this the Jordan was crossed just as if the mystery of Baptism were completed, and the people of Israel entered the promised land" (115.1). The twelve stones laid in the Jordan "typify the patriarchs," and the twelve taken out of it "prefigured the apostles" (115.3). He does not mention the sun's standing still at Gibeon in any of these sermons.

Rahab and the spies

(3) Now Josue sent spies to Jericho and they were received hospitably by a harlot. That harlot who welcomed the spies sent by Josue, that is, the apostles and doctors, received them for the very purpose of being a harlot no longer. That harlot, dearly beloved, prefigured the Church which had been wont to commit fornication with many idols before the advent of Jesus. However, at His coming Christ not only freed her from fornication, but by a great miracle even made her a virgin … Furthermore, the fact that they were put up on a balcony signifies that the Church does not usually receive the teaching of the apostles in lowly matters but in high ones, for with the Jews she does not follow the killing letter, but she receives the vivifying Spirit which comes down from heaven. In addition, the fact that the harlot covered those men with stalks of flax is fittingly applied to the Christian people, for flax is the covering of a priest …

— Sermon 116.3.7 Rahab is the Church gathered from idolatry, as she was for Origen, who read her name as "breadth" and made her "this Church of Christ, which has been gathered out of sinners as out of harlotry" (Homilies on Joshua 3.4). The roof becomes the "lofty and exalted mysteries" in Origen and the "high" teaching of the apostles in Caesarius. The king of Jericho, "that is, the devil," pursues the spies, and they escape "through the mountains," which, as in Origen's 3.5, means that they "do not walk through low places" (116.4). In Sermon 115 the two spies stand for the two commandments of love (115.2). The scarlet cord, which Clement, Justin, Origen and Ambrose all read as the blood of Christ, does not appear in these sermons. Neither does Rahab's lie, nor the sparing of her household when the city fell.

The war within: "just as we know the people then fought under Josue"

Therefore, if we wish to follow Jesus and desire to enter the land of true promise, we must fight against the opposing nations, just as we know the people then fought under Josue the son of Nun.

(5) However, our struggle, beloved brethren, is not to be waged outside but within; for we should fight within ourselves if we want to overcome our adversaries.

— Sermon 116.4–5. The contrast is Origen's, and Ambrose's after him: Israel fought outside, and the Christian fights within. As in Origen, the contrast assumes the outer war. The people "then fought under Josue the son of Nun," and "we know" it. The short Sermon 116A, which some manuscripts attach to the end of Sermon 116, develops the inner war:

Unless with God's assistance you expel in the same way the other vices within you from your land which was sanctified by the grace of Baptism, you will in no wise receive the fullness of the promised inheritance. Indeed, there are within us throngs of vices which continually and ceaselessly assault the soul: spiritual Chanaanites are within us, daily waging an internal war against us. … For this reason consider, dearly beloved, how we must labor and watch, how long we must persevere in good works, in order that all the nations of vice may be routed from us and our land freed from wars.

— Sermon 116A.1, "On the People of Chanaan."8 This is Origen almost word for word: "unless you conquer within yourself and exterminate them from your land, which has now been sanctified through the grace of baptism, in no way will you receive the fullness of the promised inheritance," and "For within us are all these nations of vices, which continually and unceasingly assail the soul. Within us are the 'Canaanites' … How we must labor, how we must keep watch, and for how long we must persevere" (Origen, Homilies on Joshua 1.6–7). Elsewhere Caesarius applies the warning of Exodus 34:12 the same way. "When the Lord handed over to you the land of the Chanaanites," he was told not to make a covenant with its people, and a Christian who makes a covenant with the sins banished at baptism finds that "this covenant will become a snare" (Sermon 81.4).

Achan, and the nations God left in the land

However, we ought to fear what is written concerning Achan, who had stolen the golden rule from Jericho. Because of one man's sin God's anger was vented upon all the people, nor did it leave them until the one who had committed the evil was stoned at the Lord's command.

— Sermon 42.2.9 Achan appears outside the Joshua series, in a sermon against adultery, as a warning to a congregation that tolerates a sin it knows about. One man's theft brought God's anger on all the people, and the stoning was "at the Lord's command." The point needs the story to be true. Caesarius does not mention that Achan's household was stoned with him (Joshua 7:24–25), the detail that Augustine and Jerome had to face.

He nowhere discusses the book's summary formulas, such as "Joshua took all the land," which Origen said could not be literally true. He does once explain why Canaanites were still in the land long after Joshua. Preaching on the plague in David's reign and the threshing floor of Araunah the Jebusite, he says:

Although all the nations in the surrounding country were subject to King David, still God did not allow the Jebusites to be destroyed entirely, as He Himself says elsewhere: 'I for my part will not clear away for them any more of the nations. Through them the Israelites were to be made to prove whether they would fear me.'

— Sermon 122.1. The answer is the one Judges itself gives (Judges 2:21–22): God left the remaining nations in the land to test Israel. It is a historical and providential answer, not a figure of speech. The figure follows. The pagan king Araunah "represented the people of the Gentiles," and the altar built on his land shows that "the conscience of Christians" would be chosen "as the place for the Lord's temple."

Where the letter fails: the "holy ground" of Joshua 5:15

Finally, see what the Lord said to Moses and Josue: 'Remove the strap of your shoe, for the place where you stand is holy ground.' Can this be understood according to the letter, beloved brethren? How could that ground upon which they trod be holy, since doubtless it was like the rest of the earth? However, notice carefully what was said: 'For the place whereon thou standest is holy ground.' That is to say, Christ whose figure you bear and of whom you seem to be a type, is holy ground.

— Sermon 96.4, "On the Bush and the Lace of the Shoe."10 Caesarius has Origen's rule that a letter which seems impossible sends the reader to the spirit, and this is the one place he applies it to Joshua. The meeting with "the captain of the host of the Lord" before Jericho (Joshua 5:14) is told as an event. What he cannot take literally is a single phrase, that ordinary ground was "holy." Like Ambrose, who applied the rule to the law of the cities of refuge, he does not apply it to the wars.

Notes

  • Caesarius and Origen. Three of the four Joshua sermons are built from Origen's Homilies on Joshua in Rufinus's Latin: the governing sentence of 1.3, the inner war of 1.6–7, Rahab from Homily 3, and Jericho's trumpets from Homily 7. Caesarius cuts them down and adds his own exhortations. What he keeps is Origen's structure, history first and figure built on it, and Origen's refusal to let the wars serve the Church as a pattern. What he adds is Sermon 114, a literal answer to the moral objection, where Origen answered the heretics with the figure ("not teaching cruelty thereby, as the heretics suppose"). He also drops Origen's denials of particular formulas, such as "Jesus took all the land." Compare Origen of Alexandria on the Conquest of Canaan, and Ambrose of Milan, the other Latin bishop who passed on Origen's reading.
  • The moral question. Sermon 114 belongs with the retributive answer given by Eusebius, Augustine and Theodoret: the Canaanites were punished when their sins were "complete." Caesarius adds a second, legal reason, that the land was Shem's and Israel took back its own. That argument comes from Jewish tradition (Jubilees) and from Epiphanius's reply to Mani, not from the Latin exegetes before him. The patristic answers are weighed at Do the patristic defenses answer the moral problem of the ḥerem? On Noah's curse, which Caesarius invokes when he calls Canaan "that son who had been cursed by his father," see the curse of Ham; on Shem's lot, Shem.
  • Rahab. Caesarius reads Rahab as the Church made a virgin, but without the scarlet cord. See the Rahab timeline and the scarlet-cord typology.
  • Translation. The sermons are quoted in Mueller's translation (Fathers of the Church 31 and 47). Her biblical quotations follow the Douay and Confraternity versions where Caesarius's Latin allows, so the names are "Josue," "Chanaan" and "Sem." The Latin of Sermons 114 and 115 is checked against Migne's reprint of the Benedictine text; Morin's critical text was not available for this page.
  • Caesarius appears on this wiki also as a witness on the Naaman, purgatory, predestination and anointing of the sick timelines.
