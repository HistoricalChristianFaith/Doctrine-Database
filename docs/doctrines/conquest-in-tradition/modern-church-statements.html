Modern Church Statements on the Conquest of Canaan

Dates: c. 1965–2014 (Vatican II, Dei Verbum, 1965; the Chicago Statements on Biblical Inerrancy, 1978, and on Biblical Hermeneutics, 1982; the Pontifical Biblical Commission, The Inspiration and Truth of Sacred Scripture, 2014) · Wikipedia: Dei Verbum · Chicago Statement · Pontifical Biblical Commission
Summary: This page is a collective witness. It records four modern church documents that bear on how Joshua's history is to be read, and it records where they part ways. Vatican II's Dei Verbum (1965) names no passage of Joshua. It teaches that Scripture teaches "without error that truth which God wanted put into sacred writings for the sake of salvation." It directs interpreters to "literary forms," and it says the Old Testament books "contain some things which are incomplete and temporary." The evangelical Chicago Statements (1978, 1982) also name no passage of Joshua. They deny that inerrancy is limited to "spiritual, religious, or redemptive themes, exclusive of assertions in the fields of history and science." They affirm that "the biblical record of events … corresponds to historical fact." The Pontifical Biblical Commission (2014) addresses Joshua directly. It says the conquest narratives "do not present the characteristics of a historical account," since "the walls of a city do not collapse at the sound of trumpets." It reads the conquest "as a kind of parable" and the ban by a "non-literal interpretation." It also holds that salvation history "does not exist without a historical nucleus." By its own preface it is not an official statement of the Magisterium, just as the Chicago signers declined to give their statement "creedal weight."

Claims & evidence

Dei Verbum (1965): truth "for the sake of salvation," read through "literary forms"

Therefore, since everything asserted by the inspired authors or sacred writers must be held to be asserted by the Holy Spirit, it follows that the books of Scripture must be acknowledged as teaching solidly, faithfully and without error that truth which God wanted put into sacred writings for the sake of salvation.

— Second Vatican Council, Dei Verbum §11.1 The Council's constitution on revelation does not mention Joshua or the conquest. It bears on them through two general rules. The first is the scope of biblical truth in §11. The second is the instruction in §12 to attend to genre: "To search out the intention of the sacred writers, attention should be given, among other things, to 'literary forms.' For truth is set forth and expressed differently in texts which are variously historical, prophetic, poetic, or of other forms of discourse." In the same section the interpreter must weigh "the customary and characteristic styles of feeling, speaking and narrating which prevailed at the time of the sacred writer." §12 also requires that Scripture be read as a whole and "in the sacred spirit in which it was written," with "the living tradition of the whole Church" taken into account.

Dei Verbum (1965): the Old Testament as "true divine pedagogy"

Now the books of the Old Testament, in accordance with the state of mankind before the time of salvation established by Christ, reveal to all men the knowledge of God and of man and the ways in which God, just and merciful, deals with men. These books, though they also contain some things which are incomplete and temporary, nevertheless show us true divine pedagogy.

— Dei Verbum §15.2 Chapter 4 of the constitution sets out how the Church reads the Old Testament. §14 speaks of God's covenant "with Abraham" and "through Moses, with the people of Israel," to whom "He so manifested Himself through words and deeds as the one true and living God that Israel came to know by experience the ways of God with men." §15 says the old covenant was directed "to prepare for the coming of Christ" and "to indicate its meaning through various types." The phrase "incomplete and temporary" is footnoted to Pius XI's encyclical Mit brennender Sorge (1937). §16 states the patristic rule that "the New Testament be hidden in the Old and the Old be made manifest in the New." The Council does not say which things in the Old Testament are "incomplete and temporary." The 2014 Biblical Commission document (below) quotes the phrase when it turns to the ban.

The Chicago Statement on Biblical Inerrancy (1978): inerrancy reaches "history and science"

We affirm that Scripture in its entirety is inerrant, being free from all falsehood, fraud, or deceit.

We deny that Biblical infallibility and inerrancy are limited to spiritual, religious, or redemptive themes, exclusive of assertions in the fields of history and science. We further deny that scientific hypotheses about earth history may properly be used to overturn the teaching of Scripture on creation and the flood.

— Chicago Statement on Biblical Inerrancy, Article XII.3 The statement came out of a three-day consultation in Chicago in October 1978, the first summit of the International Council on Biblical Inerrancy. It is not the confession of any one church. Its preface says the signers "do not propose that this Statement be given creedal weight." It does not mention Joshua. Its fourth summary point extends inerrancy to "the events of world history": Scripture "is without error or fault in all its teaching, no less in what it states about God's acts in creation, about the events of world history, and about its own literary origins under God, than in its witness to God's saving grace in individual lives." Article XIII sets limits on what counts as error. It denies "that inerrancy is negated by Biblical phenomena such as a lack of modern technical precision, … the use of hyperbole and round numbers, the topical arrangement of material, variant selections of material in parallel accounts, or the use of free citations." The exposition puts the rule in a sentence: "So history must be treated as history, poetry as poetry, hyperbole and metaphor as hyperbole and metaphor, generalization and approximation as what they are, and so forth."

The Chicago Statement on Biblical Hermeneutics (1982): the record of events "corresponds to historical fact"

We affirm that awareness of the literary categories, formal and stylistic, of the various parts of Scripture is essential for proper exegesis, and hence we value genre criticism as one of the many disciplines of biblical study.

We deny that generic categories which negate historicity may rightly be imposed on biblical narratives which present themselves as factual.

We affirm that the biblical record of events, discourses and sayings, though presented in a variety of appropriate literary forms, corresponds to historical fact.

We deny that any event, discourse or saying reported in Scripture was invented by the biblical writers or by the traditions they incorporated.

— Chicago Statement on Biblical Hermeneutics, Articles XIII–XIV.4 The second summit (November 1982) turned from the doctrine of inerrancy to its interpretation. Like the Vatican document, Article XIII values attention to genre. Unlike it, it denies that a genre "which negate[s] historicity" may be "imposed on biblical narratives which present themselves as factual." Article XIV makes the claim general: every event the Bible reports "corresponds to historical fact," and none "was invented by the biblical writers or by the traditions they incorporated." The statement names only one block of narrative, in Article XXII: "We affirm that Genesis 1-11 is factual, as is the rest of the book." Joshua is not named. Its narrative falls under Article XIV's "biblical record of events."

The Pontifical Biblical Commission (2014): the conquest traditions "do not intend to offer a historical chronicle"

Il racconto di Gen 1–11, le tradizioni sui patriarchi e sulla conquista della terra di Israele, le storie dei re fino alla rivolta dei Maccabei contengono certamente delle verità, ma non intendono proporre una cronaca storica del popolo di Israele. Il protagonista nella storia della salvezza non è né Israele, né altri uomini, ma è Dio. I racconti biblici sono narrazioni teologizzate.

— Pontifical Biblical Commission, Ispirazione e verità della Sacra Scrittura §104 (official Italian text).5 In English (translated here): "The account of Gen 1–11, the traditions about the patriarchs and about the conquest of the land of Israel, and the stories of the kings down to the Maccabean revolt certainly contain truths, but they do not intend to offer a historical chronicle of the people of Israel. The protagonist in the history of salvation is neither Israel nor other men, but God. The biblical stories are theologized narratives." Their truth, the paragraph goes on, "is drawn from the facts narrated, but above all from the didactic, paraenetic and theological purpose" of the author. The next paragraph sets a limit: "a 'history of salvation' does not exist without a historical nucleus" (non esiste senza un nucleo storico), since God reveals himself "through deeds and words" (Dei Verbum §2). And because inspiration covers Scripture "with all their parts," "we cannot eliminate any passage from the narrative." The same paragraph reads Dei Verbum §11's "for the sake of salvation" as restricting "biblical truth to the divine revelation that concerns God himself and the salvation of the human race" (§105). §106 adds that "most exegetes" date the final redaction of the accounts "of the Exodus, of the conquest and of the Judges" after the Babylonian exile, in the Persian period.

The Pontifical Biblical Commission (2014): the ban, the walls of Jericho, and "a kind of parable"

Fin dall’inizio è necessario far notare che queste narrazioni non presentano i caratteri del resoconto storico: infatti, in una guerra reale, le mura di una città non crollano al suono delle trombe (Gs 6,20), né si vede come possa realmente avvenire una pacifica distribuzione delle terre mediante sorteggio (Gs 14,2). D’altro canto, la normativa del Deuteronomio che prescrive lo sterminio dei Cananei prende forma scritta in un momento storico in cui tali popolazioni non erano più identificabili in terra di Israele. Si impone perciò la necessità di riconsiderare accuratamente il genere letterario di queste tradizioni narrative. Come già avevano suggerito i migliori interpreti della tradizione patristica, il racconto dell’epopea della conquista va visto come una sorta di parabola, che mette in scena personaggi dal valore simbolico; la legge dello sterminio, dal canto suo, esige una interpretazione non letterale, così come si fa d’altronde per il comando del Signore di tagliarsi la mano o cavarsi un occhio se sono occasione di scandalo (Mt 5,29; 18,9).

— Ispirazione e verità della Sacra Scrittura §127, under the heading "La legge dello sterminio" ("The law of extermination").6 In English (translated here): "From the outset it must be pointed out that these narratives do not present the characteristics of a historical account: in a real war, in fact, the walls of a city do not collapse at the sound of trumpets (Josh 6:20), nor is it clear how a peaceful distribution of lands by lot could really take place (Josh 14:2). On the other hand, the norm in Deuteronomy that prescribes the extermination of the Canaanites takes written form at a historical moment when those peoples were no longer identifiable in the land of Israel. Hence the need to reconsider carefully the literary genre of these narrative traditions. As the best interpreters of the patristic tradition had already suggested, the story of the epic of the conquest is to be seen as a kind of parable, which stages characters of symbolic value; the law of extermination, for its part, requires a non-literal interpretation, as is done, after all, for the Lord's command to cut off one's hand or pluck out one's eye if they are an occasion of sin (Matt 5:29; 18:9)."

The section is the only place among these documents that takes up Joshua by chapter. It opens by saying that God commands the ban in Deuteronomy (7:1–2; 20:16–18), that "the order is faithfully carried out by Joshua (Josh 6–12)," and that the whole complex is "highly problematic." It says that to use it as "a programme of nationalist political conduct, in justification of violence against other peoples, is in any case to be condemned without qualification." It then gives a reading of the narrative's sense in three steps. First, it rejects one defense: the appeal to "God's right to distribute the land, favouring his elect, is certainly not convincing, because it disregards the legitimate claim of the native populations." Second, it finds meaning in the story. The weak defeat the strong. The Canaanites are "held by God guilty of very grave crimes (Gen 15:16; Lev 18:3, 24–30; 20:23; Deut 9:4–5)," including killing "their own children in perverted rituals." Joshua is "servant of the Lord" as "executor of justice." Third, it reads the ban as "a duty of justice, analogous to the prosecution, condemnation and execution of one guilty of a capital crime, whether an individual or a collectivity." It concludes that "the apparently violent act is to be interpreted as concern to remove evil, so as to safeguard the common good." It adds that this "literary current is corrected by others – among them the one called priestly – which, concerning the same events, suggest instead orientations of explicit pacifism." The section closes: "we must understand the whole story of the conquest as a kind of symbol, analogous to what we read in certain Gospel parables of judgment (Matt 13:30, 41–43, 50; 25:30, 41)."

The Pontifical Biblical Commission (2014): "not an official declaration of the Magisterium"

Il presente documento della Commissione Biblica non costituisce una dichiarazione ufficiale del Magistero della Chiesa sull’argomento, né intende esporre una dottrina completa sull’ispirazione e sulla verità della Sacra Scrittura, ma soltanto riportare i risultati di un attento studio esegetico dei testi biblici circa la loro provenienza da Dio e la loro verità.

— Cardinal Gerhard Müller, president of the Commission, preface to Ispirazione e verità della Sacra Scrittura (22 February 2014).7 In English (translated here): "The present document of the Biblical Commission does not constitute an official declaration of the Magisterium of the Church on the subject, nor does it intend to set out a complete doctrine on the inspiration and truth of Sacred Scripture, but only to report the results of a careful exegetical study of the biblical texts concerning their origin from God and their truth." The Commission is a body of scholars that advises the Holy See. The document's standing is therefore lower than that of Dei Verbum, a conciliar constitution, and its preface says so.

Notes

  • Where the documents diverge. The Chicago Statements and the 2014 Commission document both value attention to genre, and both say the conquest narrative carries truth. They differ on whether its events are to be read as reported fact. Chicago Hermeneutics Article XIV affirms that "the biblical record of events … corresponds to historical fact." The Commission says the conquest narratives "do not present the characteristics of a historical account." They also differ on the scope of biblical truth. Chicago Inerrancy Article XII denies that inerrancy is "limited to spiritual, religious, or redemptive themes." The Commission (§105) reads Dei Verbum §11 as restricting biblical truth "to the divine revelation that concerns God himself and the salvation of the human race."
  • The appeal to the Fathers. The Commission grounds its "parable" reading in "the best interpreters of the patristic tradition." It does not name them. The Fathers' own handling of the book is recorded on this timeline: Origen, who builds his figurative reading on top of a history he calls history, and Augustine, who defends the wars as historical and just. The moral question the Commission takes up is weighed at Do the patristic defenses answer the moral problem of the ḥerem? (assessment: contested).
  • The material record. The Commission's remark that Deuteronomy's law took written form when the Canaanite peoples "were no longer identifiable" bears on the dating questions weighed on the conquest in the material record, including the Jericho dating argument, the early date and the late date.
  • Where this stands on the timeline. These documents follow John Calvin (1564), whose last commentary reads Joshua as history throughout. The earliest layer is the New Testament, which recites the conquest as one link in Israel's history. « Back to the Conquest in Christian Tradition timeline
