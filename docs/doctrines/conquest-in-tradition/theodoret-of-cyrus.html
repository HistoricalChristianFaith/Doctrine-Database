Theodoret of Cyrus on the Conquest of Canaan

Dates: c. 453 (the Questions on the Octateuch, the Questions on Joshua; Theodoret b. c. 393, bishop of Cyrus from 423, d. c. 458/466) · Wikipedia
Summary: Theodoret, bishop of Cyrus in Syria and the last great exegete of the Antiochene school, wrote his Questions on the Octateuch late in life, when, he says, his body was "not in good health." Twenty questions on Joshua answer the puzzles a reader meets in the book. He reads it as history first. The Jordan was held back by the Creator "more than any wall of adamant." Jericho fell "without weapons or siege-engines." The war lasted seven years, a figure he works out from Caleb's age. Then he sets a figure of Christ beside the history, as he says Paul did, "not casting out the history." He answers the charge of cruelty against Joshua briefly. Whoever accuses the prophet accuses God, who commanded it, and the Canaanites had "done deeds deserving total destruction," like the generation of the Flood and Sodom. He explains the unconquered land historically: the promise was conditional and Israel broke the law. He treats the sun at Gibeon as a real miracle done "by a word alone." From the reference to an older book he concludes that a later writer composed Joshua from earlier sources.

Claims & evidence

The Questions survive in Greek. No English translation of the Questions on Joshua is freely available online, so the passages below are translated here from the Greek text printed in Migne's Patrologia Graeca 80. The Greek of each passage is given in the footnotes. The question numbers follow that text. They agree with the numbering of the critical edition (Fernández Marcos and Sáenz-Badillos, 1979) and of Robert C. Hill's English translation (2007). Where Hill's own English is available, it is quoted as his.1

The type is set beside the history, "not casting out the history"

The all-wise Paul taught us that the old was a type of the new. … And he wrote this not casting out the history, but setting the type beside the reality: Abraham beside God, the women beside the two covenants, the sons beside the peoples. So here too we must understand Moses as the law, and Jesus as the Saviour who bears the same name. … Just as, according to the history, when Moses had died, Jesus led the people into the promised land, so after the end of the law our Jesus, having appeared, opened the kingdom of heaven to the godly people.

— Questions on Joshua, preface (translated here).2 Theodoret opens with Paul's allegory of Hagar and Sarah (Galatians 4:24) and 1 Corinthians 10:11, "these things happened to them as types." He reads Paul's method as comparison, not replacement. Hill renders the key clause: Paul "wrote this, not to deny the historical facts, but to compare the type to the reality." The history comes first ("according to the history … Jesus led the people into the promised land"). The figure of Christ is then laid alongside it. The shared Greek name Iēsous carries the typology, as it did for Origen. In Question 18 Theodoret calls Joshua "the namesake Jesus," a "type" of Christ, because he took no inheritance for himself. Of his typology in the Questions generally, the editors of the translation write that it is "not the sort of allegory the Antiochenes attributed to Origen, because Theodoret still gives predominant attention to the literal sense, which is not subsumed into a spiritual interpretation."

The Jordan and Jericho as miracles that happened

Symmachus rendered "congealed mass" as "swelling." For the current of the waters, held back, swelled up as it were and rose into a peak; for the boundary set by the Creator held it back more than any wall of adamant. And the crossing took place before the Passover: for having crossed on the tenth, they celebrated the feast of the Passover on the fourteenth.

Why did he order the city of Jericho to be put under the ban? This was the first city they took after crossing the river. So, just as he had commanded them to offer the firstfruits of their crops, so he ordered them also to offer the first portion of the spoils. … And displaying his own power to them, he arranged for them to take the first city without weapons or siege-engines, and he brought it down by the sound of the trumpets alone; so that whenever they were defeated in battle they would know that they themselves had supplied the occasion of the defeat, by despising the divine laws.

— Questions on Joshua 2 and 7 (translated here).3 At the Jordan Theodoret compares the Greek versions, as he often does, and describes the piled-up water physically. He dates the crossing to the tenth of the month, before the Passover on the fourteenth (Joshua 4:19; 5:10). The miracle shows Joshua to be "a man dear to God," as Jesus after his baptism in the Jordan "made the beginning of his teaching and his miracles." He explains Jericho's ban as the firstfruits of the spoil, and the trumpets as a lesson for later defeats. In Question 9 he notes that the curse on anyone who rebuilt Jericho (Joshua 6:26) is recorded again in "the history of Kingdoms" (1 Kings 16:34). That fulfilment ties the conquest into Israel's later history.

Practical explanations, then the figure

Why did he order them to be circumcised with knives of stone? The number being circumcised was very great, and they had to be circumcised quickly and celebrate the feast of the Passover. It was likely, then, that people who had spent so long a time in the desert did not have many knives made of iron. Besides, the stone knives also prefigure our circumcision: for the rock is Christ the Lord.

Why did God order an ambush to be laid for Gai? He teaches them to use human stratagems too, while trusting, of course, in the divine support. Since they had taken the first city by the sound of the trumpets alone, they very reasonably learn to struggle and toil and to wait for the divine help.

— Questions on Joshua 4 and 11 (translated here).4 The order is typical. A practical reason comes first: there were too many to circumcise and no iron knives in the desert. The type follows as an addition ("besides"). Several answers stay entirely on the historical level. In Question 10 he identifies the "valley of Achor" in the prophets (Hosea 2:15; Isaiah 65:10) as the place where Achan was stoned, "for Emekachor is translated 'ravine of Achor.'" There he cites Aquila and Josephus on the stolen garment. In Question 17 he works out the length of the war from Caleb's words in Joshua 14:7–10. Caleb was forty when sent from Kadesh and is eighty-five now. Israel spent thirty-eight years in the desert after the sentence on the six hundred thousand, so "they divided the land after fighting for seven years." He also notes that Joshua calls Jerusalem "Jebus" and assigns it to Benjamin. The conquest thus gets a length and a place in the sequence of Israel's history.

The ban: "he who accuses the prophet accuses the one who commanded"

Some accuse the prophet of cruelty, because he destroyed all utterly and crucified the kings. He who accuses the prophet accuses the one who commanded these things. For he himself, through Moses the lawgiver, commanded that all who inhabited that land be utterly destroyed, since they had done deeds deserving total destruction and had gone to the extreme of lawlessness. For this reason he had also brought the flood long ago, and consumed Sodom and Gomorrah with fire. And the prophet ordered the commanders to set their feet on the necks of the kings, so that, taking courage, they might fight more eagerly. This our Lord Jesus also commanded: "Behold, I have given you authority to tread upon serpents and scorpions and over all the power of the enemy." May we too, then, set our feet on the necks of the opposing spirits.

— Questions on Joshua 12 (translated here).5 This is Theodoret's only direct answer to the moral charge. It has three parts. The responsibility belongs to God, who gave the command in Deuteronomy. The Canaanites deserved destruction for their lawlessness. The Flood and Sodom are earlier judgments of the same kind. Only after that does he draw a lesson for Christians, and it is about spiritual enemies, not human ones. He does not deny the slaughter, and he does not turn it into allegory. Question 15, on the hardening of the Canaanites' hearts (Joshua 11:20), adds a reason why none were spared. The law let Israel accept peace from a besieged city that asked for it. So God, "who governs all things by weight and measure, permitted these to array themselves against the people, so that they would be granted no mercy but be destroyed according to the divine decree." He reads the hardening as God's permission. Question 13, on the Gibeonites, shows how literally he takes the command. They deceived Israel "both by their appearance and by the falsehood of their words." Joshua had sworn without asking God, yet "the God of all had commanded that all those people be put to death." Joshua was therefore "caught between the divine commandment and the oaths." He made them temple servants, which fulfilled Noah's curse on Canaan (Genesis 9:25). In the Questions on Numbers Theodoret calls the Gibeonite treaty a "fault" of Joshua's, because he received them "before asking."

The sun at Gibeon: a real miracle, and a later author

What is the meaning of "Is this not written in the book that was found?" Having taught us the power of the prophet, that by using a word alone he prevented the great luminaries from advancing until he had won a decisive victory, the writer, suspecting that someone might disbelieve the account, said that he had found this in the ancient writing. It is clear from this too that someone else, one of those who came later, composed this book, taking his material from another book. This also prefigures the Saviour's wonder-working: for as the sun stood still while the prophet was fighting, so, when our Saviour was destroying death by death, the sun withheld its rays and filled the world with darkness at midday.

— Questions on Joshua 14 (translated here).6 Theodoret's Greek Bible read Joshua 10:13 as "the book that was found," where the Hebrew names the Book of Jashar. He takes the miracle as fact: the "great luminaries" stopped at Joshua's word. He offers no natural-philosophical discussion of how. The translation's editors note that "texts that might seem sensational, such as the sun stopping in its course in Jos 10.12f., do not attract particular attention." His interest is in the sentence about the book. Hill renders it: "the author of the book of Joshua lived in a subsequent age and drew his source material from that other book." The editors say that here "he develops a limited documentary hypothesis of his own," and that "it is only in Joshua that he comes to acknowledge layers in a composite text." For him a later author working from an older record is still writing history. The citation is there because the writer expected readers to doubt, and he gave them his source. The darkness at the crucifixion is added as a type.

The unconquered land: a conditional promise, not a figure of speech

Why did God, having promised to give them every place on which the sole of their feet should tread, and the desert, and Antilebanon as far as the great river Euphrates, not fulfil the promise? The text is clear and needs little interpretation. … It is likely, then, that they did not set foot on the rest of the land while Jesus son of Nave was in command. But what follows resolves the question entirely. For he added, "as I said to Moses"; and he did not make the promise to him without condition, but "if you keep my commandments and my ordinances." So they, having transgressed the law straightaway, did not obtain the promise in full. But the divine apostles took possession not only of the places on which they set foot, but also of those in which their all-wise writings were read.

— Questions on Joshua 2 (translated here).7 The gap between the promise of "all the land" and the land Israel actually held was a problem for every reader of Joshua. Origen took the sweeping formulas as signals pointing to a spiritual sense. Theodoret gives a historical answer: "the text is clear." The promise was conditional, and Israel broke the condition. The apostles then fulfil it in a larger way. At the end of Question 19, on Joshua's farewell, he returns to the point: "We have learned also the reason why they did not receive dominion over the whole land according to the divine promise. For having embraced kinship with the impious nations and imitated their impiety, they were stripped of divine providence and were compelled to serve them." In Question 16 the Jebusites and Canaanites who "were left and lived together with Israel" stand for the Jews, pagans, and heretics who still live in "the same cities and villages as we." Even here the type rests on a historical fact that he accepts.

Rahab: faith, and the scarlet cord as the Church

So too Rahab was formerly a harlot, but she received the spies faithfully, and when the king ordered them handed over she did not yield but hid them; for she believed in the God of the Hebrews.

— Questions on Joshua 2 (translated here).8 Theodoret quotes her confession in full (Joshua 2:9–11) as proof of her faith, and adds that the God of the Hebrews had become "known to all" through what he did to Pharaoh. He does not discuss her lie to the king's men. The type is the one he gives in his commentary on Hebrews. As the spies saved "the harlot who had believed," giving her the scarlet cord as a sign, so the apostles saved the Church, once a harlot devoted to idols, "not using a scarlet cord as a sign, but procuring salvation by the all-holy blood." Question 8 asks why a foreigner lived among Israel "as the history teaches." He answers that she prefigures the Gentiles, the "other sheep" of John 10:16, grafted in like a wild olive.

Notes

  • Antioch beside Alexandria. Theodoret and Origen share the Joshua–Jesus typology and many of the same figures (the twelve stones as the apostles, the cord as the blood of Christ, the remaining Canaanites as unbelievers). Where they differ is the letter. Origen reads the book's sweeping formulas as signals pointing to a spiritual sense. Theodoret explains them historically, from the conditional promise and Israel's sin, and adds the figure afterward. Compare also Augustine's Questions on the Heptateuch, written a generation earlier in the same question-and-answer form, and Jerome.
  • The ḥerem. Theodoret's Question 12 answers the charge of cruelty with the divine command and the Canaanites' guilt, like Augustine, but in a few lines and with no theory of just war. See the moral problem of the ḥerem and the patristic answers, which weighs this retributive, divine-command answer as the one most witnesses give. Theodoret is its Greek witness, alongside Eusebius.
  • The material record. Jericho taken "without weapons or siege-engines" (Jericho's dating), Gai/Ai and its ambush (Ai's identification), and the seven-year war he works out from Caleb's age all bear on the arguments of the conquest in the material record; on the dating of the whole, see the early date and the late date.
  • Rahab. See Theodoret of Cyrus on Rahab, the Rahab timeline, and the scarlet-cord typology.
