Ephrem the Syrian on the Conquest of Canaan

Dates: c. 370 (the Commentary on Genesis, written at Edessa, 363–373, and the Commentary on the Diatessaron; Ephrem b. c. 306 at Nisibis, d. 373) · Wikipedia
Summary: Ephrem, deacon of Nisibis and Edessa and the great poet of the Syriac Church, comes to Joshua in two kinds of text, which must be kept apart. His undoubted prose works mention the conquest only in passing, but both treat it as history. The Commentary on Genesis takes Noah's curse, "let Canaan be their slave," to have been fulfilled "in the days of Joshua bar-Nun," when "the Israelites destroyed the dwelling-places of [Canaan] and pressed their leaders into bondage." The Commentary on the Diatessaron counts the kings: "Moses killed only three kings, but Joshua killed thirty." A running commentary on Joshua under Ephrem's name was printed in the Roman edition of his works (1737). It comes from a ninth-century Syriac catena that mixes Ephrem with later writers, so how much of it is his own is uncertain, and it is read here in its editor's free Latin. It gives the fullest answer to the moral problem of the ban that any Syriac source on this timeline gives. The Canaanites, it says, held the land forty years after it was promised "while some hope of repentance shone." Jericho was warned seven times by the seven days' march and "did not repent." Israel "strove for perhaps thirty years to bring about the conversion of the Canaanites," and they "did not perish undeservedly."

Claims & evidence

The undoubted works: Noah's curse fulfilled, and the thirty kings

After Ham had been cursed through his one son, [Noah] blessed Shem and Japhet and said, "May God increase Japhet and may He dwell in the tent of Shem, and let Canaan be their slave." Japhet increased and became powerful in his inheritance in the north and in the west. And God dwelt in the tent of Abraham, the descendant of Shem, and Canaan became their slave when in the days of Joshua bar-Nun, the Israelites destroyed the dwelling-places of [Canaan] and pressed their leaders into bondage.

— Commentary on Genesis 7.4, trans. Edward G. Mathews Jr. and Joseph P. Amar.1 The Commentary on Genesis survives complete in Syriac and is certainly Ephrem's. On Genesis 9 he argues that it was Canaan himself, not his father Ham, who earned the curse: "Canaan was cursed because of his jesting and Ham was deprived of the blessing because of his laughter" (7.3). Then he follows each blessing and curse to its fulfilment in history. The curse on Canaan came true in the conquest. The rule of Joshua is the event that makes Noah's words a prophecy, and Ephrem states it as a plain fact of Israel's past. He does not use the curse to defend the conquest. The Church's long use of this curse is traced on the curse of Ham timeline.

Whoever believes in me will also do the works which I do, and will do even greater ones. And where is this word which he said, The disciple is not greater than his master, [illustrated]? For example, Moses killed only three kings, but Joshua killed thirty. [Moses] persevered in prayer, made supplication, but did not enter [the promised land]. It was Joshua rather who entered and shared out the inheritance. Likewise, Samuel was greater than Eli, and Elisha received a double portion of his master's spirit after his ascension, like the Lord our Saviour, for his disciples effected twice through their signs.

— Commentary on the Diatessaron 19.8, trans. Carmel McCarthy.2 Ephrem's commentary on Tatian's harmony of the Gospels survives in Armenian and in a partial Syriac text. Here, on John 14:12, he needs an example of a disciple who did more than his master, and he finds it in the number of kings each man killed. The figures are round: Joshua 12:24 counts thirty-one kings. The killing of the Canaanite kings is offered as a mark of Joshua's greatness, with no hint that it raises a question. Elsewhere, in the hymns on the faith, Ephrem names Rahab as a model of repentance: "our repentance is from Nineveh and the house of Rahab" (The Pearl 7.1). She has a timeline of her own.

The Roman edition's commentary on Joshua

Ephrem wrote commentaries on most of the Old Testament, but only those on Genesis and part of Exodus survive whole in Syriac. For the other books the Roman edition of his works, edited by the Assemani family (1732–1746), printed excerpts from a Syriac catena compiled in the ninth century by the monk Severus of Edessa. The catena gathers Ephrem's comments together with those of later writers, chiefly Jacob of Edessa (d. 708), and the excerpts cannot all be assigned with confidence. The Latin version that accompanies the Syriac, made by Pietro Benedetti (Butrus Mubarak), is, in the words of a modern translator of Ephrem, "overwhelmingly paraphrastic." The passages below are translated here from that Latin.3 They show how the Syriac tradition received under Ephrem's name read Joshua. They are not a secure witness to Ephrem's own words.

Rahab: the land held "while some hope of repentance shone"

"I know that the Lord has given you the land, for your terror has fallen upon us, and the inhabitants of this land have grown faint." As if she said: I am well aware that the land we inhabit began to belong to you forty years ago, though we have been allowed to hold it until now, while some hope of repentance shone. But since I see that this too has been taken away, I do not doubt that a small band of your men, if only they will act in earnest, will subdue the whole dominion of the Canaanites: so plainly is it seen that heaven, earth, the lower and the upper regions obey your God at his nod.

— Roman-edition commentary on Joshua 2:9 (translated here from the Latin).4 The commentator puts the theology of the conquest into Rahab's mouth. The land had been Israel's since the Exodus, forty years before. The Canaanites kept it in the meantime because God was waiting for them to repent. That time is now over. This is the old Jewish answer of Wisdom 12:10, that God judged the Canaanites "little by little" and "gave them a place of repentance," applied to the forty years in the wilderness. A note to the nineteenth-century English translation of Ephrem's hymns quotes this comment as Ephrem's: "This forty years is this land yours; and now it is that we might repent, that we have continued in it up to this day."

Jericho: warned seven times, and "they did not repent"

"Cursed be the man who builds the city of Jericho." Joshua pronounced this sentence that day against a man to be born many ages later. But what was the cause of so dire a curse? First, because the people of Jericho had watched the Jordan divided with their own eyes for many hours, and did not for that lay down their arms in reverence for the power of God made plain by so great a miracle. Second, because, warned seven times, they despised God, who was warning them and deliberately drawing out the judgment over many days, and they did not repent.

— Roman-edition commentary on Joshua 6:26 (translated here from the Latin).5 The seven days of marching round Jericho become seven warnings. God "deliberately" drew out the judgment so that the city could repent, and it did not. Its people had also seen the Jordan stand still "for many hours" and still did not lay down their arms. The same comment on Joshua 6:3 explains why Israel fought on the Sabbath: so that Jericho could not attack the Hebrews while they rested, and so that Israel would learn to beware of a misplaced scruple, and outsiders could not use God's law against them by counting on the Hebrews' day of rest. The curse on whoever rebuilt the city is explained as a guard on the ban: Joshua "wished the city, put under the ban, to remain so for ever," and Hiel, who rebuilt it in Ahab's day (1 Kings 16:34), paid the price.

The sun at Gibeon, and the kings blinded

"Then Joshua spoke to the Lord in the sight of the children of Israel: Sun, stand still at Gibeon, and moon in the valley of Ajalon; and the sun stood still and the moon stayed," while victors and vanquished looked on: so that the vanquished might unlearn the worship of the planets, and the victors might beware the error of those who worship such gods. … From Joshua's words you will gather two things: first, that both these lights are endowed with their own motion; second, that when they stood still, the heaven stood still with them. Otherwise he who bade the sun stand would also have ordered the swift turning of the heaven to cease.

So far had the augurs and soothsayers of their religion blinded the Canaanite kings with a vain hope of victory, that they never understood the just judgment of God, by which it came about that they themselves sought the wars in which they would miserably perish. Indeed they had to be blind, who would not willingly surrender to God, to whom, as they could no longer fail to know, all things obey at his nod. … and, what was the greatest and the most recent of all, He had checked the course of the sun and moon, which the Canaanites, from the ancient superstition of their ancestors, called great gods.

"That none should intercede for them." The Lord's sentence, he says, had been that all the Canaanites should perish, and that no one should show any mercy to the perishing.

— Roman-edition commentary on Joshua 10:12–13 and 11:1, 11:20 (translated here from the Latin).6 The long day is a real event with a lesson for both armies: the Canaanites worshipped the sun and moon, and they watched their gods stop at the word of Israel's God. The commentator also draws a cosmological inference from it, that sun and moon move of themselves and that the whole heaven stood still with them. He adds a figure: Joshua "joined two days into one," as Christ by dying stretched one day into two. On Joshua 11:20, the verse that says God hardened the kings' hearts, he puts the blindness in the kings' own priests and diviners and calls the judgment "just." They had seen the sea, the Jordan, Jericho and the sun, and still chose war. The sentence of total destruction is stated as God's, and the commentator does not soften it. In the summary of Joshua 10:40 he notes that Joshua spared only the Gibeonites. On the Philistine cities left standing he gives the reason of Judges 3:1–4, that Israel needed enemies to prove its "doubtful faith."

Thirty years of warning, and the land left unconquered

"These are the kings whom the children of Israel struck: the king of Jericho, the king of Ai, the king of Jerusalem," and so on. It is truly remarkable that, although Joshua and the children of Israel strove for perhaps thirty years to bring about the conversion of the Canaanites, they were nevertheless not converted. And since by a long habit of sinning they had grown deaf, and did not listen to what they were warned of, they certainly did not perish undeservedly, God avenging their crimes by a most just judgment.

"Joshua was old and advanced in years." He had not yet subdued the whole of Canaan; but neither had the Israelites grown to such a number as to suffice to occupy and cultivate the whole promised land.

— Roman-edition commentary on Joshua 12:1 and 13:1 (translated here from the Latin).7 Here the theme of the earlier comments is stated outright. The commentator reckons Israel's dealings with the Canaanites at "perhaps thirty years," and he reads those years as a mission: Israel "strove … to bring about the conversion of the Canaanites." They perished because they would not hear. No other witness on this timeline describes the conquest as a call to conversion. The land left unconquered is explained in practical terms, by the size of Israel's population, which echoes God's promise to drive out the nations "little by little," "lest the land become desolate" (Exodus 23:29–30).

Notes

  • Two kinds of evidence. The Commentary on Genesis and the Commentary on the Diatessaron are Ephrem's own, and they treat the conquest as history in passing. The Joshua commentary in the Roman edition is a later catena, read through a free Latin version, and may preserve Ephrem, Jacob of Edessa or others. The timeline summary reports it as the tradition received under Ephrem's name, not as Ephrem's certain view.
  • The moral question. The Roman-edition comments answer the problem of the ban with repentance refused. The Canaanites were given forty years, seven days of warning at Jericho and thirty years of Israel's effort, and they "did not perish undeservedly." This is the reasoning of Wisdom 12:3–11, and of the Greek fathers who spoke of sins "fulfilled" (Genesis 15:16), turned into a narrative of patience. Compare Eusebius and Theodoret, and the patristic answers weighed at Do the patristic defenses answer the moral problem of the ḥerem?
  • Translations. The Commentary on Genesis is quoted from Mathews and Amar (Fathers of the Church 91) and the Commentary on the Diatessaron from McCarthy. The Roman-edition passages are translated here from the Latin, which itself paraphrases the Syriac. The Latin of each is given in the footnotes.
  • Ephrem appears on this wiki on many other timelines, among them the Flood, the Nephilim and the curse of Ham.
