Cassiodorus on the Conquest of Canaan

Dates: c. 540–550 (the Explanation of the Psalms, written after he left public life at Ravenna c. 540 and published by the early 550s; the Chronicle, 519; the Institutions, written at Vivarium in the 550s and 560s; Cassiodorus b. c. 485, d. c. 585) · Wikipedia
Summary: Cassiodorus served the Ostrogothic kings of Italy as a senior minister, then founded the monastery of Vivarium in Calabria. He wrote no commentary on Joshua. He meets the conquest where the Psalms recite it, in his Explanation of the Psalms, and he reads it the way the Psalms do, as God's gift. Israel's victories were won "without any toil of their own," because God's power, not their sword, broke Jericho's walls and planted them in the Amorites' land. Those wonders were given to an untaught people to draw them to faith, and the land was a promise for "those who lived under the law," where the Church is promised the kingdom of heaven. He treats the miracles as facts. The Jordan divided "to right and left," and the walls of Jericho fell to the trumpets, which he cites, as against pagan "fables," as proof that music can have great power. In the Institutions the sun stopped at Gibeon by miracle. On the land left unconquered he follows Psalm 106 (105). Israel failed to destroy the nations "which it had been commanded to destroy because of their idol-worship," and joined in "forbidden fellowship" with them, so the promised land became a snare. Beyond that he never raises the moral question of the ban. In his Chronicle the conquest is a dated event, set beside the kings of Assyria. At Vivarium he passed Origen's homilies on Joshua on to his monks.

Claims & evidence

The Explanation of the Psalms numbers the psalms as the Latin Bible does, one lower than most English Bibles from Psalm 10 to Psalm 147, so its Psalm 43 is Psalm 44 in the Hebrew and English numbering. Psalms 1–50 are quoted in P. G. Walsh's translation. For the later psalms, a few passages are quoted from his translation as excerpted in the Ancient Christian Commentary on Scripture, and the rest are translated here from Migne's Latin, which is given in the notes.1

"Not by their own sword": the conquest as God's victory

2. O God, we have heard with our ears: our fathers have declared to us the work thou hast wrought in their days, in the days of old. … So they attest that their fathers have told them of the work which the Lord has wrought in their days, that is in days of old, when He transported the people of Israel unharmed across the waters of the Red Sea, and made them victorious over their enemies without any toil of their own. This proof is called "From words and deeds of ancestors," when weight of testimony is strengthened by the most telling authority of our fathers.

3. Thy hand destroyed the Gentiles, and thou plantedst them: thou didst afflict the people and cast them out. … Hand denotes power, an expression often used, for the Gentiles are said in metaphor to have been afflicted by the Lord's hand through which above all we perform our tasks. When the Amorites or the others departed from their lands, we read that the Jews are set in their abodes; they are manifestly planted, for in the course of days they swelled by steady growth through God's kindness. He afflicted the people their foes, when at the sound of the priests' trumpets the walls of Jericho broke up and collapsed at the blast; clearly they were expelled from the city, since they could not withstand such great miracles.

4. For they got not the possession of the land by their own sword: neither did their own arm save them. So that the Jews should not believe that they triumphed through their own powers, they proclaim that they were not victorious by their own sword, nor did they claim the Gentiles' land by their own courage. … Thus as the history of the Jews is expounded, teaching is offered which is of benefit to the human race.

— Explanation of the Psalms, Psalm 43 (44):2–4, trans. P. G. Walsh.2 This is the psalm on which Ambrose, the last psalm he dictated, said that the Church took the nations' lands "without any bloodshed," because Israel "fought in figure." Cassiodorus takes the verses as a summary of history. The "Gentiles" are the Amorites and the others, the "afflicting" is the fall of Jericho, and both are miracles. "Planted" is a figure of speech for a real settlement, and "hand" for real power. The victories were "without any toil of their own," so the lesson is God's power, not Israel's valour. The Latin of the last sentence is Sic dum Hebraeorum actus exponuntur, humano generi proficua doctrina praestatur: "So, while the deeds of the Hebrews are set out, teaching profitable to the human race is given." The deeds are expounded, and the teaching comes from them. He adds that the destruction of the Gentiles and the planting of the Hebrews "refers to time here," that is, to the past, while the treading down of enemies in verse 6 "belong[s] to the future Judgment."

Why Israel was given easy victories and the martyrs are not

But here we need a deeper understanding, so that we may reconcile the Lord's deeds with His splendid designs. Initially the wondrous events of the Red Sea and the painless victories over different nations were their lot, so that such a notable miracle could entice the ignorant people to believe in God's power. Then the land of Chanaan was promised specifically to those who lived under the law. But now with the increase of their faith worldly prosperity has been removed from the martyrs, so that human weakness should not aspire too tepidly to the blessings of the world to come; the kingdom of heaven was promised to those known to be subject to grace.

— Explanation of the Psalms, Psalm 43 (44):1.3 Psalm 44 sets the easy victories of the fathers against the defeats of the present generation ("for thy sake we are killed all the day long"). Cassiodorus hears the martyrs in the second half, and he explains the contrast as two stages in God's plan. The wonders of the Exodus and the conquest were real and were suited to an untaught people. Canaan was a promise for the time of the law, and the kingdom of heaven is the promise for the time of grace. He does not turn the conquest into an allegory here. Its rewards were temporal because they belonged to an earlier stage of God's plan. This is Irenaeus's contrast between the Testaments, judgments "temporarily" in the one and "enduringly" in the other, and Hebrews' contrast between the rest Joshua gave and the rest that remains.

The Jordan divided "to right and left," as it was again for Elijah

He invites the nations to look with devout minds on the miracles of the Old Testament, so that they may recognise that what befell the Hebrew people in figure has been most truly fulfilled in themselves. … They could not see with bodily eyes things that were plainly past. … He invites them to look in particular at those which the most faithful recognised spiritually: that the sea was divided and lay open for the fleeing Hebrews, that the people were fed to the full for forty years in the desert, that the waters of the Jordan gave a crossing with dry feet, and the rest that befell the Jews in figure. … He turned the sea into dry land when he made the Hebrew people have a road on land through the midst of the waves. He passed through the rivers on foot when, the Jordan being divided to right and left, he led the Jewish army into the land of promise. But we must ask why he put "rivers" in the plural, when we read that it happened only in the Jordan. Not without reason: for although it happened in that river alone, it is shown to have happened there often. For Elijah too had such a crossing there, and for his disciple, as he returned, the waters of the same river likewise opened (4 Kings 2:8, 14). … Here he hastens to show the sacraments of those works, since in what they prefigured the Christian people afterwards truly rejoiced.

— Explanation of the Psalms, Psalm 65 (66):5–6, translated here from Migne's Latin.4 The miracles are past events, which the nations "could not see with bodily eyes" because they "were plainly past." They also "befell the Hebrew people in figure," the phrase of 1 Corinthians 10:11. The figure is in the events, not instead of them. The plural "rivers" gives him a question of history: the Jordan was parted three times, for Joshua, Elijah and Elisha. Only after that does he turn to the sacraments. The crossing of the sea "announced the coming baptism of Christ." On Psalm 113 (114), "the sea saw and fled: Jordan was turned back," he reads the sea and the river as the passions and desires of the world. He first notes that "although this history is related in the Old Testament," the psalm tells it "in other words and likenesses," so that "those ancient deeds" might announce the signs of our salvation. In the next verse, where the mountains "skipped like rams," he applies the rule that a sense "utterly absurd according to the letter" must be taken spiritually. He applies it to the leaping mountains, not to the parted Jordan.

Jericho's walls, the power of music, and the "fables" of the pagans

Expounding "blow up the trumpet on the new moon" (Psalm 80 [81]:4), Cassiodorus sets out the modes of music, "through which we read in secular books that many miracles were shown. But, to pass over what may be fabulous, we read that David with his tuneful lyre put the demon to flight from Saul." He goes on:

The divine reading attests that the walls of Jericho at once collapsed at the din of trumpets. So there is no doubt that the sounds of music, at the Lord's command or with his permission, have unleashed great forces.

— Explanation of the Psalms, Psalm 80 (81):4, trans. P. G. Walsh.5 The pagan stories of music's marvels may be "fabulous," but the fall of Jericho is attested by "the divine reading," and so it can serve as evidence. Cassiodorus uses it as a fact about the world, from which "there is no doubt" what music can do, with the reservation that it acts only "at the Lord's command or with his permission." The miracle is not read as a figure here.

The sun at Gibeon: a miracle against the law of the stars

Astronomy means the law of the stars. They can neither remain at rest or move except in the way in which their Creator arranged them, unless they are changed by divine will when some miracle occurs, as Joshua is said to have ordered [asked] the sun to stand still in Gabaon and a star was shown to the wise men which announced to the world the coming of the Lord bringing salvation; also in the passion of the Lord Christ the sun was made dark for three hours and the like. These events are called miracles because wondrous things happen against the usual rules of circumstance.

— Institutions II.7.1, trans. James W. and Barbara Halporn.6 In his handbook of the liberal arts for the monks of Vivarium, Cassiodorus defines astronomy as the study of the stars' fixed courses, and then names the exceptions. The sun at Gibeon heads the list, with the star of the Magi and the darkness at the crucifixion. They are real interruptions of the order of nature by God's will, "wondrous things" that happen "against the usual rules." The text printed by Migne, a later expanded form of the Institutions, adds that Joshua held the sun "for three hours" and adds the sign of Hezekiah's dial. These are not in Mynors's critical text, which is the one translated here.

The nations left in the land: disobedience, and a ban "because of their idol-worship"

As though it had been too little that the people of the Hebrews had sinned in the desert, so, after it entered the lands of promise, forgetful of the Lord's commands, it joined itself in forbidden fellowship with those very nations which it had been commanded to destroy because of their idol-worship, adding evil to its crimes by polluting itself with their superstition too; and the land of promise, which had been granted as a great reward by the Lord's bounty, became a stumbling-block to them. Most wretched of men, who turned God's gift into sins, so that what he had given for grace became, when they overstepped, their ruin.

— Explanation of the Psalms, Psalm 105 (106):34–36, translated here from Migne's Latin.7 Psalm 106 says that Israel "did not destroy the nations of which the Lord spoke unto them, but were mingled among the heathen." Cassiodorus explains the Canaanites who remained as Judges does. The conquest was left unfinished because Israel disobeyed, not because the command was taken back. In passing he gives the reason for the command. The nations were to be destroyed "because of their idol-worship" (propter idolorum culturas). The next verses, on the sons and daughters sacrificed "unto the idols of Chanaan," he reads as the height of that idolatry, a land "defiled with blood." This is the nearest he comes to the moral problem of the ban. He gives a reason in a subordinate clause and does not defend it, because no one in the passage attacks it. Earlier in the same psalm (on verse 33), he notes that after Moses' death the Lord spoke to "Jesus Nave," calling Moses "my servant" (Joshua 1:7). On Psalm 104 (105):44, "he gave them the lands of the Gentiles," he says the psalm ends with the gift of the land as God's last benefit to Israel, "that the truth of the promises was fulfilled, so that he handed over to them the regions of the nations."

Sihon, Og, and the casting out: "done to them in figure"

Although the sons of Israel, by the Lord's favour, laid low many kings, only these two are named, so that through the interpretation of these names we might be led to the meaning of what is done in us today.

— Explanation of the Psalms, Psalm 134 (135):11, translated here from Migne's Latin.8 Where the Psalms list the kings of the conquest, Cassiodorus turns to the inner war, as Origen did. The "many nations" God struck (134:10) are "the harmful troops of vices, which afflict us within like barbarous nations." Sihon means "temptation of colours" and Og "heaping up," and both are the devil. But the figure starts from a fact. Israel "laid low many kings, by the Lord's favour," and only two are named because their names carry a meaning. On Psalm 77 (78):54–55, "he cast out the Gentiles before them, and by lot divided to them their land," the history comes first in the same way. The events "were done to them in figure (as the Apostle says)," and so "he casts out the nations from before our face when barbarous and savage errors are put to flight from us," and divides "that land of promise" when he gives each soul "a blessed portion." On Psalm 135 (136):22, "and gave their land for an heritage," he writes: "although the history seems to speak of the Hebrews," the verse is "most aptly" applied to Christians, who receive the inheritance once promised to Israel.

Rahab, whose name means "pride"

Rahab was a harlot who secretly admitted the spies of Joshua when they visited Jericho and let them out by another exit so that they should not be captured. Her name means "pride." She was converted by God's generosity and deserved to obtain mercy. She is a type of the church, which takes in souls endangered by the vice of pride, and lets them out into life by another route, the way of humility and patience.

— Explanation of the Psalms, Psalm 86 (87):4, trans. P. G. Walsh.9 "I will be mindful of Rahab and of Babylon," says the psalm, and Cassiodorus, like Augustine before him, takes the Rahab of Psalm 87 to be the harlot of Jericho. He first tells her story, then the figure. Origen had read her name as "breadth," the Church spread through the world. Cassiodorus reads it as "pride," so that Rahab is pride converted, and the Church sends proud souls out "by another route," the way of humility. He does not mention her lie.

The conquest in the Chronicle

Ascatadis ruled for 40 years.
In this time Moses received on Mount Sinai the Divine Law.
Amyntes ruled for 45 years.
In this time Joshua the successor of Moses distributes the land of the Palestinians to the Jewish nation.

— Chronicle, trans. Bouke Procee.10 Cassiodorus wrote his short Chronicle in 519, as a minister of King Theoderic, for the consulship of Theoderic's son-in-law Eutharic. Its early section runs down the list of Assyrian kings from Eusebius and Jerome, and it puts the allotment of the land in the reign of Amyntes, the same synchronism Augustine gives in the City of God. The entry is one line. It shows that the conquest stood in Cassiodorus's world history as a datable event, decades before he wrote on the Psalms.

Notes

  • Cassiodorus and Origen. At Vivarium Cassiodorus collected Origen's homilies on the Octateuch in Rufinus's Latin for his monks, "twenty-six on Joshua" among them, and marked passages "against the rules of the Fathers" with a sign of rejection (Institutions I.1.8–9). The library he gathered was one of the routes by which Origen's reading of Joshua reached the medieval West. His own reading has Origen's inner war and Augustine's habit of naming the history first. It is closest to Ambrose, but on the same psalm (Psalm 43) Cassiodorus does not repeat Ambrose's contrast between Israel's war "in figure" and the Church's conquest "without any bloodshed."
  • The moral question. Cassiodorus never answers the objection to the ban, because he never raises it. The nearest he comes is the clause on Psalm 106 (105): the nations were to be destroyed "because of their idol-worship." That places him with the witnesses who treat the ban as a just sentence on the Canaanites' sins, such as Eusebius, Augustine and Theodoret, though he states the reason without arguing for it. The patristic answers are weighed at Do the patristic defenses answer the moral problem of the ḥerem?
