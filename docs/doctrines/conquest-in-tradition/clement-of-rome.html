Clement of Rome on the Conquest of Canaan

Dates: c. AD 96 (the First Epistle to the Corinthians, ch. 12; Clement of Rome, fl. late 1st century) · Wikipedia
Summary: The first Christian writer after the New Testament to take up the conquest does so through one episode, the spies at Jericho. Clement retells Joshua 2 at length as a true story and places it in a chain of historical exempla (Enoch, Noah, Abraham, Lot, Rahab). Then, in a single sentence, he reads the scarlet thread as a prophecy that "redemption should flow through the blood of the Lord." This is the earliest case of the pattern the Fathers would follow: the history is told as history first, and the figure is laid on top of it. The destruction of Jericho is in the background, taken for granted and not discussed.

Claims & evidence

Jericho's spies retold as history, in a chain of historical exempla

Clement is writing to settle a schism at Corinth, and his tool is the example of the saints. From chapter 9 he goes through the obedient and the hospitable of Genesis in order (Enoch "translated," Noah who "preached regeneration to the world," Abraham, and Lot, saved out of Sodom), and then steps forward to the conquest. His account of Rahab is a close paraphrase of Joshua 2, with the king's officers, the roof, the flax, and Rahab's own confession that the land was already God's gift to Israel:

On account of her faith and hospitality, Rahab the harlot was saved. For when spies were sent by Joshua, the son of Nun, to Jericho, the king of the country ascertained that they were come to spy out their land, and sent men to seize them, in order that, when taken, they might be put to death. But the hospitable Rahab receiving them, concealed them on the roof of her house under some stalks of flax. … Then she said to the men, "I know assuredly that the Lord your God hath given you this city, for the fear and dread of you have fallen on its inhabitants. When therefore ye shall have taken it, keep ye me and the house of my father in safety."

The setting is the same as in Hebrews 11, a roll of real people whose conduct God rewarded. Clement's "faith and hospitality" brings together the "by faith" of Hebrews 11:31 and the "justified by works" of James 2:25. Rahab is a model for the Corinthians because she actually acted, at Jericho, under Joshua son of Nun.1

The ban assumed, not discussed

The spies' oath, as Clement gives it, contains the whole logic of the ban on Jericho in one line. Those under Rahab's roof live, and everyone outside dies:

And they said to her, "It shall be as thou hast spoken to us. As soon, therefore, as thou knowest that we are at hand, thou shall gather all thy family under thy roof, and they shall be preserved, but all that. are found outside of thy dwelling shall perish."

Clement adds nothing to this: no apology for the city's destruction and no allegory of it. His interest is the one household saved, not the many destroyed. The moral problem is not yet a Christian question. It arrives some fifty years later with Marcion.2

The scarlet thread: "not only faith, but prophecy"

Only at the close of the story does Clement draw a figure from it, and he draws it from a detail of the history, not in place of the history:

Moreover, they gave her a sign to this effect, that she should hang forth from her house a scarlet thread. And thus they made it manifest that redemption should flow through the blood of the Lord to all them that believe and hope in God. Ye see, beloved, that there was not only faith, but prophecy, in this woman.

The cord was a real signal on a real window, and it was also a prophecy. Clement's "not only… but" states the layered reading the later tradition would make standard. Jean Daniélou points out how striking this is in a letter whose "drift… is moral rather than typological": "If he retains the typology of Rahab he can have received it only from tradition". That would take the figure back to "the earliest days of the Christian Church." It is the seed of the scarlet-cord typology that Justin and Irenaeus develop next.3

Notes

  • Layered, not substituted. Clement is the earliest instance on this timeline of the figure set on top of the event: he retells the history in full and draws the prophecy from one detail of it. The history is not dissolved, as in the later spiritual-warfare reading of Origen.
  • Joshua himself is incidental. Clement names Joshua only as the man who sent the spies. He does not use the Joshua/Jesus name typology that Hebrews 4:8 opens and that Justin Martyr makes central. In Clement's hands the conquest is Rahab's story.
  • Rahab. For the rest of the Rahab tradition, including whether the scarlet cord is a type of Christ's blood, see Clement of Rome on Rahab, the scarlet-cord typology, and the Rahab timeline.
  • Where this stands on the timeline. Clement writes within a few years of Josephus, whose Rahab is an innkeeper and whose scarlet threads are only a recognition signal. The Jewish historian and the Roman bishop tell the same history and read it differently.
