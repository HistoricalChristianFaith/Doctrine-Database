Clement of Rome on the Conquest of Canaan

Dates: c. AD 96 (the First Epistle to the Corinthians, ch. 12; Clement of Rome, fl. late 1st century) · Wikipedia
Summary: The earliest extant Christian writer after the New Testament to take up the conquest does so through one episode, the spies at Jericho. Clement retells Joshua 2 at length as history and places it in a chain of historical exempla (Enoch, Noah, Abraham, Lot, Rahab). Then, in a single sentence, he reads the scarlet thread as a prophecy that "redemption should flow through the blood of the Lord." In this letter the history is told as history first, and the figure is laid on it. The destruction of Jericho is in the background, taken for granted and not discussed.

Claims & evidence

Jericho's spies retold as history, in a chain of historical exempla

Clement is writing to settle a schism at Corinth, and his tool is the example of the saints. From chapter 9 he goes through the obedient and the hospitable of Genesis in order (Enoch "translated," Noah who "preached regeneration to the world," Abraham, and Lot, saved out of Sodom), and then steps forward to the conquest. His account of Rahab is a close paraphrase of Joshua 2, with the king's officers, the roof, the flax, and Rahab's own confession that the land was already God's gift to Israel:

On account of her faith and hospitality, Rahab the harlot was saved. For when spies were sent by Joshua, the son of Nun, to Jericho, the king of the country ascertained that they were come to spy out their land, and sent men to seize them, in order that, when taken, they might be put to death. But the hospitable Rahab receiving them, concealed them on the roof of her house under some stalks of flax. … Then she said to the men, "I know assuredly that the Lord your God hath given you this city, for the fear and dread of you have fallen on its inhabitants. When therefore ye shall have taken it, keep ye me and the house of my father in safety."

The setting resembles Hebrews 11, a roll of persons commended for faith. Clement's "faith and hospitality" brings together the "by faith" of Hebrews 11:31 and the "justified by works" of James 2:25. Rahab serves as a model for the Corinthians as one who acted at Jericho under Joshua son of Nun.1

The spies' oath and the fate of the city

The spies' oath, as Clement gives it, states in one line that those under Rahab's roof live and everyone outside perishes:

And they said to her, "It shall be as thou hast spoken to us. As soon, therefore, as thou knowest that we are at hand, thou shall gather all thy family under thy roof, and they shall be preserved, but all that. are found outside of thy dwelling shall perish."

Clement gives no comment on the city's destruction; his interest is the one household saved. Marcion's objections to the God of the Old Testament are dated some fifty years after this letter.2

The scarlet thread: "not only faith, but prophecy"

Only at the close of the story does Clement draw a figure from it, taken from a detail of the history:

Moreover, they gave her a sign to this effect, that she should hang forth from her house a scarlet thread. And thus they made it manifest that redemption should flow through the blood of the Lord to all them that believe and hope in God. Ye see, beloved, that there was not only faith, but prophecy, in this woman.

In Clement's telling the cord is a signal on a window and also a prophecy. His "not only… but" sets prophecy beside faith. Jean Daniélou observes that the letter's "drift… is moral rather than typological": "If he retains the typology of Rahab he can have received it only from tradition". Daniélou places the figure's origin in "the earliest days of the Christian Church." The scarlet-cord typology also appears in Justin and Irenaeus.3

Relation to other witnesses

  • Figure and history. Clement is the earliest instance on this timeline of a figure set beside an event: he retells the history in full and draws the prophecy from one detail of it. Origen's spiritual-warfare reading of the wars is on his page.
  • Joshua in the letter. Clement names Joshua only as the man who sent the spies. He does not use the Joshua/Jesus name typology that Hebrews 4:8 opens and that Justin Martyr makes central. In the letter the conquest appears only through Rahab's story.
  • Rahab. For the rest of the Rahab tradition, including whether the scarlet cord is a type of Christ's blood, see Clement of Rome on Rahab and the Rahab timeline.
  • Placement on the timeline. Clement writes within a few years of Josephus, whose Rahab is an innkeeper and whose scarlet threads are only a recognition signal.
