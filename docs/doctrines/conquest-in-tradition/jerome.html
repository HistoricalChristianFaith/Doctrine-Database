Jerome on the Conquest of Canaan

Dates: c. 394 (Letter 53 to Paulinus; his writing on Joshua runs from the Commentary on Galatians, c. 386, through the Latin Onomasticon, c. 390, Letter 108, 404, and the Commentary on Isaiah, 408–410, to the Dialogue against the Pelagians, 415; Jerome b. c. 347, at Bethlehem from 386, d. 420) · Wikipedia
Summary: Jerome lived for thirty-four years a few miles from the places named in Joshua. He translated Eusebius's gazetteer of them into Latin, and he went over them on foot with Paula. His summary of the book is a history read as a type: Joshua, "a type of the Lord in name as well as in deed," "crossed over Jordan, subdued hostile kingdoms, divided the land among the conquering people," and in doing so "marked out the spiritual realms of the heavenly Jerusalem." His account of Paula's pilgrimage stops at Gibeon, where Joshua "gave commandments to the sun and moon," at Gilgal and its twelve stones, at the Jordan where the priests' feet "stood firm," and at Joshua's tomb. In the commentaries he counts the sun's standing still among the signs given from heaven. He answers a heretic who called Joshua "a bloodthirsty man" by pointing to Joshua's holiness. Late in life, against the Pelagians, he cites Achan's family and the hardened Canaanite cities as cases of God's will that no one may call to account. Twice he does qualify the letter. The "second circumcision" at Gilgal, taken literally, "cannot possibly stand." And the land Joshua won was small, never fully possessed, and "promised, not given."

Claims & evidence

Joshua's book in one sentence: the history, and the type it carries

I will pass on to Jesus the son of Nave—a type of the Lord in name as well as in deed—who crossed over Jordan, subdued hostile kingdoms, divided the land among the conquering people and who, in every city, village, mountain, river, hill-torrent, and boundary which he dealt with, marked out the spiritual realms of the heavenly Jerusalem, that is, of the church.

— Letter 53.8, to Paulinus of Nola (394), trans. W. H. Fremantle.1 Letter 53 is Jerome's guide to the books of the Bible for a new reader, and this is its whole entry on Joshua. The book's contents are given as deeds: the crossing, the defeat of the kings, the division of the land. Each deed is then said to mark out a spiritual counterpart. "In name" refers to the shared Greek and Latin name, Iesus. "In deed" refers to what Joshua did. The type is carried by the events, not set in their place. The same letter applies the method across the historical books. Of Kings it says: "If you merely regard the narrative, the words are simple enough, but if you look beneath the surface at the hidden meaning of it, you find a description of the small numbers of the church."

The name: "previously called Ause, or better, as in the Hebrew, Osee, that is, Saviour"

I will pass to Joshua the son of Nun, who was previously called Ause, or better, as in the Hebrew, Osee, that is, Saviour. For he, according to the epistle of Jude, saved the people of Israel and led them forth out of Egypt, and brought them into the land of promise.

— Against Jovinianus 1.21 (393), trans. Fremantle.2 Jerome reads Jude 5 as the Latin and some Greek copies give it, "Jesus … saved the people out of Egypt," and takes it to mean Joshua. As a Hebraist he corrects the Septuagint's form of the birth-name (Ause, for Hebrew Hoshea). When he reached Joshua in his own translation from the Hebrew, around 404, he named the book both ways: "we set (our) hand to Jesus son of Nave, who the Hebrews call Joshua ben Nun." The preface is otherwise a defence of the translation. It contains nothing on the conquest itself.3

Where the letter "cannot possibly stand": the second circumcision at Gilgal

As soon as this Joshua reached the Jordan, the waters of marriage, which had ever flowed in the land, dried up and stood in one heap; and the whole people, barefooted and on dry ground, crossed over, and came to Gilgal, and there was a second time circumcised. If we take this literally, it cannot possibly stand. For if we had two foreskins, or if another could grow after the first was cut off, there would be room for speaking of a second circumcision. But the meaning is that Joshua circumcised the people who had crossed the desert, with the Gospel knife, and he circumcised them with a stone knife, that what in the case of Moses' son was prefigured in a few might under Joshua be fulfilled in all.

— Against Jovinianus 1.21.4 This is the one place where Jerome says a detail of the Joshua narrative cannot be taken literally. The detail is the phrase "the second time" in Joshua 5:2. His objection is to the wording: no one can be circumcised twice. It is not an objection to the event. In the same chapter he goes on to treat the rest of the campaign as figure, in support of his case for virginity against Jovinian. The Jordan is "the waters of marriage." Jericho is "a type of the overthrow of the world by the preaching of the Gospel." The five kings hidden in the cave at Makkedah are the five senses, and "Jesus entered the body itself and slew them." This is polemic. Elsewhere Jerome treats the same circumcision as a historical measure. Writing on Galatians a few years earlier, he gives it a practical reason:

Accordingly, for forty years in the desert no one was circumcised: for they were living alone, without any admixture of another nation; as soon as the people had crossed the bank of the Jordan and the Jewish swarm had poured itself into the land of Palestine, circumcision, now necessary, provided against the confusion that would come from mingling with the nations. But that the people is written to have been circumcised a second time by Joshua (Jesu) their leader (Josh. 5) signifies both that circumcision, which had been reasonably practiced in Egypt, ceased in the desert, and that believers were to be cleansed by the Lord Jesus Christ with a spiritual circumcision.

— Commentary on Galatians, on Galatians 3:7 (c. 386).5 Here the crossing and the settlement are the background, and Israel's entry into "the land of Palestine" is the reason for the rite. The phrase "a second time" is the only part read as a sign.

The Latin Onomasticon: Joshua's places, checked and updated

Finally he worked on this little book and gathered together for us from the Holy Scriptures almost all the names of cities, mountains, rivers, villages and other places indicating if they still exist, are unchanged or are in part corrupted. And so we have translated the study of this remarkable man according to the order of the alphabet in Greek. We have left unchanged those proper records we have not seen, but have changed a few.

— Jerome's preface to his Latin version of Eusebius's Onomasticon (De situ et nominibus locorum Hebraicorum, c. 390), trans. C. Umhau Wolf.6 Around 390 Jerome put Eusebius's gazetteer into Latin. He kept its Joshua entries, which give each event and then say where the site is "now" (see Eusebius). He also added material of his own. In Wolf's combined English, Jerome's wording appears in parentheses and his longer additions in brackets. At Jericho he restates Eusebius's remark about the earlier cities: "(Remains of both older cities are pointed out up to the present day.)" At Gilgal he notes that "some men of that region hold in holy awe" the deserted site. At Ai he reports a church at Bethel, where Jacob slept, "but for Aggai, a deserted place, there are shown only ruins." At the valley of Achor, "where Achar (Achan) the condemned thief was stoned," he adds a correction from the Hebrew: "It is quite wrong to think the Valley of Achor is named for the name of the one atoned, for he is called Achan, not Achor or Achar." The changes are to spellings, distances and churches. He leaves the conquest as Eusebius reported it.

Paula's pilgrimage through Joshua's country

Starting thence she made her way up lower and higher Bethhoron, cities founded by Solomon but subsequently destroyed by several devastating wars; seeing on her right Ajalon and Gibeon where Joshua the son of Nun when fighting against the five kings gave commandments to the sun and moon, where also he condemned the Gibeonites (who by a crafty stratagem had obtained a treaty) to be hewers of wood and drawers of water.

— Letter 108.8, to Eustochium, the epitaph of Paula (404).7 Paula, a Roman noblewoman, toured the Holy Land with Jerome in 385–386 before settling in Bethlehem. On her death in 404 Jerome wrote her life for her daughter and gave a full account of the journey. Her route from the coast to Jerusalem passes the valley where the sun stood still. He recalls the miracle and the Gibeonite treaty as events that took place at the sites she was looking at. From Jerusalem she goes down to Jericho and the Jordan:

Then entering Jericho she saw the city which Hiel founded in Abiram his firstborn and of which he set up the gates in his youngest son Segub. She looked upon the camp of Gilgal and the hill of the foreskins suggestive of the mystery of the second circumcision: and she gazed at the twelve stones brought thither out of the bed of Jordan to be symbols of those twelve foundations on which are written the names of the twelve apostles. … Scarcely had the night passed away when burning with eagerness she hastened to the Jordan, stood by the brink of the river, and as the sun rose recalled to mind the rising of the sun of righteousness; how the priest's feet stood firm in the middle of the river-bed; how afterwards at the command of Elijah and Elisha the waters were divided hither and thither and made way for them to pass.

— Letter 108.12.8 This is a pilgrim's piety. The place stirs the memory of the event, and the event points to a mystery. She "looked upon" the camp, she "gazed at" the stones, and they are "symbols" of the apostles. The stones are the ones Joshua set up, still standing in the field where Eusebius's gazetteer had placed them. The Jordan crossing is set in a list with Elijah and Elisha's crossing and Christ's baptism, all events at one river. In Mount Ephraim the tour ends at Joshua's grave:

Also when she was in Mount Ephraim she made pilgrimages to the tombs of Joshua the son of Nun and of Eleazar the son of Aaron the priest, exactly opposite the one to the other; that of Joshua being built at Timnath-serah "on the north side of the hill of Gaash," and that of Eleazar "in a hill that pertained to Phinehas his son." She was somewhat surprised to find that he who had had the distribution of the land in his own hands had selected for himself portions uneven and rocky.

— Letter 108.13.9 The last sentence treats Joshua as a man who once had the land in his hands to allot. Paula's surprise is at the poor ground he chose for himself. The same section passes "the valley of Achor, that is, of 'trouble and crowds,' where theft and covetousness were condemned."

The sun at Gibeon: a "sign from heaven," and God's "strange work"

… and as in the valley of Gibeon, under Joshua the leader, when, in the confidence of God dwelling in him, he said: Let the sun stand still at Gibeon, and the moon over against the valley of Aijalon (Josh 10:12), and the sun stood still for the space of one day, and many of the foreigners perished: so the Lord shall be angry against the ungodly and the scornful, that he may do his work. For it is not the Lord's work to destroy those whom he created, but to do a strange work, which pertains to cruelty rather than to mercy.

— Commentary on Isaiah, on Isaiah 28:21 (408–410).10 Isaiah's "as in the valley of Gibeon" looks back to Joshua 10, and Jerome reads it that way. Joshua's battle is a past judgment that predicts a future one: "the sun stood still for the space of one day, and many of the foreigners perished." He then takes up Isaiah's phrase "his strange work." Destroying what he made is foreign to God's nature. It is a work "which pertains to cruelty rather than to mercy," and God does it only when driven to it. A few lines later he compares God to "a father and a physician" thought cruel by those they discipline. On Isaiah 7:11 he lists the event among the signs God gave "from heaven," with Hezekiah's shadow: "Joshua the son of Nun at Gibeon and Aijalon, when the sun and the moon stood still (Josh 10)." On Isaiah 17:9 he takes the flight of the Canaanites as a past event that prefigures the Roman siege: "As under Moses and Joshua the son of Nun the Amorites and the Hivites, and the other nations that dwelt in the land of promise, forsook their ploughshares and standing corn and the heaps in the fields and fled."

Against the charge that Joshua was "a bloodthirsty man"

For which of the heretics is not proud? who, despising the simplicity of the Church, regards the men of the Church as brute animals, and is lifted up by such a swelling of pride and insolence that he arms his mouth against the Creator himself, disparaging his prophets as though on the authority of the testimony of the Gospel, in which the Savior says: All who came before me were thieves and robbers (John 10:8): so much so that he even calls Moses, the servant of God, a murderer, and disparages Joshua the son of Nun as a bloodthirsty man, who was of such holiness that at the command of his word the sun and moon stood still.

— Commentary on Isaiah, on Isaiah 16:6.11 The heretic is not named. The charge belongs to the Marcionite tradition that Jerome mentions elsewhere in the same commentary (on Isaiah 7): Marcion called the God of the prophets "bloodthirsty, cruel, and a judge." Jerome does not reply by turning the wars into allegory. He replies with Joshua's character. A man whose word stopped the sun was holy, and calling him a murderer is pride against "the Creator himself." He accepts the miracle as history and uses it to vouch for the man.

Achan's household and the hardened cities: God's will is not to be called to account

Achan sinned, and the entire nation transgressed. … And when they made search for the guilty person and the lot discovered him hiding, Achan, and his sons and daughters, and his asses and sheep are killed; his tent and all his possessions are destroyed by fire. Granted, that he himself committed a sin. What sin did his children commit, his oxen, his asses, his sheep? Reprehend God, why one man committed a sin and a number of people were put to death; why even he is stoned to death and all his possessions are destroyed by the avenging flame? Let us also quote the other testimony: "There was not a city," he says, "that the Lord did not deliver to the children of Israel, except the Hivites who dwell in Gibeon; they took all by fight, because it was the sentence of the Lord that their hearts should be hardened and they should fight against Israel and be killed, and that they should not deserve any clemency and should be destroyed, as the Lord commanded Moses." If it was done by the will of God that they should neither make peace with Israel nor obtain peace from Israel, let us say with the apostle: "Why then does he find fault? For who can resist his will?"

— Dialogue against the Pelagians 1.37 (415), spoken by Atticus, Jerome's voice in the dialogue; in the Fathers of the Church translation by John N. Hritzu.12 This is the hardest material in Joshua, and Jerome states it in full. Achan's children and animals die with him (Joshua 7:24–25). The Canaanite cities are hardened by God "that they should not deserve any clemency and should be destroyed" (Joshua 11:19–20). He raises the obvious objection himself: "What sin did his children commit?" He does not answer it by allegory. He uses it to make his point against Pelagius. The case shows that guilt and judgment are not limited to the conscious acts of one man's free will. Then he closes with Paul's reply in Romans 9:19–20, that the creature does not call the Creator to account. In the same period, in Letter 133 to Ctesiphon, he made the same move, listing the case among the judgments a Pelagian would have to "accuse" God for: "Accuse Him of injustice because, when Achan the son of Carmi stole part of the spoil of Jericho, He butchered so many thousands for the fault of one" (133.9).13 The commentary on Isaiah 65:10 gives the same history without comment: Achan "was put to death with all his house."

The land that was won was small, and was "promised, not given"

Lege librum Josue et Judicum, et quantis possessionum angustiis sis coarctatus, intelliges. Quid diversarum urbium alienigenas commemorem, quos populus Judaeorum non quivit expellere de urbibus et sedibus suis, cum ipsa metropolis tua prius Jebus, postea Salem, tertio Jerosolyma, et nunc Aelia, Jebusaeos expellere non valuerit …

— Letter 129.5, to Dardanus (414). In the English version printed on historicalchristian.faith: "Read the book of Joshua and Judges and you will understand how cramped you are in terms of possessions. Why should I mention the foreigners of diverse cities, whom the people of the Jews were not able to expel from their cities and abodes, when your own metropolis, first called Jebus, then Salem, thirdly Jerusalem, and now Aelia, was not able to expel the Jebusites."14 Dardanus, a prefect of Gaul, had asked what "the land of promise" was. Jerome's answer is that the true land of promise is heavenly. To make the point he cuts the earthly one down to size, and he uses Joshua and Judges as his record. Israel held "from Dan to Beersheba," barely 160 Roman miles, and not even David and Solomon held more. The five Philistine cities, Edom and the desert tribes lay outside it. Even Jerusalem kept its Jebusites. He is "ashamed to say the width of the promised land" (pudet dicere latitudinem terrae repromissionis), "lest we seem to have given the heathens an opportunity to blaspheme." The larger borders of Numbers 34 were "promised, not given" (repromissa, non tradita), and they were lost through idolatry. This is the letter read as history, not set aside. The conquest Jerome describes is real but partial. It falls short of the book's summary formulas, as Origen had also noted, and it agrees with Judges 1.

Notes

  • History and figure. Jerome's reading of Joshua stands in Origen's line. Origen's homilies on the book were put into Latin around 400 by Rufinus, Jerome's friend turned rival. Like Origen, he reads the shared name as a type of Christ and the campaign as the Church's progress. Unlike Origen, he spent most of his life in the land. His gazetteer, his pilgrim narrative and his late polemics all treat the conquest as events at real places. His one stated exception is the phrase "a second time" in Joshua 5:2. Aphrahat, writing in Syriac fifty years earlier, had kept the phrase literal by taking the first circumcision to be one of the heart. Compare Origen of Alexandria on the Conquest of Canaan and Eusebius of Caesarea, whose Onomasticon and Chronicle Jerome translated.
  • The moral question. Jerome gives no single theory of the ḥerem. He offers three things in different places. Against the charge that Joshua was "bloodthirsty," he points to Joshua's holiness and the miracle that confirmed it. On Isaiah 28:21 he calls the destruction God's "strange work," foreign to His mercy. Against Pelagius he says no one may call the judgment to account (Romans 9). None of the three is an allegorical denial. The patristic answers are weighed at Do the patristic defenses answer the moral problem of the ḥerem? Compare Augustine of Hippo, Jerome's correspondent (see Jerome and Augustine).
  • Rahab. Jerome's reading of the scarlet cord and of Rahab as the Church of the Gentiles is set out in Jerome on Rahab, on the Rahab timeline.
  • The sites. For where Ai, Jericho and Gilgal are sought today, see the identification of Ai, Jericho's destruction and the dating of Jericho.
  • Jerome on other timelines: Jerome on the Historicity of Noah's Flood, on the Nephilim, on Balaam, on the Old Testament Canon, on Bethany and Bethphage (his Latin Onomasticon again) and in the Origenist Controversy.
