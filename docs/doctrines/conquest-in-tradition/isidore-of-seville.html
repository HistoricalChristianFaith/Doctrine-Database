Isidore of Seville on the Conquest of Canaan

Dates: c. 615 (the Chronicon, first edition 615, revised c. 626; the Questions on the Old Testament, written between 604 and 636; the Etymologies, compiled c. 615–630 and left unfinished at his death; Isidore b. c. 560, bishop of Seville from c. 600, d. 636) · Wikipedia
Summary: Isidore, bishop of Seville in Visigothic Spain, handed the Fathers' reading of Joshua on to the Middle Ages in two forms, and kept them apart. In his chronicles Joshua is a ruler with a date. He "led the people for twenty-seven years," ending in the year of the world 3755 or 3756, in the generation when the Greeks first yoked a four-horse chariot, two entries before the Greek "fables were devised." His short life of Joshua records that he stopped the sun, held back the Jordan and threw down Jericho's walls. It says he "overthrew the kingdoms of the Canaanites" and that his tomb "is shown to this day." His Questions on the Old Testament then read the same book as allegory, "with the foundation of history laid first." Its chapters on Joshua are a florilegium, and Isidore says so. Most of them are Origen's Homilies on Joshua cut down, with Augustine on Jericho and Gregory the Great on the Canaanites who were left in the land. Isidore keeps Origen's history-affirming lines: "these things the old history reports as done," and Jesus made the sun stand still "not only then." He keeps Origen's one denial, that a second fleshly circumcision is impossible. He drops Origen's doubt about "Jesus took all the land." He never raises the moral problem of the ban.

Claims & evidence

Isidore's Latin is quoted here from Migne's Patrologia Latina 83, which reprints Faustino Arévalo's edition (Rome, 1797–1803). The Etymologies are quoted in the English of Stephen Barney and his colleagues, and the Chronicon in the English of Kenneth B. Wolf. No English translation of the Questions on the Old Testament, the Allegories, the life of Joshua in On the Birth and Death of the Fathers, or On the Catholic Faith was found for this page, so those passages are given in the page's own translation, with the Latin in the footnotes. The Questions cannot be dated closely. They draw on Gregory the Great's homilies of the 590s, and the proposals run from before 615 to the 620s or 630s.1

Joshua in the years of the world: the two chronicles

17. Moses, at age 40, guided the people, liberated from servitude in Egypt, into the wilderness. At this time the Jews began to have, through Moses, both letters and law. At that time the Temple of Delphi was built. Viticulture was invented in Greece.

18. Joshua, the successor of Moses, led the people for twenty-seven years. In these times, Erichthonius, the prince of the Athenians, was the first to harness a team of horses in Greece.

19. Othniel led for forty years. Cadmus, the first to invent Greek letters, ruled in Thebes. …

20. Ehud led for eighty years. In these times fables were devised: about Triptolemus who, born aloft with the wings of dragons, distributed food to the poor as he flew at the command of Ceres; about the Hippocentaurs, in which were combined the characteristics of man and horse; …

— Chronicon 17–20, trans. Wolf.2 Isidore's world chronicle runs from Creation to the reigns of the emperor Heraclius and the Gothic king Sisebut. In its preface he names his models, Julius Africanus, Eusebius and Jerome, and says he has added "down the side a descending line of years" (adjicientes e latere descendentem lineam temporum) "by which the sum of past time may be known." Wolf's translation leaves that line of years out. The Latin prints it, and Joshua's entry carries the figure 3756: Moses' forty years end in the year of the world 3729, and Joshua's twenty-seven bring the count to 3756. The twenty-seven years are the figure Eusebius gave and Augustine repeated, and the synchronism with Erichthonius of Athens comes from the same tradition. The chronicle weighs its sources. Two entries later, under Ehud, it records that Greek "fables were devised" (fabulae fictae sunt), centaurs and Gorgons and Bellerophon's winged horse among them. Isidore sets the Greek fables beside Israel's judges and labels them fables. Joshua's twenty-seven years are entered without any such label.

3728 Moses, 40 years. The Hebrews began to use letters.

10. 3755 Joshua, 27 years. Ericthonius in Troy [first] yoked a chariot.

3795 Othniel, 40 years. Cadmus gave letters to the Greeks.

3875 Ehud, 80 years. The fables were composed.

— Etymologies V.39.9–10, trans. Barney et al.3 The Etymologies, the encyclopedia Isidore was still compiling at his death, end their book on law and time with a shorter version of the same chronicle, this time with the year-count beside each entry. Joshua's rule ends in the year 3755, one year off the Chronicon's figure because Moses' forty years end at 3728 rather than 3729. Either way the conquest has a place in a single reckoning of years that runs from Adam to Isidore's own day.

A life of Joshua: the sun, the Jordan, Jericho, and a tomb "shown to this day"

Joshua son of Nun, of the tribe of Ephraim, born in Egypt, disciple of Moses and successor to his power, and victor in a new kind of battle. He commanded the sky that the sun should stand still until he had won the victory. By his crossing he also made the current of the Jordan halt. He threw down the impregnable walls of Jericho by the sound of the blaring trumpets. He overthrew the kingdoms of the Canaanites, put an end to their kings, and alone led the people of God into the land of promise, and divided it by lot. He governed in Israel for twenty-seven years. He died at a hundred and ten years and was buried in Timnath-serah, his own city, which lies on the side of Mount Gaash, toward the north, where his notable tomb is shown to this day. It is in the tribe of Dan.

— On the Birth and Death of the Fathers (De ortu et obitu patrum) 27, the page's own translation.4 On the Birth and Death of the Fathers is a book of short lives of the people of the Bible, from Adam to the apostles. Its preface says it records their "birth or deeds," their "rank, and their death and burial." Joshua's life is a list of deeds, and the three miracles of the book come first. The sun stood still because Joshua "commanded the sky" (coelo imperavit), the Jordan halted at his crossing, and Jericho's walls fell to the trumpets. The figure of twenty-seven years matches the chronicles. The burial notice comes from Joshua 24:30 by way of the gazetteer of Eusebius in Jerome's Latin, which also puts Timnath-serah "in the tribe of Dan" and says that Joshua's tomb "is pointed out" there. Nothing in the entry is allegory. The entry also repeats the book's sweeping summary as it stands: Joshua "overthrew the kingdoms of the Canaanites" and "alone" brought the people into the land. Isidore does not repeat Origen's remark that Joshua "held Judaea alone."

A historical book: the Etymologies on Joshua, Jericho and the Canaanites

8. The book of Joshua takes its name from Jesus son of Nave, whose story it contains — in fact the Hebrews claim that its writer was this same Joshua. In this text, after the crossing of the Jordan the kingdoms of the enemy are destroyed, the land is divided for the people, and the spiritual kingdoms of the Church and the Heavenly Jerusalem are prefigured through the individual cities, hamlets, mountains, and borders.

— Etymologies VI.2.8, trans. Barney et al.5 "Whose story it contains" renders cuius historiam continet. The sentence takes its second half from Jerome's summary of Joshua in Letter 53, and it has the same two layers: the kingdoms are destroyed and the land divided, and in the same cities and borders the spiritual kingdoms are "prefigured." A few paragraphs earlier Isidore says that the content of both Testaments is "narrative (historia), moral instruction (mores), or allegorical meaning (allegoria)" (VI.1.11). His summary of the book in the Prefaces to the Books of the Old and New Testament has only the first layer: "In the book of Jesus Nave the children of Israel, with Jesus as leader, cross the Jordan and are circumcised a second time; and then, the nations of the Canaanites having been overthrown, they receive the lots of their inheritance by the division of their land" (Prooemia 24). His On Church Offices counts Joshua first among the "sixteen historical books" (historici libri sedecim) that follow the five books of Moses (I.11.4). The book of names gives the figure in a single line: "Joshua means 'savior,' for he, adumbrating Christ, saved the people from the wilderness and led them into the promised land" (VII.6.51).

22. There were eleven sons of Canaan, from whom descended the ten tribes of Canaanites, whose land the Jews occupied when the Canaanites were expelled. … 23. … Sixth Hivah, from whom the Hivites. Those same were the Gibeonites, from the city of Gibeon, who came as suppliants to Joshua (Joshua 9:3–15).

20. Jericho is said to have been founded by the Jebusites, from whom, it is held, it also took its name. Joshua overthrew it. After that Ozam of Bethel, of the tribe of Ephraim, built another Jericho. But this also, when Jerusalem was attacked by the Romans, was seized and destroyed, due to the treachery of its citizens; because of this a third city was built, and this remains today.

— Etymologies IX.2.22–23 and XV.1.20, trans. Barney et al.6 In the book on peoples the Canaanites are one of the nations descended from Noah's sons, and the conquest appears in a subordinate clause: the Jews occupied their land "when the Canaanites were expelled" (his expulsis). The Gibeonites are placed by descent among the Hivites. In the book on cities, Jericho has a history of three cities. Joshua destroyed the first, the man of Bethel rebuilt it under the curse of Joshua 6:26 (1 Kings 16:34, where he is Hiel), the Romans destroyed the second "due to the treachery of its citizens," and the third still stands. The entry is Eusebius's gazetteer abridged, which has the same three cities, the same "Ozan from Bethel," and the same "faithlessness" of the inhabitants. The conquest is one event in the record of a real city.

The preface to the Questions: "with the foundation of history laid first"

1. The history of the sacred law was not enacted and written down without some foretelling of things to come. …

3. … And since some time ago we composed a whole discourse according to the letter, it is necessary that, with the foundation of history laid first, the allegorical sense should follow. For certain of these things are understood figuratively, as truly prophetic signs going before the things to come.

4. Not everything written in the law and the prophets is veiled in the riddles of mysteries. Together with the things that signify something, things that signify nothing are joined as well. …

5. These figures of things done we have drawn, as we said, from the mystical treasuries of the wise, and gathered into one form for brevity's sake. In them the reader will not read what is ours but will read again what is the ancients'. For what I say, they say, and my voice is their tongue. They are taken, then, from the authors Origen, Victorinus, Ambrose, Jerome, Augustine, Fulgentius, Cassian, and Gregory, outstandingly eloquent in our own time.

— Questions on the Old Testament (Mysticorum expositiones sacramentorum), preface 1, 3–5, the page's own translation.7 The Questions are Isidore's allegorical commentary on the historical books of the Old Testament, from Genesis to Maccabees, and the preface sets out how the allegory stands to the history. The history was "enacted and written down" (gesta atque conscripta) with a view to what was to come. Isidore says he has already written a literal commentary, and the allegory follows "with the foundation of history laid first" (praecedente historiae fundamento). It is not certain which work he meant. Like Augustine before him, he says that not every detail is a figure: some things in the narrative "signify nothing" and are there to hold the significant ones together, as the frame of a harp holds the strings. The preface also says plainly that the work is a florilegium. The reader "will not read what is ours" but the ancients', and the first name on the list of sources is Origen. In the chapters on Joshua that is literally true.

Moses dead, the Jordan halted, the second circumcision: Origen's homilies cut down

2. Moses, then, is dead; the law is dead; the precepts of the law now cease, and Jesus, that is, the Saviour Christ, the Son of God, holds the leadership. He leads the people into the land of which the Lord says: "Blessed are the meek, for they shall possess the land."

… This Jesus, the leader of the people, showed in himself the Lord Jesus Christ both by his name and by his deed. …

Then the people cross the Jordan with the ark of the Testament. But what does it mean that, as the same ark crosses the Jordan, the crowd is led by the priests and Levites, and so, with the water of the river standing in a heap, the people of God make their way unharmed? All these things are fulfilled in the peoples of the Church by a mystical working. … That part of the river's waters sprang back and remained sweet, while part flowed down into the bitter whirlpool of the sea, this figure marks the variety of the baptized …

After the crossing of the Jordan, Jesus comes to Gilgal, and there he is commanded to make knives of stone and to circumcise the children of Israel a second time. Let us ask the Jews how anyone can be circumcised a second time with a fleshly circumcision. For once a man is circumcised, he has nothing more that can be taken away a second time. But by us, for whom the law of Christ is spiritual, these things are fittingly resolved.

— Questions on the Old Testament, "On Joshua" 1.2, 2.1, 3.1–3, 6.1–2, the page's own translation.8 Almost every sentence here is Origen's, from Rufinus's Latin. "Moses died, therefore; for the law ceased" is Origen's reading of Joshua 1:2 (Homilies on Joshua 1.3, 2.1). The sweet and bitter waters of the Jordan are Origen's figure for the baptized who keep their grace and those who fall back (4.2). The two and a half tribes who cross armed to help their brothers are, in both writers, the righteous of the law coming to our aid, with Isaiah, Jeremiah, Daniel, Ezekiel and Hosea named in the same order (Isidore 4.3–4; Origen 3.1). The question to the Jews about the second circumcision is Origen's word for word: "I would in this place inquire of the Jews how anyone can 'be circumcised a second time' with a carnal circumcision" (5.5). This is the one place in the Joshua chapters where Isidore says the letter cannot stand, and the phrase he means is a single detail, "a second time." Jerome said the same of it. The miracle itself is told as it happened. The ark crosses and the river's water stands "in a heap" (in cumulum) before the reader is told that these things are "fulfilled" in the Church. The phrase about Joshua's name and deed is Jerome's: Joshua was "a type of the Lord in name as well as in deed."

Jericho and Rahab: Augustine's figure on Origen's frame

1. After this Jericho is surrounded and stormed. Against it no sword is drawn, no ram is aimed, no spears are brandished. Only the priests' trumpets are used, for seven days on end, and so, with the ark carried round, the walls of Jericho are brought down. Now Jericho is interpreted "moon." …

3. … In this time, which turns in a cycle of seven days, while the ark is carried, that is, while the Church moves round the whole world at the voices of the preachers, as at the sound of trumpets, the walls of Jericho, that is, the pride of the world and the proud obstacles of unbelief, fall down, until at the end of time the last enemy, death, is destroyed, and out of the destruction of the ungodly the one house of Rahab, as the one Church, is set free, cleansed of the shame of fornication through the window of confession, in the blood of remission. …

4. … Those who were found in her house were saved from the destruction of the city, and whoever are found within the Church, they alone are saved. Outside this house, that is, outside the Church, no one is saved.

— Questions on the Old Testament, "On Joshua" 7.1, 3–4, the page's own translation.9 The first sentence tells the siege as a wonder, in terms like those Caesarius used: no sword, no ram, only trumpets. The figure that follows is not Origen's but Augustine's. Augustine's reply to the Manichaean Faustus had read the seven circuits as "the seven days of time" in which the covenant "compasses the whole globe, that in the end, death, the last enemy, may be destroyed, and the Church, like one single house, be saved from the destruction of the ungodly, purified from the defilement of fornication by the window of confession" (Reply to Faustus 12.31). Isidore lifts the sentence. The closing words "outside this house, that is, outside the Church, no one is saved" are Origen's (Homilies on Joshua 3.5). Rahab herself is "the type of the Church, gathered from strange and foreign nations," and the spies are the two Testaments (2.2); her scarlet cord is "the sign of blood" (2.3) and "the sign of the blood of Christ" (7.4). Isidore's shorter Allegories give the same readings in a line each: Rahab "held the figure of the Church, which is saved from the destruction of the world through the scarlet, that is, through the sign of the Lord's passion" (Allegoriae 73). He says nothing about Rahab's lie to the king's men. The destruction of the city is the background the figure needs. Rahab's house is saved "out of the destruction of the ungodly," as the Church will be at the end.

Achan's golden tongue, and "these things the old history reports as done"

1. Meanwhile Achan steals from the devoted things of the city of Jericho a cloak and a golden tongue, and for this, God being angry, some of the people perished at the hand of the enemy. Jericho, then, as has been said, bears the figure of the world. He who defrauds it of its devoted things and hides them in his tent is the one who brings worldly ways into the hidden places of the Church … who seeks out fortune-tellers, soothsayers and augurs …

2. He too defrauds the devoted things of Jericho who brings into the Church the doctrines of heretics or the superstitious study of secular letters. For this is the golden tongue, fitted with brilliant speech. Arius, Marcion and Basilides stole it.

3. For they stole golden rules from Jericho, who tried to bring the sect of the philosophers into the Church and to stain the whole Church of the Lord, so that many were destroyed through them. Therefore they were cast out by anathema, and, as if by a heap of stones, they were crushed and extinguished by the multitude of their sins. …

— Questions on the Old Testament, "On Joshua" 8.1–3, the page's own translation.10 The golden tongue as the eloquence of the philosophers comes from Origen, who wrote that "Valentinus did, and Basilides; this Marcion also did. These men stole 'tongues of gold' from Jericho" (Homilies on Joshua 7.7). Isidore's list has Arius where Origen's had Valentinus. Arianism was the heresy of the Visigoths themselves until their king renounced it at the Third Council of Toledo in 589. The defeat at Ai is told as fact, "some of the people perished at the hand of the enemy," and the figure of the heretics "crushed" as if "by a heap of stones" follows the stoning and the cairn of Joshua 7:25–26. Isidore does not mention that Achan's sons and daughters were stoned with him, the case Augustine and Jerome had to face. In the Allegories Achan simply "signifies the wicked man and the sinner, who after faith seeks worldly ways" (Allegoriae 75).

Two chapters later Isidore copies Origen's clearest statement of the order of the two senses. He sets out which tribes stood on Gerizim to bless and which on Ebal to curse, with Reuben among the second for having "defiled his father's bed," and then says: "These things, indeed, the old history reports as done. But we must examine what is conveyed in the account of the mystical understanding" (10.2). Origen had written, "These things indeed the histories of the ancients relate as deeds done; but how shall we refer this narration of the history to a mystical understanding?" (9.7). The tribes who bless are those who serve God from love of the promises, and those who curse are those who serve from fear of punishment.

The Gibeonites and the sun at Gibeon: "not only then"

1. Meanwhile the Gibeonites, terrified by fear of destruction, came to Jesus with fraud and cunning, dressed in rags and old shoes, begging to be saved, and at once they receive salvation from Jesus. Yet when he learned of their trick, he made them hewers of wood and carriers of water. In their figure are shown those who come from the world to the Church and have faith in God … but in their habits are held by their former way of life, putting on the old man with his deeds, wrapped in old vices, as these were covered in old rags and shoes.

1. That at Gibeon, while Jesus son of Nave was fighting, the sun and the moon are reported to have stood still until the enemies of Israel were destroyed, Scripture says: "The sun stood still over Gibeon, and the moon over the valley of Aijalon"; and Scripture added: "For never did God so hear a man."

2. Our Jesus, then, made the sun stand still, not only then, but much more now at his coming, while we wage war against the nations of vices … The Sun of Justice stands by us unceasingly; he never deserts us, and he does not hasten to set, because he himself said: "Behold, I am with you all days, even to the end of the world."

3. The five kings indicate the five bodily senses, which assault the Gibeonites, that is, fleshly men. …

— Questions on the Old Testament, "On Joshua" 11.1, 12.1–3, the page's own translation.11 Both chapters are Origen's. The Gibeonites as believers who never change their ways are from Homilies on Joshua 10.1, and the five kings as the five senses from 11.4. Joshua's oath to the Gibeonites, which Ambrose made a lesson in keeping faith with enemies, is not discussed. The long day is introduced with perhibetur, "is reported," the ordinary verb in Isidore's Latin for what a source records. The next sentence is Origen's from his first homily: "my Jesus made 'the sun stand still' not only then, but much more so now in his coming" (1.5). The figure is built on the miracle. Christ's work "now" is "much more" than what Joshua did "then," and the comparison takes the event at Gibeon as given. The life of Joshua in On the Birth and Death of the Fathers states it flatly: he "commanded the sky that the sun should stand still."

The nations destroyed or driven out, and the Canaanite who stayed

1. Then, the nations having been destroyed or driven out, Joshua divides the land of promise to the peoples by lot. So Christ too in a certain way drove out the nations from before the face of his faithful, the evil spirits of the errors of the Gentiles, and divided the land in us by lot …

1. As for this: when the land of promise had been taken and was being divided among the people of Israel, the Canaanite, a Gentile people, is said not to have been killed in the tribe of Ephraim but made to pay tribute, as it is written in the book of Judges: "The Canaanite dwelt in the midst of Ephraim, paying tribute." What does the tributary, what does the Canaanite, a Gentile people, signify, but vice? For often we enter the land of promise with great virtues, because we are strengthened by an inward hope of eternity. But while, after the great vices have been slain, we still keep certain small ones, we as it were allow the Canaanite to live in our land.

2. Yet he is made tributary, because this very vice which we cannot subdue we humbly turn to our own profit … Hence it is well written again: "These are the nations which the Lord left, to instruct Israel by them."

3. For certain very small vices of ours are kept back for this purpose, that our attention may always exercise itself anxiously in the struggle, and not be proud of victory … Israel, then, is instructed by the Gentiles that were kept back …

— Questions on the Old Testament, "On Joshua" 13.1 and 18.1–3, "On the Canaanite people not destroyed," the page's own translation.12 Chapter 13 states the conquest in a clause, "the nations having been destroyed or driven out" (exstinctis … vel ejectis gentibus), and then reads it as Christ driving out the evil spirits "in a certain way" (quodammodo). The ban itself gets no comment. The last chapter on Joshua is the only place Isidore takes up the Canaanites who were left, and it is not his own. It is a passage of Gregory the Great's Moralia on Job, copied almost word for word: "the Gentile people of Canaan are not said to be slain, but to be made tributary to the tribe of Ephraim … For what does the Canaanite, a Gentile people, denote saving a fault?" (Gregory, Moralia 4.24.44). Gregory cited Joshua 16:10 for the verse; Isidore says it is in Judges, where Judges 1:29 says much the same of Gezer. The answer is moral. God leaves a small vice in the soul to keep it humble, as he left the nations in the land to train Israel (Judges 3:1). It takes for granted that Canaanites did remain in Ephraim. It does not set this against the book's "Joshua took all the land," as Origen did, or explain it by Israel's disobedience, as Chrysostom and Theodoret did. Caesarius had given the historical answer of Judges, that God left the nations to test Israel. Isidore keeps the verse but reads it only of the soul.

Jericho's Sabbath: an argument from the event

2. … Again, if it is a crime not to keep the rest of the Sabbath, why did Jesus Nave, the disciple and successor of Moses, at the Lord's command, lead out his army in arms for seven days on end, among which there was surely a Sabbath, and, with the ark going round and the trumpets sounding, overthrow the walls of Jericho?

— On the Catholic Faith against the Jews (De fide catholica) II.15.2, "On the ceasing of the Sabbath," the page's own translation.13 Isidore's treatise against the Jews uses the siege of Jericho as evidence. The Sabbath, he argues, was never meant to be kept in the flesh, since God himself ordered Joshua to march on it. The argument works only if the march happened. It is the argument Tertullian had made four centuries earlier in his Answer to the Jews 4, where the seven days around Jericho must have included a Sabbath. Here, as everywhere in Isidore, the destruction of the city at God's command is cited and not questioned.

Notes

  • Isidore's sources. The chapters "On Joshua" in the Questions on the Old Testament are made up of Origen's Homilies on Joshua in Rufinus's Latin (1.3, 1.5, 2.1, 3.1, 3.5, 4.2, 5.5, 7.7, 9.1–7, 10.1, 11.4), Augustine's Reply to Faustus 12.31 on Jericho and Rahab, and Gregory's Moralia 4.24.44 on the Canaanites who were left. Isidore says in his preface that "my voice is their tongue." He keeps Origen's statements that the events happened ("these things the old history reports as done"; the sun stood still "not only then") and his one denial of a detail, the fleshly "second" circumcision. He leaves out Origen's denials of the sweeping formulas ("Jesus took all the land," 15.7; Ai deserted "forever," 8.5). Compare Origen of Alexandria on the Conquest of Canaan, Augustine, and the other Latin bishops who passed Origen's reading on, Ambrose and Caesarius of Arles.
  • History and figure kept apart. Isidore puts the two readings in different books. The chronicles, the life of Joshua, the Etymologies and the Prefaces give the conquest as dated history and use no figure. The Questions and the Allegories give the figure on the "foundation" of that history. Where the two meet in one sentence (Etymologies VI.2.8), the kingdoms are first "destroyed" and only then "prefigure" the Church. On the chronicles, compare Eusebius, whose Chronicle gave the twenty-seven years and the Greek synchronisms, and Augustine's City of God XVIII.
  • The moral question. Isidore never raises the moral problem of the ban. He did not take up the answers that were open to him: Augustine's questions on the Heptateuch, with the ambush at Ai as a just war and Achan's children left to God's judgment, or Caesarius's "according to the letter" reply to the Manichaeans. The patristic answers are weighed at Do the patristic defenses answer the moral problem of the ḥerem? His Canaanites are the descendants of Ham's son Canaan (Etymologies IX.2.22); on Noah's curse, see the curse of Ham.
  • Rahab. Isidore reads Rahab as the Church and keeps the scarlet cord as the sign of Christ's blood, which Caesarius had dropped. He says nothing of her lie. See the Rahab timeline and the scarlet-cord typology.
  • Translation. The Latin of the Questions, the Allegories, On the Birth and Death of the Fathers, the Prefaces, On Church Offices and On the Catholic Faith is Arévalo's text as reprinted in Migne. The critical editions now in progress were not consulted for this page. The English of those works is the page's own. The Etymologies are quoted from Barney et al. (2006), checked against Lindsay's Latin; the Chronicon from Wolf's translation, with the year-count from the Latin.
