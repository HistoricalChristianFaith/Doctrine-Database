Martianus Capella — the seven arts as bridesmaids

Dates: c. 420 (De Nuptiis Philologiae et Mercurii, composed after the sack of Rome in 410 and before the Vandal conquest of Africa in 429; Martianus fl. c. 410–420, a Latin writer of Roman Africa) · Wikipedia
Summary: Where Varro listed nine arts, Martianus Capella presents seven. His sprawling allegory The Marriage of Philology and Mercury stages a wedding of the gods at which the seven liberal arts, personified as learned bridesmaids, are given to the bride Philology and each in turn delivers a compendium of her discipline. Without Varro’s medicine and architecture, his set is grammar, dialectic, and rhetoric plus geometry, arithmetic, astronomy, and harmony (music). The Catholic Encyclopedia places his book at the head of the medieval series of textbooks on the seven arts (see below). He does not use the later labels trivium and quadrivium.

Claims & evidence

The seven arts as the wedding gift — each a bridesmaid who then teaches her discipline

The frame is an elaborate mythological fiction: the god Mercury weds the maiden Philology (“Love of Learning”), and among the wedding gifts the bride receives seven serving-maids — the seven liberal arts. At the close of Book II, Phoebus (Apollo) rises to present them one by one:

hic Phoebus exsurgit, fratris officium non detrectans, ac singulas ex famulitio delectuque Cyllenio incipit admovere, quae tam pulchrae cunctis quam ornatissimae refulsere.

— De Nuptiis Philologiae et Mercurii II.218, Latin text (“Here Phoebus rises, not shrinking from his brother’s duty, and begins to lead forward one by one, from Mercury’s household and retinue, [maids] who shone out as beautiful to all as they were richly adorned”).1 Each maid then speaks across the remaining seven books — Grammar in Book III, Dialectic in IV, Rhetoric in V, Geometry in VI, Arithmetic in VII, Astronomy in VIII, and Harmony (music) in IX — so that the allegory is also a one-volume compendium of the curriculum. The Catholic Encyclopedia describes the work as carrying out “the allegory that Phoebus presents the Seven Liberal Arts as maids to the bride Philology,” and “in the seven books that follow, each of the Liberal Arts presents the sum of her teaching.”2

Grammar the aged healer — the knife and file that cut away faults of speech

Grammar, first of the seven, is the only one drawn as an old woman — born, she says, at Memphis when Osiris still reigned — and she carries a physician’s case of instruments for tending the wounds of childish speech:

gestabat haec autem teres quoddam ex compactis annexionibus ferculum, quod levi exterius elephanto praenitebat, unde velut medendi sollers magistra curandorum vulnerum insignia proferebat. nam ex eodem scalprum primo vibranti demonstrabat acumine, quo dicebat circumcidi infantibus vitia posse linguarum.

— De Nuptiis III.223–224, Latin text (“She carried a rounded case of joined ivory panels, from which, like a skilled mistress of healing, she drew the instruments for tending wounds: first she displayed a scalpel with a quivering point, with which, she said, the faults of children’s speech could be cut away” — and, just after, a gold-inlaid file to smooth away roughness).3 In the figure grammar is the surgeon who corrects the tongue, an art that forms the speaker.

Notes

  • The number and the labels. Martianus never uses the words trivium or quadrivium: “quadrivium” is credited to Boethius as his coinage (⚠ unverified — not yet sourced) and “trivium” is medieval. His four mathematical arts run in a different internal order (Geometry, Arithmetic, Astronomy, Harmony) from Boethius’s (Arithmetic, Geometry, Music, Astronomy). Martianus’s set of seven omits the medicine and architecture that Varro had included.4
  • Dialectic. Dialectic, the fourth maid, enters pale and keen-eyed, a serpent coiled in her left hand and wax tablets fitted with a hidden hook in her right — “because her left hid its viperous ambush under her cloak, it was the right hand she offered to all” — an emblem of an art whose subtlety can ensnare as well as instruct.5 Personifications of the seven arts are reported on cathedral portals and in manuscripts down to the Renaissance (⚠ unverified — not yet sourced).
  • A pagan frame and Christian readers. Martianus never states his own religion, and the machinery of his allegory is pagan. The book is reported to have been copied, glossed, and lectured on by Christian schoolmasters for centuries (⚠ unverified — not yet sourced). Cassiodorus and Isidore, later on this timeline, treat the same seven arts and (with Augustine) order them to the reading of Scripture.
