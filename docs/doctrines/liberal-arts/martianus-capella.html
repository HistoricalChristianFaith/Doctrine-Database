Martianus Capella — the seven arts canonized as bridesmaids

Dates: c. 420 (De Nuptiis Philologiae et Mercurii, composed after the sack of Rome in 410 and before the Vandal conquest of Africa in 429; Martianus fl. c. 410–420, a Latin writer of Roman Africa) · Wikipedia
Summary: Where Varro gave the West a list of nine arts, Martianus Capella fixed the enduring seven. His sprawling allegory The Marriage of Philology and Mercury stages a wedding of the gods at which the seven liberal arts, personified as learned bridesmaids, are given to the bride Philology and each in turn delivers a compendium of her discipline. By dropping the two arts of the body and of building — medicine and architecture — from Varro’s nine, Martianus set the canon at grammar, dialectic, and rhetoric plus geometry, arithmetic, astronomy, and harmony (music); and because his book became the standard schoolroom textbook of the early Middle Ages, it was his seven that the cathedral schools and universities inherited. He canonized the number and the set — not the later labels trivium and quadrivium, which he never uses.

Claims & evidence

The seven arts as the wedding gift — each a bridesmaid who then teaches her discipline

The frame is an elaborate mythological fiction: the god Mercury weds the maiden Philology (“Love of Learning”), and among the wedding gifts the bride receives seven serving-maids — the seven liberal arts. At the close of Book II, Phoebus (Apollo) rises to present them one by one:

hic Phoebus exsurgit, fratris officium non detrectans, ac singulas ex famulitio delectuque Cyllenio incipit admovere, quae tam pulchrae cunctis quam ornatissimae refulsere.

— De Nuptiis Philologiae et Mercurii II.218, Latin text (“Here Phoebus rises, not shrinking from his brother’s duty, and begins to lead forward one by one, from Mercury’s household and retinue, [maids] who shone out as beautiful to all as they were richly adorned”).1 Each maid then speaks across the remaining seven books — Grammar in Book III, Dialectic in IV, Rhetoric in V, Geometry in VI, Arithmetic in VII, Astronomy in VIII, and Harmony (music) in IX — so that the allegory doubles as a one-volume encyclopedia of the whole curriculum. As the standard reference of the medieval schools put it, the work carries out “the allegory that Phoebus presents the Seven Liberal Arts as maids to the bride Philology,” and “in the seven books that follow, each of the Liberal Arts presents the sum of her teaching.”2

Grammar the aged healer — the knife and file that cut away faults of speech

The personifications became the stock images of every later depiction of the arts. Grammar, first of the seven, is the only one drawn as an old woman — born, she says, at Memphis when Osiris still reigned — and she carries a physician’s case of instruments for tending the wounds of childish speech:

gestabat haec autem teres quoddam ex compactis annexionibus ferculum, quod levi exterius elephanto praenitebat, unde velut medendi sollers magistra curandorum vulnerum insignia proferebat. nam ex eodem scalprum primo vibranti demonstrabat acumine, quo dicebat circumcidi infantibus vitia posse linguarum.

— De Nuptiis III.223–224, Latin text (“She carried a rounded case of joined ivory panels, from which, like a skilled mistress of healing, she drew the instruments for tending wounds: first she displayed a scalpel with a quivering point, with which, she said, the faults of children’s speech could be cut away” — and, just after, a gold-inlaid file to smooth away roughness).3 The figure — grammar as the surgeon who corrects the tongue — captures the whole ancient conviction that the arts form a person; it is the picture the medieval classroom kept.

Notes

  • He fixed the number, not the labels. Martianus never uses the words trivium or quadrivium: “quadrivium” is Boethius’s later coinage and “trivium” is medieval. His four mathematical arts even run in a different internal order (Geometry, Arithmetic, Astronomy, Harmony) from Boethius’s (Arithmetic, Geometry, Music, Astronomy). What Martianus canonized was the set of seven — by dropping the medicine and architecture that Varro had added to the Greek circle of studies — and it was this seven that the schools received.4
  • The images that furnished a millennium. Dialectic, the fourth maid, enters pale and keen-eyed, a serpent coiled in her left hand and wax tablets fitted with a hidden hook in her right — “because her left hid its viperous ambush under her cloak, it was the right hand she offered to all” — the emblem of an art whose subtlety can as easily ensnare as instruct.5 Such personifications, endlessly copied, put the seven arts on cathedral portals and manuscript pages down to the Renaissance.
  • A pagan frame, a Christian afterlife. Martianus never states his own religion, and the machinery of his allegory is thoroughly pagan; yet the book was copied, glossed, and lectured on for a thousand years by Christian schoolmasters — the neutral scaffolding onto which Cassiodorus and Isidore hung the same seven arts, now ordered (with Augustine) to the reading of Scripture. He supplies the timeline’s content: the fixed list of “the liberal arts” that every later witness presupposes.
