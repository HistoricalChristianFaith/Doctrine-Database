John Milton — learning as the repair of the ruins of the Fall

Dates: 1644 · Wikipedia
Summary: Milton (1608–1674), English poet and controversialist, published the tractate Of Education. To Master Samuel Hartlib as a single sheet at London in June 1644, among his prose works of that decade. In a brief letter on the reform of schooling Milton states that the end of learning is to “repair the ruines of our first Parents” — to restore in fallen man the knowledge and love of God that the Fall defaced — and true education is that which forms a whole and virtuous person fit for “all the offices both private and publick of Peace and War.” Study is subordinated throughout to the knowledge of things and of God, with language treated as its instrument.

Claims & evidence

The end of learning is to repair the ruins of the Fall

The end then of Learning is to repair the ruines of our first Parents by regaining to know God aright, and out of that knowledge to love him, to imitate him, to be like him, as we may the neerest by possessing our souls of true vertue, which being united to the heavenly grace of faith makes up the highest perfection.

— Of Education (1644).1 Milton’s answer to what is learning for? is not information, nor the perfection of the mind for its own sake, but the restoration of the divine image the Fall defaced — knowing God, and out of that knowledge loving and imitating him. Like Basil and Augustine earlier on this timeline, he orders study to virtue and the knowledge of God. The next sentence of the tractate sets the method of the curriculum that follows: because the understanding “cannot in this body found it self but on sensible things, nor arrive so clearly to the knowledge of God and things invisible, as by orderly conning over the visible and inferior creature,” the ascent to God runs through the ordered study of the created world — the languages, the sciences, and the arts he goes on to prescribe.

A “complete and generous” education forms the whole person

I call therefore a compleat and generous Education that which fits a man to perform justly, skilfully and magnanimously all the offices both private and publick of Peace and War.

— Of Education.2 Milton’s definition measures a schooling not by the trade it lands but by the person it makes: just, skilful, and magnanimous, and equal to the full range of private and public life. On that end he builds a demanding course, running from grammar and the languages (Latin, Greek, Italian, Hebrew) through arithmetic, geometry, astronomy, and natural philosophy, into medicine, ethics, politics, law, and theology, and on into literature, all proposed for a single academy.

Language as the instrument of knowledge of things

Milton subordinates the languages that open the curriculum to the substance they carry. A man who learned every tongue but never the “solid things” the tongues convey has, for Milton, missed the point of learning:

And though a Linguist should pride himself to have all the Tongues that Babel cleft the world into, yet, if he have not studied the solid things in them as well as the Words & Lexicons, he were nothing so much to be esteem’d a learned man, as any Yeoman or Tradesman competently wise in his Mother Dialect only.

— Of Education.3 In Milton’s account languages are the instrument of knowing real things and, through them, God. The remark also ranks the “Yeoman or Tradesman competently wise in his Mother Dialect” alongside the linguist who has learned only words.

Notes

  • Occasion and form. Of Education is a short open letter to the reformer Samuel Hartlib, written amid the educational and social projects of the Hartlib circle and printed as a single sheet in June 1644 — the same busy prose decade as Milton’s Areopagitica. It is a programme, not a treatise. Its curriculum is reported to have been thought impossibly demanding in its own day (⚠ unverified — not yet sourced).
  • Placement on the timeline. He stands between the founding of Harvard (1636) and Newman (1852), and orders learning to godliness and to “all the offices both private and publick,” making virtue and the knowledge of God the end of study.
