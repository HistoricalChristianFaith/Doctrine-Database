John Milton — learning as the repair of the ruins of the Fall

Dates: 1644 (the tractate Of Education. To Master Samuel Hartlib, a single sheet published at London in June 1644, among Milton’s prose works of that decade). Milton 1608–1674, English poet and controversialist · Wikipedia
Summary: In a brief letter on the reform of schooling Milton gave the knowledge-for-virtue tradition its boldest Protestant statement: the whole end of learning is to “repair the ruines of our first Parents” — to restore in fallen man the knowledge and love of God that the Fall defaced — and true education is that which forms a whole and virtuous person fit for “all the offices both private and publick of Peace and War.” Study is subordinated throughout to the knowledge of things and of God, with language treated as its mere instrument.

Claims & evidence

The end of learning is to repair the ruins of the Fall

The end then of Learning is to repair the ruines of our first Parents by regaining to know God aright, and out of that knowledge to love him, to imitate him, to be like him, as we may the neerest by possessing our souls of true vertue, which being united to the heavenly grace of faith makes up the highest perfection.

— Of Education (1644).1 This is the sharpest form of the ancient Christian answer to what is learning for?: not information, not even the perfection of the mind for its own sake, but the restoration of the divine image the Fall defaced — knowing God, and out of that knowledge loving and imitating him. It sets Milton squarely with Basil and Augustine in ordering all study upward to virtue and the knowledge of God, and gives the knowledge-for-virtue thesis its most emphatic Protestant voice. The same sentence sets the method of the curriculum that follows: because the understanding “cannot in this body found it self but on sensible things, nor arrive so clearly to the knowledge of God and things invisible, as by orderly conning over the visible and inferior creature,” the ascent to God runs through the ordered study of the created world — the languages, the sciences, and the arts he goes on to prescribe.

A “complete and generous” education forms the whole person

I call therefore a compleat and generous Education that which fits a man to perform justly, skilfully and magnanimously all the offices both private and publick of Peace and War.

— Of Education.2 Milton’s definition — still quoted as a motto by the modern classical-education movement — measures a schooling not by the trade it lands but by the person it makes: just, skilful, and magnanimous, and equal to the full range of private and public life. On that end he builds a rigorous and famously ambitious course, running from grammar and the languages (Latin, Greek, Italian, Hebrew) through arithmetic, geometry, astronomy, and natural philosophy, into medicine, ethics, politics, law, and theology, and out again into the literature that crowns them — the whole “circle of knowledge” the seven arts had long organized, now proposed for a single academy.

Language is but the instrument — knowledge is of things

Milton subordinates even the languages that open the curriculum to the substance they carry. A man who learned every tongue but never the “solid things” the tongues convey has, for Milton, missed the point of learning:

And though a Linguist should pride himself to have all the Tongues that Babel cleft the world into, yet, if he have not studied the solid things in them as well as the Words & Lexicons, he were nothing so much to be esteem’d a learned man, as any Yeoman or Tradesman competently wise in his Mother Dialect only.

— Of Education.3 The remark keeps the whole enterprise pointed past cleverness at wisdom: languages and the arts are “but the instrument” of knowing real things and, through them, God — a thoroughly Protestant echo of the fathers’ refusal to let the liberal arts become an end in themselves. It also, in passing, honours the practical wisdom of the “Yeoman or Tradesman” over the merely verbal scholar — a Reformation valuation of honest work that keeps company with the dignity of the mechanical arts.

Notes

  • Occasion and form. Of Education is a short open letter to the reformer Samuel Hartlib, written amid the educational and social projects of the Hartlib circle and printed as a single sheet in June 1644 — the same busy prose decade as Milton’s Areopagitica. It is a programme, not a treatise, and its curriculum was widely thought impossibly demanding even in its own day; its enduring weight is in its statement of the end of learning, not its detailed syllabus.
  • Where Milton stands on the timeline. He fills the long gap between the Puritan founding of Harvard (1636) and Newman (1852), and states the thesis they all share — learning ordered to godliness and the good of church and commonwealth — with unusual force. Against Newman’s later, careful separation of the intellectual good from the moral (“Knowledge is one thing, virtue is another”), Milton frankly makes virtue and the knowledge of God the whole point of study; the two are the poles of the wiki’s knowledge-for-virtue and knowledge-as-its-own-end arguments.
