Aristotle on the liberal vs. the servile arts

Dates: c. 335 BC · Wikipedia
Summary: Aristotle (384–322 BC), in Book VIII of the Politics, divides occupations into the liberal (free) and the illiberal, vulgar, or (in his word) banausic — the crafts, trades, and paid employments learned for wages and bodily need — and lays down a test: any pursuit that makes a free man less fit for virtue is vulgar. Education, he argues, is ordered to the right use of leisure (scholē) and to virtue, to be valued for its own sake, not for utility or pay.

Claims & evidence

Occupations divide into liberal and illiberal; what unfits a free man for virtue is vulgar

occupations are divided into liberal and illiberal; and to young children should be imparted only such kinds of knowledge as will be useful to them without vulgarizing them. And any occupation, art, or science, which makes the body or soul or mind of the freeman less fit for the practice or exercise of virtue, is vulgar; wherefore we call those arts vulgar which tend to deform the body, and likewise all paid employments, for they absorb and degrade the mind.

— Politics VIII.2 (1337b), trans. Benjamin Jowett, source. Aristotle states the criterion in moral terms: an art is “vulgar” (banausic) when it leaves body, soul, or mind less fit for virtue — and he names paid employments among the cases, because “they absorb and degrade the mind.”

Some learning is to be pursued for its own sake, with a view to leisure

there are branches of learning and education which we must study merely with a view to leisure spent in intellectual activity, and these are to be valued for their own sake; whereas those kinds of knowledge which are useful in business are to be deemed necessary, and exist for the sake of other things.

— Politics VIII.3 (1338a), trans. Benjamin Jowett, source. For Aristotle the highest education aims at the use of leisure — not idleness, but intellectual activity valued for its own sake. Knowledge “useful in business” is necessary but exists for the sake of other things; the liberal studies are valued for themselves. Compare John Henry Newman (1852) on knowledge as its own end, later on this timeline.

A liberal art pursued for others’ sake becomes servile

some liberal arts quite proper for a freeman to acquire, but only in a certain degree, and if he attend to them too closely, in order to attain perfection in them, the same evil effects will follow. The object also which a man sets before him makes a great difference; if he does or learns anything for his own sake or for the sake of his friends, or with a view to excellence the action will not appear illiberal; but if done for the sake of others, the very same action will be thought menial and servile.

— Politics VIII.2 (1337b), trans. Benjamin Jowett, source. Here Aristotle ties liberal and servile not to the subject but to the end for which it is done, and to the degree of application: the same action is not illiberal when done for oneself, for friends, or with a view to excellence, and is “menial and servile” when done for the sake of others.

Notes

  • Aristotle addresses the education of free citizens in a society whose leisure rested on the labour of others, including slaves. Later writers on this timeline argue for the dignity of the mechanical arts and manual work (Hugh of St. Victor), and John Dewey criticizes the liberal/servile distinction as the product of a slave economy.
  • Later Christian writers on this timeline keep the liberal/servile language while ordering learning to the knowledge and love of God. His rejection of “all paid employments” bears on the later question whether an education pursued for a living is “illiberal.”
  • His teacher Plato had described education as the soul’s conversion toward the Good; the Latin writers — Cicero, Varro, Seneca, Quintilian — carried the distinction into the Roman world as artes liberales.
