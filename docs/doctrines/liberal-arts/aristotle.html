Aristotle on the liberal vs. the servile arts

Dates: c. 335 BC · Wikipedia
Summary: Aristotle (384–322 BC) is the fountainhead of the whole question. Book VIII of the Politics divides occupations into the liberal (free) and the illiberal, vulgar, or (in his word) banausic — the crafts, trades, and paid employments learned for wages and bodily need — and lays down the test that has framed the debate ever since: any pursuit that makes a free man less fit for virtue is vulgar. Education, he argues, is ordered to the right use of leisure (scholē) and to virtue, to be valued for its own sake, not for utility or pay.

Claims & evidence

Occupations divide into liberal and illiberal; what unfits a free man for virtue is vulgar

occupations are divided into liberal and illiberal; and to young children should be imparted only such kinds of knowledge as will be useful to them without vulgarizing them. And any occupation, art, or science, which makes the body or soul or mind of the freeman less fit for the practice or exercise of virtue, is vulgar; wherefore we call those arts vulgar which tend to deform the body, and likewise all paid employments, for they absorb and degrade the mind.

— Politics VIII.2 (1337b), trans. Benjamin Jowett, source. The criterion is moral, not snobbish in Aristotle’s own eyes: an art is “vulgar” (banausic) when it cramps the soul and leaves it less able to pursue virtue — and he names paid employment as the paradigm case, because working for a wage subordinates the mind to another’s ends. This single sentence is the seed of the entire liberal/servile distinction the West would inherit.

Some learning is to be pursued for its own sake, with a view to leisure

there are branches of learning and education which we must study merely with a view to leisure spent in intellectual activity, and these are to be valued for their own sake; whereas those kinds of knowledge which are useful in business are to be deemed necessary, and exist for the sake of other things.

— Politics VIII.3 (1338a), trans. Benjamin Jowett, source. For Aristotle the highest education aims at the noble use of leisure — not idleness, but the free activity of the mind that is its own end. Knowledge “useful in business” is necessary but subordinate, pursued for the sake of something else; the liberal studies are pursued for themselves. This is the ancient charter of learning as an end in itself, the thesis Newman would restate in The Idea of a University.

Even a liberal art becomes vulgar if pursued for use, pay, or others’ sake

some liberal arts quite proper for a freeman to acquire, but only in a certain degree, and if he attend to them too closely, in order to attain perfection in them, the same evil effects will follow. The object also which a man sets before him makes a great difference; if he does or learns anything for his own sake or for the sake of his friends, or with a view to excellence the action will not appear illiberal; but if done for the sake of others, the very same action will be thought menial and servile.

— Politics VIII.2 (1337b), trans. Benjamin Jowett, source. The crucial refinement: for Aristotle liberal and servile are not fixed to the subject but to the end for which it is done. The very same activity is free when done for the sake of virtue or a friend, and menial when done for hire or at another’s command. This is why the whole later argument turns on purpose: is study ordered to wisdom and virtue, or to pay? — and it is the ground on which the fathers can baptize the pagan curriculum by re-directing it to God.

Notes

  • Aristotle’s scheme is aristocratic and rests on a leisured class supported by the labour of others (including slaves), a premise the gospel unsettles — hence the later Christian argument for the dignity of the mechanical arts and manual work (Paul the tentmaker; the monastic ora et labora; Hugh of St. Victor), which contests precisely his contempt for the banausic.
  • The end-not-subject criterion is the hinge the whole timeline turns on: it lets a Christian keep the liberal/servile language while re-ordering learning to a new highest end — the knowledge and love of God — rather than to leisured self-cultivation. It also feeds the argument weighing whether an education pursued for a living is “illiberal” — where his contempt for “all paid employments” is flagged as the point his aristocratic premise, resting on a slave economy, presses too far.
  • His teacher Plato supplied the deeper premise (education as the soul’s conversion toward the Good); the Latin transmitters — Cicero, Varro, Seneca, Quintilian — carried the distinction into the Roman world as artes liberales.
