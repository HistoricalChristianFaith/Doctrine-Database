Cassiodorus — the seven arts as a Christian vocation at Vivarium

Dates: c. 560 (Institutiones divinarum et saecularium litterarum, composed across the 550s with later revisions; Cassiodorus c. 485–c. 585) · Wikipedia
Summary: A former praetorian prefect who retired to found the monastery of Vivarium in Calabria, Cassiodorus wrote a manual for monks that sets the secular curriculum beside the study of Scripture. His Institutions of Divine and Secular Learning joins the two in a single course of study: Book I guides the monk through Scripture and the commentators, Book II surveys the seven secular arts (grammar, rhetoric, dialectic; arithmetic, geometry, music, astronomy) as aids to understanding the sacred text. In the same work he describes the copying of manuscripts as a form of Christian warfare — the scribe is “to preach to men with the hand.”

Claims & evidence

Secular learning joined to Scripture — the arts as preparation for the sacred page

In the second book on the arts and disciplines of liberal studies some few things ought to be deleted; and yet in this material there is little harm to the person who slips, if he errs while keeping his faith firm. Whatever has been found in Divine Scripture on such matters will be better understood if one has prior acquaintance with them. It is well-known that, at the beginning of spiritual wisdom, information on these subjects was sowed, as it were, which secular teachers afterwards cleverly transferred to their own rules, as we have noted at suitable places in our Psalm Commentary.

— Institutiones I, Preface §6, trans. James W. & Barbara Halporn.1 For Cassiodorus the liberal arts matter because they clarify Scripture, and their contents were, on his reading, sown in the sacred books first and only later transferred by the secular teachers to their own rules. Compare the “handmaid” ordering of Clement and Augustine earlier on this timeline. In the same preface he explains his purpose — “I was moved by divine love to devise for you, with God’s help, these introductory books to take the place of a teacher” — so that both “the textual disposition of Holy Scripture and a compact sketch of secular letters may be unfolded.”2

The seven arts enumerated — the three of language and the four of number

In this book we must first speak about the art of grammar, which is clearly the origin and basis of the liberal letters. … Second, we will discuss the art of rhetoric, which we consider entirely indispensable and honorable particularly in civil cases because of its brilliance and eloquence. Third, logic, which is called dialectic. This discipline, to the extent that the secular teachers speak of it, separates truth from falsity by subtle and concise discussion. Fourth, mathematics, which includes four disciplines: arithmetic, geometry, music, and astronomy.

— Institutiones II, Preface §4, trans. Halporn.3 Book II is “arranged according to the seven divisions of secular letters”: the three arts of language — grammar, rhetoric, dialectic — and the four of quantity gathered under “mathematics.” Cassiodorus does not use the words trivium and quadrivium (the first label is medieval; the second is used by Boethius), but his seven-part scheme divides the arts the same way.

The dignity of the copyist — “to preach to men with the hand”

A happy purpose, a praiseworthy zeal, to preach to men with the hand, to set free tongues with one’s fingers and in silence to give mankind salvation and to fight with pen and ink against the unlawful snares of the devil.

— Institutiones I.30, trans. Halporn.4 The chapter on the monastery’s copyists (antiquarii) presents their manual labour as a ministry: by reading Scripture the scribe instructs his own mind, and by copying it he spreads the Lord’s precepts far and wide — so that “Satan receives as many wounds as the scribe writes words of the Lord.”

Notes

  • Vivarium. Cassiodorus founded the monastery of Vivarium and made the study and copying of texts — sacred first, secular in their service — the monks’ daily work. His Institutiones is the reading-list and rationale for that programme.
  • Placement on the timeline. On this timeline Cassiodorus comes after Martianus Capella’s set of seven arts and Boethius’s mathematical handbooks, orders the arts to Scripture as Augustine had, and precedes Isidore of Seville, whose Etymologiae also treats the seven.
