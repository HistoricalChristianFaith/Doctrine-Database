Cassiodorus — the seven arts as a Christian vocation at Vivarium

Dates: c. 560 (Institutiones divinarum et saecularium litterarum, composed across the 550s with later revisions; Cassiodorus c. 485–c. 585) · Wikipedia
Summary: A former praetorian prefect who retired to found the monastery of Vivarium in Calabria, Cassiodorus wrote the manual that fused the pagan curriculum with the monastic life. His Institutions of Divine and Secular Learning yokes the two in a single course of study: Book I guides the monk through Scripture and the commentators, Book II surveys the seven secular arts (grammar, rhetoric, dialectic; arithmetic, geometry, music, astronomy) as tools that unlock the sacred text. In the same spirit he raised the copying of manuscripts to a form of Christian warfare — the scribe “preaches with the hand.” Where Augustine gave the principle and Martianus Capella the textbook, Cassiodorus built the institution: the monastery as the place where classical learning would be preserved by being pressed into the service of the Word.

Claims & evidence

Secular learning yoked to Scripture — the arts as preparation for the sacred page

In the second book on the arts and disciplines of liberal studies some few things ought to be deleted; and yet in this material there is little harm to the person who slips, if he errs while keeping his faith firm. Whatever has been found in Divine Scripture on such matters will be better understood if one has prior acquaintance with them. It is well-known that, at the beginning of spiritual wisdom, information on these subjects was sowed, as it were, which secular teachers afterwards cleverly transferred to their own rules, as we have noted at suitable places in our Psalm Commentary.

— Institutiones I, Preface §6, trans. James W. & Barbara Halporn.1 The move is the same “handmaid” ordering the fathers had drawn, now built into a curriculum: the liberal arts matter because they clarify Scripture, and their contents were, on Cassiodorus’s reading, sown in the sacred books first and only later borrowed by the pagan schools. In the same preface he explains his purpose — “I was moved by divine love to devise for you, with God’s help, these introductory books to take the place of a teacher” — so that both “the textual disposition of Holy Scripture and a compact sketch of secular letters may be unfolded.”2

The seven arts enumerated — the three of language and the four of number

In this book we must first speak about the art of grammar, which is clearly the origin and basis of the liberal letters. … Second, we will discuss the art of rhetoric, which we consider entirely indispensable and honorable particularly in civil cases because of its brilliance and eloquence. Third, logic, which is called dialectic. This discipline, to the extent that the secular teachers speak of it, separates truth from falsity by subtle and concise discussion. Fourth, mathematics, which includes four disciplines: arithmetic, geometry, music, and astronomy.

— Institutiones II, Preface §4, trans. Halporn.3 Book II is “arranged according to the seven divisions of secular letters”: the three arts of language — grammar, rhetoric, dialectic — and the four of quantity gathered under “mathematics.” Cassiodorus does not yet use the words trivium and quadrivium (the labels are, respectively, medieval and Boethius’s), but the architecture is exactly theirs, and it is this seven-part scheme he hands to the monastic schoolroom.

The dignity of the copyist — “to preach to men with the hand”

A happy purpose, a praiseworthy zeal, to preach to men with the hand, to set free tongues with one’s fingers and in silence to give mankind salvation and to fight with pen and ink against the unlawful snares of the devil.

— Institutiones I.30, trans. Halporn.4 The chapter on the monastery’s copyists (antiquarii) turns manual labour into ministry: by reading Scripture the scribe instructs his own mind, and by copying it he spreads the Lord’s precepts far and wide — so that “Satan receives as many wounds as the scribe writes words of the Lord.” This is the charter under which the monasteries of the Latin West became the copying-houses that carried both the Bible and the classics across the centuries; the work of transmission is itself the vocation.

Notes

  • Vivarium and the preservation of learning. Cassiodorus founded the monastery of Vivarium precisely to make the study and copying of texts — sacred first, secular in their service — the monk’s daily work. His Institutiones is the reading-list and rationale for that programme, and it did as much as any single book to ensure that classical learning survived the collapse of the Roman schools by migrating into the cloister.
  • Between Capella and Isidore. Cassiodorus stands in the middle of the transmission cluster: he inherits the fixed seven of Martianus Capella and the mathematical rationale of Boethius, orders them (with Augustine) to Scripture, and hands the whole to Isidore of Seville, whose Etymologiae would make the same seven the reference work of the Middle Ages.
