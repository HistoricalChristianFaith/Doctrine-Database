Hugh of St. Victor — the dignity of the mechanical arts (the Didascalicon)

Dates: c. 1127 (the Didascalicon de studio legendi, the first full medieval philosophy of education; Hugh c. 1096–1141, a canon regular and master of the abbey school of Saint-Victor at Paris) · Wikipedia
Summary: Where the ancients had split study into the liberal arts of a free man and the servile or banausic crafts beneath his dignity, Hugh did something no earlier writer had done: he drew a single map of all knowledge in which the trades take their place as a genuine part of philosophy. His Didascalicon names seven mechanical arts — fabric-making, armament, commerce, agriculture, hunting, medicine, and theatrics — and sets them deliberately alongside the seven liberal arts, all of them ordered to one end: the restoration of the fallen image of God in man. It is the strongest pre-modern Christian warrant that manual and vocational skill is not merely servile drudgery but part of the recovery of human nature — a direct answer to Aristotle’s contempt for the paid trades.

Claims & evidence

The seven mechanical (trade) arts, set beside the seven liberal arts

Mechanical science contains seven sciences: fabric making, armament, commerce, agriculture, hunting, medicine, and theatrics. Of these, three pertain to external cover for nature, by which she protects herself from harms, and four to internal, by which she feeds and nourishes herself. In this division we find a likeness to the trivium and quadrivium, for the trivium is concerned with words, which are external things, and the quadrivium with concepts, which are internally conceived. The mechanical sciences are the seven handmaids which Mercury received in dowry from Philology, for every human activity is servant to eloquence wed to wisdom.

— Didascalicon II.20, trans. Jerome Taylor.1 The move is quietly revolutionary. Hugh takes the crafts that Aristotle and Cicero had ranked as vulgar and gives them a scheme of their own, mirroring the trivium and quadrivium art for art, and folds the whole set into a single division of philosophy. His nod to “the seven handmaids which Mercury received in dowry from Philology” deliberately echoes Martianus Capella’s allegory of the liberal arts as bridesmaids — Hugh answers it with a matching set of mechanical handmaids.

“Mechanical, that is, adulterate” — and why the liberal arts are “liberal”

These sciences are called mechanical, that is, adulterate, because their concern is with the artificer’s product, which borrows its form from nature. Similarly, the other seven are called liberal either because they require minds which are liberal, that is, liberated and practiced (for these sciences pursue subtle inquiries into the causes of things), or because in antiquity only free and noble men were accustomed to study them, while the populace and the sons of men not free sought operative skill in things mechanical.

— Didascalicon II.20, trans. Taylor.2 Hugh reports the old social snobbery plainly — the liberal arts were the study of “free and noble men,” the mechanical the “operative skill” of “the populace and the sons of men not free” — but the very act of cataloguing the trades as a science, on the same map as grammar and geometry, undercuts the contempt. (The etymology he gives is a medieval one: he derives mechanical from a sense of “adulterate,” because the craftsman’s work merely copies nature rather than creating it. The word in fact comes from the Greek mēchanē, “contrivance,” not from any root meaning “adulterer” — but the moralized etymology is exactly how Hugh frames the trades’ humbler standing before dignifying them.)

Human reason “shines forth much more brilliantly” in the arts it must invent

… man alone is brought forth naked and unarmed. For it is fitting that nature should provide a plan for those beings which do not know how to care for themselves, but that from nature’s example, a better chance for trying things should be provided to man when he comes to devise for himself by his own reasoning those things naturally given to all other animals. Indeed, man’s reason shines forth much more brilliantly in inventing these very things than ever it would have had man naturally possessed them. Nor is it without cause that the proverb says: “Ingenious want hath mothered all the arts.”

— Didascalicon I.9, trans. Taylor.3 This is the positive case for the mechanical arts. Where the beasts are born clothed and armed — bark on the tree, feathers on the bird, a shell on the tortoise — man is born naked, and must clothe, arm, and feed himself by craft. Far from degrading him, that necessity is the occasion of his glory: reason “shines forth much more brilliantly” in weaving, building, and founding than it ever would have had nature simply provided for him. The trades are not a fall below the free man’s dignity but a theatre of it.

All the arts, mechanical included, restore the fallen image of God

Of all human acts or pursuits, then, governed as these are by Wisdom, the end and the intention ought to regard either the restoring of our nature’s integrity, or the relieving of those weaknesses to which our present life lies subject. … The integrity of human nature, however, is attained in two things — in knowledge and in virtue, and in these lies our sole likeness to the supernal and divine substances.

— Didascalicon I.5, trans. Taylor.4 Hugh gives the whole enterprise of learning — and the work that flows from it — a redemptive purpose. Every human pursuit aims at one of two things: restoring our nature’s wounded integrity, or relieving the weaknesses of this mortal life. The theoretical and practical arts do the first; the mechanical arts do the second, meeting the bodily needs of a fallen and vulnerable creature. So even a trade earns its dignity within the single project of mending the human being. He states the goal of the arts most sharply as he turns to survey them:

This, then, is what the arts are concerned with, this is what they intend, namely, to restore within us the divine likeness, a likeness which to us is a form but to God is his nature. The more we are conformed to the divine nature, the more do we possess Wisdom, for then there begins to shine forth again in us what has forever existed in the divine Idea or Pattern.

— Didascalicon II.1, trans. Taylor.5 This is the frame that lets Hugh dignify the trades without flattening the hierarchy: mechanical, liberal, practical, and theoretical arts differ in nobility, but all of them are ordered to the one end of restoring the divine likeness in man.

“Learn everything”

Some things are to be known for their own sakes, but others, although for their own sakes they do not seem worthy of our labor, nevertheless, because without them the former class of things cannot be known with complete clarity, must by no means be carelessly skipped. Learn everything; you will see afterwards that nothing is superfluous. A skimpy knowledge is not a pleasing thing.

— Didascalicon VI.3, trans. Taylor.6 Hugh’s famous maxim — omnia disce, videbis postea nihil esse superfluum — is the practical consequence of his map of knowledge. If every art, down to the humblest trade, has its place in the recovery of human nature, then nothing a student can learn is wasted; the branches that look useless in isolation prove “as necessary as they are fitting” once seen in the whole. It is a charter for breadth against the utilitarian instinct to study only what pays.

Notes

  • The first Christian dignity of the trades. Hugh is generally credited as the first writer to name the artes mechanicae as a formal part of philosophy and to fix them at a scheme of seven paralleling the liberal arts. His inclusion of the trades gave manual skill a standing it had never had in the classical hierarchy, and the ramifications run forward through the medieval schools’ growing esteem for craft and technology.
  • The counterweight to the ancient contempt for the banausic. On this timeline Hugh answers Aristotle (paid trades “absorb and degrade the mind”), Cicero (“no workshop can have anything liberal about it”), and Seneca (the liberal arts alone worthy of a free man) point for point — not by denying the old free/servile distinction, which he reports, but by re-ordering both liberal and mechanical arts to a single Christian end that lifts the trades into the same redemptive story.
  • The “adulterate” etymology is Hugh’s, not philology’s. His derivation of mechanical from “adulterate” (the artificer’s work being a mere copy of nature) is a moralized medieval etymology; the word actually descends from Greek mēchanē, “contrivance, machine.” The point is preserved here because it is how Hugh himself frames the trades’ lower rank before he raises it.
  • An answer to Martianus, and a preface to the university. Hugh’s “seven handmaids… from Philology” consciously mirror Martianus Capella’s allegory of the seven liberal bridesmaids; and the Didascalicon, written at the abbey school of Saint-Victor at the dawn of the twelfth-century renaissance, stands at the threshold of the cathedral schools and universities that would build higher study on the seven arts.
