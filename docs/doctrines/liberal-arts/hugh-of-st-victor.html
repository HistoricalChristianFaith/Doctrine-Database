Hugh of St. Victor — the dignity of the mechanical arts (the Didascalicon)

Dates: c. 1127 (the Didascalicon de studio legendi, a guide to the whole of study; Hugh c. 1096–1141, a canon regular and master of the abbey school of Saint-Victor at Paris) · Wikipedia
Summary: Earlier writers on this timeline divide study into the liberal arts of a free man and the servile or banausic crafts (Aristotle, Cicero). Hugh’s Didascalicon draws a single map of knowledge in which the trades take their place as a part of philosophy. It names seven mechanical arts — fabric-making, armament, commerce, agriculture, hunting, medicine, and theatrics — and sets them alongside the seven liberal arts, all of them ordered to one end: the restoration of the divine likeness in fallen man.

Claims & evidence

The seven mechanical (trade) arts, set beside the seven liberal arts

Mechanical science contains seven sciences: fabric making, armament, commerce, agriculture, hunting, medicine, and theatrics. Of these, three pertain to external cover for nature, by which she protects herself from harms, and four to internal, by which she feeds and nourishes herself. In this division we find a likeness to the trivium and quadrivium, for the trivium is concerned with words, which are external things, and the quadrivium with concepts, which are internally conceived. The mechanical sciences are the seven handmaids which Mercury received in dowry from Philology, for every human activity is servant to eloquence wed to wisdom.

— Didascalicon II.20, trans. Jerome Taylor.1 Hugh gives the crafts that Aristotle and Cicero ranked as vulgar a scheme of their own, which he likens to the trivium and quadrivium, within a single division of philosophy. His “seven handmaids which Mercury received in dowry from Philology” echoes Martianus Capella’s allegory of the liberal arts as bridesmaids, here applied to a matching set of mechanical handmaids.

“Mechanical, that is, adulterate” — and why the liberal arts are “liberal”

These sciences are called mechanical, that is, adulterate, because their concern is with the artificer’s product, which borrows its form from nature. Similarly, the other seven are called liberal either because they require minds which are liberal, that is, liberated and practiced (for these sciences pursue subtle inquiries into the causes of things), or because in antiquity only free and noble men were accustomed to study them, while the populace and the sons of men not free sought operative skill in things mechanical.

— Didascalicon II.20, trans. Taylor.2 Hugh reports the older social division — the liberal arts were the study of “free and noble men,” the mechanical the “operative skill” of “the populace and the sons of men not free” — and catalogues the trades as a science on the same map as grammar and geometry. He derives mechanical from “adulterate,” because the craftsman’s product borrows its form from nature (on the etymology, see Notes).

Human reason “shines forth much more brilliantly” in the arts it must invent

… man alone is brought forth naked and unarmed. For it is fitting that nature should provide a plan for those beings which do not know how to care for themselves, but that from nature’s example, a better chance for trying things should be provided to man when he comes to devise for himself by his own reasoning those things naturally given to all other animals. Indeed, man’s reason shines forth much more brilliantly in inventing these very things than ever it would have had man naturally possessed them. Nor is it without cause that the proverb says: “Ingenious want hath mothered all the arts.”

— Didascalicon I.9, trans. Taylor.3 Here Hugh makes his case for the mechanical arts. Where the beasts are born clothed and armed — bark on the tree, feathers on the bird, a shell on the tortoise — man is born naked, and must clothe, arm, and feed himself by craft. In Hugh’s account that necessity is the occasion for reason to “shine forth much more brilliantly” in weaving, building, and founding than it would have had nature simply provided for him.

All the arts, mechanical included, restore the fallen image of God

Of all human acts or pursuits, then, governed as these are by Wisdom, the end and the intention ought to regard either the restoring of our nature’s integrity, or the relieving of those weaknesses to which our present life lies subject. … The integrity of human nature, however, is attained in two things — in knowledge and in virtue, and in these lies our sole likeness to the supernal and divine substances.

— Didascalicon I.5, trans. Taylor.4 Hugh gives the whole enterprise of learning — and the work that flows from it — a redemptive purpose. Every human pursuit aims at one of two things: restoring our nature’s wounded integrity, or relieving the weaknesses of this mortal life. The theoretical and practical arts do the first; the mechanical arts do the second, meeting the bodily needs of a fallen and vulnerable creature. In this scheme the trades too have a place in the single project of restoring the human being. He states the goal of the arts as he turns to survey them:

This, then, is what the arts are concerned with, this is what they intend, namely, to restore within us the divine likeness, a likeness which to us is a form but to God is his nature. The more we are conformed to the divine nature, the more do we possess Wisdom, for then there begins to shine forth again in us what has forever existed in the divine Idea or Pattern.

— Didascalicon II.1, trans. Taylor.5 In Hugh’s scheme mechanical, liberal, practical, and theoretical arts differ in rank, but all of them are ordered to the one end of restoring the divine likeness in man.

“Learn everything”

Some things are to be known for their own sakes, but others, although for their own sakes they do not seem worthy of our labor, nevertheless, because without them the former class of things cannot be known with complete clarity, must by no means be carelessly skipped. Learn everything; you will see afterwards that nothing is superfluous. A skimpy knowledge is not a pleasing thing.

— Didascalicon VI.3, trans. Taylor.6 Hugh’s maxim — omnia disce, videbis postea nihil esse superfluum — follows from his map of knowledge: things that do not seem worth the labour for their own sakes are not to be skipped, because without them the others cannot be known with complete clarity.

Notes

  • The artes mechanicae. Hugh is credited as the earliest writer to name the artes mechanicae as a formal part of philosophy and to fix them at a scheme of seven paralleling the liberal arts (⚠ unverified — the attribution is not yet sourced to a named scholar).
  • Placement on the timeline. Earlier on this timeline Aristotle (paid trades “absorb and degrade the mind”), Cicero (“no workshop can have anything liberal about it”), and Seneca (the liberal arts alone worthy of a free man) rank the trades below the liberal arts. Hugh reports the old free/servile distinction and orders both liberal and mechanical arts to a single Christian end.
  • The “adulterate” etymology. Hugh’s derivation of mechanical from “adulterate” (the artificer’s product borrowing its form from nature) is a moralized medieval etymology; the word descends from Greek mēchanē, “contrivance, machine.”
  • Martianus Capella and the schools. Hugh’s “seven handmaids… from Philology” echo Martianus Capella’s allegory of the seven liberal bridesmaids. The Didascalicon was written at the abbey school of Saint-Victor in Paris; Thomas Aquinas quotes Hugh on the trivium and quadrivium later on this timeline.
