Seneca on the liberal studies

Dates: c. AD 64 · Wikipedia
Summary: Seneca (c. 4 BC–AD 65), the Stoic philosopher and tutor of Nero, discusses the liberal arts in his 88th Moral Letter, “On liberal and vocational studies.” He grants that the studies are called liberal because they befit a free man, and holds that they are preparatory: they set the soul moving toward virtue but do not themselves make it good. The one truly “liberal” study, he says, is the study of wisdom, which alone gives a man his liberty.

Claims & evidence

The liberal studies are so called because they are worthy of a free man

Hence you see why “liberal studies” are so called; it is because they are studies worthy of a free-born gentleman.

— Moral Letters to Lucilius, Letter 88, trans. Richard M. Gummere, source. Seneca states the etymology of the term: studia liberalia are the studies of the liber, the free man — not the trades of the hireling or the slave. In the same passage he goes on to limit the claim.

Only the study of wisdom is truly liberal — it alone gives a man his liberty

But there is only one really liberal study,—that which gives a man his liberty. It is the study of wisdom, and that is lofty, brave, and great-souled.

— Moral Letters, Letter 88, trans. Gummere, source. In Seneca’s Stoic account freedom is inward — freedom from the passions and from fortune — and only philosophy confers it. So the honorific “liberal” belongs in the fullest sense to wisdom alone; grammar, geometry, and music are “liberal” only in a lower, derived sense.

The arts prepare the soul for virtue but do not confer it

the liberal arts do not conduct the soul all the way to virtue, but merely set it going in that direction.

— Moral Letters, Letter 88, trans. Gummere, source. Seneca’s qualification follows. In his account the liberal studies “prepare the soul for the reception of virtue” but are not virtue; a man may be learned and still be a fool or a knave. Learning, for Seneca, is a road toward the good, not the good itself.

Reception and context

  • Seneca is a pagan Stoic, not a Christian witness. Christian writers read him, and a correspondence presented as between Seneca and the apostle Paul circulated in antiquity (⚠ unverified — not yet sourced).
  • Later entries on this timeline set the arts beneath a higher study in their own terms: the “handmaid” account of Clement of Alexandria, and the warnings of Tertullian and Jerome against pagan philosophy and letters.
  • Read with Aristotle, who treats the liberal studies as worth pursuing for their own sake: Seneca agrees they are free and worthy, but holds that they are not ends — they serve the one end, wisdom.
