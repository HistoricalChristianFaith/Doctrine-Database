John Dewey — the progressive critique of the liberal/servile divide

Dates: c. 1916 (Democracy and Education: An Introduction to the Philosophy of Education, New York: Macmillan, 1916; building on The School and Society, 1899. Dewey 1859–1952, American philosopher, psychologist, and the foremost theorist of progressive education) · Wikipedia
Summary: This timeline’s great modern antagonist. Dewey mounted the most serious twentieth-century attack on the very distinctions the liberal-arts tradition defends — liberal versus servile, culture versus utility, theory versus practice, knowing versus doing — arguing that they are not intrinsic truths about the mind but the fossil of an aristocratic, slave-owning social order that split humanity into a class that labored and a class at leisure. His remedy is not to invert the hierarchy (job training over contemplation) but to abolish the dualism: education is not preparation-by-contemplation for a life apart from work but “a continuous reconstruction of experience” that makes thought the guide of free practice for everyone. He is the counter-pole to Newman and Pieper as Tertullian and Seneca are to the ancients — voiced here in his own words, not as a foil to knock down.

Claims & evidence

The culture/utility divide is not intrinsic but a social inheritance from aristocratic Greece

Of the segregations of educational values discussed in the last chapter, that between culture and utility is probably the most fundamental. While the distinction is often thought to be intrinsic and absolute, it is really historical and social. It originated, so far as conscious formulation is concerned, in Greece, and was based upon the fact that the truly human life was lived only by a few who subsisted upon the results of the labor of others.

— Democracy and Education (1916), ch. 19 (“Labor and Leisure”), § 1.1 This is Dewey’s central historical thesis and his direct challenge to the whole tradition: what Aristotle and Cicero presented as an eternal ranking of the free arts above the servile is, for Dewey, a datable product of a particular social arrangement. He makes the class basis explicit:

The separation of liberal education from professional and industrial education goes back to the time of the Greeks, and was formulated expressly on the basis of a division of classes into those who had to labor for a living and those who were relieved from this necessity.

— ch. 19, § 1.2 The liberal/servile line, on this reading, does not track a real hierarchy of mind over body or wisdom over craft; it tracks the boundary between the citizens who were exempt from work and the slaves, artisans, and women who did it.

The classical ideal of leisure rested on slavery

Hence slaves, artisans, and women are employed in furnishing the means of subsistence in order that others, those adequately equipped with intelligence, may live the life of leisurely concern with things intrinsically worth while.

— ch. 19, § 1.3 Dewey reads Aristotle carefully and fairly — he grants that the ancient distinction was a coherent philosophy for the society that held it — but presses that its coherence depended on that society’s injustice: the leisure that Aristotle’s free man devoted to contemplation was bought by the unfreedom of those who supplied his bread. Detach the ideal from the slave economy that underwrote it, Dewey argues, and the contempt it packed for “servile” work loses its ground. (Whether the ideal must stand or fall with that origin — a genetic account of a claim is not by itself a refutation of it — is weighed on the argument page, which grants the historical diagnosis but rates the destructive conclusion contested.)

The theory/practice split springs from the same social soil — and knowledge is born of doing

As livelihood and leisure are opposed, so are theory and practice, intelligence and execution, knowledge and activity.

— ch. 20 (“Intellectual and Practical Studies”).4 The chain of dualisms is, for Dewey, one dualism wearing many faces, all descended from the first division of society into a working class and a leisure class. Against the whole chain he sets his pragmatist counter-claim that knowing is itself a mode of doing:

If this progress has demonstrated anything, it is that there is no such thing as genuine knowledge and fruitful understanding except as the offspring of doing.

— ch. 20.5 Where the tradition from Plato to Pieper ranks contemplation above action — the highest learning being the least “useful” — Dewey holds that understanding grows out of active experiment and engagement with the world, so that the “pure” knowledge prized as liberal is not the root but a late fruit of practical inquiry.

Education is not preparation-by-contemplation but the reconstruction of experience

It is that reconstruction or reorganization of experience which adds to the meaning of experience, and which increases ability to direct the course of subsequent experience.

— Democracy and Education, ch. 6 (“Education as Conservative and Progressive”) — Dewey’s technical definition of education.6 This is the positive core beneath the critique, and it displaces the classical picture at its root. For the tradition, learning is ordered to contemplation of the highest — a good enjoyed at leisure, apart from and above the world of work (Plato’s turning of the soul toward being, Newman’s knowledge “independent of sequel”). For Dewey, education has no end outside the ongoing enrichment and redirection of experience itself; it is a continuous process, not a preparation for a separate contemplative life to be entered once schooling is done.

The remedy is to abolish the dualism, not to invert it

It would misread Dewey to file him as a simple champion of job training over the liberal arts — the “utilitarian age” the modern tradition decries. His quarrel is with the split as such, in either direction: a bookish culture walled off from useful work is as deformed, for him, as a narrow vocationalism walled off from thought. What a democratic society owes every child is an education that unites the two the old order held apart:

The problem of education in a democratic society is to do away with the dualism and to construct a course of studies which makes thought a guide of free practice for all and which makes leisure a reward of accepting responsibility for service, rather than a state of exemption from it.

— ch. 19, § 3.7 Notice how closely this tracks, and how sharply it departs from, Hugh of St. Victor’s dignifying of the mechanical arts: both refuse to despise honest labor, but where Hugh dignifies the trades without flattening the hierarchy — ordering every art up to the one end of restoring the divine likeness — Dewey wants the hierarchy itself dissolved, thought and practice interwoven “for all” rather than ranked. That is the precise point on which the tradition and its most serious modern critic part.

Notes

  • Dewey and the argument over the purpose of the liberal arts. He is the anchor witness for the modern challenge to the tradition — the counter-pole to the liberal vs. servile distinction (assessed contested) and to knowledge as its own end (assessed contested), and the proponent of the genetic critique of the whole distinction (also contested). His genetic thesis — that the free/servile ranking is the residue of a slave society — overlaps the tradition’s own strongest self-criticism (that the contempt the ancients packed for the “banausic” wage-earner runs against Scripture’s dignifying of craft), which is exactly why the dignity of the mechanical arts comes out sound. On that much, the wiki and Dewey converge.
  • Where the tradition answers him. Two replies are worth keeping distinct. First, a genetic account of a belief is not a refutation of it: that the free/servile ideal arose in a slave economy does not show that its core claim — that knowing has a good beyond earning — is false; the ideal may have been carried by the wrong social vehicle yet still be true. Second, the best proponents already grant Dewey’s practical point without surrendering the hierarchy of ends: Newman’s “the good is always useful” and Hugh’s dignifying of the mechanical arts both refuse the very liberal-versus-gainful either/or Dewey attacks — so a hierarchy of ends (knowing is not merely for earning) can survive the collapse of the hierarchy of persons (scholar above tradesman) that Dewey rightly rejects.
  • The reservation the tradition presses back. If education has no end outside the ongoing reconstruction of experience, the tradition asks what fixed good that reconstruction is toward — Pieper’s worry that dissolving leisure into “service” and knowing into doing leaves the “world of total work” with no contemplative space it must respect, and the Christian conviction (Basil, Augustine) that learning is finally ordered to the knowledge and love of God, an end Dewey’s naturalism does not supply. His is a rival philosophy of the end of learning, not merely of its method.
