John Dewey — the progressive critique of the liberal/servile divide

Dates: c. 1916 (Democracy and Education: An Introduction to the Philosophy of Education, New York: Macmillan, 1916; building on The School and Society, 1899. Dewey 1859–1952, American philosopher, psychologist, and theorist of progressive education) · Wikipedia
Summary: Dewey criticizes the distinctions the liberal-arts tradition draws — liberal versus servile, culture versus utility, theory versus practice, knowing versus doing — arguing that they are not intrinsic truths about the mind but the product of an aristocratic, slave-owning social order that split humanity into a class that labored and a class at leisure. His proposal is not to invert the hierarchy (job training over contemplation) but to do away with the dualism: education is not preparation for a life apart from work but a continuous “reconstruction or reorganization of experience” that makes thought the guide of free practice for everyone. On this timeline his position stands opposite that of Newman and Pieper.

Claims & evidence

Dewey: the culture/utility divide is not intrinsic but a social inheritance from aristocratic Greece

Of the segregations of educational values discussed in the last chapter, that between culture and utility is probably the most fundamental. While the distinction is often thought to be intrinsic and absolute, it is really historical and social. It originated, so far as conscious formulation is concerned, in Greece, and was based upon the fact that the truly human life was lived only by a few who subsisted upon the results of the labor of others.

— Democracy and Education (1916), ch. 19 (“Labor and Leisure”), § 1.1 This is Dewey’s central historical thesis: what Aristotle and Cicero presented as an eternal ranking of the free arts above the servile is, for Dewey, a datable product of a particular social arrangement. He makes the class basis explicit:

The separation of liberal education from professional and industrial education goes back to the time of the Greeks, and was formulated expressly on the basis of a division of classes into those who had to labor for a living and those who were relieved from this necessity.

— ch. 19, § 1.2 On Dewey’s reading the liberal/servile line does not track a real hierarchy of mind over body or wisdom over craft; it tracks the boundary between the citizens who were exempt from work and the slaves, artisans, and women who did it.

Dewey: the classical ideal of leisure rested on slavery

Hence slaves, artisans, and women are employed in furnishing the means of subsistence in order that others, those adequately equipped with intelligence, may live the life of leisurely concern with things intrinsically worth while.

— ch. 19, § 1.3 Dewey grants that the ancient distinction was a coherent philosophy for the society that held it, but argues that its coherence depended on that society’s social order: the leisure that Aristotle’s free man devoted to contemplation was bought by the unfreedom of those who supplied his bread. Detach the ideal from the slave economy that underwrote it, Dewey argues, and the low estimate of “servile” work loses its ground.

The theory/practice split springs from the same social soil — and knowledge is born of doing

As livelihood and leisure are opposed, so are theory and practice, intelligence and execution, knowledge and activity.

— ch. 20 (“Intellectual and Practical Studies”).4 The chain of dualisms is, for Dewey, one dualism in many forms, all descended from the first division of society into a working class and a leisure class. Against the whole chain he sets his pragmatist counter-claim that knowing is itself a mode of doing:

If this progress has demonstrated anything, it is that there is no such thing as genuine knowledge and fruitful understanding except as the offspring of doing.

— ch. 20.5 Where writers on this timeline from Plato to Pieper rank contemplation above action, Dewey holds that understanding grows out of active experiment and engagement with the world, so that the “pure” knowledge prized as liberal is not the root but a late fruit of practical inquiry.

Education is not preparation-by-contemplation but the reconstruction of experience

It is that reconstruction or reorganization of experience which adds to the meaning of experience, and which increases ability to direct the course of subsequent experience.

— Democracy and Education, ch. 6 (“Education as Conservative and Progressive”) — Dewey’s technical definition of education.6 This is the positive core beneath his critique. For Plato and Newman on this timeline, learning is ordered to contemplation of the highest — a good enjoyed at leisure, apart from and above the world of work (Plato’s turning of the soul toward being, Newman’s knowledge “independent of sequel”). For Dewey, education has no end outside the ongoing enrichment and redirection of experience itself; it is a continuous process, not a preparation for a separate contemplative life to be entered once schooling is done.

Dewey: do away with the dualism, not invert it

Dewey does not argue for job training over the liberal arts. His quarrel is with the split as such, in either direction: a bookish culture walled off from useful work is as deformed, for him, as a narrow vocationalism walled off from thought. In his account a democratic society owes every child an education that unites the two:

The problem of education in a democratic society is to do away with the dualism and to construct a course of studies which makes thought a guide of free practice for all and which makes leisure a reward of accepting responsibility for service, rather than a state of exemption from it.

— ch. 19, § 3.7 Hugh of St. Victor, earlier on this timeline, also gives the mechanical arts a place among the arts, ordering every art to the one end of restoring the divine likeness while keeping a ranking among them; Dewey proposes that thought and practice be interwoven “for all” rather than ranked.

Notes

  • Placement on the timeline. Dewey is the timeline’s principal modern critic of the liberal/servile distinction and of knowledge as its own end, and the author of the historical (genetic) critique of the distinction quoted above: that the free/servile ranking derives from a slave society.
  • Other positions on this timeline. Newman (“the good is always useful”) and Hugh of St. Victor (who counts the mechanical arts among the arts) each join knowledge pursued for its own sake to practical use while keeping a hierarchy of ends. Pieper (1948) defends leisure and contemplation against “the world of total work”; Basil and Augustine order learning to the knowledge and love of God. Dewey’s is a philosophy of the end of learning as well as of its method.
