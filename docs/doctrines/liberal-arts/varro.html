Varro and the canon of the liberal arts

Dates: c. 33 BC · Wikipedia
Summary: Marcus Terentius Varro (116–27 BC), Rome’s greatest antiquarian scholar, gave the Latin West its list of the liberal arts. His lost Disciplinarum Libri Novem (“Nine Books of Disciplines”) treated nine subjects — the seven that would become the medieval canon plus medicine and architecture — and became the model from which, four and a half centuries later, Martianus Capella fixed the enduring seven: the trivium of grammar, dialectic, and rhetoric and the quadrivium of arithmetic, geometry, astronomy, and music.

Claims & evidence

Varro’s Disciplinae fixed the Latin canon of the arts — nine, later reduced to seven

The “Libri IX Disciplinarum” of the learned M. Terentius Varro of Reate, an earlier contemporary of Cicero, treats of the seven liberal arts adding to them medicine and architectonics.

— The Catholic Encyclopedia, “The Seven Liberal Arts,” source. Varro’s own text does not survive; it is known through later testimonia and reconstruction (the nine-book division argued from Vitruvius and defended by modern scholarship). Its importance is that it first gathered the Greek circle of studies into a fixed Latin curriculum of named disciplinae. When Martianus Capella wrote his allegory De Nuptiis Philologiae et Mercurii (c. AD 420) on Varro’s model, he dropped medicine and architecture — the two arts concerned with the body and with earthly building — leaving the seven that the schools of the Middle Ages would organize into the trivium and quadrivium and set at the foundation of the university.

The “circle of learning” — enkyklios paideia — behind the word “encyclopedia”

Varro’s enterprise gave Latin form to the Greek ideal of enkyklios paideia, the “circle” or all-round course of general education — the notion that the free studies form a single connected round that the educated person should traverse whole. His younger contemporary Quintilian names that circle explicitly (“that… general education” the Greeks call enkyklios paideia), and it is from this phrase, by way of the Renaissance, that the modern word encyclopedia descends — a book covering the whole round of the arts. Varro stands at the head of that tradition of comprehensive Latin learning.

— See The Catholic Encyclopedia, “The Seven Liberal Arts,” source, on Varro’s place in the transmission of the arts; and Quintilian, Institutio Oratoria I.10.1 for the enkyklios paideia.

Notes

  • Varro is a pagan Roman witness, not a Christian one; he matters here because he supplied the form of the curriculum — the list of the arts as a bounded canon — that the fathers and the medieval schools took over. Cassiodorus and Isidore of Seville, who hand the seven arts to the Latin Middle Ages, work within the scheme Varro first drew.
  • Because the Disciplinae is lost, everything said of its contents rests on later report and scholarly reconstruction; the security of the nine-book structure is a matter of ongoing debate, but Varro’s role as the ancestor of the Latin liberal-arts canon is not in doubt.
  • Compare Cicero, Varro’s exact contemporary, who supplied the vocabulary (artes liberales, humanitas) while Varro supplied the syllabus.
