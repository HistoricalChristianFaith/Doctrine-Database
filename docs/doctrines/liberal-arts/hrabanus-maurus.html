Rabanus Maurus — the seven arts for the clergy (the De institutione clericorum)

Dates: c. 819 (the De institutione clericorum, written at Fulda; Rabanus, also spelled Hrabanus, Maurus c. 780–856, monk and then abbot of Fulda, from 847 archbishop of Mainz) · Wikipedia
Summary: Alcuin’s pupil and the “teacher of Germany” (Praeceptor Germaniae), Rabanus turned the Carolingian revival of the arts into a handbook for the clergy. Book III of his De institutione clericorum (819) enumerates the seven liberal arts as the training a cleric who reads Scripture must have — and grounds their Christian use, almost word for word, on Augustine’s “spoiling the Egyptians.” He is the channel through which the patristic warrant for Christian humanism, and the very phrase septem artes liberales, pass into the schools of the Frankish church.

Claims & evidence

The seven liberal arts enumerated; grammar the foundation

Prima ergo liberalium artium est grammatica, secunda rhetorica, tertia dialectica, quarta arithmetica, quinta geometria, sexta musica, septima astronomia.

…origo et fundamentum est litterarum liberalium. Hanc itaque scholam dominicam legere convenit, quia scientia recte loquendi et scribendi ratio in ipsa consistit.

— De institutione clericorum III.18 (“De arte grammatica”), Latin ed. A. Knöpfler, Rabani Mauri De institutione clericorum libri tres (Munich, 1900), pp. 223–224.1 Literal English: “The first, then, of the liberal arts is grammar; the second, rhetoric; the third, dialectic; the fourth, arithmetic; the fifth, geometry; the sixth, music; the seventh, astronomy”; and grammar “is the origin and foundation of liberal letters. It is therefore fitting that this be read in the school of the Lord, since the knowledge of speaking correctly and the method of writing consist in it.” Following Isidore and Cassiodorus, Rabanus fixes the seven as a set and makes grammar the first rung. Because he gives them their collective name after describing each in turn, he is often credited with the first datable use of the phrase septem artes liberales.

Augustine named as the source: “whence we have excerpted these things”

Ceterum qui plenius de his scire desiderat, in libris sancti Augustini de doctrina christiana, unde haec excerpsimus, quaerat et inveniet.

— De institutione clericorum III.15, ed. Knöpfler (1900), pp. 216–217.2 Literal English: “For the rest, whoever desires to know these things more fully, let him seek in the books of St. Augustine, On Christian Doctrine, whence we have excerpted these things, and he will find them.” Rabanus is unusually candid about his method: Book III is largely an epitome of Augustine’s De doctrina christiana, reorganized as a clergyman’s manual. That honesty is itself part of the point — the authority for a Christian’s study of the pagan arts is the greatest of the Latin fathers.

The gold of the Egyptians carried into the Carolingian schools

…“philosophi ipsi qui vocantur, si qua forte vera et fidei nostrae accommodata” in disputationibus suis seu scriptis “dixerunt, maxime Platonici, non solum formidanda non sunt, sed ab eis etiam tamquam iniustis possessoribus in usum nostrum vindicanda. Sicut enim Aegyptii non tantum idola habebant… sed etiam vasa atque ornamenta de auro et argento et vestem, quae ille populus exiens de Aegypto sibi tamquam ad usum meliorem clanculo vindicavit…”

— De institutione clericorum III.26 (“De philosophorum libris”), ed. Knöpfler (1900), pp. 240–242.3 The chapter reproduces Augustine, De doctrina christiana II.40 (§§60–61) almost verbatim — Rabanus inserting only the words in disputationibus suis seu scriptis, “in their disputations or writings.” In Augustine’s own words, which Rabanus carries over:

Moreover, if those who are called philosophers, and especially the Platonists, have said aught that is true and in harmony with our faith, we are not only not to shrink from it, but to claim it for our own use from those who have unlawful possession of it. For, as the Egyptians had not only the idols and heavy burdens which the people of Israel hated and fled from, but also vessels and ornaments of gold and silver, and garments, which the same people when going out of Egypt appropriated to themselves, designing them for a better use… in the same way all branches of heathen learning… contain also liberal instruction which is better adapted to the use of the truth, and some most excellent precepts of morality… These, therefore, the Christian… ought to take away from them, and to devote to their proper use in preaching the gospel.

— Augustine, On Christian Doctrine II.40 (§§60–61), trans. J. F. Shaw, NPNF 1st ser. 2, the passage Rabanus reproduces (text here). By copying Augustine’s warrant into a manual meant to be read across the Frankish monasteries and cathedral schools, Rabanus makes the “spoiling the Egyptians” the standing charter under which a ninth-century cleric could study grammar, rhetoric, dialectic, and the rest without fear.

Notes

  • English translation. The one complete English of the work — On the Formation of Clergy (in the Fathers of the Church: Medieval Continuations series) — is not freely online, so the Latin is quoted verbatim here (from Knöpfler’s 1900 edition; also PL 107, cols. 293–420), with literal renderings supplied and the “spoils” passage given in Augustine’s own published English, which Rabanus reproduces.
  • Chapter numbers differ by edition. Knöpfler numbers the arts-enumeration chapter III.18 and the “spoils” chapter III.26; older citations keyed to Migne/PL number the latter III.27. Cite whichever edition is being linked.
  • On this wiki. Rabanus appears also as a witness on the rabbinic corruption of Scripture timeline (where his encyclopedia De rerum naturis copies Isidore almost word for word) — a measure of how much of what the Carolingian schools “knew” came filtered through the earlier compilers. Here he does the same service for the liberal arts, carrying Augustine and Isidore into the ninth century. His own teacher was Alcuin of York.
