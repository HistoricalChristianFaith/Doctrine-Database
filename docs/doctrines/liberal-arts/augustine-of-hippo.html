Augustine on spoiling the Egyptians — the liberal arts in the service of Scripture

Dates: c. 397 (the argument of On Christian Doctrine Book II; Augustine c. 354–430) · Wikipedia
Summary: Augustine gave the Latin West its governing image for the Christian use of pagan learning. Just as Israel, leaving Egypt, carried off the Egyptians’ gold and silver to furnish the tabernacle, so the Christian may take what is true and useful in pagan liberal learning and turn it to the service of the Gospel. In On Christian Doctrine II he surveys the disciplines a student of Scripture should master — languages, history, dialectic, number, natural knowledge — sorts them from the superstitious arts to be shunned, and subordinates all of them to charity and the understanding of Scripture, which he holds surpasses every treasure brought out of Egypt. Where Clement made philosophy the handmaid of theology, Augustine supplied the proof-text and the metaphor — “spoiling the Egyptians” — that the schools would quote for a thousand years. In his early, newly-converted On Order (386) he had gone further, treating the seven arts as a graded ladder up which the mind climbs from the sensible to God; the aged bishop of the Retractations pulled that enthusiasm back.

Claims & evidence

The gold of the Egyptians — plunder the pagans’ truth for the service of the Gospel

Moreover, if those who are called philosophers, and especially the Platonists, have said aught that is true and in harmony with our faith, we are not only not to shrink from it, but to claim it for our own use from those who have unlawful possession of it. For, as the Egyptians had not only the idols and heavy burdens which the people of Israel hated and fled from, but also vessels and ornaments of gold and silver, and garments, which the same people when going out of Egypt appropriated to themselves, designing them for a better use… in the same way all branches of heathen learning have not only false and superstitious fancies and heavy burdens of unnecessary toil, which every one of us, when going out under the leadership of Christ from the fellowship of the heathen, ought to abhor and avoid; but they contain also liberal instruction which is better adapted to the use of the truth, and some most excellent precepts of morality; and some truths in regard even to the worship of the One God are found among them. Now these are, so to speak, their gold and silver, which they did not create themselves, but dug out of the mines of God’s providence which are everywhere scattered abroad… These, therefore, the Christian, when he separates himself in spirit from the miserable fellowship of these men, ought to take away from them, and to devote to their proper use in preaching the gospel.

— On Christian Doctrine II.40, trans. J. F. Shaw.1 This is the passage the medieval and Reformation schools quoted whenever they defended reading the pagans. The typology is drawn from Exodus 3:22 and 12:35–36 (Israel “spoiled the Egyptians”): the gold was Egyptian metal, but God’s providence had put it there, and it was destined all along for the tabernacle. So too the truths lodged in pagan philosophy are not the pagans’ own invention but God’s, “dug out of the mines of God’s providence,” and the believer reclaims them for their proper use rather than leaving them in the service of idols. The reclaiming is discriminating, not wholesale: the “idols and heavy burdens” — the superstition — stay behind in Egypt; only the gold comes out.

The precedent: the fathers who came out of Egypt laden — and Moses before them all

And what else have many good and faithful men among our brethren done? Do we not see with what a quantity of gold and silver and garments Cyprian, that most persuasive teacher and most blessed martyr, was loaded when he came out of Egypt? How much Lactantius brought with him? And Victorinus, and Optatus, and Hilary, not to speak of living men! How much Greeks out of number have borrowed! And prior to all these, that most faithful servant of God, Moses, had done the same thing; for of him it is written that he was learned in all the wisdom of the Egyptians.

— On Christian Doctrine II.40.2 Augustine grounds the principle in precedent. The Latin fathers he names — Cyprian, Lactantius, Marius Victorinus, Optatus, Hilary of Poitiers — were all classically trained rhetoricians and philosophers who carried their learning over into the church’s service; Augustine, himself a former professor of rhetoric, plainly counts himself in their line (“not to speak of living men”). And behind them stands the archetype: Moses, of whom Acts 7:22 says he “was learned in all the wisdom of the Egyptians” — the Old-Testament warrant that the finest pagan schooling can be turned to God’s purposes.

Which studies, and in what spirit — the useful arts weighed and sorted

Before the Egyptian-gold image, Book II runs through the disciplines a student of Scripture actually needs and rates each in turn: the languages, Greek and Hebrew above all, without which the words of Scripture stay dark (chs. 11–15); history, which fixes the sense of the narrative — here he praises Eusebius’s chronicle and Ambrose’s use of dates to refute the claim that Christ borrowed from Plato (ch. 28); the natural knowledge of “animals, trees, herbs, stones” that unlocks Scripture’s figures (ch. 16, 29); the useful mechanical arts (ch. 30); dialectic and the science of definition, whose valid inferences “exist eternally in the reason of things, and [have their] origin with God” (chs. 31–35); the true rules of rhetoric (chs. 36–37); and the science of number, which “was not created by man, but was discovered by investigation” (ch. 38). Against these he sets the arts to be shunned outright — astrology and the divinatory “superstitions” that rest on “a baleful fellowship between men and devils” (chs. 20–24). The governing rule is that truth, wherever it turns up, is God’s property:

let every good and true Christian understand that wherever truth may be found, it belongs to his Master; and while he recognizes and acknowledges the truth, even in their religious literature, let him reject the figments of superstition… we ought not to give up music because of the superstition of the heathen, if we can derive anything from it that is of use for the understanding of Holy Scripture… For we ought not to refuse to learn letters because they say that Mercury discovered them.

— On Christian Doctrine II.18.3 The pagan origin of an art — even a mythological one — does not disqualify it; the truth in it is God’s and may be taken. But the taking is disciplined. Augustine warns the “studious and able young men” to “soberly and carefully… discriminate,” not to chase the fashionable studies “as if these could secure for them the happiness they seek,” and to keep to the maxim “not too much of anything.”4 The arts are instruments, valued exactly for the service they render Scripture — and no further.

Ordered to charity, and surpassed by Scripture itself

But when the student of the Holy Scriptures, prepared in the way I have indicated, shall enter upon his investigations, let him constantly meditate upon that saying of the apostle’s, “Knowledge puffeth up, but charity edifieth.” For so he will feel that, whatever may be the riches he brings with him out of Egypt, yet unless he has kept the passover, he cannot be safe.

— On Christian Doctrine II.41.5 The whole enterprise is fenced by charity. Learning that is not ruled by love merely inflates its owner (1 Corinthians 8:1); the gold carried out of Egypt is safe only in a heart “rooted and grounded in love.” And the plunder, however precious, is never the treasure it serves. Augustine ends the argument by putting the arts firmly in their place beneath the Scripture they exist to open:

just as poor as the store of gold and silver and garments which the people of Israel brought with them out of Egypt was in comparison with the riches which they afterwards attained at Jerusalem… so poor is all the useful knowledge which is gathered from the books of the heathen when compared with the knowledge of Holy Scripture. For whatever man may have learnt from other sources, if it is hurtful, it is there condemned; if it is useful, it is therein contained.

— On Christian Doctrine II.42.6 This is the same subordination Seneca drew (“there is only one really liberal study… the study of wisdom”) and Clement drew (wisdom the queen, the arts her handmaids), now stamped with the Egyptian-gold image and the Pauline warning about knowledge that puffs up. The arts are real riches, worth carrying out of Egypt — but poor beside Jerusalem.

Notes

  • The younger Augustine went further — and the older pulled back. In On Order (De Ordine, 386), written days after his conversion, Augustine treated the seven liberal arts as a graded ascent — “an order of studies by which one can proceed from corporeal to incorporeal things” — by which the mind is trained to rise from the sensible world to the intelligible and so to God, and he planned a whole set of handbooks on the disciplines (of which only On Music was substantially finished). Decades later, reviewing that book in the Retractations (I.3), the aged bishop drew the enthusiasm back: he no longer believed the arts a sure road to God, noting that “many saintly persons” do not know them at all, while “some, in truth, know them and are not saintly.” The mature judgment of On Christian Doctrine is the settled middle between the two: the arts are genuinely useful instruments for reading Scripture, to be reclaimed like Egyptian gold — but they are instruments, not a ladder to salvation.7
  • The anchor exhibit for “the handmaiden of theology.” Augustine’s Egyptian-gold argument is the classic Western warrant for the thesis that pagan liberal learning may be ordered to, and subordinated under, the knowledge of God — the case Clement had already made from Sarah and Hagar (“philosophy the handmaid of theology,” philosophia ancilla theologiae) and Basil had turned into a schoolboy’s manual. The three together — Clement’s principle, Basil’s practice, Augustine’s proof-text — are the leading patristic witnesses for that argument, weighed in the summary’s “Arguments” section as those pages are written.
  • On the wiki. Augustine appears across many timelines as a witness — on astrology (where the same Book II attack on the “superstition of astrologers” supplies his verdict), the Old Testament canon, friendship, original sin, and many more — a measure of how far his settlement of the church’s relation to pagan letters, no less than his theology, shaped the Latin West.
