Tertullian on Athens and Jerusalem — pagan learning as the mother of heresy

Dates: c. 203 (The Prescription Against Heretics; Tertullian c. 155–220) · Wikipedia
Summary: Tertullian gives the timeline its sharpest anti-classical voice. Against the confident “yes” of Clement and the later fathers, he asks the question that became the motto of every Christian suspicion of secular learning — “What has Athens to do with Jerusalem?” — and traces the church’s heresies back to pagan philosophy, their “mother.” Yet the case is not as flatly anti-intellectual as the slogan sounds: Tertullian was himself a formidably trained rhetorician who used every weapon of the schools, and his target is philosophy as a rival authority and source of speculation, not letters or learning as such. He is the necessary counter-pole on this timeline — the witness that the church’s reception of the liberal arts was contested from the first.

Claims & evidence

“What has Athens to do with Jerusalem?” — philosophy and the church have nothing in common

What indeed has Athens to do with Jerusalem? What concord is there between the Academy and the Church? what between heretics and Christians? Our instruction comes from “the porch of Solomon,” who had himself taught that “the Lord should be sought in simplicity of heart.” Away with all attempts to produce a mottled Christianity of Stoic, Platonic, and dialectic composition! We want no curious disputation after possessing Christ Jesus, no inquisition after enjoying the gospel! With our faith, we desire no further belief.

— The Prescription Against Heretics 7, trans. Peter Holmes.1 This is the single most-quoted sentence in the whole history of Christian misgiving about pagan learning. “The porch of Solomon” (an echo of the Stoa, the philosophers’ “porch,” turned against them) marks the contrast: the Christian’s school is the revealed wisdom of God, not the lecture-hall. The demand is for a faith that rests when it has found Christ — against the restless curiositas that keeps searching after the gospel has already answered.

Philosophy is the mother of the heresies

These are “the doctrines” of men and “of demons” produced for itching ears of the spirit of this world’s wisdom: this the Lord called “foolishness,” and “chose the foolish things of the world” to confound even philosophy itself. For (philosophy) it is which is the material of the world’s wisdom, the rash interpreter of the nature and the dispensation of God. Indeed heresies are themselves instigated by philosophy… The same subject-matter is discussed over and over again by the heretics and the philosophers; the same arguments are involved.

— The Prescription Against Heretics 7.2 Tertullian’s charge is genealogical, and specific: he names names. The “æons” and the “trinity of man” in Valentinus come from Plato’s school; Marcion’s serene higher god comes from the Stoics; the mortality of the soul from Epicurus; the denial of the resurrection of the body from “the aggregate school of all the philosophers.” Even Aristotle he indicts by name — “Unhappy Aristotle! who invented for these men dialectics, the art of building up and pulling down… embarrassing even to itself, retracting everything, and really treating of nothing.” Philosophy is dangerous not because it is learned but because, as a rival authority, it breeds the endless speculations Paul warned against (Colossians 2:8; 1 Timothy 1:4).

Notes

  • Not a blanket rejection of the liberal arts — and Tertullian knew it. The slogan reads like a rout of all learning, but two things pull against that. First, the target is philosophy as a competing source of doctrine and a spur to speculative curiositas — the “mother of the heresies” — not grammar, rhetoric, or letters as such; the very next lines fasten on Paul’s “philosophy and vain deceit” (Colossians 2:8), not on literacy. Second, Tertullian is himself one of the most rhetorically accomplished writers of the Latin church: the polished antitheses, the forensic vocabulary (his whole treatise is built on the Roman legal doctrine of praescriptio), and the classical allusions all come from exactly the schooling he appears to disown. He fights the philosophers with the arts of the schools. The tension is real and is the point of putting him here: the anti-classical case, even at its sharpest, is aimed at pagan learning as a rival authority, and is argued by a man steeped in the pagan curriculum.
  • The counter-pole on this timeline. Where Clement makes philosophy the God-given “schoolmaster” of the Greeks and Augustine bids the Christian carry off the Egyptians’ gold, Tertullian is the voice that answers no — or at least beware. His “Athens and Jerusalem” became the standing banner of every later movement that distrusted secular letters in the church, and it is the objection that the summary’s “handmaiden of theology” argument has to answer: does pagan liberal learning belong in a Christian’s formation at all? Jerome’s nightmare of being scourged as “a Ciceronian, not a Christian” is the same anxiety in a later and more personal key.
  • On the wiki. Tertullian appears across many timelines as a witness — on the Old Testament canon, the Trinity (he coined the Latin vocabulary), infant baptism, the perpetual virginity of Mary, and more — a rigorist and a rhetorician at once.
