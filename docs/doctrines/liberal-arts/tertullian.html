Tertullian on Athens and Jerusalem — philosophy and the heresies

Dates: c. 203 (The Prescription Against Heretics; Tertullian c. 155–220) · Wikipedia
Summary: Tertullian, a Latin Christian writer of Carthage, argues in The Prescription Against Heretics against joining pagan philosophy to the faith. In the same years Clement of Alexandria was treating philosophy as a preparation for the gospel. Tertullian asks “What indeed has Athens to do with Jerusalem?” and derives the heresies of his day from the philosophical schools. The passage’s named target is philosophy, as the source of the heresies and of “curious disputation.”

Claims & evidence

“What has Athens to do with Jerusalem?”

What indeed has Athens to do with Jerusalem? What concord is there between the Academy and the Church? what between heretics and Christians? Our instruction comes from “the porch of Solomon,” who had himself taught that “the Lord should be sought in simplicity of heart.” Away with all attempts to produce a mottled Christianity of Stoic, Platonic, and dialectic composition! We want no curious disputation after possessing Christ Jesus, no inquisition after enjoying the gospel! With our faith, we desire no further belief.

— The Prescription Against Heretics 7, trans. Peter Holmes.1 The ANF translator explains “the porch of Solomon” by the apostles’ teaching in Solomon’s porch at the beginning of the church (Acts 3), and notes that “curious disputation” renders curiositas.3 Tertullian sets the instruction of the church against that of the philosophical schools, and asks for a faith that rests once it has found Christ, with no further inquiry after the gospel.

Philosophy as the source of the heresies

These are “the doctrines” of men and “of demons” produced for itching ears of the spirit of this world’s wisdom: this the Lord called “foolishness,” and “chose the foolish things of the world” to confound even philosophy itself. For (philosophy) it is which is the material of the world’s wisdom, the rash interpreter of the nature and the dispensation of God. Indeed heresies are themselves instigated by philosophy… The same subject-matter is discussed over and over again by the heretics and the philosophers; the same arguments are involved.

— The Prescription Against Heretics 7.2 The ANF translation heads the chapter “Pagan Philosophy the Parent of Heresies.”4 Tertullian traces each heresy to a named school. The “æons” and the “trinity of man” in Valentinus come from Plato’s school; Marcion’s serene higher god comes from the Stoics; the mortality of the soul from Epicurus; the denial of the resurrection of the body from “the aggregate school of all the philosophers.” He indicts Aristotle by name — “Unhappy Aristotle! who invented for these men dialectics, the art of building up and pulling down… embarrassing even to itself, retracting everything, and really treating of nothing.” In the same chapter he cites Paul’s warnings against “fables and endless genealogies” (1 Timothy 1:4) and against being beguiled “through philosophy and vain deceit” (Colossians 2:8), and adds that Paul “had been at Athens.”

Reception and context

  • The target of the passage. The objects named in chapter 7 are philosophy and its schools (the Academy, the Stoa, Epicurus, Aristotle’s dialectic) as sources of what he calls the heresies; the chapter cites Paul’s “philosophy and vain deceit” (Colossians 2:8) in the lines before the Athens–Jerusalem question. The chapter does not discuss grammar or the other arts of the school curriculum. The treatise takes its title from the Roman legal plea of praescriptio (⚠ unverified — not yet sourced), and Tertullian’s own Latin draws on the rhetorical training of the schools (⚠ unverified — not yet sourced).
  • Placement on the timeline. Where Clement calls philosophy the “schoolmaster” of the Greeks and Augustine bids the Christian carry off the Egyptians’ gold, Tertullian sets Athens against Jerusalem. Jerome’s later account of a dream in which he was rebuked as “a Ciceronian, not a Christian” is entered separately on this timeline. The question the summary follows across these entries is whether pagan liberal learning belongs in a Christian’s formation.
  • Elsewhere on the wiki. Tertullian appears across many timelines as a witness — on the Old Testament canon, the Trinity, infant baptism, the perpetual virginity of Mary, and more.
