Gregory of Nazianzus on the value of secular learning

Dates: c. 380 (the funeral oration on Basil, Oration 43, delivered c. 381; Gregory c. 329–390) · Wikipedia
Summary: Preaching the funeral oration over his lifelong friend Basil the Great, Gregory of Nazianzus — himself schooled at Athens alongside Basil, and the most polished rhetorician of the Cappadocian fathers — pauses to mount a ringing defense of “external culture,” the pagan liberal curriculum “which many Christians ill-judgingly abhor, as treacherous and dangerous.” His argument is the tradition’s own: secular letters are like the elements — useful or harmful only in the using — so the Christian takes from them “principles of enquiry and speculation” while rejecting “their idolatry.” He turns the charge back on the despisers as men who “desir[e] all men to be as they themselves are, in order to… escape the detection of their want of culture.” He reinforces the pro-learning pole beside Clement and Basil, and answers, from a bishop’s pulpit, the anti-classical pole of Tertullian’s “What has Athens to do with Jerusalem?”

Claims & evidence

The first of our advantages is education — even “external culture” the Christian must not dishonour

I take it as admitted by men of sense, that the first of our advantages is education; and not only this our more noble form of it, which disregards rhetorical ornaments and glory, and holds to salvation, and beauty in the objects of our contemplation: but even that external culture which many Christians ill-judgingly abhor, as treacherous and dangerous, and keeping us afar from God… so from secular literature we have received principles of enquiry and speculation, while we have rejected their idolatry, terror, and pit of destruction. Nay, even these have aided us in our religion, by our perception of the contrast between what is worse and what is better… We must not then dishonour education, because some men are pleased to do so, but rather suppose such men to be boorish and uneducated, desiring all men to be as they themselves are, in order to hide themselves in the general, and escape the detection of their want of culture.

— Oration 43 (Funeral Oration on Basil), §11, trans. Browne & Swallow, source.1 This is Gregory’s charter passage. He ranks education first among human goods and refuses to let the “more noble” Christian learning crowd out “external culture” — the pagan liberal arts. His mechanism is the same one Basil and Augustine use: secular letters are like fire, food, or iron, “of itself” neither useful nor harmful “except according to the will of those who use it,” and just as physicians “compounded healthful drugs from certain of the reptiles,” so the believer distils “principles of enquiry and speculation” from the classics and discards the poison. The sting is in the tail: those who abhor learning are not holier but only “boorish and uneducated,” wanting company in their ignorance — the exact inversion of the pious pose that made Tertullian’s “Athens and Jerusalem” a badge of honour.

Both learning and virtue, or you are “one-eyed”

he was trained in general education, and practised in the worship of God… For those who are successful in life or in letters only, while deficient in the other, seem to me to differ in nothing from one-eyed men, whose loss is great, but their deformity greater, both in their own eyes, and in those of others. While those who attain eminence in both alike, and are ambidextrous, both possess perfection, and pass their life with the blessedness of heaven.

— Oration 43, §12, trans. Browne & Swallow, source.2 Gregory refuses the either/or the whole timeline turns on. To have “letters” without “life” (virtue), or virtue without letters, is to be “one-eyed” — a real good, but maimed. The Christian ideal is the “ambidextrous” person “eminent in both alike.” Learning is thus neither an idol to be pursued for itself nor a snare to be shunned, but one of the two eyes of a complete soul — the same ordering of study to a good and God-loving character that anchors the summary’s argument that education is finally for virtue and the knowledge of God.

Gather from every flower — the bee, and the “metropolis of letters”

When sufficiently trained at home, as he ought to fall short in no form of excellence, and not be surpassed by the busy bee, which gathers what is most useful from every flower, he set out for the city of Caesarea… the metropolis of letters… Letters form our distinction here, and are our badge… Eloquence was his by-work, from which he culled enough to make it an assistance to him in Christian philosophy, since power of this kind is needed to set forth the objects of our contemplation. For a mind which cannot express itself is like the motion of a man in a lethargy.

— Oration 43, §13, trans. Browne & Swallow, source.3 The bee that “gathers what is most useful from every flower” is Basil’s own governing image for the selective reading of the classics, here turned back on Basil himself as the praise of a whole education. And Gregory names the pay-off precisely: eloquence, though only a “by-work,” is “an assistance… in Christian philosophy,” for “a mind which cannot express itself is like the motion of a man in a lethargy” — the arts of language put in the service of setting forth the faith, exactly the case the summary makes for the liberal curriculum as the handmaid of theology.

He traversed every branch of learning — and subordinated it to godliness

What branch of learning did he not traverse; and that with unexampled success, passing through all, as no one else passed through any one of them… Who had such power in Rhetoric… Who in Grammar, which perfects our tongues in Greek… Who in Philosophy, that really lofty and high reaching science… which is called Dialectic… Of Astronomy, Geometry, and numerical proportion he had such a grasp, that he could not be baffled by those who are clever in such sciences: excessive application to them he despised, as useless to those whose desire is godliness… But what are these, illustrious though they be, compared with the moral discipline of the man?

— Oration 43, §23, trans. Browne & Swallow, source.4 Gregory runs through the whole curriculum by name — Rhetoric, Grammar, Philosophy and Dialectic, then Astronomy, Geometry, and “numerical proportion” (arithmetic): in effect the trivium and quadrivium — and praises Basil for mastering every one. Yet the praise is fenced by the tradition’s guard-rail: “excessive application” to these sciences Basil “despised, as useless to those whose desire is godliness,” and all of it is as nothing “compared with the moral discipline of the man.” Learning is to be had in full and had well — but it is a road, not a home. Gregory thus holds together the two things the timeline keeps pulling apart: the arts pursued to real eminence, and the arts kept firmly beneath the knowledge and love of God.

Notes

  • The pro-learning pole, from a bishop’s pulpit. Gregory stands with Clement and Basil against the distrust of pagan letters. What makes his voice distinctive is the occasion: this is not a treatise on education but a public eulogy before the church of Caesarea, in which the defense of “external culture” is offered as plain common sense — “admitted by men of sense” — and the despisers are gently mocked as merely uneducated. It is the confident maturity of a case that Clement had to argue defensively two centuries earlier.
  • A direct answer to Tertullian. Where Tertullian asked “What indeed has Athens to do with Jerusalem?” Gregory — who had actually studied at Athens and loved the place as “a city truly of gold” — answers that the two need not be enemies: the elements of pagan culture, like fire and iron, are made good or evil only by the use, and the Christian may take the good. His whole education alongside Basil at Athens is the lived refutation of the slogan.
  • The same friendship, told for a different end. Oration 43 is best known as the tradition’s great portrait of a friendship — Gregory’s bond with Basil, told at length in §§14–24 — and it serves that role on this wiki’s friendship timeline. Here the same oration is mined for a different thread: its opening defense of secular learning (§§11–13) and its catalogue of the arts (§23). The two readings are complementary — the friendship that Athens made was, for Gregory, inseparable from the education Athens gave.
  • On the wiki. Gregory of Nazianzus appears across many timelines as a witness — on the Old Testament canon (his canon-poem), the Trinity, friendship, and more — a mark of how central “the Theologian” was to the fourth-century church’s reception of both the faith and the classical inheritance.
