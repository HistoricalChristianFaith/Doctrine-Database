Gregory of Nazianzus on the value of secular learning

Dates: c. 380 (the funeral oration on Basil, Oration 43, delivered c. 381; Gregory c. 329–390) · Wikipedia
Summary: Preaching the funeral oration over his lifelong friend Basil the Great, Gregory of Nazianzus — himself schooled in rhetoric at Athens alongside Basil — includes a defense of “external culture,” the pagan liberal curriculum “which many Christians ill-judgingly abhor, as treacherous and dangerous.” His argument: secular letters are like the elements — useful or harmful only in the using — so the Christian takes from them “principles of enquiry and speculation” while rejecting “their idolatry.” He describes those who despise it as men “desiring all men to be as they themselves are, in order to… escape the detection of their want of culture.” On this timeline he stands with Clement and Basil in favour of secular learning, in contrast to Tertullian’s “What has Athens to do with Jerusalem?”

Claims & evidence

“The first of our advantages is education” — including “external culture”

I take it as admitted by men of sense, that the first of our advantages is education; and not only this our more noble form of it, which disregards rhetorical ornaments and glory, and holds to salvation, and beauty in the objects of our contemplation: but even that external culture which many Christians ill-judgingly abhor, as treacherous and dangerous, and keeping us afar from God… so from secular literature we have received principles of enquiry and speculation, while we have rejected their idolatry, terror, and pit of destruction. Nay, even these have aided us in our religion, by our perception of the contrast between what is worse and what is better… We must not then dishonour education, because some men are pleased to do so, but rather suppose such men to be boorish and uneducated, desiring all men to be as they themselves are, in order to hide themselves in the general, and escape the detection of their want of culture.

— Oration 43 (Funeral Oration on Basil), §11, trans. Browne & Swallow, source.1 Gregory ranks education first among human goods and counts in it, beside the “more noble” Christian learning, “external culture” — the pagan liberal arts. In the same section (compare Basil and Augustine) he likens secular letters to fire, food, or iron, “of itself” neither useful nor harmful “except according to the will of those who use it,” and just as physicians “compounded healthful drugs from certain of the reptiles,” so the believer distils “principles of enquiry and speculation” from the classics and discards the poison. Those who abhor learning he calls “boorish and uneducated,” wanting company in their want of culture.

Both learning and virtue, or you are “one-eyed”

he was trained in general education, and practised in the worship of God… For those who are successful in life or in letters only, while deficient in the other, seem to me to differ in nothing from one-eyed men, whose loss is great, but their deformity greater, both in their own eyes, and in those of others. While those who attain eminence in both alike, and are ambidextrous, both possess perfection, and pass their life with the blessedness of heaven.

— Oration 43, §12, trans. Browne & Swallow, source.2 Gregory joins letters and virtue. To have “letters” without “life” (virtue), or virtue without letters, is in his image to be “one-eyed”; his ideal is the “ambidextrous” person “eminent in both alike.” In this image learning is one of the two eyes of a complete person.

Gather from every flower — the bee, and the “metropolis of letters”

When sufficiently trained at home, as he ought to fall short in no form of excellence, and not be surpassed by the busy bee, which gathers what is most useful from every flower, he set out for the city of Caesarea… the metropolis of letters… Letters form our distinction here, and are our badge… Eloquence was his by-work, from which he culled enough to make it an assistance to him in Christian philosophy, since power of this kind is needed to set forth the objects of our contemplation. For a mind which cannot express itself is like the motion of a man in a lethargy.

— Oration 43, §13, trans. Browne & Swallow, source.3 The bee that “gathers what is most useful from every flower” is the image Basil uses in his Address to Young Men for the selective reading of the classics, here applied to Basil himself in praise of his education. Gregory states the use of eloquence: though only a “by-work,” is “an assistance… in Christian philosophy,” for “a mind which cannot express itself is like the motion of a man in a lethargy” — the arts of language put in the service of setting forth the faith.

Basil’s command of every branch of learning, set beneath “moral discipline”

What branch of learning did he not traverse; and that with unexampled success, passing through all, as no one else passed through any one of them… Who had such power in Rhetoric… Who in Grammar, which perfects our tongues in Greek… Who in Philosophy, that really lofty and high reaching science… which is called Dialectic… Of Astronomy, Geometry, and numerical proportion he had such a grasp, that he could not be baffled by those who are clever in such sciences: excessive application to them he despised, as useless to those whose desire is godliness… But what are these, illustrious though they be, compared with the moral discipline of the man?

— Oration 43, §23, trans. Browne & Swallow, source.4 Gregory runs through the whole curriculum by name — Rhetoric, Grammar, Philosophy and Dialectic, then Astronomy, Geometry, and “numerical proportion” (arithmetic) — and praises Basil for mastering every one. He adds that “excessive application” to these sciences Basil “despised, as useless to those whose desire is godliness,” and asks what they are “compared with the moral discipline of the man.” In Gregory’s portrait the arts are pursued to eminence and set beneath moral discipline and godliness.

Notes

  • The occasion. Oration 43 is not a treatise on education but a public eulogy before the church of Caesarea, in which the defense of “external culture” is offered as “admitted by men of sense” and its despisers are called “boorish and uneducated.”
  • Athens. Gregory had studied at Athens alongside Basil and speaks of the place as “a city truly of gold.” Compare Tertullian’s “What indeed has Athens to do with Jerusalem?”, earlier on this timeline; Gregory holds that the elements of pagan culture, like fire and iron, are made good or evil by their use, and that the Christian may take the good.
  • The same oration on the friendship timeline. Oration 43 is also read as a portrait of a friendship — Gregory’s bond with Basil, told at length in §§14–24 — and is cited for that on this wiki’s friendship timeline. Here the same oration is cited for its defense of secular learning (§§11–13) and its catalogue of the arts (§23).
  • Other timelines. Gregory of Nazianzus appears as a witness on other timelines — on the Old Testament canon (his canon-poem), the Trinity, friendship, and others.
