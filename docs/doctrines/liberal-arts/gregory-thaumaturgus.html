Gregory Thaumaturgus on the Purpose of the Liberal Arts

Dates: c. 238 (the year of the Address; Gregory lived c. 213–c. 270) · Wikipedia
Summary: On leaving the school of Origen at Caesarea in Palestine, Gregory (later bishop of Neocaesarea, “the Wonderworker”) delivered a farewell oration of thanks that is the earliest firsthand Christian description of a whole liberal-arts curriculum put to Christian use. He recounts Origen leading his pupils through a graded ascent — dialectic, then natural science with geometry and astronomy, then ethics and the virtues, and at the summit theology and the Scriptures — reading the pagan philosophers and poets critically along the way, “rejecting nothing… except only the productions of the atheists.”

Claims & evidence

He describes a graded curriculum, beginning with logic and the disciplining of loose thought

The Address of Thanksgiving (the Oratio Panegyrica), delivered about 238 as Gregory left Origen’s circle after some five years, narrates the master’s method as a deliberate sequence. Origen begins by clearing the ground — exercising his pupils first in logic and refutation, weeding out hasty assent and the merely verbal, and prizing the pursuit of the things themselves over “beauty of language”:

In this way, that capacity of our mind which deals critically with words and reasonings, was educated in a rational manner; not according to the judgments of illustrious rhetoricians… for theirs is a discipline of little value and no necessity: but in accordance with that which is most needful for all.

— The Oration and Panegyric Addressed to Origen, Argument VII, source.1 Origen takes the untrained mind “as a skilled husbandman may take in hand some field unwrought,” cutting out “thorns and thistles” of undisciplined reasoning “by the processes of refutation and prohibition… in the genuine Socratic fashion” before sowing the “good seed.”

Then natural science, geometry, and astronomy — “ladders reaching the skies”

Upon that foundation Origen builds the study of the physical world and the mathematical arts. Natural philosophy turns a “reasonless” wonder at creation into a rational one; geometry is set as the “immutable groundwork,” and astronomy lifts the mind upward:

And the one he presented lucidly as the immutable groundwork and secure foundation of all, namely geometry; and by the other, namely astronomy, he lifted us up to the things that are highest above us, while he made heaven passable to us by the help of each of these sciences, as though they were ladders reaching the skies.

— The Oration and Panegyric Addressed to Origen, Argument VIII, source.2 Three of the four disciplines the West would fix as the quadrivium — geometry, astronomy, and (in the “sacred mathematics”) arithmetic — here sit as rungs on a graded ascent, not ends in themselves.

Then ethics and the four virtues — taught by deed, not word alone

Above the sciences Origen sets moral philosophy, and Gregory is emphatic that his teacher formed character by example and not by lecture. Prudence, temperance, righteousness, and fortitude are cultivated not as definitions but as habits:

Not thus, however, in mere words only did this teacher go over the truths concerning the virtues with us; but he incited us much more to the practice of virtue, and stimulated us by the deeds he did more than by the doctrines he taught.

— The Oration and Panegyric Addressed to Origen, Argument IX, source.3 The whole ascent is ordered to piety as its crown — “piety, which men rightly designate when they call it the mother of the virtues. For this is the beginning and the end of all the virtues” — whose end Gregory states as the ancient homoiōsis theōi: “By the pure mind make thyself like to God, that thou mayest draw near to Him, and abide in Him.”4

Reading the pagan philosophers and poets critically — “rejecting nothing… except only the atheists”

The curriculum was frankly built on the whole inheritance of Greek learning, read discerningly rather than shunned. Origen required his students to read widely, refusing only the writers who denied God and providence:

we should read with utmost diligence all that has been written, both by the philosophers and by the poets of old, rejecting nothing, and repudiating nothing… except only the productions of the atheists, who, in their conceits, lapse from the general intelligence of man, and deny that there is either a God or a providence.

— The Oration and Panegyric Addressed to Origen, Argument XIII, source.5 Origen would not tie his pupils to any one sect but introduced them to all, “determined that we should be ignorant of no kind of Grecian doctrine,” himself “selecting and setting before us all that was useful and true in all the various philosophers, and putting aside all that was false.”6 This is the practical shape of the same discernment Basil would later liken to bees culling honey and avoiding thorns.

The summit: theology and the Scriptures — the whole circle of knowledge ordered to God

All of it — logic, science, ethics — is preparatory to the reading of Scripture, which crowns the course. Origen “counselled us to attach ourselves to none of” the human teachers “but to devote ourselves to God alone, and to the prophets,” and became himself “the interpreter of the prophets.” Yet no branch of learning was forbidden along the way:

we had it in our power to learn every kind of discourse, both foreign and Greek, both spiritual and political, both divine and human; and we were permitted with all freedom to go round the whole circle of knowledge, and investigate it.

— The Oration and Panegyric Addressed to Origen, Argument XV, source.7 Gregory calls the school “a paradise to us after the similitude of the paradise of God” — the classical enkyklios paideia, the “whole circle of knowledge,” run through and re-consecrated to the knowledge of God. It is the earliest eyewitness account of the arrangement the later fathers would argue for in principle.

Notes

  • Gregory’s ascent — the arts as a graded preparation crowned by the knowledge of God — is the lived practice behind the case Clement of Alexandria made in principle a generation earlier (philosophy as the “handmaid,” the arts as “preparatory culture” beneath philosophy and wisdom) and that Basil would turn into a manual a century and a half later. His Origen is the great systematizer named in the wiki’s argument on philosophy as the handmaid of theology.
  • The critical-reading rule — read all, reject only the impious — anticipates Augustine’s “spoiling the Egyptians” and Basil’s bee: the believer takes what is true from pagan letters and leaves the rest. It also stands opposite Tertullian’s near-contemporary “What has Athens to do with Jerusalem?” — Origen’s Caesarean school is precisely the marriage of Athens and Jerusalem that Tertullian distrusted.
  • The oration is variously titled the Address of Thanksgiving to Origen, the Oratio Panegyrica, or (in the older English) The Oration and Panegyric Addressed to Origen. Some scholars have questioned whether the Gregory of the Address is Gregory the Wonderworker of Neocaesarea, but the traditional identification remains standard.
