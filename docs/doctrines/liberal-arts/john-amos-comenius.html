John Amos Comenius on the Purpose of the Liberal Arts

Dates: c. 1638 · Wikipedia
Summary: The Moravian bishop and “father of modern education” (1592–1670) grounded a programme of universal education — “the whole Art of Teaching all Things to all Men” — in an explicitly Christian anthropology: man is made in the image of God for knowledge, virtue, and piety, and education is what forms that image and recalls it toward its “former estate.” His Didactica Magna (The Great Didactic, c. 1633–1638) answers the timeline’s question — what is learning for? — by ordering all study upward to God, and answers who is it for? with a then-radical “all children, of both sexes, rich and poor, none excepted.”

Claims & evidence

Education is “the whole Art of Teaching all Things to all Men” — a universal, God-directed enterprise

The whole Art of Teaching all Things to all Men. A certain Inducement to found such Schools in all the Parishes, Towns, and Villages of every Christian Kingdom, that the entire Youth of both Sexes, none being excepted, shall Quickly, Pleasantly, & Thoroughly Become learned in the Sciences, pure in Morals, trained to Piety, and in this manner instructed in all things necessary for the present and for the future life…

— The Great Didactic, title-page (trans. M. W. Keatinge), A. & C. Black ed. The Latin ideal behind it — omnes omnia, “all men, all things” — is the programme of pansophia (universal wisdom): the aim named on the very title page is at once knowledge (“the Sciences”), virtue (“Morals”), and piety, and at once everyone’s.

The end of learning is knowledge, virtue, and piety — the three “main issues of our life”

It is evident, then, that the ultimate end of man is eternal happiness with God. The subordinate ends, also, at which we aim in this transitory life, are evident from the words of the divine soliloquy which the Creator uttered when about to make man. “Let us make man,” He said, “in our image, after our likeness…” (Gen. i. 26).

(i.) Erudition. (ii.) Virtue or seemly morals. (iii.) Religion or piety. Under Erudition we comprehend the knowledge of all things, arts, and tongues; under Virtue, not only external decorum, but the whole disposition of our movements, internal and external; while by Religion we understand that inner veneration by which the mind of man attaches and binds itself to the supreme Godhead.

It follows, therefore, that we advance towards our ultimate end in proportion as we pursue Learning, Virtue, and Piety in this world. These three are undoubtedly the main issues of our life; all else are side channels, hindrances, or ornamentations.

— The Great Didactic IV (“There Are Three Stages in the Preparation for Eternity”), trans. Keatinge, pp. 36, 38, 39. Comenius roots the whole enterprise in the image of God of Genesis 1:26: because man is made to know, to rule himself, and to refer all things to God, education’s three subordinate ends — erudition, virtue, piety — are simply the unfolding of that image toward its eternal end. It is the same ordering the fathers made (learning subordinate to wisdom and the knowledge of God), now built into a whole method.

A person becomes fully human only by education

The seeds of knowledge, of virtue, and of piety are, as we have seen, naturally implanted in us; but the actual knowledge, virtue, and piety are not so given. These must be acquired by prayer, by education, and by action. He gave no bad definition who said that man was a “teachable animal.” And indeed it is only by a proper education that he can become a man.

Let none believe, therefore, that any can really be a man, unless he have learned to act like one, that is, have been trained in those elements which constitute a man.

— The Great Didactic VI (“If a Man Is to Be Produced, It Is Necessary That He Be Formed by Education”), trans. Keatinge, pp. 51–52. The capacities are innate seeds; education is the cultivation without which they never come to actual knowledge, virtue, or piety — so schooling is not an ornament of human life but its making.

Universal education: all the young, of both sexes, rich and poor, are to be taught

[N]ot the children of the rich or of the powerful only, but of all alike, boys and girls, both noble and ignoble, rich and poor, in all cities and towns, villages and hamlets, should be sent to school.

— The Great Didactic IX (“All the Young of Both Sexes Should Be Sent to School”), trans. Keatinge, p. 65. His reason is the image of God again: all “have been born to man’s state… that they may be men, that is to say, rational creatures… and the images of their Creator,” so to admit some and exclude others “is an injury… against God Himself, who wishes to be acknowledged… by all upon whom He has impressed His image.”

Women too, as bearers of the divine image, are to be educated

They also are formed in the image of God, and share in His grace and in the kingdom of the world to come. They are endowed with equal sharpness of mind and capacity for knowledge (often with more than the opposite sex), and they are able to attain the highest positions…

— The Great Didactic IX, trans. Keatinge, p. 68. Against the near-universal assumption of his age that Latin learning was for boys, Comenius argues the girl’s title to education from the same premise as the boy’s — she too bears God’s image — a striking early statement of what “all men” in the title actually meant.

Education recalls fallen man toward his “former estate”

[S]o to us, who are trees rooted out of God’s Paradise, the roots are left, and these can germinate afresh when the rain and the sun of God’s grace are shed upon them… Did He not send His Son to restore us to our former estate?

— The Great Didactic V (“The Seeds of These Three… Are Naturally Implanted in Us”), trans. Keatinge, p. 50; the chapter opens by defining “nature” not as post-Fall corruption but as “our first and original condition, to which, as to a starting-point, we must be recalled,” quoting Vives — “What else is a Christian but a man restored to his own nature…?” (p. 40). This is the same theological end that Milton would name six years later — learning as the repair of the ruin the Fall worked in the image of God — here made the ground of a whole pedagogy.

Notes

  • Comenius (Jan Amos Komenský) was a bishop of the Unity of the Brethren (Bohemian/Moravian), exiled by the Thirty Years’ War; he wrote the Didactica Magna first in Czech (c. 1628–1632) and issued the Latin version in his collected Opera Didactica Omnia (Amsterdam, 1657). He is anchored here at c. 1638, the decade of the Great Didactic, between the Puritan founding of Harvard (1636) and Milton’s Of Education (1644).
  • He shares the decade — and the theme of learning as the repair of the divine image — with Milton, and he pushes the older patristic ordering of study-to-piety (Basil, Augustine) toward a new answer to who education is for: not the free-born few of the ancient liberal/servile division but everyone made in God’s image. His knowledge-ordered-to-virtue-and-piety is the boldest institutional form of the knowledge-for-virtue thesis this wiki traces.
  • Quotations are from M. W. Keatinge’s standard English translation (The Great Didactic of John Amos Comenius, London: A. & C. Black, 1896; the scan linked here is the Cornell copy of the reissue). The online full text is OCR; the wording above has been checked against the page images.
