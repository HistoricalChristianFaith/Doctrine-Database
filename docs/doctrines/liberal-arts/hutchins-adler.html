Robert Hutchins & Mortimer Adler and the Great Books movement

Dates: c. 1936–1982 — Robert Maynard Hutchins (1899–1977), president and then chancellor of the University of Chicago from 1929 to 1951, and Mortimer J. Adler (1902–2001), whom Hutchins brought to Chicago in 1930 to co-teach its two-year great-books seminar. The block is anchored at Hutchins’s The Higher Learning in America (1936) and runs to Adler’s The Paideia Proposal (1982), with the fifty-four-volume Great Books of the Western World (1952) between them. · Hutchins (Wikipedia) · Adler (Wikipedia)
Summary: The twentieth century’s major secular recovery of liberal learning. Against a university surrendered to vocationalism and “the love of money,” Hutchins and Adler pressed a common core of “permanent studies” — the great books of the Western “Great Conversation” — as the education proper to free citizens; and Adler carried the same conviction into the schools with one required liberal curriculum for all, against sorting some children into job-training. Their answer to this timeline’s core question — why not just train for a paying job? — is philosophical rather than theological, but presses the same point the fathers did: an education aimed only at earning has ceased to be the education of free people.

Claims & evidence

Hutchins: the modern university has lost its soul to “the love of money” and vocationalism

Hutchins opened The Higher Learning in America by naming the first cause of the university’s confusion, and it was not high-minded:

If then the problem is to clarify the higher learning, let us examine the causes of its confusion. The first of them is very vulgar; it is the love of money. It is sad but true that when an institution determines to do something in order to get money it must lose its soul, and frequently does not get the money.

— The Higher Learning in America, ch. I.1 The money-chasing university reshapes itself to whatever the public will pay for — the “service-station” university that “must help the farmers look after their cows” and “help adults get better jobs by giving them courses in the afternoon and evening,” a drift that “led by easy stages to vocationalism.”2 The end of the road is a paradox: a university that no longer believes the mind can reach truth at all — “that strangest of modern phenomena, an anti-intellectual university.”3

Hutchins: a common core of “permanent studies” — the great books — against the elective chaos

His remedy was a curriculum with a spine. Because education aims at what is common to all human minds, Hutchins argued, its core cannot be a cafeteria of electives tailored to each student’s trade:

Education implies teaching. Teaching implies knowledge. Knowledge is truth. The truth is everywhere the same. Hence education should be everywhere the same.

— The Higher Learning in America, ch. III (“General Education”).4 That common core he called the permanent studies:

We propose the permanent studies because these studies draw out the elements of our common human nature, because they connect man with man, because they connect us with the best that man has thought, because they are basic to any further study and to any understanding of the world. What are the permanent studies?

— The Higher Learning in America, ch. III.5 Chief among them are the great books: “A classic is a book that is contemporary in every age,” and so “How can we call a man educated who has never read any of the great books in the western world?”6 The disorder of the modern university, he held, is that it has no ordering principle to unify its knowledge; the discipline that once did that work (metaphysics for the Greeks, theology for the Middle Ages) must be recovered, for “without theology or metaphysics a unified university cannot exist.”7

“The Great Conversation”: liberal education as the education of free men — and of everyone

In 1952 Hutchins and Adler carried the program out of the University of Chicago into the fifty-four-volume Great Books of the Western World, and Hutchins wrote its opening volume, The Great Conversation. Its governing image gives the whole project its name:

The tradition of the West is embodied in the Great Conversation that began in the dawn of history and that continues to the present day. Whatever the merits of other civilizations in other respects, no civilization is like that of the West in this respect.

— The Great Conversation (1952).8 Liberal education is the education proper to free people because it treats the human being as an end, not a tool — the direct heir of the ancient free/servile distinction this timeline opened with:

The aim of liberal education is human excellence, both private and public (for man is a political animal). Its object is the excellence of man as man and man as citizen. It regards man as an end, not as a means; and it regards the ends of life, and not the means to it. For this reason it is the education of free men. Other types of education or training treat men as means to some other end, or are at best concerned with the means of life, with earning a living, and not with its ends.

— The Great Conversation (1952).9 But Hutchins broke with the ancient assumption that such education belonged to a leisured few: its second novelty, he wrote, was “the proposition that democracy requires liberal education for all” — not merely the trades for the many and the liberal arts for the few.10

Adler’s Paideia Proposal: one required course of study for all, against tracking

Thirty years later Adler pressed the same conviction down into the schools. The Paideia Proposal (1982) called for a single required liberal curriculum for every child, with no electives and no vocational track — because to sort some children into job-training and reserve real learning for others is to betray a democracy. Its anthem is a line Adler took from Hutchins: “the best education for the best is the best education for all.”11 The premise is that no child is beyond such an education:

There are no unteachable children. There are only schools and teachers and parents who fail to teach them.

— The Paideia Proposal (1982).12 And the conclusion is the timeline’s question put in civic terms — that narrow job-training is unworthy of a free citizenry: “Vocational training, training for particular jobs, is not the education of free men and women,” for “we are politically a classless society… We should, therefore, be an educationally classless society.”13

Notes

  • The secular Great Books thread, distinct from the classical-Christian revival. This is the modern recovery of liberal learning in its secular form — the counterpart to, and largely a generation earlier than, the classical-Christian-school revival (Douglas Wilson, the Association of Classical Christian Schools) on the summary timeline. Its institutional homes are the University of Chicago, the Great Books of the Western World, and, on the same model, St. John’s College (Annapolis, later also Santa Fe), which adopted its all-required Great Books “New Program” in 1937. The lineage runs back further still: the great-books seminar Hutchins and Adler taught at Chicago descends from the “General Honors” course John Erskine began at Columbia in 1921, where Adler had studied and taught it before Hutchins recruited him to Chicago.
  • Whose line is “the best education for the best is the best education for all”? It is Hutchins’s — from his The Conflict in Education in a Democratic Society (1953) — which Adler adopted as the watchword of the Paideia Proposal (it appears on its p. 6). It is quoted here as Adler quotes it, not as his own coinage.
  • Where they stand in this timeline’s debate. Hutchins and Adler are the modern secular voice of the same case Newman made for knowledge as its own end and Pieper made against “the world of total work”: that a liberal education is degraded when bent wholly to earning. They bear on the arguments liberal vs. servile arts and knowledge as its own end. Their distinctive contribution is the democratic turn — that the education once reserved for free citizens is owed to all citizens — which answers the ancient objection (education for the free man rests on someone else’s servile toil) not by lowering the ideal but by extending it.
