Robert Hutchins & Mortimer Adler and the Great Books movement

Dates: c. 1936–1982 — Robert Maynard Hutchins (1899–1977), president and then chancellor of the University of Chicago from 1929 to 1951, and Mortimer J. Adler (1902–2001), whom Hutchins brought to Chicago in 1930 to co-teach its two-year great-books seminar. The block is anchored at Hutchins’s The Higher Learning in America (1936) and runs to Adler’s The Paideia Proposal (1982), with the fifty-four-volume Great Books of the Western World (1952) between them. · Hutchins (Wikipedia) · Adler (Wikipedia)
Summary: A twentieth-century secular program of liberal learning. Hutchins faults the university for vocationalism and “the love of money” and proposes a common core of “permanent studies” — the great books of the Western “Great Conversation” — as the education proper to free citizens; Adler proposes one required liberal curriculum for all schoolchildren, with no vocational track. Their answer to this timeline’s question — why not just train for a paying job? — is argued on philosophical and civic rather than theological grounds: in their account an education aimed only at earning is not the education of free people.

Claims & evidence

Hutchins: the modern university has lost its soul to “the love of money” and vocationalism

Hutchins opened The Higher Learning in America by naming the first cause of the university’s confusion:

If then the problem is to clarify the higher learning, let us examine the causes of its confusion. The first of them is very vulgar; it is the love of money. It is sad but true that when an institution determines to do something in order to get money it must lose its soul, and frequently does not get the money.

— The Higher Learning in America, ch. I.1 In his account the university that seeks money reshapes itself to whatever the public will pay for — the “service-station” university that “must help the farmers look after their cows” and “help adults get better jobs by giving them courses in the afternoon and evening,” a drift that “led by easy stages to vocationalism.”2 The end point he describes is a university that no longer believes the mind can reach truth at all — “that strangest of modern phenomena, an anti-intellectual university.”3

Hutchins: a common core of “permanent studies” — the great books — against the elective chaos

His proposal was a common required curriculum. Because education aims at what is common to all human minds, Hutchins argued, its core cannot be a cafeteria of electives tailored to each student’s trade:

Education implies teaching. Teaching implies knowledge. Knowledge is truth. The truth is everywhere the same. Hence education should be everywhere the same.

— The Higher Learning in America, ch. III (“General Education”).4 That common core he called the permanent studies:

We propose the permanent studies because these studies draw out the elements of our common human nature, because they connect man with man, because they connect us with the best that man has thought, because they are basic to any further study and to any understanding of the world. What are the permanent studies?

— The Higher Learning in America, ch. III.5 Chief among them are the great books: “A classic is a book that is contemporary in every age,” and so “How can we call a man educated who has never read any of the great books in the western world?”6 The disorder of the modern university, he held, is that it has no ordering principle to unify its knowledge; the discipline that once did that work (metaphysics for the Greeks, theology for the Middle Ages) must be recovered, for “without theology or metaphysics a unified university cannot exist.”7

“The Great Conversation”: liberal education as the education of free men — and of everyone

In 1952 Hutchins and Adler carried the program out of the University of Chicago into the fifty-four-volume Great Books of the Western World, and Hutchins wrote its opening volume, The Great Conversation. Its governing image gives the whole project its name:

The tradition of the West is embodied in the Great Conversation that began in the dawn of history and that continues to the present day. Whatever the merits of other civilizations in other respects, no civilization is like that of the West in this respect.

— The Great Conversation (1952).8 Hutchins defines liberal education as the education proper to free people because it treats the human being as an end, not a tool, using the ancient distinction between free and servile education:

The aim of liberal education is human excellence, both private and public (for man is a political animal). Its object is the excellence of man as man and man as citizen. It regards man as an end, not as a means; and it regards the ends of life, and not the means to it. For this reason it is the education of free men. Other types of education or training treat men as means to some other end, or are at best concerned with the means of life, with earning a living, and not with its ends.

— The Great Conversation (1952).9 Hutchins departed from the ancient restriction of such education to a leisured few: its second novelty, he wrote, was “the proposition that democracy requires liberal education for all” — not merely the trades for the many and the liberal arts for the few.10

Adler’s Paideia Proposal: one required course of study for all, against tracking

Thirty years later Adler applied the same conviction to the schools. The Paideia Proposal (1982) called for a single required liberal curriculum for every child, with no electives and no vocational track, holding that to sort some children into job-training and reserve liberal learning for others is incompatible with a democracy. Its watchword is a line Adler took from Hutchins: “the best education for the best is the best education for all.”11 Adler holds that no child is beyond such an education:

There are no unteachable children. There are only schools and teachers and parents who fail to teach them.

— The Paideia Proposal (1982).12 Adler puts the timeline’s question in civic terms: “Vocational training, training for particular jobs, is not the education of free men and women,” for “we are politically a classless society… We should, therefore, be an educationally classless society.”13

Notes

  • The secular Great Books program and the classical-Christian schools. This is a modern program of liberal learning in a secular form, largely a generation earlier than the classical-Christian-school movement (Douglas Wilson, the Association of Classical Christian Schools) on the summary timeline. Its institutional homes are the University of Chicago, the Great Books of the Western World, and, on the same model, St. John’s College (Annapolis, later also Santa Fe), which adopted its all-required Great Books “New Program” in 1937. The great-books seminar Hutchins and Adler taught at Chicago descends from the “General Honors” course John Erskine began at Columbia in 1921, where Adler had studied and taught it before Hutchins recruited him to Chicago.
  • Whose line is “the best education for the best is the best education for all”? It is Hutchins’s — from his The Conflict in Education in a Democratic Society (1953) — which Adler adopted as the watchword of the Paideia Proposal (it appears on its p. 6). It is quoted here as Adler quotes it, not as his own coinage.
  • Placement on the timeline. Hutchins and Adler argue on secular grounds that a liberal education should not be bent wholly to earning, a position also taken on this timeline, on other grounds, by Newman (knowledge as its own end) and Pieper (against “the world of total work”). What they add is the claim that the education once reserved for free citizens is owed to all citizens.
