Isidore of Seville — the seven arts at the head of the medieval encyclopedia

Dates: c. 620 (the Etymologiae Books I–III on the seven arts; the encyclopedia compiled c. 615–630 and left unfinished at his death; Isidore c. 560–636, archbishop of Seville) · Wikipedia
Summary: Isidore’s Etymologiae, an encyclopedia compiled in Visigothic Spain, begins in its first books with the seven liberal arts: Book I on grammar, Book II on rhetoric and dialectic, Book III on the four mathematical disciplines. He enumerates the seven as a set and defines what a “discipline” and an “art” are. Earlier entries on this timeline treat the same set in Martianus Capella, Boethius (who names the quadrivium), and Cassiodorus (who sets the arts in a monastic program).

Claims & evidence

The seven liberal disciplines enumerated

There are seven disciplines of the liberal arts. The first is grammar, that is, skill in speaking. The second is rhetoric, which, on account of the brilliance and fluency of its eloquence, is considered most necessary in public proceedings. The third is dialectic, otherwise known as logic, which separates the true from the false by very subtle argumentation. The fourth is arithmetic, which contains the principles and classifications of numbers. The fifth is music, which consists of poems and songs. The sixth is geometry, which encompasses the measures and dimensions of the earth. The seventh is astronomy, which covers the law of the stars.

— Etymologiae I.ii, trans. Barney, Lewis, Beach & Berghof.1 The list is the Varronian seven — the three arts of language and the four of quantity — each given a short definition. (Isidore’s own order runs grammar, rhetoric, dialectic, then arithmetic, music, geometry, astronomy, placing music before geometry; the trivium/quadrivium sequence is a later regularization, not his wording.)

What a “discipline” and an “art” are

A discipline (disciplina) takes its name from ‘learning’ (discere), whence it can also be called ‘knowledge’ (scientia)… And an art (ars) is so called because it consists of strict (artus) precepts and rules. Others say this word is derived by the Greeks from the word [aretē], that is, ‘virtue,’ as they termed knowledge.

— Etymologiae I.i, trans. Barney et al.2 Isidore’s etymological method — explain a thing by the roots of its name — here yields his working definitions: a discipline is what is learned and known with certainty; an art binds its practitioner by “strict precepts and rules,” with a nod to the Greek link between art and virtue. Following Plato and Aristotle, he adds that an art treats of matters that can turn out variously while a discipline concerns what has one outcome only

Notes

  • Manuscript circulation. The Etymologiae is reported to survive in roughly a thousand manuscripts (⚠ unverified — not yet sourced). Its account of the seven arts draws on earlier material also found on this timeline — Varro’s scheme, Martianus’s seven, Boethius’s and Cassiodorus’s handbooks.
  • The labels trivium and quadrivium. The grouping of the first three arts as the trivium and the four mathematical arts as the quadrivium is drawn by his modern editors (following Varro and the later schools); Isidore’s own Book I.ii simply lists the seven. The paired words are not in his text.3
  • Other timelines. Isidore appears also as a witness on the Old Testament canon, the Septuagint’s origin, and the pillars of Seth.
