Isidore of Seville — the seven arts at the head of the medieval encyclopedia

Dates: c. 620 (the Etymologiae Books I–III on the seven arts; the encyclopedia compiled c. 615–630 and left unfinished at his death; Isidore c. 560–636, archbishop of Seville) · Wikipedia
Summary: Isidore closes the late-antique transmission of the arts and opens their medieval career. His Etymologiae — the most-copied reference work of the early Middle Ages — begins, in its very first books, with the seven liberal arts: Book I on grammar, Book II on rhetoric and dialectic, Book III on the four mathematical disciplines. He enumerates the seven as a fixed set and defines what a “discipline” and an “art” even are, so that every later schoolboy who reached for Isidore found the curriculum already laid out. Where Martianus Capella canonized the number, Boethius named the quadrivium, and Cassiodorus housed the arts in the monastery, Isidore fixed them in the one book the Middle Ages could not do without.

Claims & evidence

The seven liberal disciplines enumerated

There are seven disciplines of the liberal arts. The first is grammar, that is, skill in speaking. The second is rhetoric, which, on account of the brilliance and fluency of its eloquence, is considered most necessary in public proceedings. The third is dialectic, otherwise known as logic, which separates the true from the false by very subtle argumentation. The fourth is arithmetic, which contains the principles and classifications of numbers. The fifth is music, which consists of poems and songs. The sixth is geometry, which encompasses the measures and dimensions of the earth. The seventh is astronomy, which covers the law of the stars.

— Etymologiae I.ii, trans. Barney, Lewis, Beach & Berghof.1 The list is the Varronian seven — the three arts of language and the four of quantity — now given a terse, memorable definition apiece. (Isidore’s own order runs grammar, rhetoric, dialectic, then arithmetic, music, geometry, astronomy, placing music before geometry; the tidy trivium/quadrivium sequence is the later tradition’s regularization, not his wording.) It is this compact catalogue, standing at the threshold of the Etymologiae, that carried the definition of “the liberal arts” into a thousand medieval classrooms.

What a “discipline” and an “art” are

A discipline (disciplina) takes its name from ‘learning’ (discere), whence it can also be called ‘knowledge’ (scientia)… And an art (ars) is so called because it consists of strict (artus) precepts and rules. Others say this word is derived by the Greeks from the word [aretē], that is, ‘virtue,’ as they termed knowledge.

— Etymologiae I.i, trans. Barney et al.2 Isidore’s etymological method — explain a thing by the roots of its name — here yields the tradition’s working definitions: a discipline is what is learned and known with certainty; an art binds its practitioner by “strict precepts and rules,” with a nod to the Greek link between art and virtue. Following Plato and Aristotle, he adds that an art treats of matters that can turn out variously while a discipline concerns what has one outcome only — a distinction that lets the seven be graded and taught in order.

Notes

  • The reference book of the Middle Ages. The Etymologiae survives in roughly a thousand manuscripts and was, for centuries, the encyclopedia every library owned; its opening books made Isidore’s account of the seven arts the standard one. He gathers up the whole prior transmission — Varro’s scheme, Martianus’s seven, Boethius’s and Cassiodorus’s handbooks — and fixes it in a form the medieval West would quote for eight hundred years.
  • The labels are the tradition’s, not Isidore’s. The grouping of the first three arts as the trivium and the four mathematical arts as the quadrivium is drawn by his modern editors (following Varro and the later schools); Isidore’s own Book I.ii simply lists the seven. The division is real and ancient, but the paired words are not in his text.3
  • On the wiki. Isidore is one of the wiki’s most-cited seventh-century compilers, appearing as a witness on the Old Testament canon, the Septuagint’s origin, and the pillars of Seth — a measure of how much of what the Middle Ages “knew” came filtered through the Etymologiae.
