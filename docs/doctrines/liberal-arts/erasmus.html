Erasmus of Rotterdam — the “good letters,” ad fontes, and learning made for Christ

Dates: c. 1511 (the relevant works: De ratione studii, “On the Method of Study,” 1511, and De pueris statim ac liberaliter instituendis, “On the Liberal Education of Children,” 1529; also the Ciceronianus, 1528; Erasmus c. 1466–1536, the prince of the Christian humanists) · Wikipedia
Summary: Erasmus made the classical liberal arts — the bonae litterae, “good letters” — the foundation of a Christian education, on the program of ad fontes: back to the undefiled sources, the Scriptures in their original tongues and the best of antiquity. A human being, he held, is not born but fashioned, and it is liberal study that fashions the animal into a man; and the whole end of learning and eloquence is to know and celebrate Christ.

Claims & evidence

A man is not born but fashioned — and it is liberal study that does it

Now it is the possession of Reason which constitutes a Man. If trees or wild beasts grow, men, believe me, are fashioned.

Nature, in giving you a son, presents you, let me say, a rude, unformed creature, which it is your part to fashion so that it may become indeed a man. If this fashioning be neglected you have but an animal still: if it be contrived earnestly and wisely, you have, I had almost said, what may prove a being not far from a God.

— De pueris statim ac liberaliter instituendis (1529), trans. W. H. Woodward.1 The famous Latin behind it — homines… non nascuntur, sed finguntur, “men are not born, but made” — is Erasmus’s charter for education itself: what is distinctively human is not given by birth but formed by the liberal arts, so that to neglect a child’s formation is to leave him a mere animal. This is the Renaissance humanist’s recovery of the ancient ideal of paideia — learning as the making of a person — put to Christian use.

Ad fontes: back to the undefiled sources

For I affirm that with slight qualification the whole of attainable knowledge lies enclosed within the literary monuments of ancient Greece. This great inheritance I will compare to a limpid spring of whose undefiled waters it behoves all who truly thirst to drink and be restored.

— De ratione studii (1511), trans. Woodward.2 The “limpid spring” is the image of Erasmus’s life-program, ad fontes — to the sources. Against a late-medieval learning grown thick with commentary upon commentary, he sends the student back to the pure founts: the classical authors in Greek and Latin, and above all Scripture in its original tongues, the labor that produced his own printed Greek New Testament of 1516. The liberal arts are the means of that return; good letters are the road back to the clean water.

The whole end of learning is to know and celebrate Christ

… of studying philosophy, of studying eloquence, to know Christ, to celebrate the glory of Christ. This is the goal of all learning and all eloquence.

— Ciceronianus (1528), trans. Betty I. Knott.3 Here Erasmus turns the humanist program decisively to its Christian end. He had no patience with the “Ciceronians” who idolized pagan style for its own sake — the very trap Jerome’s dream had warned against — and answers them that eloquence and philosophy are not ends in themselves but instruments “to know Christ, to celebrate the glory of Christ.” The bonae litterae are baptized: classical learning is spoil to be claimed for the Gospel, the same “spoiling the Egyptians” logic Augustine had made the tradition’s warrant.

Notes

  • The bridge from the fathers to the Reformers’ schools. Erasmus gathers up the patristic case for Christian humanism — Clement’s handmaid, Basil’s selective reading of the pagans, Augustine’s Egyptian gold — and hands it, refined by the new philology, to the sixteenth century. His educational writings stand behind the school programs of Luther, Melanchthon, and Calvin, even where they broke with him on doctrine.
  • Erasmus elsewhere on this wiki. He appears as a witness on other timelines; here his subject is the purpose of the liberal arts, on which he is the fountainhead of the Christian-humanist recovery of learning.
  • A verbatim Antibarbari passage on “plunder for Christ” is not quoted here. The standard English translation of the Antibarbari (Collected Works of Erasmus 23) was not freely available to verify against; the Ciceronianus line above carries the “learning-for-Christ” point cleanly and is verifiable.
