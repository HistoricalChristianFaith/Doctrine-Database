Erasmus of Rotterdam — the “good letters,” ad fontes, and learning made for Christ

Dates: c. 1511 (the relevant works: De ratione studii, “On the Method of Study,” 1511, and De pueris statim ac liberaliter instituendis, “On the Liberal Education of Children,” 1529; also the Ciceronianus, 1528; Erasmus c. 1466–1536, Dutch humanist scholar and editor of the Greek New Testament) · Wikipedia
Summary: Erasmus argued for the classical liberal arts — the bonae litterae, “good letters” — as the foundation of a Christian education, on the program of ad fontes: back to the sources, the Scriptures in their original tongues and the best of antiquity. A human being, he held, is not born but fashioned, and it is liberal study that fashions the animal into a man; and the whole end of learning and eloquence is to know and celebrate Christ.

Claims & evidence

Erasmus on education: a man is not born but fashioned

Now it is the possession of Reason which constitutes a Man. If trees or wild beasts grow, men, believe me, are fashioned.

Nature, in giving you a son, presents you, let me say, a rude, unformed creature, which it is your part to fashion so that it may become indeed a man. If this fashioning be neglected you have but an animal still: if it be contrived earnestly and wisely, you have, I had almost said, what may prove a being not far from a God.

— De pueris statim ac liberaliter instituendis (1529), trans. W. H. Woodward.1 The Latin behind it — homines… non nascuntur, sed finguntur, “men are not born, but made” — states Erasmus’s view that what is distinctively human is not given by birth but formed by education, so that to neglect a child’s formation is to leave him an animal still. Compare Quintilian’s formation of the orator, earlier on this timeline.

Ad fontes: back to the sources

For I affirm that with slight qualification the whole of attainable knowledge lies enclosed within the literary monuments of ancient Greece. This great inheritance I will compare to a limpid spring of whose undefiled waters it behoves all who truly thirst to drink and be restored.

— De ratione studii (1511), trans. Woodward.2 The “limpid spring” is Erasmus’s image for his program, ad fontes — to the sources. He sends the student to the classical authors in Greek and Latin, and above all to Scripture in its original tongues, the labor that produced his own printed Greek New Testament of 1516. In his program the liberal arts are the means of that return.

The whole end of learning is to know and celebrate Christ

… of studying philosophy, of studying eloquence, to know Christ, to celebrate the glory of Christ. This is the goal of all learning and all eloquence.

— Ciceronianus (1528), trans. Betty I. Knott.3 In the Ciceronianus Erasmus answers the “Ciceronians,” whom he presents as imitating Cicero’s style for its own sake, that eloquence and philosophy are not ends in themselves but instruments “to know Christ, to celebrate the glory of Christ.” Compare Jerome’s dream and Augustine’s “spoiling the Egyptians,” earlier on this timeline.

Notes

  • Placement on the timeline. Erasmus writes after the patristic texts on Christian use of pagan learning on this timeline — Clement’s handmaid, Basil’s selective reading of the pagans, Augustine’s Egyptian gold — and brings the new philology to the same question. His educational writings are reported to have influenced the school programs of Luther, Melanchthon, and Calvin, who differed from him on doctrine (⚠ unverified — not yet sourced).
  • Erasmus elsewhere on this wiki. He appears as a witness on other timelines; here his subject is the purpose of the liberal arts.
  • A verbatim Antibarbari passage on “plunder for Christ” is not quoted here. The standard English translation of the Antibarbari (Collected Works of Erasmus 23) was not freely available to verify against; the Ciceronianus line above makes the “learning-for-Christ” point.
