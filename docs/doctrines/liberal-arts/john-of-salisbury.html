John of Salisbury — the defense of the trivium against “Cornificius” (the Metalogicon)

Dates: c. 1159 (the Metalogicon, completed 1159 and addressed to Thomas Becket; John of Salisbury c. 1115/1120–1180, secretary to two archbishops of Canterbury, later bishop of Chartres) · Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy
Summary: The fullest twelfth-century Christian defense of the trivium was written to answer a man who called it a waste of time. In his Metalogicon John of Salisbury — the age’s most cultivated humanist — takes up the challenge of a critic he pseudonymously names “Cornificius,” whose sect held that the study of grammar, logic, and eloquence is useless labour that earns no living. John defends the verbal and logical arts as the indispensable tools of thought and eloquence, insists that logic serves wisdom and use together rather than being pursued for its own sake, and leaves along the way the richest surviving report of the schools of Paris and Chartres. His book is the bridge between Hugh of St. Victor’s abbey school and the universities about to rise, and the medieval form of the answer this timeline gives to the utilitarian question.

Claims & evidence

“Cornificius” and the utilitarian attack on the arts

John wrote the Metalogicon expressly to refute a contemporary he satirizes under the name “Cornificius” (after the detractor of Virgil), whose followers held that training in grammar, logic, and eloquence is wasted effort. Their case is the medieval version of the objection this whole timeline turns on — why study what does not pay? John reports it plainly:

In the judgment of Cornificius (if a false opinion may be called a judgment), there is no point in studying the rules of eloquence, which is a gift that is either conceded or denied to each individual by nature. Work and diligence are superfluous where nature has spontaneously and gratuitously bestowed eloquence, whereas they are futile and silly where she has refused to grant it. … Even though rules may be of some help in acquiring eloquence, still they involve more trouble than they are worth, and the return never compensates for the investment.

— Metalogicon I.6, trans. Daniel D. McGarry.1 Behind the objection John sees a simpler motive. The Cornificians have “gravitated to common, worldly occupations,” caring nothing for what philosophy teaches:

They have only one concern: to “Make money, by fair means, if possible, but otherwise in any way at all.” … They deem nothing sordid and inane, save the straits of poverty. Wisdom’s only fruit, for them, is wealth.

— Metalogicon I.4, trans. McGarry.2 This is the ancient contempt for learning-that-does-not-earn — Aristotle’s and Cicero’s scale of gainful callings, now turned against the liberal arts themselves — and John set out to answer it. He tells the reader why the book exists, and why he coined its odd title:

Since, however, the labors of the latter [logicians] were being lampooned as a waste of time, and my opponent was goading me on by his almost daily controversies, finally, indignant and objecting, I took up his challenge and determined to strike down his calumnies even as they issued from his mouth. Hence, I have planned my work to answer his objections. … This treatise … is entitled THE METALOGICON. For, in it, I undertake to defend logic.

— Metalogicon, Prologue, trans. McGarry.3

Wisdom and eloquence: to attack the one is to uproot all

John’s central answer, drawn from Cicero’s De Inventione, is that wisdom and eloquence belong together and neither can safely be dropped. Eloquence without wisdom is dangerous; but wisdom that cannot speak is nearly useless to anyone but its owner:

Just as eloquence, unenlightened by reason, is rash and blind, so wisdom, without the power of expression, is feeble and maimed. Speechless wisdom may sometimes increase one’s personal satisfaction, but it rarely and only slightly contributes to the welfare of human society.

— Metalogicon I.1, trans. McGarry.4 It follows that an attack on the teaching of eloquence is not the small, technical matter it pretends to be. To pull out the arts of language is to bring down everything that stands on them:

One who would eliminate the teaching of eloquence from philosophical studies, begrudges Mercury [Eloquence] his possession of Philology, and wrests from Philology’s arms her beloved Mercury. Although he may seem to attack eloquence alone, he undermines and uproots all liberal studies, assails the whole structure of philosophy, tears to shreds humanity’s social contract, and destroys the means of brotherly charity and reciprocal interchange of services.

— Metalogicon I.1, trans. McGarry.5 His image of “Mercury” (eloquence) wedded to “Philology” (learning) is a deliberate echo of Martianus Capella’s allegory of the seven arts as the bride’s maids — the same figure Hugh of St. Victor had turned to a generation earlier.

Grammar, “the cradle of all philosophy”

Against Cornificius’s scorn for the elementary art of grammar, John makes it the indispensable beginning of every higher study — the mother-art from which the rest of philosophy is nursed:

Grammar is the cradle of all philosophy, and in a manner of speaking, the first nurse of the whole study of letters. It takes all of us as tender babes, newly born from nature’s bosom. It nurses us in our infancy, and guides our every forward step in philosophy. With motherly care, it fosters and protects the philosopher from the start to the finish [of his pursuits].

— Metalogicon I.13, trans. McGarry.6 The point tells directly against the utilitarian: the art that looks most childish and least profitable is the one without which none of the rest can be had at all.

Logic serves wisdom and use — but is “powerless and sterile” if left alone

Yet John is no mere partisan of words, and the Metalogicon is not a defense of logic-chopping for its own sake. He had watched the dialecticians of his own day pursue logic in isolation, and judged the result barren. Logic is a most useful tool — but only when joined to the other studies it is meant to serve:

I was accordingly convinced by experience of something which can easily be inferred [by reason]: that just as dialectic expedites other studies, so, if left alone by itself, it lies powerless and sterile. For if it is to fecundate the soul to bear the fruits of philosophy, logic must conceive from an external source.

— Metalogicon II.10, trans. McGarry.7 This is the balance the whole book keeps: the verbal and logical arts are to be defended against those who despise them as useless, and equally against those who would exalt them into an end in themselves. They are ordered to wisdom and to use together — the same subordination of the arts to a higher end that Clement, Basil, and later Aquinas draw on this timeline.

A window on the twelfth-century schools: Bernard of Chartres and the giants

Because John had studied in France for the better part of a dozen years (c. 1136–1147), the Metalogicon doubles as the richest surviving eyewitness account of the cathedral-school world just before the universities — his teachers at Paris and Chartres named one by one, beginning with Peter Abelard:

When, still but a youth, I first journeyed to Gaul for the sake of study … I betook myself to the Peripatetic of Pallet, who was then teaching at Mont Ste. Genevieve. The latter was a famed and learned master, admired by all. At his feet I learned the elementary principles of this art, drinking in, with consuming avidity, and to the full extent of my limited talents, every word that fell from his lips.

— Metalogicon II.10, trans. McGarry (the “Peripatetic of Pallet” is Abelard).8 His most admiring portrait is of Bernard of Chartres, whose patient way of teaching grammar through the daily reading of the authors he holds up as a model:

Bernard of Chartres, the greatest font of literary learning in Gaul in recent times, used to teach grammar in the following way. He would point out, in reading the authors, what was simple and according to rule. On the other hand, he would explain grammatical figures, rhetorical embellishment, and sophistical quibbling, as well as the relation of given passages to other studies. He would do so, however, without trying to teach everything at one time.

— Metalogicon I.24, trans. McGarry.9 And it is John who preserves, from Bernard, the most famous single image the Middle Ages ever gave of its debt to the ancients — the figure of the moderns as small men made tall by those who went before:

Bernard of Chartres used to compare us to [puny] dwarfs perched on the shoulders of giants. He pointed out that we see more and farther than our predecessors, not because we have keener vision or greater height, but because we are lifted up and borne aloft on their gigantic stature.

— Metalogicon III.4, trans. McGarry.10 The saying is itself a charter for the liberal arts: the whole point of an education steeped in the old authors is that a student who climbs onto their shoulders sees farther than he ever could unaided.

Notes

  • The medieval form of the utilitarian objection. “Cornificius” is John’s pseudonym for a real contemporary (or a type); the name is borrowed from an ancient enemy of Virgil. His sect’s argument — that eloquence cannot be taught and that the arts of language earn nothing — is the twelfth-century version of the “why not just learn a trade that pays?” question this timeline is built around, which is why the Metalogicon answers it so directly.
  • Wisdom and use held together. John defends the trivium on two fronts at once: against those who despise the verbal and logical arts as useless, and against the pure dialecticians who would pursue logic in isolation. His verdict that logic “left alone by itself … lies powerless and sterile” is the same ordering-to-a-higher-end that runs through the fathers and the schoolmen — the arts are tools for wisdom, neither to be scorned nor idolized.
  • The best source we have for the schools of Paris and Chartres. Historians rely on the Metalogicon for the twelfth-century renaissance: it names John’s masters (Abelard, William of Conches, Gilbert of Poitiers, Thierry of Chartres, and others), describes Bernard of Chartres’s teaching, and preserves the “dwarfs on the shoulders of giants” saying — later reused by Newton and now proverbial.
  • Text and translation. Quotations follow the standard English translation by Daniel D. McGarry, The Metalogicon of John of Salisbury: A Twelfth-Century Defense of the Verbal and Logical Arts of the Trivium (University of California Press, 1955), the first complete translation into any modern language; a full-text scan is freely available online. The critical Latin edition is C. C. J. Webb’s (Oxford, 1929).
