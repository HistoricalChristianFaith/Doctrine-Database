John of Salisbury — the defense of the trivium against “Cornificius” (the Metalogicon)

Dates: c. 1159 (the Metalogicon, completed 1159 and addressed to Thomas Becket; John of Salisbury c. 1115/1120–1180, secretary to two archbishops of Canterbury, later bishop of Chartres) · Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy
Summary: John of Salisbury’s Metalogicon is a twelfth-century defense of the trivium written to answer a man who called it a waste of time. In it John takes up the challenge of a critic he pseudonymously names “Cornificius,” whose sect held that the study of grammar, logic, and eloquence is useless labour that earns no living. John defends the verbal and logical arts as the necessary tools of thought and eloquence, argues that logic serves wisdom and use together rather than being pursued for its own sake, and along the way reports on his teachers in the schools of Paris and Chartres. On this timeline he follows Hugh of St. Victor, and his book is a twelfth-century answer to the utilitarian question the timeline traces.

Claims & evidence

“Cornificius” and the utilitarian attack on the arts

John wrote the Metalogicon expressly to answer a contemporary he satirizes under the name “Cornificius” (after the detractor of Virgil), whose followers held that training in grammar, logic, and eloquence is wasted effort. Their case is a twelfth-century form of the objection this timeline traces — why study what does not pay? John reports it:

In the judgment of Cornificius (if a false opinion may be called a judgment), there is no point in studying the rules of eloquence, which is a gift that is either conceded or denied to each individual by nature. Work and diligence are superfluous where nature has spontaneously and gratuitously bestowed eloquence, whereas they are futile and silly where she has refused to grant it. … Even though rules may be of some help in acquiring eloquence, still they involve more trouble than they are worth, and the return never compensates for the investment.

— Metalogicon I.6, trans. Daniel D. McGarry.1 Behind the objection John sees a simpler motive. The Cornificians have “gravitated to common, worldly occupations,” caring nothing for what philosophy teaches:

They have only one concern: to “Make money, by fair means, if possible, but otherwise in any way at all.” … They deem nothing sordid and inane, save the straits of poverty. Wisdom’s only fruit, for them, is wealth.

— Metalogicon I.4, trans. McGarry.2 Where Aristotle and Cicero, earlier on this timeline, ranked gainful callings below the liberal arts, the Cornificians as John reports them measure the liberal arts themselves by gain. He tells the reader why the book exists, and why he coined its odd title:

Since, however, the labors of the latter [logicians] were being lampooned as a waste of time, and my opponent was goading me on by his almost daily controversies, finally, indignant and objecting, I took up his challenge and determined to strike down his calumnies even as they issued from his mouth. Hence, I have planned my work to answer his objections. … This treatise … is entitled THE METALOGICON. For, in it, I undertake to defend logic.

— Metalogicon, Prologue, trans. McGarry.3

Wisdom and eloquence: to attack the one is to uproot all

John’s central answer, drawn from Cicero’s De Inventione, is that wisdom and eloquence belong together. In his account eloquence without wisdom is dangerous, and wisdom that cannot speak profits little beyond its owner:

Just as eloquence, unenlightened by reason, is rash and blind, so wisdom, without the power of expression, is feeble and maimed. Speechless wisdom may sometimes increase one’s personal satisfaction, but it rarely and only slightly contributes to the welfare of human society.

— Metalogicon I.1, trans. McGarry.4 For John, an attack on the teaching of eloquence is therefore not a small, technical matter. To pull out the arts of language is to bring down everything that stands on them:

One who would eliminate the teaching of eloquence from philosophical studies, begrudges Mercury [Eloquence] his possession of Philology, and wrests from Philology’s arms her beloved Mercury. Although he may seem to attack eloquence alone, he undermines and uproots all liberal studies, assails the whole structure of philosophy, tears to shreds humanity’s social contract, and destroys the means of brotherly charity and reciprocal interchange of services.

— Metalogicon I.1, trans. McGarry.5 His image of “Mercury” (eloquence) wedded to “Philology” (learning) is the pairing of Martianus Capella’s allegory, in which the seven arts attend the bride — a figure Hugh of St. Victor had also used a generation earlier.

Grammar, “the cradle of all philosophy”

Against Cornificius’s scorn for the elementary art of grammar, John makes it the beginning of every higher study — the art from which the rest of philosophy is nursed:

Grammar is the cradle of all philosophy, and in a manner of speaking, the first nurse of the whole study of letters. It takes all of us as tender babes, newly born from nature’s bosom. It nurses us in our infancy, and guides our every forward step in philosophy. With motherly care, it fosters and protects the philosopher from the start to the finish [of his pursuits].

— Metalogicon I.13, trans. McGarry.6 In John’s account the most elementary art is the one on which all the rest depend.

Logic serves wisdom and use, and is “powerless and sterile” if left alone

The Metalogicon does not defend logic pursued for its own sake. John had watched the dialecticians of his own day pursue logic in isolation, and judged the result barren. In his account logic is a useful tool when joined to the other studies it is meant to serve:

I was accordingly convinced by experience of something which can easily be inferred [by reason]: that just as dialectic expedites other studies, so, if left alone by itself, it lies powerless and sterile. For if it is to fecundate the soul to bear the fruits of philosophy, logic must conceive from an external source.

— Metalogicon II.10, trans. McGarry.7 John defends the verbal and logical arts against those who despise them as useless, and against those who would make them an end in themselves; he orders them to wisdom and to use together. Clement, Basil, and Aquinas also subordinate the arts to a higher end on this timeline.

A window on the twelfth-century schools: Bernard of Chartres and the giants

Because John had studied in France for the better part of a dozen years (c. 1136–1147), the Metalogicon is also an eyewitness account of the twelfth-century cathedral and city schools — his teachers at Paris and Chartres named one by one, beginning with Peter Abelard:

When, still but a youth, I first journeyed to Gaul for the sake of study … I betook myself to the Peripatetic of Pallet, who was then teaching at Mont Ste. Genevieve. The latter was a famed and learned master, admired by all. At his feet I learned the elementary principles of this art, drinking in, with consuming avidity, and to the full extent of my limited talents, every word that fell from his lips.

— Metalogicon II.10, trans. McGarry (the “Peripatetic of Pallet” is Abelard).8 He gives a portrait of Bernard of Chartres, whose patient way of teaching grammar through the daily reading of the authors he holds up as a model:

Bernard of Chartres, the greatest font of literary learning in Gaul in recent times, used to teach grammar in the following way. He would point out, in reading the authors, what was simple and according to rule. On the other hand, he would explain grammatical figures, rhetorical embellishment, and sophistical quibbling, as well as the relation of given passages to other studies. He would do so, however, without trying to teach everything at one time.

— Metalogicon I.24, trans. McGarry.9 John also records, from Bernard, the figure of the moderns as small men made tall by those who went before:

Bernard of Chartres used to compare us to [puny] dwarfs perched on the shoulders of giants. He pointed out that we see more and farther than our predecessors, not because we have keener vision or greater height, but because we are lifted up and borne aloft on their gigantic stature.

— Metalogicon III.4, trans. McGarry.10 In Bernard’s saying the moderns see farther because they are “lifted up and borne aloft” by their predecessors.

Notes

  • “Cornificius.” “Cornificius” is John’s pseudonym for a real contemporary (or a type); the name is borrowed from an ancient enemy of Virgil. His sect’s argument — that eloquence cannot be taught and that the arts of language earn nothing — is a twelfth-century form of the “why not just learn a trade that pays?” question this timeline traces.
  • Wisdom and use held together. John defends the trivium on two fronts: against those who despise the verbal and logical arts as useless, and against dialecticians who would pursue logic in isolation, which he says “left alone by itself … lies powerless and sterile.”
  • A source for the schools of Paris and Chartres. The Metalogicon names John’s masters (Abelard, William of Conches, Gilbert of Poitiers, Thierry of Chartres, and others), describes Bernard of Chartres’s teaching, and preserves the “dwarfs on the shoulders of giants” saying, later used by Newton.
  • Text and translation. Quotations follow the English translation by Daniel D. McGarry, The Metalogicon of John of Salisbury: A Twelfth-Century Defense of the Verbal and Logical Arts of the Trivium (University of California Press, 1955), the first complete translation into any modern language; a full-text scan is freely available online. The critical Latin edition is C. C. J. Webb’s (Oxford, 1929).
