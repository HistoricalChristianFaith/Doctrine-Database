Thomas Aquinas — why the arts are “liberal,” and the arts as a path to philosophy

Dates: c. 1270 (the relevant texts: the Summa Theologiae Prima Secundae, c. 1271, and the earlier commentary Super Boethium De Trinitate, c. 1258–59; Aquinas c. 1225–1274, a Dominican friar and the master theologian of the medieval university) · Wikipedia
Summary: Aquinas gives the free/servile division its sharpest philosophical rationale and, at the same time, puts the liberal arts firmly in their place. An art is “liberal” when it is ordered to a work of the mind — the free work of knowing — and “servile” when ordered to a work done by the body, which serves bodily need; so the line falls on the end of the work, following Aristotle. But the seven arts are not the summit: they are a propaedeutic, the “paths” that lead the beginner into philosophy, which in turn serves the knowledge of God — the institutional shape the medieval university then gave to “the arts first, then the higher faculties.”

Claims & evidence

Why some arts are called “liberal” and others “servile”

Even in speculative matters there is something by way of work: e.g. the making of a syllogism or of a fitting speech, or the work of counting or measuring. Hence whatever habits are ordained to such like works of the speculative reason, are, by a kind of comparison, called arts indeed, but “liberal” arts, in order to distinguish them from those arts that are ordained to works done by the body, which arts are, in a fashion, servile, inasmuch as the body is in servile subjection to the soul, and man, as regards his soul, is free [liber].

— Summa Theologiae I-II, q.57, a.3, reply to objection 3, trans. Fathers of the English Dominican Province.1 Here is the tradition’s free/servile split given its cleanest reason. The word liberal is heard in its root sense — liber, “free” — and the freedom in question is the mind’s: an art is liberal when its work stays within reason itself (a proof, a speech, a calculation), servile when its work passes out into the body and its needs, “inasmuch as the body is in servile subjection to the soul.” This is Aristotle’s hinge — the line falls on the end, not the subject — made precise by a Christian anthropology of soul over body. (Aquinas at once adds a caution against reading the honorific too grandly: it does not follow “if the liberal arts be more excellent” that they are more properly arts — the sciences pursued for pure knowing, ordered to no work at all, are strictly speaking not “arts” but “sciences simply.”)

Art is a virtue of the practical intellect — “right reason about things to be made”

Art is nothing else but “the right reason about certain works to be made.” And yet the good of these things depends, not on man’s appetitive faculty being affected in this or that way, but on the goodness of the work done. For a craftsman, as such, is commendable, not for the will with which he does a work, but for the quality of the work. Art, therefore, properly speaking, is an operative habit.

— Summa Theologiae I-II, q.57, a.3, trans. Fathers of the English Dominican Province.2 Aquinas defines art (Latin ars, the Greek technē) as “right reason about things to be made” — a habit of the practical intellect, ordered to making (factio) rather than to acting well (actio, the province of prudence) or to pure knowing (the speculative virtues of wisdom, science, and understanding). It is a real intellectual virtue, but a modest one: it perfects the craftsman’s work, not his will, so “one may make bad use of art” — a good artist may be a bad man. This is the metaphysical backing for the whole liberal/servile picture: the mechanical arts are ordered to a bodily product for use, and even the liberal arts, where they involve a “work” of reason, are means, not the mind’s final rest.

The seven arts as “paths” introducing the mind to philosophy

… as Hugh of St. Victor says, seven arts are grouped together (leaving out certain other ones), because those who wanted to learn philosophy were first instructed in them. And the reason why they are divided into the trivium and quadrivium is that “they are as it were paths (viae) introducing the mind to the secrets of philosophy.”

— Super Boethium De Trinitate, q.5, a.1, reply to objection 3, trans. Armand Maurer.3 This is Aquinas’s decisive placement of the seven arts: they are propaedeutic — not the whole of learning, nor its summit, but the road up to it. Answering an objection that philosophy cannot be divided into the seven liberal arts (since they leave out natural and divine science), he grants the point and explains why only seven were gathered and set in order: they are the training a student passes through “first,” the trivium (logic foremost) teaching the method common to every science, the quadrivium (mathematics) coming next because “the young can know mathematics but not physics, which requires experience.” The arts are viae, ways in. Beyond them lie philosophy proper, and beyond philosophy the sacred doctrine it serves — the same subordination Clement drew with his “handmaid” and Basil with his shadows-before-the-sun. Aquinas, quoting Hugh of St. Victor by name, hands the whole scheme to the university he taught in.

Notes

  • A key exhibit for two arguments on this timeline. Aquinas’s liberal-knowing / servile-making distinction is the pivot of the liberal-versus-servile utility question (is study for the free work of the mind higher than training for a paying trade — or is that division a snobbery the gospel overturns?) and equally of the dignity of the mechanical arts question. On the first, he supplies the strongest classical-Christian statement of the hierarchy; on the second, note that he does not despise the mechanical arts — they are genuine intellectual virtues, “right reason about things to be made” — he only ranks them below the arts of the free mind. That is the opposite pole from Hugh of St. Victor, whom he cites approvingly on the arts as “paths” yet who had gone further in dignifying the trades themselves.
  • The arts are a means, not the end. Where the newly-converted Augustine of On Order had made the seven arts a ladder climbing all the way to God, Aquinas keeps them firmly propaedeutic: a preparation for philosophy, which serves theology — sacra doctrina, the “queen” of the sciences. The liberal arts are honoured, but never idolized; the caution is Seneca’s (“the liberal arts do not conduct the soul all the way to virtue”) worked into a Christian order of learning.
  • The institutional embodiment. Aquinas taught at the University of Paris, whose Faculty of Arts was the gateway all students passed through before the higher faculties of theology, law, and medicine. His account of the arts as viae into philosophy is the intellectual charter of that structure — “the arts first, then the profession” — which the summary timeline notes as the rise of the universities.
