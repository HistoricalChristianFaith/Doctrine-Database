Thomas Aquinas — why the arts are “liberal,” and the arts as a path to philosophy

Dates: c. 1270 (the relevant texts: the Summa Theologiae Prima Secundae, c. 1271, and the earlier commentary Super Boethium De Trinitate, c. 1258–59; Aquinas c. 1225–1274, a Dominican friar who taught theology at the University of Paris) · Wikipedia
Summary: Aquinas gives a philosophical rationale for the free/servile division of the arts. In his account an art is “liberal” when it is ordered to a work of the speculative reason and “servile” when ordered to a work done by the body; as in Aristotle, the line falls on the end of the work. He also treats the seven arts as a propaedeutic: quoting Hugh of St. Victor, he calls them “paths” that introduce the mind to philosophy.

Claims & evidence

Why some arts are called “liberal” and others “servile”

Even in speculative matters there is something by way of work: e.g. the making of a syllogism or of a fitting speech, or the work of counting or measuring. Hence whatever habits are ordained to such like works of the speculative reason, are, by a kind of comparison, called arts indeed, but “liberal” arts, in order to distinguish them from those arts that are ordained to works done by the body, which arts are, in a fashion, servile, inasmuch as the body is in servile subjection to the soul, and man, as regards his soul, is free [liber].

— Summa Theologiae I-II, q.57, a.3, reply to objection 3, trans. Fathers of the English Dominican Province.1 Aquinas reads the word liberal in its root sense — liber, “free” — and places the freedom in the soul: an art is liberal when its work stays within reason itself (a syllogism, a speech, counting or measuring), servile when it is ordered to works done by the body, “inasmuch as the body is in servile subjection to the soul.” As in Aristotle, the line falls on the end of the work, not its subject; Aquinas grounds it in the relation of soul and body. (In the same reply he adds that it does not follow “if the liberal arts be more excellent” that they are more properly arts — the sciences pursued for knowing alone, ordered to no work, are strictly speaking not “arts” but “sciences simply.”)

Art is a virtue of the practical intellect — “right reason about things to be made”

Art is nothing else but “the right reason about certain works to be made.” And yet the good of these things depends, not on man’s appetitive faculty being affected in this or that way, but on the goodness of the work done. For a craftsman, as such, is commendable, not for the will with which he does a work, but for the quality of the work. Art, therefore, properly speaking, is an operative habit.

— Summa Theologiae I-II, q.57, a.3, trans. Fathers of the English Dominican Province.2 Aquinas defines art (Latin ars, the Greek technē) as “right reason about things to be made” — a habit of the practical intellect, ordered to making (factio) rather than to acting well (actio, the province of prudence) or to pure knowing (the speculative virtues of wisdom, science, and understanding). In his account it is an intellectual virtue that perfects the craftsman’s work, not his will: art “falls short of being a perfect virtue, because it does not make its possessor to use it well.”4 On this account the mechanical arts are ordered to a bodily product for use, and the liberal arts, where they involve a “work” of reason, are likewise ordered to a work.

The seven arts as “paths” introducing the mind to philosophy

… as Hugh of St. Victor says, seven arts are grouped together (leaving out certain other ones), because those who wanted to learn philosophy were first instructed in them. And the reason why they are divided into the trivium and quadrivium is that “they are as it were paths (viae) introducing the quick mind to the secrets of philosophy.”

— Super Boethium De Trinitate, q.5, a.1, reply to objection 3, trans. Armand Maurer.3 Here Aquinas places the seven arts as propaedeutic — the training that precedes philosophy proper. Answering an objection that philosophy cannot be divided into the seven liberal arts (since they leave out natural and divine science), he grants that “the seven liberal arts do not adequately divide theoretical philosophy” and explains why seven were gathered and set in order: they are what those who wanted to learn philosophy were “first instructed in,” the trivium (logic) teaching the method of all the sciences, the quadrivium (mathematics) coming next because, citing Aristotle’s Ethics, “the young can know mathematics but not physics, because it requires experience.” He quotes Hugh of St. Victor by name for the image of the arts as viae. Other entries on this timeline also set the arts beneath a higher study: Clement with his “handmaid” and Basil with his shadows before the sun.

Reception and context

  • Liberal and mechanical arts. Aquinas’s liberal-knowing / servile-making distinction bears on two questions this timeline follows: whether study for the work of the mind ranks above training for a trade, and what standing the mechanical arts have. In the texts above he counts art, including the mechanical arts, as an intellectual virtue, “right reason about certain works to be made,” and ranks the arts ordered to bodily works below those of the free mind. He cites Hugh of St. Victor, whose own entry on this timeline treats the mechanical arts separately.
  • The arts as preparation. In On Order Augustine presents the seven arts as a ladder of ascent to God; Aquinas presents them as a preparation for philosophy, within an order of the sciences headed by sacra doctrina. Seneca had written that “the liberal arts do not conduct the soul all the way to virtue”.
  • The university setting. Aquinas taught at the University of Paris, whose Faculty of Arts preceded the higher faculties of theology, law, and medicine (⚠ unverified — not yet sourced). The summary timeline enters the rise of the universities separately.
