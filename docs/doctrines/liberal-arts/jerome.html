Jerome on the classics — the Ciceronian dream and the captive-woman defense

Dates: c. 384 (Letter 22 to Eustochium, recounting the dream of c. 374; the “captive woman” defense of Letter 70 to Magnus is c. 397; Jerome c. 347–420) · Wikipedia
Summary: Jerome supplies two texts on the Christian and the classics. In Letter 22 he tells the dream in which Christ scourges him before the judgment seat — “Thou art a follower of Cicero and not of Christ” — for loving the pagan authors too well. In Letter 70, answering a charge that he quotes secular literature, he defends Christian use of pagan learning with the “captive woman” of Deuteronomy 21, shaved and pared and taken to wife, his image for secular wisdom cleansed and made “a matron of the true Israel.”

Claims & evidence

The dream: “Thou art a follower of Cicero and not of Christ”

Many years ago, when for the kingdom of heaven’s sake I had cut myself off from home, parents, sister, relations, and—harder still—from the dainty food to which I had been accustomed… I still could not bring myself to forego the library which I had formed for myself at Rome with great care and toil. And so, miserable man that I was, I would fast only that I might afterwards read Cicero. After many nights spent in vigil… I would once more take up Plautus. And when at times I returned to my right mind, and began to read the prophets, their style seemed rude and repellent… Suddenly I was caught up in the spirit and dragged before the judgment seat of the Judge… Asked who and what I was I replied: “I am a Christian.” But He who presided said: “Thou liest, thou art a follower of Cicero and not of Christ. For ‘where thy treasure is, there will thy heart be also.’” Instantly I became dumb, and amid the strokes of the lash—for He had ordered me to be scourged—I was tortured more severely still by the fire of conscience… Accordingly I made oath and called upon His name, saying: “Lord, if ever again I possess worldly books, or if ever again I read such, I have denied Thee.”

— Letter 22 (to Eustochium) 30, trans. W. H. Fremantle.1 The dream came during a grave fever, about 374, as Jerome pursued the ascetic life near Antioch; he set it down a decade later (384) in his great treatise-letter on virginity. Just before it he had put the objection as an antithesis: “How can Horace go with the psalter, Virgil with the gospels, Cicero with the apostle?”2 — a question of the same form as Tertullian’s “Athens and Jerusalem.” In the dream the rebuke falls on a reader to whom the prophets’ style “seemed rude and repellent” beside Cicero and Plautus.

The captive woman of Deuteronomy 21: secular wisdom cleansed and married to the truth

You ask me… why it is that sometimes in my writings I quote examples from secular literature and thus defile the whiteness of the church with the foulness of heathenism… who is there who does not know that both in Moses and in the prophets there are passages cited from Gentile books… The Apostle Paul… in writing to Titus, has used a line of the poet Epimenides… In another epistle Paul quotes a line of Menander… He had read in Deuteronomy the command given by the voice of the Lord that when a captive woman had had her head shaved, her eyebrows and all her hair cut off, and her nails pared, she might then be taken to wife. Is it surprising that I too, admiring the fairness of her form and the grace of her eloquence, desire to make that secular wisdom which is my captive and my handmaid, a matron of the true Israel? Or that shaving off and cutting away all in her that is dead whether this be idolatry, pleasure, error, or lust, I take her to myself clean and pure and beget by her servants for the Lord of Sabaoth?

— Letter 70 (to Magnus, an orator of Rome) 2, trans. W. H. Fremantle.3 Written about 397, the letter argues for the practice the dream rebuked. The image is Deuteronomy 21:10–13: an Israelite who desired a beautiful woman among his captives might marry her only after she had shaved her head, pared her nails, and mourned a month — stripped, that is, of everything of her old life. So the believer may take pagan learning to wife, but only when what is “dead” in it — idolatry, error, lust — has been shorn away. Jerome grounds the practice in the apostolic precedent of Paul, who quotes Epimenides (Titus 1:12), Menander (1 Corinthians 15:33), and Aratus (Acts 17:28). The letter goes on to catalogue classically learned Christian writers in reply to the charge that Christian writers ought to keep clear of pagan letters.

Notes

  • The two texts together. Jerome was trained in classical rhetoric, and his own writing quotes the classical authors. The dream’s oath forswears possessing or reading “worldly books”; in Letter 70, written some thirteen years later, he defends quoting them. The dream frames its rebuke in terms of the heart (“where thy treasure is”); the captive-woman image concerns the use of secular wisdom once what is “dead” in it has been cut away.
  • Related images on this timeline. Jerome’s “captive woman” stands beside Augustine’s “Egyptian gold” and Clement’s “handmaid,” images each writer uses for Christian use of pagan learning; Basil also commends selective reading of the pagan authors. His dream’s antithesis has the same form as Tertullian’s “Athens and Jerusalem.” Both texts bear on the “handmaiden of theology” question on the summary timeline.
  • Other timelines. Jerome appears across many timelines as a witness — on Mary as the New Eve, the divine name, rabbinic corruption of the text, and more.
