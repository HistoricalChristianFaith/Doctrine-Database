Jerome on the classics — the Ciceronian dream and the captive-woman defense

Dates: c. 384 (Letter 22 to Eustochium, recounting the dream of c. 374; the “captive woman” defense of Letter 70 to Magnus is c. 397; Jerome c. 347–420) · Wikipedia
Summary: No father embodies the tension of the Christian and the classics more sharply than Jerome, and he holds both poles at once. In Letter 22 he tells the famous dream in which Christ scourges him before the judgment seat — “Thou art a follower of Cicero and not of Christ” — for loving the pagan authors too well; it became the church’s classic warning against letting the classics rival Scripture in the heart. Yet Jerome’s own Latin is saturated in Cicero and Virgil, and when challenged he mounts one of antiquity’s finest defenses of Christian use of pagan learning: the “captive woman” of Deuteronomy 21, shaved and pared and taken to wife, is his image for secular wisdom cleansed and married to the true Israel. Capture both, and Jerome is not a caricature but the debate itself, lived in one man.

Claims & evidence

The dream: “Thou art a follower of Cicero and not of Christ”

Many years ago, when for the kingdom of heaven’s sake I had cut myself off from home, parents, sister, relations, and—harder still—from the dainty food to which I had been accustomed… I still could not bring myself to forego the library which I had formed for myself at Rome with great care and toil. And so, miserable man that I was, I would fast only that I might afterwards read Cicero. After many nights spent in vigil… I would once more take up Plautus. And when at times I returned to my right mind, and began to read the prophets, their style seemed rude and repellent… Suddenly I was caught up in the spirit and dragged before the judgment seat of the Judge… Asked who and what I was I replied: “I am a Christian.” But He who presided said: “Thou liest, thou art a follower of Cicero and not of Christ. For ‘where thy treasure is, there will thy heart be also.’” Instantly I became dumb, and amid the strokes of the lash—for He had ordered me to be scourged—I was tortured more severely still by the fire of conscience… Accordingly I made oath and called upon His name, saying: “Lord, if ever again I possess worldly books, or if ever again I read such, I have denied Thee.”

— Letter 22 (to Eustochium) 30, trans. W. H. Fremantle.1 The dream came during a grave fever, about 374, as Jerome pursued the ascetic life near Antioch; he set it down a decade later (384) in his great treatise-letter on virginity. Just before it he had put the objection as an antithesis: “What has Horace to do with the psalter, Virgil with the gospels, Cicero with the apostle?”2 — a deliberate echo of Tertullian’s “Athens and Jerusalem.” The Latin tag Ciceronianus es, non Christianus became shorthand for the danger of loving pagan style so much that Scripture’s plainer voice grates on the ear.

The captive woman of Deuteronomy 21: secular wisdom cleansed and married to the truth

You ask me… why it is that sometimes in my writings I quote examples from secular literature and thus defile the whiteness of the church with the foulness of heathenism… who is there who does not know that both in Moses and in the prophets there are passages cited from Gentile books… The Apostle Paul… in writing to Titus, has used a line of the poet Epimenides… In another epistle Paul quotes a line of Menander… He had read in Deuteronomy the command given by the voice of the Lord that when a captive woman had had her head shaved, her eyebrows and all her hair cut off, and her nails pared, she might then be taken to wife. Is it surprising that I too, admiring the fairness of her form and the grace of her eloquence, desire to make that secular wisdom which is my captive and my handmaid, a matron of the true Israel? Or that shaving off and cutting away all in her that is dead whether this be idolatry, pleasure, error, or lust, I take her to myself clean and pure and beget by her servants for the Lord of Sabaoth?

— Letter 70 (to Magnus, an orator of Rome) 2, trans. W. H. Fremantle.3 Written about 397, this is the positive pole — and, unlike the dream, a reasoned argument rather than a scruple of conscience. The image is Deuteronomy 21:10–13: an Israelite who desired a beautiful woman among his captives might marry her only after she had shaved her head, pared her nails, and mourned a month — stripped, that is, of everything of her old life. So the believer may take pagan learning to wife, but only when what is “dead” in it — idolatry, error, lust — has been shorn away. Jerome grounds the practice where Augustine would ground it a little later (in the Egyptian gold): in the apostolic precedent of Paul, who quotes Epimenides (Titus 1:12), Menander (1 Corinthians 15:33), and Aratus (Acts 17:28). The letter goes on to catalogue the classically learned fathers — a roll-call answering the charge that Christian writers ought to keep clear of pagan letters.

Notes

  • Both poles are Jerome — do not read him as only the dream. The scourging vision is quoted so often on its own that Jerome is remembered as an enemy of the classics, when he is in fact their greatest Christian craftsman: his prose, his polemics, and the Vulgate itself are the work of a consummate classical stylist. He never kept the oath of the dream, and in Letter 70 and elsewhere he defends the very reading the dream forswore. The two texts are not a contradiction but the two ends of a settled position: pagan letters are a captive to be cleansed and put to service, never a rival treasure for the heart to rest in. The dream polices the heart (“where thy treasure is”); the captive-woman rule licenses the use.
  • The tension he shares with the timeline. Jerome sits between the anti-classical Tertullian and the confident synthesizers Clement, Basil, and Augustine — and in a sense contains both. His “captive woman” is the twin of Augustine’s “Egyptian gold” and Clement’s “handmaid”: three images for the one thesis that pagan learning may be reclaimed for Christ. His dream is the twin of Tertullian’s “Athens and Jerusalem”: the standing warning that it may also enslave the heart. Both feed the summary’s “handmaiden of theology” argument — Jerome supplying, uniquely, both the objection and the answer.
  • On the wiki. Jerome appears across many timelines as a witness — on Mary as the New Eve, the divine name, rabbinic corruption of the text, and more — the translator whose learning shaped the Latin Bible he sometimes feared he loved too well.
