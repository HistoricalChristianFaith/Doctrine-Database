Boethius and the quadrivium — the fourfold path to wisdom

Dates: c. 500 (De institutione arithmetica, among his earliest works; Anicius Manlius Severinus Boethius, c. 477–524, executed under Theodoric) · Wikipedia
Summary: Rendering the Greek of Nicomachus of Gerasa into Latin, Boethius names the four mathematical arts together as the quadruvium (“quadrivium”) — the “fourfold way” of arithmetic, geometry, music, and astronomy by which the mind is led from the evidence of the senses to the more certain things of the intellect — and sets arithmetic first as the mother of the rest. The handbook belongs to his program to translate Greek learning into Latin. He was also a Christian theological writer, author of the theological tractates.

Claims & evidence

The coining of the “quadrivium” — four roads from the senses to the intellect

Hoc igitur illud quadruvium est, quo his viandum sit, quibus excellentior animus a nobiscum procreatis sensibus ad intellegentiae certiora perducitur.

— De institutione arithmetica I.1, ed. Friedlein (Latin text). Michael Masi renders it: this is “the quadrivium by which we bring a superior mind from the knowledge offered by the senses to the more certain things of the intellect.”1 The word renders Nicomachus’s Greek tessares methodoi, “four methods,” and is credited to Boethius as his coinage (⚠ unverified — not yet sourced). In his account the four mathematical disciplines form a single graded road: because they all treat quantity — number at rest and in motion, magnitude fixed and moving — they lead the mind from the senses to the more certain things of the intellect.

The order of the four — arithmetic the mother of the rest

Arithmetic also precedes spherical and astronomical science insofar as these two remaining studies follow the third [geometry] naturally… geometry understands the doctrine of immoveable things while astronomy comprehends the science of mobile things. In astronomy, the very movement of the stars is celebrated in harmonic intervals. From this it follows that the power of music logically precedes the course of the stars; and there is no doubt that arithmetic precedes astronomy since it is prior to music, which comes before astronomy.

— De institutione arithmetica I.1, trans. Michael Masi.2 Arithmetic “holds the principal place and position of mother to the rest,” because the other three presuppose number: geometry measures, astronomy counts the movements of the spheres, and music is ratios of whole numbers. Boethius presents the sequence as a logical dependency among the four.

Notes

  • Quadrivium and trivium. The matching term “trivium” (grammar, rhetoric, dialectic) is a later medieval formation. The two terms divide the seven arts into three of language and four of quantity; on this timeline the set of seven appears in Martianus Capella, Cassiodorus, and Isidore.
  • A Christian’s classical program. Boethius set out to translate and harmonize the whole of Plato and Aristotle for the Latin West; the arithmetic and (unfinished) music handbooks are the surviving mathematical fruit of that plan. He was himself a Christian — author of the Opuscula sacra, the theological tractates including De Trinitate and Contra Eutychen et Nestorium (against Eutyches and Nestorius).3
  • Other timelines. Boethius appears also as a witness on the problem of evil, for The Consolation of Philosophy (c. 523–524), written in prison. The De arithmetica quoted here is by contrast one of his earliest works (dedicated to his father-in-law Symmachus, c. 500); the timeline anchors him at that early date, before his imprisonment and execution in 524.4
