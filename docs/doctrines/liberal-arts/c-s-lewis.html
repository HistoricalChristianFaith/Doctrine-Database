C. S. Lewis — education as the training of the affections

Dates: c. 1943 (The Abolition of Man, the three Riddell Memorial Lectures delivered at Durham in Feb. 1943 and published that year; the sermon Learning in War-Time was preached at St Mary the Virgin, Oxford, on 22 Oct. 1939. Lewis 1898–1963, Oxford and Cambridge scholar of English literature, Christian apologist) · Wikipedia
Summary: Lewis restated the ancient conviction that education is not the mere transfer of information but the ordering of the whole person: its office is to train the pupil’s emotions into harmony with reason, so that he comes to love and loathe what is truly lovely and loathsome. A schooling that debunks the “just sentiments” makes “men without chests” and then vainly demands of them the virtue it has made impossible. In Learning in War-Time he defended the learned life itself as a legitimate Christian vocation — one of the “natural activities” that, offered to God, become an approach to Him.

Claims & evidence

The aim of education is to train the affections into “ordinate” love

Against the textbook (Lewis calls it “the Green Book”) that taught children to treat every value-judgment as a mere report of the speaker’s feelings, Lewis set the older doctrine, common to Plato, Aristotle, Augustine, and the whole tradition, that right education forms the emotions before the age of reason:

St Augustine defines virtue as ordo amoris, the ordinate condition of the affections in which every object is accorded that kind of degree of love which is appropriate to it. Aristotle says that the aim of education is to make the pupil like and dislike what he ought. When the age for reflective thought comes, the pupil who has been thus trained in ‘ordinate affections’ or ‘just sentiments’ will easily find the first principles in Ethics; but to the corrupt man they will never be visible at all and he can make no progress in that science. Plato before him had said the same. The little human animal will not at first have the right responses. It must be trained to feel pleasure, liking, disgust, and hatred at those things which really are pleasant, likeable, disgusting and hateful.

— The Abolition of Man, ch. 1 (“Men Without Chests”).1 This is the same ordering of study to virtue that Quintilian demanded of the orator and Basil made the criterion for reading the poets — recast for the twentieth-century classroom.

“Men without chests”

The consequence of an education that debunks the trained sentiments is a mutilation Lewis states in his most quoted image. Reason must govern appetite through the “chest” — the stable, habituated sentiments that stand between intellect and desire:

As the king governs by his executive, so Reason in man must rule the mere appetites by means of the ‘spirited element’. The head rules the belly through the chest—the seat, as Alanus tells us, of Magnanimity, of emotions organized by trained habit into stable sentiments… It may even be said that it is by this middle element that man is man: for by his intellect he is mere spirit and by his appetite mere animal.

And all the time—such is the tragi-comedy of our situation—we continue to clamour for those very qualities we are rendering impossible… In a sort of ghastly simplicity we remove the organ and demand the function. We make men without chests and expect of them virtue and enterprise. We laugh at honour and are shocked to find traitors in our midst. We castrate and bid the geldings be fruitful.

— The Abolition of Man, ch. 1.2 An education stripped to information and technique, having abolished the formation of the sentiments, cannot then produce the courage and honour it still expects — the modern edge of the ancient claim that learning is ordered to the making of a good person.

The old education initiated; the new merely conditions

Lewis draws the contrast between the tradition’s handing-on of a shared inheritance and a modern “conditioning” that shapes pupils toward ends they never see and never assent to:

Where the old initiated, the new merely ‘conditions’. The old dealt with its pupils as grown birds deal with young birds when they teach them to fly; the new deals with them more as the poultry-keeper deals with young birds—making them thus or thus for purposes of which the birds know nothing. In a word, the old was a kind of propagation—men transmitting manhood to men; the new is merely propaganda.

— The Abolition of Man, ch. 2 (“The Way”).3 Education, on the older model, is initiation into “the mystery of humanity which over-arched him and them alike” — “old birds teaching young birds to fly” — not the imposition of a pattern the educators have chosen.

The learned life is a lawful calling, offered to God

Six years earlier, preaching to Oxford undergraduates on the eve of war, Lewis answered the doubt of whether intellectual and cultural life is a defensible thing to pursue before God when the world is burning. His ground is that all lawful human activity, offered to God, is thereby sanctified:

“Whether ye eat or drink or whatsoever ye do, do all to the glory of God.” All our merely natural activities will be accepted, if they are offered to God, even the humblest: and all of them, even the noblest, will be sinful if they are not.

— Learning in War-Time.4 The scholar’s work is not a heaven built on earth — that hope Lewis explicitly refuses — but it is a genuine, if modest, road to God:

But if we thought that for some souls, and at some times, the life of learning, humbly offered to God, was, in its own small way, one of the appointed approaches to the Divine reality and the Divine beauty which we hope to enjoy hereafter, we can think so still.

— Learning in War-Time.5 “Human culture,” he adds, “has always had to exist under the shadow of something infinitely more important than itself” — a defence of learning that neither idolizes it (as Seneca warned) nor despises it, but subordinates it to the knowledge of God, exactly as the fathers had.

Notes

  • Lewis and the modern revival. Lewis wrote no program of schooling; his contribution is the philosophical spine of the twentieth-century recovery — that education forms loves, not just minds, and that the learned life is a Christian vocation. He stands beside Dorothy Sayers (his friend and fellow member of the wartime Christian literary world) and Josef Pieper as the thinkers the later classical-Christian-school movement drew on.
  • The Tao. Lewis’s larger argument in The Abolition of Man — that there is a single traditional body of objective value (he calls it the Tao) which all education presupposes and which cannot be “seen through” without abolishing the very idea of a human being — belongs to a fuller treatment of his moral philosophy; here his subject is the formation of the affections as the proper work of teaching.
