Quintilian on the liberal formation of the orator

Dates: c. AD 95 · Wikipedia
Summary: Quintilian (c. AD 35–100), a teacher of rhetoric at Rome, sets out in the Institutio Oratoria a program of liberal education. The future orator must first pass through the whole “circle of learning” — the enkyklios paideia of letters, music, and geometry — before rhetoric proper; and the whole formation aims at a moral, not merely a technical, result: the orator is, in Cato’s phrase, “a good man, skilled in speaking.” In his account learning and virtue are bound together: “no man can be an orator unless he is a good man.”

Claims & evidence

The orator is formed by the whole “circle of learning” (enkyklios paideia)

it is by such studies that the course of education described by the Greeks as ἐγκύκλιος παιδεία or general education will be brought to its full completion.

— Institutio Oratoria I.10.1, trans. H. E. Butler, source. Before the boy is handed to the teacher of rhetoric he must be grounded in the subsidiary arts — literature, music, geometry, and the rest — for these together complete the enkyklios paideia, the rounded general education named by the Greeks. The Greek phrase, a connected circle of studies, is the source of the word “encyclopedia.”

The orator is “a good man, skilled in speaking” — learning ordered to virtue

The orator then, whom I am concerned to form, shall be the orator as defined by Marcus Cato, “a good man, skilled in speaking.”

I do not merely assert that the ideal orator should be a good man, but I affirm that no man can be an orator unless he is a good man.

— Institutio Oratoria XII.1.1–3, trans. H. E. Butler, source. Here Quintilian names the purpose of the liberal formation. In his account the years of study aim not at cleverness or success but at a character: eloquence and virtue are inseparable, and a training that produced a skilled scoundrel would have failed. He subordinates learning to the making of a good person, not to gain.

Notes

  • Quintilian is a pagan witness. His program is a broad liberal grounding completed by the formation of a good speaker; its formula vir bonus dicendi peritus is reported to have become a commonplace among later Christian educators (⚠ unverified — not yet sourced).
  • The enkyklios paideia he names is the “circle of the arts” that Varro had catalogued in Latin; later entries on this timeline (Martianus Capella, Cassiodorus, Isidore) list seven liberal arts.
  • Read with Seneca, his near-contemporary: where Seneca warns that the arts only prepare the soul for virtue, Quintilian holds that the orator’s whole formation must produce a good man — two Roman answers to the question of learning’s moral end.
