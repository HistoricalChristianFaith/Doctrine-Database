Quintilian on the liberal formation of the orator

Dates: c. AD 95 · Wikipedia
Summary: Quintilian (c. AD 35–100), Rome’s foremost teacher of rhetoric, wrote in the Institutio Oratoria the ancient world’s fullest program of a liberal education. The future orator must first pass through the whole “circle of learning” — the enkyklios paideia of letters, music, and geometry — before rhetoric proper; and the whole formation aims at a moral, not merely a technical, result: the orator is, in Cato’s phrase, “a good man, skilled in speaking.” Learning and virtue are bound together, so that no one can be truly eloquent who is not first good.

Claims & evidence

The orator is formed by the whole “circle of learning” (enkyklios paideia)

it is by such studies that the course of education described by the Greeks as ἐγκύκλιος παιδεία or general education will be brought to its full completion.

— Institutio Oratoria I.10.1, trans. H. E. Butler, source. Before the boy is handed to the teacher of rhetoric he must be grounded in the subsidiary arts — literature, music, geometry, and the rest — for these together complete the enkyklios paideia, the rounded general education the Greeks prized. It is this ideal of a connected circle of studies (the phrase behind the word “encyclopedia”) that the medieval trivium and quadrivium would systematize into the seven liberal arts.

The orator is “a good man, skilled in speaking” — learning ordered to virtue

The orator then, whom I am concerned to form, shall be the orator as defined by Marcus Cato, “a good man, skilled in speaking.”

I do not merely assert that the ideal orator should be a good man, but I affirm that no man can be an orator unless he is a good man.

— Institutio Oratoria XII.1.1–3, trans. H. E. Butler, source. Here Quintilian gives the whole liberal formation its purpose. The years of study do not aim at cleverness or success but at a character: eloquence and virtue are inseparable, and a training that produced a skilled scoundrel would have failed. This subordination of learning to the making of a good person — not to gain — is the pagan tradition’s nearest approach to what the Christian schools would make explicit.

Notes

  • Quintilian is a pagan witness, but his program — a broad liberal grounding crowned by the formation of a good speaker — was congenial to Christian educators; his vir bonus dicendi peritus becomes a commonplace, and his defence of a wide preliminary education underwrites the church’s retention of the classical curriculum.
  • His naming of the enkyklios paideia ties the head of this timeline together: it is the same “circle of the arts” that Varro had catalogued in Latin and that Martianus Capella, Cassiodorus, and Isidore would hand to the Middle Ages as the seven liberal arts.
  • Read with Seneca, his near-contemporary: where Seneca warns that the arts only prepare the soul for virtue, Quintilian insists the orator’s whole formation must actually produce a good man — two Roman answers to the same question of learning’s moral end.
