Martin Luther — languages, the liberal arts, and keeping children in school against the profit motive

Dates: c. 1524 (the two education treatises: To the Councilmen of All Cities in Germany That They Establish and Maintain Christian Schools, 1524, and A Sermon on Keeping Children in School, 1530; Luther 1483–1546, the Wittenberg reformer) · Wikipedia
Summary: Luther re-founded schooling on the languages and the liberal arts, arguing that both the church and the civil order need liberally educated people — and, in the 1530 sermon, answered the profit-minded parent who wanted a child pulled out of school and into a paying trade with a bluntness no earlier witness on this timeline matches. Languages are the “sheath” in which the sword of the Gospel is carried; the study of Latin, Greek, Hebrew, and the arts serves both Scripture and government; and to school a child only “to seek a living” is to serve Mammon and despise God’s gifts.

Claims & evidence

Languages are the scabbard in which the sword of the Spirit is carried (1524)

And let us be sure of this: we shall not long preserve the Gospel without the languages. The languages are the sheath in which this sword of the Spirit is contained; they are the casket in which we carry this jewel; they are the vessel in which we hold this wine; they are the larder in which this food is stored; and as the Gospel itself says, they are the baskets in which we bear these loaves and fishes and fragments.

— To the Councilmen of All Cities in Germany That They Establish and Maintain Christian Schools (1524), trans. A. T. W. Steinhaeuser.1 Luther’s argument for the languages is not antiquarian: the Gospel itself, humanly speaking, cannot survive without the tongues in which it was written and expounded. This is the Protestant recasting of the whole tradition’s case for learning — where Clement and Augustine made the arts the handmaid or the plunder that serves Scripture, Luther makes the languages the very container without which Scripture cannot be kept pure or preached with authority. “Languages are absolutely necessary in the Church,” he adds, “just as prophets or expositors are necessary.”

Even the secular city needs educated men most of all — and the “German Bible is enough” objection, answered (1524)

“But,” you say again, “granted that we must have schools, what is the use of teaching Latin, Greek, Hebrew, and the other liberal arts? We can still teach the Bible and God’s Word in German, which is sufficient for our salvation.” … Arts and languages, which are not only not harmful, but a greater ornament, profit, honor and benefit, both for the understanding of Scripture and for the conduct of government, these we despise…

— To the Councilmen (1524), trans. Steinhaeuser.2 Luther puts the utilitarian objection into the mouth of his reader in almost the words a modern parent uses — if the German Bible saves, why bother with dead languages and the liberal arts? — and answers that the arts serve two masters at once, Scripture and the commonwealth. He then presses the civil half of the case to its sharpest point, asking the reader to bracket salvation entirely:

Let us suppose that there were no soul and no heaven or hell and we had to consider only the temporal government after the manner of the world, and let us see whether it does not need good schools and educated persons more sorely even than the spiritual realm.

— To the Councilmen (1524), trans. Steinhaeuser.3 A city’s true strength, he insists, is not its walls or its treasure but its people: “a city’s best and highest welfare, safety and strength consist in its having many able, learned, wise, honorable and wellbred citizens.”4 This is the Reformer’s answer to the ancient contempt for the trades read from the other side: schooling is a public good the magistrate is bound to provide, because the offices of law, government, and the church all require it.

The profit-minded parent, answered head-on (1530)

If God has given you a child who has the ability and the talent for this office, and you do not train him for it, but look only to the belly and to temporal livelihood, then take the list of things mentioned above … and see what a pious prig and small potato you are.

— A Sermon on Keeping Children in School (1530), trans. C. M. Jacobs.5 This sermon is the timeline’s most direct engagement with the question the whole page poses — why not just learn a trade that pays? Luther confronts the parent who eyes only the belly and rebukes the greed of schooling a child merely to earn a living:

It is a shameful despising of God that we do not grant this glorious and divine work to our children, and only stick them into the service of the belly and of avarice, and do not let them study except to seek a living, like hogs, wallowing forever with noses in the filth, and do not train them to so worthy a rank and duty. Certainly we must either be crazy, or without love for our children.

— A Sermon on Keeping Children in School (1530), trans. Jacobs.6

The preaching office provides as well as a trade — and the service of God is worth more than Mammon (1530)

To put it in a word, your son can easily get as good a living in the preaching office as in a trade; unless it be that you are thinking of great wealth and of making your son a great lord in the eyes of the world, such as the bishops and canons are. If that is in your mind, then what I am saying does not concern you.

— A Sermon on Keeping Children in School (1530), trans. Jacobs.7 Luther does not answer the utilitarian by denying that a living matters; he answers that learned service to God provides a living too, and that to reach past it for “great wealth” is to have set one’s sights on Mammon. He puts the scale of values in a single line:

For my part, I would rather earn ten gulden by a work that is a service of God, than a thousand gulden by a work that is not a service of God, but only of my own profit and of Mammon.

— A Sermon on Keeping Children in School (1530), trans. Jacobs.8 The disinterested good of learning is thus not set against earning a living but ranked above the pursuit of wealth for its own sake — the Reformer’s version of the tradition’s ordering of study to a good beyond gain.

Notes

  • The key exhibit for the utility-versus-formation question. Where Aristotle and Cicero demeaned the paid trades from above (the free man does not work for hire) and Hugh of St. Victor dignified them from below, Luther takes up the modern form of the quarrel directly: a parent choosing between schooling and a paying trade for a gifted child. His answer — that to educate only “to seek a living” is to serve Mammon, and that learned service to church and commonwealth is a higher calling that provides a living besides — is the sharpest pre-modern statement of the case for formation over mere utility, and the direct ancestor of the classical-Christian-schooling argument the introduction traces down to the present.
  • Not a rejection of the trades. Luther is not repeating the ancient contempt for manual work; his own doctrine of vocation dignifies every honest calling. His target is the reduction of a child’s education to money-getting — “the service of the belly and of avarice” — when God has given the ability for the higher offices of church and state. The claim is about the end to which learning is ordered, the hinge the whole timeline turns on.
  • The wider Reformation program. Luther’s 1524 call for city-funded Christian schools was carried into an actual curriculum by Melanchthon, the “teacher of Germany,” who rebuilt the schools and universities on the languages and the liberal arts; Calvin did the same at Geneva. Luther appears elsewhere on this wiki as a witness on other timelines; here he is the fountainhead of the Protestant school movement.
