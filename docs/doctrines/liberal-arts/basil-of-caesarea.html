Basil the Great on the right use of pagan Greek literature

Dates: c. 370 (the Address to Young Men on the Right Use of Greek Literature, Ad adolescentes; Basil c. 330–379) · Wikipedia
Summary: Basil wrote the single most influential patristic charter for a Christian liberal education. Addressed to his young nephews, the Ad adolescentes answers the timeline’s question — may a believer study the arts of the free man? — with a confident but disciplined yes: the pagan classics may and should be read as a preparation for the deeper study of Scripture, so long as one reads them selectively, taking the useful and leaving the harmful “as the bee gathers honey from flowers” and as one plucks the rose while avoiding its thorn. Study of the poets, orators, and philosophers trains the soul in virtue — a shadow and rehearsal of the Christian’s true goal, the life eternal. Where Clement had argued the case philosophically from Alexandria, Basil gave it its enduring practical form: a bishop, himself trained at Athens, telling the next generation exactly how to mine the pagan canon without being poisoned by it. His little essay became the standard humanist justification for reading the classics in Christian schools for a millennium and more.

Claims & evidence

Pagan letters are a preparatory training for Scripture — truth seen “in shadows and in mirrors”

Into the life eternal the Holy Scriptures lead us, which teach us through divine words. But so long as our immaturity forbids our understanding their deep thought, we exercise our spiritual perceptions upon profane writings, which are not altogether different, and in which we perceive the truth as it were in shadows and in mirrors. Thus we imitate those who perform the exercises of military practice… Consequently we must be conversant with poets, with historians, with orators, indeed with all men who may further our soul’s salvation. Just as dyers prepare the cloth before they apply the dye… so indeed must we also, if we would preserve indelible the idea of the true virtue, become first initiated in the pagan lore, then at length give special heed to the sacred and divine teachings, even as we first accustom ourselves to the sun’s reflection in the water, and then become able to turn our eyes upon the very sun itself.

— Address to Young Men on the Right Use of Greek Literature II, trans. Frederick Morgan Padelford (Yale Studies in English 15, 1902).1 Basil sets Scripture at the summit and pagan learning below it as a graded ascent: the young mind not yet ready for “the very sun itself” is trained on its “reflection in the water.” Profane letters are a “preliminary” drill — like the dyer who readies the cloth, or the soldier who trains before the battle — ordered to the one great contest, “our soul’s salvation.” He glosses the same point with the image of the fruit tree, whose leaves are not the fruit but shelter and adorn it: “the real fruit of the soul is truth, yet it is not without advantage for it to embrace the pagan wisdom, as also leaves offer shelter to the fruit.”2 Learning is real and worth having, but it is preparatory — never the end. This is the exact ordering Clement drew from Sarah and Hagar and Seneca from the Stoic side; Basil turns it into counsel a schoolboy can follow.

Read selectively — take the useful “as bees gather honey,” and cull the rose without the thorn

For just as bees know how to extract honey from flowers, which to men are agreeable only for their fragrance and color, even so here also those who look for something more than pleasure and enjoyment in such writers may derive profit for their souls. Now, then, altogether after the manner of bees must we use these writings, for the bees do not visit all the flowers without discrimination, nor indeed do they seek to carry away entire those upon which they light, but rather, having taken so much as is adapted to their needs, they let the rest go. So we, if wise, shall take from heathen books whatever befits us and is allied to the truth, and shall pass over the rest. And just as in culling roses we avoid the thorns, from such writings as these we will gather everything useful, and guard against the noxious.

— Address to Young Men IV.3 This is the passage the whole later tradition remembered. Reading the pagans is not surrender to them “as a ship is surrendered to the rudder”4 but active discrimination: the good is to be loved and imitated, the base fled from — “you must flee from them and stop up your ears, as Odysseus is said to have fled past the song of the sirens, for familiarity with evil writings paves the way for evil deeds.”5 The bee and the rose supplied Christian humanism with its governing metaphor for centuries: one may enter the garden of pagan letters and come away enriched rather than stung, provided one gathers with judgment. Basil is not naive about the danger — he will not praise the poets when they slander the gods or celebrate vice — but the answer to the danger is trained selectivity, not flight from the books.

The end of the reading is virtue — a rehearsal of the Christian’s true goal

Since we must needs attain to the life to come through virtue, our attention is to be chiefly fastened upon those many passages from the poets, from the historians, and especially from the philosophers, in which virtue itself is praised. For it is of no small advantage that virtue become a habit with a youth, for the lessons of youth make a deep impression, because the soul is then plastic, and therefore they are likely to be indelible.

— Address to Young Men V.6 Basil supplies the criterion that makes the selection possible: read the classics for what they teach about virtue. Hesiod on the steep hard road to excellence, Homer’s whole poetry as “a praise of virtue,” Prodicus’ choice of Heracles between Virtue and Vice, Pericles and Socrates and Euclid enduring insult without retaliation — these he holds up because they “almost coincide with our teachings,” rehearsing in pagan dress the gospel precepts to turn the other cheek and to bless one’s persecutors.7 The pagan authors sketch “the silhouette of virtue” that Scripture will later fill in with its full colour: “it is incumbent upon us, for the present, to trace, as it were, the silhouette of virtue in the pagan authors.”8 Because the goal of the whole reading is a good and God-loving character, the classics keep their place precisely as they serve that goal — and no further.

Notes

  • The charter of Christian humanism. No patristic text did more to license the reading of the pagan canon in Christian schools. The bee-and-honey image was quoted and re-quoted from the Byzantine schoolmasters to the Italian humanists; Leonardo Bruni translated the Ad adolescentes into Latin around 1400 and it became one of the most widely copied and printed of all patristic works in the Renaissance, the standard answer to whether a Christian could study the classics. Basil’s own authority for the case was unimpeachable: he had studied at Athens alongside Gregory of Nazianzus, and knew the classical curriculum from the inside.
  • Between the two poles of the timeline. Basil holds the accommodating middle. He is warmer toward the classics than the anti-classical pole — Tertullian’s “what has Athens to do with Jerusalem?” and Jerome’s dream of being flogged before the judgment seat for loving Cicero — but his enthusiasm is fenced by the same guard-rail Seneca and Clement set: the arts are a road, not a home; they are to be used, not idolized. Where Clement argued the principle, Basil wrote the manual.
  • A key exhibit for two of this doctrine’s arguments. Basil’s essay is a leading witness for the case that the liberal arts are the “handmaiden of theology” — pagan learning ordered to, and subordinated under, the knowledge of God — and for the case that education is properly for virtue rather than for use or gain. Both threads are weighed in the summary’s “Arguments” section as those pages are written.
  • On the wiki. Basil appears across many timelines as a witness — on astrology (where the same “take the useful, leave the harmful” temper lets him praise astronomy while condemning the horoscope), the Trinity, the Old Testament canon, and more — a mark of how central his judgment was to the church’s reception of both pagan learning and the natural world.
