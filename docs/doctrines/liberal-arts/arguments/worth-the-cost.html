Is a liberal-arts education worth its cost? The modern return-on-investment objection

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — the prudential core is sound (counting the cost is a Christian duty, and a debt-financed degree with poor prospects, pursued by a student with no calling to it, can be an imprudent choice — the more so because a paying trade is itself a dignified vocation, not a fallback); but the strong verdict, that a liberal-arts education is simply “not worth the money,” prices worth in dollars alone and so commits the reduction the whole tradition exists to expose, and even on the financial measure it is contested by the long-run earnings data · the objection is advanced by the contemporary “is college worth it?” debate (Bryan Caplan’s signaling case and the wider student-debt / skills-gap discourse), with a medieval ancestor in the “Cornificius” whom John of Salisbury answered; the Christian replies are drawn from Luther, Newman, and Pieper

The claim

Why the Education System Is a Waste of Time and Money.

— Bryan Caplan, The Case Against Education (Princeton University Press, 2018), subtitle.1 The interpretation weighed here is the specifically modern economic form of the utilitarian objection this whole timeline turns on: not the philosophical claim that knowledge has only instrumental worth (that foil is weighed on the own-end page), but the practical verdict a family now actually faces — that a liberal-arts education, and especially a humanities degree, is not worth its price, so a young person should study something with a measurable return, or learn a paying trade, instead. It carries a genuine Christian charge, because stewardship of money is a duty and ruinous debt is no small thing; and it draws real force from the dignity of the trades, which this wiki rates a worthy vocation in its own right. It is a live and independently advocated position, not a mere label to knock down.

Who advances it

The objection is old in form and sharp in its present statement. Its medieval ancestor is the sect John of Salisbury pseudonymously called “Cornificius,” who held the study of grammar, logic, and eloquence a waste of time because it earns no living — “Wisdom’s only fruit, for them, is wealth.” Its modern academic form is the signaling theory of education, running from the economists Kenneth Arrow and Michael Spence to its most forceful popular statement, George Mason economist Bryan Caplan’s The Case Against Education (2018): most of a degree’s wage premium, Caplan argues, comes not from skill the schooling imparts but from the credential’s signal of intelligence, conscientiousness, and conformity — so much tuition and time buys a positional good in a “runaway credential” race, and society would do better to fund practical and vocational training instead.2 Around it stands the whole popular “is college worth it?” discourse: the burden of student debt, the opportunity cost of years and tuition, the underemployed humanities graduate, and the parallel revival of the skilled trades as a debt-free calling that pays. For a Christian the argument is not bare economics but a question of stewardship — whether financing a costly education of uncertain return is a prudent use of what God has entrusted, or a failure of the counting Christ commends: “For which of you, intending to build a tower, sitteth not down first, and counteth the cost, whether he have sufficient to finish it?”3

The evidence for

Counting the cost is a Christian duty, and the cost is real. The stewardship premise is not a modern intrusion but a Gospel commonplace: the servant is answerable for the talents entrusted to him, and the wise man counts whether he can finish the tower before he builds. Applied to schooling, the premise has teeth. Tuition and student debt have risen far faster than wages; the years and money a degree consumes are gone whether or not it repays them; and a graduate who has borrowed heavily for a credential the market does not reward has, on any honest accounting, made a poor investment of goods he was meant to steward. To sink a family’s means, or a young person’s early working years, into an education whose return is doubtful is not obviously wiser than the alternatives — and prudence about it is a virtue, not a philistinism.

Much of the wage premium may not be the learning at all. Caplan’s signaling case sharpens the objection where it bites hardest. If a large share of a degree’s payoff comes from what the credential signals rather than from skill it imparts — students forget most of what they are taught, and employers reward schooling they never use — then the money buys position in a credential race, not human capital, and the private return conceals a low social one.2 And the near-term numbers are not friendly: a Georgetown analysis of federal earnings data found that ten years out, graduates of liberal-arts colleges show a return on investment about forty percent below the median across all institutions.4 A report sympathetic to the liberal arts concedes the same early gap: in the years just after graduation, humanities and social-science majors earn some five thousand dollars less than peers with professional or pre-professional degrees.5

The trades are a dignified alternative, not a demotion. The objection’s strongest move is to refuse the old contempt for manual work. If honest craft is a genuine Christian vocation — as this wiki holds, rating the dignity of the mechanical arts sound (Bezalel the Spirit-filled craftsman, the carpenter Christ, Paul the tentmaker, the Reformation’s doctrine of vocation) — then choosing a paying trade over a costly liberal-arts degree is not a fall into “belly service” but a legitimate calling that happens also to be prudent. The objection thereby deflates the elitist form of the liberal-arts defense on its own ground: one need not think the tradesman’s life a lesser thing to think a particular student better served by it.

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The prudential core is sound and, stated modestly, hard to gainsay. Stewardship is a real duty; debt and opportunity cost are real; the trades are a real vocation. So the counsel count the cost before you finance this degree, and for this student a skilled trade may be both the wiser and the worthier choice survives every reply below — indeed Scripture and this wiki’s own rating of the mechanical arts positively support it. Where a family is choosing between crushing debt for a credential of doubtful return and a debt-free calling that pays and honors God, the objection is not philistine but faithful.

Where it overreaches. The strong verdict — that a liberal-arts education simply is “not worth the money” — overreaches on three counts.

(1) It prices worth in dollars alone. To ask only after the financial return is to assume the very thing the whole tradition denies: that the return on learning is the sort of thing money measures. Pieper named the assumption the “world of total work,” where “the demands of the working world grow ever more total, grasping ever more completely the whole of human existence,” so that no study may exist that cannot be “incorporated into a five-year plan.”6 The goods a liberal education aims at — wisdom, virtue, the formed judgment of a free citizen, and for the Christian the knowledge and love of God — are goods the ROI ledger has no column for, and an argument that scores worth by that ledger alone begs the question rather than answering it (the intrinsic good of knowing is weighed on its own page, and its ordering to virtue and God on the for-virtue page).

(2) Even the price often vindicates it — over a life, not a decade. Where the objection meets the tradition on the tradition’s weaker ground — the money — the data cut both ways, and the long view favors the arts. The same Georgetown analysis that found liberal-arts colleges forty percent below the median at ten years found them, over a forty-year horizon, about twenty-five percent above it — the third-highest long-term return of any institution type.4 The sympathetic report tells the same story of a slow start and a catch-up: liberal-arts majors earn less early but about two thousand dollars more at peak earning ages.5 This is exactly what Newman predicted when he ordered the useful under the good: “though the useful is not always good, the good is always useful,” and “a cultivated intellect, because it is a good in itself, brings with it a power and a grace to every work and occupation which it undertakes, and enables us to be more useful, and to a greater number.”7 Aim at the formed mind and the useful graduate tends to follow; aim only at the payoff and you may get neither. (Honesty cuts here too: the long-run catch-up leans heavily on a later graduate degree, and liberal-arts majors never quite close the unemployment gap5 — so the earnings reply softens the strong verdict without dissolving the prudential worry for the student who stops at the bachelor’s.)

(3) The reduction it shares is the one Luther condemned. Luther aimed his sharpest words at parents who would school a child “only to the belly and to temporal livelihood”8 — and the ROI objection, in its strong form, makes the belly the measure just as surely, only substituting a lucrative degree or a paying trade for the “living” Luther’s parent had in view. Notably, Luther’s own case is not the mirror-image error: he wants the arts for Scripture, for the commonwealth, and grants that learned service provides a living besides — a both/and that ranks the service of God above the pursuit of wealth without despising a livelihood. The honest reply to the ROI objection is the same both/and, not a counter-reduction that pretends the money never matters.

Assessment

Contested. The argument is strong by the economic and stewardship lights and weak by the tradition’s, with no consensus winner — which is what contested names. Its prudential core survives every objection: counting the cost is a Christian duty, debt and opportunity cost are real, and because the trades are themselves a dignified vocation, preferring one to a costly degree can be both wise and worthy, not a betrayal of the higher things. But the strong verdict — that a liberal-arts education is not worth the money — fails, on two grounds that pull in opposite directions: it mis-measures worth by pricing in dollars goods the ledger cannot hold (wisdom, virtue, the knowledge of God), and it mis-measures even the dollars, since the long-run return on liberal learning is high and the mid-career catch-up is real. What survives is not the slogan but its disciplined remainder: count the cost, and honor the trades — but do not mistake the price for the worth. The objection rightly deflates the elitist defense of the liberal arts; it overreaches when it deflates the liberal arts themselves.
