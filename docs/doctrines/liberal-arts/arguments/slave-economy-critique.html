Does the liberal/servile distinction depend on a slave economy? The genetic critique

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — the historical diagnosis is sound (the ancient contempt for the “banausic” arts really was bound up with a leisured citizen class supported by slaves, artisans, and women), so the critique genuinely dissolves the hierarchy of persons — the scholar ranked above the tradesman; but the destructive conclusion — that the distinction is nothing but slaveholding ideology and loses all ground once slavery ends — overreaches: it turns on a genetic inference (origin ≠ truth) and ignores that the Christian tradition had already relocated the line from persons to the ends of acts (knowing vs. making), so a hierarchy of ends survives the abolition of slavery even where the social contempt cannot; strong by the sociology-of-ideas lights, weak by the logical/metaphysical ones · advanced by John Dewey (with modern histories of education and critics of Aristotle’s Politics)

Related arguments: free vs. servile learning — see also the liberal vs. servile distinction · the dignity of the mechanical arts

The claim

Of the segregations of educational values… that between culture and utility is probably the most fundamental. While the distinction is often thought to be intrinsic and absolute, it is really historical and social. It originated, so far as conscious formulation is concerned, in Greece, and was based upon the fact that the truly human life was lived only by a few who subsisted upon the results of the labor of others.

— John Dewey, Democracy and Education (1916), ch. 19, “Labor and Leisure.”1 The interpretation weighed here is the genetic critique of the whole free/servile hierarchy: that the reason the ancients despised the “banausic” arts and exalted leisured contemplation is that a slave class did the manual labor, freeing the citizen for scholē — so the liberal/servile split is the ideology of a slaveholding aristocracy, and once slavery is gone the contempt for “servile” work loses its ground.

Who advances it

Its sharpest modern statement is Dewey’s in Democracy and Education (chs. 19–20), where he argues that the chain of dualisms the tradition trades in — culture/utility, theory/practice, knowing/doing — is “really historical and social,” a fossil of a society split into a class that labored and a class at leisure. The point is not idiosyncratic to him: it is standard in modern histories of education and in the critical literature on Aristotle’s Politics, which reads the doctrine of “natural slavery” (Book I) and the disdain for the banausic (Book VIII) as two faces of one aristocratic social order. Dewey’s own remedy, it should be said, is not to crown vocational training over contemplation but to abolish the dualism — to make “thought a guide of free practice for all”; the destructive thesis weighed here is a component of that larger program, and it is the component that bears on whether the liberal/servile distinction can survive its origin.

The evidence for

The origin is real, and the ancients say so themselves. Aristotle does not hide the social substructure of his scheme; he builds it in. The slave is defined as “a living possession, and property a number of such instruments,” and some human beings are held to be servile by nature: “from the hour of their birth, some are marked out for subjection, others for rule.”2 On that foundation the educational hierarchy is raised: the citizen is free precisely because the labor of others supplies his needs, leaving him at leisure (scholē) for the studies pursued “for their own sake,” while “all paid employments… absorb and degrade the mind.”3 Dewey draws the connection out and names the human cost: “slaves, artisans, and women are employed in furnishing the means of subsistence in order that others… may live the life of leisurely concern with things intrinsically worth while.”4 The historical thesis is therefore strong on its own terms: the contempt the classical ideal packs for the wage-earner and the trades is not an incidental prejudice but is bolted to a leisure that a slave economy made possible. So far the critique holds, and it holds against the very point where the tradition is weakest.

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The genetic diagnosis is sound as history, and it does real destructive work. The social layer of the ancient distinction — the ranking of leisured citizen over laboring slave, of the scholar over the tradesman as a kind of person — genuinely does depend on the slaveholding order that produced it, and genuinely does lose its ground once that order is gone. This is not a concession wrung from the tradition; the wiki reaches the same place from the other side, rating the dignity of the mechanical arts sound and the strong form of the liberal vs. servile distinction a failure. To that extent Dewey and the Christian critique of the banausic converge: the “hierarchy of persons” falls, and it falls in part for the reason he gives.

What is overstated. Two moves carry the argument from its sound core to its destructive conclusion, and both are weaker than the diagnosis.

(1) Origin does not settle truth — the genetic step. That a belief arose in a particular social order does not show the belief is false; to argue otherwise is the genetic fallacy — discrediting a claim “because of its origin… when such an appeal to origins is irrelevant.”5 The core claim of the distinction — that knowing has a good not exhausted by earning, and that a formation reduced to job-training forfeits something — may have been carried by an unjust social vehicle and still be true. A slave society may also have discovered that leisure can be spent on contemplation; the discovery is not annulled by the injustice of the discoverers. Dewey, at his most careful, concedes the ancient view was internally coherent; what he needs, and the genetic point cannot supply, is that its coherence died with the slave economy.

(2) The distinction had already been relocated from persons to ends. The critique is aimed at a target the Christian tradition had, centuries earlier, moved. For Aquinas the words “liberal” and “servile” no longer sort people into free and slave; they sort acts by their end: the arts “ordained to… works of the speculative reason… are called ‘liberal,’ ” while those “ordained to works done by the body… are, in a fashion, servile, inasmuch as the body is in servile subjection to the soul, and man, as regards his soul, is free.”6 Once the line runs between knowing and making rather than between the freeman and the slave, it no longer requires a slave to stand on; a free craftsman in a slaveless economy still performs an act whose end lies in a bodily work, and a mathematician still performs one whose end stays within the mind. The hierarchy of ends is thus detachable from the hierarchy of persons, and the abolition of slavery removes only the latter. The best proponents make the same detachment from the other direction: Newman refuses the liberal-versus-gainful either/or Dewey attacks — “though the useful is not always good, the good is always useful” — so that an education can be liberal and equip a person to earn.7

Assessment

Contested. The critique wins and loses on different fronts, and which way it reads depends on which discipline holds the floor. By the lights of the sociology and history of ideas it is strong and largely right: the ancient social contempt for the banausic really was the ideology of a slaveholding aristocracy, and the “hierarchy of persons” it underwrote loses its ground once slavery is gone — a verdict the wiki already reaches from the other side. By the lights of logic and metaphysics the destructive conclusion overreaches: a genetic account of a belief is not a refutation of it, and the distinction the tradition actually defends had long since been relocated from persons to the ends of acts, where it survives the abolition of slavery intact. What stands, once the argument is spent, is exactly the settlement the sibling pages reach independently — a hierarchy of ends (knowing is not merely for earning) without a hierarchy of persons (the scholar above the tradesman). Dewey’s overreach — treating the origin as if it demolished the whole — is flagged on his page; his diagnosis of the origin is credited there and here.
