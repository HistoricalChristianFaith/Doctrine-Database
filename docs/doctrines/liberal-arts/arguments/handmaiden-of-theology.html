May Christians take up pagan learning? Philosophy as the handmaid of theology

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: sound — that secular liberal learning is legitimate and useful for the Christian when subordinated to theology — the arts as ancilla theologiae, the “gold of the Egyptians” carried off for the tabernacle, the bee gathering honey — is the patristic mainstream and the church’s settled practice; it rests securely on the principle that all truth is God’s wherever found, even where the particular proof-texts adduced for it (Egyptian gold, the captive woman) are homiletical illustrations rather than exegetical proofs · advanced by Clement, Basil, Augustine, and Jerome; the counter-caution (“Athens and Jerusalem”) is folded here as its limit, not split off as a rival

The claim

If those who are called philosophers, and especially the Platonists, have said aught that is true and in harmony with our faith, we are not only not to shrink from it, but to claim it for our own use from those who have unlawful possession of it. For, as the Egyptians had not only the idols and heavy burdens which the people of Israel hated and fled from, but also vessels and ornaments of gold and silver, and garments, which the same people when going out of Egypt appropriated to themselves, designing them for a better use… in the same way all branches of heathen learning have not only false and superstitious fancies… but also liberal instruction which is more adapted to the use of the truth, and some most excellent precepts of morality.

— Augustine, On Christian Doctrine II.40, trans. J. F. Shaw.1 The interpretation weighed here is the church’s standing answer to a question it faced from the first: may a believer take up the pagan liberal curriculum — grammar and rhetoric, dialectic, the poets and philosophers — or is that learning a snare to be shunned? The advocated reading is that secular learning is not merely permitted but useful, provided it is kept in its rank: a handmaid (ancilla) to theology, gold spoiled from Egypt and re-consecrated, honey gathered selectively from many flowers — taken up, purified, and ordered to the knowledge of God. The rival pole it must answer is Tertullian’s “What has Athens to do with Jerusalem?”

Who advances it

It is the voice of the patristic mainstream and, after them, of the medieval schools and the Reformers alike. Clement of Alexandria gives the reading its first principled defence: Greek philosophy “was a schoolmaster to bring the Hellenic mind, as the law the Hebrews, to Christ,” and the whole field forms a hierarchy of service — “the encyclical branches of study contribute to philosophy… so also philosophy itself co-operates for the acquisition of wisdom,” secular culture the “handmaid” and divine knowledge the “true wife.”2 A generation later Gregory Thaumaturgus shows the principle already at work in an actual Christian classroom: his farewell Address of Thanksgiving to Origen (c. 238) describes Origen marching his pupils up through the liberal arts — logic, natural science, geometry, astronomy, ethics — and reading the pagan philosophers and poets critically, “rejecting nothing… except only the productions of the atheists,” all ordered upward to theology and the Scriptures. Basil writes the manual — read the pagans “after the manner of bees,” culling the useful and passing over the rest — and Augustine supplies the proof-text the Latin West would quote for a thousand years, the “gold of the Egyptians.”3 Jerome, challenged by the orator Magnus for salting his letters with the classics, mounts the tradition’s most elaborate defence — the “captive woman” of Deuteronomy 21, shorn and cleansed before she may be wed, is his figure for the secular wisdom a Christian may “marry” once its idolatry is pared away.4 From these the scholastic formula philosophia ancilla theologiae descends, and with it the university the seven arts would build.

The evidence for

The appropriation logic: what is true is not the pagans’ to keep. The strongest form of the case is not “pagan learning happens to be handy” but a claim about where truth comes from. Augustine’s Egyptian gold is “their gold and silver, which they did not create themselves, but dug out of the mines of God’s providence”1 — so carrying it off is not theft but restitution, returning to God’s service what was always His. The rule beneath the image is stated plainly: “wherever truth may be found, it belongs to his Master.” On this ground the case does not depend on any nation’s virtue; it depends on the unity of truth, and a Christian who refuses a true thing because a pagan said it insults not the pagan but the Giver — the very point Calvin would press (“in despising the gifts, we insult the Giver”).

The biblical precedents. The proponents adduce a chain of scriptural warrants. Israel “despoiled the Egyptians,” carrying their gold and silver out of bondage.5 The captive woman of Deuteronomy 21:10–13, shaved and pared and given a month to mourn before her captor may take her to wife, is read as the pattern for taking up alien wisdom cleansed of its defilement.6 Moses “was learned in all the wisdom of the Egyptians” and Daniel and his companions were schooled in “the learning and the tongue of the Chaldeans” — pagan curricula, in men God then used.7 And Paul himself, on the Areopagus, quotes the Greek poets as witnesses to the truth: “as certain also of your own poets have said, For we are also his offspring.”8 The apostolic precedent is Jerome’s clinching move: if Paul may press Epimenides, Menander, and Aratus into the gospel’s service, so may the church.

The subordination is built in from the start. What keeps the reading from collapsing into a simple endorsement of pagan letters is the rank it always assigns them. For Clement the arts are handmaids, philosophy their mistress, and “wisdom… queen”; the danger he names is not reading the pagans but “growing old” in grammar or rhetoric and never rising past the tools. For Basil the pagans give only “the silhouette of virtue” that Scripture fills in, and the reading is to be selective — the rose without the thorn. For Augustine the whole enterprise is fenced with charity (“knowledge puffeth up, but charity edifieth”) and all the plunder of Egypt is “poor… when compared with the knowledge of Holy Scripture.” The claim, at its best, is never that Athens is self-sufficient but that Athens, subordinated, serves Jerusalem.

The counter-pole, folded in

The reading must answer a genuine and forceful objection, which the timeline carries in Tertullian and in Jerome’s own dream. Tertullian traces the church’s heresies to philosophy as their mother — Valentinus from Plato, Marcion from the Stoics — and demands: “What indeed has Athens to do with Jerusalem?… We want no curious disputation after possessing Christ Jesus.”9 Scripture seems to back him: “Beware lest any man spoil you through philosophy and vain deceit… after the rudiments of the world, and not after Christ”;10 “the wisdom of this world is foolishness with God.”11 And Jerome, dragged before Christ’s judgment-seat in a fever-dream, hears the verdict “Thou art a follower of Cicero and not of Christ” and is scourged for loving pagan style above the psalter.12

This counter-pole is folded here rather than split into its own advocacy page, because — weighed honestly — it is a defeater (a limit on the pro case) and not an independently-advocated positive program. None of its voices actually held that a Christian may learn no letters: Tertullian argues “Athens and Jerusalem” with the full rhetorical armory of the pagan schools he had mastered, and his target is philosophy as a rival authority and a spur to heretical speculation, not grammar and rhetoric as such; Jerome broke his oath the moment his fever passed, and his own letters remained steeped in the classics; Paul’s “philosophy and vain deceit” is qualified in the very verse (“after the rudiments of the world, and not after Christ”) and stands beside his own quotation of the poets. Read as an absolute ban on secular learning, the counter-pole has no serious proponent and no church that practised it; read as a warning — that learning may become a rival master, that style may be loved above substance, that philosophy breeds heresy when it sits in judgment on revelation — it is a caution the handmaid-reading already contains. It bites not against the interpretation but against its overreach, and so it belongs inside the assessment, not on a rival page.

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The conclusion — that a Christian may licitly and profitably take up secular learning subordinated to the faith — is as close to a settled verdict as the tradition offers. It is affirmed across every century and every party: the Greek fathers (Clement, Basil, the Cappadocians), the Latin (Jerome, Augustine), the schoolmen (Aquinas’s ancilla), the Christian humanists (Erasmus), and the Reformers (Luther, Melanchthon, Calvin) with equal force. The church did not merely permit the classical curriculum; it built its schools and universities on the seven arts. And the deep ground is sound: if all truth is God’s, a truth is not disqualified by the mouth that first spoke it. The burden lies heavily on anyone who would forbid the believer a true or useful thing merely because a pagan discovered it.

What is overstated — the proof-texts. The weak link is not the conclusion but the biblical proofs marshalled for it. The Egyptian gold and the captive woman are allegorical readings, not the plain sense of their texts: Exodus recounts a historical despoiling with no hint that it means “study pagan books,” and Deuteronomy 21 legislates the treatment of a war-bride, not a hermeneutic of secular wisdom. These are homiletical illustrations — vivid, memorable, and typical of patristic exegesis — and they persuade only a reader who already grants the conclusion; they do not establish it. Moses and Daniel come nearer to real evidence (Scripture does report them trained in pagan learning and then used by God), and Paul’s quotation of the poets is the genuinely load-bearing apostolic precedent — a New Testament writer actually pressing pagan verse into Christian argument. The honest accounting is that the interpretation is true but its most-quoted proof-texts are ornaments: the case stands on the unity of truth and the Pauline precedent, not on the gold of Egypt. A defence that leans its whole weight on the allegories over-claims for them.

The real limit. The counter-pole marks a boundary the reading must keep, and the best proponents keep it: the handmaid must not become the mistress. Where secular learning is loved for its own elegance above the plainer voice of Scripture (Jerome’s Ciceronianus es), or where philosophy is let sit in judgment on revelation rather than in service to it (Tertullian’s heresies-from-philosophy), the arts have slipped their rank and the caution bites. This is why the interpretation is stated with a proviso — subordinated to theology — and not as a blanket endorsement; the subordination is not a rhetorical flourish but the exact point on which the reading is right and its abuse is wrong.

Assessment

Sound. That the Christian may take up pagan and secular liberal learning, purified and subordinated to the knowledge of God, is the patristic mainstream, the medieval and Reformation consensus, and the church’s lived practice; it is well grounded and, in its qualified form, essentially unrefuted. The rating tracks the interpretation, not the strength of any one father’s prop for it: the Egyptian-gold and captive-woman analogies are homiletical rather than exegetical, and a page that rested the case on them alone would be weaker than the case truly is — but the reading does not need them, standing instead on the unity of truth (all truth is God’s) and the apostolic precedent of Paul quoting the poets. The counter-pole (“Athens and Jerusalem”) is not an independently-advocated rival but the reading’s own built-in limit: it fails as an absolute ban (no father held it, no church practised it) and succeeds only as a caution the handmaid-reading already carries — that learning subordinated is a servant, learning unsubordinated a usurper. The closely related question of whether learning ordered to a living is thereby “illiberal” is weighed at the liberal-vs-servile distinction.
