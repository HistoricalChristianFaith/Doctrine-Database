Is education pursued for a living “illiberal”? The liberal vs. servile distinction

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — the core is well grounded (learning has a real good beyond earning, and a formation reduced to job-training loses something), but the strong form — that studying to earn a living is degrading, “illiberal,” “banausic” — carries a contempt for the wage-earner and the trades that a biblical theology of work resists; genuinely strong by the classical/Thomist lights, weak by the biblical ones · advanced by Aristotle (with Seneca, Aquinas, Newman, and Pieper)

Related arguments: free vs. servile learning — see also the dignity of the mechanical arts

The claim

occupations are divided into liberal and illiberal… And any occupation, art, or science, which makes the body or soul or mind of the freeman less fit for the practice or exercise of virtue, is vulgar; wherefore we call those arts vulgar which tend to deform the body, and likewise all paid employments, for they absorb and degrade the mind.

— Aristotle, Politics VIII.2 (1337b), trans. Jowett.1 The interpretation weighed here is the whole tradition’s answer to the modern parent’s question, why not just study for something that pays? — that the studies “worthy of a free man” are pursued for their own sake, for wisdom and virtue and (for the Christian) the knowledge of God, and are degraded when bent to earning a living, so that an education aimed merely at utility or pay has become “illiberal” or “banausic.”

Who advances it

It is the near-unanimous voice of the pre-modern tradition and its modern recoverers. Aristotle frames it (Politics VIII); Seneca sharpens it from within Stoicism (Epistle 88); Aquinas gives it its cleanest metaphysical reason (an art is “liberal” when ordered to knowing, “servile” when ordered to a bodily work); and in the modern age Newman (“Knowledge is capable of being its own end”) and Josef Pieper (the liberal arts as acts “justified in themselves,” against “the world of total work”) restate it against a utilitarian order. The reading is old, mainstream, and still live in every defence of a liberal-arts college against a purely vocational schooling.

The evidence for

The line falls on the end, not the subject. Aristotle’s distinction is not a caste of “high” and “low” subjects but of ends: “if he does or learns anything for his own sake or for the sake of his friends, or with a view to excellence the action will not appear illiberal; but if done for the sake of others, the very same action will be thought menial and servile.” The highest education is ordered to the noble use of leisure (scholē): “there are branches of learning and education which we must study merely with a view to leisure spent in intellectual activity, and these are to be valued for their own sake; whereas those kinds of knowledge which are useful in business… exist for the sake of other things.”2 So the claim, at its strongest, is not that manual work is beneath notice but that there is a real human good — knowing, contemplating, becoming wise and virtuous — that is not reducible to economic utility, and that an education which recognizes only the useful has amputated it.

Aquinas grounds the distinction in the soul’s freedom. The habits “ordained to… works of the speculative reason… are… called ‘liberal’ arts, in order to distinguish them from those arts that are ordained to works done by the body, which arts are, in a fashion, servile, inasmuch as the body is in servile subjection to the soul, and man, as regards his soul, is free [liber].”3 The word liberal is heard in its root sense: the free work is the mind’s own, which stays within reason (a proof, a poem, a calculation) rather than passing out into the body and its needs. Pieper draws the same line into a critique of modernity: quoting Aquinas, “‘Liberal arts’… are ways of human action which have their justification in themselves; ‘servile arts’ are ways of human action that have a purpose outside of themselves… The liberality… of the liberal arts consists in their not being disposable for purposes.”4 That the very word school descends from the Greek for leisure is, for Pieper, the ancients’ own proof that they ordered education to something beyond work and wages.

Newman states the modern classic form. “Knowledge is capable of being its own end. Such is the constitution of the human mind, that any kind of knowledge, if it be really such, is its own reward.”5 The moment a study is valued only for its professional payoff it has, in his words, passed from the liberal into the merely useful. On this best case the interpretation is powerful: a human being is more than a worker, contemplation is a genuine good, and a culture that can justify no learning it cannot turn into work has lost the ability to say what education is for.

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The ordering of ends is sound and widely shared: learning has a good that is not exhausted by its cash value, and an education collapsed entirely into job-training really does forfeit something — the formation of mind and character, the capacity for contemplation, the pursuit of truth for its own sake. On this the tradition speaks with near one voice, and the burden lies on the utilitarian to explain what is lost when a university becomes a trade school. The end-not-subject criterion is also genuinely illuminating: it locates the freedom of a study not in its topic but in the spirit and purpose with which it is pursued.

What is overstated. Three things weigh against the strong form — that studying to earn a living is positively degrading, “illiberal,” “banausic.”

(1) It carries a contempt for labor that Scripture unsettles. Aristotle’s scheme — and Cicero’s after it (“no workshop can have anything liberal about it”) — rests on a leisured citizen class supported by the labor of slaves, and names “all paid employments” as the paradigm of what “degrade[s] the mind.” The Bible refuses that valuation. The craftsman Bezalel is “filled… with the spirit of God, in wisdom, and in understanding, and in knowledge, and in all manner of workmanship… to work in gold, and in silver… and in carving of timber” — manual craft as a Spirit-given endowment.6 Paul, an apostle, “wrought… for by their occupation they were tentmakers,”7 and lays down the rule “that if any would not work, neither should he eat.”8 Working for one’s bread is not, in this frame, a mark of servility but an ordinary dignity — a point the later Christian defence of the mechanical arts (Hugh of St. Victor; the monastic ora et labora) presses directly against Aristotle’s disdain for the banausic.

(2) The dichotomy is false. Liberal formation and gainful skill need not exclude each other, and the tradition itself knows it. Newman refuses the either/or: “though the useful is not always good, the good is always useful,” and a cultivated intellect “enables us to be more useful, and to a greater number” — the practical end of a university being “that of training good members of society.”9 A study can be pursued for its own sake and equip a person to earn; that a doctor or an engineer is paid does not un-form the mind that learned. The strong claim needs the two ends to be rivals, and they are not.

(3) The tradition itself caps the honorific. The reading is at its weakest where it makes disinterested learning an end so absolute that any utility taints it — and the fathers guard against exactly that. Seneca: “the liberal arts do not conduct the soul all the way to virtue, but merely set it going in that direction”;10 Paul: “Knowledge puffeth up, but charity edifieth.”11 Learning pursued as its own final good, cut loose from wisdom and love, is not the Christian summit but a temptation the tradition names — so “study for its own sake, never for use” over-reaches even by its own lights.

Assessment

Contested. The interpretation is genuinely strong and genuinely limited, and which way it reads depends on which discipline holds the floor. By the classical and Thomist lights — the philosophy of ends, anthropology, the theory of education — the core is well grounded and largely unrefuted: there is a real good in knowing and contemplating that economic utility does not measure, and a formation reduced to job-training is deficient. By the lights of a biblical theology of work, the strong form fails: the contempt packed into “illiberal” and “banausic” for the wage-earner and the trades runs against Scripture’s dignifying of craft and labor, and the sharp either/or between liberal and gainful is a false dichotomy the best proponents (Newman) themselves refuse. The honest verdict keeps both: affirm that learning has an end beyond earning — that is the durable truth of the distinction — while dropping the aristocratic claim that studying toward a living is degrading. What survives is a hierarchy of ends (knowing is not for earning) without a hierarchy of persons (the scholar above the tradesman). The genuinely-advocated counter-thesis — the positive dignity of the mechanical arts and manual trades (rated sound on its own advocated core) — is weighed on its own page; here it enters only as the defeater that keeps this reading from its overreach.
