Is there neutral ground? Faith must transform the disciplines, not just add theology

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — that there is no religiously neutral scholarship (every discipline rests on ultimate commitments), so a Christian education requires the disciplines to be renewed from within and not merely a secular curriculum with theology added, is strong by the Reformed / presuppositionalist lights and well corroborated for the interpretive disciplines and the level of first premises; but it overreaches as a claim about all knowing (its own tradition concedes, via common grace, that believer and unbeliever share one logic and one mathematics), and it is genuinely disputed by the Thomist / Catholic integration tradition · advanced by Abraham Kuyper, Cornelius Van Til, and (in the classical-school movement) Douglas Wilson

Related arguments: the Christian university — see also does excluding theology mutilate the circle of knowledge?

The claim

… there is not a square inch in the whole domain of our human existence over which Christ, who is Sovereign over all, does not cry: ‘Mine!’

— Abraham Kuyper, “Sphere Sovereignty,” the inaugural address at the founding of the Free University of Amsterdam, 20 October 1880.1 Kuyper spoke those words at the founding of a self-consciously Calvinist university, and they carry the thesis weighed here: the lordship of Christ does not stop at the chapel door but claims the method and premises of every science, so there is no religiously neutral territory onto which a Christian school may simply graft a theology faculty. Where Newman’s integrationism answers the secularizer by restoring theology to its place among the sciences, this reading answers a different question — does that restoration suffice? — with a flat no: adding theology to disciplines that go on working by naturalist assumptions leaves their governing creed untouched. Faith has to transform the arts from within.

Who advances it

The thesis is the Dutch neo-Calvinist and presuppositionalist answer to the nature of Christian learning. Its fountainhead is Abraham Kuyper (1837–1920), theologian and Dutch prime minister, whose doctrine of “sphere sovereignty” and whose Principles of Sacred Theology argue that regeneration (palingenesis) “breaks humanity in two” and so yields “two kinds of science” — one that reads the cosmos as normal, one that reads it as fallen and awaiting redemption — between which there is no neutral higher vantage.2 His American heir Cornelius Van Til (1895–1987) sharpened it into a theory of knowledge: there are no “brute” (uninterpreted) facts, because every fact is already God-created and God-interpreted, and so “the idea of disinterested or neutral knowledge is out of accord with the basic ideas of Christianity” — every method “presupposes either the truth or the falsity of Christian theism.”3 In the modern classical-Christian-school movement the same conviction is the first premise of Douglas Wilson’s Recovering the Lost Tools of Learning (1991): “it is not possible for education to be nonreligious… All education builds on the foundation of someone’s worldview” — the thesis already noted on this timeline in the classical-education revival block.4 Behind them all stands Augustine’s conviction that right knowing is ordered by right loving, and that the mind darkened by sin does not see the world as the renewed mind sees it.

The evidence for

Scripture makes knowing a matter of the heart’s allegiance, not a neutral faculty. The proof-texts are pointed: “The fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge” (Proverbs 1:7); in Christ “are hid all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge” (Colossians 2:3); the Christian is to be “casting down imaginations… and bringing into captivity every thought to the obedience of Christ” (2 Corinthians 10:5); and in Romans 1 unbelief is not ignorance but the active “suppression” of a truth already known.5 On this reading, knowledge is never posture-free: one either receives the world as God’s or refuses it as one’s own, and that fundamental orientation reaches down into how a discipline frames its object, chooses its method, and weighs its evidence.

The interpretive disciplines visibly turn on the interpreter’s commitments. The claim is most obviously true where it matters most for a curriculum. History, philosophy, literature, ethics, psychology, and the social sciences do not read the same under a materialist and a Christian construal of the human person: what counts as a cause, what the self is, whether there is a moral order, whether miracles can be reported as facts — these are settled before the evidence is weighed, by premises the discipline does not itself supply. A university that leaves those premises naturalist and merely appends a theology chair has, on this thesis, conceded the substance while decorating the edge.

Secular philosophy of science reached the same verdict about “neutral facts.” Van Til’s “no brute facts” is no longer an eccentric theological claim: the twentieth century’s own philosophers of science — Michael Polanyi on the tacit and personal dimension of all knowing, Thomas Kuhn on paradigm-laden observation, the wide post-positivist consensus on the theory-ladenness of data — converged on the point that there is no presupposition-free view from nowhere.6 And the history of the modern university corroborates it from the other side: the “neutral” secular academy did not turn out to be neutral about God and the human person; it carried a naturalist creed of its own — the very colonizing of the vacated ground that Newman too predicted. That common ground with Newman is real: both deny that dropping theology leaves a neutral blank. They divide only over the remedy.

Adversarial assessment

What holds. In its modest form the thesis is close to won. That scholarship is shaped by the scholar’s ultimate commitments — decisively in the humanities and the interpretive sciences, and at the level of first premises everywhere — is broadly granted, and the naive picture of the researcher as a neutral receptor of uninterpreted facts is abandoned by believing and unbelieving epistemologists alike. On that core, and against the specific target it was built to answer (the claim that a secular curriculum plus a chaplaincy is a Christian education), the reading is strong: mere addition really does leave a discipline’s working assumptions where they were, and the lordship-of-Christ claim that no region of thought is sealed off from the gospel is a coherent and seriously held position.

What is overstated — the absolute antithesis, answered by its own tradition. The strong form — that all knowing is so divided by regeneration that believer and unbeliever share no science — is refuted not mainly by its critics but by Kuyper himself, through the companion doctrine of common grace. Kuyper limited the antithesis to the “higher” matters of principle and worldview; in “weighing and measuring,” in formal logic, and in mathematics, he held that the noetic effects of sin are restrained and the two kinds of scientist do the same work and reach the same result — the same theorem, the same titration, the same sum.7 So “there is no neutral ground” is true of the foundational and interpretive level and false as a claim about every operation of knowing; a physics or a philology is not made Christian, or discovered to be secretly sectarian, at the bench. Pressed past that limit the thesis courts real dangers its best proponents avoid: fideism, a retreat into a self-enclosed subculture, and the dismissal of genuine truths found by unbelievers — the very error Calvin warned against (“if we regard the Spirit of God as the sole fountain of truth, we shall neither reject the truth itself, nor despise it wherever it shall appear, unless we wish to dishonour the Spirit of God”) and Augustine’s “gold of the Egyptians” forbids.

The live rival: the Thomist integration tradition. The deepest opposition is not a mere caution but a rival program with its own advocates: the Thomist and Catholic natural-law tradition — Newman’s own — holds that there is a shared human rational nature and a body of truths of reason (including a genuine natural theology) accessible to believer and unbeliever alike, so that grace perfects nature rather than standing antithetically over against it. On that account the disciplines have a real autonomy in their own spheres, and the Christian university is secured by integration (theology restored to the circle, ordering the rest) rather than by a wholesale “transformation” that risks collapsing every science into a confessional exercise. This is a genuine, respectable, and unsettled dispute — which is why the transformationist thesis is contested, not sound: strong where premises and interpretation govern, weaker as a universal claim, and squarely opposed by a tradition with as long a pedigree as its own.

Assessment

Contested. That there is no religiously neutral scholarship, so a Christian education must renew the disciplines from within rather than bolt theology onto a secular curriculum, is strong by the Reformed and presuppositionalist lights and well corroborated for the interpretive disciplines and the level of first premises — and its polemical target (the secular-curriculum-plus-chaplaincy model) is fairly hit. But the strong, absolute form is checked by its own tradition’s doctrine of common grace, which concedes a shared logic and mathematics in which regenerate and unregenerate reason alike; and the thesis is genuinely opposed by the Thomist / Catholic integration tradition, which holds a shared rational nature on which grace builds. A modest core — worldview shapes scholarship, and “neutral facts” is naive — is near-consensus; the antithesis pressed to the whole of knowing is not. It is best read against its rival, Newman’s integrationism, under the Christian university; on the shared conviction that all truth is God’s wherever found, see the handmaid-of-theology argument.
