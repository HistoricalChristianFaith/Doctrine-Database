Clement of Alexandria on philosophy and the liberal arts as handmaid to theology

Dates: c. 200 (the Stromateis, c. 198–203; Clement fl. c. 150–215) · Wikipedia
Summary: With Clement the classical curriculum passes from pagan hands into Christian ones, and the question the timeline poses — may a believer study the arts of the free man? — gets its first confident yes. Against Christians who thought Greek learning a snare, Clement argued that philosophy was God’s own preparatory gift to the Greeks: “a schoolmaster to bring the Hellenic mind, as the law the Hebrews, to Christ.” He ranks the whole field into a hierarchy of service — the encyclical studies (the general liberal curriculum: grammar, geometry, music, rhetoric) are handmaids to philosophy their mistress, and philosophy in turn is handmaid to wisdom, the queen, which is the knowledge of God. Read through the figures of Sarah and Hagar, secular culture is the handmaid a believer may embrace on the way to the “true wife,” divine knowledge. This is the seed of the medieval formula philosophia ancilla theologiae — philosophy the handmaid of theology — and of the whole “spoil the Egyptians” case that Origen and Augustine would systematize.

Claims & evidence

Philosophy “a schoolmaster to bring the Greek mind… to Christ”

Accordingly, before the advent of the Lord, philosophy was necessary to the Greeks for righteousness. And now it becomes conducive to piety; being a kind of preparatory training to those who attain to faith through demonstration… Perchance, too, philosophy was given to the Greeks directly and primarily, till the Lord should call the Greeks. For this was a schoolmaster to bring “the Hellenic mind,” as the law, the Hebrews, “to Christ.” Philosophy, therefore, was a preparation, paving the way for him who is perfected in Christ.

— Stromata I.5, trans. William Wilson (ANF 2), source. The whole fifth chapter is headed “Philosophy the Handmaid of Theology.” Clement takes Paul’s image of the law as a paidagōgos (Galatians 3:24) and applies it to Greek learning: as the Mosaic law schooled the Hebrews toward Christ, so philosophy schooled the Greeks. The arts of the pagans are not a rival gospel but a preparation — a good from God “by consequence,” ordered to the same end as the covenants. Here the church’s decision to keep the classical curriculum is given its first principled defence.

The liberal arts are handmaids, philosophy their mistress, wisdom the queen

For already some men, ensnared by the charms of handmaidens, have despised their consort philosophy, and have grown old, some of them in music, some in geometry, others in grammar, the most in rhetoric. But as the encyclical branches of study contribute to philosophy, which is their mistress; so also philosophy itself co-operates for the acquisition of wisdom. For philosophy is the study of wisdom, and wisdom is the knowledge of things divine and human; and their causes. Wisdom is therefore queen of philosophy, as philosophy is of preparatory culture.

— Stromata I.5, source. The “encyclical branches of study” (enkyklios paideia) are the general liberal curriculum the Roman schoolmasters had already named — grammar, geometry, music, rhetoric. Clement orders the whole into a chain of service: the arts serve philosophy, philosophy serves wisdom, and wisdom is the knowledge of God. His warning is against mistaking the servant for the mistress — growing “old in grammar” and never rising past the tools — not against the arts themselves, which have their honoured place so long as they are kept in their rank. This hierarchy of handmaid-and-mistress is the germ of the scholastic tag philosophia ancilla theologiae.

Secular culture the handmaid, divine knowledge the “true wife” (Sarah and Hagar)

Wherefore also, when Sarah was jealous at Hagar being preferred to her, Abraham, as choosing only what was profitable in secular philosophy, said, “Behold, thy maid is in thine hands: deal with her as it pleases thee;” manifestly meaning, “I embrace secular culture as youthful, and a handmaid; but thy knowledge I honour and reverence as true wife.”

— Stromata I.5, source. Clement reads the household of Abraham as an allegory of the studious life: Hagar the Egyptian handmaid is “secular culture” (by Egypt “the world is designated figuratively”), Sarah the wife is the wisdom that is the knowledge of God. A believer may “have intercourse with secular culture first” and only afterward “approach” divine wisdom — using the arts, honouring them, but never confusing the handmaid with the wife. It is the same “plunder from Egypt” logic Origen would draw out and Augustine fix as the church’s charter for reading pagan books: take what is true and useful, leave the idolatry behind.

Notes

  • The seed of “philosophy the handmaid of theology.” Clement never writes the Latin tag philosophia ancilla theologiae — that crystallizes only in the medieval schools — but his chapter title and his Sarah/Hagar figure supply its whole substance: learning is a servant kept in the house, valued in its rank and subordinated to the knowledge of God. The formula’s later career (Peter Damian’s suspicion of it, Aquinas’s confident use of it) is a working-out of what Clement first put in Christian terms.
  • Against the anti-classical pole. Clement writes with the other answer in view. Where Tertullian would ask “what has Athens to do with Jerusalem?” and Jerome would dream of being flogged for loving Cicero, Clement answers that Athens is Jerusalem’s schoolmaster — a preparatory gift, not a temptation. His is the accommodating temper of Alexandria; the argument between the two poles runs the length of this timeline.
  • Use, but do not linger. Clement’s enthusiasm has a guard-rail. Quoting Proverbs — “Be not much with a strange woman” — he warns the student to “use indeed, but not to linger and spend time with, secular culture.” The arts are a road, not a home; the man who grows old in rhetoric and never reaches wisdom has mistaken the appliance for the end. This keeps his defence of learning from collapsing into an idolatry of learning — the very balance Seneca struck from the pagan side.
  • On the wiki. Clement appears also as a witness on the astrology timeline, where the same “spoil the Egyptians” temper lets him praise astronomy as a study that “lifts the mind to God” while fencing off the horoscope. His placement of the liberal arts as handmaid to theology feeds directly into the summary’s forthcoming argument on the “handmaiden of theology” thesis.
