Clement of Alexandria on philosophy and the liberal arts as handmaid to theology

Dates: c. 200 (the Stromateis, c. 198–203; Clement fl. c. 150–215) · Wikipedia
Summary: Clement is the earliest Christian writer on this timeline to answer its question — may a believer study the arts of the free man? — and he answers yes. Against Christians who thought Greek learning a snare, Clement argued that philosophy was God’s own preparatory gift to the Greeks: “a schoolmaster to bring the Hellenic mind, as the law the Hebrews, to Christ.” He ranks the whole field into a hierarchy of service — the encyclical studies (the general liberal curriculum: grammar, geometry, music, rhetoric) are handmaids to philosophy their mistress, and philosophy in turn is handmaid to wisdom, the queen, which is the knowledge of God. Read through the figures of Sarah and Hagar, secular culture is the handmaid a believer may embrace on the way to the “true wife,” divine knowledge. The Latin tag philosophia ancilla theologiae — philosophy the handmaid of theology — is a later medieval formula, not his own wording (see Notes).

Claims & evidence

Philosophy “a schoolmaster to bring the Greek mind… to Christ”

Accordingly, before the advent of the Lord, philosophy was necessary to the Greeks for righteousness. And now it becomes conducive to piety; being a kind of preparatory training to those who attain to faith through demonstration… Perchance, too, philosophy was given to the Greeks directly and primarily, till the Lord should call the Greeks. For this was a schoolmaster to bring “the Hellenic mind,” as the law, the Hebrews, “to Christ.” Philosophy, therefore, was a preparation, paving the way for him who is perfected in Christ.

— Stromata I.5, trans. William Wilson (ANF 2), source. The whole fifth chapter is headed “Philosophy the Handmaid of Theology.” Clement takes Paul’s image of the law as a paidagōgos (Galatians 3:24) and applies it to Greek learning: as the Mosaic law schooled the Hebrews toward Christ, so philosophy schooled the Greeks. For Clement the learning of the Greeks is not a rival gospel but a preparation — a good from God “by consequence,” ordered to the same end as the covenants.

The liberal arts are handmaids, philosophy their mistress, wisdom the queen

For already some men, ensnared by the charms of handmaidens, have despised their consort philosophy, and have grown old, some of them in music, some in geometry, others in grammar, the most in rhetoric. But as the encyclical branches of study contribute to philosophy, which is their mistress; so also philosophy itself co-operates for the acquisition of wisdom. For philosophy is the study of wisdom, and wisdom is the knowledge of things divine and human; and their causes. Wisdom is therefore queen of philosophy, as philosophy is of preparatory culture.

— Stromata I.5, source. The “encyclical branches of study” (enkyklios paideia) are the general liberal curriculum the Roman schoolmasters had already named — grammar, geometry, music, rhetoric. Clement orders the whole into a chain of service: the arts serve philosophy, philosophy serves wisdom, and wisdom is the knowledge of things divine and human. His warning is aimed at those who, “ensnared by the charms of handmaidens,” grow old in music, geometry, grammar, or rhetoric and despise philosophy; the arts themselves he places in the service of philosophy.

Secular culture the handmaid, divine knowledge the “true wife” (Sarah and Hagar)

Wherefore also, when Sarah was jealous at Hagar being preferred to her, Abraham, as choosing only what was profitable in secular philosophy, said, “Behold, thy maid is in thine hands: deal with her as it pleases thee;” manifestly meaning, “I embrace secular culture as youthful, and a handmaid; but thy knowledge I honour and reverence as true wife.”

— Stromata I.5, source. Clement reads the household of Abraham as an allegory of the studious life: Hagar the Egyptian handmaid is “secular culture” (by Egypt “the world is designated figuratively”), Sarah the wife is the wisdom that is the knowledge of God. A believer may “have intercourse with secular culture first” and only afterward “approach” divine wisdom — using the arts, honouring them, but not confusing the handmaid with the wife. Compare Gregory Thaumaturgus’s account of Origen’s school and Augustine’s gold of the Egyptians, later on this timeline.

Notes

  • “Philosophy the handmaid of theology.” Clement does not write the Latin tag philosophia ancilla theologiae, a formula of the medieval schools; in the translation quoted his chapter is headed “Philosophy the Handmaid of Theology,” and his Sarah/Hagar figure makes learning a servant kept in the house, subordinated to the knowledge of God. Peter Damian and Aquinas later take differing attitudes to the formula (⚠ unverified — not yet sourced).
  • Placement on the timeline. Clement writes against Christians who thought Greek learning a snare. His contemporary Tertullian asks “what has Athens to do with Jerusalem?”, and Jerome later dreams of being flogged for loving Cicero; Clement presents philosophy as the Greeks’ schoolmaster to Christ, a preparatory gift. The timeline records both answers.
  • Use, but do not linger. Clement also sets a limit. Quoting Proverbs — “Be not much with a strange woman” — he warns the student to “use indeed, but not to linger and spend time with, secular culture.” Compare Seneca’s subordination of the arts to wisdom, earlier on this timeline.
  • Other timelines. Clement appears also as a witness on the astrology timeline, where he praises astronomy as a study that “lifts the mind to God” while rejecting the horoscope.
