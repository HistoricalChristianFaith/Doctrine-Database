Dorothy L. Sayers — the lost tools of learning and the recovery of the trivium

Dates: c. 1947 (the essay The Lost Tools of Learning, delivered as a paper at Oxford in 1947 and printed the next year). Sayers 1893–1957, English novelist, playwright, classical and medieval scholar, and translator of Dante · Wikipedia
Summary: Sayers argued that modern education teaches “subjects” but has lost the medieval Trivium’s older aim — to hand pupils the very tools of learning (grammar, then logic, then rhetoric) so that they can afterwards teach themselves anything. She recast the three arts of the Trivium as three natural stages of a child’s development, which she nicknamed the “Poll-Parrot,” “Pert,” and “Poetic” ages. Though she disclaimed any hope of seeing it tried, the essay became the acknowledged seedbed of the modern classical-education revival.

Claims & evidence

Modern schooling teaches subjects but not the art of learning

Sayers’s indictment is that pupils leave school stocked with information yet unable to reason, judge evidence, or follow an argument — because the one thing a school ought to teach has been dropped:

Is not the great defect of our education today—a defect traceable through all the disquieting symptoms of trouble that I have mentioned—that although we often succeed in teaching our pupils ‘subjects,’ we fail lamentably on the whole in teaching them how to think: they learn everything, except the art of learning.

— The Lost Tools of Learning.1 The remedy she finds in the medieval curriculum, whose first half was designed for exactly this:

The syllabus was divided into two parts: the Trivium and Quadrivium. The second part—the Quadrivium—consisted of ‘subjects,’ and need not for the moment concern us. The interesting thing for us is the composition of the Trivium, which preceded the Quadrivium and was the preliminary discipline for it.

The whole of the Trivium was, in fact, intended to teach the pupil the proper use of the tools of learning, before he began to apply them to ‘subjects’ at all.

— The Lost Tools of Learning.2 The Trivium she is retrieving is precisely the grammar-logic-rhetoric sequence that Martianus Capella, Boethius, and Isidore had fixed and the universities had made the gateway to every higher study.

The end of all education is to teach people to learn for themselves

For the sole true end of education is simply this: to teach men how to learn for themselves; and whatever instruction fails to do this is effort spent in vain.

— The Lost Tools of Learning.3 The liberal arts are “tools” in the strict sense: a person who has mastered grammar, logic, and rhetoric can turn them on any subject whatever, for life — the durable freedom that a training in one trade cannot give. It is Newman’s “philosophical habit of mind” approached from the side of method rather than of dignity.

The Trivium as three stages of a child’s development — the “Poll-Parrot, Pert, and Poetic” ages

Sayers’s most influential (and most contested) move is to align the three arts of the Trivium with three natural phases of childhood she claims to observe:

Looking back upon myself (since I am the child I know best and the only child I can pretend to know from inside) I recognize three states of development. These, in a rough-and-ready fashion, I will call the Poll-Parrot, the Pert, and the Poetic—the latter coinciding, approximately, with the onset of puberty.

The Poll-Parrot age loves to memorize and recite (“learning by heart is easy and, on the whole, pleasurable… one rejoices in the chanting of rhymes”); the Pert age “is characterized by contradicting, answering back, liking to ‘catch people out’… and by the propounding of conundrums”; the Poetic age “is self-centered; it yearns to express itself… and… should show the beginnings of creativeness.” She then maps the Trivium onto them:

Now it seems to me that the layout of the Trivium adapts itself with a singular appropriateness to these three ages: Grammar to the Poll-Parrot, Dialectic to the Pert, and Rhetoric to the Poetic age.

— The Lost Tools of Learning.4 This developmental reading — teach facts and recitation early, formal logic in the argumentative middle years, and self-expression in adolescence — became the organizing scheme of the modern classical school, dividing the grades into “grammar,” “logic,” and “rhetoric” stages. (Whether the developmental claim is psychologically sound, or a rhetorical framing grafted onto a curriculum the medievals never understood as a theory of child development, is weighed on its own; see the Notes.)

Notes

  • The hinge of the modern revival. Sayers herself expected nothing to come of the essay — she called her proposals “wildly impractical” and doubted any school would attempt them. Yet it is the acknowledged charter of the twentieth-century classical-Christian-school movement: Douglas Wilson’s Recovering the Lost Tools of Learning (1991) takes its very title from her, and the graded “grammar / logic / rhetoric” structure of today’s classical schools is her three ages made a syllabus.
  • The developmental claim is contested. Sayers offered the Poll-Parrot / Pert / Poetic scheme as introspection (“the only child I can pretend to know from inside”), not as established developmental psychology, and the medieval Trivium was a sequence of arts, not a theory of the stages of a growing mind. Whether the mapping holds is a distinct sub-question, flagged here and to be weighed on its own argument page rather than assumed on her authority.
