Dorothy L. Sayers — the lost tools of learning and the trivium

Dates: c. 1947 (the essay The Lost Tools of Learning, delivered as a paper at Oxford in 1947 and printed the next year). Sayers 1893–1957, English novelist, playwright, classical and medieval scholar, and translator of Dante · Wikipedia
Summary: Sayers argued that modern education teaches “subjects” but has lost the medieval Trivium’s older aim — to hand pupils the very tools of learning (grammar, then logic, then rhetoric) so that they can afterwards teach themselves anything. She recast the three arts of the Trivium as three natural stages of a child’s development, which she nicknamed the “Poll-Parrot,” “Pert,” and “Poetic” ages. She judged it improbable that her proposals would be carried into effect; later classical schools took up the essay’s scheme (see Notes).

Claims & evidence

Sayers on modern schooling: subjects without the art of learning

Sayers’s complaint is that pupils leave school stocked with information yet unable to reason, judge evidence, or follow an argument — because, in her account, the art of learning has been dropped:

Is not the great defect of our education today—a defect traceable through all the disquieting symptoms of trouble that I have mentioned—that although we often succeed in teaching our pupils ‘subjects,’ we fail lamentably on the whole in teaching them how to think: they learn everything, except the art of learning.

— The Lost Tools of Learning.1 She finds the remedy in the medieval curriculum, whose first part, she writes, was intended for this:

The syllabus was divided into two parts: the Trivium and Quadrivium. The second part—the Quadrivium—consisted of ‘subjects,’ and need not for the moment concern us. The interesting thing for us is the composition of the Trivium, which preceded the Quadrivium and was the preliminary discipline for it.

The whole of the Trivium was, in fact, intended to teach the pupil the proper use of the tools of learning, before he began to apply them to ‘subjects’ at all.

— The Lost Tools of Learning.2 Compare the seven arts in Martianus Capella, Boethius, and Isidore, and the arts course of the universities, earlier on this timeline.

Sayers on the end of education: to teach people to learn for themselves

For the sole true end of education is simply this: to teach men how to learn for themselves; and whatever instruction fails to do this is effort spent in vain.

— The Lost Tools of Learning.3 For Sayers the liberal arts are “tools”: a person who has mastered grammar, logic, and rhetoric can turn them on any subject whatever. Compare Newman’s “philosophical habit of mind” on this timeline.

The Trivium as three stages of a child’s development — the “Poll-Parrot, Pert, and Poetic” ages

Sayers aligns the three arts of the Trivium with three phases of childhood she describes from her own experience:

Looking back upon myself (since I am the child I know best and the only child I can pretend to know from inside) I recognize three states of development. These, in a rough-and-ready fashion, I will call the Poll-Parrot, the Pert, and the Poetic—the latter coinciding, approximately, with the onset of puberty.

The Poll-Parrot age loves to memorize and recite (“learning by heart is easy and, on the whole, pleasurable… one rejoices in the chanting of rhymes”); the Pert age “is characterized by contradicting, answering back, liking to ‘catch people out’… and by the propounding of conundrums”; the Poetic age “is self-centered; it yearns to express itself… and… should show the beginnings of creativeness.” She then maps the Trivium onto them:

Now it seems to me that the layout of the Trivium adapts itself with a singular appropriateness to these three ages: Grammar to the Poll-Parrot, Dialectic to the Pert, and Rhetoric to the Poetic age.

— The Lost Tools of Learning.4 On this scheme facts and recitation are taught early, formal logic in the argumentative middle years, and self-expression in adolescence. Shawn Barnett (CiRCE Institute, 2019) calls the stage-scheme “a nearly ubiquitous presupposition of classical education literature and the central organizing tenet of many a school’s curriculum.”5 Whether it reflects the medieval trivium is disputed; see the Notes.

Notes

  • Reception. Sayers wrote that “it is in the highest degree improbable that the reforms I propose will ever be carried into effect.”6 Douglas Wilson’s Recovering the Lost Tools of Learning (1991) takes its title from her essay and builds its graded “grammar / logic / rhetoric” structure on her three ages.7
  • The developmental reading and its critics. Sayers offered the Poll-Parrot / Pert / Poetic scheme from introspection (“the only child I can pretend to know from inside”), and wrote that her views on child psychology were “neither orthodox nor enlightened.”8 Shawn Barnett (CiRCE Institute, 2019) holds that “medieval educational theorists never conceived of the trivium in terms of developmental stages” and that the correlation is Sayers’s own.9 Douglas Wilson (2021) restates the developmental reading against its critics within the classical-school movement.10
