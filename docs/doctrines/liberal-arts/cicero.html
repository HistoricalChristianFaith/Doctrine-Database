Cicero on the liberal arts and the liberal callings

Dates: 44 BC · Wikipedia
Summary: Cicero (106–43 BC) is the great Latin transmitter of the Greek ideal — it is his vocabulary of artes liberales and humanitas that the Latin West inherits. In De Officiis (44 BC) he restates the free/servile division in Roman dress: which callings befit a gentleman and which are “vulgar,” with hired wage-labour singled out because “the very wage… is a pledge of… slavery,” the professions requiring intelligence or conferring benefit ranked highest, and agriculture praised as most becoming to a free man. This is the same distinction Aristotle drew, recast for the tradition the fathers actually read.

Cicero also appears on this wiki as a witness on the friendship and parity-of-sins timelines; here he stands for the liberal-arts inheritance.

Claims & evidence

Hired wage-labour is vulgar — the wage is a pledge of slavery

Unbecoming to a gentleman, too, and vulgar are the means of livelihood of all hired workmen whom we pay for mere manual labour, not for artistic skill; for in their case the very wage they receive is a pledge of their slavery.

— De Officiis I.150, trans. Walter Miller, source. Cicero grades the trades exactly as Aristotle graded occupations: retailers who cannot profit “without a great deal of downright lying” are vulgar, “all mechanics are engaged in vulgar trades; for no workshop can have anything liberal about it,” and the trades catering to sensual pleasure are “least respectable of all.” The Latin — merces auctoramentum servitutis — makes the wage itself the mark of servility, the mirror image of the free arts pursued for their own sake.

The liberal callings require intelligence or confer benefit — and agriculture best befits a free man

of all the occupations by which gain is secured, none is better than agriculture, none more profitable, none more delightful, none more becoming to a freeman.

— De Officiis I.151, trans. Walter Miller, source. The callings Cicero counts honourable are those “in which a greater degree of intelligence is required or from which no small benefit to society is derived” — medicine, architecture, and teaching — and, crowning them, farming, which unites gain with the independence of a free man. The scale of value is the same one running through the whole tradition: the more an art engages the mind and serves a good beyond mere gain, the more it is fit for the free.

Notes

  • Cicero is not a Christian witness but the channel by which the Greek artes liberales and the ideal of humanitas reached the Latin fathers. Ambrose reworked Cicero’s De Officiis for the clergy; Jerome and Augustine were steeped in his prose; the medieval schoolroom’s very word for the free studies is his.
  • The Roman contempt for wage-labour and “the workshop” is precisely what the Christian defence of manual work and the mechanical arts later contests — Paul boasts of his tentmaking, and the monasteries make labour a school of virtue. Cicero’s scale sets up the argument; the gospel revises it.
  • Compare Aristotle, whose liberal/illiberal division Cicero here Latinizes, and Varro, his contemporary, who fixed the canon of the arts themselves.
