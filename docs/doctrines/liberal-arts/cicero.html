Cicero on the liberal arts and the liberal callings

Dates: 44 BC · Wikipedia
Summary: Cicero (106–43 BC) writes of the free and the servile callings in Latin, in the vocabulary of artes liberales and humanitas. In De Officiis (44 BC) he sets out the free/servile division in Roman terms: which callings befit a gentleman and which are “vulgar,” with hired wage-labour singled out because “the very wage… is a pledge of… slavery,” the professions requiring intelligence or conferring benefit ranked highest, and agriculture praised as most becoming to a free man. Compare the division Aristotle drew, earlier on this timeline.

Cicero also appears on this wiki as a witness on the friendship and parity-of-sins timelines; here he is cited on the liberal and the servile callings.

Claims & evidence

Hired wage-labour is vulgar — the wage is a pledge of slavery

Unbecoming to a gentleman, too, and vulgar are the means of livelihood of all hired workmen whom we pay for mere manual labour, not for artistic skill; for in their case the very wage they receive is a pledge of their slavery.

— De Officiis I.150, trans. Walter Miller, source. Cicero grades the trades: retailers who cannot profit “without a great deal of downright lying” are vulgar, “all mechanics are engaged in vulgar trades; for no workshop can have anything liberal about it,” and the trades catering to sensual pleasure are “least respectable of all.” The Latin — merces auctoramentum servitutis — makes the wage itself the mark of servility.

The liberal callings require intelligence or confer benefit — and agriculture best befits a free man

of all the occupations by which gain is secured, none is better than agriculture, none more profitable, none more delightful, none more becoming to a freeman.

— De Officiis I.151, trans. Walter Miller, source. The callings Cicero counts honourable are those “in which a greater degree of intelligence is required or from which no small benefit to society is derived” — medicine, architecture, and teaching — and, above them all among the means of gain, farming. On Cicero’s scale, the more an art requires intelligence or benefits society, the more honourable it is for the free.

Notes

  • Cicero is not a Christian writer. Ambrose reworked Cicero’s De Officiis for the clergy, and Jerome and Augustine were steeped in his prose (see Jerome’s dream on this timeline).
  • Later on this timeline Hugh of St. Victor counts seven mechanical arts within philosophy. Paul’s tentmaking and monastic labour are other Christian precedents for manual work.
  • Compare Aristotle, whose liberal/illiberal division Cicero here puts into Latin, and Varro, his contemporary, who wrote on the arts themselves.
