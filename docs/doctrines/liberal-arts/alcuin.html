Alcuin of York — the seven arts rebuilt in the Carolingian schools

Dates: c. 800 (the palace-school years and the textbooks on the arts; the reform capitularies of 789 and c. 784–785; Alcuin c. 735–804, a Northumbrian deacon, master of the cathedral school of York, then of Charlemagne’s palace school, and finally abbot of St. Martin’s at Tours) · Wikipedia
Summary: Alcuin is where the seven liberal arts, dying with the ancient schools, are rebuilt into a working institution. As the animating mind of Charlemagne’s revival of learning he helped make schooling a matter of imperial law, framed the whole enterprise as the servant of Scripture and right worship, and in his own dialogue textbooks cast the seven arts as a graded ascent — the “seven pillars” of wisdom — up toward the knowledge of God. He fills the long gap between Isidore’s seventh-century encyclopedia and the twelfth-century schools of Hugh of St. Victor, turning the patristic case for Christian learning into a curriculum backed by a throne.

Claims & evidence

Schools made a matter of imperial law: the Admonitio generalis (789)

Et ut scolae legentium puerorum fiant. Psalmos, notas, cantus, compotum, grammaticam per singula monasteria vel episcopia et libros catholicos bene emendate…

— Admonitio generalis (789), c. 72, Latin ed. A. Boretius, MGH Capitularia regum Francorum I (Hannover, 1883), pp. 59–60.1 The chapter orders, in sense, that there be schools for boys who can read; let the psalms, the [shorthand] notes, chant, the computus, and grammar be taught, and the catholic books carefully corrected, throughout every monastery and bishopric. Issued in Charlemagne’s name with Alcuin at the head of the court school, it is the legislative core of the Carolingian educational reform — the moment the seven arts pass from a private ideal into a public mandate binding on the whole Frankish church. (There is no freely-linkable verbatim English translation of the chapter online; the Latin is quoted verbatim above and its sense given in framing prose.)

Learning ordered to the understanding of Scripture: the letter De litteris colendis

Therefore, we exhort you not only not to neglect the study of letters, but also with most humble mind, pleasing to God, to study earnestly in order that you may be able more easily and more correctly to penetrate the mysteries of the divine Scriptures. Since, moreover, images, tropes and similar figures are found in the sacred pages, no one doubts that each one in reading these will understand the spiritual sense more quickly if previously he shall have been fully instructed in the mastery of letters.

…so that those who desire to please God by living rightly should not neglect to please him also by speaking correctly… For although correct conduct may be better than knowledge, nevertheless knowledge precedes conduct.

— Epistola de litteris colendis (Charlemagne to Abbot Baugulf of Fulda, c. 784–785), trans. D. C. Munro, Translations and Reprints from the Original Sources of European History, Vol. VI, No. 5 (University of Pennsylvania), pp. 12–14.2 This circular letter — drafted within Alcuin’s reform circle and sent to the monasteries — gives the program its rationale, and it is the fathers’ own: letters are not cultivated for their own sake but because a mind untrained in them cannot rightly read the figures and tropes of the sacred page. The famous antithesis — right doing is better than right knowing, yet knowing must come first, and one who pleases God by living rightly should please him also by speaking correctly — is the Carolingian charter for a lettered clergy.

The seven arts as the “seven pillars” of wisdom: De grammatica

…I think that ye should be led by the steps of erudition from lower to higher things until your wings gradually grow stronger, so that ye may mount on them to view the loftier visions of the pure ether.

We have read how Wisdom herself saith by the mouth of Solomon, ‘Wisdom hath builded her house, she hath hewn out her seven pillars.’… yet Wisdom is also built upon the seven pillars of liberal letters, and it can in no wise afford us access to any perfect knowledge, unless it be set upon these seven pillars, or ascents.

— Alcuin, De grammatica (the dialogue prologue), trans. Andrew Fleming West, Alcuin and the Rise of the Christian Schools (Scribner’s), pp. 94–96.3 In his textbooks Alcuin does not merely list the arts but orders them into a ladder: grammar and its sisters are the lower rungs by which the pupil climbs toward the higher vision. Applying Proverbs 9:1 — the same verse Cassiodorus had already used, and which Alcuin consciously takes up from him — he identifies Wisdom’s “seven pillars” with the seven liberal arts, without which “perfect knowledge” has no foundation to stand on. It is the graded ascent Gregory Thaumaturgus had described in Origen’s classroom, now written into the schoolbook of the West.

A Christian Athens in Francia

If only there were many who would follow the illustrious desire of your intent, perchance a new, nay, a more excellent Athens might be founded in Frankland; for our Athens, being ennobled with the mastership of Christ the Lord, would surpass all the wisdom of the studies of the Academy. That was instructed only in the Platonic disciplines and had fame for its culture in the seven arts, but ours being enriched beyond [that]…

— Alcuin, letter to Charlemagne (Epistola 170 in Dümmler’s numbering, c. 799), trans. West, Alcuin and the Rise of the Christian Schools, pp. 48–49.4 The ambition is exact: not to abandon the seven arts of the pagan Academy but to inherit them and surpass them, because in a Christian school they are “ennobled with the mastership of Christ.” It is the same move Augustine made with the gold of Egypt and Jerome with the captive woman — the classical curriculum carried off, cleansed, and re-consecrated — now framed as an imperial cultural program.

Notes

  • Authorship of the capitularies. The Admonitio generalis and De litteris colendis were issued in Charlemagne’s name and drafted within his court circle; the exact wording is not certainly Alcuin’s own pen. They belong to the reform program he served and largely shaped, and are cited here as the Carolingian educational legislation, not as compositions provably his. His personal voice on the arts is clearest in the dialogue textbooks (De grammatica, De rhetorica et virtutibus, De dialectica).
  • A misattribution to avoid. The line sometimes quoted as Alcuin’s — “It is now time that we should hasten through the text of the book we have in hand under seven other titles suitable to secular letters” — is in fact West’s translation of Cassiodorus (the Institutiones preface), which Alcuin builds upon; it is not Alcuin’s own.
  • On this wiki. Alcuin’s pupils carried his program on: his student Rabanus Maurus (Rabanus Maurus on this timeline) turned it into a handbook for the clergy a generation later, reproducing Augustine’s “spoiling the Egyptians” almost word for word.
