Alcuin of York — the seven arts in the Carolingian schools

Dates: c. 800 (the palace-school years and the textbooks on the arts; the reform capitularies of 789 and c. 784–785; Alcuin c. 735–804, a Northumbrian deacon, master of the cathedral school of York, then of Charlemagne’s palace school, and finally abbot of St. Martin’s at Tours) · Wikipedia
Summary: Alcuin served at the head of Charlemagne’s palace school during the Carolingian reform of learning, whose capitularies made schooling a matter of royal law and set it in the service of Scripture and right worship. In his own dialogue textbooks he cast the seven arts as a graded ascent — the “seven pillars” of wisdom — toward the knowledge of God. On this timeline he stands between Isidore’s seventh-century encyclopedia and the twelfth-century schools of Hugh of St. Victor.

Claims & evidence

Schools made a matter of imperial law: the Admonitio generalis (789)

Et ut scolae legentium puerorum fiant. Psalmos, notas, cantus, compotum, grammaticam per singula monasteria vel episcopia et libros catholicos bene emendate…

— Admonitio generalis (789), c. 72, Latin ed. A. Boretius, MGH Capitularia regum Francorum I (Hannover, 1883), pp. 59–60.1 The chapter orders, in sense, that there be schools for boys who can read; let the psalms, the [shorthand] notes, chant, the computus, and grammar be taught, and the catholic books carefully corrected, throughout every monastery and bishopric. Issued in Charlemagne’s name while Alcuin headed the court school, the chapter is part of the legislation of the Carolingian educational reform, addressed to every monastery and bishopric of the Frankish church. (There is no freely-linkable verbatim English translation of the chapter online; the Latin is quoted verbatim above and its sense given in framing prose.)

Learning ordered to the understanding of Scripture: the letter De litteris colendis

Therefore, we exhort you not only not to neglect the study of letters, but also with most humble mind, pleasing to God, to study earnestly in order that you may be able more easily and more correctly to penetrate the mysteries of the divine Scriptures. Since, moreover, images, tropes and similar figures are found in the sacred pages, no one doubts that each one in reading these will understand the spiritual sense more quickly if previously he shall have been fully instructed in the mastery of letters.

…so that those who desire to please God by living rightly should not neglect to please him also by speaking correctly… For although correct conduct may be better than knowledge, nevertheless knowledge precedes conduct.

— Epistola de litteris colendis (Charlemagne to Abbot Baugulf of Fulda, c. 784–785), trans. D. C. Munro, Translations and Reprints from the Original Sources of European History, Vol. VI, No. 5 (University of Pennsylvania), pp. 12–14.2 This circular letter — drafted within Alcuin’s reform circle and sent to the monasteries — states the program’s rationale: letters are to be studied so that the figures and tropes of the sacred page may be read rightly. It sets out the antithesis that right doing is better than right knowing, yet knowing comes first, and that one who pleases God by living rightly should please him also by speaking correctly.

The seven arts as the “seven pillars” of wisdom: De grammatica

…I think that ye should be led by the steps of erudition from lower to higher things until your wings gradually grow stronger, so that ye may mount on them to view the loftier visions of the pure ether.

We have read how Wisdom herself saith by the mouth of Solomon, ‘Wisdom hath builded her house, she hath hewn out her seven pillars.’… yet Wisdom is also built upon the seven pillars of liberal letters, and it can in no wise afford us access to any perfect knowledge, unless it be set upon these seven pillars, or ascents.

— Alcuin, De grammatica (the dialogue prologue), trans. Andrew Fleming West, Alcuin and the Rise of the Christian Schools (Scribner’s), pp. 94–96.3 In his textbooks Alcuin orders the arts into a ladder: grammar and its sisters are the lower rungs by which the pupil climbs toward the higher vision. Applying Proverbs 9:1 — the verse Cassiodorus had also applied to the arts — he identifies Wisdom’s “seven pillars” with the seven liberal arts, without which, he writes, “perfect knowledge” has no access. Compare the graded course of study Gregory Thaumaturgus describes in Origen’s classroom.

A Christian Athens in Francia

If only there were many who would follow the illustrious desire of your intent, perchance a new, nay, a more excellent Athens might be founded in Frankland; for our Athens, being ennobled with the mastership of Christ the Lord, would surpass all the wisdom of the studies of the Academy. That was instructed only in the Platonic disciplines and had fame for its culture in the seven arts, but ours being enriched beyond [that]…

— Alcuin, letter to Charlemagne (Epistola 170 in Dümmler’s numbering, c. 799), trans. West, Alcuin and the Rise of the Christian Schools, pp. 48–49.4 Alcuin proposes not to abandon the seven arts of the Academy but to inherit and surpass them, because in a Christian school they are “ennobled with the mastership of Christ.” Compare Augustine’s gold of Egypt and Jerome’s captive woman, images for taking over the classical learning for Christian use; here the proposal is addressed to Charlemagne.

Notes

  • Authorship of the capitularies. The Admonitio generalis and De litteris colendis were issued in Charlemagne’s name and drafted within his court circle; the exact wording is not certainly Alcuin’s own pen. They belong to the reform program he served, and are cited here as the Carolingian educational legislation, not as compositions provably his. His personal voice on the arts is clearest in the dialogue textbooks (De grammatica, De rhetorica et virtutibus, De dialectica).
  • A line attributed to Alcuin. The line sometimes quoted as Alcuin’s — “It is now time that we should hasten through the text of the book we have in hand under seven other titles suitable to secular letters” — is West’s translation of Cassiodorus (the Institutiones preface), which Alcuin builds upon; it is not Alcuin’s own.
  • Pupils. Alcuin’s student Rabanus Maurus (Rabanus Maurus on this timeline) wrote a handbook for the clergy a generation later, reproducing Augustine’s “spoiling the Egyptians” almost word for word.
