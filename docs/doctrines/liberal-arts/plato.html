Plato on the purpose of the liberal arts

Dates: c. 375 BC · Wikipedia
Summary: Plato (c. 428–348 BC) is the earliest witness on this timeline. In the Republic he sets the schooling of the guardians on two pillars — music (in the broad Greek sense of the arts of the Muses) for the soul and gymnastic for the body — and defines education not as the pouring of information into an empty mind but as the turning of the whole soul away from the world of becoming toward true being and the Good. In his account learning is ordered to contemplation, not to use.

Claims & evidence

Education rests on “music for the soul and gymnastic for the body”

And what shall be their education? Can we find a better than the traditional sort?—and this has two divisions, gymnastic for the body, and music for the soul.

— Republic II (376e), trans. Benjamin Jowett, source. Under “music” Plato includes literature (“When you speak of music, do you include literature or not? I do”), so that the formation of the soul runs through stories, poetry, harmony, and letters. The double curriculum is aimed at producing a certain kind of person, harmonious and just, not a certain kind of worker.

Education is the turning of the soul, not the implanting of knowledge

certain professors of education must be wrong when they say that they can put a knowledge into the soul which was not there before, like sight into blind eyes.

our argument shows that the power and capacity of learning exists in the soul already; and that just as the eye was unable to turn from darkness to light without the whole body, so too the instrument of knowledge can only by the movement of the whole soul be turned from the world of becoming into that of being, and learn by degrees to endure the sight of being, and of the brightest and best of being, or in other words, of the good.

— Republic VII (518b–d), trans. Benjamin Jowett, source. For Plato education is a conversion (periagōgē) — “some art which will effect conversion… not implanting the faculty of sight, for that exists already” — a reorientation of the mind’s eye from shadows to the Good. Higher study is, on this account, not a technique for getting on in the world but the ascent of the soul toward what is highest, valuable in itself.

Notes

  • Plato is not a Christian witness but a pagan source read by Christian writers. Later entries on this timeline — Clement, Basil, Augustine — also hold that learning is ordered to contemplation of the highest good and that the arts are steps the soul climbs; Augustine reads the liberal arts as steps toward the vision of God.
  • His pupil Aristotle draws an explicit division of liberal from illiberal (banausic) occupations; Latin writers (Cicero, Varro, Quintilian) write of the artes liberales.
