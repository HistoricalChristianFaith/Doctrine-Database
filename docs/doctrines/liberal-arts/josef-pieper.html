Josef Pieper — leisure, contemplation, and the liberal arts against the world of total work

Dates: c. 1948 (Muße und Kult, two lectures given in 1947 and published in 1948; English as Leisure: The Basis of Culture, trans. Alexander Dru, 1952, with an introduction by T. S. Eliot; a later translation by Gerald Malsbary, 1998). Pieper 1904–1997, German Catholic philosopher in the Thomist tradition · Wikipedia
Summary: Pieper mounted the twentieth century’s deepest philosophical defence of learning pursued for its own sake. Recovering the classical and Thomist meaning of leisure (Greek scholē) as receptive contemplation — not idleness — he argued that the very word “school” derives from it, that the “world of total work” threatens to swallow every human activity into utility, and that the artes liberales are precisely the acts of the mind “justified in themselves,” needing no useful payoff to legitimate them.

Claims & evidence

Leisure is contemplative affirmation, not idleness — against “the world of total work”

Pieper’s target is a civilization that recognizes no human good but work and its products. Against it he restores the older sense of leisure as a stillness that receives reality:

Leisure lives on affirmation. It is not the same as the absence of activity; it is not the same thing as quiet, or even as an inner quiet. It is rather like the stillness in the conversation of lovers, which is fed by their oneness.

Leisure is a form of that stillness that is the necessary preparation for accepting reality; only the person who is still can hear, and whoever is not still, cannot hear.

— Leisure: The Basis of Culture (trans. Malsbary).1 The danger is that work ceases to be one province of life and becomes the whole of it:

…the world of work begins to become—threatens to become—our only world, to the exclusion of all else. The demands of the working world grow ever more total, grasping ever more completely the whole of human existence.

— Leisure: The Basis of Culture (trans. Malsbary).2

“School” itself means “leisure”

The Greek word for leisure (σχολή) is the origin of Latin scola, German Schule, English school. The name for the institutions of education and learning means ‘leisure.’

— Leisure: The Basis of Culture (trans. Malsbary).3 The etymology carries the whole argument: that a place of learning is, by its very name, a place of leisure — of studies pursued outside the order of work and wages — is Pieper’s philological proof that the ancients ordered education to something beyond utility. As he renders Aristotle’s paradox, “We work in order to be at leisure,” a statement that “appears almost immoral to the man and woman of the world of ‘total work.’”

The liberal arts are “justified in themselves”; the servile arts serve an end outside themselves

Following Aquinas’s commentary on Aristotle’s Metaphysics, Pieper grounds the ancient distinction in the end of the act — knowing versus useful making:

Thomas Aquinas provides some conceptual clarification in his Commentary on Aristotle’s Metaphysics: ‘Every art is called liberal which is ordered to knowing; those which are ordered to some utility to be attained through action are called servile arts.’

‘Liberal arts,’ therefore, are ways of human action which have their justification in themselves; ‘servile arts’ are ways of human action that have a purpose outside of themselves… The liberality or ‘freedom’ of the liberal arts consists in their not being disposable for purposes, that they do not need to be legitimated by a social function, by being ‘work.’

— Leisure: The Basis of Culture (trans. Malsbary).4 This is the same criterion Aristotle and Aquinas drew — the line falls on the end, not the subject — now turned as a critique of a modern order that would legitimate no study it cannot turn into work.

Philosophy, pursued for its own sake, is the most liberal of the arts

Pieper reaches for Newman to state the point, then makes it his own:

Philosophy can be called the most liberal of the liberal arts. ‘Knowledge is most truly free when it is philosophical knowledge,’ Newman said.

— Leisure: The Basis of Culture (trans. Malsbary).5 The whole question, he insists, is “whether there is still an area of human action, or human existence as such, that does not have its justification by being incorporated into a five-year plan” — whether, in short, the liberal arts may still exist. “The architects of the total world of work,” he answers, “would have to answer, ‘No.’”6

Notes

  • Which translation. Pieper’s book is best known in English through Alexander Dru’s 1952 version (with Eliot’s introduction), where the etymology famously reads “‘School’ does not, properly speaking, mean school, but leisure.” The quotations above are taken from the later, freely verifiable Gerald Malsbary translation (St. Augustine’s Press, 1998); the wording differs slightly from Dru’s but the argument is the same.
  • Pieper and the tradition. Pieper is the philosophical bridge from the medieval and Thomist ordering of the arts to the modern defence of them: his scholē is Seneca’s and Aristotle’s leisured contemplation, his liberal/servile distinction is Aquinas’s, and his non-utilitarian defence stands beside Newman’s. He supplies the deepest twentieth-century answer to the question with which this timeline opened: whether learning that earns nothing is worth pursuing.
