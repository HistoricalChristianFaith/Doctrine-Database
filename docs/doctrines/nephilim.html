The Sons of God and the Nephilim (Genesis 6)

Genesis 6:1–4 reports that the "sons of God" saw that the "daughters of men" were fair, took wives, and that the Nephilim — rendered giants (gigantes) by the Septuagint — "were on the earth in those days, and also afterward." Two questions divide its interpreters:

  1. Who are the "sons of God"? Fallen angels (the "Watchers"), or the righteous line of Seth?
  2. What are the Nephilim? Hybrid offspring of an angelic–human union, or mighty, violent men of renown?

The earliest extant interpretations read the passage angelically: in 1 Enoch the sons of God are rebel angels, the Nephilim are their giant offspring, and the giants' spirits become evil spirits on the earth.1 The Septuagint (c. 250 BC) renders the Nephilim gígantes ("giants") and, in many copies, the sons of God as "angels of God."2 A second reading identifies the sons of God with the godly line of Seth and the daughters of men with the line of Cain, making the Nephilim mighty or violent men; its earliest extant statement is in Julius Africanus (c. 240),3 and Augustine (c. 420) adopted it, rejecting the angelic interpretation and the apocryphal Enochic "fables."4 Cassian, Cyril of Alexandria, Philoxenus, Bede and Aquinas, in their entries below, hold the Sethite reading; George Syncellus (c. 810) copies the Enochic Watcher story into his chronicle. Among modern writers, Fr. Stephen De Young argues for recovering the angelic reading and connects the Nephilim to the later "giant" tribes (Rephaim, Anakim) and Israel's conquest.5

Who holds what. Among the ancient witnesses below, the angelic reading is held by the Second Temple Jewish writings (1 Enoch, Jubilees, Philo alongside his allegory, Josephus) and by Christian writers from Justin and Athenagoras to Lactantius, Eusebius and Ambrose; the Sethite reading by Julius Africanus, Ephrem, the Genesis homilies of Chrysostom, Augustine and the later Latin, Greek and Syriac writers named above. Among modern writers the angelic reading is held by the Old Testament scholars Margaret Barker (Methodist) and Michael S. Heiser, by Fr. Stephen De Young (Orthodox) and Doug Wilson (Reformed). Taylor Marshall (Roman Catholic) sets out the Enochic Watcher reading and its New Testament use, calls the Sethite reading "plausible" and "the accepted tradition of Catholicism," and in 2023 reads the Watcher legend as a Maccabean allegory.6 De Young (in the Lord of Spirits podcast) holds that the angelic reading was the "universal view of the first three centuries, almost four centuries, of the Church," that the Sethite reading originated in Rabbinic Judaism (which had by then rejected the Enochic literature as "too Christian") and entered Christianity through Julius Africanus, Ephrem and Augustine, and that the later Fathers rejected a misunderstanding — literal angel–human interbreeding — rather than the ancient claim, which he locates in a pagan sacral-kingship fertility ritual.7 Questions these claims raise are set out among the disputed readings below: the rabbinic origin of the Sethite reading, Augustine's case against the angelic reading, and the reception of 1 Enoch as Scripture.

Related doctrines: Primeval history (Genesis 6–9) — see also the historicity of Noah's Flood (the same Genesis 6–9 narrative; several witnesses below, and the disputed identity of the "sons of God," recur there) · the dating of Noah's Flood (the biblical chronologies of the Flood and their reception).

In Michael S. Heiser's The Unseen Realm (2015) the Genesis 6 episode is the second of three rebellions — after the serpent's revolt in Eden and before Babel, where he places God's disinheriting of the nations and their allotment to the rebel elohim (Deuteronomy 32:8–9).8

Timeline

Book of Enoch / 1 Enoch (c. 300 BC)

1 Enoch gives the fullest ancient form of the angelic reading: two hundred angels ("Watchers"), led by Semjaza, descended on Mount Hermon, took human wives, and "bare great giants," while teaching humanity sorcery, metallurgy, and astrology.1 It teaches that when the giants die, "the spirits of the giants" become "evil spirits upon the earth" — the origin of demons.9

The Septuagint (LXX) (c. 250 BC)

The Septuagint, the Greek Torah of Alexandria and the version the New Testament quotes, renders the Nephilim as gígantes — "giants" — and, in many copies (so Augustine testifies), the "sons of God" as "angels of God."2 As the earliest extant translational interpretation, it reads Genesis 6 as angelic beings begetting giants.

Sirach / Ecclesiasticus (c. 180 BC)

Sirach (the Wisdom of Ben Sira), reciting God's historic judgments on the proud, names "the old giants… who fell away in the strength of their foolishness," toward whom God "was not pacified" — like Wisdom (both deuterocanonical in Catholic and Orthodox Bibles, among the Apocrypha in Protestant ones), a witness to the destroyed giants of Genesis 6.10

The Book of Giants (Qumran) (c. 150 BC)

The Book of Giants, a distinct Enochic work preserved in as many as ten Aramaic copies among the Dead Sea Scrolls, retells the Genesis 6 event from the giants' own side: the offspring of the Watchers and human women, "giants and monsters" who "were seeking to devour many," troubled by dream-visions of the coming Flood, who send the giant Mahway to petition Enoch and are refused.11 It names the Mesopotamian heroes Gilgamesh and Humbaba among the giants, a link between the Nephilim and Babylonian tradition that Fr. Stephen De Young draws on ("the Book of the Giants … identifies Gilgamesh as one of the Nephilim").12

Book of Jubilees (c. 100 BC)

Jubilees retells the same event: "the angels of God … took themselves wives … and they bare unto them sons and they were giants." God bound the angels in the depths of the earth and had their giant sons slay one another by the sword.13 After the Flood, when the dead giants' spirits afflict mankind, the chief spirit Mastema wins God's leave for "the tenth part" to remain and torment the wicked — a 90% / 10% schema that Fr. Stephen De Young cites and connects with the Gospel demons' cry, "Have you come to torment us before the time?"14

The Wisdom of Solomon (c. 50 BC)

Wisdom names "the proud giants" as those who perished in Noah's flood,15 and traces idolatry to the deified dead — a dead man "honoured… as a god" with "ceremonies and sacrifices" — a cult that ran to child sacrifice and "secret ceremonies."16 Fr. Stephen De Young connects this cult with the giants.5

Philo of Alexandria (c. 50)

Philo identifies the angels of Genesis 6 with the "souls" or "demons" that fill the air, and states that "these giants were sprung from a combined procreation of two natures, namely, from angels and mortal women."17 He also reads the passage allegorically — men "born of earth," "of heaven," and "of God" — treating giant partly as a moral type of the earth-bound soul (Nimrod).18

The New Testament: Jude & 2 Peter (c. AD 60–90)

In the New Testament, Jude speaks of "the angels which kept not their first estate" reserved "in everlasting chains under darkness," and quotes Enoch, "the seventh from Adam," as a prophet;19 2 Peter sets "the angels that sinned," cast into Tartarus, immediately beside Noah and the flood.20 Justin, Clement, Tertullian and Origen, below, connect the fallen angels with the Genesis 6 event. 1 Peter 3:19's "spirits in prison," which De Young also cites, is read as human by the patristic commentators — Christ's descent to the dead, or his preaching through Noah — and as the imprisoned Watchers by De Young and by modern commentators including William Dalton, Peter Davids and Thomas Schreiner; Richard Bauckham is also named as holding that reading (⚠ unverified).21

Josephus (c. 100)

Josephus records that "many angels of God accompanied with women, and begat sons that proved unjust," comparing them to the giants of Greek legend.22

Justin Martyr (c. 165)

Justin holds that the angels appointed over humanity "were captivated by love of women, and begat children who are those that are called demons," who then enslaved the human race and were mistaken by the poets for gods.23

Athenagoras of Athens (c. 177)

Athenagoras, pleading the Christians' cause before Marcus Aurelius, reads Genesis 6 as angelic history: angels set over matter "fell into impure love of virgins," and "of these lovers of virgins … were begotten those who are called giants," whose souls are "the demons who wander about the world."24

Irenaeus (c. 202)

Irenaeus writes that "angels were united with the daughters of the race of mankind; and they bore to them sons who for their exceeding greatness were called giants," and that the angels brought "teachings of wickedness" — leading to the flood.25 He also offers "Titan" as his leading candidate for the name of 666, tying the Antichrist to the Titan/giant tradition.26

Clement of Alexandria (c. 215)

Clement refers to "the angels who … having sunk into pleasures, told to the women the secrets which had come to their knowledge" — the angelic reading, applied to the origin of forbidden arts.27

Tertullian (c. 220)

Tertullian speaks of "those angels … who rushed from heaven on the daughters of men," who taught women metallurgy, the properties of herbs, enchantments, astrology, and cosmetics — and whom Christians "are destined to judge."28 He also treats the Book of Enoch as inspired Scripture, defending its survival of the Flood through Noah — the earliest extant patristic answer to that objection.28

Julius Africanus (c. 240)

Julius Africanus gives the earliest extant statement of the Sethite reading: the "sons of God" are "the descendants of Seth … on account of the righteous men and patriarchs," and the daughters are the line of Cain — though he records the angelic view as the alternative.3

Commodian (c. 250)

Commodian, in catechetical Latin verse, keeps the whole Enochic sequence: angels "sent down" despised God's laws, were turned by "the beauty of women," and "from their seed giants are said to have been born"; the giants taught the arts, and their dead spirits "now subvert many bodies" and are worshipped "as gods."29

Origen of Alexandria (c. 253)

Origen groups the "sons of God" of Genesis 6 with the fallen beings who "left their own dwelling" and "did not preserve their own principality," reading the episode through the lens of angelic fall.30

Lactantius (c. 310)

Lactantius, in his Divine Institutes, makes the fall the "fountain of errors" of paganism: God's guardian angels were "polluted … by intercourse with women" and "fell to the earth," and their offspring — "neither angels nor men, but bearing a kind of mixed nature" — became the earthly demons, "the authors of all the evils which are done."31

Eusebius of Caesarea (c. 339)

Eusebius takes the giants born of "the angels of God" and the daughters of men to be daemons whose "spirits have been deified by the subsequent generations of men" — the pagan gods of Greek legend.32

Ephrem the Syrian (c. 373)

Ephrem holds the Sethite reading: "[Moses] called the sons of Seth 'sons of God,' … The beautiful daughters of men … were the daughters of Cain."33

Basil of Caesarea (c. 379)

Basil does not adjudicate the origin question but uses the giants morally, as types of prideful strength: "Such was the arrogance of the giants because of their strength. Such also was the God-defying pride of the witless Goliath."34

Nemesius of Emesa (c. 390)

Nemesius describes a fall of some angels: "of the incorporeal beings, only angels fell away, and not all of them, but some only, that inclined to things below." He does not name Genesis 6 in this passage.35

Ambrose of Milan (c. 397)

Ambrose explicitly rejects the "children of the earth" reading of the poets and "asserts that they were generated from angels and women," the name giant expressing their bodily magnitude — though he draws a moral lesson about flesh over soul.36

John Chrysostom (c. 407)

Homily 22 on Genesis, transmitted under Chrysostom's name (its authorship is disputed), rejects the angelic reading as "blasphemy" and "the height of absurdity" — angels are nowhere called "sons of God," and being incorporeal they cannot marry — and identifies the sons of God as the descendants of Seth and Enosh.37

Jerome (c. 420)

Jerome reports that Aquila rendered the phrase "sons of gods," understanding "the Holy Ones or Angels," and judges that "it is fitting for the name of falling ones to apply to angels."38,39

Augustine of Hippo (c. 420)

Augustine reads the passage without the angels: the "sons of God" were "the sons of Seth" who "sunk into this community when they forsook righteousness," the giants were real but ordinary men, and the Enochic writings "about the giants, saying that their fathers were not men" are apocryphal "fables" without canonical authority.4 He leaves open only whether some aerial spirits can assault women, while denying that God's holy angels fell this way.40

John Cassian (c. 435)

Cassian follows the Sethite reading: the "seed of Seth … was by reason of its sanctity termed 'angels of God,' or … 'sons of God,'" the daughters were of Cain's line, and their offspring "were termed giants by reason of the size of their bodies and their cruelty."41

Cyril of Alexandria (c. 444)

Cyril reads the "sons of the gods" (Aquila) or "sons of the rulers" (Symmachus) as "the descendants of Seth and of Enosh … because of the piety and godliness which was in them."42

Philoxenus of Mabbug (c. 523)

Philoxenus assumes the Sethite reading, treating the episode as the conquest of "the children of Seth" by "the lust of the belly," for which "they were rejected from the household of God."43

Bede (c. 735)

Bede transmits the Sethite reading to the medieval West: "sons of God" are the lineage of Seth, "men" the offspring of Cain; the giants were "men with immense bodies and excessive power," and he notes the Hebrew "fallen ones."44,45

George Syncellus (c. 810)

George Syncellus, a monk of Constantinople, transcribes long Greek extracts of 1 Enoch's "Book of the Watchers" into the antediluvian section of his universal chronicle, treating them as history: the angels (Egregori) descended in the days of Jared on Mount Hermon, took the daughters of men, and "bare … great Giants. Now the Giants begat the Naphilim."46 He is the latest pre-modern witness on this timeline to the angelic reading, and the source through which both the Greek Enoch and Julius Africanus's Sethite fragment survive. De Young calls 1 Enoch an "apostolic apocryphon," crediting the description to St. Nikephoros I, Patriarch of Constantinople (d. 828);47 Nikephoros's Stichometry lists Enoch among the apocrypha of the Old Testament.48

Bonaventure (c. 1274)

Bonaventure uses the passage typologically rather than to settle its identity: the sons of God taking the daughters of men, alongside Lamech's bigamy, he reads as prefiguring the lawlessness that brings judgment and points to the Antichrist.49

Thomas Aquinas (c. 1274)

Aquinas holds the Sethite reading, citing Augustine — the "sons of God" are the line of Seth, and "they were not all giants" — and also sets out the incubus tradition: demons do procure intercourse with women, but any offspring "is not the child of a demon, but of a man," begotten from human seed the demon transfers.50 On 1 Peter 3:19 he reads the "spirits in prison" as human, preferring Augustine's "preached through Noah" reading.51

Margaret Barker (c. 1988)

Margaret Barker, a British Methodist Old-Testament scholar and founder of "Temple Theology," reads the "sons of God" of Genesis 6 as real fallen angels — the "fuller version" of the creation-and-fall story, of which Genesis preserves only a fragment — and identifies those fallen angels with the apostate priesthood ("the priests… were the angels in the temple"). She holds that the angel-beings are real and were edited out of the canonical text.52 Taylor Marshall, in his 2023 podcast, paraphrases her as reading the Nephilim symbolically, as a figure of human wickedness.6

Michael S. Heiser (c. 2015)

Michael S. Heiser (1963–2023), an Old-Testament and Semitic-languages scholar, set out a "Divine Council" reading of the angelic interpretation; De Young and Wilson, below, also read the sons of God as divine beings. In The Unseen Realm (2015) and Reversing Hermon (2017) he holds that the "sons of God" are divine beings (not the line of Seth), the Nephilim their giant offspring (the word from Aramaic naphila, "giant," matching the Septuagint's gígantes), and the Babylonian apkallu sages the background of 1 Enoch's Watchers; he extends the theme to the post-flood giant clans (Rephaim, Anakim, Og of Bashan) and reads the conquest of Canaan as a holy war against them. Like De Young he holds that the sons of God are real spirit beings. In The Unseen Realm he holds that reading ancient kingship into Genesis 6 is anachronistic, and rests his case on the textual and comparative-Semitic evidence; De Young's later sacral-kingship mechanism (c. 2020) is a kingship reading of Genesis 6.8

Fr. Stephen De Young (with Fr. Andrew Stephen Damick) (2018–2026)

De Young argues for recovering the ancient supernatural reading: the Nephilim are the offspring of demonic spirits and human women, both giant and tyrannical, continuous with the Babylonian apkallu traditions preserved in 1 Enoch and the Book of the Giants. He extends the theme to the later "giant" tribes — the Rephaim (Og of Bashan), the Anakim, the Emim — and reads Israel's conquest as a war against demonic powers, fulfilled when David's men slew the last giants of Gath.5 In the Lord of Spirits episode "Land of Giants" (with Fr. Andrew Stephen Damick) he adds the mechanism — a pagan sacral-kingship fertility ritual producing a child of "two fathers," so that, he argues, the objection that demons cannot interbreed misses what the tradition claimed — and a "demonization" theology in which the giants are an inverse of theosis and their dead spirits become the "unclean spirits" of the Gospels.7 In a later long-form interview on the Tucker Carlson Show (2026) he ties the antediluvian pattern to the Sumerian King List (its seventh king, inventor of the solar calendar, mirroring Enoch — "the seventh from Adam," who lives 365 years), develops a "ladder" by which a man becomes a fully "demonized" giant, and — while reading the giants as ritual kings and demonized men rather than literal fossils — argues the same cult persists covertly today.53

Dr. Taylor Marshall (2018–2023)

Dr. Taylor Marshall, a Roman-Catholic writer of a Thomist cast, lays out the Enochic Watcher-legend (the Watchers copulate with human women and beget the Nephilim), points to the New Testament's use of it — Jude directly quotes 1 Enoch and 2 Peter borrows its word "Tartarus" — and reads the Wisdom of Solomon as already presupposing the giant-tradition ("the proud giants" perishing in the Flood, Wis 14:6). He proposes no mechanism for the begetting, and he commends the angelic reading's antiquity while leaving its literal history open ("regardless of whether Jude and 2 Peter believed the… Watcher-Angel legend," they wield it as a teaching paradigm against heretics); in the same post he writes that Augustine and Aquinas "both had not read 1 Enoch."54 Presenting the Sethite reading as "the accepted tradition of Catholicism" and calling it "plausible," he asks whether Augustine and Aquinas would have revised it had they possessed 1 Enoch and the Dead Sea Scrolls. In the same podcast he reads the Watcher episode as a Maccabean allegory of the Greek Titanomachy, with the Nephilim as the children of Greek–Jewish mixed marriages.6

Doug Wilson (c. 2021)

Doug Wilson, a Reformed/CREC pastor, is another modern proponent of the angelic reading: the "sons of God" (bene elohim) are celestial beings — never the line of Seth — and the Nephilim their giant offspring (Gen 6:1–8). Where De Young proposes a ritual mechanism, Wilson holds a literal-embodiment one — the angels "relinquished their celestial bodies" (Jude 6's habitation; cf. 2 Cor 5:2) to take human wives — and reads 1 Peter 3:18–20 as Christ heralding victory (not offering the gospel) to those angels imprisoned in Tartarus, framing the Flood as judgment on a "genetic-engineering" / "being-as-God" rebellion of which he holds modern bioethics is the continuation. He writes that the Sethite reading "doesn't really come into its own until the modern era."55
