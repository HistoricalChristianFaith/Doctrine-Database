Does Romans 5:12 (eph’ hō / in quo) teach that all sinned “in Adam”?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — the realist reading (all sinned in Adam, inheriting a real guilt) has a genuine case in the passage’s corporate logic and a weighty Latin tradition behind it, but its sharpest form rests on a Latin “in whom” that renders a Greek eph’ hō most naturally read “because”; the Greek tradition takes the inheritance as mortality, not guilt, and the corporate-solidarity teaching of Romans 5 survives on either construal. Strong by Latin/Augustinian lights, denied by the Greek, with no consensus winner · advanced by Ambrosiaster and Augustine, against Chrysostom and the Antiochenes

The claim

…all have sinned in Adam as though in a lump. For, being corrupted by sin himself, all those whom he fathered were born under sin. For that reason we are all sinners, because we all descend from him.

— Ambrosiaster, Commentary on Paul’s Epistles, on Romans 5:12, link. The asserted reading: Paul’s clause “in quo [eph’ hō] all sinned” means all sinned in Adam — a real, inherited implication in his sin (not merely inherited mortality, and not merely his bad example), so that the newborn bears a guilt drawn from its seminal head. This is the exegetical engine of the Western doctrine of original sin as inherited reatus.

Who advances it

The reading is Latin in its classic form. Ambrosiaster (c. 375) fixes it: in quo, id est in Adam, omnes peccaverunt. Augustine builds his whole anti-Pelagian case on it — men “bound by the chain of death in that one man in whom all men sinned” — citing “Ambrose” (this same commentary, under its false attribution) as patristic warrant. It passes into the Vulgate (in quo omnes peccaverunt), the councils of Carthage 418 and Orange 529 (“by propagation, not imitation”), Aquinas (who parses in quo as “in the first man… all men were in him as in their first origin”), and the Reformers’ imputation. Notably it is not only Latin: the Greek Theophylact of Ohrid also glosses the phrase “all sinned in Adam” — though he draws inherited mortality from it, not guilt.1

The evidence for

Three things genuinely support the realist reading.2

  • The passage’s own logic is corporate. Romans 5:12–19 is built on a one-man/many structure: by “one man’s disobedience many were made sinners,” as by “one… obedience shall many be made righteous” (v. 19); “judgment was by one to condemnation” (v. 16). Whatever the precise force of eph’ hō in v. 12, Paul plainly makes Adam a representative head in whom the many are implicated — the parallel with Christ (in whom the many are justified) is the whole argument, and it points to more than a coincidence of everyone happening to sin.
  • Seminal solidarity has scriptural form. The realist reading is not arbitrary: Hebrews 7:9–10 argues that Levi “paid tithes in Abraham… for he was yet in the loins of his father” — exactly the model Origen applies to Adam (“all men… were in the loins of Adam”). A biblical writer can speak of the posterity acting in the ancestor, so “all sinned in Adam” is a coherent biblical idiom, not a Latin invention.
  • The witnesses converge on a real inherited condition. Psalm 51:5 (“in sin did my mother conceive me”), Ephesians 2:3 (“by nature the children of wrath”), and Job 14:4 in the Septuagint were read together by Origen, Jerome, Augustine, and Aquinas as teaching an inherited iniquity drawn from Adam. That the newborn is not a moral blank is not an Augustinian novelty; it is broadly attested, East and West.

Adversarial assessment

What holds. Romans 5 teaches a real solidarity in Adam — a corporate fall with corporate consequences — on any reading of v. 12. That much is secure, and even the traditions that reject inherited guilt (the Christian East, most credobaptists) affirm an inherited sinful, death-bound condition in some form. If the question is only “does Adam’s sin reach his posterity?”, the answer of the passage is yes.

Where the sharpest form is overstated or imprecise.3

  • The grammar does not compel “in whom.” The Greek eph’ hō (ἐφ’ ὅ) is a preposition plus a relative that is neuter or masculine, not the feminine that would agree with “sin” (ἡ ἗μαρτία) — and, more to the point, the phrase is idiomatic and most naturally causal: “because” / “inasmuch as all sinned” (so Fitzmyer, Cranfield, and most modern commentators). To make ὅ a personal relative reaching back over five words to “one man” (Adam) is possible but strained; the Latin in quo flattens the idiom into a personal “in whom,” and it is that Latin — not the Greek — that the seminal-guilt reading leans on. Augustine, who read Greek haltingly, built on the Latin and on “Ambrose.”
  • The whole Greek tradition read it the other way — and drew mortality, not guilt. This is not a modern discovery. Chrysostom construes the clause “for that all have sinned” and names the consequence as mortality: Adam “having once fallen, even they that had not eaten of the tree did from him, all of them, become mortal.” Theodoret: each “receives the sentence of death not because of the sin of his first ancestor but because of his own sin.” Cyril, Oecumenius, and Gennadius run the same way (the last conceding that children die “only because of Adam’s condemnation” — i.e. of its penalty, death, not of inherited guilt). So the “in whom” reading is contested precisely by the fathers who read Paul in his own language.
  • The corporate teaching survives, but the specific step does not follow. Granting solidarity in Adam does not by itself yield an imputed guilt that damns the newborn and that only sacramental remission can lift. The move from “death passed upon all” to “a personal reatus inherited at conception” is a theological inference layered on the text, and it is exactly the step the East declines while keeping the solidarity. Even Ambrosiaster, who coined “in a lump,” distinguished the inherited bodily-death sentence from the “second death” each incurs “by our own sins.”

Assessment

Contested. As a reading of Romans 5, the realist “all sinned in Adam” has a real anchor: the passage’s one-man/many logic and its Adam–Christ parallel do point to a corporate implication in Adam’s sin, and the seminal idiom has scriptural precedent (Hebrews 7). But its sharpest form — an inherited, personally-damning guilt — rests in its classic presentation on the Latin in quo (“in whom”), which over-specifies a Greek eph’ hō that is most naturally causal, and it is denied by the entire Greek exegetical tradition, which reads the inheritance as mortality and lays each person’s condemnation on his own sins. The corporate solidarity of Romans 5 stands on any construal; the further step to imputed guilt is a theological inference the text permits but does not compel, strong by Latin lights and rejected by Greek ones — the signature of a contested claim. One proponent imprecision attaches to the strongest advocates: Augustine (and, downstream, the sharpest apologetic restatements) present the Latin “in whom” as if it settled the Greek, which it does not — flagged on his page and Ambrosiaster’s. This is the exegetical root of the practical infant-baptism “inherited sin” argument (also contested), which turns the same crux to the question of the newborn’s need of the font.
