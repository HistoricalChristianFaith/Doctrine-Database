Ambrosiaster on original sin

Dates: c. 375 (“Ambrosiaster” is the name given to an anonymous Latin commentator at Rome, writing c. 366–384, whose Commentary on Paul’s Epistles long circulated under the name of Ambrose) · Wikipedia
Summary: Ambrosiaster is the exegetical hinge of the whole Western doctrine of original sin. Commenting on Romans 5:12 in a Latin text that renders Paul’s Greek eph’ hō (ἐφ’ ὅ) as in quo — “in whom all sinned” — he takes the “whom” to be Adam and draws the seminal, realist conclusion: all sinned in Adam “as though in a lump,” because a corrupted head fathered a corrupted race. This is the reading Augustine would lean on almost verbatim. It is also, as it happens, grammatically a mistranslation — Paul’s relative is neuter and the phrase most naturally causal (“because”) — so the load-bearing proof-text of Latin original sin rests, in its sharpest form, on a Latin construal the Greek does not compel. That crux is weighed on its own argument page; here is the witness who fixed the reading.

Claims & evidence

“All have sinned in Adam as though in a lump”

Ambrosiaster faces the obvious objection — that it was Eve, not Adam, who sinned first — and answers that Paul speaks of the race’s universal head, not the first individual transgressor. From the corruption of that head the whole progeny is born corrupted:

Paul said that all have sinned in Adam even though in fact it was Eve who sinned because he was not referring to the particular but to the universal. For it is clear that all have sinned in Adam as though in a lump. For, being corrupted by sin himself, all those whom he fathered were born under sin. For that reason we are all sinners, because we all descend from him.

— Ambrosiaster, Commentary on Paul’s Epistles, on Romans 5:12, source.1 “As though in a lump” (quasi in massa) is the germ of Augustine’s massa perditionis: the race is one corrupted mass in Adam. And the ground given is descent — “we are all sinners, because we all descend from him” — a transmission by propagation, not by imitation, the very distinction the councils would canonize against Pelagius.

The two deaths: bodily death from Adam, the “second death” from our own sins

Ambrosiaster is careful about what the descent transmits. Adam’s sin brought the sentence of bodily death on all; the “second death” of Gehenna, however, each incurs by his own sins, Adam’s fall only making it possible:

He lost God’s blessing because he transgressed and was made unworthy to eat of the tree of life. For that reason he had to die. Death is the separation of body and soul. There is another death as well, called the second death, which takes place in Gehenna. We do not suffer this death as a result of Adam’s sin, but his fall makes it possible for us to get it by our own sins.

— Ambrosiaster, on Romans 5:12.2 The nuance is worth marking: even the man who forged the Latin realist reading distinguishes the inherited penalty (the “sentence meted out in Adam,” loosed by Christ) from the damnation a person draws on himself “by our own sins.” The wholesale identification of the inherited condition with a personally-damning guilt is a further step, taken most fully by Augustine.

Notes

  • The in quo Augustine built on. Augustine did not read Greek fluently, and the Latin Bible he used carried the same in quo omnes peccaverunt that Ambrosiaster expounds; Augustine cites “Ambrose” (i.e. this commentary, under its false attribution) as a patristic authority for the reading. The line of dependence — Latin text → Ambrosiaster’s exegesis → Augustine’s massa and inherited reatus — is the backbone of the Western doctrine, and is exactly why the Greek/Latin divergence over eph’ hō matters so much.3
  • Mistranslation, not misreading. The point against the sharpest form of the reading is grammatical, not theological: Paul’s eph’ hō is most naturally “because” / “inasmuch as,” the relative neuter and causal, so “in whom” (masculine, referring to Adam) over-specifies the Greek. This does not by itself refute a real inherited condition — Romans 5 teaches a corporate solidarity on any construal — but it does mean the move from that solidarity to an imputed guilt cannot rest on the word in quo alone. See the Romans 5:12 argument.
  • The same voice, a different doctrine elsewhere. Ambrosiaster is the lone permissive Latin witness on divorce and remarriage (his comment on 1 Corinthians 7, later revived by Erasmus); here, on original sin, he is the most consequential. He appears on this wiki also as a perpetual-virginity witness.
