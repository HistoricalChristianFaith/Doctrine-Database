Ambrosiaster on original sin

Dates: c. 375 (“Ambrosiaster” is the name given to an anonymous Latin commentator at Rome, writing c. 366–384, whose Commentary on Paul’s Epistles long circulated under the name of Ambrose) · Wikipedia
Summary: Commenting on Romans 5:12 in a Latin text that renders Paul’s Greek eph’ hō (ἐφ’ ὅ) as in quo — “in whom all sinned” — Ambrosiaster takes the “whom” to be Adam and concludes that all sinned in Adam “as though in a lump,” because a corrupted head fathered a corrupted race. He also distinguishes the bodily death inherited from Adam from the “second death” each incurs by his own sins. Augustine cites this commentary, under the name of “Hilary,” for the “in whom” reading. The construal of the Greek phrase is disputed; see the notes below.

Claims & evidence

“All have sinned in Adam as though in a lump”

Ambrosiaster faces the objection — that it was Eve, not Adam, who sinned first — and answers that Paul speaks of the race’s universal head, not the first individual transgressor. From the corruption of that head the whole progeny is born corrupted:

Paul said that all have sinned in Adam even though in fact it was Eve who sinned because he was not referring to the particular but to the universal. For it is clear that all have sinned in Adam as though in a lump. For, being corrupted by sin himself, all those whom he fathered were born under sin. For that reason we are all sinners, because we all descend from him.

— Ambrosiaster, Commentary on Paul’s Epistles, on Romans 5:12, source.1 “As though in a lump” (quasi in massa) presents the race as one mass in Adam, and the ground given is descent: “we are all sinners, because we all descend from him.”

The two deaths: bodily death and the “second death”

Ambrosiaster distinguishes what the descent transmits. Adam’s sin brought the sentence of bodily death on all; the “second death” of Gehenna, however, each incurs by his own sins, Adam’s fall only making it possible:

He lost God’s blessing because he transgressed and was made unworthy to eat of the tree of life. For that reason he had to die. Death is the separation of body and soul. There is another death as well, called the second death, which takes place in Gehenna. We do not suffer this death as a result of Adam’s sin, but his fall makes it possible for us to get it by our own sins.

— Ambrosiaster, on Romans 5:12.2 Ambrosiaster thus distinguishes the inherited penalty (the “sentence meted out in Adam,” loosed by Christ) from the damnation a person incurs “by our own sins.”

Reception and debate

  • Augustine’s use of the commentary. The Latin Bible Augustine used carried the same in quo omnes peccaverunt that Ambrosiaster expounds. In Against Two Letters of the Pelagians Augustine quotes this commentary’s “wherein,” that is, in Adam, “all have sinned,” and “as it were in the mass,” attributing it to “the sainted Hilary.”3
  • The construal of eph’ hō. The Council of Trent (1546) cites Romans 5:12 as “in whom all have sinned.”4 Charles Hodge (Reformed, Princeton, 1864) takes eph’ hō as “because” (“All men die, because all sinned”) and reads “all sinned” through verses 18–19: “They sinned in Adam. His sin was regarded as theirs”; “the only possible way in which all men can be said to have sinned in Adam, is putatively.”5 Stéphane Harent (Catholic, 1911) writes that several Latin Fathers understood “in whom all have sinned” to mean all have sinned in Adam, an interpretation that “would be an extra proof of the thesis of original sin, but it is not necessary”; he accepts the translation “because all have sinned,” which “Modern exegesis, as well as the Greek Fathers, prefer,” and reads the clause through verse 19 (“by the disobedience of one man many were made sinners”).6 John Meyendorff (Orthodox, 1974) writes that the Latin rendering “was used in the West to justify the doctrine of guilt inherited from Adam” but that “such a meaning cannot be drawn from the original Greek,” eph’ hō being translatable as “because.” The Catechism of the Catholic Church (1992) quotes the verse as “because all men sinned,” beside verse 19 (“many… were made sinners”), and grounds the transmission of original sin on the “unity of the human race,” the whole race being in Adam “as one body of one man.”7
  • Elsewhere on the timelines. Ambrosiaster is also a witness on divorce and remarriage (his comment on 1 Corinthians 7, later cited by Erasmus) and on perpetual virginity.
