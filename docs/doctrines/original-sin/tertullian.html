Tertullian on original sin

Dates: c. 210 (Tertullian, c. 155–c. 220; Carthaginian presbyter, the first great Latin theologian. A Treatise on the Soul is usually dated c. 210–213) · Wikipedia
Summary: Tertullian supplies the Latin West with its first mechanism for the transmission of sin. Because he holds that each individual soul is propagated from the parents’ souls as the body is from their bodies (traducianism), the whole race descends from Adam as a single stock — and the soul carries with it a birth-fault, a vitium originis, an “antecedent and in a certain sense natural evil which arises from its corrupt origin.” Every soul is therefore “unclean” and “actively sinful” from birth until it is “born again in Christ.” This is a doctrine of inherited corruption a generation before the phrase “original sin,” and it is the root of the seminal-transmission theory Augustine will later systematize. Notably, Tertullian did not draw from it the urgency of infant baptism the Africans after him would: he counselled delaying a child’s baptism — the inherited stain and the age at which it should be washed were, for him, separable questions.

Claims & evidence

The soul is propagated from Adam “as its root” (traducianism)

Tertullian’s doctrine of transmission rests on his theory of the soul’s origin. Against those who held each soul freshly created, he taught that soul comes from soul as body from body, so that the race is one propagation flowing from the first man:

…how much more may that of a human being, whose soul (which may be compared with the nascent sprout of a tree) has been derived from Adam as its root, and has been propagated amongst his posterity by means of woman, to whom it has been entrusted for transmission…

— A Treatise on the Soul 19, source.1 The vine-shoot image (tradux) gives the theory its name. Adam and Eve “amalgamated and mixed their proper seminal rudiments in one, and ever afterwards communicated to the human race the normal mode of its propagation… from the one primeval man comes the entire outflow and redundance of men’s souls.” If the soul descends from Adam, then whatever befell Adam’s soul is carried in the stock.2

The vitium originis: “the corruption of our nature is another nature”

What is carried is a real fault of origin. Tertullian distinguishes the evil that later assails the soul from outside (the tempter’s work) from an antecedent, quasi-natural evil bred in the soul by its descent from a corrupted head:

There is, then, besides the evil which supervenes on the soul from the intervention of the evil spirit, an antecedent, and in a certain sense natural, evil which arises from its corrupt origin. For, as we have said before, the corruption of our nature is another nature having a god and father of its own, namely the author of (that) corruption. Still there is a portion of good in the soul… Just as no soul is without sin, so neither is any soul without seeds of good.

— A Treatise on the Soul 41.3 “The corruption of our nature is another nature” is the phrase later theologians would echo. Two things are worth marking. Tertullian does not say the fault destroys the soul’s created goodness — “no soul is without seeds of good” — so his is a corruption, not the total ruin later Augustinianism would press. And he traces the corruption to a hostile author, framing the birth-fault in the sharp dualist idiom of his anti-Gnostic milieu.

Every soul “unclean… actively sinful” from birth until reborn

The conclusion is that every human being, simply by being born of Adam, begins in a state of defilement that only the new birth removes:

Every soul, then, by reason of its birth, has its nature in Adam until it is born again in Christ; moreover, it is unclean all the while that it remains without this regeneration; and because unclean, it is actively sinful, and suffuses even the flesh (by reason of their conjunction) with its own shame.

— A Treatise on the Soul 40.4 This is as clear a statement of an inherited sinful condition as the pre-Nicene West produces: the newborn’s uncleanness is not a metaphor for future sins but a present state — “in Adam until… born again in Christ” — requiring regeneration to reverse. The vocabulary (“in Adam,” the flesh “of sin,” new birth by water) is the raw material the fourth-century doctrine will refine.

Notes

  • Inherited corruption, yes; the urgency of infant baptism, no. It would be natural to expect Tertullian to reason, as Cyprian soon would, from the newborn’s uncleanness to hastening it to the font. He does the opposite: in On Baptism he counsels delaying a child’s baptism — “let them come while they are growing up… why does the innocent period of life hasten to the remission of sins?” The apparent tension is instructive: for Tertullian the inherited vitium and the pastoral question of when to baptize are distinct, and one can hold a strong doctrine of birth-corruption while preferring adult baptism. The later African tradition welded the two together; Tertullian shows they need not be.5
  • Traducianism as the engine. Tertullian’s account of how sin passes — through the soul propagated from Adam — ties the doctrine of original sin to the disputed question of the origin of the soul. Traducianism makes inherited sin easy to explain (the fallen soul comes from fallen parents); the rival creationism (God makes each soul afresh) makes it harder, and Augustine himself would waver on the point precisely because of what it cost original sin. Tertullian is the tradition’s first and boldest traducian.
  • A corruption short of total ruin. “No soul is without seeds of good” keeps Tertullian on the near side of the later Augustinian–Reformed doctrine of a will wholly bound. His is a genuine inherited fault, but not yet the massa damnata of Augustine nor the total depravity of the Reformers; the distance between “unclean and actively sinful” and “damnable guilt requiring the font” is exactly the distance the fourth and fifth centuries would travel.
