Tertullian on original sin

Dates: c. 210 (Tertullian, c. 155–c. 220; Carthaginian Christian writer. A Treatise on the Soul is usually dated c. 210–213) · Wikipedia
Summary: Tertullian ties the transmission of sin to his account of the soul’s origin. Because he holds that each individual soul is propagated from the parents’ souls as the body is from their bodies (traducianism), the whole race descends from Adam as a single stock — and the soul carries with it a birth-fault, a vitium originis, an “antecedent and in a certain sense natural evil which arises from its corrupt origin.” Every soul is therefore “unclean” and “actively sinful” from birth until it is “born again in Christ.” In On Baptism he counselled delaying the baptism of little children.

Claims & evidence

The soul is propagated from Adam “as its root” (traducianism)

Tertullian’s doctrine of transmission rests on his theory of the soul’s origin. Against those who held each soul freshly created, he taught that soul comes from soul as body from body, so that the race is one propagation flowing from the first man:

…how much more may that of a human being, whose soul (which may be compared with the nascent sprout of a tree) has been derived from Adam as its root, and has been propagated amongst his posterity by means of woman, to whom it has been entrusted for transmission…

— A Treatise on the Soul 19, source.1 The vine-shoot image (tradux) gives the theory its name. Adam and Eve “amalgamated and mixed their proper seminal rudiments in one, and ever afterwards communicated to the human race the normal mode of its propagation… from the one primeval man comes the entire outflow and redundance of men’s souls.” On this account, whatever befell Adam’s soul is carried in the stock.2

The vitium originis: “the corruption of our nature is another nature”

What is carried is a real fault of origin. Tertullian distinguishes the evil that later assails the soul from outside (the tempter’s work) from an antecedent, quasi-natural evil bred in the soul by its descent from a corrupted head:

There is, then, besides the evil which supervenes on the soul from the intervention of the evil spirit, an antecedent, and in a certain sense natural, evil which arises from its corrupt origin. For, as we have said before, the corruption of our nature is another nature having a god and father of its own, namely the author of (that) corruption. Still there is a portion of good in the soul… Just as no soul is without sin, so neither is any soul without seeds of good.

— A Treatise on the Soul 41.3 In the same passage Tertullian holds that “no soul is without seeds of good,” and he traces the corruption to a hostile author, “the author of (that) corruption.”

Every soul “unclean… actively sinful” from birth until reborn

The conclusion is that every human being, simply by being born of Adam, begins in a state of defilement that only the new birth removes:

Every soul, then, by reason of its birth, has its nature in Adam until it is born again in Christ; moreover, it is unclean all the while that it remains without this regeneration; and because unclean, it is actively sinful, and suffuses even the flesh (by reason of their conjunction) with its own shame.

— A Treatise on the Soul 40.4 The newborn’s uncleanness is described as a present state — “in Adam until… born again in Christ” — which regeneration reverses.

Reception and debate

  • The timing of baptism. In On Baptism Tertullian counsels delaying the baptism of little children — “let them come while they are growing up… why does the innocent period of life hasten to the remission of sins?” In 253 Cyprian, writing for an African council in answer to bishop Fidus, held that a newborn’s baptism need not wait for the eighth day.5
  • Traducianism. Tertullian’s account of how sin passes — through the soul propagated from Adam — ties it to the question of the origin of the soul, where traducianism (the soul from the parents) stands beside creationism (God makes each soul afresh).
