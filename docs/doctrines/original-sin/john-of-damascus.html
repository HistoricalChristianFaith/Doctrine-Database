John of Damascus on original sin

Dates: c. 730 (John of Damascus, c. 675–749; monk of Mar Saba near Jerusalem, who gathered Greek patristic theology in An Exact Exposition of the Orthodox Faith) · Wikipedia
Summary: John of Damascus describes the effect of the Fall on Adam’s descendants in the vocabulary of death and corruption. Adam’s transgression, on his account, left the race mortal and corruptible: “clothed about with death… subject to corruption,” banished from Paradise and condemned to die. In Book 2 he describes man as “a creature endowed with free-will,” with “the power to curb his appetite or to indulge it as he pleases”; in Book 4 he writes that “the law of sin, settling in the members of our flesh,” “takes me captive (even though I make the law of God my will and set my love on it, and make not sin my will).” John Meyendorff (Orthodox, 1974) describes the inheritance of the Fall in Greek patristic and Byzantine tradition as “essentially of mortality rather than of sinfulness”; see the notes below.

Claims & evidence

What Adam bequeathed: death and corruption

John narrates the Fall’s effect as a stripping of grace and a clothing in mortality — the “garments of skin” read as the grossness of a now-corruptible flesh:

Man, then, was thus snared by the assault of the arch-fiend, and broke his Creator’s command, and was stripped of grace… and was clothed about with death, that is, mortality and the grossness of flesh (for this is what the garment of skins signifies); and was banished from Paradise by God’s just judgment, and condemned to death, and made subject to corruption.

— An Exact Exposition of the Orthodox Faith 3.1, source.1 The inheritance is stated in the vocabulary of death and corruption. Elsewhere he puts the same point: through the transgression “we became subject to the corruption of death” — a subjection to mortality from which Christ’s incarnation and resurrection rescue.2

Man as “endowed with free-will” (Exact Exposition 2.27–28)

In 2.27–28 John describes man as the author of his own acts:

We are left then with this fact, that the man who acts and makes is himself the author of his own works, and is a creature endowed with free-will.

— Exact Exposition 2.27, source.3 He presses it: “man, being rational, leads nature rather than nature him, and so when he desires aught he has the power to curb his appetite or to indulge it as he pleases.”4

“The law of God and the law of sin” (Exact Exposition 4.22)

In Book 4 John describes the law of sin that settles in the flesh after the first transgression:

Further, the assault of the wicked one, that is the law of sin, settling in the members of our flesh, makes its assault upon us through it. For by once voluntarily transgressing the law of God and receiving the assault of the wicked one, we gave entrance to it, being sold by ourselves to sin. Wherefore our body is readily impelled to it.

He continues that the law of sin “is in opposition to the law of my mind, that is to conscience, and takes me captive (even though I make the law of God my will and set my love on it, and make not sin my will), by reason of commixture,” and that the Spirit “affordeth power to the law of our mind, against the law that is in our members.”

— Exact Exposition 4.22, source.5

Reception and debate

  • Meyendorff on the Greek patristic reading. John Meyendorff (Orthodox, 1974) writes that in the Greek patristic anthropology “there would be no place … for the concept of inherited guilt, or for a ‘sin of nature,’” although “human nature incurs the consequences of Adam’s sin,” and describes “a consensus in Greek patristic and Byzantine traditions in identifying the inheritance of the Fall as an inheritance essentially of mortality rather than of sinfulness, sinfulness being merely a consequence of mortality.” He finds the idea in Chrysostom, whose reading of Romans 5:12 has all “become mortal”; Theodoret writes that each receives the sentence of death “not because of the sin of his first ancestor but because of his own sin.”6
  • The baptism of infants. The Orthodox churches baptize infants. Meyendorff sets Augustine’s argument from baptism “for the remission of sins” beside Theodoret’s (“If the only meaning of baptism is the remission of sins… why would we baptize the newborn children who have not yet tasted of sin?”) and writes that “the Church baptizes children not to ‘remit’ their yet nonexistent sins but in order to give them a new and immortal life, which their mortal parents are unable to communicate to them.”7
  • Romans 5:12. Ambrosiaster and Augustine read the Latin in quo (“in whom”). Stéphane Harent (Catholic, 1911) writes that “Modern exegesis, as well as the Greek Fathers, prefer to translate ‘and so death passed upon all men because all have sinned’”; he accepts that translation and reads the clause through verse 19 (“by the disobedience of one man many were made sinners”) as speaking of “one first sin which was enough to transmit equally to all men a state of sin and the title of sinners.”8 Meyendorff writes that the Greek eph’ hō “can be translated as ‘because,’” and that it can also be read as masculine, referring to “death,” which he calls “the meaning which most Greek Fathers accepted.”9
