John of Damascus on original sin

Dates: c. 730 (John of Damascus, c. 675–749; monk of Mar Saba near Jerusalem, the great synthesizer of Greek patristic theology. An Exact Exposition of the Orthodox Faith is his dogmatic summa) · Wikipedia
Summary: John of Damascus states, in its settled form, the Eastern doctrine of the Fall that the Latin West never adopted — what later theology calls “ancestral sin.” Adam’s transgression, on this account, left the race mortal and corruptible: “clothed about with death… subject to corruption,” banished from Paradise and condemned to die. What is inherited is death and corruption — the very consequence Chrysostom had named — not Adam’s guilt. And the will is not enslaved: man remains “a creature endowed with free-will… master of his acts,” able “to curb his appetite or to indulge it as he pleases.” There is no reatus handed down, no “mass of perdition” justly damned in Adam. This is the deepest fault line between the two halves of the church on original sin, and it is why the Christian East, baptizing the same infants, grounds the rite in grace rather than in the remission of an inherited guilt.

Claims & evidence

What Adam bequeathed: death and corruption

John narrates the Fall’s effect as a stripping of grace and a clothing in mortality — the “garments of skin” read as the grossness of a now-corruptible flesh:

Man, then, was thus snared by the assault of the arch-fiend, and broke his Creator’s command, and was stripped of grace… and was clothed about with death, that is, mortality and the grossness of flesh (for this is what the garment of skins signifies); and was banished from Paradise by God’s just judgment, and condemned to death, and made subject to corruption.

— An Exact Exposition of the Orthodox Faith 3.1, source.1 The inheritance is stated in the vocabulary of death and corruption, not of imputed sin. Elsewhere he puts the same point: through the transgression “we became subject to the corruption of death” — the human plight is a slavery to mortality from which Christ’s incarnation and resurrection rescue, rather than a load of inherited culpability requiring acquittal.2

The will remains free after the Fall

Where the Augustinian tradition holds the will bound until liberated by grace, John insists that the rational creature retains genuine self-determination — the premise the East never surrenders:

We are left then with this fact, that the man who acts and makes is himself the author of his own works, and is a creature endowed with free-will.

— Exact Exposition 2.27, source.3 He presses it: “man, being rational, leads nature rather than nature him, and so when he desires aught he has the power to curb his appetite or to indulge it as he pleases.” A will with that power, weakened by mortality but not enslaved by inherited guilt, is a very different anthropology from the one Augustine built against Pelagius — and it is why the Pelagian question never convulsed the East as it did the West.4

Notes

  • “Ancestral sin,” not inherited guilt. The Eastern position, gathered here, is that Adam’s descendants inherit the consequences of his sin — death, corruption, a nature inclined to sin — but not its guilt (reatus). Each person is condemned for his own sins; the newborn is mortal, not culpable. This is the direct continuation of Chrysostom’s reading of Romans 5:12 (“all… become mortal”) and Theodoret’s (“each… not because of the sin of his first ancestor but because of his own sin”), and it remains the confession of the Orthodox churches. Modern Orthodox theology sharpens the contrast with the West precisely here.5
  • Why the East still baptizes infants. That the East denies inherited guilt does not make it a credobaptist tradition — it baptizes infants as unbrokenly as the West. But it grounds the rite differently: not in remitting a damnable Adamic reatus (Augustine’s ground) but in incorporating the child into Christ, into the new and deathless life that answers the inherited corruption. The same practice, a different rationale — the point turned against the strict Augustinian reading on the infant-baptism “inherited sin” argument.
  • A translation issue, magnified. Much of the West/East divergence traces to Romans 5:12: the Latin in quo (“in whom”) that Ambrosiaster and Augustine read as inherited guilt renders a Greek eph’ hō that the Greek fathers took causally (“because”), yielding inherited mortality. John of Damascus writes on the Greek side of that divide. The crux is weighed on the Romans 5:12 argument page.
