Augustine of Hippo on original sin

Dates: c. 412–430 (Augustine, 354–430; bishop of Hippo Regius in Roman North Africa. The anti-Pelagian works below span the last two decades of his life: On the Merits and Forgiveness of Sins (412), On Nature and Grace (415), On Marriage and Concupiscence (419–421), On the Grace of Christ and on Original Sin (418), the Enchiridion (c. 421). c. 412 is used as the timeline anchor) · Wikipedia
Summary: Augustine is the architect of the Western doctrine of original sin. Against Pelagius he welds the African tradition (Tertullian’s vitium, Cyprian’s “contagion,” Ambrosiaster’s “in whom”) into a system with four moving parts: (1) inherited guilt — all “sinned in” Adam and are bound in his condemnation, even having added no acts of their own; (2) transmission by propagation, not imitation, carried through the disordered concupiscence of generation, so that “what is generated must be regenerated”; (3) the whole race as one massa perditionis, a “mass of perdition” justly condemned, from which the elect are drawn as “vessels of mercy”; and (4) the universal baptism of infants as the decisive proof, since the sinless-in-act newborn is nonetheless washed “for the remission of sins.” The system is formidable; its hard edge — that the unbaptized infant is condemned — is the point at which even Augustine’s own tradition would not follow him.

Claims & evidence

Inherited guilt: bound in the one man “in whom all men sinned”

The center of the doctrine is that Adam’s posterity is implicated in his sin — not by copying it, but by being generated from him — and so shares his condemnation before adding any sins of its own:

Why did death reign on account of the sin of one, unless it was that men were bound by the chain of death in that one man in whom all men sinned, even though they added no sins of their own?

— On the Merits and Forgiveness of Sins, and on the Baptism of Infants 1.17, source.1 The phrase “in whom all men sinned” is the Latin in quo omnes peccaverunt of Romans 5:12, read (after Ambrosiaster) as “in whom — that is, in Adam.” Augustine draws the express contrast with Pelagius: the “all” of Romans is universal because sin is transmitted by generation, not because Adam merely set an example.

By propagation, not imitation; through concupiscence

Against the Pelagian claim that sin spreads only by bad example, Augustine locates the transmission in the flesh — in the disordered desire (concupiscentia) that attends generation, which passes the fault from parent to child as surely as generation itself:

The guilt of this concupiscence, regeneration alone remits, even as natural generation contracts it. What, then, is generated must be regenerated… so what is born in the flesh, either of a sinner or of a just man, is in both instances a sinner… He that is begotten is no sinner as yet in act, and is still new from his birth; but in guilt he is old. Human from the Creator, he is a captive of the destroyer, and needs a redeemer.

— On Marriage and Concupiscence 1.21, source.2 “No sinner as yet in act… but in guilt he is old” captures the whole doctrine in a sentence: the newborn is innocent of deeds and guilty of origin. Concupiscence is the conduit — itself a penalty of the first sin and the means by which sin descends — which is why marriage’s good use of it does not keep the offspring from contracting it.3

The “mass of perdition”

The corporate result is that the race is one condemned mass, justly liable in Adam, so that no one is wronged by condemnation and the saved are pure gift:

From the moment… when “by one man sin entered into the world, and death by sin, and so death passed upon all men, in whom all sinned,” the entire mass of our nature was ruined beyond doubt, and fell into the possession of its destroyer. And from him no one — no, not one — has been delivered, or is being delivered, or ever will be delivered, except by the grace of the Redeemer.

— On the Grace of Christ, and on Original Sin 2.34, source.4 Elsewhere he names it a massa outright: the human race “was under condemnation, was lying steeped and wallowing in misery,” and those redeemed are “not vessels of their own merits, but vessels of mercy” drawn “out of the mass of perdition.” This is where the doctrine of the Fall feeds directly into predestination: if all justly deserve condemnation, election is God’s free mercy to some, not injustice to the rest.5

Infant baptism as the proof

Augustine’s most effective move is evidential. The whole church baptizes infants “for the remission of sins”; infants commit no sins of their own; therefore what is remitted can only be inherited sin — and to deny that is to make the universal practice absurd:

If they were hurt by no malady of original sin, how is it they are carried to the Physician Christ, for the express purpose of receiving the sacrament of eternal salvation…? Why rather is it not said to them in the Church: Take hence these innocents: “they that are whole need not a physician, but they that are sick”…?

— On the Merits and Forgiveness of Sins 1.23.6 He forces the conclusion on both rival parties — those who grant infants actual sins and those who grant them none — that “nothing remains but original sin, which can be remitted in baptism to infants.” This is the argument weighed on the infant-baptism “inherited sin” page.7

Notes

  • Proponent overreach: the condemnation of unbaptized infants. Because Augustine grounds infant baptism in a damnable Adamic guilt the rite must remit, he draws the corollary Pelagius resisted: the infant who dies unbaptized is delivered from no condemnation and so is lost — softened, in Augustine, only to a “mildest” punishment (mitissima poena). This is the “proves too much” edge of the doctrine, and the holding tradition would not follow it to the end: Ambrose left the question open, Gregory of Nazianzus held such infants “neither glorified nor punished,” the medieval West devised the limbo of infants to spare them hell’s pain, and modern Catholic teaching entrusts them to God’s mercy. The persistent softening marks the strict form as overreach; it is weighed on the original-sin-ground argument (contested).8
  • The Latin proof-text, and its crux. Augustine’s inherited-guilt reading leans on the Latin “in whom all sinned,” which he took from his Bible and from “Ambrose” (Ambrosiaster). The Greek eph’ hō is most naturally causal (“because all sinned”), and the whole Greek tradition read the inheritance as mortality, not guilt. Augustine’s doctrine survives the crux only in its broad form (a real inherited sinful condition, which Romans 5 teaches on any construal); the specific step to imputed guilt is contested, and weighed on the Romans 5:12 argument page.
  • The East’s road not taken. Augustine’s system is a Latin achievement; the Greek church, from Chrysostom to John of Damascus, kept the inheritance to death and corruption and kept the will free, declining both the inherited reatus and the massa damnata. The two doctrines of the Fall are the deepest anthropological difference between the halves of the church, and downstream of it lie the divergences on grace, predestination, and the necessity of infant baptism.
