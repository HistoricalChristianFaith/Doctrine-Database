Augustine of Hippo on original sin

Dates: c. 412–430 (Augustine, 354–430; bishop of Hippo Regius in Roman North Africa. The anti-Pelagian works below span the last two decades of his life: On the Merits and Forgiveness of Sins (412), On Nature and Grace (415), On Marriage and Concupiscence (419–421), On the Grace of Christ and on Original Sin (418), the Enchiridion (c. 421). c. 412 is used as the timeline anchor) · Wikipedia
Summary: Against Pelagius, Augustine sets out a doctrine of original sin with four parts: (1) inherited guilt — all “sinned in” Adam and are bound in his condemnation, even having added no acts of their own; (2) transmission by propagation, not imitation, carried through the disordered concupiscence of generation, so that “What, then, is generated must be regenerated”; (3) the whole race as one massa perditionis, a “mass of perdition” justly condemned, from which the elect are drawn as “vessels of mercy”; and (4) the universal baptism of infants as evidence, since the sinless-in-act newborn is nonetheless washed “for the remission of sins.” He held that an infant dying unbaptized is condemned, though to a “mildest” punishment; later treatments of that question are noted below.

Claims & evidence

Inherited guilt: bound in the one man “in whom all men sinned”

The center of the doctrine is that Adam’s posterity is implicated in his sin — not by copying it, but by being generated from him — and so shares his condemnation before adding any sins of its own:

Why did death reign on account of the sin of one, unless it was that men were bound by the chain of death in that one man in whom all men sinned, even though they added no sins of their own?

— On the Merits and Forgiveness of Sins, and on the Baptism of Infants 1.17, source.1 The phrase “in whom all men sinned” is the Latin in quo omnes peccaverunt of Romans 5:12, read (with Ambrosiaster) as “in whom — that is, in Adam.” Augustine draws the contrast with Pelagius: the “all” of Romans is universal because sin is transmitted by generation, not because Adam merely set an example.

By propagation, not imitation; through concupiscence

Against the Pelagian claim that sin spreads only by bad example, Augustine locates the transmission in the flesh — in the disordered desire (concupiscentia) that attends generation, which passes the fault from parent to child as surely as generation itself:

The guilt of this concupiscence, regeneration alone remits, even as natural generation contracts it. What, then, is generated must be regenerated… so what is born in the flesh, either of a sinner or of a just man, is in both instances a sinner… He that is begotten is no sinner as yet in act, and is still new from his birth; but in guilt he is old. Human from the Creator, he is a captive of the destroyer, and needs a redeemer.

— On Marriage and Concupiscence 1.21, source.2 “No sinner as yet in act… but in guilt he is old”: on this account the newborn is innocent of deeds and guilty of origin. Concupiscence is the conduit — itself a penalty of the first sin and the means by which sin descends — so that, on his account, marriage’s good use of it does not keep the offspring from contracting it.3

The “mass of perdition”

The corporate result is that the race is one condemned mass, justly liable in Adam, so that no one is wronged by condemnation and the saved are pure gift:

From the moment… when “by one man sin entered into the world, and death by sin, and so death passed upon all men, in whom all sinned,” the entire mass of our nature was ruined beyond doubt, and fell into the possession of its destroyer. And from him no one — no, not one — has been delivered, or is being delivered, or ever will be delivered, except by the grace of the Redeemer.

— On the Grace of Christ, and on Original Sin 2.34, source.4 Elsewhere he names it a massa outright: the human race “was under condemnation, was lying steeped and wallowing in misery,” and those redeemed are “not vessels of their own merits, but vessels of mercy” drawn “out of the mass of perdition.” Augustine connects this with predestination: if all justly deserve condemnation, election is God’s free mercy to some, not injustice to the rest.5

Infant baptism as evidence

Augustine argues from the church’s practice. The whole church baptizes infants “for the remission of sins”; infants commit no sins of their own; therefore, he argues, what is remitted can only be inherited sin, and to deny that would leave the practice without a reason:

If they were hurt by no malady of original sin, how is it they are carried to the Physician Christ, for the express purpose of receiving the sacrament of eternal salvation…? Why rather is it not said to them in the Church: Take hence these innocents: “they that are whole need not a physician, but they that are sick”…?

— On the Merits and Forgiveness of Sins 1.23.6 He presses the conclusion on both rival parties — those who grant infants actual sins and those who grant them none — that “nothing remains but original sin, which can be remitted in baptism to infants.” The same argument appears on his infant-baptism detail page.7 He argues the same way from the exorcism and “exsufflation” (breathing on the candidate) that precede the font: “What does my exorcism work in that babe, if he be not held in the devil’s family?” (1.63). That rite-to-doctrine argument, which Prosper of Aquitaine later restated, is gathered on Augustine’s lex orandi page.

Reception and debate

  • Infants dying unbaptized. Because Augustine grounds infant baptism in an Adamic guilt the rite remits, he holds, against Pelagius, that the infant who dies unbaptized is not delivered from condemnation, though its punishment is the “mildest” (mitissima poena). Others treated the question differently: Ambrose, having written that John 3:5 excepts “not the infant, not the one prevented by some necessity,” added: “They may, however, have an undisclosed exemption from punishments; but I do not know whether they have the honor of the kingdom,” and Gregory of Nazianzus held such infants “neither glorified nor punished.” The International Theological Commission (2007) reports that later medieval authors, “from Peter Abelard on,” read the “mildest punishment” as the privation of the vision of God, a teaching it describes as having “modified the strict opinion of St. Augustine”; that limbo “never entered into the dogmatic definitions of the Magisterium”; and that the 1992 Catechism entrusts such infants “to the mercy of God.”8
  • Greek and Latin witnesses. Against Julian of Eclanum, Augustine names Irenaeus, Cyprian, Reticius, Olympius, Hilary, Ambrose, Gregory, Innocent, John, Basil and Jerome as bishops and a presbyter whose judgement on original sin he sets against Julian; Stéphane Harent (Catholic, 1911) describes this as invoking “the testimony of eleven Fathers, Greek as well as Latin.”9 John Meyendorff (Orthodox, 1974) describes “a consensus in Greek patristic and Byzantine traditions in identifying the inheritance of the Fall as an inheritance essentially of mortality rather than of sinfulness,” an idea he finds in Chrysostom, and sets Augustine’s argument for infant baptism beside Theodoret’s, for whom the “remission of sins” is not the only meaning of baptism.10
  • The Latin text of Romans 5:12. Augustine reads the Latin “in whom all sinned,” which he found in his Bible, and cites for it the commentary now called Ambrosiaster under the name of “the sainted Hilary.” Harent (1911) writes that this interpretation “would be an extra proof of the thesis of original sin, but it is not necessary,” accepts the translation “because all have sinned,” and reads the clause through verse 19 (“by the disobedience of one man many were made sinners”). Meyendorff (1974) writes that the Latin translation “was used in the West to justify the doctrine of guilt inherited from Adam” but that “such a meaning cannot be drawn from the original Greek.” The Catechism of the Catholic Church (1992) quotes the verse as “because all men sinned” and grounds the transmission on the “unity of the human race,” the whole race being in Adam “as one body of one man.”11
