Origen on original sin

Dates: c. 248 (Origen of Alexandria, c. 185–c. 253; the greatest scholar of the early Greek church. The Commentary on Romans and the exegetical homilies belong to his later Caesarean period, c. 244–249) · Wikipedia
Summary: Origen argues from the church’s practice to the newborn’s condition. Pairing Psalm 51:5 with the Septuagint of Job 14:4 (“no one is clean from filth, not even if his life be of one day”), he concludes that “whatever soul is born in the flesh is polluted with the filth of iniquity and sin,” and takes the church’s baptizing of infants “for the remission of sins” to be an apostolic tradition that would be “superfluous” unless there were something in the child to remit. On Romans 5 he reads solidarity seminally — all were “in the loins of Adam while he was still in paradise” — and his Latin translator Rufinus renders Paul’s crux “in whom all sinned.” Origen is thus a major Greek witness to an inherited defilement of birth, even as his own theory of the soul’s pre-existence gave that defilement a very different explanation from Augustine’s.

Claims & evidence

Even the one-day-old infant is “not clean from filth”

In the eighth homily on Leviticus, treating the sin-offering the Law commands for a newborn (Leviticus 12), Origen gathers Scripture’s witnesses that no one is born clean, and infers the point directly:

…hear David saying: “In iniquities,” he says, “was I conceived, and in sins did my mother bear me,” showing that whatever soul is born in the flesh is polluted with the filth “of iniquity and sin”; and that on this account was said what we have already mentioned above, that: “No one is clean from filth, not even if his life be of one day.” To these things this too may be added, that it be inquired what is the cause that, whereas the baptism of the Church is given for the remission of sins, according to the observance of the Church baptism is given even to little children; when indeed, if there were nothing in little children that ought to pertain to remission and indulgence, the grace of baptism would seem superfluous.

— Homilies on Leviticus 8.3, source.1 The argument runs backward from the rite to the need: infant baptism is universal and is “for the remission of sins”; the remission cannot be of the infant’s own acts; therefore there must be an inherited filth it removes. This is the same inference Augustine would make the hinge of his anti-Pelagian case, here already in the mid-third century, and in the Greek East.

Infant baptism as an apostolic tradition, for inborn defilement

The Commentary on Romans makes the appeal to apostolic tradition explicit, again reasoning from the Law’s sin-offering for the newborn:

Finally, in the law also a sacrifice is commanded to be offered for the little child that has been born… For what sin is this one young bird offered? Can a newly born infant already have sinned? And yet it has a sin for which a sacrifice is commanded to be offered, from which sin no one is said to be clean, not even if his life has been of a single day… For this reason the Church also received a tradition from the apostles, to give baptism even to little children. For those to whom the secrets of the divine mysteries were committed knew that there are in all people inborn defilements of sin, which must be washed away by water and the Spirit.

— Commentary on Romans 5.9 (Rufinus’s Latin), source.2 “Inborn defilements of sin… washed away by water and the Spirit” is a firm statement of inherited stain, and Origen grounds it not in his own speculation but in what “the Church received… from the apostles.”

Solidarity in Adam — “in the loins of Adam” — and the Latin “in whom”

On Romans 5:12 itself, Origen reads the passing of death through a seminal solidarity, arguing as Hebrews argues that Levi paid tithes “in Abraham” while still in his loins:

If therefore Levi, who is born in the fourth generation after Abraham, is declared to have been in the loins of Abraham, much more were all men who are born and have been born in this world in the loins of Adam while he was still in paradise; and all men were cast out of paradise with him or in him, when he himself was driven thence; and through him death, which had come upon him from the transgression, consequently passed also into those who were held in his loins.

— Commentary on Romans 5.1.3 The lemma Rufinus supplies reads Paul’s clause as “…death passed through to all men, in whom all sinned” — the personal (“in whom”) rendering of eph’ hō that the Latin tradition would run with. How far Rufinus’s Latin reflects Origen’s Greek here is uncertain, but the transmitted text puts Origen among the readers of “in whom.”4

Notes

  • Inherited defilement — but explained by pre-existence, not seminal guilt. Origen affirms as strongly as any Latin that the newborn is not clean; but his own account of why is his distinctive doctrine of the soul’s pre-existence and pre-natal fall — the soul contracts filth in being joined to flesh, and the diversity of human lots reflects choices made before birth. So the same datum (“filth of birth”) that grounds Augustine’s seminally-transmitted reatus is, in Origen, read through a framework the church would later condemn. The witness to inherited defilement is firm; the mechanism is Origen’s own.
  • A pre-Augustinian, and Eastern, anticipation. With Cyprian’s “contagion of the ancient death,” Origen’s “stains of birth” shows that the raw materials of the doctrine of original sin — inherited defilement, infant baptism for its remission — were in place well before the Pelagian controversy, and in the Greek as well as the Latin church. What the East would not take from these materials is the further step to inherited guilt: the divergence marked by Chrysostom and settled by John of Damascus.
  • Text and translation. Origen’s Commentary on Romans survives mainly in Rufinus’s early-fifth-century Latin, itself made in the thick of the original-sin and Origenist controversies; the “in whom” lemma should be read with that filter in view, and is weighed on the Romans 5:12 argument page.
