Origen on original sin

Dates: c. 248 (Origen of Alexandria, c. 185–c. 253; scholar and exegete at Alexandria and Caesarea. The Commentary on Romans and the exegetical homilies belong to his later Caesarean period, c. 244–249) · Wikipedia
Summary: Origen argues from the church’s practice to the newborn’s condition. Pairing Psalm 51:5 with the Septuagint of Job 14:4 (“no one is clean from filth, not even if his life be of one day”), he concludes that “whatever soul is born in the flesh is polluted with the filth of iniquity and sin,” and takes the church’s baptizing of infants “for the remission of sins” to be an apostolic tradition that would be “superfluous” unless there were something in the child to remit. On Romans 5 he reads solidarity seminally — all were “in the loins of Adam while he was still in paradise” — and his Latin translator Rufinus renders Paul’s clause “in whom all sinned.” In the Homilies on Luke he writes: “Every soul that has been clothed with a human body has its own ‘filth’”; and in baptism “the ‘filth’ of birth is laid aside.” His teaching on the soul’s origin is traced on the origin-of-the-soul timeline.

Claims & evidence

The one-day-old infant “not clean from filth”

In the eighth homily on Leviticus, treating the sin-offering the Law commands for a newborn (Leviticus 12), Origen gathers Scripture’s witnesses that no one is born clean, and infers the point directly:

…hear David saying: “In iniquities,” he says, “was I conceived, and in sins did my mother bear me,” showing that whatever soul is born in the flesh is polluted with the filth “of iniquity and sin”; and that on this account was said what we have already mentioned above, that: “No one is clean from filth, not even if his life be of one day.” To these things this too may be added, that it be inquired what is the cause that, whereas the baptism of the Church is given for the remission of sins, according to the observance of the Church baptism is given even to little children; when indeed, if there were nothing in little children that ought to pertain to remission and indulgence, the grace of baptism would seem superfluous.

— Homilies on Leviticus 8.3, source.1 The argument runs backward from the rite to the need: infant baptism is universal and is “for the remission of sins”; the remission cannot be of the infant’s own acts; therefore there is something in little children that the remission concerns. Augustine argues from the same practice against the Pelagians (c. 412).

Infant baptism as an apostolic tradition, for inborn defilement

The Commentary on Romans makes the appeal to apostolic tradition explicit, again reasoning from the Law’s sin-offering for the newborn:

Finally, in the law also a sacrifice is commanded to be offered for the little child that has been born… For what sin is this one young bird offered? Can a newly born infant already have sinned? And yet it has a sin for which a sacrifice is commanded to be offered, from which sin no one is said to be clean, not even if his life has been of a single day… For this reason the Church also received a tradition from the apostles, to give baptism even to little children. For those to whom the secrets of the divine mysteries were committed knew that there are in all people inborn defilements of sin, which must be washed away by water and the Spirit.

— Commentary on Romans 5.9 (Rufinus’s Latin), source.2 Origen speaks of “inborn defilements of sin… washed away by water and the Spirit,” and grounds the practice in what “the Church received… from the apostles.”

Solidarity in Adam — “in the loins of Adam” — and the Latin “in whom”

On Romans 5:12 itself, Origen reads the passing of death through a seminal solidarity, arguing as Hebrews argues that Levi paid tithes “in Abraham” while still in his loins:

If therefore Levi, who is born in the fourth generation after Abraham, is declared to have been in the loins of Abraham, much more were all men who are born and have been born in this world in the loins of Adam while he was still in paradise; and all men were cast out of paradise with him or in him, when he himself was driven thence; and through him death, which had come upon him from the transgression, consequently passed also into those who were held in his loins.

— Commentary on Romans 5.1.3 The lemma Rufinus supplies reads Paul’s clause as “…death passed through to all men, in whom all sinned” — the personal (“in whom”) rendering of eph’ hō. The commentary survives in Rufinus’s Latin, so the lemma as transmitted is in Rufinus’s wording.4

Reception and debate

  • The “filth” of birth and the soul’s origin. In the fourteenth homily on Luke, on the purification after Jesus’ birth, Origen distinguishes “filth” from “sin” (“He did not say: ‘no one is clean from sin,’ but: ‘no one is clean from filth’”), writes, “Every soul that has been clothed with a human body has its own ‘filth’”, and answers the question why little children are baptized “for the remission of sins”: “because through the sacrament of baptism the ‘filth’ of birth is laid aside, therefore little children too are baptized.”5 His teaching on the soul’s origin, including its pre-existence, is traced on the origin-of-the-soul timeline; Augustine ascribes the newborn’s condition to a transmission from Adam by generation. The condemnation of propositions attributed to Origen is traced on the condemnation of Origen timeline.
  • Origen and Cyprian. Origen’s “filth” of birth (which he distinguishes from sin) and Cyprian’s “contagion of the ancient death” (253) both connect a condition of birth with infant baptism, the one in a Greek and the other in a Latin church.
  • Text and translation. Origen’s Commentary on Romans survives mainly in Rufinus’s early-fifth-century Latin, made during the years of the original-sin and Origenist controversies. The Homilies on Leviticus also survive only in Rufinus’s Latin (c. 403–405), and the Homilies on Luke only in Jerome’s (c. 391).6
