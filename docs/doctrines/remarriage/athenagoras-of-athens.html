Athenagoras of Athens on remarriage

Dates: c. 177 (A Plea for the Christians) · Wikipedia
Summary: In his Plea addressed to the emperors, Athenagoras writes that Christians either remain unmarried or are “content with one marriage; for a second marriage is only a specious adultery,” and he applies the phrase to the man whose first wife has died.

Claims & evidence

A second marriage is “a specious adultery”

…each of us reckoning her his wife whom he has married according to the laws laid down by us, and that only for the purpose of having children. For as the husbandman throwing the seed into the ground awaits the harvest, not sowing more upon it, so to us the procreation of children is the measure of our indulgence in appetite. Nay, you would find many among us, both men and women, growing old unmarried, in hope of living in closer communion with God. But if the remaining in virginity and in the state of an eunuch brings nearer to God, while the indulgence of carnal thought and desire leads away from Him, in those cases in which we shun the thoughts, much more do we reject the deeds. For we bestow our attention, not on the study of words, but on the exhibition and teaching of actions, — that a person should either remain as he was born, or be content with one marriage; for a second marriage is only a specious adultery.

— Athenagoras, A Plea for the Christians 33, ANF vol. 2. He presses it further, to the widowed themselves:

For he who deprives himself of his first wife, even though she be dead, is a cloaked adulterer, resisting the hand of God, because in the beginning God made one man and one woman, and dissolving the strictest union of flesh with flesh, formed for the intercourse of the race.

— Athenagoras, A Plea for the Christians 33, ANF vol. 2. Whether the phrase represents common Christian opinion independently of the New Prophecy is disputed; see below.

Reception and debate

  • The same chapter 33 also carries Athenagoras’s teaching on divorce — between the two sentences quoted above he cites the Lord’s saying that “whosoever puts away his wife… and marries another, commits adultery,” glossing it as God “not permitting a man to send her away… nor to marry again.” That teaching is treated on his divorce page; this page treats only second marriage after a spouse’s death.
  • Athenagoras is the earliest witness on this timeline to use the word “adultery” of a second marriage after a spouse’s death; the Shepherd of Hermas (c. 140) calls such a marriage “no sin.”
  • Readings of the phrase — as a trace of Montanism (Tillemont, as reported by Schaff), as one of the “expressions trop fortes” beside a church that never censured second marriage (the Encyclopédie), and as a view shared with his age (Schaff, Erickson, Grappone, Ishak) — are set out at Does Athenagoras’s phrase represent common Christian opinion independently of the New Prophecy?
