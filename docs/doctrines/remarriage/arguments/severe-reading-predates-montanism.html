Does Athenagoras’s “a second marriage is only a specious adultery” (Plea 33, c. 177) represent common Christian opinion independently of the New Prophecy?

Disputed reading · on Did the early church permit remarriage? (the deprecation of digamy) · witness: Athenagoras of Athens

Related readings: Does 1 Corinthians 7:39 free every Christian widow to remarry, or only one widowed before her conversion (Tertullian, On Monogamy 11)? · Was Tertullian’s ban on second marriage a matter of discipline or of doctrine?

The passage

…a person should either remain as he was born, or be content with one marriage; for a second marriage is only a specious adultery. “For whosoever puts away his wife,” says He, “and marries another, commits adultery”… For he who deprives himself of his first wife, even though she be dead, is a cloaked adulterer.

— Athenagoras, A Plea for the Christians 33, ANF vol. 2.1

The question

Athenagoras addressed his Plea to the emperors Marcus Aurelius and Commodus about 177, a generation before Tertullian’s treatises against second marriage. What is disputed is where his phrase stands in the second-century church: as an expression of a view widely shared among Christians independently of the New Prophecy, as a trace of Montanist influence, or as a strong expression beside a church that held remarriage after a spouse’s death to be no sin. The answer bears on whether Tertullian’s later ban continues an older severity found outside the New Prophecy or belongs to the New Prophecy.2 At Carthage a generation later, on Douglas Powell’s account of Tertullian’s treatise to a widowed brother, “clerical monogamia is the recognised rule, and lay monogamia a discipline of the New Prophecy.”3 No modern academic holder of the first or second reading below was located.

A trace of Montanism in Athenagoras

On this reading Athenagoras’s condemnation of second marriage, with his account of prophetic inspiration, shows the influence of the Montanist movement.

Held by: Louis-Sébastien Le Nain de Tillemont (Catholic, Mémoires pour servir à l’histoire ecclésiastique; 1637–1698), ⚠ unverified, as reported by Schaff.4

Philip Schaff reports that “Tillemont found traces of Montanism in the condemnation of second marriage and the view of prophetic inspiration”; a Coptic Orthodox patrology likewise reports that “Tillemont thinks he was… depending on two things”: “He compared the prophet to a flute, on which the Holy Spirit plays,” and he “agrees with the Montanists on the subject of second marriage.” Both features stand in the Plea. Athenagoras describes the prophets as men “lifted in ecstasy above the natural operations of their minds by the impulses of the Divine Spirit,” “the Spirit making use of them as a flute-player breathes into a flute”; Schaff records that “Montanus used the comparison of the plectrum and the lyre,” and that “the Montanists and Novatians condemned re-marriage, and made it a subject of discipline.” On Tillemont’s reading, as these reports give it, the ecstatic prophet and the “specious adultery” are two marks that the Plea shares with the New Prophecy.5

A strong expression beside a church that never condemned second marriage

On this reading the phrase is a forceful turn of speech: the church tolerated second marriages without praising them, and never condemned them.

Held by: the Encyclopédie, article “Polygamie,” unsigned section “(Théolog.)” (1st ed., begun 1751, vol. 12), citing Augustin Calmet (Catholic, Benedictine abbot, Dictionnaire de la Bible).6

The Encyclopédie sets Athenagoras beside Basil and the canons: successive marriage is “tolérée dans l’Eglise, quoiqu’avec assez de répugnance” (tolerated in the Church, though with some reluctance), and “On lit dans Athénagore, que les secondes noces sont un adultere honorable, adulterium decorum; & dans S. Basile, qu’elles sont une fornication mitigée, castigatam fornicationem, expressions trop fortes” (we read in Athenagoras that second marriages are an honourable adultery, and in Basil that they are a mitigated fornication: expressions too strong). It continues: “Les Montanistes & les Novatiens condamnoient aussi les secondes noces; mais l’Eglise sans en faire l’éloge, ni les conseiller, ne les a jamais blâmées” (the Montanists and Novatians also condemned second marriages, but the Church, without praising or advising them, never censured them).6

The paragraph closes with Jerome as its witness to the Church’s practice: “Je déclare hautement, dit S. Jerôme… qu’on ne condamne pas dans l’Eglise ceux qui se marient deux, trois, quatre, cinq & six fois, & même davantage ; mais si on ne proscrit pas cette répétition, on ne la loue pas” (I declare openly, says St Jerome, that those who marry two, three, four, five and six times, and more, are not condemned in the Church; but if this repetition is not proscribed, it is not praised).6 On this reading Athenagoras’s “honourable adultery,” like Basil’s “mitigated fornication,” is one of the “expressions trop fortes” within a church that tolerated successive marriage without praising it.

A view widely shared before and apart from the New Prophecy

On this reading Athenagoras voices, in its sharpest form, an aversion to second marriage common among second-century Christians, not a Montanist trait.

Held by: Philip Schaff (Protestant, History of the Christian Church, vol. II, 1910 ed.); John H. Erickson (Orthodox, “The Council in Trullo: Monogamy and the Ordained Priesthood”); Antonio Grappone (Catholic, ZENIT, 2014); Shenouda Maher Ishak (Coptic Orthodox, 2019).7

Schaff holds that the condemnation of second marriage “was common among the Greeks,” and of Athenagoras: “Here comes out his ascetic tendency which he shares with his age.” He traces “a very, prevalent aversion to re-marriage, particularly of widows” to “the high view of marriage, and also… an ascetic over-estimate of celibacy,” and cites Theophilus of Antioch (To Autolycus III.15) that among Christians monogamia tēreitai, monogamy is kept.8

Erickson finds the patristic texts “remarkable for their unanimity in insisting on perpetual monogamy as the norm for Christian marriage even in the face of death,” differing “mainly in the tone that they adopt in speaking of second and subsequent marriage.” Against Clement, “comparatively mild,” he writes: “More typical is the apologist Athenagoras.” Second marriage, he adds, “is not utterly condemned,” but “at best… is regarded as a lesser good, precisely because it lacks the sign value of single marriage.”9

Grappone writes that “only slowly did the Church recognize the complete legitimacy of the second marriage of widows,” not “until at least the end of the 4th century”; before then such marriages “were permitted but not at all encouraged,” and Athenagoras, who spoke of a “presentable adultery,” “is not even considered a rigorist.” Ishak lists Athenagoras, “so convinced of the indissolubility of marriage that, according to him, even death cannot dissolve the marital bond,” first among the Fathers opposed to second marriage, with Clement, and counts Hermas as permitting remarriage “contrary to a number of early Christian authors.”10 On this reading Athenagoras’s ground, that “in the beginning God made one man and one woman,” is the common Christian ideal of one marriage stated at full strength, and it was already current in an apologist writing to the emperors about 177.
