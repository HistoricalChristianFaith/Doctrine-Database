Does 1 Corinthians 7:39 free every Christian widow to remarry, or only one widowed before her conversion (Tertullian, On Monogamy 11)?

Disputed reading · on Did the early church permit remarriage? (the deprecation of digamy) · witness: Tertullian

Related readings: Does Athenagoras’s “a second marriage is only a specious adultery” (Plea 33, c. 177) represent common Christian opinion independently of the New Prophecy? · Was Tertullian’s ban on second marriage a matter of discipline or of doctrine?

The passage

The wife is bound by the law as long as her husband liveth; but if her husband be dead, she is at liberty to be married to whom she will; only in the Lord.

— 1 Corinthians 7:39 (KJV), source. Tertullian’s comment on the verse:

Let us plainly know that, in the Greek original, it does not stand in the form which (through the either crafty or simple alteration of two syllables) has gone out into common use, “But if her husband shall have fallen asleep,” as if it were speaking of the future, and thereby seemed to pertain to her who has lost her husband when already in a believing state. If this indeed had been so, licence let loose without limit would have granted a (fresh) husband as often as one had been lost.

— Tertullian, On Monogamy 11, ANF vol. 4.1

The question

Paul’s permission at 1 Cor. 7:39 is read by Clement of Alexandria and John Calvin, among others, as freeing any Christian widow to marry again, or, by Tertullian in his treatise On Monogamy (c. 208–212), as addressed only to a woman whose husband died before she came to faith, so that her first Christian marriage is not a second one. The answer decides whether the verse can be cited for the lawfulness of a second marriage after baptism. What Tertullian’s appeal to the “authentic Greek” (Graeco authentico) contrasts is itself described differently: H. A. G. Houghton (2016) says that he “contrasts a Latin text of 1 Corinthians 7:39 omitting the second occurrence of uir eius (‘her husband’) with the reading in Graeco authentico,” and Roger Pearse (2019) takes the phrase to mean “the correct reading, the authentic reading of scripture, rather than the somewhat dodgy Old Latin translation then in circulation,” not “the original manuscript of 1 Corinthians.” Robertson and Plummer’s apparatus (1911) records two variants in the verse: “by the law” (nomōi) added after “is bound” in some witnesses, and apothanē (“should die”) for koimēthē (“should fall asleep”) in Codex Alexandrinus, Origen and Basil.2 No modern Catholic exegetical comment on the verse was located in openly accessible sources; the Catholic holder named below writes on the canonical status of second marriage.

A permission to every widow, “only in the Lord”

On this reading Paul frees any Christian woman whose husband has died to marry again, so long as she marries “in the Lord,” while counselling that she is happier if she remains a widow.

Held by: Clement of Alexandria (Stromateis III, c. 200); John Calvin (Reformed, Commentary on 1 Corinthians, 16th c.); P. M. J. Rock (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1907, on second marriage generally); Triantafyllos K. Boltetsos (Eastern Orthodox, Θεολογíα, 2021, on Paul’s permission generally).3

Clement quotes the verse as Paul’s allowance of a second marriage: “if from sympathy the apostle allows a man a second marriage because he cannot control himself and burns with passion,” and “if he dies, she is free to marry, only in the Lord. But she is happier… if she remains as she [is].” Basil cites it to rule that “the woman in widowhood, who is independent, may dwell with a husband without blame, if there is no one to prevent their cohabitation; for the Apostle says; ‘but if her husband be dead, she is at liberty to be married to whom she will; only in the Lord.’”4

Calvin reads the verse as Paul’s word to women in particular: “He now, therefore, teaches that women are not less at liberty than men to marry a second time, on their becoming widows.” He reads the apostle as declaring “that no hindrance ought to be thrown in the way of widows’ marrying, if they think proper.”5

Robertson and Plummer take verses 39–40 as “a few words… about the remarriage of widows,” a case “covered by vv. 8 and 34,” on which the Corinthians had probably asked; they read “only in the Lord” as “‘Only as a member of Christ,’ which implies that she marries a Christian,” and set it beside Romans 7, where the widow who remarries “is no adulteress.”6

Two modern statements of church discipline read Paul’s permission the same way. The Catholic canonist P. M. J. Rock writes that “there is no sin nor fault of any kind in a man marrying a second wife after the death of his first, or a third after the death of his second”; the Orthodox rite of second marriage, quoted by Boltetsos, prays for those who, “unable to bear the burden of the day and the burning of the flesh… have come to a second communion of marriage, in accordance with that which thou hast lawfully appointed by thy chosen vessel, Paul the Apostle.” Holders of this reading differ over the discipline that may attend the liberty: Basil’s canons set a year’s penance for digamy and the rite asks pardon for those who marry a second time, while Calvin reads the apostle as condemning the refusal of a blessing to second marriages (n. 5).7 On this reading the verse is Paul’s general rule for every widow: the bond lasts while the husband lives, his death sets her free, and the counsel of verse 40 is advice that leaves that liberty in place.

A permission for the woman found widowed when she came to faith

On this reading the verse speaks to a woman whose husband died before her conversion: her first marriage as a Christian is not a second one, and the verse does not license remarriage after baptism.

Held by: Tertullian (On Monogamy 11, c. 208–212).8

Tertullian reads 7:39 within the run of the chapter that addresses converts in the state in which faith found them: “Let every man abide in the same calling wherein he was called,” and “You have been bound to a wife, seek not loosing; you have been loosed from a wife, seek not a wife.” In the same passage, he writes, Paul “definitely rules that each one ought permanently to remain in that calling in which he shall be called,” and adds the rule about the widow; “he hence also demonstrates that such a woman is to be understood as has withal herself been found (by the faith) loosed from a husband.” Because “it is from (the time of our) believing that our life itself dates its origin,” a woman so loosed “will not sin; because he will not be reckoned a second husband who is, subsequently to her believing, the first.”9

The closing words fit the same case: “he therefore adds, only in the Lord; because the question in agitation was about her who had had a heathen (husband), and had believed subsequently to losing him: for fear, to wit, that she might presume herself able to marry a heathen even after believing.” Tertullian holds that the form in common use, by an alteration “of two syllables,” makes the verse sound as if it spoke of a future death, and so of a woman widowed after believing. He adds that the sense holds either way: “even if it had been so… the future would just as much pertain to her whose husband shall die before she believed. Take it which way you will, provided you do not overturn the rest.” He makes the agreement of the whole chapter his test of the reading, “for nothing is so much to be guarded as (the care) that no one be found self-contradictory.” Read this way, 7:39 stands with Paul’s counsel to the unmarried and widows to remain as they are, and monogamy after baptism is the apostle’s own rule.10
