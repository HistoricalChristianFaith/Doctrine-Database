The Council in Trullo on remarriage

Dates: 692 (the Quinisext Council) · Wikipedia
Summary: Canon 3 of the Council in Trullo describes its rule as a blend of the Roman “rule of exact perfection” and the Constantinopolitan one “of kindness and consideration,” “that neither gentleness fall into licence, nor severity into harshness,” renews the canon barring from the clergy a man married twice after baptism, and disciplines clergy who had entered a second marriage.

Claims & evidence

“Neither gentleness fall into licence, nor severity into harshness” (canon 3)

…whereas they of the most holy Roman Church purpose to keep the rule of exact perfection, but those who are under the throne of this heaven-protected and royal city keep that of kindness and consideration, so blending both together… that neither gentleness fall into licence, nor severity into harshness…

— Council in Trullo, canon 3, NPNF2 vol. 14. The canon renews the old rule that a man “joined in two marriages after his baptism” cannot be a bishop, presbyter, or deacon, and disciplines clergy who had entered a second marriage. On the Pauline texts cited for the rule, see Paul.

Third and fourth marriages: the tetragamy of Leo VI and the Tomos of Union (920)

The Byzantine rule on third and fourth marriages was set after the tetragamy of the emperor Leo VI, who took an uncanonical fourth wife (c. 906) to secure an heir; after the deposition of the patriarch who resisted him, the dispute closed with the Tomos of Union (920), which condemned fourth marriages outright and regulated third ones.1 A graded saying on successive marriages is Gregory of Nazianzus’s, in his Oration 37: “The first is law, the second is indulgence, the third is transgression, and anything beyond this is swinish.”2

Reception and debate

  • Trullo’s canons quote the earlier canons of Basil; on the penances for digamists see also Neocaesarea and Laodicea. Fr. John H. Erickson (Orthodox) writes that the council “did not believe that the Church’s position on the subjects in question needed to be redefined. What was needed was observance of the received canons.”3
  • The council’s canon 87, invoking Basil and Jeremiah, treats divorce-and-remarriage (leaving a living spouse) as adultery — a distinct question from the remarriage of the widowed traced on this timeline.
