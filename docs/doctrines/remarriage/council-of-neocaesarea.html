The Council of Neocaesarea on remarriage

Dates: c. 315 · Wikipedia
Summary: The canons of Neocaesarea forbid a presbyter to attend the wedding-feast of a second marriage, “since the digamist is worthy of penance,” and refer to an appointed time of penance for those “who fall into many marriages.”

Claims & evidence

A presbyter not to attend a second wedding (canon 7)

A presbyter shall not be a guest at the nuptials of persons contracting a second marriage; for, since the digamist is worthy of penance, what kind of a presbyter shall he be, who, by being present at the feast, sanctioned the marriage?

— Council of Neocaesarea, canon 7, NPNF2 vol. 14. Canon 3 likewise sets a term of penance for those “who fall into many marriages,” noting that “their manner of living and faith shortens the time.”

Readings of the penance for digamy

The Louvain canonist Zeger Bernhard van Espen (Catholic, 1646–1728), quoted in Percival’s notes to the canon, reads it as showing that “although the Church never disapproved of, nor reputed second or still later marriages illicit, nevertheless the Fathers enjoined a penance upon digamists and those repeating marriage, because by this iteration they shewed their incontinence.”1

The Orthodox theologian John Meyendorff (Marriage: An Orthodox Perspective, 1975), as quoted by Triantafyllos K. Boltetsos (2021), writes of the Church that “she never encourages any remarriage ‒ we have seen that even in the case of widowers ‒ because of the eternal character of the marriage bond; but only tolerates it when, in concrete cases, it appears as the best solution for a given individual,” and that “second and third marriages, after widowhood, were concluded at a civil ceremony only, and implied a penance of one to five years of excommunication. After this period of penance, the couple was again considered as full members of the Church.”2

Placement on the timeline

  • Terms of penance for second and third marriages are given by Basil of Caesarea (a year for a digamist, three or four for a trigamist); Laodicea restores communion to the twice-married “after a short space” of prayer and fasting.
