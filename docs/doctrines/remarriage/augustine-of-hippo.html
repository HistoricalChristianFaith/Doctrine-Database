Augustine of Hippo on remarriage

Dates: c. 414 (On the Good of Widowhood) · Wikipedia
Summary: In On the Good of Widowhood Augustine writes that second marriages “are not condemned, but are set in lower honor,” declines to condemn third or fourth marriages, faults Tertullian for attacking second marriages “as though unlawful,” and calls widowed continence “a better good” than marriage chastity.

Claims & evidence

“Not condemned, but… set in lower honor” (ch. 6)

…second marriages are not condemned, but are set in lower honor… For hence, specially, the heresies of the Cataphryges and of the Novatians swelled, which Tertullian also, inflated with cheeks full of sound not of wisdom, whilst with railing tooth he attacks second marriages, as though unlawful, which the Apostle with sober mind allows to be altogether lawful.

— Augustine, On the Good of Widowhood 6, NPNF1 vol. 3. Augustine links the attack on second marriages with the Cataphrygians (the Montanists) and the Novatians; he names the same point in his entry on Tertullian in De haeresibus 86.

Third and fourth marriages (ch. 15)

Men are wont to move a question concerning a third or fourth marriage, and even more numerous marriages than this. On which to make answer strictly, I dare neither to condemn any marriage, nor to take from these the shame of their great number… For who am I, that I should think that that must be defined which I see that the Apostle hath not defined?

— Augustine, On the Good of Widowhood 15, NPNF1 vol. 3. Augustine declines to define what he finds the apostle has not defined.

A good and “a better good”

…marriage chastity is a good, but widowed continence is a better good. Therefore this better good is honored by the submission of that other, not that other condemned by the praise of this that is better.

— Augustine, On the Good of Widowhood 6, NPNF1 vol. 3. To the widow herself Augustine puts it as a counsel of the will: “let this be your mind, not that you condemn them, but that you despise them” (On the Good of Widowhood 14).

Reception and debate

  • Augustine illustrates the two goods from Scripture: Ruth, twice-married, is “blessed,” and Anna, the long widow, “more blessed” (On the Good of Widowhood 5, 10).
  • Cataloguing Tertullian among the heresies in De haeresibus 86, Augustine set aside Tertullian’s teaching on the corporeality of the soul and of God as the ground and named instead the turn to the Cataphrygians, the ban on second marriages “against apostolic teaching, as though they were fornication,” and the final schism; see the disputed reading Was Tertullian’s ban on second marriage a matter of discipline or of doctrine?
