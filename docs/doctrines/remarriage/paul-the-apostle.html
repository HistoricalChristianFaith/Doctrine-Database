Paul the Apostle on remarriage

Dates: c. 55 (1 Corinthians) · Wikipedia
Summary: Paul’s letters permit the widowed to remarry — a widow is “at liberty to be married to whom she will” and is “no adulteress” if she does — and counsel that she is “happier” if she remains unmarried; the Pastoral Epistles require a bishop to be “the husband of one wife” and an enrolled widow to have been “the wife of one man.”

Claims & evidence

The widow “at liberty to be married” and “no adulteress”

The wife is bound by the law as long as her husband liveth; but if her husband be dead, she is at liberty to be married to whom she will; only in the Lord.

— 1 Corinthians 7:39, 1 Cor. 7:39. Romans states the same rule of the bond and its release:

For the woman which hath an husband is bound by the law to her husband so long as he liveth… but if the husband be dead, she is free from that law; so that she is no adulteress, though she be married to another man.

— Romans 7:2–3, Rom. 7:2–3. Of the younger widows he writes: “I will therefore that the younger women marry, bear children, guide the house” (1 Tim. 5:14).

The counsel to remain unmarried

I say therefore to the unmarried and widows, It is good for them if they abide even as I. But if they cannot contain, let them marry: for it is better to marry than to burn.

— 1 Corinthians 7:8–9, 1 Cor. 7:8–9. Of the widow free to remarry he adds, “she is happier if she so abide, after my judgment” (1 Cor. 7:40). Later writers who discouraged second marriages cite these verses, among them Tertullian and Ambrose.

“The husband of one wife” and “the wife of one man”

A bishop then must be blameless, the husband of one wife…

— 1 Timothy 3:2, 1 Tim. 3:2; and the order of enrolled widows is confined to her who has been “the wife of one man” (1 Tim. 5:9). Origen reads the phrase as excluding the twice-married from church office, and the canons renewed at the Council in Trullo bar from the clergy a man “joined in two marriages after his baptism.” The Orthodox canonist Fr. John H. Erickson cites 1 Tim. 3:1–3 and Titus 1:5–6 among “the requirements for ordination enunciated in the Pastoral Epistles” and writes that “Bigamists (i.e., those twice married) were excluded from ordination” (“The Council in Trullo: Monogamy and the Ordained Priesthood,” Pemptousia, 2017). The Catholic canonist P. M. J. Rock traces the canonical irregularity of “bigamy,” “the marrying of a second after the death of the first wife,” to this “apostolic injunction of St. Paul,” by which “he forbids bigamists to be admitted to Sacred orders,” and adds that the irregularity “is not a punishment… as there is no sin nor fault of any kind in a man marrying a second wife after the death of his first” (“Bigamy (in Canon Law),” Catholic Encyclopedia, 1907). John Calvin reports that this exposition “has been more generally received,” and reads the verse otherwise: “The only true exposition, therefore, is that of Chrysostom, that in a bishop he expressly condemns polygamy,” resting on the present tense of the phrase (“Who is,” and not “Who hath been”) (Commentary on Timothy, Titus, Philemon, trans. W. Pringle, on 1 Tim. 3:2).
