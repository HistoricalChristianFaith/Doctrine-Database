Julian of Norwich on universal restoration (apokatastasis)

Dates: c. 1343–after 1416 · Wikipedia
Summary: Julian, an English anchoress and the first known woman to write a book in English, is the medieval bridge witness — the hope of restoration surviving in Western devotion in the long silence between Eriugena and the moderns. Her Revelations of Divine Love (the Long Text, c. 1393) circles the assurance she says Christ gave her, “all shall be well, and all shall be well, and all manner of thing shall be well,” and in the parable of the lord and the servant she is shown a God who looks on fallen humanity with pity and assigns no blame. Yet she is emphatically not an Origenist who asserts the restoration as doctrine: she confesses that she was shown no soul in hell, that this left her unable to see how all could be well, and that she nonetheless held fast the Church's teaching that many are condemned — resolving the tension not in a doctrine of universal salvation but in a hidden “Great Deed” God has promised and reserved to Himself. Her position is therefore nearer Maximus's disciplined reserve, and it anticipates almost exactly the “hopeful universalism” Balthasar would frame six centuries later.

Claims & evidence

“All shall be well” — the refrain

It behoved that there should be sin; but all shall be well, and all shall be well, and all manner of thing shall be well.

— Revelations of Divine Love, ch. 27 (Grace Warrack tr., 1901), source. The word Julian records is the answer, she says, to her anguished wondering why God did not simply prevent sin: “sin is behovely” (needful, playing a necessary part) — yet the outcome is total repair. The saying is the seed of the whole hope: sin is real and grievous, but it is not final, and God has bound Himself to make well “all that is not well.”

The fourfold assurance of the mending

I may make all thing well, I can make all thing well, I will make all thing well, and I shall make all thing well; and thou shalt see thyself that all manner of thing shall be well.

— Revelations of Divine Love, ch. 31, source. Christ, on Julian's account, presses the assurance through the four verbs of possibility, capacity, will, and deed — and then adds that she herself shall see it accomplished. It is as strong a statement of the hope as anything in the tradition; what she never does is convert it into the claim that no soul is finally lost.

The tension owned: the Church teaches that many are condemned

One point of our Faith is that many creatures shall be condemned… all these shall be condemned to hell without end, as Holy Church teacheth me to believe.

— Revelations of Divine Love, ch. 32, source. Julian does not soften this. Setting the Church's teaching beside the assurance she has been given, she confesses that she could not reconcile them — it seemed to her “impossible that all manner of thing should be well.” The answer she reports is not a revision of the faith but a referral upward:

That which is impossible to thee is not impossible to me: I shall save my word in all things and I shall make all things well.

— Revelations of Divine Love, ch. 32, source. The reconciliation is deferred into a “Great Deed the which the blessed Trinity shall do in the last Day” — a work “unknown of all creatures that are beneath Christ” until it is done. She holds both the Church's hell and God's promised mending, and refuses to collapse the one into the other; the resolution is God's secret, not her doctrine.

She was shown no soul in hell — but did not conclude hell empty

But for all my desire, I could see of this right nought, save as it is aforesaid in the First Shewing.

— Revelations of Divine Love, ch. 33, source. Julian says she deliberately desired a sight of hell and purgatory, “to have been more learned” and to see her faith confirmed — and was shown nothing of it. This is the hinge of her whole reserve: from being shown no damned soul she draws no inference that none is damned. The absence of the vision is exactly that — an absence — and she leaves the reproved and the fiends to the faith of the Church rather than reasoning from her silence to an empty hell.

The lord and the servant: God assigns no blame

The longest and most freighted of the showings (ch. 51) is the parable Julian pondered for twenty years. A lord sends a servant on an errand; the servant, running eagerly to obey, falls into a hollow and is sorely hurt, unable to rise or even to see his lord near. The point is where the lord's eyes rest:

…there was none [blame] seen: for only his goodwill and his great desire was cause of his falling.

— Revelations of Divine Love, ch. 51, source. The servant, she is given to understand, “was shewed for Adam” — that is, for all humankind — and the lord looks on his fallen servant not with wrath but with “pity and compassion,” rewarding the hurt rather than punishing the fall. The parable is her deepest ground for the hope: it re-reads the whole story of the Fall as a wounding that God does not hold against His creature, so that the last word about “All-Man” is mercy. It stops short, again, of a stated universalism — but it leans the whole weight of the Revelations toward the mending.

Notes

  • Reserve, not asserted doctrine. Julian belongs on this timeline as a witness that the hope endured in the medieval West, not as a teacher of apokatastasis in Origen's or Gregory of Nyssa's sense. She never denies that any soul is lost, keeps the Church's teaching on damnation intact, and lodges the reconciliation in a Deed hidden with God. Her stance is the medieval twin of Maximus the Confessor's “honourable silence” — and it prefigures the modern “we may hope, but cannot know” of Balthasar far more closely than the confident restoration of Hart.
  • How far she can be claimed. Modern readers divide. Some (Ilaria Ramelli among them) read her as a genuine, carefully-hedged carrier of the universalist hope in the Balthasarian key; others (e.g. Denys Turner, Julian of Norwich, Theologian) argue she is precisely not a universalist — that her whole achievement is to hold the promise of universal mending and the Church's hell together in unresolved theological patience. The evidence surveyed here supports the more modest reading: the hope is unmistakably present and strong, but she disciplines it into reserve rather than asserting the doctrine.
  • Text and language. Quotations follow Grace Warrack's 1901 modernized edition of the Long Text (Sloane MS). Julian's Middle English of the keynote reads “Synne is behovabil, but al shal be wel & al shal be wel & al manner of thyng shal be wele”; Warrack glosses behovable as “playeth a needful part.” The Long Text (c. 1393) is the reflective expansion of the Short Text she wrote soon after the visions of May 1373.
