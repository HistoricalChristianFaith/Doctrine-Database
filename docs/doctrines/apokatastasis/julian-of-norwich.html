Julian of Norwich on universal restoration (apokatastasis)

Dates: c. 1343–after 1416 · Wikipedia
Summary: Julian, an English anchoress, writes some five centuries after Eriugena. Her Revelations of Divine Love (the Long Text, c. 1393) circles the assurance she says Christ gave her, “all shall be well, and all shall be well, and all manner of thing shall be well,” and in the parable of the lord and the servant she is shown a God who looks on fallen humanity with pity and assigns no blame. She does not assert the restoration as doctrine: she writes that she was shown no soul in hell, that she could not see how all could be well, and that she held fast the Church's teaching that many are condemned, referring the reconciliation to a hidden “Great Deed” God has promised and reserved to Himself. Ilaria Ramelli reads her as a hedged carrier of the universalist hope; Denys Turner reads her as holding the promise and the Church's teaching together without being a universalist.

Claims & evidence

“All shall be well” — the refrain

It behoved that there should be sin; but all shall be well, and all shall be well, and all manner of thing shall be well.

— Revelations of Divine Love, ch. 27 (Grace Warrack tr., 1901), source. The word Julian records is the answer, she says, to her anguished wondering why God did not simply prevent sin: “sin is behovely” (needful, playing a necessary part) — and the outcome she is promised is that all shall be well. In her account sin is real and grievous but not final, and God will make well “all that is not well.”

The fourfold assurance of the mending

I may make all thing well, I can make all thing well, I will make all thing well, and I shall make all thing well; and thou shalt see thyself that all manner of thing shall be well.

— Revelations of Divine Love, ch. 31, source. Christ, on Julian's account, presses the assurance through the four verbs of possibility, capacity, will, and deed — and then adds that she herself shall see it accomplished. She does not go on to state that no soul is finally lost.

The Church's teaching that many are condemned (ch. 32)

One point of our Faith is that many creatures shall be condemned… all these shall be condemned to hell without end, as Holy Church teacheth me to believe.

— Revelations of Divine Love, ch. 32, source. Setting the Church's teaching beside the assurance she has been given, she confesses that she could not reconcile them — it seemed to her “impossible that all manner of thing should be well.” The answer she reports is:

That which is impossible to thee is not impossible to me: I shall save my word in all things and I shall make all things well.

— Revelations of Divine Love, ch. 32, source. The reconciliation is deferred into a “Great Deed the which the blessed Trinity shall do in the last Day” — a work “unknown of all creatures that are beneath Christ” until it is done. She sets the Church's teaching and the promised mending side by side and refers their reconciliation to God.

No sight of hell (ch. 33)

But for all my desire, I could see of this right nought, save as it is aforesaid in the First Shewing.

— Revelations of Divine Love, ch. 33, source. Julian says she deliberately desired a sight of hell and purgatory, “to have been more learned” and to see her faith confirmed — and was shown nothing of it. She draws from this no conclusion that none is damned, and leaves the reproved and the fiends to the faith of the Church.

The parable of the lord and the servant (ch. 51)

The longest of the showings (ch. 51) is the parable Julian pondered for twenty years. A lord sends a servant on an errand; the servant, running eagerly to obey, falls into a hollow and is sorely hurt, unable to rise or even to see his lord near. Julian describes how the lord regards him:

…there was none [blame] seen: for only his goodwill and his great desire was cause of his falling.

— Revelations of Divine Love, ch. 51, source. The servant, she is given to understand, “was shewed for Adam” — that is, for all humankind — and the lord looks on his fallen servant not with wrath but with “pity and compassion,” rewarding the hurt rather than punishing the fall. In the parable the Fall is a wounding the lord does not hold against his servant. It does not state that all will be saved.

Reception and debate

  • Hope and doctrine. Julian does not deny that any soul is lost, keeps the Church's teaching on damnation, and lodges the reconciliation in a Deed hidden with God. Maximus the Confessor's reserve and Balthasar's “we may hope, but cannot know” appear elsewhere on this timeline; Hart asserts the restoration.
  • Readings of Julian. Modern readers divide. Some (Ilaria Ramelli among them) read her as a genuine, carefully-hedged carrier of the universalist hope in the Balthasarian key; others (e.g. Denys Turner, Julian of Norwich, Theologian) argue she is precisely not a universalist — that her whole achievement is to hold the promise of universal mending and the Church's hell together in unresolved theological patience.
  • Text and language. Quotations follow Grace Warrack's 1901 modernized edition of the Long Text (Sloane MS). Julian's Middle English of the keynote reads “Synne is behovabil, but al shal be wel & al shal be wel & al manner of thyng shal be wele”; Warrack glosses behovable as “playeth a needful part.” The Long Text (c. 1393) is the reflective expansion of the Short Text she wrote soon after the visions of May 1373.
