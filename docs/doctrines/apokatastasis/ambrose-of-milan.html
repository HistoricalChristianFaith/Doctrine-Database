Ambrose of Milan on universal restoration (apokatastasis)

Dates: c. 339–397 · Wikipedia
Summary: Ambrose, bishop of Milan, who baptized Augustine in 387, teaches a purgative “baptism of fire” through which the saved pass, are cleansed of the “lead of iniquity,” and enter into rest. Preaching on Psalm 118 (c. 390) he says that all who return to paradise, John and Peter among them, must be proved by the fiery sword set at Eden's gate, and that only Christ, who “did no sin,” passes untouched. In these sermons the fire cleanses and then gives way to rest. Ilaria Ramelli places him at the head of a Latin line that taught a purifying, terminating penalty for believers, distinct from Origen's restoration of all; the same passages are read on purgatory.

Claims & evidence

A purgative “baptism in the vestibule of paradise” — through water here, through fire there

There is also a baptism in the vestibule of paradise, which was not before, but after the sinner had been shut out, there began to be the fiery flaming sword… that, going forth, we may say: We have passed through fire and water: here through water, there through fire.

— Exposition on Psalm 118, Sermon 3 (Gimel) §14, source. Ambrose distinguishes the Church's water-baptism from a second, post-mortem cleansing at the gate barred to Adam by the “fiery… sword” of Genesis 3:24: “here through water, there through fire.”

The consuming fire and the “lead of iniquity”

Let, therefore, the consuming fire come, that may burn out in us the lead of iniquity, the iron of sin… He, then, who has passed through the fire enters into rest, passes from things material and worldly to things incorruptible and everlasting.

— Exposition on Psalm 118, Sermon 3 (Gimel) §16–17, source. In this passage the fire does its cleansing work and the one who has passed through it “enters into rest,” passing to “things incorruptible and everlasting.” Whether a penalty after death ends or is endless is the question on which the debate over restoration turns.

All who return to paradise pass through the fire; Christ alone does not feel it

All who desire to return to paradise must be tested through the fire… God placed at the exit of paradise a flaming sword turning every way. All must pass through the flames, whether it be John the Evangelist… or … Peter… We have passed through fire and water, and thou hast brought us out into a refreshment.

One alone could not feel that fire, who is the justice of God, Christ — because he did no sin; for the fire found nothing in him which it could burn.

— Exposition on Psalm 118, Sermon 20 (Resch) §12–14, source. Ambrose names John the Evangelist and Peter among those who must pass through the fire, and exempts Christ alone, “because he did no sin; for the fire found nothing in him which it could burn.” Jacques Le Goff treats this as a classic Latin “baptism of fire” text in the prehistory of purgatory (⚠ unverified).

The resurrection in its ordered ranks: “each in his own order” (1 Corinthians 15)

All indeed shall rise again, but, as says the Apostle, ‘each in his own order.’ The fruit of the Divine Mercy is common to all, but the order of merit differs.

— On the Decease of His Brother Satyrus II.92, source (a decade earlier, c. 378). Reading the resurrection through 1 Corinthians 15:22–28 (“as in Christ all die, so too in Christ shall all be made alive”), Ambrose speaks of a mercy “common to all,” with the order of merit differing.

Reception and debate

  • Scope of the passages. In the passages quoted here the fire cleanses those on their way to paradise; they do not speak of the release of the devil or of the impious, as Origen and Gregory of Nyssa do. Ilaria Ramelli places Ambrose at the head of the Latin “mitigationist” line: a terminating, purifying penalty for believers.
  • Purgatory. The same fire-passages are read on purgatory as an early Western witness to a cleansing after death. The 1 Corinthians 3:12–15 “wood, hay, stubble… saved yet so as by fire” motif is in the background of both.
  • Augustine. Ambrose baptized Augustine in 387. In City of God XXI Augustine answered the “tender-hearted” Christians (the misericordes) who expected the damned finally released, and called the exchange an “amicable controversy.”
  • The Exposition on Psalm 118 has no complete NPNF English; the wording quoted here follows the parallel Latin/English edition at ambrose.wrootpress.com (Latin critical text CSEL 62). Ilaria Ramelli places Ambrose at the head of the Latin mitigationist line in The Christian Doctrine of Apokatastasis (Brill, 2013).
