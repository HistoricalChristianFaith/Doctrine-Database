George MacDonald on universal restoration (apokatastasis)

Dates: 1824–1905 · Wikipedia
Summary: MacDonald, the Scottish minister, poet, and novelist whom C. S. Lewis called his “master,” wrote of the restoration in sermons and fiction for a general readership. In the Unspoken Sermons he re-reads the “consuming fire” of Scripture as the purity and love of God that burns away only what is destructible, saving the person through the burning, and he describes punishment as remedial, “for the sake of amendment and atonement,” not “for divine revenge.” He looks toward an eventual restoration through this purgative mercy, and casts it as first-person belief (“I believe that no hell will be lacking…”) rather than setting out apokatastasis as a system. Gregory of Nyssa and Ambrose also describe the divine fire as purgative.

Claims & evidence

The consuming fire as “the active form of Purity”

The consuming fire is just the original, the active form of Purity,—that which makes pure, that which is indeed Love, the creative energy of God.

— Unspoken Sermons, First Series (1867), “The Consuming Fire” (on Hebrews 12:29), source. In this sermon MacDonald identifies the fire of God in Scripture with God's holiness at work: what it consumes, he argues, is sin, not the sinner, and the fire is “indeed Love.”

“So as by fire”: the destructible burned away

The destructible must be burned out of it, or begin to be burned out of it, before it can partake of eternal life. When that is all burnt away and gone, then it has eternal life. … Many a man's work must be burned, that by that very burning he may be saved—“so as by fire.”

— Unspoken Sermons, First Series, “The Consuming Fire,” source (the ellipsis marks a skipped clause). Reading 1 Corinthians 3:15, MacDonald describes the burning as the means by which the person “may be saved.” He does not confine the fire's saving work to the baptized, as Ambrose and Jerome do.

“God is not bound to punish sin; he is bound to destroy sin”

Primarily, God is not bound to punish sin; he is bound to destroy sin.

— Unspoken Sermons, Third Series (1889), “Justice,” source. In the sermon “Justice” MacDonald argues against a punishment owed as satisfaction or retribution. The end God seeks, he says, is the destruction of sin in the creature:

Not for its own sake, not as a make-up for sin, not for divine revenge—horrible word, not for any satisfaction to justice, can punishment exist. Punishment is for the sake of amendment and atonement. God is bound by his love to punish sin in order to deliver his creature; he is bound by his justice to destroy sin in his creation.

— Unspoken Sermons, Third Series, “Justice,” source. On MacDonald's account punishment exists “for the sake of amendment” and is meant “to deliver his creature.”

The hope stated as belief

I believe that no hell will be lacking which would help the just mercy of God to redeem his children.

— Unspoken Sermons, Third Series, “Justice,” source. MacDonald frames the statement in the first person (“I believe”): whatever hell exists serves “the just mercy of God to redeem his children.”

Reception and debate

  • Belief rather than system. MacDonald argues against endless retributive punishment and for a purgative discipline that redeems; he does not adopt the “Universalist” label or lays out apokatastasis as a system in Origen's or Gregory of Nyssa's manner. His positive statements are cast as belief and as a refusal to set bounds to God's mercy. Compare the reserve of Julian and of Barth.
  • MacDonald and Lewis. MacDonald set out the restorative reading of hell in his sermons and fantasies. C. S. Lewis, who called him his “master,” made him the guide of The Great Divorce, but did not follow him into universal salvation, holding instead to a hell that the damned choose and hold shut from the inside (The Great Divorce, 1945, ch. 13, text).
  • Text. Quotations follow the Christian Classics Ethereal Library edition of the Unspoken Sermons; “The Consuming Fire” is the third sermon of the First Series (1867), “Justice” a sermon of the Third Series (1889). The two “Justice” pull-quotes stand some pages apart in the sermon and are given here as distinct extracts. The timeline anchors MacDonald at c. 1867, the year of the First Series.
