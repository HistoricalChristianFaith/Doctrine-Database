Sergius Bulgakov on universal restoration (apokatastasis)

Dates: 1871–1944 · Wikipedia
Summary: Bulgakov, the Russian Orthodox theologian and dean of the St Sergius Institute in Paris, is the modern figure who most fully asserts the patristic hope — and the direct Orthodox forerunner of David Bentley Hart. In his posthumous eschatology The Bride of the Lamb (written in the 1930s–40s, published 1945) he holds that endless torment “in the sense of infinite duration” cannot be the last word, that an eternal division of humanity into saved and damned is “clearly not the final meaning of creation,” and that hell is a real but purgative and transitional state on the way to a universal deification in which God is all in all. He grounds this explicitly on Gregory of Nyssa, whose universalist answer he judges never to have been refuted — tying the modern recovery back across fifteen centuries to the Cappadocian and Alexandrian source. Unlike Barth and Balthasar, he does not stop at hope; his is an asserted apokatastasis, the Orthodox bridge between Isaac of Nineveh and Hart.

Claims & evidence

Endless torment “in the sense of infinite duration” cannot stand

reason and conscience do not accept the doctrine of the eternity of torments, in the sense of infinite duration, of an unchanging stasis.

— The Bride of the Lamb, tr. Boris Jakim (Grand Rapids: Eerdmans, 2002), p. 484 (borrowable at the Internet Archive; quoted with page cite at Bob Cornwall and Eclectic Orthodoxy). Bulgakov takes over the lexical point that had run through the tradition — that the “eternal” of the Last Judgment is age-long and qualitative, not an endless clock — and turns it against the notion of hell as a fixed, unchanging final state. Torment is real, but it is a process, not a terminus.

Hell is purgative: every person carries the “gehennic burning” kindled by Christ's coming

Every person bears within himself the principle of gehennic burning, which is ignited by the power of the parousia of Christ in glory.

— The Bride of the Lamb, tr. Jakim, p. 484 (as above; also at Eclectic Orthodoxy). Bulgakov's hell is not a place set over against the redeemed but the searing self-knowledge every creature undergoes in the light of Christ's return — a fire that purifies rather than merely retributes. Paul Gavrilyuk's study names this Bulgakov's doctrine of a “universal purgatory.” It is the same purgative reading of the fire found in Gregory of Nyssa and, in the West, in Ambrose, now made universal in scope.

An eternal division of humanity is not the final meaning of creation

An eternal separation of humanity in the elect and the reprobate is clearly not the final meaning of creation.

— The Bride of the Lamb, tr. Jakim, p. 501 (quoted at Eclectic Orthodoxy). For Bulgakov the point is not sentiment but the coherence of creation itself: a world that ends in a permanent rift between the saved and the lost would be a world God could not finally call “very good.” The restoration is the vindication of the act of creation —

Only deification is capable of justifying creation. It is the only theodicy.

— The Bride of the Lamb, tr. Jakim, p. 501 (as above). The universal deification of all is, on this view, the sole answer to the problem of evil: creation is justified only if it ends in God being all in all, without remainder.

Standing with Gregory of Nyssa, whose answer “has never been refuted”

St. Gregory of Nyssa's answer was a mighty No! And his arguments have never been refuted.

— The Bride of the Lamb, tr. Jakim, p. 486 (quoted at Bob Cornwall). Bulgakov consciously places himself in the line of Gregory of Nyssa and Origen — the “mighty No” to the eternity of hell — and judges their case never answered. This is the explicit tie by which the modern Orthodox recovery reconnects with the uncondemned Cappadocian witness at the head of this timeline.

Notes

  • Asserted, not merely hoped. Bulgakov's is the boldest form the doctrine takes between Eriugena and Hart: he does not confine himself to Barth's or Balthasar's “we may hope” but teaches the final restoration of all as the meaning of creation. He is careful to distance this from a mechanical or necessitarian universalism — the restoration is achieved through a real, transformative judgment, not by bypassing freedom — but it is an asserted apokatastasis, not a reserve.
  • The forerunner of Hart. Paul Gavrilyuk's “Universal Salvation in the Eschatology of Sergius Bulgakov” (Journal of Theological Studies 57, 2006) sets him, “following the universalist insights of Origen and Gregory of Nyssa,” as the major modern Orthodox exponent of the hope; and Hart's That All Shall Be Saved names Bulgakov as “the greatest of Eastern Church theologians in the modern era” in this line. Bulgakov is thus the immediate Orthodox ancestor of the boldest modern statement of the doctrine.
  • Text and provenance. Quotations are from the standard Boris Jakim English translation (Eerdmans, 2002) of Nevesta Agntsa, written in Bulgakov's Paris years and published posthumously in 1945; the timeline anchors him at c. 1945. The Jakim edition is available to borrow at the Internet Archive; the wording here is carried from that edition's page-cited reproduction in the scholarly and Orthodox literature (Gavrilyuk; Fr Aidan Kimel's Eclectic Orthodoxy), the key line on p. 484 corroborated across independent transcriptions. The often-repeated epigram “apokatastasis… sung in an eschatological key” is a commentator's phrase, not Bulgakov's, and is not used here.
