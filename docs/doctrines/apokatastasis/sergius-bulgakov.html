Sergius Bulgakov on universal restoration (apokatastasis)

Dates: 1871–1944 · Wikipedia
Summary: Bulgakov, the Russian Orthodox theologian and dean of the St Sergius Institute in Paris, teaches the final restoration of all. In his posthumous eschatology The Bride of the Lamb (written in the 1930s–40s, published 1945) he holds that endless torment “in the sense of infinite duration” cannot be the last word, that an eternal division of humanity into saved and damned is “clearly not the final meaning of creation,” and that hell is a real but purgative and transitional state on the way to a universal deification in which God is all in all. He appeals explicitly to Gregory of Nyssa, whose arguments, he writes, “have never been refuted.” He presents the restoration as a teaching, not only as a hope.

Claims & evidence

On “the eternity of torments” (p. 484)

reason and conscience do not accept the doctrine of the eternity of torments, in the sense of infinite duration, of an unchanging stasis.

— The Bride of the Lamb, tr. Boris Jakim (Grand Rapids: Eerdmans, 2002), p. 484 (borrowable at the Internet Archive; quoted with page cite at Bob Cornwall and Eclectic Orthodoxy). Bulgakov reads the “eternal” of the Last Judgment as age-long and qualitative, not an endless clock, and rejects the notion of hell as a fixed, unchanging final state. Torment, on his account, is real, but it is a process, not a terminus.

“Gehennic burning” and the parousia (p. 484)

Every person bears within himself the principle of gehennic burning, which is ignited by the power of the parousia of Christ in glory.

— The Bride of the Lamb, tr. Jakim, p. 484 (as above; also at Eclectic Orthodoxy). In Bulgakov's account hell is not a place set over against the redeemed but the searing self-knowledge every creature undergoes in the light of Christ's return — a fire that purifies rather than merely retributes. Paul Gavrilyuk's study names this Bulgakov's doctrine of a “universal purgatory.” A purgative reading of the fire appears earlier on this timeline in Gregory of Nyssa and, in the West, in Ambrose.

The division of humanity and the meaning of creation (p. 501)

An eternal separation of humanity in the elect and the reprobate is clearly not the final meaning of creation.

— The Bride of the Lamb, tr. Jakim, p. 501 (quoted at Eclectic Orthodoxy). For Bulgakov the question concerns the coherence of creation: a world that ends in a permanent rift between the saved and the lost would be a world God could not finally call “very good.” The restoration is the vindication of the act of creation —

Only deification is capable of justifying creation. It is the only theodicy.

— The Bride of the Lamb, tr. Jakim, p. 501 (as above). The universal deification of all is, on this view, the sole answer to the problem of evil: creation is justified only if it ends in God being all in all, without remainder.

Bulgakov on Gregory of Nyssa (p. 486)

St. Gregory of Nyssa's answer was a mighty No! And his arguments have never been refuted.

— The Bride of the Lamb, tr. Jakim, p. 486 (quoted at Bob Cornwall). Bulgakov places himself in the line of Gregory of Nyssa and Origen — the “mighty No” to the eternity of hell — and holds that their case was never answered.

Reception and debate

  • Teaching and hope. Bulgakov does not confine himself to Barth's or Balthasar's “we may hope” but teaches the final restoration of all as the meaning of creation. He distances this from a mechanical or necessitarian universalism: on his account the restoration is achieved through a real, transformative judgment, not by bypassing freedom.
  • Gavrilyuk and Hart. Paul Gavrilyuk's “Universal Salvation in the Eschatology of Sergius Bulgakov” (Journal of Theological Studies 57, 2006) sets him, “following the universalist insights of Origen and Gregory of Nyssa,” as the major modern Orthodox exponent of the hope; and David Bentley Hart's That All Shall Be Saved (2019) names Bulgakov as “the greatest of Eastern Church theologians in the modern era” in this line.
  • Text and provenance. Quotations are from the standard Boris Jakim English translation (Eerdmans, 2002) of Nevesta Agntsa, written in Bulgakov's Paris years and published posthumously in 1945; the timeline anchors him at c. 1945. The Jakim edition is available to borrow at the Internet Archive; the wording here is carried from that edition's page-cited reproduction in the scholarly and Orthodox literature (Gavrilyuk; Fr Aidan Kimel's Eclectic Orthodoxy), the key line on p. 484 corroborated across independent transcriptions. The often-repeated epigram “apokatastasis… sung in an eschatological key” is a commentator's phrase, not Bulgakov's, and is not used here.
