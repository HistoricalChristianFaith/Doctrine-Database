Origen of Alexandria on universal restoration (apokatastasis)

Dates: c. 229 · Wikipedia
Summary: In Peri Archōn (On First Principles, c. 229) Origen grounds the restoration on 1 Corinthians 15:28 — that God will finally be “all in all” — and argues that the goodness of God will “recall all His creatures to one end,” the end answering the beginning, so that after “countless and unmeasured ages” every rational nature is restored. He introduces the discussion under an announced caution, as an inquiry into questions the apostolic preaching left open, and in places describes the restoration as the consummation of all things; how far he held it as a doctrine is disputed (see below). In a letter he denied teaching that the devil would be saved, a thing he wrote that no one could say “even if he has taken leave of his senses”; how that denial is read is disputed (see below).

Claims & evidence

“Recall all His creatures to one end” (De Principiis I.6.1)

We think, indeed, that the goodness of God, through His Christ, may recall all His creatures to one end, even His enemies being conquered and subdued.

— De Principiis I.6.1, source. The whole argument of the chapter “On the End or Consummation” hangs on 1 Corinthians 15:28: when “all enemies will be subdued to Christ, when death — the last enemy — shall be destroyed,” then the kingdom is delivered up to the Father and God is “all and in all.” Origen reads that subjection as a saving submission — the same by which the saints wish to be subject to Christ — not a crushing.

“Countless and unmeasured ages” (De Principiis III.6.6)

And this result must be understood as being brought about, not suddenly, but slowly and gradually, seeing that the process of amendment and correction will take place imperceptibly in the individual instances during the lapse of countless and unmeasured ages…

— De Principiis III.6.6, source. In this chapter “On the End of the World” the restoration is described as the term of a long process: “when the end has been restored to the beginning,” all rational souls restored, the body changed “into the glory of a spiritual body,” “then verily God will be ‘all in all.’” Whether Origen held the restoration as a doctrine or as a tentative hope is a disputed reading on the condemnation timeline.

Origen's letter on the charge that he taught the devil's salvation

…they declare that I hold that the father of wickedness and perdition, and of those who are cast forth from the kingdom of God, that is the devil, is to be saved, a thing which no man can say even if he has taken leave of his senses and is manifestly insane.

— Origen, letter to friends at Alexandria, preserved in Rufinus, On the Falsification of the Works of Origen, source. Origen complained that opponents had garbled his teaching in public debate and circulated a doctored transcript. In the letter he denies holding that “the father of wickedness and perdition” is to be saved. Whether the later charge represents his teaching is the disputed reading on the devil-salvation charge.

Reception and debate

  • Caution and argument. Origen's preface assigns the origin of the soul and the nature of the devil to the questions the Church “has not explained with sufficient clearness,” open for inquiry — and he treats the restoration “in the manner rather of an investigation and discussion, than… of fixed and certain decision.” Whether he held the restoration as a hope or as a doctrine is the disputed reading on whether Origen taught universal restoration as a doctrine, and whether it was condemnable in his day.
  • The keystone text is 1 Corinthians 15:28; the doctrine takes its name from the “restitution of all things” (apokatastasis pantōn) of Acts 3:21, and is paired with the reconciliation of “all things” of Colossians 1:20.
  • Origen appears across this wiki on some sixteen timelines — the pre-existence of souls on the origin of the soul, the cleansing fire on purgatory — and the judgements passed on him are traced on the condemnation of Origen. Gregory of Nyssa (c. 380), whose restoration-passages are disputed (see the disputed reading on Gregory of Nyssa), was not condemned; the anathemas of 543 and 553 appear on this timeline.
