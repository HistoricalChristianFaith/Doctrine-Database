Theodore of Mopsuestia on universal restoration (apokatastasis)

Dates: c. 350–428 · Wikipedia
Summary: Theodore, pupil of Diodore of Tarsus and fellow-student of John Chrysostom, was bishop of Mopsuestia from 392 and an exegete of the Antiochene school — “the Interpreter” without further name to the Church of the East, which honours him still. Like his teacher he teaches a punishment that ends, and he grounds it in pedagogy: punishment as a stage in the schooling of a humanity God means finally to bring into a fixed state of blessedness. His Christology, read by his opponents as a source of Nestorianism, was condemned at the Second Council of Constantinople (553), where he was named among the “Three Chapters,” and most of his output was destroyed. His eschatology survives in Syriac fragments preserved by East Syriac writers and in the notice of Photius, who charged him with echoing Origen “in that which concerns the end of punishment.”

Claims & evidence

The penalties of the wicked “should finally come to an end”

Theodore reads the Gospel's own measures of punishment — the debtor held “till thou hast paid the uttermost farthing,” the servant beaten with “many” or “few” stripes — as showing that the penalty is finite: a graded, exhaustible payment, not an endless one. The wicked are set right by it, come to “choose good things,” and are at last “deemed worthy of the happiness of the Divine liberality”:

Those who have here chosen fair things will receive in the world to come the pleasure of good things with praises; but the wicked who have turned aside to evil things all their life, when they are become ordered in their minds by penalties and the fear that springs from them, and choose good things… will be deemed worthy of the happiness of the Divine liberality. For He would never have said, “Until thou payest the uttermost farthing,” unless it had been possible for us to be freed from our sins through having atoned for them by paying the penalty; neither would He have said, “he shall be beaten with many stripes,” or “he shall be beaten with few stripes,” unless it were that the penalties, being meted out according to the sins, should finally come to an end.

— Theodore of Mopsuestia (“Theodore the Expositor”), preserved in Solomon of Basra's Book of the Bee, which adds that “these things the Expositor has handed down in his books clearly and distinctly.”1 His rationale is sin as a passing phase in a two-age scheme — this present age of mutability schooling humanity toward the coming age of a settled, immutable good.

Photius: Theodore “echoes Origen… concerning the end of punishment”

Photius, reviewing Theodore's lost treatise Against those who say that men sin by nature, praises much of its argument but faults its author for straying into Nestorianism and Origenism — specifically, he says, Theodore “seems, in many places, entangled… in the [error] of Origen, at least in that which concerns the end of [eternal] punishment.”2 Photius's ninth-century notice and the Syriac fragment both ascribe to Theodore a punishment that ends.

Reception and debate

  • How the fragments survive. Like Diodore's, Theodore's works were largely destroyed after 553; his eschatology is preserved because the Church of the East, which never accepted that condemnation and honoured him as “the blessed Interpreter,” kept quoting him — in Isaac of Nineveh, in John of Dara, and in Solomon of Basra's Book of the Bee. These are fragments at second hand, not passages from a surviving book of Theodore's own; Photius's notice is a separate witness.
  • Scholarly readings. H. B. Swete summarised his doctrine as holding that the punishments of the condemned, “eternal” in belonging to the age to come rather than to time, are nonetheless “remissible upon repentance”; E. H. Plumptre likewise read him as teaching that those who have done evil all their lives will be “made worthy of the sweetness of the divine beauty,” and Ilaria Ramelli places him in the patristic universalist line.3
  • Condemned among the Three Chapters (553). Theodore's condemnation was Christological: at the Second Council of Constantinople his person and his “heretical writings” were anathematized as the source of Nestorianism, the first of the three condemned “chapters.” Photius, in the ninth century, charged him with Origen's error on the end of punishment. The council's sentence fell on Origen and Theodore; that it also named Diodore is ⚠ unverified. Whether the hope of a final restoration was a defined error when these men taught it is the disputed reading on whether Origen taught universal restoration as a doctrine, and whether it was condemnable in his day; the condemnation is traced on the condemnation of Origen.
