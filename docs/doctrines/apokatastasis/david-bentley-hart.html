David Bentley Hart on universal restoration (apokatastasis)

Dates: b. 1965 · Wikipedia
Summary: David Bentley Hart, an American Eastern Orthodox philosopher and theologian, argues in That All Shall Be Saved: Heaven, Hell, and Universal Salvation (Yale, 2019) that the restoration of all is certain, not only to be hoped for, as Balthasar had put it: if Christianity is a coherent whole, he holds, universalism is the only reading of it that is not self-contradictory. He appeals to Origen and Gregory of Nyssa, to 1 Corinthians 15:28, and to the claim that a God who creates knowing some will be lost forever is thereby implicated in that loss. His book appeared a year after Michael McClymond's The Devil's Redemption (2018), a history of universalism that argues against the doctrine.

Claims & evidence

Universalism is the only coherent reading of the Christian message

I mean only that, if Christianity taken as a whole is indeed an entirely coherent and credible system of belief, then the universalist understanding of its message is the only one possible. I say that without the least hesitation or qualification.

— That All Shall Be Saved: Heaven, Hell, and Universal Salvation (New Haven: Yale University Press, 2019), p. 3, as reproduced in the Wikipedia summary. Hart states the thesis “without the least hesitation or qualification,” where Balthasar spoke of a justified hope. He adds a historical claim: that “the universalist faction was at its most numerous, at least as a relative ratio of believers, in the church's first half millennium” (p. 1, as quoted by Paul Axton), the people Augustine called the misericordes, the “merciful-hearted,” and he places himself with Origen, Gregory of Nyssa and Isaac of Nineveh.

An eternal hell is a contradiction in the doctrine of God

To say that, on the one hand, God is infinitely good, perfectly just, and inexhaustibly loving, and that, on the other, he has created a world under such terms as oblige him either to impose, or to permit the imposition of, eternal misery on finite rational beings, is simply to embrace a complete contradiction.

— That All Shall Be Saved, as reproduced at Goodreads. Hart's core argument is metaphysical rather than exegetical: because God creates freely and from nothing, the final state of creation reveals God's own purpose, so a cosmos in which any rational creature is eternally lost would make that loss part of what God willed in creating — and a God who wills it “is not the savior of all,” in which case “the Kingdom is only a dream, and creation something considerably worse than a nightmare.” The appeal to free will, he argues, cannot sustain the eternal-hell view, since no finite, ignorant, conditioned choice could bear the weight of an infinite and irrevocable consequence.

Reception and debate

  • Certainty and hope. Hart distinguishes his position from Balthasar's “hope” and states the restoration as a conclusion he holds “without the least hesitation.”
  • McClymond. Hart's book appeared one year after Michael McClymond's two-volume The Devil's Redemption (2018), a history of universalism that argues against the doctrine; Hart reviewed McClymond's book in “Gnosticism and Universalism.”
  • Free will. Hart argues that a freely self-chosen eternal hell is “logically incoherent”: no rational agent “in full possession of his or her faculties” could freely reject God forever, because the will is by nature ordered to the Good. He also calls the free-will defence “the only defense of the infernalist position…worthy of being either taken seriously or refuted scrupulously.”
  • The translation behind the argument. Hart's earlier translation of The New Testament (Yale, 2017) rendered the Greek aiōnios as “of the Age” rather than “eternal,” a lexical point made by Didymus the Blind and argued at length by Ilaria Ramelli and David Konstan. Michael McClymond grants that aiōnios “need not mean ‘eternal’ in the absolute, unqualified sense”, but holds that Ramelli's rule for aïdios does not fit Jude 6, where the word describes the fallen angels' “eternal chains” (“Origenes Vindicatus,” Theological Studies 76.4, 2015, p. 824 n. 23); Ramelli replies that she treats Jude 6 “as the only biblical occurrence of aidios as describing punishment—but of fallen angels, not of fallen humans” (“Reply to Professor Michael McClymond,” p. 833).
