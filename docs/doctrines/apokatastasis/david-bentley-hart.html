David Bentley Hart on universal restoration (apokatastasis)

Dates: b. 1965 · Wikipedia
Summary: The American Orthodox philosopher and theologian David Bentley Hart gives the tradition its boldest modern statement. Where Balthasar defended a hope that all may be saved, Hart's That All Shall Be Saved: Heaven, Hell, and Universal Salvation (Yale, 2019) argues the restoration as certain: if Christianity is a coherent whole, he holds, universalism is the only reading of it that is not self-contradictory. He revives, without reserve, the case of Origen and Gregory of Nyssa, resting it on 1 Corinthians 15:28 and on the claim that a God who creates knowing some will be lost forever is thereby implicated in that loss. As the defining twentieth-first-century universalist statement, he is the natural counter-pole to Michael McClymond's contemporaneous case against the doctrine.

Claims & evidence

Universalism is the only coherent reading of the Christian message

I mean only that, if Christianity taken as a whole is indeed an entirely coherent and credible system of belief, then the universalist understanding of its message is the only one possible. I say that without the least hesitation or qualification.

— That All Shall Be Saved: Heaven, Hell, and Universal Salvation (New Haven: Yale University Press, 2019), p. 3, as reproduced in the Wikipedia summary. This is the thesis that separates Hart from Balthasar's reserve: not “we may hope” but “it must be so.” Hart adds a historical claim — that the universalists were “at their most numerous… in the church's first half millennium,” the very people Augustine dismissed as the misericordes, the “merciful-hearted” — aligning himself with the reading this timeline traces from Origen through Gregory of Nyssa and Isaac of Nineveh.

An eternal hell is a contradiction in the doctrine of God

To say that, on the one hand, God is infinitely good, perfectly just, and inexhaustibly loving, and that, on the other, he has created a world under such terms as oblige him either to impose, or to permit the imposition of, eternal misery on finite rational beings, is simply to embrace a complete contradiction.

— That All Shall Be Saved, as reproduced at Goodreads. Hart's core argument is not exegetical but metaphysical: because God creates freely and from nothing, the final state of creation reveals God's own purpose, so a cosmos in which any rational creature is eternally lost would make that loss part of what God willed in creating — and a God who wills it “is not the savior of all,” in which case “the Kingdom is only a dream, and creation something considerably worse than a nightmare.” The appeal to free will, he argues, cannot rescue the eternal-hell view, since no finite, ignorant, conditioned choice could bear the weight of an infinite and irrevocable consequence.

Notes

  • Certainty, not hope. Hart marks the far end of the modern spectrum from Balthasar: he treats the “hope” framing as an evasion and states the restoration as a conclusion he holds “without the least hesitation.” In this he stands closer to Origen and Gregory of Nyssa than any modern before him — and draws the same charge of overreach that Origen did.
  • Counter-pole to McClymond. Hart's book appeared one year after Michael McClymond's two-volume The Devil's Redemption (2018), the major recent case against the doctrine noted in this timeline's “modern recovery” section; the two are routinely read together as the defining contemporary exchange on universal salvation.
  • The translation behind the argument. Hart's earlier translation of The New Testament (Yale, 2017) rendered the Greek aiōnios as “of the Age” rather than “eternal,” the same lexical point urged by Didymus the Blind and defended at length by Ilaria Ramelli — the philological ground on which much of the modern universalist case rests.
