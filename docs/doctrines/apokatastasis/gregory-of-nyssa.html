Gregory of Nyssa on universal restoration (apokatastasis)

Dates: c. 380 · Wikipedia
Summary: Gregory of Nyssa, bishop of Nyssa and the youngest of the three Cappadocians, writes of a restoration of all creation in On the Soul and the Resurrection and The Great Catechism. He draws it from 1 Corinthians 15:28, the verse Origen had also built on, and writes that evil will be annihilated and that even the evil spirits will confess Christ's Lordship; whether this restores every rational creature, the demons included, and whether all of these passages are his, is disputed (see the disputed reading). He was not condemned: he is venerated as a saint and was named among the “holy Fathers” by the Seventh Ecumenical Council. In these passages Gregory writes that evil has no eternal existence and that the purgative fire is medicine, not vengeance, and that “after long periods of time” there will be “a restoration of those who are now lying in Sin to their primal state” and a “harmony of thanksgiving” from all creation.

Claims & evidence

“All in all” (1 Corinthians 15:28) read as the annihilation of evil

But He that becomes “all” things will be “in all” things too; and herein it appears to me that Scripture teaches the complete annihilation of evil. If, that is, God will be “in all” existing things, evil, plainly, will not then be amongst them… The excepting of that one thing, evil, mars the comprehensiveness of the term “all.”

— On the Soul and the Resurrection, source. Gregory reads 1 Corinthians 15:28 as teaching “the complete annihilation of evil”: if God is to be “in all,” evil cannot remain among existing things. Whether this restores every rational creature, or restores human nature while some are punished, is disputed (see the disputed reading).

The evil spirits' confession of Christ's Lordship

…when evil shall have been some day annihilated in the long revolutions of the ages, nothing shall be left outside the world of goodness, but… even from those evil spirits shall rise in harmony the confession of Christ's Lordship.

— On the Soul and the Resurrection, source. Here Gregory writes that even from the evil spirits will rise the confession of Christ's Lordship, reading Philippians 2:10 (“things under the earth”) alongside 1 Corinthians 15. Ilaria Ramelli and Pierre Batiffol read this as a restoration of the devil and the demons; Maximus the Confessor, a Byzantine scholiast, Patriarch Germanus I and Metropolitan Hierotheos Vlachos read Gregory otherwise (see the disputed reading).

Purgation as medicine: the image of the physician

…when, after long periods of time, the evil of our nature, which now is mixed up with it and has grown with its growth, has been expelled, and when there has been a restoration of those who are now lying in Sin to their primal state, a harmony of thanksgiving will arise from all creation…

— The Great Catechism 26, source. Gregory's controlling image is the physician who cauterizes: the patient “may be angry with the doctors, and wince with the pain of the incision; but if recovery of health be the result… they will feel grateful.” In Gregory's account the suffering is real and can be agonizing, but its end is healing. The same chapter speaks of the purgation of “the author of evil”, which “becomes a blessing to that nature, though the separation is agonizing”; how far this reaches, and whether the passage is Gregory's, is disputed (see the disputed reading).

Reception and debate

  • Not condemned. Gregory wrote of the restoration more than a century after Origen and was not condemned; he is venerated as a saint. How his standing bears on whether the restoration was condemnable in Origen's day is a disputed reading on the condemnation timeline: the disputed reading on whether Origen taught universal restoration as a doctrine, and whether it was condemnable in his day.
  • The grounding of the restoration. Gregory denied the pre-existence of souls (On the Making of Man 28–29); Kallistos Ware writes that he “abandoned Origen's speculations concerning preexistence and the precosmic fall, while holding fast to his belief in an ultimate restoration” (“Dare We Hope for the Salvation of All?”, 1998). The first anathema of 553 condemns “the monstrous restoration which follows from” pre-existence. How his teaching relates to Origen's and to what the council condemned, and why he escaped condemnation, are set out as rival readings on “How does Gregory of Nyssa's teaching on the restoration relate to Origen's and to the anathemas of 553?”
  • Medicinal fire and purgatory. The Latin delegates at Ferrara (1438) cited Gregory for a purifying fire after death; his place in the history of purgatory is treated on his purgatory page.
  • Gregory's sister Macrina, his interlocutor in On the Soul and the Resurrection, voices the doctrine in the dialogue; his brother Basil is cited on this timeline against it, from a text whose authenticity Ilaria Ramelli disputes (see Basil's page).
