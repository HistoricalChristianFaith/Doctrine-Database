Karl Barth on universal restoration (apokatastasis)

Dates: 1886–1968 · Wikipedia
Summary: Barth, the most influential Protestant theologian of the twentieth century, is the doctrine's great hedged near-witness. His revised doctrine of election in Church Dogmatics II/2 (1942) — in which Jesus Christ is both the electing God and the one elected and rejected man, so that grace's scope in Him is without evident limit — drives hard toward the salvation of all; yet Barth pointedly refused to assert it. To respect the freedom of divine grace, he held, we may neither deny that God will finally embrace all nor claim that He must — we are “forbidden to count on it” as a claim we could make on God, and yet “commanded the more definitely to hope and pray for it.” This deliberate reserve — George Hunsinger's “reverent agnosticism” — is the direct Protestant anticipation of the “hopeful universalism” Balthasar would frame a generation later, and it is why Barth belongs on this timeline not as an advocate of apokatastasis but as its most powerful modern near-neighbour.

Claims & evidence

The double refusal: neither assert apokatastasis nor close the door against it

If we are to respect the freedom of divine grace, we cannot venture the statement that it must and will finally be coincident with the world of man as such (as in the doctrine of the so-called apokatastasis). No such right or necessity can legitimately be deduced. Just as the gracious God does not need to elect or call any single man, so He does not need to elect or call all mankind.

— Church Dogmatics II/2, §35 (“The Election of the Individual”), tr. G. W. Bromiley & T. F. Torrance (Edinburgh: T&T Clark, 1957), p. 417 (borrowable at the Internet Archive; quoted with page cite in Ryan Koonz, “The Old Question of Barth's Universalism,” Theology in Scotland, p. 33). Barth guards both sides. He will not deduce universal salvation as a necessity — that would make grace a thing owed, and so no longer grace. But he immediately refuses the opposite dogmatism too:

But, again, in grateful recognition of the grace of the divine freedom we cannot venture the opposite statement that there cannot and will not be this final opening up and enlargement of the circle of election and calling.

— Church Dogmatics II/2, §35, p. 418 (Koonz, p. 38). The two sentences together are the whole of Barth's position in miniature: the circle of the elect may open to embrace all — God is free to do it — but we may neither predict it nor rule it out.

Forbidden to count on it, commanded to hope for it

There is no good reason why we should forbid ourselves, or be forbidden, openness to the possibility that in the reality of God and man in Jesus Christ there is contained much more than we might expect and therefore the supremely unexpected withdrawal of that final threat… If we are certainly forbidden to count on this as though we had a claim to it… we are surely commanded the more definitely to hope and pray for it…

— Church Dogmatics IV/3, first half, §70 (“The Falsehood and the Condemnation of Man”), tr. Bromiley (Edinburgh: T&T Clark, 1961), pp. 477–478 (borrowable at the Internet Archive). Here the tendency of the whole theology surfaces: the “super-abundant promise of the final deliverance of all men” is a possibility left genuinely open in Christ — but held as hope, never as a claim. In the same section Barth states the prohibition just as sharply the other way:

We should be denying or disarming that evil attempt and our own participation in it if… we were to permit ourselves to postulate a withdrawal of that threat and in this sense to expect or maintain an apokatastasis or universal reconciliation as the goal and end of all things.

— Church Dogmatics IV/3, first half, §70, pp. 477–478. To postulate apokatastasis — to hold it as the assured end — is, for Barth, itself a failure of faith in the divine freedom. The hope is permitted, even commanded; the doctrine is not.

No theological right to limit the loving-kindness of God

This much is certain, that we have no theological right to set any sort of limits to the loving-kindness of God which has appeared in Jesus Christ. Our theological duty is to see and understand it as being still greater than we had seen before.

— The Humanity of God (1956; ET Richmond: John Knox, 1960), pp. 61–62. Often quoted (and often mis-assigned to the Dogmatics), this is the positive edge of the same reserve: the ban on asserting apokatastasis never becomes a licence to deny that grace reaches all. Barth's refusal cuts against the confident population of hell far more than against the hope of an empty one.

Notes

  • “Reverent agnosticism,” not apokatastasis. Barth's mature position is standardly read (George Hunsinger's phrase) as a reverent agnosticism: his election doctrine tends unmistakably toward the salvation of all, but he judged it a violation of grace's freedom to convert that tendency into a doctrine. Roger Olson sums the textual record plainly — in II/2 Barth “directly addressed the doctrine of apokatastasis… and he rejected it,” even as his theology pulls toward universal scope. He belongs here, then, as the near-witness whose reserve is a method, not a hesitation of nerve.
  • The Balthasar bridge. Barth's “forbidden to count on it, commanded to hope” is the seed of Balthasar's Dare We Hope, which builds its Catholic “hopeful universalism” on precisely this posture. Read on this timeline, Barth stands to Balthasar much as Julian stands to both — the hope disciplined into reserve — while Hart, a Protestant-formed Orthodox, is the one who finally drops the reserve and asserts the doctrine.
  • Dating and text. The load-bearing statement is Kirchliche Dogmatik II/2, composed and published 1942 (English 1957, the Bromiley–Torrance translation quoted here); the timeline anchors him at c. 1942 accordingly. The IV/3 passages date from 1959 (German) / 1961 (English). The II/2 quotations are verified against a peer-reviewed reproduction with exact page cites; the IV/3 quotations follow the widely-attested Bromiley wording.
