Karl Barth on universal restoration (apokatastasis)

Dates: 1886–1968 · Wikipedia
Summary: Barth, the Swiss Reformed theologian, sets out a revised doctrine of election in Church Dogmatics II/2 (1942), in which Jesus Christ is both the electing God and the one elected and rejected man; he declines to assert the salvation of all. To respect the freedom of divine grace, he held, we may neither deny that God will finally embrace all nor claim that He must — we are “forbidden to count on it” as a claim we could make on God, and yet “commanded the more definitely to hope and pray for it.” George Hunsinger calls this position a “reverent agnosticism”.

Claims & evidence

Two statements Barth declines to make (Church Dogmatics II/2, §35)

If we are to respect the freedom of divine grace, we cannot venture the statement that it must and will finally be coincident with the world of man as such (as in the doctrine of the so-called apokatastasis). No such right or necessity can legitimately be deduced. Just as the gracious God does not need to elect or call any single man, so He does not need to elect or call all mankind.

— Church Dogmatics II/2, §35 (“The Election of the Individual”), tr. G. W. Bromiley & T. F. Torrance (Edinburgh: T&T Clark, 1957), p. 417 (borrowable at the Internet Archive; quoted with page cite in Ryan Koonz, “The Old Question of Barth's Universalism,” Theology in Scotland, p. 33). Barth will not deduce universal salvation as a necessity; on his account that would make grace a thing owed. He then declines the opposite statement:

But, again, in grateful recognition of the grace of the divine freedom we cannot venture the opposite statement that there cannot and will not be this final opening up and enlargement of the circle of election and calling.

— Church Dogmatics II/2, §35, p. 418 (Koonz, p. 38). In these two sentences the circle of the elect may open to embrace all, God being free to do it, but it may be neither predicted nor ruled out.

“Forbidden to count on this”, “commanded… to hope” (Church Dogmatics IV/3, §70)

There is no good reason why we should forbid ourselves, or be forbidden, openness to the possibility that in the reality of God and man in Jesus Christ there is contained much more than we might expect and therefore the supremely unexpected withdrawal of that final threat… If we are certainly forbidden to count on this as though we had a claim to it… we are surely commanded the more definitely to hope and pray for it…

— Church Dogmatics IV/3, first half, §70 (“The Falsehood and the Condemnation of Man”), tr. Bromiley (Edinburgh: T&T Clark, 1961), pp. 477–478 (borrowable at the Internet Archive). Here Barth leaves the “super-abundant promise of the final deliverance of all men” open as a possibility in Christ, held as hope rather than as a claim. In the same section he writes:

We should be denying or disarming that evil attempt and our own participation in it if… we were to permit ourselves to postulate a withdrawal of that threat and in this sense to expect or maintain an apokatastasis or universal reconciliation as the goal and end of all things.

— Church Dogmatics IV/3, first half, §70, pp. 477–478. On Barth's account the hope is commanded, while to postulate apokatastasis as the assured end is excluded.

“No theological right to set any sort of limits” (The Humanity of God)

This much is certain, that we have no theological right to set any sort of limits to the loving-kindness of God which has appeared in Jesus Christ. Our theological duty is to see and understand it as being still greater than we had seen before.

— The Humanity of God (1956; ET Richmond: John Knox, 1960), pp. 61–62. The passage is often quoted, and often assigned to the Dogmatics.

Reception and debate

  • Readings of Barth. George Hunsinger reads Barth's mature position as a reverent agnosticism. Roger Olson writes that in II/2 Barth “directly addressed the doctrine of apokatastasis… and he rejected it.”
  • Barth and Balthasar. Balthasar's Dare We Hope (1986/87) likewise distinguishes a hope for the salvation of all from the doctrine of apokatastasis; Julian of Norwich holds the promise of mending beside the Church's teaching on damnation; Hart (2019) asserts the restoration.
  • Dating and text. The load-bearing statement is Kirchliche Dogmatik II/2, composed and published 1942 (English 1957, the Bromiley–Torrance translation quoted here); the timeline anchors him at c. 1942 accordingly. The IV/3 passages date from 1959 (German) / 1961 (English). The II/2 quotations are verified against a peer-reviewed reproduction with exact page cites; the IV/3 quotations follow the widely-attested Bromiley wording.
