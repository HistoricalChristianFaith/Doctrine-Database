Jerome on universal restoration (apokatastasis)

Dates: c. 347–420 · Wikipedia
Summary: Jerome is the most divided witness on this timeline. In his early Origenist enthusiasm he let stand a bold “restoration of all things” in which even “the fallen angel will begin to revert to what he was created for”; when the anti-Origenist storm broke he became the doctrine's fiercest Latin prosecutor, translating Origen's Peri Archōn to expose it and charging his own teacher Didymus the Blind with it. Yet to the end he held a limited mitigation that never wholly left him: the devil and the impious “perish eternally,” but sinning Christians “must be saved after they have been punished” by a purgative, terminating fire. He belongs here as the boundary case — a man who denied Origen's universal restoration while keeping a bounded one for the baptized.

Claims & evidence

The limit he kept to the end: the impious perish eternally, but sinning Christians are “saved after they have been punished”

And if Origen does maintain that no rational creatures ought to be lost, and allows repentance to the devil, what is that to us, who say that the devil and his attendants, and all impious persons and transgressors, perish eternally, and that Christians, if they be overtaken by sin, must be saved after they have been punished?

— Dialogue against the Pelagians I.28 (c. 415), source. This is the clearest statement of Jerome's settled position, written decades after his break with Origenism — proof that the mitigation survived the turn. It is a two-tier eschatology: eternal ruin for the devil and the “impious”, but for the baptized who fall into sin a punishment that is real and then ends in salvation. He rests it on the graded penalties of Scripture: “Many are the scourges of the sinner… it is not said that he is eternally destroyed,” and “Under the law is the sinner who believes in God… and will not perish.” The NPNF editor flags that the sense of the closing sentence “is much disputed” — whether Jerome means a genuine limited mitigation or something narrower.

The same limit, more precisely: the Christian's purgative fire is “a moderated and mixed sentence of the judge's mercy”

And just as we believe in eternal torments for the devil and all deniers and impious ones… so we consider that the punishment of sinners and impious ones, even of Christians, whose works are to be tested and purified by fire, is a moderated and mixed sentence of the judge's mercy (1 Corinthians 3:13).

— Commentary on Isaiah XVIII (on Isaiah 66:24, c. 410); English in Thomas P. Scheck, tr., St. Jerome: Commentary on Isaiah, Ancient Christian Writers 68 (2015). Commenting on the “worm that does not die,” Jerome first lays out, at length and fairly, the case of “those who desire that the punishments will eventually end” from Romans 11:25–26, Galatians 3:22, and Micah 7:9 — then declines to affirm it in full (“this we God alone must leave to knowledge… he knows whom, how, and how long he ought to judge”), and settles on the two-tier reading: the devil eternal, the Christian's fire a “moderated” purgation keyed to 1 Corinthians 3:13–15. It is the most doctrinally exact statement of the retained mitigation.

The early Origenist phase he retreated from: “the restoration of all things… the fallen angel will begin to revert to what he was created for”

Therefore, in the restoration of all things, when the true physician Christ Jesus comes to heal the entire body of the church, now dispersed and torn, each person… will regain his place and start to be what he was… For example, that the fallen angel will begin to revert to what he was created for, and that man, who was ejected from paradise, will be restored to the cultivation of paradise again.

— Commentary on Ephesians II (on Ephesians 4:16, c. 388); English in Ronald E. Heine, tr., The Commentaries of Origen and Jerome on St. Paul's Epistle to the Ephesians (Oxford, 2002). Written in a single burst at Bethlehem while Jerome was steeped in Origen, this is the passage the Origenist controversy would throw back at him. Its force is genuinely contested in scope: read strongly it restores even “the fallen angel,” but Jerome (and later defenders) read “start to be what he was” as bounded to the baptized — the crux of the scholarly debate (Elizabeth A. Clark; Ilaria Ramelli). What is not in doubt is that here Jerome let the fuller restoration stand; after his break with Origenism he would insist he had only been reporting others' views, and turned the same learning against it.

Notes

  • A witness for the limited form, not Origen's. Jerome never emptied hell of the impious or the devil; his lifelong position is the two-tier one (Against the Pelagians I.28; Commentary on Isaiah 66:24). He belongs on this timeline as the Latin boundary case — alongside Ambrose, whose purgative “baptism of fire” is the same milder strand — not with the Greek universalists Origen and Gregory of Nyssa.
  • The prosecutor. After c. 393 Jerome became Origenism's chief Latin adversary: he made a literal translation of Peri Archōn to expose Rufinus's softened one, and it is his Apology against Rufinus III.27–28 that preserves, and denounces, Didymus's teaching on the restoration of the wicked. The reversal is the datum: the man who first let the doctrine stand became the reason the Latin West could name and reject it. His side of that quarrel is traced on the Origenist controversy.
  • The disputed sentence. The NPNF editor's note that Against the Pelagians I.28 “is much disputed” — possibly meaning sincere Christians rather than “merely covenanted” ones — is a real caveat on how far to press Jerome's mercy. But paired with the Isaiah passage, which is explicit that the Christian's fire is a “moderated… sentence of the judge's mercy,” the limited mitigation is hard to explain away.
  • His teacher Didymus's block already records that the restoration was “just what his pupil Jerome, turned prosecutor, had charged him with” — and Augustine would argue the milder Latin hope as the “tender-hearted” view a generation later.
