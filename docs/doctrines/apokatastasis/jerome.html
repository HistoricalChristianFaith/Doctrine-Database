Jerome on universal restoration (apokatastasis)

Dates: c. 347–420 · Wikipedia
Summary: In his Commentary on Ephesians (c. 388), written at Bethlehem while he drew heavily on Origen, Jerome let stand a “restoration of all things” in which “the fallen angel will begin to revert to what he was created for”. After the Origenist controversy broke out he wrote against Origen's teaching, translating Origen's Peri Archōn at Pammachius's request and charging his own teacher Didymus the Blind with the restoration. In works of c. 410–415 he holds that the devil and the impious “perish eternally,” but sinning Christians “must be saved after they have been punished” by a purgative, terminating fire: he denies Origen's universal restoration while affirming a terminating punishment for the baptized.

Claims & evidence

The impious and sinning Christians (Dialogue against the Pelagians I.28, c. 415)

And if Origen does maintain that no rational creatures ought to be lost, and allows repentance to the devil, what is that to us, who say that the devil and his attendants, and all impious persons and transgressors, perish eternally, and that Christians, if they be overtaken by sin, must be saved after they have been punished?

— Dialogue against the Pelagians I.28 (c. 415), source. Jerome wrote this some two decades after his break with Origenism. It sets out two tiers: eternal ruin for the devil and the “impious”, but for the baptized who fall into sin a punishment that is real and then ends in salvation. He rests it on the graded penalties of Scripture: “Many are the scourges of the sinner… it is not said that he is eternally destroyed,” and “Under the law is the sinner who believes in God… and will not perish.” The NPNF editor notes that the sense of the closing sentence “is much disputed” — whether Jerome means a limited mitigation or something narrower.

The Christian's purgative fire (Commentary on Isaiah, on 66:24, c. 410)

And just as we believe in eternal torments for the devil and all deniers and impious ones… so we consider that the punishment of sinners and impious ones, even of Christians, whose works are to be tested and purified by fire, is a moderated and mixed sentence of the judge's mercy (1 Corinthians 3:13).

— Commentary on Isaiah XVIII (on Isaiah 66:24, c. 410); English in Thomas P. Scheck, tr., St. Jerome: Commentary on Isaiah, Ancient Christian Writers 68 (2015). Commenting on the “worm that does not die,” Jerome first lays out at length the case of “those who desire that the punishments will eventually end” from Romans 11:25–26, Galatians 3:22, and Micah 7:9 — then declines to affirm it in full (“this we God alone must leave to knowledge… he knows whom, how, and how long he ought to judge”), and settles on the two-tier reading: the devil eternal, the Christian's fire a “moderated” purgation keyed to 1 Corinthians 3:13–15.

“The restoration of all things” (Commentary on Ephesians, on 4:16, c. 388)

Therefore, in the restoration of all things, when the true physician Christ Jesus comes to heal the entire body of the church, now dispersed and torn, each person… will regain his place and start to be what he was… For example, that the fallen angel will begin to revert to what he was created for, and that man, who was ejected from paradise, will be restored to the cultivation of paradise again.

— Commentary on Ephesians II (on Ephesians 4:16, c. 388); English in Ronald E. Heine, tr., The Commentaries of Origen and Jerome on St. Paul's Epistle to the Ephesians (Oxford, 2002). Jerome wrote the commentary at Bethlehem, drawing on Origen, and the passage was raised against him in the Origenist controversy. Its scope is disputed: on the wider reading it restores “the fallen angel”; Jerome himself, and later defenders, read “start to be what he was” as bounded to the baptized. Elizabeth A. Clark and Ilaria Ramelli read the passage as restorationist. After his break with Origenism Jerome said he had been reporting others' views, and wrote against the teaching.

Reception and debate

  • Two tiers. In Against the Pelagians I.28 and the Commentary on Isaiah on 66:24 Jerome holds that the devil and the impious are punished eternally and that sinning Christians are saved after punishment. Ambrose taught a purgative “baptism of fire” for the saved; Origen and Gregory of Nyssa wrote of the restoration of all (what each meant by it is disputed; see the disputed reading on Gregory of Nyssa).
  • Against Origenism. After c. 393 Jerome wrote as an adversary of Origenism in the Latin West: at Pammachius's request he translated Peri Archōn to “give the true sense of the Greek” and “set down the writer's words for good or for evil without bias in either direction”, against Rufinus's version, which he called a “misrendering” (Letter 124, to Avitus, 1), and it is his Apology against Rufinus III.27–28 that preserves, and denounces, Didymus's teaching on the restoration of the wicked. His side of that quarrel is traced on the Origenist controversy.
  • The disputed sentence. The NPNF editor notes that the closing sentence of Against the Pelagians I.28 “is much disputed”, possibly meaning sincere Christians rather than “merely covenanted” ones. The Commentary on Isaiah passage quoted above calls the Christian's fire “a moderated and mixed sentence of the judge's mercy”.
  • The timeline's entry for his teacher Didymus records that Jerome charged him with the restoration. Augustine, in City of God XXI (c. 420), answered the “tender-hearted” Christians who expected punishment to end.
