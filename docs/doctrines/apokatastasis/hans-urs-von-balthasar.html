Hans Urs von Balthasar on universal restoration (apokatastasis)

Dates: 1905–1988 · Wikipedia
Summary: The Swiss Catholic theologian Hans Urs von Balthasar gives the tradition its most influential twentieth-century form — but a deliberately minimal one. In Dare We Hope “That All Men Be Saved”? (1986/87; English 1988) he insists that neither Scripture nor the Church authorizes anyone to know, ahead of the judgment, that a single soul is damned — and that we are therefore permitted, indeed obliged in charity, to hope that all will be saved. He is careful to distinguish this “hopeful universalism” from the asserted apokatastasis of Origen, which he explicitly declines to teach: he does not claim that all will be saved, only that we may hope so. He is thus the pivotal modern witness — the one who recovered the patristic hope for mainstream Catholic theology while draining it of Origen's confident doctrine.

Claims & evidence

Hope, not certainty: we have a right to hope for all men

I claim nothing more than this: that these statements give us a right to have hope for all men… Certainty cannot be attained, but hope can be justified.

— Dare We Hope “That All Men Be Saved”? with A Short Discourse on Hell (San Francisco: Ignatius, 1988), p. 187, as reproduced in Regis Martin, “Von Balthasar and Salvation”. Balthasar's whole case turns on the gap between two verbs. Scripture, he argues, sets two series of texts side by side — those warning of a real, possible hell (Matthew 25) and those promising that God will draw “all” to himself (John 12:32) — and it does not let us synthesize them into a prediction either way. What remains open is precisely hope: not the knowledge that hell is empty, but the permission, and the duty, to desire it.

The obligation to hope for all

Whoever reckons with the possibility of even only one person's being lost besides himself is hardly able to love unreservedly.

— Dare We Hope, p. 211 (the chapter “The Obligation to Hope for All”), as reproduced in Martin, “Von Balthasar and Salvation”. For Balthasar the hope is not optional sentiment but a demand of charity: to love another truly is to will their salvation, and one cannot will it while calmly consigning them to a hell one is sure exists. He grounds this in the Church's own practice — her liturgy prays for the salvation of all without exception, and she has never declared any particular human being damned.

Against the “massa damnata”: the trick of placing oneself on the saved side

It can be taken as a motif running through the history of theology that, whenever one fills hell with a massa damnata of sinners, one also, through some kind of conscious or unconscious trick (perhaps cautiously, and yet reassuredly), places oneself on the other side.

— Dare We Hope, as reproduced at Goodreads; the full text is available to borrow at the Internet Archive. This is Balthasar's sharpest charge against the Augustinian tradition (see Augustine on this timeline): the confident population of hell with the damned always secretly exempts the theologian doing the populating. His answer is not to empty hell by decree but to refuse the standpoint from which one could count its inhabitants at all.

Notes

  • Hope, not apokatastasis. Balthasar's position is deliberately weaker than the doctrine this timeline traces. He rejects Origen's asserted restoration (and Augustine's certainty of a massa damnata) as the two errors between which the hope stands: a chapter of his book is titled “Apokatastasis: Universal Reconciliation,” and he distances himself from it as a fixed teaching even as he defends the hope. So he belongs on this page as the modern transmitter of the tradition's form — the merciful hope for all — not of its boldest content. Contrast David Bentley Hart, who a generation later abandons the reserve and argues the restoration as certain.
  • Also on this wiki as a reader of Maximus. Balthasar's monograph Kosmische Liturgie supplied the rendering of Maximus the Confessor's three senses of apokatastasis cited on this timeline; his reading of Maximus's “honourable silence” as a veiled universalism is one root of his own hope.
  • A contested but tolerated voice. The book drew heavy criticism (Balthasar answered it in the appended Short Discourse on Hell), yet he was never censured — John Paul II named him a cardinal shortly before his death in 1988 — and the hope he defended has since been voiced at the highest levels of the Catholic Church. His case shows the patristic hope re-entering mainstream Western theology, chastened into a hope rather than reasserted as Origen's doctrine.
