Hans Urs von Balthasar on universal restoration (apokatastasis)

Dates: 1905–1988 · Wikipedia
Summary: The Swiss Catholic theologian Hans Urs von Balthasar argues in Dare We Hope “That All Men Be Saved”? (1986/87; English 1988) that neither Scripture nor the Church authorizes anyone to know, ahead of the judgment, that a single soul is damned — and that we are therefore permitted, indeed obliged in charity, to hope that all will be saved. He distinguishes this hope from the asserted apokatastasis of Origen, which he declines to teach: he does not claim that all will be saved, only that we may hope so.

Claims & evidence

Hope without certainty (Dare We Hope, p. 187)

I claim nothing more than this: that these statements give us a right to have hope for all men… Certainty cannot be attained, but hope can be justified.

— Dare We Hope “That All Men Be Saved”? with A Short Discourse on Hell (San Francisco: Ignatius, 1988), p. 187, as reproduced in James T. O’Connor, “Von Balthasar and Salvation”. Balthasar's whole case turns on the gap between two verbs. Scripture, he argues, sets two series of texts side by side — those warning of a real, possible hell (Matthew 25) and those promising that God will draw “all” to himself (John 12:32) — and it does not let us synthesize them into a prediction either way. What remains open, on his account, is hope: not the knowledge that hell is empty, but the permission, and the duty, to desire it.

The obligation to hope for all

Whoever reckons with the possibility of even only one person's being lost besides himself is hardly able to love unreservedly.

— Dare We Hope, p. 211 (the chapter “The Obligation to Hope for All”), as reproduced in O’Connor, “Von Balthasar and Salvation”. For Balthasar the hope is a demand of charity: to love another truly is to will their salvation, and one cannot will it while calmly consigning them to a hell one is sure exists. He grounds this in the Church's own practice — her liturgy prays for the salvation of all without exception, and she has never declared any particular human being damned.

On the “massa damnata”

It can be taken as a motif running through the history of theology that, whenever one fills hell with a massa damnata of sinners, one also, through some kind of conscious or unconscious trick (perhaps cautiously, and yet reassuredly), places oneself on the other side.

— Dare We Hope, as reproduced at Goodreads; the full text is available to borrow at the Internet Archive. Balthasar directs the charge at the Augustinian tradition (see Augustine on this timeline): on his account, those who fill hell with the damned place themselves on the other side. His own position is not to declare hell empty but to decline the standpoint from which its inhabitants could be counted.

Reception and debate

  • Hope and apokatastasis. Balthasar sets his hope between Origen's asserted restoration and Augustine's certainty of a massa damnata: a chapter of his book is titled “Apokatastasis: Universal Reconciliation,” and he distances himself from it as a fixed teaching while defending the hope. David Bentley Hart (2019) argues the restoration as certain.
  • Reader of Maximus. Balthasar's monograph Kosmische Liturgie supplied the rendering of Maximus the Confessor's three senses of apokatastasis cited on this timeline. His reading of Maximus's “honourable silence” is one of those set out on “How is Maximus the Confessor's reserve on universal restoration to be read?”
  • Reception. The book drew criticism, which Balthasar answered in the appended Short Discourse on Hell. James T. O’Connor (Catholic; chairman of the dogma department at St Joseph’s Seminary, Yonkers), reviewing the English edition in 1989, reports Balthasar’s own account of his thesis being “cut to pieces, almost interminably” in certain German theological journals; O’Connor himself found the book’s treatment of the Magisterium “most disappointing” and his statement that some early Fathers held that no Christian, even one who had sinned grievously, ends in hell “disappointingly inaccurate” (“Von Balthasar and Salvation,” Homiletic & Pastoral Review, July 1989, pp. 10–21). He was not censured; John Paul II named him a cardinal shortly before his death in 1988.
