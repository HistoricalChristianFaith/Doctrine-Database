Friedrich Schleiermacher on universal restoration (apokatastasis)

Dates: 1768–1834 · Wikipedia
Summary: Schleiermacher, the “father of modern theology,” is the Protestant pivot — the first major modern systematic Protestant to lean, in a dogmatics, toward universal restoration. In the appendix to The Christian Faith (2nd ed., 1830–31) he weighs the traditional doctrine of eternal damnation against “the milder view… that through the power of redemption there will one day be a universal restoration of all souls,” and holds that this milder view has at least equal right — grounding it not in Origen's exegesis but in his own universalized doctrine of election, on which the redemption Christ works cannot be given a narrower scope than the fore-ordination to blessedness that carries it. He is emphatically not a bold Origenist: he places the restoration in an appendix, sets it beside the received doctrine rather than in its place, and claims only its equal standing, not its certainty. His is the hopeful-reserve key — nearer Julian and Balthasar than Origen — but voiced for the first time from within modern Protestant systematics, which is why he opens the doctrine's modern chapter.

Claims & evidence

The “milder view”: a universal restoration of all souls, with equal right

we ought at least to admit the equal rights of the milder view … that through the power of redemption there will one day be a universal restoration of all souls.

— The Christian Faith, §163 (appendix on eternal damnation), tr. H. R. Mackintosh & J. S. Stewart (T&T Clark, 1928), p. 722, as reproduced in Wojciech Szczerba, “The Concept of Universal Salvation: Apokatastasis in the Thought of Friedrich Schleiermacher,” Forum Philosophicum 26.1 (2021), pp. 116–117 (the ellipsis is the article's elision; the Mackintosh & Stewart text may be consulted at the Internet Archive, p. 722). Schleiermacher's reasoning is that eternal bliss and eternal damnation cannot finally coexist: if some are “lost in irrevocable misery” while others enjoy the highest blessedness, the sympathy that binds the redeemed to all humanity would leave the blessed forever incompletely blessed. Since Scripture supplies no “decisive testimony” that Christ Himself looked forward to such a permanently divided outcome, the milder view of a final restoration deserves, he argues, at least equal standing with the received doctrine of endless punishment.

Grounded in election: universal redemption entails a universal fore-ordination

if we take the universality of redemption in its whole range… then we must also take fore-ordination to blessedness quite universally; and that limits can be imposed on neither without curtailing the other.

— The Christian Faith, §120 (postscript), tr. Mackintosh & Stewart, p. 560, as reproduced in Szczerba (2021), pp. 115–116. This is the mechanism that makes Schleiermacher's restoration a Protestant and specifically a Reformed conclusion rather than a revived Origenism: he reaches it not through Origen's allegory or the lexical argument over aiōnios, but by refusing to let the doctrine of election be narrower than the redemption it serves. If Christ's redeeming work is genuinely universal in scope, then the divine decree that ordains its beneficiaries must be equally universal — a single election embracing all, so that the last issue of grace is the blessedness of all souls, not the eternal loss of some.

Notes

  • Reserve, not assertion — the milder view, not a dogma. Schleiermacher does not declare that all will be saved. He lodges the restoration in an appendix to the doctrine of the Last Things, sets it alongside the traditional doctrine of eternal damnation, and claims for it only “equal rights.” His scholarly reader Szczerba puts it exactly: Schleiermacher “does not explicitly advocate… apokatastasis, but gets very close,” and “expresses his hope for the future, universal and final restoration of all souls.” His stance therefore belongs with the tradition's hopeful strand — Julian before him, Barth and Balthasar after — rather than with the asserted restoration of Origen, Gregory of Nyssa, or Hart.
  • The Protestant hinge. Whatever his own reserve, Schleiermacher is standardly named the point at which the hope re-enters Protestant systematic theology: Michael McClymond — no friend of universalism, and the sharpest modern critic of the “patristic mainstream” thesis on this timeline — calls him “the first great theologian of modern times to teach universalism” (“Universalism: A Historical Survey,” Themelios, citing The Christian Faith §§117–120, 163). After Schleiermacher the doctrine is no longer merely a patristic memory condemned in the sixth century but a live option in mainstream Protestant dogmatics.
  • Text and provenance. The quotations follow the standard Mackintosh & Stewart English translation (1928) as reproduced with its pagination in the Szczerba article; the later Tice–Kelsey critical edition (2016) renders the key line to the same effect (“that by the power of redemption a general restoration of all human souls would eventually occur,” §163, pp. 997–98). The underlying German is the second edition of Der christliche Glaube (1830–31), the mature form of the work; the timeline anchors him at c. 1830 accordingly.
