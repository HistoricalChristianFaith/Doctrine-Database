Friedrich Schleiermacher on universal restoration (apokatastasis)

Dates: 1768–1834 · Wikipedia
Summary: Schleiermacher, a Reformed theologian, is called by Michael McClymond “the first great theologian of modern times to teach universalism.” In the appendix to The Christian Faith (2nd ed., 1830–31) he weighs the traditional doctrine of eternal damnation against “the milder view… that through the power of redemption there will one day be a universal restoration of all souls,” and holds that this milder view has at least equal right. He grounds it in his doctrine of election, on which the fore-ordination to blessedness cannot be given a narrower scope than the redemption Christ works. He places the restoration in an appendix, sets it beside the received doctrine rather than in its place, and claims for it equal standing. Wojciech Szczerba reads him as not explicitly advocating apokatastasis but getting “very close.”

Claims & evidence

The “milder view”: a universal restoration of all souls, with equal right

we ought at least to admit the equal rights of the milder view … that through the power of redemption there will one day be a universal restoration of all souls.

— The Christian Faith, §163 (appendix on eternal damnation), tr. H. R. Mackintosh & J. S. Stewart (T&T Clark, 1928), p. 722, as reproduced in Wojciech Szczerba, “The Concept of Universal Salvation: Apokatastasis in the Thought of Friedrich Schleiermacher,” Forum Philosophicum 26.1 (2021), pp. 116–117 (the ellipsis is the article's elision; the Mackintosh & Stewart text may be consulted at the Internet Archive, p. 722). Schleiermacher reasons that eternal bliss and eternal damnation cannot finally coexist: if some are “lost in irrevocable misery” while others enjoy the highest blessedness, the sympathy that binds the redeemed to all humanity would leave the blessed forever incompletely blessed. Since Scripture supplies no “decisive testimony” that Christ Himself looked forward to such a permanently divided outcome, the milder view of a final restoration deserves, he argues, at least equal standing with the received doctrine of endless punishment.

The ground in election: redemption and fore-ordination equally universal

if we take the universality of redemption in its whole range… then we must also take fore-ordination to blessedness quite universally; and that limits can be imposed on neither without curtailing the other.

— The Christian Faith, §120 (postscript), tr. Mackintosh & Stewart, p. 560, as reproduced in Szczerba (2021), pp. 115–116. Schleiermacher argues here from the doctrine of election, which he places in the setting of Reformed dogmatics, not from Origen's allegory or from the meaning of aiōnios: if Christ's redeeming work is universal in scope, the divine decree that ordains its beneficiaries must be equally universal, a single election embracing all.

Reception and debate

  • How firmly he holds it. Schleiermacher does not declare that all will be saved. He lodges the restoration in an appendix to the doctrine of the Last Things, sets it alongside the traditional doctrine of eternal damnation, and claims for it only “equal rights.” Wojciech Szczerba writes that Schleiermacher “does not explicitly advocate… apokatastasis, but gets very close,” and “expresses his hope for the future, universal and final restoration of all souls.”
  • McClymond's account. Michael McClymond, a historian who argues against both universalism and the “patristic mainstream” thesis on this timeline, calls him “the first great theologian of modern times to teach universalism” (“Universalism: A Historical Survey,” Themelios, citing The Christian Faith §§117–120, 163).
  • Text and provenance. The quotations follow the standard Mackintosh & Stewart English translation (1928) as reproduced with its pagination in the Szczerba article; the later Tice–Kelsey critical edition (2016) renders the key line to the same effect (“that by the power of redemption a general restoration of all human souls would eventually occur,” §163, pp. 997–98). The underlying German is the second edition of Der christliche Glaube (1830–31); the timeline anchors him at c. 1830 accordingly.
