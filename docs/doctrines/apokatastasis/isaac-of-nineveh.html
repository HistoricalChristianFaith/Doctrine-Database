Isaac of Nineveh on universal restoration (apokatastasis)

Dates: c. 613–700 · Wikipedia
Summary: Isaac the Syrian, a seventh-century East-Syriac bishop of Nineveh and ascetic writer, writes of Gehenna and the restoration in terms of God's mercy. Gehenna, on his account, is real and terrible but not the last word and not God's vengeance: those punished there “are scourged by the scourge of love,” the torment being the anguish of having sinned against a love that never withdraws. To think God created rational beings only to hand them over to endless affliction would be, he says, blasphemy; the kingdom and Gehenna alike “are matters belonging to mercy.” He is venerated as a saint in the Byzantine, Oriental and Assyrian churches and, since November 2024, is listed in the Roman Martyrology.

Claims & evidence

“Scourged by the scourge of love” (Homily 28)

I also maintain that those who are punished in Gehenna are scourged by the scourge of love. For what is so bitter and vehement as the punishment of love? I mean that those who have become conscious that they have sinned against love suffer greater torment from this than from any fear of punishment. For the sorrow caused in the heart by sin against love is sharper than any torment that can be.

— The Ascetical Homilies of Saint Isaac the Syrian, Homily 28, source. In Isaac's account the fire of Gehenna is within the sinner: it is not that God's love is withdrawn from the damned but that it is present and unbearable to those who have set themselves against it. “It would be improper,” he continues, “for a man to think that sinners in Gehenna are deprived of the love of God.” The same love that is joy to the saved is torment to the lost — a single divine reality met two ways.

The Kingdom and Gehenna as “matters belonging to mercy” (Second Part, Discourse 39)

The Kingdom and Gehenna are matters belonging to mercy, which were conceived of in their essence by God as a result of His eternal goodness.

That we should further say or think that the matter is not full of love and mingled with compassion would be an opinion full of blasphemy.

— The Second Part (Discourse 39), tr. Sebastian Brock, source. In the discourses of his Second Part Isaac reasons from the nature of the Creator: “It is not the way of the compassionate Maker to create rational beings in order to deliver them over mercilessly to unending affliction.” God “is not one who requites evil, but He sets evil aright.” Punishment is real but purposive — ordered, like a physician's cautery, to “the advantage that is going to come from all these things” — and the outcome he describes is the triumph of the kingdom over Gehenna.

Reception and debate

  • Veneration. Isaac is a canonized saint of the Byzantine, Oriental, and Assyrian churches, and in November 2024 was added to the Roman Martyrology of the Latin Church. (Wikipedia) Gregory of Nyssa, whose restoration-passages are disputed (see the disputed reading on Gregory of Nyssa), is likewise venerated as a saint.
  • Translations. The “scourge of love” passage is quoted from the Holy Transfiguration Monastery (Miller) translation, where it stands as Homily 28; the older Wensinck numbering places the same homily at 27 or 85, and renders the phrase “scourging of love.” The Second Part quotations follow Sebastian Brock's edition (CSCO 554–555, Peeters 1995), Discourse 39.
  • Isaac wrote in the Church of the East, outside the reach of the Greek anathemas of 553; in the Greek world Maximus the Confessor wrote on the restoration in the same century.
