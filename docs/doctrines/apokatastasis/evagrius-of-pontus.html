Evagrius of Pontus on universal restoration (apokatastasis)

Dates: c. 345–399 · Wikipedia
Summary: Evagrius, the desert monk and disciple of the Cappadocians who systematized the tradition of contemplative prayer, sets the restoration within a cosmology of fall and return. In the Kephalaia Gnostica (Gnostic Chapters) he teaches that all rational beings (the logikoi), fallen from an original unity, will at the last be reunited in the knowledge of the Trinity — worlds dissolved, distinctions abolished, “all… gods.” Whether the anathemas of 553 describe this Evagrian scheme rather than Origen's own text is disputed (see below). Evagrius was among those condemned by name in the reception of the Second Council of Constantinople (553), alongside Origen and Didymus.

Claims & evidence

In the final Unity, distinctions of rank vanish and “all will be gods”

In second natural contemplation, it is said that some are leaders, and others are submissive to leaders, of necessity. But, in the Unity there will not be those who are leaders, neither those who are submitted to leaders; but all will be gods.

— Kephalaia Gnostica IV.51 (unreformed Syriac S2), tr. Luke Dysinger, source. Evagrius's cosmology runs from an original Unity (henad), through a fall or “movement” that shatters it into the graded world of bodies and ranks, to a final reunification in which every rational being is restored to equality in the knowledge of God. The end answers the beginning, as in Origen; in this chapter the hierarchy of “leaders” and “submissive” that governs the present order ceases in the Unity.

The destruction of worlds and the dissolution of bodies

Accompanying the knowledge that concerns the logikoi are the destruction of worlds, the dissolution of bodies, and the abolition of names; while [there] remains the equality of knowledge according to the equality of substances.

— Kephalaia Gnostica II.17 (unreformed Syriac S2), tr. Luke Dysinger, source. The Kephalaia Gnostica survives complete only in Syriac, and in two recensions: an expurgated version (S1) with the Origenist content softened away, and the unreformed version (S2), recovered and edited by Antoine Guillaumont, which preserves statements like this one. Greek fragments confirm S2's priority. Modern reconstructions of Evagrian “Origenism” draw chiefly on S2.

Reception and debate

  • Reserve about the outcome. As Dysinger observes, Evagrius “appears to have avoided ever stating openly that ‘all will be saved’”; the restoration is carried implicitly, through the logikoi returning to their primordial state, rather than announced as a doctrine of universal salvation. Ilaria Ramelli reads him as a central heir of Origen's apokatastasis who speaks of it “rather overtly” (Ramelli, The Christian Doctrine of Apokatastasis, Brill 2013; and her translation of the unreformed Kephalaia Gnostica, SBL 2015).
  • The condemned system. The fifteen anti-Origenist anathemas of 553 describe a scheme of undifferentiated minds, a fall into graded bodies, and a final state in which every spirit is equal. Whether that scheme is drawn from the Kephalaia Gnostica rather than from Peri Archōn, so that the council condemned an Evagrian system rather than Origen himself, is the subject of the disputed reading on whether the anathemas track Evagrian Origenism.
  • Condemnation by name. Evagrius is not named in the surviving conciliar canons of 553 (which name Origen), but the council's reception — Cyril of Scythopolis, and the definitions of the Third Council of Constantinople (680–81) and Nicaea II (787) — condemns “Origen, Didymus, and Evagrius” together. See Didymus the Blind, condemned with him.
