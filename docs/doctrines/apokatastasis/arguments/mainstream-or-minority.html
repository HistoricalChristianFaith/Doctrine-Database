How widely was universal restoration held among the Church Fathers?

Disputed reading · on Universal restoration (apokatastasis)

Related readings: How does Gregory of Nyssa's teaching on the restoration relate to Origen's and to the anathemas of 553? · How is Maximus the Confessor's reserve on universal restoration to be read?

The passage

It is in vain, then, that some, indeed very many, make moan over the eternal punishment, and perpetual, unintermitted torments of the lost, and say they do not believe it shall be so; not, indeed, that they directly oppose themselves to Holy Scripture, but, at the suggestion of their own feelings, they soften down everything that seems hard, and give a milder turn to statements which they think are rather designed to terrify than to be received as literally true

— Augustine, Enchiridion 112.1

Although Origen is credited with being the founder of this doctrine [apokatastasis] in Christianity, I shall argue that he had several antecedents. I shall also argue that this doctrine was abundantly received throughout the Patristic era, up to the one who can be regarded as the last of the Fathers: John Eriugena.

— Ilaria Ramelli, The Christian Doctrine of Apokatastasis (Brill, 2013), p. 1.2

The question

Universal restoration was taught by some Fathers (among them Origen and Gregory of Nyssa) and opposed by others (among them Augustine), and Augustine reports that “some, indeed very many” Christians of his day did not believe in unending punishment. The dispute is how widely the restoration was held across the patristic centuries: as a minority view beside a much larger body of Fathers who taught unending punishment, or as a doctrine received throughout the period. The answer shapes how the condemnations of 543 and 553 and the modern revival of the doctrine are read.

A minority view beside a majority who taught unending punishment

On this reading a small number of Fathers, Origen and his heirs above all, taught universal restoration, while most Fathers of every region and century taught that the punishment of the wicked has no end.

Held by: Kallistos Ware (Orthodox bishop, “Dare We Hope for the Salvation of All?”, 1998); Michael McClymond (historian, Saint Louis University; Theological Studies, 2015; The Devil's Redemption, 2018).3

Ware, who himself argues that Christians may dare to hope for the salvation of all, describes the historical record this way: “in East and West alike—but more particularly in the West because of the influence of St Augustine of Hippo—the voices raised in favor of universal salvation remain a small minority”. He names three patristic authors who “dared to hope”: Origen, Gregory of Nyssa and Isaac the Syrian.4 McClymond counts the authors in Brian Daley's survey of patristic eschatology, The Hope of the Early Church (1991): “68 authors and texts that clearly affirm the eternal punishment of the wicked, while seven authors are unclear, two teach something like eschatological pantheism, and perhaps four authors appear to be universalists in the Origenian sense”: Origen, Gregory of Nyssa, Didymus the Blind and Isaac the Syrian. The 68 “come from each of the centuries surveyed, from both East and West, and wrote in Greek, Latin, Coptic, Syriac, and Armenian”.5 He finds “no unambiguous cases of universalist teaching prior to Origen”: the second-century authors teach everlasting punishment, so that “the claim of an unbroken tradition of Christian universalism is called into question”.6 He also holds that the universalist authors taught different things. For Origen “the end is always like the beginning”, while Gregory of Nyssa “rejected the idea of souls existing outside of mortal bodies, and so he offered a teaching on apokatastasis no longer consonant with Origen's”; in a single history of the doctrine, Origen, Bardaisan, Gregory, Isaac and Eriugena “all go into the blender and come out looking alike”. McClymond takes Basil's statements on everlasting punishment and the anathema of 553 naming Origen as authentic, and reads Augustine's Retractations 1.6 as withdrawing the early passage on the return of fallen creatures (On the Morals of the Manichees 2.7.9).7 On this reading universal restoration was a minority view held by reputable Fathers, and endless punishment was the common teaching of the patristic church.

A doctrine received throughout the patristic era

On this reading universal restoration had antecedents before Origen, was widely held in the early centuries, and was taught by many of the most influential Fathers down to Eriugena.

Held by: Ilaria Ramelli (historian of ancient philosophy and patristics; The Christian Doctrine of Apokatastasis, 2013; reply in Theological Studies, 2015); David Bentley Hart (Orthodox, That All Shall Be Saved, 2019).8

Ramelli argues that the doctrine was “abundantly received throughout the Patristic era”, from the New Testament to Eriugena, and cites Augustine and Basil as attesting that it was still widely held: “Augustine himself, after rejecting apokatastasis, and Basil attest that still late in the fourth and fifth centuries this doctrine was upheld by the vast majority of Christians (immo quam plurimi)”.9 She reads the count of 68 differently. An author should not be listed as teaching endless punishment “just because he uses πῦρ αἰώνιον, κόλασις αἰώνιος, θάνατος αἰώνιος, or the like”, because universalists from Origen to Evagrius used the same phrases for “otherworldly” or “long-lasting” fire and educative punishment; the lexical study behind this is her and David Konstan's Terms for Eternity (2007). On her reading several of “the 68” were not anti-universalist, and several of the “uncertain” were universalists: Clement of Alexandria, the Apocalypse of Peter, Eusebius, Gregory of Nazianzus, Ambrose, Jerome before his change of mind, and Augustine in his anti-Manichaean years. She weighs the names as well: Origen, the Cappadocians, Athanasius and Maximus, “on all of whom much of Christian doctrine and dogmas depends”, against lesser figures on the other list. Before Origen she points to Bardaisan, Clement, the Rainer fragment of the Apocalypse of Peter, parts of the Sibylline Oracles, and “arguably… the NT, especially Paul's letters”.10 Hart writes that “the universalist faction was at its most numerous, at least as a relative ratio of believers, in the church's first half millennium”, and that its members still believed in hell, as a purifying fire.11 On this reading the restoration was a central strand of early Christian teaching, held by the Church's leading theologians, before the sixth-century condemnations.
