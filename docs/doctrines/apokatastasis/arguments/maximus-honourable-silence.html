Was Maximus the Confessor's “honourable silence” a concealed universalism?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — the question is genuinely disputed, and turns on how much the silence is made to carry. That Maximus declines to assert the restoration, endorses the Nyssen “restoration of the soul's powers,” applies only aiōnios (not aïdios) to punishment, and writes under the shadow of the 553 condemnation is not in doubt; that his reserve therefore conceals a settled universalist doctrine is. The strongest single text pulls the other way — he restores every soul to the knowledge of God but expressly not to participation in Him — and he roots final destiny in the freely exercised will, refusing an automatic salvation. So the reading is strong by the logic-of-the-system lights of its advocates and weak by the plain-text lights of its critics, with the best-supported middle position being a reserved hope rather than a hidden dogma. Originated by Hans Urs von Balthasar; pressed to a confident universalism by Ilaria Ramelli, Mark Chenoweth, and David Bentley Hart; answered by Brian Daley, Jean-Claude Larchet, and Andreas Andreopoulos.

The claim

…a better and more secret explanation, which is kept in the minds of the mystics, but we, as well, will honour by silence.

— Maximus, Quaestiones ad Thalassium (on the restoration), tr. Andreas Andreopoulos, link.1 The interpretation weighed here is that this reticence is a screen: that Maximus knew, accepted, and could not safely publish the doctrine of universal restoration after Justinian's edict of 543 and the fifth ecumenical council of 553, and so buried it in a “silence” that his readers were meant to decode — a coded apokatastasis, not an open question. The label itself is not Maximus's: “honourable silence” is Brian Daley's coinage for these passages, and much of the debate is a quarrel over what Daley named.

Who advances it

The reading was originated by Hans Urs von Balthasar in Cosmic Liturgy: he was, as Chenoweth puts it, “the first to argue that there are certain passages where Maximus implicitly refers to universal salvation as a theological opinion to be ‘honored with silence.’” Balthasar gathered the texts in which Maximus “uses the language of Gregory of Nyssa to speak…of God's universal will for human salvation and of the redemption of the whole of human nature” — the whole species saved from death, all sharing the resurrection, the world “subject” to the Father (1 Corinthians 15:28) — and read the reserve as a mystical withholding rather than an absence of conviction.2 Balthasar himself stopped at hope (his own eschatology is the “hopeful universalism” of Dare We Hope); the harder claim — that the silence hides a confident doctrine — is pressed by Ilaria Ramelli, who enrolls Maximus among the universalists outright; by Mark Chenoweth, the reading's most credentialed academic defender; and by David Bentley Hart, for whom Maximus's system is “irresistibly universalist in its logic” and the restoration “almost certainly the issue on which he chose to maintain his famous ‘honorable silence.’”3

The evidence for

He endorses the Nyssen restoration, and speaks its universal-sounding language. In the Quaestiones et dubia Maximus lists three senses of “restoration,” the third being the one “Gregory especially” loves — the fallen powers of the soul returned to their created state. He does not brand it heresy; he defends Gregory of Nyssa against the charge of Origenism by re-reading his apokatastasis in an acceptable sense. Alongside this run the passages Balthasar collected, in which the whole of human nature is redeemed and, at the end, God is “all in all” (1 Corinthians 15:28) — the keystone proof-text of the whole tradition.4

He speaks of punishment only as aiōnios, never aïdios. This is Ramelli's lexical linchpin, carried over from her and David Konstan's Terms for Eternity: Maximus “speaks only of punishment aiōnios” — “of the age” — “not aïdios,” the word for strictly endless duration, “and talks about restoration with circumspection after Justinian, also using a persona to express it.” On her reading the reserve is exactly what one would expect of a man who held the doctrine but wrote after it had been anathematized.5

The silence is aimed at “beginners.” In Ad Thalassium 43 Maximus says he will give the answer “suitable for all, and meaningful for both beginners and the more advanced,” and elsewhere that “the deeper secrets of the divine doctrines must not be committed to writing.” Chenoweth reads this as pedagogical concealment: the universalist hope is real but withheld from the spiritually immature, “since beginners grow in their salvation through the fear of hell” — strategy, not doctrinal rejection. A doctrine one merely doubts, the argument runs, one does not bother to hide.6

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The negative half is secure. Maximus is plainly no opponent of the restoration in the manner of Augustine: he defends Gregory of Nyssa, adopts his restoration-language, confines punishment to the age-word, and keeps a deliberate reserve on a doctrine the councils had just condemned. The context is real, and it tells: a man who wrote what Maximus wrote, when he wrote it, plausibly had more in mind than he set down. If the claim is only that Maximus was drawn toward the hope and declined to attack it, the evidence carries it.

What is overstated. The step from “drawn toward the hope, and reticent” to “a concealed universalist doctrine” is where the reading outruns its texts.

  • The strongest single text limits the restoration to knowledge, not salvation. In the very passage on the three senses (Quaestiones et dubia, PG 90:796; Prassas no. 19), Maximus says the restored souls, after long ages, reach God and “are restored to their original state through their knowledge [of God], but do not participate in [his] gifts.” This is the load-bearing distinction, and it cuts against the reading: at the end every soul regains the knowledge of the good (epignōsis) and sees that God is not the cause of its ruin, but not the participation (methexis) that is beatitude. Jean-Claude Larchet reads this as Maximus quietly correcting Gregory rather than echoing him — universal knowledge, not universal salvation — and answers his own governing question, whether deification reaches all creatures without exception, in the negative. So the boldest restoration-text in Maximus is also the one that most clearly stops short of universal salvation.7
  • He grounds destiny in the will and refuses an automatic restoration. In Ambiguum 7 Maximus dismantles the Origenist machinery that made the older apokatastasis work — he inverts Origen's “rest–motion–becoming” into “becoming–motion–rest,” so that rest is the God-given end, not a primal state to lapse from — and locates salvation and sin in the freely exercised will, not in nature: for Maximus neither is determined by nature, so all rational beings are not saved simply by following it. The “ever-moving rest” he puts in place of Origen's henad is built precisely to answer the anathemas from within, told on the timeline of Origen's condemnation. And in Ambiguum 42 and 65 he speaks of eternal punishment and of “ever-ill-being” for those who used their being against nature — texts that, whatever their weight, an outright universalist would have to explain away.8
  • The originator read the silence more modestly than his heirs. Balthasar coined the “honourable silence” framing but did not make Maximus a covert dogmatist; he held that Origen and Gregory's “universal apokatastasis…must have struck Maximus as not taking seriously enough either the gratuitousness of grace or the seriousness of created freedom.” The confident-concealed reading (Ramelli, Chenoweth, Hart) is a development past Balthasar, not a report of him — and Ramelli's own supports are partly her characterization of other scholars and partly the contested aiōnios/aïdios rule that independent specialists find overstated (weighed on its own page).9

What the silence most likely names. Two sincere readings stand against the concealed-doctrine one, and neither is a mere dodge. Brian Daley, who named the phenomenon, reads the reserve as reverence before a genuine paradox — that God's love, offered equally to all, becomes hell for those who refuse it — “a paradox one honors best by a reverential silence”; his Maximus hopes the restoration may be true while stressing that after the Judgment “change, repentance and forgiveness will then no longer be possible.” Andreas Andreopoulos, in the fullest recent treatment, reaches the same middle: the silence “suggests that he distanced himself from a simplistic approach and recognized the contentious issue as a meaningful mystery”; the three-senses passage “may be the boldest statement in support of the apokatastasis of all beings…although he certainly keeps a safe distance from any bold and sweeping arguments about it”; and, decisively, “Maximus never gives his clear support to the idea, and…never engages with it at length.” The real question, Andreopoulos writes, is “the distance between the certainty of a universal restoration and the hope and possibility for all souls to be saved” — and Maximus sits on the hope side of that distance, not the certainty side.10

Assessment

contested. The field is really three-way, and the page title names its two poles. That the silence conceals a settled universalism is genuinely advocated, on real textual and contextual grounds — the endorsement of Nyssa, the universal-restoration language, the age-word for punishment, the post-553 reserve aimed at “beginners” — and it cannot be refuted, because a silence by definition withholds its content. But it cannot be established either: the one text that engages the restoration most directly limits it to knowledge and expressly denies participation, Maximus roots final destiny in the freely exercised will and refuses an automatic salvation, and he leaves genuine punishment-texts on the books. The strongest denial (Larchet) reads him as positively excluding universal salvation; the best-supported single position (Daley, Andreopoulos, and Andrew Louth) is neither pole but the middle — a reserved hope before a mystery, the reverence of a man who will not dogmatize what he cannot see. So the concealed- universalism reading is strong by the logic-of-the-system lights of Ramelli, Chenoweth, and Hart and weak by the plain-text lights of Larchet and Andreopoulos — which is what contested names. Balthasar's own “veiled hope” is the more defensible form of the reading; the “confident concealed doctrine” his heirs built on it is the overreach, flagged on Hart's detail page. Maximus's reserve is of a piece with a wider thread of hedging on the last things — Gregory of Nazianzus's door left ajar, Julian of Norwich's hidden “Great Deed,” and Barth's “reverent agnosticism” — a reticence that is its own eschatological posture, not a cipher for a suppressed doctrine.
