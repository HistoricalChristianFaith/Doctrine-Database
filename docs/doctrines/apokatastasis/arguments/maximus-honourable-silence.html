How is Maximus the Confessor's reserve on universal restoration to be read?

Disputed reading · on Universal restoration (apokatastasis) · witness: Maximus the Confessor

Related readings: How does Gregory of Nyssa's teaching on the restoration relate to Origen's and to the anathemas of 553? · How widely was universal restoration held among the Church Fathers?

The passage

…a better and more secret explanation, which is kept in the minds of the mystics, but we, as well, will honour by silence.

— Maximus, Quaestiones ad Thalassium (on the restoration), tr. Andreas Andreopoulos.1

For, just as the whole of human nature in the resurrection must have back the incorruptibility of the flesh in the time we hope for, so also the subverted faculties of the soul, during a long succession of aeons, will have to lose all memories of evilness found in it. Then the soul, after crossing all aeons without finding rest, will arrive at God, who has no limit, and thus, by virtue of knowledge of, if not yet of participation in, the goods, will recover its faculties and be restored to its original state. And the Creator will be manifested to it—the Creator, who is not responsible for sin.

— Maximus, Quaestiones et dubia 19, on the restoration “which Gregory of Nyssa especially” speaks of, tr. Ilaria Ramelli.2

The question

Maximus the Confessor (c. 580–662), writing after the condemnations of 543 and 553, accepts Gregory of Nyssa's “restoration” of the soul's faculties, writes elsewhere of punishment for those who misuse their nature, and in three passages of the Questions to Thalassius declines to set down a “more secret explanation”. Brian Daley's title for these passages, “honorable silence”, has become the name of the question. The dispute is what Maximus held about the final destiny of all rational creatures, and what the silence withholds. The answer bears on whether a hope of universal restoration continued in Greek theology after 553.

No universal salvation: one presence of God, received as glory or as torment

On this reading Maximus did not hold universal salvation: all are restored to the knowledge of God, but those who refused him experience the same presence of God as punishment, without end.

Held by: Georges Florovsky (Orthodox, The Byzantine Fathers of the Sixth to Eighth Century, from lectures of 1928–31); Brian Daley (Jesuit patristic scholar, 1982) ⚠ unverified; Jean-Claude Larchet (La divinisation de l'homme selon saint Maxime le Confesseur, 1996) ⚠ unverified.3

Florovsky reads Maximus’s scheme as Gregory’s and, through him, Origen’s: “that is, apokatastasis, but an apokatastasis of nature, not of freedom.” “To recognize good does not mean to love or choose it”, and “The Logos will be everything for everybody, but it will not be a blessed Sabbath and repose for everybody.” In his account “St. Maximus flatly rejects Origen’s conception of the apokatastasis”.4 Elsewhere he writes that Maximus “admitted an ‘apokatastasis’ in the sense of a restitution of all beings to an integrity of nature… but it does not mean that all will equally participate in this revelation of the Good”, and that he “draws a clear distinction between an έπίγνωσις and a μέθεξις”, knowledge and participation.5 Larchet is reported to read Quaestiones et dubia 19, where the soul is restored “by virtue of knowledge of, if not yet of participation in, the goods”, as Maximus correcting Gregory: universal knowledge, not universal salvation ⚠ unverified.6 Maximus's account of the end then joins the universal statements to the punitive ones as one event. God “offers Himself wholly and simply to all – worthy and unworthy”, and gives each “the permeance of eternal being”, so that “there is an intensification and increase of punishment for those who cannot participate, and of enjoyment for those who can participate”.7 Bradshaw and Green, as Chenoweth reports them, read both sets of statements as applying “to the same event: the Parousia where Christ is ‘all in all,’ but for the wicked, this is experienced as eternal torment”, and Chenoweth calls this “the dominant… interpretation of the Orthodox Church”.8 Daley, whom Chenoweth calls “skeptical that Maximus espoused universalism”, concludes that God, “who loves all creatures equally – even the damned”, “will not (perhaps cannot) prevent his love and presence from becoming Hell itself for those who have refused them”: “It is a paradox – perhaps even a paradox one honors best by a reverential silence – but a paradox at the very heart of the Christian doctrine of creation.”9 Andreopoulos notes that in Ambiguum 7 Maximus criticizes Origen's idea of a final rest that returns to the first: for Maximus “the end is not the same as the beginning”, and “neither salvation nor sin is determined by nature” but by the will.10 On this reading the restoration Maximus accepts from Gregory is the restoration of nature and knowledge, and the “all in all” is the one presence of God, which the righteous enjoy and the wicked suffer.

Universal salvation, kept in silence for the mature

On this reading Maximus held that all will be saved, and kept the teaching back from readers not ready for it, as Origen had advised.

Held by: Hans Urs von Balthasar (Catholic, Kosmische Liturgie, 1941; Dare We Hope, 1988, n. 21), as Chenoweth reports him; Ilaria Ramelli (The Christian Doctrine of Apokatastasis, 2013; 2015; 2022); David Bentley Hart (Orthodox, That All Shall Be Saved, 2019) ⚠ unverified; Mark Chenoweth (2020).11

Balthasar gathered the texts in which Maximus uses “the language of Gregory of Nyssa to speak, in general terms, of God's universal will for human salvation and of the redemption of the whole of human nature”: “the whole species will be saved from death”, “all will share in the resurrection”, the whole world will be “subject” to the Father (1 Corinthians 15:28), and the Logos “saves everyone, the whole race”.12 Chenoweth reports that Balthasar was the first to argue that certain passages refer to universal salvation “as a theological opinion to be ‘honored with silence’”, a doctrine “that should only be disclosed to the spiritually mature”.13 Ramelli notes that “among Maximus' criticisms of Origenistic doctrines” the restoration “is never included”, and that “the form of apokatastasis supported by St Gregory was overtly endorsed by St Maximus”, who adopted it “with prudence (by means of strategies such as the veil of silence, the attribution to another person, and the like)”. She adds that he “speaks only of punishment aiōnios, not aīdios”, the age-word rather than the word for strict endlessness.14 Chenoweth argues that “the entire thrust of Maximus' theology points almost unequivocally in a universalist direction”, and that the silence passages “make the best sense when interpreted as referring to universal salvation—spiritual beginners should never be let in on the ‘universalist secret,’ lest it make them spiritually lazy”. A silence about the unknowable essence of God, he notes, has nothing to withhold; Maximus says he knows an interpretation and will not give it.15 Hart, as reported, calls the system “irresistibly universalist in its logic” and the restoration “almost certainly the issue on which he chose to maintain his famous ‘honorable silence’”.16 On this reading the silence is pastoral reserve about a doctrine Maximus held.

A reverent hope before a mystery

On this reading Maximus allowed a hope that all may be saved but declined to teach it as certain, because salvation needs the free response of each will.

Held by: Andrew Louth (Orthodox, Introducing Eastern Orthodox Theology, 2013); Andreas Andreopoulos (Orthodox, University of Winchester, 2015).17

Louth writes that “St. Maximos the Confessor likewise holds out the hope of the salvation of all”, grounded on “the long-suffering love of God for all creation” and on evil being “without substance”.18 Andreopoulos reads the silence as showing “that he distanced himself from a simplistic approach and recognized the contentious issue as a meaningful mystery”. “Maximus never gives his clear support to the idea”, and several passages “speak of eternal punishment for the ones who freely used the logos of their being contrary to nature” (Ambigua 42 and 65; Ad Thalassium 59). The restoration of the soul's faculties in Quaestiones et dubia 19 is common to all, like the resurrection, but Maximus distinguishes the knowledge of God it brings from “full participation in God, which is a condition to which one proceeds by exercising one's natural (restored) yet always free will”. Andreopoulos calls the passage “the boldest statement in support of the apokatastasis of all beings that we can find in the writings of Maximus, although he certainly keeps a safe distance from any bold and sweeping arguments about it”, and frames the question as “the distance between the certainty of a universal restoration and the hope and possibility for all souls to be saved”. In his summary, Maximus's approach “allows us to hope and pray for the repentance, forgiveness, and salvation of all”, while “a salvation that is automatically and mechanically common for all would deny the freedom of the soul”.19 On this reading the silence is reverence before an open question: Maximus hoped, and did not dogmatize.
