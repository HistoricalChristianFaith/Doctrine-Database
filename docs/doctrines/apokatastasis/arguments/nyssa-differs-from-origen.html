How does Gregory of Nyssa's teaching on the restoration relate to Origen's and to the anathemas of 553?

Disputed reading · on Universal restoration (apokatastasis) · witness: Gregory of Nyssa

Related readings: How widely was universal restoration held among the Church Fathers? · How is Maximus the Confessor's reserve on universal restoration to be read?

The passage

In like manner, when, after long periods of time, the evil of our nature, which now is mixed up with it and has grown with its growth, has been expelled, and when there has been a restoration of those who are now lying in Sin to their primal state, a harmony of thanksgiving will arise from all creation, as well from those who in the process of the purgation have suffered chastisement, as from those who needed not any purgation at all.

— Gregory of Nyssa, The Great Catechism 26.1

IF anyone asserts the fabulous pre-existence of souls, and shall assert the monstrous restoration which follows from it: let him be anathema.

— The Anathemas against Origen, I (received at Constantinople, 553).2

The question

Gregory of Nyssa speaks of a restoration of all creation, reads 1 Corinthians 15:28 as the end of evil, and the same chapter of the Great Catechism speaks of the purgation of “the author of evil”, which “becomes a blessing to that nature”; how far this reaches, and whether the passage is Gregory's, is part of the dispute below. He was never named in the sentences of 543 and 553 that condemned a restoration taught under Origen's name, and he is honoured as a saint. The dispute is what his restoration-passages teach, whether they are his, and how they stand to Origen's teaching and to the first anathema of 553. The answer bears on whether a hope of universal restoration was held without censure after Origen. For the reading that Gregory's restoration was one Father's private opinion, the modern holders located are Orthodox priests writing for a general readership (Fr Lawrence Farley; Fr John Whiteford, as a fall-back); the academic literature for that reading was not located.

A restoration of human nature and of the soul's powers, not of every person

On this reading Gregory's “restoration” restores human nature and the soul's faculties to their first state, and does not teach that every person will at last be saved.

Held by: Maximus the Confessor (Quaestiones et dubia 19, seventh century); Metropolitan Hierotheos Vlachos (Life After Death) ⚠ unverified; Mario Baghos (Phronema, 2012) ⚠ unverified; Fr Ignatius Green (introduction to his translation of the Catechetical Discourse) ⚠ unverified.3

Maximus, in Ramelli's paraphrase, “claims that the Church knows and therefore accepts three kinds of apokatastasis”, and names the third as the one Gregory uses: the restoration of the soul's faculties, corrupted by sin, to their original state. Its scope is the nature as a whole, set beside the resurrection: “just as the whole of human nature in the resurrection must have back the incorruptibility of the flesh”, so the soul's faculties lose the memory of evil, and the soul, “by virtue of knowledge of, if not yet of participation in, the goods, will recover its faculties and be restored to its original state”.4 A Byzantine scholiast in a manuscript of On the Soul and the Resurrection glosses Gregory in the same direction: “Do not think that the Author said this … concerning all human beings”, but of those with only “small, rare traces” of the passions; God “will be glorified and worshipped by all, but for some this will happen through the enjoyment of the Kingdom, while for others while being punished”.5 On this reading Gregory's other works carry the same limit. In On Infants' Early Deaths he says of Judas that “on account of the depth of the ingrained evil, the chastisement in the way of purgation will be extended into infinity”.6 Vlachos, as reported by John Whiteford, argues that Gregory taught that Gehenna and its punishments are unending, and that the universalist reading fails to take his teaching as a whole; Baghos, as quoted by Ene Drăghici-Vasilescu, reads any allusion to universal salvation in Gregory “as an expression of God's intention for humanity”, a salvation each person may accept or refuse.7 On this reading Gregory's restoration is not the one that 553 condemned.

Passages inserted into Gregory's works by Origenists

On this reading the passages that teach a final restoration of demons and the damned are not Gregory's: later Origenists wrote them into his works.

Held by: Germanus I, Patriarch of Constantinople (715–730), in his lost Antapodotikos; Tillemont (Mémoires pour l'histoire ecclésiastique, 1703), who, Pierre Batiffol reports, “inclines to the opinion that St. Germanus had good grounds”.8

Germanus wrote to show that Gregory's works were free of Origenism. Photius, summarizing the lost work, reports his charge that those who taught the restoration of demons and of the damned had dared “to instill into the pure and most healthful spring of his writings the black and dangerous poison of the error of Origen, and to cunningly attribute this foolish heresy to a man famous alike for his virtue and his learning”. Such interpolators, Germanus held, “sometimes by faked additions, sometimes by their constant efforts to pervert orthodox thinking, have attempted to falsify many of Gregory's works, which were beyond reproach”.9 Against the restoration Germanus set “the words of the Lord, the preaching of the apostles, and the testimony of the prophets, which clearly expound that, just as the ineffable beatitude of the just is eternal, so is also the punishment of sinners infinite and unbearable”. Batiffol traces the theory to the “verbal contradictions” between these passages and others in which Gregory calls the punishment of the lost eternal, aionia. A gloss in one manuscript of On the Soul and the Resurrection records the result: “Blessed Germanus expunged as spurious a part of the preceding words, marking them with an obelos.”10 On this reading the anathemas of 553 and Gregory's own teaching agree, and the disputed passages belong to the Origenists the council condemned.

A Father's private opinion, if it is his

On this reading Gregory may have held the restoration, but as one Father's opinion on a question no council had yet examined, which does not bind the Church.

Held by: the Greek answer on purgatory at the Council of Ferrara (1438), which Syropoulos ascribes to Mark of Ephesus; Fr Lawrence Farley (Orthodox priest, Pravoslavie.ru).11

At Ferrara the Latin delegates cited Gregory of Nyssa for a purifying fire after death, and the Greek delegates answered that he was the one Father who “apparently, speaks more to your advantage than any of the other Fathers”, and answered by placing the passages rather than contesting them: “Preserving all the respect due to this Father, we cannot refrain from noticing, that he was but a mortal man, and man, however great a degree of holiness he may attain, is very apt to err, especially on such subjects, which have not been examined before or determined upon in a general Council by the Fathers.” Speaking of Gregory, the Greek teachers, as Ivan Ostroumoff narrates, more than once qualified their words with “if such was his idea”, and closed: “we must view the general doctrine of the Church, and take the Holy Scripture as a rule for ourselves, nor paying attention to what each has written in his private capacity.”12 Farley puts the same view in modern terms: “even if Gregory of Nyssa did actually teach that all will be saved, his was still simply a single individual opinion”; Whiteford adds that, if Gregory did teach it, “that would only prove St. Gregory to be in error, because Ecumenical Councils are infallible, whereas no Church Father, as an individual, is”.13 On this reading the question of what Gregory meant can stay open, because a saint's opinion on a question not yet settled when he wrote is not the Church's teaching.

Origen's universal restoration, carried forward from Origen's arguments

On this reading Gregory taught the restoration of all rational creatures, the devil included, as Origen did and from Origen's own arguments.

Held by: Pierre Batiffol (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1907); Ilaria Ramelli (The Christian Doctrine of Apokatastasis, 2013; Studia UBB Philosophia, 2022).14

Batiffol writes that “this doctrine was explicitly taught by St. Gregory of Nyssa, and in more than one passage”: On the Soul and the Resurrection, the Great Catechism 26 and On the Dead. With Bardenhewer and Petavius he holds that Germanus's explanation “cannot hold”, and that “the doctrine of the apokatastasis is not, indeed, peculiar to St. Gregory of Nyssa, but is taken from Origen”. Where Gregory calls punishment eternal, Bardenhewer, as Batiffol reports, finds that he “understood this eternity as a period of very long duration, yet one which has a limit”.15 Ramelli calls Gregory one of Origen's “most insightful followers”. In In illud: Tunc et ipse Filius he reads 1 Corinthians 15:28 against the neo-Arians, “deriving each argument from Origen”, and argues for “the eventual apokatastasis of all creatures, including even the devil”; he writes that “no creature of God will fall out of the Kingdom of God”. The Great Catechism says that the divine fire, by purging evil from the nature of “the author of evil”, “becomes a blessing to that nature, though the separation is agonizing”.16 On pre-existence Ramelli holds that “Origen probably never supported either the theory of transmigration of souls or their disembodied pre-existence”, so that this point does not divide the two. She explains Gregory's escape from condemnation by several reasons that “reinforce one another”: his restoration-passages were later read as references to purgatory and glossed; Germanus ascribed them to Origenist interpolators; Maximus the Confessor endorsed Gregory's form of the restoration; and Gregory himself became a touchstone of orthodoxy.17

Origen's hope without Origen's account of the beginning

On this reading Gregory kept Origen's hope that all will be saved but dropped the pre-existence of souls and the pre-cosmic fall, which the first anathema of 553 ties to the condemned restoration.

Held by: Kallistos Ware (Orthodox bishop, “Dare We Hope for the Salvation of All?”, 1998); Ene Drăghici-Vasilescu (“Gregory of Nyssa”, encyclopaedia entry, 2017); Michael McClymond (Saint Louis University, Theological Studies, 2015), who himself argues against universalism.18

Ware notes that the first anathema “does not speak only about apocatastasis but links together two aspects of Origen's theology: first, his speculations about the beginning, that is to say, about the preexistence of souls and the precosmic fall; second, his teaching about the end”; in it “Origen's eschatology is seen as following directly from his protology, and both are rejected together”. Gregory, he writes, “abandoned Origen's speculations concerning preexistence and the precosmic fall, while holding fast to his belief in an ultimate restoration”.19 Ware cites On the Soul and the Resurrection, where Gregory repudiates Origen's doctrine of pre-existence; in On the Making of Man Gregory rejects the view of “those who ascribe to souls a fabulous pre-existence in a special state”, and places “neither the soul before the body, nor the contrary”. He grounds the end of evil in its lack of being: vice is opposed to virtue “not as being any existence in itself, but only as becoming thinkable by the absence of the better”.20 Drăghici-Vasilescu writes that “Nyssen managed to escape this circularity because for him Restoration (apokatastasis) does not imply the existence of primordial logikoi, and even less that of the episode concerning their fall”; his end is reached “when the complete whole of our race shall have been perfected from the first man to the last”. McClymond puts the same point historically: Gregory “rejected the idea of souls existing outside of mortal bodies, and so he offered a teaching on apokatastasis no longer consonant with Origen's”. Ware draws the consequence for the anathemas: if Gregory was treated differently from Origen, “perhaps it was because, while retaining Origen's hope in the eventual triumph of good over evil, he abandoned the notion of preexistence and so avoided the circularity of the Origenist scheme”, so that, “if dissociated from speculations about a precosmic fall, a carefully qualified expression of universal hope is acceptable, even within the bounds of strict orthodoxy”.21 On this reading Gregory's restoration stands apart from the one the first anathema ties to pre-existence, though its hope is Origen's.
