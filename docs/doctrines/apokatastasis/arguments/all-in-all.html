Does “that God may be all in all” (1 Corinthians 15:28) entail universal restoration?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: plausible — the keystone proof-text of the whole tradition genuinely bears the universalist weight put on it: read at full strength, “God all in all” leaves no province of the rational creation permanently outside God, and the reading is old, serious, and anciently acknowledged as available even by its opponents. But it is a reading the verse permits, not one it compels: the immediate context is the subjection of Christ's enemies under his feet and the handing-over of the mediatorial kingdom, and “all in all” is at least as naturally the undisputed sovereignty of God as the salvation of all. So the verse grounds the hope without establishing it — and the proponents who call it the only possible reading overstate. Advanced by Origen and Gregory of Nyssa.

Related arguments: the scriptural hinges — see also Does “eternal punishment” (Matt 25:46) mean age-long, not everlasting?

The claim

God will be all things in each person in such a way that everything which the reasoning mind can feel or understand or think will be all God… the mind will no longer be conscious of anything besides or other than God… In this way God will be all in all.

— Origen, On First Principles III.6.3 (c. 229), link.1 The argument reads 1 Corinthians 15:28 — “when all things shall be subdued unto him, then shall the Son also himself be subject unto him that put all things under him, that God may be all in all” — together with the “restitution of all things” (apokatastasis pantōn) of Acts 3:21 and the reconciliation of “all things” of Colossians 1:20, and concludes that a final state in which God is genuinely all in all cannot leave any rational creature permanently outside God. To take the phrase at full strength, on this reading, is to take it as universal restoration.

Who advances it

The verse is the fountainhead's fountainhead. Origen builds the whole argument of Peri Archōn I.6 and III.6 on it: the subjection of all things to Christ is a saving submission — the subjection “by which we also wish to be subject to Him” — so that when death, the last enemy, is destroyed, the end answers the beginning and God is all in all.2 A century and a half later Gregory of Nyssa makes the same verse the exegetical heart of his own statement, reading it as teaching “the complete annihilation of evil”: “He that becomes ‘all’ things will be ‘in all’ things too… The excepting of that one thing, evil, mars the comprehensiveness of the term ‘all.’”3 In the modern recovery it is again the keystone: Ilaria Ramelli treats 1 Corinthians 15:24–28 as the biblical spine of the entire doctrine, and David Bentley Hart makes “God…all in all” the hinge of his exegetical case for a salvation without remainder.4

The evidence for

The phrase is maximal, and Paul chose it. “All in all” (panta en pasin) is about as comprehensive a phrase as Greek affords, and it is the announced telos of the whole passage — the term to which the subjection of every power, and finally of death itself, is ordered. Gregory presses the grammatical point: if God is to be “in all” existing things, then evil, plainly, will not then be among them, for evil has no share in God; a single province of the rational creation left in permanent enmity is a thing God is not “in,” and so a limit on the “all.” The universalist reading is not a foreign import onto the verse but an attempt to honour the reach of its own language.

The subjection at issue is, in Paul, a saving one. Origen's reading turns on the sense of “subjected.” The subjection Christ renders to the Father in v. 28 is the very subjection the faithful render — “the subjection of Christ to the Father reveals the blessedness of our perfection”5 — not a crushing but a glad self-ordering to God. If the subjection that crowns the passage is the redemptive kind, then the “all things” subjected are, in the end, brought in rather than merely put down.

Its opponents concede the reading is available. The strongest circumstantial evidence that the reading is a real one is that the Fathers who reject universalism still record it as an ancient option on this verse. John Chrysostom, expounding 1 Corinthians 15:28, gives his own sense first (“that all things may be dependent upon Him… that nothing may exist independent of him”) but then adds: “some say that he spake this to declare the removal of wickedness, as though all would yield thenceforth and none would resist nor do iniquity. For when there is no sin, it is evident that ‘God shall be all in all.’”6 Theophylact repeats the same report almost verbatim.7 That a hostile witness preserves the universalist gloss as a known reading of the verse shows it was neither invented by Origen nor idiosyncratic to him.

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The verse is the natural home of the hope, and the hope takes its language seriously rather than evading it. “All in all” is genuinely maximal; the passage really does order the whole cosmic drama toward a single consummation in which God is unqualifiedly all; and the reading has an unbroken pedigree from Origen through Nyssa to the present, acknowledged even by those who reject it. If any single text carries the doctrine, it is this one, and it carries real weight.

What is overstated. The reading is permitted by the verse; it is not forced by it, and the claim that it is the only coherent reading — Hart's “without the least hesitation or qualification” register — goes well past what the text delivers.

  • The immediate context is the subjection of enemies, not the salvation of all. Verses 24–27 are the language of conquest: Christ reigns “till he hath put all enemies under his feet” (Psalm 110:1), the last enemy destroyed being death. To put an enemy under one's feet is, in its native idiom, to subjugate, not to convert; the passage can describe the pacification of all opposition — every hostile power finally rendered powerless — without asserting that every hostile person is saved. The universalist has to read the salvific sense of “subjection” back over the martial one the psalm supplies, and the verse does not settle which governs.
  • “God all in all” is, on the majority reading, sovereignty, not universal salvation. Chrysostom's own sense — that God be all in all means “that all things may be dependent upon Him, that none may suppose two authorities… that nothing may exist independent of him”8 — is a claim about God's undisputed final sovereignty, not the roster of the redeemed. Gregory of Nazianzus reads the same phrase of the saved's maturity into God (“we…are fully like God, with room for God and God alone”), not of a universal terminus.9 On these readings the verse is fully satisfied by a cosmos in which God's reign is total and unopposed, whether or not every creature shares the blessedness.
  • The inference smuggles in a distributive premise. “No creature left outside God” follows from “God all in all” only if “all in all” is read distributively over every rational person rather than as God's being the all-determining reality of the redeemed order. That is precisely the point in dispute, so citing the verse to prove it is close to assuming the conclusion. Paul elsewhere can speak of those “whose end is destruction” (Philippians 3:19) and of “everlasting destruction” (2 Thessalonians 1:9), which at least shows he did not take “all in all” to have foreclosed such language in his own mind.

Assessment

plausible. 1 Corinthians 15:28 is the strongest single text for universal restoration, and the reading is no eisegesis: it takes “all in all” at the full stretch of its language, rests on a real sense of “subjection” that is salvific for the willing, and was recognized as an available reading of the verse even by Fathers who opposed it. That is enough to make it coherent, seriously grounded, and not ruled out — the definition of plausible. It falls short of sound because the verse permits the reading without compelling it: the immediate context is the subjugation of Christ's enemies and the handover of the mediatorial kingdom, and “God all in all” is at least as naturally God's undisputed sovereignty (Chrysostom, Theophylact) or the theosis of the saved (Nazianzus) as the salvation of every creature. The universalist inference — that nothing left permanently outside God is possible — requires a distributive reading the text does not itself supply. So the verse genuinely grounds the hope and is its keystone, but it does not establish it; Origen's and Hart's stronger register, that it makes universalism the only possible reading, is an overreach flagged on Origen's and Hart's pages.
