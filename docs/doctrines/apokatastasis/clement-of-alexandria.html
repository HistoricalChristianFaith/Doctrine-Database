Clement of Alexandria on universal restoration (apokatastasis)

Dates: c. 200 · Wikipedia
Summary: Clement, head of the Alexandrian catechetical school before Origen, does not state in so many words that all will be saved. He holds that God's punishments are “saving and disciplinary, leading to conversion”, and that this discipline continues after death, the wayward being chastised “till they become ashamed and repent.” He reads the descent of Christ to Hades as a saving preaching to those who died before, and holds that God “saves all,” some by teaching and some by correction. Whether he belongs among the universalists is disputed: Ilaria Ramelli counts him among Origen's antecedents; Michael McClymond finds no unambiguous universalist before Origen.

Claims & evidence

God's punishments are corrective and saving, aimed at repentance, not death

… then all who believe shall be saved, although they may be of the Gentiles, on making their profession there; since God's punishments are saving and disciplinary, leading to conversion, and choosing rather the repentance than the death of a sinner; and especially since souls, although darkened by passions, when released from their bodies, are able to perceive more clearly, because of their being no longer obstructed by the paltry flesh.

— Stromata VI.6, source. The setting is the descent of Christ to Hades to preach the Gospel to the dead. For Clement, God chastises “choosing rather the repentance than the death of a sinner” (an echo of Ezekiel 18:23, 33:11): divine punishment aims at conversion. He adds that souls released from the body “are able to perceive more clearly,” so that the dead in Hades may believe on hearing the Gospel.

The wayward chastised by “paternal admonitions” until they repent

But if, like the deaf serpents, they listen not to the song called new, though very old, may they be chastised by God, and undergo paternal admonitions previous to the Judgment, till they become ashamed and repent, but not rush through headlong unbelief, and precipitate themselves into judgment.

— Stromata VII.16, source. Clement is writing of the teachers he has been answering; his wish for them is that they be corrected “previous to the Judgment.” The chastisement is paternal, and its stated aim is that they “become ashamed and repent.” He goes on to distinguish these “partial corrections, which are called chastisements,” from the final judgment.

Reception and debate

  • Clement's place in the history of the doctrine. Clement nowhere states in so many words that all will finally be saved. Ilaria Ramelli argues that Origen “had several antecedents” and reads a continuous doctrine from the New Testament onward; Michael McClymond answers that there are “no unambiguous cases of universalist teaching prior to Origen.” See How widely was universal restoration held among the Church Fathers?
  • Clement appears on this wiki also as a witness on the purgatory and intermediate-state timelines, where the same corrective-fire and Hades-preaching passages do related work.
  • Later writers argued for an endless punishment from Matthew 25:46 (“these shall go away into eternal punishment”); see Basil of Caesarea and Augustine.
