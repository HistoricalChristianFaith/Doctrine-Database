Gregory of Nazianzus on universal restoration (apokatastasis)

Dates: c. 381 · Wikipedia
Summary: Gregory of Nazianzus, “the Theologian,” neither teaches the restoration nor rejects it in the texts quoted here. Preaching on baptism he calls the unquenchable fire “eternal for the wicked” and then allows, without adopting it, that “some may prefer… to take a more merciful view” of that fire. Elsewhere he speaks of a “last Baptism… more painful and longer” that “consumes the stubble of every evil”; he does not draw that language out into a teaching of universal restoration. With Basil he compiled the Philocalia, the anthology that preserved much of Origen in the original Greek.

Claims & evidence

A “more merciful view” of the last fire

…and one even more fearful still than these, the unquenchable fire which is ranged with the worm that dieth not but is eternal for the wicked. For all these belong to the destroying power; though some may prefer even in this place to take a more merciful view of this fire, worthily of Him That chastises.

— Oration 40.36 (On Holy Baptism), source. Gregory has just described the unquenchable fire as “eternal for the wicked” and belonging to “the destroying power”; he then allows that a reader may “prefer… a more merciful view… worthily of Him That chastises” — that is, a view on which the fire is corrective, fitting a God who disciplines. He neither adopts that view nor forbids it.

Reception and debate

  • Two fire-texts. The second passage is in a different oration: in Oration 39.19 (On the Holy Lights, source) Gregory warns that the unrepentant “will be baptized with Fire, in that last Baptism which is more painful and longer, which devours wood like grass, and consumes the stubble of every evil.” In context that sentence is addressed as a warning to the unrepentant; the phrase “consumes the stubble of every evil” describes the fire as purging evil. The two passages come from different orations.
  • The Philocalia. Gregory and Basil, during their retreat in Pontus (late 350s), compiled the Philocalia of Origen, selections from Origen's works that preserve the Greek of parts of Peri Archōn (notably Book III, on free will) otherwise surviving only in Latin. (Lewis's 1911 introduction) On the restoration, Gregory leaves the “more merciful view” open in Oration 40, while Basil, in a rule whose authenticity Ilaria Ramelli disputes, rejects an end to punishment.
  • He was a friend and ally of the brothers Gregory of Nyssa, whose restoration-passages are disputed (see the disputed reading on Gregory of Nyssa), and Basil.
