Diodore of Tarsus on universal restoration (apokatastasis)

Dates: c. 330–390 · Wikipedia
Summary: Diodore, head of the great school of Antioch and teacher of both John Chrysostom and Theodore of Mopsuestia, was bishop of Tarsus from 378 and, in his own lifetime, a celebrated champion of Nicene orthodoxy against the Arians and Apollinarians. He is the earliest named witness for the restoration outside Origen's Alexandrian line — the Antiochene school, working from the plain grammatical sense of Scripture rather than Origen's allegory, reached the same terminating, medicinal hell. His works were nearly all destroyed a century after his death, when Cyril of Alexandria wrote against him and his Christology was read back as the root of Nestorianism; what survives of his eschatology is a fragment preserved in the Syriac tradition of the Church of the East, which kept his memory as a saint. In it he teaches plainly that the torment of the wicked is not everlasting but proportionate and brief, a purgation ending in an “immortal and unending happiness” prepared for all.

Claims & evidence

The torment of sinners is real but “not everlasting”

In a treatise on the divine economy — rendered by his Syriac transmitters as the “Book of the Dispensation” — Diodore sets a proportionate, terminating punishment against the endless happiness that is its goal. The wicked are chastised, he grants, and justly; but the chastisement is measured to the sin and then ends, lest the immortality God has prepared for them be made “worthless”:

A lasting reward, which is worthy of the justice of the Giver, is laid up for the good, in return for their labours; and torment for sinners, but not everlasting, that the immortality which is prepared for them may not be worthless. They must however be tormented for a short time, as they deserve, in proportion to the measure of their iniquity and wickedness, according to the amount of the wickedness of their deeds. This they will have to bear, that they suffer for a short time; but immortal and unending happiness is prepared for them.

— Diodore of Tarsus, from the “Book of the Dispensation,” preserved in Solomon of Basra's Book of the Bee.1 His ground is the disproportion of the two ages: because the reward is endless and the punishment is not, the mercy that ends the torment must far outweigh the justice that imposed it — “the torments for many and great sins must be very much less than the greatness of mercy.” The restoration is argued not from Origen's mysticism but from the sheer arithmetic of a God who is “not only just, but also merciful.”

Notes

  • How the fragment survives. Diodore's own books are almost wholly lost; this passage reaches us because the East-Syrian church, which never accepted the sentence later passed on him, treasured his eschatology and copied it. The line was gathered by Solomon of Basra into his thirteenth-century compilation the Book of the Bee, whose closing chapter weighs “whether mercy will be shewn to sinners and the devils in Gehenna,” and drew in turn on the earlier Syriac transmission catalogued by J. S. Assemani. Diodore stands there beside Isaac of Nineveh and his own pupil Theodore as one of the “Fathers… who encourage us and bid us rely upon Divine mercy.”2 It is a fragment at second hand, not a text from Diodore's own surviving pen; but it is quoted as his by a tradition that revered him, and its doctrine is the settled scholarly reading of his eschatology.
  • Scholarly attestation. Modern study places Diodore securely in the universalist line: Ilaria Ramelli counts him, with Titus of Bostra, among the fourth-century Antiochenes who held the restoration, and argues that Diodore inherited from Origen the conviction that the final apokatastasis is the triumph of God's providence — even while discarding Origen's allegory and pre-existence of souls. The doctrine was recognised in him by the later Syriac writers who cite him by name, among them John of Dara and Theodore bar Konai.3
  • Condemned with the Antiochenes. Diodore died in the Church's honour, but in the sixth-century campaign against Nestorianism his writings were attacked as its fountainhead. Cyril of Alexandria had already written Against Diodore and Theodore, and Photius records that the Fifth Ecumenical Council (553), in condemning the “Three Chapters,” excommunicated Origen “together with Diodorus of Tarsus, and Theodore of Mopsuestia.” The reception of that sentence, and how far it reached a hope rather than a heresy, is traced on the condemnation of Origen.4
