Didymus the Blind on universal restoration (apokatastasis)

Dates: c. 313–398 · Wikipedia
Summary: Didymus, blind from early childhood and for some fifty years the head of the catechetical school of Alexandria, was in his own day a celebrated teacher — Jerome studied under him and called him “the Seer.” He followed Origen; Jerome charged him with the final restoration of all and also with the pre-existence of souls. For a long time his restoration teaching was known only at second hand; the commentaries recovered in the 1941 Tura papyrus find preserve it in his own words, reading the “eternal” punishment of Scripture as a bounded, remedial correction and the end as a return of all rational creatures to God. After the Origenist controversy he was anathematized, with Origen and Evagrius, in the reception of the sixth-century condemnation and by name at the councils of 680–681 and 787.

Claims & evidence

The senses of aiōnios (“eternal”)

In his Commentary on Job, one of the works recovered at Tura, Didymus discusses the word rendered “eternal” in the punishment-sayings. Aiōnios, he argues, does not by itself mean “everlasting”: it means “beginningless and endless” only of God, and elsewhere carries a bounded sense, so that the “aiōnios” punishment of the Gospel need not be eternal at all:

It must be noted that αιώνιος has several meanings: in the expression, ‘αιώνιος God,’ it means ‘beginningless and endless’; for the divinity is called αιώνιος by virtue of having neither a beginning nor an end of its existence… Indeed, it is laid down concerning the Hebrew who did not wish to be freed in the seventh year, that ‘he will be your slave unto the αιών’: for no slave of a human being remains one forever, even after his death.

— Didymus the Blind, Commentary on Job 76, in Ilaria Ramelli's translation.1 He draws the corollary directly: punishment and death are only “aiōnioi,” belonging to the ages, whereas salvation is “hyper-aiōnios” — it outlasts all the ages; and the fire of judgement, he says elsewhere, will consume not the creatures but the evil in them.

The restoration of rational creatures, the wicked included

Didymus speaks of the end as a return. Commenting on the Father's giving all things into the Son's hand (John 3:35), Didymus states that the rational beings who have become wicked will finally be restored, once they have put off their evil and put on virtue:

This is said about rational creatures [τῶν λογικῶν]. Since, among all of them, there are also some who have become wicked, know how these will have a restoration [κατάστασιν] once they have arrived in the hands of the Son, obviously after rejecting the evilness [κακίαν] that they had, and assuming virtue [ἀρετήν].

— Didymus the Blind, Commentary on John, fragment 2, in Ramelli's translation.2 The katástasis, the restoration, here is of those “who have become wicked.” (The John material is a catena fragment, not a Tura text; Ramelli reads it together with the Tura Job, Psalms and Zechariah commentaries.)

Jerome's report that Didymus followed Origen on the restoration

Jerome, who had studied under Didymus and praised him warmly, turned against him during the Origenist controversy and listed him among those who had swallowed Origen's errors whole. He praises Didymus on the Trinity and faults him where he trusted Origen — “we extol… the purity of his faith in the Trinity, while on the other point in which he erred in trusting to Origen we withdraw from him” — and names among those errors the final restoration (apokatastasis) and the pre-existence of souls.3 Jerome writes as Didymus's former pupil and, by then, as an opponent of Origen; Ramelli sets his report beside the Tura commentaries.

“In the liberation of all, no one remains a captive” — a commentary of disputed authorship

Didymus is quoted, on his teaching of Christ's descent to Hades, as having written that “in the liberation of all no one remains a captive” — that at the Lord's passion the devil alone was injured, losing all the captives he held. The line belongs to a work that travels under Didymus's name — the Brief Commentary on the Catholic Epistles (In epistolas canonicas brevis enarratio), in its notes on 1 Peter — but that work survives only in the Latin translation Epiphanius Scholasticus made for Cassiodorus, and its Didymian authorship is disputed: it is cited as Pseudo-Didymus, since the same commentary calls 2 Peter a forgery (falsata), whereas the Didymus of the Tura texts quotes that epistle as canonical. It is marked here (⚠) as belonging to a text of disputed authorship.4 The remedial reading of punishment and the restoration of the wicked are attested above from the Tura Job and the Commentary on John fragment.

Reception and debate

  • Modern scholarship. Ilaria Ramelli writes that “like Origen and then Gregory Nyssen… Didymus also included the fallen angels and Satan in the eventual restoration”; Michael McClymond, who disputes Ramelli's “mainstream” thesis, also counts Didymus among “perhaps four authors” who “appear to be universalists in the Origenian sense.”5 With the Tura commentaries on Job and the Psalms, his teaching survives in his own words, as Gregory of Nyssa's does.
  • Condemnation with Origen and Evagrius. Didymus's name travels with Origen's and Evagrius's in the anti-Origenist reception. The extant acts of the Second Council of Constantinople (553) do not name him; the report that the assembled fathers anathematized him with Origen and Evagrius is Cyril of Scythopolis's, and his name is then fixed by the definitions of the Third Council of Constantinople (680–681) and Nicaea II (787). The condemnation of the three together is traced on the condemnation of Origen, and he figures in the disputed reading on who else held the condemned views and what became of them. He is commemorated as “St. Didymus the Blind” on 18 October in the Prologue of Ohrid.
