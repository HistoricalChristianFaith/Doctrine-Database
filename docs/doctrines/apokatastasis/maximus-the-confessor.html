Maximus the Confessor on universal restoration (apokatastasis)

Dates: c. 630 · Wikipedia
Summary: Maximus, the seventh-century Greek monk and theologian, writes on the restoration after the condemnations of 543 and 553. He distinguishes three senses of apokatastasis, endorsing the restoration of the soul's powers (the sense “Gregory of Nyssa especially loves”), and declines to commit a fuller explanation to writing — “a better and more secret explanation… we… will honour by silence.” Modern scholars read that reserve in three ways: as compatible with unending punishment, as a concealed universalism, or as a reverent hope (see below).

Claims & evidence

Three senses of restoration (Quaestiones et dubia)

The third meaning [of apokatastasis] is used by Gregory especially in reference to the qualities of the soul that had been corrupted by sin and then are restored to their original state.

— Quaestiones et dubia (PG 90:796A–C), as rendered by Hans Urs von Balthasar, source. Maximus lists three things the church means by “restoration”: the return of each person to virtue in this life; the resurrection restoration of human nature to incorruption; and the restoration of the fallen powers of the soul to their created condition — the sense he assigns to Gregory of Nyssa. In the same passage he distinguishes the souls' regaining the knowledge of the good from participation in it; how that distinction bears on universal salvation is among the readings set out on “How is Maximus the Confessor's reserve on universal restoration to be read?” (The passage is numbered I.13 in Declerck's edition, 19 in the older Deconinck numbering; it is one text, not two.)

“A better and more secret explanation” (Quaestiones ad Thalassium)

…a better and more secret explanation, which is kept in the minds of the mystics, but we, as well, will honour by silence.

— Quaestiones ad Thalassium (on the restoration), tr. Andreas Andreopoulos, source. This passage is the source of the phrase “honourable silence” used in the modern debate. Elsewhere in the same work (Ad Thalassium 43) Maximus says that “the deeper secrets of the divine doctrines must not be committed to writing.” He refers to a fuller explanation and declines to set it down.

Reception and debate

  • Readings of the reserve. The modern readings are set out, with their sources, on “How is Maximus the Confessor's reserve on universal restoration to be read?”, where they are filed under three headings: that Maximus did not hold universal salvation, the one presence of God being received as glory or as torment (Georges Florovsky; Brian Daley, “Apokatastasis and ‘Honorable Silence’…,” 1982, and The Hope of the Early Church, 1991; Jean-Claude Larchet; David Bradshaw and Ignatius Green, as Mark Chenoweth reports them); that he held it and kept it in silence for the mature (Hans Urs von Balthasar, Cosmic Liturgy, as Chenoweth reports him; Ilaria Ramelli, The Christian Doctrine of Apokatastasis, 2013; David Bentley Hart; Chenoweth); and that he allowed a hope without teaching it as certain (Andrew Louth; Andreas Andreopoulos).
  • Translations. The three-senses passage is quoted in Balthasar's rendering (a verbatim English translation of PG 90:796); the wording of the “honourable silence” and “not… committed to writing” lines follows Andreopoulos and Constas respectively. The Quaestiones et dubia and the Quaestiones ad Thalassium are distinct works and are kept distinct here.
  • Maximus wrote in the same century as Isaac of Nineveh. Ramelli's history of the doctrine includes Maximus and ends with Eriugena. His refutation of the Origenist fall of minds in Ambiguum 7 is told on the timeline of Origen's condemnation: there he rejects the pre-existence of souls and describes an “ever-moving rest” in which the end surpasses the beginning.
