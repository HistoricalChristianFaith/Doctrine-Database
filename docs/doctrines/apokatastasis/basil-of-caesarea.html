Basil of Caesarea on universal restoration (apokatastasis)

Dates: c. 375 · Wikipedia
Summary: Basil of Caesarea, bishop and monastic legislator, brother of Gregory of Nyssa, is cited for an endless punishment. In a monastic rule ascribed to him, answering a question about those “who say that the punishment will be without end,” he presses the parallel grammar of Matthew 25:46: if “eternal punishment” has an end, so must “eternal life,” and he calls the notion of an end to punishment “a demonic deceit.” Ilaria Ramelli argues that this rule is a later interpolation; his Letter 46 also speaks of “no release after death.” With Gregory of Nazianzus, Basil compiled the Philocalia, an anthology of Origen.

Claims & evidence

“If the punishment should once end, the heavenly life would necessarily have to end too”

…it is a demonic deceit that many people forget so frequent and clear words and statements of our Lord and invent a kind of an end of the punishments so that they may have more courage to sin. For if the punishment should once end, the heavenly life would necessarily have to end too. But if we cannot bear the mere thought of it when speaking about the life in heaven, how then can we speak about the end of the eternal suffering, if as the former so the latter is preceded by the word ‘eternal’ — ‘and these will go away,’ says the Lord, ‘into eternal punishment, but the righteous into eternal life.’

— Regulae brevius tractatae (Shorter Rules), Q. 267, source (Greek: PG 31.1264–65). The argument rests on Matthew 25:46: the two clauses share the one adjective (Greek aiōnios), so on Basil's reasoning a temporary punishment would entail a temporary life. Basil adds that the varying “severe” and “light” beatings of Luke 12:47–48 mark a difference among torments, “not an end” of them.

Reception and debate

  • The authenticity of Reg. brev. 267. Ilaria Ramelli argues that Reg. brev. 267 is most likely a later interpolation into Basil's Asceticon, whose short rules she calls “a highly interpolated work structured in questions and answers”, noting that its charge (that those who deny eternal punishment are under “a demonic deceit”) would implicitly condemn Origen, whose writings Basil and Gregory of Nazianzus anthologized in the Philocalia, and Basil's own siblings Macrina and Gregory of Nyssa, all of whom she reads as holding the restoration. (Ramelli, “St Basil and Apokatastasis”) Michael McClymond lists Ramelli's interpolation claim about Basil among the moves he disputes in her work (see How widely was universal restoration held among the Church Fathers?).
  • Letter 46. In Letter 46 (“To a Fallen Virgin”), §5 — a pastoral letter — he sets before her “those endless and unbearable pangs… and anguish without end,” adding that “from all these woes there is no release after death.” (Letter 46, NPNF 2.8) This letter is cited for Basil on the eternal-conscious-torment timeline.
  • The Philocalia. With Gregory of Nazianzus, Basil compiled the Philocalia of Origen, the anthology preserving the Greek of parts of Peri Archōn. (Lewis's 1911 introduction)
  • Augustine also argues from the Matthew 25:46 parallelism, in City of God XXI. Ilaria Ramelli, on the restoration side, reads aiōnios as “of the age to come” rather than strictly endless.
