John Scotus Eriugena on universal restoration (apokatastasis)

Dates: c. 800–877 · Wikipedia
Summary: Eriugena, the Irish philosopher of Charles the Bald's court, is the figure with whom Ilaria Ramelli ends her history of the doctrine (2013). His Periphyseon builds a whole cosmology on the movement of all things out from God (exitus) and their return to God (reditus): the entire created order at last flows back to its source, God becomes “all in all,” and nothing of created nature is annihilated — only evil, which has no substance, is excluded. Linked with the pantheism of Amalric of Bène, the Periphyseon was condemned at the Council of Paris (1210), and in 1225 Pope Honorius III ordered copies burned.1

Claims & evidence

The return (reditus) of all things (Periphyseon V)

There is a general return of all things to God. Corporeal things will return to their incorporeal causes, the temporal to the eternal, the finite will be absorbed in the infinite.

— summarizing Periphyseon Book V, Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, source. For Eriugena all of nature is a single dynamic process: it proceeds from God through the primordial causes into the world of effects, and it returns by the same steps to God. Beyond this general return, the blessed attain a special return — deification (theosis), in which, in the words of Moran and Guiu (SEP), the elect “will merge with God completely, as lights blend into the one light, as voices blend in the choir, as a droplet of water merges with the stream.” In this account “God shall be all in all (omnia in omnibus, Periphyseon, V 935c).”2

Nature, evil and punishment in the return (Periphyseon V 944B–C)

Therefore what is punished is the irrational impulse of the perverse will which arises in rational nature, but that nature remains everywhere what it was and in all things that partakes of it it is good, unmenaced, undamaged, unharmed, uncontaminated, incorruptible, impassible, immutable, for it eternally participates in the Supreme Good ; everywhere it is blessed, most glorious in the Saints, in whom it is deified, most excellent in sinners, to whose sins it sets the limit, so that their proper substance shall never be reduced to not being and so that they shall never suffer the destruction of those natural goods which were bestowed upon them at their creation

— Periphyseon V 944B–C (PL 122), Latin and English selections, source. The return does not destroy what returns: punishment falls on the perverse will, not on any nature, and the substance of sinners is not "reduced to not being."3 Moran and Guiu (SEP) report that for Eriugena hell is a state rather than a place: those who cling to temporal things "remain trapped in their own phantasies, and it is to this mental state that the scriptural term ‘hell’ applies," and "the real hell is deprivation of the vision of God."2

Reception and debate

  • In Ramelli's history. Ramelli closes The Christian Doctrine of Apokatastasis: A Critical Assessment from the New Testament to Eriugena (Brill, 2013) with him, placing him at the end of the line Origen → the Cappadocians → Evagrius → Pseudo-Dionysius → Maximus → Eriugena.4
  • Condemnations. Eriugena's earlier On Divine Predestination was condemned by the councils of Valence (855) and Langres (859).5 The Periphyseon was linked with the pantheism of Amalric of Bène and condemned at the Council of Paris (1210); by a letter of 23 January 1225 Pope Honorius III ordered copies sought out and sent to Rome “solempniter comburendum” (to be solemnly burned), or else burned publicly by the bishops themselves; and it was later placed on the Index of Prohibited Books, where it remained until the Index was abolished.1 Moran and Guiu (SEP) give the thirteenth-century ground of condemnation as “promoting the identity of God and creation.”5
