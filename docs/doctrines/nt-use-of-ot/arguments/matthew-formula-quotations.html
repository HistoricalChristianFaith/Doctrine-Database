Matthew’s formula quotations: do they evidence direct use of a Hebrew (non-Septuagintal) text?

Disputed reading · on Which text did the New Testament quote? · witness: Jerome

Related readings: Zechariah 12:10 at John 19:37 · Isaiah 28:11–12 at 1 Corinthians 14:21

The passage

“And was there until the death of Herod: that it might be fulfilled which was spoken of the Lord by the prophet, saying, Out of Egypt have I called my son.”

— Matthew 2:15 (KJV), quoting Hosea 11:1.1

The question

The Hebrew of Hosea 11:1 reads מִמִּצְרַיִם קָרָאתִי לִבְנִי (mimmiṣrayim qārāʼtî li-bnî), “out of Egypt I called my son,” singular; the Septuagint reads ἐξ Αἰγύπτου μετεκάλεσα τὰ τέκνα αὐτοῦ, “out of Egypt I called his children”; Matthew 2:15 reads ἐξ Αἰγύπτου ἐκάλεσα τὸν υἱόν μου, “my son,” with the Hebrew.1 Krister Stendahl (1954; 2nd ed. 1968) distinguished Matthew’s formula quotations, the fulfilment citations the evangelist introduces himself, which have a mixed, non-Septuagintal text-form, from his other quotations, shared with Mark and set in dialogue and discourse, which are largely Septuagintal; Archer and Chirichigno list Matthew 2:15 among quotations that follow the Hebrew against the Greek.2 The three verses most often cited together differ in kind: Matthew 2:15 follows the Hebrew; Matthew 2:18 (Jeremiah 31:15) matches neither the Septuagint nor the Hebrew verbatim; and Matthew 2:23, “he shall be called a Nazarene,” matches no single Old Testament sentence and is introduced as spoken by “the prophets,” plural.3 The dispute is over how the fulfilment quotations came to stand with the Hebrew: by the evangelist’s own use of a Hebrew text, by a Greek text already revised toward the Hebrew, or by a collection of proof-texts he received.

The evangelist following the Hebrew

On this reading Matthew read his proof-texts in Hebrew and set them into Greek himself.

Held by: Jerome (Latin, Lives of Illustrious Men 3, c. 393; Commentaries on Matthew, Hosea and Jeremiah).4,3

“In this it is to be noted that wherever the Evangelist, whether on his own account or in the person of our Lord the Saviour, quotes the testimony of the Old Testament, he does not follow the authority of the translators of the Septuagint but the Hebrew. Wherefore these two forms exist, ‘Out of Egypt have I called my son,’ and ‘for he shall be called a Nazarene.’”

Defending his translation “from the Hebrew,” Jerome pressed the Hosea citation: “Let those who deny the authenticity of the Hebrew copies show us this passage in the Septuagint, and when they have failed to find it, we will show it them in the Hebrew.” In his commentaries he concludes that “there is no doubt that Matthew took this testimony according to the Hebrew truth,” and answers the emperor Julian, who had mocked the citation. On Matthew 2:18 he holds that “the Evangelists and Apostles did not follow the interpretation of any [translator], but rather expressed in their own words, as if Hebrews, what they read in Hebrew.” On Matthew 2:23 he reads “the prophets” as a sign that the evangelist “did not take words from any one passage of Scripture, but the sense,” and offers the Hebrew נֵצֶר (nēṣer, “branch”) of Isaiah 11:1 as the anchor, a sound-play the Septuagint’s ἄνθος (“flower”) does not carry. On this reading the Hebrew singular, the free Rachel-lament and the Hebrew word-play are the marks of an evangelist working from the Hebrew himself.3

A Septuagint revised toward the Hebrew

On this reading Matthew quoted a continuous Greek text that had already been corrected toward the Hebrew and polished in its Greek.

Held by: M. J. J. Menken (Matthew’s Bible, 2004; on Hosea 11:1, 2005).5

Menken judges that the textual form of the fulfilment quotations “is best explained as a Septuagint text that was revised to make it better agree with the Hebrew and to improve the quality of its Greek; the evangelist took these quotations from a continuous text.” At Hosea 11:1 the later Jewish revisers Aquila, Symmachus and Theodotion also read the singular “my son,” so Matthew’s wording there is the wording of a Hebraizing Greek tradition as well as of the Hebrew. Such revision of the Greek Bible toward the Hebrew is attested before and around the first century in the kaige or proto-Theodotionic recension, which is also adduced for Paul’s quotation of Isaiah 25:8. On this reading the formula quotations, with their Hebrew-aligned wording and good Greek, are Matthew’s faithful use of a revised Greek Bible.5

A collection of Christian testimonia

On this reading the fulfilment quotations reached Matthew already shaped, from a collection of proof-texts in Christian use.

Held by: ⚠ unverified — no named proponent located yet.

The fulfilment quotations form a distinct group within the gospel, with a text-form of their own and a shared introductory formula. On this reading that is the profile of a source: proof-texts gathered and worded before Matthew wrote, which he set into his narrative. The mechanism is set out as a reading on Were the composite citations drawn from a pre-formed testimonia collection?6
