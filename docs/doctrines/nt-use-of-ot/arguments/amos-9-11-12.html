Amos 9:11–12 at Acts 15:16–18: a Septuagintal “seek the Lord,” and does the Council’s decision depend on it?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: sound — the catalog’s LXX-distinctive tag on this row holds. At the Jerusalem Council Acts 15:16–18 quotes Amos 9:11–12 in the Septuagint’s form — “that the residue of men might seek after the Lord” (ὅπως ἀν ἐκζητήσωσιν οἱ κατάλοιποι τῶν ἀνθρώπων τὸν κύριον) — where the Hebrew reads “that they may possess the remnant of Edom”; so the citation cannot have come from the Hebrew as we have it, and the row is genuinely LXX-distinctive. Two refinements keep the tag honest. Like Isaiah 7:14 and Psalm 40:6 this is a Greek reading of a stable Hebrew — the consonants of “possess Edom” stand in the Masoretic Text and in the Wadi Murabba’at Minor Prophets scroll, and no Hebrew witness attests the Greek reading — so the Septuagint here diverges by reading the same consonants differently (ʾədōm “Edom” as ʾādām “mankind,” and a dālet/rêš confusion turning “possess” into “seek”), not by preserving an older Hebrew the Masoretes lost (unlike Hebrews 1:6 or Goliath’s height); and the Council’s decision does not strictly hang on the Greek-only wording, because the load-bearing clause — “all the nations upon whom my name is called” — stands in both text-forms, so the Greek sharpens James’s argument (Gentiles as active seekers, not conquered vassals) rather than being the foundation it would collapse without.

This page adds the piece those do not carry: the text-form adjudication of the catalog row itself, and the question of how load-bearing the Greek is to the Council’s ruling.

The claim

“… that they may possess the remnant of Edom and all the nations who are called by my name… LXX: … that they may seek me, the remnant of humanity, and all the nations over whom my name has been called… In the book of Acts … James … spoke these words … ‘so that the rest of mankind may seek the Lord, even all the Gentiles who are called by my name.’ … Where the authority of the apostles prevails … all suspicion of various explanations is to be removed.”

— Jerome, Commentary on Amos 9:11–12 (c. 406), historicalchristian.faith.1 Jerome — the champion of the Hebraica veritas, no friend of preferring the Greek to the Hebrew — sets the two text-forms side by side and concedes the fact this argument turns on: the Hebrew of Amos 9:12 reads “that they may possess the remnant of Edom,” while the Septuagint that Acts 15:17 follows reads “that the remnant of mankind may seek the Lord.” The interpretation this argument serves is the catalog’s classification: that the Council’s proof-text is LXX-distinctive — drawn from the Greek text-form at the very point it diverges from the Hebrew.

Who advances it

That James’s citation follows the Greek and not the Hebrew is not a modern discovery: the ancient expositors quoted the verse in its Septuagintal form as a matter of course and built the Gentile mission on it. Irenaeus gives the whole speech — “that the residue of men may seek after the Lord, and all the Gentiles, among whom my name has been invoked” — as the prophets’ agreement with the calling of the nations (Against Heresies III.12.14–15); John Chrysostom takes “that the residue of men may seek the Lord” as the whole point of the rebuilt tabernacle, “the cause … the calling of the Gentiles”; and Bede glosses it “so that not only Jews but also all nations of the Gentiles may seek his name.” Jerome (above) alone among them names both readings, because he alone was reading the Hebrew. In the modern text-critical surveys the same fact is a flat classification: Archer & Chirichigno place Acts 15:16–18 among the quotations following the Septuagint where it deviates from the Hebrew, and the standard quotation tables (Kalvesmaki; the loci citati of the Nestle–Aland / UBS Greek New Testament) list it the same way. The stronger form of the claim — that James’s Gentile-inclusion argument is “rooted entirely in the LXX and impossible in the Hebrew,” which Luke “has James exploit” — is pressed by Luke Timothy Johnson and many commentators; it is that stronger form the assessment below tests.2

The evidence for

Acts reproduces the Greek reading, not a rendering of the Hebrew. The Hebrew of Amos 9:12 makes a restored Israel the subject of a verb of conquest: “that they may possess (יִירְשׁוּ, yîrəšû) the remnant of Edom (אֶדוֹם) and all the nations.” Acts 15:17 instead makes “the residue of men” (οἱ κατάλοιποι τῶν ἀνθρώπων) the subject of a verb of seeking — “that the residue of men might seek after the Lord” (ἐκζητήσωσιν τὸν κύριον), which is the Septuagint’s rendering of the line, not a fresh translation of the Hebrew. The two readings say almost opposite things: on the Hebrew the nations are the object of a Davidic re-conquest; on the Greek they are the subject who turn to God. No handling of “possess Edom” yields “mankind seeks the Lord”; the quotation is the Greek text.1

The divergence is where James’s point becomes vivid. James adduces the prophet to confirm Peter’s report that God has “visit[ed] the Gentiles, to take out of them a people for his name” (Acts 15:14). The Greek Amos supplies exactly that: a prophecy that after the tabernacle of David is rebuilt the nations themselves seek the Lord. On the Hebrew’s “possess the remnant of Edom” the picture is a restored kingdom annexing its old vassals — a far less natural warrant for admitting uncircumcised Gentiles as Gentiles. So the Greek form is not idle packaging: it is the wording on which the argument reads most directly, which is why the ancient expositors and modern commentators alike treat the citation as Septuagint-driven.2

The Hebrew side of the line reads “Edom” wherever it is attested. The Masoretic Text reads “possess … Edom,” and so does the Hebrew Minor Prophets scroll from Wadi Murabba’at (Mur 88, c. AD 100), a text famously close to the later Masoretic recension — which preserves Amos 9:12 with ʾədōm. The later Greek tradition splits exactly on this seam: the Old Greek and Acts on the “mankind … seek” side, the Hebrew and its witnesses on the “Edom … possess” side. That split is the signature of an LXX-distinctive reading.3

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The catalog’s classification is correct. The Council’s proof-text is the Septuagint’s Greek, reproduced at the point it diverges from the Hebrew: “the residue of men might seek after the Lord” against “they may possess the remnant of Edom.” No rendering of the Hebrew supplies it, and the Greek form is where James’s Gentile argument reads most naturally. This is a genuine, live instance of the New Testament following the Greek against the plain Hebrew — not an inert agreement, and not a free paraphrase. The row belongs where the seeded catalog put it.

What must be refined, so the tag is not misread.

  1. This is a Greek reading of a stable Hebrew, not an older Hebrew the Greek preserved. The “LXX-distinctive” bucket hides a fork that matters for the wider “which text?” question, and this row sits on the same side of it as Isaiah 7:14 and Psalm 40:6, not the same side as Hebrews 1:6. In the older-Hebrew cases the Greek diverges from the Masoretic Text because it rests on a genuinely different Hebrew now recovered at Qumran (Hebrew-over-Hebrew). Amos 9:12 is not that: no Hebrew witness attests the Greek reading. The Wadi Murabba’at scroll preserves the verse with “Edom,” the fragmentary Qumran Minor Prophets copy (4QXIIg) is Masoretic in character, and the Naḥal Ḥever Greek Minor Prophets scroll — often name-dropped here — does not contain Amos at all. The divergence lives entirely on the Greek side: it arises by reading the same consonants differently. ʾədōm (“Edom,” often spelled defectively אדם) is read as the generic ʾādām (“mankind”); “they shall possess” (yîrəšû, root yāraš) is read as “they shall seek” (yidrəšû, root dāraš) — the classic dālet/rêš (ד/ר) graphic confusion; and the Hebrew object-marker ʾēt before “Edom” is re-analyzed so the verse gains a new subject and object. So the honest sub-tag is LXX-reading-of-a-stable-Hebrew, not older-Hebrew; whoever files this row with Hebrews 1:6 has conflated two different phenomena.3

  2. Whether that reading is a slip or a theology is debated — but both accounts are Greek-side. One camp (A. Gelston and the older consensus) treats the change as a mechanical accident: revocalization plus the dālet/rêš confusion. Another (Jobes & Silva, Jennifer Dines) holds it is more than a slip — a deliberate universalizing by the Greek translator of the Twelve, of a piece with his broader tendencies, turning “Israel possessing Edom” into “mankind seeking the Lord.” The most defensible middle position: the graphic/vocalic ambiguity is the raw material, but the selection among the options — and the supplying of an object for “seek” — looks interpretive, ambiguity resolved in a theologizing direction. (A minor textual note that cuts the same way: the Old Greek as printed has no object for “seek”; some manuscripts add “me,” and it is Acts that supplies “the Lord” — so even the object James quotes is a filling-out of the Greek, not a survival of the Hebrew.) Either way the phenomenon is a fact about the Greek tradition, not a window onto a different Hebrew.4

  3. The Council’s decision does not strictly depend on the Greek-only wording. Here the row is closer to Psalm 40:6 than to Isaiah 7:14: the distinctive Greek word is exploited, not the sole support. Richard Bauckham has shown that the clause actually carrying James’s argument is Amos 9:12b — “all the nations upon whom my name is called” — and that clause stands in both the Hebrew and the Greek. James joins it by a rabbinic gezerah shavah (verbal analogy) to Peter’s report that God took “a people for his name” (Acts 15:14): the shared catchword is “name.” And the Hebrew idiom “to have God’s name called over” someone denotes belonging to him — used of Israel, the Temple, and Jerusalem — so even the Masoretic v. 12b already says the nations come to belong to the Lord. On this reading the historical James could have argued essentially this from a Hebrew text, and the Greek “seek” is a translation-level sharpening, not the argument’s foundation. The honest seam: the Masoretic v. 12a still wraps the nations in conquest imagery (Edom “possessed”), so the Septuagint’s re-framing does add real rhetorical force — nations as worshippers rather than as annexed vassals. So the lead framing that “the Gentile-inclusion argument depends on the Greek reading” overstates it: the citation is LXX-distinctive and the Greek makes the point vivid, yet the load-bearing clause is shared, and the strong claim that the argument is “impossible in the Hebrew” does not survive.5

  4. “A Hebrew-speaking James quoting Greek in Jerusalem” is a real puzzle, but not a defeater of the row. Three handles, which most commentators combine. (a) Luke composed the speech in Greek for his Greek-reading audience, as ancient historians regularly did, and naturally used his Bible, the Septuagint. (b) Greek scriptures circulated in first-century Palestine, and Jerusalem had a substantial Greek-speaking community (the “Hellenists” of Acts 6:1), so a Greek citation there is not anachronistic on its face. (c) Because the load-bearing clause (v. 12b) survives in the Hebrew (refinement 3), the exegetical substance could be the historical James’s while the Septuagintal wording is Luke’s — the text-form question and the historicity question are therefore separable. A small datum cuts toward a Semitic underlay: the citation opens “I will return and rebuild” (Acts 15:16), the Hebraism “return and [verb]” = “do again,” and the quotation is a composite (Amos framed by echoes of Hosea, Jeremiah, Isaiah, and Zechariah), not a flat reproduction of the Greek — consistent with a real exegetical tradition rendered into the Greek Luke and his readers knew. None of this unsettles the flat text-form finding: as Acts gives it, the citation reproduces the Septuagint’s reading and depends on it at the point of quotation.6

Assessment

Sound. The interpretation this argument serves — that the Jerusalem Council’s proof-text is an LXX-distinctive quotation, following the Septuagint’s “the residue of men might seek after the Lord” where the Greek diverges from the Hebrew “they may possess the remnant of Edom” — holds. Acts reproduces the Greek reading; no rendering of the Hebrew supplies it; and the Hebrew side of the line reads “Edom” wherever it is attested. The row is correctly tagged. What the tag must be trusted to mean, and no more, is fixed by three refinements: (a) this is the Septuagint reading a stable Hebrew — “possess Edom” stands in the Masoretic Text and the Wadi Murabba’at scroll, and no Hebrew witness carries the Greek reading — so unlike Hebrews 1:6 or Goliath’s height there is no older Hebrew here for the Greek to have preserved; the divergence arises Greek-side, by revocalizing ʾədōm as ʾādām and a dālet/rêš confusion turning “possess” into “seek”; (b) whether that was a mechanical slip or a theologizing rendering is debated, but on both accounts the phenomenon is Greek-internal; and (c) the Council’s decision does not strictly hang on the Greek-only wording — the clause that carries James’s argument, “all the nations upon whom my name is called” (v. 12b), stands in both text-forms and links by verbal analogy to “a people for his name” (v. 14), so the Greek sharpens the argument (nations as seekers rather than conquered vassals) rather than being the foundation it would collapse without. Kept to that, the classification is a clean instance of the New Testament following the Greek against a literal Hebrew; stretched into “the Greek preserves an older Hebrew here” or “the Council’s ruling is impossible on the Hebrew,” it overreaches. This row joins Isaiah 7:14 and Psalm 40:6 as the worked instances of the same species — a distinctively Septuagintal reading of a stable Hebrew, pressed by the Church — as against the older-Hebrew cases the Dead Sea Scrolls recovered.
