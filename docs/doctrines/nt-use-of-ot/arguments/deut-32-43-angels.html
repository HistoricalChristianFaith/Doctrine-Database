Does Hebrews 1:6 (“let all the angels of God worship him”) quote Deuteronomy 32:43 or Psalm 97:7?

Disputed reading · on Which text did the New Testament quote? · witness: Justin Martyr

Related readings: Hebrews and the Greek Old Testament

The passage

“And let all the angels of God worship him.”

— Hebrews 1:6 (KJV).1 Justin Martyr (c. 155) quotes Deuteronomy 32:43 in its Greek form:

“Rejoice, O you heavens, with Him, and let all the angels of God worship Him” and “Rejoice, O you nations, with His people, and let all the angels of God be strengthened in Him.” … by these words He declares that we, the nations, rejoice with His people — to wit, Abraham, and Isaac, and Jacob, and the prophets, and, in short, all of that people who are well-pleasing to God.”

— Justin Martyr, Dialogue with Trypho 130 (c. 155), newadvent.org.1

The question

Two Old Testament verses summon heavenly beings to worship. The Masoretic Hebrew of Deuteronomy 32:43 has no such summons; the Septuagint has “let all the sons of God worship him” and “let all the angels of God be strengthened in him”; and the Qumran scroll 4QDeutq (c. 100 BC) reads “bow down to him, all gods.” Psalm 97:7 (96:7 in the Greek) reads in Hebrew “bow down to him, all gods” and in the Septuagint “worship him all his angels.” The dispute is which verse Hebrews quotes; on the answer turns whether Hebrews here follows a Deuteronomy line that the Masoretic Text lacks, or a Psalm whose Hebrew and Greek both carry the summons.2

A quotation of Deuteronomy 32:43, preserving a line older than the Masoretic Text

On this reading Hebrews quotes the Song of Moses in a Greek form that keeps a worship-summons the later Masoretic Hebrew no longer has.

Held by: Harold W. Attridge (Hebrews, Hermeneia, 1989); Paul Ellingworth (NIGTC, 1993); Gareth Lee Cockerill (Bulletin for Biblical Research, 1999); Gert J. Steyn (2011).3

Its holders rest the identification on Hebrews’ “angels of God” (ἄγγελοι θεοῦ). The Psalm reads “his angels” (οἱ ἄγγελοι αὐτοῦ); the Deuteronomy line has “of God.” In the main Septuagint text of Deuteronomy “worship” and “angels of God” sit in separate couplets, but in the form of the Song carried by the Odes appended to the Greek Psalter (Ode 2) they stand together in one colon, in Hebrews’ exact words. Cockerill concludes that the author quotes a Greek rendering of a 4QDeutq-type Hebrew, represented by Ode 2:43, and that the extant Septuagint of Deuteronomy 32:43 conflates two Greek translations.3

The Hebrew scroll gives the line its age. Patrick Skehan, who first published 4QDeutq in 1954, and Eugene Ulrich, who edited it for Discoveries in the Judaean Desert, report the worship-summons in a Hebrew copy about a thousand years older than the Masoretic codices. Emanuel Tov treats Deuteronomy 32:43 as a textbook case of Masoretic abbreviation, the versions preserving the longer song; Arie van der Kooij and Julie Duncan argue to the same effect. On their reconstruction the summons to “all the gods” was dropped from the proto-Masoretic line, as the “sons of God” of Deuteronomy 32:8, still read at Qumran and in the Greek, became “sons of Israel.” Hebrews, on this reading, quotes a Deuteronomy that still had the line, in the Greek form the church read; the Septuagint’s “angels” for the Hebrew ʾĕlōhîm matches its rendering of the “sons of God” as “angels” at Job 1:6.4

A quotation of Psalm 97:7 (96:7 in the Greek)

On this reading Hebrews quotes the enthronement psalm “The Lord reigneth,” whose call on the angels to worship is read of Christ’s coming into the world.

Held by: Thomas Aquinas (Catholic, Commentary on Hebrews, d. 1274); John Owen (Reformed) ⚠ unverified.5

Aquinas finds the verse in the Psalter: “he adduces the authority of a psalm when he says, And let all God’s angels worship him. This is from Ps. 96: ‘The Lord has reigned, let the earth rejoice.’ For worship is paid only to the Lord; therefore, if the angels worship Him, He is their Lord.” He reads Hebrews’ introductory words, “when he bringeth in the firstbegotten into the world,” as the Apostle touching “on the Psalmist’s intention”: “Thus the Psalmist is speaking of Christ’s coming into the world.”5

The Psalm offers the verb and its object together: Psalm 96:7 in the Greek is a single imperative line, “worship him all his angels” (προσκυνήσατε αὐτῷ πάντες οἱ ἄγγελοι αὐτοῦ), whereas the main Septuagint text of Deuteronomy divides worship and the angels of God between two couplets. Its Hebrew is stable: the Masoretic Text already reads “bow down to him, all gods,” and the Septuagint renders ʾĕlōhîm by “angels” elsewhere. On this reading Hebrews draws on a psalm whose summons stands in both Hebrew and Greek, and whose theme, the Lord coming to reign over the earth, suits the bringing of the firstborn into the world.6
