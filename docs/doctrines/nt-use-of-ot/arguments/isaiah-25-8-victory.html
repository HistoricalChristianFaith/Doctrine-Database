Isaiah 25:8 at 1 Corinthians 15:54: which Greek text gave Paul “death is swallowed up in victory”?

Disputed reading · on Which text did the New Testament quote? · witness: Jerome

Related readings: Isaiah 28:11–12 at 1 Corinthians 14:21 · 1 Corinthians 2:9 · Zechariah 12:10 at John 19:37

The passage

“So when this corruptible shall have put on incorruption, and this mortal shall have put on immortality, then shall be brought to pass the saying that is written, Death is swallowed up in victory. O death, where is thy sting? O grave, where is thy victory?”

— 1 Corinthians 15:54–55 (KJV), c. AD 54, joining Isaiah 25:8 to Hosea 13:14.1

The question

The Hebrew of Isaiah 25:8a, בִּלַּע הַמָּוֶת לָנֶצַח (billaʿ ham-māwet lā-neṣaḥ), reads “he will swallow up death for ever,” with death the object. The Old Greek has κατέπιεν ὁ θάνατος ἰσχύσας, “Death, having prevailed, swallowed [them] up,” with death the subject. Paul’s κατεπόθη ὁ θάνατος εἰς νῖκος, “death is swallowed up in victory,” is passive, with death again the thing swallowed, and renders lā-neṣaḥ by “victory,” a sense reached through the Aramaic and later Hebrew root nṣḥ, “to prevail”; the KJV of Isaiah 25:8 has “in victory” too.1 Jerome (c. 406), expounding Hosea 13:14, set his Hebrew-based rendering, the Septuagint, Paul’s wording and the revisers Symmachus, Aquila and Theodotion side by side, and cited Isaiah 25:8 in Paul’s form as the reading of the Greek.2 The standard quotation tables list 15:54 among citations that follow a Greek diverging from the received Septuagint.3 The dated Greek manuscript of the early Hebraizing (kaige) revision, the Naḥal Ḥever scroll 8ḤevXIIgr, contains the Minor Prophets and no Isaiah, so no first-century copy of a revised Isaiah 25:8 survives.4 The second half of the taunt, Hosea 13:14 at 15:55, has “victory” where the Septuagint has “penalty” (δίκη), and Stanley attributes that change to Paul’s own “sophisticated literary artistry.”5 The dispute concerns the Isaiah line: whether Paul quoted the revision later transmitted under Theodotion’s name, or a Hebraizing Greek tradition older than any of the named revisers. The wider question is set out on “Did the New Testament authors quote the Septuagint?”

A Hebraizing Greek tradition older than the revisers

On this reading Paul quoted a Greek text already corrected toward the Hebrew, from a stream of revision that later fed Aquila and Theodotion without being either of their editions.

Held by: Dietrich-Alex Koch (Die Schrift als Zeuge des Evangeliums, 1986); Christopher D. Stanley (Paul and the Language of Scripture, 1992).6

Koch concluded that Paul here “neither freely varied nor independently translated ad hoc, but followed a given, LXX-correcting Vorlage that also influenced the later translations.” Origen’s Hexapla preserves all three later revisers at this verse: Theodotion κατεπόθη ὁ θάνατος εἰς νῖκος, Aquila καταποντίσει τὸν θάνατον εἰς νῖκος (“he will sink death unto victory”), Symmachus καταποθῆναι ποιήσει τὸν θάνατον εἰς τέλος (“he will make death to be swallowed up, to the end”). All three make death the object again, and Aquila and Theodotion both have νῖκος. Stanley reads that shared rendering, from revisers with distinct techniques, as a sign that “some sort of common tradition… lies behind each of the texts of The Three,” so that “Paul has most likely followed a pre-existing Greek text at this point.”6

The verbatim Theodotion form survives as a marginal gloss in one uncial (Q), while the Syro-Hexapla presupposes an active verb; Rahlfs and Stanley note that the Q reading “could have arisen through assimilation to the text of 1 Cor 15:54,” and McLay marks the Theodotion attribution as doubtful. Stanley therefore rests the case on Aquila’s εἰς νῖκος and on the revisers’ common restoration of the Hebrew direction. On this reading Paul agrees with them in the whole shape of the clause, its direction, voice and key word together, and a Hebraizing reading of the kaige type, a tendency of revision rather than one edition, stood behind Paul and the later revisers alike.7

The proto-Theodotionic (kaige) revision itself

On this reading Paul’s words are the text of the early Hebraizing revision that Dominique Barthélemy named “proto-Theodotion.”

Held by: Roy E. Ciampa and Brian S. Rosner (“1 Corinthians,” in Beale & Carson, 2007).7

Ciampa and Rosner state that Paul’s citation “agrees word for word with Theodotion Isaiah… not OG-Isa,” and identify it with the “proto-Theodotionic” or kaige-Theodotion revision. Theodotion’s transmitted reading, κατεπόθη ὁ θάνατος εἰς νῖκος, is letter for letter Paul’s.7 The historical Theodotion is dated c. AD 150–200, after Paul. Barthélemy’s 1963 study of the Greek Minor Prophets scroll from Naḥal Ḥever, dated palaeographically c. 50 BC–AD 50, identified in it an early Hebraizing revision of the Old Greek, the kaige recension, whose technique resembles Aquila’s and into which the later “Theodotion” material folds. A revision of that type was in existence before or during Paul’s lifetime. On this reading a Theodotion-type wording in a letter of c. AD 54 is the revised Greek Bible of Paul’s own day, quoted as it stood, with the resurrection chapter drawing its proof-text from a Greek Isaiah already brought into line with the Hebrew.4
