Isaiah 25:8 at 1 Corinthians 15:54: “death is swallowed up in victory” — did Paul quote a revised Greek (a Theodotion/kaige-type text) against the Old Greek?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: sound — the catalog’s revised-Greek tag on 1 Corinthians 15:54 holds, and it is the clearest place in the New Testament where a citation agrees with an early Jewish revision of the Greek against the “Old Greek” Septuagint. When Paul clinches the resurrection chapter with “Death is swallowed up in victory” (κατεπόθη ὁ θάνατος εἰς νῖκος), he quotes Isaiah 25:8 in a form the Old Greek does not carry — the Old Greek runs the sentence the other way (“Death, having prevailed, swallowed [them]”) — but which the later Hebraizing revisers (Aquila, Theodotion) do, rendering the Hebrew lā-neṣaḥ by “victory” (εἰς νῖκος). So unlike Isaiah 7:14 or Psalm 40:6 — where the Greek glosses a stable Hebrew — and unlike Deuteronomy 32:43 — where the Greek preserves an older Hebrew — here the New Testament sides with a Greek that has been corrected back toward the Hebrew: evidence that “the Septuagint” the apostles used was itself a plural family of Greek texts, some already revised. Three refinements keep the tag from being over-read: the paired Hosea 13:14 half of the taunt (15:55) is Paul’s own redaction, not a witness to a revised text; the verbatim agreement with “Theodotion” rests on a text-critically fragile witness, so the non-circular anchor is Aquila’s independent “victory”; and the specific label “kaige/proto-Theodotion recension of Isaiah” is a reconstruction — the datable kaige manuscript we actually possess is of the Minor Prophets, not Isaiah — so what is demonstrated is a pre-Christian Hebraizing reading surfacing in Paul, not a documented recension of this verse.

See also: the contrasting species — a distinctively Septuagintal reading of a stable Hebrew — is the Septuagint’s interpretive gloss group (Isaiah 7:14 · Psalm 40:6 · Amos 9:11–12); the Greek preserving an older Hebrew is worked at Deuteronomy 32:43. The catalog’s second revised-Greek row is the stone-of-stumbling of Isaiah 8:14 at 1 Peter 2:8 (with Romans 9:33), a fixed testimonium following the same Hebraizing revision against an Old Greek that negated the image — not yet worked as its own page. The big-picture Septuagint-dependence thesis is weighed on “did the New Testament authors quote the Septuagint?” (assessment sound); this page adds the text-form adjudication of the 1 Corinthians 15:54 catalog row itself.

The claim

“So when this corruptible shall have put on incorruption, and this mortal shall have put on immortality, then shall be brought to pass the saying that is written, Death is swallowed up in victory. O death, where is thy sting? O grave, where is thy victory?”

— 1 Corinthians 15:54–55 (KJV), c. AD 54, welding Isaiah 25:8 to Hosea 13:14.1 The interpretation this argument serves is the catalog’s classification of the 15:54 row: that Paul’s “victory” reproduces not the “Old Greek” Septuagint of Isaiah 25:8 — which reads the verse in the opposite direction, with death as the conqueror — but an early Jewish revision of the Greek that had already been pulled back toward the Hebrew, of the type later represented by Aquila and Theodotion. If that is right, the resurrection chapter’s climactic proof-text is direct evidence, inside the New Testament, that the pre-Christian Greek Bible circulated in more than one form.

Who advances it

That Paul’s Isaiah 25:8 is not the Old Greek is not in dispute; the modern point is which text he did use. The foundational statement is Dietrich-Alex Koch, who concluded that Paul here “neither freely varied nor independently translated ad hoc, but followed a given, LXX-correcting Vorlage that also influenced the later translations.” Christopher D. Stanley, in the fullest treatment, argues that the shared “victory” of Aquila and Theodotion (against the Old Greek) points to “some sort of common tradition” behind the revisers, so that “Paul has most likely followed a pre-existing Greek text at this point.” Roy E. Ciampa & Brian S. Rosner, in the standard reference on the New Testament’s use of the Old, state that Paul’s citation “agrees word for word with Theodotion Isaiah… not OG-Isa,” and identify it with the “proto-Theodotionic” or “kaige-Theodotion” revision first isolated by Dominique Barthélemy; John Paul Heil, Anthony Thiselton, and Richard Hays concur that a pre-existing, non-Septuagintal text of Isaiah 25:8a stands behind the verse.2

The observation is ancient. Jerome, expounding Hosea 13:14, sets the text-forms side by side with a precision no modern apparatus improves on: he gives his own Hebrew-based rendering (“I will be thy death, O death; I will be thy bite, O hell”), notes that “the LXX translated: Where is thy cause (δίκη), O death? Where is thy stimulus (κέντρον), O hell?,” observes that “the Apostle placed: Death has been swallowed up… where is thy… O death?,” and then lists the revisers by name — Symmachus, Aquila, and Theodotion with “the fifth edition” — on the very words in question. He even cites Isaiah 25:8 in Paul’s “Death has been swallowed up by victory” form, calling it the reading of the Greek. Jerome, that is, already saw that Paul’s wording matched neither the plain Old Greek nor the Hebrew, and reached for the Hexaplaric revisers to explain it.3 On the flat classificatory side, Archer & Chirichigno and the quotation tables (Kalvesmaki; the loci citati of the Nestle–Aland / UBS Greek New Testament) list 15:54 among the citations that follow a Greek diverging from the received Septuagint.4

The evidence for

The Old Greek reads the verse the other way round — so Paul cannot have taken his form from it. The Hebrew of Isaiah 25:8a is בִּלַּע הַמָּוֶת לָנֶצַח (billaʿ ham-māwet lā-neṣaḥ), “he [God] will swallow up death for ever” — death is the object God destroys. The Old Greek renders it κατέπιεν ὁ θάνατος ἰσχύσας, “Death, having prevailed, swallowed [them] up” — here θάνατος is the subject, and death is the victor, the exact reverse of the Hebrew. Paul’s κατεπόθη ὁ θάνατος εἰς νῖκος, “death was swallowed up in victory,” restores the Hebrew direction: the verb is now passive, death is again the thing swallowed. A writer quoting the Old Greek would have inherited its reversal; Paul did not. His form agrees with the Hebrew’s sense while remaining in Greek — the signature of a Greek text corrected against the Hebrew.1

Paul’s wording matches the revisers, and two of them independently. Isaiah 25:8 is one of the rare verses where Origen’s Hexapla preserves all three later revisers against the Old Greek. Theodotion reads κατεπόθη ὁ θάνατος εἰς νῖκος — identical, word for word, to Paul; Aquila reads καταποντίσει τὸν θάνατον εἰς νῖκος (“he will sink death unto victory”); Symmachus, καταποθῆναι ποιήσει τὸν θάνατον εἰς τέλος (“he will make death to be swallowed up, to the end”). All three agree against the Old Greek in two respects — death is again the object swallowed, restoring the Hebrew direction — and two of the three, Aquila and Theodotion, render the difficult lā-neṣaḥ by the noun νῖκος, “victory.” That two revisers with independent, well-known techniques converge on the same unusual rendering is the datum Koch and Stanley read as a common tradition: a stream of Hebraizing correction older than any of The Three, from which Paul and they alike drew.5

The rendering is a genuine reading of the Hebrew, not a Christian invention. Neṣaḥ means “perpetuity, for ever” in native biblical Hebrew, and lā-neṣaḥ is the ordinary idiom the Old Greek elsewhere renders temporally. But the same consonants carry a second, Aramaic-derived sense — the verbal root nṣḥ, “to prevail, be victorious” (whence the later noun niṣṣāḥôn, “victory”) — and a reviser reading the noun through that homonym reaches “victory.” So “εἰς νῖκος” is not an arbitrary Christian gloss on the Hebrew but a defensible Jewish reading of it, of a piece with the whole Hebraizing program; the KJV of Isaiah 25:8 itself inherits it (“he will swallow up death in victory”). This is what makes the row a text-form case rather than a dogmatic one: Paul took an existing Greek reading of the Hebrew, not a Greek reading he had to manufacture.1

“Theodotion” can be pre-Christian: the kaige discovery. The historical Theodotion is dated c. AD 150–200, long after Paul, and the obvious objection is that Paul cannot depend on a translation not yet made. Barthélemy’s 1963 study of the Greek Minor Prophets scroll from Naḥal Ḥever (8ḤevXIIgr, palaeographically c. 50 BC–AD 50) dissolved the objection: he identified in it an early Hebraizing revision of the Old Greek — the kaige recension — whose technique anticipates Aquila and into which the later “Theodotion” material folds, so that Barthélemy could call it “proto-Theodotion.” A revision of that type demonstrably existed before or during Paul’s lifetime. On that footing a “Theodotion-type” reading in a first-century letter is not an anachronism but exactly what one expects: the New Testament catching the Greek Bible mid-revision.6

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The catalog’s classification is correct, and it is the doctrine’s clearest single instance of its kind. Paul’s Isaiah 25:8 is demonstrably not the Old Greek — the Old Greek reverses the sentence — and it agrees with a Hebraizing Greek that two of the later revisers independently attest. Read as “Paul’s citation follows a revised, Hebraizing Greek of Isaiah 25:8 against the Old Greek,” the reading is near-consensus and well grounded: it is the standing verdict of Koch, Stanley, Ciampa–Rosner, Heil, and Hays, and it was already seen, in outline, by Jerome. It is also the natural companion to the older-Hebrew row: both are ways the New Testament’s “Septuagint” turns out not to be the single fixed book the old corruption-debate assumed, but a Greek tradition living alongside the Hebrew and repeatedly measured against it.

What must be refined, so the tag is not over-read.

  1. Verse 55 (Hosea 13:14) is Paul’s own redaction, not a revised text — the revised-Greek claim rests on verse 54 alone. The taunt half of the citation is a different animal. The Old Greek of Hosea 13:14 reads ποῦ ἡ δίκη σου, θάνατε; ποῦ τὸ κέντρον σου, ᾅδη; (“Where is thy penalty, O death? Where is thy sting, O Hades?”); Paul reads ποῦ σου, θάνατε, τὸ νῖκος; ποῦ σου, θάνατε, τὸ κέντρον; (“Where, O death, is thy victory? Where, O death, is thy sting?”). Paul has imported the νῖκος of verse 54 to replace the Septuagint’s δίκη — welding the two prophets by a shared catchword, the rabbinic gezera shava device — and has read “death” for the Septuagint’s second vocative “Hades.” No text-form carries this; there is no path from the Hebrew dəbārêkā (“thy plagues”) through δίκη to νῖκος. The Hosea line is therefore neither / free — Pauline artistry — and the argument for a revised Greek stands on the Isaiah line only. (That Paul freely redacts here is also a mild caution against imagining he only ever reproduced fixed texts.)7

  2. The “word-for-word Theodotion” match rests on a fragile witness; the non-circular anchor is Aquila. The Theodotion form that matches Paul letter for letter — the passive κατεπόθη — survives only as a marginal gloss in one uncial (Q), while the Syro-Hexapla presupposes an active verb (κατέπιεν… εἰς νῖκος). Rahlfs and Stanley both warn that the Q gloss “could have arisen through assimilation to the text of 1 Cor 15:54” — a scribe conforming the Theodotion note to the famous Pauline verse — which would make the verbatim match partly circular. The honest, non-circular datum is the one Stanley falls back on: Aquila’s independent εἰς νῖκος, plus the whole revisers’ agreement in restoring the Hebrew direction. “Paul’s Isaiah follows the revisers’ Hebraizing tradition” is secure; “Paul’s Isaiah is Theodotion’s exact text” leans on a witness that may have been copied from Paul.5

  3. “Victory” alone could be independent; the fuller agreement is what resists coincidence. Because νῖκος for neṣaḥ is a natural move for any Greek-writing Jew who reads the noun through the Aramaic “prevail” root, the single word could in principle be reached more than once independently (polygenesis), so the “victory” agreement by itself is suggestive rather than probative. What lifts the case above coincidence is that Paul agrees with the revisers not only in the word but in the whole reconstruction of the clause — the reversed direction, the passive swallowing, death as object — the composite of changes that a shared Vorlage explains far more economically than three separate strokes of the same luck.2

  4. “The kaige recension of Isaiah, datable pre-Christian” outruns the manuscripts; what is proved is a pre-Christian Hebraizing reading. The datable kaige manuscript actually in hand — 8ḤevXIIgr — is of the Minor Prophets (Jonah, Micah, Nahum, Habakkuk, Zephaniah, Zechariah); it contains no Isaiah. So there is no first-century manuscript of a revised Isaiah 25:8; the revised Isaiah reading is reconstructed from Paul (c. AD 54) plus the later Hexaplaric Three (2nd c. AD and after). The attribution of this verse to the pre-Christian kaige stratum is therefore an inference by analogy, not a documented recension — and “kaige-Theodotion” is now understood as a tendency or family of Hebraizing revision, not one uniform edition, so “Paul quotes the kaige Isaiah” over-specifies. The defensible statement is the one the evidence carries: Paul’s Isaiah 25:8 reflects a pre-Christian Hebraizing reading of the kaige type, one that later fed Aquila and Theodotion — a revised Greek reading surfacing inside the New Testament, whether or not we can name the edition it came from.6

Assessment

Sound. The interpretation this argument serves — that 1 Corinthians 15:54 quotes Isaiah 25:8 from a revised, Hebraizing Greek against the Old Greek — holds, and it is the New Testament’s clearest single demonstration that the apostolic “Septuagint” was a plural family of texts, some already corrected back toward the Hebrew. The Old Greek reverses the verse (death as conqueror); Paul’s passive “death is swallowed up in victory” restores the Hebrew direction and matches the revisers, with two of them (Aquila, Theodotion) independently rendering lā-neṣaḥ by “victory” — the convergence Koch and Stanley trace to a common Hebraizing tradition older than any of the Three, and one Jerome already reached for. What the tag must be trusted to mean, and no more, is fixed by four refinements: (a) the paired Hosea 13:14 half of the taunt (15:55) is Paul’s own gezera shava redaction, not a revised text, so the revised-Greek claim rests on the Isaiah line alone; (b) the word-for-word “Theodotion” match rests on a uncial-Q gloss that may itself have been assimilated to Paul, so the non-circular anchor is Aquila’s independent “victory”; (c) “victory” alone could be reached independently, so it is the composite agreement — direction, voice, and word together — that carries the case; and (d) the datable kaige manuscript (8ḤevXIIgr) is of the Minor Prophets, not Isaiah, so “the kaige recension of Isaiah 25:8, securely pre-Christian” is a reconstruction, while “a pre-Christian Hebraizing reading of the kaige type underlies the verse” is what the evidence proves. Kept to that, the row is a genuine, well-grounded revised-Greek case — the pole opposite the interpretive-gloss group and the twin of the older-Hebrew row in showing the same larger truth: that the first-century Greek Bible was a moving, plural text, and the New Testament quotes it in the middle of its revision.
