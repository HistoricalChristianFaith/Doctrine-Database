Isaiah 53:4 at Matthew 8:17 — is bodily healing “in the atonement,” and does the claim turn on the Hebrew over the Greek?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: two sub-claims, rated separately — the text-form call is sound (Matthew renders the Hebrew’s bodily illness — “infirmities… sicknesses” — against the Septuagint, which spiritualizes the same line to “sins”), so the catalog’s MT-distinctive tag holds; the doctrinal claim built on it — that bodily healing is guaranteed “in the atonement,” claimable now by faith on the same footing as forgiveness — is weak, because Matthew ties the fulfilment to Jesus’ healing ministry before the cross, not to the atonement, and the New Testament’s own second use of the Servant line (1 Peter 2:24) reads it of healing from sin. Advanced by the divine-healing movement (A. J. Gordon, A. B. Simpson, F. F. Bosworth).

Related arguments: the Hebrew (Masoretic) text-form — see also Matthew’s formula quotations (Matthew 8:17 is itself a formula quotation) · Zechariah 12:10 at John 19:37.

The claim

“‘He made him to be sin for us who knew no sin,’ so he made him to be sick for us who knew no sickness. He who entered into mysterious sympathy with our pain which is the fruit of sin, also put himself underneath our pain which is the penalty of sin. In other words the passage seems to teach that Christ endured vicariously our diseases as well as our iniquities… we urge the transgressor to accept the Lord Jesus as his sin-bearer, that he may himself no longer have to bear the pains and penalties of his disobedience. But should we shrink utterly from reasoning thus concerning Christ as our pain-bearer?”

— A. J. Gordon, The Ministry of Healing: Miracles of Cure in All Ages (Boston, 1882), 17–18.1 The reading this page weighs has two layers, and they must be kept apart. The lower layer is a text-form observation, and it is correct: when Matthew 8:17 glosses Jesus’ evening of healings with “Himself took our infirmities, and bare our sicknesses,” the evangelist quotes Isaiah 53:4 in a form that follows the Hebrew’s physical illness against the Septuagint, which had rendered the same colon “this one bears our sins.” The upper layer is the doctrine the healing movement builds on that gloss — that because Christ “bore our sicknesses” vicariously, as he bore our sins, bodily healing is provided in the atonement and may be claimed now by faith on the same footing as forgiveness. This page rates each layer on its own evidence.

Who advances it

The text-form observation is old and uncontroversial: Jerome already set the Hebrew (“he has borne our infirmities and carried our sorrows”) beside the Septuagint (“he carries our sins, and grieves for us”) in his Commentary on Isaiah, and every modern commentary on Matthew notes that 8:17 is non-Septuagintal here.2 The doctrinal reading is the load-bearing one, and it belongs to the modern divine-healing tradition. Its exegetical spine was laid by the Baptist pastor A. J. Gordon (The Ministry of Healing, 1882) and the Christian and Missionary Alliance founder A. B. Simpson (The Gospel of Healing, 1888), who paired Isaiah 53:4–5 (“with his stripes we are healed”), Matthew 8:17, and 1 Peter 2:24 (“by whose stripes ye were healed”) into the thesis that healing, like forgiveness, is a purchased benefit of the cross. It was systematized by the Pentecostal evangelist F. F. Bosworth (Christ the Healer, 1924) and T. J. McCrossan (Bodily Healing and the Atonement, 1930), and pressed to its strongest form — guaranteed present healing, refusal of which is unbelief — in the later Word-of-Faith stream (Kenneth Hagin, Kenneth Copeland). The whole tradition rests on Matthew’s reading of the Hebrew’s physical sense over the Septuagint’s spiritualizing one; the Greek Bible, taken alone, would not give it a proof-text.3

The evidence for

The text-form: Matthew follows the Hebrew’s sickness, not the Septuagint’s sin. The Masoretic Isaiah 53:4a reads אָכֵן חֳלָיֵנוּ הוּא נָשָׂא וּמַכְאֹבֵינוּ סְבָלָם (ʾākēn ḥŏlāyēnû hû&702; nāśā&702; û-mak&702;ōbênû səbālām), “surely our sicknesses (ḥŏlî) he bore, and our pains (mak&702;ōb) he carried” — both nouns denote bodily affliction and both verbs mean to lift and bear a weight. The Old Greek spiritualizes the colon: οὗτος τὰς ἁμαρτίας ἡμῶν φέρει καὶ περὶ ἡμῶν ὀδυνᾶται, “this one bears our sins and suffers pain for us” — reading ḥŏlî (“sickness”) as though it were moral evil. Matthew 8:17 reads αὐτὸς τὰς ἀσθενείας ἡμῶν ἔλαβεν καὶ τὰς νόσους ἐβάστασεν, “himself took our infirmities (ἀσθενείας) and bore our diseases (νόσους)” — restoring the Hebrew’s physical nouns against the Greek’s “sins,” and rendering the two Hebrew load-bearing verbs by λαμβάνω / βαστάζω. So Matthew cannot have taken this citation from the Old Greek: the whole point of his fulfilment note — that Jesus’ cures of the body fulfil the Servant song — is present in the Hebrew and absent from a Greek that speaks only of sins. The catalog’s MT-distinctive tag on the row is therefore secure.4

Jerome’s Hebrew, and the medieval puzzlement, confirm the divergence. Reading from the Hebrew, Jerome renders 53:4 “surely he has borne our infirmities and carried our sorrows” and prints the Septuagint’s rival line “he carries our sins, and grieves for us” immediately beside it — the divergence stated by the one Father who worked from both texts. The measure of how sharply the two part is that when a later commentator read Matthew from a Bible shaped by the Greek/Vulgate, the physical wording looked anomalous: Thomas Aquinas, expounding Matthew 8:17 with the Servant text in its “sins” form before him, has to ask outright, “since the text from Isaiah spoke of sins, why does this quotation speak of bodily infirmities?” — answering that bodily illness “very often… arise[s] from spiritual sins.” The question only arises because Matthew is following a text-form (the Hebrew’s illnesses) that Aquinas’s own Isaiah (the Greek’s sins) did not carry.5

The doctrinal reading has a real exegetical footing, and an ancient echo. The healing movement is not inventing a connection Matthew disowns: Matthew does tie the healings to the Servant with his strongest formula (“that it might be fulfilled”), and he chooses illness-language that reads Jesus’ cures as a bearing of what the Servant was to bear. Read maximally, that makes bodily affliction part of the burden the Servant assumes — and Isaiah 53:5’s “with his stripes we are healed,” taken up at 1 Peter 2:24, supplies a second Servant-text with a healing verb. An early patristic voice reads in just this direction: Apollinaris of Laodicea, on Matthew 8:17, says Isaiah “pointed toward the cross,” and that Christ “became the source of healing to humanity” “not in his activity alone but in his passion.” So the reading that Christ’s sickness-bearing is of a piece with his redemptive suffering is not a modern fabrication; a moderate form of it — that bodily redemption is among the ultimate fruits of the atonement — is even conceded by the movement’s own founder, Gordon, who locates the body’s share in that work at “the coming and kingdom of Christ.”6

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The text-form sub-claim is secure and near-consensus: Matthew 8:17 reproduces a Hebrew-aligned form of Isaiah 53:4 (“infirmities… sicknesses”), against the Old Greek’s “sins,” so the row is a genuine MT-distinctive datum — one more Gospel case, with Hosea 11:1 at Matthew 2:15 and Zechariah 12:10 at John 19:37, of the New Testament reaching past the Septuagint to the Hebrew where its point needs it. That much of the healing movement’s foundation is correct: its proof-text really does depend on the Hebrew’s physical sense, and the Greek Bible alone would not yield it.

What is overstated, so the doctrine is not over-read.

  1. Matthew locates the fulfilment in the healing ministry, not the atonement. This is the decisive point. Matthew attaches the Servant citation to an evening of cures (“he cast out the spirits with his word, and healed all that were sick”) that precedes the cross — “that it might be fulfilled… Himself took our infirmities.” If Matthew had meant that sickness-bearing was accomplished at Calvary, he could not have declared Isaiah 53:4 already fulfilled in Galilee, chapters before the passion. The evangelist reads the Servant’s sickness-bearing as discharged in Jesus’ compassionate healing work, not deferred to his death — so “healing is in the atonement” imports into Matthew a claim Matthew’s own placement resists.7

  2. “Took/bore” here leans toward removed, not substituted for. The movement’s whole inference runs through reading ἔλαβεν / ἐβάστασεν as strictly vicarious bearing (“he bore them that we might not bear them”). But βαστάζω also means to take away, and Tertullian already noted that “the Greeks are accustomed to use for ‘carry’ a word that also signifies ‘to take away.’” In a paragraph about Jesus curing the sick, “took our infirmities” most naturally means he lifted them off by healing — an act of removal in the ministry — not that he carried each disease penally in his body. The substitutionary sense is possible, but it is not forced by the verbs, and the pre-cross setting tells against it.8

  3. The New Testament’s own second use of the Servant song spiritualizes the healing. When Peter takes up the same oracle he applies it not to bodily cure but to moral rescue: “who his own self bare our sins in his own body on the tree, that we, being dead to sins, should live unto righteousness: by whose stripes ye were healed” (1 Peter 2:24). Peter’s “healed” is healing from sin, framed by sin on both sides — the very reading the Septuagint had given Isaiah 53:4. So the two New-Testament witnesses the movement welds together in fact pull apart: Matthew reads the Servant’s burden as bodily sickness lifted in the ministry, Peter reads it as sin borne at the cross. That split is fatal to “therefore bodily healing is in the atonement exactly as forgiveness is” — the one text that plainly speaks of the atonement (Peter) is the one that spiritualizes the healing.9

  4. Even a moderate “healing in the atonement” does not yield guaranteed present healing. Grant the strongest defensible form — that bodily redemption is an ultimate benefit secured at the cross. It still does not follow that healing is claimable on demand now, because that benefit, like the resurrection body, is an “already/not yet” possession consummated at the resurrection — which is exactly where Gordon himself puts it (“resurrection is the consummation of his redemptive work for the body… fulfilled at the coming and kingdom of Christ”). The apostolic record confirms the gap: Paul leaves Trophimus “at Miletum sick,” prescribes wine for Timothy’s “often infirmities,” reports Epaphroditus “sick nigh unto death,” and is himself denied relief from his “thorn in the flesh” with “my grace is sufficient for thee.” The strong Word-of-Faith form — that a believer need never be sick and that remaining ill betrays unbelief — runs straight into that evidence, and turns the comfort of the cross into a burden laid on the sick.10

  5. The text-form point does not carry the doctrine. It is worth separating cleanly: the movement is right that Matthew reads the Hebrew, and that observation is this page’s sound half. But being right about the text-form settles only that Matthew speaks of bodily sickness-bearing — not when (ministry vs. cross), not how (removal vs. penal substitution), and not whether it is guaranteed now. The doctrinal edifice is built on those further questions, and the Hebrew text-form answers none of them. So the correct text-form call is quietly doing rhetorical work it cannot bear: “Matthew follows the Hebrew” is true and “therefore healing is in the atonement, claimable by faith” does not follow from it.8

Assessment

Text-form sub-claim: sound. Matthew 8:17 reproduces a Hebrew-aligned form of Isaiah 53:4 — “infirmities… sicknesses” for the Hebrew’s ḥŏlî / mak&702;ōb — against the Septuagint’s spiritualizing “this one bears our sins,” so the catalog’s MT-distinctive tag is correct and the row joins Hosea 11:1 and Zechariah 12:10 as a Gospel case of the New Testament following the Hebrew where its argument needs it. (Whether Matthew translated the Hebrew freshly or drew a Hebraizing Greek form is the same open mechanism question the group’s other members carry; what is proven is the text-form, not the workshop.)

Doctrinal sub-claim (“bodily healing is in the atonement, claimable now by faith”): weak. The reading holds that the correct text-form guarantees present healing, and it does not. Three things defeat the load-bearing form: Matthew ties the fulfilment to Jesus’ healing ministry before the cross, not to the atonement, so the citation cannot mean healing was purchased at Calvary in the way forgiveness was; the New Testament’s own second use of the Servant song (1 Peter 2:24) reads its “healed” of rescue from sin; and even the strongest defensible form — bodily redemption as an ultimate, resurrection-consummated fruit of the cross, which is where Gordon himself locates it — is an “already/not yet” hope, not a present entitlement, as the sick Trophimus, Timothy, Epaphroditus, and the unrelieved Pauline thorn attest. The moderate claim (healing among the atonement’s eschatological benefits) is defensible; the movement’s claim (guaranteed healing now, refusal of which is unbelief) is not — it over-reads a true text-form into a doctrine the text will not carry. Where the divine-healing writers press the guaranteed-now form as the plain sense of Matthew 8:17, that is the overstatement to flag: the verse yields a Hebrew-aligned text-form, not a healing warrant.
