Zechariah 12:10 at John 19:37 — “whom they pierced”: does the pierced-Messiah proof follow the Hebrew against the Greek?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: sound — the Hebrew-aligned (MT-distinctive) text-form is secure, but the strong form (John “translated word for word… as he had read it in Hebrew”) outruns the evidence on mechanism. When John 19:37 proves the crucifixion from Zechariah 12:10 — “they shall look on him whom they pierced” (ἐξεκέντησαν) — he follows the Hebrew dāqārû (“they pierced”), not the Old Greek, which had misread the near-identical Hebrew letters dālet and rêš and rendered “they danced / mocked” (κατωρχήσαντο). The same “pierced” form stands at Revelation 1:7, and it is exactly the reading the later Hebraizing revisers Aquila and Theodotion carry against the Old Greek. So the catalog’s MT-distinctive tag holds — this is the Gospels’ single strongest case of the New Testament standing closer to the Hebrew than to the Septuagint. What holds firmly is the text-form (Hebrew-aligned, not Old-Greek); what is contested is the mechanism — whether John translated the Hebrew fresh (so Jerome), quoted an already-Hebraizing Greek revision (so Menken), or drew a fixed pre-formed testimonium the early church already shared.

Related arguments: the Hebrew (Masoretic) text-form — see also Matthew’s formula quotations.

See also: the contrasting species that dominates the doctrine — the New Testament following a distinctively Septuagintal reading of a stable Hebrew — is the Septuagint’s interpretive gloss group (Isaiah 7:14 · Psalm 40:6 · Amos 9:11–12 · Psalm 16:10). The “mechanism open” verdict here is the same reached on Matthew’s formula quotations, and the revised-Greek possibility it turns on is the phenomenon proven at Isaiah 25:8 and weighed at Isaiah 28:11–12.

The claim

“The Hebrew letters Daleth and Res, that is, D and R, are similar and are distinguished only by a small mark… For if it is read as Dacaru, it is understood to mean ‘they pierced or nailed’, but if the order is reversed, with the letters transposed, it is understood to mean ‘they danced’, as Racadu… However, the beloved disciple John, who drew wisdom from the heart of the Lord and was a Hebrew among the Hebrews, did not greatly care about what the Greek letters contained; but he translated word for word, as he had read it in Hebrew, and said that it was fulfilled at the time of the Lord’s passion.”

— Jerome, Commentary on Zechariah 12:10 (c. 406).1 Jerome is the ancient champion of the reading this page weighs: that John’s “pierced” follows the Hebrew against a Greek that had gone astray by a scribal look-alike, and so proves the evangelist reached past the Septuagint to the Hebrew. The interpretation this page assesses is the sober core of that thesis — that Zechariah 12:10 at John 19:37 is the Gospels’ clearest MT-distinctive datum — kept apart from Jerome’s stronger claim about how John got there (“translated word for word… as he had read it in Hebrew”), which the evidence leaves open.

Who advances it

The observation that John’s “pierced” is the Hebrew’s and not the Old Greek’s is old and now near-universal. Jerome (above) states both the dālet/rêš mechanism and the strong conclusion. The dālet/rêš confusion is a textbook one — the two Hebrew letters (ד / ר) differ only in the length of one stroke, and the same interchange lies behind the Old Greek of Amos 9:12 (“possess” → “seek”). The modern text-critical treatment of the John citation is Maarten J. J. Menken’s study “The Textual Form and Meaning of the Quotation from Zechariah 12:10 in John 19:37” (New Testament Studies 39, 1993), which every later commentary builds on, and Andreas J. Köstenberger’s John section in Beale & Carson’s Commentary on the New Testament Use of the Old Testament (2007), the scholarly control for this doctrine’s Gospel rows.2 Menken, notably, defends the text-form (Hebrew-aligned, against the Old Greek) while arguing the mechanism was a Greek revision, not a fresh translation — so on this page he is both a witness for the claim and the sharpest voice against its strong form.

The evidence for

The Hebrew, the Old Greek, and John part three ways. The Masoretic Zechariah 12:10 reads וְהִבִּיטוּ אֵלַי אֵת אֲשֶׁר־דָּקָרוּ (wə-hibbîṭû ʾēlay ʾēt ʾăšer-dāqārû), “and they shall look to me whom they pierced” — the verb dāqārû from דקר (“pierce, thrust through”). The Old Greek renders it καὶ ἐπιβλέψονται πρός με ἀνθ᾽ ὧν κατωρχήσαντο, “they shall look to me because they danced in triumph / treated [me] despitefully” — having read the same three consonants transposed, רקדו (rāqadû, “they danced”), the dālet/rêš slip Jerome names. John 19:37 reads ὄψονται εἰς ὃν ἐξεκέντησαν, “they shall look on him whom they pierced” (ἐκκεντέω, “pierce through”) — which is the Hebrew’s sense and not the Greek’s. Whatever else is true, John cannot have taken this proof from the Old Greek: the whole point of the citation — that Israel would look on the one they had run through — is present in the Hebrew and absent from a Greek that has them merely mocking.3

John’s word is the revisers’ word, and Revelation carries it too. ἐξεκέντησαν is exactly the reading the later Hebraizing Greek revisers restored against the Old Greek: Theodotion and Aquila both read ἐξεκέντησαν at Zechariah 12:10 (Symmachus, ἐπεξεκέντησαν). And the same “pierced” form recurs at Revelation 1:7 — καὶ… οἵτινες αὐτὸν ἐξεκέντησαν — and in the second-century Fathers who cite the testimonium (Justin, First Apology 52 and Dialogue 32, 64; Epistle of Barnabas 7:9; Tertullian, On the Resurrection of the Flesh 22; Hippolytus, On the End of the World 40), always with a “pierce” verb, never with the Old Greek’s “dance.” The reading John used was thus not his private rendering but a fixed, Hebrew-aligned form of the verse already circulating in the apostolic and sub-apostolic church.4

The Hebrew “pierced” is stable. There is no contrary Hebrew witness: the Masoretic Text, the medieval manuscripts, and the consonantal tradition all read dāqārû, and the “danced” of the Old Greek is a translator’s misreading of that stable Hebrew, not evidence of a variant Vorlage. So — unlike Deuteronomy 32:43, where the Greek preserves an older Hebrew the Masoretes lost — here the divergence is entirely Greek-side, and the Hebrew John follows is the one everyone still reads. That makes the row a clean “New-Testament-follows-the-Hebrew” case: not a dispute between two Hebrew texts, but the evangelist siding with the Hebrew against a demonstrably corrupted Greek.3

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The load-bearing claim is secure and near-consensus: John 19:37 reproduces a Hebrew-aligned form of Zechariah 12:10 (“pierced”), against the Old Greek (“danced / mocked”) and with the later Hebraizing revisers. So the catalog’s MT-distinctive tag on the row stands, and with it the point the row exists to make: the New Testament is not uniformly Septuagintal, and John — alongside John 13:18’s “lifted up his heel” (Psalm 41:9, Hebrew against the LXX’s “magnified supplanting”) — is the Gospels’ clearest home of the reverse lean, the mirror image of the gloss cases where the argument turns on a distinctively Greek reading.

What must be refined, so the claim is not over-read.

  1. “Hebrew-aligned” is proven; “translated fresh from the Hebrew” is not. The data fix that John’s form agrees with the Hebrew against the Old Greek; they do not fix how. Because ἐξεκέντησαν is precisely the word the Hebraizing revisers (Aquila, Theodotion) restored, John’s agreement with the Hebrew is also agreement with a revised Greek — so he may be quoting a pre-existing Hebraizing Greek text (the kaige / proto-Theodotion phenomenon proven at Isaiah 25:8), not rendering Hebrew himself. This is Menken’s conclusion: the citation’s wording “is best explained as deriving from a revised LXX,” not a fresh translation. A third mechanism fits equally — a fixed Christian testimonium, since the same “pierced” form is shared by Revelation 1:7 and the second-century Fathers, suggesting a proof-text John received rather than made. The strong, Jerome-style form (“the beloved disciple… translated word for word, as he had read it in Hebrew”) is therefore a possible reading, not a demonstrated one — exactly the trim applied to Jerome’s universal “wherever” on Matthew’s formula quotations. What the tag reports, and all it should, is the text-form (Hebrew-aligned, not Old-Greek), not the translator’s workshop.2

  2. John has already adapted the verse, which slightly complicates “word for word.” John does not reproduce even the Hebrew verbatim: where the Masoretic Text reads “they shall look to me (ʾēlay) whom they pierced,” John writes “they shall look on him whom they pierced,” shifting the first person to the third to fit the crucified Christ as its object (a shift Revelation 1:7 shares, and one a minority of Hebrew manuscripts read as ʾēlāyw, “to him”). The change is christological framing, not a text-form signal — but it shows the citation is handled, not mechanically copied, whichever Vorlage lay behind it. Jerome’s “word for word” is rhetorical over-precision; the honest description is Hebrew-aligned and adapted.4

  3. John is plural, not simply Hebraizing. The Zechariah row is not John proving he owned a Hebrew Bible: elsewhere John keeps distinctively Septuagintal readings. At John 12:38 he opens Isaiah 53:1 with the vocative “Lord” that is a Septuagintal plus (with Paul at Romans 10:16), and at John 12:40, though he abandons the LXX’s descriptive self-hardening of Isaiah 6:10 for the Hebrew’s causative divine agency, his wording matches neither text-form verbatim. So John, like Matthew, is a witness to textual plurality: a Gospel that quotes the Greek in one place and reaches past it in another. The Zechariah citation is the sharpest of his Hebrew-leaning cases, not proof of a uniform practice.5

  4. This is a counterweight, not a counter-verdict. The row keeps the doctrine’s verdict two-part; it does not overturn it. The wider New-Testament pattern — the divergence proof-texts in Acts, Hebrews, Romans, 1 Peter — still leans Septuagintal where Greek and Hebrew part ways, and the honest headline stays “a plural text, quoted plurally.” John’s “pierced” is the Gospels’ strongest single instance of the New Testament following the Hebrew, which is precisely why the doctrine needs it on the page: it is the evidence that the Septuagintal lean is a lean, not a law.3

Assessment

Sound. The interpretation this argument serves — that John 19:37 (with Revelation 1:7) reproduces a Hebrew-aligned form of Zechariah 12:10, “whom they pierced,” against the Old Greek’s “danced / mocked,” and so stands as the Gospels’ clearest MT-distinctive datum — holds, and is near-consensus. The catalog’s tag on the row is correct: John’s ἐξεκέντησαν is the Hebrew’s and not the Old Greek’s, the divergence arising from a dālet/rêš misreading over a stable Hebrew. Three refinements keep the claim from overreaching: (a) what is proven is the text-form (Hebrew-aligned, not Old-Greek), not the mechanism — a fresh Hebrew translation (Jerome), a Hebraizing revised Greek (Menken), or a fixed church testimonium (shared with Revelation and the second-century Fathers) all fit, and John’s agreement with the Hebrew is also agreement with Aquila and Theodotion, so Jerome’s “John translated word for word, as he had read it in Hebrew” is possible, not demonstrated (flagged on his detail page); (b) John has adapted the verse (“to me” → “on him”), so “word for word” is over-precise even for the Hebrew he follows; and (c) John is textually plural, keeping Septuagintal readings elsewhere (John 12:38), so this row evidences Hebrew attention at a key proof-text, not a uniform Hebrew Bible. Kept to that, Zechariah 12:10 at John 19:37 is the honest Gospel counterweight that makes the doctrine’s verdict truthful: not “Greek over Hebrew” but “a plural text, quoted plurally,” with John — like Matthew — reaching past the Septuagint at the point where his argument needs the Hebrew.
