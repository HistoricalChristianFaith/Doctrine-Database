Zechariah 12:10 at John 19:37 — “whom they pierced”: how did John come by a Hebrew-aligned form?

Disputed reading · on Which text did the New Testament quote? · witnesses: Jerome · Justin Martyr

Related readings: Matthew’s formula quotations · Revelation’s Old Testament wording · Isaiah 25:8 at 1 Corinthians 15:54

The passage

“And again another scripture saith, They shall look on him whom they pierced.”

— John 19:37 (KJV), quoting Zechariah 12:10.1

The question

The Masoretic Zechariah 12:10 reads וְהִבִּיטוּ אֵלַי אֵת אֲשֶׁר־דָּקָרוּ (wə-hibbîṭû ʾēlay ʾēt ʾăšer-dāqārû), “and they shall look to me whom they pierced.” The Old Greek has καὶ ἐπιβλέψονται πρός με ἀνθ᾽ ὧν κατωρχήσαντο, “because they danced in triumph / treated [me] despitefully,” having read the consonants as רקדו (rāqadû, “they danced”), the look-alike letters dālet and rêš interchanged. John reads ὄψονται εἰς ὃν ἐξεκέντησαν, “they shall look on him whom they pierced,” with the Hebrew, and writes “on him whom” for the Hebrew “to me whom.”1 The later Hebraizing revisers Aquila and Theodotion also read ἐξεκέντησαν (Symmachus ἐπεξεκέντησαν), and the “pierced” form recurs at Revelation 1:7 and in Justin, the Epistle of Barnabas, Tertullian and Hippolytus, always with a “pierce” verb.2 Elsewhere John keeps Septuagintal readings, such as the vocative “Lord” of Isaiah 53:1 at John 12:38; Andreas J. Köstenberger notes John’s agreement with Theodotion at 19:37 and canvasses how it arose.3 The dispute is over how John came by the Hebrew-aligned wording: by rendering the Hebrew himself, from a Greek text already revised toward the Hebrew, or from a proof-text already fixed in Christian use.

John rendering the Hebrew

On this reading John read Zechariah in Hebrew and translated the verse himself.

Held by: Jerome (Latin, Commentary on Zechariah, c. 406).4

“The Hebrew letters Daleth and Res, that is, D and R, are similar and are distinguished only by a small mark… For if it is read as Dacaru, it is understood to mean ‘they pierced or nailed’, but if the order is reversed, with the letters transposed, it is understood to mean ‘they danced’, as Racadu… However, the beloved disciple John, who drew wisdom from the heart of the Lord and was a Hebrew among the Hebrews, did not greatly care about what the Greek letters contained; but he translated word for word, as he had read it in Hebrew, and said that it was fulfilled at the time of the Lord’s passion.”

Jerome sets the Hebrew “whom they have pierced” beside the Septuagint’s “because they have insulted me,” names the cause of the divergence in the two similar letters, and traces John’s wording to the Hebrew itself. On this reading the evangelist, a Hebrew among Hebrews, passed over the Greek and gave the sense of the Hebrew verb, the one on which his citation turns: that those who stood at the cross would look on the one they had run through.4

A Greek text revised toward the Hebrew

On this reading John quoted a Greek Zechariah of the Theodotionic, kaige type, already corrected to read “pierced.”

Held by: Maarten J. J. Menken (New Testament Studies, 1993).5

Menken accepts that its text-form agrees with the Hebrew against the Old Greek, and holds that its wording “is best explained as deriving from a revised LXX” of the Theodotionic type rather than from a fresh rendering of the Hebrew. ἐξεκέντησαν is the very word the Hebraizing revisers Aquila and Theodotion have at Zechariah 12:10. Revisions of that kind, correcting the Old Greek toward the Hebrew, were under way before and during the first century, as the kaige recension attests. On this reading John’s agreement with the Hebrew is his agreement with a revised Greek Zechariah, a text in which the translator’s “danced” had already been put right as “pierced.”5

A fixed Christian testimonium

On this reading John received the verse as a proof-text already worded and in circulation among Christians.

Held by: ⚠ unverified — no named proponent located yet.

The same “pierced” form, with the same shift from “to me” to a third person, stands at Revelation 1:7, and the second-century writers who cite the verse (Justin, Barnabas, Tertullian, Hippolytus) always use a “pierce” verb. On this reading these agreements mark a shared testimonium about the crucified one, adapted to him and handed on in Christian teaching, which John cites as he received it.2
