Isaiah 6:9–10 in the New Testament — one verse in two text-forms: does the divine-hardening theology ride on Matthew’s Septuagint against John’s Hebrew?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: two sub-claims, rated separately — the text-form call is sound: the same commissioning oracle is quoted in the New Testament in two genuinely different shapes — Matthew 13:14–15 and Acts 28:26–27 reproduce the Septuagint’s descriptive form (“this people’s heart is waxed gross… their eyes they have closed,” a people self-hardening), while John 12:40 recasts the citation toward the Hebrew’s causative agency (“He hath blinded their eyes, and hardened their heart”). The doctrinal claim built on that difference — that the New Testament’s theology of divine judicial hardening rides on the text-form, standing on John’s causative Hebrew and dissolving into mere self-hardening on Matthew’s descriptive Greek — is weak: the doctrine is underdetermined by the text-form. Both forms keep a divine judicial note (God withholds the healing, “and I should heal them”); the Septuagint form is quoted by Paul in Acts precisely to announce a God-directed turning-away; John’s form neither names God as the blinder nor compels efficient reprobation (the Greek Fathers who noticed it read it as permission); and the systematic hardening doctrine is argued from Romans 9–11 and Pharaoh, not from which Isaiah-form an evangelist happened to carry. The divine-hardening reading is itself a genuinely contested doctrine — strong in the Augustinian–Reformed tradition, resisted in the Greek-patristic and Arminian ones — but that contest is decided elsewhere, not by Isaiah 6’s wording.

See also: this row sits astride both text-form groups on the summary and joins neither, because it is the one verse that appears in the New Testament in both shapes at once — the Septuagint-gloss group (Isaiah 7:14, Psalm 40:6, Amos 9:11–12, Psalm 16:10), which Matthew and Acts here illustrate, and the Hebrew (Masoretic) text-form group (Matthew’s formula quotations, Zechariah 12:10, Isaiah 53:4), toward which John here leans.

The claim

The reading this page weighs has two layers, and they must be kept apart. The lower layer is a text-form observation, and it is correct: Isaiah’s commissioning oracle enters the New Testament in two measurably different forms. The upper layer is the inference some draw from it — that the theology of divine hardening (judicial blinding, reprobation) hangs on that textual fork, so that Matthew’s Septuagint teaches a people who harden themselves while John’s Hebrew teaches a God who hardens them. The one Father who read both texts states the fork exactly:

“According to the easy interpretation of the Septuagint, Isaiah the prophet declares what the people will do by the command of the Lord. In Hebrew, there is a difficulty in how God Himself commands the people to hear but not understand, to see but not perceive, and then the prophet comes and prays to the Lord and says: Blind the heart of this people, and make their ears heavy, and shut their eyes…”

— Jerome, Commentary on Isaiah on 6:9–10.1 Jerome names both the datum and the theological pressure it carries: the Greek reads as a description of what an obstinate people will do, while the Hebrew reads as an imperative to dull them, and the difficulty — who is the hardening agent? — is felt at once. This page rates each layer on its own evidence: first that the two forms are really there (they are), then whether the divine-hardening doctrine rides on which one an evangelist quotes (it does not).

Who advances it

The text-form observation is old and uncontroversial. Jerome set the Septuagint’s descriptive form (which Paul quotes at Acts 28) beside the Hebrew’s imperative in his Commentary on Isaiah; Augustine juxtaposed Matthew’s “they have shut their eyes” with John’s “He hath blinded their eyes” and worried openly at the difference; and Cyril of Alexandria stated it most precisely of all — “in this way the Seventy have rendered the passage, but it is likely that the Evangelist followed the text of the Hebrews, which differs from that of the Seventy.” Modern text-criticism agrees and sharpens it: the Old Greek of Isaiah has a documented tendency to soften the Hebrew’s harsh commands to harden into descriptions of a people already hardened, and the standard studies (Craig Evans’s monograph To See and Not Perceive; the Beale–Carson Commentary on the New Testament Use of the Old Testament) treat Matthew’s full Septuagint citation and John’s Hebraizing recast as the textbook case of one oracle carried in two forms.2

The doctrinal layer is the load-bearing one, and it belongs to the long debate over divine hardening and reprobation. On one side the Augustinian–Reformed tradition presses John’s causative form: “therefore they could not believe, because that Esaias said again, He hath blinded their eyes and hardened their heart” is read as an efficient decree, and Isaiah 6 is folded into the hardening theology of Romans 9:18 (“whom he will he hardeneth”) and the Pharaoh narrative. On the other side the Greek-patristic and later Arminian traditions press Matthew’s descriptive form: Origen and Chrysostom read the “waxed gross” heart as self-inflicted, God only ratifying a hardening the people chose. The interpretive claim this page tests is the bridge some build between the two debates — that the text-form choice is itself the theological move, so that the divine-hardening reading stands or falls with John’s Hebrew over Matthew’s Greek.3

The evidence — the two text-forms are real

The Hebrew: imperatives to harden. The Masoretic Isaiah 6:9–10 commissions the prophet with imperatives addressed to him: “Make the heart of this people fat (הַשְׁמֵן, hašmēn), and make their ears heavy (הַכְבֵּד), and shut their eyes (הָשַׁע); lest they see… and convert, and be healed” — the prophet is told to dull the people by his very preaching, so the hardening is God’s command executed through the prophet, and the final clause is impersonal (“and one heal him,” וְרָפָא לוֹ), not “I heal them.” The Septuagint: description of a people already hard. The Old Greek turns each imperative into a statement: “the heart of this people has grown fat (ἐπαχύνθη), and with their ears they heard heavily, and their eyes they closed (ἐκάμμυσαν)… lest they should see… and turn, and I will heal them (ἰάσομαι αὐτούς).” Two shifts matter: the eye-closing becomes something the people do to themselves (active, with the people as subject), and the healing becomes God’s in the first person. The Greek thus reads as an oracle about an obstinate people; the Hebrew as a commission to harden.4

Matthew and Acts reproduce the Septuagint; John recasts toward the Hebrew. Matthew’s parable-chapter citation (Matthew 13:14–15) is the fullest verbatim Septuagint quotation in the Gospels — “this people’s heart is waxed gross… their eyes they have closed… and I should heal them” — word for word the Greek, self-hardening and all. Paul’s closing oracle at Rome (Acts 28:26–27) carries the identical Septuagint form. But John 12:40 abbreviates and rearranges the verse and, decisively, restores active divine agency: “He hath blinded (τετύφλωκεν) their eyes, and hardened (ἐπώρωσεν) their heart; that they should not see… and be converted, and I should heal them.” Where the Septuagint has the people close their own eyes, John has a single third-person subject blind and harden them — the Hebrew’s causative direction. So within the New Testament the same oracle stands in both text-forms: the descriptive Greek in Matthew/Acts, a Hebraizing recast in John. (Mark and Luke abbreviate to the short, text-form-neutral opening; Mark 4:12 ends instead with the Targum’s “their sins should be forgiven them.”)5

The evidence — divine hardening is genuinely in the New Testament’s use

The doctrinal reading is not read into an unwilling text. John frames the whole citation as an explanation of a divinely-fixed unbelief — “therefore they could not believe, because that Esaias said again, He hath blinded their eyes” — language of sovereign necessity, not of a merely-observed obstinacy; and he keeps the Septuagint’s first-person “and I should heal them,” so that even in the causative form it is God who withholds the healing. The same theology runs through the wider New Testament: Paul’s Romans 9:18 (“whom he will he hardeneth”) reads the Pharaoh narrative as active divine hardening, and his Romans 11:8 blends Isaiah 29:10 with Deuteronomy 29:4 into “God hath given them the spirit of slumber, eyes that they should not see” — a divine giving, not a self-closing. Augustine builds directly on John’s form: “God hardens and blinds a man, by forsaking and not supporting him… by a secret sentence, by an unjust one He cannot,” and on the same oracle: “mercy upon the elect… but judgment upon the others who have been blinded… this election is through grace, not at all through merits.” So the reading that God judicially hardens is a live and well-attested use of Isaiah 6 — the page does not rate it weak.6

Adversarial assessment

What holds: the text-form is sound. The two forms are secure and were seen in antiquity. Matthew 13:14–15 and Acts 28:26–27 reproduce the Septuagint’s descriptive self-hardening verbatim; John 12:40 restores the Hebrew’s active divine agency (“he hath blinded… hardened”). That much of the claim is correct, and it is a genuinely instructive datum — the catalog’s single clearest case of one Old-Testament verse entering the New Testament in both a Septuagintal and a Hebraizing shape, the tightest internal proof that the apostolic writers drew on a plural text. It is even fair to say the forms carry a difference of emphasis: the Septuagint’s stative “has grown fat” and its “they closed their own eyes” foreground human culpability, while John’s causative verbs and his “therefore they could not believe” foreground divine sovereignty. That much is real.

What is overstated, so the doctrine is not over-read. The load-bearing claim — that the divine-hardening doctrine rides on the text-form, so it stands on John and falls on Matthew — does not hold, on four counts.

  1. The Septuagint’s descriptive form does not exclude divine hardening. This is the decisive point. Paul quotes the Septuagint form at Acts 28:26–27 not to teach self-hardening but to pronounce a God-authored judicial turning-away (“Well spake the Holy Ghost by Esaias… the salvation of God is sent unto the Gentiles”), and the descriptive form keeps God as the one who would “heal them” and now does not. Jerome saw the problem plainly: even reading the “easy” Septuagint, one still meets divine hardening across Scripture — Pharaoh whom God “raised up” and hardened, Romans 11:8’s “God hath given them the spirit of insensibility” — so “it is in vain for us to resort to the Septuagint translation” to escape the difficulty; “the same question remains in the churches” whichever form one reads. The descriptive Greek relocates the emphasis; it does not remove the divine agent.7

  2. John’s causative form neither names God nor compels efficient reprobation. John does not write “God hath blinded”: the subject of “he hath blinded… hardened” is left grammatically open, exactly as the Hebrew leaves the imperative’s ultimate agent unspecified. Cyril of Alexandria — the very Father who identified John’s form as the Hebrew against the Seventy — presses just this: “so far as the actual wording of the prophet goes, he has not said that ‘God’ blinded them… it is likely that some one else did this” (the devil), and even granting God as subject “it must be understood … that He allowed them to suffer blinding… He gives up to a reprobate mind those who are of a disposition like theirs.” Theophylact reads John’s “God blinds” as “the abandonment by God… He blinds because He is not present,” and Augustine, building his hardest predestinarian reading on John’s form, still glosses it as God hardening “by forsaking and not supporting,” a desert justly earned. The causative form is compatible with efficient reprobation, but it does not force it: the mainstream ancient reading of John’s own wording is permissive.8

  3. John’s form is not simply “the Hebrew,” and its choice tracks argument, not a hardening-system. John does not reproduce the Masoretic text either: he keeps the Septuagint’s first-person “and I should heal them” (the Hebrew has an impersonal “and it be healed for him”), drops the hearing-clause, and reverses eyes and heart — a free recast, which is why the catalog tags it neither / free, not MT-distinctive. The evangelists’ forms are best explained by their working Bibles and rhetorical aims, not by rival doctrines of hardening: Matthew and Paul quote the full Septuagint because it was the Greek church’s Bible and its “lest they turn and I heal them” served the parable-secret and the turn to the Gentiles; John, writing to explain a Messiah rejected despite his signs, recasts the oracle to state that the unbelief was foreseen and sovereignly ordered. Chrysostom, reading Matthew’s Septuagint, even makes the descriptive wording load-bearing for the opposite theology — “He said not simply, they see not, but seeing they see not; so the blindness is of their own wickedness” — which shows the form can be pressed either way by a determined reader.9

  4. The divine-vs-self hardening question is decided elsewhere, and the New Testament holds both. The systematic doctrine of judicial hardening is argued from Romans 9 (Pharaoh, “he hardeneth whom he will”) and the Pauline “God gave them up” sequence — not from whether Matthew or John phrases Isaiah 6 descriptively or causatively. And the New Testament’s own settled shape is neither bare self-hardening nor bare decree but a judicial hardening that ratifies a hardening the people began: the people “closed their eyes” (Matthew’s form) and God confirmed them in it (John’s form), the two readings naming the two sides of one event, as Augustine’s “they well deserved this” and Cyril’s “He gives up… those of a disposition like theirs” both insist. So the text-form does not deliver a clean fork between two doctrines; it delivers two emphases within one.10

Assessment

Text-form sub-claim: sound. The same commissioning oracle enters the New Testament in two measurably different shapes. Matthew 13:14–15 and Acts 28:26–27 reproduce the Septuagint’s descriptive form — “this people’s heart is waxed gross… their eyes they have closed” (a people self-hardening) — while John 12:40 recasts the citation toward the Hebrew’s causative agency — “He hath blinded their eyes, and hardened their heart.” This is the catalog’s clearest single instance of one Old-Testament verse quoted in the New in both a Septuagintal and a Hebraizing form — a tight internal proof of the plural text — and the two-form datum was already stated by Cyril, Jerome, and Augustine.

Doctrinal sub-claim (“the divine-hardening theology rides on the text-form”): weak. The doctrine is underdetermined by which form an evangelist quotes. The Septuagint’s descriptive form does not exclude divine hardening — Paul quotes it at Acts 28 to announce a God-authored judgment, and Jerome shows the hardening difficulty “remains in the churches” whichever text one reads; John’s causative form neither names God as the blinder nor compels efficient reprobation — the Greek Fathers who identified it as the Hebrew (Cyril, Theophylact) and even Augustine, who built his hardest reading on it, all read it as permission or just desertion; John’s form is in any case a free recast keeping the Septuagint’s “I should heal them,” so the choice tracks argument and working-Bible, not a hardening-system; and the systematic doctrine is decided on Romans 9–11 and Pharaoh, where the New Testament in fact holds a judicial hardening that ratifies a self-hardening — the two forms naming the two sides of one event, not two rival doctrines. What is fair to grant is a difference of emphasis (the Septuagint foregrounds human culpability, John divine sovereignty); what over-reaches is making the whole doctrine of divine hardening stand or fall with the textual fork. The underlying divine-hardening reading is a separate, genuinely contested question — strong by the Augustinian–Reformed lights, resisted by the Greek-patristic and Arminian ones — and this page leaves that contest where it belongs, not on Isaiah 6’s wording. Where a writer presses “John’s Hebrew proves the decree, Matthew’s Greek proves free self-hardening,” that is the overstatement to flag: the verse yields a two-form text-form, not a settled doctrine of hardening.
