Why does Paul quote Habakkuk 2:4 (“the just shall live by faith”) without a possessive?

Disputed reading · on Which text did the New Testament quote? · witnesses: Jerome · Thomas Aquinas

Related readings: Isaiah 59:20 at Romans 11:26 · Hebrews and the Greek Old Testament

The passage

“For therein is the righteousness of God revealed from faith to faith: as it is written, The just shall live by faith.” (Romans 1:17)

“But that no man is justified by the law in the sight of God, it is evident: for, The just shall live by faith.” (Galatians 3:11)

— Paul, Romans 1:17 and Galatians 3:11, quoting Habakkuk 2:4 as ὁ δὲ δίκαιος ἐκ πίστεως ζήσεται, with no possessive pronoun.1

The question

The Masoretic Hebrew of Habakkuk 2:4 reads “the righteous, by his faithfulness (be-ʾəmûnātô), shall live”; the Septuagint, “the righteous shall live by my faith” (ἐκ πίστεως μου), God speaking. Old Greek manuscripts divide between placing the μου with “faith” and with “righteous” (“my righteous one”), and Hebrews 10:38 in its earliest witnesses has the latter. Paul, in both letters, has neither pronoun. The dispute is how to describe his form: as Paul following the Septuagint, or as Paul deliberately dropping the possessive. The separate theological question of whose faith Habakkuk means is not taken up here.2

Paul following the Septuagint, for readers who knew the Greek

On this reading Paul cites the Septuagint’s form of the verse rather than the Hebrew, because he wrote to Greek-reading churches and the meaning was not at stake.

Held by: Jerome (Latin, Commentary on Habakkuk, d. 420); Thomas Aquinas (Catholic, Commentary on Romans, d. 1274).3

Jerome, commenting on Habakkuk from the Hebrew, sets the two forms side by side: the Septuagint “placed: But the just shall live by my faith,” while “all equally translated, he shall live by his own faith” (so Symmachus, ὁ δίκαιος τῇ ἑαυτοῦ πίστει ζήσει). He traces the fork to the Hebrew letters: had Baemunatho ended in Yod rather than Vau, the Greek “in my faith” would be correct; “the similarity of the letters Vau and Jod, which are only distinguished by their size, has been a cause of error.”3

He then explains why the apostle used the Greek: “the Apostle used the testimony of the Septuagint more, writing to the Romans: ‘The just shall live by faith’ (Rom. 1:17), and not what is contained in the Hebrew… For he was writing to the Romans, who did not know the Hebrew Scriptures: and he did not care about the words, since the meaning was safe… Otherwise, wherever the meaning is different… he noted it.” Aquinas, reading the Vulgate’s iustus meus ex fide vivit, says the same: “my just man lives by faith. This follows the Septuagint text, for in our text, which follows the Hebrew truth, it says the just man lives by his faith.” On this reading Paul’s word order, with ἐκ πίστεως before the verb, is the Greek’s, and his lean away from the Hebrew “his” places him on the Septuagint side of the fork.4

Paul’s own abridgement of the Greek, letting “faith” stand absolute

On this reading Paul starts from the Septuagint but deliberately omits its pronoun, so that “faith” is no longer tied to God or to the believer.

Held by: Dietrich-Alex Koch (Die Schrift als Zeuge des Evangeliums, 1986); Richard B. Hays (Echoes of Scripture in the Letters of Paul, 1989); Christopher D. Stanley (Paul and the Language of Scripture, 1992); Mark A. Seifrid (in Beale & Carson, 2007).5

The omission is the same in both letters, written to different churches, so on this reading it is no slip of memory. No Old Greek witness of Habakkuk 2:4 lacks the pronoun altogether; the manuscripts carry μου and differ only in where it stands. Paul’s form keeps the Greek word order and removes the pronoun: the Septuagint, freely abridged.2

The holders of this reading find a purpose in the abridgement. Both the Hebrew ʾəmûnāh and the Greek πίστις span “faith,” “faithfulness” and “fidelity,” and each received possessive fixes the sense: the Hebrew toward the believer’s own fidelity, the Septuagint toward God’s faithfulness to his promise. Without the pronoun, πίστις stands absolute, as the reception of the gospel and the ground of the righteousness Paul has just named, “the righteousness of God revealed from faith to faith.” On this reading Paul shaped the verse to say exactly what his argument for righteousness by faith required.5
