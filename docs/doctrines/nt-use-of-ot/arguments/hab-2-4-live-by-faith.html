Habakkuk 2:4 at Romans 1:17 & Galatians 3:11: “the just shall live by faith” — does Paul’s pronoun-less form match either received text?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: sound — the catalog’s neither / free tag on Romans 1:17 holds, and this is the doctrine’s most compact internal proof of a plural text: one verse is quoted three times in the New Testament in three forms that diverge among themselves. Habakkuk 2:4 carries a possessive in both received Old-Testament traditions — the Masoretic Hebrew reads “the just shall live by his faithfulness” (be-ʾəmûnātô, the righteous man’s own fidelity), the Septuagint “the just shall live by my faith” (ἐκ πίστεως μου, God’s faithfulness) — yet Paul at Romans 1:17 and Galatians 3:11 quotes it without any pronoun at all: “the just shall live by faith” (ὁ δὲ δίκαιος ἐκ πίστεως ζήσεται). And when Hebrews 10:38 quotes the same verse it keeps the Septuagint’s “my,” but shifted to modify the righteous one (“my righteous one shall live by faith,” ὁ δὲ δίκαιός μου in the earliest witnesses). So three New-Testament citations of one clause give three text-forms — and Paul’s is the pronoun-less one, matching neither the Hebrew nor the Greek verbatim. Two findings, kept separate. (a) The text-form call is secure: Paul reproduces neither received possessive; his form is closest to the Septuagint’s (same word order, ἐκ πίστεως before the verb) but drops the μου, so it is an abridged Septuagintal form, not a fresh translation of the Hebrew. (b) Paul’s justification-by-faith argument does not hang on the omission: the load-bearing clause — “the just shall live by faith” (ἐκ πίστεως ζήσεται) — stands in every text-form; what the dropped pronoun does is let “faith” stand absolute, unmoored from either God’s covenant-faithfulness or the believer’s fidelity, which suits Paul’s reading but is exploited, not the foundation. This is the same “does the argument depend on the wording?” trim applied at Psalm 40:6 and Amos 9:12.

See also: this is the first of the three Romans citations the catalog tags neither / free; its nearest sibling is Isaiah 59:20 at Romans 11:26 (assessment sound), where Paul again quotes in a form matching no surviving copy of the prophet — the Deliverer coming “out of Zion” — and the Targum-shaped “vengeance is mine” of Deuteronomy 32:35 at Romans 12:19 is the third. Habakkuk 2:3–4 is also one of the argument-hinges gathered in the book-level synthesis “Does the Epistle to the Hebrews argue from a Greek Old Testament?” (assessment sound), where Hebrews 10:37–38 quotes the fuller Septuagint form (“the coming one… if he draw back”); this page carries the piece that one does not — the three-way divergence of the “live by faith” clause itself across Romans, Galatians, and Hebrews. The big-picture Septuagint-dependence thesis these rows instance is weighed at “did the New Testament authors quote the Septuagint?” (assessment sound). The separate, huge soteriological question — whose faith Habakkuk means, and how “the righteous shall live by faith” grounds justification — is not this page’s subject; this page adjudicates only the text-form.

The claim

“For therein is the righteousness of God revealed from faith to faith: as it is written, The just shall live by faith.” (Romans 1:17)

“But that no man is justified by the law in the sight of God, it is evident: for, The just shall live by faith.” (Galatians 3:11)

— Paul, Romans 1:17 and Galatians 3:11, quoting Habakkuk 2:4. In both places Paul reproduces the clause ὁ δὲ δίκαιος ἐκ πίστεως ζήσεται — “the just shall live by faith” — with no possessive pronoun.1 The interpretation this argument serves is the catalog’s classification: that Paul’s citation matches neither the Masoretic Hebrew (“by his faithfulness”) nor the Septuagint (“by my faith”) exactly, and that the three New-Testament uses of the one verse — Romans, Galatians, Hebrews — diverge among themselves, a compact demonstration of the doctrine’s central thesis that the first-century text was plural and quoted plurally. The sub-claim this page tests is whether the omission is load-bearing for Paul’s argument (it is not) and what best explains it.

Who advances it

That the “live by faith” verse runs differently in the Hebrew and the Greek was seen by the ancient expositors who read both. Jerome, commenting on Habakkuk from the Hebrew, prints the two forms side by side — his own version (with “all” the other translators, and Symmachus, ὁ δίκαιος τῇ ἑαυτοῦ πίστει ζήσει) reading “the just shall live by his own faith,” the Septuagint “by my faith” — and ascribes the fork to a scribal confusion of two near-identical Hebrew letters: had the word ended in yôd rather than waw (baʾəmûnātò read as baʾəmûnātî), the Greek “in my faith” would be correct; “the similarity of the letters waw and yôd, which are only distinguished by their size, has been a cause of error.” He then explains Paul’s choice: the apostle “used the testimony of the Septuagint more, writing to the Romans… and not what is contained in the Hebrew… because he was writing to the Romans, who did not know the Hebrew Scriptures: and he did not care about the words, since the meaning was safe.”2 Thomas Aquinas, expounding Romans 1:17, makes the same call — “my just man lives by faith. This follows the Septuagint text, for in our text, which follows the Hebrew truth, it says the just man lives by his faith.”3 In the modern surveys the same divergence is a flat classification — Archer & Chirichigno and the Nestle–Aland loci citati file Romans 1:17 / Galatians 3:11 as agreeing with neither received text at the pronoun — and the citation-technique scholars (Christopher Stanley, Dietrich-Alex Koch, Richard Hays) press the stronger form: that Paul deliberately drops the possessive to let “faith” stand absolute, and it is that stronger form the assessment below tests.4

The evidence for

Both received Old-Testament traditions carry a possessive; Paul carries none. The Masoretic Hebrew of Habakkuk 2:4 reads וְצַדִּיק בֶּאֱמוּנָתוֹ יִחְיֶה — “and the righteous, by his faithfulness (be-ʾəmûnātô, third-person suffix), shall live,” the possessive attaching to the righteous person’s own steadfast fidelity. The Septuagint renders ὁ δὲ δίκαιος ἐκ πίστεως μου ζήσεται — “the righteous shall live by my faith(fulness),” the possessive now first-person, God the speaker (so the righteous lives by God’s fidelity to his promise). Paul, at both Romans 1:17 and Galatians 3:11, writes ὁ δὲ δίκαιος ἐκ πίστεως ζήσεται — the same clause, minus the μου. The omission is unanimous across his two uses, so it is not a slip of memory in one letter.1

The one verse appears in the New Testament in three divergent forms. Set the three citations together and the plural text is visible on the surface: Romans 1:17 and Galatians 3:11 read “the just shall live by faith” (no pronoun); Hebrews 10:38, in its earliest Greek witnesses (𝚾46, Sinaiticus, Alexandrinus), reads ὁ δὲ δίκαιός μου ἐκ πίστεως ζήσεται — “my righteous one shall live by faith,” keeping the Septuagint’s μου but relocating it to qualify “righteous” rather than “faith.” That relocation is itself a genuine Septuagint variant: the Old Greek manuscripts split, some reading ἐκ πίστεως μου (“by my faith,” the pronoun with “faith”) and others ὁ δίκαιός μου (“my righteous one,” the pronoun with “righteous”). So the Masoretic possessive (“his”), two Septuagint placements of “my,” and Paul’s no-pronoun form give four attested shapes of one short clause — the doctrine’s tightest single illustration that no single standardized wording stood behind the apostolic citations.5

The omission exactly serves Paul’s argument, and the word bears the weight. Both the Hebrew ʾəmûnāh and the Greek πίστις span a genuine range — “faith,” “faithfulness,” “fidelity” — and each received possessive fixes that range in a definite direction: the Hebrew toward the believer’s own fidelity, the Septuagint toward God’s covenant-faithfulness. By dropping the pronoun Paul removes the anchor and lets πίστις stand absolute — “faith” as the reception of the gospel, the ground of righteousness he has just named (“the righteousness of God … from faith to faith”). The abridged form thus reads more naturally into his justification-by-faith argument than either possessive would. That is the honest strength of the “Paul reads it his own way” thesis: the omission is unanimous, and it fits.4

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The catalog’s classification is correct: Paul’s Romans-1:17 / Galatians-3:11 form matches neither the Masoretic Hebrew nor the Septuagint of Habakkuk 2:4 verbatim, because it drops a possessive that both received traditions carry. And the three New-Testament citations of the verse genuinely diverge among themselves. As a demonstration that one verse could be current in more than one shape, the row belongs where the catalog put it — and it does that work more economically than any other row in the catalog, because the divergence sits inside the New Testament, not only between the Testaments.

What must be refined, so the finding is neither understated nor stretched.

  1. Paul’s form is an abridged Septuagintal form, not a fresh rendering of the Hebrew. The defeater to test was whether Paul might follow an attested pronoun-less Greek copy — which would make the row “revised Greek” or simply LXX, not “free.” But no Old-Greek witness of Habakkuk 2:4 lacks the pronoun altogether: the manuscripts carry μου, differing only in whether it qualifies “faith” or “righteous.” Paul’s form is closest to the Septuagint — the word order (ἐκ πίστεως before ζήσεται) is the Greek’s, not the Hebrew’s (which ends on the verb) — so the honest description is “the Septuagint with the possessive removed,” a free abridgement of the Greek, which is why the catalog’s neither / free tag (rather than “MT-distinctive” or a clean “LXX-distinctive”) is the right one.5

  2. The argument does not depend on the omission; the pronoun is exploited, not load-bearing. As at Psalm 40:6 and Amos 9:12, the clause that carries Paul’s weight — “the just shall live by faith” (ἐκ πίστεως) — stands in every text-form, Hebrew and Greek alike; it is the prepositional phrase “by faith,” not the possessive, that does the justifying work. Even the Masoretic “by his faithfulness” yields “the righteous lives by faith,” and even the Septuagint’s “by my faith” keeps “lives by faith” at its centre. So the dropped pronoun sharpens the reading — freeing “faith” from a possessor — without founding it; a reduced form of Paul’s point survives on any of the three received wordings. This is the opposite pole from Psalm 16:10, where the diverging Greek word is the empirical proof.4

  3. Jerome and Aquinas call Paul “Septuagintal,” but the modern datum is finer. Both Fathers read Paul as siding with the Septuagint against the Hebrew — and half of that is right (Paul’s word order and his lean away from the Hebrew “his” are Greek-ward). But Jerome, quoting Paul, actually gives the clause pronoun-less (“Iustus ex fide vivet”) while calling it the Septuagint — so even the ancient champion of the Hebrew transmits the trimmed Pauline form under a Septuagint label. The exact modern finding is therefore neither “Paul quoted the Septuagint” (he dropped its μου) nor “Paul quoted the Hebrew” (he dropped its “his” and kept the Greek word order): Paul quoted a Septuagintal base freely abridged. Jerome’s scribal waw/yôd explanation of the Hebrew-vs.-Greek fork is a separate, sound philological observation about how the Old-Testament traditions split, and does not bear on Paul’s further omission.2

  4. Keep the text-form question apart from the soteriology. “Whose faith does Habakkuk mean, and how does ‘the righteous shall live by faith’ ground justification?” is a large, separate debate — the possessive Paul removed is exactly the pivot of that theological dispute (God’s faithfulness, the believer’s faith, or, on some readings, the Messiah’s faithfulness). This page does not adjudicate it. What is a text-form fact is only this: Paul’s wording leaves the question open by dropping the pronoun, where the Hebrew and the Greek had each closed it in opposite directions. That the omission has such theological convenience is evidence that it is deliberate (refinement 2), not evidence for any one soteriology.4

Assessment

Sound. The interpretation this argument serves — that Paul’s “the just shall live by faith” at Romans 1:17 and Galatians 3:11 reproduces neither the Masoretic “by his faithfulness” nor the Septuagint’s “by my faith” exactly, and that the one verse is quoted in three divergent forms across Romans, Galatians, and Hebrews — plainly holds; the citation is correctly tagged neither / free, and it is the doctrine’s most economical single proof of a plural text, because the divergence lies within the New Testament. Kept to what the evidence shows, three things fix the finding’s bounds: (a) Paul’s form is an abridged Septuagintal one — the Greek word order with the possessive removed — not a fresh translation of the Hebrew, since no Old-Greek copy lacks the pronoun entirely; (b) the omission is exploited, not load-bearing — “by faith” carries the argument in every text-form, so the dropped pronoun sharpens Paul’s absolute “faith” without which the argument would still stand (as at Psalm 40:6 and Amos 9:12, and unlike Psalm 16:10, where the Greek word is the proof); and (c) the omission is best read as Paul’s own deliberate abridgement — unanimous across two letters, exactly fitted to his justification-by-faith reading — so “Paul quoted the Septuagint” (the ancient verdict of Jerome and Aquinas) is only half the finding: he quoted a Septuagintal base freely trimmed. Stated so, the row is a clean instance of the New Testament handling one verse in more than one shape; stretched into “Paul followed a lost pronoun-less Greek text” (unattested) or “Paul’s doctrine of justification rests on the omission” (the clause survives the pronoun either way), it overreaches. The separate soteriological question — whose faith — is left where it belongs.
