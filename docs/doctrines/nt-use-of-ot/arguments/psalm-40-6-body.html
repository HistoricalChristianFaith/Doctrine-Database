Psalm 40:6 at Hebrews 10:5: a Septuagintal “body,” and does the argument depend on it?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: sound — the catalog's LXX-distinctive tag on this row holds. Hebrews 10:5 quotes Psalm 40:6 as “a body hast thou prepared me” (Septuagint σῶμα δὲ κατηρτίσω μοι) where the Hebrew reads “mine ears hast thou opened” (literally “ears thou hast dug for me,” אָזְנַיִם כָּרִיתָ לִּי) — and the author reuses the Greek word in his own application: “we are sanctified through the offering of the body of Jesus Christ” (Hebrews 10:10). So the citation cannot have come from the Hebrew as we have it, and the row is genuinely LXX-distinctive. Two refinements keep the tag honest: like Isaiah 7:14 this is a Greek rendering of a stable, identical Hebrew — no Hebrew manuscript or Qumran witness reads anything but “ears,” so the Septuagint here glosses the Hebrew rather than preserving an older Hebrew the Masoretes lost (unlike Hebrews 1:6 or Goliath's height); and the doctrinal weight of Hebrews' argument rests on the antithesis it shares with the Hebrew — “sacrifice thou wouldest not … I come to do thy will” — so the Greek σῶμα is exploited (a bridge to “the offering of the body,” 10:10) rather than the foundation the argument would collapse without.

Related arguments: the Septuagint's interpretive gloss — see also Isaiah 7:14 at Matthew 1:23 — is the “virgin” a Septuagintal gloss, and does Matthew depend on it?

This page adds the piece those do not carry: the text-form adjudication of the catalog row itself.

The claim

“… the things pertaining to the quotation have become unambiguous as far as I am concerned. Changing it he speaks in this way about the person of Christ, saying, ‘I will establish a body’ instead of ‘I will establish ears.’”

— Theodore of Mopsuestia, Fragments on the Epistle to the Hebrews 10.5 (c. 400), stating the divergence outright — the apostle “changing it” reads “body” where the Psalm's Hebrew has “ears.”1 Eight centuries later Thomas Aquinas, expounding the Psalm, records both readings in the same breath: “‘But you have fashioned ears for me.’ Another reading has, ‘a body.’ And thus the Apostle introduces it in Heb. 10: ‘But a body you have prepared for me.’” The interpretation this argument serves is the catalog's classification: that Hebrews' citation is LXX-distinctive — driven by the Greek text-form, and inseparable from it at the point of quotation.1

Who advances it

That Hebrews 10:5 follows the Greek against the Hebrew is not a modern discovery: the ancient expositors saw it plainly and simply built on the Greek. Augustine reads “a Body hast thou perfected for me” and hangs a whole eucharistic exposition on the word — “What is that which has been given as its fulfilment? That Body” — while Chrysostom, Theophylact, Oecumenius, and Photius all quote the verse as “a body thou hast prepared / fitted for me” without a flicker of the Hebrew “ears.” Theodore of Mopsuestia and Aquinas (above) go further and name the substitution. In the modern text-critical surveys the same fact is a flat classification, not a marvel: Archer & Chirichigno place Hebrews 10:5–7 among the quotations that follow the Septuagint where it deviates from the Hebrew, and the standard quotation tables (Kalvesmaki; the loci citati of the Nestle–Aland / UBS Greek New Testament) list it the same way. The claim weighed here is that flat, text-form claim.2

The evidence for

Hebrews reproduces the Greek word, not a rendering of the Hebrew. The Hebrew of Psalm 40:6 reads “ears thou hast dug for me” — the verb kārāh is “to dig, hollow out,” the King James smoothing it to “mine ears hast thou opened.” Hebrews 10:5 instead has “a σῶμα (body) hast thou prepared me,” which is the Septuagint's rendering of the line (LXX Psalm 39:7, σῶμα δὲ κατηρτίσω μοι), not a fresh translation of the Hebrew. No literal handling of “ears … dug” yields “body … prepared”; the quotation is the Greek text, verbatim.1

The author weaves the Greek word into his own conclusion. Hebrews does not merely cite σῶμα and pass on; he picks it up at the climax of the paragraph: “we are sanctified through the offering of the body (σώματος) of Jesus Christ once for all” (Hebrews 10:10). The Greek reading is therefore not incidental packaging; the author has read the Psalm's “body” as the incarnate body offered on the cross, and the word travels from proof-text to application. This is what makes the row a live, load-bearing instance of following the Greek, not an inert coincidence.3

The Hebraizing Greek tradition confirms that “body” is the Septuagint's distinctive reading. Where the great Greek Psalters (Vaticanus, Sinaiticus, Alexandrinus) read σῶμα, the witnesses that pull the Greek back toward the Hebrew read ὠτία (“ears”) — and Jerome's Psalterium iuxta Hebraeos, translated straight from the Hebrew, has aures (“ears”), against the corpus (“body”) of his Gallican Psalter made from the Greek. The split is exactly the pattern of a Septuagint-distinctive reading: “body” on the Old Greek side of the line, “ears” on the Hebrew side, and Hebrews on the Greek side.4

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The catalog's classification is correct. Hebrews' proof-text is the Septuagint's Greek, the word σῶμα is reproduced verbatim and then reused in the author's own conclusion, and no literal rendering of the Hebrew “ears … dug” would deliver it. This is a genuine case of the New Testament following the Greek where the Greek diverges from the plain Hebrew — not an inert agreement and not a free paraphrase. The row belongs where the seeded catalog put it.

What must be refined, so the tag is not misread.

  1. This is a gloss over a stable, identical Hebrew, not an older Hebrew the Greek preserved. The “LXX-distinctive” bucket hides a fork that matters for the wider “which text?” question, and this row sits on the same side of it as Isaiah 7:14, not the same side as Hebrews 1:6. In the older-Hebrew cases the Greek diverges from the Masoretic Text because it rests on a genuinely different Hebrew now recovered at Qumran (so it is Hebrew-over-Hebrew). Psalm 40:6 is not that: the Hebrew “ears thou hast dug” is textually stable — the Masoretic Text, and every Hebrew witness known, reads אָזְנַיִם (“ears”); there is no variant Hebrew to recover. The whole divergence lives at the level of the Greek. So the honest sub-tag is LXX-gloss, not older-Hebrew; whoever files this row with Hebrews 1:6 has conflated two different phenomena.4

  2. How the Greek got “body” is debated — but both explanations are Greek-internal. Two accounts compete, and neither reaches behind the Greek to a different Hebrew. (a) An interpretive rendering. “Ears thou hast dug for me” is a Hebrew idiom for being given the capacity — and so the disposition — to hear and obey (cf. “the Lord God hath opened mine ear,” Isaiah 50:5); a translator could render the part for the whole, the fashioned organ of obedience generalized to the fashioned obedient person — “a body thou hast prepared me,” i.e. thou hast fitted me to do thy will. On this reading “body” is a defensible paraphrase, not an error. (b) An early inner-Greek variant. The Old Greek may have read ὠτία (“ears”), with σῶμα arising by scribal accident: the preceding word ends in sigma (ἤθέλησας), and …ΗΘΕΛΗΣΑΣ ΩΤΙΑ could be re-divided and smoothed to the sense-word ΣΩΜΑ. The manuscript picture — the great uncials carrying σῶμα, the Hebraizing witnesses and Jerome's Hebrew Psalter carrying “ears” — is most economically read as “body” already standing in the Greek Psalter the author of Hebrews used; a minority holds instead that Christian scribes later conformed the Greek Psalter to Hebrews. Either way, the divergence is a fact about the Greek tradition, not about the Hebrew.5

  3. Hebrews exploits the Greek word; it does not rest its case on the divergence. Here the row differs from Isaiah 7:14, where the proof stands or falls with the single word — delete παρθένος and there is no argument. The spine of Hebrews 10:5–9 is the antithesis between the cult and obedience: “Sacrifice and offering thou wouldest not … then said I, Lo, I come … to do thy will, O God. … He taketh away the first, that he may establish the second” — and that antithesis is present identically in the Hebrew, whose “ears thou hast dug” is itself an image of readied obedience. The Greek σῶμα hands the author a convenient bridge from the Psalm to his conclusion about “the offering of the body of Jesus Christ” (10:10), and he takes it — but the theology of Christ's obedient self-offering superseding the sacrifices does not hang on the variant. So the lead framing that “Hebrews' whole argument leans on the Greek reading” overstates it: the citation is LXX-distinctive and the word is really used, yet the doctrine is not built on the divergence the way it is at Isaiah 7:14.6

Assessment

Sound. The interpretation this argument serves — that Hebrews 10:5 is an LXX-distinctive quotation, reproducing and then reusing the Septuagint's σῶμα where the Greek diverges from the Hebrew “ears” — holds. The author quotes the Greek word verbatim and carries it into his conclusion at 10:10; no literal rendering of the Hebrew supplies it; the Hebraizing Greek tradition and Jerome's Hebrew Psalter read “ears” on the other side of the line. The row is correctly tagged. What the tag must be trusted to mean, and no more, is fixed by three refinements: (a) this is the Septuagint glossing a stable, identical Hebrew — “ears thou hast dug” is the same in the Masoretic Text and every Hebrew witness known, so unlike Hebrews 1:6 or Goliath's height there is no older Hebrew here for the Greek to have preserved; (b) how the Greek reached “body” — whether by interpretive synecdoche (the fashioned obedient person for the fashioned organ of obedience) or by an early inner-Greek scribal variant from ὠτία — is debated, but on both accounts the divergence is Greek-internal, not Hebrew-based; and (c) Hebrews exploits σῶμα (a bridge to “the offering of the body,” 10:10) rather than resting its case on it, since the load-bearing antithesis — “sacrifice thou wouldest not … I come to do thy will” — is shared by the Hebrew. Kept to that, the classification is a clean instance of the New Testament following the Greek against a literal Hebrew; stretched into “the Greek preserves an older Hebrew here” or “the epistle's whole doctrine hangs on the variant,” it overreaches. This row and Isaiah 7:14 are the two worked instances of the same species — a distinctively Septuagintal reading of a stable Hebrew, pressed by the Church — as against the older-Hebrew cases the Dead Sea Scrolls recovered.
