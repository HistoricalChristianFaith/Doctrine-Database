Isaiah 28:11–12 at 1 Corinthians 14:21: “with men of other tongues” — did Paul quote a revised, Hebraizing Greek, or render the Hebrew himself?

Disputed reading · on Which text did the New Testament quote? · witnesses: Origen · Jerome

Related readings: Isaiah 25:8 at 1 Corinthians 15:54 · Matthew’s formula quotations · 1 Corinthians 2:9

The passage

“Brethren, be not children in understanding: howbeit in malice be ye children, but in understanding be men. In the law it is written, With men of other tongues and other lips will I speak unto this people; and yet for all that will they not hear me, saith the Lord. Wherefore tongues are for a sign, not to them that believe, but to them that believe not…”

— 1 Corinthians 14:20–22 (KJV), c. AD 55, citing Isaiah 28:11–12 as “the law” (Paul’s regular name for the whole Old Testament).1

The question

The Masoretic Isaiah 28:11 reads כִּי בְּלַעֲגֵי שָׂפָה וּבְלָשׁוֹן אַחֶרֶת יְדַבֵּר אֶל־הָעָם הַזֶּה (“For with stammering lips and another tongue he will speak to this people”), with the LORD as speaker. The Old Greek reads διὰ φαυλισμὸν χειλέων, διὰ γλώσσης ἑτέρας, ὅτι λαλήσουσιν τῷ λαῷ τούτῳ (“through the contempt of lips, through another tongue, because they will speak to this people”), taking laʿag as “mocking” and the verb as plural. Paul writes ἐν ἑτερογλώσσοις καὶ ἐν χείλεσιν ἑτέρων λαλήσω τῷ λαῷ τούτῳ, καὶ οὐδ᾽ οὕτως εἰσακούσονταί μου, λέγει κύριος, with God again the speaker (“I will speak”), in wording that matches neither received text verbatim.1 The dispute, which goes back to Origen and Jerome, is what stands behind that wording: a Greek text of Isaiah already revised toward the Hebrew, or Paul’s own rendering of the Hebrew.

A Hebraizing Greek text of the kind Aquila later carried

On this reading Paul quoted a Greek Isaiah already corrected toward the Hebrew, from the same stream of revision that later produced Aquila’s version.

Held by: Origen (Greek, Commentary on 1 Corinthians and Philocalia, c. 240); Jean Héring (1962); Otto Michel (Paulus und seine Bibel); Hans Conzelmann (1975).2,3

Origen, commenting on the verse, wrote that Paul “is quoting Isaiah [28:11] according to Aquila’s translation, and not according to the Septuagint,” and in the Philocalia: “for I found the equivalent of this phrase lying in the translation of Aquila” (ἐν τῇ τοῦ Ἀκύλου ἑρμηνείᾳ). Where the Old Greek has the abstract φαυλισμὸν χειλέων and γλώσσης ἑτέρας, Paul has the personal ἑτερογλώσσοις (“men of other tongues”) and χείλεσιν ἑτέρων (“lips of others”), closer to the Hebrew’s “stammerers of lip.” Michel, noting that ἑτερογλώσσοις is found in no other Greek translation of the verse, held that it “must derive directly from Aquila’s Vorlage.” Héring allowed that Paul is either quoting from memory or “dependent upon an ancient translation of Isaiah in Greek which Aquila used also,” and Conzelmann leaned to the latter, warning against concluding that Paul is “simply altering the text freely.”3

Aquila worked c. AD 130, after Paul, so the reading does not have Paul using Aquila’s version. The Hebraizing revision of the Greek Bible, the kaige stream that Aquila carried further, is attested before Paul in the Greek Minor Prophets scroll from Naḥal Ḥever, dated palaeographically c. 50 BC–AD 50. On this reading a Greek Isaiah already pulled toward the Hebrew stood behind both Paul (c. AD 55) and Aquila, and 1 Corinthians 14:21 quotes that Greek text.4

Paul’s own rendering of the Hebrew, shaped to his argument

On this reading Paul worked from the Hebrew sense himself, from memory or by fresh rendering, and fitted the wording to the point he was making about tongues.

Held by: Jerome (Latin, Commentary on Isaiah, c. 410); H. B. Swete (1914); Gordon D. Fee (1987); Christopher D. Stanley (Paul and the Language of Scripture, 1992).5,6

Jerome, expounding Isaiah 28, judged Paul’s “in other tongues and with other lips” to be “taken from the present chapter according to the Hebrew,” adding: “Wherever something is said from the Old Testament, we do not follow the Septuagint, but the Hebrew.”5 Swete took the quotation to be “probably from memory… but the Apostle’s knowledge of the original has enabled him to improve upon the faulty rendering of the LXX.” Stanley calls the relation of Paul’s wording to the Septuagint “one of the greatest challenges in the entire corpus of Pauline citations,” since it stands far from both the Old Greek and the Hebrew, and his study traces such forms to Paul’s own “interpretive renderings”; Fee reads the citation as Paul adapting the text to his point.6

Paul argues that tongues are a sign to those who do not believe, so he needs God as the speaker and the stress on Israel’s refusal. His departures from both text-forms supply exactly that: the first-person λαλήσω (“I will speak”), the emphatic οὐδ᾽ οὕτως (“not even so”) for the Hebrew’s bare “yet,” the added μου (“hear me”), the future tense for the Hebrew’s past “they would not hear,” and the omission of the verse’s positive half (“This is the rest…”). Conzelmann too credits the first person and the “even so” to Paul. On this reading the citation is Paul’s own reading of the Hebrew, set in the words his argument in 14:22 required.6
