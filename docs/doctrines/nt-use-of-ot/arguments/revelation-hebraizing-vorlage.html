Does Revelation’s Old Testament wording reflect a Hebraizing Vorlage or the Septuagint? (the book-level case)

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — the thesis that the Revelation of John renders its Old-Testament matter from a Hebrew (or Aramaic) original rather than from the Greek Septuagint is genuinely and independently advocated — historically the majority view — and it rests on real data: Revelation’s Greek is drenched in Semitisms, and its two cleanest near-quotations include one that sides with the Hebrew against the Old Greek (Revelation 1:7’s “whom they pierced,” Zechariah 12:10). But it is undercut just as sharply from within the same author: Revelation 2:26–27 (repeated at 12:5 and 19:15) follows the Septuagint of Psalm 2:9 (“shepherd” for the Hebrew “break”), and a sustained methodological critique (Daryl Schmidt) shows that much of the alleged “Semitic syntax” is itself drawn from the language of the Septuagint. The strongest single leg — John’s Daniel 7:13 allusions tracking Theodotion against the Old Greek — makes the Hebraizing effect real but locates it in a revised Greek, not a Hebrew autograph, so it tells against the strong “translated-from-Hebrew” form even as it vindicates the tilt. One author carrying an MT-distinctive datum and an LXX-distinctive datum side by side is why the honest resting point is Steve Moyise’s: John knew and used both Greek and Semitic forms, and the majority “Semitic-preference” reading “remains unproven.” Genuinely disputed, strong by one discipline’s lights and weak by another’s — contested.

See also: this is the third of the doctrine’s book-level arguments, the companion to “Does the Epistle to the Hebrews argue from a Greek Old Testament?” (assessment sound) and “Does the Epistle to the Romans argue from a Greek Old Testament?” (assessment sound) — but its verdict runs the other way, because where Hebrews reasons uniformly from the Greek and Romans predominantly so, Revelation splits the difference. The two near-quotations it turns on are tagged and set in the whole New-Testament picture in the catalog; the big-picture thesis that the New Testament leans Greek — and the deflation of the “340 of 386” headline — is on “did the New Testament authors quote the Septuagint?” (assessment sound).

The claim

“While he writes in Greek, he thinks in Hebrew…. He never quotes the Old Testament according to the Septuagint…; he translates directly from the Old Testament text.”

— R. H. Charles, summarizing the position his Critical and Exegetical Commentary on the Revelation of St. John (ICC, 1920) argues at length: that the Seer did not borrow an existing Greek version but rendered the Hebrew (and, for Torrey and Trudinger, an Aramaic) text for himself, so that Revelation’s Old-Testament wording is a window onto a Semitic Vorlage, not onto the Septuagint.1 The interpretation this argument serves is the book-level claim built on that observation: that where the Greek and Hebrew Old Testaments diverge, Revelation stands with the Hebrew, the mirror image of the verdict Hebrews and Romans return.

Who advances it

It was, for most of the twentieth century, the consensus. R. H. Charles’s ICC (1920) made the strong case — the author “translates directly from the Old Testament text” and “does not use” the Greek version — and C. G. Ozanne (“The Language of the Apocalypse,” Tyndale Bulletin 16 [1965] 3–9) argued the linguistic half: that while many of Revelation’s Semitisms could be paralleled as Aramaisms or Septuagintalisms, “the only source from which every one can be paralleled is the classical Hebrew of the Old Testament,” and that their deliberate character points to a Hebrew mind, not an accident of translation-Greek.2 L. P. Trudinger pressed the point in a pair of articles (JTS 17 [1966] 82–88; NovT 14 [1972] 277–279), and — with C. C. Torrey — pushed the antecedent back to an Aramaic source; A. Vanhoye, A. T. Hanson, Jan Fekkes, Frederick Mazzaferri, and J. P. M. Sweet each read the OT texture of Revelation as owing more to the Hebrew than to the Old Greek. The Greek-leaning counter-tradition runs from H. B. Swete’s Apocalypse of St John (1906), which found the Seer’s allusions closer to the Septuagint than Charles allowed, to Daryl D. Schmidt’s pointed methodological critique (“Semitisms and Septuagintalisms in the Book of Revelation,” NTS 37 [1991] 592–603). Steve Moyise’s The Old Testament in the Book of Revelation (JSNTSup 115, 1995) is the modern mediating default: John used both, and the majority Semitic-preference verdict “remains unproven.”3

The evidence for

The Greek is written by a Semitic hand. Revelation’s Greek famously breaks concord and idiom in ways that scan as Hebrew or Aramaic laid under a Greek surface — the “grace and peace from he who is” (Revelation 1:4, an indeclinable divine title rendering the Hebrew “YHWH”), the resumptive pronouns, the casus pendens constructions, the “wondered after the beast” (13:3) that reproduces a Hebrew preposition. Ozanne’s claim is that this is not random solecism but sustained Hebraic grammar, and that a writer whose syntax is Hebrew is likeliest to have handled his Old-Testament matter the same way — carrying the Hebrew across himself rather than reaching for a Greek crib.2

Zechariah 12:10 → Revelation 1:7: “whom they pierced.” The cleanest text-form datum runs the Semitic way. Revelation 1:7 alludes to Zechariah 12:10 with “they … which pierced him” (ἐξεκέντησαν), tracking the Hebrew dāqarû against the Old Greek’s wholly divergent “they danced/mocked” (κατωρχήσαντο, reading rāqadû). This is the very testimonium John 19:37 carries and Justin Martyr repeats (Dialogue with Trypho 14, 32) — a fixed, early “pierced” reading of the proto-Theodotion type, MT-distinctive against the Old Greek.4

Daniel 7:13 → Revelation 1:7: “with the clouds.” The strongest single leg is the Danielic one. Revelation’s dense allusions to Daniel 7:13 — the “one like a son of man” who comes “with the clouds of heaven” (μετὰ τῶν νεφελῶν, so 1:7) — align with the Hebraizing Greek of Theodotion (and the proto-Theodotion / kaige revision that preceded it) against the Old Greek’s “upon the clouds” (ἐπὶ τῶν νεφελῶν). G. K. Beale’s study of John’s use of Daniel documented this Theodotionic pattern across the book. It is powerful evidence that Revelation’s Old-Testament forms are not smooth Old-Greek Septuagint — but note what it actually shows, because it also constrains the thesis (see below): the Hebraizing effect here is carried by a revised Greek text, not necessarily by John’s own translation from a Hebrew scroll.5

Adversarial assessment

What holds. Revelation is emphatically not a book that leans on the smooth Old Greek the way Hebrews does. Its Semitisms are real and pervasive; its sharpest christological allusion (Zech 12:10 at 1:7) is MT-distinctive; and its Daniel matter tracks a Hebraizing revised Greek against the Old Greek. Anyone who imagined Revelation as another Hebrews — theology done out of a Greek Bible — is corrected by the data. To that extent the tradition from Charles to Ozanne to Trudinger caught something true: the book’s Old-Testament texture tilts Semitic, and the burden is on anyone who would call it simply “Septuagintal.”

What is overstated or imprecise. The strong form — that the Seer “never quotes according to the Septuagint” and “translates directly from the Hebrew” throughout — does not survive its own author, on three counts.

  1. An LXX-distinctive datum in the same hand. At Revelation 2:26–27 — repeated at 12:5 and 19:15 — the one who will “rule” (ποιμανεῖ, shepherd) the nations “with a rod of iron” follows the Septuagint of Psalm 2:9, which read the unpointed Hebrew consonants as rāʿāh (“shepherd”) where the Masoretic Text vocalizes rāʿaʿ (“break them,” confirmed by the parallel “dash in pieces like a potter’s vessel”). This is not a stray echo: John uses it three times as a load-bearing image of messianic rule. One author cannot be quoting exclusively from the Hebrew when his most-repeated Psalter allusion is the Septuagint’s distinctive reading.6

  2. “Semitisms” can be Septuagintalisms. Daryl Schmidt’s methodological counter is the sharpest blow to the strong thesis: much of the “Semitic syntax” adduced for a Hebrew original is the syntax of the Septuagint itself, which had already rendered Hebrew idiom into a recognizable biblical-Greek register. A Greek-writing author steeped in the Greek scriptures could imitate that register — deliberately writing biblical-sounding Greek — without any Hebrew crib, and (with Max Wilcox) that is the more parsimonious explanation than a reconstructed Hebrew autograph. On this account John’s Hebraisms testify to a mind saturated in scriptural Greek, which is not the same as translating from Hebrew.3

  3. The best leg locates the effect in a revised Greek, not a Hebrew scroll. The Daniel–Theodotion pattern is the strongest evidence that Revelation is not Old-Greek-dependent — but it is evidence for a Hebraizing Greek (proto-Theodotion / kaige), the early Jewish revision that had already pulled the Greek back toward the Hebrew, not for John translating Hebrew for himself. That distinction is fatal to the strong “Vorlage” claim while confirming the weaker “not smooth Septuagint” one: the Hebraizing is real, and it is Greek. This is also why the thesis should not be hung on G. K. Beale by name — his Daniel work supplies the Theodotionic mechanism, but on the book-level question he sits nearer the Greek-leaning side, not with Charles.5

Put the three together and the picture is Moyise’s: John draws on both a Semitic (Hebrew and/or Aramaic and/or Hebraizing-Greek) stream and the Greek scriptures, and the two cleanest data points — the MT-distinctive Zechariah 12:10 and the LXX-distinctive Psalm 2:9 — sit in the same author, pointing opposite ways. The majority Semitic-preference verdict is not refuted so much as it is unproven; the Greek-only mirror image is refuted outright by 1:7. That is a genuine standoff, not a settled result.3

Assessment

Contested. The interpretation this argument serves — that Revelation’s Old-Testament wording reflects a Hebraizing/Semitic Vorlage rather than the Septuagint — is genuinely and independently advocated, was for most of the twentieth century the majority view, and rests on real data (the pervasive Semitisms, the MT-distinctive “pierced” of 1:7, the Theodotionic Daniel allusions). It is strong by the lights of Semitic-syntax study and weak by the lights of Septuagint text-criticism, and no clear winner emerges — the textbook shape of a contested verdict. What must be trusted, and no more: the negative half is secure (Revelation is not a smooth-Septuagint book the way Hebrews is), but the positive strong form — that John “translated directly from the Hebrew” and “never” used the Greek — overreaches, defeated by his own thrice-used Septuagintal Psalm 2:9, by Schmidt’s demonstration that the Semitisms can be Septuagintalisms, and by the fact that his best Hebraizing leg (Daniel) runs through a revised Greek, not a Hebrew autograph. The honest resting point is Moyise’s blend: John used both, and the Semitic-preference reading “remains unproven.” The book’s value for the wider “which text?” question is exactly this internal split — one author carrying an MT-distinctive and an LXX-distinctive reading at once is the catalog’s tightest single proof that the first-century Old Testament was textually plural.
