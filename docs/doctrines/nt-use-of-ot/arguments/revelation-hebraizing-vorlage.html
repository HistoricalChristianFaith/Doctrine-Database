Does Revelation’s Old Testament wording reflect a Hebraizing Vorlage or the Septuagint?

Disputed reading · on Which text did the New Testament quote? · witness: the Revelation of John

Related readings: Hebrews and its Greek Old Testament · Romans and the Greek Old Testament · Zechariah 12:10 at John 19:37

The passage

“Behold, he cometh with clouds; and every eye shall see him, and they also which pierced him: and all kindreds of the earth shall wail because of him. Even so, Amen.”

“And he shall rule them with a rod of iron; as the vessels of a potter shall they be broken to shivers: even as I received of my Father.”

— Revelation 1:7 and 2:27 (KJV), alluding to Zechariah 12:10 with Daniel 7:13, and to Psalm 2:9.1

The question

Revelation alludes to the Old Testament constantly but seldom quotes it formally, and its Greek breaks concord and idiom in ways that read as Hebrew or Aramaic beneath a Greek surface. Its two cleanest near-quotations point different ways. At 1:7 “they… which pierced him” (ἐξεκέντησαν) follows the Hebrew dāqarû of Zechariah 12:10 against the Old Greek’s “they danced/mocked.” At 2:26–27, repeated at 12:5 and 19:15, the one who will “rule” (ποιμανεῖ, shepherd) the nations with a rod of iron follows the Septuagint of Psalm 2:9, which read the consonants as rāʿāh (“shepherd”) where the Masoretic Text has rāʿaʿ (“break”).1 The same verse 1:7 has “with the clouds” (μετὰ τῶν νεφελῶν) of Daniel 7:13, as in the Hebraizing Greek of Theodotion, where the Old Greek has “upon the clouds.”2 The dispute is whether John drew his Old Testament from the Greek scriptures, from a Hebrew (or Aramaic) text he rendered himself, or from both.

The Greek scriptures, in a biblical-Greek register

On this reading John worked from the Greek scriptures, and his Semitic-sounding Greek is the idiom of the Septuagint itself.

Held by: H. B. Swete (The Apocalypse of St John, 1906); Daryl D. Schmidt (New Testament Studies, 1991).3

Swete found the Seer’s allusions closer to the Septuagint than a Hebrew-only account allows. Schmidt’s study of “Semitisms and Septuagintalisms” argues that much of the syntax adduced for a Hebrew original is the syntax of the Septuagint, which had already carried Hebrew idiom into a recognizable biblical-Greek register; a writer steeped in the Greek scriptures could write in that register by imitation, and on Schmidt’s account that explains the evidence more simply than a reconstructed Hebrew source. The Psalm 2:9 “shepherd,” used three times as the book’s image of messianic rule, is the Septuagint’s own reading. Where John’s wording stands with the Hebrew against the Old Greek, the Greek tradition supplies it too: G. K. Beale’s study of John’s use of Daniel aligns the Danielic allusions, with their “with the clouds,” with the Hebraizing Greek of Theodotion and the earlier kaige revision. On this reading Revelation’s Old Testament is the Greek Bible of its day, the Old Greek and its revised forms, voiced by an author at home in scriptural Greek.2

A Semitic text rendered by the Seer himself

On this reading John thought in Hebrew and translated his Old Testament matter directly from a Hebrew (or Aramaic) text rather than borrowing a Greek version.

Held by: R. H. Charles (ICC, 1920); C. C. Torrey (1958, for an Aramaic source); C. G. Ozanne (Tyndale Bulletin, 1965); L. P. Trudinger (1966, 1972).4,5,6

“While he writes in Greek, he thinks in Hebrew…. He never quotes the Old Testament according to the Septuagint…; he translates directly from the Old Testament text.”

— R. H. Charles, Critical and Exegetical Commentary on the Revelation of St. John (ICC, 1920).4

Steve Moyise (1995) calls this position “the consensus view.”7 The evidence begins with the Greek: the “grace and peace from he who is” of 1:4, an indeclinable divine title rendering “YHWH”; resumptive pronouns; casus pendens; the “wondered after the beast” of 13:3, which reproduces a Hebrew preposition. Ozanne grants that many of these can be paralleled as Aramaisms or Septuagintalisms, but holds that “the only source from which every one can be paralleled is the classical Hebrew of the Old Testament,” and that their deliberate character points to a Hebrew mind; a writer whose syntax is Hebrew is, on this reading, a writer who carries his Old Testament across from the Hebrew himself. The “pierced” of 1:7 is the clearest text-form datum: it follows the Hebrew against the Old Greek, as do John 19:37 and Justin Martyr (Dialogue 14, 32). Trudinger, with Torrey, traced such readings to a Semitic text, Aramaic as well as Hebrew.6

Both Greek and Semitic sources

On this reading John drew on Greek and Semitic forms of the Old Testament alike, so no single source accounts for his wording.

Held by: Steve Moyise (The Old Testament in the Book of Revelation, 1995).7

Moyise concludes that John “knew and used both Greek and Semitic sources,” and that “the consensus view, that John preferred Semitic texts, remains unproven.” The book’s own data carry both strands at once: a Hebrew-aligned “pierced” in 1:7 and the Septuagint’s “shepherd” of Psalm 2:9 in 2:27, 12:5 and 19:15, from the same author. The Semitic stream may reach John as Hebrew, as Aramaic, or as a Greek already revised toward the Hebrew, and the Septuagint stream alongside it. On this reading Revelation draws on a textually plural Old Testament, taking its wording from whichever form lay to hand at each point.7
