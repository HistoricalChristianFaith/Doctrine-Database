Does the Epistle to the Hebrews reproduce its Greek Old Testament, or reshape it?

Disputed reading · on Which text did the New Testament quote? · witnesses: Eusebius of Caesarea · Theodore of Mopsuestia · John Chrysostom

Related readings: Hebrews 1:6: Deuteronomy 32:43 or Psalm 97:7? · Habakkuk 2:4 at Romans 1:17 and Galatians 3:11 · Romans and the Greek Old Testament

The passage

For yet a little while, and he that shall come will come, and will not tarry. Now the just shall live by faith: but if any man draw back, my soul shall have no pleasure in him.

— Hebrews 10:37–38 (KJV), quoting Habakkuk 2:3–4 in its Greek form, with the two clauses of verse 4 in reverse order.1

The question

Hebrews quotes the Old Testament more heavily than any other New Testament book, and its citations follow a Greek text-form. At several points its argument uses wording found in the Greek and not in the Hebrew: “a little lower than the angels” (Psalm 8:5, Hebrew “than God”) at Hebrews 2:7–9; the vocative “Lord” of Psalm 102:25, which the Hebrew lacks, at Hebrews 1:10; the angels as “spirits” of Psalm 104:4 at Hebrews 1:7; “a body hast thou prepared me” (Psalm 40:6, Hebrew “ears”) at Hebrews 10:5; and “the coming one” who must not “draw back” of Habakkuk 2:3–4, where the Hebrew has “his soul is puffed up, not upright in him.” From Chrysostom onward commentators have expounded these Greek proof-texts directly. The dispute is whether the author reproduced the wording of the Greek Bible before him, or reshaped it for his argument.2

The author arranging and changing his texts for the argument

On this reading the author of Hebrews worked the wording of his proof-texts himself, reordering and altering them to bring out what he saw in them.

Held by: Eusebius of Caesarea (Greek, Proof of the Gospel 6.14; d. 339); Theodore of Mopsuestia (Greek, Fragments on Hebrews, c. 400).3

Eusebius lays the epistle’s form of Habakkuk 2:3–4 beside the prophet’s and beside Aquila’s Greek rendering, and describes how “the epistle arranges what was obscure in the prophetic writing, because of the inversion of the clauses.” In the epistle’s order “my righteous one shall live by faith” comes first and is answered by its opposite, “if he draw back, my soul has no pleasure in him”; the participle becomes “the coming one” (ὁ ἐρχόμενος), a person who “shall come and shall not tarry.” The reworked oracle then drives the exhortation that follows: “we are not of them who draw back unto perdition, but of them that believe” (Hebrews 10:39).3

Theodore of Mopsuestia reads Hebrews 10:5 the same way, as a deliberate change made to speak of Christ:

“… the things pertaining to the quotation have become unambiguous as far as I am concerned. Changing it he speaks in this way about the person of Christ, saying, ‘I will establish a body’ instead of ‘I will establish ears.’”

The word then travels from proof-text to conclusion: “we are sanctified through the offering of the body of Jesus Christ once for all” (Hebrews 10:10). On this reading the reordered clauses and the chosen word are the author’s exegesis, shaping the prophetic text so that it speaks plainly of the Christ who came and offered his body.4

The author reproducing the Greek text before him

On this reading the author quoted his Greek Old Testament faithfully, and its distinctive readings stood in the copy he used.

Held by: Gareth Lee Cockerill (1999); Martin Karrer (2006); George H. Guthrie (in Beale & Carson, 2007); Gert J. Steyn (New Testament Studies, 2014).5

Karrer states the premise: “The number and the length of the quotations are outstanding in the New Testament…. Hebrews is the climax of New Testament citing.” Guthrie grades each citation by text-tradition (Hebrew, Septuagint or mixed) and finds the Greek dominant. Steyn’s study of the chapter-1 catena concludes that the author “reproduced his scriptural citations faithfully” from a Greek Vorlage; on his account the burden of proof lies with anyone who claims the author altered them.5

On this reading the divergences belong to the Greek tradition. The Greek Old Testament circulated in more than one form in the first century, some copies already revised toward the Hebrew, so a reading absent from today’s Septuagint manuscripts may have stood in the author’s. At Hebrews 1:6 Cockerill holds that the author quotes a Greek rendering of the Hebrew text preserved at Qumran in 4QDeutq, older than the Masoretic Deuteronomy. At Hebrews 10:5 the great Greek Psalters (Vaticanus, Sinaiticus, Alexandrinus) read “body”; Attridge and Ellingworth account for it within the Greek tradition, as an interpretive rendering of “ears thou hast dug” or an early inner-Greek variant of ὠτία, and Ellingworth weighs against the view that the Psalter was later conformed to Hebrews. The author then argues from the Bible the Greek-speaking church read, building each step on the words as they stood in it.6
