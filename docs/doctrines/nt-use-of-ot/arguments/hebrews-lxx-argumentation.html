Does the Epistle to the Hebrews argue from a Greek Old Testament? (the book-level case)

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: sound — the epistle to the Hebrews is the strongest single-book case that the New Testament reasons from a Greek Old Testament, not merely quotes one. It is the most quotation-saturated book in the New Testament, its citations reproduce a Greek text-form throughout, it shows no independent recourse to the Hebrew, and a whole chain of its arguments turns on wording that is distinctively Greek: the Son made “a little lower than the angels” (Psalm 8:5 LXX for Hebrew “than God”), the “body thou hast prepared me” of Psalm 40:6, the personalized “the coming one shall come” and the “if he draw back” antithesis of Habakkuk 2:3–4, and the vocative “Lord” supplied at Psalm 102:25. So the thesis holds at the book level. Three refinements keep it honest and stop it over-reaching: (1) the dependence is graded — in some places the Greek word is the proof (Ps 8, Ps 102, Hab 2), in others it is only exploited as a verbal bridge (Ps 40) while the logic sits on wording shared with the Hebrew; (2) “Septuagint-shaped” must not swallow the one older-Hebrew case — at Hebrews 1:6 the Greek preserves an older Hebrew now recovered at Qumran, so it is Hebrew-over-Hebrew, not Greek-over-Hebrew; and (3) “the Septuagint” the author used was a plural family of Greek texts, so the honest verdict is that each citation is decided on its own evidence, not by a blanket “LXX-only.”

This page adds what none of those carry: the book-level question — whether Hebrews’ argumentation, and not just its wording, is Greek-shaped. Its companion is “Does the Epistle to the Romans argue from a Greek Old Testament?” (assessment sound) — the same question put to the densest quotation-field in the Pauline corpus, and a deliberately harder case, since Romans (unlike Hebrews) does not come out uniformly Septuagintal.

The claim

“The number and the length of the quotations are outstanding in the New Testament…. Hebrews is the climax of New Testament citing.”

— Martin Karrer, The Epistle to the Hebrews and the Septuagint, stating the premise the thesis builds on: no other New-Testament book quotes the Old Testament so heavily — some two dozen or more formal citations, many of them long, plus a dense field of allusions — and every one is drawn from a Greek text.1 The interpretation this argument serves is the stronger claim built on that premise: that Hebrews is the New Testament’s clearest demonstration that its theology — the Son’s superiority to the angels, his incarnate body, his once-for-all self-offering, the endurance of faith — is developed out of the Greek form of its proof-texts, at points where that Greek diverges from the Hebrew.

Who advances it

The observation is ancient. The Fathers who expounded Hebrews built without hesitation on its Greek proof-texts: John Chrysostom reads the whole opening catena (Ps 104:4, Ps 45:6–7, Ps 102:25–27) straight off the Greek to distinguish the created angels from the uncreated Son, and Eusebius of Caesarea went so far as to analyze how Hebrews rearranges the clauses of Habakkuk 2:3–4 in their Greek form, comparing the epistle’s wording with Aquila’s rival Greek rendering.2 None of them treated the Greek as a problem to be reconciled with a Hebrew original; they simply reasoned from the text the church read. In modern scholarship the point has hardened into a near-consensus and a research programme: George H. Guthrie’s survey of Hebrews’ use of the Old Testament grades each citation by text-tradition (Hebrew, Septuagint, or mixed) and finds the Greek dominant; Radu Gheorghita and Susan Docherty have devoted monographs to the role of the Septuagint in the epistle; and Gert Steyn’s close study of the chapter-1 catena concludes that the author “reproduced his scriptural citations faithfully” from a Greek Vorlage, so that the burden of proof now rests on anyone claiming he altered them.3

The evidence for

The book quotes a Greek Bible, and only a Greek Bible. Hebrews never gives a sign of consulting the Hebrew behind its Greek. Where the two text-forms part company, the epistle stands with the Greek every time it can be tested, and it reproduces the Greek closely enough that its variations are now read as reflecting the state of his Greek manuscript rather than his own retranslation from the Hebrew. That is what makes it the strongest single-book case: not one striking verse but a sustained pattern across an unusually citation-heavy book.1

Psalm 8:4–6 → Hebrews 2:6–9: “a little lower than the angels.” The Hebrew of Psalm 8:5 says man was made “a little lower than God” (אֱלֹהִים, ʾelōhîm); the Septuagint renders it “a little lower than the angels” (παρὠ ἀγγέλους), and Hebrews follows the Greek. The rendering is load-bearing: it is the hinge that lets the author swing from the Son-versus-angels argument of chapter 1 into the Son’s temporary humiliation in chapter 2 — “we see Jesus, who was made a little lower than the angels… crowned with glory and honour” (Hebrews 2:9). Had the author read the Hebrew “lower than God,” the whole angelic frame that binds the two chapters together would have no proof-text. He also exploits the ambiguity of the Greek βραχύ τι, which can mean “a little” in degree or “for a little while” in time — reading the humiliation as a bounded episode ended by the crowning. Chrysostom already applies the verse “more properly to Christ,” and C. S. Lewis noticed the same double move: Christ “having been made (for a while) ‘lower than the angels.’”4

Habakkuk 2:3–4 → Hebrews 10:37–38: “the coming one” who will not “draw back.” Here the Greek does two things the Hebrew does not. First, the author adds the definite article to the Septuagint’s participle — ὁ ἐρχόμενος, “the coming one” — turning Habakkuk’s impersonal “it (the vision) will surely come” into a personal, messianic figure who “shall come and shall not tarry.” Second, he takes over the Septuagint’s “if he draw back (ἡν ἐποστείληται), my soul has no pleasure in him” — a faith-versus-apostasy antithesis the Hebrew (“his soul is puffed up, not upright in him”) does not yield — and reverses the order of the two clauses so that “my righteous one shall live by faith” is answered by its opposite. That reworked Greek is the engine of the epistle’s closing exhortation: “we are not of them who draw back unto perdition, but of them that believe” (Hebrews 10:39). The Hebrews form of the oracle is old: already c. AD 96 Clement of Rome quotes it in the epistle’s wording (“Speedily will He come, and will not tarry”), and two centuries later Eusebius laid the epistle’s clause-order beside the prophet’s and beside Aquila’s Greek to show how “the epistle arranges what was obscure in the prophetic writing.”2

The chapter-1 catena: Psalm 104:4 and Psalm 102:25–27 → Hebrews 1:7, 1:10–12. The opening string of proof-texts that establishes the Son’s deity leans on Greek renderings at two points. At Hebrews 1:7 the author needs the angels to be the changeable servants God “maketh… winds… a flame of fire” — a contrast with the enthroned Son of the next verse — and the Septuagint’s rendering of Psalm 104:4 (Hebrew ambiguous both ways: rûaḥ = wind/spirit, mal’āk = messenger/angel) secures exactly that reading. More strikingly, at Hebrews 1:10–12 the author addresses a creation-hymn to the Son — “Thou, Lord, in the beginning hast laid the foundation of the earth” — because the Septuagint of Psalm 102:25 supplies a vocative κύριε (“O Lord”) that the Hebrew lacks. That added address is what lets a psalm spoken to God be routed onto the Son, ascribing to him the divine name, the work of creation, and immutability. Chrysostom reads all of this straight off the Greek, noting that the author “appl[ies] to the Son the things which relate to the Father.”5

Psalm 40:6 → Hebrews 10:5: “a body thou hast prepared me.” The most famous instance is adjudicated on its own page: Hebrews quotes the Septuagint’s σῶμα (“body”) where the Hebrew reads “ears thou hast dug for me,” and carries the Greek word into his conclusion (“the offering of the body of Jesus Christ,” 10:10). It belongs in the tally — but it also illustrates the first refinement below, because there the Greek word is exploited as a bridge rather than being the foundation the argument would collapse without.6

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The core thesis is correct and, if anything, understated by treating any single verse as its centre. The strength of the Hebrews case is cumulative: an exceptionally citation-heavy book that draws exclusively on a Greek text-form, shows no independent recourse to the Hebrew, and repeatedly builds its distinctive arguments on Greek-specific wording — the “angels” of Psalm 8, the “the coming one” and “draw back” of Habakkuk 2, the vocative “Lord” of Psalm 102, the “body” of Psalm 40. No other New-Testament book concentrates so many text-form-dependent arguments in one place. That Hebrews reasons from a Greek Bible is not a conjecture; it is the plainest datum in the whole “which text?” question.

What must be refined, so the thesis is not over-read.

  1. The dependence is graded — distinguish the proof-word from the bridge-word. “Turns on the Greek” is not one thing. At Psalm 8, Psalm 102, and Habakkuk 2 the Greek reading is the argument: delete the “angels,” the vocative “Lord,” or the “draw back” antithesis and the specific exegetical move disappears. At Psalm 40:6 the case is weaker — the author uses σῶμα as a convenient verbal bridge to “the offering of the body” (10:10), but the spine of the passage (the cult-versus-obedience antithesis, “I come to do thy will”) is present in the Hebrew too, so the doctrine does not hang on the variant. A careful form of the thesis reports this gradient rather than flattening every case into “the theology depends on the Greek.”6

  2. “Septuagint-shaped” is not always “Greek-over-Hebrew.” The one place in Hebrews where the divergence has been tested against a Hebrew manuscript turns the simple story on its head. At Hebrews 1:6 the “let all God’s angels worship him” of Deuteronomy 32:43 is absent from the Masoretic Hebrew but present in the Septuagint — and in a Hebrew scroll from Qumran (4QDeutq). So there the Greek is not glossing the Hebrew; it is preserving an older Hebrew the Masoretes later dropped. The book’s Christology at that point is Greek-text-mediated but not Greek-invention-based. So “Hebrews reasons from a Greek Bible” is exactly right; “Hebrews’ theology rests on readings the Greek made up” is not — at least not everywhere, and demonstrably not here. This case is the honest counterweight, worked in full on its own page.7

  3. “The Septuagint” the author used was plural, and the verdict is case-by-case. There was no single fixed “LXX” in the first century; the Greek Old Testament circulated in divergent forms, some already revised back toward the Hebrew. Several Hebrews quotations diverge from all extant Septuagint manuscripts, which is why scholars split, verse by verse, over whether the author found a reading in his Greek copy or reworked it himself (Psalm 40:6’s “body” is the standing example). The mature scholarly position is therefore not a blanket “Hebrews used only the LXX” but a probabilistic, citation-by-citation judgement — and the wider “which text did the New Testament use?” picture is itself one of a pluriform text, not a duel between two fixed Bibles. The thesis survives this — the author still reasons from a Greek text-tradition throughout — but it should be stated as a family-of-Greek-texts claim, not a single-book claim.8

Assessment

Sound. The interpretation this argument serves — that Hebrews is the New Testament’s clearest demonstration that its argumentation, and not merely its wording, is shaped by a Greek Old Testament — holds on the cumulative evidence. It is the most quotation-saturated book in the canon; it draws its proof-texts from a Greek text-form throughout, with no sign of independent recourse to the Hebrew; and a chain of its signature arguments turns on distinctively Greek readings — the Son “lower than the angels” (Ps 8), “the coming one” who will not “draw back” (Hab 2), the vocative “Lord” addressed to the Son (Ps 102), the “body” offered once for all (Ps 40). What the thesis must be trusted to mean, and no more, is fixed by three refinements: (a) the dependence is graded — sometimes the Greek word is the proof (Ps 8, Ps 102, Hab 2), sometimes only a bridge the argument could survive without (Ps 40); (b) “Septuagint-shaped” is not “Greek-invented,” because at Hebrews 1:6 the Greek preserves an older Hebrew now attested at Qumran, so that case is Hebrew-over-Hebrew; and (c) “the Septuagint” was a plural family of Greek texts, so the honest verdict is reached citation by citation, not by a blanket “LXX-only.” Kept to that, Hebrews is the single strongest witness that the apostolic church did its theology in Greek, out of a Greek Bible; stretched into “every doctrine of the epistle hangs on a purely Greek invention,” it overreaches on both the graded-dependence and the older-Hebrew counts.
