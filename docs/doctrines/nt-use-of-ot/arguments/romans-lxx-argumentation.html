Does the Epistle to the Romans argue from a Greek Old Testament? (the book-level case)

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: sound — but with the verdict graded and two-sided, and that is the point. Romans carries the densest field of explicit Old-Testament quotation in the Pauline corpus — some fifty formal citations — and where the Greek and the Hebrew part ways it leans, predominantly, on a Greek text-form: a whole chain of Paul’s arguments turns on wording that is distinctively Septuagintal, and at the sharpest of them the Greek word is the proof — “shall not be ashamed” (Isaiah 28:16 LXX at Romans 9:33 / 10:11, where the Hebrew “make haste” would not serve), “their sound went into all the earth” (Psalm 19:4 at 10:18, where the Hebrew “line” would not prove a universal preaching), the “mind of the Lord” (Isaiah 40:13 at 11:34), “every tongue shall confess” (Isaiah 45:23 at 14:11). So the Septuagintal lean is real, and load-bearing. Yet Romans is a stronger test than Hebrews precisely because it does not come out uniformly Greek: it carries a genuine non-Septuagintal residue that rules out any “LXX-only” account. One citation stands with the Hebrew against a wholly different extant Greek (Job 41:11 at 11:35); three of Paul’s sharpest citations match neither received text (the pronoun-less “the just shall live by faith” of Habakkuk 2:4 at 1:17, the Deliverer “out of Zion” of Isaiah 59:20 at 11:26, the Targum-shaped “vengeance is mine” of Deuteronomy 32:35 at 12:19); and Paul freely conflates, abridges, and reapplies (the Romans 3:10–18 catena, the Deuteronomy-30 midrash at 10:6–8). So the honest book-level verdict is the doctrine’s own headline, instantiated in one book: Romans argues predominantly from a Greek text-form, with a real residue that keeps Paul plural, not Septuagint-only.

Its three non-Septuagintal legs are worked in full at Habakkuk 2:4 at Romans 1:17 and Isaiah 59:20 at Romans 11:26; the one clean Pauline case where the Greek word is genuinely load-bearing (the universalizing “all” of the curse) is Galatians 3:10–13 (assessment sound), and the sign that Paul’s “Septuagint” was itself plural is Isaiah 25:8 at 1 Corinthians 15:54 (a revised Greek). The big-picture thesis that the New Testament as a whole leans Greek — and the deflation of the “340 of 386” headline — is on “did the New Testament authors quote the Septuagint?” (assessment sound); the proof that a “Greek” reading can be an older Hebrew is Deuteronomy 32:43 at Hebrews 1:6 and, christologically neutral, Goliath’s height. This page adds what none of those carry: the book-level question — whether Romans’ argumentation, and not just its wording, is Greek-shaped, and how the answer stays honest about the residue.

The claim

“This follows the Septuagint text, for in our text, which follows the Hebrew truth, it says the just man lives by his faith.”

— Thomas Aquinas, Commentary on Romans, at Romans 1:17, opening the letter with the observation that turns into a refrain: Paul quotes a Greek Old Testament, and where it diverges from “our text” (the Hebrew-based Vulgate) Aquinas keeps flagging the fork — “the Apostle takes these words according to the Septuagint” (Romans 9:33), “the Apostle uses the Septuagint” (Romans 11:26), “our text has this… every tongue shall swear” where Paul reads “confess” (Romans 14:11).1 The interpretation this argument serves is the stronger, book-level claim built on that ancient observation: that Romans — Paul’s most sustained doctrinal argument, and his densest quotation-field — develops its case out of the Greek form of its proof-texts at the points where that Greek diverges from the Hebrew, so that the book reasons from a Greek Bible and not merely quotes one. The honest form of the claim, and the one worth testing, adds the qualifier the Hebrews case does not need: predominantly, with a real residue that runs the other way.

Who advances it

The observation is ancient, and it was made by readers who could see both texts. Origen, in his Commentary on Romans, collates Paul’s citations against the Hebrew verse by verse — at Romans 11:26 noting that Paul’s “out of Zion” stands where Isaiah has “for the sake of Zion,” and that Paul’s closing clause “is not written there at all, nor indeed among the Hebrews.” Jerome, reading from the Hebraica veritas, flags the same forks in situ in his commentaries on Isaiah (28:16’s “ashamed,” 40:13’s “mind,” 11:10’s “hope”) and on Habakkuk (the pronoun of 2:4). And Thomas Aquinas systematizes the whole into a recurring formula — “our text has this… but the Apostle uses the Septuagint” — at Romans 1:17, 9:33, 10:11, 11:26, 12:19, and 14:11, the most methodical ancient witness to Paul’s text-forks.1 None of them treated the Greek as illegitimate; each simply registered that Paul’s proof-texts ran with the Seventy. In modern scholarship the point has become a research programme: Christopher D. Stanley’s Paul and the Language of Scripture and Dietrich-Alex Koch’s Die Schrift als Zeuge des Evangeliums reconstruct Paul’s citation-technique and text-forms citation by citation, and Mark A. Seifrid’s survey of Romans grades each quotation by tradition; their converged result is that Paul’s Bible is fundamentally a Greek one, adapted freely, with a minority of citations that reach past it.2

The evidence for

Where the Greek word is the proof. The strongest form of the thesis is not that Romans happens to quote a Greek Bible but that some of Paul’s arguments would fail on the Hebrew. Twice the case is airtight. At Romans 9:33 and again at 10:11 Paul’s gospel-clinching “whosoever believeth on him shall not be ashamed” reproduces the Septuagint’s rendering of Isaiah 28:16, where the Hebrew reads “he that believeth shall not make haste” (lō’ yāḥîš) — a promise of vindicated faith that the Hebrew verb does not carry, and Aquinas notes the fork exactly (“the Apostle takes these words according to the Septuagint”). At Romans 10:18 Paul proves that the gospel has already gone out to all from Psalm 19:4 — “their sound (φθόγγος) went into all the earth” — where the Hebrew reads “their line” (qawwām, a measuring-cord): only the Greek, which read the cord as a voice, yields the universal preaching the argument needs. These are the Romans counterparts of the Hebrews cases where the reading is load-bearing, not decorative.3

The sustained Septuagintal lean across the letter. Beyond those two, a broad band of Romans’ citations follows the Greek at the point it diverges from the Hebrew. Paul asks “who hath known the mind (νοῦν) of the Lord?” (Romans 11:34, Isaiah 40:13 LXX for Hebrew “spirit,” rûaḥ); he clinches universal homage with “every tongue shall confess” (Romans 14:11, Isaiah 45:23 LXX for Hebrew “shall swear” — the fork Aquinas marks with “our text has this… every tongue shall swear”); he indicts the nations’ blasphemy with the Septuagint’s longer Isaiah 52:5 (“among the Gentiles… because of you,” Romans 2:24); he vindicates God with the Greek passive of Psalm 51:4 (“mightest overcome when thou art judged,” Romans 3:4, against the Hebrew active “when thou judgest”); he reads a saved remnant from “a remnant shall be saved” (Romans 9:27, Isaiah 10:22 LXX for Hebrew “shall return”); he keeps the Septuagintal vocative “Lord” of Isaiah 53:1 (Romans 10:16); and he grounds the Gentile mission of chapter 15 on the Greek of Isaiah 11:10 (“in him shall the Gentiles hope… he that shall rise to reign,” Romans 15:12, against the Hebrew “ensign… the nations shall seek”). No single one of these carries the letter, but their number is the point: Romans’ working Bible, tested again and again, is a Greek one.4

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The core thesis is correct: Romans predominantly argues from a Greek text-form, and does so at load-bearing points. Where Hebrews makes the case by saturation (a citation-heavy book that never once reaches past the Greek), Romans makes it by argumentative dependence at its hinges — the justification proof of “not ashamed,” the universal-preaching proof of “their sound,” the confession of “every tongue.” That Paul’s Bible is fundamentally Greek is not a conjecture; it is what the ancient collators (Origen, Jerome, Aquinas) and the modern monographs (Stanley, Koch, Seifrid) alike report.

What keeps it honest — and why Romans is the harder case. Three refinements, and the second is what a Romans synthesis must carry that a Hebrews one need not.

  1. The dependence is graded — distinguish the proof-word from the bridge-word. As with Hebrews, “turns on the Greek” is not one thing. At Isaiah 28:16 and Psalm 19:4 the Greek reading is the argument: delete the “ashamed” or the “sound” and the specific move disappears. But in most of the band above the Greek word is exploited as a serviceable bridge, not the foundation — the “mind” of Isaiah 40:13 makes the same point the Hebrew “spirit” would (God’s counsel is unsearchable), and the confession of “every tongue” is universal homage whether the verb is “confess” or “swear.” A careful thesis reports this gradient rather than flattening every Septuagintal citation into “the doctrine depends on the Greek.”4

  2. The non-Septuagintal residue is real — and it rules out “LXX-only.” This is where Romans diverges sharply from Hebrews. One citation stands with the Hebrew against a wholly different extant Greek: at Romans 11:35 Paul’s “who hath first given to him, and it shall be recompensed?” tracks the Hebrew of Job 41:11, where the Old Greek of Job runs quite differently — a doublet of the same phenomenon at 1 Corinthians 3:19 (Job 5:13), and a sign that Paul could reach the Hebrew directly (or a lost Greek Job) when he chose. And three of Paul’s sharpest citations match neither received text: the pronoun-less “the just shall live by faith” (Romans 1:17, an abridged Septuagintal form with the possessive both traditions carry removed), the Deliverer “out of Zion” (ἐκ Σιων, Romans 11:26, a preposition in no known copy of Isaiah, best read as Paul’s harmonization to the Psalter’s stock “out of Zion” formula), and the Targum-shaped “vengeance is mine” (Deuteronomy 32:35 at Romans 12:19, the very form Hebrews 10:30 also uses). Add Paul’s freedom — the abridged catena of Romans 3:10–18, the midrashic reworking of Deuteronomy 30 at Romans 10:6–8 (“the deep” for Deuteronomy’s “over the sea”) — and the picture is not a copyist reproducing a Greek codex but an apostle handling Scripture in more than one text-form, and often reshaping it himself. So “Romans reasons from a Greek Bible” is right; “Romans used only the Septuagint” is false, and demonstrably so.5

  3. “Septuagint-shaped” is not always “Greek-over-Hebrew” — but in Romans, uncharacteristically, it has no older-Hebrew foothold. The one refinement that rescued Hebrews from over-reading — that at Hebrews 1:6 the “Septuagintal” reading is really an older Hebrew now attested at Qumran (4QDeutq) — has, notably, no clean counterpart in Romans. Paul draws on the very same expanded Song of Moses at Romans 15:10, but he quotes the shared colon (“Rejoice, ye Gentiles, with his people,” present in both text-forms), not the fuller-text line the christological argument of Hebrews needs. So where Hebrews carries an honest Hebrew-over-Hebrew counterweight, Romans’ Septuagintal cases are — on the evidence surveyed — Greek renderings of a stable Hebrew (the “ashamed” and “sound” forks sit on identical consonants), which if anything sharpens the two-sided verdict: Romans’ Greek lean is not softened into “preserved old Hebrew,” and its counterweight is the MT-distinctive and match-neither residue, not an older-Hebrew rescue. Underneath both books lies the same fact: “the Septuagint” Paul used was a plural family of Greek texts — some already revised toward the Hebrew (the revised-Greek “victory” of 1 Corinthians 15:54 proves it) — so every citation is decided on its own evidence, not by a blanket “LXX-only.”6

Assessment

Sound — for the graded, two-sided claim, which is the honest one. Romans predominantly argues from a Greek text-form, and does so at load-bearing hinges: “not ashamed” (Isaiah 28:16), “their sound” (Psalm 19:4), the “mind of the Lord” (Isaiah 40:13), “every tongue shall confess” (Isaiah 45:23), a Septuagintal lean the ancient collators and the modern monographs alike report. What the thesis must be trusted to mean, and no more, is fixed by three refinements: (a) the dependence is graded — sometimes the Greek word is the proof (Isaiah 28:16, Psalm 19:4), sometimes only a bridge the argument could survive without (Isaiah 40:13, 45:23); (b) the residue is real and rules out “LXX-only” — Job 41:11 at 11:35 stands with the Hebrew, and Habakkuk 2:4, Isaiah 59:20, and Deuteronomy 32:35 match neither text, so Paul is plural, not Septuagint-only; and (c) “the Septuagint” was itself a plural family of Greek texts, so the verdict is reached citation by citation. Kept to that, Romans is the New Testament’s clearest demonstration that a book can argue from a Greek Bible and reach past it — the doctrine’s own headline (“predominantly Greek, but a plural text quoted plurally”) instantiated in the densest quotation-field in the Pauline corpus. Stretched into “Romans is a Septuagint letter through and through,” it over-reads the evidence on the residue count; deflated into “Paul just quoted whatever came to hand,” it under-reads the load-bearing Septuagintal hinges. The truth is the graded middle, and it is why Romans is the stronger test.
