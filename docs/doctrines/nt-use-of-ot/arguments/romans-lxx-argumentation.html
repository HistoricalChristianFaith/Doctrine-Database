Does the Epistle to the Romans argue from a Greek Old Testament?

Disputed reading · on Which text did the New Testament quote? · witnesses: Origen · Jerome · Thomas Aquinas

Related readings: Hebrews and its Greek Old Testament · Habakkuk 2:4 at Romans 1:17 · Isaiah 59:20 at Romans 11:26 · Isaiah 25:8 at 1 Corinthians 15:54

The passage

“But I say, Have they not heard? Yes verily, their sound went into all the earth, and their words unto the ends of the world.”

— Romans 10:18 (KJV), quoting Psalm 19:4.1

The question

At Romans 10:18 Paul’s “their sound (φθόγγος)” is the Septuagint’s reading of a Hebrew that has “their line” (qawwām, a measuring-cord); at 9:33 and 10:11 “shall not be ashamed” is the Septuagint of Isaiah 28:16, where the Hebrew has “shall not make haste.”1 Other citations in Romans follow the Greek where it parts from the Hebrew: “mind” for “spirit” (Isaiah 40:13 at 11:34), “confess” for “swear” (Isaiah 45:23 at 14:11), the longer Isaiah 52:5 (2:24), the passive of Psalm 51:4 (3:4), “a remnant shall be saved” (Isaiah 10:22 at 9:27), the vocative “Lord” of Isaiah 53:1 (10:16), and “in him shall the Gentiles hope” (Isaiah 11:10 at 15:12).2 Some citations run otherwise. Romans 11:35 follows the Hebrew of Job 41:11 against a quite different Greek Job; Habakkuk 2:4 at 1:17, Isaiah 59:20 at 11:26 and Deuteronomy 32:35 at 12:19 match neither received text verbatim; and the catena of 3:10–18 and the use of Deuteronomy 30 at 10:6–8 rework their sources.3 Readers from Origen onward have collated these citations against the Hebrew. The dispute is over which account of Romans’ Old Testament the evidence fits: a letter that argues from the wording of a Greek Bible, or an apostle who handles Scripture in more than one form and shapes it himself.

An apostle shaping his citations from more than one text

On this reading Paul drew on more than one form of the Old Testament and adapted the wording himself, so the form of each citation is his own work.

Held by: Origen (Greek, Commentary on Romans, on 11:26); Dietrich-Alex Koch (Die Schrift als Zeuge des Evangeliums, 1986); Christopher D. Stanley (Paul and the Language of Scripture, 1992).4,5

Origen, collating Romans 11:26 against Isaiah, notes that Paul’s “out of Zion” stands where Isaiah has “for the sake of Zion,” and that Paul’s closing clause “is not written there at all, nor indeed among the Hebrews.”4 Koch and Stanley reconstruct Paul’s citation technique case by case, and read his adaptations as his own handling of the text rather than as copies of a fixed Vorlage: the abridged catena of Romans 3:10–18, and the reworking of Deuteronomy 30 at 10:6–8, with “the deep” for Deuteronomy’s “over the sea.” The forms that match no received text point the same way: the pronoun-less “the just shall live by faith” of 1:17, the Deliverer “out of Zion” (ἐκ Σιων) of 11:26, in no known copy of Isaiah, and the “vengeance is mine” of 12:19, the form Hebrews 10:30 also uses. At 11:35 Paul’s “who hath first given to him, and it shall be recompensed?” tracks the Hebrew of Job 41:11, as 1 Corinthians 3:19 tracks the Hebrew of Job 5:13, so Paul could reach the Hebrew, or a Greek Job now lost, when he chose. On this reading Romans presents an apostle working with Scripture in several text-forms and setting each citation in the words his argument required.5

A letter that argues from the Greek wording

On this reading the Bible Paul worked from in Romans is a Greek one, and at its turning points the argument rests on the Greek wording.

Held by: Jerome (Latin, Commentaries on Isaiah and Habakkuk); Thomas Aquinas (Latin, Commentary on Romans, thirteenth century); Mark A. Seifrid (“Romans,” in Beale & Carson, 2007).6,7

“This follows the Septuagint text, for in our text, which follows the Hebrew truth, it says the just man lives by his faith.”

Aquinas opens the letter with this note at Romans 1:17 and repeats it as a refrain: “the Apostle takes these words according to the Septuagint” (9:33), “the Apostle uses the Septuagint” (11:26), “our text has this… every tongue shall swear” where Paul reads “confess” (14:11). Jerome, reading from the Hebrew, marks the same forks at Isaiah 28:16, 40:13 and 11:10 and at the pronoun of Habakkuk 2:4.6 Seifrid grades each citation in Romans by text-tradition. At two points the argument runs on the Greek: “whosoever believeth on him shall not be ashamed” (9:33, 10:11) carries a promise of vindicated faith that the Hebrew “make haste” does not, and only the Greek “sound,” a voice where the Hebrew has a measuring-line, yields the universal preaching that 10:18 asserts. Around these hinges stands the wider band of Septuagintal readings: the “mind” of the Lord, the confession of every tongue, the hope of the Gentiles in the root of Jesse. Holders of this reading treat the Hebrew at these forks as stable, so that the Greek readings are the Greek Bible’s own renderings. On this reading Romans reasons from the Bible of the Greek-speaking synagogue and church, building its proofs on the words as they stood there.7
