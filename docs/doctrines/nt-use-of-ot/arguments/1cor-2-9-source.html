1 Corinthians 2:9: “eye hath not seen” — a free rendering of Isaiah, or a lost source Paul quoted?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — the catalog’s neither / free / targumic tag on 1 Corinthians 2:9 is secure at its core: Paul introduces “Eye hath not seen, nor ear heard…” with the citation formula kathōs gégraptai (“as it is written”), yet the words reproduce neither the Old Greek nor the Masoretic Hebrew of Isaiah 64:4 — the nearest candidate — verbatim, and the divergences run deep. What is genuinely disputed is the source: whether the wording is a free, conflated Pauline rendering of Isaiah 64:4 (with 65:16) — the reading of Jerome, who read the Hebrew, and of much modern commentary — or whether Paul is quoting a now-lost fixed source (a Jewish apocryphon or a liturgical/testimonia saying), as the oldest witness, Origen, reported when he traced the words to a “Secrets of Elijah,” and as a real strand of modern scholarship still argues. The two readings are strong by different lights, with no decisive datum either way. (This page weighs the text-form and source. The distinct question of whether citing such words with a Scripture-formula proves a wider apostolic canon is weighed — and rated weak — on its own page; see below. The two are kept apart on purpose.)

The claim

“But as it is written, Eye hath not seen, nor ear heard, neither have entered into the heart of man, the things which God hath prepared for them that love him.”

— 1 Corinthians 2:9 (KJV), c. AD 55; Greek ἃ ὀφθαλμὸς οὐκ εἶδεν καὶ οὖς οὐκ ἤκουσεν καὶ ἐπὶ καρδίαν ἀνθρώπου οὐκ ἀνέβη, ἃ ἡτοίμασεν ὁ θεὸς τοῖς ἀγαπῶσιν αὐτόν, introduced by the standard citation formula kathōs gégraptai (“as it is written”).1 The interpretation this argument serves is the catalog’s classification of the row — that Paul’s wording follows neither received Old-Testament text, a free rendering or conflation owing to none of them — sharpened to the honest question the classification leaves open: what stands behind the words? The steelman of the majority reading is that they are Paul’s own free, conflated citation of Isaiah, quoted loosely from memory; it is weighed against its live and anciently-attested rival, that Paul is quoting a fixed source now lost — a Jewish apocryphon (the “Secrets of Elijah”) or a stock liturgical saying — which would make the wording a quotation, not a paraphrase.

Who advances it

That the words match no extant Old-Testament text verbatim is undisputed; the debate is over what that non-match implies. Two camps have stood opposed since antiquity.

The lost-source camp is the older. Origen (c. 240), commenting on the verse in his Commentary on Matthew, wrote that the words “are found in no canonical book, except in the Secrets of Elijah the prophet” (in nullo regulari libro hoc positum invenitur, nisi in Secretis Eliae prophetae) — the earliest surviving attempt to name a source for the citation, and it names a book, not a paraphrase. Ambrosiaster (c. 370), on 1 Corinthians 2:9, repeats the ascription while coupling it with Isaiah: “These words were expressed somewhat differently by Isaiah, and they are also found in the apocryphal Apocalypse of Elijah.” So the two Latin witnesses who reach for a fixed source both name an Elijah-apocryphon.2

The free-Isaiah camp answers from the Hebrew. Jerome (c. 396), writing to Pammachius on the art of translation, takes up this very passage as a case of the apostles giving “the meaning rather than the words”: those who trace it to an apocryphon “betake themselves to the ravings of the apocryphal books… whereas the truth is that it is found in Isaiah according to the Hebrew text.” He then prints both text-forms of Isaiah 64:4 to show the apostle paraphrasing his Hebrew original. Modern commentary has largely followed him: the standard surveys and most recent commentators file 1 Corinthians 2:9 as a free (often conflated) rendering of Isaiah 64:4, perhaps with Isaiah 65:16–17, rather than a quotation of a lost book — H. F. D. Sparks and Otfried Hofius, answering E. von Nordheim’s bid to trace it instead to the Testament of Abraham, both concluded that Isaiah remains the more probable source.3

Among moderns the lost-source reading is far from dead. Michael E. Stone and John Strugnell, in the standard collection of the relevant testimonia (The Books of Elijah, Parts 1–2), gathered the patristic evidence and argued that Paul alludes to a lost Apocryphon of Elijah; and a recurring internal argument is pressed on the same side — the citation sits so awkwardly in Paul’s Greek sentence that it reads like an imported block, not a phrase he shaped himself from memory.4

The evidence for

The words are demonstrably not the extant Isaiah — in either Greek or Hebrew — so the “neither / free” tag is secure at its core. Jerome himself lays the two text-forms side by side. The Hebrew of Isaiah 64:4 (64:3 in the Greek) runs, in his rendering, “Since the beginning of the world men have not heard nor perceived by the ear, neither hath the eye seen, O God, beside thee what thou hast prepared for them that wait for thee”; the Septuagint “quite differently” — “we have not heard, neither have our eyes seen any God beside thee and thy true works, and thou wilt shew mercy to them that wait for thee.” Set beside Paul, three differences are not slips of memory but shifts of substance: the Hebrew and Greek both make the point “no God beside thee” — a confession of monotheism — which Paul drops entirely; both put the ear before the eye, where Paul reverses them; and both end with those who “wait for” God, where Paul has those who “love” him. Whatever its source, the citation is not a transcription of Isaiah 64:4 in any surviving form.1

Yet Isaiah supplies the raw material — and so does its neighbourhood, which is why the free-conflation reading is strong. The triad “eye… ear… heart” and the closing “the things which God hath prepared” are recognisably Isaiah 64:4; and the one phrase with no basis in that verse — “neither have entered into (ἀνέβη ἐπὶ, ‘come up upon’) the heart of man” — is itself a Septuagintal idiom lying a few verses on, at Isaiah 65:16–17 (LXX “shall not come up upon their heart”) and again at Jeremiah 3:16. So the whole citation can be assembled from Isaiah and its idiom without positing any lost book: a conflation of 64:4 with the “come up upon the heart” of 65:16 is exactly the paraphrastic, memory-driven citation Jerome catalogues across a dozen apostolic quotations in the same letter. This is the honest strength of the majority reading: a fully canonical source is available, and the mechanism (free conflation) is one Paul demonstrably uses elsewhere.3

But the saying circulated as a fixed, self-standing block — which is what keeps the lost-source reading live. The same tripartite form — often with “the things which God hath prepared for them that love him” that is not in Isaiah — turns up across early Christian literature independently of Paul’s argument: in 2 Clement 11:7 and the Martyrdom of Polycarp 2, in Clement of Alexandria (who quotes it in four different settings, once ascribing it to “the Word” saying “Lo, I make new things”), in the Ascension of Isaiah 11:34, in the Gospel of Thomas log. 17, and in the Latin and later fathers. A phrase that recurs this widely, in this stable a shape, and with the un-Isaianic “love him” ending intact, behaves like a traditional saying with a life of its own — a liturgical or testimonia unit — rather than an ad hoc paraphrase each writer independently spun from Isaiah 64:4. That is the datum the lost-source camp presses, and it is a real one.4

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The catalog’s tag is secure and the row is a genuine crux, not a false alarm: Paul introduces with a Scripture-formula words that reproduce no surviving Old-Testament text, and the divergences from the nearest candidate (Isaiah 64:4) are substantive — the dropped monotheism-clause, the reversed senses, “love” for “wait.” The row belongs where the catalog puts it, among the citations owing to neither received text.

What must be refined, so neither camp is over-read.

  1. Origen’s “Secrets of Elijah” names a source, but cannot be run through the extant book. The surviving Apocalypse of Elijah is a late Coptic Christian recension (c. AD 150–300) that postdates Paul and does not contain the phrase Origen ascribes to it — so the extant book cannot be Paul’s source, and if there is literary contact it more likely runs from the famous Pauline verse into the later apocryphon. The most a careful reading sustains is a lost Jewish saying standing behind both, not a recoverable book we can point to. Origen’s testimony proves that a fixed-source reading is ancient, not that it is right.2

  2. The patristic witnesses are divided, and their best reader chose Isaiah. Even in antiquity the ascription did not line up: Origen affirms the apocryphon, Ambrosiaster couples it with Isaiah, and Jerome — the one Father who actually read the Hebrew — denies it outright and prints the Isaiah text to prove the point. A divided tradition in which the most competent Hebraist opts for the canonical source is weak ground for a confident lost-book verdict.3

  3. The wide circulation cuts both ways. That the saying recurs in 2 Clement, Polycarp’s Martyrdom, Clement of Alexandria, and the rest shows it became a fixed unit — but most of those witnesses are later than Paul and quote it as “Scripture,” which is precisely what one expects if Paul’s own letter is what fixed and broadcast the wording. The independent-circulation argument is only decisive if the tradition can be shown to be pre-Pauline, and it cannot be: the earliest datable witnesses all stand downstream of 1 Corinthians. (A Hellenistic pedigree — Plutarch attributes a kindred saying to Empedocles — shows the topos was in the air, not that Paul copied a specific text.) So the shared form is compatible with a lost source and with Paul-as-fountainhead; it does not adjudicate between them.4

  4. The proven claim is the text-form, not the source — and the two rivals are hard to separate. What the evidence establishes is that 1 Corinthians 2:9 is non-canonical in wording — matching no surviving Old-Testament text. Whether that arose from Paul’s free conflation of Isaiah 64:4 (+65:16) or from a fixed lost Jewish saying (itself very possibly a reworking of Isaiah) is underdetermined, because the two hypotheses are not even cleanly exclusive: the lost source, if it existed, may have been a liturgical elaboration of Isaiah, in which case “Paul conflated Isaiah” and “Paul quoted a source that conflated Isaiah” predict nearly the same wording — the same methodological knot that ties 1 Corinthians 14:21 and Matthew’s formula quotations.1

Assessment

Contested. Two questions have two answers. First: does Paul’s 1 Corinthians 2:9 reproduce a surviving Old-Testament text? No, securely — it matches neither the Old Greek nor the Hebrew of Isaiah 64:4 (the dropped “no God beside thee,” the reversed senses, “love” for “wait”), so the catalog’s “neither / free / targumic” tag holds and the row sits with the doctrine’s other no-extant-text cruxes. Second: what is the source — a free, conflated Pauline rendering of Isaiah, or a now-lost fixed source (apocryphon or liturgical saying) that Paul quoted? Genuinely disputed, with no clear winner. For the free-Isaiah reading: Jerome, who read the Hebrew and printed it, plus the fact that Isaiah 64:4 and the “come up upon the heart” of Isaiah 65:16 together supply the whole citation without any lost book, and free conflation is a mechanism Paul demonstrably uses. For the lost-source reading: the oldest witness (Origen) named a book, the saying circulated as a stable self-standing unit with an un-Isaianic “love him” ending, and the citation fits Paul’s sentence awkwardly, like an imported block. Neither side lands a decisive blow — the extant Elijah-apocryphon is too late and lacks the phrase (defeating the specific book), while the independent-circulation evidence is all downstream of Paul (so it cannot prove a pre-Pauline source). What to trust is the non-canonical, no-extant-text text-form; what to hold loosely is any confident identification of the source — the reading is strong text-critically for a lost fixed saying (Origen; the stable wide circulation; the awkward syntax) and strong by the lights of canonical economy for a free Isaiah conflation (Jerome; 64:4 + 65:16 supply it; Paul’s known habit), the signature of a truly contested case. (That this un-fixable source does not prove a wider apostolic canon is a separate finding, rated weak on its own page.)
