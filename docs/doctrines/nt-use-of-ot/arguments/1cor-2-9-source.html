Does 1 Corinthians 2:9 (“eye hath not seen”) render Isaiah freely, or quote a lost source?

Disputed reading · on Which text did the New Testament quote? · witnesses: Origen · Jerome

Related readings: Isaiah 28:11–12 at 1 Corinthians 14:21 · Matthew’s formula quotations

The passage

“But as it is written, Eye hath not seen, nor ear heard, neither have entered into the heart of man, the things which God hath prepared for them that love him.”

— 1 Corinthians 2:9 (KJV), c. AD 55; Greek ἃ ὀφθαλμὸς οὐκ εἶδεν καὶ οὖς οὐκ ἤκουσεν καὶ ἐπὶ καρδίαν ἀνθρώπου οὐκ ἀνέβη, ἃ ἡτοίμασεν ὁ θεὸς τοῖς ἀγαπῶσιν αὐτόν, introduced by the citation formula kathōs gégraptai (“as it is written”).1

The question

Paul introduces these words as Scripture, yet they reproduce no surviving Old Testament text verbatim. The nearest candidate, Isaiah 64:4, runs in the Hebrew, as Jerome renders it, “Since the beginning of the world men have not heard nor perceived by the ear, neither hath the eye seen, O God, beside thee what thou hast prepared for them that wait for thee”; the Septuagint has “we have not heard, neither have our eyes seen any God beside thee and thy true works, and thou wilt shew mercy to them that wait for thee.” Both include “beside thee,” put the ear before the eye, and end with those who “wait” for God, where Paul has those who “love” him.1 The dispute, which goes back to the third century, is whether Paul quotes a now-lost fixed source or renders Isaiah freely. The related question of whether the citation bears on the extent of the apostolic canon is set out on a page of its own.

A quotation of a lost source, an Elijah-apocryphon

On this reading Paul quotes, under the formula “as it is written,” a fixed saying from a book now lost, which early readers named the Secrets or Apocalypse of Elijah.

Held by: Origen (Greek, Commentary on Matthew, c. 240); Ambrosiaster (Latin, Commentary on 1 Corinthians, c. 370); Michael E. Stone and John Strugnell (The Books of Elijah, 1979).2

Origen, commenting on the verse, wrote that the words “are found in no canonical book, except in the Secrets of Elijah the prophet” (in nullo regulari libro hoc positum invenitur, nisi in Secretis Eliae prophetae), naming a book as their source. Ambrosiaster repeats the ascription while setting it beside Isaiah: “These words were expressed somewhat differently by Isaiah, and they are also found in the apocryphal Apocalypse of Elijah.” Stone and Strugnell, gathering the patristic testimonia, argue that Paul alludes to a lost Apocryphon of Elijah, distinct from the surviving Coptic Apocalypse of Elijah, a later Christian recension (c. AD 150–300) that does not contain the phrase.2

Holders of the reading point to the saying’s life as a stable block. The same three-part form, often with the closing “the things which God hath prepared for them that love him,” recurs in 2 Clement 11:7, the Martyrdom of Polycarp 2, Clement of Alexandria (who quotes it in four settings, once ascribing it to “the Word” saying “Lo, I make new things”), the Ascension of Isaiah 11:34 and the Gospel of Thomas log. 17. A phrase that recurs this widely, in this shape, and with the “love him” ending that Isaiah lacks, behaves on this reading like a traditional saying, liturgical or from a testimonia collection, with a life of its own. Its proponents add that the citation sits awkwardly in Paul’s Greek sentence, like a block brought in from outside rather than a phrase he shaped himself.3

A free, conflated rendering of Isaiah 64:4

On this reading Paul gives the sense of Isaiah 64:4 in his own words, drawing one phrase from the neighbouring Isaiah 65:16–17.

Held by: Jerome (Latin, Letter 57, c. 396); H. F. D. Sparks; Otfried Hofius; Roy E. Ciampa and Brian S. Rosner (in Beale & Carson, 2007).4

Jerome, writing to Pammachius on the art of translation, takes up this passage as a case of the apostles giving the meaning rather than the words: those who trace it to an apocryphon “betake themselves to the ravings of the apocryphal books… whereas the truth is that it is found in Isaiah according to the Hebrew text.” He prints both text-forms of Isaiah 64:4 to show the apostle paraphrasing his Hebrew original, and he files the verse the same way in his Preface to the Pentateuch (c. 403), among citations drawn from the Hebrew Old Testament.4

On this reading Isaiah supplies the whole citation, and Paul’s letter stands at the head of the saying’s circulation: the early Christian writers who repeat it (2 Clement, the Martyrdom of Polycarp, Clement of Alexandria) are all later than 1 Corinthians. The triad of eye, ear and heart and the clause “the things which God hath prepared” come from Isaiah 64:4. The one phrase with no basis in that verse, “neither have entered into (ἀνέβη ἐπὶ, ‘come up upon’) the heart of man,” is a Septuagint idiom found a few verses on, at Isaiah 65:16–17 (“shall not come up upon their heart”), and again at Jeremiah 3:16. Sparks and Hofius, answering E. von Nordheim’s proposal of another source, both judged Isaiah the more probable one; Ciampa and Rosner treat the verse as a free rendering of Isaiah 64:4, perhaps with 65:16–17. A conflation of 64:4 with the idiom of 65:16 is the kind of paraphrase from memory that Jerome catalogues across a dozen apostolic citations in the same letter.4
