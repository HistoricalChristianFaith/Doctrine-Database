Why does Ephesians 4:8 read “gave gifts” where Psalm 68:18 reads “received gifts”?

Disputed reading · on Which text did the New Testament quote? · witness: Jerome

Related readings: Isaiah 28:11–12 at 1 Corinthians 14:21 · 1 Corinthians 2:9 and its source

The passage

Wherefore he saith, When he ascended up on high, he led captivity captive, and gave gifts unto men.

— Ephesians 4:8 (KJV), quoting

Thou hast ascended on high, thou hast led captivity captive: thou hast received gifts for men; yea, for the rebellious also, that the LORD God might dwell among them.

— Psalm 68:18 (KJV; 68:19 in the Hebrew, 67:19 in the Greek).1

The question

The Masoretic Hebrew reads lāqaḥtā mattānôt bā-ʾādām, “you received gifts among men,” and the Septuagint follows it (ἔλαβες δόματα ἐν ἀνθρώπῳ). Ephesians has ἔδωκεν δόματα τοῖς ἀνθρώποις, “he gave gifts to men,” in the third person rather than the second. The Aramaic Targum of Psalms and the Syriac Peshitta read “gave.” The dispute is what accounts for the “gave”: the author’s own rendering, a text-tradition already reading “gave,” or a Jewish interpretive tradition taken up and shaped by the author.1

The author’s own change of verb, read from the psalm and its fulfilment

On this reading the apostle himself wrote “gave” for “received,” to say what the ascended Christ had done.

Held by: Jerome (Latin, Commentary on Ephesians, c. 386); Gary V. Smith (JETS, 1975); Timothy G. Gombis (Novum Testamentum, 2005); Jonathan Lunde and John Anthony Dunne (Westminster Theological Journal, 2012).2

Jerome reads the change as the apostle’s deliberate choice:

“And elegantly, he placed here, ‘he gave gifts to men,’ whereas in the Psalter it is written, ‘he received gifts from men’ (Ps. LXVII, 19). But there, because it had not yet happened, but was promised for the future, it is said, ‘he received.’ Here, however, since the Apostle writes that he had already given, and the Church was established throughout the world, he does not write ‘received,’ but ‘gave.’”

The Greek commentators and Aquinas read receiving and giving as one act: Chrysostom writes “The one is the same as the other,” and Aquinas that “he as God bestows the gifts which he as man receives in the faithful who are his members.”3

Gombis and Lunde–Dunne ground the change in the psalm as a whole. Psalm 68 is a Divine-Warrior hymn in which the victorious, ascending God distributes the spoils of conquest; read against the whole psalm, a giving Christ follows its own movement (ascent, captives led, gifts disbursed). The verb then carries the passage: the ἔδωκεν of v. 8 is resumed in Ephesians 4:11, “and he gave some, apostles; and some, prophets,” so that the gift-giving Christ of the proof-text sets up the distribution of ministries. On this reading the shift of verb belongs with the author’s other adaptations of person and preposition, and the Targum and Peshitta reach the same “gave” by the same interpretive step.4

A “gave” text-tradition already current

On this reading the author drew “gave” from a form of the psalm already circulating, attested later in the Aramaic and Syriac versions.

Held by: Richard Rubinkiewicz (Novum Testamentum, 1975); Richard A. Taylor (Bibliotheca Sacra, 1991).5

Ephesians’ verb is the verb of the Aramaic tradition. The Targum of Psalms renders v. 19: “You ascended to the firmament, O prophet Moses; you captured captives, you taught the words of Torah, you gave gifts to the sons of men.” The majority of Peshitta manuscripts likewise read “you gave gifts to the sons of men.” Both agree with Ephesians where the Hebrew and the Septuagint read “received.”6

Rubinkiewicz asks whether the reading reflects “another textual tradition or Targum.” Taylor, working from the ancient versions, takes the Syriac as an independent witness and argues that a “gave” text-form was current “in the early Christian period, and no doubt earlier as well,” while granting that “extant evidence does not allow this to be stated with certainty.” On this reading the author of Ephesians quotes the psalm as he found it in such a form, and the agreement of two independent versions with his wording points to a reading older than all three.5

A Jewish Moses-ascent tradition, taken up and shaped by the author

On this reading the “gave” comes from a known Jewish way of reading the psalm, of Moses ascending to receive the Torah and giving it to Israel, which the author selected and applied to Christ.

Held by: Andrew T. Lincoln (Ephesians, Word Biblical Commentary, 1990); W. Hall Harris III (The Descent of Christ, 1996); Frank Thielman (in Beale & Carson, 2007).7

Harris documents the Jewish reading: “every time Psalm 68:19 is mentioned in the rabbinic literature, it is (without exception) interpreted of Moses and his ascent to heaven to receive the Torah.” In that frame Moses ascends, receives the Torah, and brings it down to give to Israel, and the Targum writes “gave” into the verse. The Bavli’s fullest treatment (Shabbat 88b–89a) keeps the Hebrew “took,” telling how the angels each “became his friend and handed him a gift.” On this reading the Moses frame supplies the pivot from receiving to giving.6

Its holders place the Targum after Ephesians. The Targum of Psalms is a late compilation, commonly placed in the fourth to sixth centuries; Thielman states that the Targum texts “were composed centuries after” Ephesians but “give witness to an older textual tradition.” Lincoln, after Barnabas Lindars, notes that “the reading at this point in the Peshitta may be a corruption,” an assimilation to Ephesians. On this reading the author drew on a living exegetical tradition rather than on a targumic text before him, and shaped it to the ascended Christ who gives gifts to his church.7
