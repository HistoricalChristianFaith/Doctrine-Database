Psalm 16:10 at Acts 2:27 / 13:35: “see no corruption” — does the apostolic resurrection proof depend on the Greek?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: sound — the catalog’s LXX-distinctive tag on this pair of rows holds, and this is the single most load-bearing Septuagintal case in the book of Acts. Both Peter (Acts 2:25–31) and Paul (Acts 13:35–37) build an explicit resurrection argument on Psalm 16:10’s “thou wilt not suffer thine Holy One to see corruption” — the Septuagint’s διαφθοράν (“decay, putrefaction”) — where the Hebrew reads šaḥat (שַׁחַת), “the pit / the grave,” in parallel with “Sheol” in the line above. The proof runs: David’s body did decay in a tomb still visible in Jerusalem, so the psalm’s promise that God’s Holy One would not see decay must speak of another — the risen Messiah. So the “LXX-distinctive” tag holds; the honest question, and the reason this row was queued for its own adjudication, is how load-bearing the Greek word is. Two findings, kept separate. (a) Like Isaiah 7:14 and Amos 9:12 this is a Greek reading of a stable Hebrew — the same consonants שׁחת stand, and the Septuagint diverges by deriving them from the homonymous root šāḥat (“destroy, corrupt”) rather than šûaḥ (“sink down” → a pit), not by preserving an older Hebrew the Masoretes lost — so it belongs with the gloss group. (b) But unlike Amos 9 (where the load-bearing clause survives in both text-forms) or Psalm 40:6 (where the Greek word is a bridge the argument survives without), here the Greek word is the empirical proof: the vivid “David decayed, this man did not” prong bites only on διαφθορά “decay,” not on šaḥat “the grave.” A reduced resurrection reading does survive on the Hebrew — via the parallel, text-form-neutral line “thou wilt not leave my soul in Sheol” set against David, who plainly remained in the grave — so it is the sharpest prong, not the whole argument, that needs the Greek. That makes this the strongest “the argument needs the Greek” case in the New Testament, stronger than Psalm 40:6.

Related arguments: the Septuagint’s interpretive gloss — see also Isaiah 7:14 at Matthew 1:23 — is the “virgin” a Septuagintal gloss, and does Matthew depend on it? · Psalm 40:6 at Hebrews 10:5 — a Septuagintal “body,” and does the argument depend on it? · Amos 9:11–12 at Acts 15:16–18 — a Septuagintal “seek the Lord,” and does the Council’s decision depend on it?

This page adds the piece those do not carry: the text-form adjudication of the Acts rows themselves, and the finding that the Greek word is here the most load-bearing in the whole catalog of apostolic quotations.

The claim

“Moreover also my flesh shall rest in hope: because thou wilt not leave my soul in hell, neither wilt thou suffer thine Holy One to see corruption… Men and brethren, let me freely speak unto you of the patriarch David, that he is both dead and buried, and his sepulchre is with us unto this day… he seeing this before spake of the resurrection of Christ, that his soul was not left in hell, neither his flesh did see corruption.”

— Peter at Pentecost, Acts 2:26–31, quoting Psalm 16:10 in the Septuagint’s form (οὐδὲ δώσεις τὸν ὅσιόν σου ἰδεῖν διαφθοράν); Paul repeats the same proof verbatim at Acts 13:35–37 — “he whom God raised again saw no corruption” — in his synagogue sermon at Pisidian Antioch.1 The interpretation this argument serves is the catalog’s classification: that the apostolic resurrection proof is LXX-distinctive — drawn from the Greek text-form at the very point it diverges from the Hebrew — and the sub-claim this page tests is that the Greek word is not merely present but load-bearing, the pivot on which the argument turns.

Who advances it

That the proof reads “corruption” and turns on it is not a modern discovery; the ancient expositors built the whole resurrection argument on the word. Jerome, expounding the psalm in his Homily 61, dwells on the very verb: the Lord “did not say ‘shall dwell’… but… ‘shall sojourn,’ ” his flesh “shall rest in hope” because it “will not be granted by the Father to see corruption” — and he glosses the idiom, “he said ‘to see’ for ‘to suffer,’ according to that idiom of the Scriptures.”2 Augustine makes the Davidic contrast the hinge: only he “who rose on the third day” could say “that his flesh would not undergo corruption as other corpses rot away—surely no one can maintain that all of this was verified in David!” Bede defines the term exactly — “that corruption, that is, putrefaction… had no power to come upon His most sacred body” — and Gregory of Nyssa, Diodorus of Tarsus, Cassiodorus, and Theodoret all read the line of the risen Christ’s uncorrupted flesh.3 In the modern surveys the same fact is a flat classification — Archer & Chirichigno, the Kalvesmaki table, and the loci citati of the Nestle–Aland / UBS Greek New Testament all file Acts 2:27 and 13:35 as following the Septuagint against the Hebrew — and the commentators press the stronger form that the argument itself is possible only on the Greek: I. Howard Marshall and Darrell Bock both note that Peter’s and Paul’s reasoning rests on διαφθορά “decay” rather than the Hebrew “pit,” and it is that stronger form the assessment below tests.4

The evidence for

Acts reproduces the Greek word, and the Greek word means bodily decay. The Septuagint of Psalm 15:10 (Hebrew 16:10) renders the last clause οὐδὲ δώσεις τὸν ὅσιόν σου ἰδεῖν διαφθοράν — “nor wilt thou give thy Holy One to see corruption” — and Acts 2:27 quotes it letter for letter, Acts 13:35 with only the trivial drop of the connective οὐδέ. διαφθορά is a physical term: the rotting of a corpse. That is the sense every Father who expounds the verse assumes, and Bede states it outright — “corruption, that is, putrefaction” — the decay “which generally devastates human flesh.” The proof depends on precisely that sense.1

The Hebrew reads “the pit,” in poetic parallel with “Sheol.” The Masoretic Hebrew of the verse runs “thou wilt not abandon my soul to Sheol (שְׁאוֹל); thou wilt not give thy faithful one (ḥāsîd, LXX ὅσιος) to see šaḥat (שַׁחַת).” The parallelism pairs šaḥat with “Sheol,” and the ordinary sense of the word so paired is a pit or the grave — the noun most naturally derived from the root šûaḥ, “to sink down.” On that reading the couplet is deliverance-from-death language: confidence that God will not abandon his faithful one to the realm of the dead. The Septuagint instead treats the identical consonants שׁחת as belonging to the homonymous root šāḥat (“destroy, corrupt”) and so renders “corruption” — a defensible but not the more natural reading of the parallel line. The Latin Psalter Jerome inherited (and printed in his Gallican revision) follows the Greek with corruptionem; the divergence is real, live, and lexical.5

The empirical prong bites only on “decay.” Peter and Paul do not merely quote the verse; they run an argument from an observable fact. David, the psalm’s speaker, “is both dead and buried, and his sepulchre is with us unto this day” (Acts 2:29); Paul sharpens it — David “fell on sleep… and saw corruption: but he, whom God raised again, saw no corruption” (Acts 13:36–37). The whole force is a contrast in bodily fate: David’s flesh decayed; the Messiah’s, raised before the third day was out, did not. That contrast is available only if the psalm-word denotes decay. Read as “the pit / the grave,” the verse states that God will not abandon his faithful one to the realm of the dead — and David, for all that he died, could be said to be there like anyone; the sharp, checkable “his body rotted, this one’s did not” is a thing you can say only with διαφθορά. This is what sets the row apart from Amos 9:12 and Psalm 40:6, where the argument’s load-bearing clause survives the switch to the Hebrew.4

Paul frames the proof with a second Greek-only catchword. In the Antioch sermon Paul leads into Psalm 16 through Acts 13:34, where he cites Isaiah 55:3 in its Septuagintal form — “I will give you the holy things (τὰ ὅσια) of David, the sure” — and the adjective ὅσια (“holy things”) is the catchword that binds it to the “Holy One” (ὅσιον) of Psalm 16:10 in the next verse. That verbal hook works only in Greek: the Hebrew of Isaiah 55:3 reads “the faithful mercies (ḥasdê) of David,” a wholly different word from ḥāsîd in the psalm. Bede saw it precisely, contrasting the Greek with “the Hebrew truth… the faithful mercies of David.” So the sermon does its exegesis by a chain of specifically Greek links — a second, distinct Septuagintal mechanism reinforcing the same conclusion, though (unlike the “corruption” prong) a catchword bridge rather than the empirical proof itself.6

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The catalog’s classification is correct, and its tag is if anything understated. Peter and Paul quote the Septuagint’s διαφθοράν verbatim, at the exact point the Greek diverges from the Hebrew šaḥat, and they build a resurrection argument on it. This is a genuine, live instance of the New Testament reasoning from the Greek against the plain Hebrew — not an inert agreement, not a free paraphrase, and (as the evidence shows) the single case in Acts where a doctrinal argument, not merely a wording, rides on the Greek. The row belongs where the catalog put it.

What must be refined, so the finding is neither understated nor stretched.

  1. This is a Greek reading of a stable Hebrew, not an older Hebrew the Greek preserved. As with Isaiah 7:14 and Amos 9:12, the divergence lives entirely on the Greek side. There is no rival Hebrew Vorlage in view: the consonants שׁחת are the same, and the fork is one of derivation — the Septuagint takes them from šāḥat (“destroy, corrupt”) where the parallelism with “Sheol” favours šûaḥ (“sink down” → a pit). So the row sits with the gloss group, not with Deuteronomy 32:43 / Hebrews 1:6 or Goliath’s height, where the Greek rests on a genuinely different Hebrew recovered at Qumran (Hebrew-over-Hebrew). Whoever files this row as an older-Hebrew case has conflated two different phenomena. (The honest converse: because šaḥat really is lexically ambiguous, the Greek “corruption” is a defensible pre-Christian reading of the word, not a Christian fabrication — the Septuagint of the Psalms was fixed long before the resurrection was in view.)5

  2. The Greek is genuinely load-bearing here — more so than anywhere else in the catalog — yet a reduced Hebrew argument survives. This is the crux the row was queued to settle, and honesty cuts both ways. On one side: the vivid, checkable prong of the proof — “David’s flesh decayed (his tomb is here); this man’s did not” — works only on διαφθορά “decay.” It cannot be reconstructed from “the pit”: a grave is where David is, not a fate his body escaped. So unlike Psalm 40:6, where the antithesis “sacrifice thou wouldest not… I come to do thy will” carries the argument and σῶμα is only a bridge, and unlike Amos 9:12, where the load-bearing clause (“all the nations upon whom my name is called”) stands in both text-forms, here the diverging Greek word is the argument’s sharpest edge. That is why this is fairly called the strongest “the argument needs the Greek” case in the New Testament. On the other side, the argument is not wholly Greek-dependent: Peter quotes the parallel line first — “thou wilt not leave my soul in Sheol” — which is text-form-neutral (“Sheol” stands in Hebrew and Greek alike), and that line, set against a David who plainly was left among the dead, already yields a resurrection reading (the promise outruns David, so it points past him to one not held by death). A Hebrew-only expositor could reach “this psalm looks beyond David to a risen Holy One” on that line and on the confidence of the whole psalm. What he could not reach is the specific bodily-incorruption prong — the empirical clincher. So the precise finding is: the sharpest prong needs the Greek; a weaker resurrection reading survives on the Hebrew. That is a stronger dependence than Psalm 40:6’s and Amos 9’s, and a real one, without tipping into the overclaim that “no argument at all survives the Hebrew.”4

  3. The “holy things” catchword (Acts 13:34) is a second Greek-only link, but a lexical bridge, not the proof. Paul’s Antioch sermon compounds the Septuagintal dependence: the move from Isaiah 55:3’s τὰ ὅσια (“the holy things of David”) to Psalm 16:10’s ὅσιον (“thy Holy One”) is a gezerah shavah-style verbal analogy that exists only in Greek — the Hebrew words (ḥasdê, “mercies,” and ḥāsîd, “faithful one”) are cognate in sense but not the identical catchword the Greek supplies. Bede flagged the seam (“the Hebrew truth… the faithful mercies of David”). But this link is a framing bridge — it explains why Paul reaches for Psalm 16 at this point — not the empirical proof, which is still the “corruption” contrast of vv. 36–37. It is best read as a fold-in support to this page’s finding (the sermon does its exegesis by Greek links throughout), not as an independently advocated thesis needing its own page.6

  4. “Hebrew-speaking apostles quoting Greek” is a real puzzle, but not a defeater of the row. As at Amos 9:12, the text-form question and the historicity question are separable. Luke composed the speeches in Greek for his Greek-reading audience, using his Bible, the Septuagint; Peter addresses Diaspora pilgrims and “Hellenists” at Pentecost, and Paul a Greek-speaking synagogue at Pisidian Antioch, so a Greek citation is not anachronistic on its face. And because a reduced resurrection reading survives on the text-form-neutral “soul… in Sheol” line (refinement 2), the exegetical substance — that Psalm 16 outruns David — could belong to the historical apostolic preaching while the decisive διαφθορά wording, and the incorruption prong it enables, is Septuagintal. None of this unsettles the flat text-form finding: as Acts gives the speeches, the proof reproduces the Septuagint’s “corruption” and turns on it.4

Assessment

Sound. The interpretation this argument serves — that the apostolic resurrection proof at Acts 2:27 and 13:35 reads a Septuagintal “corruption” (διαφθοράν) where the Hebrew Psalm 16:10 reads “the pit” (šaḥat) — plainly holds; the citation is LXX-distinctive and correctly tagged. The interesting, queued sub-finding is that the Greek word is the most load-bearing in the entire catalog of apostolic quotations: the vivid empirical prong (“David decayed, this man did not”) bites only on the sense “decay,” so this is the strongest “the argument needs the Greek” case in the New Testament — stronger than Psalm 40:6, which the wiki rates a bridge the argument survives without. Kept to what the evidence shows, three things fix the finding’s bounds: (a) this is the Septuagint reading a stable consonantal Hebrew — a lexical/etymological choice between šûaḥ (“pit”) and šāḥat (“corrupt”), not a rival Vorlage — so it joins the gloss group, not the older-Hebrew cases; (b) the dependence is real but not total: a reduced resurrection reading survives on the parallel, text-form-neutral “soul… in Sheol” line set against David’s death, so what needs the Greek is the sharpest prong, not the whole argument; and (c) Paul’s ὅσια → ὅσιον catchword (Acts 13:34) is a second Greek-only link, but a framing bridge folded into this page, not the empirical proof. Stated so, the row is a clean — and unusually strong — instance of the New Testament arguing from the Greek against a literal Hebrew; stretched into “the Greek preserves an older Hebrew here” or “no resurrection reading is possible on the Hebrew,” it overreaches. This row joins Isaiah 7:14, Psalm 40:6, and Amos 9:12 as a worked instance of the same species — a distinctively Septuagintal reading of a stable Hebrew, pressed by the apostles — and stands as the group’s clearest case of a Greek word doing argumentative, not merely decorative, work.
