The Purpose of the Liberal Arts

Ask a parent today why a young person should read Homer, prove a theorem, or parse a Latin sentence rather than learn a trade that pays, and you are asking a question the ancient world had already framed and named. The Greeks and Romans divided the fields of study into two kinds. The liberal arts (artes liberales, from liber, “free”) were the studies thought worthy of a free citizen — pursued for their own sake, to form a mind and a character — and the West eventually fixed them at seven: the trivium of grammar, rhetoric, and dialectic (logic), and the quadrivium of arithmetic, geometry, music, and astronomy. Set against them were the servile, mechanical, or (in Aristotle’s word) banausic arts — the crafts and trades learned for wages and bodily need, which the ancients held to occupy the body and cramp the soul, and so to be beneath the dignity of a free man.

The belief that develops on this page is the Christian answer to the question that division poses: what is learning for? Is higher study ordered to wisdom and virtue — and, for the Christian, to the knowledge and love of God — so that it is worth pursuing even when it earns nothing? Or is it idle, even self-indulgent, unless it feeds a family and serves the common life? The church inherited the pagan curriculum and had to decide what to do with it: whether the arts of a free man could be baptized, whether pagan letters were plunder to be carried off from Egypt or an idol to be shunned, whether the mechanical arts the ancients despised might be dignified rather than scorned, and whether knowledge is an end in itself or a means to something higher. That argument runs from Athens and Rome, through the fathers and the medieval schools that turned the seven arts into the foundation of the university, through the Reformers and Newman, down to the modern classical-education revival — Dorothy Sayers, C. S. Lewis, Josef Pieper, and the Great Books and classical-Christian colleges — and straight into the live present-day choice between a liberal-arts education and job training.

The timeline below traces that development across time, earliest witness first: the pagan philosophers the church was reading, the fathers who Christianized (or resisted) the classical curriculum, the medieval transmitters and schoolmen who built the university on the seven arts, the Reformers and their schools, and the modern voices who recovered the tradition against a utilitarian age.

Timeline

The witnesses are listed earliest first — the pagan philosophers who framed the free/servile division, then the fathers who received the classical curriculum, then the medieval schools and universities that systematized it, then the Reformers and moderns who defended it against utility. This spine is being built out; the figures named in the introduction are added here in chronological order as each is researched.

Plato (c. 375 BC)

Christian reflection on what learning is for begins from a book it did not write. In the Republic Plato set the education of the guardians on two pillars — “gymnastic for the body, and music for the soul” (music in the broad Greek sense, taking in literature and the arts of the Muses)1 — and then gave the whole enterprise its aim. Education is not the pouring of information into an empty mind: those who say “they can put a knowledge into the soul which was not there before, like sight into blind eyes” are wrong; rather “the power and capacity of learning exists in the soul already,” and true education is the turning of “the whole soul… from the world of becoming into that of being,” up toward “the brightest and best of being… the good.”2 Learning is ordered to contemplation of the highest, valuable in itself — the fountain from which the ideal of paideia and of learning-for-its-own-sake flows.

Aristotle (c. 335 BC)

Plato’s pupil drew the distinction that names the whole debate. In Book VIII of the Politics Aristotle held that “occupations are divided into liberal and illiberal,” and that “any occupation, art, or science, which makes the body or soul or mind of the freeman less fit for the practice or exercise of virtue, is vulgar” — naming as the paradigm case “all paid employments, for they absorb and degrade the mind.”3 Against these servile or banausic arts he set the studies pursued for their own sake: “there are branches of learning and education which we must study merely with a view to leisure spent in intellectual activity, and these are to be valued for their own sake,” while knowledge “useful in business… exist[s] for the sake of other things.”4 Crucially, the line falls not on the subject but on the end: the same act is free when done “for his own sake… or with a view to excellence” and “menial and servile” when done for hire or at another’s command — the hinge on which the whole later argument, and the church’s power to re-order the arts to God, would turn.

Cicero (44 BC)

What the Greeks argued, Cicero Latinized — and it is his vocabulary of artes liberales and humanitas that the Latin West inherited. In De Officiis (44 BC) he graded the callings just as Aristotle had graded occupations: “unbecoming to a gentleman, too, and vulgar are the means of livelihood of all hired workmen whom we pay for mere manual labour… for in their case the very wage they receive is a pledge of their slavery,” and “no workshop can have anything liberal about it.”5 The honourable callings are those requiring intelligence or conferring public benefit — medicine, architecture, teaching — and, crowning them, farming: “of all the occupations by which gain is secured, none is better than agriculture… none more becoming to a freeman.”6 The scale is the tradition’s own: the more an art engages the mind and serves a good beyond gain, the freer it is. It is this Cicero — whose De Officiis Ambrose would rewrite for the clergy — whom the Latin fathers actually read.

Varro (c. 33 BC)

If Cicero gave the arts their name, his contemporary Varro gave them their list. Rome’s greatest antiquarian, in his lost Disciplinarum Libri Novem, first gathered the Greek circle of studies into a fixed Latin curriculum: the “Libri IX Disciplinarum… treats of the seven liberal arts adding to them medicine and architectonics.”7 His nine gave the West its canon: when Martianus Capella built his allegory of the arts on Varro’s model (c. AD 420) he dropped medicine and architecture — the arts of the body and of earthly building — fixing the enduring seven that the schools would organize into the trivium (grammar, dialectic, rhetoric) and quadrivium (arithmetic, geometry, astronomy, music) and set at the foot of the university. Varro also stands for the ideal of a single connected “circle of learning,” the enkyklios paideia behind the word encyclopedia.

Seneca (c. AD 64)

The tradition’s sharpest early critique of the liberal arts comes from within it. In his 88th Moral Letter the Stoic Seneca grants the honorific — “‘liberal studies’ are so called… because they are studies worthy of a free-born gentleman” — but at once relativizes it: “there is only one really liberal study,—that which gives a man his liberty. It is the study of wisdom.”8 The rest are merely preparatory: “the liberal arts do not conduct the soul all the way to virtue, but merely set it going in that direction.”9 A man may be learned and still a fool; study is a road toward the good, not the good itself. This subordination of all learning to a single highest wisdom — value the arts, but do not idolize them — is exactly the move the fathers make in ordering the classical curriculum to the knowledge of God.

Quintilian (c. AD 95)

Rome’s foremost schoolmaster wrote the ancient world’s fullest program of a liberal education. In the Institutio Oratoria the future orator must first pass through the whole “circle of learning” — letters, music, geometry — for “it is by such studies that the course of education described by the Greeks as… general education will be brought to its full completion,” the enkyklios paideia that the seven arts would systematize.10 And the whole formation aims at a moral end: the orator is, in Cato’s phrase, “a good man, skilled in speaking,” for “no man can be an orator unless he is a good man.”11 Learning and virtue are inseparable, and a training that produced a skilled scoundrel would have failed — the pagan tradition’s nearest approach to the Christian schools’ ordering of study to the making of a good person rather than to gain.

Clement of Alexandria (c. 200)

With Clement the classical curriculum passes from pagan hands into Christian ones, and the question the timeline poses — may a believer study the arts of the free man? — gets its first confident yes. Against Christians who thought Greek learning a snare, Clement argued that philosophy was God’s own preparatory gift to the Greeks: it “was a schoolmaster to bring ‘the Hellenic mind,’ as the law, the Hebrews, ‘to Christ,’” a “preparation, paving the way for him who is perfected in Christ.”12 He orders the whole field into a hierarchy of service: the “encyclical branches of study” — the general liberal curriculum of music, geometry, grammar, and rhetoric — “contribute to philosophy, which is their mistress; so also philosophy itself co-operates for the acquisition of wisdom… Wisdom is therefore queen of philosophy, as philosophy is of preparatory culture.”13 Read through Sarah and Hagar, secular culture is the handmaid a believer may embrace — “I embrace secular culture… as a handmaid; but thy knowledge I honour and reverence as true wife” — on the way to divine knowledge.14 This chapter, headed “Philosophy the Handmaid of Theology,” is the seed of the later formula philosophia ancilla theologiae and of the whole “spoil the Egyptians” case that Origen and Augustine would systematize.
