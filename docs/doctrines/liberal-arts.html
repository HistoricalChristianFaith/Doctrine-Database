The Purpose of the Liberal Arts

Ask a parent today why a young person should read Homer, prove a theorem, or parse a Latin sentence rather than learn a trade that pays, and you are asking a question the ancient world had already framed and named. The Greeks and Romans divided the fields of study into two kinds. The liberal arts (artes liberales, from liber, “free”) were the studies thought worthy of a free citizen — pursued for their own sake, to form a mind and a character — and the West eventually fixed them at seven: the trivium of grammar, rhetoric, and dialectic (logic), and the quadrivium of arithmetic, geometry, music, and astronomy. Set against them were the servile, mechanical, or (in Aristotle’s word) banausic arts — the crafts and trades learned for wages and bodily need, which the ancients held to occupy the body and cramp the soul, and so to be beneath the dignity of a free man.

The belief that develops on this page is the Christian answer to the question that division poses: what is learning for? Is higher study ordered to wisdom and virtue — and, for the Christian, to the knowledge and love of God — so that it is worth pursuing even when it earns nothing? Or is it idle, even self-indulgent, unless it feeds a family and serves the common life? The church inherited the pagan curriculum and had to decide what to do with it: whether the arts of a free man could be baptized, whether pagan letters were plunder to be carried off from Egypt or an idol to be shunned, whether the mechanical arts the ancients despised might be dignified rather than scorned, and whether knowledge is an end in itself or a means to something higher. That argument runs from Athens and Rome, through the fathers and the medieval schools that turned the seven arts into the foundation of the university, through the Reformers and Newman, down to the modern classical-education revival — Dorothy Sayers, C. S. Lewis, Josef Pieper, and the Great Books and classical-Christian colleges — and straight into the live present-day choice between a liberal-arts education and job training.

The timeline below traces that development across time, earliest witness first: the pagan philosophers the church was reading, the fathers who Christianized (or resisted) the classical curriculum, the medieval transmitters and schoolmen who built the university on the seven arts, the Reformers and their schools, and the modern voices who recovered the tradition against a utilitarian age.

Timeline

The witnesses are listed earliest first — the pagan philosophers who framed the free/servile division, then the fathers who received the classical curriculum, then the medieval schools and universities that systematized it, then the Reformers and moderns who defended it against utility. This spine is being built out; the figures named in the introduction are added here in chronological order as each is researched.

Plato (c. 375 BC)

Christian reflection on what learning is for begins from a book it did not write. In the Republic Plato set the education of the guardians on two pillars — “gymnastic for the body, and music for the soul” (music in the broad Greek sense, taking in literature and the arts of the Muses)1 — and then gave the whole enterprise its aim. Education is not the pouring of information into an empty mind: those who say “they can put a knowledge into the soul which was not there before, like sight into blind eyes” are wrong; rather “the power and capacity of learning exists in the soul already,” and true education is the turning of “the whole soul… from the world of becoming into that of being,” up toward “the brightest and best of being… the good.”2 Learning is ordered to contemplation of the highest, valuable in itself — the fountain from which the ideal of paideia and of learning-for-its-own-sake flows.

Aristotle (c. 335 BC)

Plato’s pupil drew the distinction that names the whole debate. In Book VIII of the Politics Aristotle held that “occupations are divided into liberal and illiberal,” and that “any occupation, art, or science, which makes the body or soul or mind of the freeman less fit for the practice or exercise of virtue, is vulgar” — naming as the paradigm case “all paid employments, for they absorb and degrade the mind.”3 Against these servile or banausic arts he set the studies pursued for their own sake: “there are branches of learning and education which we must study merely with a view to leisure spent in intellectual activity, and these are to be valued for their own sake,” while knowledge “useful in business… exist[s] for the sake of other things.”4 Crucially, the line falls not on the subject but on the end: the same act is free when done “for his own sake… or with a view to excellence” and “menial and servile” when done for hire or at another’s command — the hinge on which the whole later argument, and the church’s power to re-order the arts to God, would turn.

Cicero (44 BC)

What the Greeks argued, Cicero Latinized — and it is his vocabulary of artes liberales and humanitas that the Latin West inherited. In De Officiis (44 BC) he graded the callings just as Aristotle had graded occupations: “unbecoming to a gentleman, too, and vulgar are the means of livelihood of all hired workmen whom we pay for mere manual labour… for in their case the very wage they receive is a pledge of their slavery,” and “no workshop can have anything liberal about it.”5 The honourable callings are those requiring intelligence or conferring public benefit — medicine, architecture, teaching — and, crowning them, farming: “of all the occupations by which gain is secured, none is better than agriculture… none more becoming to a freeman.”6 The scale is the tradition’s own: the more an art engages the mind and serves a good beyond gain, the freer it is. It is this Cicero — whose De Officiis Ambrose would rewrite for the clergy — whom the Latin fathers actually read.

Varro (c. 33 BC)

If Cicero gave the arts their name, his contemporary Varro gave them their list. Rome’s greatest antiquarian, in his lost Disciplinarum Libri Novem, first gathered the Greek circle of studies into a fixed Latin curriculum: the “Libri IX Disciplinarum… treats of the seven liberal arts adding to them medicine and architectonics.”7 His nine gave the West its canon: when Martianus Capella built his allegory of the arts on Varro’s model (c. AD 420) he dropped medicine and architecture — the arts of the body and of earthly building — fixing the enduring seven that the schools would organize into the trivium (grammar, dialectic, rhetoric) and quadrivium (arithmetic, geometry, astronomy, music) and set at the foot of the university. Varro also stands for the ideal of a single connected “circle of learning,” the enkyklios paideia behind the word encyclopedia.

Seneca (c. AD 64)

The tradition’s sharpest early critique of the liberal arts comes from within it. In his 88th Moral Letter the Stoic Seneca grants the honorific — “‘liberal studies’ are so called… because they are studies worthy of a free-born gentleman” — but at once relativizes it: “there is only one really liberal study,—that which gives a man his liberty. It is the study of wisdom.”8 The rest are merely preparatory: “the liberal arts do not conduct the soul all the way to virtue, but merely set it going in that direction.”9 A man may be learned and still a fool; study is a road toward the good, not the good itself. This subordination of all learning to a single highest wisdom — value the arts, but do not idolize them — is exactly the move the fathers make in ordering the classical curriculum to the knowledge of God.

Quintilian (c. AD 95)

Rome’s foremost schoolmaster wrote the ancient world’s fullest program of a liberal education. In the Institutio Oratoria the future orator must first pass through the whole “circle of learning” — letters, music, geometry — for “it is by such studies that the course of education described by the Greeks as… general education will be brought to its full completion,” the enkyklios paideia that the seven arts would systematize.10 And the whole formation aims at a moral end: the orator is, in Cato’s phrase, “a good man, skilled in speaking,” for “no man can be an orator unless he is a good man.”11 Learning and virtue are inseparable, and a training that produced a skilled scoundrel would have failed — the pagan tradition’s nearest approach to the Christian schools’ ordering of study to the making of a good person rather than to gain.

Clement of Alexandria (c. 200)

With Clement the classical curriculum passes from pagan hands into Christian ones, and the question the timeline poses — may a believer study the arts of the free man? — gets its first confident yes. Against Christians who thought Greek learning a snare, Clement argued that philosophy was God’s own preparatory gift to the Greeks: it “was a schoolmaster to bring ‘the Hellenic mind,’ as the law, the Hebrews, ‘to Christ,’” a “preparation, paving the way for him who is perfected in Christ.”12 He orders the whole field into a hierarchy of service: the “encyclical branches of study” — the general liberal curriculum of music, geometry, grammar, and rhetoric — “contribute to philosophy, which is their mistress; so also philosophy itself co-operates for the acquisition of wisdom… Wisdom is therefore queen of philosophy, as philosophy is of preparatory culture.”13 Read through Sarah and Hagar, secular culture is the handmaid a believer may embrace — “I embrace secular culture… as a handmaid; but thy knowledge I honour and reverence as true wife” — on the way to divine knowledge.14 This chapter, headed “Philosophy the Handmaid of Theology,” is the seed of the later formula philosophia ancilla theologiae and of the whole “spoil the Egyptians” case that Origen and Augustine would systematize.

Tertullian (c. 203)

Almost in the same years, from Latin Africa, comes the timeline’s sharpest voice against the marriage of pagan learning and the faith. In The Prescription Against Heretics Tertullian traces the church’s heresies to a single mother — “heresies are themselves instigated by philosophy” — deriving Valentinus from Plato, Marcion from the Stoics, the soul’s mortality from Epicurus, and indicting Aristotle by name for the dialectics “so far-fetched in its conjectures… retracting everything, and really treating of nothing.”15 Then the sentence that became the banner of every later Christian distrust of the classics: “What indeed has Athens to do with Jerusalem? What concord is there between the Academy and the Church?… We want no curious disputation after possessing Christ Jesus.”16 The rejection is narrower than the slogan sounds — the target is philosophy as a rival authority and a spur to restless speculation, not letters as such, and Tertullian argues it with all the rhetorical art of the pagan schools he himself had mastered — but he is the necessary counter-pole: the church’s reception of the liberal arts was contested from the first. His “Athens and Jerusalem” is the objection every later defense of Christian humanism, Jerome’s included, has to answer.

Basil the Great (c. 370)

What Clement argued in principle, Basil turned into a manual. His Address to Young Men on the Right Use of Greek Literature — written for his nephews by a bishop himself schooled at Athens — became the most influential patristic charter for a Christian liberal education. Pagan letters are a preparation for Scripture, a training ground where “so long as our immaturity forbids our understanding” the deep thought of the sacred books, “we exercise our spiritual perceptions upon profane writings… in which we perceive the truth as it were in shadows and in mirrors” — as one first grows used to “the sun’s reflection in the water” before turning the eyes “upon the very sun itself.”17 But they are to be read selectively: “altogether after the manner of bees must we use these writings, for the bees do not visit all the flowers without discrimination… So we, if wise, shall take from heathen books whatever befits us and is allied to the truth, and shall pass over the rest. And just as in culling roses we avoid the thorns, from such writings as these we will gather everything useful, and guard against the noxious.”18 The criterion of selection is virtue: attention is “to be chiefly fastened upon those many passages… in which virtue itself is praised,” for the pagan authors trace “the silhouette of virtue” that Scripture will fill in — a rehearsal of the Christian’s true goal.19 The bee-and-honey and rose-and-thorn images became the governing metaphor of Christian humanism for a millennium.

Jerome (c. 384)

No father embodies the whole debate more sharply than Jerome, who holds both poles of it at once. In Letter 22, his great treatise on virginity, he recounts a fever-dream of about 374 in which he is dragged before Christ’s judgment seat and, on professing “I am a Christian,” is answered: “Thou liest, thou art a follower of Cicero and not of Christ. For ‘where thy treasure is, there will thy heart be also,’” and is scourged until he swears never again to read “worldly books.”20 The Latin tag Ciceronianus es, non Christianus — framed just before as “What has Horace to do with the psalter, Virgil with the gospels, Cicero with the apostle?” — became the church’s classic warning against loving pagan style so well that Scripture’s plainer voice grates. Yet Jerome never kept the oath: his own Latin is steeped in the classics, and when the orator Magnus challenged him he mounted antiquity’s finest defense of the practice — the “captive woman” of Deuteronomy 21, shaved and pared and mourned before she may be wed, is his figure for “that secular wisdom which is my captive and my handmaid” cleansed of “idolatry, pleasure, error, or lust” and married to the true Israel, on the apostolic precedent of Paul quoting Epimenides, Menander, and Aratus.21 His dream is the twin of Tertullian’s “Athens and Jerusalem”; his captive woman, the twin of Augustine’s Egyptian gold — the objection and the answer, lived in one man.

Augustine of Hippo (c. 397)

Augustine gave the Latin West the image it would quote for a thousand years. In On Christian Doctrine II he answers the question every Christian schoolmaster faced — may a believer plunder the pagan curriculum? — with the typology of the Exodus: just as Israel, leaving Egypt, carried off the Egyptians’ “vessels and ornaments of gold and silver, and garments… designing them for a better use,” so “all branches of heathen learning” contain, beside their superstitions, “liberal instruction… and some most excellent precepts of morality” — “their gold and silver, which they did not create themselves, but dug out of the mines of God’s providence” — which the Christian “ought to take away from them, and to devote to their proper use in preaching the gospel.”22 He surveys the disciplines a student of Scripture needs — languages, history, dialectic, number, the knowledge of things — sorting the useful from the divinatory “superstitions” to be shunned, on the rule that “wherever truth may be found, it belongs to his Master.” But he fences the whole enterprise with charity and subordinates it to the Scripture it serves: “knowledge puffeth up, but charity edifieth,” and all the plunder of Egypt is “poor… when compared with the knowledge of Holy Scripture.”23 Where Clement argued the principle and Basil wrote the manual, Augustine supplied the proof-text — “spoiling the Egyptians” — that became the standard warrant for Christian humanism. (His newly-converted On Order of 386 had gone further, making the seven arts a ladder up to God; the aged bishop of the Retractations drew that enthusiasm back.)

Martianus Capella (c. 420)

With Rome fallen and the classical schools dying, what the Middle Ages would know of the liberal arts it would learn largely from the textbooks that now fixed them — and the first and most enduring was Martianus Capella’s The Marriage of Philology and Mercury. In this Latin allegory the seven arts, personified as learned bridesmaids, are given to the bride Philology and each in turn delivers a compendium of her discipline — Grammar an aged healer with a knife to cut away faults of speech, Dialectic a pale woman with a serpent coiled in her hand.24 By pruning Varro’s nine arts to seven — dropping medicine and architecture, the arts of the body and of building — Capella set the canon at grammar, dialectic, and rhetoric plus geometry, arithmetic, astronomy, and harmony; and because his book was copied and lectured on for a thousand years, it was his seven that the cathedral schools inherited. He fixed the number and the set, though not yet the labels trivium and quadrivium.

Boethius (c. 500)

If Capella fixed the seven, Boethius named their mathematical half and gave it a rationale. Rendering the Greek of Nicomachus of Gerasa into Latin in his On Arithmetic, he coined the word quadrivium — the “fourfold way” of arithmetic, geometry, music, and astronomy “by which we bring a superior mind from the knowledge offered by the senses to the more certain things of the intellect,” with arithmetic as “mother to the rest.”25 The four are not a heap of separate subjects but a single graded road up to wisdom, because all of them treat quantity. Boethius’s larger project — to translate and harmonize Plato and Aristotle for the Latin West — made him the schoolmaster of the medieval quadrivium; a Christian himself, and author of the theological tractates, he set the transmission of pagan learning and the defence of orthodox doctrine in one hand.

Cassiodorus (c. 560)

A retired Roman statesman who founded the monastery of Vivarium, Cassiodorus built the arts into the monastic life. His Institutions of Divine and Secular Learning yokes the two in a single course of study: Book I on Scripture and its commentators, Book II a survey of the seven secular arts as tools that unlock the sacred page — “arranged according to the seven divisions of secular letters,” grammar, rhetoric, and dialectic, then the four mathematical disciplines.26 In the same book he raised the copying of manuscripts to a form of Christian warfare — the scribe who “preach[es] to men with the hand” wounds Satan with every word of the Lord he writes — the charter under which the monasteries became the copying-houses that carried Bible and classics alike across the centuries.

Isidore of Seville (c. 620)

The transmission closes with the book the Middle Ages could not do without. Isidore’s Etymologiae, the most-copied reference work of the early medieval West, opens in its very first books with the seven liberal arts, enumerating them as a fixed set — “There are seven disciplines of the liberal arts. The first is grammar… the seventh is astronomy, which covers the law of the stars” — and defining what a “discipline” and an “art” even are.27 Gathering up Varro’s scheme, Martianus’s seven, and the handbooks of Boethius and Cassiodorus, Isidore fixed the curriculum in a form the schools would quote for eight hundred years, handing the fully-formed “seven liberal arts” to the medieval university that later schoolmen would build upon.

Hugh of St. Victor (c. 1127)

At the abbey school of Saint-Victor in Paris, on the eve of the universities, Hugh answered the ancient contempt for the trades. His Didascalicon — the first full medieval philosophy of education — drew a single map of all knowledge in which the crafts take their place as a genuine part of philosophy, naming seven mechanical arts — “fabric making, armament, commerce, agriculture, hunting, medicine, and theatrics” — and setting them art-for-art alongside the seven liberal arts, so that “in this division we find a likeness to the trivium and quadrivium.”28 He reports the old snobbery — the liberal arts were for “free and noble men,” the mechanical the “operative skill” of “the populace and the sons of men not free” — but undercuts it, for man is born “naked and unarmed” and must clothe and feed himself by craft, and so “man’s reason shines forth much more brilliantly in inventing these very things than ever it would have had man naturally possessed them.”29 The trades are no fall below the free man’s dignity but a theatre of it. Hugh dignifies them without flattening the hierarchy by ordering every art — mechanical, liberal, practical, theoretical — to one end: “to restore within us the divine likeness,” the mechanical arts relieving “those weaknesses to which our present life lies subject” in a fallen creature.30 Hence his charter for breadth against a utilitarian age: “Learn everything; you will see afterwards that nothing is superfluous.”31 His is the strongest pre-modern Christian answer to Aristotle’s and Cicero’s disdain for the paid trades — the counter-pole to the liberal-versus-servile split with which this timeline opened.

The rise of the universities (c. 1200)

Between the cathedral schools and the friars, the seven arts found a permanent home. Across the twelfth and thirteenth centuries the studia of Bologna, Paris, and Oxford hardened into universities — chartered corporations of masters and scholars — and gave the ancient curriculum an institutional shape it has never lost: the Faculty of Arts became the gateway every student passed through before the higher faculties of theology, law, and medicine, so that “graduation in arts was the common preparation for professional study, being regularly required for theology and usual for intending lawyers and physicians.”32 The arts course itself was the seven — grammar, rhetoric, and logic “grouped as the trivium; the remaining four, arithmetic, geometry, astronomy, and music, made up the quadrivium” — and from that structure descends “the notion of a curriculum of study… leading to a degree, as well as many of the degrees themselves — bachelor, as a stage toward the mastership, master, doctor, in arts, law, medicine, and theology.”33 The old principle — the liberal arts first, then the profession — was now built into the very degree a student climbed toward, and remains the architecture of the university to this day.

Thomas Aquinas (c. 1270)

The university’s master theologian gave the free/servile division its sharpest philosophical reason and, at the same time, put the seven arts firmly in their place. In the Summa Theologiae Aquinas explains why some arts alone are called “liberal”: the habits “ordained to… works of the speculative reason… are… called ‘liberal’ arts, in order to distinguish them from those arts that are ordained to works done by the body, which arts are, in a fashion, servile, inasmuch as the body is in servile subjection to the soul, and man, as regards his soul, is free [liber].”34 The line falls, as in Aristotle, on the end of the work — knowing versus making — now grounded in the soul’s freedom over the body. Yet the arts are not the summit: quoting Hugh of St. Victor, Aquinas calls the trivium and quadrivium mere “paths (viae) introducing the mind to the secrets of philosophy,”35 a propaedeutic ordered upward to philosophy and, beyond it, to the knowledge of God — the same subordination Clement and Basil had drawn, now the working assumption of the schools the seven arts had built.
