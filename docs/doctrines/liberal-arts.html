The Purpose of the Liberal Arts

Ask a parent today why a young person should read Homer, prove a theorem, or parse a Latin sentence rather than learn a trade that pays, and you are asking a question the ancient world had already framed and named. The Greeks and Romans divided the fields of study into two kinds. The liberal arts (artes liberales, from liber, “free”) were the studies thought worthy of a free citizen — pursued for their own sake, to form a mind and a character — and the Latin West eventually fixed them at seven: the trivium of grammar, rhetoric, and dialectic (logic), and the quadrivium of arithmetic, geometry, music, and astronomy. Set against them were the servile, mechanical, or (in Aristotle’s word) banausic arts — the crafts and trades learned for wages and bodily need, which the ancient writers below held to occupy the body and cramp the soul, and so to be beneath the dignity of a free man.

This page records how Christian writers answered the question that division poses: what is learning for? Some held that higher study is ordered to wisdom and virtue — and, for the Christian, to the knowledge and love of God — and so is worth pursuing even when it earns nothing; others asked whether it is idle unless it feeds a family and serves the common life. Christian writers inherited the pagan curriculum and debated what to do with it: whether the arts of a free man could be put to Christian use, whether pagan letters were plunder to be carried off from Egypt or an idol to be shunned, whether the mechanical arts the ancient writers ranked low should be ranked otherwise, and whether knowledge is an end in itself or a means to something higher. The record runs from Athens and Rome, through the fathers and the medieval schools in which the seven arts formed the arts course of the university, through the Reformers and Newman, to the twentieth century — John Dewey’s critique of the liberal/servile division, and the writing of Dorothy Sayers, C. S. Lewis, and Josef Pieper, the Great Books movement, and the classical-Christian schools and colleges.

The timeline below is ordered earliest witness first: the pagan philosophers Christian writers read, the fathers who adopted (or resisted) the classical curriculum, the medieval transmitters and schoolmen of the seven arts, the Reformers and their schools, and the modern writers who defended or criticized the tradition.

Related doctrines: Learning, the liberal arts, and Christian education — see also Christian humanism (ad fontes / bonae litterae) · vocation and the dignity of work

Timeline

The witnesses are listed earliest first — the pagan philosophers who drew the free/servile division, then the fathers who received the classical curriculum, then the medieval schools and universities, then the Reformers and modern writers — down to the twentieth-century Great Books and classical-education movements.

Plato (c. 375 BC)

Christian reflection on what learning is for draws on a book it did not write. In the Republic Plato set the education of the guardians on two pillars — “gymnastic for the body, and music for the soul” (music in the broad Greek sense, taking in literature and the arts of the Muses)1 — and then gave the whole enterprise its aim. Education, on his account, is not the pouring of information into an empty mind: he rejects those who say “they can put a knowledge into the soul which was not there before, like sight into blind eyes”; rather “the power and capacity of learning exists in the soul already,” and true education is the turning of “the whole soul… from the world of becoming into that of being,” up toward “the brightest and best of being… the good.”2 For Plato learning is ordered to contemplation of the highest, and is valuable in itself.

Aristotle (c. 335 BC)

Plato’s pupil drew the distinction between liberal and illiberal studies. In Book VIII of the Politics Aristotle held that “occupations are divided into liberal and illiberal,” and that “any occupation, art, or science, which makes the body or soul or mind of the freeman less fit for the practice or exercise of virtue, is vulgar” — naming as the paradigm case “all paid employments, for they absorb and degrade the mind.”3 Against these servile or banausic arts he set the studies pursued for their own sake: “there are branches of learning and education which we must study merely with a view to leisure spent in intellectual activity, and these are to be valued for their own sake,” while knowledge “useful in business… exist[s] for the sake of other things.”4 In Aristotle’s account the line falls not on the subject but on the end: the same act is free when done “for his own sake… or with a view to excellence” and “menial and servile” when done for hire or at another’s command.

Cicero (44 BC)

Cicero set the distinction out in Latin, in the vocabulary of artes liberales and humanitas. In De Officiis (44 BC) he graded the callings as Aristotle had graded occupations: “unbecoming to a gentleman, too, and vulgar are the means of livelihood of all hired workmen whom we pay for mere manual labour… for in their case the very wage they receive is a pledge of their slavery,” and “no workshop can have anything liberal about it.”5 The honourable callings are those requiring intelligence or conferring public benefit — medicine, architecture, teaching — and, crowning them, farming: “of all the occupations by which gain is secured, none is better than agriculture… none more becoming to a freeman.”6 On Cicero’s scale, the more an art engages the mind and serves a good beyond gain, the freer it is. Ambrose of Milan later wrote a De Officiis for the clergy on the model of this work (⚠ unverified).

Varro (c. 33 BC)

Cicero’s contemporary, the Roman antiquarian Varro, set out a list of the arts. His lost Disciplinarum Libri Novem gathered the Greek circle of studies into a Latin curriculum: the “Libri IX Disciplinarum… treats of the seven liberal arts adding to them medicine and architectonics.”7 When Martianus Capella built his allegory of the arts on Varro’s model (c. AD 420) he dropped medicine and architecture — the arts of the body and of earthly building — leaving the seven that the medieval schools grouped as the trivium (grammar, dialectic, rhetoric) and quadrivium (arithmetic, geometry, astronomy, music). Varro’s scheme is also associated with the ideal of a single connected “circle of learning,” the enkyklios paideia behind the word encyclopedia.

Seneca (c. AD 64)

The Stoic Seneca, in his 88th Moral Letter, grants the honorific — “‘liberal studies’ are so called… because they are studies worthy of a free-born gentleman” — and then qualifies it: “there is only one really liberal study,—that which gives a man his liberty. It is the study of wisdom.”8 The rest are, in his account, preparatory: “the liberal arts do not conduct the soul all the way to virtue, but merely set it going in that direction.”9 For Seneca a man may be learned and still a fool; study is a road toward the good, not the good itself, and all learning is subordinate to the single study of wisdom.

Quintilian (c. AD 95)

The Roman rhetorician Quintilian set out a full program of liberal education in the Institutio Oratoria. The future orator must first pass through the whole “circle of learning” — letters, music, geometry — for “it is by such studies that the course of education described by the Greeks as… general education will be brought to its full completion,” the enkyklios paideia.10 And the whole formation aims at a moral end: the orator is, in Cato’s phrase, “a good man, skilled in speaking,” for “no man can be an orator unless he is a good man.”11 For Quintilian learning and virtue are inseparable, and a training that produced a skilled scoundrel would have failed.

Clement of Alexandria (c. 200)

Clement of Alexandria’s Stromata takes up the question whether a Christian may study the arts of the free man, and answers yes. Addressing Christians who distrusted Greek learning, Clement argued that philosophy was God’s own preparatory gift to the Greeks: it “was a schoolmaster to bring ‘the Hellenic mind,’ as the law, the Hebrews, ‘to Christ,’” a “preparation, paving the way for him who is perfected in Christ.”12 He orders the whole field into a hierarchy of service: the “encyclical branches of study” — the general liberal curriculum of music, geometry, grammar, and rhetoric — “contribute to philosophy, which is their mistress; so also philosophy itself co-operates for the acquisition of wisdom… Wisdom is therefore queen of philosophy, as philosophy is of preparatory culture.”13 Read through Sarah and Hagar, secular culture is the handmaid a believer may embrace — “I embrace secular culture… as a handmaid; but thy knowledge I honour and reverence as true wife” — on the way to divine knowledge.14 In the Ante-Nicene Fathers translation the chapter is headed “Philosophy the Handmaid of Theology.”

Tertullian (c. 203)

In the same years, in Latin Africa, Tertullian argued against joining pagan philosophy to the faith. In The Prescription Against Heretics he traces the heresies to a single mother — “heresies are themselves instigated by philosophy” — deriving Valentinus from Plato, Marcion from the Stoics, the soul’s mortality from Epicurus, and indicting Aristotle by name for the dialectics “so far-fetched in its conjectures… retracting everything, and really treating of nothing.”15 He then asks: “What indeed has Athens to do with Jerusalem? What concord is there between the Academy and the Church?… We want no curious disputation after possessing Christ Jesus.”16 The passage’s named target is philosophy, as the source of the heresies and of “curious disputation.”

Gregory Thaumaturgus (c. 238)

The farewell oration ascribed to Gregory Thaumaturgus gives a firsthand Christian account of a whole course of liberal study put to Christian use. On leaving the school of Origen at Caesarea about 238, the young Gregory (identified by tradition with the later bishop of Neocaesarea, “the Wonderworker”) delivered a farewell oration of thanks that narrates his master’s method as a graded ascent: first logic, to school “that capacity of our mind which deals critically with words and reasonings” and to weed out hasty assent; then natural science with the mathematical arts, geometry set as “the immutable groundwork… of all,” and astronomy lifting the mind “to the things that are highest above us… as though they were ladders reaching the skies”; then ethics and the four virtues, taught “by the deeds he did more than by the doctrines he taught”; and at the summit theology and the Scriptures, all crowned by piety, “the mother of the virtues… the beginning and the end of all the virtues.”17 Along the way Origen had them read the pagan philosophers and poets discerningly rather than shun them — “we should read with utmost diligence all that has been written, both by the philosophers and by the poets of old, rejecting nothing, and repudiating nothing… except only the productions of the atheists… [who] deny that there is either a God or a providence” — leaving the student “permitted with all freedom to go round the whole circle of knowledge.”18

Basil the Great (c. 370)

Basil’s Address to Young Men on the Right Use of Greek Literature — written for his nephews by a bishop himself schooled at Athens — gives a practical rule for a Christian liberal education. Pagan letters are a preparation for Scripture, a training ground where “so long as our immaturity forbids our understanding” the deep thought of the sacred books, “we exercise our spiritual perceptions upon profane writings… in which we perceive the truth as it were in shadows and in mirrors” — as one first grows used to “the sun’s reflection in the water” before turning the eyes “upon the very sun itself.”19 But they are to be read selectively: “altogether after the manner of bees must we use these writings, for the bees do not visit all the flowers without discrimination… So we, if wise, shall take from heathen books whatever befits us and is allied to the truth, and shall pass over the rest. And just as in culling roses we avoid the thorns, from such writings as these we will gather everything useful, and guard against the noxious.”20 His criterion of selection is virtue: attention is “to be chiefly fastened upon those many passages… in which virtue itself is praised,” for in Basil’s image the pagan authors trace “the silhouette of virtue” that Scripture fills in.21 The bee-and-honey and rose-and-thorn images are Basil’s figures for that selective reading.

Gregory of Nazianzus (c. 380)

Basil’s fellow-student Gregory of Nazianzus, schooled with him at Athens, took up the question in the funeral oration he preached over Basil. He pauses the eulogy to defend secular learning against the Christians who despised it: “the first of our advantages is education; and not only this our more noble form of it… but even that external culture which many Christians ill-judgingly abhor, as treacherous and dangerous… so from secular literature we have received principles of enquiry and speculation, while we have rejected their idolatry… We must not then dishonour education, because some men are pleased to do so, but rather suppose such men to be boorish and uneducated, desiring all men to be as they themselves are, in order to… escape the detection of their want of culture.”22 He likens secular letters to fire or iron, useful or harmful “according to the will of those who use it.” He also sets limits on the arts: to have letters or virtue alone is to be “one-eyed,” while the aim is to be “ambidextrous… eminent in both alike”;23 and though he praises Basil for traversing “every branch of learning” — Rhetoric, Grammar, Philosophy and Dialectic, Astronomy, Geometry, and “numerical proportion” alike — he adds that “excessive application to them he despised, as useless to those whose desire is godliness,” and that all of it is as nothing “compared with the moral discipline of the man.”24 In the same oration he calls Athens “a city truly of gold.”25

Jerome (c. 384)

Jerome’s letters contain both a warning against classical reading and a defence of it. In Letter 22, his treatise on virginity, he recounts a fever-dream of about 374 in which he is dragged before Christ’s judgment seat and, on professing “I am a Christian,” is answered: “Thou liest, thou art a follower of Cicero and not of Christ. For ‘where thy treasure is, there will thy heart be also,’” and is scourged until he swears never again to read “worldly books.”26 The rebuke, known in Latin as Ciceronianus es, non Christianus, is preceded in the letter by the question “How can Horace go with the psalter, Virgil with the gospels, Cicero with the apostle?” About 397, when the orator Magnus questioned his use of pagan authors, Jerome defended the practice: the “captive woman” of Deuteronomy 21, shaved and pared and mourned before she may be wed, is his figure for “that secular wisdom which is my captive and my handmaid” cleansed of “idolatry, pleasure, error, or lust” and married to the true Israel, on the apostolic precedent of Paul quoting Epimenides, Menander, and Aratus.27

Augustine of Hippo (c. 397)

In On Christian Doctrine II Augustine takes up the question whether a believer may draw on the pagan curriculum, and answers with the typology of the Exodus: just as Israel, leaving Egypt, carried off the Egyptians’ “vessels and ornaments of gold and silver, and garments… designing them for a better use,” so “all branches of heathen learning” contain, beside their superstitions, “liberal instruction… and some most excellent precepts of morality” — “their gold and silver, which they did not create themselves, but dug out of the mines of God’s providence” — which the Christian “ought to take away from them, and to devote to their proper use in preaching the gospel.”28 He surveys the disciplines a student of Scripture needs — languages, history, dialectic, number, the knowledge of things — sorting the useful from the divinatory “superstitions” to be shunned, on the rule that “wherever truth may be found, it belongs to his Master.” He sets the whole enterprise under charity and subordinates it to the Scripture it serves: “knowledge puffeth up, but charity edifieth,” and all the plunder of Egypt is “poor… when compared with the knowledge of Holy Scripture.”29 The image is known as “spoiling the Egyptians.” (In the earlier On Order of 386, written soon after his conversion, he had presented the seven arts as a ladder up to God; in the later Retractations he qualified that treatment.)

Martianus Capella (c. 420)

Martianus Capella’s The Marriage of Philology and Mercury is a Latin allegory in which the seven arts, personified as learned bridesmaids, are given to the bride Philology and each in turn delivers a compendium of her discipline — Grammar an aged healer with a knife to cut away faults of speech, Dialectic a pale woman with a serpent coiled in her hand.30 His list leaves out the medicine and architecture of Varro’s nine, giving seven: grammar, dialectic, and rhetoric plus geometry, arithmetic, astronomy, and harmony. The book was copied and lectured on in the schools through the Middle Ages, and its seven are the set the cathedral schools taught (⚠ unverified — not yet sourced). Martianus does not use the labels trivium and quadrivium.

Boethius (c. 500)

Rendering the Greek of Nicomachus of Gerasa into Latin in his On Arithmetic, Boethius names the mathematical half of the arts the quadrivium — a word credited to Boethius as his coinage (⚠ unverified — not yet sourced) — the “fourfold way” of arithmetic, geometry, music, and astronomy “by which we bring a superior mind from the knowledge offered by the senses to the more certain things of the intellect,” with arithmetic as “mother to the rest.”31 In his account the four are not a heap of separate subjects but a single graded road up to wisdom, because all of them treat quantity. His larger project was to translate and harmonize Plato and Aristotle for Latin readers; a Christian, he also wrote theological tractates.

Cassiodorus (c. 560)

A retired Roman statesman who founded the monastery of Vivarium, Cassiodorus built the arts into the monastic life. His Institutions of Divine and Secular Learning yokes the two in a single course of study: Book I on Scripture and its commentators, Book II a survey of the seven secular arts as tools that unlock the sacred page — “arranged according to the seven divisions of secular letters,” grammar, rhetoric, and dialectic, then the four mathematical disciplines.32 In the same work he describes the copying of manuscripts as a form of Christian warfare — the scribe who “preach[es] to men with the hand” wounds Satan with every word of the Lord he writes.

Isidore of Seville (c. 620)

Isidore’s Etymologiae, an encyclopedic reference work widely copied in the early medieval West, opens in its first books with the seven liberal arts, enumerating them as a fixed set — “There are seven disciplines of the liberal arts. The first is grammar… the seventh is astronomy, which covers the law of the stars” — and defining what a “discipline” and an “art” are.33 His treatment stands in the line of the earlier Latin handbooks of Varro, Martianus, Boethius, and Cassiodorus.

Alcuin of York (c. 800)

The Northumbrian deacon Alcuin of York was master of the palace school at the court of Charlemagne during the Carolingian reform of learning. The Admonitio generalis of 789 ordered that schools be established for boys who could read, and that the psalms, the shorthand notes, chant, the computus, and grammar be taught — and the catholic books carefully corrected — in every monastery and bishopric.34 The circular letter on the cultivation of learning gives as its rationale that letters serve Scripture, urging the houses to study earnestly “in order that you may be able more easily and more correctly to penetrate the mysteries of the divine Scriptures,” since the “images, tropes and similar figures” of the sacred page are grasped more quickly by one “fully instructed in the mastery of letters” — “so that those who desire to please God by living rightly should not neglect to please him also by speaking correctly.”35 In his own dialogue textbooks Alcuin cast the seven arts as a graded ascent: taking up Solomon’s word that “Wisdom hath builded her house, she hath hewn out her seven pillars,” he taught that “Wisdom is also built upon the seven pillars of liberal letters,” which alone “afford us access to any perfect knowledge,” the pupils to be “led by the steps of erudition from lower to higher things.”36 In a letter to Charlemagne he wrote of a Christian Athens: “if only there were many who would follow” the emperor’s intent, “perchance a new, nay, a more excellent Athens might be founded in Frankland,” one “ennobled with the mastership of Christ” that would “surpass all the wisdom of the studies of the Academy.”37

Rabanus Maurus (c. 819)

Alcuin’s pupil Rabanus Maurus (Hrabanus Maurus) — abbot of Fulda, later archbishop of Mainz, known as the Praeceptor Germaniae, the “teacher of Germany” — wrote a handbook for the clergy. His De institutione clericorum (“On the Training of the Clergy,” 819) gives the whole of its third book to the arts a cleric who reads Scripture must master, enumerating them as a fixed set — “The first of the liberal arts is grammar; the second, rhetoric; the third, dialectic; the fourth, arithmetic; the fifth, geometry; the sixth, music; the seventh, astronomy” — and making grammar “the origin and foundation of liberal letters,” fit to be read “in the school of the Lord”. Harper’s Dictionary of Classical Antiquities calls his naming of the seven, after a full description of each, “apparently the first instance in history of the use of this term” (septem artes liberales).38 He names Augustine as his source and reproduces him almost word for word — the reader who wants more, he says, should seek it “in the books of St. Augustine, On Christian Doctrine, whence we have excerpted these things.”39 That includes the “spoiling the Egyptians” passage: if the philosophers, “especially the Platonists, have said aught that is true and in harmony with our faith, we are not only not to shrink from it, but to claim it for our own use from those who have unlawful possession of it,” just as Israel carried off Egypt’s “vessels and ornaments of gold and silver” and turned them to a better use.40 Rabanus adds only that the philosophers may have said such things “in their disputations or writings.”

Hugh of St. Victor (c. 1127)

At the abbey school of Saint-Victor in Paris, Hugh wrote the Didascalicon, a guide to the whole of study that draws a single map of knowledge in which the crafts take their place as a part of philosophy. He names seven mechanical arts — “fabric making, armament, commerce, agriculture, hunting, medicine, and theatrics” — and sets them art-for-art alongside the seven liberal arts, so that “in this division we find a likeness to the trivium and quadrivium.”41 He reports the older division — the liberal arts were for “free and noble men,” the mechanical the “operative skill” of “the populace and the sons of men not free” — and adds that man is born “naked and unarmed” and must clothe and feed himself by craft, so that “man’s reason shines forth much more brilliantly in inventing these very things than ever it would have had man naturally possessed them.”42 Hugh orders every art — mechanical, liberal, practical, theoretical — to one end: “to restore within us the divine likeness,” the mechanical arts relieving “those weaknesses to which our present life lies subject” in a fallen creature.43 His counsel to the student is breadth: “Learn everything; you will see afterwards that nothing is superfluous.”44

John of Salisbury (c. 1159)

John of Salisbury — secretary to two archbishops of Canterbury and later bishop of Chartres — wrote the Metalogicon (1159) in defence of the trivium against a critic he pseudonymously names “Cornificius,” whose sect, as John reports it, held that the study of grammar, logic, and eloquence is useless labour, since eloquence is a gift of nature that rules cannot teach and that earns no living: they “make money, by fair means, if possible, but otherwise in any way at all,” for “Wisdom’s only fruit, for them, is wealth.”45 John replies that to attack the arts of language is to attack everything built on them, for “just as eloquence, unenlightened by reason, is rash and blind, so wisdom, without the power of expression, is feeble and maimed,” and one who would banish the teaching of eloquence “undermines and uproots all liberal studies, assails the whole structure of philosophy, tears to shreds humanity’s social contract, and destroys the means of brotherly charity.”46 He also argues, against those who would pursue logic for its own sake, that the logica serves wisdom and use together, for “just as dialectic expedites other studies, so, if left alone by itself, it lies powerless and sterile” — a tool that must “conceive from an external source” to bear the fruits of philosophy.47 Along the way he describes the twelfth-century schools of Paris and Chartres — Abelard on Mont-Sainte-Geneviève, Bernard of Chartres teaching grammar through the daily reading of the authors — and preserves Bernard’s image of the moderns as “dwarfs perched on the shoulders of giants,” who see farther only because the ancients bear them aloft.48

The rise of the universities (c. 1200)

Across the twelfth and thirteenth centuries the studia of Bologna, Paris, and Oxford became universities — chartered corporations of masters and scholars — with a Faculty of Arts preparatory to the higher faculties of theology, law, and medicine. In Charles Homer Haskins’s account, “graduation in arts was the common preparation for professional study, being regularly required for theology and usual for intending lawyers and physicians.”49 The arts course itself was the seven — grammar, rhetoric, and logic “grouped as the trivium; the remaining four, arithmetic, geometry, astronomy, and music, made up the quadrivium” — and Haskins traces to this structure “the notion of a curriculum of study… leading to a degree, as well as many of the degrees themselves — bachelor, as a stage toward the mastership, master, doctor, in arts, law, medicine, and theology.”50 In this structure the liberal arts came first and the profession after, within the degree course itself.

Thomas Aquinas (c. 1270)

In the Summa Theologiae Aquinas explains why some arts alone are called “liberal”: the habits “ordained to… works of the speculative reason… are… called ‘liberal’ arts, in order to distinguish them from those arts that are ordained to works done by the body, which arts are, in a fashion, servile, inasmuch as the body is in servile subjection to the soul, and man, as regards his soul, is free [liber].”51 As in Aristotle, the distinction rests on the end of the work — knowing versus making — which Aquinas grounds in the soul’s freedom over the body. He also treats the arts as introductory: quoting Hugh of St. Victor, he calls the trivium and quadrivium “paths (viae) introducing the quick mind to the secrets of philosophy,”52 a propaedeutic ordered to philosophy.

Erasmus of Rotterdam (c. 1511)

The Renaissance humanist Erasmus joined classical study to Christian aims. Education for him is the very making of a human being: a child is “a rude, unformed creature, which it is your part to fashion so that it may become indeed a man,” for “if trees or wild beasts grow, men, believe me, are fashioned” (the Latin homines… non nascuntur, sed finguntur) — and in his account it is liberal study that fashions the animal into a man.53 His program was ad fontes, back to the sources: the inheritance of antiquity he likened to “a limpid spring of whose undefiled waters it behoves all who truly thirst to drink and be restored”; his printed Greek New Testament appeared in 1516.54 He orders the bonae litterae to a Christian end: philosophy and eloquence are studied “to know Christ, to celebrate the glory of Christ. This is the goal of all learning and all eloquence.”55

Martin Luther (1524)

In To the Councilmen of All Cities in Germany That They Establish and Maintain Christian Schools (1524), Luther grounded the schools in the languages: “we shall not long preserve the Gospel without the languages. The languages are the sheath in which this sword of the Spirit is contained.”56 He states the objection — “what is the use of teaching Latin, Greek, Hebrew, and the other liberal arts? We can still teach the Bible and God’s Word in German” — and answers that the arts are “a greater ornament, profit, honor and benefit, both for the understanding of Scripture and for the conduct of government”; setting the soul aside, he argues, the temporal realm needs “good schools and educated persons more sorely even than the spiritual realm.”57 His Sermon on Keeping Children in School (1530) addresses the parent who would school a gifted child while looking “only to the belly and to temporal livelihood”: Luther calls this a “shameful despising of God,” sticking children into “the service of the belly and of avarice… like hogs, wallowing forever with noses in the filth.”58

Philip Melanchthon (c. 1528)

Luther’s colleague Philip Melanchthon — also known as the Praeceptor Germaniae, the “teacher of Germany” — worked to rebuild the German schools and universities on the classical languages and the liberal arts. Theology, he argued, is “partly Hebrew and partly Greek… the eternal languages are to be learned, and not be just mute actors when we deal with theology”; and he held that neglect of the arts, dialectic above all, harms the church, for “without a frequent and well-timed practice of dialectics no one can study” the doctrines of Law and Gospel, the spiritual and the civil, “with sufficient safety.”59 For Melanchthon good schools are the commonwealth’s nursery: “the first task for schools is to teach youth, for they are the seedbed for the city… nor is anyone sufficiently fit to govern republics without a knowledge of that literature in which is contained all thought on the ruling of cities.”60 He wrote school ordinances and textbooks in the arts and took part in university reforms.

John Calvin (1559)

In the Institutes Calvin holds that since “the Spirit of God is the only fountain of truth,” the truth found in pagan writers is His gift, and “in despising the gifts, we insult the Giver”; where the Lord assists us “by the work and ministry of the ungodly in physics, dialectics, mathematics, and other similar sciences, let us avail ourselves of it.”61 In 1559 he founded the Geneva Academy — dedicated “in the church of St. Peter, in the presence of the whole Council, the ministers, and six hundred students” — which joined a lower school of languages and the liberal arts to a higher school of theology, with professors for “grammar, logic, mathematics, physics, music, and the ancient languages” — an academy Philip Schaff calls “the chief nursery of Protestant ministers and teachers for France.”62 The Academy served as a model for Reformed colleges elsewhere in Europe and in the New World (⚠ unverified).

The Puritans and the founding of Harvard (1636)

Six years after landing, the Massachusetts Bay colonists founded a college — the future Harvard — and in New England’s First Fruits (1643) gave their reason: “After God had carried us safe to New England, and we had builded our houses, provided necessaries for our livelihood, reared convenient places for God’s worship, and settled the civil government, one of the next things we longed for and looked after was to advance learning and perpetuate it to posterity, dreading to leave an illiterate ministry to the churches, when our present ministers shall lie in the dust.”63

John Amos Comenius (c. 1638)

The exiled Moravian bishop John Amos Comenius (Jan Komenský) set out in his Great Didactic (Didactica Magna, c. 1633–1638) “the whole Art of Teaching all Things to all Men” — the programme of pansophia, universal wisdom, whose title-page aim is at once knowledge, virtue, and piety, and at once everyone’s.64 He grounds the whole enterprise in Genesis: “the ultimate end of man is eternal happiness with God,” toward which we “advance… in proportion as we pursue Learning, Virtue, and Piety,” the three “main issues of our life” whose innate seeds are implanted in every person made in the image of God — seeds that come to actual knowledge, virtue, and piety only by cultivation, so that “it is only by a proper education that he can become a man,” and education is finally the recall of fallen man toward “our former estate.”65 From the same premise — the divine image borne by all — he answered who learning is for: “not the children of the rich or of the powerful only, but of all alike, boys and girls, both noble and ignoble, rich and poor… should be sent to school,” women no less than men, since “they also are formed in the image of God… endowed with equal sharpness of mind.”66 Comenius thus extends schooling beyond the free-born of the ancient liberal/servile division to all children.

John Milton (1644)

In the tractate Of Education (1644), an open letter to the reformer Samuel Hartlib, the poet John Milton set study on a theological end: “The end then of Learning is to repair the ruines of our first Parents by regaining to know God aright, and out of that knowledge to love him, to imitate him, to be like him, as we may the neerest by possessing our souls of true vertue, which being united to the heavenly grace of faith makes up the highest perfection.”67 For Milton learning restores in fallen man the image of God the Fall defaced, ordering all study to the knowledge and love of God. Because the mind “cannot in this body found it self but on sensible things,” the ascent to “the knowledge of God and things invisible” runs by “orderly conning over the visible and inferior creature” — so Milton lays out a classical-and-scientific course (the languages held to be “but the instrument” of knowing solid things, then grammar and the mathematical arts, natural philosophy and medicine, ethics, politics, law, and theology) whose aim he defines in a single sentence: “I call therefore a compleat and generous Education that which fits a man to perform justly, skilfully and magnanimously all the offices both private and publick of Peace and War.”68

John Henry Newman (1852)

In the discourses he gave for the founding of the Catholic University of Ireland, Newman defended liberal learning. His thesis is a single sentence: “Knowledge is capable of being its own end. Such is the constitution of the human mind, that any kind of knowledge, if it be really such, is its own reward.”69 Liberal knowledge is that “which stands on its own pretensions, which is independent of sequel… refuses to be… absorbed into any art”; in Newman’s account, the moment learning is valued only for its professional payoff it has passed from the liberal into the merely useful.70 He also holds that liberal learning is useful: “though the useful is not always good, the good is always useful,” and “a cultivated intellect, because it is a good in itself… enables us to be more useful, and to a greater number” — so the practical end of a university is “that of training good members of society… fitness for the world.”71,72 And he sets a limit: “Knowledge is one thing, virtue is another… Liberal Education makes not the Christian, not the Catholic, but the gentleman.”73 Newman thus treats learning both as a good in itself and as no substitute for holiness.

John Dewey (1916)

The American pragmatist John Dewey argued in Democracy and Education that the division of the free arts from the servile is no fixed truth about the mind but a historical and social arrangement: of “the segregations of educational values… that between culture and utility is probably the most fundamental,” yet “while the distinction is often thought to be intrinsic and absolute, it is really historical and social,” originating in a Greece where “the truly human life was lived only by a few who subsisted upon the results of the labor of others.”74 The liberal ideal Aristotle had grounded in leisure rested, Dewey pressed, on unfreedom — “slaves, artisans, and women are employed in furnishing the means of subsistence in order that others… may live the life of leisurely concern with things intrinsically worth while”75 — and the same class division fathered the whole chain of dualisms in the tradition: “as livelihood and leisure are opposed, so are theory and practice, intelligence and execution, knowledge and activity,” against which he set the pragmatist conviction that “there is no such thing as genuine knowledge and fruitful understanding except as the offspring of doing.”76 His remedy is not to set job-training over contemplation but to abolish the split itself: education is “that reconstruction or reorganization of experience” that enriches life as it is lived, not a preparation for a contemplative life apart from work, and a democratic schooling must “do away with the dualism… [and] make thought a guide of free practice for all.”77

C. S. Lewis (c. 1943)

In The Abolition of Man Lewis argued, appealing to Plato, Aristotle, and Augustine’s ordo amoris, that education’s first office is to train the emotions, so that “the aim of education is to make the pupil like and dislike what he ought”; in his account a schooling that dismisses those “just sentiments” instead “remove[s] the organ and demand[s] the function… We make men without chests and expect of them virtue and enterprise.” Where “the old initiated, the new merely ‘conditions.’”78 Four years earlier, preaching Learning in War-Time to Oxford undergraduates as war broke out, he had defended the learned life itself as a lawful Christian calling: “all our merely natural activities will be accepted, if they are offered to God,” and “the life of learning, humbly offered to God,” is “in its own small way, one of the appointed approaches to the Divine reality and the Divine beauty which we hope to enjoy hereafter.”79 Lewis writes no curriculum; his concern is that education forms loves, not merely minds.

Dorothy L. Sayers (c. 1947)

In The Lost Tools of Learning, a paper read at Oxford in 1947, Dorothy L. Sayers charged that modern schooling stocks pupils with “subjects” but has dropped the medieval Trivium’s older aim: “although we often succeed in teaching our pupils ‘subjects,’ we fail lamentably on the whole in teaching them how to think: they learn everything, except the art of learning.” The Trivium of grammar, dialectic, and rhetoric (see Martianus Capella and Isidore above) existed, she argued, “to teach the pupil the proper use of the tools of learning, before he began to apply them to ‘subjects’ at all,” for “the sole true end of education is simply this: to teach men how to learn for themselves.”80 She recast the three arts as three stages of childhood — the “Poll-Parrot,” “Pert,” and “Poetic” ages — mapping “Grammar to the Poll-Parrot, Dialectic to the Pert, and Rhetoric to the Poetic age.”81 The grammar/logic/rhetoric stages were later taken up by classical schools (see the classical-education revival below).

Josef Pieper (c. 1948)

In Leisure: The Basis of Culture the German Thomist Josef Pieper recovers the classical sense of leisure (Greek scholē) as receptive contemplation rather than idleness, and sets it against “the world of total work” that “threatens to become… our only world.” His philological point is that “the name for the institutions of education and learning means ‘leisure’” — the word school descends from scholē.82 Following Aquinas, he grounds the ancient distinction in the end of the act: “‘Liberal arts’… are ways of human action which have their justification in themselves; ‘servile arts’ are ways of human action that have a purpose outside of themselves,” so that the liberal arts “do not need to be legitimated by a social function, by being ‘work.’” The question, he presses, is whether there survives “an area of human existence… that does not have its justification by being incorporated into a five-year plan.”83

Robert Hutchins & Mortimer Adler and the Great Books movement (1936–1982)

A secular case for liberal learning came from the University of Chicago. In The Higher Learning in America (1936) its president Robert Maynard Hutchins charged the modern university with surrendering to vocationalism and “the love of money” — the “service-station” university that would “help adults get better jobs” but had become “that strangest of modern phenomena, an anti-intellectual university” — and against the elective system proposed a common core of “permanent studies,” the great books, on the ground that “the truth is everywhere the same. Hence education should be everywhere the same.”84 With Mortimer J. Adler, whom he had brought to Chicago in 1930 to co-teach its great-books seminar, Hutchins carried the program into the fifty-four-volume Great Books of the Western World (1952), whose opening essay names the Western tradition “the Great Conversation” and defines liberal education as “the education of free men,” unlike training “concerned with the means of life, with earning a living, and not with its ends” — and holds, unlike the ancient restriction to the free-born, that “democracy requires liberal education for all.”85 Adler carried that democratic argument into the schools in The Paideia Proposal (1982), calling for one required liberal curriculum for every child with no vocational track, since “vocational training, training for particular jobs, is not the education of free men and women” and a classless polity should be “an educationally classless society.”86

The classical-education revival (c. 1991–present)

Douglas Wilson’s Recovering the Lost Tools of Learning (1991) took its title and its framework from Sayers’s essay, arguing that education is never neutral but always rests on someone’s worldview, and building the grammar/logic/rhetoric stages into a Christian school.87 In Moscow, Idaho, New Saint Andrews College, a Reformed liberal-arts college whose integrated undergraduate course “follows the Trivium and Quadrivium” and reads the great books rather than textbooks, opened in 1994; the Association of Classical Christian Schools, formed in the early 1990s, is today the accrediting body for hundreds of such schools.88 New Saint Andrews’ curriculum follows the Trivium and Quadrivium; the secular Great Books movement of Hutchins and Adler is described above.
