Tahrif and the Islamic Restoration (were the earlier scriptures corrupted, needing a final prophet?)

The Islamic doctrine of tahrif (“alteration,” “corruption”) holds that the scriptures God gave before the Qur’an — the Tawrat of Moses, the Zabur of David, and above all the Injil (Gospel) revealed to Jesus — did not survive intact: their communities altered them, whether in the actual words (tahrif al-lafz) or in the meaning read into them (tahrif al-ma‘na), so that the pure monotheism Jesus preached was overlaid by the doctrines of the Trinity, the divinity of Christ, and the crucifixion. The charge rests on the Qur’anic passages that accuse People of the Book of “distorting” (yuharrifuna) the word of God.1 It is the negative premise of Islam’s own apostasy-and-restoration story: humanity was one believing community, each nation corrupted the message it was sent and God raised up a prophet to restore it, until Muhammad, the “Seal of the Prophets,” came to restore and complete the original faith of Abraham.2

This is the Islamic counterpart to the Christian Great Apostasy: where the Latter-day Saint thesis says the church fell and had to be restored, the tahrif thesis says the earlier scriptures were corrupted and had to be superseded by a final revelation. This wiki is well placed to weigh it, because tahrif is at bottom a historical claim about the transmission of texts — the terrain its canon, Old Testament canon, and textual-corruption timelines already document. Each strand below is steelmanned and rated on the evidence, and the supernatural is not excluded a priori; this is adjudication, not polemic. The claim is also contested among Muslims themselves: many classical and modern authorities — from al-Qasim b. Ibrahim in the ninth century to the reformer Muhammad ‘Abduh — are read as holding that only the meaning, not the text, was corrupted, ‘Abduh judging a coordinated textual corruption across the scattered churches impossible to have achieved (al-Qasim’s own practice, as it turns out, is less tidy than the label — see below).3

Related doctrines: The apostasy-and-restoration thesis — see also the Great Apostasy (the Christian-restoration form: a church said to have fallen wholesale, needing restoration) · the Book of Mormon and the material record (the new scripture one such restoration produced) · the First Vision (that restoration’s founding theophany, weighed source-critically).

Timeline

The Qur’an (c. 610–632)

The charge begins in the Qur’an itself. Four passages accuse People of the Book of “distorting” (yuharrifuna) the word of God — twisting it with their tongues, shifting words “from their [right] places,” and concealing what had been revealed (the four verses cited above). Yet alongside the accusation the Qur’an affirms the earlier books: it says God gave Jesus the Injil, “in which was guidance and light,” and presents its own revelation as confirming the scripture before it and standing muhaymin (“guardian”) over it.4 Whether these verses charge a corruption of the text or only of its reading — and whether they presuppose a Gospel still intact in Christian hands in Muhammad’s own day — is the question the later tradition divides over.

al-Qasim b. Ibrahim al-Rassi (c. 785–860)

The earliest surviving sustained Muslim refutation of Christianity, the Radd ‘ala al-Nasara of the Zaydi imam al-Qasim b. Ibrahim al-Rassi (written in Egypt c. 826), is conventionally cited as an early home of the tahrif al-ma‘na reading: al-Qasim attacks Christian doctrine by re-reading the Gospels, faulting the church’s interpretation rather than charging that the words were rewritten. His editor Di Matteo concluded that “al-Qasim does not accuse Christians of corrupting the actual text of scripture,” and this is the ninth-century head of the “meaning-only” counter-current that later runs through to Muhammad ‘Abduh.5 The label is tidier than the man, however: al-Qasim’s opening argument is a transmission suspicion (the Gospels reached Christians through Jews “whom they regard as untrustworthy”), and in translating parts of Matthew he does not merely reinterpret but silently excises and rewrites — omitting Jesus’ teaching on turning the other cheek, recasting the temptation narrative — so that David Thomas can say his method “did not involve new interpretation but actual editing and rewriting of the transmitted text itself.” The founder of the interpretive-corruption reading in practice treats the text as contaminated and emends it.6

Ibn Hazm of Cordoba (c. 994–1064)

Where al-Qasim faulted the reading, the Andalusian Zahiri jurist Ibn Hazm of Cordoba attacks the words. His Kitab al-Fisal fi al-Milal wa’l-Ahwa’ wa’l-Nihal (mid-11th century) devotes some 140 pages to a catalogue of alleged contradictions and impossibilities in the Torah and the four Gospels, argued to prove that the biblical text is “other than that which Allah… revealed” — the most systematic classical case for tahrif al-lafz, corruption of the very wording. He grounds it on the fragility of the Bible’s transmission (the Torah, unlike the mass-transmitted Qur’an, kept in a single priestly line across periods when Israel, he claims, wholly lapsed) and on the divergence of the Hebrew, Samaritan, and Greek Torahs; the Gospels, contradicting one another on the call of the apostles, he brands “the works of accursed liars.”7 His novelty is to name a culprit — recasting Ezra the scribe from the pious restorer of earlier tradition into a deliberate forger. He is not quite the first to charge textual corruption (al-Maqdisi, d. after 966, precedes him, and his eastern contemporary al-Juwayni argued it independently), but he is its most thoroughgoing exponent, “a departure from the prevailing opinion before his time” that later writers followed only with careful qualification.8

al-Qurtubi and Ibn Kathir: the single revealed Injil (c. 1270–1370)

By the classical high period the accusation of corruption rests on a settled positive doctrine of what the Gospel was. Two of the most influential Sunni commentators — the Andalusian Maliki jurist al-Qurtubi (Muhammad ibn Ahmad al-Qurtubi, d. 671/1273), in his al-Jami‘ li-Ahkam al-Qur’an, and the Damascene traditionist Ibn Kathir (d. 774/1373), in the most widely read of the classical tafsirs — read the Qur’an’s Injil verses to mean that the Injil is a single revelation given directly to Jesus, parallel to the Torah given to Moses and the Qur’an given to Muhammad, and not the four canonical Gospels the church transmits, which are human accounts holding at most a partial, altered residue of it.9 Ibn Kathir supplies the exegetical spine: on Q 5:46 the Injil’s “guidance and light” is “a guidance that directs to the truth and a light that removes the doubts,” and on Q 5:48 the Qur’an is muhaymin — “trustworthy over the Books that preceded it,” so that “whatever in these previous Books conforms to the Qur’an is true, and whatever disagrees with the Qur’an is false.” The Gospel in Christian hands is thereby demoted from a self-authenticating revelation to a text the Qur’an must certify.10 This “one lost Injil, not the four Gospels” reading — put crisply by the modern translator Yusuf Ali, that the Injil is “not the four Gospels now received as canonical” but “the single Gospel which… was revealed to Jesus,” of which only “fragments” survive — is the premise on which the whole charge against the Gospel turns: if the true Injil is lost, the Christians’ books can only be its corrupted remains.11

Ibn Taymiyya and the moderate middle (al-Jawab al-Sahih, c. 1316–1321)

The major systematic Muslim polemic against Christianity is the al-Jawab al-Sahih li-man baddala din al-Masih (“The Correct Answer to Those Who Changed the Religion of Christ”) of the Damascene Hanbali jurist Ibn Taymiyya (d. 1328) — written as a reply to a Christian Letter from the People of Cyprus, and, though composed a generation before his own student Ibn Kathir’s tafsir above, placed here because the two single-Injil tafsirs read together as one block. On corruption he takes a deliberately intermediate line: against Ibn Hazm’s verdict that the Bible is a wholesale forgery not worth engaging, and against the Ash‘arite view (al-Ghazali, Fakhr al-Din al-Razi) that the text is basically sound and merely misread, he holds that alteration of both wording and meaning occurred — but that the whole text was not rewritten, so the Gospels are “reduced, not rejected,” demoted to a secondary, Sunnah-level testimony rather than a Qur’an-level revelation.12 He grants a real tahrif al-lafz — the Gospels, unlike the mass-memorized Qur’an, rest on manuscript copies held by a learned few and so are alterable, and errors crept in through translation — but treats these as largely technical defects, secondary to the graver charge: the corrupted meaning. “There is nothing in their books,” he writes, “that proves through the text or its obvious meaning the Creed that is the root of their religion… such as the Trinity, the Union and Incarnation”; the defining doctrines are innovations (bida‘) that Christians substituted for the prophetic message, traced to Constantine’s council of “three hundred eighteen participants” — so that the religion of Christ was, per the title, changed, and stands in need of the final revelation that restores it.13 The Torah he rates higher — “the second Book after the Qur’an” — yet not immune, its transmission “interrupted when Jerusalem was destroyed the first time” and dictated by Ezra; but where Ibn Hazm made Ezra a deliberate forger, Ibn Taymiyya is careful and non-hostile, allowing only that a text preserved by a few is liable to error. He is the bridge witness between the hard-textual (Ibn Hazm) and meaning-only (al-Qasim, ‘Abduh) camps.14
