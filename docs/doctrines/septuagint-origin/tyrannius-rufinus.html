Rufinus of Aquileia on the Origin of the Septuagint

Dates: c. AD 401 · The Apology of Rufinus II.33–35
Summary: The account as an argument. Rufinus, the monk and translator of Aquileia, does not retell the origin story but invokes it against his former friend Jerome, who was then rendering the Old Testament afresh from the Hebrew. The seventy translators, Rufinus insists, worked "each in their separate cells" and produced "a version couched in consonant and identical words, under the inspiration, as we cannot doubt, of the Holy Spirit" — and he argues that a single man's correction of that version from the Hebrew is a "change" to "the divine record handed down to the Churches by the Apostles." Jerome's Hebrew "truth," he says, was "bought with money from the Synagogue." The corruption charge is recorded on its own timeline.

Claims & evidence

The separate cells, the Holy Spirit, and the authority of the Seventy

"In all this abundance of learned men, has there been one who has dared to make havoc of the divine record handed down to the Churches by the Apostles and the deposit of the Holy Spirit? For what can we call it but havoc, when some parts of it are transformed, and this is called the correction of an error? For instance, the whole of the history of Susanna, which gave a lesson of chastity to the churches of God, has by him been cut out, thrown aside and dismissed. The hymn of the three children, which is regularly sung on festivals in the Church of God, he has wholly erased from the place where it stood… This, however, cannot be passed over. The seventy translators, each in their separate cells, produced a version couched in consonant and identical words, under the inspiration, as we cannot doubt, of the Holy Spirit; and this version must certainly be of more authority with us than a translation made by a single man under the inspiration of Barabbas."

— The Apology of Rufinus II.33, source.1 Rufinus repeats the separate-cells miracle in the form found in Irenaeus and Augustine — the translators sealed apart ("each in their separate cells") yet producing "consonant and identical words" by "the inspiration… of the Holy Spirit" — and he argues from it to the authority of the version. The argument is from authority: a version the Holy Spirit produced through seventy men in sealed isolation outranks "a translation made by a single man," and that single man is Jerome, whose Hebrew teacher (Bar-anina, here called "Barabbas") Rufinus names as the inspiration behind the new rendering. The "havoc" he names is specific: Jerome's Latin from the Hebrew dropped the Greek additions to Daniel — the story of Susanna and the Song of the Three Children — that the Septuagint contained and the Church sang.

Jerome's revision as a change to the divine record

"But this emendation of the Seventy, what are we to think of it? Is it not evident, how greatly the grounds for the heathens' unbelief have been increased by this proceeding? For they take notice of what is going on amongst us. They know that our law has been amended, or at least changed; and do you suppose they do not say among themselves, 'These people are wandering at random, they have no fixed truth among them, for you see how they make amendments and corrections in their laws whenever they please,' and indeed it is evident that there must have been previous error where amendment has supervened, and that things which undergo change at the hand of man cannot possibly be divine… Now therefore after four hundred years the truth of the law comes forth for us, it has been bought with money from the Synagogue."

— The Apology of Rufinus II.35, source.2 Because the Septuagint was the inspired, Spirit-given text, Rufinus reasons, any revision of it from the synagogue's Hebrew is not a recovery of the original but a corruption of the divine record — and a scandal to the watching pagans, who conclude that a faith that "amend[s]… their laws whenever they please" has "no fixed truth." For Rufinus the contemporary Hebrew, not the Septuagint, is the suspect text: the new "truth of the law… has been bought with money from the Synagogue." The inference that the divergence between the Greek and the Jews' Hebrew lies with the Hebrew is recorded on the rabbinic-corruption timeline.

Notes

  • Arguing from the account. The earlier witnesses on this timeline narrate the origin — the king, the elders, the cells. Rufinus takes it as settled and argues from it: the inspired-cells account is the major premise of his case against Jerome's Hebrew translation, and in his argument an inspired Septuagint places the synagogue's divergent Hebrew under suspicion. Jerome holds the opposite — he writes that he does not know "who was the first author to construct with his lying the seventy cells in Alexandria," and prefers the Hebraica veritas — so Rufinus and Jerome stand on opposite sides of the dispute.
  • Cells and inspiration. Rufinus has both the cells and the inspiration, in the Irenaean form: "each in their separate cells… under the inspiration… of the Holy Spirit." He adds no new physical detail (no cots on Pharos, no paired chambers); in his argument the cells guarantee the independent agreement.
  • "Barabbas." Throughout the Apology Rufinus calls Jerome's Jewish Hebrew tutor "Barabbas" — a pun on the man's real name (Bar-anina) and a charge that Jerome learned his Old Testament "from the Synagogue" rather than from the Church. The contrast structures the whole passage: the Seventy under the Holy Spirit, against one man under "Barabbas."
  • Date and text. Rufinus wrote the two books of his Apology (against Jerome) at Aquileia c. AD 401, a decade before his death in 411. The English above is the W. H. Fremantle translation (Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, 2nd series, vol. 3; New Advent). Rufinus is placed on the timeline at c. 401, after Epiphanius of Salamis and before Augustine.
