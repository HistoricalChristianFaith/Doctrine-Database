Ibn al-Rahib on the Origin of the Septuagint

Dates: c. AD 1257 · Kitāb al-Tawārīkh (Book of Histories) · Wikipedia
Summary: The account in Coptic Arabic, with a reconciled count and two biblical figures among the translators. Ibn al-Rāhib — the Coptic Egyptian encyclopedist Abū Shākir ibn Buṭrus al-Rāhib — gives the origin of the Greek Scriptures a single sentence in the historical section of his Kitāb al-Tawārīkh: “Batlamiyus, called also al-Iskandarus… summoned seventy two elders of the elders of the Children of Israel. And two of them died on the way, so that there remained (only) seventy. They interpreted for him the book of the Torah and the prophets; and among them was El’azar, whom Antiyakhus slew when he did not bow down to his idol; and also among their number was Sam’an al-kahin who carried our lord in his arms.”1 For the through-line this timeline traces, Ibn al-Rāhib carries neither the separate cells nor the inspiration miracle — the royal commission of the Arabic notices — and two features of his notice are not found in earlier witnesses here: he reconciles the “seventy” / “seventy-two” count by having two of the seventy-two die on the road, and he places two biblical figures among the translators — the martyr Eleazar of 2 Maccabees and Simeon of the Nunc Dimittis.

Claims & evidence

The notice in the chronicle

Ibn al-Rāhib is a chronologist by trade — the bulk of the Kitāb al-Tawārīkh is calendrical and astronomical, the historical matter compressed into its closing chapters — and the Septuagint enters as one annotated entry among the Ptolemaic kings, not as a story to be expounded. Like his fellow chroniclers Solomon of Basra and Michael the Syrian he gives it in the register of the king-list, pinned to a reign:

“Batlamiyus, called also al-Iskandarus; and it was he who summoned seventy two elders of the elders of the Children of Israel. And two of them died on the way, so that there remained (only) seventy. They interpreted (Ar. fassaru) for him the book of the Torah (Ar. tawrat) and the prophets; and among them was El’azar, whom Antiyakhus slew when he did not bow down to his idol (Ar. li-wathanihi); and also among their number was Sam’an al-kahin who carried our lord (Ar. sayyidana) in his arms.”1

The frame has only king and translators: no Demetrius the librarian, no high priest sending the men from Jerusalem, no Pharos, no count of days. He widens the scope past the Law to “the Torah and the prophets” — as do Justin, Clement, Cyril of Alexandria, and the Syriac tellers — and gives the king a double name, “Batlamiyus, called also al-Iskandarus,” Ptolemy and Alexander, as Eutychius had.

Two die on the road — seventy-two summoned, seventy arriving

His notice addresses a difference in the count found across the tradition. The Letter of Aristeas had “seventy-two” (six from each of the twelve tribes); but from Irenaeus onward many tellers rounded the number to “seventy,” and both figures appear down the timeline — the version named for “the Seventy” yet staffed, on the roster, by seventy-two. Epiphanius had reconciled them typologically: Moses took seventy elders up the mountain, and two were added for evenness among the tribes. Ibn al-Rāhib reconciles them narratively instead: seventy-two are summoned, “two of them died on the way, so that there remained (only) seventy” — attrition on the road from Jerusalem turning the Aristean seventy-two into the round seventy by the time the work begins. This explanation, by an incident rather than a symbol, has its earliest attestation on this timeline here.

Eleazar the martyr among the translators

Ibn al-Rāhib names one translator: “El’azar, whom Antiyakhus slew when he did not bow down to his idol.” The name is an old one in the legend — in Aristeas and the fuller classical tellers Eleazar is the high priest at Jerusalem who sends the translators — but here an Eleazar is one of the Seventy, and the notice identifies him (“whom Antiyakhus slew”) with the aged scribe of 2 Maccabees 6, martyred under Antiochus IV Epiphanes for refusing to eat or to feign eating the forbidden meat. Ptolemy II reigned in the third century BC, and Antiochus IV’s persecution falls in the second. Other tellers also place figures of later date among the translators (Aristobulus “among the seventy interpreters” in Anatolius as quoted by Eusebius; the Simeon of Luke, below).

Simeon of the Nunc Dimittis among the translators

The last clause reads: “among their number was Sam’an al-kahin who carried our lord in his arms.” Sam’an al-kahin — Simeon the priest — is the Simeon of Luke 2:25–35, the just and devout man to whom it was revealed “that he should not see death, before he had seen the Lord’s Christ,” who took the infant Jesus up in his arms in the Temple and sang the Nunc Dimittis. Ibn al-Rāhib makes him one of the seventy-two translators — the identification at the centre of the story, told earlier by Eutychius and the Syriac bar Kepha, in which one of the Seventy, balking at the messianic prophecies (in some tellings the “virgin” of Isaiah 7:14), is kept alive some three hundred and fifty years until he holds the promised Child and is released. Ibn al-Rāhib gives only the identification; his near-contemporary and reader al-Makin (Jirjis al-Makin Ibn al-‘Amid, c. 1273), who drew heavily on the Kitāb al-Tawārīkh, spells out the full story — Sam’an grieving over the messianic prophecies he translates, praying for length of days, living 350 years, taking the Christ in his arms in the Temple and dying at once with the words of the Nunc Dimittis. In the story the translator of the prophecies lives to see them fulfilled.

Cells and miracle both absent

For the question this timeline follows — whether each witness carries the separate-cells device and the miracle of independently-identical drafts — Ibn al-Rāhib carries neither. There is no isolation, no agreement-test, no language of the Holy Spirit; the elders “interpreted for him the book of the Torah and the prophets.” In this he stands with the commission-only tellers — Aristeas, Tertullian, Ishodad of Merv, Solomon of Basra — rather than with the cells form of Cyril of Jerusalem, Epiphanius, or the Syriac bar Kepha and Michael. His notice has no separate cells or inspired unanimity, and places the martyr and the Christ-bearer among the band.

Notes

  • The work. The Kitāb al-Tawārīkh (Book of Histories) is the principal work of Abū Shākir ibn Buṭrus al-Rāhib, a vast calendrical-historical encyclopedia in fifty-one chapters: the first forty-seven on chronology and astronomy, the last few on world history, Islamic history, and the patriarchs of Alexandria. The Septuagint notice falls in the world-history matter. The work is precisely dated by its own multi-calendar colophon to 655 of the Hijra / 973 of the Martyrs / 1257 of the Christian era (the conventional “c. 1260” rounds this).
  • Who he was. Ibn al-Rāhib (Nushū’ al-Khilāfa Abū Shākir ibn Buṭrus al-Rāhib, c. 1205 – c. 1295) was a Coptic polymath and encyclopedist of Old Cairo, from a prominent family of Coptic notables and a deacon of the Church of the Virgin known as al-Mu‘allaqah (the Hanging Church). His contemporary al-Makin Ibn al-‘Amid (c. 1273) drew heavily on his chronicle, and through al-Makin later Muslim historians (Ibn Khaldūn, al-Maqrīzī, al-Qalqashandī) cite him (⚠ unverified).
  • Cells and inspiration: both absent. No separate cells, no agreement-test, no Holy Spirit — the royal commission alone. On this timeline he stands with the commission-only tellers, rather than with the cells form told by Michael half a century before.
  • What he adds. The reconciliation of seventy / seventy-two by the death of two translators on the road; the scope widened to “the Torah and the prophets”; an Eleazar among the translators, identified with the 2 Maccabees martyr; and Simeon of Luke 2 (“Sam’an al-kahin who carried our lord in his arms”) placed among the band — the Simeon-the-Just identification in brief, told more fully by al-Makin (c. 1273).
  • Text and provenance. ⚠ This block is cited from the verbatim English translation printed by Abraham & David J. Wasserstein, The Legend of the Septuagint (Cambridge, 2006); the underlying Arabic of the Kitāb al-Tawārīkh — critically edited by S. Q. Mu‘awwaḍ (Samuel Moawad), Kitāb al-Tawārīkh, part 1 (Cairo, 2016), with source-studies by Adel Sidarus — has not been independently collated here. (The anonymous Chronicon Orientale, ed. L. Cheikho in CSCO, is a separate work long misattributed to “Peter ibn al-Rāhib” and should not be taken for the Kitāb al-Tawārīkh itself.)
