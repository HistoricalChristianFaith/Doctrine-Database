Moses bar Kepha on the Origin of the Septuagint

Dates: c. AD 903 (d. 903) · Hexaemeron Commentary · Wikipedia
Summary: The cells, without naming the Spirit. Moses bar Kepha (Mushe bar Kipho, c. 813–903), bishop of the Syriac Orthodox Church, retells the origin in his Hexaemeron Commentary: Ptolemy Philadelphus, a lover of wisdom collecting books, sends to the Hebrews for the Old Testament and summons "seventy two men… who knew the Hebrew and the Greek languages very well," then assigns "each of them a separate cell… in order to see whether they agreed with each other or not," and "all of them agreed… very exactly." He has the separate cells — absent from the notice of his fellow Syriac writer Ishodad (Church of the East) a half-century before — and the seventy-two, but stops at the agreement: he reports the cells-test and the perfect match without pronouncing, as Eustathius had, that the work was "made by the power of God." In a separate note he argues from the Septuagint's antiquity to the corruption charge against the later Jewish versions. He is also, in his Book of Homilies, the earliest dated witness on these pages to the story that the Simeon of Luke 2 who took the infant Christ had himself been one of the seventy-two, kept alive after balking at the "virgin" of Isaiah 7:14 — treated below and on the Simeon-the-Just disputed reading. ⚠ This witness is cited from the verbatim translation printed by the Wassersteins; the underlying Syriac of the Hexaemeron has not been independently collated against a critical edition.

Claims & evidence

The account retold — the cells, and the agreement reported

Bar Kepha opens his account of the Greek versions with the origin story in the separate-cells form:

"This translation by the Seventy arose in the following manner: Ptolemy the king of Egypt who was called Philadelphus loved wisdom and collected many books from many places. And he sent to the Hebrews as well and had the books of the Old Testament brought from them. And he summoned seventy two men of the Hebrews who knew the Hebrew and the Greek languages very well. And he had the Old Testament translated, and they translated it from Hebrew into Greek. He assigned each of them a separate cell to live in, in order to see whether they agreed with each other or not (in their translations). And it turned out that all of them agreed with each other very exactly."1

For the through-line this timeline traces — whether each teller carries the separate cells and the inspiration claim — bar Kepha carries the cells but not the explicit miracle. The isolation is present, with the function it has in Irenaeus: Ptolemy seals each translator apart "in order to see whether they agreed… or not," the test also found in Irenaeus and the Cohortatio. And the test succeeds — "all of them agreed… very exactly." But where Eustathius of Tarihan a century and a half earlier had drawn the conclusion in so many words — the king "believed that the translation had been made by the power of God" — bar Kepha stops at the agreement and does not state a cause.

Cells in the Syriac tellings

The Syriac tellings on this timeline differ. Eustathius of Tarihan (c. 750) told it whole — cells, isolation, and "the power of God." Ishodad of Merv (c. 850) gives a royal commission without the cells, describing the translators by their competence. Bar Kepha (c. 903) has the cells — "a separate cell to live in" for each man — and with them the agreement-test, and shares with Ishodad the note that the translators "knew the Hebrew and the Greek languages very well." Across these three Syriac tellings, over a century and a half, the account appears with cells and "the power of God," then without cells, then with cells but without the named Spirit.

The count, the scope, and the king

The number is the "seventy-two" of Aristeas (which Ishodad also has), where the round "seventy" is given by Eusebius and the Syriac Zacharias. The king is named — "Ptolemy… called Philadelphus" — and given the familiar library-builder motive ("loved wisdom and collected many books"). The scope is the whole "Old Testament," not the Pentateuch alone, as in Justin, Clement, and Cyril of Alexandria. He gives no Demetrius, no Eleazar, no Pharos, no count of days, no roster of names; his account has king, motive, seventy-two, cells, and exact agreement.

A second notice: Simeon among the Seventy, and the Isaiah 7:14 doubt (c. 903)

Bar Kepha is also the earliest dated witness on these pages to a story connected with the origin account and attested in later Syriac, Arabic and Coptic writers: the tale that the aged Simeon of Luke 2 — who took the infant Christ and sang the Nunc Dimittis — had himself been one of Ptolemy's seventy-two translators, and that, balking at the "virgin" of Isaiah 7:14 as he rendered it, he was kept alive until he could hold the promised Child. It comes not in the Hexaemeron but in his Book of Homilies: appended to a homily "On the Circumcision of Christ," and introduced "from the commentary on Luke" — that is, drawn from his now-lost Luke commentary — bar Kepha gives a list of seven rival identifications of the Gospel Simeon. The fifth is the translator identification:

"Others say that he was one of the 72 translators; and when he was rendering the book of Isaiah from Hebrew into Greek at the command of Ptolemy in Egypt, he doubted and said, 'A virgin cannot conceive and give birth,' and he was bound 272 years until Christ."2

The same seven-fold list reappears a quarter-millennium later in the Luke commentary of Dionysius bar Salibi (d. 1171), with the lifespan given as 278 years; J. F. Coakley judges the list older than bar Salibi and likely drawn from bar Kepha's lost Luke commentary (note 2). The story is offered as one option among seven. In it the translator's doubt is tied explicitly to the "virgin" of Isaiah 7:14, at c. 903 and in Syriac. Eutychius (c. 938) ties the doubt to the messianic prophecies in general; the Coptic-Arabic tellers (from Ibn al-Rāhib, c. 1257) and the Coptic Synaxarium are later, the Synaxarium adding the written "girl" and the rebuking angel. The story's bearing on the Septuagint's "virgin" is taken up on the Simeon-the-Just disputed reading.

Notes

  • The same author on the other timeline. Bar Kepha appears on two timelines. Here he is a witness to the origin account. On the Rabbinic Corruption timeline, in a note appended to this same survey of the Greek versions, he ranks the Septuagint the best — because the New Testament quotes it and (citing Philoxenus of Mabbug) because it was made "long before the appearance of Our Lord," so "the Jews had neither reason nor pretext… to corrupt the text; but in the other translations the relevant passages were corrupted through the envy of the Jews." Both the origin account and the corruption charge thus appear in his work, as they do in Rufinus's in the Latin West.
  • Who he was. Moses bar Kepha was a monk and then bishop (taking the name Severus) of Beth-Raman, Beth-Kiyonaya, and Mosul, and a prolific Syriac Orthodox author — commentaries on much of the Old and New Testaments, the Hexaemeron from which this passage comes, and treatises on Paradise, the soul, and the liturgy. He compiled and reframed earlier Syriac and Greek sources, and his survey of the Greek versions gathers the origin account and the corruption argument together.
  • Text and provenance. The English is quoted verbatim as printed in Abraham & David J. Wasserstein, The Legend of the Septuagint: From Classical Antiquity to Today (Cambridge, 2006), which translates the relevant passage of bar Kepha's Hexaemeron Commentary. There is no critical edition of the Syriac (the Hexaemeron survives in manuscript, chiefly BnF Syr. 241); the standard published rendering is L. Schlimme, Der Hexaemeronkommentar des Moses bar Kepha (Wiesbaden: Harrassowitz, 1977), 2 vols. (German translation and study). Neither the manuscript nor Schlimme's German has been collated against the Wassersteins' English here, so the quotation is sourced to the Wassersteins' verbatim translation. ⚠ Not independently collated against the Syriac.
