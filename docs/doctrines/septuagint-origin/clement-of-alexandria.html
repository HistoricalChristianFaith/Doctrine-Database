Clement of Alexandria on the Origin of the Septuagint

Dates: c. AD 200 · Wikipedia
Summary: An inspired translation, without separate cells. In the Stromata, arguing that the Greek philosophers borrowed from Moses, Clement reports the Septuagint as itself an inspired work: the people of Jerusalem sent "seventy elders" who, when their separately-made drafts were "compared together," "agreed both in meaning and expression," for the God "who gave the prophecy" also "produced the translation, and made it as it were Greek prophecy." He has the inspiration claim and the Esdras parallel found earlier in Irenaeus, but not Irenaeus's separate-cells device — his translators reach their agreement by comparison, not by isolation. He counts "seventy," not seventy-two, and is openly unsure which Ptolemy commissioned the work.

Claims & evidence

An inspired translation, with the agreement reached by comparison

"It is said that the Scriptures both of the law and of the prophets were translated from the dialect of the Hebrews into the Greek language in the reign of Ptolemy the son of Lagos, or, according to others, of Ptolemy surnamed Philadelphus; Demetrius Phalereus bringing to this task the greatest earnestness… the king being ambitious of adorning the library he had at Alexandria with all writings, desired the people of Jerusalem to translate the prophecies they possessed into the Greek dialect. And they being the subjects of the Macedonians, selected from those of highest character among them seventy elders, versed in the Scriptures, and skilled in the Greek dialect, and sent them to him with the divine books. And each having severally translated each prophetic book, and all the translations being compared together, they agreed both in meaning and expression. For it was the counsel of God carried out for the benefit of Grecian ears. It was not alien to the inspiration of God, who gave the prophecy, also to produce the translation, and make it as it were Greek prophecy."

— Stromata I.22, source. Clement can be compared with his two nearest predecessors on the timeline. Like Irenaeus (c. AD 189) he holds the translation inspired — God reproducing his own prophecy in Greek; Tertullian (c. AD 197), a few years earlier, had told the story as a royal commission with no miracle. Clement does not have Irenaeus's separation of the translators: there are no "little cells," no elders "separated from each other." In Clement the translators each render the books and then their versions are "compared together," and the agreement is the marvel.

The Esdras parallel — Scripture once before restored by inspiration

"Since the Scriptures having perished in the captivity of Nebuchadnezzar, Esdras the Levite, the priest, in the time of Artaxerxes king of the Persians, having become inspired in the exercise of prophecy restored again the whole of the ancient Scriptures."

— Stromata I.22, source. Irenaeus had made the same pairing: the inspired Greek translation set beside the inspired restoration of the lost Hebrew by Esdras after the exile. Clement gives the pairing in nearly the same form, defending the LXX's authority by appeal to an earlier act of inspired transmission. (Clement caps the chapter by quoting Aristobulus "in his first book addressed to Philometor" and Numenius's tag that Plato is "Moses speaking in Attic Greek" — the borrowing-from-Moses thesis the chapter argues.)

Notes

  • Cells: absent. Inspiration: present. In date order the miraculous elements do not accumulate steadily. Philo has the inspiration but translators working together; Irenaeus has the isolation, with the agreement as proof; Tertullian has no miracle; Clement, writing alongside Tertullian, has the inspiration but — like Philo — has the elders compare their work together rather than in sealed chambers. The physical "little cells" on Pharos and the autopsy claim have their earliest extant attestation in the Cohortatio ad Graecos (c. AD 260).
  • "Seventy," not seventy-two. Like Irenaeus, Clement writes "seventy elders" — the round number that gave the version its name — rather than the precise seventy-two of Aristeas. Tertullian, citing Aristeas by name, gives seventy-two; Clement, a few years later, gives the round figure.
  • Unsure of the king. Clement does not decide between "Ptolemy the son of Lagos" (Ptolemy I Soter, Irenaeus's choice) and "Ptolemy surnamed Philadelphus" (Ptolemy II, the king named in Aristeas), reporting both. He begins the whole notice with "It is said that…," presenting the story as report.
  • Scope widened to the prophets. Where Aristeas, Josephus, and Tertullian have the Law translated, Clement says "the Scriptures both of the law and of the prophets" and "the prophecies they possessed" — the same wider scope found in Justin. (Clement does not set the story against Aquila and the later Jewish versions, as Irenaeus had; on the corruption question see the companion timeline. His argument here is with the Greek philosophers, whom he means to show were Moses' debtors.)
