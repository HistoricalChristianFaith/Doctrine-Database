Isidore of Seville on the Origin of the Septuagint

Dates: c. AD 636 · Etymologiae VI.iii–iv
Summary: The account in an encyclopedia entry on libraries and translators. Isidore, bishop of Seville and compiler of late-antique Latin learning, gives the origin of the Greek Scriptures two terse paragraphs in his Etymologies: Ptolemy Philadelphus, emulating Pisistratus in his zeal for libraries, gathered "seventy thousand books" at Alexandria and, requesting the Old Testament from the high priest Eleazar, "took care to have it translated… by seventy translators," each "separated in his own cell," who rendered everything "through the Holy Spirit" so that no copy "differed from the rest even in the order of words." The inspiration miracle and the separate cells are both present, in a brief round-seventy form (one cell per translator, no Pharos, no eyewitness ruins, no number of days).

Claims & evidence

"Each separated in his own cell… through the Holy Spirit" — the miracle with seventy translators

Isidore's notice on translators (De interpretibus) gives the account in two sentences:

"Hic etiam et ab Eleazaro pontifice petens Scripturas Veteris Testamenti, in Graecam vocem ex Hebraica lingua per septuaginta interpretes transferre curavit, quas in Alexandrina bibliotheca habuit. Siquidem singuli in singulis cellulis separati ita omnia per Spiritum sanctum interpretati sunt, ut nihil in alicuius eorum codice inventum esset quod in ceteris vel in verborum ordine discreparet."1

"He also, requesting the Scriptures of the Old Testament from the high priest Eleazar, took care to have them translated from the Hebrew tongue into the Greek language by seventy translators, and he kept them in the library at Alexandria. For each one, separated in his own cell, translated everything through the Holy Spirit in such a way that nothing was found in the codex of any one of them that differed from the rest even in the order of words."

For the through-line this timeline traces — whether each teller carries the separate cells and the inspiration claim — Isidore carries both. He keeps the cells (singuli in singulis cellulis separati, "each separated in his own cell") and the explicit inspiration (per Spiritum sanctum), with the agreement reaching down "even to the order of words" (in verborum ordine). His account is brief: the round "seventy" translators of Eusebius and Clement (not the seventy-two of Aristeas), one cell apiece (not the Cohortatio's cots on Pharos, nor Epiphanius's thirty-six paired chambers), no claim of surviving ruins, and no count of days. He names the high priest Eleazar by name — where the Syriac Zacharias a century earlier has "Izra‘el" — and names Ptolemy as Philadelphus, as Aristeas does. The elements (cells, one Spirit, agreement to the word) are those of Augustine's account, here with seventy translators, in an encyclopedia entry.

The encyclopedist's interest in numbers — seventy thousand books, seventy translators, seventy cells

The companion entry, on libraries (De bibliothecis), supplies the setting and Isidore's round figures:

"…maxime Ptolomaeus cognomento Philadelphus omnis litteraturae sagacissimus, cum studio bibliothecarum Pisistratum aemularetur, non solum gentium scripturas, sed etiam et divinas litteras in bibliothecam suam contulit. Nam septuaginta milia librorum huius temporibus Alexandriae inventa sunt."2

"…above all Ptolemy surnamed Philadelphus, most discerning in all literature, since in his zeal for libraries he emulated Pisistratus, brought into his library not only the writings of the nations but also the divine writings. For seventy thousand books were found at Alexandria in his time."

Isidore's notice is given in numbers: seventy thousand books in the library, seventy translators, and seventy cells. It gives no count of the days the work took, a figure the fuller tellers from Aristeas on include. The number seventy recurs across the library's holdings, the translators and their chambers. The frame is the library-history one — Ptolemy as a second Pisistratus, the Greek tyrant credited with founding the first public library — in which the Jewish Scriptures appear as one more acquisition "of the divine writings," and the origin account as a paragraph in a reference work rather than in an apology or a polemic.

Notes

  • What the source is. The Etymologiae (or Origines) is Isidore's vast encyclopedia of all knowledge in twenty books, left unfinished at his death in 636 and widely copied in the Latin Middle Ages (⚠ unverified). Book VI treats "the books and offices of the Church"; chapters iii (De bibliothecis, "On libraries") and iv (De interpretibus, "On translators") carry the Septuagint notice, placed among entries on libraries and translators.
  • Related Latin accounts. Isidore's notice shares its elements with the Latin accounts before him, Augustine's above all (cells, the one Spirit, agreement to the very word), and has the round "seventy" of the chronicle tradition of Eusebius and Jerome rather than the seventy-two of Aristeas and Augustine's own fuller telling. He adds no new element to the account.
  • Text and translation. The Latin is quoted verbatim from W. M. Lindsay's 1911 Oxford critical edition (Isidori Hispalensis Episcopi Etymologiarum sive Originum Libri XX); the English is the editor's own rendering of it. The standard modern English is Stephen A. Barney, W. J. Lewis, J. A. Beach & Oliver Berghof, The Etymologies of Isidore of Seville (Cambridge University Press, 2006).
