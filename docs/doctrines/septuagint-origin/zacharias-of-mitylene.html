Zacharias of Mitylene on the Origin of the Septuagint

Dates: c. AD 518 · Chronicle (the Syriac compilation), Book 12, ch. 7
Summary: A brief providential notice, citing Eusebius. The Syriac Chronicle transmitted under the name of Zacharias Rhetor (Zacharias Scholasticus, later bishop of Mitylene) reports the origin of the Greek Scriptures in a single sentence that cites "the Chronicle of Eusebius of Caesarea" by name: Ptolemy Philadelphus freed the Jewish captives, sent offerings to Jerusalem, and "assembled seventy men learned in the law" to translate the Holy Scriptures from Hebrew into Greek. The notice has no separate cells and no marvel of identical independent renderings; it gives the royal commission in a providential, Trinitarian frame: the king "was indeed moved by God… for the calling of the nations… that they might be true worshippers of the glorious Trinity through the ministration of the Spirit."

Claims & evidence

"Seventy men learned in the law"

The whole notice is a single sentence, set inside a chapter otherwise given over to a world-map ascribed to Ptolemy Philometor:

"Now Ptolemy Philadelphus, king of Egypt, as the Chronicle of Eusebius of Caesarea declares, two hundred and eighty years and more before the birth of our Lord, at the beginning of his reign, set the Jewish captives in Egypt free and sent offerings to Jerusalem to Izra‘el, who was priest at that time; and he assembled seventy men learned in the law and had the Holy Scriptures translated from the Hebrew tongue into Greek; and he stored them up and kept them with him; for in this matter he was indeed moved by God, in order to prepare for the calling of the nations who should attain to knowledge, that they might be true worshippers of the glorious Trinity through the ministration of the Spirit."1

Zacharias's notice has neither the separate-cells miracle nor the claim that the translating was inspired. He has "seventy men" (not the seventy-two of Aristeas), no Demetrius, no Eleazar (the high priest is named "Izra‘el," whom the 1899 translators identify as El‘azar; Justin had written "Herod"), no Pharos, no cells, and no marvel of word-for-word agreement.1 The notice gives the royal commission in the form found in Tertullian and Josephus; Zacharias names Eusebius as his source and keeps his round "seventy."

The providential frame and the Trinitarian phrase

What Zacharias adds is theological rather than narrative. Eusebius had read the translation as God's providence for the Gentile mission — "God put it into the mind of King Ptolemy" so the prophecies of Christ stood ready in Greek "for the calling of the nations" before He came. Zacharias compresses that frame and adds Trinitarian language: the king "was indeed moved by God, in order to prepare for the calling of the nations… that they might be true worshippers of the glorious Trinity through the ministration of the Spirit." The providential reading is Eusebius's; the explicitly Trinitarian and pneumatological language — "the glorious Trinity," "the ministration of the Spirit" — is the sixth-century chronicler's own; compare Cyril of Jerusalem, who says the work was "of the Holy Ghost accomplished," and Augustine, that "the one Spirit had been in them all." Zacharias states the inspiration of the occasion (God moved Ptolemy) without claiming the inspiration of the translating (no identical-renderings miracle): the providence is in the commissioning.

Notes

  • The source. The Chronicle that carries this passage is the Syriac compilation conventionally called Pseudo-Zachariah Rhetor: an anonymous sixth-century author wove the lost Greek Ecclesiastical History of Zacharias (which ran only to 491) into a larger world-chronicle, of which Book 12 — where this notice stands — is the compiler's own continuation, not Zacharias's. The work is named for Zacharias because his history furnishes its middle books (3–6).2 It is placed on this timeline at c. AD 518 — Zacharias's own floruit, the conventional anchor used for the witness — though the compilation itself was assembled a half-century later. The notice records the Eusebian account in Syriac, with a Trinitarian phrase added.
  • The Ptolemies of the chapter. The chapter heading assigns its world-map to "Ptolemy Philometor, king of Egypt." The translators of the 1899 edition identify the geographical epitome that follows the Septuagint notice as the work of Claudius Ptolemaeus, the second-century AD geographer, "whom our author has taken for an Egyptian king," and omit that long geography as serving "no good purpose."1 The world-map is separate from the Septuagint notice.
  • Citing Eusebius by name. Zacharias cites his authority outright — "as the Chronicle of Eusebius of Caesarea declares" — and reproduces a Eusebian chronological figure ("two hundred and eighty years and more before the birth of our Lord"). He thus records the reception of Eusebius's Chronicon in the Syriac world, the same Eusebian notice that this timeline records at c. AD 313.
