George, Bishop of the Arabs on the Origin of the Septuagint (the Simeon question)

Dates: c. 640–724 (bishop of the Arab tribes from 686; d. 724) · Wikipedia
Summary: George, a West Syriac bishop, wrote what J. F. Coakley calls the earliest surviving Syriac discussion of who the Simeon of Luke 2 was. Answering the recluse Yeshu (Josua), he lists the rival identifications, rejects the one that made him Simon son of Sirach — on chronology and what he takes as a false etymology — and concludes the Gospel Simeon "need have been neither a priest nor unusually old." His list does not include the identification, found in later Syriac writers, of Simeon with one of the seventy-two Septuagint translators, kept alive three centuries after rendering (and doubting) Isaiah 7:14's "virgin"; Coakley notes its absence from the options George records. His letter is taken up on the disputed reading on the Simeon tradition.

Claims & evidence

The identity of the Gospel Simeon: the options George lists

"Concerning this old man Simeon about whom you have asked, brother, whether he is Simon Sirach or not: you should know, O lover of truth, that people say many things about him. For some say that he is Simon Sirach, as indeed you, brother, have heard; some (say) that he lived 500 years, from the time of the captivity into Babylon until he received Christ in his arms in the temple and cried out to him saying 'Now, Lord, release thy servant in peace'; some (say) that he was a priest with Zechariah and shared with Joseph the secret of our Saviour's conception and birth from the Virgin."

— Letter to the recluse Yeshu (Josua), "On the old man Simeon who took our Lord in the temple" (c. 724), trans. J. F. Coakley.1 George canvasses three current guesses — Simeon the son of Sirach; a man kept alive five centuries from the Babylonian deportation; a priest who served alongside Zechariah and shared Joseph's secret — and introduces them as popular report ("people say many things"). The identification of Simeon as one of Ptolemy's seventy-two translators, who stumbled over the "virgin" of Isaiah 7:14, attested by later Syriac writers on the origin timeline, is not among the options he lists.

George's rejection of the identification with Simon son of Sirach

George goes on to reject the first of these guesses. Against the identification of the Gospel Simeon with Simon son of Sirach (the sage behind Ecclesiasticus) he argues from the dates: Simeon Sirach, he holds, lived "more than 244 years before the coming of Christ in the flesh," in "the year 65 of the Greeks [Seleucid era], in the days of King Ptolemy Euergetes of Egypt" — generations too early, on his reckoning, to have held the infant Christ. And he traces the identification to what he takes as a false etymology: the supposition that the name Sirach derives from ʿasir ("bound," "captive"), read back into a story of a man "bound" to long life. George concludes that the Simeon of Luke 2 "need have been neither a priest nor unusually old."2 The Sirach identification George rejects reappears five and a half centuries later in Bar Hebraeus (d. 1286), who joins "the father of Jesus the son of Asira [Ben Sira]" to the translator identification (see his detail page).

Notes

  • George's testimony bears not on the origin of the Septuagint — which he nowhere retells — but on a separate question, the identity of the Simeon of Luke 2. It is taken up on the disputed reading on the Simeon tradition. On this timeline the Septuagint-translator identification, in its Isaiah-7:14 shape, is first attested nearly two centuries after George, in the Syriac Moses bar Kepha (d. 903).
  • The Letter survives in Syriac (edited by P. A. de Lagarde, Analecta Syriaca, 1858) with a full German translation by Victor Ryssel (1891); the Simeon chapter and its place in the Syriac Luke-tradition are analyzed by J. F. Coakley (1981). The English renderings here follow those editions; the Syriac has not been independently collated against a critical edition.
  • George's conclusion that the Gospel Simeon need not have been "unusually old" also sets aside, by implication, the "500 years / from the Babylonian captivity" survival guess; the priestly-with-Zechariah guess he leaves without the chronological objection but does not adopt.
