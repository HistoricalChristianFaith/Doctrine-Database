Ishodad of Merv on the Origin of the Septuagint

Dates: c. AD 850 · Commentaries (Introduction)
Summary: The account as a royal commission. Ishodad of Merv — bishop of Ḥedatta and an East Syriac (Church of the East) biblical commentator — opens his Commentaries with the origin of the Greek Old Testament in a single sentence: the version "was made under the patronage of Ptolemy Philadelphus… on the island of Pharos near Alexandria by seventy-two elders of the Jews, who were very well versed in the Hebrew and the Greek languages from their youth." He keeps the seventy-two of Aristeas and names both Pharos and Philadelphus, but carries neither the separate cells nor the inspiration miracle, and describes the translators by their competence ("very well versed" in both languages) without naming the Holy Spirit. ⚠ This witness is cited from the verbatim translation printed by the Wassersteins; the underlying Syriac of the commentary's introduction has not been independently collated against a critical edition.

Claims & evidence

A one-sentence commission — seventy-two, Pharos, Philadelphus, and no miracle

The introduction to Ishodad's biblical Commentaries gives the origin story in a single clause:

"The first (Greek version of the Old Testament) was made under the patronage of Ptolemy Philadelphus king of Egypt on the island of Pharos near Alexandria by seventy-two elders of the Jews, who were very well versed in the Hebrew and the Greek languages from their youth."1

For the through-line this timeline traces — whether each teller carries the separate cells and the inspiration claim — Ishodad carries neither. There are no individual cots, no sealed isolation, no identical drafts adduced as proof of the Spirit's guidance. The marvel found in Irenaeus, the Cohortatio, and Eustathius of Tarihan a century before him does not appear. His notice has the royal commission of Aristeas, Josephus, and Tertullian — a king, a place, a number of translators, a finished version.

The translators described by their competence

His one description of the translators differs from the inspiration accounts. Where Philo made the elders "like men inspired" with "some unseen prompter" behind their words, and Cyril of Jerusalem said the work was "spoken by the Holy Ghost," Ishodad describes the translators by their qualifications: they were "very well versed in the Hebrew and the Greek languages from their youth." He does not mention inspiration or a miracle. His commentaries cite the Septuagint and the later Greek versions (Aquila, Symmachus, Theodotion) alongside the Syriac (⚠ unverified), and the notice names who translated, from what, and with what skill.

The scope and the count

Two further details place him. The scope is the whole "Greek version of the Old Testament," not the Law alone, as in Justin, Clement, Cyril of Alexandria, and Eustathius. The count is the "seventy-two" of Aristeas, where the round "seventy" is given by Eusebius, Clement, Isidore, Zacharias, and his fellow Syriac Eustathius.

Notes

  • Who he is. Ishodad (Išoʿdad) of Merv, bishop of Ḥedatta (Hadatha) on the Tigris near Mosul, flourished about AD 850 under the Abbasid Caliphate and was a biblical commentator of the Church of the East. His Commentaries cover most of the Old and New Testaments and draw heavily on the earlier Antiochene tradition (Theodore of Mopsuestia above all). They cite the Septuagint and the later Greek versions — Aquila, Symmachus, Theodotion — alongside the Syriac Peshitta (⚠ unverified), and a headnote on the Greek's origin stands at the front of the Old Testament commentary.
  • Other Syriac and Arabic tellers. On this timeline the Syriac and Arabic tellers run from Zacharias of Mitylene (c. 518) through Eustathius of Tarihan (c. 750), Ishodad here, Moses bar Kepha, and the Arabic chroniclers down to the thirteenth century. Eustathius, a century before Ishodad, gives the cells and the inspiration miracle; Ishodad's notice gives neither.
  • Text and provenance. The English is quoted verbatim as printed in Abraham & David J. Wasserstein, The Legend of the Septuagint: From Classical Antiquity to Today (Cambridge, 2006), which translates the general introduction prefixed to Ishodad's Old Testament commentary. The critical edition of that text is C. van den Eynde (with J.-M. Vosté), Commentaire d'Išoʿdad de Merv sur l'Ancien Testament, I. Genèse — Syriac in CSCO 126 (Scr. Syri 67), French translation in CSCO 156 (Scr. Syri 75), Louvain 1950/1955. (M. D. Gibson's The Commentaries of Isho'dad of Merv, Horae Semiticae, 1911–16, covers only the New Testament and does not carry this Septuagint-origin headnote, so it is not the source here.)
