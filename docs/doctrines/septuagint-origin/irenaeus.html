Irenaeus on the Origin of the Septuagint

Dates: c. AD 189 (Against Heresies Book 3) · Wikipedia
Summary: In Against Heresies III.21.2 Irenaeus is the earliest extant Christian writer to give the account in its inspiration-miracle form: Ptolemy, fearing the elders might collude to conceal the truth, separates them from one another and orders each to write the whole translation alone; when their independent versions prove identical "in the very same words and the very same names, from beginning to end," even the Gentiles present recognize that "the Scriptures had been interpreted by the inspiration of God." Philo had the marvel of unanimity; Irenaeus adds the isolation of the translators, so that their agreement serves in his telling as a demonstration — and he tells the story in the course of a polemic, to defend the Septuagint's "virgin" of Isaiah 7:14 against the Jewish proselytes Aquila and Theodotion, who rendered it "young woman." In the same passage (III.21.3) he adds that "the apostles… agree with this aforesaid translation; and the translation harmonizes with the tradition of the apostles" — the earliest extant patristic form of the claim that the apostolic church's Old Testament was the Greek Septuagint.

Claims & evidence

The seventy elders separated to write alone, their identical result taken as proof of divine inspiration

"For before the Romans possessed their kingdom, while as yet the Macedonians held Asia, Ptolemy the son of Lagus, being anxious to adorn the library which he had founded in Alexandria, with a collection of the writings of all men, which were [works] of merit, made request to the people of Jerusalem, that they should have their Scriptures translated into the Greek language. And they—for at that time they were still subject to the Macedonians—sent to Ptolemy seventy of their elders, who were thoroughly skilled in the Scriptures and in both the languages, to carry out what he had desired. But he, wishing to test them individually, and fearing lest they might perchance, by taking counsel together, conceal the truth in the Scriptures, by their interpretation, separated them from each other, and commanded them all to write the same translation. He did this with respect to all the books. But when they came together in the same place before Ptolemy, and each of them compared his own interpretation with that of every other, God was indeed glorified, and the Scriptures were acknowledged as truly divine. For all of them read out the common translation [which they had prepared] in the very same words and the very same names, from beginning to end, so that even the Gentiles present perceived that the Scriptures had been interpreted by the inspiration of God."

— Against Heresies III.21.2, source. The earlier witnesses on the timeline have these elements separately: Aristeas and Josephus have the seventy-two elders but no marvel (their translators confer, reaching a text by collation); Philo has the marvel of identical language but his translators still work together "in a secret place." Irenaeus's account has both: the translators are isolated, their separate drafts are found word-for-word identical, and the agreement is presented as a public demonstration of inspiration before the Gentiles present. The physical "little cells" on Pharos, and the claim "we ourselves have seen the traces," do not appear here; they are reported in the Cohortatio ad Graecos.

The precedent of Esdras: God can re-inspire a corrupted Scripture

"And there was nothing astonishing in God having done this—He who, when, during the captivity of the people under Nebuchadnezzar, the Scriptures had been corrupted, and when, after seventy years, the Jews had returned to their own land, then, in the times of Artaxerxes king of the Persians, inspired Esdras the priest, of the tribe of Levi, to recast all the words of the former prophets, and to re-establish with the people the Mosaic legislation."

— Against Heresies III.21.2, source. Irenaeus sets the Septuagint marvel beside a second one: just as God once re-created the corrupted Scriptures through an inspired Ezra after the Babylonian captivity, so He guided the seventy. In both cases, on Irenaeus's account, the text of Scripture is restored or rendered by God's inspiration. Later charges that the Hebrew text had been corrupted are recorded on the Rabbinic Corruption page.

The apostles agree with this translation — and the translation harmonizes with apostolic tradition

"For the apostles, since they are of more ancient date than all these [heretics], agree with this aforesaid translation; and the translation harmonizes with the tradition of the apostles. For Peter, and John, and Matthew, and Paul, and the rest successively, as well as their followers, did set forth all prophetical [announcements], just as the interpretation of the elders contains them."

— Against Heresies III.21.3, source. Having told the inspiration-marvel, Irenaeus closes the argument with the apostles: on his account the apostolic preaching and the Greek of the seventy agree, each vouching for the other. It is the earliest extant patristic statement of the thesis that the apostles' Old Testament was the Greek Septuagint; what that thesis implies for the canon is a disputed reading on the Old Testament canon timeline ("Does the apostles' use of a Greek Old Testament attest the deuterocanon?"). In this passage Irenaeus uses the point in his argument over Isaiah 7:14: the Greek text the apostles used carries his "virgin" against Aquila and Theodotion, and its agreement with "the tradition of the apostles" is what, in Irenaeus's terms, makes the fresh translations of Aquila and Theodotion "impudent and presumptuous." He draws no inference here about the extent of the Old Testament.

Notes

  • Separation and demonstration. Irenaeus has the translators' separation ("separated them from each other") and the identical-result demonstration, but not the cells as buildings nor the "we have seen the traces" claim of eyewitness, which are reported in the Cohortatio ad Graecos (c. AD 260); see the timeline.
  • The polemical point: defending "virgin" against Aquila. Irenaeus tells the story in the course of an argument. In the immediately preceding section (III.21.1) he attacks "Theodotion the Ephesian… and Aquila of Pontus, both Jewish proselytes," for translating Isaiah 7:14 as "young woman shall conceive" instead of "virgin," and the Ebionites who follow them in denying the virgin birth. The inspiration account is his answer: on his argument the older, divinely-guided Greek of the seventy is authoritative, and the post-Christian Jewish versions that revise it are not. Later charges that the Jewish text had been altered are taken up as a disputed reading on the rabbinic-corruption timeline.
  • The apostles and the translation. The same chapter also contains the statement that the apostles "agree with this aforesaid translation." In context it comes in his argument over the wording of Isaiah 7:14 (the Greek "virgin" against Aquila's "young woman"); Irenaeus gives no list of books here and draws no inference about the deuterocanon. Whether the apostles' use of a Greek Old Testament bears on the canon is a disputed reading on the Old Testament canon timeline ("Does the apostles' use of a Greek Old Testament attest the deuterocanon?").
  • Details of the account. Irenaeus writes "seventy of their elders" (not the seventy-two of Aristeas), the round number that gives the version its lasting name; and he assigns the commission to "Ptolemy the son of Lagus" — Ptolemy I Soter — where Aristeas, Philo and Josephus name his son Ptolemy II Philadelphus.
