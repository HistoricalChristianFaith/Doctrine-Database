Solomon of Basra on the Origin of the Septuagint

Dates: c. AD 1222 · Book of the Bee, ch. 52 · Wikipedia
Summary: The account as a king-list entry. Solomon, bishop of Basra in the Church of the East, sets the origin of the Greek Scriptures into the chronological king-list of his Book of the Bee — a Syriac compendium of sacred history — in the form of a chronicler’s note: “Ptolemy Philadelphus, 38 years. In his third year the fifth millennium ended. This (king) asked the captive Jews who were in Egypt, and seventy old men translated the Scriptures for him, from Hebrew into Greek, in the island of Pharos. In return for this he set them free, and gave back to them also the vessels of their temple.”1 Solomon’s notice has neither the separate cells nor the inspiration miracle: a generation after his fellow Syriac Michael the Great told the cells in their Epiphanian paired form, Solomon gives the royal commission found in Aristeas and Tertullian. He keeps the round “seventy” of the Eusebian and Syriac line (not the seventy-two of Aristeas), names Pharos and Philadelphus, and adds a concrete detail of his own — the return of “the vessels of their temple” — before listing the translators by the twelve tribes.

Claims & evidence

The account as a king-list entry

Like his fellow Syriac chronicler Michael the Great, Solomon is a compiler rather than a commentator or catechist, and the notice takes a compiler’s form. It stands not in a preface to Scripture (as in Ishodad or bar Kepha) nor in a doctrinal exposition (as in Cyril of Jerusalem or Augustine), but inside a running king-list — the reigns of the Greek kings of Egypt, each with its tally of years — pinned to a world-chronology synchronism:

“Ptolemy the son of Lag, 40 years. Ptolemy Philadelphus, 38 years. In his third year the fifth millennium ended. This (king) asked the captive Jews who were in Egypt, and seventy old men translated the Scriptures for him, from Hebrew into Greek, in the island of Pharos. In return for this he set them free, and gave back to them also the vessels of their temple. Their names are these…”1

The dating-by-millennium (“in his third year the fifth millennium ended”) is the chronicler’s own frame, the kind of annus mundi synchronism Michael gave as a regnal-Olympiad-Seleucid annal — the Septuagint’s origin presented as a datable event in the sequence of reigns. The notice then turns, as Michael’s did, to the roster: “Their names are these,” followed by the list of translators arranged under the twelve tribes.

No cells and no miracle

Just before him on this timeline, Michael the Syrian (c. 1199) had given Epiphanius’s thirty-six paired cells, the seventy-two days, and the marvel of identical copies “as though… translated by a single person.” A generation later Solomon’s notice has none of these: no separate cells, no isolation, no agreement-test, no identical drafts, no inspiration named. It gives a king, a request, translators, Hebrew into Greek, on Pharos — the royal commission of Aristeas and Tertullian, and of the earlier Syriac Ishodad of Merv (c. 850), who likewise gave the origin as a one-sentence notice. The Syriac witnesses on this timeline do not transmit the account in a single fixed shape: Ishodad’s one-sentence commission (c. 850), bar Kepha’s cells without the Spirit (c. 903), Michael’s paired-cells marvel (c. 1199) and Solomon’s king-list note (c. 1222).

The temple vessels

Solomon’s notice also reports the king’s recompense. The freeing of the Jewish captives is old — it stands in the Letter of Aristeas and recurs in the Syriac chain (Zacharias, Michael) — but Solomon couples it with a detail none of the others on this timeline carry: in return for the translation Ptolemy “gave back to them also the vessels of their temple.” The motif stands beside the manumission — the king repaying the gift of the Law with the restoration of what is sacred to the givers — and biblical tradition also tells of foreign monarchs returning the temple vessels (as Cyrus and Darius are said to have done at the return from exile). The detail is narrative rather than doctrinal; the notice carries no marvel.

Seventy, and the roster of seventy-two

Solomon counts “seventy old men,” the round number of Eusebius, Clement, and the Syriac line, not the seventy-two that his predecessor Michael gives, as Aristeas does. The list of names he appends descends from the seventy-two tradition: a parallel Syriac manuscript of his own roster is headed as the names of the seventy-two translators, the count rounded in the narrative even as the underlying twelve-tribes roster (six per tribe, seventy-two in all) is preserved — the same tension between a round “seventy” and an Aristean roster of seventy-two that runs through the whole tradition. He names neither Demetrius the librarian nor (in this notice) Eleazar the high priest, naming only Ptolemy and the translators.

Notes

  • The work. The Book of the Bee (Syriac Kthbā d-debborithā) is a prose compendium of sacred history in fifty-five chapters — creation, the patriarchs, the life of Christ, lists of kings and patriarchs, and the last things — composed about 1222 by Solomon, metropolitan bishop of Basra (Prāth d-Maišān) in the Church of the East. The Septuagint notice falls in ch. 52, the chapter of king-lists, “the names of the seventy old men who brought out the Scriptures and translated them.”
  • No cells, no inspiration. Solomon gives the royal commission — no separate cells, no isolation, no agreement-test, no language of the Holy Spirit. On this timeline he stands with Ishodad of Merv and the early Latin and classical royal-commission tellers (Aristeas, Tertullian); his immediate predecessor on this timeline, Michael, has the cells and the marvel of agreement.
  • The frame details. He keeps the round seventy, Pharos, and Ptolemy Philadelphus named; drops Demetrius and (in the notice) Eleazar; dates the event by a world-millennium synchronism; adds the return of “the vessels of their temple”; and lists the translators by the twelve tribes — a roster a parallel manuscript heads as seventy-two names.
  • Who he was. Solomon (Solomon of Akhlat), bishop of Basra in the early thirteenth century, was a compiler of the East Syriac tradition; the Book of the Bee is his best-known work and a late witness to that tradition’s store of biblical and historical lore. On this timeline he is the last Syriac witness in chronological order, placed just before the thirteenth-century Arabic notices (Ibn al-Rahib, al-Makin) that follow.
  • Text and provenance. The English is quoted verbatim from E. A. Wallis Budge’s edition and translation, The Book of the Bee (Anecdota Oxoniensia, Semitic Series I.2; Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1886), ch. 52; the passage is also reprinted in Wasserstein, Legend of the Septuagint.
