Epiphanius of Salamis on the Origin of the Septuagint

Dates: c. AD 392 · On Weights and Measures §9–12
Summary: The cells arranged in pairs, the seventy-two named, and the divergences from the Hebrew defended as inspired. Epiphanius, bishop of Salamis, gives the most detailed ancient telling on this timeline in his Treatise on Weights and Measures: he has the full Aristean frame and physical cells, as in the Cohortatio, but arranges them differently — thirty-six cells, the translators shut in two by two, one book to each pair — lists all seventy-two by name, and argues that the places where the Greek differs from the Hebrew are not errors but Spirit-guided additions that mend a "lame" original (his demonstration from Psalm 140). The same treatise's later notice on Aquila charges the proselyte's rival Greek with distortion.

Claims & evidence

The paired cells: thirty-six chambers, two by two, one book each

"For, having constructed the thirty-six cells already mentioned, over on the island, and formed them into pairs, Ptolemy shut them up in them two by two, as I have said… To every pair one book was given. That is to say, the book of the Genesis of the world to one pair, the Exodus of the Israelites to another pair, that of Leviticus to another, and the next book in order to the next."

— On Weights and Measures §11, source. This arrangement is distinctive to Epiphanius among the tellings on this timeline. Irenaeus has the king "separate" the elders; the Cohortatio housed each man alone in his own cot and gave each "the whole Scriptures"; Cyril has the same one-man-one-cell form. Epiphanius instead pairs them — thirty-six cells, two translators to a cell — and assigns each pair a single book, so the agreement to be proved is not seventy-two whole Bibles matching but the cross-checking of pair against pair on the shared text. The proof from agreement remains: when the king's readers compared the copies "no disagreement was found," the work was recognized as the gift "of the Holy Spirit, because they agreed in translation."

The seventy-two named, and why seventy-two and not seventy

"Thereupon the seventy-two translators above mentioned the teachers of the Hebrews chose and sent, according to the example that Moses once set when he went up the mountain at the command of the Lord, having heard: 'Take with thee seventy men and go up the mountain.' But for the sake of peace among the tribes, that he might not take five men from some and six from others and create discord among the tribes, he made up his mind rather to take seventy-two and to add to the number."

— On Weights and Measures §10, source. Epiphanius is the earliest extant of these witnesses to explain the difference between the round and the precise number in the tellings — Irenaeus, Clement, and the Cohortatio all said "seventy," while Aristeas, Tertullian, and Cyril kept the exact seventy-two. He harmonizes the two by appeal to Moses's seventy elders (Numbers 11), the extra two added so that six could come evenly from each of the twelve tribes. He also sets down the full roster — six names per tribe, "as Aristeas has transmitted it in his work" — Reuben's Josephus, Hezekiah, Zechariah, Johanan, Hezekiah, Elisha, and so on through all twelve.

The divergences from the Hebrew defended as inspired additions

"And wherever they had added a word all of them had added the same, and where they had made an omission all alike had made the omission… how marvelously, under the guidance of God and in the harmony of the Holy Spirit, they translated harmoniously and were not at variance with one another… In the one hundred fortieth Psalm… the Hebrew does not have 'of my request.' Behold, then, how lame it is found to be! So the seventy-two translators, when they added 'of my request,' made the line unhalting and translated: 'O Lord, I have cried unto thee; answer me; consider the voice of my request.'… the words are well added in explanation and for the advantage of the peoples about to be called to the faith of God."

— On Weights and Measures §12, source. The earlier tellers on this timeline present inspiration as shown by identity — the translators agreeing with each other word for word. Epiphanius states that the Greek differs from the Hebrew — the translators "added a word," "made an omission" — and makes the difference itself part of the proof: because all of them added or omitted the same thing, and because the additions improve a Hebrew that without them is "lame," the divergence is itself the Spirit's work, "well added in explanation and for the advantage of the peoples about to be called to the faith of God." On this account the Septuagint reading is to be followed where it and the Hebrew part. The same treatise later turns to Aquila.

Notes

  • Companion to the Aquila notice. The origin legend (§9–12) and the attack on Aquila (§15–16) are two halves of one treatise. Here Epiphanius argues for the inspired Septuagint; there the proselyte Aquila, revising the Greek "embittered in mind," is charged with distorting on purpose the very words "testified to about Christ." The defense of the LXX's divergences from the Hebrew (this page) and the charge against a rival Greek's divergences from the LXX (the rabbinic-corruption page) both take the Septuagint as the standard.
  • The through-line: paired cells. On the timeline the separate cells of the Cohortatio and Cyril, one man to a cell, are here thirty-six paired cells with one book per pair. The detail is unique to Epiphanius among these tellings and differs from the "each man translated the whole Scripture alone" form; the account was still being retold with new details in the 390s.
  • The additions argument. In the Psalm 140 (141) demonstration Epiphanius presents the Greek's additions as inspired, so that the Greek is to be followed where it and the Hebrew differ. His charge against Aquila's Greek is recorded on the rabbinic-corruption timeline, which is why Epiphanius is a witness on both pages.
  • Date and text. The Treatise on Weights and Measures (De mensuris et ponderibus) is conventionally dated 392. It survives complete only in Syriac; the English above is from the standard translation of that Syriac (the Tertullian Project text). Epiphanius is placed on the timeline at c. 392, after Cyril of Jerusalem.
