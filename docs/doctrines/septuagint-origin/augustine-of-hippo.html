Augustine of Hippo on the Origin of the Septuagint

Dates: c. AD 425 · The City of God XVIII.42
Summary: The separate-cells account in The City of God, with an argument that the Greek's differences from the Hebrew are also prophetic. Augustine, bishop of Hippo, retells the origin in The City of God with the full Aristean frame (Eleazar the high priest, Ptolemy Philadelphus, seventy-two interpreters, six from each tribe) and keeps the separate-cells form — the elders working "each one apart" yet agreeing so exactly that it was "as if the translators had been one, because in very deed the one Spirit had been in them all." In the chapter that follows he argues that the Seventy had the same prophetic Spirit as the prophets, so where their Greek differs from the Hebrew, both readings are to be received as inspired. The charge that the synagogue's later Hebrew was corrupted, made by other writers, is recorded on the rabbinic-corruption page.

Claims & evidence

The royal commission and the seventy-two

"But another Ptolemy, called Philadelphus, who succeeded him, permitted all whom he had brought under the yoke to return free; and more than that, sent kingly gifts to the temple of God, and begged Eleazar, who was the high priest, to give him the Scriptures, which he had heard by report were truly divine, and therefore greatly desired to have in that most noble library he had made. When the high priest had sent them to him in Hebrew, he afterwards demanded interpreters of him, and there were given him seventy-two, out of each of the twelve tribes six men, most learned in both languages, to wit, the Hebrew and Greek and their translation is now by custom called the Septuagint."

— The City of God XVIII.42, source.1 Augustine gives the Aristean frame, as Cyril does and several earlier Christian tellers do not: Eleazar named as high priest, Ptolemy Philadelphus (he even distinguishes him from his predecessor, "the son of Lagus," who had carried Jews captive), six men from each of the twelve tribes making seventy-two, "most learned in both languages." He alone among these witnesses comments on the name — the version "is now by custom called the Septuagint" — the round "seventy" serving as the title alongside a count of seventy-two.

The cells, the agreement, and "the one Spirit in them all"

"It is reported, indeed, that there was an agreement in their words so wonderful, stupendous, and plainly divine, that when they had sat at this work, each one apart (for so it pleased Ptolemy to test their fidelity), they differed from each other in no word which had the same meaning and force, or, in the order of the words; but, as if the translators had been one, so what all had translated was one, because in very deed the one Spirit had been in them all. And they received so wonderful a gift of God, in order that the authority of these Scriptures might be commended not as human but divine, as indeed it was, for the benefit of the nations who should at some time believe, as we now see them doing."

— The City of God XVIII.42, source. Augustine has the separate-cells account — the elders sitting "each one apart" so Ptolemy could "test their fidelity," as in Irenaeus and the Cohortatio — in its shorter form: no row of cots on Pharos, no eyewitness ruins, no paired chambers, only the isolation and the matching result, introduced with "it is reported, indeed." His own emphasis is the theology of the agreement: where Philo had an "unseen prompter" and Cyril the "Holy Ghost," Augustine makes the unity of the translation mirror the unity of its source — "as if the translators had been one, so what all had translated was one, because in very deed the one Spirit had been in them all." He names the purpose of the gift as the Gentile mission, a providential frame Eusebius had also given it: the gift was given "that the authority of these Scriptures might be commended not as human but divine… for the benefit of the nations who should at some time believe."

Augustine on the differences between the Greek and the Hebrew

In the chapter immediately following, Augustine extends the inspiration claim to the differences between the texts. Because "the same Spirit" was in the Seventy as in the prophets who first spoke the Hebrew, the Septuagint's divergences from the Hebrew are not errors to be corrected but a second, equally prophetic utterance: "the same Spirit who was in the prophets when they spoke these things was also in the seventy men when they translated them," so where the Greek and the Hebrew differ, the Spirit is to be understood as having said both — through the prophets in the one, through the translators in the other, "thus showing that both were prophets."2 Epiphanius, writing in Greek about a generation earlier, had likewise treated the Greek's additions as Spirit-guided. The charge, made by other writers, that the synagogue's later Hebrew and its rival Greek versions had been altered is recorded on the rabbinic-corruption timeline.

Notes

  • The shorter form of the cells. By Augustine's day the account circulated in two forms of the miracle: the elaborated, physical cells of the Cohortatio (cots on Pharos, claimed still visible) and Epiphanius (thirty-six paired chambers), and the bare isolation of Irenaeus. Augustine gives the shorter form — "each one apart" — and adds no physical detail, prefacing it with "it is reported." The physical detail of the later tellings does not increase in strict date order; Augustine's account, like Tertullian's before him, has less of it than the Cohortatio's.
  • Augustine and Jerome. Augustine's view of the inspired Septuagint set him at odds with his contemporary Jerome, who was then translating the Old Testament afresh from the Hebrew and who called the cells a fable; their correspondence over the authority of the Hebrew "truth" (Hebraica veritas) and the Septuagint is recorded on the Jerome and Augustine page. Later Latin tellings on this timeline include Isidore of Seville and Hugh of Saint Victor.
  • Date and text. The City of God was written across 413–426; Book XVIII belongs to the later stretch, conventionally c. 425. The English above is the standard Marcus Dods translation (New Advent / Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers). Augustine is placed on the timeline at c. 425, after Epiphanius of Salamis.
