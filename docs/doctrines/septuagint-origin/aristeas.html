The Letter of Aristeas on the Origin of the Septuagint

Dates: c. 130 BC (narrating events set under Ptolemy II Philadelphus, r. 285–246 BC) · Wikipedia
Summary: The fullest early narrative of the translation. The Letter of Aristeas is cast as a memoir by "Aristeas," a Greek official at the court of Ptolemy II; R. H. Charles, whose translation is quoted here, printed it among the Old Testament Pseudepigrapha, and it is dated here c. 130 BC, over a century after the reign in which it is set. It gives the narrative elements that later tellings repeat: the royal library, Demetrius of Phalerum's proposal to add the Jewish Law, Ptolemy's embassy to the high priest Eleazar, the seventy-two elders (six from each of the twelve tribes), the translation completed in seventy-two days, the public reading, and the curse pronounced against any alteration. It has no miracle: Aristeas's translators consult and compare their work — they are not shut in separate cells to produce independently identical versions. The separate cells appear in later extant tellings (see Notes).

Claims & evidence

The commission: the Law is "absent from the library" and "must be translated"

"I am told that the laws of the Jews are worth transcribing and deserve a place in your library." "What is to prevent you from doing this?" replied the king… "They need to be translated," answered Demetrius…

"The books of the law of the Jews (with some few others) are absent from the library. They are written in the Hebrew characters and language… If it please you, O king, a letter shall be written to the High Priest in Jerusalem, asking him to send six elders out of every tribe — men who have lived the noblest life and are most skilled in their law — that we may find out the points in which the majority of them are in agreement, and so having obtained an accurate translation may place it in a conspicuous place…"

— The Letter of Aristeas, trans. R. H. Charles, source. The frame is a librarian's: Demetrius of Phalerum, keeper of Ptolemy's library at Alexandria, wants the collection complete, and the Jewish Law — divine, "full of wisdom and free from all blemish" — is missing and in need of an accurate Greek rendering. In Aristeas the translation is a scholarly and civic project; the Letter describes no prophetic or miraculous element in the work itself.

Seventy-two elders, six from each tribe, finishing in seventy-two days

"In the presence of all the people I selected six elders from each tribe, good men and true, and I have sent them to you with a copy of our law."

"They were seventy-two in all. Such was the answer which Eleazar and his friends gave to the king's letter… And it so chanced that the work of translation was completed in seventy-two days, just as if this had been arranged of set purpose."

— The Letter of Aristeas, source. The numbers — twelve tribes × six, and the seventy-two days — recur through the later tellings, though some later writers round "seventy-two" to "seventy" (whence Septuaginta) and vary the days. The Letter has the translators sent by the high priest with a copy of the Hebrew law.

The text fixed by a curse: nothing to be added, changed, or omitted

"… since so excellent and sacred and accurate a translation had been made, it was only right that it should remain as it was and no alteration should be made in it. And when the whole company expressed their approval, they bade them pronounce a curse in accordance with their custom upon any one who should make any alteration either by adding anything or changing in any way whatever any of the words which had been written or making any omission. This was a very wise precaution to ensure that the book might be preserved for all the future time unchanged."

— The Letter of Aristeas, source. The closing scene declares the Greek text authoritative and fixed against alteration from the moment of its completion. Later Christian writers on this timeline, among them Irenaeus and Epiphanius, set the Septuagint against the rival Greek versions of Aquila and others; the charge that the synagogue's text had been altered is recorded on the Rabbinic Corruption timeline.

Notes

  • No separate-cells miracle. In Aristeas the translators work together, comparing their renderings to "find out the points in which the majority… are in agreement"; the agreement is reached by collation, not by a supernatural coincidence of independently-sealed scribes. The miracle of word-for-word identical Greek has its earliest extant attestation in Philo (who says they wrote "as if under inspiration… the self-same nouns and verbs"), and the translators are set apart or shut in separate cells in the tellings of Irenaeus, the Cohortatio ad Graecos, Cyril of Jerusalem, Epiphanius, and Augustine. Aristeas is the earliest extant full narrative, and the later tellings add details absent from it.
  • Authorship and date. The Letter presents itself as the account of a contemporary Greek courtier, whose "Aristeas" admires Jewish wisdom as an outsider; R. H. Charles printed it among the Old Testament Pseudepigrapha, and it is dated here c. 130 BC as the work of an Alexandrian Jew commending the Greek Torah and Jewish law to a Hellenistic audience ⚠ unverified (no dating study cited here). Its dramatic date is the reign of Ptolemy II Philadelphus (285–246 BC).
  • It translates only the Law. Aristeas describes the rendering of the Pentateuch alone — as the rabbinic tradition also states ("The sages allowed to write in Greek only the Pentateuch"). Several later Christian writers extend "the Seventy" to cover the Prophets and the whole Old Testament, as in Cyril of Alexandria.
