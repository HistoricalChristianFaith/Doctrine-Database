Tertullian on the Origin of the Septuagint

Dates: c. AD 197 · Wikipedia
Summary: The earliest extant Latin telling, in the royal-commission form. In Apology 18, written to persuade pagan readers that the Christian Scriptures are ancient and publicly verifiable, Tertullian retells the origin without any inspiration miracle: Ptolemy Philadelphus, prompted by Demetrius of Phalerum, "applied to the Jews for their writings" and was given "seventy-two interpreters." He gives the seventy-two of Aristeas (Irenaeus has seventy), names Aristeas as his source, and points the reader to where the proof can still be inspected: the libraries of Ptolemy "at the temple of Serapis," with "the identical Hebrew originals in them," read publicly by the Jews of Alexandria every Sabbath.

Claims & evidence

Ptolemy obtains the Jewish books through seventy-two interpreters

"Ptolemy, surnamed Philadelphus, the most learned of his race, a man of vast acquaintance with all literature, emulating, I imagine, the book enthusiasm of Pisistratus, among other remains of the past which either their antiquity or something of peculiar interest made famous, at the suggestion of Demetrius Phalereus, who was renowned above all grammarians of his time, and to whom he had committed the management of these things, applied to the Jews for their writings — I mean the writings peculiar to them and in their tongue, which they alone possessed… But that the understanding of their books might not be wanting, this also the Jews supplied to Ptolemy; for they gave him seventy-two interpreters — men whom the philosopher Menedemus, the well-known asserter of a Providence, regarded with respect as sharing in his views. The same account is given by Aristæus."

— Apology 18, source. Irenaeus, a few years earlier on the timeline, tells of the elders isolated and their identical drafts taken as proof of "the inspiration of God." Tertullian gives the royal-commission frame of Aristeas and Josephus: a king, his librarian Demetrius, a request to the Jews, and seventy-two translators. There is no separation, no marvel of identical renderings, no inspiration claim. He names his authority: "the same account is given by Aristæus."

The Hebrew originals at the Serapeum, read every Sabbath

"So the king left these works unlocked to all, in the Greek language. To this day, at the temple of Serapis, the libraries of Ptolemy are to be seen, with the identical Hebrew originals in them. The Jews, too, read them publicly. Under a tribute-liberty, they are in the habit of going to hear them every Sabbath."

— Apology 18, source. The Apology is addressed to pagan magistrates, and Tertullian's argument here is from antiquity and public availability: the Jewish Scriptures are older than all Greek letters, and anyone may go and check — the books are on the shelves of the Serapeum in Alexandria and read aloud in the synagogue every Sabbath. He does not speak of inspiration here. (It is the same apologetic use to which Justin had put the account a generation before: the prophecies are "on record" in Greek for the pagan reader to examine.) The Letter of Aristeas sets the commission in the king's library; Tertullian places "the libraries of Ptolemy" at the temple of Serapis. In his use the account supports an evidential claim, which he invites the reader to check in person.

Notes

  • Seventy-two interpreters. Irenaeus had written "seventy of their elders," the round number that gave the version its lasting name; Tertullian writes "seventy-two interpreters," the precise count of Aristeas (six from each of the twelve tribes). The particulars vary among the witnesses of this period; Tertullian, citing Aristeas by name, has the Aristean number.
  • The king. Where Irenaeus assigned the commission to "Ptolemy the son of Lagus" (Ptolemy I Soter), Tertullian names "Ptolemy, surnamed Philadelphus" — Ptolemy II, the king of the Letter of Aristeas. With Demetrius of Phalereus as librarian and Aristeas as cited source, his frame follows the Letter in king, librarian and number.
  • No cells, no inspiration miracle. The physical "little cells" on Pharos and the autopsy claim "we ourselves have seen the traces" appear on this timeline in the Cohortatio ad Graecos (c. AD 260); Tertullian, like Aristeas and Josephus before him, has neither the cells nor the miracle. Irenaeus, a few years before him, reports the elders separated and their drafts identical; Tertullian does not.
  • A Latin voice. Tertullian is the earliest Latin-speaking witness on this timeline, and his account has the royal commission without the cells. Irenaeus told the story in defence of Isaiah 7:14's "virgin" against Aquila and Theodotion (see the corruption charge); Tertullian's Apology addresses pagans and uses the account for the antiquity and availability of the Scriptures.
  • Later judgements on Tertullian. Tertullian's association with the “New Prophecy” (Montanism) in his later years, and how later writers judged him, are recorded on the timeline “Was Tertullian a heretic?”, which notes that no synod is recorded as having tried him. He is a witness on some twenty belief-timelines, this among them.
