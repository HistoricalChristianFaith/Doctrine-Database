Euthymius Zigabenus on the Simeon-the-Just legend (a Byzantine Greek witness)

Dates: c. 1120 (the Commentary on the Four Gospels, on Luke 2:25); Euthymius c. 1050–1120, writing under Alexios I Komnenos (d. 1118) · Wikipedia
Summary: Euthymius Zigabenus is one of two Byzantine Greek witnesses to the Simeon-the-Just legend identified by Coakley and Oancea, the other being George Kedrenos. Kedrenos gives the legend a patristic source (Simeon among the Seventy "as the divine Chrysostom says in his Hexaemeron"), an ascription Jean Bolland rejected in 1665; Euthymius reports it as something he has "found in certain writings," citing no father and no named authority. The two Greek writers come about one and a half to two centuries after the explicitly Isaiah-7:14 form is attested in Syriac, in Moses bar Kepha (d. 903). Euthymius bears not on the origin of the Septuagint, which he does not retell, but on the Simeon-the-Just legend; he has no timeline slot.

Claims & evidence

"I too have found in certain writings… that he was among the seventy translators"

"I too have found in certain writings concerning this righteous man that he was among the seventy translators…"

— Euthymius Zigabenus, Commentarius in quatuor Evangelia, on Luke 2:25, in Patrologia Graeca 129, col. 890, as translated by J. F. Coakley.1 So Euthymius introduces the legend, and goes on to retell its core — that this Simeon, rendering the prophet Isaiah, stumbled over the word that "a virgin shall conceive" (Isaiah 7:14) and was kept alive until the prophecy he doubted was fulfilled in his arms. Two things set his telling apart from Kedrenos's. First, its framing: the whole notice is presented as something "found in certain writings," a report he passes on. Second, its sources: where Kedrenos cites Chrysostom (an ascription Bolland traced to a work not by Chrysostom), Euthymius names no father and no source beyond those "certain writings," as Oancea notes.

The Byzantine witnesses to the legend

According to Coakley and Oancea, only two Byzantine writers are known to carry the legend — George Kedrenos (c. 1050) and Euthymius Zigabenus (c. 1120) — and they differ in how they present it: Kedrenos with the Chrysostom ascription, Zigabenus with none.2 For the Simeon-the-Just question, the dates are these. The explicitly Isaiah-7:14 shape of the story — the translator stumbling over the "virgin" — is attested in Syriac Luke-exegesis in Moses bar Kepha (d. 903), before either Greek writer, and again later in Dionysius bar Salibi (d. 1171). George of the Arabs (d. 724), who surveyed the rival Simeon-identifications, does not mention the translator-form.

Notes

  • Euthymius does not retell the origin of the Septuagint — the separate cells, the miracle of identical drafts — but reports only the Simeon offshoot, in passing, at Luke 2:25. His testimony therefore bears on the Simeon-the-Just question, not on the origin timeline, and he carries no timeline slot — the same standing as Kedrenos.
  • His other page. Euthymius has a second detail page, on a different doctrine: as a Byzantine commentator on the woman-taken-in-adultery pericope, where he brackets John 7:53–8:11 as absent or obelized "in the accurate copies." See Euthymius Zigabenus on the writing on the ground. The Commentary on the Four Gospels is one work; the two pages draw on its notes at Luke 2:25 and John 7:53 respectively.
