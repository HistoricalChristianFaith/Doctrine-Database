Hugh of Saint Victor on the Origin of the Septuagint

Dates: c. AD 1141 (c. 1096–1141) · De scripturis et scriptoribus sacris, ch. 9 · Wikipedia
Summary: The account told, then Jerome's doubt reported. Hugh of Saint Victor, the Paris schoolman whose De scripturis et scriptoribus sacris served as an introduction to the study of the Bible, gives the origin story in its full inspiration form — Ptolemy Philadelphus had the Old Testament rendered into Greek "by seventy translators, whom he received from Eleazar the High Priest," split into "individual cells" so that, "as some say," none could deceive the king, yet "they translated everything by the Holy Spirit in such a way that nothing was to be found in one codex which was not to be found in the others" — and then, in the same breath, adds: "But Jerome says that no credence should be given to this matter." He is the earliest witness on this timeline to report doubt of the cells, which he does by citing Jerome. He also reports that the version was later found to lack things "the authority both of Christ himself and of the apostles" had announced were in it, so "others undertook to translate the holy scriptures from the Hebrew." Where Augustine and Epiphanius treat the Greek's differences as inspired, Hugh presents what the Septuagint lacked as a reason for new translation from the Hebrew.

Claims & evidence

The account reported, then qualified

Hugh sets the origin of the Greek Scriptures at the head of his account of how the sacred text reached the Church. He gives the inspiration form, then immediately attaches Jerome's dissent:

"Afterwards Ptolemy, who was called Philadelphus and was the second to hold the throne of Egypt after Alexander the Great, had the library (bibliotheca) of the Old Testament translated into Greek from Hebrew by seventy translators, whom he received from Eleazar the High Priest (pontifex). And, as some say, in order not to be deceived by them, through false translations, he split them up, so that by sitting (alone) in individual cells they should be apart from each other. But they translated everything by the Holy Spirit in such a way that nothing was to be found in one codex which was not to be found in the others in similar form. On account of this, theirs is a single version. But Jerome says that no credence should be given to this matter. And after the ascension of the Lord, as the apostles preached the gospel, this same translation was found among the gentiles, and the sacred scriptures began to be read by the churches of Christ at first in accordance with it. But later on, since it was proven that there were lacking from this version some things which the authority both of Christ himself and of the apostles in their preaching had announced were in it, others undertook to translate the holy scriptures from the Hebrew tongue into the Greek."1

For the through-line this timeline traces — whether each teller carries the separate cells and the inspiration claim — Hugh carries both, as reports. The cells are present ("by sitting alone in individual cells they should be apart"), and so is the miracle: the translators "translated everything by the Holy Spirit" and produced "a single version" with no divergence "in one codex which was not to be found in the others." These are the elements of Irenaeus's account — isolation, the Spirit, word-for-word identity — also found in Rufinus and Augustine. Hugh reports them rather than asserting them: he introduces the cells with "as some say," and closes the account with Jerome's dissent — "But Jerome says that no credence should be given to this matter."

Jerome's doubt reported

Jerome, who called the cells a fable — "I know not who was the first author to construct… the seventy cells at Alexandria… whereas Aristeas and Josephus… report no such thing" — sits on the corruption-charge timeline, where his preference for the Hebrew is recorded. Rufinus had defended the inspired cells against him. Hugh, three and a half centuries later, repeats the account and then gives Jerome's dissent, the dismissal of the cells found in Jerome's Prologus in Pentateucho. His closing sentences go on to report new translation from the Hebrew.

The Septuagint found lacking

Hugh's closing sentences treat the Greek's divergence differently from Epiphanius and Augustine. Where Epiphanius in the East and Augustine in the West had met the Septuagint's departures from the Hebrew by declaring the differences themselves inspired — the Greek's additions mending a "lame" Hebrew (Epiphanius), both readings the work of "the one Spirit… in the prophets… and… in the seventy men" (Augustine) — Hugh reports that the Greek was found deficient: it lacked "some things which the authority both of Christ himself and of the apostles… had announced were in it," and so "others undertook to translate… from the Hebrew." In his account the divergence is a lack in the Greek that occasioned new translation from the Hebrew. The use of an inspired Septuagint in the corruption charge, and by Moses bar Kepha (c. 903) in the Syriac East, is recorded on the rabbinic-corruption timeline.

Notes

  • The work and its purpose. The passage opens chapter 9 of Hugh's De scripturis et scriptoribus sacris (PL 175, 9–28), a short treatise that functioned as an accessus — an introduction to the craft of reading and interpreting the Bible — and circulated as a preface to scriptural study in the schools. The origin of the versions is thus presented not as polemic (as in Rufinus) nor as catechesis (as in Cyril of Jerusalem) but as a student's first orientation to where the Latin Bible came from, given in a "some say… but Jerome says" register.
  • The frame details. Hugh keeps the round "seventy" translators (not the seventy-two of Aristeas), names Eleazar the high priest and Ptolemy Philadelphus, and retains the cells and the Holy Spirit; he drops Demetrius, the Pharos, the count of days, and the roster of names. He uses bibliotheca ("library") for the whole Old Testament — the medieval sense of the word for the collected books of Scripture — so that he can say Ptolemy had "the library of the Old Testament" translated.
  • Who he was. Hugh of Saint Victor (c. 1096–1141) was a master of the Abbey of Saint Victor in Paris and the author of the Didascalicon and the De scripturis, both guides to the reading of Scripture. On this timeline he is the earliest witness to pass on Jerome's refusal of the cells.
  • Text and provenance. The English is quoted verbatim as printed in Abraham & David J. Wasserstein, The Legend of the Septuagint: From Classical Antiquity to Today (Cambridge, 2006), which translates the relevant passage of De scripturis et scriptoribus sacris, ch. 9. Unlike several Syriac witnesses on this timeline, the underlying Latin work is freely accessible (PL 175, 17), and a full English translation is available online (Three Pillars, On Sacred Scripture, ch. 9), against which the substance has been confirmed — so this witness is treated as verified.
