Bar Hebraeus on the Origin of the Septuagint (the Simeon legend)

Dates: c. 1226–1286 (Gregory Abū al-Faraj, "Bar Hebraeus," maphrian of the East; d. 30 July 1286) · Wikipedia
Summary: The Syriac Orthodox maphrian and scholar is the latest Syriac teller of the Simeon-the-Just legend recorded on these pages, and he combines two identifications that the earlier Syriac lists keep separate into a single Simeon. In his Gospel commentary, the Storehouse of Mysteries (Awṣar Rāzē), at Luke 2:25, this Simeon is at once "the father of Jesus the son of Asira [Ben Sira]" and one of the seventy-two translators who doubted Isaiah's "virgin" and was bound 280 years to see the Christ. Where bar Kepha and bar Salibi list those as separate options, Bar Hebraeus combines them, and states the Ben Sira identification that George of the Arabs had argued against on chronology five centuries earlier. He bears on the Simeon-the-Just legend, not on the origin of the Septuagint, and carries no timeline slot. (For his role on the Genesis-chronology strand, see his rabbinic-corruption page.)

Claims & evidence

The translator-legend combined with the "father of Ben Sira," and a lifespan of 280 years

"'There was a certain man in Jerusalem whose name was Simeon.' The same was the father of Jesus the son of Asira, and was one of those seventy and two interpreters who translated the Scriptures from Hebrew into Greek in the days of Ptolemy. And because he was doubtful concerning that verse: 'Behold, a virgin shall conceive and bear a son,' which is spoken in the prophet Isaiah, he was bound two hundred and eighty years to the sacrifice until he should see Him who was born of a virgin."

— Commentary on the Gospels from the Horreum Mysteriorum (the Storehouse of Mysteries, Awṣar Rāzē), on Luke 2:25 (d. 1286), trans. & ed. Wilmot Eardley W. Carr, p. 98.1 The Syriac catalogues before bar Hebraeus — the seven-fold list in bar Kepha and bar Salibi — had listed "the son of Onias… and father of Jesus bar Asira" (their no. 3) and "one of the 72 translators who doubted Isaiah" (their no. 5) as distinct options. Bar Hebraeus combines the two into one identity, so that a single Simeon is both the parent of Ben Sira the sage and a member of Ptolemy's band — a combination not found in the earlier tellings on this timeline.

George of the Arabs' earlier objection to the Sirach identification

The "father of Ben Sira" element in bar Hebraeus is the identification George of the Arabs (d. 724) had argued against in the eighth century, on chronology (Simeon Sirach "lived more than 244 years before the coming of Christ… in the days of King Ptolemy Euergetes of Egypt") and as an etymology George rejected (the derivation of the name Sirach from ʻasir, "bound," read back into a story of a man "bound" to long life). Five centuries later Bar Hebraeus states the identification without qualification and combines it with the translator-legend, which George does not mention. His lifespan, 280 years, is one of several figures given in the sources: 272 in bar Kepha, 278 in bar Salibi, 280 here, 350 in the Arabic tellers, 360 in living Orthodox devotion.2

Notes

  • Bar Hebraeus does not retell the origin of the Septuagint proper — the separate cells, the miracle of identical drafts — but folds only the Simeon offshoot into his Luke commentary. His testimony therefore bears on the Simeon-the-Just question, where his is the latest Syriac form and the one that combines the most identifications, not on the origin timeline; he carries no timeline slot.
  • Two pages, two strands. Bar Hebraeus has two pages on this site, for two unrelated bodies of his work: here, for the Simeon legend in the Storehouse of Mysteries; and on his rabbinic-corruption page, for the Genesis 5–11 chronology in his Chronography, where he is a negative witness — he tabulates the divergent Septuagint and Hebrew totals but levels no charge of deliberate Jewish tampering. The two are distinct claims about distinct texts.
  • Sourcing. Cited from Carr's 1925 English edition of the Storehouse of Mysteries; the underlying Syriac has not been independently collated against a critical edition.
