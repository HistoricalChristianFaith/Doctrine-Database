The Septuagint 3 Kingdoms reorganization: an alternate Hebrew edition, or a Greek reshuffling?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — the Greek Kings is a genuinely different edition, and the divergence probably reaches the Hebrew stage (the current majority), but Gooding's inner-Greek-reviser thesis remains live and no consensus exists; and the reorganization does not underwrite the further claim that the Greek here preserves a form more original than the Hebrew — every camp reads the "miscellanies" as a secondary reworking · advanced by the text-critical study of 3 Kingdoms (D. W. Gooding; Emanuel Tov; Zipora Talshir; Julio Trebolle Barrera; Guy Darshan)

The claim

The Old Greek of 1 Kings — called 3 Kingdoms (or 3 Reigns) in the Greek Bible — is not a straight translation of the Masoretic Hebrew but a reorganized book. Scattered notices about Solomon that the Hebrew disperses through 1 Kings 3–11 are gathered in the Greek into two supplementary blocks — the "miscellanies" (also "additions" or "supplements") — standing after 3 Kingdoms 2:35 (the fourteen verses lettered 2:35a–o) and after 2:46 (the eleven verses lettered 2:46a–l). One consequence surfaces on the Solomon's-horses timeline: the horse-count appears twice with different numbers — "forty thousand" brood-mares in the 2:46 supplement (agreeing with the Masoretic 1 Kings 4:26) and "four thousand" at 3 Kingdoms 10:26 (agreeing with the Chronicler).1 The interpretation weighed here: this reorganization is a witness to how the Greek and Hebrew textual traditions diverged at the Hebrew stage — the Greek reflects a different Hebrew edition (Vorlage) of Kings, not merely a translator's or copyist's Greek reshuffling — with the sharper corollary, pressed in some apologetic use, that the Greek here preserves a more original form against a later, disordered Hebrew.

Who advances it

The reorganization is an undisputed fact of the Greek text, noticed since at least J. A. Montgomery (1932); the explanation is what divides scholars, along one main axis — did the reworking happen in Hebrew or in Greek? D. W. Gooding, in the foundational monograph Relics of Ancient Exegesis: A Study of the Miscellanies in 3 Reigns 2 (Cambridge, 1976), argued that the blocks are a deliberate, midrashic editorial arrangement — a "discourse on two aspects of Solomon's reign" — carried out in Greek by a later reviser, not present in the Hebrew the translator read.2 Emanuel Tov (following Zipora Talshir and Frank Polak) reached a conclusion "close to Gooding's" on the character of the reworking but placed it earlier: the Vorlage of 3 Kingdoms had itself reworked a Hebrew text resembling the Masoretic — a Hebrew-stage rewriting, not a Greek one.3 Later work has pushed further toward a Hebrew origin: Guy Darshan reads the miscellanies as a separate late Hebrew composition circulated alongside Kings and integrated differently in the two recensions (dispersed in the Masoretic line, kept as an intact block — a doublet — in the Greek), and Julio Trebolle Barrera and others have tied the supplements to the composition history of 1 Kings 4–10.4 The current tendency favours a Hebrew origin, "but… no consensus on the Miscellanies has been reached."5

The evidence for

The Greek Kings really is a different book. This is not a matter of a few variant readings but of arrangement: the Greek relocates whole blocks, carries a chronological system at odds with the Hebrew, and (at 3 Kingdoms 12:24a–z) even hosts a second, alternative account of Jeroboam's revolt beside the canonical one. That the same book stands in two materially different editions is exactly the sort of large-scale, edition-level divergence the Dead Sea Scrolls independently established for other books — the short Greek Jeremiah against the longer Masoretic, both now attested in Hebrew at Qumran; 4QSamuela agreeing with the Old Greek against the Masoretic Samuel.6 Against that backdrop, a case that the reorganized Greek Kings reflects a genuinely different Hebrew stage — rather than a translator's whim — is fully live, and is the reading a majority of specialists now favour.

The reworking is coherent, not random. Gooding's own demonstration — that the miscellanies are a purposeful thematic composition, gathering Solomon's wisdom, his marriage to Pharaoh's daughter, and his building works into an organic unit — is widely accepted; the dispute is only over the language in which that purposeful composition was done. Tov's clearest instance is the story of Pharaoh's daughter, whose episodes are scattered across several chapters in the Masoretic text but combined "into one organic unit" only in the added Summary 1.7 A deliberate, well-planned rearrangement is easier to credit to an editor working a Hebrew source than to a mechanical Greek translator — which is part of why the Hebrew-stage reading has gained ground.

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The reorganization is real and consequential; it is one of the strongest single specimens of the general text-critical finding this wiki traces — that Greek and Hebrew are often divergent editions, not an original and its corruption. The double horse-count is a genuine, verifiable symptom of it: the notice sits once in the 2:46 supplement (as forty thousand) and again at 10:26 (as four thousand), so the Greek Bible carries both numbers that the Solomon's-horses dispute turns on. And the majority reading — that the divergence reaches the Hebrew stage — is well grounded in the parallel, Qumran-confirmed cases of edition-level variety.

What is contested. The headline question — Hebrew edition or Greek reshuffling? — has no settled answer. Gooding's inner-Greek-reviser thesis is not a defeated relic; it is the reason the field still speaks of the miscellanies as "a strange phenomenon," and serious voices continue to locate the rewriting on the Greek side. Calling the reorganization a witness to an "alternate Hebrew Vorlage" states the probable, not the proven.

What is overstated. The corollary sometimes pressed — that the Greek here preserves a more original form that a disordered Hebrew later lost, feeding the rabbinic-corruption charge — does not follow, and every major analysis cuts against it. Gooding, Tov, Darshan, and Joosten alike read the miscellanies as a secondary composition: an anthology stitched from verses found elsewhere in Kings, out of chronological sequence, in places (Summary 1) not even connected to its context, and producing an outright doublet — the very reason the horse-count appears twice with two figures.8 A reworked anthology of an MT-like base is precisely not a preserved older edition; the double horse-count is evidence of compilation, not of either number's originality (that question is settled, if at all, on the internal grounds weighed on the scribal-error page, not by the antiquity of the Greek). So the phenomenon supports "divergent editions, not corruption" — but it does not supply the stronger apologetic claim that the Septuagint's Kings is the truer text.

Assessment

Contested. That 3 Kingdoms is a genuinely reorganized edition is fact; that the reorganization reaches the Hebrew textual stage (a variant Vorlage, not a mere Greek reshuffling) is the current majority view but remains genuinely disputed — Gooding's inner-Greek-reviser case is still live and no consensus has formed — so the interpretation rates contested. Two guards keep it honest. First, the two answers agree on more than they divide over: whether the hand was Hebrew or Greek, the miscellanies are a secondary, purposeful reworking of a text like the Masoretic, so the reading does not become a witness that the Greek preserves a more original Kings — that further, corruption-charge corollary is weak, and is flagged as such on the rabbinic-corruption side. Second, the reorganization decides nothing about the horse-count: the Greek carries both "forty thousand" and "four thousand," so it cannot be enlisted as a clean witness for either — the merits of that dispute are weighed on the Solomon's-horses pages, not here.
