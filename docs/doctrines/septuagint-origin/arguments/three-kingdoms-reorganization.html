The 3 Kingdoms reorganization: an alternate Hebrew edition, or a Greek reshuffling?

Disputed reading · on The Origin and Inspiration of the Septuagint · evidence: the Old Greek of 1 Kings (3 Kingdoms), its "miscellanies" at 2:35a–o and 2:46a–l

Related readings: Did the New Testament authors quote the Septuagint? · The Simeon-the-Just tradition: did one of the Seventy balk at Isaiah 7:14's "virgin"? · on Solomon's horses: Is the "forty thousand" of 1 Kings 4:26 a scribal error?

The passage

46ι And Solomon had forty thousand brood mares for his chariots, and twelve thousand horses.

26 And Solomon had four thousand mares for his chariots, and twelve thousand horsemen: and he put them in the cities of his chariots, and with the king in Jerusalem:

— Septuagint, 3 Kingdoms 2:46 (supplement) and 10:26, Elpenor bilingual edition.1 The Greek book carries the notice twice with different numbers: "forty thousand" in the supplement after 2:46 (agreeing with the Masoretic 1 Kings 4:26) and "four thousand" at 10:26 (agreeing with the Chronicler).

The question

The Old Greek of 1 Kings, called 3 Kingdoms (or 3 Reigns), gathers notices about Solomon that the Hebrew disperses through 1 Kings 3–11 into two supplementary blocks, the "miscellanies," after 2:35 (verses 2:35a–o) and after 2:46 (2:46a–l); it also carries an alternative account of Jeroboam's revolt at 12:24a–z and a chronology that differs from the Hebrew. J. Hänel (1929) and J. A. Montgomery (1932) discussed the supplements, Hänel as a "Variantensammlung" and Montgomery as "supplementary material."2 Whether the reworking was done in Greek by a reviser, or reflects a Hebrew edition of Kings, and if so one earlier or later than the Masoretic text, is disputed; Emanuel Tov wrote in 2007 that "there is no consensus concerning the evaluation of that version."3 The answer bears on how the Greek and Hebrew texts of Kings are related, and on the Solomon's-horses dispute, where the Greek Bible carries both figures.

A reworking by an inner-Greek reviser

On this reading the miscellanies were composed and placed by a reviser of the Greek text, an exegete arranging material to make a point about Solomon.

Held by: D. W. Gooding (Relics of Ancient Exegesis, 1976; "Problems of Text and Midrash in the Third Book of Reigns," 1969).4

In a series of studies from 1964 to 1969 and a monograph on the miscellanies, Gooding read the deviations of 3 Kingdoms as a deliberate scheme of re-ordering and re-interpretation. Of the miscellanies he wrote that they "are collections of alternative translations, variant readings, glosses, doublets and the like," but that "the items have been carefully edited and worked up into themes": "they are carefully arranged so that their very arrangement serves the purpose of making some midrashic point. They are the work not merely of a textual critic but of a Biblical expositor." Their diverse elements, on his account, "have been carefully edited and arranged by some ancient scholar-exegete," forming a discourse on two aspects of Solomon's reign.5 Gooding traced several of the Greek changes to exegetical traditions known from rabbinic literature, such as the treatment of David and Uriah in b. Shabbat 56a, and placed the revising hand in the Greek: "This revision was probably based, at least in part, on written Hebrew (or Aramaic) traditions of one kind or another," which he provisionally called "haggadic midrash."6 He read the Greek Tabernacle account of Exodus in the same way, as an inner-Greek revision. On this reading the miscellanies are the work of a Greek-speaking expositor who drew on Hebrew and Aramaic tradition and arranged known material around themes in Solomon's reign.

An earlier Hebrew edition preserved in the Greek

On this reading 3 Kingdoms translates a Hebrew edition of Kings older than the Masoretic text, which underwent further editing after the Greek translation was made.

Held by: Julio Trebolle Barrera (Salomón y Jeroboán, 1980; with others, in Textual and Literary Criticism of the Books of Kings, 2020).7

Trebolle Barrera developed what he called a "historia de la recensión": the Greek is a faithful translation of a Hebrew composition that differs from the Masoretic text and is, in Tov's summary of this line, "anterior rather than subsequent to MT, and superior to it. MT is a later text in which various editorial developments have taken place since the time of the LXX translation." On the supplements of chapter 2 he spoke of a "compilación de textos hebreos fragmentarios," and the 2020 study by Trebolle Barrera and others ties them to the composition history of 1 Kings 4–10. A. Schenker, as Tov reports him, points to particular readings: at 1 Kings 2:35 the Greek has Zadok appointed "the first priest," read as reflecting the appointment of high priests by kings, which the Masoretic "Zadok the priest" would have replaced once kings no longer made such appointments.8 The reading draws on the edition-level variety the Dead Sea Scrolls attest for other books: the short Greek Jeremiah against the longer Masoretic, both editions now attested in Hebrew at Qumran, and 4QSamuela agreeing with the Old Greek against the Masoretic Samuel.9 Within that pattern, a Greek Kings with whole blocks relocated, a second Jeroboam narrative and its own chronology is read as the witness to an earlier Hebrew stage of the book, before the editorial work that produced the Masoretic arrangement.

A Hebrew rewriting of a text like the Masoretic

On this reading the reworking was done in Hebrew, on a text close to the Masoretic, and the Greek translator rendered that rewritten Hebrew book faithfully.

Held by: Emanuel Tov ("The LXX Additions (Miscellanies) in 1 Kings 2," 1984, rev. 1999; "3 Kingdoms Compared with Similar Rewritten Compositions," 2007); Zipora Talshir; Frank H. Polak (2001).10

Tov's starting point is that most verses of the additions repeat material found elsewhere in Kings, yet in a different Greek wording: "The text of the Additions has not been taken from the Greek text of the parallel verses, since there are too many differences between the two sets of verses, showing that different Hebrew Vorlagen are involved." Hebraistic renderings in verses without a parallel point the same way: "The Additions were originally written in Hebrew." In 1999 he described them as "collections of variants and other miscellaneous material in which an editor intervened slightly."11 In 2007 he set them within a rewritten Hebrew book: two "theme summaries" meant to stress the God-given wisdom of Solomon, of which "Summary 1 is not connected to the context, while Summary 2 is. These summaries repeat verses occurring elsewhere in 1 Kgs 3–11. They are out of chronological order." "The clearest indication of the assumed rewriting process is probably the reworking of the story of Pharaoh's daughter": episodes scattered over several chapters "have been combined into one organic unit" only in Summary 1. Tov holds an inner-Greek revision "intrinsically less likely" for "the lack of parallels for inner-Greek content revisions in the LXX," and concludes that "this Greek translation rendered a Hebrew composition that reworked 1 Kings."12 Talshir, as Tov reports her, describes a Midrashic edition elaborating on the Masoretic text, "created in Hebrew and translated faithfully"; Polak describes the Greek as a late recension ("RecL") beside the Masoretic main recension ("RecM"). Guy Darshan (2006) also locates the additions in Hebrew, but reads them as a separate late composition, integrated as dispersed comments in the Masoretic text and kept as an intact block, a doublet, in the Greek (⚠ unverified; his article, in Hebrew, was not opened; see n. 10). On this reading the doubled horse-count is a mark of compilation within the rewritten Hebrew book.
