Did the New Testament authors quote the Septuagint?

Disputed reading · on The Origin and Inspiration of the Septuagint · evidence: the Old Testament quotations of the New Testament (catalogued on Which text did the New Testament quote?)

Related readings: The Simeon-the-Just tradition: did one of the Seventy balk at Isaiah 7:14's "virgin"? · The 3 Kingdoms reorganization: an alternate Hebrew edition, or a Greek reshuffling?

The passage

Sacrifice and offering thou didst not desire; mine ears hast thou opened: burnt offering and sin offering hast thou not required.

Wherefore when he cometh into the world, he saith, Sacrifice and offering thou wouldest not, but a body hast thou prepared me:

— Psalm 40:6 and Hebrews 10:5 (King James Version). The King James Old Testament follows the Masoretic Hebrew ("mine ears hast thou opened"); its New Testament follows the Greek of Hebrews, which agrees with the Septuagint of the psalm (σῶμα… κατηρτίσω μοι, "a body you prepared for me").1 A popular formulation of the count attached to such cases, whose source has not been identified (⚠ unverified):

"… many of their quotations of the Old Testament in the New Testament come directly from the Septuagint — and the same verse in the Masoretic Text is rendered differently… of the 386 times that the New Testament explicitly quotes from the Old Testament, 340 of those are from the Septuagint translation."

The figure rests on G. L. Archer & G. C. Chirichigno, Old Testament Quotations in the New Testament: A Complete Survey (Chicago: Moody, 1983), which sets the Masoretic Hebrew, the Septuagint and the Greek New Testament side by side for each quotation.2

The question

The writers cited on this page agree that the New Testament authors, writing in Greek, often quote the Old Testament in a Greek form that matches the Septuagint where it differs from the Masoretic Hebrew. They differ on how far the quotations depend on the Septuagint, and on what Archer and Chirichigno's count of about 340 agreements measures. Totals of "quotations" in the literature vary with what is counted (allusions, conflated citations, repeated passages).3 Hebrew manuscripts from Qumran agree with the Septuagint against the Masoretic Text in some books (the shorter Jeremiah; 4QSamuela, as at Goliath's height).4 The question feeds two further debates: the standing of the Hebrew text (the rabbinic-corruption timeline) and the sense of adelphos, "brother," read against Septuagint usage (the perpetual-virginity timeline).5 For the second reading below, academic literature was not located among the sources surveyed for this page; its named holder is a Presbyterian writer drawing on Archer and Chirichigno's categories.

The Septuagint as the Old Testament of the apostolic writers

On this reading the Greek Septuagint was the Bible the New Testament writers read and argued from, and their quotations predominantly reflect it.

Held by: H. B. Swete (An Introduction to the Old Testament in Greek, 1900); Natalio Fernández Marcos (The Septuagint in Context, 2000); Karen H. Jobes & Moisés Silva (Invitation to the Septuagint, 2nd ed., 2015); Jason Evert (Catholic Answers).6

The case rests on quotations that reproduce the Septuagint at the very point where it departs from the Masoretic Hebrew. Matthew 21:16 has "thou hast perfected praise" (αἶνον) where the Hebrew of Psalm 8:2 reads "ordained strength"; Hebrews 10:5 has "a body" for "mine ears"; Hebrews 11:21 has Jacob lean on his staff (ῥάβδου) where Genesis 47:31 has "the bed's head" (the same consonants מטה, vowelled maṭṭeh or miṭṭah); Acts 7:43 has "Remphan" for "Chiun"; Acts 13:41 has "ye despisers" for "ye among the nations"; James 4:6 and 1 Peter 5:5 have "God resisteth the proud" for "he scorneth the scorners"; 1 Peter 4:18 has "if the righteous scarcely be saved"; and Acts 8:33 gives the Ethiopian's Isaiah 53:7–8 in its Greek form. The pattern reaches words ascribed to Jesus: at Mark 7:6–7 he cites Isaiah 29:13 as "in vain do they worship me, teaching for doctrines the commandments of men," where the Hebrew reads that their "fear toward me is taught by the precept of men."7 Matthew 1:23 quotes Isaiah 7:14 with the Septuagint's παρθένος, "virgin," for Hebrew ʿalmah.8 Fernández Marcos states the general finding: "most of the Old Testament quotations in the New follow the text of the LXX in one of its known forms." On this reading the qualifier allows for a Greek Bible that already circulated in several forms, including early revisions (the kaige and proto-Theodotionic readings), so that some quotations match a revised Greek rather than the Old Greek. Evert counts with Archer and Chirichigno about 340 agreements with the Septuagint against 33 that follow the Masoretic Text, and holds that the Greek New Testament reflects the Holy Spirit's choice to have its authors use the Septuagint, the Scripture common to Greek-speaking Jews.

A selective use of the Greek, alongside the Hebrew

On this reading the apostles used the Septuagint because it was in common use, quoting it where its sense matched the Hebrew and translating the Hebrew themselves elsewhere.

Held by: Paul J. Barth ("Did the Apostles Favor the Septuagint?", Purely Presbyterian, 2020), on Archer & Chirichigno's categories.9

Barth, who judges Archer and Chirichigno's survey "a balanced and fair analysis," builds the case on its six categories of quotation. The largest, 268 quotations (64.4%), are "reasonably or completely accurate": the Hebrew, the Greek and the New Testament substantially agree, so the quotation can be called equally "from the Hebrew" or "from the Septuagint." Fifty (11.2%) "quite closely adhere to the wording of the LXX"; 33 (7%) "adhere more closely to the MT than the LXX does," where a writer "may have consulted his Hebrew Bible directly in the preparation of his own account or letter"; 13 (3%) "do not precisely match either the MT or the LXX"; 32 (8%) "are not explicitly adduced by the New Testament writers as quotations"; and 22 (6%) "adhere quite closely to the LXX rendering" where the Greek departs from the Hebrew. On this count the figure of 340 is mostly made up of passages where the two texts agree, and the cases that set the Greek against the Hebrew form a much smaller group. Barth concludes that "only these last 22 citations, 6% of the whole, are seemingly problematic" for a Hebrew-based view of the Old Testament, and that "there are also many places where they quoted from the Hebrew text, giving their own translation instead." In his summary, "The Apostles referenced the LXX because it was widely used at the time, not because they believed it was infallible. Even where it is a highly dynamic paraphrase, the NT writers quoted it when the meaning aligned with the Hebrew text." The apostolic writers, on this reading, wrote for Greek-speaking churches in the Greek those churches read, while keeping the Hebrew as the text they rendered.
