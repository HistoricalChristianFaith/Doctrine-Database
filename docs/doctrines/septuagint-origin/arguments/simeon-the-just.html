The Simeon-the-Just tradition: did one of the Seventy balk at Isaiah 7:14's "virgin"?

Disputed reading · on The Origin and Inspiration of the Septuagint · witnesses: Eutychius of Alexandria, Moses bar Kepha, Bar Hebraeus, George of the Arabs, Jean Bolland

Related readings: Did the New Testament authors quote the Septuagint? · The 3 Kingdoms reorganization: an alternate Hebrew edition, or a Greek reshuffling? · on the rabbinic-corruption timeline: Aquila's revision: anti-Christian distortion, or literalizing toward the Hebrew? · Did the Jews deliberately corrupt the Hebrew Old Testament text? · The Septuagint legend: were the seventy-two divinely moved to change the text?

The passage

"Among the Seventy was a man named Simeon the Just, who took Christ our Lord [into his arms] in the temple. This Simeon, in explaining the Torah and translating the Prophets from Hebrew into Greek, found in every letter that he was transcribing, a prophecy about Christ our Lord, and in his heart he tried not to admit it by saying, 'This is not possible!' God therefore delayed his death, and he lived three hundred and fifty years until he saw Christ our Lord. When he saw Him he said: 'Now send your servant out in peace, O Lord, according to your word, for our eyes have seen your salvation…'"

— Eutychius of Alexandria, Annals, ch. 7 (c. 938), trans. Roger Pearse.1 The Gospel text the tradition builds on:

And, behold, there was a man in Jerusalem, whose name was Simeon; and the same man was just and devout, waiting for the consolation of Israel: and the Holy Ghost was upon him. And it was revealed unto him by the Holy Ghost, that he should not see death, before he had seen the Lord's Christ.

— Luke 2:25–26 (King James Version).2

The question

A tradition of the Christian East identifies the Simeon of Luke 2 with one of the translators of the Septuagint, who doubted the "virgin" of Isaiah 7:14 (Hebrew ʿalmah, Septuagint parthenos, "virgin"; Aquila, Theodotion and Symmachus neanis, "young woman") and was kept alive some three centuries until he held the Christ.3 Whether the Gospel Simeon was that translator, or a man of his own generation whom later readers linked to earlier figures, is disputed; the answer bears on how the origin story of the Septuagint grew in the medieval East.

The earliest dated attestation of the translator identification surveyed here is a list in the Book of Homilies attributed to Moses bar Kepha (d. 903), which gives it as the fifth of seven views ("Others say…"); the same list recurs in Dionysius bar Salibi (d. 1171). George, bishop of the Arabs (d. 724), canvasses identifications of Simeon without it. It is told by Eutychius (c. 938); in Greek by George Kedrenos (c. 1050) and Euthymius Zigabenus (c. 1120); in Coptic-Arabic by Ibn al-Rāhib (c. 1257), al-Makīn (c. 1273) and a Coptic redaction of the Arab Jacobite Synaxarium; and in Syriac by Bar Hebraeus (d. 1286). Eutychius's contemporary Agapius of Hierapolis (c. 942) calls the translation "the command of God because of his foreknowledge" and tells the Presentation without the translator identification. None of the earlier Septuagint witnesses on the origin timeline (Aristeas, Philo, Josephus, Justin, Irenaeus, Epiphanius, Augustine) gives it.4

A man of Christ's own generation

On this reading the Simeon of Luke 2 was a devout man of Jerusalem in the time of Christ, and his identification with earlier figures arose from later attempts to explain Luke's promise that he would not see death.

Held by: George, bishop of the Arabs (Letter to Yeshu, before 724); Jean Bolland (Catholic, Acta Sanctorum, 1665); Abraham & David J. Wasserstein (The Legend of the Septuagint, 2006).5

Asked whether this Simeon "is Simeon Sirach or not," George sets out the views current in his day (Simeon Sirach; a man who "lived 500 years, from the time of the deportation to Babylon"; a priest with Zechariah) and answers from chronology: Simeon Sirach "lived more than 244 years before the coming of Christ… in the days of King Ptolemy Euergetes of Egypt." He traces the Sirach identification to a derivation of the name from 'asir, "bound," and concludes that the Gospel Simeon "need have been neither a priest nor unusually old." Bolland, quoting Kedrenos in the Acta Sanctorum, holds that "it is not read in his genuine works that Simeon was among the seventy-two interpreters," and that the great age "does not seem to have arisen from any other source than from the error by which Saint Simeon is confused with another Simon, surnamed the Just and high priest of the Jews," calling the lifespan "a mere Talmudic invention."6 Jewish tradition, which names Shim‘on ha-Tzaddik in Pirqei Avot 1:2 as one of the last of the Great Assembly, places him as high priest c. 300 BC (Simeon I) or c. 200 BC (Simeon II).7 The Wassersteins explain how the identification could be built: Luke's promise "naturally raised the question not only who he might be but also of when he lived," since it "seemed right that someone who would 'not see death before he had seen the Lord's Christ' could not be a normal contemporary of the birth of Jesus"; the seventy-two names of Ps.-Aristeas include three men called Simon; and Luke's adjective "just" became the name of Shim‘on ha-Tzaddik. Sam‘an al-Siddiq, they write, "seems here to be a composite," and his introduction into the story of the Seventy "is wholly Christian, serving purely Christian polemical aims."8 On this reading Luke's Simeon is the man the Gospel describes: righteous, devout and waiting for the consolation of Israel in the days of Christ's birth.

Simeon the God-Receiver, one of the Seventy

On this reading Simeon was among the translators of the Septuagint, doubted the prophecy that a virgin would conceive, and was kept alive by God until he held the Christ in the Temple.

Held by: Eutychius of Alexandria (Melkite, Annals, c. 938); al-Makīn Ibn al-‘Amīd (Coptic, al-Majūmū‘ al-Mubārak, c. 1273); Bar Hebraeus (Syriac maphrian, Storehouse of Mysteries, d. 1286); the Orthodox Church in America (life of Simeon the God-Receiver, 3 February).9

The tradition reads Luke 2:26 as a lengthening of life: the man to whom it was revealed that he would not see death before seeing the Lord's Christ was one who had stood at the translation of the prophets. In the Syriac list, "when he was rendering the book of Isaiah from Hebrew into Greek at the command of Ptolemy in Egypt, he doubted and said, 'A virgin cannot conceive and give birth,' and he was bound 272 years until Christ." Al-Makīn has him pained at the prophecies of the Messiah and asking God "to lengthen his life so that he could see the lord Messiah," until he "placed him on his arms and said Now, O Lord, release thy servant in peace."10 The Coptic Synaxarium, read on the feast of the Presentation (8 Amshir / 2 February), names the verse: Simeon "struggled with the words of Isaiah [7:14]," "wrote, in place of 'virgin', 'girl'," and was told by an angel that he would see "the masīh who is born of a virgin."11 Kedrenos gives the story "as the divine Chrysostom says in his Hexaemeron"; Euthymius Zigabenus has "found in certain writings concerning this righteous man that he was among the seventy translators."12 Bar Hebraeus makes this Simeon also "the father of Jesus the son of Asira," "bound two hundred and eighty years… until he should see Him who was born of a virgin."13 The tellers give the span as 272, 278, 280, 350 or "nearly three hundred" years. In the Orthodox commemoration of 3 February the story is told whole: Simeon wished to correct "virgin" to "woman," an angel held back his hand, saying "You shall not die until you behold Christ the Lord born of a pure and spotless Virgin," and "Tradition says he was 360." On this reading the Nunc Dimittis is the release of a servant held in life for that one sight.
