George Kedrenos on the Origin of the Septuagint (the Simeon legend)

Dates: fl. c. 1050 (Byzantine chronicler; the Synopsis Historiarum reaches to 1057) · Wikipedia
Summary: George Kedrenos is the fullest Byzantine Greek witness to the Simeon-the-Just tradition, which he ascribes to Chrysostom. Narrating the Presentation in his world-chronicle, he makes the Simeon of Luke 2 one of Ptolemy's seventy translators, who doubted Isaiah's "virgin" and was kept alive to see the Christ, "as the divine Chrysostom says in his Hexaemeron." Jean Bolland, quoting Kedrenos verbatim in the Acta Sanctorum (1665), judged the ascription spurious, tracing it to one of the many works he held were falsely ascribed to Chrysostom — "it is not read in his genuine works that Simeon was among the seventy-two interpreters" — and explained Simeon's great age as a confusion of the Gospel Simeon with the high priest Simeon the Just. Kedrenos does not retell the origin of the Septuagint; his testimony bears on the disputed reading on the Simeon tradition, and he has no timeline slot.

Claims & evidence

Kedrenos's account: Simeon of Luke 2 as one of the Seventy, kept alive to see the Christ he had doubted — "as Chrysostom says in his Hexaemeron"

"…the receiver of God, Symeon the Jew, was one of those seventy interpreters who had translated the Hebrew Old Testament into Greek for Ptolemy Philadelphus, king of Egypt, before the coming of Christ, as D. Chrysostom reports in his work on the six days. And here Simeon, when he had read the prophecy of Isaiah, that a virgin should be pregnant and give birth to a son named Immanuel, completely derogated his faith by saying that human nature would not allow a virgin to bear at all. Thus it was divinely revealed to him that he would live until the prophet's oracle was fulfilled… Seeing him therefore in the temple, a child forty days old, he took him in his arms, saying, 'Now, Lord, let your servant depart in peace according to your word.'"

— Synopsis Historiarum, pp. 327–328 (c. 1050), from the Latin of Immanuel Bekker's edition; the Greek (PG 121) opens "This Simeon θεοδόχος [God-receiver], being a Jew, was one of the seventy translators… as the divine Chrysostom says in his Hexaemeron."1 Kedrenos tells it without a "some say" and without rival identifications: the Gospel Simeon is one of the Seventy, his survival across three centuries is stated as fact, and the whole is ascribed to a named patristic authority. Chrysostom's origin notice in Homily 4 on Genesis, which does treat "the work of the six days," tells the Septuagint's origin with no Simeon in it; Bolland (below) identified the Hexaemeron Kedrenos cites as one of the works spuriously ascribed to Chrysostom.

Bolland's objections: the attribution and the longevity

"But as regards St. Chrysostom, praised by Kedrenos in the Work of the Six Days — just as that holy Father was most famous for the multitude of his lucubrations, so a great many spurious offspring were ascribed to him, from one of which (for it is not read in his genuine works that Simeon was among the seventy-two interpreters) Kedrenos had excerpted this… the great age of Simeon does not seem to have arisen from any other source than from the error by which Saint Simeon is confused with another Simon, surnamed the Just and high priest of the Jews… a mere Talmudic invention."

— Jean Bolland, Acta Sanctorum, October IV (8 October), "On S. Simeon the Prophet of Jerusalem" (1665), quoting Kedrenos verbatim and then rejecting both the source and the chronology.2 Bolland's two objections are taken up on the disputed reading on the Simeon tradition. On the attribution, he holds that the citation is drawn from a pseudo-Chrysostom, not from the genuine works; on the chronology, he holds that the three-century lifespan arises from confusing the aged Simeon of the Nunc Dimittis with Simeon the Just (Shimʻon ha-Tzaddik), the high priest at the head of the rabbinic chain of transmission. The Syriac tellers list several rival identifications of the Gospel Simeon (see Moses bar Kepha). Bolland's treatment is given more fully on his detail page.

Notes

  • Kedrenos does not retell the origin of the Septuagint — the separate cells, the miracle of identical drafts — but reports only the Simeon offshoot, in passing, at the Presentation. His testimony therefore bears on the disputed reading on the Simeon tradition, not on the origin timeline, and he carries no timeline slot.
  • The other Byzantine witness. Constantin Horia Oancea (2024; see note 1) names Kedrenos and the early-twelfth-century commentator Euthymius Zigabenus as the only Byzantine historians to carry the tradition. Zigabenus reports it in his Commentary on Luke with a qualification — "I too have found in certain writings concerning this righteous man that he was among the seventy translators" — and names no Chrysostom and no other authority, only "certain writings." Kedrenos names Chrysostom as his authority; Zigabenus names none.
  • Syriac witnesses. The Isaiah-7:14 form of the tradition is attested in Syriac Luke-exegesis by Moses bar Kepha (d. 903), about a century and a half before Kedrenos, and later by Dionysius bar Salibi (d. 1171). Earlier still, George of the Arabs (d. 724) lists rival identifications of the Gospel Simeon without the translator form.
  • Sourcing. The English is cited from the Latin parallel of Bekker's Synopsis Historiarum (as quoted verbatim by Bolland) and from Coakley's rendering of the Greek (PG 121); the Greek/Latin has not been independently collated against a critical edition.
