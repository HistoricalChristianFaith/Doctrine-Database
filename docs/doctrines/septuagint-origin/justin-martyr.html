Justin Martyr on the Origin of the Septuagint

Dates: c. AD 155 · Wikipedia
Summary: The earliest extant Christian retelling of the origin story. In the First Apology (ch. 31) Justin gives the account briefly: Ptolemy, building his library, sends for the books of "the prophets," receives them in Hebrew, and has them rendered into Greek. He keeps the royal-commission frame, with no seventy-two elders, separate cells or inspiration miracle, and names the Jewish party "Herod… king of the Jews," where Aristeas has the high priest Eleazar. He uses the story for an apologetic purpose: his point is that the Hebrew prophecies of Christ have been publicly available in Greek "until now," so the pagan reader can check them. (In his later Dialogue with Trypho Justin refers to the fuller tradition — "your seventy elders that were with Ptolemy.")

Claims & evidence

Ptolemy's library, the books of the prophets, and a Greek translation that "remained… until now"

"And when Ptolemy king of Egypt formed a library, and endeavoured to collect the writings of all men, he heard also of these prophets, and sent to Herod, who was at that time king of the Jews, requesting that the books of the prophets be sent to him. And Herod the king did indeed send them, written, as they were, in the foresaid Hebrew language. And when their contents were found to be unintelligible to the Egyptians, he again sent and requested that men be commissioned to translate them into the Greek language. And when this was done, the books remained with the Egyptians, where they are until now."

— First Apology, ch. 31, source. The frame of the earlier accounts is here — a book-collecting Ptolemy, a request to Judea, the Hebrew originals, the rendering into Greek — told briefly. There is no Demetrius, no Eleazar, no count of translators, no festival, no marvel. Justin's account dwells not on the mechanics of the translation but on its apologetic use: the prophetic books, which (he has just argued) foretold Christ "before He appeared," have long lain open in Greek among the Egyptians, so the emperor can verify the prophecies for himself.

Notes

  • No cells, no inspiration miracle. Like Aristeas and Josephus before him, Justin has no separate chambers and no miracle of word-for-word identical renderings; the translators are "commissioned." On the timeline, the earliest extant Christian account of the inspiration marvel is Irenaeus's (c. AD 189), and the cells are described ("we have seen the traces") in the Cohortatio ad Graecos.
  • Differences from the earlier accounts. Justin names no seventy-two elders, and where the Letter of Aristeas has the Jewish high priest Eleazar he has "Herod… king of the Jews"; the Herodian kings reigned from the first century BC, about two centuries after Ptolemy II. He also extends the scope from the Law to "the prophets," in keeping with his argument from the messianic prophecies: in his telling the point is not "the Law in Greek for the library," but "the prophecies of Christ, in Greek, on public record."
  • The fuller form in the Dialogue. A decade or so later, in the Dialogue with Trypho (c. AD 165), Justin appeals repeatedly to "your seventy elders that were with Ptolemy the king of the Egyptians" and treats their translation as the standard against which he charges that the Jewish teachers have altered the text. That charge is recorded on the rabbinic-corruption timeline.
