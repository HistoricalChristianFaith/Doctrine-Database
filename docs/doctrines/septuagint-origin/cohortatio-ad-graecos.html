The Cohortatio ad Graecos on the Origin of the Septuagint

Dates: c. AD 260 · Hortatory Address to the Greeks
Summary: The cells described as buildings, with the earliest extant eyewitness claim on this timeline. The Cohortatio ad Graecos (Hortatory Address to the Greeks), an apology addressed to pagan readers, transmitted under the name of Justin Martyr and cited in modern editions as Pseudo-Justin, describes the separate cells concretely: Ptolemy housed the seventy translators in "as many little cots as there were translators," seven stadia off on the island of Pharos, each man working alone and forbidden to communicate, "in order that the accuracy of the translation might be discernible even by their agreement." When the drafts matched word for word the king judged the version "written by divine power." The author then makes the earliest extant eyewitness claim on this timeline — "we ourselves having been in Alexandria, saw the remains of the little cots at the Pharos still preserved" — and names Philo and Josephus as his authorities.

Claims & evidence

The separate cells as buildings, and the agreement as proof of divine power

"Ptolemy, king of Egypt, when he had built the library in Alexandria, and by gathering books from every quarter had filled it, then learnt that very ancient histories written in Hebrew happened to be carefully preserved; and wishing to know their contents, he sent for seventy wise men from Jerusalem, who were acquainted with both the Greek and Hebrew language, and appointed them to translate the books. And that in freedom from all disturbance they might the more speedily complete the translation, he ordered that there should be constructed, not in the city itself, but seven stadia off (where the Pharos was built), as many little cots as there were translators, so that each by himself might complete his own translation; and enjoined upon those officers who were appointed to this duty, to afford them all attendance, but to prevent communication with one another, in order that the accuracy of the translation might be discernible even by their agreement."

"And when he ascertained that the seventy men had not only given the same meaning, but had employed the same words, and had failed in agreement with one another not even to the extent of one word, but had written the same things, and concerning the same things, he was struck with amazement, and believed that the translation had been written by divine power, and perceived that the men were worthy of all honour, as beloved of God; and with many gifts ordered them to return to their own country. And having, as was natural, marvelled at the books, and concluded them to be divine, he consecrated them in that library."

— Hortatory Address to the Greeks 13, source. This is the separation found earlier in Irenaeus — the translators kept apart, their agreement taken as proof — described as buildings. Irenaeus says that the king "separated them from each other"; Clement has no isolation and has the elders reach agreement by comparison. Here, in the earliest extant such description on this timeline, the separation is a set of buildings: a row of individual "cots" (Greek oikiskoi, little houses) on Pharos, one per translator, with guards posted to enforce silence. The miracle is the same as Irenaeus's — identical independent renderings down to the last word — with the physical setting described.

"We ourselves … saw the remains of the little cots" — the eyewitness claim

"These things, ye men of Greece, are no fable, nor do we narrate fictions; but we ourselves having been in Alexandria, saw the remains of the little cots at the Pharos still preserved, and having heard these things from the inhabitants, who had received them as part of their country's tradition, we now tell to you what you can also learn from others, and specially from those wise and esteemed men who have written of these things, Philo and Josephus, and many others."

— Hortatory Address to the Greeks 13, source. No earlier extant teller on this timeline offers physical evidence. Aristeas, Philo, Josephus, Irenaeus, Tertullian, and Clement tell the story as received tradition; this author presents it as an eyewitness report, saying he has been in Alexandria and seen the remains of the cells himself and heard the tradition from the inhabitants. The appeal parallels Tertullian's appeal to the Hebrew originals on display "at the temple of Serapis" — both offer a pagan audience something that could be seen — but Tertullian pointed to books, this author to the buildings the miracle is said to have happened in. Of the authorities he names, "Philo and Josephus" both wrote of the origin; neither of their extant accounts on this timeline has separate cells.

Notes

  • Authorship and date. The Cohortatio ad Graecos is transmitted among the works of Justin Martyr and is cited in modern editions as Pseudo-Justin, as the work of a later, unidentified writer ⚠ unverified (the grounds and their proponents are not cited here). It is placed here at c. AD 260, after Clement; datings into the fourth century have also been proposed ⚠ unverified. A later date would move it further down the timeline.
  • The through-line: cells explicit, and "still preserved." In this witness the separate cells, a bare separation in Irenaeus and absent in Clement, are described concretely — individual cells, counted one per translator, on a named site, and said to be visible remains in the author's own day. Later tellings on this timeline describe the arrangement differently (Epiphanius pairs the translators in thirty-six cells); the eyewitness claim is this author's own.
  • "Seventy," not seventy-two. Like Irenaeus and Clement, the author writes "seventy wise men," the round number that named the version, rather than the precise seventy-two of Aristeas and Tertullian — and has "as many little cots as there were translators," so the count of cells follows the count of men.
  • Apologetic aim. Like Justin and Tertullian, the author tells the story to commend the antiquity and reliability of the Hebrew Scriptures to a Greek readership — "no fable, nor do we narrate fictions" — and does not charge the synagogue with corrupting the text. The inspired-translation claim is present; the contrast with Aquila and the rival Greek versions drawn by Irenaeus is not. (On the corruption question, see the companion timeline.)
