Jean Bolland on the Simeon-the-Just tradition (Acta Sanctorum, 1665)

Dates: 1665 (the Acta Sanctorum, October IV, 8 October, "On S. Simeon the Prophet of Jerusalem"); Jean Bolland 1596–1665, the Jesuit scholar who founded the critical hagiographical enterprise that bears his name (the Bollandists) · Wikipedia
Summary: Jean Bolland is the earliest critic of the Simeon-the-Just tradition recorded on these pages. Editing the feast of the Gospel Simeon in the Acta Sanctorum, he quotes the fullest Byzantine teller, George Kedrenos, verbatim, and then rejects the story on two counts: the patristic authority Kedrenos cites (Simeon among the Seventy "as Chrysostom says in his Hexaemeron") he judges spurious, drawn from one of the many works falsely ascribed to Chrysostom; and the long lifespan he explains as a confusion — the aged Simeon of the Nunc Dimittis taken for Simeon the Just, high priest of the Jews, the great age "a mere Talmudic invention." Kedrenos and Euthymius Zigabenus are the tradition's two Greek tellers on these pages; on the origin account, the comparable dissent is Jerome's doubt of the separate cells. Bolland does not retell the origin of the Septuagint; his testimony bears on the disputed reading on the Simeon tradition, and he has no timeline slot.

Claims & evidence

Bolland on the Chrysostom attribution — "it is not read in his genuine works that Simeon was among the seventy-two interpreters"

Bolland reproduces Kedrenos's notice in full — the "receiver of God, Simeon the Jew," one of the seventy interpreters under Ptolemy Philadelphus, who doubted Isaiah's word that a virgin should conceive and was kept alive until the oracle was fulfilled, "as D. Chrysostom reports in his work on the six days" (de sex diebus opere, the so-called Hexaemeron). Then he addresses the citation:

"But as regards St. Chrysostom, praised by Kedrenos in the Work of the Six Days — just as that holy Father was most famous for the multitude of his lucubrations, so a great many spurious offspring were ascribed to him, from one of which (for it is not read in his genuine works that Simeon was among the seventy-two interpreters) Kedrenos had excerpted this."

— Jean Bolland, Acta Sanctorum, October IV (8 October), "On S. Simeon the Prophet of Jerusalem," § II ("Refutantur aliquot de S. Simeone errores"), 1665.1 Kedrenos rests the story on a named father; Bolland identifies the work cited as a pseudo-Chrysostom, one of the many writings ascribed to the archbishop. Chrysostom's origin notice in his Homily 4 on Genesis, which does treat "the work of the six days" and does recount the Septuagint's origin, has no Simeon in it.

Bolland on the 350-year age: two Simeons — "a mere Talmudic invention"

"…the great age of Simeon does not seem to have arisen from any other source than from the error by which Saint Simeon is confused with another Simon, surnamed the Just and high priest of the Jews, the father of Onias — … and it appears a mere Talmudic invention."

— Bolland, Acta Sanctorum, October IV, § II (1665).2 On Bolland's account the lifespan arises from identifying the aged Simeon of Luke 2 with Simeon the Just (Shimʻon ha-Tzaddik), the high priest named in Pirqei Avot 1:2 as one of the last of the Great Assembly (c. 300 or c. 200 BC); the identification requires a longevity spanning the centuries between them. Bolland traces the great age to that identification and calls it a "Talmudic invention." The identification is discussed on the disputed reading on the Simeon tradition; the Syriac tellers list it among several rival identifications of the Gospel Simeon.

Bolland's chronology

Bolland also does the arithmetic. Following the Eusebian dating, he places the translation at about 283 BC; on the 350-year lifespan reported by Eutychius of Alexandria, whom he cites, and with Simeon's death set at the beginning of the Christian era, the translator-Simeon would have been about sixty-seven when he translated. He then traces so great an age to the identification of the Gospel Simeon with the high priest Simeon the Just (above).3

Notes

  • Bolland does not retell the origin of the Septuagint — the separate cells, the miracle of identical drafts — but addresses only the Simeon tradition, in the course of editing the saint's feast. His testimony therefore bears on the disputed reading on the Simeon tradition, not on the origin timeline, and he carries no timeline slot — the same standing as the two Byzantine tellers, Kedrenos (whom he quotes) and Euthymius Zigabenus.
  • Bolland and Jerome. On the origin account, Jerome, who called the separate cells a fable, is the dissenting voice recorded on these pages; Bolland's critique of the Simeon tradition works by source-criticism and chronology. He writes in 1665, some six centuries after Kedrenos and more than three centuries before the Wassersteins' survey of the legend (2006). His three points — spurious source, confused persons, invented age — are taken up on the disputed reading on the Simeon tradition.
  • Who Bolland was. Jean Bolland (1596–1665) was the Antwerp Jesuit who launched the Acta Sanctorum, the critical edition of the lives of the saints arranged by feast-day, continued after him by the Bollandists.
  • Sourcing. The passage is cited here from the Latin of the Acta Sanctorum (October IV, 8 October) as reproduced at heiligenlexikon.de, with the English rendered from that Latin; it has not been independently collated against the printed folio.
