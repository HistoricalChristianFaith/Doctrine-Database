Michael the Syrian on the Origin of the Septuagint

Dates: c. AD 1199 (patriarch 1166–1199) · Chronicle, Book V, ch. 6 · Wikipedia
Summary: Epiphanius's paired cells, in a chronicle entry. Michael the Great (“the Syrian”), patriarch of the Syriac Orthodox Church and author of a large medieval Syriac universal history, sets the origin of the Greek Scriptures into his Chronicle as a dated annal: “In the 5th year of his reign… the king Ptolemy Philadelphus sent to find the High Priest of the Jews, Eleazar,” who sent “men who knew Hebrew and Greek, to the number of seventy two,” whom the king “established… on the island of Pharos” in “thirty six cells – one for every pair,” warning them “not to change anything”; the thirty-six copies, “when they were compared with each other… were as though they had been translated by a single person.”1 For the through-line this timeline traces, Michael carries the cells in their distinctive Epiphanian paired form — “thirty six cells – one for every pair” — found earlier on this timeline in Epiphanius and, as thirty-six separated groups, in Agapius of Hierapolis; and he keeps the marvel of identical drafts (“as though… by a single person”) — but, like his fellow Syriac bar Kepha, he stops at the agreement and does not name the Holy Spirit. He has Aristeas’s seventy-two, Eleazar, Pharos, and the seventy-two days, details the Syriac witnesses just before him on this timeline lack in part.

Claims & evidence

The legend as a dated chronicle entry

Unlike the commentators and catechists earlier on this timeline, Michael is a chronicler: he sets the translation into a universal history reaching from creation to his own day, and gives it the apparatus of an annal — a regnal year, an Olympiad, a Seleucid and an indiction date — in the manner of the chronographic tradition he draws on (above all Eusebius’s Chronicon):

“Ptolemy of Alexandria gave freedom to the Jews who were captive in Egypt and sent offerings to the High Priest Eleazar. In the 5th year of his reign, which is the 33rd of Seleucus, in the CXXVth Olympiad, the first year of Antiochus Soter, in the 10th indiction, the king Ptolemy Philadelphus sent to find the High Priest of the Jews, Eleazar, who sent him all the books of the Hebrews and men who knew Hebrew and Greek, to the number of seventy two. The king established them on the island of Pharos; he had built for them thirty six cells – one for every pair – and warned them not to change anything. They translated the books in seventy two days. Each pair translated all the books, and there were thirty six copies; when they were compared with each other, they were as though they had been translated by a single person. The king placed these copies in the library, in his library in Alexandria. From that comes the version which is known everywhere under the name of Septuagint. Here are the names of the seventy two men who translated the books…”1

The historian’s frame shows in three features the commentators lack: the precise dating-synchronisms; the freeing of the captives and the offerings to Eleazar at the head, the diplomatic-historical setting of the Letter of Aristeas; and the closing turn to the roster, “Here are the names of the seventy two men,” which (like Epiphanius) Michael then lists out. He presents the account as a datable event.

The paired cells

Michael’s notice is distinctive in the form of its cells. The earlier tellers describe the isolation in several shapes — the Cohortatio and Cyril sealed each man alone with the whole Scripture; Isidore and several Syriac tellers after him have “each in his own cell” or no cells at all. Epiphanius (c. 392) made them paired — “thirty-six cells… formed… into pairs,” the translators shut up “two by two” — and Agapius (c. 942) has thirty-six groups, each with an attendant to keep them apart. Michael’s “thirty six cells – one for every pair” matches Epiphanius’s count and pairing.

But Michael diverges from Epiphanius on what each pair did. Epiphanius assigned one book to each pair — Genesis to the first, Exodus to the next — so the thirty-six cells together produced one Bible. Michael instead has “each pair” translate “all the books,” yielding “thirty six copies” of the whole, which are then “compared with each other.” His account combines Epiphanius’s cell-count and pairing with the separate-cells proof, in which every cell renders the entire Scripture so that the independent agreement of complete copies is the marvel: thirty-six paired cells, thirty-six full Bibles, one identical text. Agapius likewise has the finished work yield thirty-six copies.

The marvel kept, the Spirit unnamed

Michael reports the agreement but does not use the language of inspiration. The thirty-six copies, “when they were compared with each other… were as though they had been translated by a single person” — the independent-and-identical proof, the wonder reported by Irenaeus. Yet where Irenaeus drew the conclusion (“interpreted by the inspiration of God”), Cyril named the Holy Ghost, and Augustine spoke of “the one Spirit… in them all,” Michael stops at the simile: the copies are as if one man’s work, and he does not say the Spirit made them so. In this he resembles his fellow Syriac bar Kepha (c. 903), who likewise reports the cells and the exact agreement without naming the Spirit. The fidelity of the work appears as the king’s command — he “warned them not to change anything”; compare the Aristean curse against alteration.

The frame details beside the earlier Syriac witnesses

The Syriac witnesses before him on this timeline give shorter frames — Eustathius’s round “seventy” with no high priest named, Ishodad’s one-sentence commission, bar Kepha’s cells without Pharos or Eleazar. Michael has the seventy-two (not the round seventy of Eustathius and the Eusebian line), names Eleazar the high priest, sets the work on Pharos, counts the seventy-two days, and adds the roster, features found in Aristeas and Epiphanius. On this timeline he is followed by the briefer Arabic notices of Ibn al-Rahib and al-Makin.

Notes

  • The work. Michael’s Chronicle (Syriac Maktbānūt zabnē) is a large medieval Syriac universal history, running from creation to AD 1195 and preserving long extracts from earlier chronicles now lost. The Septuagint notice stands in Book V, in the chronographic sequence on the Ptolemies, framed as a dated annal with the regnal, Olympiad, Seleucid, and indiction synchronisms that the commentary-and-catechesis tellers omit.
  • The frame details. Michael keeps the seventy-two translators and seventy-two days, names Eleazar the high priest and Ptolemy Philadelphus, sets the cells on Pharos, and gives the roster of names; he frames the whole as a freeing of the Jewish captives with offerings sent to Eleazar. He drops Demetrius the librarian, whom the fuller tellers from Aristeas to Cyril keep.
  • Who he was. Michael I, called “the Great” and “the Syrian,” was patriarch of Antioch of the Syriac Orthodox Church from 1166 until his death in 1199. On this timeline his Chronicle follows Hugh of Saint Victor (c. 1141), who reports Jerome's doubt of the cells, by about half a century.
  • Text and provenance. The English is quoted verbatim as printed in Abraham & David J. Wasserstein, The Legend of the Septuagint: From Classical Antiquity to Today (Cambridge, 2006), translating the passage of Michael’s Chronicle (Book V, ch. 6). It is corroborated by the critical edition itself: J.-B. Chabot, Chronique de Michel le Syrien (Paris, 1899–1910), whose French translation carries the same annal at tome I, pp. 115–118 (the Syriac in tome IV), both freely available; Matti Moosa’s The Chronicle of Michael the Great (2014) gives a full modern English.
