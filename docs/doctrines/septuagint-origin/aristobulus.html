Aristobulus of Alexandria on the Origin of the Septuagint

Dates: c. 150 BC (a Jewish philosopher of Alexandria who dedicated his exegesis of the Law to Ptolemy VI Philometor, r. 180–145 BC) · Wikipedia
Summary: The earliest datable extant mention of the Greek translation on this timeline. Aristobulus was a Hellenistic-Jewish philosopher who wrote an allegorical commentary on the Law for the Ptolemaic court; his work survives only in fragments quoted by Eusebius and Clement of Alexandria. Arguing that the Greek philosophers borrowed from Moses, he remarks in passing that "the entire translation of all the contents of our Law" was made under Ptolemy II Philadelphus through Demetrius of Phalerum — and that partial renderings of the exodus story and the Law had circulated even earlier. His notice is briefer than the Letter of Aristeas: there are no seventy-two elders, no separate cells, no inspiration miracle — a royal commission only.

Claims & evidence

The whole Law was translated under Ptolemy Philadelphus, managed by Demetrius

"For others before Demetrius Phalereus, and prior to the supremacy of Alexander and the Persians, have translated both the narrative of the exodus of the Hebrews our fellow countrymen from Egypt, and the fame of all that had happened to them, and the conquest of the land, and the exposition of the whole Law… But the entire translation of all the contents of our law was made in the time of the king surnamed Philadelphus, thy ancestor, who brought greater zeal to the work, which was managed by Demetrius Phalereus."

— Aristobulus, quoted in Eusebius, Praeparatio Evangelica XIII.12, source. The passage argues that Plato and Pythagoras drew on Moses, and the translation is mentioned to date how the Law became available in Greek. It has the elements later tellings repeat: a complete rendering of the Law, under Ptolemy II Philadelphus, with Demetrius of Phalerum directing it. Aristobulus also reports what the later tellings on this timeline do not: that incomplete Greek versions of the exodus and the Law had existed before Demetrius — a text translated more than once, with no miracle described.

Addressed to a Ptolemy — the dating anchor

"And Aristobulus, in his first book addressed to Philometor, writes in these words: 'And Plato followed the laws given to us, and had manifestly studied all that is said in them.'"

— Clement of Alexandria, Stromata I.22, source. Both Clement and Eusebius preserve that Aristobulus dedicated his work to a reigning Ptolemy ("thy ancestor" Philadelphus addressed to a later king); the addressee is Ptolemy VI Philometor (r. 180–145 BC), which places Aristobulus around the middle of the second century BC — on the dates used here, the earliest extant reference to the Septuagint's making, a generation or so before the Letter of Aristeas (c. 130 BC), which tells the story as a full narrative.

Notes

  • The briefest form. Aristobulus has less than Aristeas: no count of translators, no twelve tribes, no seventy-two days, no separate-cells miracle and no claim of inspired unanimity. The miracle that later writers (Philo, Irenaeus, the Cohortatio, Cyril of Jerusalem, Epiphanius, Augustine) place at the centre of the story does not appear in this earliest extant notice. He also reports that partial Greek versions predated the one made under Philadelphus, a detail the later tellings on this timeline do not carry.
  • Placement on the timeline. On the dates used here Aristobulus (c. 150 BC) precedes the Letter of Aristeas (c. 130 BC), so he leads this timeline; the two are close in time, and whether Aristobulus's notice draws on an early form of the Aristeas tradition or is independent of it has been argued both ways ⚠ unverified (no proponent named here). His notice is the shorter; the Letter gives the fuller narrative that later tellings repeat.
  • Later counted among "the Seventy." Aristobulus — a witness to the translation — is named by a later writer as one of the translators. Anatolius of Laodicea, quoted by Eusebius, calls him "the famous Aristobulus, who was chosen among the seventy interpreters of the sacred and divine Hebrew Scriptures by Ptolemy Philadelphus and his father." On the dates used here, Aristobulus wrote about a century after Ptolemy II's reign.
