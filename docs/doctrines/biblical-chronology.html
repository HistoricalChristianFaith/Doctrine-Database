Reckoning the Age of the World

How old is the world, and when did its great anchors fall — Creation, the Flood, the dispersion at Babel, Abraham, the Exodus, the building of Solomon's Temple? For two and a half millennia Jewish and Christian chronographers tried to reckon those dates from the numbers of Scripture and to set them against the fixed points of secular history — the Babylonian, Egyptian, Persian, Greek, and Roman king-lists. This page records that enterprise across time: who reckoned the age of the world, on what figures, and how Christian and Jewish reckoners' answers differed as the text-traditions diverged and the secular anchors came into view.

The dating of Noah's Flood records the Masoretic "short," Septuagint "long," and Samaritan reckonings for that one anchor. Here the subject is the whole span: the Egyptian sojourn, the Exodus, and the Persian period, where the rabbinic reckoning and the conventional one part by a century and a half. The question of which numbers to reckon from connects this page with the Septuagint's origin and the transmission of the Hebrew text.

A note on ordering: the "witnesses" below are the datable systems and chronographers that reckoned the age of the world, arranged by the date of the source (when each wrote), not by the date of the events they reckon, which is what they differ about.

Related doctrines: Reckoning sacred time.

Timeline

Datable chronographers and world-eras that reckoned the age of the world, earliest source first (see the ordering note above). Several of these figures also stand as witnesses on the dating of the Flood, where their reckoning is developed for that one anchor; here they appear in their whole-span role and are cross-linked rather than re-quoted. Disputed readings of the anchors are listed in the "Disputed readings" section below.

Berossus' Babyloniaca: ten kings before the Flood, 432,000 years (composed c. 278 BC)

The Babylonian priest Berossus, writing in Greek for Antiochus I, recorded ten kings reigning before the Flood for 120 saroi — 120 × 3,600 = 432,000 years — a pagan world-antiquity many times longer than any biblical reckoning.1 His ten antediluvian kings match in number the ten antediluvian patriarchs of Genesis 5, and every span is a whole multiple of the šár (3,600 years). Berossus is the earliest dated source on this shelf: a ten-generation frame like that of Genesis, on a span of 432,000 years where the biblical figures give a few thousand.

Demetrius the Chronographer: the span reckoned from the Septuagint's numbers (fl. c. 210 BC)

The earliest extant chronology computed from the biblical text is that of Demetrius, a Jewish writer in Ptolemaic Alexandria and the earliest known Jewish author to compose in Greek. Reckoning the genealogies age by age from the Septuagint, he fixed the interval "from Adam until Joseph's brethren came into Egypt" at 3,624 years and "from the Deluge until Jacob's coming into Egypt" at 1,360 — figures that seat the Flood at Anno Mundi 2,264, all but identical to the Septuagint's 2,262 and nowhere near the Masoretic 1,656.2 He wrote some four centuries before Julius Africanus' Chronographiai (below). Henry B. Smith Jr. treats him as the earliest external witness to the longer Genesis 5–11 figures, in support of the case that the Hebrew was later shortened. His fuller reckoning, and the reported requirement that his numbers read Methuselah begetting at 187 in Genesis 5:25 (⚠ unverified), are on his detail page.

Josephus' Antiquities: a continuous Jewish reckoning of the span (composed c. AD 93)

Flavius Josephus, retelling the sacred history in Greek for a Roman readership, ran an explicit chronology across the whole arc — from Adam to the Flood ("two thousand six hundred and fifty-six years from Adam the first man," Antiquities 1.82, a figure famous as a textual crux), onward through Abraham, the sojourn in Egypt, the Exodus, and the building of the Temple, closing his summaries with running totals from the Creation.3 Felix Schlichter sets out that his patriarch-ages carry the same centenary additions as the Septuagint's long chronology, so his summed interval runs some six centuries longer than the received Hebrew; Henry B. Smith Jr. counts him among the early witnesses to the long chronology in the case that the Hebrew was later shortened. The transmitted total at §82 varies among the manuscripts, and Isaac Vossius emended it to agree with the Septuagint. His running totals carry the reckoning across the whole span from the Creation to the Temple. He appears on this wiki also as a witness on several timelines, gathered on his detail page.

Seder Olam Rabbah: the rabbinic Anno Mundi and the compressed Persian period (c. AD 160)

Between Josephus and Africanus on this shelf stands Seder Olam Rabbah ("The Great Order of the World"), attributed to the second-century tanna R. Yose ben Halafta and the earliest extant post-biblical Hebrew chronology of the whole span. It reckons the primeval and patriarchal intervals on the Masoretic figures — Creation to the Flood 1,656 years, not the Septuagint's 2,262 — and is the source of the count the Hebrew calendar still carries, seating Creation at c. 3761 BC (so the Jewish year running through most of AD 2026 is AM 5786).4 Its best-known feature is a compressed Persian period: it allots the whole Persian empire only about 52 years and a short line of kings (Cyrus, Ahasuerus, Darius), against some 207 years and fourteen kings in conventional chronology — roughly 165 "missing years" that carry through every later date. Mitchell First's survey of the Jewish responses counts 41 authorities for the conventional chronology and 17 for Seder Olam.5 Its fuller reckoning is on its detail page.

Julius Africanus' Chronographiai: a Christian universal chronology (c. 221)

Sextus Julius Africanus (c. 160–c. 240) wrote the earliest extant Christian reckoning of the whole span — five books running from Creation to his own day (c. AD 221) and synchronising the sacred history with the Olympiads and the Greek, Persian, and Egyptian records on one Anno Mundi frame. Reckoning the Septuagint's longer figures he fixed the Flood at Anno Mundi 2262 (all but Demetrius' 2,264, and the number the Alexandrians later trimmed to 2242) and the Incarnation at Anno Mundi 5500 — setting the "5,500 years to Christ" against the Egyptian and Chaldean claims of tens of thousands of years. Richard Landes and William Adler discuss the later reading of the 5,500 as the midpoint of a six-thousand-year world-week.6 His fuller reckoning — the 2262-to-the-Flood figure, the 5500/6000 scheme, and his computation of Daniel's seventy weeks to Christ — is on his detail page.

Eusebius' Chronicon: the three reckonings tabulated and synchronized (c. 320)

In the Chronicon Eusebius of Caesarea set the Hebrew, Septuagint, and Samaritan totals of Genesis 5–11 side by side and chose among them — the earliest extant work to tabulate the three — and, in its famous parallel columns (the Chronological Canons), he ran the sacred reckoning alongside the Chaldean, Assyrian, Egyptian, Hebrew, Greek, and Roman king-lists so that the anchors of Scripture could be read against the fixed points of secular history.7 He preferred the longer Septuagint reckoning to the Hebrew; his reasons are treated at Eusebius of Caesarea. His comparative framework survives chiefly through Jerome's Latin version and the Chronography of George Syncellus, and Scaliger reconstructed it in 1606 (below).

The Alexandrian world era: the 5,500-year architecture (Panodorus and Annianus, c. 400–412)

The Alexandrian monks Panodorus (fl. c. 400) and Annianus (fl. c. 400–412, under Patriarch Theophilus of Alexandria) built the Alexandrian world era on the Septuagint figures. Reckoning the Greek begetting-ages they fixed Creation at 25 March 5492 BC (Annianus) / c. 5493 BC (Panodorus) and placed the Flood at Anno Mundi 2242, on the signature scheme of exactly 5,500 years from Creation to the Incarnation, all on 25 March (Creation, Annunciation, and Resurrection aligned).8 George Syncellus, who preserves the scheme, states his aim as confirming "the incarnation of Christ at the completion of the 5500th year." The Byzantine world era (Creation 1 September 5509 BC) and the Chronicon Paschale (5507 BC, c. 630) keep the same Flood at Anno Mundi 2242. They appear with the fuller reckoning on their detail page.

Augustine's City of God: the world "not yet 6,000 years," and the Hebrew preferred (c. 420)

Writing in Roman Africa at the very moment the Alexandrian monks were building the long chronology, Augustine took up the same enterprise and pulled it two ways. In City of God XII.10–11 he set the short biblical age against the Egyptians' and others' claims of "many thousand years," insisting "reckoning by the sacred writings, we find that not 6,000 years have yet passed"; and in XV.10–14 he weighed the hundred-year offset between the Hebrew and his own Latin's Septuagint parent and — discussing the Methuselah crux, where the Greek figures leave the patriarch alive fourteen years after the Flood he never boarded — judged the Hebrew reading true, tracing the Greek's added centuries to a single copyist's design and laying down the rule that where the versions diverge one should prefer "that language out of which the translation was made."9 The fuller treatment is on his detail page.

Joseph Scaliger's De emendatione temporum: technical chronology and the Julian Period (1583)

Joseph Justus Scaliger's (1540–1609) Opus novum de emendatione temporum (Paris, 1583) carried the reckoning of the ancient world beyond the Greeks and Romans to the Persian, Babylonian, Egyptian, and Jewish records read together under systematic rules of criticism — what Mark Pattison called raising chronology into "a rational procedure subject to fixed laws" — and in it Scaliger devised the Julian Period, a continuous 7,980-year count (28 × 19 × 15, the solar, lunar, and indiction cycles) with its year 1 set before all history at 4713 BC.10 That count — still used by astronomers — is a single continuous scale onto which the sacred anchors (Creation, the Flood, the Exodus) and the secular king-lists can be plotted and converted. His Thesaurus temporum (1606) reconstructed the lost Greek Chronicle of Eusebius (above) and gathered every surviving chronological relic in Greek or Latin. The Julian Period does not depend on which biblical numbers one reckons from; it fixes the frame, not the figures. His fuller contribution is on his detail page.

Petavius' De doctrina temporum: reckoning years "before Christ" (1627)

Denis Pétau — Dionysius Petavius (1583–1652) — was a French Jesuit who took up Scaliger's chronology. His Opus de doctrina temporum (1627) compiled a universal history from the earliest times to his own day in strict chronological order, and systematised counting years ante Christum ("before Christ") backward from the Incarnation — the proleptic BC frame that runs continuous with the Anno Domini count and let any pre-Christian date be stated as a single BC number rather than by Anno Mundi, Olympiad, or regnal year.11 Isolated "before the incarnation" datings existed earlier (Bede; the fifteenth-century time-lines), but it is Petavius' standard, spread through the reprintings and translations of his abridged Rationarium temporum (1633), that became the working BC/AD convention of Western historiography; Ussher (below) states his dates in this form. Like Scaliger's, his work concerns the form in which a biblical date is stated, not which text-tradition's numbers to use. His fuller contribution is on his detail page.

Ussher's Annals of the World: Creation 4004 BC on the Masoretic figures (1650)

James Ussher's Annales veteris testamenti fixed Creation at 4004 BC and carried the reckoning down through every anchor — the Flood at 2348 BC, the call of Abraham, the Exodus, Solomon's Temple, and on to the fixed points of Persian and Roman history — on the Masoretic figures for the primeval interval.12 Ussher noted that the primeval interval depends on the text chosen (Hebrew 1656 to the Flood, Samaritan 1307, Septuagint/Ethiopic longer) and adopted the Hebrew.

The modern Septuagint revival and the Masoretic-priority reply (c. 2015–present)

In the twenty-first century the Septuagint long chronology has been argued as a text-critical thesis by Henry B. Smith Jr. and the Associates for Biblical Research (the Genesis 5 & 11 Research Project), who argue that the longer Greek figures are original and that "most of the MT's chronology in Genesis 5 and 11… is the result of a deliberate and systematic post–AD 70 corruption" — reckoning Adam to Abraham at 3,394 years against the Masoretic 2,008, and seating Creation at c. 5554 BC and the Flood at c. 3298 BC. The revival draws on Jeremy Sexton's argument (2015) that the Genesis 5 and 11 begetting-ages leave no chronological gap, so that on his and Smith's account the question becomes which set of ages is original. In a Masoretic-priority position, Lita Sanders (Answers Research Journal, 2025) holds that the received Hebrew is "the better-preserved text and that the MT chronology was changed to create the chronologies in the LXX and SP", a position Lita Cosner and Robert Carter of Creation Ministries International also defend; on the separate gaps question Andrew Steinmann argues for chronological gaps against Sexton. The two sides have published replies to each other.13 The exchange concerns the same choice between the Hebrew and Greek figures that Eusebius, Augustine, and Ussher made in different ways. The Flood-date consequences are recorded on the dating of the Flood; the fuller account of the debate and its principals is on its detail page.

Disputed readings

How particular anchors of the reckoning have been read. The question of which text-tradition's figures fix the primeval interval (Masoretic, Septuagint, or Samaritan) is recorded on the dating of the Flood.

The length of the Egyptian sojourn

Exodus 12:40 gives the sojourn as 430 years: the Masoretic Hebrew reads it "in Egypt," while the Septuagint and Samaritan Pentateuch read "in Egypt and in Canaan."

  • Do the 430 years of Exodus 12:40 cover Israel's stay in Egypt alone, or Canaan and Egypt together? — on it turns whether Israel spent about 215 years in Egypt or 430.14,15
