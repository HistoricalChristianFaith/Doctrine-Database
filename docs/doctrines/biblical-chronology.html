Reckoning the Age of the World

How old is the world, and when did its great anchors fall — Creation, the Flood, the dispersion at Babel, Abraham, the Exodus, the building of Solomon's Temple? For two and a half millennia Jewish and Christian chronographers tried to reckon those dates from the numbers of Scripture and to lock them onto the fixed points of secular history — the Babylonian, Egyptian, Persian, Greek, and Roman king-lists — until, at the Persian period, sacred and secular chronology finally close on the same century. This page tracks that enterprise across time: who reckoned the age of the world, on what figures, and how the church's and the synagogue's answers shifted as the text-traditions diverged and the secular anchors came into view.

This is the tier above the dating of Noah's Flood, which is deliberately narrowed to one anchor and already weighs the Masoretic "short," Septuagint "long," and Samaritan reckonings for the Flood alone. Here the subject is the whole span and the secular anchors that page never reaches: the pyramids, the Egyptian chronology and the Sothic cycle, the date of the Exodus, and the Persian-period "missing years" where the rabbinic reckoning and the conventional one part by a century and a half. The dispute reaches into the Septuagint's origin and the transmission of the Hebrew text, because which numbers one reckons from is itself the first question.

A note on ordering: like the other reception-history timelines on this wiki, the "witnesses" below are the datable systems and chronographers that reckoned the age of the world — arranged by the age of the source (when each wrote), not by the age of the events they date, which is exactly what they disagree about. The order is a shelf of reckoners across time, not a claimed chronology of the world.

Related doctrines: Reckoning sacred time — see also the anchors under competing systems (the same anchor-shelf read under the fixed Masoretic, Septuagint, and rabbinic bundles, each beside its bill).

Timeline

Datable chronographers and world-eras that reckoned the age of the world, earliest source first (see the ordering note above). Several of these figures also stand as witnesses on the dating of the Flood, where their reckoning is developed for that one anchor; here they appear in their whole-span role and are cross-linked rather than re-quoted. The arguments that weigh the contested anchors are gathered in the "Arguments" section below.

Berossus' Babyloniaca: ten kings before the Flood, 432,000 years (composed c. 278 BC)

The enterprise begins with the scale it had to answer. The Babylonian priest Berossus, writing in Greek for Antiochus I, recorded ten kings reigning before the Flood for 120 saroi — 120 × 3,600 = 432,000 years — a pagan world-antiquity of a scope no biblical reckoning approaches.1 The ten antediluvian kings map onto the ten antediluvian patriarchs of Genesis 5, but the numbers are transparent sexagesimal artifice — every span a whole multiple of the šár (3,600 years) — figures generated inside a numbering system, not a chronology anyone kept. Berossus stands at the head of the timeline as the benchmark of deep antiquity that Jewish and Christian chronography set itself to out-reckon: the same ten-generation frame Genesis carries, inflated to a span the sacred figures answer with a few thousand years, not four hundred thousand.

Demetrius the Chronographer: the span first reckoned from the Septuagint's numbers (fl. c. 210 BC)

The enterprise proper — chronology computed from the biblical text — begins with Demetrius, a Jewish writer in Ptolemaic Alexandria and the earliest known Jewish author to compose in Greek. Reckoning the genealogies age by age from the Septuagint, he fixed the interval "from Adam until Joseph's brethren came into Egypt" at 3,624 years and "from the Deluge until Jacob's coming into Egypt" at 1,360 — figures that seat the Flood at Anno Mundi 2,264, all but identical to the Septuagint's 2,262 and nowhere near the Masoretic 1,656.2 He is the fountainhead of the whole tradition this timeline traces — the very method Africanus, Eusebius, and Ussher would practise, here attested five centuries before the first Christian universal chronology — and, as an early Septuagint reckoner, the earliest external witness that the longer Genesis 5–11 figures were already current, grist for the case that the Hebrew was later shortened. His fuller reckoning, and the detail that his numbers require the Methuselah-187 reading of Genesis 5:25, are on his detail page.

Josephus' Antiquities: the first continuous Jewish reckoning of the span (composed c. AD 93)

Flavius Josephus, retelling the sacred history in Greek for a Roman readership, ran an explicit chronology across the whole arc — from Adam to the Flood ("two thousand six hundred and fifty-six years from Adam the first man," Antiquities 1.82, a figure famous as a textual crux), onward through Abraham, the sojourn in Egypt, the Exodus, and the building of the Temple, closing his summaries with running totals from the Creation.3 His patriarch-ages carry the same centenary additions as the Septuagint's long chronology, so his interval runs some six centuries longer than the received Hebrew — making him an early first-century witness that the longer figures were already circulating among Jews, grist for the case that the Hebrew was later shortened. Yet his numbers are a law unto themselves, internally inconsistent and at odds with the Greek, the Hebrew, and even their own transmitted sums, so he fixes no calendar; his bearing here is as the earliest surviving attempt to reckon the whole span continuously. He appears on this wiki also as a witness on several timelines, gathered on his detail page.

Seder Olam Rabbah: the rabbinic Anno Mundi and the compressed Persian period (c. AD 160)

Against the Christian reckoners on either side of it stands the Jewish counterpart: Seder Olam Rabbah ("The Great Order of the World"), attributed to the second-century tanna R. Yose ben Halafta and the earliest post-biblical Hebrew chronology of the whole span. It reckons the primeval and patriarchal intervals on the Masoretic figures — Creation to the Flood 1,656 years, not the Septuagint's 2,262 — and is the source of the count the Hebrew calendar still carries, seating Creation at c. 3761 BC (so the Jewish year running through most of AD 2026 is AM 5786).4 Its signature and most consequential feature is a drastically compressed Persian period: it allots the whole Persian empire only about 52 years and a short line of kings (Cyrus, Ahasuerus, Darius), against some 207 years and fourteen kings in secular chronology — roughly 165 "missing years" that ripple down through every later date. That compression is the seed of a sharp argument, weighed in the Arguments section; here the system is recorded as it states itself — the Masoretic short chronology's rabbinic spine, against which the Septuagint world-eras on this shelf defined a rival age of the world. Its fuller reckoning is on its detail page.

Julius Africanus' Chronographiai: the first Christian universal chronology (c. 221)

Sextus Julius Africanus (c. 160–c. 240) wrote the first Christian reckoning of the whole span — five books running from Creation to his own day (c. AD 221) and synchronising the sacred history with the Olympiads and the Greek, Persian, and Egyptian records on one Anno Mundi frame, the enterprise Eusebius would bring to its tabular form a century later. Reckoning the Septuagint's longer figures he fixed the Flood at Anno Mundi 2262 (all but Demetrius' 2,264, and the number the Alexandrians later trimmed to 2242) and the Incarnation at Anno Mundi 5500 — laying down, against the Egyptian and Chaldean boasts of tens of thousands of years, the "5,500 years to Christ" architecture that later writers read as the midpoint of a six-thousand-year world-week and that the Alexandrian monks and the whole Byzantine tradition inherited.5 He is the hinge between Demetrius' bare Septuagint reckoning and Eusebius' comparative tables, and the Christian fountainhead of the long chronology the Flood-dating witnesses presuppose; his fuller reckoning — the 2262-to-the-Flood figure, the 5500/6000 scheme, and his computation of Daniel's seventy weeks to Christ — is on his detail page.

Eusebius' Chronicon: the three reckonings tabulated and synchronized (c. 320)

Eusebius of Caesarea built the first comparative Christian chronology, and it became the model for every later universal one. In the Chronicon he set the Hebrew, Septuagint, and Samaritan totals of Genesis 5–11 side by side and had to choose among them — the earliest surviving witness to the very text-critical dispute this topic turns on — and, in its famous parallel columns (the Chronological Canons), he ran the sacred reckoning alongside the Chaldean, Assyrian, Egyptian, Hebrew, Greek, and Roman king-lists so that the anchors of Scripture could be read against the fixed points of secular history.6 His own preference for the longer Septuagint reckoning against the Hebrew is itself part of the case that the shorter Hebrew figures were later shortened. His comparative framework survives chiefly through Jerome's Latin version and the Chronography of George Syncellus, and it is the tabular apparatus the whole later tradition reuses.

The Alexandrian world era: the 5,500-year architecture (Panodorus and Annianus, c. 400–412)

The Alexandrian monks Panodorus (fl. c. 400) and Annianus (fl. c. 400–412, under Patriarch Theophilus of Alexandria) built the Alexandrian world era on the Septuagint figures — the institutional carriers of the long chronology between Eusebius and Ussher. Reckoning the Greek begetting-ages they fixed Creation at 25 March 5492 BC (Annianus) / c. 5493 BC (Panodorus) and placed the Flood at Anno Mundi 2242, on the signature scheme of exactly 5,500 years from Creation to the Incarnation, all on 25 March (Creation, Annunciation, and Resurrection aligned).7 Their reckoning was carried forward unbroken by the Byzantine world era (Creation 1 September 5509 BC) and the Chronicon Paschale (5507 BC, c. 630), and it is the concrete evidence for a worry the Septuagint argument turns on — that the long figures rode inside a chronology committed in advance to seating Christ at Anno Mundi 5500, so the Creation date was set to reach the theological total rather than read off raw. They appear with the fuller reckoning on their detail page.

Augustine's City of God: the world "not yet 6,000 years," and the Hebrew preferred (c. 420)

Writing in Roman Africa at the very moment the Alexandrian monks were building the long chronology, Augustine took up the same enterprise and pulled it two ways. In City of God XII.10–11 he set the short biblical age against the Egyptians' and others' boasts of "many thousand years," insisting "reckoning by the sacred writings, we find that not 6,000 years have yet passed"; and in XV.10–14 he weighed the hundred-year offset between the Hebrew and his own Latin's Septuagint parent and — forced by the Methuselah crux, where the Greek figures leave the patriarch alive fourteen years after the Flood he never boarded — judged the Hebrew reading true, tracing the Greek's added centuries to a single copyist's design and laying down the rule that where the versions diverge one should prefer "that language out of which the translation was made."8 He is the earliest surviving Latin witness both to the short-age apologetic against pagan deep time and to the text-critical axis this topic turns on — and a pointed one, since the great champion of the Septuagint's authority nonetheless sided with the shorter Hebrew figures. The fuller treatment is on his detail page.

Joseph Scaliger's De emendatione temporum: chronology made a technical science (1583)

With Joseph Justus Scaliger (1540–1609) the enterprise becomes a science. His Opus novum de emendatione temporum (Paris, 1583) carried the reckoning of the ancient world beyond the Greeks and Romans to the Persian, Babylonian, Egyptian, and Jewish records read together under systematic rules of criticism — what Mark Pattison called raising chronology into "a rational procedure subject to fixed laws" — and in it Scaliger devised the Julian Period, a continuous 7,980-year count (28 × 19 × 15, the solar, lunar, and indiction cycles) with its year 1 set before all history at 4713 BC.9 That count — still used by astronomers — gave every historian a single neutral spine onto which the sacred anchors (Creation, the Flood, the Exodus) and the secular king-lists could be plotted and converted exactly, rather than merely harmonised. His Thesaurus temporum (1606) then reconstructed the lost Greek Chronicle of Eusebius (above) and gathered every surviving chronological relic in Greek or Latin, restoring the whole comparative apparatus the later universal chronologies reuse. The Julian Period is neutral as to which biblical numbers one reckons from — it fixes the frame, not the figures — so the Masoretic-vs-Septuagint dispute passed through him unresolved to Petavius and Ussher; his bearing here is as the scientific hinge between the ancient Anno Mundi reckoners and the early-modern ones. His fuller contribution is on his detail page.

Petavius' De doctrina temporum: reckoning years "before Christ" (1627)

Denis Pétau — Dionysius Petavius (1583–1652), the French Jesuit who carried Scaliger's chronology further — supplied the last piece of the modern apparatus. His Opus de doctrina temporum (1627) compiled a universal history from the earliest times to his own day in strict chronological order, and, most consequentially, systematised counting years ante Christum ("before Christ") backward from the Incarnation — the proleptic BC frame that runs continuous with the Anno Domini count and let any pre-Christian date be stated as a single BC number rather than by Anno Mundi, Olympiad, or regnal year.10 Isolated "before the incarnation" datings existed earlier (Bede; the fifteenth-century time-lines), but it is Petavius' standard, spread through the reprintings and translations of his abridged Rationarium temporum (1633), that became the working BC/AD convention of Western historiography — the very form in which Ussher (below) would write "4004 BC" a generation later. Like Scaliger he fixed the form a biblical date is stated in, not which text-tradition's numbers are right. His fuller contribution is on his detail page.

Ussher's Annals of the World: Creation 4004 BC, the canonical short chronology (1650)

James Ussher's Annales veteris testamenti fixed Creation at 4004 BC and carried the reckoning down through every anchor — the Flood at 2348 BC, the call of Abraham, the Exodus, Solomon's Temple, and on to the fixed points of Persian and Roman history where sacred and secular chronology lock — all on the Masoretic figures, the canonical statement of the short chronology and the number most readers still mean by "the biblical date" of anything.11 Ussher himself noted that the primeval interval depends on the text chosen (Hebrew 1656 to the Flood, Samaritan 1307, Septuagint/Ethiopic longer) and simply preferred the Hebrew; his dates are the fixed points the "short vs. long" arguments are organized around, and the tradition against which the modern Septuagint revival defines itself.

The modern Septuagint revival: the old dispute reopened with manuscript-critical tools (c. 2015–present)

The timeline closes where it began — on the same textual question, now live again. In the twenty-first century the Septuagint long chronology has been revived as a serious text-critical thesis by Henry B. Smith Jr. and the Associates for Biblical Research (the Genesis 5 & 11 Research Project), who argue that the longer Greek figures are original and that "most of the MT's chronology in Genesis 5 and 11… is the result of a deliberate and systematic post–AD 70 corruption" — reckoning Adam to Abraham at 3,394 years against the Masoretic 2,008, and seating Creation at c. 5554 BC and the Flood at c. 3298 BC. The revival turned on Jeremy Sexton's prior demonstration that the Genesis 5 and 11 begetting-ages leave no chronological gap to hide missing time in, so that which set of ages is original becomes the decisive question. Against them a Masoretic-priority defence — Lita Cosner/Sanders and Robert Carter of Creation Ministries International, and, on the gaps question, Andrew Steinmann — holds that the received Hebrew is "the better-preserved text and that the MT chronology was changed to create the chronologies in the LXX and SP," a published back-and-forth still unresolved.12 It is the living continuation of the very dispute Eusebius, Augustine, and Ussher took different sides of — the same two readings of the same genealogies, now argued on manuscript grounds — and so this timeline's modern terminus. Whether the reckoning fixes a defensible Flood date is weighed on the Flood-dating argument pages this timeline inherits rather than re-argues; the fuller account of the debate and its principals is on its detail page.

Arguments

The contested anchors of the reckoning, each weighed adversarially on its own page with an assessment of the interpretation it serves. The first axis — which biblical reckoning fixes the primeval interval (Masoretic, Septuagint, or Samaritan) — is already developed in full on the dating of the Flood, whose three text-tradition argument pages this timeline inherits rather than duplicates; that dispute reaches from the Flood down to Abraham and is the foundation of every world-era on the shelf above.

The second axis is the one flood-date never reaches: the secular anchors where sacred chronology meets the datable ground — the date of the Exodus (a fifteenth-century 1446 BC reckoning against a thirteenth-century one), the length of the Egyptian sojourn (430 years or 215), the Egyptian dynastic spine and the Sothic cycle that anchors it (which the short chronologies must compress), the pyramids built before Ussher's Flood, and the Persian-period "missing years" where the rabbinic Anno Mundi and the conventional reckoning part by roughly 165 years. These lines are being developed as their own argument pages; where one is not yet weighed, the question is stated here without a verdict rather than pre-judged, and the scoring of every anchor under the rival systems is gathered on the scenario ledger.

Related arguments — the date of the Exodus

The first of the secular anchors to be weighed. Scripture's own numbers place the Exodus in the mid-fifteenth century, but the Egyptian material record points to the thirteenth — and the date chosen reorganises the whole synchronism (which pharaoh oppressed, which Jericho fell, which conquest horizon). The two datings are each independently advocated, so each is steelmanned on its own page; they are best read as a set.

Does 1 Kings 6:1 fix the Exodus at c. 1446 BC? (assessment: contested)

The Bible's own numbers, taken at face value — 1 Kings 6:1 sets Solomon's fourth year (c. 966 BC) 480 years after the Exodus, giving c. 1446 BC, and Jephthah's "three hundred years" (Judges 11:26) is a second, independent internal datum pointing the same way; Bryant Wood's redating of Jericho's fall to c. 1400 BC gives the figure a live, if minority, archaeological anchor. The reckoning is textually straightforward and seriously defended (Merrill, Bimson, Wood). But it is genuinely disputed: it must read the Ramesside store-city name of Exodus 1:11 as a later editorial update, it sits awkwardly against an eighteenth-dynasty Egypt then controlling the very Canaan Israel is said to have conquered, and the Jericho redating that carries its conquest case is rejected by most excavators. Contested — strong on the internal-biblical chronology, weak on the material record.13

Does the Ramesside archaeology fix the Exodus in the thirteenth century BC? (assessment: contested)

The Egyptian material record, read against the number — the mainstream reading dates the Exodus c. 1250 BC under Rameses II, anchored on the store-city name of Exodus 1:11 (Raamses = Pi-Ramesse, the Delta capital Rameses II built) and the Merneptah Stele's terminus (Israel a people in Canaan by c. 1208 BC), reading 1 Kings 6:1's 480 years as a schematic 12 × 40. This is the better-anchored reading on Egyptian material culture (Kitchen, Hoffmeier) and the majority position. But it too is disputed: it must treat the Bible's two plain internal figures as symbol and rhetoric, it inherits the same empty Jericho and Ai the early date faces, and the Merneptah terminus squeezes an Exodus-plus-wandering into a tight window. Contested — strong on the Egyptian archaeology, weak on the internal-biblical chronology.14
