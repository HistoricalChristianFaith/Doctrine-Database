Peter Lombard on confirmation (Sentences IV, dist. 7: minister, power, rank, repetition)

Dates: c. 1155 · Wikipedia
Summary: In Book IV of the Sentences Peter Lombard gave confirmation its own distinction (dist. 7), after listing seven sacraments in dist. 2. In five short chapters he treats its minister, power, rank and repetition: the rite is reserved to bishops; its power is “the giving of the Holy Spirit for strength” (ad robur) as against baptism “for remission” (ad remissionem); it is in some sense of “greater veneration” than baptism, though baptism avails more for the forgiveness of sin; and, like baptism and orders, it is not to be repeated.

Claims & evidence

The form is assumed; the question is the power (dist. 7, c. 1)

Lombard opens the distinction by declining to argue the rite's form — it is already familiar — and pointing the inquiry instead at its virtus, its power or effect:

Nunc de Sacramento confirmationis addendum est, de cuius virtute quaeri solet. Forma enim aperta est, scilicet verba quae dicit episcopus, cum baptizatos in frontibus sacro signat chrismate.

— “Now something must be added concerning the sacrament of confirmation, about whose power a question is wont to be raised. For its form is plain — namely, the words which the bishop says when he signs the baptized on their foreheads with the holy chrism.” Lombard takes the bishop's signing of the forehead with chrism as known and asks what the seal does.1

Reserved to the bishop (dist. 7, c. 2)

Quod Sacramentum ab aliis perfici non potest nisi a summis sacerdotibus, nec tempore Apostolorum ab aliis quam ab ipsis Apostolis legitur peractum, nec ab aliis quam qui locum eorum tenent, perfici potest aut debet… Licet autem presbyteris baptizatos tangere in pectore, sed non chrismate signare in fronte.

— “This sacrament cannot be performed by any but the highest priests [bishops]; nor in the time of the Apostles do we read of its being performed by any but the Apostles themselves, nor can or ought it be performed by any but those who hold their place… Presbyters are permitted to touch the baptized on the breast, but not to sign the forehead with chrism.” If usurped by others, he adds, it is “held void and empty, and not reckoned among the ecclesiastical sacraments.” The rule matches the reservation of the forehead-seal to bishops in Innocent I and Bede.2

The power: the Spirit given “for strength” (dist. 7, c. 3)

Virtus vero huius Sacramenti est donatio Spiritus sancti ad robur, qui in baptismo datus est ad remissionem.

— “The power of this sacrament is the giving of the Holy Spirit for strength (ad robur), who in baptism was given for remission (ad remissionem).” Lombard grounds this antithesis in Rabanus Maurus: “A summo sacerdote per impositionem manus Paraclitus traditur baptizato, ut roboretur per Spiritum sanctum ad praedicandum aliis illud quod ipse in baptismo consecutus est” (“by the highest priest, through the laying on of the hand, the Paraclete is given to the baptized, that he may be strengthened by the Holy Spirit to preach to others that which he himself received in baptism”), and adds that all the faithful ought, through the bishops' hand-laying after baptism, to receive the Spirit “ut pleni Christiani inveniantur” — “that they may be found full Christians.”3

Is confirmation “greater” than baptism? (dist. 7, c. 4)

Lombard then faces the awkward question his own reservation raises: if only the bishop may confirm, but any priest may baptize, is confirmation the greater sacrament? He quotes the authority head-on —

Scitote, utrumque magnum esse Sacramentum, sed unum maiori veneratione tenendum, sicut a maioribus datur.

— “Know that both are a great sacrament, but one [confirmation] is to be held in greater veneration, since it is given by greater [ministers]” — and then carefully qualifies in what sense it can be called “greater”:

Ecce maius dicit Sacramentum confirmationis; forte non ob maiorem virtutem vel utilitatem, quam conferat, sed quia a dignioribus datur, et in digniori parte corporis fit, id est in fronte; vel forte, quia maius virtutum augmentum praestat, licet baptismus ad remissionem plus valeat.

— “Behold, it calls confirmation the greater sacrament — perhaps not on account of a greater power or benefit that it confers, but because it is given by worthier ministers, and is performed on a worthier part of the body, that is, the forehead; or perhaps because it bestows a greater increase of virtues, although baptism avails more for remission.” He backs the “increase” reading again from Rabanus: in baptism's anointing the Spirit descends “to consecrate a dwelling-place for God,” but in confirmation the Spirit's “sevenfold grace… with all fullness of holiness and power comes into a man.” Lombard thus ranks confirmation greater in veneration and in the increase of grace, while baptism “avails more for remission.” Aquinas later took up the question under the heading utrum dignius baptismo.4

Not to be repeated (dist. 7, c. 5)

Nec debet iterari, sicut nec baptismus vel ordo. Nulli enim Sacramento facienda est iniuria; quod fieri putatur, quando non-iterandum iteratur.

— “Nor ought it be repeated, as neither baptism nor orders; for no injury is to be done to any sacrament, as is thought to happen when what should not be repeated is repeated.” Aquinas later taught that these three sacraments imprint an indelible character.5

Notes

  • Confirmation in the sevenfold list. In dist. 2 of this same book Lombard lists the sacraments of the new law as seven — “baptism, confirmation, the bread of blessing (that is, the Eucharist), penance, extreme unction, orders, and marriage” (see the seven-sacraments timeline). Distinction 7 treats confirmation as the second member of that list. The councils of Florence (1439) and Trent (1547) later issued their own decrees on the sacrament.
  • The ad robur / augmentum language. The remission-versus-strengthening antithesis and the language of “increase” (augmentum, fullness) reach Lombard through Rabanus Maurus and, behind him, a homily the Middle Ages ascribed to Pope Melchiades, which Belcher and Chase report is usually attributed to Faustus of Riez, with questions about its dating.6 Aquinas also cites it as “Pope Melchiades” — “in Baptism we are born again unto life; after Baptism we are strengthened.”
  • The presbyteral exception. Within c. 3 Lombard reproduces Gregory the Great's letter to Januarius permitting presbyters to sign the forehead with chrism where no bishop is at hand — a concession he describes as granted “once only, for the sake of allaying scandal.” The bishop-only rule and the presbyteral exception stand side by side on his page.
  • On sourcing. The Latin above is the Quaracchi critical text (Sententiae in IV libris distinctae, ed. Ad Claras Aquas / Grottaferrata), Book IV, dist. 7, cc. 1–5. An English translation is Giulio Silano, The Sentences, Book 4: On the Doctrine of Signs (Toronto: Pontifical Institute of Mediaeval Studies, 2010), dist. 7.
