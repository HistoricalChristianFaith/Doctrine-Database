Peter Lombard on confirmation (the rite slotted into the sevenfold count)

Dates: c. 1155 · Wikipedia
Summary: In Book IV of the Sentences — the theology textbook of the medieval West for four centuries — Peter Lombard gave confirmation its own distinction (dist. 7) in the list of sacraments his enumeration had just fixed at seven. In five short chapters he settles the questions the schools would inherit: the rite is reserved to bishops; its power is “the giving of the Holy Spirit for strength” (ad robur) as against baptism “for remission” (ad remissionem); it is in some sense of “greater veneration” than baptism, though baptism avails more for the forgiveness of sin; and, like baptism and orders, it is not to be repeated. This block sits between Bede and Aquinas and marks the moment the sealing is formally slotted into the canonical count and handed, in textbook form, to every theologian who would lecture on it.

Claims & evidence

The form is assumed; the question is the power (dist. 7, c. 1)

Lombard opens the distinction by declining to argue the rite's form — it is already familiar — and pointing the inquiry instead at its virtus, its power or effect:

Nunc de Sacramento confirmationis addendum est, de cuius virtute quaeri solet. Forma enim aperta est, scilicet verba quae dicit episcopus, cum baptizatos in frontibus sacro signat chrismate.

— “Now something must be added concerning the sacrament of confirmation, about whose power a question is wont to be raised. For its form is plain — namely, the words which the bishop says when he signs the baptized on their foreheads with the holy chrism.” The chrism-on-the-forehead-by-the-bishop shape is simply presupposed as the Western rite; what Lombard takes to need settling is what the seal does.1

Reserved to the bishop (dist. 7, c. 2)

Quod Sacramentum ab aliis perfici non potest nisi a summis sacerdotibus, nec tempore Apostolorum ab aliis quam ab ipsis Apostolis legitur peractum, nec ab aliis quam qui locum eorum tenent, perfici potest aut debet… Licet autem presbyteris baptizatos tangere in pectore, sed non chrismate signare in fronte.

— “This sacrament cannot be performed by any but the highest priests [bishops]; nor in the time of the Apostles do we read of its being performed by any but the Apostles themselves, nor can or ought it be performed by any but those who hold their place… Presbyters are permitted to touch the baptized on the breast, but not to sign the forehead with chrism.” If usurped by others, he adds, it is “held void and empty, and not reckoned among the ecclesiastical sacraments.” This restates the Latin reservation of the forehead-seal to the episcopate that Innocent I had drawn and Bede had carried forward.2

The power: the Spirit given “for strength” (dist. 7, c. 3)

Virtus vero huius Sacramenti est donatio Spiritus sancti ad robur, qui in baptismo datus est ad remissionem.

— “The power of this sacrament is the giving of the Holy Spirit for strength (ad robur), who in baptism was given for remission (ad remissionem).” This one-line antithesis — remission in baptism, strengthening in confirmation — is the keynote the whole scholastic tradition would take over. Lombard grounds it in Rabanus Maurus: “A summo sacerdote per impositionem manus Paraclitus traditur baptizato, ut roboretur per Spiritum sanctum ad praedicandum aliis illud quod ipse in baptismo consecutus est” (“by the highest priest, through the laying on of the hand, the Paraclete is given to the baptized, that he may be strengthened by the Holy Spirit to preach to others that which he himself received in baptism”), and adds that all the faithful ought, through the bishops' hand-laying after baptism, to receive the Spirit “ut pleni Christiani inveniantur” — “that they may be found full Christians.”3

Is confirmation “greater” than baptism? (dist. 7, c. 4)

Lombard then faces the awkward question his own reservation raises: if only the bishop may confirm, but any priest may baptize, is confirmation the greater sacrament? He quotes the authority head-on —

Scitote, utrumque magnum esse Sacramentum, sed unum maiori veneratione tenendum, sicut a maioribus datur.

— “Know that both are a great sacrament, but one [confirmation] is to be held in greater veneration, since it is given by greater [ministers]” — and then carefully qualifies in what sense it can be called “greater”:

Ecce maius dicit Sacramentum confirmationis; forte non ob maiorem virtutem vel utilitatem, quam conferat, sed quia a dignioribus datur, et in digniori parte corporis fit, id est in fronte; vel forte, quia maius virtutum augmentum praestat, licet baptismus ad remissionem plus valeat.

— “Behold, it calls confirmation the greater sacrament — perhaps not on account of a greater power or benefit that it confers, but because it is given by worthier ministers, and is performed on a worthier part of the body, that is, the forehead; or perhaps because it bestows a greater increase of virtues, although baptism avails more for remission.” He backs the “increase” reading again from Rabanus: in baptism's anointing the Spirit descends “to consecrate a dwelling-place for God,” but in confirmation the Spirit's “sevenfold grace… with all fullness of holiness and power comes into a man.” The careful ranking — greater in veneration and in the increase of grace, yet not in saving necessity, since baptism “avails more for remission” — is exactly the balance Aquinas would weigh again under his own heading utrum dignius baptismo.4

Not to be repeated (dist. 7, c. 5)

Nec debet iterari, sicut nec baptismus vel ordo. Nulli enim Sacramento facienda est iniuria; quod fieri putatur, quando non-iterandum iteratur.

— “Nor ought it be repeated, as neither baptism nor orders; for no injury is to be done to any sacrament, as is thought to happen when what should not be repeated is repeated.” Grouping confirmation with baptism and orders as unrepeatable is the seed of the later doctrine — developed by Aquinas — that these three imprint an indelible character.5

Notes

  • Where the rite enters the canonical count. In dist. 2 of this same book Lombard fixed the sacraments of the new law at seven — “baptism, confirmation, the bread of blessing (that is, the Eucharist), penance, extreme unction, orders, and marriage” — the first appearance of the exact sevenfold list that becomes the Western standard. Distinction 7 is where he then treats confirmation as the second member of that list. Because the Sentences became the set text every medieval theologian lectured on, this placement propagated with the curriculum and stood behind the definitions of Florence (1439) and Trent (1547).
  • The ad robur / augmentum frame he hands on. The remission-versus-strengthening antithesis and the language of “increase” (augmentum, fullness) reach Lombard through Rabanus Maurus and, behind him, a homily the Middle Ages ascribed to Pope Melchiades (in fact drawn from Faustus of Riez by way of the False Decretals). It is the same source-frame Aquinas would cite as “Pope Melchiades” — “in Baptism we are born again unto life; after Baptism we are strengthened” — so Lombard is the channel through which this vocabulary became the standard scholastic account.
  • A crack already showing on the minister. Within c. 3 Lombard reproduces Gregory the Great's letter to Januarius permitting presbyters to sign the forehead with chrism where no bishop is at hand — a concession he flags as granted “once only, for the sake of allaying scandal.” The bishop-only rule and the presbyteral exception sit side by side on his page, the very tension the wiki weighs on the minister arguments.
  • On sourcing. The Latin above is the Quaracchi critical text (Sententiae in IV libris distinctae, ed. Ad Claras Aquas / Grottaferrata), Book IV, dist. 7, cc. 1–5. An English translation is Giulio Silano, The Sentences, Book 4: On the Doctrine of Signs (Toronto: Pontifical Institute of Mediaeval Studies, 2010), dist. 7.
