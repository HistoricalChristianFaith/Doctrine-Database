Thomas Aquinas on confirmation (the mature scholastic theology)

Dates: c. 1274 · Wikipedia
Summary: In the twelve articles of Summa Theologiae III, q.72 (c. 1272), Aquinas gives confirmation its mature scholastic theology — the account the Councils of Florence (1439) and Trent (1547) would draw on directly. Confirmation is a distinct sacrament instituted by Christ (a.1); its matter is chrism of oil and balsam consecrated by a bishop (a.2–3); it imprints a sacramental character (a.5); its proper effect is spiritual growth to maturity and strengthening (robur) for the public confession of faith — the soldier-of-Christ theme (a.5, a.7); and its proper minister is the bishop (a.11). This block fills the long gap the timeline otherwise leaves between Pseudo-Dionysius (c. 500) and Florence.

Claims & evidence

Confirmation is a distinct sacrament, promised by Christ (a.1)

Aquinas grounds confirmation's distinctness on the principle that a special effect of grace marks a special sacrament, and reads its effect through the analogy of bodily life: as baptism is spiritual birth, confirmation is spiritual growth to maturity.

So therefore does man receive spiritual life in Baptism, which is a spiritual regeneration: while in Confirmation man arrives at the perfect age, as it were, of the spiritual life. Hence Pope Melchiades says: “The Holy Ghost… bestows at the font, the fulness of innocence; but in Confirmation He confers an increase of grace. In Baptism we are born again unto life; after Baptism we are strengthened.” And therefore it is evident that Confirmation is a special sacrament.

Against the view (he names Alexander of Hales and Bonaventure) that the sacrament was instituted only later by a council, and the view (Pierre de Tarentaise) that the apostles instituted it, Aquinas holds that Christ instituted it — “not by bestowing, but by promising it” in the promise of the Paraclete (John 16:7), “because in this sacrament the fulness of the Holy Ghost is bestowed, which was not to be given before Christ's Resurrection and Ascension.”1

Matter: chrism of oil and balsam, consecrated by a bishop (a.2–3)

Chrism is the fitting matter of this sacrament… Now the grace of the Holy Ghost is signified by oil; hence Christ is said to be “anointed with the oil of gladness”… And balm is mixed with the oil, by reason of its fragrant odor, which spreads about: hence the Apostle says (2 Cor. 2:15): “We are the good odor of Christ.”

He answers the obvious objection — that the apostles conferred the Spirit by the bare imposition of hands, without chrism (Acts 8:17) — by distinguishing the miraculous bestowal of the sacrament's effect under visible signs (fire and tongues at Pentecost, answering to oil and balm) from the ordinary sacramental use: “the apostles commonly made use of chrism in bestowing the sacrament, when such like visible signs were lacking.” And because “Christ did not make use of visible anointings,” the chrism (unlike baptismal water) must be consecrated beforehand by a bishop — here he quotes Innocent I's letter to Decentius almost verbatim: priests may anoint with chrism “previously consecrated by a bishop: but they must not sign the brow with the same oil; this belongs to the bishop alone, when he gives the Paraclete.”2

Form and the soldier-of-Christ theme (a.4, a.9)

The above form is appropriate to this sacrament… in this sacrament the Holy Ghost is given for strength in the spiritual combat… The third is the sign which is given to the combatant, as in a bodily combat: thus are soldiers marked with the sign of their leaders. And to this refer the words, “I sign thee with the sign of the cross,” in which sign, to wit, our King triumphed.

The seal is placed on the forehead — the most conspicuous, uncovered part of the body — so that the confirmed “may show publicly that he is a Christian” and so that “neither fear nor shame may hinder him from confessing the name of Christ” (a.9). This is the martial, public-confession keynote that Florence would echo (“so that the Christian might boldly confess the name of Christ”).3

It imprints a sacramental character (a.5–6)

Aquinas holds that confirmation, being unrepeatable, imprints an indelible character — a spiritual power ordained to sacred actions beyond those of baptism:

by the sacrament of Confirmation man is given a spiritual power in respect of sacred actions other than those in respect of which he receives power in Baptism. For in Baptism he receives power to do those things which pertain to his own salvation… whereas in Confirmation he receives power to do those things which pertain to the spiritual combat with the enemies of the Faith. This is evident from the example of the apostles, who, before they received the fulness of the Holy Ghost, were in the “upper room… persevering… in prayer”… whereas afterwards they went out and feared not to confess their faith in public.

The confirmation character “of necessity supposes the baptismal character”: an unbaptized person who was “confirmed” would receive nothing (a.6). Whether confirmation in fact imprints an indelible character — the sub-claim this article advances — is a distinct question weighed on its own terms elsewhere on this wiki; here the block reports Aquinas's position rather than adjudicating it.4

Effect: an increase of sanctifying grace unto strength and growth (a.7)

sanctifying grace is given not only for the remission of sin, but also for growth and stability in righteousness. And thus it is bestowed in this sacrament.

The grace of confirmation does not differ in kind from baptismal grace taken broadly — “it does not differ from that bestowed in Baptism, but increases what was already there” — yet as sacramental grace it adds a special effect, strengthening for the outward combat. Because it “confirms what it finds already there,” it presupposes a state of grace, so an adult in sin should first be restored by penance.5

Proper minister: the bishop (a.11)

the conferring of this sacrament is reserved to bishops, who possess supreme power in the Church: just as in the primitive Church, the fulness of the Holy Ghost was given by the apostles, in whose place the bishops stand (Acts 8).

Aquinas frames the reservation by an artisan analogy — “the final completion is reserved to the supreme act or power” — confirmation being “the final completion of the sacrament of Baptism.” He is careful, however, about how tight the reservation is: replying to Gregory the Great's permission for simple priests to confirm where no bishop is at hand, he attributes it to the pope's “plenitude of power… in virtue of which he can commit to certain lower orders things that belong to the higher orders.” The bishop is thus the proper minister, but the rite can be committed to a presbyter by papal authority — the very tension the wiki weighs on the minister arguments.6

Notes

  • The hinge to Florence and Trent. Aquinas's q.72 is the direct doctrinal source of the definitions already on this timeline: the chrism-of-oil-and-balsam matter, the “I sign thee with the sign of the cross… and confirm thee with the chrism of salvation” form, the effect of strength for bold confession “as at Pentecost,” and the bishop as ordinary minister all pass into Florence's Exsultate Deo (1439) and stand behind Trent's canons (1547).
  • Reads Innocent I forward. His sed contra in a.3 quotes Innocent I's letter to Decentius — the same reservation of the forehead-seal to the bishop that Bede had carried into the early-medieval West — showing the continuity of the Latin discipline from the fifth century through the scholastic synthesis.
  • The seal proof-texts. In his Pauline commentaries Aquinas expounds the “anointing” and “sealing” of 2 Corinthians 1:21–22 — God “has anointed us… He anointed us, I say, as kings and priests,” and “has put his seal upon us with the sign of Christian faith… i.e., the sign of the cross” — though there he reads the sealing chiefly as the Spirit's configuring of the believer to Christ rather than tying it narrowly to the sacrament.7
