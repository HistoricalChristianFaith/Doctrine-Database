Is confirmation a distinct sacrament, or the completion of baptism / a human ceremony? (the Reformed denial)

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — whether confirmation is a sacrament turns on what a sacrament is, and that is the disputed premise. Judged by the Reformed rule — a sign instituted by Christ in the Gospel — the case that there is no distinct confirmation-sacrament is strong: no dominical word commands a chrism-sealing, the seven-count and the “increase of grace” theology are medieval, and a weighty patristic reading (Chrysostom's) takes the Acts gift to be the extraordinary Spirit of the apostolic age. Judged by the older sacramental grammar it collides with an ancient, near-universal practice of sealing the baptized that is no medieval fiction, and Calvin's charge that the rite “insults” baptism is polemical overreach against a tradition (the chrismating East above all) that never held baptism incomplete. Genuinely disputed, no neutral tiebreaker · advanced by John Calvin (Institutes IV.19) and the Reformed and Anglican confessions.

The claim

But those miraculous powers and manifest operations, which were distributed by the laying on of hands, have ceased. They were only for a time.

— John Calvin, Institutes IV.19.6, source.1 The interpretation assessed: that confirmation is not a distinct sacrament instituted by Christ — the apostolic laying-on-of-hands having been an extraordinary, unrepeatable charism, and the medieval rite a human addition that wrongs baptism by implying it was incomplete — so that a genuine “confirmation” can be restored only as a catechetical profession of the baptized child's faith.

Who advances it

The denial is the Reformers'. Calvin gives it its fullest statement in the Institutes, treating confirmation among “the five sacraments, falsely so called”: the gift conferred by the apostles' hands “have ceased… only for a time,” and a later age, “having almost obliterated the reality, introduced a kind of fictitious confirmation as a divine sacrament.”2 The Anglican Thirty-Nine Articles (art. XXV) formalize the same verdict: confirmation is among “those five commonly called Sacraments… not to be counted for Sacraments of the Gospel,” having “grown partly of the corrupt following of the Apostles,” lacking “any visible sign or ceremony ordained of God.”3 Against it stands the whole sacramental tradition, dogmatized at Trent, Session VII, which anathematized any who call confirmation “an idle ceremony” and “not rather a true and proper sacrament.”4

The evidence for

Three planks carry the Reformed reading, and each is real. (1) No dominical institution. Baptism and the Supper have an express command of Christ in the Gospel; a chrism-and-hand-seal has none. The point is conceded even from within the sacramental camp: Aquinas can say only that Christ instituted confirmation “not by bestowing, but by promising it” — an institution by promise, not by any recorded ordinance.5 (2) The apostolic gift was extraordinary. The hand-laying of Acts 8 and 19 conferred the visible, charismatic Spirit — tongues and prophecy — and that reading has a patristic ally: John Chrysostom takes the Samaritans to have received in baptism “the Spirit… of remission of sins,” and the apostles' hands to impart “the Spirit of miracles,” the sign proper to the apostolic age, not a standing sacrament.6 When “the Lord ceased from these,” Calvin argues, no repeatable rite was left to imitate them. (3) The enumeration and its theology are medieval. Numbering the sacraments at seven and defining confirmation's virtue as an “increase of grace” added to baptismal righteousness are later constructions — a point the scriptural-warrant page makes on other grounds: the distinctness is theology, not narrative. Calvin's positive proposal is not iconoclasm but retrieval — he would “retain the custom… which existed in the early Church” of a baptized child, “in boyhood, or immediately beyond it,” coming forward to “give an account of their faith in the face of the Church.”7

Adversarial assessment

What holds. On the definition the Reformers work from — a sacrament is a sign instituted by Christ in the Gospel — the conclusion follows cleanly: there is no gospel word instituting a distinct chrism-rite, and even its ablest defender rests its institution on a promise rather than a command. The claim that the Acts gift was the extraordinary Spirit is not a Reformed novelty but a reading a major Greek Father already held, and the seven-fold enumeration is demonstrably medieval. As a denial that Scripture mandates a second, distinct sacrament, the argument stands on solid ground.

What is overstated. The denial overreaches at two of its hinges. (1) The rite is no medieval “fiction.” Calvin's history is wrong when it makes confirmation a late invention that “almost obliterated” reality: a post-baptismal sealing of the neophyte is attested from the earliest Latin witness (Tertullian, c. 200) and the earliest liturgies (the Apostolic Tradition, c. 215), was read as conferring the Spirit by Cyprian and Cyril of Jerusalem long before the schools, and survives unbroken to this day in the Eastern chrismation the Reformers never confronted. Whatever its status as a sacrament, the practice is ancient and near-universal, not a fabrication. (2) The “insult to baptism” is polemic, not argument. Calvin charges that the rite is “a noted insult to baptism, the use of which it obscures — nay, abolishes,” because its defenders “deny that baptism can be duly completed without confirmation.”8 But the older grammar — especially the East's — does not set the seal against baptism: it reads baptism-and-chrism as one initiation, the sealing perfecting what the water begins, no more insulting baptism than the second half of a rite insults the first. Calvin answers the Latin separation of the rites (which the timeline's own witnesses, from Jerome to Innocent I, treat as a matter of church order) as though it were an attack on baptism's sufficiency, which even the tradition he opposes did not intend. (3) The verdict is definition-bound. That confirmation is “not a sacrament” is true on the two-sacrament definition and false on the broader patristic mysterion; the argument does not settle which definition is right, it presupposes one. That is why the dispute does not resolve: the premise is the whole question.

Assessment

Contested. As a denial that Christ instituted a distinct confirmation-sacrament, the Reformed reading is strong — there is no dominical institution, the enumeration is medieval, and the extraordinary-Spirit reading of Acts is held even by a Greek Father. As a total account it overreaches: the post-baptismal sealing is an ancient, unbroken practice, not a medieval fiction, and the charge that it “insults” baptism answers a Western separation the East never made and the tradition never intended. The disagreement is finally about the definition of “sacrament” — strong by the Reformed rule of dominical institution, weak by the patristic grammar of the mysterion — and Scripture and the Fathers underdetermine the choice. Genuinely disputed, with a real case on each side and no neutral arbiter (contested).
