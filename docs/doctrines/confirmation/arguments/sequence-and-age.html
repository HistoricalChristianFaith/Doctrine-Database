Does the Western deferral of confirmation reverse the original order of initiation? (the Eastern all-at-once reading)

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: sound on the history — that the unified order baptism → chrism → eucharist is the older, near-universal shape, and that the Latin West progressively deferred the seal and (later) inverted the sequence, is near-consensus among liturgical historians and conceded by Rome’s own Catechism; the further charge that the deferral distorts initiation is a contestable value-judgment the West answers · advanced by the Christian East and by liturgical scholarship (J. D. C. Fisher’s “disintegration” thesis).

The claim

In the first centuries Confirmation generally comprised one single celebration with Baptism, forming with it a “double sacrament,” according to the expression of St. Cyprian… In the West the desire to reserve the completion of Baptism to the bishop caused the temporal separation of the two sacraments. The East has kept them united, so that Confirmation is conferred by the priest who baptizes.

— Catechism of the Catholic Church §1290, link.1 The interpretation assessed: the ancient, normative shape of Christian initiation is a single continuous rite — water, then the chrism-seal, then the eucharist — administered together to a candidate of any age; the Christian East preserves that order unbroken (chrismating and historically communicating even infants immediately after baptism), while the Latin West, by reserving the forehead-seal to the bishop, progressively pushed confirmation off to an “age of discretion,” and in the modern rite has even let first communion precede it — reversing the primitive sequence.

Who advances it

Three converging voices. (1) The Christian East, whose practice simply is the older shape: the presbyter who baptizes chrismates at once with myron the bishop or patriarch consecrates, and the newly initiated — infants included — are communicated the same day, keeping baptism → chrismation → eucharist intact. (2) Liturgical scholarship, above all J. D. C. Fisher, whose Christian Initiation: Baptism in the Medieval West (1965) is subtitled A Study in the Disintegration of the Primitive Rite of Initiation — the thesis that the West’s one integrated rite came apart into three separated moments not on principle but under practical pressure, precisely because it kept the bishop as confirmation’s minister.2 (3) The Catholic Church’s own Catechism, which does not dispute the history but narrates it exactly as above — the East “has kept them united,” the West separated them out of “the desire to reserve the completion of Baptism to the bishop.”

The evidence for

(1) The oldest attested shape is the unified one. Every early full description of the seal places it in one continuous initiation with the font, not as a later free-standing rite: Tertullian (c. 200) narrates font, unction, and hand-laying as one act; the Apostolic Tradition (c. 215) runs baptism straight into the presbyter’s anointing and the bishop’s forehead-seal; Cyril of Jerusalem (c. 350) and Pseudo-Dionysius (c. 500) describe a chrism-sealing joined directly to baptism and followed by the eucharist. The order baptism → seal → eucharist is the datum from which both traditions start.

(2) The West’s own documents trace the split to the bishop-reservation. The wedge is Innocent I (416): presbyters may anoint with the bishop’s chrism, “but… are not permitted to sign the forehead… which signing pertains to bishops only.” Once the forehead-seal is the bishop’s alone, and one bishop cannot be present at every rural infant-baptism through the year, the seal must wait for his visitation — so confirmation drifts later and later. This is Fisher’s mechanism and the Catechism’s stated cause alike: the separation was “owing to difficulties of a practical nature,” not a doctrinal decision.3

(3) The modern Latin sequence actually inverts the order — and Rome knows it. The Catechism itself records that in the Latin rite infant baptism “is followed by years of catechesis before being completed later by Confirmation and the Eucharist.”4 But in much of the Latin West today it is Eucharist that comes before confirmation — the reverse of the ancient climax. That inversion is recent and traceable: Pius X’s Quam singulari (1910) anchored first communion at the “age of discretion,” about the seventh year, while confirmation — still the bishop’s — stayed later, so communion slipped ahead of the seal.5 That a “Restored Order” movement now works to put confirmation before communion again is itself an admission from inside the Latin church that the current sequence departs from the traditional one.

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The descriptive core is about as secure as a historical thesis gets. That the older shape was one continuous initiation, of any age, in the order baptism → seal → eucharist is uncontested; that the East preserves it and the West took it apart is granted by Roman and Eastern sources alike; and that the immediate cause of the Western deferral was the reservation of the seal to the bishop is stated by the Catechism in as many words. The later inversion — communion before confirmation — is equally well documented and even named an anomaly to be “restored” by those who defer least happily to it. On the history, the reading is sound.

What is contested. The step from “the West reversed the order” to “the West distorts initiation” is a value-judgment, and there the Latin tradition answers rather than concedes. Rome frames the two sequences as two legitimate traditions, each with its own emphasis: the East “gives greater emphasis to the unity of Christian initiation,” the Latin practice “more clearly expresses the communion of the new Christian with the bishop” and lets confirmation carry a distinct note of mature Christian commitment (the robur, the “soldier of Christ” keynote weighed elsewhere on this timeline).6 A development driven by practical necessity, and defensible on its own theological terms, is not obviously a “distortion” — that word imports the Eastern norm as the measure. The honest split: the East’s claim to the older and more unified order is sound; the charge that the Western order is thereby corrupt rather than a divergent-but-valid development is the genuinely disputed part, and belongs to the same East–West standoff as the minister question.

Assessment

Sound — as a claim about which order is older and how the Western one arose. The unified rite (baptism, then the chrism-seal, then the eucharist, at any age) is the primitive and near-universal shape; the East keeps it, and the Latin West deferred the seal precisely because it reserved the forehead-anointing to the bishop, later even letting communion precede confirmation. That much is conceded across the traditions, the Catholic Catechism included. The proponent’s further step — that the Western sequence therefore distorts initiation — is a normative overreach on an otherwise sound historical reading: the West answers with a coherent theology of a distinct, bishop-linked maturity-rite, so whether the reversal is a corruption or a legitimate development is contested, not settled. The datum also bears on infant baptism: the East’s unbroken practice of chrismating and communicating infants immediately is first-order evidence of how the primitive church treated the newborn children of believers.
