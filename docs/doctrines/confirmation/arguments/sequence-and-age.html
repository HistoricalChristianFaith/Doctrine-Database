Did early churches share one order of initiation, and how does the Latin West's separate confirmation relate to it?

Disputed reading · on Confirmation & Chrismation · witness: Innocent I

The passage

For it is permitted presbyters, when they baptize either without a bishop or in the presence of a bishop, to anoint the baptized with chrism, but with chrism which has been consecrated by a bishop; they are not permitted, however, to sign the forehead with that same oil, which signing pertains to bishops only, when they confer the Spirit Paraclete.

— Innocent I, Letter to Decentius of Gubbio (Ep. 25, 416), Denzinger no. 98.1

Today in all the rites, Latin and Eastern, the Christian initiation of adults begins with their entry into the catechumenate and reaches its culmination in a single celebration of the three sacraments of initiation: Baptism, Confirmation, and the Eucharist. In the Eastern rites the Christian initiation of infants also begins with Baptism followed immediately by Confirmation and the Eucharist, while in the Roman rite it is followed by years of catechesis before being completed later by Confirmation and the Eucharist, the summit of their Christian initiation.

— Catechism of the Catholic Church §1233.2

The question

The earliest accounts on this timeline (Tertullian, c. 200; the Apostolic Tradition, c. 215) narrate the water, the anointing and the hand-laying as one ceremony; Innocent I (416) reserves the signing of the forehead to bishops; today the Eastern churches chrismate and communicate infants at their baptism, while in the Latin rite those baptized as infants are confirmed years later, in many places after their first communion. In 1897 Leo XIII commended confirming children before their first communion “as being more in accord with the ancient usage of the Church,” as T. B. Scannell reports. Pius X’s Quam singulari (1910) set the age of discretion for first communion at “about the seventh year, more or less.”3 The dispute is whether early Christians shared one order of initiation, whether the Latin West's separate confirmation departs from it, and what that separate rite is.

Baptism is the whole of sacramental initiation; any later rite for those baptized as infants adds no sacramental gift to it

On this reading baptism admits a person fully to the church and gives the Spirit; whatever rite follows for those baptized as infants (catechesis and profession, or a commissioning) adds no sacramental gift of initiation.

Held by: Martin Luther (Lutheran, The Babylonian Captivity of the Church, 1520); John Calvin (Reformed, Institutes, 1559); G. W. H. Lampe (Anglican, The Seal of the Spirit, 1951) ⚠ unverified; the Commission on Theology of the Reformed Church in America (Reformed, report to General Synod, 1992).4

Luther holds that “in order that there be a sacrament, there is required above all things a word of divine promise,” and finds none given for confirmation: “it is sufficient to regard confirmation as a certain churchly rite or sacramental ceremony, similar to other ceremonies, such as the blessing of holy water and the like.” He wishes for “such a laying on of hands as there was in apostolic times, whether we called it confirmation or healing!”5

Calvin reads the early sources as describing a catechetical rite for those baptized in infancy: “It was anciently customary for the children of Christians, after they had grown up, to appear before the bishop to fulfil that duty which was required of such adults as presented themselves for baptism.” Such children, “toward the end of their boyhood, or on adolescence,” were “examined by the bishop in terms of the Catechism which was then in common use,” and “the ceremony of laying on of hands was also used. Thus the boy, on his faith being approved, was dismissed with a solemn blessing.” He cites Leo and Jerome for the hand-laying.6

Philip E. Hughes reports that the compilers of the first English Book of Common Prayer (1549) treated baptism as “the fully adequate sacrament of Christian initiation.” Lampe, as Hughes quotes him, holds that “Pauline thought affords no ground whatever for the modern theories which seek to effect a separation in the one action and to distinguish a ‘Spirit-baptism’ and a ‘water-baptism’, not as the inward and outward parts of one sacrament, but as independent entities,” for “baptism effects incorporation into Christ. If we are in Christ, we are in the Spirit, or the Spirit is in us.” Lampe reads confirmation as “a commissioning for active service in the missionary enterprise.”7

The 1992 Reformed Church in America report reads the early sources as showing the anointing and hand-laying of Tertullian and the Apostolic Tradition “not yet separated from baptism. Infants and adults received them equally, and baptism was regarded as granting admission to the Lord’s Table.” The report holds that the Reformed practice of a profession of faith arose “not as a kind of confirmation, but instead of confirmation,” and that “in the Dutch Reformed tradition, there was nothing liturgical per se between baptism and communion. What was in between was catechism.” Baptism makes the member; catechesis and profession prepare the baptized for the Table.8

Confirmation gives the Spirit for strength, and is fittingly conferred once the baptized reach the use of reason

On this reading the separate Western rite has its own purpose: baptism gives new birth, and confirmation, conferred once a child can use reason, gives the Spirit for the strength of Christian maturity.

Held by: the Roman Catechism (Catholic, Catechism of the Council of Trent, 1566); T. B. Scannell (Catholic, The Catholic Encyclopedia, 1908).9

The Roman Catechism compares the Church to a mother who wishes her children to “grow up and attain full maturity”: she “earnestly desires that, in those whom she has regenerated by Baptism, the perfection of Christian manhood be completed,” and “this is accomplished through the Sacrament of mystic Unction.” Confirmation “may indeed be administered to all” after baptism, “but that, until children shall have attained the use of reason, its administration is inexpedient”; “it is most proper to postpone this Sacrament at least to that of seven years,” since it was instituted “that by virtue thereof we may be found very well armed and prepared when called upon to fight for the faith of Christ.” Scannell defines confirmation as “a sacrament in which the Holy Ghost is given to those already baptized in order to make them strong and perfect Christians and soldiers of Jesus Christ.” He gives its history: “In the early ages of the Church, confirmation was part of the rite of initiation, and consequently was administered immediately after baptism. When, however, baptism came to be conferred by simple priests, the two ceremonies were separated in the Western Church. Further, when infant baptism became customary, confirmation was not administered until the child had attained the use of reason.” Its effects include “the seven gifts of the Holy Ghost and notably … the strength and courage to confess boldly the name of Christ.” Belcher and Chase describe this “theological-maturity” model as having “dominated before Vatican II,” with its focus on “the giving of the Holy Spirit for human growth and strength.” Scannell, citing Aquinas, relates confirmation to baptism as growth to birth.10

One primitive rite of initiation, which the Latin West took apart

On this reading the early church celebrated baptism, the seal and the eucharist as one rite, and the West's separate, later confirmation is that rite come apart.

Held by: Gregory Dix (Anglican, The Theology of Confirmation in Relation to Baptism, 1946) ⚠ unverified; J. D. C. Fisher (Anglican, Christian Initiation: Baptism in the Medieval West, 1965) ⚠ unverified; Aidan Kavanagh (Catholic, Confirmation: Origins and Reform, 1988) ⚠ unverified; Thomas Hopko (Orthodox, The Orthodox Faith, vol. II, Orthodox Church in America, © 1981).11

The early descriptions place the seal inside one continuous initiation: Tertullian narrates font, unction and hand-laying as one sequence; the Apostolic Tradition runs the water into the presbyter’s anointing, the bishop’s hand-laying and forehead-seal, and the eucharist; Cyril of Jerusalem and Pseudo-Dionysius join the chrism directly to baptism and then to the eucharist. Dix, as Hughes quotes him, held baptism “a most mighty operation of God’s grace” and yet “radically incomplete without its positive complement of confirmation and its fulfilment in the communion”: confirmation “is a necessary part of initiation, since it is the sacramental bestowal of the positive principle of Christian life, the gift of the Holy Ghost.”12 Mitchell Kalpakgian reports that Fisher’s 1965 study calls the Western change a “disintegration” “because the three sacraments possess a logical, intrinsic unity.” In Fisher’s words, as Kalpakgian quotes them, in the bishop’s absence “the presbyter had to baptize, anoint, and communicate his candidates in the expectation that the missing hand-laying and chrismation would be supplied on the occasion of the next episcopal visitation”; the separation was “owing to difficulties of a practical nature,” so that “baptism, confirmation, and first communion are now three events separated from one another in time.” Fisher holds up the Eastern churches’ practice as a model of the rite “in its primitive wholeness.”13

Hopko’s catechism for the Orthodox Church in America states that chrismation “is always done in the Orthodox Church together with baptism,” and that the Orthodox Church “differs from the Roman Catholic and Protestant churches where the two sacraments are often separated and given other interpretations than those found in traditional Orthodoxy.” Baptism and chrismation “are fulfilled in the immediate reception by the ‘newly-enlightened’ of Holy Communion”; the catechism adds: “This is the case with infants as well as adults.”14 Alexander Schmemann, describing the Orthodox rite, writes that chrismation “is not so much another sacrament as the very fulfillment of baptism, its ‘confirmation’ by the Holy Spirit.”15 Belcher and Chase report that the “liturgical-initiation” school, in which they group Kavanagh, influenced the “restored order,” in which children baptized as infants are confirmed between baptism and first communion. Kavanagh, in Carl Volz’s report, asks that the original sequence baptism–confirmation–eucharist be restored and that the church stop offering first communion before confirmation: “The liturgical norm is clear: the sacraments of initiation are regularly and ordinarily celebrated together beginning with baptism and culminating in eucharist.”16

One ideal pattern, kept in two legitimate usages

On this reading East and West share one ideal order of initiation, and each has kept it in a usage suited to pastoral needs, the West delaying confirmation to keep the baptized in contact with the bishop.

Held by: the Joint International Commission for Theological Dialogue between the Catholic Church and the Orthodox Church (Catholic and Orthodox, joint commission, Bari document, 1987); the Catechism of the Catholic Church (Catholic, 1992).17

The Bari document describes an “early pattern”: instruction, baptism, “confirmation or chrismation in the West by the bishop, or in the East by the priest when the bishop was absent,” and the eucharist, “administered in the course of a single, complex liturgical celebration.” “This pattern remains the ideal for both churches.” Later “local changes took place in liturgical practice in consideration of the pastoral needs of the faithful. These changes did not concern the theological understanding of the fundamental unity, in the Holy Spirit, of the whole process of Christian initiation.” “In the East, the temporal unity of the liturgical celebration of the three sacraments was retained… In the West, it was often preferred to delay confirmation so as to retain contact of the baptized person with the bishop.” The commission recalls that the Council of Constantinople of 879–880 “determined that each See would retain the ancient usages of its tradition.” On first communion before confirmation it notes that “the disciplinary directives which called for the traditional order of the sacraments of Christian initiation have never been abrogated,” and that the inversion “calls for deep theological and pastoral reflection.”18

The Catechism gives the same account of the separation: in the first centuries confirmation “generally comprised one single celebration with Baptism,” and as infant baptisms multiplied and dioceses grew, “the desire to reserve the completion of Baptism to the bishop caused the temporal separation of the two sacraments.” It names a Roman custom that “facilitated the development of the Western practice”: a first anointing by the priest and “a second anointing on the forehead of the newly baptized by the bishop.” Each usage carries its own emphasis: “The practice of the Eastern Churches gives greater emphasis to the unity of Christian initiation. That of the Latin Church more clearly expresses the communion of the new Christian with the bishop as guarantor and servant of the unity, catholicity and apostolicity of his Church, and hence the connection with the apostolic origins of Christ’s Church.”19

No single primitive order; the Western rite drew together distinct local practices

On this reading early churches initiated in different ways, and Western confirmation grew from several local practices rather than from the break-up of one rite.

Held by: Maxwell E. Johnson (Lutheran, The Rites of Christian Initiation, 2nd ed., 2007) ⚠ unverified; Paul Bradshaw (Anglican, Early Christian Worship, 2nd ed., 2010); Kimberly Hope Belcher and Nathan P. Chase (Catholic, Theological Studies, 2025).20

Bradshaw reads the early evidence as plural: “while North African and Roman sources certainly do seem to have known a post-baptismal anointing and imposition of hands, the early Syrian practice apparently did not include any post-baptismal ceremonies at all, although it was familiar with a pre-baptismal anointing. This later diversity suggests that the whole idea of the existence of a uniform baptismal ritual in primitive Christianity is misconceived.”21

Belcher and Chase trace Western confirmation to two sources. In Spain and Gaul the bishop’s visit to rural parishes came to be called “confirmation,” and they adopt Gabriele Winkler’s reading of the Council of Orange (441) that in Gaul “only one postbaptismal anointing was customary.” They quote Johnson: in confirmatione “refers, primarily, to a visit of the bishop to the parishes of his diocese, who on those occasions ‘confirmed’ or ratified what had already been done by the presbyter or deacon.” At Rome a second, episcopal hand-laying and anointing followed the presbyter’s. When the Roman rites were adopted elsewhere, Johnson writes, “it is not so much the case that ‘confirmation’ gets separated from baptism outside of Rome but that the Roman postbaptismal rites, which came to be interpreted as confirmation, became an addition to the rites of Christian initiation elsewhere.” Belcher and Chase conclude that “the order baptism–confirmation–Eucharist was not a universal practice later replaced with baptism–Eucharist–confirmation”; in their “ecclesial-anamnetic” model, confirmation developed in the West “as a way of rendering visible the connection between an initiand, the local community into which that initiand is baptized, and the broader ecclesial communion.”22
