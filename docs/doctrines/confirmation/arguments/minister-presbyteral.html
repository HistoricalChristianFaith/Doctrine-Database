May a presbyter be the proper minister of confirmation? (the presbyteral reading)

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — that a presbyter's chrismation is a valid seal is broadly granted (the East has never separated it from baptism, and Rome accepts it); that the presbyter is its proper minister is disputed against the Western reservation to the bishop · advanced by the Christian East (with Cyril of Jerusalem and Pseudo-Dionysius as its early witnesses).

Related arguments: the minister — see also Must confirmation be reserved to the bishop? (the bishop-reserved reading)

The claim

In the Christian East the seal is not a separate bishop's rite but part of one continuous initiation: the presbyter who baptizes immediately chrismates the newly baptized with myron (chrism) consecrated by the bishop or patriarch, and this presbyteral chrismation is held to confer the Holy Spirit truly and validly. The interpretation assessed: a presbyter may be the proper minister of confirmation/chrismation, the episcopal role being preserved in the consecration of the chrism rather than in the anointing itself.

Who advances it

The whole Eastern tradition, and behind it the oldest shape of the rite. Cyril of Jerusalem's catecheses describe a chrism-sealing joined directly to the font; Pseudo-Dionysius has the hierarch seal with the myron as one act with baptism. The East continued this unbroken, so that infants are chrismated immediately after baptism — the practice the Catholic Encyclopedia notes survives “in the Greek Church and in Spain… as in earlier times.”1

The evidence for

Three things support it. (1) Antiquity of the fused rite. The earliest full descriptions of the seal — Cyril, Pseudo-Dionysius — place it with baptism, administered by the same minister, well before the Western separation; the East's practice is the continuation of the older, undivided shape, not an innovation.2 (2) Episcopal authority preserved in the chrism. The presbyter anoints, but with myron the bishop consecrates — so the bishop's role is not abolished but relocated to the making of the chrism, exactly the arrangement Innocent I already presupposes when he lets presbyters anoint “with chrism which has been consecrated by a bishop.” (3) Conceded validity. Even the tradition that reserves the rite to the bishop treats the Eastern presbyteral seal as a true sacrament, and Trent's own “ordinary minister” wording leaves room for a presbyteral one — a striking concession from the reserving side.

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The validity of presbyteral chrismation is about as well grounded as a contested point gets: it is the continuous practice of half of historic Christendom, rests on the oldest attested form of the rite, and is granted even by the church that reserves confirmation to bishops. On validity, the presbyteral reading is strong.

What is contested. The step from “valid” to “the presbyter is the proper minister” is where the dispute bites. The West answers from Acts 8 that it was the apostles, not Philip the evangelist, who conferred the Spirit — a pattern favouring the bishop as the rite's own minister — and Innocent I and Trent make the episcopal seal the norm, the presbyteral case an exception requiring the bishop's chrism (and, in the West, delegation). So the Eastern reading wins the question of validity and the West the question of the norm; the tie between two ancient, self-consistent traditions has no neutral arbiter.

Assessment

Contested. That a presbyter's chrismation is a valid seal of the Spirit is strongly grounded — the older, unbroken Eastern shape, conceded even by the reserving West. But whether the presbyter is the proper minister, or only an extraordinary one acting with the bishop's chrism, is genuinely disputed between East and West, each reading coherent on its own terms and neither refuting the other. Strong on validity, unresolved on ordinariness — a real dispute with no clear winner. The bishop-reserved reading is weighed on its own page.
