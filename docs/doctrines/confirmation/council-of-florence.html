The Council of Florence on confirmation (matter, form, minister)

Dates: 1439 · Wikipedia
Summary: The decree of union with the Armenians (Exsultate Deo) gives confirmation its first full ecumenical definition — its matter (chrism of oil and balsam, blessed by a bishop), its form (“I sign thee… and confirm thee with the chrism of salvation”), its effect (the Holy Spirit given for strength, as at Pentecost), and its ordinary minister (the bishop).

Claims & evidence

Matter and form

The second sacrament is confirmation; its matter is the chrism prepared from the oil, which signifies the excellence of conscience, and from the balsam, which signifies the fragrance of a good reputation, and is blessed by a bishop. The form is: I sign thee with the sign of the cross and I confirm thee with the chrism of salvation, in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Ghost.

— Council of Florence, Bull of union with the Armenians Exsultate Deo (22 Nov. 1439) = Denzinger no. 697 (trans. R. J. Deferrari); the full decree is at EWTN.

Effect: the Holy Spirit given for strength, as at Pentecost

The effect of this sacrament, because in it the Holy Spirit is given for strength, was thus given to the Apostles on the day of Pentecost, so that the Christian might boldly confess the name of Christ.

— Exsultate Deo (Denzinger 697), source. The forehead is anointed as the seat of reverence, “so that he may not be ashamed to confess the name of Christ… which is indeed a ‘stumbling block to the Jews and unto the Gentiles foolishness.’”

Ordinary minister: the bishop

The decree names the bishop as the ordinary minister — grounding the reservation in Acts 8, where the apostles, whose place the bishops hold, conferred the Holy Spirit through the imposition of hands — while noting the historical dispensation whereby a simple priest, by authority of the Apostolic See, has at times administered confirmation with chrism prepared by a bishop.1

Notes

  • Lombard's list, made ecumenical. Florence lifts confirmation from Peter Lombard's sevenfold enumeration into conciliar teaching — the same decree that fixes the seven sacraments (see the seven-sacraments timeline). The bishop-as-ordinary-minister clause, with its priest-dispensation caveat, is weighed on the minister argument.
