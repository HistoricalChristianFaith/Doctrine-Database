The Council of Trent on confirmation (a true and proper sacrament)

Dates: 1547 · Wikipedia
Summary: Against the Reformers — who read the apostolic hand-laying as an extraordinary, unrepeatable event and reduced confirmation to a catechetical ceremony — Trent's seventh session defined confirmation as “a true and proper sacrament,” upheld the virtue of the chrism, and anathematized the denial that the bishop is its ordinary minister.

Claims & evidence

Confirmation is a true and proper sacrament, not an idle ceremony

If any one saith, that the confirmation of those who have been baptized is an idle ceremony, and not rather a true and proper sacrament; or that of old it was nothing more than a kind of catechism, whereby they who were near adolescence gave an account of their faith in the face of the Church; let him be anathema.

— Council of Trent, Session VII (3 March 1547), Canons on the Sacrament of Confirmation, Canon I, trans. J. Waterworth, source.

The virtue of the chrism, and the bishop as ordinary minister

If any one saith, that they who ascribe any virtue to the sacred chrism of confirmation, offer an outrage to the Holy Ghost; let him be anathema.

If any one saith, that the ordinary minister of holy confirmation is not the bishop alone, but any simple priest soever; let him be anathema.

— Council of Trent, Session VII, Canons II–III on Confirmation, source.

Notes

  • The dogmatic close of the Western rite. Trent turns the definitions of Florence into defended dogma, and its Canon III — “the bishop alone” — makes the Western reservation a test of orthodoxy, in tension with the continuous Eastern practice of presbyteral chrismation (weighed on the presbyteral-minister argument).
  • Trent across the wiki. Its sessions also define penance, the seven sacraments in general, and much else.
