The Apostolic Tradition on the post-baptismal anointing and seal

Dates: c. 215 · Wikipedia
Summary: The church order traditionally ascribed to Hippolytus of Rome and dated c. 215 (treated as composite, second- to fourth-century material by Bradshaw, Johnson and Phillips) gives a detailed liturgical description of Christian initiation: after the water-bath the newly baptized is anointed by the presbyter, then comes to the bishop, who lays his hand on them with prayer, pours consecrated oil on the head, and signs the forehead, all within one ceremony with baptism. It sets out a two-stage anointing, first by the presbyter and then by the bishop.

Claims & evidence

Coming up from the font, the anointing by the presbyter

And afterward, when he has come up [out of the water], he is anointed by the presbyter with the oil of thanksgiving, the presbyter saying: I anoint thee with holy oil in the name of Jesus Christ.

— The Apostolic Tradition of Hippolytus, ch. XXI (end), trans. Burton Scott Easton (1934), source.

The bishop lays on the hand, with prayer (versions differ on whether it asks for the Spirit)

O Lord God, who hast made them worthy to obtain remission of sins through the laver of regeneration of [the] Holy Spirit, send into them thy grace, that they may serve thee according to thy will; for thine is the glory, to the Father and the Son, with [the] Holy Spirit in the holy church, both now and world without end. Amen.

— Apostolic Tradition, ch. XXII (the bishop’s laying on of the hand), source. Translations of this prayer differ. Easton’s, above, contains no petition for the Spirit; the translation by Alistair Stewart-Sykes (On the Apostolic Tradition, St Vladimir’s Seminary Press, 2001, para. 21), as quoted by Tyler Robbins, reads: “make them worthy to be filled with the Holy Spirit, send your grace upon them that they may serve you in accordance with your will” ⚠ unverified (edition not opened here; SharperIron).

The bishop pours oil on the head and signs the forehead

Then, pouring the oil of thanksgiving from his hand and putting it on his forehead, he shall say: I anoint thee with holy oil in the Lord, the Father Almighty and Christ Jesus and [the] Holy Ghost. And signing them on the forehead he shall say: The Lord be with thee; and he who is signed shall say: And with thy spirit.

— Apostolic Tradition, ch. XXII, source. The rite then continues into the eucharist.

Notes

  • Two anointings, presbyter then bishop. The order distinguishes a first anointing done by the presbyter as the neophyte leaves the water and a second, the bishop’s pouring of oil on the head with the sealing of the forehead. In 416 Innocent I permitted presbyters to anoint with chrism but reserved the forehead-signing “when they confer the Spirit Paraclete” to bishops.
  • One ceremony with the font. As in Tertullian, the hand-laying and anointing are narrated as one ceremony with the font. On later counts of the sacraments, see the seven-sacraments timeline; on how this order relates to the later Western rite, see Did early churches share one order of initiation, and how does the Latin West's separate confirmation relate to it?
  • Authorship and date — a caveat. The work is traditionally credited to Hippolytus of Rome (c. 170–235) and dated c. 215, and it is cited here by that horizon. But the Greek original is lost, surviving in a Latin palimpsest and Coptic, Arabic, and Ethiopic versions. Paul F. Bradshaw, Maxwell E. Johnson and L. Edward Phillips (The Apostolic Tradition: A Commentary, Hermeneia; Fortress, 2002, archive.org) treat it as composite, layered liturgical material from the second through fourth centuries rather than a single Roman document of c. 215; the traditional ascription and date are used here for placement on the timeline.
