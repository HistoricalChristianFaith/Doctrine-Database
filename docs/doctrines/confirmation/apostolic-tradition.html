The Apostolic Tradition on the post-baptismal anointing and seal

Dates: c. 215 · Wikipedia
Summary: The early-third-century Roman church order traditionally ascribed to Hippolytus gives the earliest detailed liturgical description of Christian initiation: after the water-bath the newly baptized is anointed by the presbyter, then comes to the bishop, who lays his hand on them with prayer, pours consecrated oil on the head, and signs the forehead. It is the rite the West would later separate out and call confirmation, here still one continuous ceremony with baptism — and it lays out, verbatim, the two-stage anointing (presbyter, then bishop) that Innocent I two centuries on would formalize into a bishop’s reserved forehead-seal.

Claims & evidence

Coming up from the font, the anointing by the presbyter

And afterward, when he has come up [out of the water], he is anointed by the presbyter with the oil of thanksgiving, the presbyter saying: I anoint thee with holy oil in the name of Jesus Christ.

— The Apostolic Tradition of Hippolytus, ch. XXI (end), trans. Burton Scott Easton (1934), source.

The bishop lays on the hand, with prayer for the Spirit’s grace

O Lord God, who hast made them worthy to obtain remission of sins through the laver of regeneration of [the] Holy Spirit, send into them thy grace, that they may serve thee according to thy will; for thine is the glory, to the Father and the Son, with [the] Holy Spirit in the holy church, both now and world without end. Amen.

— Apostolic Tradition, ch. XXII (the bishop’s laying on of the hand), source. The prayer ties the gift to the font just completed — the Spirit already given in regeneration is now invoked as grace to serve, the second gesture completing the first.

The bishop pours oil on the head and signs the forehead

Then, pouring the oil of thanksgiving from his hand and putting it on his forehead, he shall say: I anoint thee with holy oil in the Lord, the Father Almighty and Christ Jesus and [the] Holy Ghost. And signing them on the forehead he shall say: The Lord be with thee; and he who is signed shall say: And with thy spirit.

— Apostolic Tradition, ch. XXII, source. The rite closes into the eucharist that follows — water, twofold anointing, seal, and communion held together as one initiation.

Notes

  • Two anointings, presbyter then bishop. The order distinguishes a first anointing done by the presbyter as the neophyte leaves the water and a second, the bishop’s pouring of oil on the head with the sealing of the forehead. That very structure — a presbyteral chrismation plus a bishop’s forehead-seal — is what Innocent I (416) would later regularize, permitting presbyters to anoint but reserving the forehead-signing “when they confer the Spirit Paraclete” to bishops alone.
  • One initiation, not yet a separate sacrament. As with Tertullian a few years earlier, the hand-laying and anointing are narrated as one seamless ceremony with the font, not a distinct later rite; the numbering of confirmation among the seven sacraments is centuries away (see the seven-sacraments timeline).
  • Authorship and date — a caveat. The work is traditionally credited to Hippolytus of Rome (c. 170–235) and dated c. 215, and it is cited here by that horizon. But the Greek original is lost, surviving in a Latin palimpsest and Coptic, Arabic, and Ethiopic versions, and much recent scholarship (notably Bradshaw, Johnson, and Phillips) treats it as composite — layered liturgical material from the second through fourth centuries, not straightforwardly a single Roman document of c. 215. The rite it describes is early third-century in character; the precise author and date are genuinely disputed.*

* On the composite-text thesis, see Paul F. Bradshaw, Maxwell E. Johnson, and L. Edward Phillips, The Apostolic Tradition: A Commentary (Hermeneia; Fortress, 2002), archive.org.
