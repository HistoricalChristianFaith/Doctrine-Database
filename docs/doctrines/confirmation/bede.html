The Venerable Bede on confirmation (the forehead-seal reserved to bishops)

Dates: c. 710 · Wikipedia
Summary: Three centuries after Innocent I, the Northumbrian monk Bede (c. 672/673–735), commenting on the apostles laying hands on the baptized Samaritans (Acts 8:17) in his Commentary on Acts (Expositio Actuum Apostolorum, c. 709–716), restates the Western reservation almost word for word: presbyters may baptize and anoint with bishop-consecrated chrism, but the signing of the forehead — the giving of “the Paraclete Spirit” — is reserved to bishops alone. He carries the patristic discipline of Innocent I into the early-medieval Latin church.

Claims & evidence

Philip could not confer the Spirit because he was a deacon, not an apostle — the hand-laying is reserved to bishops

It should be noted, however, that Philip, who evangelized Samaria, was one of the seven. For if he had been an Apostle, he would certainly have been able to lay hands on them so that they might receive the Holy Spirit. For this is reserved to bishops alone. For presbyters, whether outside the bishop’s presence or in his presence, are allowed to baptize and anoint the baptized with chrism, but only with chrism consecrated by the bishop; however, they are not to anoint the forehead with the same oil, which is reserved to bishops alone when they impart the Paraclete Spirit to the baptized.

— Bede, Commentary on Acts (Expositio Actuum Apostolorum), on Acts 8:17, source. Bede reads the two-step of Acts 8 exactly as the West did: the deacon-evangelist Philip could baptize the Samaritans but not confer the Spirit; that awaited the apostles Peter and John — and, in the church's continuing order, the bishops who succeed them.

Notes

  • Innocent I restated verbatim. Bede's rule is Innocent I's letter to Decentius of Gubbio (416) almost word for word: presbyters anoint with bishop-consecrated chrism, but only the bishop signs the forehead “when they confer the Spirit Paraclete” / “when they impart the Paraclete Spirit.” The dependence shows the reservation had hardened into settled Latin discipline by the eighth century and was transmitted through the standard exegesis of Acts.
  • A further Western witness to the bishop-reserved reading. Bede feeds the bishop-reserved argument as a Latin voice reading the Acts 8 pattern (apostles, not the deacon Philip, conferred the Spirit) as reserving the seal to the apostles' successors. On that argument's adversarial assessment the discipline is ancient and coherent (contested) but does not carry the stronger claim that the bishop is the necessary minister — against which stands the unbroken Eastern presbyteral practice and Jerome's “honour, not compulsory law.”
  • Also a witness elsewhere on this wiki. Bede appears on the perpetual virginity timeline as a Latin proponent of the vow-of-virginity reading of Luke 1:34.
