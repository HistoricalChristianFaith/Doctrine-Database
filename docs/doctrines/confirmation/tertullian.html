Tertullian on confirmation (the unction and the imposition of hands)

Dates: c. 200 · Wikipedia
Summary: The earliest Latin description of what follows the baptismal water: an anointing with blessed oil (the “chrism” from which Aaron — and Christ — are called “anointed”), and then the laying on of the hand invoking the Holy Spirit, who thereupon descends. Tertullian names the two rites the later West would separate out as confirmation, though he treats them as one continuous initiation with the font.

Claims & evidence

After the font, the anointing with a blessed unction (the chrism)

After this, when we have issued from the font, we are thoroughly anointed with a blessed unction,—(a practice derived) from the old discipline, wherein on entering the priesthood, men were wont to be anointed with oil from a horn, ever since Aaron was anointed by Moses.

— Tertullian, On Baptism, ch. VII (“Of the Unction”), trans. S. Thelwall (Ante-Nicene Fathers, vol. III), source. Tertullian draws the etymology out explicitly, tying the rite to the name of Christ:

Whence Aaron is called “Christ,” from the “chrism,” which is “the unction;” which, when made spiritual, furnished an appropriate name to the Lord, because He was “anointed” with the Spirit by God the Father… Thus, too, in our case, the unction runs carnally, (i.e. on the body,) but profits spiritually; in the same way as the act of baptism itself too is carnal, in that we are plunged in water, but the effect spiritual, in that we are freed from sins.

— On Baptism, ch. VII, source.

Then the imposition of the hand, inviting the Holy Spirit, who descends

In the next place the hand is laid on us, invoking and inviting the Holy Spirit through benediction.

— Tertullian, On Baptism, ch. VIII (“Of the Imposition of Hands”), source. The Spirit answers the invocation:

Then, over our cleansed and blessed bodies willingly descends from the Father that Holiest Spirit. Over the waters of baptism, recognising as it were His primeval seat, He reposes: (He who) glided down on the Lord “in the shape of a dove”…

— On Baptism, ch. VIII, source.

Notes

  • Two rites, one initiation. Tertullian already distinguishes the water, the unction, and the hand-laying — the components the medieval West would gather into a separate rite of confirmation — but he narrates them as one seamless sequence, and does not reserve the hand-laying to a bishop or number confirmation as a distinct sacrament. That closure is centuries away (see the seven-sacraments timeline).
  • The chrism / “Christ” etymology he draws on (unction → “anointed” → Christ) becomes the standard patristic reading of the seal, developed at length by Cyril of Jerusalem a century and a half later (“ye are properly called Christs”).
  • Tertullian across the wiki. He is also a witness on the sacraments in general (sacramentum as the soldier's oath), on penance, and elsewhere.
