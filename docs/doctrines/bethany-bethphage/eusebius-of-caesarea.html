Eusebius of Caesarea on Bethany and Bethphage

Dates: c. 260 – 339 (the Onomasticon c. 290–325) · Wikipedia
Summary: The bishop of Caesarea's Onomasticon — a gazetteer of biblical place-names — carries the earliest surviving topographical notices of both Holy-Week villages in consecutive entries, and reports that the tomb of Lazarus at Bethany was already being “pointed out” in his own day.

Claim & evidence

Consecutive gazetteer entries for both villages (c. 325)

Bēthphagē (Bethfage). (Little) village on the Mt. of Olives where the Lord Jesus came.

Bēthania. Village two miles from Jerusalem on the slope of the Mt. of Olives where the Christ (Savior) raised Lazarus. The place (tomb) of Lazarus is pointed out even now [where a church has been constructed].

— Eusebius, Onomasticon, entries no. 288 (Bethphage) and no. 289 (Bethany), in the first-hand English translation of C. Umhau Wolf (1971) (Klostermann's critical Greek text, GCS 11.1). The bracketed clause — that a church “has been constructed” on the spot — is not Eusebius's Greek but the supplement of Jerome's Latin recension of the work (the Liber locorum, c. 390), so the single entry preserves two stages: a shown tomb under Eusebius, a church over it by Jerome's day.

What it attests — and what it does not

The Onomasticon is the hinge between the Gospel geography and the built sites. Three things stand out. First, both villages are located exactly where the Gospels put them — on the Mount of Olives, about two miles east of Jerusalem — independent early confirmation of the topography. Second, and unlike his Gethsemane entry (a place of prayer, no monument yet), Eusebius already reports a specific tomb at Bethany “pointed out even now”: the grave was a fixed, shown station a generation before any church is attested on it, corroborated the same decade by the Bordeaux Pilgrim's “crypt where Lazarus was laid.” Third, what the entry does not give is a verified tomb or a building — it fixes the general ground and the existence of a venerated grave, not proof that this rock-cut chamber is Lazarus's. That is the layer the later church, convent, mosque, and basilica all build upon.

Notes

  • Eusebius also appears on this wiki as the fullest ancient witness to the recovery of the tomb of Jesus under Constantine and, in the same Onomasticon, to the location of Gethsemane — the eastern Passion sites all run through his gazetteer.
  • The two-stage structure of the Bethany entry — “pointed out even now” in the Greek, a church “constructed” in Jerome's Latin — frames the moment the grave went from shown tomb to sanctuary, between Eusebius (early 4th c.) and Jerome (c. 390); Egeria's fully-liturgical Lazarium c. 384 catches it built.
