Egeria on the Lazarus-Saturday liturgy at the Lazarium

Dates: fl. c. 381–384 (the Itinerarium Egeriae) · Wikipedia
Summary: The Western nun and pilgrim whose Holy-Land travel diary is the fullest firsthand account of fourth-century Jerusalem liturgy. Her description of the Saturday that opens Holy Week is the first firm evidence of an actual church — the Lazarium — on the site of the raising of Lazarus, and of a developed stational procession from the city out to Bethany.

Claim & evidence

The station at Bethany, the Saturday before Palm Sunday (c. 384)

… the archdeacon raises his voice to say, “At one o'clock today let us all be ready at the Lazarium.” … About half a mile before you get to the Lazarium from Jerusalem there is a church by the road. It is the spot where Lazarus' sister Mary met the Lord. … By the time they arrive there so many people have collected that they fill not only the Lazarium itself, but all the fields around. … He mounts a platform, and reads the Gospel passage which begins “When Jesus came to Bethany six days before the Passover.”

— Egeria, Itinerarium §29 (the “Saturday before Palm Sunday” station at Bethany), trans. John Wilkinson, Egeria's Travels (1981), as reproduced with the Egeria excerpt at the Hebrew University Corpus of Christian sites. The Gospel read at the wayside church is the meeting of Mary and Jesus (John 11); the Gospel read at the Lazarium is the arrival “six days before the passover” (John 12:1), which also gives notice of the approaching Easter.

What it attests — and what it does not

Egeria's Bethany paragraph carries three firsts for this timeline. It is the earliest firm attestation of a church at the site — the Lazarium, the building Eusebius's tomb had acquired by the time Jerome's recension mentions it, and the fabric whose fourth-century mosaic floor still shows in the courtyard of the modern basilica. It shows a fully-developed stational liturgy that reads each Gospel scene on its own ground — the meeting of Mary at one church, the raising and the anointing at the Lazarium — proof of an established, crowded cult by the 380s. And it distinguishes a wayside church from the tomb-church, an early instance of the two-station pattern that recurs across the Holy-Land sites. What it does not give is the tomb's authentication: Egeria attests a flourishing fourth-century devotion, which is evidence of an old and continuous tradition, not first-century proof that this is the grave of Lazarus.

Notes

  • Egeria's separate Palm Sunday procession (Itinerarium §31) still begins at the Imbomon on the summit of the Mount of Olives, not yet at Bethphage; the extension of the palm-procession down to the Bethphage church is a later, ninth-century growth. So her diary catches the eastern Holy-Week road half-formed: Bethany already a built station, Bethphage not yet one.
  • Egeria also appears on this wiki as a witness on the birthplace of Christ timeline and, for the Maundy-Thursday procession, on the Garden of Gethsemane timeline — her diary is the single richest source for how fourth-century Jerusalem walked the Passion sites.
  • Her account survives only in part (the sole manuscript, an 11th-century codex, is defective at both ends and loses a leaf mid-text); the Bethany station falls within the surviving middle portion and is intact.
