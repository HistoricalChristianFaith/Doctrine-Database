Egeria on the Lazarus-Saturday liturgy at the Lazarium

Dates: fl. c. 381–384 (the Itinerarium Egeriae) · Wikipedia
Summary: The Western pilgrim whose Holy-Land travel diary is the fullest extant firsthand account of fourth-century Jerusalem liturgy. Her description of the Saturday that opens Holy Week is the earliest extant description on this timeline of a church — the Lazarium — on the site of the raising of Lazarus, and of a stational procession from the city out to Bethany.

Claim & evidence

The station at Bethany, the Saturday before Palm Sunday (c. 384)

… the archdeacon raises his voice to say, “At one o'clock today let us all be ready at the Lazarium.” … About half a mile before you get to the Lazarium from Jerusalem there is a church by the road. It is the spot where Lazarus' sister Mary met the Lord. … By the time they arrive there so many people have collected that they fill not only the Lazarium itself, but all the fields around. … He mounts a platform, and reads the Gospel passage which begins “When Jesus came to Bethany six days before the Passover.”

— Egeria, Itinerarium §29 (the “Saturday before Palm Sunday” station at Bethany), trans. John Wilkinson, Egeria's Travels (1981), as reproduced with the Egeria excerpt at the Hebrew University Corpus of Christian sites. The Gospel read at the wayside church is the meeting of Mary and Jesus (John 11); the Gospel read at the Lazarium is the arrival “six days before the passover” (John 12:1), which also gives notice of the approaching Easter.

Placement on the timeline

Egeria's Bethany paragraph is the earliest extant attestation on this timeline of a church at the site — the Lazarium, the building over the tomb Eusebius reports as shown, which Jerome's recension also mentions, and whose fourth-century mosaic floor is preserved in the courtyard of the modern church. It describes a stational liturgy that reads each Gospel scene on its own ground — the meeting of Mary at one church, the raising and the anointing at the Lazarium — with crowds that fill “all the fields around.” And it distinguishes a wayside church from the tomb-church.

Notes

  • Egeria's separate Palm Sunday procession (Itinerarium §31) begins at the Imbomon on the summit of the Mount of Olives, not at Bethphage; the extension of the palm-procession down to the Bethphage church is dated by the Custody of the Holy Land to the ninth century. In her diary Bethany is a built station; Bethphage does not appear as one.
  • Egeria is also a witness on the birthplace of Christ timeline and, for the Maundy-Thursday procession, on the Garden of Gethsemane timeline — her diary describes the fourth-century Jerusalem processions to several of the Passion sites.
  • Her account survives only in part (the sole manuscript, an 11th-century codex, is defective at both ends and loses a leaf mid-text); the Bethany station falls within the surviving middle portion and is intact.
