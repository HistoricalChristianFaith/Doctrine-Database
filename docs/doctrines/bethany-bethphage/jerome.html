Jerome on Bethany, the Lazarium, and Bethphage

Dates: c. 347 – 420 (in Palestine from 386) · Wikipedia
Summary: The translator of Eusebius's gazetteer and, from 386, a resident of nearby Bethlehem, Jerome supplies two things for these sites: the note (in his Latin recension of the Onomasticon) that a church now stood over the tomb of Lazarus — the first author to call it the Lazarium — and, in his memorial letter for the noblewoman Paula, a firsthand pilgrimage through both villages.

Claim & evidence

The church over the tomb (Latin Onomasticon, c. 390)

Jerome's Latin recension of Eusebius's Onomasticon (the Liber locorum) carries the Bethany entry forward but adds to Eusebius's “the tomb of Lazarus is pointed out even now” the further clause that a church has now been built on the spot — the earliest notice of the Lazarium as a standing church, and the wording by which the two-stage growth of the site (shown tomb → sanctuary) passed into the Latin West (cf. “Tomb of Lazarus,” Wikipedia: “The first mention of a church dedicated to Saint Lazarus, called the Lazarium, is by Jerome in 390”).

Paula at the tomb of Lazarus and at Bethphage (Letter 108, c. 404)

After this Paula visited the tomb of Lazarus and beheld the hospitable roof of Mary and Martha, as well as Bethphage, “the town of the priestly jaws.” Here it was that a restive foal typical of the Gentiles received the bridle of God…

— Jerome, Letter 108 (to Eustochium, the pilgrimage of Paula), §12 (Fremantle trans.). Written to memorialize the Roman noblewoman Paula, who had settled with Jerome's circle in the Holy Land, the letter retraces her tour of the sacred sites; in a single sentence it fixes the whole eastern circuit — the tomb of Lazarus, the remembered house of Mary and Martha, and Bethphage as the colt's sending-place.

What it attests — and what it does not

Jerome is the witness who names the fabric. Where Eusebius and the Bordeaux Pilgrim record a shown tomb (c. 325–333), Jerome records a shown church over it and a house of the sisters shown as well, and his contemporary Egeria confirms the church in full liturgical use. His two notices bracket the same development from the two sides — the gazetteer glossed with “a church has been built,” the letter with an eyewitness who walked in and prayed. What Jerome does not supply is authentication of the relics themselves: like Eusebius he reports what the fourth-century church shows — a tomb, a roof, a village — not independent proof that this grave is Lazarus's or that a particular stone at Bethphage bore the Lord. His etymology of Bethphage (“the town of the priestly jaws”) is a philological gloss, not a topographical claim.

Notes

  • Jerome also appears on this wiki supplying the disputed etymology of Gethsemane and as a witness on the recovery of the tomb of Jesus — his translation of the Onomasticon and his residence at Bethlehem make him the pen through which the eastern Passion sites reached the Latin West.
  • Paula (Letter 108) is Jerome's firsthand pilgrim for this circuit; her tour dates c. 404 and is the Latin counterpart to Egeria's Greek-liturgical account of the same ground a generation earlier.
