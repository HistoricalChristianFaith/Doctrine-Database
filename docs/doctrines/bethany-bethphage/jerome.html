Jerome on Bethany, the Lazarium, and Bethphage

Dates: c. 347 – 420 (in Palestine from 386) · Wikipedia
Summary: The translator of Eusebius's gazetteer and, from 386, a resident of nearby Bethlehem, Jerome supplies two things for these sites: the note (in his Latin recension of the Onomasticon) that a church now stood over the tomb of Lazarus, calling it the Lazarium, and, in his memorial letter for the noblewoman Paula, a firsthand pilgrimage through both villages.

Claim & evidence

The church over the tomb (Latin Onomasticon, c. 390)

Jerome's Latin recension of Eusebius's Onomasticon (the Liber locorum) carries the Bethany entry forward but adds to Eusebius's “the tomb of Lazarus is pointed out even now” the further clause “where a church has been constructed,” a clause absent from Eusebius's Greek. The “Tomb of Lazarus,” Wikipedia article credits Jerome with the first mention: “The first mention of a church dedicated to Saint Lazarus, called the Lazarium, is by Jerome in 390”; Egeria's diary (c. 381–384) already describes the church in use under that name.

Paula at the tomb of Lazarus and at Bethphage (Letter 108, c. 404)

After this Paula visited the tomb of Lazarus and beheld the hospitable roof of Mary and Martha, as well as Bethphage, “the town of the priestly jaws.” Here it was that a restive foal typical of the Gentiles received the bridle of God…

— Jerome, Letter 108 (to Eustochium, the pilgrimage of Paula), §12 (Fremantle trans.). Written to memorialize the Roman noblewoman Paula, who had settled with Jerome's circle in the Holy Land, the letter retraces her tour of the sacred sites; in a single sentence it names the tomb of Lazarus, the remembered house of Mary and Martha, and Bethphage as the colt's sending-place.

Placement on the timeline

Where Eusebius and the Bordeaux Pilgrim record a shown tomb (c. 325–333), Jerome records a church over it and a house of the sisters shown as well; his contemporary Egeria describes the church in liturgical use. His two notices come from two kinds of writing — the gazetteer glossed with “where a church has been constructed,” and the letter's account of a pilgrim who visited the sites. Like Eusebius he reports what was shown in the fourth century — a tomb, a roof, a village. His etymology of Bethphage (“the town of the priestly jaws”) is a gloss on the name.

Notes

  • Jerome also supplies the disputed etymology of Gethsemane and is a witness on the recovery of the tomb of Jesus — his Latin translation of the Onomasticon carried its notices of the eastern Passion sites into Latin, and he lived at Bethlehem from 386.
  • Paula (Letter 108) is Jerome's firsthand pilgrim for this circuit; the letter is dated 404, and with Egeria's diary (c. 381–384) it is one of two late-fourth/early-fifth-century Latin accounts of the same ground.
