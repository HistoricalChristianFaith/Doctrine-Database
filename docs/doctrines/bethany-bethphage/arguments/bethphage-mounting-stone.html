Is the Bethphage stone the block from which Jesus mounted the colt?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: weak — the painted cube in the Bethphage church is a genuine and vivid twelfth-century relic of medieval Holy-Week devotion, but the claim that it is the very stone Jesus stepped on to mount the colt is a Crusader identification with no ancient attestation and no basis in the Gospel text.

The claim

Displayed in the Franciscan church at Bethphage, behind an iron grille, is a dressed cube of rock painted on its faces with the raising of Lazarus and the triumphal entry. Tradition holds it to be the mounting-block — the stone from which Jesus climbed onto the colt for his entry into Jerusalem on Palm Sunday.

The evidence for

The stone is real and its imagery is genuinely tied to this place. It is a block of native rock the Crusaders cut square and covered in plaster and paint: on the Bethany-facing side, Martha, Mary, and the risen Lazarus; on another, the colt and its foal and a crowd bearing palms, with the Latin name Bethfage inscribed. Buried when the site was lost after the Crusader period, it was rediscovered in the nineteenth century during Franciscan building and has stood in the church since, its paintings restored in 1950.1 As a fixed marker of the right village — Bethphage, the Synoptic sending-place for the colt — and as a twelfth-century witness to how the Crusader church staged the Palm Sunday story on its own ground, the stone is authentic and valuable.

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The stone genuinely belongs to Bethphage, genuinely dates to the twelfth century, and genuinely depicts the two Gospel scenes the site commemorates. As a medieval relic and a marker of the traditional village it is not in question.

What is overstated. That it is the actual mounting-block is a Crusader-era identification with no ancient support — no father, pilgrim, or gazetteer before the Crusaders knows of such a stone, and Eusebius's Bethphage entry marks only the village, not any monument in it. The Gospel scene itself supplies no mounting-stone: the disciples “set him thereon,” casting their garments on the colt (Luke 19:35), with no word of a block.2 And the very notion of a dressed mounting-block fits the Crusaders' own world — a knight climbing onto a tall war-charger — far better than a rider settling onto a small Palestinian donkey, as the archaeologist Jerome Murphy-O'Connor observed: the Franks “forgot that a Palestinian donkey was in no way comparable to their huge battle-chargers.”3

Assessment

Weak as a claim about a first-century object: an unattested Crusader identification, resting on a Gospel scene that mentions no stone and on a mounting-custom anachronistic to a donkey-rider. The defensible residue is real but modest — the stone is an authentic twelfth-century relic and a legitimate marker of the Bethphage tradition, valuable for the history of Holy-Week devotion, not as a relic of the entry itself. Nothing here turns on the supernatural; the shortfall is ordinary historical attestation.
