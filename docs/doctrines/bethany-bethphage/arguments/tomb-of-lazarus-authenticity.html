Is the tomb shown at al-Eizariya the tomb of Lazarus?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: plausible — the village of Bethany is soundly located and the shown tomb has an unbroken pedigree from the early fourth century, but that this rock-cut chamber is the actual grave of Lazarus is plausible, not proven. The tradition is early, continuous, and geographically right; it is not independently verified.

The claim

The grave shown to pilgrims at al-Eizariya — down a flight of worn steps to a small burial chamber — is the tomb from which Jesus raised Lazarus (John 11). It has been pointed out on that spot since at least the early fourth century and honoured by an unbroken succession of churches, a convent, a mosque, and a modern basilica ever since.

The evidence for

The village is securely placed. John fixes Bethany “about fifteen furlongs” (some two miles) from Jerusalem;1 Eusebius's Onomasticon gives the same two miles, “on the slope of the Mt. of Olives”;2 and the settlement at al-Eizariya — whose very name, from the Arabic al-‘Azar (Lazarus), preserves the identification — occupies that ground continuously. On the location of the village there is no serious dispute.

The tomb has the best pedigree of any Passion-adjacent grave. Two independent witnesses of the 320s–330s already report a shown tomb: Eusebius says “the place (tomb) of Lazarus is pointed out even now,” and the Bordeaux Pilgrim (333) records “the crypt where Lazarus was laid.”3 That is a full generation earlier than the earliest localized notices at Gethsemane, and the identification then runs unbroken — the fourth-century Lazarium, Melisende's twelfth-century Crusader church, the al-Uzair Mosque, and Barluzzi's 1954 basilica are all built on the one spot, with a fourth-century mosaic floor still in the courtyard.4 A rock-cut chamber-tomb of the right type in a first-century village exactly where the Gospel puts Bethany is a credible candidate on its face.

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The convergence of Gospel distance, Eusebius's gazetteer, and continuous settlement makes the village identification sound. And the tomb-tradition is genuinely old and unbroken — not a medieval invention, but a fix already in place when our earliest Holy-Land travellers wrote.

What is overstated. Early attestation fixes a shown tomb, not a verified one. The earliest witnesses are already ~300 years after the event; they report what the fourth-century church pointed to, and even a genuine memory of the right village need not carry a genuine memory of the right tomb within it (the same gap the Holy Sepulchre's continuity argument runs into). No first-century inscription, no excavation, and no independent line of evidence singles out this chamber over any other rock-cut tomb the village must once have held. The devotional layout has also drifted — the entrance was recut by the Franciscans in the sixteenth century and the approach rearranged — so the pilgrim's experience is not a pristine first-century survival.5

Assessment

Plausible. Kept at two levels: the location of Bethany at al-Eizariya is sound (Gospel, gazetteer, and continuous name and settlement agree), while the identification of this specific tomb as the grave of Lazarus is plausible — early, continuous, and topographically right, but a shown tradition rather than a proven one, short of probable for want of any first-century marker or decisive dig. This wiki does not discount the raising of Lazarus for requiring a miracle; the limit here is evidential, about which hole in the hillside, not about whether the miracle could occur.
