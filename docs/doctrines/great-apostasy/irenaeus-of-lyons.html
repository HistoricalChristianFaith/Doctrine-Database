Irenaeus of Lyons on the continuity of the church

Dates: c. 180 (Against Heresies, esp. Book III, written c. 175–185; Irenaeus of Lyons, c. 130–c. 202, bishop of Lugdunum in Gaul and hearer of Polycarp, who, Irenaeus reports, had been “instructed by apostles” (Against Heresies III.3.4)) · Wikipedia
Summary: Irenaeus is the earliest extant witness on this timeline to the church’s continuity. He does not address the total-apostasy thesis, which had not yet been proposed; arguing against the Gnostics, he holds that the apostolic faith has been preserved unbroken and in public view through the succession of teachers in every apostolic church “from the apostles until now,” and answers the Gnostic claim of a secret tradition by dating their doctrines as recent: “prior to Valentinus, those who follow Valentinus had no existence.” His succession argument is treated in full on his Government of the Church and Roman primacy detail pages; this page reports only the bearing of that argument on whether the church wholly fell.

Related readings: Did the church wholly apostatize after the apostolic age?, where his words are cited for the continuity reading.

Claims & evidence

“One and the same… faith, which has been preserved in the Church from the apostles until now” (Against Heresies III.3.3)

The sentence cited on this timeline closes Irenaeus’s Roman succession-list. Having traced the bishops of Rome from the apostles down to Eleutherius, the bishop reigning as he wrote, he draws the conclusion the whole list was built to reach:

In this order, and by this succession, the ecclesiastical tradition from the apostles, and the preaching of the truth, have come down to us. And this is most abundant proof that there is one and the same vivifying faith, which has been preserved in the Church from the apostles until now, and handed down in truth.

— Against Heresies III.3.3.1 Irenaeus presents the church’s unbroken continuity — “from the apostles until now” — as public and checkable, and uses it as his proof against the Gnostics: the faith was “preserved… and handed down.”

The tradition public and traceable in every church (III.3.1)

The premise beneath that conclusion is that the apostolic teaching is open to inspection everywhere, precisely because the apostles left it in the hands of the men they made bishops:

It is within the power of all, therefore, in every Church, who may wish to see the truth, to contemplate clearly the tradition of the apostles manifested throughout the whole world; and we are in a position to reckon up those who were by the apostles instituted bishops in the Churches, and [to demonstrate] the succession of these men to our own times…

— Against Heresies III.3.1.2 Irenaeus appeals to the succession “to our own times” as a public standard open to anyone who wishes to check it.

Irenaeus on the church as depository and the heresies as recent (III.4.1)

Irenaeus then argues that, since the truth abides in the church, the systems he opposes are later arrivals that cannot reach back to the apostles:

Since therefore we have such proofs, it is not necessary to seek the truth among others which it is easy to obtain from the Church; since the apostles, like a rich man [depositing his money] in a bank, lodged in her hands most copiously all things pertaining to the truth: so that every man, whosoever will, can draw from her the water of life.

— Against Heresies III.4.1 (the translators’ chapter heading, not Irenaeus’s words, reads: “The truth is to be found nowhere else but in the Catholic Church, the sole depository of apostolic doctrine. Heresies are of recent formation, and cannot trace their origin up to the apostles”).3 Irenaeus makes the church the bank in which the apostles “lodged… most copiously all things pertaining to the truth,” and dates the teachings he opposes: “prior to Valentinus, those who follow Valentinus had no existence; nor did those from Marcion exist before Marcion” (III.4.3). The restorationist account, recorded on this timeline from Roger Williams to Talmage, holds instead that the church decayed and its authority had to be restored.

Reception and debate

  • Not an answer to a named thesis. Irenaeus is not answering the Latter-day Saint or any restorationist claim, which did not yet exist; he writes two generations from the apostles, as a hearer of Polycarp, whom he reports “instructed by apostles.” His words are cited for the continuity reading on the interpretation page, which also records the reformation reading’s holders, who likewise hold that the church never ceased.
  • Scope. He argues against teachings he identifies as Gnostic; later developments are recorded on their own timelines.
  • Same author, elsewhere. The succession argument in its own right — as a rule of faith and a theory of church order — is set out on his Government of the Church page; its bearing on Rome specifically is on his Roman primacy page. How Christ’s promises bear on this continuity is recorded on the interpretation page.
