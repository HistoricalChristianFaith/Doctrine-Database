Vincent of Lérins on the continuity of the faith

Dates: c. 434 (the Commonitory/Commonitorium; Vincent of Lérins, d. before 450, a Gallic monk and priest of the island monastery of Lérins off the southern coast of Gaul, writing under the pen-name Peregrinus) · Wikipedia
Summary: Vincent states the test for telling catholic truth from heresy known as the “Vincentian canon,” that we hold “what has been believed everywhere, always, and by all.” He describes the faith it measures as continuous: the church “never changes” the doctrine deposited in her charge, which “has continued one and the same through the several successions of ages… and so will continue for ever.” Novelty is, for him, the mark of heresy. (He also appears as a witness on the Old Testament canon timeline.)

Related readings: Did the church wholly apostatize after the apostolic age?, where his words are cited for the continuity reading.

Claims & evidence

The canon: “believed everywhere, always, by all” (Commonitory ch. 2)

The rule — quod ubique, quod semper, quod ab omnibus — includes antiquity among its marks:

Moreover, in the Catholic Church itself, all possible care must be taken, that we hold that faith which has been believed everywhere, always, by all. For that is truly and in the strictest sense “Catholic,” which, as the name itself and the reason of the thing declare, comprehends all universally. This rule we shall observe if we follow universality, antiquity, consent.

— Commonitory ch. 2 (§6).1 “Antiquity” — “believed… always” — is one of the three legs of the test, with universality and consent.

The church as guardian that “never changes… does not lose her own” (Commonitory ch. 23)

Vincent states the continuity his canon assumes as a positive claim about the church:

But the Church of Christ, the careful and watchful guardian of the doctrines deposited in her charge, never changes anything in them, never diminishes, never adds, does not cut off what is necessary, does not add what is superfluous, does not lose her own, does not appropriate what is another’s…

— Commonitory ch. 23 (§59).2 Vincent allows that doctrine be deepened and more sharply defined over time (his chapter on progress in religion compares it to a body growing while remaining the same body); what he denies is that the deposit itself is lost, replaced, or interrupted.

“One and the same… and so will continue for ever” (Commonitory ch. 24)

Vincent states the faith’s perpetuity across the whole course of history, past and future alike:

… the Catholic and universal doctrine, which has continued one and the same through the several successions of ages by the uncorrupt tradition of the truth and so will continue for ever.

— Commonitory ch. 24 (§60).3 The claim runs in both directions: the doctrine has come down “one and the same through the several successions of ages,” and it “will continue for ever.”

Reception and debate

  • Not an answer to a named thesis. Like Irenaeus three centuries before him, Vincent is not answering a restorationist thesis; he writes against teachings condemned in his day (Arianism, Nestorianism, Pelagianism). His words are cited for the continuity reading on the interpretation page, which also records the reformation reading’s holders, who likewise hold that the church never ceased.
  • Development within the same faith. Vincent allows for doctrinal progress (“let there be growth… but only within its own kind”).
  • Use of the canon. Vincent’s canon is a rule for identifying error; it has been invoked by Protestants against medieval developments and by Catholics against Protestant ones ⚠ unverified.
