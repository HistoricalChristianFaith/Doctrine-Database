Vincent of Lérins against a total apostasy

Dates: c. 434 (the Commonitory/Commonitorium; Vincent of Lérins, d. before 450, a Gallic monk and priest of the island monastery of Lérins off the southern coast of Gaul, writing under the pen-name Peregrinus) · Wikipedia
Summary: Vincent gives the Latin church its classic test for telling catholic truth from heresy — the “Vincentian canon,” that we hold “what has been believed everywhere, always, and by all.” That rule is only intelligible if there is an unbroken, self-identical faith to appeal to across the whole span of the church’s life — and Vincent says so outright: the church “never changes” the doctrine deposited in her charge, which “has continued one and the same through the several successions of ages… and so will continue for ever.” His whole method thus presupposes the contradictory of a total apostasy. A Restoration needs the deposit to have been lost and later restored; Vincent’s canon works only because it was never lost — the continuity is the very yardstick against which he measures every novelty as heresy. (He also appears on this wiki as a witness on the Old Testament canon timeline.)

Related arguments: Vincent’s continuity-of-faith canon is one of the later voices behind the indefectibility answer to the thesis (assessment: sound) and tells directly against the total-apostasy claim (assessment: weak).

Claims & evidence

The canon: “believed everywhere, always, by all” (Commonitory ch. 2)

The famous rule — quod ubique, quod semper, quod ab omnibus — is a test of continuity: the true faith is the one the church has held without interruption from the beginning:

Moreover, in the Catholic Church itself, all possible care must be taken, that we hold that faith which has been believed everywhere, always, by all. For that is truly and in the strictest sense “Catholic,” which, as the name itself and the reason of the thing declare, comprehends all universally. This rule we shall observe if we follow universality, antiquity, consent.

— Commonitory ch. 2 (§6).1 “Antiquity” — “believed… always” — is one of the three legs of the test. A faith that had ceased to be held at some point, only to be restored centuries later, would fail Vincent’s canon by definition: it would not have been believed “always,” and the interval of its loss would be the very mark of novelty he is warning against.

The deposit is never lost: the church “never changes… does not lose her own” (Commonitory ch. 23)

Vincent states the continuity his canon assumes as a positive claim about the church:

But the Church of Christ, the careful and watchful guardian of the doctrines deposited in her charge, never changes anything in them, never diminishes, never adds, does not cut off what is necessary, does not add what is superfluous, does not lose her own, does not appropriate what is another’s…

— Commonitory ch. 23 (§59).2 “Does not lose her own” is the exact denial of the premise a Restoration needs. Vincent allows — indeed requires — that doctrine be deepened and more sharply defined over time (his chapter on progress in religion compares it to a body growing while remaining the same body); what he rules out is that the deposit itself could be lost, replaced, or interrupted.

“One and the same… and so will continue for ever” (Commonitory ch. 24)

Most pointed of all against a total apostasy is Vincent’s statement of the faith’s perpetuity across the whole course of history — past and future alike:

… the Catholic and universal doctrine, which has continued one and the same through the several successions of ages by the uncorrupt tradition of the truth and so will continue for ever.

— Commonitory ch. 24 (§60).3 This is a claim of unbroken continuity in both directions: the doctrine has come down “one and the same through the several successions of ages,” and it “will continue for ever.” A wholesale apostasy — a rupture at which the true church ceased and the deposit was lost — is precisely the break Vincent denies could ever occur.

Notes

  • A presupposed continuity, not a direct rebuttal. Like Irenaeus three centuries before him, Vincent is not answering a restorationist thesis — he is arming the church against heresy (Arianism, Nestorianism, Pelagianism). His value as a counter-witness is that the unbroken continuity a total apostasy denies is not a conclusion he defends but the bedrock his whole test stands on: novelty is the mark of error precisely because the true faith has never lapsed.
  • Development is not defection. Vincent’s celebrated allowance for doctrinal progress (“let there be growth… but only within its own kind”) is the ancient anticipation of the same continuity-with-development that the modern historians of doctrine describe against the rupture thesis — see the historians of doctrine block on this timeline. Both hold that the church’s teaching can mature without ever ceasing to be the same faith; neither leaves room for the church to have wholly fallen.
  • What this does and does not settle. Vincent’s canon is a rule for identifying error, not a proof that any particular later development passed his test; his own rule has been turned by Protestants against medieval accretions as readily as by Catholics against Protestant novelties. What his witness bears on here is narrower and firmer: that the deposit was never lost — the direct contradictory of the total-apostasy premise, from one of the most quoted authorities of the Latin tradition.
