Joseph Smith on the Great Apostasy

Dates: 1805–1844 · Wikipedia
Summary: The founding prophet of the Latter-day Saint movement makes a total apostasy the premise of the Restoration: in his First Vision account he is told that all the existing churches are wrong, their creeds an abomination and their professors corrupt; the loss he describes is not of doctrine only but of divine authority — a broken priesthood requiring literal restoration by heavenly ministration.

Claims & evidence

“They were all wrong” — the First Vision verdict on the churches

I was answered that I must join none of them, for they were all wrong; and the Personage who addressed me said that all their creeds were an abomination in his sight; that those professors were all corrupt; that: ‘they draw near to me with their lips, but their hearts are far from me, they teach for doctrines the commandments of men, having a form of godliness, but they deny the power thereof.’

— Joseph Smith—History 1:19 (Pearl of Great Price), churchofjesuschrist.org. The question that prompts it (v. 18) is “which of all the sects was right… which I should join”; the answer is that none is. This is the total-apostasy premise stated as personal revelation. 1

Universal straying, and the “only true” church raised to repair it

For they have strayed from mine ordinances, and have broken mine everlasting covenant;

— Doctrine and Covenants 1:15, churchofjesuschrist.org. The same revelation names the newly organized church “the only true and living church upon the face of the whole earth” (1:30) — a claim that only makes sense if every other church has fallen away. The apostasy is the negative space that the Restoration fills.2

The loss is of authority, not doctrine only

They told me that all religious denominations were believing in incorrect doctrines and that none of them was acknowledged of God as His Church and kingdom

— the Wentworth Letter (1842), as reprinted in Ensign, July 2002. The distinctively Latter-day Saint form of the claim is that valid priesthood authority disappeared with the apostles and had to be restored by angelic ordination — so that reform from within an existing church could never suffice.3

Notes

  • Proponent’s overreach, flagged here per house practice. The revelatory framing states the apostasy as a settled fact (“they were all wrong… all corrupt”) rather than as a historical finding argued from evidence. As a historical claim about the patristic and medieval church — that all valid authority, ordinances, and doctrine were lost, so that no legitimate church survived — it is rated weak on this wiki’s own continuity record; see “Did the church wholly apostatize after the apostolic age?” The narrower observation that real corruptions and innovations crept in is defensible in places, but it is a different and lesser claim than the total rupture the Restoration requires.
  • The restoration’s attestation, flagged here too. The positive half of the claim — that the lost priesthood was restored in 1829 by John the Baptist (Aaronic) and by the resurrected Peter, James, and John (Melchizedek) — is not weak for requiring a miracle, but the named, dated narrative appears years after the fact: Smith’s earliest (1832) history mentions an angelic conferral only generically, the naming of Peter, James, and John enters the canon with the 1835 expansion of an earlier revelation, and the whole rests on two non-independent witnesses (Smith and Oliver Cowdery). See “Was priesthood authority restored by angelic ministration after a total apostasy?” (weak).
  • The systematic marshalling of this premise into a historical case is James E. Talmage’s The Great Apostasy (1909); the pre-Mormon analogue is Roger Williams.
