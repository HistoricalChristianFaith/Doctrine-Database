Did the church wholly apostatize after the apostolic age?

Disputed reading · on The Great Apostasy · witness: James E. Talmage

The passage

We affirm that with the passing of the so-called apostolic age the Church gradually drifted into a condition of apostasy, whereby succession in the priesthood was broken; and that the Church, as an earthly organization operating under divine direction and having authority to officiate in spiritual ordinances, ceased to exist.

— James E. Talmage, The Great Apostasy (1909), ch. II, source.1

The question

Talmage states the Latter-day Saint teaching that after the apostles the church lost its priesthood authority and ceased to exist as a divinely authorized body until a restoration; Catholic and Orthodox teaching holds that the church has continued without a break since the apostles, and the Lutheran and Reformed confessions hold that a church always remained on earth, though corrupted in places. What is disputed is whether the post-apostolic record shows a church that ceased and needed restoring, one that endured and needed reforming, or one that endured and kept the apostolic faith; each tradition also grounds its answer in what it receives as promise or revelation. A further answer, Baptist successionism, traces a true church through a succession of dissenting congregations and has its own timeline. No modern academic historian holding the second reading was located; it is sourced below to Luther, the confessions and earlier Protestant church histories.

The apostolic faith kept without a break

In brief: the church the apostles founded never ceased; its faith was handed down without a break.

Held by: Irenaeus of Lyons (Against Heresies, c. 180);2 John Henry Newman (Catholic, An Essay on the Development of Christian Doctrine, 1845);3 Georges Florovsky (Orthodox, “The Catholicity of the Church,” 1934);4 the Catechism of the Catholic Church (1992);5 with further holders in the note.6

Irenaeus, answering teachers who claimed a secret tradition, appeals to the public succession of bishops in the apostolic churches as “most abundant proof that there is one and the same vivifying faith, which has been preserved in the Church from the apostles until now, and handed down in truth” (III.3.3).2 Vincent of Lérins makes antiquity part of his test of catholic truth, what has been “believed everywhere, always, by all,” and calls the church the guardian of the deposit that “does not lose her own” (Commonitory 2, 23).7 On this reading continuity is a matter of public record: succession lists, creeds, councils and liturgies are handed on in every century, and the disputes of each age are arguments within one body. Newman, for his part, gives a Catholic account of change within that body: a revealed idea unfolds while remaining itself (“here below to live is to change, and to be perfect is to have changed often”), and seven notes tell a genuine development from a corruption, so that change is not of itself a sign of loss.3 J. N. D. Kelly describes the early pattern as one in which “the broad outline of revealed truth was respected as a sacrosanct inheritance from the apostles, its theological explication… left unfettered.”8 Florovsky, for the Orthodox, grounds the continuity in Pentecost and the abiding of the Spirit: “The Holy Spirit descended once and for always… He lives and abides ceaselessly in the church,” and “does not descend upon earth again and again, but abides in the ‘visible’ and historical Church.”4 Kallistos Ware writes that the outward catastrophes of Orthodox history “have never broken the inward continuity of the Orthodox Church,” and that “Orthodoxy believes that Christ’s promise cannot fail.”9 The Catechism teaches that the church “is indestructible (cf Mt 16:18)” and “upheld infallibly in the truth.”5 Catholic and Orthodox writers each identify the continuing church with their own communion: the Catechism says that the church of the Creed “subsists in the Catholic Church,”5 and Ware places the continuity in the Orthodox Church, resting it on Christ’s promise to guide the church “into all truth” (John 16:13).9

A church that endured under corruption, to be reformed

In brief: the church never ceased to exist, but its visible institutions became corrupted, so that what was needed was reformation within it, not a new founding.

Held by: Martin Luther (Lutheran, Concerning Rebaptism, 1528; Smalcald Articles, 1537);10,11 the Augsburg Confession (Lutheran, 1530);12 John Calvin (Reformed, Institutes, 1559);13 the Westminster Confession of Faith (Reformed, 1646);14 with further holders in the note.15

Luther names the papacy the Antichrist of 2 Thessalonians 2: “the Pope is the very Antichrist, who has exalted himself above, and opposed himself against Christ.”11 He holds in the same years that the church persisted under it: “in the papal church there are the true holy Scriptures, true baptism, the true sacrament of the altar, the true keys to the forgiveness of sins, the true office of the ministry… I contend that in the papacy there is true Christianity,” and “we are all still under the papacy and therefrom have received our Christian treasures.”10 The Augsburg Confession teaches “that one holy Church is to continue forever,” “the congregation of saints, in which the Gospel is rightly taught and the Sacraments are rightly administered.”12 Calvin, while “unwilling simply to concede the name of Church to the Papists,” does “not deny that there are churches among them”: “we deny not to the Papists those vestiges of a Church which the Lord has allowed to remain among them,” baptism first, and “other remains also to prevent the Church from utterly perishing.”13 The Westminster Confession holds both sides together: “The purest churches under heaven are subject both to mixture and error; and some have so degenerated as to become no churches of Christ, but synagogues of Satan. Nevertheless, there shall be always a Church on earth to worship God according to his will,” citing Matthew 16:18 and 28:19–20; its next section calls the pope “that Antichrist, that man of sin and son of perdition.”14 On this reading the warnings of “a falling away” describe a corruption that arises inside the church, while the promises secure that a church remains to be reformed. The Magdeburg Centuries (1559–1574), directed by Matthias Flacius, narrated church history as a decline from apostolic purity within a continuing body.15 Later Protestant histories kept that frame: Matthew Bowman reports that for the Lutheran historian Johann Lorenz von Mosheim “God sustained the true church throughout its history,” and that the Anglican Joseph Milner “insisted that the true church had always been on the face of the earth, merely in a different guise.”16 Because the church endured, with Scripture, baptism and the ministry still in it, its renewal is a reformation of what it had received.

Authority lost and the church ceased, to be restored

In brief: after the apostles the church lost the authority to act in God’s name and ceased to exist as a divine institution, so that it had to be restored by new revelation and ordination.

Held by: Roger Williams (Seeker, c. 1639–1645, partly as reported by others);17,18 Joseph Smith (Latter-day Saint, Joseph Smith—History; Wentworth Letter, 1842);19 James E. Talmage (Latter-day Saint, The Great Apostasy, 1909);1 Kent P. Jackson (Latter-day Saint, “New Testament Prophecies of Apostasy,” 2006);20 with further holders in the note.21

Williams judged the worship of Christendom’s national churches “a whole forme of Antichristian worship” and reasoned that a church under a false head has a “body, faith, baptisme, hope… all false also.”17 Cotton Mather reports that he concluded “there was none upon earth that could administer baptism” and advised waiting “for the coming of a new apostle”; Robert T. Handy summarizes his view as an apostasy “since Constantine” that left the true church undiscernible “until God raised up new apostles.”18 Joseph Smith’s First Vision account answers his question which church to join: “I must join none of them, for they were all wrong.”19 The Doctrine and Covenants says that men “have strayed from mine ordinances, and have broken mine everlasting covenant,” and calls the newly organized church “the only true and living church upon the face of the whole earth.”22 The loss on this reading is of authority: Latter-day Saint teaching holds that after the apostles’ deaths “the Lord withdrew the authority of the priesthood from the earth,” that “Many churches were established, but they did not have priesthood power,” and that the Restoration is “‘restored,’ not ‘reformed,’ Christianity.”23 Talmage builds the case in two parts: the New Testament warnings (2 Thessalonians 2:3; Acts 20:29–30; 1 Timothy 4:1) foretell the apostasy, and the post-apostolic changes in organization, ordinances and doctrine fulfil it.1 Jackson calls the New Testament “possibly the best single witness of the Apostasy” and states the teaching that “the Church of Jesus and His Apostles came to an end well within a century of its formation.”20 S. Kent Brown finds at the end of the first century “a church full of dissensions,” left after its leaders’ deaths “to wander, making its way haltingly until its original form and authority were lost.”24 Noel B. Reynolds locates the cause in “internal rebellion against God’s covenants and against his authorized servants.”25 On this reading, once priesthood authority was withdrawn no existing church could supply it; it was “brought back to the earth” through the priesthood conferred on Joseph Smith “by the ministering of angels.”23
