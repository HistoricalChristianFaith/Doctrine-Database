Is baptism for the dead (1 Corinthians 15:29) a lost apostolic ordinance restored?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: weak — the Latter-day Saint reading takes Paul’s lone reference to being “baptized for the dead” as proof that vicarious proxy baptism was a genuine, salvific apostolic ordinance — practised in the early church, lost in the apostasy, and restored through Joseph Smith. The surface of the verse does most naturally describe some act of baptism on the dead’s behalf, and such a rite is attested in the early centuries — but where the fathers name a real proxy baptism they uniformly attribute it to heretics (Marcion, Cerinthus) and condemn it, so what the record actually preserves is a repudiated custom, not an orthodox ordinance there to be restored; the whole exegetical tradition reads the clause for the resurrection of the body, not for aid to the departed; Paul neither describes nor commends the practice; and the thesis inherits the total apostasy it presupposes, which is itself rated weak

See also: the same verse read under a different framework — does “baptized for the dead” support aid for the dead? (on purgatory, where it is adduced for prayer and offering for the departed, assessment weak) — and the parallel restoration claim, was priesthood authority restored by angelic ministration? (weak).

The claim

Else what shall they do which are baptized for the dead, if the dead rise not at all? why are they then baptized for the dead?

— 1 Corinthians 15:29 (KJV).1 On the Latter-day Saint reading this clause is direct biblical evidence that the earliest church baptized the living on behalf of their dead as a real, saving ordinance — an ordinance later lost in the general apostasy and restored through Joseph Smith, who made proxy baptism a temple rite and called it “the most glorious of all subjects belonging to the everlasting gospel.” The sub-claim weighed here is not whether the practice can be read off the verse (it can, on one construal), but whether 1 Corinthians 15:29 establishes that proxy baptism was a legitimate apostolic ordinance whose loss a Restoration was needed to repair.

Who advances it

The reading is the Latter-day Saint one. Joseph Smith first taught baptism for the dead publicly at a Nauvoo funeral in August 1840, citing this verse; the first proxy baptisms followed in the Mississippi, then in the font of the unfinished Nauvoo Temple from November 1841.2 He set the doctrine on a scriptural footing in an 1842 epistle now canonized as Doctrine and Covenants 128, quoting 1 Corinthians 15:29 verbatim — “in relation to the baptism for the dead, I will give you another quotation of Paul” — and binding the fate of living and dead together: “they without us cannot be made perfect—neither can we without our dead be made perfect.”3 Church teaching presents the practice as the recovery of an ancient Christian ordinance: 1 Corinthians 15:29 is “the only Bible passage that mentions vicarious baptism for the dead,” but “other ancient texts attest that baptism for the dead was practised by early Christians” — a rite the apostasy is said to have swept away and the Restoration brought back.4

The evidence for

Steelmanned, the case has three legs:

  1. The surface of the verse most naturally describes a proxy rite. “Baptized for (ὑπέρ) the dead” reads, on first hearing, as the living being baptized on behalf of someone who has died. Many non-Latter-day Saint interpreters grant that Paul is referring to an actual practice of vicarious baptism among the Corinthians rather than inventing a hypothetical — the reading is old and respectable, not a Mormon novelty.5
  2. Paul does not condemn it. He mentions the practice in passing, as a premise his hearers accept, to argue that it would be pointless “if the dead rise not.” He neither corrects nor rebukes it in the way he rebukes other Corinthian irregularities — which, on this reading, tells for its being an accepted part of the apostolic church’s life.
  3. A proxy rite is attested in the early centuries. The custom of baptizing on behalf of the dead is not a Nauvoo invention: several ancient witnesses report it in the second and third centuries. If a practice existed then and does not exist in the mainstream church now, something was lost — and a lost saving ordinance is exactly the kind of thing a Restoration is for.

Adversarial assessment

Each leg is real, and none reaches the conclusion.

  1. The one proxy rite the record actually preserves is condemned as heresy — so nothing orthodox was “lost.” This is the decisive point. Where the fathers take “for the dead” as one person baptized in a dead person’s stead, they uniformly ascribe the custom to heretics and ridicule it: Chrysostom and Theophylact describe the Marcionite trick of hiding a living man under the corpse’s bier to answer for it, “like men jesting upon the stage”; Epiphanius reports the same vicarious baptism among the Cerinthians; Didymus the Blind says flatly the Marcionites “baptize the living on behalf of dead unbelievers, not knowing that baptism saves only the person who receives it”; and the Council of Carthage of 419 legislated against baptizing the dead by canon.6 The “other ancient texts” that attest proxy baptism are precisely these — they attest a practice the early church catalogued among heresies and forbade. A rite the apostolic and patristic church repudiated cannot be the lost apostolic ordinance a Restoration recovers; the restorationist thesis needs the practice to have been orthodox and then lost, whereas the record shows it heterodox and rejected from the first.
  2. The whole exegetical tradition reads the clause for the resurrection of the body, not for saving the dead. Paul’s point is an ad hominem for the resurrection: a practice his hearers know of makes no sense unless the dead rise — so the dead rise. Read that way, the verse concerns the candidate’s own future body. Tertullian takes “baptized for the dead” as baptized “for the [dead] body”; Chrysostom ties it to the baptismal creed — “I believe in the resurrection of the dead” — so “for the dead” means “for thy dead body, that it no longer remains dead”; Cosmas and Theophylact say the same.7 On the reading the verse actually received across the tradition, it proves the resurrection and says nothing of a salvific ordinance performed for the departed.
  3. “Paul does not condemn it” cuts both ways, and does not make it doctrine. Even granting that Paul reports a genuine Corinthian custom, a passing, unexplained reference — in the third person (“what shall they do?”), a clause many read as marking a practice he cites but does not own — is not an apostolic institution of the rite. Paul commends nothing here; he leverages a practice for a resurrection argument. The Corinthian church that Paul is at pains to correct for its abuses is a strange place to locate a normative ordinance from its one uncorrected aside. And the verse is a byword among interpreters for difficulty — surveys count dozens of competing readings — the thinnest possible foundation for a saving ordinance the apostolic church supposedly required.8
  4. The thesis inherits an apostasy that did not occur. A restored ordinance answers a rupture, and the rupture this depends on — that the church wholly fell, losing its valid ordinances — is rated weak against the continuous, footnoted record. If no total apostasy emptied the church of its ordinances, there is no lost ordinance for a Restoration to recover; and the one candidate offered here was, on the evidence, never an orthodox ordinance to begin with.

Assessment

Weak. That the church once possessed proxy baptism for the dead as a saving ordinance, lost it, and needed it restored is not established by 1 Corinthians 15:29 on its best case. The surface reading grants only that some baptism-on-behalf-of-the-dead may lie behind the clause — but the sole proxy rite the ancient record actually names, it condemns as heretical (Marcionite, Cerinthian) and forbids by canon, so the “ancient attestation” the argument leans on documents a repudiated custom, not a lost apostolic ordinance. The exegetical tradition, East and West, reads the verse for the resurrection of the body, not for aid to the dead; Paul neither describes nor commends the practice; and the clause is a notorious crux interpretum too contested to bear the weight. Finally the reading is parasitic on the total-apostasy premise it presupposes, which the continuous record does not support — a restoration of an ordinance that was neither orthodox nor lost. The same verse is weighed under a different framework, as a prop for prayer and offering for the dead, on the purgatory argument page (also weak); on neither reading does this single, unexplained clause carry the doctrine built on it.
