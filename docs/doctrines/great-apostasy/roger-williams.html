Roger Williams on the Great Apostasy

Dates: c. 1603–1683 · Wikipedia
Summary: Two centuries before the Latter-day Saints, the founder of Rhode Island reached a total-apostasy position from Puritan premises: judging established Christendom’s church, baptism, and worship “Antichristian” and false, he withdrew from all church communion — even from the Baptist congregation he had helped gather — and became a “Seeker,” holding that no valid ministry or baptism existed and awaiting a fresh apostolic restoration.

Claims & evidence

Established Christendom’s worship is “Antichristian” and false (his own words)

a whole forme of Antichristian worship in life and death

— Christenings Make Not Christians (1645), reprint. In the same tract Williams reasons that a church under a “false head” has a “body, faith, baptisme, hope… all false also” — the national churches of Christendom being, on his view, drawn from “one false worship to another.” The apostasy has left the visible church without a true form. 1

No valid baptism or ministry remains — await a new apostle (Mather’s report)

he was now satisfied that there was none upon earth that could administer baptism, and so that their last baptism, as well as their first, was a nullity, for the want of a called administrator; he advised them thereupon to forego all, to dislike everything, and wait for the coming of a new apostle

— Cotton Mather, Magnalia Christi Americana (1702), 1.498, as reproduced in John T. Christian, A History of the Baptists. This is the total step, and it is reported by a hostile Puritan opponent, not stated in Williams’s own surviving hand — so it is given here as Mather’s characterization of what Williams told his followers.2

The scholarly summary: apostasy since Constantine, the true church undiscernible until new apostles

Williams became convinced that the apostasy of the churches since Constantine had engulfed Christendom and that until God raised up new apostles the true church could not be discerned.

— Robert T. Handy, “Roger Williams,” in the Encyclopedia of Religion. This is the clean statement of the position the timeline is documenting: a post-Constantinian total apostasy, requiring a restoration by newly commissioned apostles — the same logical shape the Latter-day Saint Restoration would later give.3

Notes

  • A famous quotation to distrust. The line most often put in Williams’s mouth — “There is no regularly constituted church of Christ on earth, nor any person qualified to administer any church ordinances; nor can there be until new apostles are sent… for whose coming I am seeking” — is not his. It is a nineteenth-century first-person reworking of a third-person summary; the near-contemporary source (Richard Scott’s letter in George Fox’s A New England Fire-Brand Quenched) reports only that Williams “turned Seeker” and contains no such sentence. It is cited here only to flag it as spurious. 4
  • Williams is the strongest pre-Mormon witness that the total-apostasy–and–restoration logic is not unique to the Latter-day Saints but a recurring conclusion — which is why the family this page belongs to is framed around the shared thesis rather than any one tradition. The thesis itself is weighed at “Did the church wholly apostatize after the apostolic age?”
