James E. Talmage on the Great Apostasy

Dates: 1862–1933 · Wikipedia
Summary: A geologist and Latter-day Saint apostle whose book The Great Apostasy (1909) is the movement’s systematic statement of the thesis: he marshals the New-Testament prophecies of a falling-away and a survey of patristic-to-medieval innovations into the claim that the primitive church lost its authority and ceased to exist as a divine institution, a two-part case (prophecy and historical change) recorded on the interpretation page.

Claims & evidence

The primitive church “degenerated into an earthly organization only”

The restored Church affirms that a general apostasy developed during and after the apostolic period, and that the primitive Church lost its power, authority, and graces as a divine institution, and degenerated into an earthly organization only.

— The Great Apostasy (Salt Lake City: Deseret News, 1909), Preface, Project Gutenberg; full text also at archive.org. Talmage’s word is “lost”: on his account the authority was forfeited, so that what remained was “an earthly organization only.”1

The church “ceased to exist” and the priesthood succession “was broken”

We affirm that with the passing of the so-called apostolic age the Church gradually drifted into a condition of apostasy, whereby succession in the priesthood was broken; and that the Church, as an earthly organization operating under divine direction and having authority to officiate in spiritual ordinances, ceased to exist.

— The Great Apostasy, ch. II. Talmage states the claim as a broken priesthood and a church that ceased to exist as an authoritative body, the condition to which Latter-day Saint teaching presents the Restoration as the answer.2

The case is built from the scriptural prophecies plus the historical innovations

Talmage’s method is to read the New-Testament warnings (2 Thessalonians 2, Acts 20, 1 Timothy 4) as predicting the apostasy, then to catalogue the post-apostolic changes — in organization, ordinances, and doctrine — as its fulfilment. The official Latter-day Saint teaching restates the same two-part structure: “After the deaths of the Savior and His Apostles, men corrupted the principles of the gospel and made unauthorized changes in Church organization and priesthood ordinances.”3

Reception and debate

  • Talmage’s reading of the prophecies and of the historical record is set beside Catholic, Orthodox and Protestant readings of the same centuries at “Did the church wholly apostatize after the apostolic age?” Several of the developments he catalogues have their own timelines here.
  • Later Latter-day Saint scholarship revisited his account: Early Christians in Disarray (2005), edited by Noel B. Reynolds, is described in a BYU Studies notice as using early Christian manuscripts “to reevaluate the formative research on the apostasy that has already been performed by James E. Talmage, Joseph Fielding Smith, and B. H. Roberts,” and as placing the apostasy’s beginning “as early as the first century ad.”4
  • Related witnesses on this timeline: Joseph Smith (the revelatory accounts), Roger Williams (the Seeker position), and Luther (a church to be reformed).
