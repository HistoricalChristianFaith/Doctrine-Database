James E. Talmage on the Great Apostasy

Dates: 1862–1933 · Wikipedia
Summary: A geologist and Latter-day Saint apostle whose book The Great Apostasy (1909) is the movement’s systematic statement of the thesis: he marshals the New-Testament prophecies of a falling-away and a survey of patristic-to-medieval innovations into the claim that the primitive church lost its authority and ceased to exist as a divine institution — the fullest form of exactly the two sub-claims this timeline weighs.

Claims & evidence

The primitive church “degenerated into an earthly organization only”

The restored Church affirms that a general apostasy developed during and after the apostolic period, and that the primitive Church lost its power, authority, and graces as a divine institution, and degenerated into an earthly organization only.

— The Great Apostasy (Salt Lake City: Deseret News, 1909), Preface, Project Gutenberg; full text also at archive.org. The word doing the work is “lost”: not diminished or corrupted but forfeited, so that what remained was “an earthly organization only.”1

The church “ceased to exist” and the priesthood succession “was broken”

We affirm that with the passing of the so-called apostolic age the Church gradually drifted into a condition of apostasy, whereby succession in the priesthood was broken; and that the Church, as an earthly organization operating under divine direction and having authority to officiate in spiritual ordinances, ceased to exist.

— The Great Apostasy, ch. II. This is the total claim in its clearest form: a broken priesthood, a church that ceased to exist as an authoritative body — precisely the wholesale rupture that a restoration (rather than a reformation) presupposes.2

The case is built from the scriptural prophecies plus the historical innovations

Talmage’s method is to read the New-Testament warnings (2 Thessalonians 2, Acts 20, 1 Timothy 4) as predicting the apostasy, then to catalogue the post-apostolic changes — in organization, ordinances, and doctrine — as its fulfilment. The official Latter-day Saint teaching restates the same two-part structure: “After the deaths of the Savior and His Apostles, men corrupted the principles of the gospel and made unauthorized changes in Church organization and priesthood ordinances.”3

Notes

  • Proponent’s overreach, flagged here per house practice. Talmage’s two moves are weighed separately and both come out weak in their total form: that the church “ceased to exist” is tested against the continuous, footnoted record at “Did the church wholly apostatize after the apostolic age?”; that the New-Testament prophecies foretell a complete falling-away is tested at “Do the New Testament prophecies foretell a total falling-away?” The catalogue of genuine innovations is his strongest material — several such developments are rated contested elsewhere on this wiki — but that catalogue establishes change, not the loss of the church itself, which is the conclusion he needs.
  • Talmage stands in a lineage: Joseph Smith gives the revelatory premise, Talmage the historical argument; the pre-Mormon analogue is Roger Williams, and the Reformation’s reformable alternative is Luther.
