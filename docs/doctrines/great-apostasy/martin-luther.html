Martin Luther on the Great Apostasy

Dates: c. 1483–1546 · Wikipedia
Summary: Luther supplies the Reformation’s harshest apostasy-language — the papacy is the “very Antichrist,” the Man of Sin of 2 Thessalonians 2 — yet he explicitly denies that the true church had ceased to exist, insisting that Scripture, baptism, the sacrament, the keys, and the ministry remained valid under the papacy. His is the model of a reformable, partial apostasy, and it marks the line the total-rupture thesis must cross.

Claims & evidence

The papacy is the Man of Sin / Antichrist (2 Thessalonians 2)

This teaching shows forcefully that the Pope is the very Antichrist, who has exalted himself above, and opposed himself against Christ… This is, properly speaking to exalt himself above all that is called God as Paul says, 2 Thess. 2, 4.

— Smalcald Articles (1537), Part II, Article IV, “Of the Papacy,” Triglot Concordia (Bente–Dau); also at Project Wittenberg. Luther reads the papal office — not the individual pope only — as the prophesied apostate power seated within the church.1

Yet the true church survived under the papacy

We on our part confess that there is much that is Christian and good under the papacy; indeed everything that is Christian and good is to be found there and has come to us from this source. For instance we confess that in the papal church there are the true holy Scriptures, true baptism, the true sacrament of the altar, the true keys to the forgiveness of sins, the true office of the ministry… I contend that in the papacy there is true Christianity, even the right kind of Christianity and many great and devoted saints.

— Concerning Rebaptism: A Letter to Two Pastors (1528), Luther’s Works (American Edition), vol. 40, 231–232.2 And, still more sharply, on continuity:

The Christendom that now is under the papacy is truly the body of Christ and a member of it… So we are all still under the papacy and therefrom have received our Christian treasures.

— ibid., 232. This is the decisive point for the present timeline: however corrupt Luther judged Rome, he held the church had persisted, its ordinances and its ministry valid, so that what was needed was reformation, not a fresh restoration of a church that had vanished from the earth. 3

Notes

  • The wider Lutheran project agreed: the Magdeburg Centuries (1559–74), directed by Matthias Flacius, narrated church history as a long declension from apostolic purity through Roman corruption — but as the decay of a continuous body whose true doctrine never wholly failed, the Reformation being recovery, not re-founding.4
  • Luther’s position is the hinge of the whole doctrine: it uses the very apostasy proof-texts the restorationists use, yet stops firmly short of the total claim. The distance between Luther’s reformable corruption and Roger Williams’s total rupture is exactly the distance the total-apostasy argument has to travel.
  • A caution on wording: the phrase “the very kernel of Christianity,” sometimes attributed to Luther here, is a loose paraphrase, not the Luther’s Works translation; the verified wording is “true Christianity, even the right kind of Christianity.”
