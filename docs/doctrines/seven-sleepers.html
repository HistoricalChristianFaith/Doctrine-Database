The Seven Sleepers of Ephesus

Seven young Christians of Ephesus, so the story runs, were walled up alive in a cave for refusing to sacrifice during the persecution of the emperor Decius (c. 250) — and did not die, but slept, waking almost two centuries later under Theodosius II to find the empire become Christian, gave their witness to the resurrection of the body, and died. The legend crossed many languages and religions: its earliest extant forms are Syriac, of the fifth and early sixth centuries; Gregory of Tours carried it into Latin; the Qur’an tells of the Aṣḥāb al-Kahf (“the Companions of the Cave,” Sūrat al-Kahf 18:9–26), whom the Muslim commentators elaborated; the Golden Legend fixed the story for the medieval West; and the sleepers are kept as saints in the Roman and Byzantine calendars and venerated in Islamic devotion. Like Barlaam and Josaphat, it is a case study in how a holy-lives legend crosses languages and religions — here a story venerated by both Christians and Muslims, whose cave is shown at Ephesus and at half a dozen other sites from Jordan to Central Asia.

This page is not a life of seven men; it is a history of a legend and of how the churches — and Islam — came to hold it, set out earliest source first: the Syriac witnesses, Gregory’s Latin, the Qur’anic account and its commentators, the Byzantine and Latin diffusion down to the Golden Legend, and the modern scholarship that classed the tale among the world’s wandering “sleeper” folktales and argued over where it began. Its “witnesses” are the datable sources that carry the story, ordered by the age of the source — not a claimed chronology of the events they narrate.

Timeline

The earliest extant witnesses: the Syriac Story and Jacob of Serugh (5th–early 6th c.)

The earliest extant witnesses to the legend are in the Christian East. A Syriac prose Story of the Youths of Ephesus circulated by the later fifth century, and the West-Syrian poet Jacob of Serugh (c. 451–521) devoted to it a verse homily (mēmrā) — the earliest extant treatment that can be dated by a named author. In it the sleepers are a proof of the resurrection of the body: God “sealed their senses” and kept them whole in the cave so that, waking, they might refute those who denied that the dead rise. Whether the first written form was Syriac or a now-lost Greek text of Ephesus is disputed; see the question of the earliest written form. The whole story is attested in the East well before Gregory of Tours.1

Gregory of Tours brings the tale to the Latin West (573–594)

Gregory of Tours (538–594) wrote the earliest extant Latin form. He tells the story briefly in his Glory of the Martyrs (ch. 94) and at length in a Passio sanctorum septem dormientium apud Ephesum, and he names his source: the fuller account, he says, was one “which, interpreted by a certain Syrian, I translated into Latin” (Siro quodam interpretante in Latino transtulimus) — the colophon of the Passio names the interpreter John the Syrian (interpretante Iohanne Syro). Here the Western names take their familiar form — Maximianus, Malchus, Martinianus, Constantinus, Dionysius, John, and Serapion — and from Gregory the legend passed into Paul the Deacon’s History of the Lombards and the mainstream of Western hagiography.2

The Qur’an: the Companions of the Cave (early 7th c.)

Within a century of Gregory, Sūrat al-Kahf (18:9–26) tells of “the companions of the cave and the inscription” — youths who believed in their Lord and fled to a cave, over whose ears God “cast a cover of sleep… for a number of years,” then “awakened them.” The Qur’anic account shares with the Christian legend the faithful youths, the sheltering cave, the miraculous sleep, and the waking as a sign that “the promise of God is truth” and the Hour real; it names neither persecutor nor city; it tells of the sleepers’ dog stretched across the threshold (v. 18), sets out rival counts of their number (“three… five… seven, and the eighth of them was their dog,” v. 22), and gives their term as “three hundred years, and exceeded by nine” (v. 25). Whether the Qur’anic account draws on the Christian legend is disputed; see the question of the Qur’anic account’s source.3

The Islamic elaboration: the commentators, the dog, and the number (8th–10th c.)

The Qur’an tells the tale allusively, and the Muslim commentators (tafsīr) — al-Ṭabarī above all — filled it out from the surrounding lore: they named the persecuting king (Decianus/Daqyānūs), gave the dog the name Qiṭmīr and counted it among the beasts admitted to Paradise, and read the Qur’anic caution about the number (v. 22) as settling on seven. The three hundred and nine years were harmonized as 300 solar and 309 lunar. The sleepers became figures of Muslim piety and protection — their names worn as amulets, Qiṭmīr inscribed for a blessing — and rival caves were shown at Ephesus, at ar-Raqīm near Amman in Jordan, and elsewhere.4

The Byzantine and Latin diffusion, down to the Golden Legend (8th–13th c.)

In Byzantium the earliest surviving Greek witness is a chronicle notice — Theophanes the Confessor (Chronographia, c. 810–814) enters the “remarkable miracle of the seven holy youths who arose after 184 years” in his account of the reign of Theodosius II, without telling the story, and George the Monk repeats it in the same century. Symeon Metaphrastes gave the tale its standard Byzantine narrative form in his tenth-century menologion, and the synaxaria keep it (4 August; the “second sleep” on 22 October). The date of the earliest surviving Greek text figures in the dispute over the legend’s earliest written form. In the West the legend descended through Paul the Deacon into the great medieval collections, an Anglo-Norman poem (Chardry’s Li set dormanz) and an Old Norse fragment, and at last into Jacobus de Voragine’s Golden Legend (c. 1260), the book that fixed the story for late-medieval Europe. Voragine dates their waking to “the year of our Lord four hundred and seventy-eight” and reports the tradition that they slept 362 years — then does the arithmetic against Decius’s reign and writes: “so they slept but two hundred and eight years.” The legend was by now a standard saints’ life, read across Europe.5

The calendars: veneration as saints (medieval)

The seven were commemorated as martyr-saints across the churches: the Roman Martyrology keeps them on 27 July, the Byzantine calendar on 4 August (and again, with the tradition of their “second sleep,” on 22 October), and they are venerated in Islamic devotion as well — holy figures honoured, under their two sets of names, in three religious traditions at once.6

Modern scholarship: the wandering folktale traced, the origins argued (16th c. → today)

Baronius, editing the Roman Martyrology in the sixteenth century, questioned the story’s historicity. Modern scholarship took up its literary history: John Koch (1883) and Michael Huber (1910) placed the Ephesus legend among the world’s wandering “sleeper” tales — kin to the Jewish Ḥoni/Abimelech and the Greek Epimenides, and later to Rip Van Winkle — and mapped its diffusion. Ignazio Guidi and Theodor Nöldeke held the Syriac form to be the earliest; Ernest Honigmann (1953) argued for a Greek original and attributed it to Stephen, bishop of Ephesus (448–451). Louis Massignon (1955–1963) catalogued the whole Christian-and-Islamic cult and called it a living “trait d’union” — a hyphen — between the two faiths.7

Disputed readings

Questions about how the legend’s texts have been read, cutting across the timeline above.

al-Raqīm and the sleepers’ cave

How a single Qur’anic word has been read, and the places that reading has been taken to name.

What does al-raqīm in Qur’an 18:9 mean: “the inscription” or a place-name?

Read as “the inscription/tablet” (al-Ṭabarī), as a place-name such as Petra (Shaddel), or as a copyist’s alteration of al-ruqūd (Bellamy); a place-name reading underlies the localising of the sleepers’ cave.

The recensions and their transmission

How the legend’s many surviving forms, in over two hundred manuscripts across a dozen languages, relate to one another.

Do the divergent Greek and Latin name-rosters point to independent transmission from a common source?

Turns on whether the sleepers’ names, which differ between the Greek and Latin lists, can trace the branches of the tradition.

Was the earliest written form of the Seven Sleepers legend Syriac or Greek?

Syriac priority (Guidi, Nöldeke) and a lost Greek original written at Ephesus (Huber, Peeters, Honigmann).

Other disputed readings

  • Was the Seven Sleepers legend composed for the fifth-century dispute over the resurrection of the body? Composed for the controversy at Ephesus (Honigmann), or an older long-sleeper tale taken up as a resurrection sign.
  • Does the Qur’an’s account of the Companions of the Cave (18:9–26) draw on the Christian Seven Sleepers legend? A retelling of the Christian legend, or an account given by revelation.
