The Seven Sleepers of Ephesus

Seven young Christians of Ephesus, so the story runs, were walled up alive in a cave for refusing to sacrifice during the persecution of the emperor Decius (c. 250) — and did not die, but slept, waking almost two centuries later under Theodosius II to find the empire become Christian, gave their witness to the resurrection of the body, and died. It is one of the best-travelled legends of late antiquity: told first in Syriac and Greek in the fifth and sixth centuries, carried into the Latin West by Gregory of Tours, retold in the Qur’an as the Aṣḥāb al-Kahf (“the Companions of the Cave,” Sūrat al-Kahf 18:9–26), elaborated by the Muslim commentators, fixed for the medieval West by the Golden Legend, and kept as a saints’ feast in the Roman, Byzantine, and Islamic calendars alike. Like Barlaam and Josaphat, it is a case study in how a holy-lives legend crosses languages and religions — here uniquely a story venerated by both Christians and Muslims, whose cave is still shown at Ephesus and at half a dozen other sites from Jordan to Central Asia.

This page is not a life of seven men; it is a history of a legend and of how the churches — and then Islam — came to hold it, set out earliest source first: the earliest Syriac and Greek witnesses, Gregory’s Latin, the Qur’anic retelling and its commentators, the Byzantine and Latin diffusion down to the Golden Legend, and the modern scholarship that classed the tale among the world’s wandering “sleeper” folktales and argued out where it began. Its “witnesses” are the datable sources that carry the story, ordered by the age of the source — not a claimed chronology of the events they narrate.

Timeline

The earliest witnesses: the Syriac Story and Jacob of Serugh (5th–early 6th c.)

The legend surfaces first in the Christian East. A Syriac prose Story of the Youths of Ephesus circulated by the later fifth century, and the West-Syrian poet Jacob of Serugh (c. 451–521) devoted to it a verse homily (mēmrā) — the earliest datable literary treatment we can put a name and a death-date to. In it the sleepers are the church’s own standing proof of the resurrection of the body: God “sealed their senses” and kept them whole in the cave so that, waking, they might refute those who denied that the dead rise. Whether the very first written form was Syriac or a now-lost Greek text of Ephesus itself is disputed; either way the tale is already fully formed a century before Gregory of Tours.1

Gregory of Tours brings the tale to the Latin West (573–594)

Gregory of Tours (538–594) gave the West its first Latin form. He tells the story briefly in his Glory of the Martyrs (ch. 94) and at length in a Passio sanctorum septem dormientium apud Ephesum, and he is candid about his source: the fuller account, he says, was one “which, interpreted by a certain Syrian, I translated into Latin” (Siro quodam interpretante in Latino transtulimus) — the colophon of the Passio names the interpreter John the Syrian (interpretante Iohanne Syro). Here the Western names settle into their familiar form — Maximianus, Malchus, Martinianus, Constantinus, Dionysius, John, and Serapion — and from Gregory the legend passed into Paul the Deacon’s History of the Lombards and the mainstream of Western hagiography.2

The Qur’an: the Companions of the Cave (early 7th c.)

Within a century of Gregory the legend enters Islamic scripture. Sūrat al-Kahf (18:9–26) tells of “the companions of the cave and the inscription” — youths who believed in their Lord and fled to a cave, over whose ears God “cast a cover of sleep… for a number of years,” then “awakened them.” The Qur’anic version keeps the core of the Christian tale — faithful youths, the sheltering cave, the miraculous sleep, the waking as a sign that “the promise of God is truth” and the Hour real — while stripping the persecutor’s name and the Ephesian setting; it adds the sleepers’ dog stretched across the threshold (v. 18), notes an old dispute over their number (“three… five… seven, and the eighth of them was their dog,” v. 22), and gives their term as “three hundred years, and exceeded by nine” (v. 25). That a datable seventh-century text should carry the fifth-century Christian legend is the clearest measure of how far and fast it travelled; whether the Qur’an drew on the Christian story is weighed below.3

The Islamic elaboration: the commentators, the dog, and the number (8th–10th c.)

Because the Qur’an tells the tale allusively, the Muslim commentators (tafsīr) — al-Ṭabarī above all — filled it out from the surrounding lore: they named the persecuting king (Decianus/Daqyānūs), gave the dog the name Qiṭmīr and counted it among the beasts admitted to Paradise, and read the Qur’anic caution about the number (v. 22) as settling on seven. The three hundred and nine years were harmonized as 300 solar and 309 lunar. The sleepers became figures of Muslim piety and protection — their names worn as amulets, Qiṭmīr inscribed for a blessing — and rival caves were shown at Ephesus, at ar-Raqīm near Amman in Jordan, and elsewhere.4

The Byzantine and Latin diffusion, down to the Golden Legend (8th–13th c.)

In Byzantium the earliest surviving Greek witness is a bare chronicle notice — Theophanes the Confessor (Chronographia, c. 810–814) enters the “remarkable miracle of the seven holy youths who arose after 184 years” in his account of the reign of Theodosius II, assuming the story already known, and George the Monk repeats it in the same century. Only later was the tale given its polished narrative form by Symeon Metaphrastes in his tenth-century menologion, and kept in the synaxaria (4 August; the “second sleep” on 22 October). That the oldest Greek text we possess is three centuries younger than the Syriac bears directly on the priority question below. In the West the legend descended through Paul the Deacon into the great medieval collections, an Anglo-Norman poem (Chardry’s Li set dormanz) and an Old Norse fragment, and at last into Jacobus de Voragine’s Golden Legend (c. 1260), the book that fixed the story for late-medieval Europe. Voragine dates their waking to “the year of our Lord four hundred and seventy-eight” and reports the tradition that they slept 362 years — then, tellingly, does the arithmetic himself and admits it will not close: “so they slept but two hundred and eight years.” The legend was by now a standard saints’ life, read across Europe.5

The calendars: veneration as saints (medieval)

The seven were commemorated as martyr-saints across the churches: the Roman Martyrology keeps them on 27 July, the Byzantine calendar on 4 August (and again, with the tradition of their “second sleep,” on 22 October), and they hold a place in the Islamic devotional calendar as well — a single set of holy figures honoured, under their two sets of names, in three religious traditions at once.6

Modern scholarship: the wandering folktale traced, the origins argued (16th c. → today)

Doubt about the story’s history is itself old: Baronius, editing the Roman Martyrology, already questioned it in the sixteenth century. Modern scholarship placed the Ephesus legend among the world’s wandering “sleeper” tales — kin to the Jewish Ḥoni/Abimelech and the Greek Epimenides, and later to Rip Van Winkle — and argued out its literary origin: John Koch (1883) and Michael Huber (1910) mapped its diffusion, while Ernest Honigmann (1953) tied the first Greek account to Stephen, bishop of Ephesus (448–451), against those (Guidi, Nöldeke) who held the Syriac form the earliest. Louis Massignon (1954–1963) catalogued the whole Christian-and-Islamic cult as a rare living “trait d’union” — a hyphen — between the two faiths. Whether any of it happened is weighed below.7

Arguments

Claims adduced about the legend, weighed adversarially and cutting across the timeline above.

Related arguments — al-Raqīm and the sleepers’ cave

Two questions cluster around a single Qur’anic word and the places it has been made to name. Sūrat al-Kahf pairs “the companions of the cave” with al-raqīm (18:9) — but is that word “the inscription” or a place-name? And does any physical cave — the long-shown grotto at Ephesus, the excavated site at ar-Raqīm near Amman, or another — have a real claim to be the sleepers’ own? The philology feeds the archaeology (a place-name reading is what pins the legend to a spot), so the two are best read together, each weighed on its own page.

What is al-raqīm (Qur’an 18:9) — “the inscription” or the place-name ar-Raqīm? — contested

A lexical crux over a word used only here in the Qur’an and opaque from the start (Ibn ʿAbbās listed it among words he could not explain). The reading “the inscription/tablet” has the stronger morphology (the root r-q-m means “to write,” and the sura’s own kitāb marqūm fixes the sense), was al-Ṭabarī’s own choice, and matches the sealed leaden name-tablet of the Christian legend. But a place-name reading is attested from the earliest commentary and, in Mehdy Shaddel’s identification of al-raqīm with Petra (Nabataean Raqmū), now has a serious epigraphic case; neither pole has closed the other off, and Bellamy’s emendation to al-ruqūd is the weak end (contested).

Does any site — Ephesus, ar-Raqīm near Amman, or another — have a real claim to be the cave of the sleepers? — weak

No surviving cave can be shown to be the sleepers’ own. The Ephesus complex is securely excavated and fixes the cult at a mid-fifth-century church — but built over an older (1st–2nd c.) necropolis, so it anchors the veneration, not a miracle; the Jordanian ar-Raqīm/al-Rajīb site (excavated by al-Dajānī, 1963) is a genuine Byzantine shrine but its identification rests on a place-name pun over a contested word and a common tomb-type; and the dozen rival caves from Anatolia to China are the tell — localizations bred by the Qur’an’s silence about place (weak).

Related arguments — the recensions and their transmission

The legend survives in over two hundred manuscripts across a dozen languages, and two questions about how those forms relate are best read together. One is settled, one is open. The shape of the tree — that the surviving Greek and Latin forms descend through independent branches from a common Eastern archetype — is not really in dispute, and the two divergent name-rosters are one of its clearest signs. Where that tree is rooted — in the Syriac, or in a now-lost Greek original of Ephesus — is the genuinely contested crux.

Do the two divergent name-rosters reveal independent Greek and Latin transmission from a common archetype? — sound

The Greek Passion names the sleepers Maximillian, Jamblichos, Martin, John, Dionysios, Exakostodianos, and Antoninos; Gregory’s Latin names them Maximianus, Malchus, Martinianus, Constantinus, Dionysius, John, and Serapion. Four of seven match or are cognate, three differ wholesale — a shared core with divergent tails, the classic signature of two lines descending independently from one source rather than one copying the other. The branching is confirmed off the names too (Gregory’s colophon has him translating from a Syrian interpreter, not the Greek; the Arabic names descend from the Greek set, not the Latin). The interpretation is sound; the caveat is only that the rosters themselves are a noisy fine-grained tool — names drift within a tradition as well as between traditions — so they corroborate a branching established on firmer ground rather than proving it alone.

Which came first — the Syriac Story or a lost Greek account of Ephesus? — contested

A genuinely open question of literary history: Syriac priority (Guidi, Nöldeke), resting on the oldest surviving datable texts, versus a lost Greek original written at Ephesus itself (Huber, Peeters, Honigmann, who ties it to Stephen of Ephesus), resting on the Ephesian, Greek-speaking setting. Each side wins on one measure and cannot dispose of the other’s; what is not in doubt is that the legend was fully formed in the Christian East a century before it reached the West (contested).

Other arguments

  • Was the legend composed as a purpose-built icon for the fifth-century resurrection controversy? — plausible. Its earliest datable voice — Jacob of Serugh’s homily — builds the whole miracle to a doctrinal end: God kept the youths whole in the cave so that, waking bodily after two centuries, they would refute those who deny the dead rise. That the legend’s earliest function is a resurrection-of-the-body apologetic is near-certain; the contested step is whether the tale was composed for the fifth-century dispute (Honigmann’s Stephen of Ephesus, 448–451) or is an older “long-sleeper” folktale adopted and reframed as a resurrection-icon. The moderate form (decisively shaped for the debate) is well grounded; the strong form (minted from nothing for it) outruns the evidence and leans on the contested Greek-priority dating. It ties the legend to the wiki’s eschatology cluster on the fate of the body (the intermediate state; the resurrection of the flesh).
  • Did the Qur’an’s Companions of the Cave (18:9–26) draw on the Christian Seven Sleepers legend? — sound. The Christian legend is attested in Syriac and Greek a century and more before the Qur’an, and travelled in exactly the milieu (Syriac Christianity of the Near East) from which the Qur’an drew much of its narrative material; the shared, specific core — persecuted youths, the cave, the God-sent sleep of “a number of years,” the waking as a resurrection-sign — makes a common origin certain. The direction of dependence runs from the older Christian tale to the Qur’anic retelling, which reshapes it to its own theology (the persecutor and the city drop away, the sign of the resurrection is foregrounded) and folds in the surrounding lore of the dog and the disputed number. This says nothing about whether either version is historically true — only that they are one story.
  • Did the Seven Sleepers really fall asleep under Decius and wake two centuries later? — weak. Not because a miracle is involved — a wonder is not disproved by being a wonder — but because the evidence for this one is thin and late: no contemporary notice, a first witness (Syriac / Stephen of Ephesus) some two centuries after the supposed event, names, numbers, and dates that vary freely from recension to recension, and a plot that is the stock pattern of a widespread “long-sleeper” folktale told of many heroes in many cultures. Its early function was frankly didactic — a living icon of the bodily resurrection produced amid a fifth-century dispute over it — which is where its force lies; as a report of a datable event it does not stand.
