Who Was Rahab of Jericho?

Rahab is the woman of Jericho who sheltered the two Israelite spies and was spared, with her household, when the city fell. Joshua’s men “came into an harlot’s house, named Rahab, and lodged there” (Joshua 2:1). When the king’s men search for them she hides them under the flax on her roof, sends the pursuers off on another road, and tells the spies: “I know that the LORD hath given you the land… for the LORD your God, he is God in heaven above, and in earth beneath” (Joshua 2:9–11). She strikes a bargain for her family, lets the spies down by a cord through her window, and binds in that window the “line of scarlet thread” by which her house will be spared (Joshua 2:18–21). When Jericho is put to the ban, “only Rahab the harlot shall live, she and all that are with her in the house” (Joshua 6:17); Joshua saves her alive, “and she dwelleth in Israel even unto this day” (Joshua 6:25).

The New Testament names her three times. Matthew sets her in the genealogy of Christ, “Salmon begat Booz of Rachab” (Matthew 1:5), one of the four women he names in the line. Hebrews lists her among the examples of faith: “By faith the harlot Rahab perished not with them that believed not, when she had received the spies with peace” (Hebrews 11:31). James sets her beside Abraham as an example of justification by works: “Likewise also was not Rahab the harlot justified by works, when she had received the messengers?” (James 2:25). Among the Fathers on this timeline, Clement of Rome, Justin, Origen, Jerome and Theodoret read her scarlet cord as a sign of the blood of Christ, and Origen, Cyprian, Chrysostom and Jerome read her, or her house, as a type of the church. Josephus, the Targum and Rashi describe her as the keeper of an inn, and the Babylonian Talmud records that she converted, married Joshua and was the ancestress of priests and prophets.

This page is a biographical tradition-timeline: not a life of Rahab but a record of what was said and believed about her across the centuries, earliest extant source first. Its “witnesses” are the datable sources that report on or interpret her: the narrative of Joshua and its three New Testament notices, Josephus and the rabbinic tradition, and Clement of Rome, Justin, Irenaeus, Origen, Cyprian, Cyril of Jerusalem, Chrysostom, Jerome, Theodoret and Bede. Whether she married Joshua is set out under Disputed readings. Other figures in Scripture who are foreigners confessing the God of Israel have timelines of their own: the Syrian commander Naaman (“there is no God in all the earth, but in Israel”), the Midianite priest Jethro (“now I know that the LORD is greater than all gods”), and the Queen of Sheba (who comes “from the uttermost parts of the earth” and blesses the LORD).

Timeline

The biblical portrait (Joshua 2 and 6)

Later readings of Rahab draw on two chapters of Joshua. She is introduced by her trade: the spies “came into an harlot’s house, named Rahab.” When Jericho’s king sends to seize the men, Rahab hides them under the flax drying on her roof and turns the search aside (“there came men unto me, but I wist not whence they were… whither the men went, I wot not”). Then she tells the spies: “I know that the LORD hath given you the land… for the LORD your God, he is God in heaven above, and in earth beneath.” She has heard how the LORD dried up the Red Sea and destroyed Sihon and Og, and says that the hearts of her people have melted. She binds her house to the spies by an oath, lets them down by a cord through her window, and hangs in that window the “line of scarlet thread” that is to mark her house when the city is taken.1

At the ban on Jericho she is the one exception named: “only Rahab the harlot shall live, she and all that are with her in the house, because she hid the messengers.” Joshua sends the same two spies to bring her out with “her father, and her mother, and her brethren, and all that she had.” The narrative closes: “Joshua saved Rahab the harlot alive… and she dwelleth in Israel even unto this day.”2

The New Testament: faith, works, and the genealogy of Christ (c. AD 60–90)

The New Testament takes up Rahab three times. Matthew sets her in the genealogy of the Messiah, “Salmon begat Booz of Rachab,” one of the four women (with Tamar, Ruth, and “her that had been the wife of Urias”) he names in the royal line, four generations above David. Hebrews lists her among the examples of faith, immediately after the fall of the walls: “By faith the harlot Rahab perished not with them that believed not, when she had received the spies with peace.” James makes her the second of his two examples (after Abraham) of justification by works: “Likewise also was not Rahab the harlot justified by works, when she had received the messengers, and had sent them out another way?” Hebrews and James both call her “the harlot” (Greek hē pornē).3

Josephus (Antiquities of the Jews 5.1.2, c. AD 93) — Rahab as the keeper of an inn

The earliest extant post-biblical retelling of the episode is Josephus’s, written in Greek for a Greco-Roman readership. In it the spies retire to “a certain inn,” and Rahab is its keeper: “they were in the inn kept by Rahab.” She hides the men, is spared with her family “who had fled to her inn,” and Joshua “gave her certain lands immediately, and had her in great esteem ever afterwards.” The Greek word for her house is katagōgion, a lodging; no word for harlot appears in the passage. The Targum and Rashi also describe her as an innkeeper (see the rabbinic tradition below).4 Full detail »

Clement of Rome (First Epistle to the Corinthians 12, c. AD 96) — the scarlet thread as a prophecy of the blood of the Lord

Clement’s letter to Corinth, among the earliest surviving Christian writings outside the New Testament, praises Rahab, “on account of her faith and hospitality, Rahab the harlot was saved,” and reads the scarlet thread as a prophecy: the spies gave her the sign of a scarlet thread, “and thus they made it manifest that redemption should flow through the blood of the Lord to all them that believe and hope in God.” He concludes: “You see, beloved, that there was not only faith, but prophecy, in this woman.” For Clement the scarlet cord is a foreshadowing of the blood of Christ, not only a token of rescue.5 Full detail »

Justin Martyr (Dialogue with Trypho 111, c. AD 160) — the scarlet thread as the symbol of the blood of Christ

Arguing from the Scriptures with Trypho, a Jew, Justin writes that the scarlet thread the spies gave Rahab “manifested the symbol of the blood of Christ, by which those who were at one time harlots and unrighteous persons out of all nations are saved, receiving remission of sins, and continuing no longer in sin.” With “out of all nations” Rahab becomes a figure of the saved sinner from among the Gentiles, and Justin presents the reading as a proof drawn from the Hebrew Scriptures.6 Full detail »

Irenaeus of Lyons (Against Heresies IV.20.12, c. AD 180) — the three spies and the scarlet sign

Irenaeus places Rahab within his argument that one God speaks across both Testaments. Rahab the harlot, “a Gentile, guilty of all sins,” received the three spies, “doubtless [a type of] the Father and the Son, together with the Holy Spirit,” and when the city fell at the seven trumpets she “was preserved… together with all her house, through faith of the scarlet sign.” He ties the thread to the Passover and the exodus, and joins it to the Lord’s word to the unbelieving: “The publicans and the harlots go into the kingdom of heaven before you.” Jerome (c. 400) reads the spies differently, as sent to Jew and Gentile.7 Full detail »

Origen of Alexandria (Homilies on Joshua 3, 6, 7, c. AD 240) — Rahab the Gentile church, outside whose house none is saved

In Origen’s homilies the scarlet sign is the blood of Christ: “no other sign would have been accepted, except the scarlet-colored one that carried the sign of blood… there was no salvation for anyone except in the blood of Christ.” Rahab’s house is the church: “if anyone wants to be saved, let him come into the house of this one who was once a prostitute… Outside this house, that is, outside the church, no one is saved.” And the harlot is the bride: “once she was a prostitute, but now ‘a pure virgin, to one man’ she has been united, ‘to Christ.’” The homilies survive in Rufinus’s Latin translation; the Greek is lost.8 Full detail »

Cyprian of Carthage (Epistle 75, c. AD 255) — Rahab’s house as the one Church

Cyprian uses the figure in his controversy over the unity of the Church. Rahab “bore a type of the Church”: she was told to gather her father, mother, and kindred into her house, “and whosoever shall go out of the doors of thine house into the street, his blood shall be upon him,” and so “they who will live, and escape from the destruction of the world, must be gathered together into one house alone, that is, into the Church.” Origen (c. 240) had likewise read the house as the church.9 Full detail »

Cyril of Jerusalem (Catechetical Lectures 2.9, c. AD 350) — Rahab as an example of salvation by repentance

Instructing catechumens, Cyril offers Rahab as an example of repentance: “Fix your eyes, woman, upon Rahab, and look for salvation for yourself too. For if she who openly and publicly practiced fornication was saved through repentance…” He comments on the wording of her confession: she said only “the Lord, your God, is God in heaven above and on earth below,” “your God,” not daring to call him “her God, because of her wantonness.” Cyril uses Rahab as an example for the penitent rather than as a figure of the church.10 Full detail »

John Chrysostom (homilies on Repentance, Matthew, and Hebrews, c. AD 390–407) — the pearl in the mire

Chrysostom returns to Rahab in several homilies. She is “a pearl mixed up in mire, like gold thrown in mud, the rose of piety hidden in thorns”; her confession is faith taken “on her lips”; and she “is a prefigurement of the church, which was at one time mixed up in the prostitution of the demons and which now accepts the spies of Christ.” On her place in the genealogy he writes that Matthew named “Ruth also and Rahab, the one an alien, the other an harlot,” so that “thou mayest learn that He came to do away with all our ills. For He hath come as a Physician, not as a Judge.” To his hearers he holds her up as a rebuke: it would be “disgraceful, if you should appear more faithless even than a harlot.”11 Full detail »

Jerome (Letter 52 and the Homilies on the Psalms, c. AD 400) — the scarlet cord and the church of the Gentiles

Jerome writes of Rahab in Latin. The scarlet cord is “a mystic reference to the shedding of blood” which “the harlot Rahab (a type of the church) hung in her window that she might be saved.” The two spies are the mission to Jew and Gentile, “one to the circumcised; the other to the Gentiles, Peter and Paul,” so the harlot who takes them in is “the church gathered together from the Gentiles.” Of her name he says that it means “a broad space” or “pride,” and that she who “walked the broad, spacious road to death… was later converted unto humility.”12 Full detail »

Theodoret of Cyrus (On Divine Providence and the Interpretation of Hebrews, c. AD 450) — the foreign woman among the saints of faith

Theodoret presents Rahab as a witness that faith, not descent, saves. A foreigner and a prostitute, she “relied solely on their reputation, accepted their religion, abandoned her own beliefs”; her “lawlessness of life did not prevent salvation; faith covered the wounds of sin.” He writes that the Spirit set “a foreign woman and prostitute” beside “Moses, Abraham, Noah, Enoch, and the other saints… to establish the power of faith and repress the Jews’ conceit,” and he keeps the figure of the cord: “as she was given the scarlet cord as a sign of salvation, so the church through the Lord’s blood enjoyed the eternal goods.”13 Full detail »

The rabbinic tradition (Targum Jonathan and the Babylonian Talmud; traditions from c. AD 200, redacted by c. 500) — the innkeeper and the proselyte who married Joshua

The Targum of the Prophets renders the Hebrew zonah of Joshua 2:1 as pundeqita, an “innkeeper,” and Rashi (c. 1075) follows it (“a lady innkeeper… who sold various kinds of foodstuff”); Josephus had already called her house an inn. Where Scripture leaves her “dwelling in Israel,” the Babylonian Talmud records, in the words of Rav Naḥman, that Rahab “converted and married Joshua,” and, from a baraita, that from her descended “eight prophets who were also priests… Neriah, Baruch, Seraiah… Jeremiah… Hilkiah,” with “Huldah the prophetess” added by Rabbi Yehuda. The Talmud also numbers her among the four women of surpassing beauty in the world (Sarah, Abigail, Rahab, Esther), and says that the mention of her name alone aroused desire.14 Full detail »

The Venerable Bede (Commentary on the Catholic Epistles, on James 2:25, c. AD 710) — justified by works, written into the genealogy

Bede, commenting on James, joins the faith of Hebrews and the works of James. He asks “was not Rahab the harlot also justified by works when she received the messengers?” and answers with her reward: “a sinful woman, a foreign woman, who nevertheless by acts of mercy, by the duty of hospitality, even at the risk of her life, received the servants of God… was deemed worthy to be justified from sins, to be numbered among the citizens of the people of Israel… to be inserted in the genealogies of our Lord and Saviour.” Bede ties her faith, her works and her place in Christ’s line together.15 Full detail »

Disputed readings

How the sources assign Rahab a husband and descendants.

  • Did Rahab marry Joshua and become the ancestress of priests and prophets? — the Talmud’s account (Megillah 14b) beside Matthew’s naming of Salmon (Matthew 1:5).
