What kind of peace did Jerome and Rufinus make at the Holy Sepulchre at Easter 397?

Disputed reading · on The Origenist Controversy (Jerome and Rufinus) · witnesses: Rufinus and Jerome

The passage

Far be such conduct from these reverend persons. It is from your school that such actions proceed. You gave us all the signs of peace at our departure, and then threw missiles charged with venom from behind our backs.

— Rufinus, in a now-lost private letter, quoted by Jerome, Apology against Rufinus, III.24, with Jerome's answer in the same section:

It is true we gave you the signs of peace, but not to embrace heresy; we joined hands, we accompanied you as you set forth on your journey, on the understanding that you were catholic not that we were heretical

— Jerome, Apology against Rufinus, III.24, source.1

The question

At Easter 397, before Rufinus sailed for Italy, he and Jerome joined hands at the Church of the Resurrection; W. H. Fremantle's NPNF account describes the scene and records the influence of Theophilus of Alexandria and of Melania on the wider settlement, and F. Prat (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1911) records that the reconciliation “was not lasting.”2 The two men later described the parting differently: as a settled peace that the other side broke, or as a peace given on a doctrinal condition. The answer bears on how each assigned the blame for the quarrel that reopened in 398.

A settled peace, broken by Jerome's party

On this reading the hand-joining was a full reconciliation, and the men later sent from Bethlehem to Rome broke it.

Held by: Rufinus (private letter to Jerome, c. 402, as quoted by Jerome, Apology against Rufinus III.24); the NPNF editor (headnote to Jerome's Letter 81), on the suppression of that letter only.1

The peace was made in the most solemn setting available: in Fremantle's account, “the two old companions who had been so long parted by ecclesiastical strife” met “at the Church of the Resurrection at a solemn eucharistic feast, and joined hands in token of reconciliation,” and “Jerome accompanied his friend some way on his journey before their final parting.”2 Jerome records the same acts — “we joined hands, we accompanied you as you set forth on your journey” — and says of the moment, “When we parted as friends I believed you a true believer.” On Rufinus's account, a peace so given bound both parties. After the first exchanges over Origen, Jerome wrote Rufinus a private letter (Letter 81) protesting that when reconciled to a man he did not “while offering him bread with one hand, hold a stone in the other”; the NPNF editor's headnote holds that Pammachius, through whom it was sent, “treacherously suppressed it and thus put an end to all hope of the reconciliation of the two friends.”3 What followed the peace was, for Rufinus, its breach from the Bethlehem side: “You gave us all the signs of peace at our departure, and then threw missiles charged with venom from behind our backs.” The men of Jerome's circle who came to Rome and carried his uncorrected Peri Archon through Italy are, on this reading, the missiles, and the peace itself had been whole.1

A peace given on condition of Rufinus's orthodoxy

On this reading the hand-joining was sincere but conditional, and Rufinus's own later translation forfeited it.

Held by: Jerome (Apology against Rufinus III.24, 402/403).1

Jerome grants the gestures and gives their terms: “we gave you the signs of peace, but not to embrace heresy,” and the hands were joined “on the understanding that you were catholic not that we were heretical.” The personal reconciliation came in the wake of a wider church settlement between the Bethlehem monastery and Bishop John of Jerusalem, a settlement in which Fremantle records the influence of Theophilus and Melania, after a quarrel in which the two men had stood on opposite sides over Origen.2 On Jerome's account the condition was broken by Rufinus. Late in 397 Rufinus's preface to his Latin Peri Archon cast Jerome as the translator who had already “smoothed down” Origen, and in 398 Jerome answered with a literal version and his Letter 84.4 As for the men said to have been sent after Rufinus, Jerome gives their dates: “Vincentius went to Rome long before you; Paulinianus and Eusebius set out a year after you had sailed,” and they went on their own business “before you had committed the crime.” His summary is that “no one was sent to Rome to injure you.” Letter 81 fits this reading as well: in 398 Jerome was still offering peace on the same terms, so that the peace of Easter 397 stood for him as long as its condition held.1
