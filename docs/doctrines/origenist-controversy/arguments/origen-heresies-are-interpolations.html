Were the passages charged against Origen inserted by others, or his own teaching? (Rufinus's De Adulteratione)

Disputed reading · on The Origenist Controversy (Jerome and Rufinus) · witness: Rufinus

Related readings: Was Rufinus's draft Peri Archon altered before it was read against him in Italy?

An argument of the same form was made about another author on the condemnation timeline: Germanus of Constantinople argued that the restoration passages in Gregory of Nyssa were Origenist insertions. That case is set out on its own page.

The passage

Whenever they found in any of the renowned writers of old days a discussion of those things which pertain to the glory of God so full and faithful that every believer could gain profit and instruction from it, they have not scrupled to infuse into their writings the poisonous taint of their own false doctrines … either by inserting things which the writers had not said or by changing by interpolation what they had said … they meant it to appear that well-known and orthodox men had held as they did.

— Rufinus, On the Falsification of the Works of Origen, source.1 Rufinus drew the translator's rule from it in his preface to the Peri Archon: “Wherever therefore I have found in his books anything contrary to the truth concerning the Trinity which he has in other places spoken of in a strictly orthodox sense, I have either omitted it as a foreign and not genuine expression or set it down in terms agreeing with the rule of faith.”2

The question

In 397 Rufinus appended to his Latin version of Pamphilus's Apology for Origen a short treatise, On the Falsification of the Works of Origen (the De Adulteratione), holding that the passages on the Trinity, the soul and the end that were charged against Origen had been inserted by others. What is disputed is whether those passages are insertions or Origen's own teaching; the answer decides whether Rufinus's pruning recovered Origen's text or changed it. A related question, how far Rufinus's Latin can be trusted where the Greek is lost, divides modern editors: Paul Koetschau's edition (1913) distrusted it and set excerpts from Origen's opponents into the text, while John Behr's edition (2017) is built on Rufinus's Latin and keeps the opponents' excerpts apart.3

Heretics corrupted Origen's text

On this reading the passages that contradict Origen's statements elsewhere were inserted by heretics, and a translator who removes them restores the genuine Origen.

Held by: Rufinus (De Adulteratione and preface to the Peri Archon, 397).4

Rufinus's first ground is Origen's own testimony. He quotes a letter Origen wrote “to certain intimate friends at Alexandria” complaining that an opponent had taken the notes of a public disputation and “added or struck out whatever he chose, and changed things as he thought right, and published it abroad as if it were my work.” Origen compares his fate to the forged letter that alarmed the Thessalonians and to Marcion's mutilation of the Gospels.4 His second ground is internal: a learned and self-consistent author does not contradict himself in “successive sentences,” so where such contradictions appear, another hand has been at work. His third is precedent, and the treatise is a dossier of cases: the Clementine Recognitions, into which “the heresy of Eunomius” had been worked; Clement of Alexandria and Dionysius of Alexandria, under whose names Arianizing sentences circulated, so that Athanasius wrote a defence of Dionysius; Hilary of Poitiers, whose book was falsified by his enemies, his secretary bribed, so that he was excommunicated on words inserted into his own manuscript; the letters of Cyprian, into which a treatise of Tertullian on the Trinity was slipped at Constantinople; and, from “recent memory,” the erasure and rewriting of a book of Athanasius in the affair of a creed drafted for Pope Damasus, which the NPNF note identifies as drafted by Jerome.1 The NPNF editor adds that in a hand-copied world “there was room for infinite variations in the copies, whether through negligence, ignorance or fraud,” and that the principles Rufinus adopted in translating “were precisely those acknowledged by his great opponent Jerome” in De Optimo Genere Interpretandi.5 On this footing Rufinus held that what readers would not grant to a heretic — that his errors were someone else's insertions — they should grant to Origen, whose own words attested the tampering, and that a translator who set such passages “in terms agreeing with the rule of faith,” adding only “words which I had read in other books of his,” gave Latin readers Origen as he was.

The doctrines are Origen's own

On this reading the pre-existence of souls, the subordination of the Son and the restoration of all things belong to Origen's own system, and Rufinus's pruning softened his author.

Held by: Jerome (Apology against Rufinus II, 402); Paul Koetschau (ed., De principiis, 1913).6

Jerome's case is structural. A rule that assigns every offensive line to an unknown corrupter can clear any author of anything:

If, I reply, we admit that everything in a book which is offensive is corruptly inserted by others, nothing will remain belonging to the author under whose name the book passes, but everything can be assigned to those by whom it is supposed to have been corrupted.

Applied to Rufinus's own labour, Jerome holds, the rule yields the opposite result: “what you cut out was not due to the heretics, but to Origen himself, and … you translated the bad things he had written because you considered them good.” Rufinus's preface, on this reading, describes not deletion of intrusions but rewriting, since he set passages “down in terms agreeing with the rule of faith” and supplied words from elsewhere; and Jerome made his own literal version so that Latin readers could see what the Greek said.7 The doctrines at issue recur, on this reading, across works whose Greek is not in doubt, which makes them load-bearing parts of Origen's cosmology rather than stray sentences (no named holder of this argument located yet ⚠ unverified). Where the Peri Archon survives in Greek (fragments of Books 3 and 4 in the Philocalia, quotations in Justinian's Letter to Mennas) and in Jerome's literal fragments in his Letter 124 to Avitus, Rufinus's Latin can be compared, and on this reading it shows Origen's thought toned toward orthodoxy rather than a graft cut away (likewise ⚠ unverified). Koetschau's edition acted on that view: in a review's summary of Behr, Koetschau held that Rufinus “cannot be trusted at all,” and he printed excerpts from Origen's opponents in the running text as Origen's words.3
