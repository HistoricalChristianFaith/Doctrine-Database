Does Jerome's Commentary on Ephesians (c. 386) teach Origen's doctrines of pre-existent souls and universal restoration, or report them?

Disputed reading · on The Origenist Controversy (Jerome and Rufinus) · witness: Jerome

The passage

Another, who wishes to vindicate the justice of God, and to shew that he does not choose men according to a prejudgment and foreknowledge of his own but according to the deserts of the elect, thinks that before the visible creation of sky, earth, sea and all that is in them, there existed the invisible creation, part of which consisted of souls, which, for certain causes known to God alone, were cast down into this valley of tears, this scene of our affliction and our pilgrimage.

— Jerome, Commentary on Ephesians (on 1:4), as quoted and owned by Jerome, Apology against Rufinus, I.22, source.1

The question

Jerome's Commentary on Ephesians, written about 386, sets out readings of Ephesians 1:4, 1:12 and 5:28–29 that speak of the pre-existence and fall of souls and the restoration of all things, several of them in the voice of an unnamed “another” (alius). In 400 Rufinus argued that these were Jerome's own teaching; Jerome answered that a commentator reports the opinions of others. The question bears on whether Jerome's opposition to Origen's doctrines after 393 continued or changed his earlier position.

Readings reported by a commentator

On this reading Jerome relayed Origen's interpretations as a commentator must, and recorded his own different view beside them.

Held by: Jerome (Letter 84, 398; Apology against Rufinus I, 402).2

Jerome's formula for his relation to Origen is that “I have praised the commentator but not the theologian, the man of intellect but not the believer.” He grounds it in the genre. A commentary's task “is to interpret another man's words … it enumerates the opinions of many persons, and says, Some interpret the passage in this sense, some in that … Is the commentator to be held responsible for all these different interpretations … because he puts down the expositions given by many?” His preface to the Ephesians commentary had said that he culled from Origen, Didymus and Apollinarius, and would mark “how far the work is due to me, how far to others.” Jerome points to his own conclusions in the text. On Eph. 1:4 he first gives the reading from foreknowledge and closes against pre-existence: “the Apostle does not say ‘He chose us … because we were then holy,’ but ‘that we might be holy.’” Of the unnamed voice he notes the wording he chose: “I said ‘Another who wishes to vindicate,’ I did not say ‘who succeeds.’” On the restoration passage he frames the restoration of all things as “another heresy,” two heresies compared rather than one professed. Jerome sets the commentary in its time: “It is now almost eighteen years since I composed those books, at a time when the name of Origen was highly esteemed in the world, and when as yet his work the Peri Archon had not reached the ears of the Latins: and yet I distinctly stated my belief and pointed out what I did not agree with.” On this reading the commentary shows an exegete using Origen's learning and marking his own dissent, the same relation to Origen that Jerome described in 398.3

Origen's doctrines in Jerome's own voice

On this reading the “another” of the commentary is Jerome himself, and the young Jerome taught what he later condemned.

Held by: Rufinus (Apology against Jerome, 400); Elizabeth A. Clark (Vigiliae Christianae, 1987), in a qualified form.4

Rufinus turned Jerome's own proof-text against him:

You made an exception in favour of the Commentaries on the Ephesians, in which you imagined that you had written more correctly. But even you must have seen … how like they are all through to Origen's views; and, indeed, how they contain something more extreme than the views of which you demand the condemnation.

He quoted the commentary passage by passage. On the resurrection body (on Eph. 5:28–29) he reads it as pressing toward the dissolution of flesh and sex:

Let us men then cherish our wives and let our souls cherish our bodies in such a way as that the wives may be turned into men and the bodies into spirits … so we who are to be like the angels may begin here to be what it is promised that we shall be in heaven.

On the restitution of all things it says that “the apostate angel shall begin to be that which he was by his creation, and that man who had been cast out of paradise shall be restored again to the cultivation of paradise.” Rufinus's sharpest point is at Eph. 1:12 (“we who had before hoped in Christ,” proêlpikotas), where Jerome explains the word “according to the same ideas which we argued in a former place to be necessary” and then introduces “another, who does not admit” a previous existence. Here, Rufinus argues, Jerome speaks the pre-existence reading in his own person, and the dilemma follows: if the “another” who teaches it is a heretic, Jerome has condemned himself; if a catholic, the doctrine is catholic.5 Clark reads the same text as showing that Jerome dissociated himself so weakly that one “would be hard-pressed to decide if he considered Origen's views mistaken at all,” and places his vehement rejection of the apokatastasis after 393, when he “wished to extricate himself from any connection with Origen's theology.”4
