Was Rufinus's draft Peri Archon altered before it was read against him in Italy?

Disputed reading · on The Origenist Controversy (Jerome and Rufinus) · witness: Rufinus

Related readings: Were the passages charged against Origen inserted by others, or his own teaching?

The passage

Well, in the place of the words which I had written, “I will at once give you my reason for it” they put the following words: “Do not dismiss my answer as if it were impious or absurd, for, as the Son does not see the Father, so the Holy Spirit also does not see the Son.”

— Rufinus, Apology against Jerome, I.19, source.1

The question

Rufinus's Latin Peri Archon, where Origen asks whether the divine nature is visible even to the Only-begotten, reads “do not dismiss my answer as if it were impious or absurd, for I will at once give you my reason for it,” and goes on to distinguish seeing (a property of bodies) from knowing. The copy recited against him at Milan in 398 read the sentence above. What is disputed is how the recited line came to be in that copy: whether the uncorrected draft was altered by his opponents, or whether it reflected Origen's own sentence as it stood before Rufinus's revision. The clause is in no manuscript of Rufinus's Latin; where Koetschau's edition prints it at I.1.8 it is the editor's insertion, taken from Jerome. Elizabeth A. Clark (1992) relates the episode in Rufinus's terms without adjudicating it, and Christa Gray (2025) does not take up the charge.2

The draft was doctored by his opponents

On this reading someone in Jerome's circle obtained Rufinus's unfinished papers and substituted the disputed line before carrying them through Italy.

Held by: Rufinus (Apology against Jerome I, 400; private letter to Jerome, c. 402, as quoted by Jerome).3

Rufinus makes the charge specific and public. He reproduces both readings, states where the substitution falls, and appeals to two men who had the papers in hand, Apronianus and Macarius: “both of you read my papers themselves at the first, even before they had been completed, and you have by you the completely corrected copies. You can bear witness to what I say.” He anathematizes the imputed opinion twice over, and argues that the same four books treat the Trinity “almost as much as one in each page,” so that one paragraph cannot outweigh the thousand others.1 The conduct of the accuser, on this reading, fits the charge. The two men were living as neighbours in Rome and “joined together in prayer,” yet the papers went “to private houses, to ladies, to monasteries, to Christian men one by one,” produced as their bearer was leaving the city “so that he might not be arraigned.” Rufinus later added that Jerome's friends had bribed his secretary to obtain papers “which no one possessed, or at least very few.”3 The means were at hand in Latin. The formula stood almost word for word in Jerome's 395 version of Epiphanius's letter to John, made at the request of Eusebius of Cremona, who knew no Greek: “The Son, he tells us, cannot see the Father, and the Holy Spirit cannot see the Son. These words occur in his book On First Principles.”4 The milieu supplied precedents: Rufinus had recorded in 397 how Hilary of Poitiers's book was falsified into heresy “whether, as some said, by bribing his secretary, or by no matter what other cause,” and Jerome in 395 called the thief of that Epiphanius translation “a second Judas,” “bribed as there is much reason to believe.”5 On Rufinus's account, an unfinished draft held by a few friends was taken, altered in a single clause, and read against its author in his absence.

An unrevised draft of Origen's own sentence

On this reading no forger was needed: the line was Origen's, a draft not yet revised would carry it, and Rufinus's revision removed it.

Held by: Jerome (Apology against Rufinus III, 402/403); Paul Koetschau (ed., De principiis, 1913), on the clause's authenticity only.6

Jerome puts the case as a dilemma: “If the papers were uncorrected and had not undergone their final revision, the errors in them must be imputed not to Eusebius but to your sloth … But if, as you assert, Eusebius falsified them, why do you put forward the allegation that they were uncorrected? For papers whether corrected or uncorrected are equally susceptible of falsification.” He defends Eusebius as “a man who only knows Latin,” holds that in a work so closely knit an insertion “must at once show out like a patch on a garment,” asks for “the secretary's name, the amount of the bribe, the place, the intermediary, the recipient,” and challenges Rufinus to “produce the genuine papers which have not been falsified.” The circulating copies, he says, “agree and coincide with one another so completely that there is not the difference of a single stroke.”7 Koetschau printed the clause as Origen's and recorded that the opponents maintained “in Graeco ita haberi.” The formula had a long pedigree as a quotation of that chapter: Epiphanius cites it in the Ancoratus (374) and the Panarion, where it is Origen's “first downfall”; Jerome's Latin of Epiphanius's letter (395) refers it to On First Principles; it heads the eight errors in Jerome's Against John of Jerusalem; and Justinian and Isidore of Seville cite it later.8 Rufinus's own words, on this reading, fit an unrevised draft. He writes that “there were some words in the Greek, which perhaps were somewhat incautiously used, and which I thought it well to avoid using,” and, to an accuser who said “that it was so in the Greek,” replies that “even supposing that the words did stand so in the Greek, and I had changed them in the Latin, this is nothing more than I had said in my Preface that I should do.” Jerome holds that at this very passage Rufinus “suppl[ied] the reasons for this, as if in the name of the writer.”9 On this reading a literal first draft of Origen's sentence, taken before revision, accounts for the recited copy.
