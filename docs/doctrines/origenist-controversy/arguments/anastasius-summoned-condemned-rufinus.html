Did Pope Anastasius I summon Rufinus to Rome and condemn him, or condemn the translation and leave Rufinus to his conscience?

Disputed reading · on The Origenist Controversy (Jerome and Rufinus) · witnesses: Anastasius I's letter to John of Jerusalem (401) and Rufinus's Apology to Anastasius (400)

The passage

I beg you to be assured of this, that he is so completely separate from all part or lot with us, that I neither know nor wish to know either what he is doing or where he is living.

— Anastasius to John of Jerusalem, concerning the Character of Rufinus, §6, source. Earlier in the same letter Anastasius writes that Rufinus “must bring his conscience to the bar of the divine majesty. It is for him to see how he can approve himself to God,” and he closes, “it is for him to consider where he may obtain absolution.”1

The question

After Anastasius I (399–401) succeeded Siricius, Jerome's Roman friends pressed the charge that Rufinus's Latin Peri Archon had brought Origen's errors into Rome; Anastasius condemned Origenism, and Rufinus, at Aquileia, sent him a written profession of faith. What is disputed is the form Rome's action against Rufinus took: whether he was summoned to appear and condemned, or whether the pope condemned Origen and the translation and left the translator to his own conscience. The Catholic Encyclopedia (1913) records only that “it was in connection with this controversy that he wrote his short Apology to Pope Anastasius.”2

A summons from Rome, answered in writing

On this reading Rome called Rufinus to come and defend himself, and his Apology was his answer in place of appearing.

Held by: Jerome (Letter 127, a memorial of Marcella written after her death); Encyclopædia Britannica (11th ed., 1911), on the summons.3

Jerome's account of Marcella's part in the Roman campaign describes a summons. She “had the volumes of the Peri Archon brought in, and pointed out the alterations” the translator had made, “till at last letters were written, and that more than once, summoning the heretics to come and defend themselves, but they did not dare to come”; and “she was the cause of the condemnation.” The 1911 Britannica states:

At the instigation of Theophilus of Alexandria, Anastasius (pope 398–402) summoned Rufinus from Aquileia to Rome to vindicate his orthodoxy; but he excused himself from a personal attendance in a written Apologia pro fide sua.

Rufinus's own Apology fits a call to appear. It opens by addressing an attack on his reputation and pleads absence: “It was impossible for me to do this in person. I have just returned to my family after an absence of nearly 30 years.”4 The pope's hostility to the translation was open. He had, at the earnest request of Theophilus, “formally condemned Origenism,” and he told John of Jerusalem that a man “who gives his encouragement to vicious acts committed by another” does not “differ at all from the guilty party.” Separating Rufinus “from all part or lot with us” is, on this reading, the sentence that followed when the summoned man did not come.1

The book condemned, the man left to his conscience

On this reading Anastasius condemned Origen and the translation but issued no summons and passed no sentence of heresy on Rufinus himself.

Held by: W. H. Fremantle (introductory note to Rufinus's Apology, NPNF² vol. 3).5

Fremantle judges that “it is improbable that Anastasius should have summoned Rufinus, as some suppose him to have done, from Aquileia, where he was living on confidential terms with the Bishop Chromatius,” and notes that “the authority of the Roman Popes at this time was not what it afterwards became.” Rufinus's letter, he writes, “bears no trace of being an answer to a summons or judgment of the Pontiff, but merely a reply to statements which were likely to prejudice him in the Pontiff's opinion.” Rufinus's opening fits that description: “since I hear it reported that my reputation has been attacked, I have thought it right to make my position clear to your Holiness in writing.”4 Anastasius's own letter draws the line this reading follows. He condemns the translation — “far be such teaching from the catholic system of the Church of Rome” — while of the translator he says that he “must bring his conscience to the bar of the divine majesty,” and that “it is for him to consider where he may obtain absolution.” A pope who writes that he neither knows nor wishes to know “where he is living” is, on this reading, describing a withdrawal of communion from a man outside his reach, not a citation to Rome. The 1911 Britannica also records that the pope, “while he condemned Origen … left the determination of Rufinus's orthodoxy to Rufinus's own conscience.”3 On this reading Rome condemned a book and left its translator, in the pope's own words, to answer to God.
