The Garden of Gethsemane

Four evangelists put the agony and the arrest in a garden, and each names the place a little differently. Matthew and Mark call it “a place called Gethsemane”; Luke says only that Jesus went “as he was wont, to the mount of Olives”; John says he crossed “the brook Cedron, where was a garden.” No evangelist gives a wall, a gate, or a distance. This page traces how the church knit those four descriptions into one location, kept it, built on it, split it into rival denominational plots, and finally let the spade test its most famous relic — the ancient olive trees.

It is a reception-history timeline: not a survey of who believed a doctrine, but a chronicle of how one identification grew and was contested. Its “witnesses” are therefore datable sources and events — a topographer's gazetteer, a pilgrim's road-log, a nun's Holy-Week diary, a translator's word-note, a bishop harmonizing the Gospels, a cave with graves cut in its floor, an Anglo-Saxon monk, a Crusader-era name, a Barluzzi basilica, and a radiocarbon lab — ordered earliest-source-first and each footnoted. The shape to watch for is stable in general, contested in detail: the broad location (the western foot of the Mount of Olives, across the Kidron) has held firm since the fourth century, but the exact spot fractured into four sites, the earliest tradition may have favored a cave over the open garden, and the veneration of the old olives is a late devotional growth that modern dating has quietly deflated. Those three questions — grotto or garden, which of the four plots, and whether any surviving olive witnessed the agony — are weighed on their own argument pages as they are added.

Timeline

The Gospels name the place four ways (1st century)

Every later identification has to reconcile four descriptions. Matthew: “Then cometh Jesus with them unto a place called Gethsemane.”1 Mark, nearly the same: “they came to a place which was named Gethsemane.”2 Luke gives no name but a habit: Jesus went “as he was wont, to the mount of Olives… And when he was at the place.”3 John gives neither name nor mount but a garden across a watercourse: “he went forth with his disciples over the brook Cedron, where was a garden… And Judas also, which betrayed him, knew the place: for Jesus ofttimes resorted thither with his disciples.”4 The name itself is a clue and a puzzle: Gethsemane transliterates a Semitic gat shemanim, “oil-press” — the installation you would expect at the foot of an olive-covered hill — though Jerome, translating, will render it instead as a “richest valley.”5 So the site must be: a named or habitual spot, on or at the foot of the Mount of Olives, east across the Kidron from the city, an enclosed garden the disciples knew. The rest is detail — and the detail is where the traditions divide.

Eusebius: the first gazetteer entry — a place of prayer (c. 325)

The earliest topographical notice is in Eusebius's Onomasticon, his gazetteer of biblical places. The Gethsemane entry places the site “at the foot of the Mount of Olives” and records that the faithful of his own day resorted there to pray — the first evidence that the spot was already a station of devotion, a generation before any church is attested on it.6 (The entry's own wording preserves two stages: the Greek notes that “even now the faithful fervently utter prayers” on the spot, and Jerome's Latin recension of the same work adds that “now a church has been built over it” — the open place of prayer of Eusebius's day already sanctuaried by Jerome's.) What Eusebius fixes is the general location — the western foot of the mount, matching Luke and John — not yet an exact plot or a building. That is exactly the layer the later disputes will build on and subdivide.

The Bordeaux Pilgrim's telling silence (333)

The oldest surviving Christian travel diary, written by an anonymous pilgrim from Bordeaux the very year he travelled, climbs the Mount of Olives and notes two monuments there — the basilica Constantine raised “where the Lord taught his apostles before his passion” (the Eleona), and nearby “a little hill, where the Lord went up to pray” with Moses and Elijah (the Transfiguration) — but he names no Gethsemane and no site of the agony or the betrayal.7 The silence is worth pausing on. By 333 the mount was already monumentalized at its summit, yet the agony-garden was not yet a marked pilgrim station with a church of its own. Eusebius's “place of prayer” and Egeria's “graceful church” a half-century later bracket the moment the open location acquired a building — and the Bordeaux Pilgrim catches it just before.

Egeria: the “graceful church” and the Holy-Thursday procession (c. 384)

By the 380s the site is fully built and fully liturgical. The pilgrim Egeria describes the Maundy-Thursday night procession descending from the summit: “at the first cockcrow they come down from the Imbomon with hymns, and arrive at the place where the Lord prayed, as it is written in the Gospel… There is in that place a graceful church,” where the Gospel of the agony is read; “and then all… go down with the Bishop… with hymns to Gethsemane.”8 Two things enter the record here. This is the first firm evidence of an actual church (the ecclesia elegans) on the prayer-spot — the building later archaeology finds beneath the modern basilica. And Egeria already distinguishes two stations: “the place where the Lord prayed” higher up, and “Gethsemane” lower down — an early hint of the grotto-versus-garden split that will run through the whole tradition.

Jerome names the valley (c. 390s)

Jerome, who had translated Eusebius's gazetteer and lived in Palestine, supplies the philology. On Matthew he notes: “Gethsemane means ‘very fertile valley,’ in which he ordered the disciples to sit for a little while and wait for the Lord to return, while he prayed for everyone alone.”9 The gloss is an etymology, not a survey — and it is arguably the wrong one (the “oil-press” derivation fits the Semitic better) — but it shows the site's name being actively interpreted by the church's leading Latin scholar, and it is the reading that carried into the medieval commentators, who read the “valley of fatness” as an allegory of humility and charity rather than as a datum of location.

Augustine harmonizes the four names (c. 400)

The four Gospel descriptions had to be shown to be one place, and it is Augustine who does the harmonizing in his Harmony of the Gospels: the Mount of Olives “is this place, whose name Matthew and Mark called Gethsemane: and there we understand there to have been a garden, which John commemorates” — adding, on the authority of an earlier commentator, that “that village is situated at the foot of the Mount of Olives.”10 This is the move that makes a single site out of four evangelists: Luke's mount, Matthew and Mark's Gethsemane, and John's garden are declared one and the same ground. Every later map of the place assumes Augustine's synthesis.

The grotto and the garden: an early fork (4th–8th centuries)

Roughly 100 m north of the modern garden, beside the Tomb of the Virgin, is a natural cave — itself an ancient oil-press — venerated from the fourth century as the place the disciples waited and slept while Jesus prayed, and where Judas came to betray him. In the fourth century the grotto was floored with white mosaic and turned into a funerary chapel; forty-two graves, dated to the fifth to eighth centuries, were later cut through its floor.11 This matters for the whole tradition: the earliest localized devotion may have clustered on the cave (a sheltered, datable station) rather than on the open olive-plot shown today — the split Egeria's two-station procession already implies. Whether the grotto or the garden has the older claim — and whether they are rivals at all — is weighed on its own argument page.

“Shown to this day”: Bede and Rabanus keep the memory (8th–9th centuries)

The Latin West kept reporting the site as a living, visible place. The Venerable Bede, drawing on the pilgrim reports, writes: “The place of Gethsemane is shown to this day, where the Lord prayed, at the foot of the Mount of Olives, now a church built above it.”12 A century later Rabanus Maurus harmonizes the Gospel names as Augustine had and confirms the fabric still stood: John's brook-Cedron garden “is the same as this Gethsemane… a place where He prayed at the foot of mount Olivet, where is a garden, and a Church now built.”13 Neither is an eyewitness — both transmit what pilgrims reported — but together they show the identification unbroken from Egeria's church down through the early medieval centuries.

“Campus Florum” and the four rival gardens (13th century onward)

Where the ancient sources fixed the general spot, the later centuries multiplied the exact ones. From the thirteenth century the traditional enclosure appears under the name Campus Florum, “the field of flowers.”14 And the single “place where the Lord prayed” hardened into four separate plots, each held by a different church, all within a few hundred metres at the western foot of the mount: the Roman Catholic (Franciscan) garden beside the Church of All Nations, over the “Rock of the Agony”; the Eastern site by the Tomb of the Virgin to the north; the Greek Orthodox plot to the east; and the Russian Orthodox orchard beside the Church of Mary Magdalene.15 The general location was never seriously in doubt; the ownership of the exact yards is a medieval-and-modern denominational inheritance, not an ancient datum — which of the four (if any) is the true plot is weighed on its own argument page.

The Church of All Nations rises over the old foundations (1919–1924)

The dominant modern site is the Franciscan Basilica of the Agony, or Church of All Nations, built 1919–1924 by the architect Antonio Barluzzi directly over the bedrock — the “Rock of the Agony” — where Jesus is remembered to have prayed. The 1919 excavation that preceded it, directed by Fr. Gaudence Orfali, uncovered beneath the site the remains of two earlier churches on the same spot: the fourth-century Byzantine basilica — Egeria's ecclesia elegans — and a twelfth-century Crusader chapel (the Church of St. Saviour), abandoned in 1345.16 The dig is the physical confirmation of the written chain: the modern basilica stands on the ruins of the very church Egeria walked into on Holy Thursday. (Its fourth-century predecessor's end is reported two ways — the Persian sack of 614 in one tradition, an earthquake in 746 in another — and both may hold across successive ruins.)

The spade tests the olive trees (2015): the relic deflated

The garden's most venerated feature is its gnarled ancient olive trees, revered by pilgrims — a devotion first attested only in the sixteenth century17 — as survivors that witnessed the agony. Two lines of evidence tell against that. First, the historical: Josephus records that in the Roman siege of AD 70 “all the trees that were about the city had been already cut down,” the whole landscape “before adorned with trees and pleasant gardens” reduced to “a desolate country every way, and its trees… all cut down” for some ninety furlongs around.18 Second, the scientific: a 2015 study led by Italy's National Research Council radiocarbon-dated the oldest wood of three of the garden's trees to AD 1092, 1166, and 1198 — the Crusader era — candidly noting that the other five giant trunks are hollow and undatable; and a companion 2014 genetic study fingerprinted all eight trees and found they share a single genotype, consistent with deliberate replanting from a common stock.19 The honest sum: the visible trees are medieval, not apostolic. Yet the coda cuts both ways — olive root-systems can regenerate for millennia from a felled stump, so the living wood being twelfth-century does not prove the rootstock is, and no test has reached the roots. The relic-claim (“these very trees saw the agony”) is deflated on the evidence; the site's continuity is not.

Arguments

Related arguments — where exactly

The broad location has held firm since the fourth century, but two questions ask not whether the tradition points to the right ground so much as where exactly within it — grotto or open garden, and which of the four rival plots. Read them together: the first weighs a cave against a garden, the second weighs the four gardens against each other.

Was the agony in the grotto or the open garden? — plausible

A hundred metres north of the modern garden, beside the Tomb of the Virgin, a natural cave — itself an ancient oil-press — was venerated from the fourth century as the place the disciples waited and slept while Jesus prayed. It is a credible, perhaps the earliest datable, localized station: fourth-century in its Christian use, unchanged where the open plot's relics (the Campus Florum name, the venerated olives) are late overlays, and already distinguished from the prayer-spot in Egeria's two-station Holy-Thursday procession. But the strongest form of the reading is not “the cave instead of the garden” but a grotto-and-garden pair — the cave for the disciples' lodging and the betrayal, the open ground a stone's throw off for Jesus' prayer, as the Gospel scene itself invites. Plausible, short of probable: the attestation, though early, is still centuries after the event and begins as a fix on the region, not the cave.

Which of the four rival Gethsemane sites is the authentic garden? — contested

The single “place where the Lord prayed” hardened into four separately-owned plots — Roman Catholic (Franciscan), Greek Orthodox, Russian Orthodox, and Armenian — all within a short walk at the western foot of the mount. A two-level verdict, kept distinct: the general location is sound (attested from the fourth century, matching the Gospel topography, and physically anchored by the fourth-century church found beneath the Franciscan basilica), but identifying one of the four plots as the authentic garden is at best plausible and cannot be adjudicated on present evidence — there is no first-century marker and no decisive dig favouring one plot over its neighbours, and the boundaries reflect later property and pilgrimage history. The Franciscan site is the reasonable default only because it stands on the earliest attested church, not because the metres are proven. Prominence is not proof; multiplicity is not refutation.

Did the garden's ancient olive trees witness Jesus' agony? — weak

The garden's most venerated feature is its gnarled ancient olives, shown to pilgrims as living witnesses of the Passion — though that devotion is first attested only in the sixteenth century. Two independent lines of evidence trim the strong claim. Josephus records that the Roman siege of AD 70 felled every tree for miles around Jerusalem, destroying the standing timber of Jesus' day; and a 2015 radiocarbon study dated the oldest datable trunks (only three of the eight hollow trees could be dated at all) to the twelfth century, the Crusader era, with a companion genetic study finding all eight share one genotype — a single medieval replanting. Weak for the relic-claim that these very trees saw the agony. The defensible residue is different and more modest: olives regenerate from rootstock, so medieval wood does not prove the ground was ever bare of olives, and the site may well have borne olives across the centuries — a claim about continuity of cultivation, not about relics, and one the evidence permits without establishing.
