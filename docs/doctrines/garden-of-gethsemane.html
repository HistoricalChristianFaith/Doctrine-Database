The Garden of Gethsemane

Four evangelists put the agony and the arrest in a garden, and each names the place a little differently. Matthew and Mark call it “a place called Gethsemane”; Luke says only that Jesus went “as he was wont, to the mount of Olives”; John says he crossed “the brook Cedron, where was a garden.” No evangelist gives a wall, a gate, or a distance. This page traces, source by source, how the four descriptions were identified as one location, how the site was built on, how it came to be shown as four separate plots held by different churches, and how the age of its olive trees has been studied.

It is a reception-history timeline: not a survey of who believed a doctrine, but a chronicle of one identification. Its “witnesses” are datable sources and events — a third-century commentator, a topographer's gazetteer, a pilgrim's road-log, a pilgrim's Holy-Week diary, a translator's word-note, a bishop harmonizing the Gospels, a cave with graves cut in its floor, an Anglo-Saxon monk, a thirteenth-century name, a Barluzzi basilica, and a radiocarbon lab — ordered earliest-source-first and each footnoted. From the fourth century the sources place the site at the foot of the Mount of Olives, across the Kidron from the city; Egeria names two stations there, “the place where the Lord prayed” and “Gethsemane”; a nearby cave has its own entry below; and from the thirteenth century onward the record shows separate plots, now four, each held by a different church.

Related doctrines: Sites of the Passion — see also the tomb of Jesus (Golgotha & the Holy Sepulchre) · Bethany & Bethphage (the tomb of Lazarus & the Palm Sunday sending-place) · the site of the Ascension

Timeline

The Gospels name the place four ways (1st century)

The Gospels give four descriptions of the place. Matthew: “Then cometh Jesus with them unto a place called Gethsemane.”1 Mark, nearly the same: “they came to a place which was named Gethsemane.”2 Luke gives no name but a habit: Jesus went “as he was wont, to the mount of Olives… And when he was at the place.”3 John gives neither name nor mount but a garden across a watercourse: “he went forth with his disciples over the brook Cedron, where was a garden… And Judas also, which betrayed him, knew the place: for Jesus ofttimes resorted thither with his disciples.”4 The reference literature cited derives the name Gethsemane from a Semitic gat shemanim, “oil-press”; Jerome's commentary renders it “most fertile valley.”5

Origen: Gethsemane as a named estate (c. 246)

About eighty years before the gazetteer and pilgrim notices below, Origen, expounding Matthew, notes that Jesus came “to an estate called Gethsemane” — the Greek chōrion, a farm or landed estate — and writes that the name was not set down “without reason”: “the name of this estate which is called Gethsemane is of use for making this place clear.”6 It is the earliest extant comment on this timeline that treats Gethsemane as a named place. Origen does not give the interpretation of the name himself (“we, passing that over”) and gives no placement; the earliest extant placement at the Mount of Olives on this timeline is Eusebius's. In the same passage he writes that “the earth of a holy place differ[s] from that of a holier one,” and continues that one who has withdrawn from “Jewish fables” will say that a prayer “does not become different from another prayer on account of the place.”

Eusebius: the earliest extant gazetteer entry — a place of prayer (c. 325)

The earliest extant topographical notice is in Eusebius's Onomasticon, his gazetteer of biblical places. The Gethsemane entry places the site at the Mount of Olives (“at (the foot of) the Mt. of Olives” in Wolf's translation, whose parentheses mark the Latin's variants) and records that the faithful of his own day resorted there to pray.7 The entry's wording preserves two stages: the Greek notes that “even now the faithful fervently utter prayers” on the spot, and Jerome's Latin recension of the same work adds that “now a church has been built over it.” Eusebius's Greek gives the general location — the Mount of Olives, with Jerome's Latin adding “the foot of” — and names neither an exact plot nor a building.

The Bordeaux Pilgrim on the Mount of Olives (333)

The oldest surviving Christian travel diary, written by an anonymous pilgrim from Bordeaux, passes “a valley which is called Jehosaphat” and in it “a rock where Judas Iscariot betrayed Christ”; it then climbs the Mount of Olives and notes two monuments there — the basilica Constantine raised “where the Lord taught his apostles before his passion” (the Eleona), and nearby “a little hill, where the Lord went up to pray” with Moses and Elijah (the Transfiguration). It names no Gethsemane and no site of the agony.8 On this timeline the itinerary falls between Eusebius's notice of a place of prayer and Egeria's report of a “graceful church” there some fifty years later.

Egeria: the “graceful church” and the Holy-Thursday procession (c. 384)

In the 380s the pilgrim Egeria describes the Maundy-Thursday night procession descending from the summit: “at the first cockcrow they come down from the Imbomon with hymns, and arrive at the place where the Lord prayed, as it is written in the Gospel… There is in that place a graceful church,” where the Gospel of the agony is read; “and then all… go down with the Bishop… with hymns to Gethsemane.”9 Hers is the earliest extant report on this timeline of a church (the ecclesia elegans) on the prayer-spot; the name is the one the site plaque gives to the fourth-century remains under the modern basilica (see 1919–1924 below). Egeria names two stations on the descent: “the place where the Lord prayed,” with the church, and, after a further descent, “Gethsemane.”

John Chrysostom: the brook Cedron and the habitual garden (c. 391)

Expounding John at Antioch, Chrysostom dwells on the crossing — Jesus “departed beyond the brook Cedron, where was a garden… He journeyeth at midnight, and crosseth a river, and hasteth to come to a place known to the traitor… and placeth Himself in the garden as in a prison.”10 Against any thought that the garden was a hiding-place he presses John's note that it was a familiar haunt: “Judas knew the place” because Jesus “often resorted thither with His disciples… especially in mountains and gardens, seeking a place free from disturbance.” Chrysostom names no Gethsemane, no church and no plot, and adds no localization beyond John's; he reads the garden across the Kidron as a real place east of the city that Jesus used regularly.

Jerome names the valley (c. 390s)

Jerome, who had translated Eusebius's gazetteer and lived in Palestine, supplies an etymology. On Matthew he writes: “Gethsemane is interpreted most fertile valley; there he ordered the disciples to sit for a little while and to wait for his return, while the Lord alone prayed for all.”11 The gloss is an etymology, not a topographical notice, and it differs from the “oil-press” derivation given in the reference literature cited above.

Augustine harmonizes the four names (c. 400)

In his Harmony of the Gospels Augustine identifies the places the evangelists name. Of the place on the Mount of Olives to which Luke says Jesus went, he writes: “That is the place which the other two have instanced under the name of Gethsemane. There, we understand, was the garden which John brings into notice.”12 Bonaventure, quoting the passage in his Commentary on John, follows it with a note he attributes to Victor: “that village is situated at the foot of the Mount of Olives.” In this passage Luke's mount, Matthew and Mark's Gethsemane, and John's garden are identified as one and the same ground.

The grotto of Gethsemane (4th–8th centuries)

Roughly 100 m north of the modern garden, beside the Tomb of the Virgin, is a natural cave — itself an ancient oil-press — which the Hebrew University Corpus of Christian sites describes as a “Place identified by some ancient pilgrims and modern scholars as the site of betrayal and arrest of Christ.” Its veneration as the place where the disciples waited and slept while Jesus prayed is ⚠ unverified. The Corpus dates to the second half of the fourth century the cave's conversion from an oil-press with a cistern into a funerary chapel, paved in white mosaic; forty-two graves, which it dates to the fifth to eighth centuries, were later cut through the floor. Virgilio Corbo excavated the grotto for the Custody of the Holy Land after the flood of 1955.13

“Shown to this day”: Bede and Rabanus Maurus (8th–9th centuries)

Latin writers continued to report the site as a visible place. The Venerable Bede, drawing on the pilgrim reports, writes: “The place of Gethsemane is shown to this day, where the Lord prayed, at the foot of the Mount of Olives, now a church built above it.” He adds that “Gethsemane is interpreted as the valley of the fat ones or the fatness,” and reads the valley as a figure of humility and of “the internal fatness of love” to be kept in prayer.14 A century later Rabanus Maurus, as excerpted in Aquinas's Catena Aurea, likewise identifies John's brook-Cedron garden with Gethsemane and reports a church standing there: John's garden “is the same as this Gethsemane… a place where He prayed at the foot of mount Olivet, where is a garden, and a Church now built.”15 Neither is an eyewitness; both transmit earlier reports of the site as shown, at the foot of the mount, with a church over it.

“Campus Florum” and the four gardens (13th century onward)

From the thirteenth century the traditional enclosure appears under the name Campus Florum, “Flower Garden” in the site plaque's rendering.16 Today four separate plots are claimed by different churches as the place where Jesus prayed, all within a few hundred metres at the western foot of the mount: the Roman Catholic (Franciscan) garden beside the Church of All Nations, over the “Rock of the Agony”; the site by the Tomb of the Virgin to the north; the Greek Orthodox plot to the east; and the Russian Orthodox orchard beside the Church of Mary Magdalene.17

The Church of All Nations rises over the old foundations (1919–1924)

The Franciscan Basilica of the Agony, or Church of All Nations, was built 1919–1924 to the design of the architect Antonio Barluzzi, over the bedrock — the “Rock of the Agony” — where Jesus is remembered to have prayed. The excavation that preceded it, credited on the site plaque to Fr. Gaudence Orfali (1919), uncovered beneath the site the remains of two earlier churches on the same spot: a fourth-century church, labelled on the plaque the Ecclesia Elegans (the name of the “graceful church” in Egeria's account), and a twelfth-century Crusader chapel (the Church of St. Saviour), abandoned in 1345.18 The Wikipedia article cited dates the destruction of the fourth-century church to an earthquake in 746.

The olive trees dated (2014–2015)

The garden's ancient olive trees are revered by pilgrims as survivors that witnessed the agony; the site plaque (“Old Olive Trees venerated by Pilgrims”) dates the first mention of that veneration to the sixteenth century.19 Josephus records that in the Roman siege of AD 70 “all the trees that were about the city had been already cut down,” the whole landscape “before adorned with trees and pleasant gardens” reduced to “a desolate country every way, and its trees… all cut down” for some ninety furlongs around.20 A 2015 study led by Mauro Bernabei of Italy's National Research Council radiocarbon-dated the oldest wood of three of the garden's trees to AD 1092, 1166, and 1198, noting that the other five giant trunks are hollow and undatable. A 2014 study by R. Petruccelli and colleagues fingerprinted all eight trees, concluded that they “have been propagated from a single genotype,” and, citing Bernabei, gave their age as “approximately nine centuries”; the same authors hold that the felling Josephus narrates took place “some hundreds of meters to the north with respect to Gethsemane,” so that it “should not have interested the olive trees in question,” and describe the trees as “very old, probably among the oldest of the species in the world”; they place the earliest reference to olive trees at Gethsemane in the Dominican pilgrim Felix Fabri (1480–1483), a century before the site plaque's date.21
