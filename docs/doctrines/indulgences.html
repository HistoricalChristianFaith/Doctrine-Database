Indulgences

An indulgence is, in the developed Latin doctrine, the remission before God of the temporal punishment still owed for sins whose guilt has already been forgiven — a remission the Church grants out of the treasury of merit of Christ and the saints, and which she teaches can be applied even to the souls in purgatory. The doctrine sits at the intersection of three prior ones: that forgiven sin may leave a debt of temporal punishment; that the Church holds a treasury of superabundant merit she may dispense; and that the dead can be aided by the living. Indulgences are the practical face of purgatory, and it was the sale of one — the St. Peter's indulgence preached by Tetzel — that lit the spark of Luther's protest in 1517 and, with it, the Reformation. This timeline traces how the practice arose, how its theology was worked out after the fact, and how it was defended and attacked, earliest first.

A note on the shape of this debate. The practice ran ahead of the theory. The early Church knew penitential remissions — the relaxation of assigned canonical penance — long before any theology of a “treasury of merits” existed; the crusade indulgence of 1095 generalized the grant, its commutation for money (from 1213) made the reward purchasable, and only in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries was the underlying doctrine (the treasury; application to the dead per modum suffragii) defined. That gap — practice first, doctrine after — is the historical crux, and the abuses it bred are the Reformation's leading grievance. The Council of Trent (1563) affirmed the Church's power to grant indulgences while ordering the “evil traffic” in them abolished. Several of the sub-questions are weighed adversarially in the Arguments section below; the fuller treatment of the crusade-origin thesis and the coherence of applying indulgences to the dead currently lives on the neighbouring purgatory timeline, to which this page is welded.

Timeline

Urban II and the First Crusade indulgence (1095)

At the Council of Clermont, Urban II granted those who marched to free Jerusalem a full remission — the crusade historian Thomas Madden calls it “the seed that in three centuries' time would grow into the weed of abuses that ultimately led to the Protestant Reformation.” This is the principal tributary of the developed indulgence, though not its sole headwater: penitential redemptions and eleventh-century episcopal grants precede it. The developmental spine — and the hinge at which Innocent III made the crusade vow redeemable for cash (1213), the moment the reward became purchasable — is weighed on the crusade-indulgence origin thesis.1

Clement VI defines the treasury of merits (1343)

The bull Unigenitus Dei Filius of Clement VI gave indulgences their formal theological ground: Christ, by his passion, acquired “a treasure… for the Church militant,” to which “the merits of the Blessed Mother of God and of all the elect… are known to give their help,” a treasure the Roman pontiff dispenses to the faithful. The doctrine of the treasury of merit is thus fixed nearly two-and-a-half centuries after the practice it justifies.2

Martin Luther, the Ninety-Five Theses (1517)

Provoked by Johann Tetzel's preaching of the St. Peter's indulgence, Luther posted his Ninety-Five Theses — an academic protest against the whole apparatus: “They preach only human doctrines who say that as soon as the money clinks into the money chest, the soul flies out of purgatory” (Thesis 27); the treasury of the Church is “the most holy gospel of the glory and grace of God,” not a fund of merits to be sold (Thesis 62). The protest against the indulgence became the opening act of the Reformation.3

The Council of Trent, Decree Concerning Indulgences (1563)

In its twenty-fifth and final session, Trent affirmed “that the power of conferring Indulgences was granted by Christ to the Church” and condemned those who deny their use as salutary — while ordering that “all evil gains for the obtaining thereof… be wholly abolished” and the abuses reformed. The doctrine is retained; the traffic Luther attacked is disowned.4

Arguments

Sub-claims marshaled for or against the doctrine, weighed adversarially on their own pages. Because indulgences are historically welded to purgatory, the arguments now live on the neighbouring purgatory timeline and are cross-linked here; dedicated argument pages under this doctrine are an open area still being built out.

Did the crusade indulgence seed the abuses that led to the Reformation? (assessment: sound)

The crusade-indulgence origin thesis — a historical claim tracing indulgences from Urban II's full remission (1095), through the First Lateran Council's extension to the Reconquista (1123), to Innocent III's fateful commutation of the crusade vow for cash (1213), and on to the St. Peter's indulgence and Luther's protest of 1517. The developmental spine holds and is mainstream crusade scholarship; the needed refinement is that the crusade indulgence is the principal tributary that became the main channel, not the sole headwater.5

Why doesn't the pope free every soul from purgatory? (assessment: weak)

Why doesn't the pope free every soul from purgatory? — an internal-coherence objection put as a dilemma: if a plenary indulgence remits the whole temporal debt, can be applied to a soul by another's act, and the pope may grant it at will, then charity would seem to demand he empty purgatory at a stroke. It is a false dilemma: the Church has always taught she has no jurisdiction over the dead — an indulgence reaches a soul in purgatory only per modum suffragii, by way of suffrage, with no compulsion — so the “limited power” horn is the express teaching, not a hidden flaw.6
