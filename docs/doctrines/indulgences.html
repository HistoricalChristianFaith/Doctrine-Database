Indulgences

An indulgence is defined in Catholic teaching as “the remission before God of the temporal punishment due sins already forgiven as far as their guilt is concerned,” granted through the Church, which “authoritatively dispenses and applies the treasury of the satisfaction won by Christ and the saints,” and which “can always be applied to the dead by way of suffrage” (Paul VI, 1967). The definition joins three teachings recorded on neighbouring timelines: that forgiven sin may leave a temporal punishment still owed; that the Church may dispense a treasury of merit; and that the living can aid the dead in purgatory. The preaching of the St. Peter's indulgence by Johann Tetzel in 1517 was the occasion of Martin Luther's Ninety-Five Theses. This timeline lists its witnesses by the date of the earliest extant attestation, from the penitential discipline of the early Church, which Catholic writers and the editors of the early canons treat as part of the history of indulgences, to the Council of Trent and Paul VI's reform of 1967.1

Who holds what. The Council of Trent (1563) teaches that “the power of conferring Indulgences was granted by Christ to the Church” and that she has used it “even in the most ancient times”; Paul VI's Indulgentiarum Doctrina (1967) describes the practice as having arisen “gradually, in the course of the centuries,” as “a progression in the doctrine and discipline of the Church rather than a change.” Lutheran writers from Martin Chemnitz (Examen Concilii Tridentini, 1573) to the seminary faculties of the Lutheran Church–Missouri Synod (1999) dispute the practice and the claims made for it. The Lutheran theologian Michael Root (2001) describes indulgences as “not a part of the universal tradition shared by East and West, but a specifically Western medieval development.” How the practice of 1517 arose, and what the Catholic reforms conceded, are taken up under Disputed readings below and on the neighbouring purgatory timeline.2

Timeline

Tertullian and the public penance of the early Church (c. 203)

Tertullian describes the public penance of the African church. After baptism the grave sinner has one further remedy — a “second, and only remaining” repentance, the “plank after shipwreck” — carried out in a public act he calls by its Greek name exomologesis: sackcloth and ashes, fasting, and prostration before the presbyters and the assembled brethren, “whereby … by confession satisfaction is settled.” He says that by this “temporal mortification” the penitent may “expunge eternal punishments.”3

Cyprian and the confessors' letters of peace (c. 250–251)

In the Decian persecution the imprisoned confessors began issuing libelli pacis — letters of peace asking that named lapsed Christians be restored to communion before their assigned penance was complete, on the credit of the confessors' own suffering. Cyprian regulates the letters: the certificate must name individuals whose “penitence is very near to full satisfaction,” not vaguely cover “such a one and his friends”; he warns that peace granted “before their sin is expiated … is not peace, but war,” and writes that the martyrs' merits avail with the Judge “when the day of judgment shall come”; he also directs that lapsed holders of martyrs' certificates who fall dangerously ill may confess before a presbyter or deacon and “come to the Lord with the peace which the martyrs have desired.” William Kent's article “Indulgences” in the Catholic Encyclopedia (1910) includes the libelli in its history of indulgences, noting that Cyprian condemned their abuses and also “recognizes the value of these memorials.”4

The Council of Nicaea fixes — and lets the bishop relax — penance (325)

Nicaea's canons on the lapsed set a graded, counted penance (canon 11 — three years as “hearers,” seven as “prostrators”) before restoration. Canon 12 gives the bishop discretion to shorten that term for those who prove their conversion “by deeds, and not pretence” — “after that the bishop may determine yet more favourably concerning them.” The editors of the canons in the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers identify this discretionary power to relax an assigned penance as “the basis of ‘indulgences’ in their original form,” and note that its abuse “led, in later times, to the practice of commuting such exercises for money payments.” The Orthodox canonist Constantin Rus (2023) reads the canon as the council “applying the principle of economy.”5

The penitentials make penance redeemable (c. 550–800)

The Irish and insular penitentials (Finnian, Columbanus, Cummean, Theodore) set a fixed satisfaction for each sin and allowed it to be commuted (Irish arreum, “redeemed”): a long fast discharged by psalms, genuflexions, almsgiving — or by a coin (“one denarius is the equivalent of one day”). In McNeill and Gamer's edition H. Leclercq is quoted as judging that these “commutations and redemptions … greatly favored the introduction of indulgences properly so-called,” and the Catholic Encyclopedia's “Penitential Redemptions” continues the line to Clermont, where “the crusade was suggested as a ransom from all penance … This was the modern indulgence.”6

Urban II and the First Crusade indulgence (1095)

At the Council of Clermont, Urban II granted that the journey of those who went to free Jerusalem “be counted in lieu of all penance.” Innocent III's Quia maior (1213) and the Fourth Lateran Council (1215) later extended the crusade indulgence to those who paid to outfit a crusader; of that provision the crusade historian Thomas Madden writes that “in this provision was planted the seed that in three centuries' time would grow into the weed of abuses that ultimately led to the Protestant Reformation.” How the crusade indulgence stands to the practice Luther protested in 1517 is disputed; see the interpretation page on the crusade indulgence.7

Boniface VIII proclaims the Jubilee of 1300

With the bull Antiquorum habet fida relatio (22 February 1300), Boniface VIII appealed to a tradition that pilgrims to Rome in past centennial years had received “great remissions and indulgences for sins,” and granted to all who, “truly repented and confessed,” visited the basilicas of St. Peter and St. Paul — Romans on thirty days, foreigners on fifteen, at least once a day — “in this present year and in every hundredth year to come, not only full and free, but the very fullest, pardon of all their sins.” The Catholic Encyclopedia calls it “the first celebration of which we have any precise record”; the chronicler Villani reckoned some two hundred thousand pilgrims present in Rome over and above the ordinary population throughout the year, and Dante likens the two-way press of the damned in the eighth circle to the crowd-control on the bridge to St. Peter's during the Holy Year (Inferno XVIII.28–33).8

Clement VI teaches the treasury of merits (1343)

The bull Unigenitus Dei Filius of Clement VI teaches that Christ, by his passion, acquired “a treasure… for the Church militant,” to which “the merits of the Blessed Mother of God and of all the elect… are known to give their help,” a treasure the Roman pontiff dispenses to the faithful. The doctrine is traced on the treasury of merit timeline.9

Sixtus IV applies an indulgence to the dead (1476)

With the bull Salvator noster (3 August 1476), Sixtus IV extended a plenary indulgence to the souls in purgatory — granted for the repair of the church of Saintes, in favour of which those who paid “a certain sum” (certam pecuniam) could secure a relaxation of the purgatorial sufferings of a departed relative or friend. Schaff's History of the Christian Church calls it “Sixtus' first bull granting indulgence for the dead,” and the historian Lawrence Duggan dates to 1476 the papal declaration “that one could indeed gain an indulgence for someone in purgatory.” Schaff records that the grant “aroused criticism” and that a second bull followed the next year; the Catholic Encyclopedia's “Purgatory” (Edward Hanna, 1911) names Sixtus IV's Romani Pontificis provida diligentia (27 November) as a bull carrying the restriction “per modum suffragii et deprecationis” — by way of suffrage. Kent's Catholic Encyclopedia article explains the limit: over the dead the Church “has no jurisdiction and therefore makes the indulgence available for them by way of suffrage … she petitions God to accept these works of satisfaction.” The Instructio Summaria preached by Tetzel in 1517 included a “fourth grace” for the dead.10

The St. Peter's indulgence and Johann Tetzel (1515–1517)

Pope Leo X proclaimed a plenary indulgence to rebuild St. Peter's Basilica, and Albrecht of Brandenburg, who had borrowed from the Fugger bankers to pay for the archbishopric of Mainz on top of two sees he already held, was licensed to preach it across his German lands, with half the proceeds going to Rome for the basilica and half to repay the Fugger loan ⚠ unverified. His sub-commissioner, the Dominican Johann Tetzel, preached near the Saxon border from 1516 with an official manual, the Instructio Summaria, whose “fourth grace” offered the release of the souls in purgatory and stated that the living contributor need not be “contrite in heart.” Of the couplet attached to Tetzel's name (“as soon as the coin in the coffer rings, the soul from purgatory springs”), Henry Ganss (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1912) writes that, “though verbally spurious,” it can “in substance” be traced to Tetzel, who “substantially acknowledged” it in his Frankfort theses, and adds: “By the teaching he laid himself open to just censure and reproach”; Wikipedia's account calls it “almost certainly not a literal quotation.”11

Martin Luther, the Ninety-Five Theses (1517)

Provoked by Tetzel's preaching, Luther sent to Archbishop Albrecht — and, by tradition, posted — his Ninety-Five Theses on 31 October 1517, which the Lutheran–Roman Catholic Commission on Unity describes as meant “to inaugurate an academic disputation on open and unresolved questions regarding the theory and practice of indulgences.” The theses attack the preachers (“those preachers of indulgences are in error, who say that by the pope's indulgences a man is freed from every penalty,” Thesis 21), the trust placed in pardon-letters (those who rely on them “will be condemned eternally,” Thesis 32), and the claim over the dead (“They preach man who say that so soon as the penny jingles into the money-box, the soul flies out,” Thesis 27; “the result of the intercession of the Church is in the power of God alone,” Thesis 28), and they voice the laity's question why the pope does not empty purgatory “for the sake of holy love” (Thesis 82). Thesis 62 names the Church's true treasure as “the Most Holy Gospel”; Thesis 71 reads: “He who speaks against the truth of apostolic pardons, let him be anathema and accursed!” The Leipzig disputation (1519), the bull Exsurge Domine (1520) and Luther's excommunication (1521) followed.12

The Council of Trent: the quaestors abolished, indulgences retained (1562–1563)

Trent addressed indulgences twice. In its twenty-first session (1562) the decree of reformation abolished the office of the itinerant quaestores of alms, the commissioned preachers of indulgences: their “name and use” were “henceforth utterly abolished; nor shall they be allowed in any wise to exercise any such office.” Indulgences were instead to be published by the local Ordinaries, aided by two members of the chapter, who might gather the alms “without their receiving any remuneration whatsoever” (chapter 9). In its twenty-fifth and final session (1563) the “Decree Concerning Indulgences” affirmed “that the power of conferring Indulgences was granted by Christ to the Church,” condemned with anathema those who call them useless or deny the Church's power to grant them, and ordered that “all evil gains for the obtaining thereof… be wholly abolished.”13

Paul VI recodifies the practice: Indulgentiarum Doctrina (1967)

After the Second Vatican Council, Paul VI's apostolic constitution Indulgentiarum Doctrina (1 January 1967) restated the definition — an indulgence is “the remission before God of the temporal punishment due sins already forgiven as far as their guilt is concerned,” granted by the Church as she “authoritatively dispenses and applies the treasury of the satisfaction won by Christ and the saints” (Norm 1), and it “can always be applied to the dead by way of suffrage” (Norm 3). It dropped the numbered days and years of partial indulgences, leaving the distinction of plenary and partial (Norm 2), a partial indulgence “henceforth… designated only with the words ‘partial indulgence’ without any determination of days or years” (Norm 4). The revised official list, the Enchiridion Indulgentiarum, followed in 1968. Whether these reforms conceded the Reformers' critique is disputed; see Disputed readings.14
