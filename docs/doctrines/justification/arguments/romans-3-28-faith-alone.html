Does Romans 3:28 teach justification by faith alone — and was Luther right to add “alone”?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — that “a man is justified by faith without the deeds of the law” excludes works from the ground of justification is Paul’s own claim and is sound; that the verse therefore establishes the full sola fide, and that Luther’s added “alone” (allein) is a faithful gloss rather than an intrusion, is the disputed part — “alone” is absent from the Greek and the Vulgate, the reach of “works of the law” is contested, and James 2:24 verbally denies “faith only”; no clear winner · advanced by Martin Luther (whose German Bible reads “allein durch den Glauben”), resisted by his Catholic critics and by the appeal to James.

Related arguments: the faith-alone texts — see also Does James 2:24 (“not by faith only”) overturn justification by faith alone?

The claim

“So halten wir nun, dass der Mensch gerecht werde ohne des Gesetzes Werke, allein durch den Glauben.” (“So we hold that a man is justified without the works of the law, alone through faith.”)

— Martin Luther, rendering Romans 3:28 in his German New Testament (1522). Luther defended the added word in his Open Letter on Translating (Sendbrief vom Dolmetschen, 1530), conceding that “the word solum is not in the Latin or Greek text,” but arguing that “the text itself and the meaning of St. Paul demand and require it forcefully,” as idiomatic German for an exclusive sense.1 The advocated interpretation: Romans 3:28 teaches that justification rests on faith to the exclusion of works as its ground, and “faith alone” is a legitimate translation of what Paul means, not an addition to what he says.

Who advances it

The proof-text is the Reformation’s banner. Luther made Romans 1–4 the hinge of his theology and printed “allein” in his 1522 German Bible; the Reformation confessions (Augsburg IV, Thirty-Nine Articles XI — “justified by Faith only, is a most wholesome Doctrine”) built on it. The counter-case is that the “alone” is Luther’s own insertion, seized on by Catholic controversialists then and since. This page weighs the interpretation of the verse, not merely Luther’s translation choice.

The evidence for

Paul really does exclude works from the ground — and does so emphatically. The verse states it: “a man is justified by faith without the deeds of the law” (Rom 3:28). The immediate context drives the exclusion home: “by the deeds of the law there shall no flesh be justified” (3:20); justification is “freely by his grace” (3:24); and Romans 4 opposes reckoning “of grace” to reckoning “of debt” (4:4), justifying “him that worketh not, but believeth” (4:5). If works are excluded from the ground and faith is the sole instrument named, then “by faith alone” captures the logic even though the adverb is supplied.2

“Faith alone” language predates Luther. Luther did not coin the phrase. A string of earlier writers used sola fide or its equivalent of Paul’s meaning — Origen in his Commentary on Romans, Ambrosiaster (“they are justified freely, because… by faith alone, as a gift of God”), and others gathered by later surveys — so the reading that Paul’s point can be put as “faith alone” is an ancient one, not a sixteenth-century novelty. Luther’s defense also makes a narrower philological point his critics usually garble: he added the adverb solum/allein, not a feminine sola attached to fide, precisely as German idiom requires when one thing is set against another.3

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The exclusion of works-of-law from the ground of justification is not an inference laid on the text — it is the text: “without the deeds of the law,” reinforced across 3:20–4:5. And the “faith alone” way of putting Paul’s meaning has genuine pre-Reformation precedent, so the charge that Luther simply invented the reading is false. As a rendering of the exclusive force of “by faith, apart from works,” “alone” is defensible German.

What is overstated or contested. Three things keep the full sola fide gloss from a clean win. First, the word is not there: neither the Greek (“logizomai… pistei chōris ergōn nomou”) nor the Vulgate (“arbitramur enim iustificari hominem per fidem sine operibus legis”) contains “alone,” so the translation supplies an interpretation, however defensible.4 Second, the scope of “works of the law” is disputed: on the older Protestant reading it is all human works, but on the “new perspective” it means the specifically Jewish boundary-markers (circumcision, food laws, sabbath) that Paul’s argument is fighting over — in which case 3:28 excludes those from the ground of Gentile inclusion without settling the later question of faith versus transformation. Third, and decisively for the “alone,” the one place Scripture actually uses the phrase “faith only” is James 2:24, which negates it: “by works a man is justified, and not by faith only” — so an unqualified “faith alone” runs straight into the sole verse that names it, and must be reconciled with James (weighed on the next page). Luther felt the collision keenly enough to disparage James as an “epistle of straw.”

Assessment

Contested. Split the claim and each half is clear. That Romans 3:28 excludes the works of the law from the ground of justification is Paul’s own, stated and repeated — that core is sound, and the “faith alone” way of expressing it has real patristic precedent. But the stronger claim — that the verse by itself establishes the whole Reformation sola fide, and that Luther’s inserted “alone” is simply Paul’s meaning — remains genuinely disputed: the adverb is absent from Greek and Latin, the reach of “works of the law” is itself contested, and the only biblical use of “faith only” is a denial. Sound as an exclusion of works from the ground; contested as a proof of unqualified sola fide. The word Luther supplied is a defensible gloss on Paul’s logic, not a neutral report of his text — which is exactly why the verse cannot carry the whole doctrine alone.
