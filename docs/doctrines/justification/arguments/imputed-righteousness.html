Is justification an imputed, forensic righteousness?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — the reading that to “justify” is to declare righteous in a law-court sense, God crediting the righteousness of Christ to the believer received by faith, has a strong exegetical core in the forensic use of dikaioō and the reckoning language of Romans 4 (God “imputeth righteousness without works,” justifying “the ungodly”); it is genuinely disputed by Paul’s “made righteous” language, by the fact that the sharp imputation/sanctification distinction is a Reformation refinement rather than an explicit patristic one, and by James 2; no clear winner · advanced by the Reformation confessions (Augsburg, the Thirty-Nine Articles, Westminster) after Luther and Calvin, resisted by the Council of Trent.

Related arguments: imputed vs. infused righteousness — see also Is justification an infused, transformative righteousness?

The claim

“Those whom God effectually calleth, he also freely justifieth: not by infusing righteousness into them, but by pardoning their sins, and by accounting and accepting their persons as righteous; not for anything wrought in them, or done by them, but for Christ’s sake alone; nor by imputing faith itself, the act of believing, or any other evangelical obedience to them, as their righteousness; but by imputing the obedience and satisfaction of Christ unto them, they receiving and resting on him and his righteousness by faith.”

— Westminster Confession of Faith XI.1 (1646), the-highway.com.1 The advocated interpretation: justification is a forensic act — a change in the believer’s standing, not (yet) in his moral substance. God declares him righteous, on the ground of Christ’s righteousness reckoned to his account, received by faith; the inward renewal (sanctification) is a real and necessary fruit, but a distinct work that follows the completed verdict.

Who advances it

The reading is Luther’s and Calvin’s, confessionalized in the sixteenth–seventeenth centuries: the Augsburg Confession IV (1530, “This faith God imputes for righteousness”), the Thirty-Nine Articles XI (1571), and the Westminster Confession XI (1646), which states the point most sharply against infusion. Behind them stands a line of exegesis of Paul’s law-court and accounting vocabulary; the page weighs that interpretation, not merely one confession’s wording.

The evidence for

“Justify” is court-language: to declare righteous, not to make righteous. The Greek dikaioō and its Hebrew counterpart (hitsdiq) belong to the law court, where to “justify” is the judge’s verdict of acquittal — the opposite of “condemn,” its constant antonym (Deut 25:1; Prov 17:15; Rom 8:33–34). A judge who “justifies” a defendant does not infuse virtue into him; he pronounces his status. Read so, Paul’s “it is God that justifieth; who is he that condemneth?” (Rom 8:33–34) is a courtroom verdict, and justification is a declaration about standing.2

Romans 4 is imputation-language, and it credits righteousness apart from works, to the ungodly. Paul’s test-case is Abraham: “Abraham believed God, and it was counted unto him for righteousness” (Rom 4:3, quoting Gen 15:6). The accounting term (logizomai, “reckon, credit”) is repeated with deliberate force: “to him that worketh not, but believeth on him that justifieth the ungodly, his faith is counted for righteousness” (4:5); God “imputeth righteousness without works” (4:6); “blessed is the man to whom the Lord will not impute sin” (4:8). Two things follow that fit imputation and strain infusion: the righteousness is credited, not produced, and it is credited to one still described as ungodly — a status-change before a substance-change. The grace/debt contrast in 4:4–5 (“to him that worketh is the reward reckoned… of debt”) sets the crediting expressly against anything earned.3

The “great exchange” texts put our sin on Christ and his righteousness on us. “He hath made him to be sin for us, who knew no sin; that we might be made the righteousness of God in him” (2 Cor 5:21) reads naturally as a double reckoning: Christ, sinless, is “made sin” (charged with what was not intrinsically his), that we might be “made the righteousness of God” (credited with what is not intrinsically ours). Paul wants to be found “not having mine own righteousness… but… the righteousness which is of God by faith” (Phil 3:9) — an alien righteousness, his by faith rather than by attainment.4

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The forensic core is genuinely strong. “Justify” really is court-language whose native sense is “declare righteous,” paired against “condemn”; Romans 4 really does speak of righteousness counted, apart from works, to the ungodly; and the grace/debt antithesis really does exclude the crediting from anything earned. An account of justification that cannot make room for a verdict credited to the undeserving has to explain away a great deal of Romans 4. So far the reading is not merely a Reformation invention but a fair rendering of Paul’s vocabulary.

Where it is overstated or contested. Three pressures keep it from a clean win. First, Paul also speaks of being made righteous: “as by one man’s disobedience many were made sinners, so by the obedience of one shall many be made righteous” (Rom 5:19), and of the “love of God shed abroad in our hearts” (5:5) — language the infusion reading marshals for a real inward change, not a bare reckoning. Second, the razor-sharp distinction between justification (verdict) and sanctification (renewal) is a Reformation refinement: the fathers — Clement, Chrysostom, Augustine — preach “justified by faith” without isolating a purely forensic moment, and Augustine’s own iustificare means “to make righteous.” The confessional imputation doctrine can claim Paul’s words but not an explicit patristic pedigree for the distinction it hangs on them. Third, the phrase “imputed righteousness of Christ” is an inference: Paul says faith is counted as righteousness, and never in so many words that Christ’s active obedience is the imputed item — a gap Trent’s canon 11 exploits, and one some later Protestants themselves (e.g. the “faith itself imputed” reading Westminster is at pains to deny) felt.5 And fourth, the whole reading must answer James 2:24 (“not by faith only”), weighed on its own page.

Assessment

Contested. The forensic-and-imputation reading is not a mere polemical construct: the law-court sense of “justify,” the reckoning verbs of Romans 4, the crediting of righteousness to the ungodly apart from works, and the exchange of 2 Corinthians 5:21 give it a real and defensible base in Paul. What it cannot claim is a clean sweep: Paul’s “made righteous” language and the “charity poured forth” feed the rival infusion reading; the crisp justification/sanctification distinction on which the doctrine leans is a sixteenth-century sharpening the fathers did not draw in those terms; and the specific claim that Christ’s obedience is the imputed content goes a step beyond Paul’s explicit words. Strong within a forensic construal of Paul’s vocabulary, disputed the moment the transformative texts and the patristic usage are set beside it: contested — the mirror of the infusion page, as an honestly two-sided doctrine requires.
