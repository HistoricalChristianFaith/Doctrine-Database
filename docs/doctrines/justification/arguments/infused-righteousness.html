Is justification an infused, transformative righteousness?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — the reading that God does not merely reckon but truly makes the sinner righteous, pouring grace and charity into the heart so that justification is a real inward renewal increased through good works, is well grounded in Paul’s “made righteous” and “charity poured forth” language, in the renewal texts, and in a patristic tradition that did not separate justification from transformation; it is genuinely disputed by the forensic sense of “justify,” the crediting language of Romans 4, and the “justifies the ungodly” that describes a verdict before a change; no clear winner · advanced by the Council of Trent (Session VI, 1547), resisted by the Reformation confessions.

Related arguments: imputed vs. infused righteousness — see also Is justification an imputed, forensic righteousness?

The claim

“Justification… is not only a remission of sins, but also the sanctification and renewal of the inward man, through the voluntary reception of the grace and of the gifts, whereby man of unjust becomes just, and of an enemy a friend, that so he may be an heir according to hope of life everlasting.”

— Council of Trent, Session VI (1547), Decree on Justification, Chapter VII, papalencyclicals.net.1 The advocated interpretation: to justify is to make just. The grace of justification is a real quality infused into the soul — the “charity… poured forth in our hearts by the Holy Ghost” (Rom 5:5) — not an external reckoning; and this inherent righteousness is genuinely the person’s own, preserved and “increased” through the good works of a life in grace (Trent, canons 24, 32).

Who advances it

The reading is the patristic and medieval default given dogmatic form by the Council of Trent (Session VI, 1547) against the Reformers, and elaborated by Aquinas’s account of justification as an infusion of grace (Summa I-II, q. 113). It reaches back through Augustine’s iustificare = “to make righteous” to the Greek fathers’ fusion of justification with the healing of the soul; the page weighs that interpretation, not merely Trent’s canons.

The evidence for

Paul says we are made righteous, and speaks of a righteousness poured in. The climactic Adam–Christ parallel is transformative in form: “as by one man’s disobedience many were made sinners, so by the obedience of one shall many be made righteous” (Rom 5:19). Just as Adam’s sin made men really (not notionally) sinful, Christ’s obedience makes them really righteous. And the mechanism Paul names is an infusion of love: “the love of God is shed abroad in our hearts by the Holy Ghost which is given unto us” (Rom 5:5). Justification, on this reading, is where sanctification begins, not a verdict sealed off from it.2

The renewal texts describe a washing and remaking, not a reckoning. Salvation comes “by the washing of regeneration, and renewing of the Holy Ghost… that being justified by his grace, we should be made heirs” (Titus 3:5–7) — regeneration and renewal are the saving act, with justification in the same breath. “If any man be in Christ, he is a new creature” (2 Cor 5:17), the verse immediately before the “made… righteousness” of 5:21, frames the exchange as a real new-making.3

The fathers do not divide justification from transformation. Augustine’s iustificare is iustum facere, “to make righteous”: God justifies the ungodly not by a legal fiction but by healing the will with the love he pours in. No father before the Reformation isolates a purely declarative justification standing apart from the renewal of the person; the Greek tradition especially reads “justified by faith” as the start of a real deification of the believer. The infusion reading can therefore claim the older and broader pedigree for its concept of justification, whatever the imputation reading claims for particular verses.4

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The transformative reading is not special pleading. “Made righteous” (Rom 5:19) is Paul’s own word; the “love shed abroad” (5:5) really is an infusion into the heart; the regeneration-and-renewal of Titus 3 really does put justification inside the saving change, not beside it; and the fathers really did read iustificare as “make righteous.” A doctrine that reduces justification to a verdict wholly external to the person has to account for all of this, and for the New Testament’s refusal ever to leave the justified unchanged.

Where it is overstated or contested. The reading strains at the points where the forensic reading is strongest. First, “justify” (dikaioō) is court-vocabulary whose native antonym is “condemn” (Rom 8:33–34): to justify is at least also to declare a status, and Trent’s collapse of the verdict into the renewal has to absorb that legal grain rather than explain it. Second, Romans 4 credits righteousness to one still called ungodly (4:5) “without works” (4:6) and sets the reckoning expressly against debt (4:4) — a status-change that precedes the substance-change, which is exactly what a pure infusion model has trouble timing. Third, Trent’s further step — that the infused righteousness is “increased… through good works” which “truly merit” eternal life (canons 24, 32) — imports the merit machinery the wiki weighs, and finds contested, on the treasury pages; the Reformers’ charge is that this makes acceptance rest partly on the believer’s own renewed righteousness, so that assurance is lost. That charge is answerable — the righteousness is itself God’s gift, per Augustine’s “God crowns His own gifts” — but it is a real cost the infusion reading carries and the imputation reading does not.5

Assessment

Contested. The infusion reading has the stronger claim on Paul’s “made righteous” and “charity poured forth” language, on the renewal texts, and on the pre-Reformation concept of justification, which the fathers did not sever from the healing of the person. What it must concede is the forensic grain the rival reading presses: “justify” is court-language, Romans 4 credits righteousness to the ungodly apart from works and against debt, and Trent’s move from an infused righteousness to one “increased” by works that “truly merit” drags in the contested merit doctrine and the assurance problem. Strong on transformation, disputed on the verdict and on merit: contested — the mirror of the imputation page, which is what an honestly two-sided doctrine yields. The 1999 Joint Declaration is the modern attempt to hold both grains at once — “by grace alone… who renews our hearts” — and its own contested status is the measure of how live this dispute remains.
