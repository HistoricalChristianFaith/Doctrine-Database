Does Arnobius of Sicca's teaching on the extinction of the lost (c. 305) continue an earlier Christian teaching, or is it his own position?

Disputed reading · on Conditional immortality (annihilationism) · witness: Arnobius of Sicca

Related readings: Did the second-century writers who denied the soul's natural immortality hold conditional immortality?

The passage

For theirs is an intermediate state, as has been learned from Christ's teaching; and they are such that they may on the one hand perish if they have not known God, and on the other be delivered from death if they have given heed to His threats and proffered favours. And to make manifest what is unknown, this is man's real death, this which leaves nothing behind. … this, I say, is man's real death, when souls which know not God shall be consumed in long-protracted torment with raging fire.

— Arnobius of Sicca, Against the Heathen (Adversus Nationes), Book II.14 (c. 303–310).1

The question

Readers on both sides of the debate over the fate of the lost agree that Arnobius taught that souls which do not know God are finally annihilated, and that the soul receives immortality as God's gift.2 The dispute is whether he voices a teaching he received as a Christian, continuous with earlier writers, or a position of his own, reached by a recently converted rhetorician reasoning against the Platonists, which makes him the earliest Christian writer known to have held it. The answer bears on how far back the conditionalist account of the lost can be traced.

The last ante-Nicene voice of an inherited teaching

On this reading Arnobius states, more explicitly than any earlier writer, a conditionalist teaching he shared with the Christian writers before him and presented as learned from Christ.

Held by: Emmanuel Pétavel-Olliff (conditionalist, The Problem of Immortality, English trans. 1892); LeRoy E. Froom (Seventh-day Adventist, The Conditionalist Faith of Our Fathers, 1966); John H. Roller (Advent Christian, The Doctrine of Immortality in the Early Church, rev. 2008).3

Arnobius presents the soul's middle state as “learned from Christ's teaching” and writes that “we have been taught by the greatest teacher that souls are set not far from the gaping jaws of death; that they can, nevertheless, have their lives prolonged by the favour and kindness of the Supreme Ruler if only they try and study to know Him.”4 Pétavel calls him “last of the apologist Fathers” and sets him in a line running from Irenaeus and Clement of Alexandria to the young Athanasius, observing that “a doctrine so definite was detrimental to the reputation of Arnobius.”5 Froom titles his chapter “Arnobius of Africa—Last Ante-Nicene Conditionalist Spokesman” and holds that “Arnobius was in line with Justin Martyr, Tatian, Irenaeus, Theophilus of Antioch, and the entire group of the Apostolic Fathers before them.” Because the book that set out these views “was accepted as sufficient evidence of his Christianity to open for him the gates of Christian church membership,” Froom concludes that “Conditionalism, therefore, still was one of the accepted and recorded beliefs of the church in the early fourth century.” He explains the sparse quotation of the Bible by the audience: Arnobius “does not quote heavily from Scripture, which was largely unknown to his readers—and would not be admitted as evidence by them anyway—so he presents the facts of Scripture rather than actual quotations from the text.”6 Froom also cites the translator George E. McCracken (1949), who grants that “For Arnobius the soul can only be mortal and he appears to be of the opinion that this view is orthodox Christianity.”7 Roller reads Arnobius's report that “among learned men…there is strife as to the nature of the soul” as “an important insight into the fact that a ‘debate’ was in progress between Naturalists and Conditionalists in Arnobius' time,” and gathers the statements of Book II (chs. 36, 53, 62, 64) to conclude, with Froom, that “Arnobius was a militant Conditionalist.”8

The earliest known Christian statement of the view, and the convert's own position

On this reading Arnobius is the earliest Christian writer certainly known to have taught the extinction of the wicked, and the view belongs to his own account of the soul, worked out as a new convert with little knowledge of Scripture.

Held by: Stewart D. F. Salmond (United Free Church of Scotland, The Christian Doctrine of Immortality, 1895); Thomas J. Shahan (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1907); H. C. G. Moule (Anglican, Dictionary of Christian Biography, 1911 ed.); George E. McCracken (Ancient Christian Writers 7, 1949).9

Salmond holds that annihilation “has been taught now and again by individual Christian theologians at least from the beginning of the fourth century onwards,” and that Arnobius “is the first writer of note certainly known to us to have been of this way of thinking”: to him “the human soul, with its imperfection and sin, could neither come direct from God, nor be itself immortal.”10 Moule ties the view to that account of the soul's origin: “Its sin, imperfection, and inborn infirmity (he holds) forbid the belief that it comes direct from the Supreme Cause. It cannot for the like reasons be immortal (i.e. absolutely and per se); it outlives the body, but depends wholly on the gift of God for eternal duration.” Arnobius thinks the soul and the lower animals “the work of some intermediate creator,” and writes that souls “are not the children of the Supreme Ruler” but that “some other is their parent.”11 Moule finds that “Of the Holy Scriptures Arnobius appears to have known very little,” quoting only one text verbatim, explains this by “the alleged circumstances of the composition of the work,” and concludes that “the authority of Arnobius on points of Christian doctrine is reduced almost ad nihilum by these indications; and we can hardly wonder that in the 5th cent. his treatise was banished by pope Gelasius to the index of apocryphal works”. Shahan writes that Arnobius “is more earnest in his defence of Christianity than correct in his tenets,” holding that “the human soul is not the work of God, but of an intermediate being, and is not immortal by nature, but capable of putting on immortality as a grace.”12 McCracken, who writes that Arnobius “does not believe that the soul is by nature immortal though it is capable of becoming so by grace,” judges him “hardly orthodox at every point” though “it would be quite another thing to accuse him of being heretical,” and traces “his divergence from accepted Christian belief” to “an imperfect acquaintance with Christian teaching and a tendency to carry over into the new faith certain pagan ideas which he did not realize were incompatible with Christianity.”13 On this reading the conditionalist conclusion and the lesser maker of souls arrive together in Book II as parts of one anthropology, the work of a rhetorician whom Jerome remembers as a teacher at Sicca and whose book, by Shahan's account of Jerome's Chronicle, was written to overcome the local bishop's doubts about the sincerity of his conversion.
