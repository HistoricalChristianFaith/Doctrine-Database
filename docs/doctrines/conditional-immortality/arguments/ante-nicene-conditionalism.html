Did the second-century writers who denied the soul's natural immortality hold conditional immortality?

Disputed reading · on Conditional immortality (annihilationism) · witnesses: Justin Martyr, Tatian, Theophilus of Antioch, Irenaeus of Lyons

Related readings: Does Arnobius of Sicca's teaching on the extinction of the lost (c. 305) continue an earlier Christian teaching, or is it his own position?

The passage

But if it lives, it lives not as being life, but as the partaker of life; but that which partakes of anything, is different from that of which it does partake. Now the soul partakes of life, since God wills it to live. Thus, then, it will not even partake [of life] when God does not will it to live.

But I do not say, indeed, that all souls die; for that were truly a piece of good fortune to the evil. What then? The souls of the pious remain in a better place, while those of the unjust and wicked are in a worse, waiting for the time of judgment. Thus some which have appeared worthy of God never die; but others are punished so long as God wills them to exist and to be punished.

— Justin Martyr, Dialogue with Trypho 6 and 5 (c. 155–160).1

The soul is not in itself immortal, O Greeks, but mortal. Yet it is possible for it not to die. If, indeed, it knows not the truth, it dies, and is dissolved with the body, but rises again at last at the end of the world with the body, receiving death by punishment in immortality. But, again, if it acquires the knowledge of God, it dies not, although for a time it be dissolved.

— Tatian, Address to the Greeks 13.2

He was by nature neither mortal nor immortal. For if He had made him immortal from the beginning, He would have made him God. Again, if He had made him mortal, God would seem to be the cause of his death. Neither, then, immortal nor yet mortal did He make him, but, as we have said above, capable of both; so that if he should incline to the things of immortality, keeping the commandment of God, he should receive as reward from Him immortality, and should become God; but if, on the other hand, he should turn to the things of death, disobeying God, he should himself be the cause of death to himself.

— Theophilus of Antioch, To Autolycus II.27.3

The question

Several second-century Christian writers deny that the soul, or man as created, is immortal by its own nature and make its life depend on the will of God. The dispute is whether these statements attest conditional immortality (the final extinction of the lost) as the teaching of the earliest post-apostolic church, later displaced by a doctrine of the naturally immortal soul; or whether they state that the soul is a creature, consistently with its unending existence; or whether they are too inconsistent to decide the matter. The answer bears on which account of the fate of the lost can claim continuity with the second century.

The original teaching of the early church, later displaced by a Platonic doctrine of the soul

On this reading the second-century writers taught that immortality is God's gift to the saved and that the wicked finally cease to exist, and this was the church's earliest view until a doctrine of the innately immortal soul entered from Greek philosophy.

Held by: Emmanuel Pétavel-Olliff (conditionalist, The Problem of Immortality, English trans. 1892); LeRoy E. Froom (Seventh-day Adventist, The Conditionalist Faith of Our Fathers, 1965–66); John H. Roller (Advent Christian, The Doctrine of Immortality in the Early Church, rev. 2008); Matthew L. Chambers (Faulkner University, “Plato's Shadow,” 2024).4

Pétavel reads Irenaeus as seeming “to exhaust the vocabulary at his disposal in order to deny the immortality of the unsaved,” calls Perpetua's hope of “a perpetual life” “the true faith of the primitive Church,” and holds that this doctrine “was, as it were, drowned in the rising tide of the Platonic theory which was made to triumph in the Church by the false Clementines, Tertullian, Minucius Felix, Cyprian, Jerome, and especially Augustine.”5 Froom presents Irenaeus's statement of faith as “the generally accepted faith of Christians at that time (between A.D. 182 and 188)” and identifies Athenagoras of Athens, who wrote that God “made man of an immortal soul and a body,” as “the first Christian ecclesiastic” to teach “the innate, inalienable, indefeasible immortality of the soul,” “about the year A.D. 187,” resting his case “not on Scripture, but on philosophical argument.”6,7 On this reading the earlier writers state the doctrine in their own words: created things “may be blotted out and cease to exist,” so that “souls both die and are punished” (Justin); the soul “is not in itself immortal” (Tatian); immortality is a “reward” for keeping God's commandment (Theophilus); and the one who rejects the gift of life “deprives himself of [the privilege of] continuance for ever and ever” (Irenaeus).8 Roller, classifying thirty writers from AD 95 to c. 310, counts twenty conditionalists to nine “Naturalists,” finds “that almost all of the Church Fathers who wrote before AD 200 were Conditionalists,” and concludes “that Conditional Immortality was the original, and predominant, doctrine of the early Church.”9 Chambers argues that “Plato's concept of the soul as innately immortal and indestructible had a profoundly unbiblical influence upon many of the early church fathers' views regarding human nature, the final judgment of the wicked, and God's gift of immortality to believers.”10

A teaching that the soul is a creature, compatible with its unending existence

On this reading the second-century writers denied that the soul is immortal by its own nature in order to affirm that it is created, while holding that it continues in being by God's will; the same writers speak elsewhere of the punishment of the wicked as everlasting.

Held by: Stewart D. F. Salmond (United Free Church of Scotland, The Christian Doctrine of Immortality, 1895); Joseph Hontheim (Catholic, “Hell,” Catholic Encyclopedia, 1910); Georges Florovsky (Orthodox, “The ‘Immortality’ of the Soul,” 1952); Thomas Farrar (Catholic, review of Fudge, 2019).11

Salmond holds that the statements adduced for conditionalism “mean that the soul is not absolutely self-subsistent, but depends for its existence and its survival on God,” and that the same writers “speak also of the ‘unquenchable’ fire (Ignatius), ‘the perpetual torment of eternal fire’ (Polycarp)” and “‘eternal punishments’ (Theophilus).” When Justin calls souls “not naturally immortal,” “he refers simply to the fact that they are created and not self-existent”; and Justin “sets the punishment which is eternal over against that which is but for a thousand years,”12 writing in the First Apology that the wicked “are now to undergo everlasting punishment; and not only, as Plato said, for a period of a thousand years,” and that “sensation remains to all who have ever lived, and eternal punishment is laid up.”13 Hontheim cites Justin (Apol. II.8) and Irenaeus (Against Heresies V.27.2) among the Fathers who, “from the very earliest times, are unanimous in teaching that the wicked will be punished after death,” holds that on the eternity of that punishment “The teaching of the fathers is not less clear and decisive,” and lists among the conditionalists the Valentinians “and later on also Arnobius.”14 Farrar reads Justin's “unstated corollary” in the Dialogue as being “that the souls of the wicked, though essentially mortal, will not actually die because God wills them to be punished perpetually,” prefers such “a coherent synthesis” to “positing that Justin's theology was self-contradictory,” and holds that “outside of Dialogue 4.6-6.2, Justin consistently takes a traditionalist view of final punishment.”15 Florovsky places the apologists' denial within “the doctrine of Creation”: a creature may cease to exist, but “‘May cease,’ however, does not mean necessarily ‘will (actually) cease.’ St. Justin was not a ‘conditionalist,’ and his name has been invoked by the defenders of a ‘conditional immortality’ quite in vain.” He traces the same teaching, that created spirits are immortal not by nature but by the grace of God, through Irenaeus, John of Damascus and the synodical letter of Sophronius of Jerusalem received at the Sixth Ecumenical Council (681): the soul's existence is “conditioned by the creative fiat of God,” within a created world that has “a contingent beginning, yet no end.”16

Statements too inconsistent to settle the question

On this reading the earliest writers' statements about the soul and about punishment point in different directions, so the patristic record cannot decide between conditional immortality and unending punishment.

Held by: Edward Fudge (conditionalist, The Fire That Consumes, 1982; 3rd ed. 2011) ⚠ unverified; Philippe Bobichon (editor of Justin's Dialogue) ⚠ unverified.17

Farrar reports that Fudge sets passages that “seem to feature a ‘Traditionalist Justin’” “against certain others that seem to feature a ‘Conditionalist Justin,’” and quotes him: “Round and round they have gone over Justin...In the end, most non-biblical references are of little help except for refuting the dogmatic extremes too commonly found on both sides. We need someone to explain the explainers. Better to read them quickly, and turn our attention back to Scripture.” In Farrar's account Fudge treats the apologists of the late second and early third centuries “(Tatian, Athenagoras, Tertullian, but not Irenaeus)” as the first he “unambiguously acknowledges to have believed in unending conscious punishment,” under the chapter title “The Apologists: A Fire That Torments.”18 Bobichon, as Farrar quotes him, finds that “Les affirmations que contiennent le Dialogue et l' Apologie à propos de la survie des âmes et de la durée du châtiment ne paraissent pas toujours cohérentes.” On this reading Justin's “souls both die and are punished” and his “everlasting punishment” stand side by side in his own writings. For Fudge, as Farrar quotes him, the second-century record leaves the question of the final fate of the lost to the exegesis of Scripture.
