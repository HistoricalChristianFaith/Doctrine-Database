Arnobius of Sicca on conditional immortality

Dates: c. 305 (the North African rhetorician Arnobius of Sicca, fl. c. 303–311, d. c. 330; a convert who wrote his seven-book apology Adversus Nationes — “Against the Heathen” — during and after the Great Persecution, teacher of Lactantius) · Wikipedia
Summary: Against the Platonists, Arnobius denies that the soul is naturally immortal: souls are of an “intermediate” nature, able either to perish or to be granted immortality by God, and the lost, after “long-protracted torment,” are finally annihilated — a “real death” that “leaves nothing behind.” Salmond (1895) calls him the earliest writer of note certainly known to have held this view; Froom (1966) calls him the “Last Ante-Nicene Conditionalist Spokesman.”

Claims & evidence

Against Plato on the soul's immortality (II.14)

Arnobius attacks the Platonic axiom of the soul's inherent immortality. Plato, he grants, is “a man of no little wisdom,” but Arnobius charges him with inconsistency: Plato calls the soul “immortal, everlasting, and without bodily substance” and also has it suffer punishment in the infernal rivers, while for Arnobius pain and immortality cannot both hold of a simple substance.

But what man does not see that that which is immortal, which is simple, cannot be subject to any pain; that that, on the contrary, cannot be immortal which does suffer pain?1

— Against the Heathen, Book II.14. In Book II Arnobius treats the soul's continuance as a gift it may or may not receive: immortality is conferred by God on those who know Him, not built into the soul as such.

The soul's “intermediate” state (II.14)

For they are cast in, and being annihilated, pass away vainly in everlasting destruction. For theirs is an intermediate state, as has been learned from Christ's teaching; and they are such that they may on the one hand perish if they have not known God, and on the other be delivered from death if they have given heed to His threats and proffered favours.2

— Against the Heathen, Book II.14. Arnobius places the soul in a media qualitas, a middle nature between two ends: destruction for those who do not know God, deliverance “from death” for those who heed Him. He grounds this “from Christ's teaching” (II.14) and, in II.32, from what “we have been taught by the greatest teacher.”3

“Man's real death” (II.14)

And to make manifest what is unknown, this is man's real death, this which leaves nothing behind. For that which is seen by the eyes is only a separation of soul from body, not the last end—annihilation: this, I say, is man's real death, when souls which know not God shall be consumed in long-protracted torment with raging fire, into which certain fiercely cruel beings shall cast them, who were unknown before Christ, and brought to light only by His wisdom.4

— Against the Heathen, Book II.14. Arnobius distinguishes two “deaths”: the ordinary parting of soul from body, which is “not the last end”; and the “real death” of the soul that does not know God, which is annihilation. The wicked are “consumed in long-protracted torment” before they cease to be.

A death “not bringing sudden annihilation” (II.61)

…a cruel death awaits you when freed from the bonds of body, not bringing sudden annihilation, but destroying by the bitterness of its grievous and long-protracted punishment.5

— Against the Heathen, Book II.61. The penalty of the lost is again a death, protracted and painful, ending in their destruction. Froom summarizes the teaching as that “the incorrigibly impenitent sinner is destined, after a due and determined period of punishment, to pass out of existence.”6

Reception and debate

  • Continuity or first statement. Readers on both sides agree that Arnobius taught the final annihilation of souls that do not know God. Pétavel (1892), Froom (1966) and Roller (2008) read him as continuing a conditionalist teaching held by earlier Christian writers, Froom placing him “in line with Justin Martyr, Tatian, Irenaeus, Theophilus of Antioch, and the entire group of the Apostolic Fathers before them.” Salmond (1895) calls him “the first writer of note certainly known to us to have been of this way of thinking,” and Moule and Shahan treat the view as part of his own account of the soul.7 The readings are set out on Does Arnobius of Sicca's teaching on the extinction of the lost (c. 305) continue an earlier Christian teaching, or is it his own position?
  • The soul's origin. In II.36 Arnobius writes that souls “are not the children of the Supreme Ruler” but that “some other is their parent.” Moule describes him as thinking the soul and the lower animals “the work of some intermediate creator,” and remarks on “the faintness of the tinge of Gnosticism in its pages”; Shahan writes that for Arnobius “the human soul is not the work of God, but of an intermediate being.”8
  • Use of Scripture. Moule counts one text quoted verbatim in the seven books (1 Cor. 3:19) and finds that “Of the Holy Scriptures Arnobius appears to have known very little”; Froom explains that he “presents the facts of Scripture rather than actual quotations from the text” to readers who would not have admitted Scripture as evidence.9
  • Immortality and moral restraint. In II.29 Arnobius asks who, hearing “that the soul is immortal,” “would not throw himself headlong into all kinds of vice.”10
  • Later reception. Jerome records him as “a most successful teacher of rhetoric at Sicca in Africa during the reign of Diocletian” and, elsewhere, as “lengthy and unequal, and often confused from not making a proper division of his subject.” Moule writes that “in the 5th cent. his treatise was banished by pope Gelasius to the index of apocryphal works” (the list's ascription to Gelasius is disputed: George McCracken, 1949, calls it a “sixth-century” decree “once ascribed to Pope Gelasius I,” and Ernst von Dobschütz, 1912 ⚠ unverified, dated it between 519 and 553). The work survives in a single ninth-century manuscript.11
  • He is distinct from the later Arnobius the Younger (fl. c. 450), a different writer.
  • The final-state claim recorded here is distinct from soul sleep, which concerns the intermediate state between death and resurrection; Arnobius's passages concern the last end of the lost.
