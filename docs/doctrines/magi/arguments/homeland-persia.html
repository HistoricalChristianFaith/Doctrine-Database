Where did the magi of Matthew 2 come from?

Disputed reading · on Who Were the Magi? · witnesses: Justin Martyr, John Chrysostom

Related readings: Were the magi Zoroastrian priests? · How is Balaam’s “star out of Jacob” related to the magi’s star?

The passage

when Jesus was born in Bethlehem of Judaea in the days of Herod the king, behold, there came wise men from the east to Jerusalem, saying, Where is he that is born King of the Jews? for we have seen his star in the east, and are come to worship him.

— Matthew 2:1–2 (KJV), source.1

The question

Matthew calls the visitors magoi and says only that they came “from the east” (apo anatolōn). Since the second century writers have placed them in Arabia, in Persia (Media) or in Babylonia, each from a different kind of evidence — the gifts and the tribute-prophecies, the word magoi, the history of astronomy — and some modern commentators hold that the text leaves the country unnamed. The question bears on how the earliest readers pictured the Gentiles who came to Bethlehem.

Arabia, the land of the gifts

On this reading the magi came from the Arabian lands south and east of Judaea, whose gold, frankincense and myrrh the prophets foresaw being brought to Zion’s king.

Held by: Justin Martyr (Dialogue with Trypho, c. 155–160); Tertullian (Against Marcion 3.13, c. 207); Epiphanius of Salamis (Expositio fidei 8); Dwight Longenecker (Mystery of the Magi, 2017).2

The earliest homeland on record. Justin calls the visitors “Magi who came from Arabia” and repeats it across the Dialogue. He reads Psalm 72 (“the kings of Arabia and Seba” bringing gifts, “the gold of Arabia”) and Isaiah 8:4 (“the power of Damascus” carried off before “the king of Assyria”) as fulfilled at Bethlehem, placing Damascus “in the region of Arabia.” Tertullian follows the same psalm and notes that “Damascus was anciently deemed to belong to Arabia.”3

The gifts and the prophets. Frankincense and myrrh are harvested from trees of southern Arabia and the Horn of Africa. The texts that foresee the nations bringing gold and incense to Zion name Arabian peoples: “the dromedaries of Midian and Ephah; all they from Sheba shall come: they shall bring gold and incense” (Isaiah 60:6), the line of Keturah in north-west Arabia, with Sheba and Seba in Psalm 72.4

The Nabataean form. Longenecker identifies the magi as court diviners of the Nabataean kingdom, whose capital was Petra. Nabataea lay east and south-east of Judaea, so “from the east” is satisfied nearby; its religion was astral, with a zodiac adorning the temple at Khirbet et-Tannur; its court kept diviners; and it controlled the frankincense and myrrh routes. He holds that by the first century magos had generalized to any court astrologer. On his reading the magi were neighbours of Herod’s kingdom, a diplomatic embassy from an adjacent Arab realm.5

Persia (Media), the homeland of the word magoi

On this reading the magi were members of the Iranian priesthood whose Old Persian name, magu-, the Greek magoi transliterates.

Held by: Clement of Alexandria (Stromata I.15, c. 200); John Chrysostom (Homilies on Matthew VI, c. 390); Edwin M. Yamauchi (Persia and the Bible, 1990); Anders Hultgård (1998; 2015), on the Iranian milieu.6

The word. Matthew withholds the number, names, rank and country, but chooses a word. In its oldest secure usage, in Herodotus, the Magi are one of the six Median tribes and a priestly office: “no sacrifice can be offered without a Magus” to chant over the victim. The term is a Greek borrowing of Old Persian magu-, attested on Darius’s Behistun inscription, and the Persian magi were the class credited with celestial and dream lore. Origen, answering Celsus, insists that the visitors were Magi and not the Babylonian “Chaldeans.”7

The patristic naming. Clement speaks of “the Magi of the Persians, who foretold the Saviour’s birth, and came into the land of Judæa guided by a star.” Chrysostom, preaching at Antioch, calls them Persians throughout: “the Persian, the barbarian” left his home for the child, and the Jews learned of their Messiah “from a Persian tongue.” A ninth-century letter reports that the Persians who overran Palestine in 614 spared the Church of the Nativity because its façade mosaic showed the magi in Persian dress.8

The Iranian milieu. Hultgård, Antonio Panaino and Yamauchi set the episode against a magu- priesthood that watched the sky, a Zoroastrian expectation of a coming saviour, and long Jewish–Iranian contact. On this reading the word Matthew chose already points east to Iran, and a priestly embassy to a newborn king is the kind of journey such men were thought to make.9

Babylon (Chaldea), the home of star-science

On this reading the magi were Babylonian star-diviners, heirs of the astronomy over whose practitioners Daniel was once set, who learned a Jewish king’s star-prophecy from the exiles among them.

Held by: Maximus of Turin (Homily 18 on the Epiphany) and Theodotus of Ancyra, as the Catholic Encyclopedia reports them; David W. Hughes (Nature, 1976; 1979) and Simo Parpola (Bible Review, 2001), on the star’s Babylonian astronomical setting.10

The astronomy. Babylonia was antiquity’s premier centre of celestial observation: its astronomical diaries run for centuries, and the zodiac and mathematical prediction were Babylonian achievements. “His star in the east” sits naturally in a Chaldean setting. Hughes reads the star as the triple conjunction of Jupiter and Saturn in Pisces in 7 BC, an event meaningful to Babylonian astrologers, with Jupiter the royal star and Pisces long linked to the Jews; Parpola argues that Babylonian astronomy “holds the key.”11

The exiles and Daniel. The Septuagint of Daniel 2 calls Babylon’s wise men magoi, and Daniel, a Jew, was made “chief of the governors over all the wise men of Babylon” (Daniel 2:48). The Jewish community planted in Babylon at the Exile remained and became a great centre of Jewish learning, so Gentile scholars in daily contact with it could know the Scriptures’ hope of an anointed king and Balaam’s oracle of his star (Numbers 24:17). On this reading Babylon supplies both halves of the episode: the observers of the sign and the channel by which they read it as a Jewish king’s.12

An eastern land Matthew leaves unnamed

On this reading Matthew fixes the visitors’ class and direction but names no country, and each homeland is a later reader’s identification.

Held by: Raymond E. Brown (The Birth of the Messiah, rev. ed. 1993).13

“From the east.” Brown reads Matthew’s magi as Gentile astrologers from an unspecified eastern land: the Gospel’s only geographic datum is apo anatolōn, and the evangelist gives no country.14

A word for a class. By the Hellenistic and Roman periods magos covered astrologers, dream-interpreters and sorcerers of many nations. The Septuagint uses it of the Babylonian wise men in Daniel, and the New Testament’s other magoi are Simon of Samaria (Acts 8:9–11) and Bar-Jesus of Cyprus (Acts 13:6–8). The Encyclopaedia Iranica places the equation of the Magi with Zoroaster in Hellenistic writing after Herodotus.15

Goods of the whole trade. Gold, frankincense and myrrh travelled by trade across the ancient world, reaching Judaea, Persia and the lands between, as the Tyndale House survey of the question records. On this reading the word marks the visitors as eastern sky-watchers, the gifts mark them as bearers of costly tribute, and Arabia, Persia and Babylon are three ways the church filled in the country the evangelist left open.16
