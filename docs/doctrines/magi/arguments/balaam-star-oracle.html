How is Balaam’s “star out of Jacob” (Numbers 24:17) related to the magi’s star?

Disputed reading · on Who Were the Magi? · witness: Origen

Related readings: Where did the magi come from? · Were the magi Zoroastrian priests?

The wider history of Numbers 24:17 as a messianic proof-text, with its Qumran, Vespasian, Bethlehem and Bar Kokhba claimants, is on the Star Prophecy timeline; the oracle’s own date and pre-Christian reception are set out on the Balaam timeline’s page “How old is the ‘Star out of Jacob,’ and was it read messianically before Christianity?”; and what the star meant for star-divination is on the astrology timeline.

The passage

“I am therefore of opinion that, possessing as they did the prophecies of Balaam, which Moses also records, inasmuch as Balaam was celebrated for such predictions, and finding among them the prophecy about the star, and the words, ‘I shall show him to him, but not now; I deem him happy, although he will not be near,’ they conjectured that the man whose appearance had been foretold along with that of the star, had actually come into the world.”

— Origen, Against Celsus 1.60, source; the oracle is “there shall come a Star out of Jacob, and a Sceptre shall rise out of Israel” (Numbers 24:17).1

The question

Balaam, the seer fetched from “Pethor, which is by the river” and “the mountains of the east,” prophesied a star out of Jacob. Matthew never quotes the oracle. At issue is how the sources relate the oracle to the magi’s star: as a prophecy the eastern magi themselves possessed and acted on, or as a text the evangelist had in view when he told the episode. The question bears on how the earliest readers explained why Gentile star-watchers read a star as a Jewish king’s.

The magi had Balaam’s prophecy and acted on it

On this reading the magi were Balaam’s successors in the east, who kept his oracle of the star and set out when the star appeared.

Held by: Origen (Against Celsus 1.59–60, c. 248); Eusebius of Caesarea (Demonstratio Evangelica 9.1, c. 314); Jacobus de Voragine (Golden Legend, c. 1260).2

From identification to history. Irenaeus (c. 180) grounds the magi’s star in the oracle: “whose star also Balaam thus prophesied.” Origen gives the mechanism: the magi possessed Balaam’s prophecies, found the one about the star, and concluded that the man foretold with it had come; he develops the link in his Homilies on Numbers, and later Fathers hint that he drew on a “book of Balaam,” now lost. Eusebius states it as history: “Balaam’s successors moved by this (for the prediction was preserved most likely among them), when they noticed in the heavens a strange star … hastened to arrive at Palestine.”3

An eastern seer and his heirs. Balaam came from Mesopotamia, a professional diviner who received a true oracle from the God of Israel: the profile of Gentile star-readers who receive a genuine revelation. An oracle spoken by an eastern seer could pass to eastern diviners, and it answers the question the Gospel raises — how foreigners knew that a star meant a king of the Jews.4

A long tradition. Anders Hultgård traces the reading from Origen and Eusebius through Gregory of Nyssa, Theophylact, Romanos the Melodist, Nicholas of Lyra, Erasmus and Lancelot Andrewes. The Golden Legend makes the descent explicit: “These kings were successors of Balaam, and came at the vision and sight of the star, by the prophecy of their father.” On this reading the magi came because they held the one prophecy that tied a star to Israel’s king.5

Matthew told the episode with Balaam’s oracle in view

On this reading the oracle is the evangelist’s template: Matthew composed the star episode with Numbers 24 in mind and cast the magi as Balaam’s successors.

Held by: W. D. Davies and Dale C. Allison (Matthew I, ICC, 1988; they also judge Eusebius’s Balaam-heirs reading “on the right track”); Raymond E. Brown (The Birth of the Messiah, rev. ed. 1993); Anders Hultgård (2015), on the transmission question.6

A live messianic text. Numbers 24:17 was a leading proof-text in Second Temple Judaism. The Qumran Testimonia (4Q175) collect it among their eschatological texts, the Damascus Document (CD 7:18–21) reads it of a coming ruler, Rabbi Akiva hailed Simon ben Kosiba as “Bar Kokhba,” son of the star, in the revolt of 132–135, and the Targums render the star as a king or the Messiah.7

The Numbers narrative in Matthew 2. The magi echo Balaam, eastern seers with a star-prophecy of a Jewish king; Herod echoes Balak, the hostile king who tries to turn the seer against Israel and fails. Philo already portrays “the magus Balaam” receiving an authentic prophetic spirit, and Brown makes Numbers 22–24 “the most likely background” for Matthew 2: the magi “receive a further revelation from the Jewish Scriptures” as Balaam did. Davies and Allison conclude that “Matthew probably thought of the magi as ‘Balaam’s successors.’”8

An allusion in the telling. Hultgård, studying the oracle, the star and the lost Balaam books, describes the fit as one of milieu, the channel by which the prophecy would have reached the magi being unrecorded. On this reading the allusion is structural: Matthew need not quote the oracle for it to shape the scene, and it belongs to the evangelist’s composition, alongside the Moses typology of the threatened infant.9
