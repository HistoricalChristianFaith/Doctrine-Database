Were the magi kings?

Disputed reading · on Who Were the Magi? · witness: Tertullian

Related readings: How many magi came to Bethlehem? · Where did the magi come from?

The passage

“The kings of Tarshish and of the isles shall bring presents: the kings of Sheba and Seba shall offer gifts. Yea, all kings shall fall down before him.”

— Psalm 72:10–11 (KJV), source; read with Isaiah 60:3, 6 and set against Matthew’s “wise men from the east” who bring gold, frankincense and myrrh.1

The question

Matthew calls the visitors magoi and gives them no rank. From the second century (Justin, c. 155–160) the psalm and Isaiah’s kings bearing gold and incense were read as fulfilled at Bethlehem, and the Western church came to call the magi the “three kings.” At issue is whether the visitors held royal office, or whether the royal title belongs to the prophecies through which the scene was read.

Kings of the nations bringing tribute

On this reading the magi were kings, the vanguard of the nations’ rulers foretold to bow before the anointed king.

Held by: “the East,” as Tertullian reports it (Against Marcion 3.13, c. 207); the Syriac Revelation of the Magi (composition late second or early third century, in its editor’s dating); Jacobus de Voragine (Golden Legend, c. 1260); John of Hildesheim (Historia Trium Regum, c. 1364).2

The earliest report. Tertullian, quoting the psalm’s “kings of Arabia and Saba” who “shall offer to Him gifts,” writes that “the East generally regarded the magi as kings” (nam et magos reges habuit fere oriens), and in An Answer to the Jews bids his reader believe “those Eastern magi, dowering with gold and incense the infancy of Christ as a king.” The Revelation of the Magi describes its twelve as “wise men and kings” of the land of Shir, in the summary of its editor Brent Landau.3

The prophecies and Matthew’s frame. Matthew stages the scene around kingship: the star heralds one “born King of the Jews,” and Herod, the reigning king, is its foil. The gifts are the tribute the prophets said kings would bring to Zion’s risen light: “kings to the brightness of thy rising” who “shall bring gold and incense” (Isaiah 60:3, 6). Matthew works by fulfilment, and read in that mode the visitors at Bethlehem are the nations’ kings doing homage.4

The feast of the nations. The Latin liturgy of the Epiphany spoke of the “three kings,” and the medieval West gave them crowns, names and a shrine. The Golden Legend calls them kings (“These kings were successors of Balaam”); the relics translated to Cologne in 1164 were venerated as the bodies of the Three Kings; and John of Hildesheim’s History of the Three Kings told their lives in full. On this reading the crown names what the episode is: the Gentile rulers of the world kneeling to the true King.5

Priest-astrologers, a learned class serving kings

On this reading magoi names a priestly and star-reading class, and the royal title came to the visitors from the prophecies through which the church read the scene.

Held by: Raymond E. Brown (The Birth of the Messiah, rev. ed. 1993); Richard C. Trexler (The Journey of the Magi, 1997).6

The word. In its oldest secure usage, in Herodotus, the Magi are a Median tribe and a priestly office, present at every Persian sacrifice to chant over the victim, a class in the king’s service. The term later widened to astrologers and sorcerers, the sense behind English magic. The Fathers who describe the magi as people treat them as a learned, star-reading class: Origen distinguishes them from the Babylonian “Chaldeans,” and Chrysostom preaches them as Persian sky-watchers.7

Wise men in Leo. Leo the Great, preaching at Rome on the Epiphany (c. 450), calls the visitors “wise men” and assigns the kingship to Christ, the gold being paid to the King. His nineteenth-century editor notes: “It will be noticed that S. Leo knows nothing of their being kings, though that tradition is apparently as old as Tertullian.” Caesarius of Arles, whom Robin Jensen cross-references on the magi’s royal status, gives the gold in his Epiphany sermon as a sign of Christ’s royal dignity.8

A title from the prophecies. Brown reads Matthew’s magi as Gentile astrologers and classes the crown, with the number three and the names, among the post-biblical additions to the story; Trexler writes the history of that reshaping. On this reading the scene fulfils the prophesied homage of kings through visitors of another rank, and the crown records how the church heard Psalm 72 and Isaiah 60 in it.9
