How many magi came to Bethlehem (Matthew 2:1–12)?

Disputed reading · on Who Were the Magi? · witness: Origen

Related readings: Were the magi kings? · Where did the magi come from?

The passage

“And when they had opened their treasures, they presented unto him gifts; gold, and frankincense, and myrrh.”

— Matthew 2:11 (KJV), source.1

The question

Matthew names three kinds of gift and gives no count of the people who brought them. At issue is whether the number of the magi can be recovered from the text or from early tradition — as three, one for each gift, or as twelve, the count of the Syriac Revelation of the Magi — or whether Matthew leaves it open. The answer bears on how the Western three and the Eastern twelve are read: as memory of the event, or as the history of its interpretation.

Three magi, one for each gift

On this reading the three gifts of Matthew 2:11 mark three givers, and the early church’s three magi follow the text’s own symmetry.

Held by: Origen (Homilies on Genesis 14.3, mid-third century; on the number three only, drawn from Genesis 26); Leo the Great (Sermons on the Epiphany, c. 450); the Western liturgy and the medieval cult of the Three Kings.2

The gifts. Matthew lists gold, frankincense and myrrh, and on this reading a gift apiece pictures three offerings. The threefold meaning the early writers read in the gifts — gold for a king, frankincense for God, myrrh for one who would die, in Irenaeus and in Origen’s Against Celsus 1.60 — gave each gift its own confession and so its own bearer.3

The oldest numbered witnesses. Origen reads the three men who came to Isaac (Genesis 26:26) as a figure of the magi: “These three … can bear the figure of the Magi, who come from the regions of the East.” The earliest surviving image of the magi, the mid-third-century fresco in the Catacomb of Priscilla at Rome, shows three figures, identical in size and dress, advancing toward the enthroned child, and Robin Jensen reports three as the commonest count in the early Roman images.4

The settled Western count. Leo the Great preaches at Rome of “the three Magi” and of the incense offered to God, the myrrh to Man and the gold to the King. The Latin liturgy of the Epiphany carried the number, and the medieval West gave the three names, ages and continents and enshrined three bodies at Cologne in 1164. On this reading the three is the text’s gift-symmetry, held from the earliest numbered witness onward.5

Twelve magi, an order of the East

On this reading the magi were a company of twelve (or more), an eastern order that kept a prophecy of the star from the beginning of the world.

Held by: the Syriac Revelation of the Magi (surviving text c. 500; its composition dated by its editor Brent Landau to the late second or early third century); the Latin Opus Imperfectum in Matthaeum (fifth century).6

A named order. The Revelation of the Magi, told in the magi’s own voice, gives twelve of them and names each with his father. It calls them a mashritha, the Syriac word the New Testament uses for a great company, so that the twelve read as the leaders of a larger order. As Landau puts it, “In Rev. Magi there are twelve Magi, or possibly more, in contrast to the traditional enumeration of three.”7

A count with its own history. The twelve are descendants of Seth who keep, generation after generation, a prophecy given by Adam of a coming star; each month they climb the Mountain of Victories to pray in silence and read Seth’s prophecies as they wait for it. The number belongs to that story of a guarded expectation, not to the gifts. The Opus Imperfectum carried the account into the Latin West, and twelve became the count of the Syriac and Armenian East, whose churches transmit their own sets of names.8

Matthew leaves the number open

On this reading Matthew records the gifts and not the givers, and every count of the magi is a later inference.

Held by: Raymond E. Brown (The Birth of the Messiah, rev. ed. 1993); Richard C. Trexler (The Journey of the Magi, 1997).9

Gifts, not givers. Matthew 2:1–12 gives the magi no number, names, homeland or rank; the three in 2:11 are kinds of treasure, which an embassy of any size could carry. Brown classes the number with the names and the crowns as post-biblical additions to Matthew’s account, and Trexler writes the history of how those additions were made.10

A spread of early counts. Early Christian art shows the number varying: two in the Catacomb of Peter and Marcellinus, three at Priscilla and the Lateran, four in the Catacomb of Domitilla, and as many as eight on a vase in the Kircherian collection, as Jensen records. The Syriac East numbered twelve. Origen’s three is itself a figure drawn from the three visitors of Genesis 26, which he first reads as the three parts of philosophy. On this reading each count records how a community pictured the scene, and the range of counts is the record of that picturing.11
