Were the magi Zoroastrian priests?

Disputed reading · on Who Were the Magi? · witness: John Chrysostom

Related readings: Where did the magi come from? · How is Balaam’s “star out of Jacob” related to the magi’s star?

The passage

“Whose name shall be the victorious Saoshyant, and whose name shall be Astvat-ereta. He shall be Saoshyant (the beneficent one), because he shall benefit the whole bodily world.”

— Farvardin Yasht (Yasht 13.129), trans. Darmesteter, source, of the Zoroastrian saviour to come; set beside Matthew’s magoi “from the east” (Matthew 2:1).1

The question

Matthew’s word magoi is the Greek form of the title of the Iranian priesthood. At issue is what that word tells about the visitors’ religion: whether they were priests of the Persians, whether they were Zoroastrians drawn by their own expectation of a coming saviour, or whether by the first century the word named eastern astrologers without fixing a creed. The question bears on how the episode is set within the religious history of the Near East.

Priests of the Persians

On this reading the magi were members of the Persian priestly order that the word magoi names.

Held by: Clement of Alexandria (Stromata I.15, c. 200); John Chrysostom (Homilies on Matthew VI, c. 390); the Opus Imperfectum in Matthaeum (fifth century).2

The word and the order. In Herodotus the Magi are a Median tribe and a priestly office: no Persian sacrifice was performed without a magus to chant over the victim. The Greek magoi transliterates Old Persian magu-, the priestly title attested on Darius’s Behistun inscription, and that priesthood was the class credited with celestial and dream lore. A star-reading magos fits the title.3

The early naming. Clement speaks of “the Magi of the Persians, who foretold the Saviour’s birth, and came into the land of Judæa guided by a star.” Chrysostom, preaching on Matthew 2 at Antioch, takes them for Persians throughout, “the wiser sort” among the barbarians, who seem “to have been at home also teachers of their countrymen.” The Opus Imperfectum calls them eastern men who came from Persia. On this reading the Fathers who described the magi as people named the order the word itself denotes.4

Zoroastrian priests awaiting the Saoshyant

On this reading the magi were Zoroastrian priests whose own religion, with its expected world-saviour, gave them a reason to follow a royal birth-star.

Held by: Geo Widengren (Die Religionen Irans, 1965); Mary Boyce and Frantz Grenet (A History of Zoroastrianism III, 1991); Roy D. Kotansky (New Testament Studies, 2024).5

A pre-Christian saviour-hope. The Saoshyant (“the one who will bring benefit”), also named Astvat-ereta, appears in the Younger Avesta (Yasht 13.129; Yasht 19.88–96): a future deliverer who will make the world perfect and defeat the Lie, born, in the developed tradition, of a virgin from the seed of Zoroaster. A priesthood that both watched the sky and awaited a world-saviour is a setting in which a journey after a royal birth-star is coherent.6

The story told in the East. Boyce and Grenet set Matthew’s magi against a real Iranian priesthood and its saviour-expectation, and print the magi-legend of the Chronicle of Zuqnin — twelve magi keeping a generations-long watch for a star at a cave — as the Iranian form of the story. Kotansky argues that Matthew’s star-source reflects Zoroastrian traditions of magi divining a saviour’s birth from a star. On this reading the patristic Persian naming and the Iranian-background scholarship meet in one picture: eastern priest-astrologers whose religion gave them a reason to come.7

Eastern astrologers whose creed the word leaves open

On this reading magoi in Matthew’s day named eastern star-readers of more than one people, and the Zoroastrian star-and-saviour scheme belongs to later Iranian texts.

Held by: Raymond E. Brown (The Birth of the Messiah, rev. ed. 1993); Anders Hultgård (“Persian Apocalypticism,” 1998; 2015), who grants the Iranian milieu.8

A widened word. By the first century magos covered astrologers, dream-interpreters and sorcerers of many nations. The Septuagint uses magoi of the Babylonian wise men in Daniel, and the New Testament’s other magoi are Simon of Samaria and Bar-Jesus of Cyprus. The Encyclopaedia Iranica describes the Magi as a diverse priestly class across the Iranian world and places their equation with Zoroaster in Hellenistic writing after Herodotus. Brown reads Matthew’s magi as Gentile astrologers from an unspecified eastern land.9

The Iranian texts in their layers. Hultgård dates the elements. The Avestan core (Yasht 13.129; Yasht 19) names a coming saviour without a heralding star. The star-portent, the virgin birth and the scheme of three future saviours are set out in the Pahlavi books — the Bundahishn, the Dēnkard, the Zānd ī Wahman Yasn — redacted in the ninth century AD as accumulative works, which he holds may reflect contact with Jewish and Christian expectation; and the star-watch legend survives in a medieval Syriac Christian chronicle. On this reading the Iranian religious world is the setting in which eastern star-watching priests are intelligible, and Matthew’s word identifies their calling rather than their creed.10
