Irenaeus on the magi — the meaning of the three gifts

Dates: c. 180 (Against Heresies Book 3, written at Lyons under Eleutherus); Irenaeus b. c. 130 at Smyrna, a hearer of Polycarp, bishop of Lyons, d. c. 202 · Wikipedia
Summary: The earliest extant reading of what the magi’s three gifts mean. Matthew names gold, frankincense, and myrrh but does not interpret them; Irenaeus does, and later writers repeated his interpretation for centuries. Each gift, he says, confesses something about the child: myrrh that He was mortal and would die and be buried, gold that He was a king, frankincense that He was God. The offering becomes a threefold confession — death, kingship, divinity. Irenaeus draws no number of magi from the gifts; how many the magi were is set out as a disputed reading on the summary.

Claims & evidence

The three gifts as a threefold confession (Against Heresies 3.9.2)

Irenaeus is arguing, against those who split the Creator from the Father of Christ, that the God of the prophets and the God of the Gospel are one and the same — and he runs through Matthew’s infancy narrative to make the case. When he reaches the magi he pauses over their gifts, reading each as a declaration of who the worshipped child is. He first anchors the star in Balaam’s oracle (“There shall come a star out of Jacob”), then turns to the offering:

But Matthew says that the Magi, coming from the east, exclaimed “For we have seen His star in the east, and are come to worship Him;” and that, having been led by the star into the house of Jacob to Emmanuel, they showed, by these gifts which they offered, who it was that was worshipped; myrrh, because it was He who should die and be buried for the mortal human race; gold, because He was a King, “of whose kingdom is no end;” and frankincense, because He was God, who also “was made known in Judea,” and was “declared to those who sought Him not.”

— Irenaeus, Against Heresies 3.9.2 (c. 180), newadvent.org (ANF 1, trans. Roberts-Donaldson).1 The reading is typological: myrrh, a burial spice (cf. John 19:39–40), marks the child as one who will die; gold, the metal of kings, marks Him as king — and Irenaeus glosses it with Luke 1:33, “of whose kingdom is no end”; frankincense, the incense of the temple offered to God, marks Him as God, glossed from Psalm 76:1 (“in Judah is God known”) and Isaiah 65:1 (“declared to those who sought Him not”). For Irenaeus kingship, death, and Godhead are read off the treasure the magi opened.

The Balaam star, in the same breath

Immediately before the gifts, Irenaeus grounds the magi’s star in the oracle of Balaam — the “star out of Jacob” of Numbers 24:17 — making the star that the wise men followed a fulfilment of prophecy rather than a chance portent:

… and Emmanuel; whose star also Balaam thus prophesied: “There shall come a star out of Jacob, and a leader shall rise in Israel.”

— Irenaeus, Against Heresies 3.9.2 (c. 180), newadvent.org.2 This is the earliest extant link on this timeline between the magi’s star and Balaam, himself remembered as an eastern seer. Origen (c. 248) gives an account of how the magi came to know the oracle; how the oracle relates to the star is set out on the disputed-reading page “How is Balaam’s ‘star out of Jacob’ related to the magi’s star?”

Reception and placement

  • Irenaeus’s gift-symbolism — myrrh = death and burial, gold = kingship, frankincense = divinity — recurs in later writers, among them Origen (Against Celsus 1.60) and Leo the Great. Later writers vary the emphasis — some make myrrh mark the humanity generally rather than the burial, some read gold as almsgiving — while keeping the king / God / mortal-man scheme.
  • Irenaeus draws no number from the gifts — he does not say “three gifts, therefore three magi.” The count of three magi, one per gift, is made in later tradition; Origen’s own “three” (Homilies on Genesis 14.3) is a tentative figure drawn from the three visitors of Genesis 26 (see Origen’s detail page). How many the magi were is set out on the disputed-reading page “How many magi came to Bethlehem?”
  • The point of the passage in context is the unity of God: the same God who spoke through Balaam and the psalmists is the God the magi worshipped in the flesh. The gift-typology serves that larger argument.
  • Quotations follow the Roberts-Donaldson translation (ANF 1); the ANF text reads “for the mortal human race.” The full work is Against Heresies, Book 3, chapter 9.
