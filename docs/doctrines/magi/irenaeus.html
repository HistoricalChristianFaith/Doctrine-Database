Irenaeus on the magi — the meaning of the three gifts

Dates: c. 180 (Against Heresies Book 3, written at Lyons under Eleutherus); Irenaeus b. c. 130 at Smyrna, a hearer of Polycarp, bishop of Lyons, d. c. 202 · Wikipedia
Summary: The earliest surviving reading of what the magi’s three gifts mean. Matthew names gold, frankincense, and myrrh but does not interpret them; Irenaeus does, and his interpretation became the standard one. Each gift, he says, confesses something about the child: myrrh that He was mortal and would die and be buried, gold that He was a king, frankincense that He was God. The offering is thus a threefold creed — death, kingship, divinity — pressed into the magi’s hands. This typology, once fixed, quietly underwrote the later inference that there were three magi (one gift apiece) and eventually three kings; that numbering question is weighed on the summary rather than settled here.

Claims & evidence

The three gifts as a threefold confession (Against Heresies 3.9.2)

Irenaeus is arguing, against those who split the Creator from the Father of Christ, that the God of the prophets and the God of the Gospel are one and the same — and he runs through Matthew’s infancy narrative to prove it. When he reaches the magi he pauses over their gifts, reading each as a declaration of who the worshipped child is. He first anchors the star in Balaam’s oracle (“There shall come a star out of Jacob”), then turns to the offering:

But Matthew says that the Magi, coming from the east, exclaimed “For we have seen His star in the east, and are come to worship Him;” and that, having been led by the star into the house of Jacob to Emmanuel, they showed, by these gifts which they offered, who it was that was worshipped; myrrh, because it was He who should die and be buried for the mortal human race; gold, because He was a King, “of whose kingdom is no end;” and frankincense, because He was God, who also “was made known in Judea,” and was “declared to those who sought Him not.”

— Irenaeus, Against Heresies 3.9.2 (c. 180), newadvent.org (ANF 1, trans. Roberts-Donaldson).1 The reading is tightly typological: myrrh, a burial spice (cf. John 19:39–40), marks the child as one who will die; gold, the metal of kings, marks Him as king — and Irenaeus glosses it with Luke 1:33, “of whose kingdom is no end”; frankincense, the incense of the temple offered to God, marks Him as God, glossed from Psalm 76:1 (“in Judah is God known”) and Isaiah 65:1 (“declared to those who sought Him not”). Kingship, death, and Godhead — the whole of who Christ is, read off the treasure the magi opened.

The Balaam star, in the same breath

Immediately before the gifts, Irenaeus grounds the magi’s star in the oracle of Balaam — the “star out of Jacob” of Numbers 24:17 — making the star that the wise men followed a fulfilment of prophecy rather than a chance portent:

… and Emmanuel; whose star also Balaam thus prophesied: “There shall come a star out of Jacob, and a leader shall rise in Israel.”

— Irenaeus, Against Heresies 3.9.2 (c. 180), newadvent.org.2 This is an early instance of the link between the magi’s star and Balaam — himself remembered as an eastern seer — that Origen would later develop into a full account of why star-watchers of all people were the ones summoned to the manger. The Balaam connection is weighed on its own on the summary.

Notes

  • The gift-symbolism is Irenaeus’s lasting contribution to the magi tradition: myrrh = death and burial, gold = kingship, frankincense = divinity. Later writers vary the emphasis — some make myrrh mark the humanity generally rather than the burial, some read gold as almsgiving — but the king / God / mortal-man scheme is essentially his, and it is repeated for centuries.
  • Irenaeus draws no number from the gifts — he does not say “three gifts, therefore three magi.” That inference is later and explicit in Origen (three gifts → three men); but by fixing one clear symbolic meaning per gift, Irenaeus supplied the raw material the number-argument would draw on. Whether the gift count settles the magi count is weighed on the summary’s “how many were they?” question, not here.
  • The point of the passage in context is not the magi at all but the unity of God: the same God who spoke through Balaam and the psalmists is the God the magi worshipped in the flesh. The gift-typology is a proof en route to that larger argument — which is exactly why it reads as settled catechesis rather than novelty even at this early date.
  • Quotations follow the Roberts-Donaldson translation (ANF 1); the ANF text reads “for the mortal human race.” The full work is Against Heresies, Book 3, chapter 9.
