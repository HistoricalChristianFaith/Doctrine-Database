Origen on the magi — the star, the failed magic, and the heirs of Balaam

Dates: c. 248 (Against Celsus, written at Caesarea c. 248); Origen b. c. 185 at Alexandria, d. c. 253 at Tyre · Wikipedia
Summary: Origen is a triple anchor on the magi timeline, and the first writer to give the episode a fully worked-out explanation. In Against Celsus (Book 1) he (1) argues from astronomy that the star was a genuine new body, comet-like, not one of the ordinary heavenly lights; (2) reads the magi as practitioners of magic whose spells failed at Christ’s birth — the powers they invoked overthrown by “the light of divinity,” so that they sought the cause and found the star; and (3) ties them to Balaam, the Eastern seer of Numbers, whose oracle “there shall arise a star out of Jacob” the magi possessed and watched for. He also carries forward Irenaeus’s reading of the three gifts (gold to a king, myrrh to a mortal, incense to a God), and is the Father most often cited as the earliest witness to the number three — though that last rests on a typological reading, not a plain count, and Origen never fixes the number dogmatically.

Claims & evidence

The star was a “new star,” comet-like (Against Celsus 1.58)

Celsus’s Jew had jeered that “Chaldeans” came to the infant Jesus; Origen first corrects the error — the Gospel says Magi, not Chaldeans, “one who cannot distinguish between Magi and Chaldeans … has falsified the Gospel narrative” — then turns to explain the star naturally as well as prophetically. He will not have it be one of the fixed or wandering lights already known; it was something that came to be:

The star that was seen in the east we consider to have been a new star, unlike any of the other well-known planetary bodies, either those in the firmament above or those among the lower orbs, but partaking of the nature of those celestial bodies which appear at times, such as comets, or those meteors which resemble beams of wood, or beards, or wine jars, or any of those other names by which the Greeks are accustomed to describe their varying appearances.

— Origen, Against Celsus 1.58 (c. 248), newadvent.org (ANF 4, trans. Crombie).1 He backs the point in the next chapter with pagan astronomy itself — Chaeremon the Stoic’s Treatise on Comets, which records comets appearing at great events and even at times of good — arguing that a new star at the birth of one “who was to introduce a new doctrine to the human race” is no more incredible than the comets the Greeks already granted marked new dynasties.

The magi’s magic failed at Christ’s birth (Against Celsus 1.60)

This is Origen’s distinctive contribution: a mechanism for why the magi set out. The magi, he says, worked by commerce with evil spirits, whose power holds only until “some greater manifestation of divinity” overthrows it. At Christ’s birth that greater manifestation came, the spirits went feeble, and the magi’s sorceries stopped working — so they went looking for the cause:

To the Greeks, then, I have to say that the Magi, being on familiar terms with evil spirits, and invoking them for such purposes as their knowledge and wishes extend to, bring about such results only as do not appear to exceed the superhuman power and strength of the evil spirits … but should some greater manifestation of divinity be made, then the powers of the evil spirits are overthrown, being unable to resist the light of divinity. … The Magi, accordingly, wishing to produce the customary results, which formerly they used to perform by means of certain spells and sorceries, sought to know the reason of their failure, conjecturing the cause to be a great one; and beholding a divine sign in the heaven, they desired to learn its signification.

— Origen, Against Celsus 1.60 (c. 248), newadvent.org (ANF 4, trans. Crombie).2 This is a fuller, more mechanical form of the reading Ignatius had struck a century earlier — that the star was the undoing of magic. Where Ignatius simply declared the magical order abolished, Origen makes the abolition the very thing that drives the magi to Bethlehem: their art breaks, and its breaking is the summons. Because this bears on the church’s quarrel with star-divination rather than on the magi’s identity, the reading is weighed adversarially on the “did the star abolish astrology?” argument on the astrology timeline rather than settled here.

The magi as heirs of Balaam’s oracle (Against Celsus 1.59–60)

Having said the magi sought the meaning of the sign, Origen supplies the book they consulted. The star, he notes, is the one thing about the nativity for which a standing prophecy existed — Balaam’s, in Numbers — and Balaam was an Eastern seer whose oracles would have been kept among the magi’s own people:

… but with respect to the appearance of a star at the birth of Jesus there is a prophecy of Balaam recorded by Moses to this effect: “There shall arise a star out of Jacob, and a man shall rise up out of Israel.”

— Origen, Against Celsus 1.59 (c. 248).3 He then closes the circuit: the magi did not merely see a star, they recognized it, because they held Balaam’s writings and found the prophecy in them:

I am therefore of opinion that, possessing as they did the prophecies of Balaam, which Moses also records, inasmuch as Balaam was celebrated for such predictions, and finding among them the prophecy about the star, and the words, “I shall show him to him, but not now; I deem him happy, although he will not be near,” they conjectured that the man whose appearance had been foretold along with that of the star, had actually come into the world.

— Origen, Against Celsus 1.60 (c. 248).4 Irenaeus had already grounded the star in Balaam’s “star out of Jacob” (Numbers 24:17); Origen turns that bare identification into a transmission-history — the magi as the custodians and successors of Balaam’s prophecy, watching for its fulfilment. He states that transmission most fully in his Homilies on Numbers, where the magi are Balaam’s own school in the East, keeping his writings — the star-oracle among them — and so reading the sign that Israel missed:

From him [Balaam], then, the race and school of the Magi is said to flourish in the regions of the East, who, keeping in writing among themselves all that Balaam had prophesied, had this also written down: “A star shall rise out of Jacob, and a man shall spring up out of Israel.” These writings the Magi kept among themselves, and therefore, when Jesus was born, they recognized the star and understood that the prophecy was being fulfilled — they, rather than the people of Israel, who scorned to hear the words of the holy prophets. And so, recognizing from those things alone which Balaam had left in writing that the time was at hand, they came, and seeking him, at once worshipped him.

— Origen, Homilies on Numbers 13.7 (surviving in Rufinus’s Latin translation; rendered from the Latin).5 Later Fathers hint that Origen drew the elaborated form on an apocryphal book of or about Balaam, now lost. Whether Balaam’s oracle really explains the magi’s star is weighed on its own argument page.

The three gifts, read as king, mortal, and God (Against Celsus 1.60)

In the same breath as the Balaam link, Origen gives the gifts the meaning Irenaeus had assigned them — the opened treasure as a confession of who the child is:

… bringing gifts, which they offered to him as one whose nature partook, if I may so speak, both of God and of a mortal man,—gold, viz., as to a king; myrrh, as to one who was mortal; and incense, as to a God …

— Origen, Against Celsus 1.60 (c. 248).6 The threefold reading — kingship, mortality, Godhead — matches Irenaeus’s gold/king, myrrh/burial, frankincense/God almost exactly (see Irenaeus’s detail page), and by Origen’s day it was becoming the standard gift-symbolism. It also quietly underwrote the inference that there were three magi — a gift apiece.

“Three” magi? — the number Origen is credited with, and the caution it needs

Matthew never numbers the magi. The three of later tradition were inferred from the three gifts, and Origen is the Father most often named as the earliest witness to that number — on the strength of a passage in his fourteenth homily on Genesis, where he reads the three men who came to Isaac (Abimelech with Ahuzzath and Phicol, Genesis 26:26) as a figure of the magi. The firsthand wording, though, shows how lightly the “three” sits there. Origen first reads the three visitors as the three parts of philosophy (“these three, as I think, hold the image of the whole of philosophy, which among them is divided into three parts — logic, physics, ethics”), and only then, tentatively, adds that they can also stand for the magi:

These three — who seek peace from the Word of God and desire to anticipate fellowship with him by a covenant — can bear the figure of the Magi, who come from the regions of the East, schooled in ancestral books and the tradition of their forefathers, and who say: “Seeing, we have seen the king born, and we have seen that God is with him, and we have come to adore him.”

— Origen, Homilies on Genesis 14.3 (surviving in Rufinus’s Latin translation; rendered from the Latin).7 Two cautions follow, and the firsthand text bears them out rather than softening them. First, it is a typological reading built on a number native to the Genesis side: the “three” is the count of Isaac’s three visitors, read forward onto the magi — and read first onto philosophy’s three parts — not a plain statement that the magi numbered three. Second, the framing is expressly tentative: Origen says the three visitors “can bear the figure” (possunt … figuram tenere) of the magi, not that the magi were three. He nowhere fixes the number dogmatically, and elsewhere shows no interest in settling it. The number itself is an inference from the gifts, not a datum of Matthew’s text; how firmly — if at all — the count should be fixed at three is weighed on the “how many magi?” argument page.

Notes

  • Against Celsus is dated c. 248, near the end of Origen’s life at Caesarea; it answers the second-century pagan Celsus’s True Discourse line by line. The magi discussion runs across chapters 58–60 of Book 1, answering the sneer that “Chaldeans” had been fooled into worshipping an infant. Quotations follow the Ante-Nicene Fathers (ANF vol. 4), Frederick Crombie’s translation.
  • Origen’s three moves interlock: the star is real (astronomy), its meaning was legible to the magi (Balaam), and their coming was forced (their magic failed). That is a tighter apologetic than any earlier Father had built — it neither concedes astrology (the star is a one-off new body, not a horoscope) nor makes the magi mere stargazers (they read a prophecy, and were driven by the collapse of their own art).
  • The Balaam-heirs tradition Origen develops became a patristic commonplace — taken up by Eusebius and Gregory of Nyssa — and is the seed of the medieval legend that made Balaam himself the magi’s ancestor. Its earliest Christian root is Justin’s and Irenaeus’s bare equation of the two stars; the elaborated “custodians of Balaam’s book” form is Origen’s.
  • The reading that the magi’s magic failed puts Origen in the same family as Ignatius (“every kind of magic was destroyed”) and, later, Chrysostom and Gregory the Great — the tradition that the star abolished star-craft. That thread is weighed on the star-and-astrology argument.
