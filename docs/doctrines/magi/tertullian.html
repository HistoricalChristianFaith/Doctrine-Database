Tertullian on the magi — “the East well-nigh held the magi as kings”

Dates: c. 207 (Against Marcion Book 3 c. 207–208 and An Answer to the Jews c. 198–206, both written at Carthage); Tertullian b. c. 155 at Carthage, converted c. 197, d. c. 220 · Wikipedia
Summary: Tertullian is a double anchor on the magi timeline. First, he is the earliest surviving writer to attach the magi to royalty: reading David’s psalm of “the kings of Arabia and Saba” who “shall offer gifts,” he twice reports that “the East” well-nigh regarded the magi as kings — a key early seed of the later “three kings” tradition, though he stops short of flatly calling them kings himself. Second, he places them in Arabia, identifying their gifts with the “strength of Damascus” of Isaiah 8:4 and noting that Damascus was anciently reckoned to Arabia — carrying forward the Arabia homeland Justin had drawn from the same prophecies. Both readings arise the same way: not from any travel report, but from prophecy pressed onto Matthew’s bare notice.

Claims & evidence

“The East well-nigh held the magi as kings” (Against Marcion 3.13; Answer to the Jews 9)

Tertullian is arguing against Marcion (and, in the parallel work, against Jewish objectors) that Isaiah’s prophecy of a child who would “take the riches of Damascus and the spoils of Samaria” (Isaiah 8:4) was fulfilled not by a warrior but by the infant Christ, to whom the magi brought the wealth of the East. Running the prophecy through the nativity, he reaches the gifts and, quoting David’s psalm of the tribute-bearing kings, reports how the East regarded the magi who brought them:

Moreover, respecting that gift of gold, David also says: “And there shall be given to Him of the gold of Arabia;” and again: “The kings of Arabia and Saba shall offer to Him gifts.” For the East generally regarded the magi as kings; and Damascus was anciently deemed to belong to Arabia, before it was transferred to Syrophoenicia on the division of the Syrias.

— Tertullian, Against Marcion 3.13 (c. 207), newadvent.org (ANF 3, trans. Holmes).1 The near-identical passage in An Answer to the Jews makes the royal note explicit in the same breath as the gifts:

Let those Eastern magi be believed, dowering with gold and incense the infancy of Christ as a king; and the infant has received “the power of Damascus” without battle and arms. … For of this gift of “gold” David likewise says, “And to Him shall be given of the gold of Arabia;” and again, “The kings of the Arabs and Saba shall bring Him gifts.” For the East, on the one hand, generally held the magi (to be) kings; and Damascus, on the other hand, used formerly to be reckoned to Arabia before it was transferred into Syrophoenicia on the division of the Syrias.

— Tertullian, An Answer to the Jews 9 (c. 203), newadvent.org (ANF 3, trans. Thelwall).2 The precision matters and is load-bearing for the “were the magi kings?” question. Tertullian does not assert that the magi were kings; he reports that “the East” regarded them so. The Latin adverb the ANF renders “generally” (fere, “well-nigh, almost, for the most part”) carries exactly that reserve — a widespread eastern estimate, not Tertullian’s own verdict. What he needs for his argument is only that the men who brought royal tribute stood, in the common reckoning, near enough to kings that David’s “kings of Arabia and Saba” could be read as fulfilled in them. That reading, grafted onto Psalm 72, is the seed from which the crowned Caspar, Melchior, and Balthasar of the Latin West would much later grow.

Arabia and the “strength of Damascus” (Isaiah 8:4)

The same passages place the magi in Arabia, and by the same method — prophecy read back onto the nativity, not geography. Tertullian identifies the gifts with the “riches” or “strength of Damascus” that Isaiah’s child would carry off before “the king of Assyria” (Isaiah 8:4), glossing the point with the note that “the riches of the East … usually consist of gold and spices” and that Damascus itself “was anciently deemed to belong to Arabia.” The gold and frankincense are thus the “power of Damascus” brought to the manger, and the magi who bring them are Arabian.3 This is the same chain Justin Martyr had forged a half-century earlier from the same two texts — Psalm 72 (“the gold of Arabia”; “the kings of Arabia and Saba”) and Isaiah 8:4 (Damascus and Samaria before the king of Assyria) — and Tertullian’s dependence on that reading is plain. Whether the magi were in fact Arabians, Persians, or Babylonians is weighed on its own argument page; here the point is that the Arabia tradition, like the kings tradition, is an inference from prophecy rather than a datum about the magi.

Notes

  • Tertullian’s two treatments are so close that Against Marcion 3.13 and An Answer to the Jews 9 are usually read as two versions of one argument — the anti-Marcionite book most likely reworking the earlier anti-Jewish tract. On the magi, both say the same two things: the East well-nigh held them kings, and their gifts were the “strength of Damascus,” anciently Arabian.
  • He is not calling the magi kings. The “three kings” of later legend are a downstream reading; the patristic seed is precisely this softer report — that the East regarded them as kings, because they brought the royal tribute the psalm assigns to “the kings of Arabia and Saba.” The distinction is worth keeping: Tertullian supplies the proof-text (Psalm 72) and the association, not the assertion.
  • The wider passage runs the whole nativity allegorically against Marcion: the magi themselves are read as “the spoils of Samaria” (i.e. of idolatry), “despoiled” of their star-craft by adoring Christ — a reading in the same family as Ignatius’s “the star abolished magic,” weighed on the star-and-astrology argument rather than here.
  • Quotations follow the Ante-Nicene Fathers (ANF vol. 3): Against Marcion in Peter Holmes’s translation, An Answer to the Jews in S. Thelwall’s. The psalm Tertullian quotes is Psalm 72:10, 15 (LXX/Vulgate 71); the ANF footnote to the Answer to the Jews passage notes that the “gold of Arabia” of verse 15 reads “Sheba” in the English versions but “Arabia” in the Septuagint and the 1539 Great Bible — which is exactly the Arabia reading Tertullian and Justin lean on.
