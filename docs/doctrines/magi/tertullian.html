Tertullian on the magi — “the East well-nigh held the magi as kings”

Dates: c. 207 (Against Marcion Book 3 c. 207–208 and An Answer to the Jews c. 198–206, both written at Carthage); Tertullian b. c. 155 at Carthage, converted c. 197, d. c. 220 · Wikipedia
Summary: Tertullian is the earliest extant writer to connect the magi with royalty: reading David’s psalm of “the kings of Arabia and Saba” who “shall offer gifts,” he twice reports that “the East” well-nigh regarded the magi as kings, without calling them kings in his own voice. He also places them in Arabia, identifying their gifts with the “strength of Damascus” of Isaiah 8:4 and noting that Damascus was anciently reckoned to Arabia, as Justin had done from the same prophecies. Both readings are drawn from the prophecies he reads as fulfilled in Matthew’s notice.

Claims & evidence

“The East well-nigh held the magi as kings” (Against Marcion 3.13; Answer to the Jews 9)

Tertullian is arguing against Marcion (and, in the parallel work, against Jewish objectors) that Isaiah’s prophecy of a child who would “take the riches of Damascus and the spoils of Samaria” (Isaiah 8:4) was fulfilled not by a warrior but by the infant Christ, to whom the magi brought the wealth of the East. Running the prophecy through the nativity, he reaches the gifts and, quoting David’s psalm of the tribute-bearing kings, reports how the East regarded the magi who brought them:

Moreover, respecting that gift of gold, David also says: “And there shall be given to Him of the gold of Arabia;” and again: “The kings of Arabia and Saba shall offer to Him gifts.” For the East generally regarded the magi as kings; and Damascus was anciently deemed to belong to Arabia, before it was transferred to Syrophoenicia on the division of the Syrias.

— Tertullian, Against Marcion 3.13 (c. 207), newadvent.org (ANF 3, trans. Holmes).1 The near-identical passage in An Answer to the Jews makes the royal note explicit in the same breath as the gifts:

Let those Eastern magi be believed, dowering with gold and incense the infancy of Christ as a king; and the infant has received “the power of Damascus” without battle and arms. … For of this gift of “gold” David likewise says, “And to Him shall be given of the gold of Arabia;” and again, “The kings of the Arabs and Saba shall bring Him gifts.” For the East, on the one hand, generally held the magi (to be) kings; and Damascus, on the other hand, used formerly to be reckoned to Arabia before it was transferred into Syrophoenicia on the division of the Syrias.

— Tertullian, An Answer to the Jews 9 (c. 203), newadvent.org (ANF 3, trans. Thelwall).2 Tertullian reports that “the East” regarded the magi as kings; he does not make the assertion in his own voice. The Latin adverb the ANF renders “generally” (fere, “well-nigh, almost, for the most part”) marks a widespread eastern estimate. His argument requires only that the men who brought royal tribute stood, in the common reckoning, near enough to kings that David’s “kings of Arabia and Saba” could be read as fulfilled in them. Whether the magi were kings is set out on the disputed-reading page “Were the magi kings?”

Arabia and the “strength of Damascus” (Isaiah 8:4)

The same passages place the magi in Arabia, again from prophecy read onto the nativity. Tertullian identifies the gifts with the “riches” or “strength of Damascus” that Isaiah’s child would carry off before “the king of Assyria” (Isaiah 8:4), glossing the point with the note that “the riches of the East … usually consist of gold and spices” and that Damascus itself “was anciently deemed to belong to Arabia.” The gold and frankincense are thus the “power of Damascus” brought to the infant Christ, and the magi who bring them are Arabian.3 Justin Martyr had drawn the same conclusion some fifty years earlier from the same two texts — Psalm 72 (“the gold of Arabia”; “the kings of Arabia and Saba”) and Isaiah 8:4 (Damascus and Samaria before the king of Assyria). Where the magi came from is set out on the disputed-reading page “Where did the magi of Matthew 2 come from?”

Reception and placement

  • Tertullian’s two treatments, Against Marcion 3.13 and An Answer to the Jews 9, are close enough to read as two versions of one argument. On the magi both say the same two things: the East well-nigh held them kings, and their gifts were the “strength of Damascus,” anciently Arabian.
  • He reports the East’s estimate rather than calling the magi kings himself: the East regarded them as kings because they brought the royal tribute the psalm assigns to “the kings of Arabia and Saba.” He supplies the proof-text (Psalm 72) and the association. The “three kings” appear in later sources.
  • The wider passage reads the whole nativity allegorically against Marcion: the magi themselves are read as “the spoils of Samaria” (i.e. of idolatry), “despoiled” of their star-craft by adoring Christ — a reading akin to Ignatius’s “every kind of magic was destroyed”; see the astrology timeline.
  • Quotations follow the Ante-Nicene Fathers (ANF vol. 3): Against Marcion in Peter Holmes’s translation, An Answer to the Jews in S. Thelwall’s. The psalm Tertullian quotes is Psalm 72:10, 15 (LXX/Vulgate 71); the ANF footnote to the Answer to the Jews passage notes that the “gold of Arabia” of verse 15 reads “Sheba” in the English versions but “Arabia” in the Septuagint and the 1539 Great Bible — the reading Tertullian and Justin quote.
