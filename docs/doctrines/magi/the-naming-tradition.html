The naming of the magi — Caspar, Melchior, Balthasar (and the rival name-sets)

Dates: the Western names anchored here c. 500 — their earliest surviving attestation, the Excerpta Latina Barbari, is a Latin rendering of a lost Greek chronicle composed at Alexandria about AD 500–530; the fixed threefold portrait (three ages, three complexions, three continents) follows in the eighth-century pseudo-Bede · Wikipedia
Summary: Matthew names none of the magi. The earliest extant attestation of the familiar Western trio — Caspar (Gaspar), Melchior, and Balthasar — dates from about AD 500, and the picture of them as three kings of three ages, three skin-tones, and three continents appears later, in an eighth- or ninth-century compilation long printed among Bede’s works. The Syriac, Armenian, and Ethiopic churches transmit different name-sets, and the East more often counted the magi at twelve. This page treats the naming as a datable stage in the reception of Matthew’s story.

Claims & evidence

The names first surface c. 500 in the Excerpta Latina Barbari

The earliest datable source to supply the Western names is the Excerpta Latina Barbari (“barbarian Latin excerpts” — the dismissive tag Joseph Scaliger gave the Latin translation): a chronicle originally written in Greek at Alexandria about AD 500–530 and rendered into rough Latin, perhaps in Merovingian Gaul, in the eighth century. In its list of events under Augustus it reports:

The names of the Magi were Bithisarea, Melichior and Gathaspa.

— Excerpta Latina Barbari, in the Roman-consuls section [49A].1 These three — Bithisarea, Melichior, Gathaspa — are early forms of the names Western usage gives as Balthasar, Melchior, and Caspar (Gaspar). The chronicle offers no ages, no homelands, no rank, and no story attached to the names; it hands them down as received, in a single line of a sixth-century Alexandrian annal.2

The earliest naming in art: the Ravenna mosaic (c. 561–565)

Some decades later the names appear in art. On the north nave wall of Sant’Apollinare Nuovo in Ravenna — in the procession-mosaic added under Justinian after the church passed to Catholic use, about AD 561–565 — the three magi stride forward in Persian dress and Phrygian caps, and above them run the inscriptions + SCS BALTHASSAR, + SCS MELCHIOR, + SCS GASPAR. The Wikipedia entry on the basilica says it “is thought to be the earliest example of these three names being assigned to the Magi in Christian art.” The tesserae bearing the names may be a somewhat later insertion into the original panel rather than part of the first laying (⚠ unverified). The figures are differentiated by age — and the white-bearded elder is here labelled Gaspar (Caspar), where later Western custom names the elder Melchior.3 The names are attested here before their assignment to particular faces and gifts is fixed.

The full portrait: pseudo-Bede’s three ages, complexions, and continents

The picture most people now carry — an old king, a middle-aged king, and a young king, one of them black-skinned, standing for the three known continents — is later than the names, and rests on a compilation long printed among Bede’s works but identified as anonymous. A Latin compilation of miscellany called the Collectanea et Flores (or Excerpta et Collectanea), long printed among the works of the Venerable Bede but identified as an anonymous, probably Irish, work of the eighth or ninth century, gives each magus a name, an age, a complexion, and a gift read symbolically: Melchior, an old man with white hair and a long beard, who offered gold to a king; Caspar (Gaspar), young and beardless and ruddy, who offered frankincense to God; and Balthasar, dark-skinned and full-bearded, who by his myrrh confessed the Son of Man who would die.4 The same tradition assigns the three to the three parts of the medieval world — Europe, Asia, and Africa — each in turn peopled (in medieval reckoning) by one of Noah’s three sons, so that the magi come to stand for the whole Gentile world doing homage. From this chain of ideas descends the familiar late-medieval and early-modern iconography: Melchior a white European, Caspar an Asian, and Balthasar a black African. None of it is in Matthew or in the sources of the first centuries; the scheme is attested some three centuries after the names first appear.

The Eastern name-sets: Syriac, Armenian, Ethiopic — and the count of twelve

The ancient Eastern churches transmit entirely different names. The Syriac tradition calls the three Larvandad, Gushnasaph, and Hormisdas; the Armenian, Kagpha, Badadakharida, and Badadilma; the Ethiopic Book of Adam (Bal. 4:15) names them Hor king of Persia, Basanater king of Saba, and Karsudan king of the East; and an earlier Syrian list gives yet a fourth set — Hormizdah of Persia, Yazdegerd of Saba, and Perozadh of Sheba. Beyond even the names, the Syriac and Armenian churches often counted the magi at twelve, as the Revelation of the Magi does, not three at all.5 No two of these traditions give the same names.

Reception and debate

Raymond E. Brown treats the magi as unnamed and unnumbered in Matthew and classes the names, with the number and the crowns, as post-biblical accretion of no historical value, and Richard C. Trexler traces the naming and the three-continents scheme as products of medieval devotion and art. The three-continents scheme presents the magi as the firstfruits of the whole Gentile world, one for each part of the world as medieval writers reckoned it.6

Placement on the timeline

  • This page treats the naming as a datable stage in the reception of Matthew’s story — a witness to how the magi were imagined in the Latin West and, differently, in the Christian East. Its place on the timeline is the point (c. 500) at which the Western names first surface in a datable source.
  • Matthew gives no number; how many the magi were, including the Eastern twelve, is set out on the disputed-reading page “How many magi came to Bethlehem?” Whether they were kings, a reading drawn from Psalm 72 and Isaiah 60, is set out on “Were the magi kings?”
  • Spellings of the Western names vary widely across manuscripts and languages — Caspar / Gaspar / Jaspar, Melchior / Melichior, Balthasar / Balthassar / Bithisarea — and the assignment of a given name to the old, the middle-aged, or the young magus is not stable even within the West (the Ravenna mosaic puts the elder as Gaspar; pseudo-Bede puts him as Melchior).
