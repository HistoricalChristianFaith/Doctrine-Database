The naming of the magi — Caspar, Melchior, Balthasar (and the rival name-sets)

Dates: the Western names anchored here c. 500 — their earliest surviving attestation, the Excerpta Latina Barbari, is a Latin rendering of a lost Greek chronicle composed at Alexandria about AD 500–530; the fixed threefold portrait (three ages, three complexions, three continents) follows in the eighth-century pseudo-Bede · Wikipedia
Summary: Matthew names none of the magi. The familiar Western trio — Caspar (Gaspar), Melchior, and Balthasar — cannot be traced back of about AD 500, and the now-standard picture of them as three kings of three ages, three skin-tones, and three continents is later still, crystallizing only in an eighth-century compilation wrongly ascribed to Bede. The names, moreover, are purely local: the Syriac, Armenian, and Ethiopic churches transmit wholly different name-sets, and the East more often counted the magi at twelve, not three. This page treats the naming as a datable stage in the reception of Matthew’s story — a sixth-century-and-later Western development with no early or historical footing — and is itself part of the “who were the magi?” question, not an answer to it.

Claims & evidence

The names first surface c. 500 in the Excerpta Latina Barbari

For the first five centuries the magi go unnamed. The earliest datable source to supply names at all is the Excerpta Latina Barbari (“barbarian Latin excerpts” — the dismissive tag Joseph Scaliger gave the clumsy Latin translation): a chronicle originally written in Greek at Alexandria about AD 500–530 and rendered into rough Latin, perhaps in Merovingian Gaul, in the eighth century. In its list of events under Augustus it reports, plainly and without embellishment:

The names of the Magi were Bithisarea, Melichior and Gathaspa.

— Excerpta Latina Barbari, in the Roman-consuls section [49A].1 These three — Bithisarea, Melichior, Gathaspa — are the raw forms that Western usage would smooth into Balthasar, Melchior, and Caspar (Gaspar). The chronicle offers no ages, no homelands, no rank, and no story to justify the names; it simply hands them down as received. That the earliest witness is a stray line in a sixth-century Alexandrian annal — five hundred years after the event — is the first and largest fact about the tradition.2

The earliest naming in art: the Ravenna mosaic (c. 561–565)

At almost the same moment the names appear in art. On the north nave wall of Sant’Apollinare Nuovo in Ravenna — in the procession-mosaic added under Justinian after the church passed to Catholic use, about AD 561–565 — the three magi stride forward in Persian dress and Phrygian caps, and above them run the inscriptions + SCS BALTHASSAR, + SCS MELCHIOR, + SCS GASPAR. This is generally called the earliest surviving naming of the magi in Christian art. Two honest caveats belong with it: the tesserae bearing the names may be a somewhat later insertion into the original panel rather than part of the first laying, and the figures are already differentiated by age — but not yet in the order the West would fix, the white-bearded elder here being labelled Gaspar (Caspar), not Melchior as later custom would have it.3 The names, in short, are attested before their assignment to particular faces and gifts is settled — a sign that they circulated as a bare trio well before the full portrait was built around them.

The full portrait: pseudo-Bede’s three ages, complexions, and continents

The picture most people now carry — an old king, a middle-aged king, and a young king, one of them black-skinned, standing for the three known continents — is later than the names, and rests on a text that is not what it claims to be. A Latin compilation of miscellany called the Collectanea et Flores (or Excerpta et Collectanea), long printed among the works of the Venerable Bede but in fact an anonymous, probably Irish, work of the eighth or ninth century, gives each magus a name, an age, a complexion, and a gift read symbolically: Melchior, an old man with white hair and a long beard, who offered gold to a king; Caspar (Gaspar), young and beardless and ruddy, who offered frankincense to God; and Balthasar, dark-skinned and full-bearded, who by his myrrh confessed the Son of Man who would die.4 The same tradition assigns the three to the three parts of the medieval world — Europe, Asia, and Africa — each in turn peopled (in medieval reckoning) by one of Noah’s three sons, so that the magi come to stand for the whole Gentile world doing homage. From this chain of ideas descends the familiar late-medieval and early-modern iconography: Melchior a white European, Caspar an Asian, and Balthasar a black African. None of it is in Matthew, or in any early source; it is a symbolic scheme grafted onto the bare names some three centuries after they first appear.

The rival name-sets: Syriac, Armenian, Ethiopic — and twelve, not three

The clearest proof that the names are late and local is that the ancient Eastern churches, no less old than the Latin West, transmit entirely different ones. The Syriac tradition calls the three Larvandad, Gushnasaph, and Hormisdas; the Armenian, Kagpha, Badadakharida, and Badadilma; the Ethiopic Book of Adam (Bal. 4:15) names them Hor king of Persia, Basanater king of Saba, and Karsudan king of the East; and an earlier Syrian list gives yet a fourth set — Hormizdah of Persia, Yazdegerd of Saba, and Perozadh of Sheba. Beyond even the names, the Syriac and Armenian churches often counted the magi at twelve, as the Revelation of the Magi does, not three at all.5 No two of these traditions agree, none is early, and none rests on any record earlier than the Gospel’s silence — which is exactly what one expects of names invented, independently and locally, to fill a gap the text left open.

Assessment of the tradition

The names Caspar, Melchior, and Balthasar carry no claim to history. They are unknown to Matthew and to every writer of the first four centuries; their earliest trace is a sixth-century Alexandrian chronicle, and the developed “three kings of three continents” portrait is eighth-century and pseudonymous. The competing Syriac, Armenian, and Ethiopic name-lists — mutually irreconcilable, and paired in the East with a count of twelve rather than three — confirm that naming the magi was a devotional and imaginative act repeated differently in each church, not the recovery of a fact. This is not to disparage the tradition: the names, the ages, and the three continents are a rich piece of Christian imagination, and the continent-scheme carries a real theological point (the magi as the firstfruits of all the Gentile world). But as history the names have no standing, and Matthew’s wise men remain, as he left them, unnamed and unnumbered.6

Notes

  • This page treats the naming as a datable stage in the reception of Matthew’s story — a witness to how the magi were imagined in the Latin West and, differently, in the Christian East — not as a report about the historical wise men. Its place on the timeline is the point (c. 500) at which the Western names first surface in a datable source.
  • The number three itself is an inference from the three gifts, not a datum of the text; where it came from, and the Eastern twelve against it, are weighed on the “how many magi?” argument page. The kingship that turns the magi into three kings is likewise a later reading, drawn from Psalm 72 and Isaiah 60, and is weighed on the “were the magi kings?” argument page.
  • Spellings of the Western names vary widely across manuscripts and languages — Caspar / Gaspar / Jaspar, Melchior / Melichior, Balthasar / Balthassar / Bithisarea — and the assignment of a given name to the old, the middle-aged, or the young magus is not stable even within the West (the Ravenna mosaic puts the elder as Gaspar; pseudo-Bede puts him as Melchior).
