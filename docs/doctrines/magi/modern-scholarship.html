Modern scholarship — what critical study makes of the magi

Dates: a survey of modern critical scholarship (chiefly late-20th to 21st century) · representative works: Raymond E. Brown, The Birth of the Messiah (1977; rev. ed. 1993); Richard C. Trexler, The Journey of the Magi (1997); Anders Hultgård on the Iranian background · Brown at archive.org
Summary: This page surveys what modern lexical, historical, and reception-critical study has said about Matthew’s wise men. Four themes recur: the sense of the word magoi, which Raymond Brown and the standard lexica take as eastern priest-astrologers; the number, names, crowns, and homelands, which Brown and Richard Trexler treat as post-biblical additions to a text that supplies none of them; the Iranian religious background that Anders Hultgård sets the episode against; and the historicity of the episode, on which scholars range from Brown’s reading of Matthew 2 as theological narrative to defenders of a real eastern embassy. The disputed readings of the number, the kingship, the homeland, the magi’s religion, and the Balaam oracle are set out on the magi timeline.

Claims & evidence

The word magoi: priest-astrologers

Matthew’s magoi (μάγοι) is a Greek borrowing of Old Persian magu-, and its oldest secure usage is in Herodotus, who lists the Magi as one of the six tribes of the Medes and reports that among the Persians no sacrifice could be performed without a magus present to chant the theogony over it — a priestly office.1 By the Hellenistic and Roman periods the term had widened to cover astrologers, dream-interpreters, and (pejoratively) sorcerers — the sense that gives English its word magic. The lexica carry the whole range (“wise men, teachers, priests, physicians, astrologers, seers, interpreters of dreams … sorcerers”), and Raymond Brown’s commentary reads Matthew’s magi as Gentile astrologers from an unspecified eastern land.2 On this reading the crowns come from the prophecies of Psalm 72 and Isaiah 60 rather than from the word.

Number, names, kings, homelands: the developed picture

Brown and Trexler treat most of what the popular Nativity supplies — that the magi were three, that they were kings, that they were named Caspar, Melchior, and Balthasar, that they came from three continents — as post-biblical growth. Matthew gives no number, no names (the earliest extant attestation of the Western trio dates from about AD 500, and the Eastern churches transmit different sets), no royal rank (Brown traces the royal reading to Psalm 72 and Isaiah 60), and no homeland more precise than “the east.” Brown catalogues the names, the number, and the crowns as post-biblical accretion of no historical value; Richard Trexler’s Journey of the Magi is a “meanings in history” account of how Western societies reshaped the Gospel notice into three crowned kings for spiritual, social, and political ends across the centuries.3 The earliest sources for each detail, and the disputed readings of the number, the kingship, and the homeland, are gathered on this timeline.4

The Iranian background

Anders Hultgård, in a run of studies on the Iranian horizon of the story, sets Matthew’s magi against the religious world of pre-Islamic Iran: a magu- priesthood that watched the sky and read celestial signs; a Zoroastrian expectation of a world-saviour (the Saošyant); and long-standing Jewish–Iranian contact through which such motifs could travel. Hultgård states the result as the plausibility of the milieu, not the historicity of the visit: a first-century audience could hear “magoi from the east, following a star to a newborn king” as coherent, given who the magi were and what they were thought to watch for.5 Ancient Eastern retellings likewise make the magi Persian sages or, in the Syriac Revelation of the Magi, silent mystics of the far East who keep a star-prophecy of Adam. Whether the magi were Zoroastrian priests is set out on the disputed-reading page “Were the magi Zoroastrian priests?”

The Old Testament templates: Balaam and Moses

Critical readings of Matthew 2 attend to two scriptural templates. The first is Balaam: the pagan seer “from the east” who, before a hostile king, prophesied “there shall come a star out of Jacob” (Numbers 24:17) — a Gentile diviner, a star, and a coming ruler, a link the Fathers themselves drew (see Irenaeus and Origen). The second is Moses: a king (Herod / Pharaoh) who, warned of a deliverer, massacres the infants while the child is preserved — a patterning Matthew shares with contemporary Jewish retellings of the Moses birth-story. Brown reads Matthew 2 largely as theological narrative composed on these templates, the evangelist writing “above all” as a theologian.6 Andreas Köstenberger and Alexander Stewart, in dialogue with Brown, hold that a real event can be narrated through a scriptural template without being generated by it, and that resemblance to the Moses story shows patterning rather than invention; Ian Paul surveys “myth and history” in the passage.7 How the Balaam oracle relates to the star is set out on the disputed-reading page “How is Balaam’s ‘star out of Jacob’ related to the magi’s star?”

Historicity: a range of views

On whether the visit happened, modern scholars differ. Brown treats the magi and the star as theologically driven narrative, pointing to the dating of Matthew (the 80s–90s), the Old-Testament templates, and the star’s behaviour — a body that leads, halts, and stands over a single house — which on his reading marks a composed scene and opens the way to the later legendary fillings, among them the star that is Christ in the Revelation of the Magi. Köstenberger and Stewart, R. T. France, and Ian Paul hold an eastern embassy to a client-kingdom historically plausible in its own right: some reconstruct a Parthian or Nabataean delegation moving through the Rome–Parthia border politics of Herod’s reign. The Roman record of an eastern king’s homage in the first century is also adduced in this debate (⚠ unverified: no named modern holder located): in AD 66 Tiridates of Armenia came to Nero, “brought Magi with him” (Pliny), and “did not return by the route that he had followed in coming” (Cassius Dio).8
