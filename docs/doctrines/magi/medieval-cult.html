The medieval cult of the Three Kings — the Golden Legend, the Cologne relics, and Marco Polo’s tomb

Dates: the cult anchored here across c. 1164–1298 — the translation of the relics to Cologne in 1164, Jacobus de Voragine’s Golden Legend (compiled c. 1260), and Marco Polo’s report of the magi’s tomb (dictated c. 1298) · Wikipedia
Summary: By the high Middle Ages the West had settled the magi into a fixed devotional figure: three crowned kings, named Caspar, Melchior, and Balthasar, with a gathered legend, a shrine, and a body of relics. This page treats that cult as a datable stage in the reception of Matthew’s story through three of its landmarks — the Golden Legend, which gathers the developed lore into the book every late-medieval reader knew; the relics translated from Milan to Cologne in 1164 and enshrined in the largest reliquary of the Western world; and Marco Polo’s notice of a rival tomb of the magi at Saveh in Persia, still shown to travellers. None of it is a recovery of first-century fact; but two of its data points bear on the wiki’s open questions — the count of three, the reading of the magi as kings, and (in Marco Polo’s Persian tomb) the old dispute over whether they came from Persia, Babylon, or Arabia.

Claims & evidence

The Golden Legend gathers the developed legend (c. 1260)

Jacobus de Voragine’s Legenda Aurea — compiled about 1260 and, in William Caxton’s English, one of the most widely read books of the later Middle Ages — opens its chapter on the Epiphany by gathering into one place the accreted lore of the magi. It fixes the trio and even supplies the three languages in which their names had come to be told:

the names be written in Hebrew, that is to wit Galgalath, Magalath, and Tharath. And in Greek Appelius, Amerius, and Damascus. And in Latin Jaspar, Melchior, and Balthasar.

It carries forward the old Balaam pedigree — “These kings were successors of Balaam, and came at the vision and sight of the star, by the prophecy of their father, which said that a star shall be born or spring out of Jacob” — and their three gifts, “that is to wit gold, incense, and myrrh.”1 On their homeland it follows the twelfth-century Historia Scholastica: “They… came from the end of Persia, from the Chaldeans whereas is the flood of Saba” — a Persian, not Arabian, origin. And it closes with the relics’ itinerary, the clause that ties the book to Cologne: “Of whom the bodies were brought to Milan, where as now is the convent of the friars preachers, and now be at Cologne in S. Peter’s Church, which is the Cathedral and See of the Archbishop.”2

One honest limit belongs here. The fullest medieval romance of the kings — their reunion late in life, their baptism by the apostle Thomas in India, and their martyrdom — is not in the Golden Legend at all but in the later Historia Trium Regum of John of Hildesheim (c. 1364), the work that also elaborated the Cologne translation into a full narrative. The Golden Legend supplies the names, the Balaam pedigree, the Persian origin, and the relics’ resting place; the death-and-baptism legend is a fourteenth-century addition on top of it.3

The relics: from Milan to Cologne (1164)

The physical cult that made Cologne the great magi-shrine of the West began as spoils of war. When Frederick Barbarossa took Milan in 1162, the relics venerated as the bodies of the Three Kings — kept at the church of Sant’Eustorgio, and traced by local legend back to a fourth-century translation from Constantinople by Bishop Eustorgius — passed to his chancellor, Rainald of Dassel, archbishop-elect of Cologne, who carried them north in 1164.4 To house them the goldsmith Nicholas of Verdun and his workshop built the Shrine of the Three Kings — a great gilded triple sarcophagus begun about 1180–1181 and finished about 1225, the largest reliquary in the Western world — and the cathedral raised over it became one of the foremost pilgrimage destinations of medieval Europe. The translation did not merely move bones; it gave the “three kings” a civic home, a feast, and a monument, and fixed their number and rank in the devotional imagination of the West.5

Marco Polo’s tomb of the Magi at Saveh (c. 1298)

While the West venerated the bodies at Cologne, Marco Polo reported being shown a wholly different tomb of the same magi in Persia. Passing through the country in the 1270s, he places their sepulchre at the city of Saba (Saveh, south of modern Tehran):

In Persia is the city of SABA, from which the Three Magi set out when they went to worship Jesus Christ; and in this city they are buried, in three very large and beautiful monuments, side by side. And above them there is a square building, carefully kept. The bodies are still entire, with the hair and beard remaining. One of these was called Jaspar, the second Melchior, and the third Balthasar.

He is candid that he could learn little on the spot — “Messer Marco Polo asked a great many questions of the people of that city as to those Three Magi, but never one could he find that knew aught of the matter, except that these were three kings who were buried there in days of old” — and that the fuller story came three days on, at a village he calls Cala Ataperistan, “which is as much as to say, ‘The Castle of the Fire-worshippers,’” where he was told the three kings came severally “of the city called Saba, the second of Ava, and the third of that very Castle where they still worship fire.”6 Two things follow. First, the two shrines cannot both hold the bodies: a Persian tomb of intact corpses and a Cologne reliquary of bones are rival claims, and Marco Polo’s notice is a reminder that the relic-tradition was contested even in the Middle Ages. Second, whatever its reliability, his report attests a live Persian local memory of the magi — a datum that tells for the Persia-origin reading against Justin Martyr’s Arabia, one weighed on the magi’s-homeland argument pages.7

Assessment of the tradition

The medieval cult is the point at which the magi become, for the Latin West and lastingly, three crowned kings with names, relics, and a tomb. As devotion and art it is rich and coherent; as history it carries no weight the earlier tradition did not already carry. The number three is still the inference from the three gifts, not a datum of Matthew; the kingship is still the reading drawn from Psalm 72 and Isaiah 60, not a report; and the relics have no verifiable chain to the first century — the earliest link, the Eustorgius translation from Constantinople to Milan, is itself a legend of the fourth century or later, and the rival tomb at Saveh shows that the location of the bodies was never fixed. Modern scholarship treats the whole cult as the growth of a Christian story rather than the recovery of fact.8 None of this is to disparage it: the Cologne shrine is one of the summits of medieval metalwork, and the legend of the three kings of three ages and three continents carries a genuine theological point (the magi as the firstfruits of all the nations). But Matthew’s wise men remain what he left them — unnamed, unnumbered, and of no homeland more precise than “the east” — and the cult’s one evidential contribution is Marco Polo’s tug, however faint, toward Persia over Arabia.
