Justin Martyr on the magi — the wise men “from Arabia”

Dates: c. 155–160 (the Dialogue with Trypho, a debate set at Ephesus and written some years after Justin’s First Apology); Justin b. c. 100 at Flavia Neapolis (Nablus), martyred at Rome c. 165 · Wikipedia
Summary: The earliest source to give the magi a homeland. Where Matthew says only “from the east” and Ignatius says nothing at all about who they were, Justin states flatly, and repeats at least six times across the Dialogue, that the wise men came “from Arabia.” He is not reporting a travel itinerary but reading prophecy: he takes Isaiah 8:4 (the “power of Damascus and spoils of Samaria” carried off “before the king of Assyria”) and Psalm 72 (the “kings of Arabia and Seba” bringing gifts, and “the gold of Arabia”) as fulfilled in the magi — and since he places Damascus “in the region of Arabia” and calls Herod the “king of Assyria,” the wise men must, on his reading, have come from Arabia. This is the fountainhead of the patristic Arabia tradition later echoed by Tertullian and Epiphanius, and it is the primary support for the Arabia argument (weighed against the rival Persia and Babylon readings).

Claims & evidence

“Magi who came from Arabia worshipped Him” (Dialogue 77–78)

The Arabia statement is embedded in Justin’s long argument that Isaiah 8:4 — “before the child knows how to call father or mother, he shall take the power of Damascus and spoils of Samaria in the presence of the king of Assyria” — was fulfilled not in Hezekiah (Trypho’s reading) but in Christ. Justin’s solution is to identify the “king of Assyria” with Herod and the “power of Damascus” with the magi, who bring their treasure “before” Herod on their way to the child.

For at the time of His birth, Magi who came from Arabia worshipped Him, coming first to Herod, who then was sovereign in your land, and whom the Scripture calls king of Assyria on account of his ungodly and sinful character.

— Justin Martyr, Dialogue with Trypho 77 (c. 155–160), newadvent.org (ANF 1, trans. Roberts-Donaldson).1 The next chapter tells the whole nativity through the same lens, and states the homeland twice more — once at Herod’s court, once at the manger:

Now this king Herod, at the time when the Magi came to him from Arabia, and said they knew from a star which appeared in the heavens that a King had been born in your country… Accordingly the Magi from Arabia came to Bethlehem and worshipped the Child, and presented Him with gifts, gold and frankincense, and myrrh; but returned not to Herod, being warned in a revelation after worshipping the Child in Bethlehem.

— Justin Martyr, Dialogue with Trypho 78 (c. 155–160), newadvent.org.2

Repeated as settled fact (Dialogue 88, 102, 103, 106)

For Justin the homeland is not a one-off flourish but a fixed datum he can appeal to in passing whenever the magi come up. He calls them “the Magi from Arabia” when arguing that Christ possessed His power from birth (88), “the Arabian Magi” when narrating Herod’s plot (102), “the Magi from Arabia” again in the same context (103), and once more when he returns to the star at the close of the Psalm 22 exposition (106).

And let this be a proof to you, namely, what I told you was done by the Magi from Arabia, who as soon as the Child was born came to worship Him, for even at His birth He was in possession of His power. … king Herod, having learned from the Arabian Magi about Him, made a plot to put Him to death… Accordingly, when a star rose in heaven at the time of His birth, as is recorded in the memoirs of His apostles, the Magi from Arabia, recognising the sign by this, came and worshipped Him.

— Justin Martyr, Dialogue with Trypho 88, 102, 106 (c. 155–160), composite of the three notices, chs. 88 and 102–106.3

Why Arabia? The prophetic engine (Isaiah 8:4, Psalm 72, and “Damascus in Arabia”)

Justin never argues from geography, tradition, or the astronomers’ guilds — he argues from Scripture, and the Arabia reading falls out of two texts. First Psalm 72 (Psalm 71 in his Septuagint), which he had already quoted in full against Trypho, names Arabia twice as the source of the king’s tribute:

The kings of Tarshish and the isles shall offer gifts; the kings of Arabia and Seba shall offer gifts… And He shall live, and to Him shall be given of the gold of Arabia.

— Justin Martyr, Dialogue with Trypho 34, quoting Psalm 72 (LXX 71), newadvent.org.4 Second, Isaiah 8:4: to make the “spoils of Samaria” and the “power of Damascus” describe the magi’s gifts, Justin has to locate Damascus in the magi’s own country — and he does, insisting on it against any objector:

And none of you can deny that Damascus was, and is, in the region of Arabia, although now it belongs to what is called Syrophoenicia.

— Justin Martyr, Dialogue with Trypho 78, newadvent.org.5 The two texts interlock: Psalm 72 makes the gift-bearers Arabian, and Isaiah 8:4 (with Damascus placed in Arabia and Herod cast as the “king of Assyria”) makes their treasure the “power of Damascus” carried off before that king. The homeland is thus a conclusion Justin draws from prophecy, not an independent geographical report — which is exactly why the later Persia/Babylon consensus, built on the word magoi as a technical term for the Iranian priesthood, cuts against it.

Notes

  • Justin is the earliest witness to name a homeland for the magi — roughly a half-century before Tertullian, who likewise places them in the East “well-nigh” as Arabs (reading Psalm 72 the same way), and well before Epiphanius. The Arabia tradition is a minority stream against the more common Persia/Babylon identification, and its whole strength rests on the same proof-texts Justin uses here; the rival readings are weighed on the Arabia, Persia, and Babylon argument pages.
  • Frankincense and myrrh are genuinely products of Arabia (South Arabia and the Horn of Africa), and gold figures in the Arabian tribute of Psalm 72 — a point in the argument’s favour. Against it, Matthew’s magoi is the standard Greek term for the astrologer-priests of Persia and Babylon, and the “star in the east” motif is at home in Babylonian celestial divination; the mainstream reading takes those as decisive.
  • Justin’s reading is exegetically driven throughout: the “king of Assyria” is Herod, the “power of Damascus” is the magi’s gifts, and even the “voice in Ramah” of the slaughter is relocated “to Arabia (for there is in Arabia at this very time a place called Rama).” The homeland claim is one thread in a dense typological weave, which is both its pedigree (it is old and theologically motivated) and its limit (it is inference from prophecy, not report).
  • Quotations follow the Roberts-Donaldson translation (ANF 1). The full Dialogue is also available as a single text at Dialogue with Trypho.
