Ignatius of Antioch on the magi’s star

Dates: c. 107 (the Epistle to the Ephesians, written under guard on the road to martyrdom at Rome); Ignatius d. c. 108 · Wikipedia
Summary: The earliest extant comment on the magi’s star after Matthew. In one paragraph of the Epistle to the Ephesians (ch. 19), Ignatius describes a new star that outshone sun, moon, and every other star, whose novelty threw the heavens and their watchers into perplexity — and whose appearing dissolved “every kind of magic” and abolished “the old kingdom.” He says nothing of who the magi were, how many, or whence; his reading of the episode is theological. It bears on a question the later tradition returned to often — that the one group Scripture shows a star guiding to Christ were astrologers — and for Ignatius the star that led the star-watchers ended their art. The place of that reading in the wider debate over star-divination is set out on the astrology timeline; here it stands as the earliest extant interpretation of the star.

Claims & evidence

A new star, above all the stars, that struck the heavens with perplexity

Ignatius introduces the star inside a meditation on the three “mysteries” hidden from “the prince of this world” — Mary’s virginity, her child-bearing, and the death of the Lord. The star is how those hidden things were at last “manifested to the world.” What he stresses is its novelty: it is unlike anything else in the sky, it eclipses the whole celestial chorus, and its strangeness provokes a general astonishment.

A star shone forth in heaven above all the other stars, the light of which was inexpressible, while its novelty struck men with astonishment. And all the rest of the stars, with the sun and moon, formed a chorus to this star, and its light was exceedingly great above them all. And there was agitation felt as to whence this new spectacle came, so unlike to everything else [in the heavens].

— Ignatius of Antioch, Epistle to the Ephesians 19 (c. 107), newadvent.org. This is the shorter recension in the Roberts-Donaldson translation; the parallel long recension expands the same lines. In Ignatius’s picture the sun, moon, and stars “form a chorus” to the new star, and its arrival is a “new spectacle” whose origin no one could place.1

Every kind of magic destroyed, the old kingdom abolished

The next sentence is the earliest extant statement that the star ended magic. In Ignatius’s account the consequence of the star’s appearing is a general collapse of the magical order — sorcery undone, the “bond of wickedness” broken, ignorance lifted, and the “old kingdom” brought to an end by God appearing in human form.

Hence every kind of magic was destroyed, and every bond of wickedness disappeared; ignorance was removed, and the old kingdom abolished, God Himself being manifested in human form for the renewal of eternal life. And now that took a beginning which had been prepared by God. Henceforth all things were in a state of tumult, because He meditated the abolition of death.

— Ignatius of Antioch, Epistle to the Ephesians 19 (c. 107), newadvent.org. Later writers read the star the same way in their own terms: Tertullian (astrology “allowed until the Gospel”), Chrysostom (“not a star at all, ” and Christ came “to put down astrology”), and Gregory the Great (the star followed the Child, not the Child the star); their readings are set out on the astrology timeline.2

Placement on the timeline

  • Ignatius is the earliest witness on this timeline after Matthew, and the earliest extant interpretation of the star of any kind. He says nothing about the magi as persons — no number, no homeland, no rank; the three, the kings, and the names Caspar, Melchior, and Balthasar appear in later sources. His interest is in what the star did.
  • The passage’s later use is chiefly in the debate over astrology. The statement that “every kind of magic was destroyed” is Ignatius’s own; Matthew’s notice does not make it.
  • Quotations follow the Roberts-Donaldson (ANF 1) translation of the shorter recension; the longer recension expands the same chapter and reads the star to the same end (“magic became utterly ridiculous… every law of wickedness vanished away”). The chapter is numbered 19 in both.
