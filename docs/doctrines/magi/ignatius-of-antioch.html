Ignatius of Antioch on the magi’s star

Dates: c. 107 (the Epistle to the Ephesians, written under guard on the road to martyrdom at Rome); Ignatius d. c. 108 · Wikipedia
Summary: The earliest datable comment on the magi’s star after Matthew himself. In a single soaring paragraph of the Epistle to the Ephesians (ch. 19), Ignatius describes a new star that outshone sun, moon, and every other star, whose novelty threw the heavens and their watchers into perplexity — and whose appearing dissolved “every kind of magic” and abolished “the old kingdom.” He is not interested in who the magi were, how many, or whence; his sole reading of the episode is theological, and it fixes at the very start of the tradition the church’s standing answer to a nagging problem — that the one group Scripture shows a star guiding to Christ were astrologers. For Ignatius the star that led the star-watchers is the star that broke their art. Because that reading cuts across the wider quarrel over star-divination, it is weighed adversarially on the astrology timeline’s “did the star abolish astrology?” argument; here it stands simply as the earliest interpretation of the star.

Claims & evidence

A new star, above all the stars, that struck the heavens with perplexity

Ignatius introduces the star inside a meditation on the three “mysteries” hidden from “the prince of this world” — Mary’s virginity, her child-bearing, and the death of the Lord. The star is how those hidden things were at last “manifested to the world.” What he stresses is its novelty: it is unlike anything else in the sky, it eclipses the whole celestial chorus, and its strangeness provokes a general astonishment — the reaction, implicitly, of the very astrologers whose sky it has invaded.

A star shone forth in heaven above all the other stars, the light of which was inexpressible, while its novelty struck men with astonishment. And all the rest of the stars, with the sun and moon, formed a chorus to this star, and its light was exceedingly great above them all. And there was agitation felt as to whence this new spectacle came, so unlike to everything else [in the heavens].

— Ignatius of Antioch, Epistle to the Ephesians 19 (c. 107), newadvent.org. This is the short (genuine) recension in the Roberts-Donaldson translation; the parallel long recension expands the same lines. Ignatius makes the star sovereign over the ordered heaven the astrologer reads — the sun, moon, and stars “form a chorus” to it — and its unaccountable arrival is precisely what the diviner cannot place.1

Every kind of magic destroyed, the old kingdom abolished

The single sentence that made Ignatius the founding witness of the “star broke astrology” reading follows at once. The consequence of the star’s appearing is not a validated horoscope but a general collapse of the magical order — sorcery undone, the “bond of wickedness” broken, ignorance lifted, and the “old kingdom” (the reign of the astral powers) brought to an end by God appearing in human form.

Hence every kind of magic was destroyed, and every bond of wickedness disappeared; ignorance was removed, and the old kingdom abolished, God Himself being manifested in human form for the renewal of eternal life. And now that took a beginning which had been prepared by God. Henceforth all things were in a state of tumult, because He meditated the abolition of death.

— Ignatius of Antioch, Epistle to the Ephesians 19 (c. 107), newadvent.org. The reading here is compact and total: the Nativity is the end of the astral powers, not their triumph. It is the earliest form of the answer later elaborated by Tertullian (astrology “allowed until the Gospel”), Chrysostom (“not a star at all, ” and Christ came “to put down astrology”), and Gregory the Great (the star followed the Child, not the Child the star) — the whole chorus weighed on the star-of-Bethlehem argument.2

Notes

  • Ignatius is the earliest witness on this timeline after Matthew, and the earliest interpretation of the star of any kind. He tells us nothing about the magi as persons — no number, no homeland, no rank; the later developments (the three, the kings, Caspar-Melchior-Balthasar) are all downstream of him. His interest is wholly in what the star did.
  • The passage’s afterlife is chiefly on the astrology side of the tradition: it is Ignatius’s theological expansion, not Matthew’s bare notice, that “every kind of magic was destroyed.” That the abolition reading is already this early — within a lifetime of the events — is one of its genuine strengths and is credited as such on the argument page; that it is an expansion of Matthew is one of the limits weighed there too.
  • Quotations follow the Roberts-Donaldson (ANF 1) translation of the shorter, genuine recension; the longer recension is a later expansion of the same chapter and reads the star to the same end (“magic became utterly ridiculous… every law of wickedness vanished away”). The chapter is numbered 19 in both.
