The Revelation of the Magi — twelve wise men from the land of Shir, the sons of Seth

Dates: the surviving Syriac text anchored here c. 500, though the tradition it carries is older — a Greek form was known by the fifth century and the composition is commonly dated to the late second or early third century, while the sole complete manuscript is eighth-century · Wikipedia
Summary: The Revelation of the Magi is a Syriac apocryphon that tells Matthew’s star-story from the magi’s own side — and every figure that the Western church would eventually settle it stands here overturned. There are not three magi but twelve (and hints of far more); they are not Persians, Babylonians, or Arabians but mystics of the far-eastern land of Shir, at the shore of the great Ocean; they are the descendants of Seth, third son of Adam, keepers of a prophecy Adam gave Seth about a coming star; and the star itself is no heavenly body at all but Christ, who takes a star’s form and then a child’s. Preserved whole only in the eighth-century Chronicle of Zuqnin, but summarized in the West by the fifth-century pseudo-Chrysostom Opus Imperfectum in Matthaeum — whence it fed the Golden Legend and the medieval “three kings” lore — it is the fullest, and the most divergent, of all early accounts of the wise men.

Claims & evidence

Twelve magi, not three — and perhaps far more

The text presents itself as the magi’s own testimony — the compiler of the Chronicle of Zuqnin heads it “About the revelation of the Magi, and about their coming to Jerusalem, and about the gifts that they brought to Christ”1 — and it numbers them twelve, naming the twelve current magi and their fathers, rather than the three the West would later canonize. The number is not even firmly held: the narrative repeatedly calls the magi a mashritha, the Syriac word the New Testament uses for a great assembly or company, so that they read as an order much larger than twelve. As Brent Landau, the text’s modern editor, sums it up:

In Rev. Magi there are twelve Magi, or possibly more, in contrast to the traditional enumeration of three. They reside in a semi-mythical land in the Far East called “Shir”; and perhaps most startlingly, the Magi’s star is actually Jesus Christ himself, who transforms from star to human and back again throughout the course of the narrative.

— Brent Landau, introduction to The Revelation of the Magi, in Burke & Long, eds., New Testament Apocrypha: More Noncanonical Scriptures, tonyburke.ca.2 This is the earliest and most detailed source behind the twelve-magi tradition of the Syriac and Armenian East, and stands directly against both the “three” inferred from the three gifts (Origen and after) and Matthew’s own silence; how many the magi were is weighed on its own argument page.

Their homeland is Shir, in the far East — not Persia, Babylon, or Arabia

Where the Fathers placed the magi in Persia (Chrysostom), Babylon, or Arabia (Justin, Tertullian), the Revelation sends them from a legendary country at the world’s eastern rim:

The Magi are described as wise men and kings who live in the land of Shir, which is on the eastern edge of the inhabited world, at the shore of the great Ocean, east of the land of Nod, in which Adam dwelt.

— Landau, summary of Rev. Magi 2, tonyburke.ca. In a number of ancient sources “Shir” (or “Seiris”) names the silk-land, roughly today’s China, whose people were held to possess extraordinary virtue — qualities the author borrows for his magi. No other ancient Christian writing places the magi there.3 Whether the magi were Persians, Babylonians, or Arabians is weighed on its own argument page; the Revelation supplies a fourth, wholly independent answer.

They are the sons of Seth, keepers of a prophecy handed down from Adam

The magi’s authority rests on a chain of transmission running back to Eden. They descend from Seth, the third son of Adam, who received from his father a prophecy of the coming star and wrote it in what the text calls the first book ever written; Seth’s books passed down the generations — carried by Noah through the Flood in the Ark — until the present magi inherited them from their fathers.

They are descendants of Adam’s son Seth, who received a prophecy from his father Adam about the coming of a star that would signify the birth of God in human form. This prophecy is written down by Seth in the world’s first book, and is transmitted through the generations by his offspring, the Magi.

— Landau, summary of Rev. Magi 2–4, tonyburke.ca. The prophecy is grounded in Eden itself: the same star, the text says, had hovered over the Tree of Life until Adam’s fall, when it vanished from his sight.4 This is the Sethite pedigree the medieval West would inherit: the gifts laid up in the Cave of Treasures by Adam and handed to Seth (so the thirteenth-century Book of the Bee) trace back to this tradition.

“Magi” means those who pray in silence — not astrologers

The text derives the very name magi not from astrology or magic but from a practice of wordless prayer — “these Magi are called by that name because they pray in silence.” Each month, in expectation of the star, they immerse in a sacred spring, ascend their holiest peak, the Mountain of Victories, pray to God in silence, and enter the Cave of Treasures of Hidden Mysteries at its summit to read Seth’s prophecies.5 The magi themselves describe their manner of life in the first person:

… because they saw our quiet way of life, that we prayed in silence, and we said our mysteries to them with honor.

— Rev. Magi 5:11, in Landau’s summary, tonyburke.ca. (Landau notes the alleged etymology has no obvious basis in any known language and is likely an exoticizing device.) The move is pointed: it detaches the magi from the star-craft that Ignatius, Origen, and Chrysostom said the star had abolished, recasting them from the outset as silent monastics rather than diviners.6

The star is Christ himself

The most startling claim is about the star. It is not a comet (Origen), nor an angelic power wearing a star’s look (Chrysostom), but the pre-incarnate Christ: the star descends to the magi’s mountain, transforms into a small luminous human being, and later, at Bethlehem, into a luminous infant, guiding them the whole way and multiplying their food. Only the magi can see it — and each of the twelve sees it in a different form, for it appears to each according to his capacity.7 No other ancient writing identifies the Star of Bethlehem with Jesus himself, and the polymorphic, shape-shifting Christ of the story places it firmly among the second- and third-century apocrypha (the Acts of John and kin) that delight in a Christ of many forms.

Sequel: Judas Thomas baptizes the magi at Shir

The narrative closes with the apostle Judas Thomas arriving in Shir on his mission to the East, meeting the magi, baptizing them, and commissioning them to preach worldwide — an ending that reads as a later addition, since the first-person voice breaks off and the magi are suddenly spoken of from outside:

And there was great joy in the entire land of the East, and the nobles, and the poor, and women, and children from the entire land were gathered together in the love of our Lord before those nobles who were called Magi.

— Rev. Magi 28:4, in Landau’s summary, tonyburke.ca. The Judas Thomas episode — the apostle par excellence of Syriac Christianity — is what integrates these outsider-magi into the church, and Landau dates its interpolation to the third century.8

Text, date, and transmission

The manuscript and its date

The complete Revelation survives in a single eighth-century Syriac manuscript — Vatican, Biblioteca apostolica, syr. 162 — where it is embedded in a world-chronicle known as the Chronicle of Zuqnin (after the southeastern-Turkish monastery that produced it). But the composition is far older: because a Greek form of the story was already summarized in the fifth century (see below), a Syriac original must have existed by the fourth century, and Landau argues on internal and comparative grounds that the text was likely composed in the late second or early third century — which would make it, on his reckoning, among the oldest surviving Syriac Christian writings.9 The anchor of c. 500 used on the timeline is a conservative marker for the form of the tradition securely attested by that date; the underlying story is older and the manuscript later.

How it reached the Latin West: the Opus Imperfectum

A much shorter Latin summary of the same narrative appears in the Opus Imperfectum in Matthaeum, an influential commentary on Matthew wrongly attributed to John Chrysostom and now generally assigned to a fifth-century Arian author. Its version tells the same story — magi of the East, descendants of Seth, a prophecy of the star, a watch on the Mons Victorialis (“Mountain of Victories”) — and, copied for centuries among Chrysostom’s works, it carried the tradition into the medieval West: into Jacob of Voragine’s Golden Legend and John of Hildesheim’s Historia Trium Regum, the great late-medieval account of the three kings.10 So the same source that made the magi twelve in the East fed, in the West, the very “three kings” lore it contradicts.

Notes

  • This page treats the Revelation of the Magi as a datable source on the timeline — a witness to how the magi were imagined in the Syriac East — not as a historical report about the wise men themselves. Its value here is that it preserves, in full, an entire alternative to the Western settlement: twelve rather than three, Shir rather than Persia/Arabia, Sethite mystics rather than astrologers, and a star that is Christ.
  • Its twelve is the principal ancient counter-evidence to the “three” that Origen’s gift-typology and later custom fixed; the two readings, together with Matthew’s own silence on number, are weighed on the “how many magi?” argument page.
  • The list of twelve names in Rev. Magi 2:3 is shared with several other Syriac writers and is never used again in the narrative; Landau judges it a later insertion into the text rather than original to it — so even the Revelation’s twelve names are traditional accretion, not a datum of the earliest story.
  • Quotations follow Brent Landau’s translation and summary in Burke & Long, eds., New Testament Apocrypha: More Noncanonical Scriptures (Eerdmans, 2016); the trade translation is Landau, Revelation of the Magi: The Lost Tale of the Wise Men’s Journey to Bethlehem (HarperOne, 2010).
