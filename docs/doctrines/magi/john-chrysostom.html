John Chrysostom on the magi — Persian barbarians, the wiser sort, led by a star that was no star

Dates: c. 390 (the sixth and seventh of the Homilies on the Gospel of Matthew, preached at Antioch, on Matthew 2); Chrysostom c. 349–407 · Wikipedia
Summary: Chrysostom’s two homilies on Matthew 2 give the fullest patristic portrait of the magi as people. He takes them for “barbarians” of Persia — men who read the sky — yet “not simply barbarians, but the wiser sort amongst them,” whose long journey and bold speech before Herod show them sincere, and who seem to him to have been “at home also teachers of their countrymen.” Their coming, he insists, was “not the work of the star only, but also of God, who moved their soul,” as He once moved Cyrus. And God chose a star to summon them out of condescension to men accustomed to the heavens — though the object was “not of the common sort, or rather not a star at all,” but “some invisible power” that moved north-to-south, shone at mid-day, halted and hid with the travellers, and came down to stand over one roof. The whole sign was staged “to reprove the Jews for their insensibility” and to open “the door to the Gentiles.” His reading of the star as the end of star-craft is set out on the astrology timeline; this page gathers what he says about the magi themselves.

Claims & evidence

The magi were Persian “barbarians” — but the wiser sort, and bold witnesses

Chrysostom places the magi’s homeland in Persia — he reckons the star’s north-to-south course “for so is Palestine situated with respect to Persia,” and has the Jews learn the news “from a Persian tongue.” He calls them “barbarians,” but raises them above the word: it was to the child’s very head that the star came, he says,

… showing that what is born is Divine; next standing there, it leads them to worship Him; being not simply barbarians, but the wiser sort amongst them.

— Homilies on the Gospel of Matthew VII, on Matthew 2:9, newadvent.org.1 Their sincerity he reads off their conduct — the sheer length of the journey and their fearless speech before a suspicious king:

See at least the virtue of these men, not only by their coming, but also by their boldness of speech. For so that they may not seem to be a sort of impostors, they tell who showed them the way, and the length of their journey… Whence to me at least they seem to have been at home also teachers of their countrymen.

— Homilies on the Gospel of Matthew VI, on Matthew 2:1–2, newadvent.org. For Chrysostom the magi are candid men who became witnesses, carrying the report home.2

Their coming was “not the work of the star only, but also of God”

Chrysostom holds that the magi were moved by God as well as by the star, who worked on their wills as He had worked on a pagan king before them:

And this again is worth inquiry, whence it was that they came to entertain such a thought, and who it was that stirred them up to this. For it doth not seem to me to be the work of the star only, but also of God, who moved their soul; which same kind of thing He did also in the case of Cyrus, disposing him to let the Jews go. He did not however so do this as to destroy their free will.

— Homilies on the Gospel of Matthew VI, on Matthew 2:1–2, newadvent.org. In his account the star draws the eye and God moves the will — without, he adds, overriding it, as when God called Paul “from above by a voice” yet left Paul’s obedience his own.3

Why a star? God’s condescension to men who read the sky

Chrysostom takes up the question why a star at all? His answer is that God met these particular men in the idiom they knew. Prophets, a voice, or an angel would have been lost on them; a star they would follow.

“And wherefore,” one may say, “did He attract them by such a vision?” Why, how should He have done? Sent prophets? But the magi would not have submitted to prophets. Uttered a voice from above? Nay, they would not have attended. Sent an angel? But even him they would have hurried by. And so for this cause dismissing all those means, God calleth them by the things that are familiar, in exceeding condescension; and He shows a large and extraordinary star.

— Homilies on the Gospel of Matthew VI, on Matthew 2:1–2, newadvent.org. He reads the star as accommodation — God stooping to the astrologers’ own frame in order to lead them out of it.4

The object was “not a star at all,” but an invisible power

Chrysostom argues at length that the star’s behaviour shows it to have been no ordinary heavenly body but a rational power wearing a star’s appearance:

… that this star was not of the common sort, or rather not a star at all, as it seems at least to me, but some invisible power transformed into this appearance, is in the first place evident from its very course.

— Homilies on the Gospel of Matthew VI, on Matthew 2:1–2, newadvent.org. It moved north to south, not east to west; it shone “in mid-day, while the sun is shining”; it hid at Jerusalem and reappeared; and it came at last to “stand over the very head of the young child,” descending from heaven because no true star could pick out “a spot so confined, just the space of a manger and shed” — conduct “not like the motion of a star, but of some power highly endued with reason.”5 His full four-fold argument — and the accompanying claim that the Magi did not practise genethlialogy at all, for they “reasoned backwards” from a star to a newborn king — belongs to the debate over star-divination and is set out on Chrysostom’s astrology detail page.

The purpose: to shame the Jews and open the door to the Gentiles

For Chrysostom the whole spectacle had a didactic end — the summoning of star-reading foreigners to Bethlehem was a rebuke to Israel, who had the prophets and would not come:

And for what intent did it appear? To reprove the Jews for their insensibility, and to cut off from them all occasion of excuse for their willful ignorance… straightway, from the beginning, He opens the door to the Gentiles, willing through strangers to admonish His own people.

— Homilies on the Gospel of Matthew VI, on Matthew 2:1–2, newadvent.org. He presses the contrast: “wise men, at the sight of a single star,” worshipped, while the Jews “after so many prophets” did not — so that, like the men of Nineveh who repented at Jonah, the magi will “rise up, and… condemn this generation.”6

Reception and placement

  • Chrysostom’s two Antioch homilies on Matthew 2 (numbered VI and VII in the NPNF edition) give the fullest patristic account of the magi as characters — their homeland, their motives, their sincerity, and the pedagogy of their calling. Justin and Tertullian draw a homeland (Arabia) from prophecy, and Origen a mechanism (the failed magic, the heirs of Balaam); Chrysostom reads the magi’s character from the narrative: bold, candid, and teachers of their countrymen.
  • He places the magi in Persia, as Clement of Alexandria had; Justin and Tertullian placed them in Arabia. The homeland readings and their holders are set out on the disputed-reading page “Where did the magi of Matthew 2 come from?”
  • “God moved their soul, not the star only” guards free will (God “did not… destroy their free will”) while making grace, not astral fate, the cause of their coming — an answer to the worry his astrology homily addresses directly, that the star made the episode a triumph of astrology.
  • Quotations follow the NPNF (first series, vol. 10) translation by George Prevost, revised by M. B. Riddle; the homilies are numbered VI (on Matthew 2:1–2) and VII (on Matthew 2:4–10) in that edition.
