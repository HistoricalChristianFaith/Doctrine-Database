John Chrysostom on the magi — Persian barbarians, the wiser sort, led by a star that was no star

Dates: c. 390 (the sixth and seventh of the Homilies on the Gospel of Matthew, preached at Antioch, on Matthew 2); Chrysostom c. 349–407 · Wikipedia
Summary: Chrysostom’s two homilies on Matthew 2 are the fullest patristic portrait of the magi as people. He takes them for “barbarians” of Persia — men who read the sky — yet “not simply barbarians, but the wiser sort amongst them,” whose long journey and bold speech before Herod prove them sincere and make them, in effect, missionaries to their own countrymen. Their coming, he insists, was “not the work of the star only, but also of God, who moved their soul,” as He once moved Cyrus. And God chose a star to summon them out of condescension to men accustomed to the heavens — though the object was “not of the common sort, or rather not a star at all,” but “some invisible power” that moved north-to-south, shone at mid-day, halted and hid with the travellers, and came down to stand over one roof. The whole sign was staged “to reprove the Jews for their insensibility” and to open “the door to the Gentiles.” His handling of the star as the ruin of star-craft is weighed on the star-and-astrology argument; this page gathers what he says about the magi themselves.

Claims & evidence

The magi were Persian “barbarians” — but the wiser sort, and bold witnesses

Chrysostom places the magi’s homeland in Persia — he reckons the star’s north-to-south course “for so is Palestine situated with respect to Persia,” and has the Jews learn the news “from a Persian tongue.” He calls them “barbarians,” but is careful to raise them above the word: it was to the child’s very head that the star came, he says,

… showing that what is born is Divine; next standing there, it leads them to worship Him; being not simply barbarians, but the wiser sort amongst them.

— Homilies on the Gospel of Matthew VII, on Matthew 2:9, newadvent.org.1 Their sincerity he reads off their conduct — the sheer length of the journey and their fearless speech before a suspicious king:

See at least the virtue of these men, not only by their coming, but also by their boldness of speech. For so that they may not seem to be a sort of impostors, they tell who showed them the way, and the length of their journey… Whence to me at least they seem to have been at home also teachers of their countrymen.

— Homilies on the Gospel of Matthew VI, on Matthew 2:1–2, newadvent.org. The magi are thus, for Chrysostom, not credulous stargazers but candid men who became witnesses — the first-fruits of the Gentiles carrying the report home.2

Their coming was “not the work of the star only, but also of God”

Against any reading that makes the magi mere followers of a celestial cue, Chrysostom insists the decisive mover was God, working on their wills as He had worked on a pagan king before them:

And this again is worth inquiry, whence it was that they came to entertain such a thought, and who it was that stirred them up to this. For it doth not seem to me to be the work of the star only, but also of God, who moved their soul; which same kind of thing He did also in the case of Cyrus, disposing him to let the Jews go. He did not however so do this as to destroy their free will.

— Homilies on the Gospel of Matthew VI, on Matthew 2:1–2, newadvent.org. The star draws the eye; grace moves the will — and it does so, he adds, without overriding it, as when God called Paul “from above by a voice” yet left Paul’s obedience his own.3

Why a star? God’s condescension to men who read the sky

Chrysostom’s most magi-specific insight answers a question the other Fathers pass over: why a star at all? His answer is that God met these particular men in the idiom they knew. Prophets, a voice, or an angel would have been lost on them; a star they would follow.

“And wherefore,” one may say, “did He attract them by such a vision?” Why, how should He have done? Sent prophets? But the magi would not have submitted to prophets. Uttered a voice from above? Nay, they would not have attended. Sent an angel? But even him they would have hurried by. And so for this cause dismissing all those means, God calleth them by the things that are familiar, in exceeding condescension; and He shows a large and extraordinary star.

— Homilies on the Gospel of Matthew VI, on Matthew 2:1–2, newadvent.org. This is the seed of a long tradition of reading the star as accommodation — God stooping to the astrologers’ own frame in order to lead them out of it.4

The object was “not a star at all,” but an invisible power

The star that led them, Chrysostom argues at length, betrays by its very behaviour that it was no ordinary heavenly body but a rational power wearing a star’s appearance:

… that this star was not of the common sort, or rather not a star at all, as it seems at least to me, but some invisible power transformed into this appearance, is in the first place evident from its very course.

— Homilies on the Gospel of Matthew VI, on Matthew 2:1–2, newadvent.org. It moved north to south, not east to west; it shone “in mid-day, while the sun is shining”; it hid at Jerusalem and reappeared; and it came at last to “stand over the very head of the young child,” descending from heaven because no true star could pick out “a spot so confined, just the space of a manger and shed” — conduct “not like the motion of a star, but of some power highly endued with reason.”5 Because the point turns on the church’s quarrel with star-divination rather than on the magi’s identity, the full four-fold demonstration — and the accompanying claim that the Magi did not practise genethlialogy at all, for they “reasoned backwards” from a star to a newborn king — is weighed adversarially on the “did the star abolish astrology?” argument, and treated more fully on Chrysostom’s astrology detail page.

The purpose: to shame the Jews and open the door to the Gentiles

For Chrysostom the whole spectacle was staged for a didactic end — the summoning of star-reading foreigners to Bethlehem was a rebuke to Israel, who had the prophets and would not come:

And for what intent did it appear? To reprove the Jews for their insensibility, and to cut off from them all occasion of excuse for their willful ignorance… straightway, from the beginning, He opens the door to the Gentiles, willing through strangers to admonish His own people.

— Homilies on the Gospel of Matthew VI, on Matthew 2:1–2, newadvent.org. He drives the contrast home: “wise men, at the sight of a single star,” worshipped, while the Jews “after so many prophets” did not — so that, like the men of Nineveh who repented at Jonah, the magi will “rise up, and… condemn this generation.”6

Notes

  • Chrysostom’s two Antioch homilies on Matthew 2 (numbered VI and VII in the NPNF edition) are the richest patristic account of the magi as characters — their homeland, their motives, their sincerity, and the pedagogy of their calling. Where earlier witnesses fix a homeland from prophecy (Justin, Tertullian: Arabia) or a mechanism from theology (Origen: the failed magic, the heirs of Balaam), Chrysostom reads the magi’s character off the narrative: bold, candid, and already half-evangelists.
  • His Persia is the majority patristic placement, against the Arabia stream that runs from Justin through Tertullian; whether the magi were Persians, Babylonians, or Arabians is weighed on its own argument page.
  • The “God moved their soul, not the star only” move is characteristically Antiochene: it guards free will (God “did not… destroy their free will”) while making grace, not astral fate, the true cause of their coming — a quiet answer to the same worry the astrology homily meets head-on, that the star made the episode a triumph of astrology.
  • Quotations follow the NPNF (first series, vol. 10) translation by George Prevost, revised by M. B. Riddle; the homilies are numbered VI (on Matthew 2:1–2) and VII (on Matthew 2:4–10) in that edition.
