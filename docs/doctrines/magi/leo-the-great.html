Leo the Great on the magi — the three, the Gentiles’ calling, but not yet kings

Dates: c. 450 (the Sermons on the Epiphany, 31–38, preached across his pontificate, 440–461, at Rome); Leo b. c. 400, pope from 440, d. 461 · Wikipedia
Summary: Leo is the fullest fifth-century Latin voice on the Epiphany, and the point at which the Western reading of the magi settles into the shape the Middle Ages inherited — but not the whole of it. He speaks throughout of three wise men; he fixes the threefold gift-creed (gold to the King, incense to God, myrrh to Man); and he reads the whole episode as the calling of the Gentiles, the wise men standing in for the promised star-multitude of Abraham while carnal Israel, reading the prophecy it will not believe, is displaced. What he pointedly does not do is crown the magi as kings: in Leo the gold honours the kingship of Christ, not the rank of the magi, who remain simply “wise men.” The explicit reading of the magi themselves as kings — the “three kings” of Ps 72 and Isa 60 — is a later development, entrenched by the Latin liturgy of the “three kings” (Caesarius of Arles, often named for the step, in fact keeps Leo’s reserve), not yet present in the greatest Latin Epiphany preacher of the fifth century.

Claims & evidence

Three wise men, and the threefold gift-creed (Sermon 31)

Preaching on the Epiphany, Leo takes the number as settled — not argued for, simply named — and reads the opened treasure as a compressed creed. The gifts are “consciously symbolical,” each naming a truth about the child:

To three wise men, therefore, appeared a star of new splendour in the region of the East, which, being brighter and fairer than the other stars, might easily attract the eyes and minds of those that looked on it …

They adore the Word in flesh, the Wisdom in infancy, the Power in weakness, the Lord of majesty in the reality of man … The incense they offer to God, the myrrh to Man, the gold to the King, consciously paying honour to the Divine and human Nature in union.

— Leo the Great, Sermon 31 (On the Epiphany, I), c. 450, newadvent.org (NPNF2 12, trans. Feltoe).1 The gift-symbolism is Irenaeus’s and Origen’s threefold reading, now standard: gold confesses kingship, incense Godhead, myrrh mortality. The crucial point for the later legend is the direction of the gold: in Leo it is paid to the King — Christ — not offered by kings. He repeats the creed in almost the same words at Sermon 33 (“with gold they honour the Person of a King, with myrrh that of Man, with incense that of God”) and Sermon 34 (“in gold royal honour, in incense Divine adoration, in myrrh the acknowledgment of mortality”).2

The Epiphany as the calling of the Gentiles — Abraham’s star-seed (Sermon 33)

Leo’s governing theme is not who the magi were but what their coming means: they are the first-fruits of the nations, the promised progeny of Abraham “compared to the stars in multitude,” summoned fittingly by a star. He reads the wise men as the heirs designated under the figure of the stars, and their worship as the moment the covenant passes from carnal Israel to “believers of every nation”:

… the heirs designated under the figure of the stars are awakened by the rising of a new star, that the ministrations of the heaven might do service in that wherein the witness of the heaven had been adduced.

In the three Magi let all people worship the Author of the universe: and let God be known not in Judaea alone, but in all the world, so that everywhere “His name” may be “great in Israel.”

— Leo the Great, Sermon 33 (On the Epiphany, III), c. 450, newadvent.org (NPNF2 12).3 The counterpoint is sharp: “truth enlightens the wise men, unbelief blinds the experts: carnal Israel understands not what it reads.” The magi matter to Leo as the Gentiles’ door into the family of the patriarchs — the theme that made the Epiphany, in the West, the feast of the nations’ calling. He also carries forward the Balaam link, quoting “A star shall rise out of Jacob, and a man shall rise out of Israel, and shall rule the nations” (Numbers 24:17) as the prophecy by which the wise men might have read the sign.4

Not kings — the crowning is later

Across all four Epiphany sermons Leo calls the visitors “wise men” or “the three Magi” and never once kings. He has every proof-text the later tradition would use — the gold, the tribute, Psalm readings of the nations coming to worship — and still does not take the step of making the magi royal; the kingship in his sermons is always Christ’s. The NPNF editor flags exactly this at the number-three note to Sermon 31: the number “three” has “no further scriptural support than the possible inference from their threefold offerings,” and “it will be noticed that S. Leo knows nothing of their being kings, though that tradition is apparently as old as Tertullian (adv. Marc. iii. 13).”5

So the “crowning” of the magi is not accomplished in the fifth-century Latin pulpit. The seed Tertullian had planted — “the East well-nigh held the magi as kings,” grafted onto Psalm 72 — still lies dormant in Leo. The explicit reading of the magi themselves as kings surfaces only later, hardened by the Latin liturgy of the “three kings” and by such texts as the pseudo-Bede Collectanea. Caesarius of Arles (bishop 502–542) is the name usually attached to that step, but the attribution does not survive inspection: his own Epiphany sermon on the magi keeps exactly Leo’s reserve — the gold signifies the royalty of Christ, not the rank of the visitors, and the journey home “by another way” is allegorized as a pilgrimage of conversion — so the crown belongs to the liturgy and the later legend, not to any located sixth-century sermon. The proof-texts pressed into that service — Psalm 72:10–11 (“the kings of Tarshish… the kings of Sheba and Seba shall offer gifts”) and Isaiah 60:3, 6 (“kings to the brightness of thy rising… they shall bring gold and incense”) — are prophecies about kings coming to the Messiah, and whether they license calling the magi kings is weighed on the “were the magi kings?” argument page.6

Notes

  • Leo’s contribution to the magi tradition is real but often mis-stated. He is the Latin fixative for two things — the three and the gift-creed — and for the Epiphany-as-Gentiles’-calling theme; he is not the crowner. Reading him as an early “three kings” witness runs past his own words, which reserve kingship for Christ.
  • On the number, Leo simply preaches “three” without defending it — the inference from the three gifts that Origen’s gift-typology had already made standard. Whether three is the right count against the Eastern twelve is weighed on the “how many magi?” question; see the Revelation of the Magi.
  • The star-and-astrology thread is muted in Leo compared with Chrysostom: he treats the star as a God-given sign to the nations, not as an occasion to attack star-craft, and the spurious astrological gloss at Sermon 33 (“which to those who gaze aright ceases not daily to appear…”) is bracketed as an interpolation in the manuscripts. The abolition-of-magic reading belongs to Ignatius, Origen, and Chrysostom, weighed on the star-and-astrology argument.
  • Quotations follow the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, series 2, vol. 12 (Charles Lett Feltoe’s translation of Leo’s Sermons). The Epiphany group is Sermons 31, 33, 34, and 36.
