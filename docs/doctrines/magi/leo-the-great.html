Leo the Great on the magi — the three, the Gentiles’ calling, and the kingship of Christ

Dates: c. 450 (the Sermons on the Epiphany, 31–38, preached across his pontificate, 440–461, at Rome); Leo b. c. 400, pope from 440, d. 461 · Wikipedia
Summary: Leo is the fullest fifth-century Latin voice on the Epiphany. He speaks throughout of three wise men; he sets out the threefold gift-creed (gold to the King, incense to God, myrrh to Man); and he reads the whole episode as the calling of the Gentiles, the wise men standing for the promised star-multitude of Abraham while carnal Israel, reading the prophecy it will not believe, is displaced. He does not call the magi kings: in Leo the gold honours the kingship of Christ, and the visitors are “wise men.” His NPNF editor notes that “S. Leo knows nothing of their being kings.” Whether the magi were kings is set out on the disputed-reading page “Were the magi kings?”

Claims & evidence

Three wise men, and the threefold gift-creed (Sermon 31)

Preaching on the Epiphany, Leo names the number without arguing for it, and reads the opened treasure as a compressed creed. The gifts are “consciously symbolical,” each naming a truth about the child:

To three wise men, therefore, appeared a star of new splendour in the region of the East, which, being brighter and fairer than the other stars, might easily attract the eyes and minds of those that looked on it …

They adore the Word in flesh, the Wisdom in infancy, the Power in weakness, the Lord of majesty in the reality of man … The incense they offer to God, the myrrh to Man, the gold to the King, consciously paying honour to the Divine and human Nature in union.

— Leo the Great, Sermon 31 (On the Epiphany, I), c. 450, newadvent.org (NPNF2 12, trans. Feltoe).1 The gift-symbolism is the threefold reading of Irenaeus and Origen: gold confesses kingship, incense Godhead, myrrh mortality. In Leo the gold is paid to the King — Christ. He repeats the creed in almost the same words at Sermon 33 (“with gold they honour the Person of a King, with myrrh that of Man, with incense that of God”) and Sermon 34 (“in gold royal honour, in incense Divine adoration, in myrrh the acknowledgment of mortality”).2

The Epiphany as the calling of the Gentiles — Abraham’s star-seed (Sermon 33)

Leo’s governing theme is what the magi’s coming means: they are the first-fruits of the nations, the promised progeny of Abraham “compared to the stars in multitude,” summoned fittingly by a star. He reads the wise men as the heirs designated under the figure of the stars, and their worship as the moment the covenant passes from carnal Israel to “believers of every nation”:

… the heirs designated under the figure of the stars are awakened by the rising of a new star, that the ministrations of the heaven might do service in that wherein the witness of the heaven had been adduced.

In the three Magi let all people worship the Author of the universe: and let God be known not in Judaea alone, but in all the world, so that everywhere “His name” may be “great in Israel.”

— Leo the Great, Sermon 33 (On the Epiphany, III), c. 450, newadvent.org (NPNF2 12).3 He sets against it: “truth enlightens the wise men, unbelief blinds the experts: carnal Israel understands not what it reads.” The magi matter to Leo as the Gentiles’ door into the family of the patriarchs — a theme under which the Epiphany was kept in the West as the feast of the nations’ calling. He also carries forward the Balaam link, quoting “A star shall rise out of Jacob, and a man shall rise out of Israel, and shall rule the nations” (Numbers 24:17) as the prophecy by which the wise men might have read the sign.4

“Wise men,” and the kingship of Christ

Across all four Epiphany sermons Leo calls the visitors “wise men” or “the three Magi” and never kings. He cites the gold, the tribute, and the psalms of the nations coming to worship, and the kingship in his sermons is Christ’s. The NPNF editor notes this at the number-three note to Sermon 31: the number “three” has “no further scriptural support than the possible inference from their threefold offerings,” and “it will be noticed that S. Leo knows nothing of their being kings, though that tradition is apparently as old as Tertullian (adv. Marc. iii. 13).”5

Tertullian had reported, two centuries before, that “the East generally regarded the magi as kings.” The Latin liturgy of the “three kings” and texts such as the pseudo-Bede Collectanea later present the magi in royal terms. Robin Jensen cross-references Caesarius of Arles (bishop 502–542) on the magi’s royal status; his Epiphany sermon on the magi reads the gold as a sign of Christ’s royalty and allegorizes the journey home “by another way” as a pilgrimage of conversion. The proof-texts later read of royal magi — Psalm 72:10–11 (“the kings of Tarshish… the kings of Sheba and Seba shall offer gifts”) and Isaiah 60:3, 6 (“kings to the brightness of thy rising… they shall bring gold and incense”) — speak of kings coming to the Messiah; how they bear on the magi is set out on the disputed-reading page “Were the magi kings?”6

Reception and placement

  • Leo’s Epiphany sermons carry the three, the gift-creed, and the Epiphany as the calling of the Gentiles; they reserve kingship for Christ. His NPNF editor reads him as knowing “nothing of their being kings.”
  • On the number, Leo preaches “three” without defending it; his editor notes that it has “no further scriptural support than the possible inference from their threefold offerings.” How many the magi were, including the Eastern twelve, is set out on the disputed-reading page “How many magi came to Bethlehem?”; see also the Revelation of the Magi.
  • Leo treats the star as a God-given sign to the nations rather than as an occasion to discuss star-craft, and the astrological gloss at Sermon 33 (“which to those who gaze aright ceases not daily to appear…”) is bracketed as an interpolation in the manuscripts. The reading that the star ended magic is found in Ignatius, Origen, and Chrysostom; see the astrology timeline.
  • Quotations follow the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, series 2, vol. 12 (Charles Lett Feltoe’s translation of Leo’s Sermons). The Epiphany group is Sermons 31, 33, 34, and 36.
