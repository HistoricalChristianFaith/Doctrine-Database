Martin Luther on purgatory and prayer for the dead

Dates: c. 1544 (the Church Postil; cf. the 1531 Apology) · Wikipedia
Summary: Martin Luther (1483–1546), the Wittenberg reformer, rejected purgatory and the endowed Masses, vigils, and anniversary offerings for the dead (the Smalcald Articles call purgatory "nothing but a specter of the devil"), while he did not forbid simple prayer for the departed. In his Church Postil sermon for the First Sunday after Trinity (on Dives and Lazarus) he reasons that, since God has given no command to pray for the dead and it is "uncertain" whether final judgment has been passed on departed souls, a Christian may pray once or twice for the dead "in uncertainty" and then commend them to God; Masses, vigils and prayers "to be repeated forever for the dead every year" he calls "nothing but blasphemy of God." Melanchthon's Apology of the Augsburg Confession (1531), later included in the Book of Concord, states: "the ancients speak of prayer for the dead, which we do not prohibit," while rejecting the application of the Lord's Supper ex opere operato on behalf of the dead.

Claims & evidence

Luther: "no command from God to pray for the dead"

We have no command from God to pray for the dead; therefore no one sins by not praying for them.

— Church Postil, sermon for the First Sunday after Trinity (on Luke 16:19–31, Dives and Lazarus), §28, godwithuslc.org. Luther reasons from the absence of a command: no one sins by omitting the prayer, and, since God has not said how the departed fare, the prayer is not forbidden. He thereby removes any obligation to pray for the dead while leaving the practice permissible.

Praying "in uncertainty," once or twice, then commending them to God

let it rest in uncertainty and speak thus: Dear God, if the departed souls be in a state that they may yet be helped, then I pray that thou wouldst be gracious.

— Church Postil, First Sunday after Trinity, §29, godwithuslc.org. Because, as he says, it is "uncertain" whether final judgment has been passed on these souls, Luther frames the prayer conditionally — if they can yet be helped — and limits it: "when you have thus prayed once or twice, then let it be sufficient and commend them unto God." Of masses, vigils and prayers "to be repeated forever for the dead every year" he says that "such prayers are nothing but blasphemy of God" (§30).

The Smalcald Articles: purgatory "a specter of the devil"

Therefore purgatory, and every solemnity, rite, and commerce connected with it, is to be regarded as nothing but a specter of the devil. For it conflicts with the chief article [which teaches] that only Christ, and not the works of men, are to help [set free] souls.

— Smalcald Articles, Part II, Article II (Of the Mass), §12, bookofconcord.org. Luther, who signed the articles and writes in their preface that "I have accordingly compiled these articles," treats purgatory under the Mass, which he describes as "used almost alone for the dead": the masses for souls, vigils, and "weekly, monthly, and yearly celebrations of obsequies," the Common Week and All Souls' Day. He grounds the rejection in "the chief article," that only Christ sets souls free, and adds that "nothing has been [divinely] commanded or enjoined upon us concerning the dead."

The Apology of the Augsburg Confession: prayer for the dead, "which we do not prohibit"

Now, as regards the adversaries' citing the Fathers concerning the offering for the dead, we know that the ancients speak of prayer for the dead, which we do not prohibit; but we disapprove of the application ex opere operato of the Lord's Supper on behalf of the dead.

— Apology of the Augsburg Confession, Article XXIV (Of the Mass), §94 (Melanchthon, 1531), thebookofconcord.org (cf. ccel.org). The Apology, later included in the Book of Concord, distinguishes prayer for the dead, which it does "not prohibit," from the ex opere operato application of the Lord's Supper on behalf of the dead, which it rejects. The same article (§96) answers the charge that the Lutherans side with Aerius, whom "Epiphanius testifies … held that prayers for the dead are useless": "Neither do we favor Aerius."

Reception and debate

  • Luther rejects purgatory, the endowed Masses, and any obligation to pray for the dead, and permits a restrained, conditional prayer for the departed. Calvin (1559) calls purgatory "a deadly device of Satan" and holds that the Law and Gospel contain not "one syllable" warranting prayer for the dead. The two Reformers agree in denying purgatory and differ on prayer for the dead.

  • Later Lutheran bodies differ on the practice. The Wisconsin Evangelical Lutheran Synod states: "We do not pray for those who have died because in the case of Christians those prayers are unnecessary, and in regard to unbelievers those prayers are futile" (wels.net). ⚠ unverified: that the Lutheran Church–Missouri Synod likewise discourages or rejects prayer for the dead.
