The Liturgy of Addai and Mari on purgatory and prayer for the dead

Dates: c. 300 · Wikipedia
Summary: The East-Syriac Anaphora of the Apostles Addai and Mari (also called the Liturgy of the Blessed Apostles) is one of the oldest Eucharistic prayers still in use, the historic liturgy of the Church of the East. In the anaphora the priest offers the oblation expressly "for all the dead that have gone from amongst us" and prays God to "spare the offences and sins of the dead." It is a liturgical witness from the Syriac East to the Eucharistic offering on behalf of the departed.

Claims & evidence

The oblation is offered for the departed

O Lord God Almighty, accept this oblation for the whole Holy Catholic Church, and for all the pious and righteous fathers who have been pleasing to Thee, and for all the prophets and apostles, and for all the martyrs and confessors, and for all that mourn, that are in straits, and are sick, and for all that are under difficulties and trials, and for all the weak and the oppressed, and for all the dead that have gone from amongst us.

— The Liturgy of the Blessed Apostles (Anaphora of Addai and Mari), source. The dead are named alongside the living within the one Eucharistic offering: the sacrifice avails "for all the dead that have gone from amongst us." The prayer names the departed among those for whom the oblation is offered, without argument for the practice.

A direct intercession that God spare the sins of the dead

O Christ, peace of those in heaven and great rest of those below … Spare the offences and sins of the dead, through Thy grace and mercies for ever.

— The Liturgy of the Blessed Apostles (Anaphora of Addai and Mari), source. The prayer asks that God "spare the offences and sins of the dead," a petition for mercy to the departed made within the anaphora.

Reception and debate

  • Dating is layered, as with all anaphoras: the prayer's written form is later than its core, and it is placed here c. 300 for that core. The Pontifical Council for Promoting Christian Unity's Guidelines for Admission to the Eucharist between the Chaldean Church and the Assyrian Church of the East (2001) calls it "one of the most ancient Anaphoras" (christianunity.va); ⚠ unverified: the third-century dating of its core. Compare the Latin witnesses Tertullian and Cyprian, and the Greek liturgical witness of Cyril of Jerusalem.
  • As an anaphora the text records the communal prayer of a church rather than one writer's opinion. The other anaphoras on the timeline are the Liturgy of Saint Mark and the Liturgy of Saint James; on how such witnesses bear on the history of the practice, see Was prayer for the dead uncontested in the ancient Church?
  • On the fire of 1 Corinthians 3, a separate strand of the history, see Origen, John Chrysostom, and Thomas Aquinas.
