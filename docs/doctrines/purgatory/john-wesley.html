John Wesley on purgatory and prayer for the dead

Dates: c. 1750 · Wikipedia
Summary: John Wesley (1703–1791), the founder of Methodism and a lifelong Church-of-England priest, held and practiced prayer for the dead. Drawing on the ancient Church, the Book of Common Prayer's burial office, and the early liturgies, he composed and published forms of prayer for the faithful departed, and defended the practice against Bishop George Lavington, who had charged that it was "Popish." He described his prayer as a general one for the departed faithful, "to the same effect" as his Church's Burial Service. The Articles of Religion he adapted for the American Methodists (1784) retain the Anglican article against "the Romish doctrine concerning purgatory."

Claims & evidence

Wesley: prayer for the faithful departed "a duty"

I believe it to be a duty to observe to pray for the Faithful Departed.

— John Wesley, from an undated manuscript, reproduced in Harrington W. Holden, John Wesley in Company with High Churchmen (5th ed., 1872), pp. 84–87, Google Books. Holden, gathering Wesley's scattered statements, observes that Wesley "taught the propriety of praying for the dead, practised it himself, and provided forms, that others might" — putting them forward "not tentatively or apologetically, but as considering such prayer a settled matter of Christian practice."

Wesley's appeal to antiquity, the Church of England, and the Lord's Prayer

In this kind of general prayer, therefore, for the Faithful Departed, I conceive myself to be clearly justified, both by the earliest Antiquity, by the Church of England, and by the Lord's Prayer.

— John Wesley, reply to Bishop George Lavington (1750), in Holden, John Wesley in Company with High Churchmen, pp. 84–87, Google Books. Lavington, in The Enthusiasm of Methodists and Papists Compared, had charged Wesley with crypto-Romanism for citing "the words of an ancient Liturgy — 'for the Faithful Departed.'" Wesley answers that whenever he uses those words he prays "to the same effect" as the Burial Service of his own Church — a general intercession for the departed faithful, which he grounds in "the earliest Antiquity."

The model: the Prayer Book's own burial collect for the departed

that we, with all those that are departed in the true faith of thy holy Name, may have our perfect consummation and bliss, both in body and soul, in thy eternal and everlasting glory.

— The Book of Common Prayer, the Burial of the Dead (the collect after the committal), churchofengland.org. This is the "kind of general prayer" Wesley defends and imitates: the Church of England's own office asks God, for the living together with "all those that are departed in the true faith," to bring both to their "perfect consummation and bliss." Wesley cites the Church of England's own office as warrant for his practice.

Reception and debate

  • Wesley practiced and defended prayer for the faithful departed. The Articles of Religion he adapted from the Church of England's Thirty-Nine Articles for the American Methodists (in The Sunday Service of the Methodists, 1784) include Article XIV, "Of Purgatory": "The Romish doctrine concerning purgatory, pardon, worshiping, and adoration, as well of images as of relics, and also invocation of saints, is a fond thing, vainly invented, and grounded upon no warrant of Scripture, but repugnant to the Word of God" (crivoice.org, text of the 1808 Discipline collated with the 1784 Sunday Service). The purgatory timeline traces the practice of prayer for the dead and the teaching on purgatory as two strands; compare Luther and Calvin.
  • Source note: Wesley's words here survive chiefly through Harrington Holden's 1872 compilation (which prints Wesley's High-Church statements in parallel with the Tractarians'); the manuscript "duty" sentence is an undated Wesley fragment, and the "earliest Antiquity … Church of England … Lord's Prayer" sentence is from his 1750 reply to Lavington. The BCP collect is quoted directly from the primary liturgy.
  • ⚠ unverified: Methodism after Wesley was divided on the practice; later evangelical Methodism in particular often dropped it, while the broader Anglican tradition retained it.
