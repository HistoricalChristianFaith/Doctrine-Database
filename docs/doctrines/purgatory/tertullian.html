Tertullian on purgatory and prayer for the dead

Dates: c. 211 · Wikipedia
Summary: Tertullian of Carthage is a Latin witness to the practice of offerings for the dead. He reports it twice as established custom: "we make offerings for the dead" on the yearly anniversary (De Corona), and a widow "prays for his soul, and requests refreshment for him meanwhile … and offers… on the anniversaries of his falling asleep" (On Monogamy). In De Anima he reads the "uttermost farthing" of Matthew 5:26 of "some compensatory discipline" undergone by the soul in Hades before the resurrection.

Claims & evidence

Offerings for the dead on the anniversary of death

As often as the anniversary comes round, we make offerings for the dead as birthday honours.

— The Chaplet, or De Corona 3, historicalchristian.faith. Tertullian lists this among customs kept "from tradition" rather than express Scripture: the yearly oblation for the departed stands beside fasting practices and the sign of the cross as something done habitually. The offerings are made "as birthday honours" on the anniversary of death, and Tertullian reports the practice as customary by c. 211.

The widow prays for her departed husband and offers on the anniversary of his death

Indeed, she prays for his soul, and requests refreshment for him meanwhile, and fellowship (with him) in the first resurrection; and she offers (her sacrifice) on the anniversaries of his falling asleep.

— On Monogamy 10, historicalchristian.faith. Arguing against remarriage (in a work of his Montanist period), Tertullian describes the widow who keeps a continuing bond with her dead husband by intercession: she "prays for his soul," seeks "refreshment for him meanwhile" — that is, in the interval before the resurrection — and makes the annual offering for him. His argument assumes that his readers accept prayer and oblation for the dead. (On the thesis of On Monogamy itself, see “Was Tertullian’s ban on second marriage a matter of discipline or of doctrine?”, on the timeline “Was Tertullian a heretic?”.)

Tertullian on "the uttermost farthing" and "compensatory discipline" in Hades

inasmuch as we understand "the prison" pointed out in the Gospel to be Hades, and as we also interpret "the uttermost farthing" to mean the very smallest offence which has to be recompensed there before the resurrection, no one will hesitate to believe that the soul undergoes in Hades some compensatory discipline, without prejudice to the full process of the resurrection, when the recompense will be administered through the flesh besides.

— A Treatise on the Soul (De Anima) 58, historicalchristian.faith. Reading the warning of Matthew 5:25–26 ("till thou hast paid the uttermost farthing"), Tertullian takes the "prison" as Hades and the "farthing" as the least unpaid offence, and concludes that the soul there "undergoes … some compensatory discipline" in the interval before the resurrection. He locates this discipline in Hades, within his account of the interim in which souls await the resurrection there (see his intermediate-state page). Cyprian (Epistle 51, To Antonianus, §20) also uses the "uttermost farthing"; whether he speaks there of the afterlife or of penance in this life is disputed (see his page).

Reception and debate

  • Tertullian attests intercession and offering for the dead and the request for their "refreshment" in the interval, and frames the interim lot in terms of Hades and Abraham's bosom (see his intermediate-state page); in De Anima 58 he speaks of a "compensatory discipline" there.
  • He reports the anniversary offering among customs kept "from tradition" (De Corona 3); by c. 211 the practice is attested at Carthage, as it is in the Passion of Perpetua (c. 203).
  • The "refreshment for him meanwhile" of On Monogamy has parallels in the language of the early liturgies (cf. Cyril of Jerusalem, who a century later makes a petition for the departed at the altar). See also the purgatory timeline.
