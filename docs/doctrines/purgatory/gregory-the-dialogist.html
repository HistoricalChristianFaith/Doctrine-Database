Gregory the Dialogist on purgatory and prayer for the dead

Dates: c. 594 · Wikipedia
Summary: Pope Gregory I (c. 540–604), "the Great" or "the Dialogist," writes in Dialogues Book IV that "there must be a cleansing fire before judgment, because of some minor faults that may remain to be purged away." He restricts it: only "little and very light sins" are purged, and only for those who in life "deserved by virtuous works to find such favour." He backs the reading from 1 Corinthians 3 ("wood, hay, stubble") and Matthew 12:32 (some sins forgiven "in the world to come").

Claims & evidence

There must be a cleansing fire before judgment, for minor faults left to be purged

Each one will be presented to the Judge exactly as he was when he departed this life. Yet, there must be a cleansing fire before judgment, because of some minor faults that may remain to be purged away.

— Dialogues, Book IV, ch. 39 ("whether there be any fire of purgatory in the next world"), tertullian.org. Augustine had written that such a fire "is not impossible" and that the question may be "left doubtful"; Gregory writes that there "must be" such a fire, resting the statement on 1 Corinthians 3:12–15 and Matthew 12:32.

Some sins are forgiven "in the world to come": trifling faults, for those who merited it in life

Hence we may gather that there are some sins that are remitted in this world, and some in the world to come; for what is denied of one sin, must be supposed to be admitted of others. And this may be believed in the case of trifling faults; such as much idle discourse, immoderate laughter, or the sin of carefulness in our worldly affairs, which indeed can hardly be managed without sin even by one who knows how he ought to avoid sin; or sins through ignorance (if they be lesser sins) which burden us even after death, if they have not been remitted to us while yet in this life. But it should be known that none will there obtain any purgation even of the least sin, but he who by good actions has merited the same in this life.

— Gregory, Dialogues IV.39, as gathered in the Catena Aurea on Matthew 12:32 (Aquinas), historicalchristian.faith. Gregory presses the implied contrast in Matthew 12:32 (the blasphemy against the Spirit forgiven "neither in this world, neither in the world to come"): if one sin is unforgivable in the age to come, others must be forgivable there. He limits the post-mortem purgation to "trifling faults," and to those who merited it by good works in life. Augustine had likewise tied the benefit of the suffrages to how the dead had lived.

Reception and debate

  • Augustine and Gregory. Augustine (Enchiridion 69) calls it "a matter that may be inquired into, and either ascertained or left doubtful, whether some believers shall pass through a kind of purgatorial fire," delivered "in proportion as they have loved with more or less devotion the goods that perish." Gregory (Dialogues IV.39–41) writes that "there must be a cleansing fire before judgment" for "little and very light sins," for those who in life "deserved by virtuous works to find such favour."
  • Gregory rests the teaching on 1 Corinthians 3:15 ("wood, hay, stubble … saved, yet so as by fire") and Matthew 12:32. John Chrysostom reads 1 Corinthians 3 differently, of the penalty of judgment; at the Council of Ferrara–Florence, Mark of Ephesus said that deliverance through "a certain purgatorial suffering and temporal fire" is not found "in the words of the teachers" (see the purgatory timeline's introduction).
  • Aquinas (c. 1274) teaches a purgatorial fire that discharges the temporal debt of sin, assigned its own "abode."
  • For Gregory's wider picture of the soul between death and resurrection — the particular judgment, Abraham's bosom as "the secret rest of the fathers," the just held back until the Mediator's coming — see his detail page on the intermediate state.
