C. S. Lewis on purgatory and prayer for the dead

Dates: c. 1964 (Letters to Malcolm) · Wikipedia
Summary: C. S. Lewis (1898–1963), Oxford and Cambridge scholar and Anglican lay writer, affirms in Letters to Malcolm: Chiefly on Prayer (published posthumously in 1964) that he prays for the dead and believes in purgatory. He writes that "the Reformers had good reasons for throwing doubt on 'the Romish doctrine concerning Purgatory' as that Romish doctrine had then become," instancing Thomas More's Supplication of Souls and John Fisher's sermon on Psalm 6, and describes purgatory as a cleansing the saved soul itself asks for before entering the presence of God.

Claims & evidence

"Of course I pray for the dead"

Of course I pray for the dead. The action is so spontaneous, so all but inevitable, that only the most compulsive theological case against it would deter me.

— Letters to Malcolm: Chiefly on Prayer (1964), Letter 20, cited at churchpop.com. Lewis goes on: "At our age the majority of those we love best are dead. What sort of intercourse with God could I have if what I love best were unmentionable to Him?" (the fuller passage is reproduced at thulcandra.wordpress.com).

"I believe in Purgatory," and what Lewis says of the sixteenth-century doctrine

I believe in Purgatory. Mind you, the Reformers had good reasons for throwing doubt on "the Romish doctrine concerning Purgatory" as that Romish doctrine had then become.

— Letters to Malcolm, Letter 20, reproduced at thulcandra.wordpress.com (cf. churchpop.com, which quotes the letter's opening on prayer for the dead). Lewis continues that he does not mean "merely the commercial scandal"; in More's Supplication of Souls "Purgatory is simply temporary Hell," and Fisher, in his sermon on Psalm 6, makes the tortures so intense that the sufferer cannot "remember God as he ought to do." Of that sixteenth-century picture Lewis writes: "Its pains do not bring us nearer to God, but make us forget Him. It is a place not of purification but purely of retributive punishment." He contrasts it with Dante's Purgatorio, and writes that "the right view returns magnificently" in Newman's Dream of Gerontius.

The soul itself desires the cleansing

Our souls demand Purgatory, don't they?

— Letters to Malcolm, Letter 20, cited at churchpop.com. Lewis pictures the saved soul, brought at last into the divine presence, asking to be made clean before entering — "With submission, sir, and if there is no objection, I'd rather be cleaned first." Of the saved soul in Newman's Dream of Gerontius he writes that it "cannot bear for a moment longer 'With its darkness to affront that light'" (thulcandra.wordpress.com). He assumes that "the process of purification will normally involve suffering," but adds: "I don't think suffering is the purpose of the purgation."

Reception and debate
