John Calvin on purgatory and prayer for the dead

Dates: c. 1559 · Wikipedia
Summary: John Calvin (1509–1564), the Geneva reformer, rejects purgatory in the definitive 1559 Institutes (III.5.6–10). He defines it as "the satisfaction for sin paid after death by the souls of the dead" and charges that it "makes void the cross of Christ" and "overthrows our faith." He likewise calls the medieval limbus patrum (II.16.9) "nothing but a fable." When his opponents adduce Judas Maccabeus's sin offering for the dead (2 Maccabees 12:43–45), Calvin declines to answer it "lest I should seem to include that work among the canonical books" (III.5.8), and he holds that "the whole Law and Gospel do not contain one syllable which countenances the right of praying for the dead" (III.5.10).

Claims & evidence

Calvin's definition: "satisfaction for sin paid after death"

What is this purgatory but the satisfaction for sin paid after death by the souls of the dead?

— Institutes of the Christian Religion, III.5.6 ("Of the Modes of Supplementing Satisfaction — viz. Indulgences and Purgatory"), ccel.org. Calvin defines the doctrine he opposes as a post-mortem payment, a satisfaction rendered by the dead for their sins. His objection, set out in the same section, is that such a satisfaction seeks expiation somewhere other than in the blood of Christ.

Calvin's charge that purgatory "makes void the cross of Christ"

We are bound, therefore, to raise our voice to its highest pitch, and cry aloud that purgatory is a deadly device of Satan; that it makes void the cross of Christ; that it offers intolerable insult to the divine mercy; that it undermines and overthrows our faith.

— Institutes of the Christian Religion, III.5.6, ccel.org. Calvin's charge is soteriological: on his account purgatory "makes void the cross" because a satisfaction paid after death would imply that Christ's satisfaction was not complete.

Calvin on the origin and support of the doctrine

It was devised by prying audacious rashness, that credit was procured for it by fictitious revelations, the wiles of Satan, and that certain passages of Scripture were ignorantly wrested to its support.

— Institutes of the Christian Religion, III.5.6, ccel.org. Calvin turns from the theological objection to the doctrine's support: on his account it rests on no command of God, on passages of Scripture "ignorantly wrested," and on "fictitious revelations."

Calvin on the limbus patrum

Though this fable has the countenance of great authors, and is now also seriously defended by many as truth, it is nothing but a fable.

— Institutes of the Christian Religion, II.16.9 ("Absurd exposition concerning the Limbus Patrum. This fable refuted"), ccel.org. Calvin also rejects the limbus patrum, the place where, on the medieval account, the souls of the Old-Testament righteous were held until Christ's descent. He grants that the view "has the countenance of great authors" and holds that it has no scriptural footing.

Calvin declines to answer from 2 Maccabees

To the passage which they produce from the history of the Maccabees (1 Macc. 12:43), I will not deign to reply, lest I should seem to include that work among the canonical books.

— Institutes of the Christian Religion, III.5.8, ccel.org. The passage his opponents adduced is Judas Maccabeus's sin offering for his fallen soldiers, 2 Maccabees 12:43–45 (Calvin's text cites it "1 Macc. 12:43," but his own reference to the book's closing apology at "2 Macc. 15:38" places it in Second Maccabees, where the sin offering stands). Calvin declines to engage it on its merits, on the ground that arguing from it would concede that the book belongs among the canonical books. His handling of prayer for the dead is thus tied to his view of the canon; on that question see the Old-Testament-canon timeline.

Calvin's reasons for denying 2 Maccabees canonical authority

As if the author himself did not sufficiently show what degree of deference is to be paid him, when in the end he asks pardon for any thing less properly expressed. He who confesses that his writings stand in need of pardon, certainly proclaims that they are not oracles of the Holy Spirit.

— Institutes of the Christian Religion, III.5.8, ccel.org. Calvin draws his reason from the book's colophon. The writer of 2 Maccabees signs off asking the reader's indulgence — "if I have done well… it is that which I desired: but if slenderly and meanly, it is that which I could attain unto" (2 Macc 15:38) — and a writer who asks pardon for his prose, Calvin reasons, thereby disclaims that his words are the dictation of the Holy Spirit. He cites Jerome ("of no authority in establishing doctrine") and the ancient De Expositione Symboli to the same end. Of the deed itself he holds that "the piety of Judas is commended for no other reason than for having a firm hope of the final resurrection," and that "the act, indeed, was not free from superstition and misguided zeal."

Calvin: "not … one syllable" in the Law and Gospel for praying for the dead

Now, since the whole Law and Gospel do not contain one syllable which countenances the right of praying for the dead, it is a profanation of prayer to go one step farther than God enjoins.

— Institutes of the Christian Religion, III.5.10, ccel.org. With 2 Maccabees set aside as non-canonical, Calvin holds that the "Law and Gospel" nowhere command or model prayer for the dead. Of the biblical burial narratives he notes (III.5.10) that they record "mourning and sepulchral rites, but not one word… of prayers."

Reception and debate

  • Calvin's objection rests on the completeness of Christ's satisfaction: a satisfactory punishment paid by the dead would, on his account, "make void the cross." He reads the fire of 1 Corinthians 3 in the same chapter as "the examination of the Holy Spirit," writing that "Paul has no idea of any other fire than the trial of the Holy Spirit" (III.5.9, ccel.org).
  • Calvin's refusal to engage 2 Maccabees 12 "lest I should seem to include that work among the canonical books" (III.5.8) ties his handling of prayer for the dead to the canon question, traced on the Old-Testament-canon timeline. The practice of prayer for the dead is traced on the purgatory timeline (see also the 2 Maccabees witness page).
