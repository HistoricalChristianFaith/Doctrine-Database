Thomas Aquinas on purgatory and prayer for the dead

Dates: c. 1274 · Wikipedia
Summary: Thomas Aquinas (c. 1225–1274), the Dominican master of scholastic theology, reads the "day of the Lord" of 1 Corinthians 3, taken of the day of death, as revealed "in the fire of purgatory, by which the elect will be cleansed." In the Summa Theologiae (Supplement, Appendix I), purgatory is one of the abodes of separated souls: those who die in grace with venial sins are detained there and "punished ere they receive their reward." In his account of Christ's descent, Christ delivered the holy Fathers from the limbus patrum; since then, on his account, the fully cleansed go to God and those still owing satisfaction pass through purgatory.

Claims & evidence

Aquinas on 1 Corinthians 3: "the fire of purgatory, by which the elect will be cleansed"

But the day of the Lord which occurs at death will be revealed in the fire of purgatory, by which the elect will be cleansed, if any require cleansing: Job (23:10) can be interpreted as referring to this fire: "When he has tried me, I shall come forth as god."

— Commentary on 1 Corinthians, ch. 3 (§164), historicalchristian.faith. Reading 1 Corinthians 3:13 ("the day … shall be revealed with fire"), Aquinas distinguishes three "days of the Lord" — the general judgment, the particular judgment at death, and the trials of this life — and assigns to the second the purgatorial fire that cleanses the elect, "if any require cleansing." Compare Augustine and Gregory the Great on a cleansing fire.

Aquinas on purgatory as an abode of souls who die in grace with venial sins

those who die in grace with venial sins an abode is assigned where they are punished ere they receive their reward, which abode is purgatory

— Summa Theologiae, Supplement, Appendix I ("On Purgatory"), newadvent.org. Purgatory is here one of the destinations of separated souls, defined as the place "where souls are detained from receiving their reward at once on account of the sins they have committed." It is a temporary station on the way to heaven: the soul is saved, and the remaining debt of temporal punishment is discharged before it enters the vision of God.

Aquinas on the holy Fathers in the limbus patrum until Christ's descent

the holy Fathers were detained in hell for the reason, that, owing to our first parent's sin, the approach to the life of glory was not opened.

— Summa Theologiae III, q. 52, a. 5, newadvent.org. The Old-Testament righteous, free of personal guilt but barred from the vision of God by the inherited debt of original sin, waited in the limbus patrum — the "Abraham's bosom" of Luke 16 — until Christ "when … He descended into hell … delivered the holy Fathers from thence" (same article). Aquinas adds that Christ's soul "descended only into that part of hell wherein the just were detained" (III, q. 52, a. 2). On this account the limbus patrum belongs to the time before Christ's descent, and since then no one waits there.

Reception and debate

  • Aquinas speaks of a purgatorial fire at the particular judgment that cleanses the elect and discharges the temporal debt of sin already forgiven as to guilt. Earlier witnesses on this timeline who speak of a cleansing fire include Augustine and Gregory the Great; John Chrysostom reads the fire of 1 Corinthians 3 of the last judgment, and Origen and Gregory of Nyssa of a medicinal fire.
  • For Aquinas the limbus patrum belongs to the past — it explains where the Old-Testament saints waited and what Christ's descent accomplished — whereas purgatory is the continuing station for the saved who still owe satisfaction.
  • Aquinas's wider scheme distinguishes five abodes for souls after death — heaven, hell, purgatory, the limbo of the Fathers (now emptied), and the limbo of children. For that full map and his account of the particular judgment and immediate beatific vision, see his detail page on the intermediate state.
