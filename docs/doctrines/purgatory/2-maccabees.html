2 Maccabees on purgatory and prayer for the dead

Dates: c. 124 BC · Wikipedia
Summary: 2 Maccabees 12:43–45 is the earliest text on this timeline to describe the living acting on behalf of the dead. Finding idolatrous tokens on his fallen soldiers, Judas Maccabeus collects silver and sends a sin offering to Jerusalem on their behalf, reasoning that prayer for the dead would be "superfluous and vain" but for the resurrection, and that the offering makes "a reconciliation for the dead, that they might be delivered from sin."

Claims & evidence

A sin offering is sent to Jerusalem for the slain

And when he had made a gathering throughout the company to the sum of two thousand drachms of silver, he sent it to Jerusalem to offer a sin offering, doing therein very well and honestly, in that he was mindful of the resurrection:

— 2 Maccabees 12:43 (KJV/Apocrypha), source. After idolatrous amulets are found under the coats of the dead (vv. 39–40), Judas does not write them off: he gathers silver and sends it to the temple as an offering on their behalf.

Prayer for the dead would be "superfluous and vain" but for the resurrection

For if he had not hoped that they that were slain should have risen again, it had been superfluous and vain to pray for the dead.

— 2 Maccabees 12:44 (KJV/Apocrypha), source. The author grounds the practice in the resurrection hope: without the expectation that the slain would rise, it would have been "superfluous and vain to pray for the dead."

A reconciliation is made for the dead, that they might be delivered from sin

And also in that he perceived that there was great favour laid up for those that died godly, it was an holy and good thought. Whereupon he made a reconciliation for the dead, that they might be delivered from sin.

— 2 Maccabees 12:45 (KJV/Apocrypha), source. The dead "that died godly" have "great favour laid up" for them, and the offering is made "that they might be delivered from sin."

Reception and debate

  • Edward Hanna, in the Catholic Encyclopedia article “Purgatory” (1911), cites 2 Maccabees 12:43–46 as the Old Testament witness to the doctrine: “At the time of the Maccabees the leaders of the people of God had no hesitation in asserting the efficacy of prayers offered for the dead, in order that those who had departed this life might find pardon for their sins and the hope of eternal resurrection” (newadvent.org).
  • 2 Maccabees is deuterocanonical: it is received in the Catholic and Orthodox canons and stands outside the Jewish and Protestant canons. John Calvin (1559) declined to answer the proof-text "lest I should seem to include that work among the canonical books."
