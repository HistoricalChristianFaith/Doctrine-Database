Ambrosiaster on purgatory and prayer for the dead

Dates: c. 380 · Wikipedia
Summary: The anonymous fourth-century Latin commentator known as Ambrosiaster reads 1 Corinthians 3:15 ("he himself shall be saved; yet so as by fire") as the believer who survives judgment but only by being purified through fire.

Claims & evidence

On 1 Corinthians 3:15: "He will be saved only by being purified through fire"

To suffer loss is to endure reproof. For what person, when subjected to punishment, does not lose something thereby? Yet the person himself may be saved. His living soul will not perish in the same way that his erroneous ideas will. Even so, however, he may suffer punishments of fire. He will be saved only by being purified through fire.

— Commentary on Paul's Epistles (on 1 Corinthians 3:15), source. Commenting on Paul's "yet so as by fire," Ambrosiaster takes the "loss" and the "fire" as real punishment that nonetheless stops short of destroying the believer: the person's "living soul will not perish" as his bad teaching does, but "he may suffer punishments of fire" and "will be saved only by being purified through fire." (On the preceding verse, 1 Cor 3:13, he glosses: "In the fire, bad teaching will become clear to everyone, though for the moment it is deceiving some.")

Reception and debate

  • "Ambrosiaster" is the conventional name for the anonymous author of this Latin Pauline commentary, long ascribed to Ambrose. The work belongs to the late fourth century (c. 380s).
  • Other readings of 1 Corinthians 3 appear on this timeline: John Chrysostom reads the fire as the penalty of judgment, and Caesarius of Arles restricts what the fire purges to slight sins. See the purgatory timeline.
  • The comment does not say whether the purifying fire comes after death or at the final judgment.
