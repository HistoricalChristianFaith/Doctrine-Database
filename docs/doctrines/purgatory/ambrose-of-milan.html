Ambrose of Milan on purgatory and prayer for the dead

Dates: c. 378 (On the Decease of His Brother Satyrus; Ambrose, c. 339–397) · Wikipedia
Summary: Ambrose, bishop of Milan, prays and offers the Eucharistic sacrifice for the dead by name — for his brother Satyrus (c. 378) and for the dead emperors Valentinian II and Theodosius (392, 395). He also teaches that those who would return to paradise pass through a purifying fire at its threshold.

Claims & evidence

His brother's funeral is itself an offering for the dead

When his brother Satyrus died (c. 378), Ambrose preached the funeral oration On the Decease of His Brother Satyrus. He opens by calling the very rite a sacrifice offered for the departed:

We have brought hither, dearest brethren, my sacrifice, a sacrifice undefiled, a sacrifice well pleasing to God, my lord and brother Satyrus.

and he closes the oration by commending his brother's soul to God and offering his own sacrifice for him:

And now to Thee, Almighty God, I commend this guileless soul, to Thee I offer my sacrifice; accept favourably and mercifully the gift of a brother, the offering of a priest.

— On the Decease of His Brother Satyrus (De excessu fratris Satyri) I.1, I.80, historicalchristian.faith (NPNF, Series II, Vol. X).1

Over the dead emperor Valentinian II: "let us beseech rest for him"

In On the Death of Valentinian (392), his eulogy for the young emperor Valentinian II, Ambrose calls the congregation to offer the Eucharist and pray rest for the dead emperor:

Date manibus sancta mysteria, pio requiem eius poscamus affectu. Date sacramenta coelestia, animam nepotis nostri oblationibus prosequamur.

"Give the holy mysteries to the dead, with devout affection let us beseech rest for him; give the heavenly sacraments, let us attend our [young emperor's] soul with our offerings." — De obitu Valentiniani consolatio 56, Wikisource (Latin). Pietro Piacenza, in the Catholic Encyclopedia (1911), quotes this line and writes: "So originated the Introit for the Mass for the Dead."2

Commemorating the dead on the set days

Ambrose preached the companion sermon On the Belief in the Resurrection (Satyrus, Book II) at the graveside on the seventh day after the burial, marking the days set apart for the dead — the standing custom of remembering the departed at the appointed commemorations:

now, inasmuch as on the seventh day, the symbol of the future rest, we return to the grave, it is profitable to turn our thoughts somewhat from my brother to a general exhortation addressed to all.

— On the Decease of His Brother Satyrus II.2, historicalchristian.faith (NPNF, Series II, Vol. X). Elsewhere Ambrose names the third and thirtieth, or the seventh and fortieth, as the days kept for the dead.3

A purifying fire at the threshold of paradise

Ambrose also teaches that those who would enter glory must pass through a purifying fire. Expounding the flaming sword set at the gate of Eden (Gen 3:24) and the psalm "we went through fire and through water" (Ps 66:12), he teaches that all who would return to paradise must be tried by fire: as water washes away sins, so the fire burns them up, yet the one who carries the fire of charity in this life need not dread the fire of the sword in the next. He sets this fire at the entrance to paradise.4

Reception and debate

  • On how the ancient record of prayer and offering for the dead has been read, see Was prayer for the dead uncontested in the ancient Church?
  • Edward Hanna, in the Catholic Encyclopedia article “Purgatory” (1911), writes that “in the fourth century in the West, Ambrose insists in his commentary on St. Paul (1 Corinthians 3) on the existence of purgatory,” and quotes his prayer for the emperor in De obitu Theodosii: “Give, O Lord, rest to Thy servant Theodosius, that rest Thou hast prepared for Thy saints.”5 On the Pauline commentary long ascribed to Ambrose, see Ambrosiaster.
  • On later Latin teaching about a purgatorial fire, see the entries for Gregory the Great and Aquinas.
  • Valentinian II died unbaptized, having invited Ambrose to Vienne to baptize him (Wikipedia); Ambrose prays for his rest, and for that of Gratian, also unbaptized ⚠ unverified.
