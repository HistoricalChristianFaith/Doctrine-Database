Lactantius on purgatory and prayer for the dead

Dates: c. 310 · Wikipedia
Summary: Lactantius, the African rhetorician writing his Divine Institutes during the Great Persecution (c. 303–311), describes a discriminating fire of judgment that one and the same divine flame inflicts on all: it scorches and burns those "whose sins shall exceed either in weight or in number," but is not so much as felt by "they whom full justice and maturity of virtue has imbued," who carry "something of God in themselves which repels … the violence of the flame." He sets this trial by fire at the Last Judgment, and holds that until then all souls are "detained in one and a common place of confinement."

Claims & evidence

Lactantius on one divine fire that tries the righteous at the judgment

The same divine fire, therefore, with one and the same force and power, will both burn the wicked and will form them again, and will replace as much as it shall consume of their bodies, and will supply itself with eternal nourishment: which the poets transferred to the vulture of Tityus. Thus, without any wasting of bodies, which regain their substance, it will only burn and affect them with a sense of pain. But when He shall have judged the righteous, He will also try them with fire. Then they whose sins shall exceed either in weight or in number, shall be scorched by the fire and burnt: but they whom full justice and maturity of virtue has imbued will not perceive that fire; for they have something of God in themselves which repels and rejects the violence of the flame. So great is the force of innocence, that the flame shrinks from it without doing harm; which has received from God this power, that it burns the wicked, and is under the command of the righteous.

— The Divine Institutes VII.21 (Of the Torments and Punishments of Souls), source. Lactantius is reasoning about how immortal souls can suffer at the hand of God. He describes a single "divine fire," different in nature from earthly fire, that at the judgment both burns the wicked and is applied to the righteous: "when He shall have judged the righteous, He will also try them with fire." The fire's effect varies with what it finds: those "whose sins shall exceed either in weight or in number, shall be scorched by the fire and burnt," while those perfected in virtue "will not perceive that fire" at all, because innocence carries "something of God" from which the flame shrinks.

Lactantius: souls "detained" until the judgment

Nor, however, let any one imagine that souls are immediately judged after death. For all are detained in one and a common place of confinement, until the arrival of the time in which the great Judge shall make an investigation of their deserts.

— The Divine Institutes VII.21, source. Lactantius places the trial by fire at the general judgment and holds all souls meanwhile in "one and a common place of confinement" until "the great Judge shall make an investigation of their deserts." The righteous are tried by the fire as they are judged.

Reception and debate

  • Lactantius describes one fire, applied at the judgment, whose effect varies with the sins it meets and which leaves the perfected untouched. Compare Cyprian a generation earlier ("cleansed and long purged by fire") and the reading of 1 Corinthians 3:15 in Ambrosiaster.
  • Lactantius sets the trial by fire at the Last Judgment and detains souls meanwhile in a common holding-place.
  • The same Divine Institutes is a witness on a different doctrine: Lactantius's account of the fallen angels and their demon offspring (II.15) appears on the Nephilim timeline.
