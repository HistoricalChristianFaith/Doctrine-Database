The Epitaph of Abercius on purgatory and prayer for the dead

Dates: c. 190 · Wikipedia
Summary: Abercius, bishop of Hieropolis in Phrygia (later second century), composed his own funerary epitaph while still living and had it carved on his tomb. After describing his travels and, in veiled mystery-cult language, the Eucharist (the "fish" caught by a "holy virgin," eaten "with wine … mingled with bread"), he closes with a request: that every fellow-believer who understands the inscription pray for Abercius. It is the earliest epigraphic request for prayer for the dead on this timeline. The stone is now in the Vatican Museum; the epitaph of one Alexander, found nearby and dated 216, reproduces parts of it almost word for word, and the Wikipedia account, citing Blank (2023), places Abercius's epitaph before 216 on linguistic and palaeographic grounds.1

Claims & evidence

Abercius asks, on his own tombstone, that every believer pray for him

These things I, Abercius, having been a witness [thereof] told to be written here. Verily I was passing through my seventy-second year. He that discerneth these things, [namely] every fellow-believer, let him pray for Abercius.

— Epitaph of Abercius, closing lines (the standard restored translation, after the Catholic Encyclopedia / Quasten), earlychristianwritings.com; cf. Catholic Encyclopedia (1913).2 Abercius asks the believers who read his stone to pray for him. He raised the monument "while living" (the inscription's opening), so the request is addressed to readers after his death.

The Eucharistic context: the "fish" eaten with bread and wine

Faith everywhere led me forward, and everywhere provided as my food a fish of exceeding great size, and perfect, which a holy virgin drew with her hands from a fountain and this it ever gives to its friends to eat, it having wine of great virtue, and giving it mingled with bread.

— Epitaph of Abercius, earlychristianwritings.com. The "fish" (ΙΧΘΥΣ, an early cipher for Christ) caught by a "holy virgin" and eaten with "wine … mingled with bread" is read by Henri Leclercq in the Catholic Encyclopedia (1913) as "the receiving of Jesus Christ, the Son of God and of Mary, in the Eucharist, … under the species of Bread and Wine." The request for prayer and the Eucharistic passage stand on the same stone. Later on this timeline, Cyril of Jerusalem and Augustine speak of the Eucharistic offering together with prayer for the departed.3

Reception and debate

  • On how the ancient record of prayer for the dead has been read, see Was prayer for the dead uncontested in the ancient Church?
  • Dating. The inscription survives in fragments of the stone, and its wording is shared with the epitaph of Alexander, dated 216.4
  • Coded idiom. Abercius addresses "he that discerneth these things … every fellow-believer"; the Catholic Encyclopedia (1913) writes that the epitaph "shows plainly that the language used is one not understood by all."5
  • Placement on the timeline. It stands between the Acts of Paul and Thecla (c. 160) and Tertullian (c. 211). See also the purgatory timeline.
