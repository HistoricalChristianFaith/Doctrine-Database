Gregory of Nyssa on purgatory and prayer for the dead

Dates: c. 382 · Wikipedia
Summary: Gregory of Nyssa, the Cappadocian bishop, teaches a post-mortem purifying fire. In On the Soul and the Resurrection (his dialogue with his dying sister Macrina, c. 380) and the Great Catechism (c. 385) he teaches that the soul still "welded" to evil at death must pass through a "purgatorial fire" that refines it as a furnace refines gold from dross — what is not cleansed "here" by repentance and baptism is purged "hereafter" by fire. Gregory sets this within his teaching on universal restoration (apokatastasis): the fire is medicinal and finite, aimed at healing, and in the Great Catechism Christ's work heals "even the introducer of evil himself."

Claims & evidence

The soul welded to evil is refined like gold in a "purgatorial fire"

Just as those who refine gold from the dross which it contains not only get this base alloy to melt in the fire, but are obliged to melt the pure gold along with the alloy, and then while this last is being consumed the gold remains, so, while evil is being consumed in the purgatorial fire, the soul that is welded to this evil must inevitably be in the fire too, until the spurious material alloy is consumed and annihilated by this fire.

— On the Soul and the Resurrection (De Anima et Resurrectione), source. The context is the soul's purification after death. Earlier in the same dialogue Gregory had set out the alternative — "whether by forethought here, or by purgation hereafter, our soul becomes free" of its attachment to material passions — and here he supplies the controlling image: the refiner's fire that consumes the dross even as it passes through the gold. The soul that has grown into evil cannot be freed of it painlessly; God "drags that which belongs to Him from the ruins of the irrational and material," and the pain of that separation is the fire.

What is not washed by "the mystic water" must be purified by fire

… just as the furnace is the proper thing for gold alloyed with dross,—in order that, the vice which has been mixed up in them being melted away after long succeeding ages, their nature may be restored pure again to God. Since, then, there is a cleansing virtue in fire and water, they who by the mystic water have washed away the defilement of their sin have no further need of the other form of purification, while they who have not been admitted to that form of purgation must needs be purified by fire.

— The Great Catechism (Oratio Catechetica) 35, source. Here Gregory sets the purgative fire beside baptism: there are "two cleansings," water and fire, and "those whose weaknesses have become inveterate, and to whom no purgation of their defilement has been applied, no mystic water … must needs be purified by fire." The purification takes time — "after long succeeding ages" — and ends in the soul being "restored pure again to God." The same gold-from-dross image recurs (and again at ch. 26, of the redemption itself, "as when some worthless material has been mixed with gold, and the gold-refiners burn up the foreign and refuse part in the consuming fire"), so that the image recurs in his writing on purgation.

Reception and debate

  • Gregory is the Greek writer on this timeline who speaks most fully of a post-mortem purifying fire, beside the Latin witness Ambrosiaster; compare Cyprian and Lactantius. Origen before him also speaks of a medicinal fire.
  • The setting of the fire. Gregory describes the purgation as medicinal and universal, part of the restoration of all things: the fire heals and finally restores the soul "pure again to God" ("after long succeeding ages"), and in the Great Catechism (ch. 26) the divine work heals "even the introducer of evil himself." On Gregory's teaching on restoration, see his page on universal restoration.
  • Two readings of Gregory's fire. Edward Hanna, in the Catholic Encyclopedia article “Purgatory” (1911), counts Gregory among his patristic witnesses: Gregory “states that man's weaknesses are purged in this life by prayer and wisdom, or are expiated in the next by a cleansing fire” (newadvent.org). Mark of Ephesus, answering at Ferrara–Florence “the words which are quoted of the blessed Gregory of Nyssa,” reads them within Gregory's teaching on restoration: “this Teacher is seen to be clearly in agreement with the dogmas of the Origenists and to introduce an end to torments,” “so that there is nothing surprising in the fact that he, being human, erred in precision (of truth)” (First Homily on Purgatorial Fire, ch. 11, translated by Seraphim Rose from Archimandrite Ambrose (Pogodin), St Mark of Ephesus & the Union of Florence (Jordanville, 1963), pp. 68–69, in Rose, The Place of Blessed Augustine in the Orthodox Church, pp. 71–72; ⚠ unverified against the book, quoted from logismoitouaaron.blogspot.com).
  • Timing. The translator's note to On the Soul and the Resurrection (NPNF) states that "the Judgment comes after the Resurrection … and after the purifying and chastising detailed above," which "is represented by Gregory as a necessary process of nature: but not till the Judgment will the moral value of each life be revealed." Lactantius sets his fire at the Last Judgment; Gregory the Great speaks of "a cleansing fire before judgment."
