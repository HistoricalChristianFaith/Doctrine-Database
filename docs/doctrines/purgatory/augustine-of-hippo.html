Augustine of Hippo on purgatory and prayer for the dead

Dates: c. 420 · Wikipedia
Summary: Augustine (354–430), bishop of Hippo, reading 1 Corinthians 3, allows that some believers may pass through a "purgatorial fire" after this life — describing it as a thing that "may be inquired into, and either ascertained or left doubtful." He states without such qualification that the dead are aided by the prayers, alms, and the Eucharistic sacrifice of their living friends — but only those who in life "earned such merit, that services of this kind can help them."

Claims & evidence

A purgatorial fire after this life is "not impossible" — but may be "left doubtful"

And it is not impossible that something of the same kind may take place even after this life. It is a matter that may be inquired into, and either ascertained or left doubtful, whether some believers shall pass through a kind of purgatorial fire, and in proportion as they have loved with more or less devotion the goods that perish, be less or more quickly delivered from it.

— The Enchiridion, ch. 69 ("It is Not Impossible that Some Believers May Pass Through a Purgatorial Fire in the Future Life"), historicalchristian.faith. Augustine reads 1 Corinthians 3:11–15 (the man "saved, yet so as by fire") and reasons from the "fire" of adversity already at work in this life, then extends it past death — but only for those who will inherit the kingdom, and not as the "saved by fire" license for the unrepentant that he rejects in ch. 67–68. He leaves the matter open: it "may be inquired into, and either ascertained or left doubtful."

The dead are aided by the prayers, alms, and Eucharistic sacrifice of the living

Nor can it be denied that the souls of the dead are benefited by the piety of their living friends, who offer the sacrifice of the Mediator, or give alms in the church on their behalf. But these services are of advantage only to those who during their lives have earned such merit, that services of this kind can help them.

— The Enchiridion, ch. 110 ("The Benefit to the Souls of the Dead from the Sacraments and Alms of Their Living Friends"), historicalchristian.faith. The offerings, he adds, are "thank-offerings for the very good," "propitiatory offerings for the not very bad," and merely "a species of consolation to the living" for the very bad — their benefit being "either in obtaining a full remission of sins, or at least in making the condemnation more tolerable." The benefit depends on how the person lived before death.

Some are saved through fire — the man whose work is burned, who loved earthly things "carnally"

For although it might be said that even those persons would be saved by fire of whom the apostle says, "But he himself shall be saved, yet so as by fire," and that therefore that fire is to be esteemed of more efficacy than anything which a man can suffer in this life; yet we have found many evil things, which though men endure they do not on that account come to the kingdom of God.

— The Enchiridion, ch. 68, historicalchristian.faith. In the chapters surrounding his "purgatorial fire" conjecture Augustine works carefully through 1 Corinthians 3, distinguishing the believer who has built "wood, hay, stubble" — saved "yet so as by fire" because he loved lawful earthly goods too much — from those whose loss is simple condemnation.

From the pulpit: the dead are helped by the Sacrifice, prayers, and alms — for those whose lives made it beneficial

However, there should be no doubt that the dead are helped by the prayers of the holy Church, and the saving sacrifice, and alms that are given for their spirits; so that the Lord may deal more mercifully with them than their sins have deserved. … There should be no doubt whatsoever that these act to the benefit of the dead; but only for those who lived in such a way before their deaths that these acts could be beneficial to them after death.

— Sermon 172.2 ("On the Words of the Apostle … On Those Who Are Asleep … By Whom the Dead Are Helped"), historicalchristian.faith. Preached to a congregation, the same teaching he sets down in the Enchiridion: the prayers and Eucharistic sacrifice and alms of the living aid the departed — yet the benefit reaches "only" those who "lived in such a way before their deaths" that such help could profit them. Augustine adds that "no new merits are acquired for the dead when their people do something good for them," for "no one finishing this life can have anything beyond what he deserved within it."

The martyr exception: the Church prays for its dead — but not for the martyrs

Therefore, the ecclesiastical discipline holds, which the faithful know, that when the martyrs are recounted at the altar of God, prayers are not made for them; but for the other deceased commemorated, prayers are made. It is an insult to pray for the martyr, whose prayers we ought to commend ourselves to.

— Sermon 159.1 (on Romans 8:30–31 and James 1:2–4), historicalchristian.faith. In the sermon, ordinary believers die "not without sin" — "if we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves" — and for them the Church prays. The martyr, who "has fought against sin to the shedding of blood," has reached "some perfection in this life," so to pray for him would be an "insult"; it is rather to him that the living commend themselves. Augustine thus distinguishes among the departed: the Church prays for the ordinary dead and commends itself to the martyrs.

Reception and debate

  • The fire as an open question. Augustine marks the post-mortem fire as something that "may be left doubtful" (Enchiridion 69), reasoning from 1 Corinthians 3.
  • The practice. He states the benefit to the dead of the living's prayer, alms, and the Eucharistic sacrifice without that qualification (Enchiridion 110; cf. On Care to Be Had for the Dead, where "supplications for the spirits of the dead" reach those who "while living in the body merited that such things should profit them"). On how the ancient record of the practice has been read, see Was prayer for the dead uncontested in the ancient Church?
  • Later Latin use. Gregory the Great (c. 594) writes that "there must be a cleansing fire before judgment," and Aquinas (c. 1274) teaches a purgatorial fire with its own "abode" that discharges the temporal debt of sin.
  • For Augustine's wider account of the soul between death and resurrection — the "hidden retreats," Abraham's bosom, Tartarus — see his detail page on the intermediate state.
