Was prayer for the dead uncontested in the ancient Church?

Disputed reading · on Purgatory and prayer for the dead · witnesses: Epiphanius of Salamis; Aerius of Sebaste

Related readings: Did the crusade indulgence give rise to the indulgence practice Luther protested in 1517?

The passage

Why do you mention the names of the dead after their deaths (i.e., in the liturgy)? … If the prayer of the people here has benefited the people there, no one should practice piety or perform good works! He should get some friends any way he wants, either by bribery or by asking friends on his death bed, and they should pray that he may not suffer in the next life, or be held to account for his heinous sins.

— Aerius, as quoted by Epiphanius, Panarion 75 (Against the Aerians) §3.5 (Frank Williams trans.).1

… the church has received it, and there was a world-wide agreement before Aerius and his Aerians.

— Epiphanius, Panarion 75 §6.4 (Williams trans.).2

The question

Aerius, a presbyter of Sebaste in Pontus (c. 355), rejected the naming of the dead in the liturgy and prayer and offering for them; his words survive only in Epiphanius's Panarion (c. 375), which calls him “a thoroughgoing Arian” and lists his teaching as the seventy-fifth heresy, and Augustine's De Haeresibus 53 (c. 428) lists the Aerians on the same ground. The dispute is what this record shows about the ancient practice: whether prayer for the dead was a received, world-wide custom from which Aerius departed, whether the Fathers held it as a custom whose meaning they did not agree on, or whether Aerius's protest was a principled dissent from practices then taking hold. The question has carried weight since the sixteenth century: Philip Schaff reports that Robert Bellarmine “styles Protestantism the Aerian heresy.”3 A comparable dispute over prayer to the saints, that of Vigilantius of Calagurris (c. 406), answered by Jerome, is treated under prayer to the saints.

Prayer for the dead was the received, world-wide custom, and Aerius departed from it

On this reading the Church East and West prayed and offered for its dead from the earliest times, and Aerius was the one named dissenter, answered as breaking with what the Church had received.

Held by: Epiphanius of Salamis (Panarion 75, c. 375); Augustine (De Haeresibus 53, c. 428); Patrick Toner (Catholic, The Catholic Encyclopedia, “Prayers for the Dead,” 1908); Michael Pomazansky (Russian Orthodox, Orthodox Dogmatic Theology, 1963).4

Epiphanius appeals to “a world-wide agreement before Aerius,” to a practice the Church has “received … from her fathers,” and lists it without controversy among the catholic Church's ordinances in his De Fide: “They make memorials for the dead by name, offering prayers and the liturgy.” His answer to Aerius is that naming the dead confesses that the departed “live with the Lord,” and that the prayer is “useful … even if it does not force back the whole of guilty charges.” Augustine enrols the Aerians among the heresies because Aerius “said that the sacrifice ought not to be offered for the dead.”5

The breadth of the practice carries this reading, from Tertullian's anniversary oblations (c. 211) to Cyril of Jerusalem (c. 350) and John Chrysostom: Fathers who differ on what the prayer does for the dead yet share the practice. Toner holds that “so overwhelming is the witness of the early Christian monuments in favour of prayer for the dead that no historian any longer denies that the practice and the belief which the practice implies were universal in the primitive Church,” and that the liturgies, “all without exception — Nestorian and Monophysite as well as Catholic,” contain the commemoration of the faithful departed; Aerius, he writes, “was branded as a heretic for denying the legitimacy and efficacy of prayers for the dead.”6 Pomazansky makes the same appeal for the Orthodox Church: the liturgies of James, Basil, Chrysostom and Gregory the Dialogist, the Roman, Spanish and Gallican rites, and those of the Jacobites, Copts, Armenians, Ethiopians and Syrians all pray for the dead: “For all their numbers, there is not a single one of these Liturgies where there is no prayer for the dead.” On this reading a single named objector, enrolled among the heresies, is the mark of a practice the whole Church held as its own.7

The commemoration of the dead was general, but the Fathers held it as custom, with hesitation and without agreement on its effect

On this reading the ancient Church did commemorate and pray for its dead, but the Fathers neither agreed on what the prayer did nor held the later doctrine it was afterwards made to serve.

Held by: the Apology of the Augsburg Confession (Lutheran, 1531); John Calvin (Reformed, Institutes III.5.10, 1559); James Ussher (Church of Ireland, An Answer to a Challenge Made by a Jesuit in Ireland, 1625); Gavin Ortlund (Protestant, 2021).8

The Apology grants that “the ancients speak of prayer for the dead, which we do not prohibit,” and disapproves only “the application ex opere operato of the Lord's Supper on behalf of the dead.” It notes that “there is a great diversity among the Fathers,” and of Aerius it says: “Neither do we favor Aerius.” The quarrel, on this account, is not over the commemoration itself.9

Calvin grants that “it has been the practice for thirteen hundred years to offer prayers for the dead” and asks “by what word of God, by what revelation, by what example it was done?” He traces the custom to affection and to rivalry with pagan rites for the dead, and holds that “when they recommended prayer for the dead it was with hesitation.” The ancient Church “made a commemoration of the dead, that they might not seem to have cast off all concern for them; but they, at the same time, acknowledged that they were doubtful as to their state.”10

Ussher reads Epiphanius closely. “Neither doth Epiphanius name this to be an heresy,” since “heresy cannot be but in matters of faith”; Epiphanius charges Aerius not with forsaking the faith on the state of the departed “but with rejecting the order observed by the Church in her commemorations of the dead.” Citing Stephanus Gobarus, Ussher holds that “the doctors were not agreed upon the point.” The commemorations concerned those whose lives “gave pregnant hope unto the living that their souls were at rest with God,” asking “their public justification and solemn acquittal at the last day,” and served “the consolation and instruction which the living might receive thereby.”11

Gavin Ortlund grants that “the practice of praying for the dead is extremely common and extremely early,” and holds that “praying for the dead and believing in purgatory are two distinct things that do not stand or fall together”: in the early Church “everybody believes in praying for the dead, but not because they’re in purgatory.” On this reading the ancient practice was general and ancient, and its meaning was the commemoration of the faithful departed in hope of the resurrection.12

Aerius's protest was a principled dissent from practices then taking hold

On this reading Aerius was a reformer who, from Scripture, opposed customs that the fourth-century Church was coming to hold, and the account of him survives in the words of a hostile opponent.

Held by: John McClintock and James Strong (Methodist, Cyclopædia of Biblical, Theological and Ecclesiastical Literature, 1869); Philipp Meyer (Protestant, New Schaff-Herzog Encyclopedia, 1908–14); the article “Aerius” signed E.V. in Wace and Piercy's Dictionary of Christian Biography (1911).13

Aerius's own argument is a moral one: if the prayers of the living avail the dead, “no one should practice piety or perform good works,” since a man could arrange for friends to pray for him instead of repenting. The Dictionary article restates it: “If they availed for the departed, no one need trouble himself to live holily,” and holds that “Aerius went so fearlessly to the root of much that the church was beginning to cling to, that we cannot feel much surprise at the vehemence of Epiphanius.” Meyer calls Aerius “one of the progressive men of the time who protested against the legalistic and hierarchic tendencies of the Church,” who, “supporting his contention by the Scriptures,” “denied the value of prayers for the dead”; he notes that the only source, Epiphanius, “treats him in a very partizan spirit,” and that the Aerians “had many followers.” McClintock and Strong judge that “the real cause, perhaps, of the accusation against him was his attempt to reform the Church,” including by “rejecting prayers for the dead, with certain fasts and festivals then superstitiously observed,” and that “the statements of Epiphanius are evidently colored by his personal prejudice against Aerius”; the sect “was still in existence at the time of Augustine.” The Dictionary adds that Augustine “merely epitomises Epiphanius,” so the whole ancient record of the dissent rests on one author. On this reading Aerius and his followers in Pontus stand for a fourth-century dissent from a spreading practice, argued from Scripture and from the call to holy living.14
