Did the crusade indulgence give rise to the indulgence practice Luther protested in 1517?

Disputed reading · on Purgatory and prayer for the dead · witness: Martin Luther

Related readings: Was prayer for the dead uncontested in the ancient Church?

The passage

Whoever, out of pure devotion and not for the purpose of gaining honor or money, shall go to Jerusalem to liberate the Church of God, let that journey be counted in lieu of all penance.

— Council of Clermont (1095), canon 2, as translated in William Kent, “Indulgences,” The Catholic Encyclopedia (1910).1

To those, moreover, who do not go thither in their own persons, but who only at their own expense, according to their wealth and quality, send suitable men; and to those likewise who, although at another's expense, go, nevertheless, in their own persons: we grant full pardon of their sins.

— Fourth Lateran Council (1215), crusade decree Ad liberandam, §17 (E. F. Henderson trans.).2

The question

Urban II's council at Clermont (1095) counted the journey to Jerusalem “in lieu of all penance”; Innocent III's Quia maior (1213) and the Fourth Lateran Council (1215) extended the same pardon to those who paid for a substitute; and in 1517 Martin Luther's Ninety-five Theses protested the preaching of the St. Peter's indulgence. Writers differ on where the practice Luther protested came from: the medieval extension of the indulgence from canonical penance to the penalties of purgatory, money conditions attached to an older exercise of the Church's power to remit penance, or the crusade indulgence once it could be gained by paying. They also differ on whether what Luther attacked was an abuse of the practice or the practice itself.

The practice grew from carrying the indulgence beyond canonical penance to the penalties of purgatory

On this reading an indulgence was at first a remission of penance imposed by the Church, and the trade of 1517 arose when it was extended to the penalty of sin before God and to the souls in purgatory.

Held by: Martin Luther (Lutheran, Ninety-five Theses, 1517); David S. Schaff (Protestant church historian, History of the Christian Church VI, 1910).3

Luther's fifth thesis reads: “The pope does not intend to remit, and cannot remit any penalties other than those which he has imposed either by his own authority or by that of the Canons.” He adds that “the penitential canons are imposed only on the living.” The step to purgatory is, for him, the departure: “This changing of the canonical penalty to the penalty of purgatory is quite evidently one of the tares that were sown while the bishops slept.” The papal “full remission of all penalties” therefore means “not actually ‘of all,’ but only of those imposed by himself,” and the preachers err who say “that so soon as the penny jingles into the money-box, the soul flies out [of purgatory].”4

Schaff traces the same shift in the record. “Originally an indulgence was the remission of a part or all of the works of satisfaction demanded by the priest in the sacrament of penance.” Then: “In the 13th century, it came to be regarded as a remission of the penalty of sin itself, both here and in purgatory.” The treasury of merits (thesaurus meritorum) was proposed by Hugh of St-Cher around 1230 and developed by Albert the Great, Alexander of Hales and Thomas Aquinas; Clement VI defined it in Unigenitus Dei Filius (1343); and Sixtus IV's bull of 1476 for the church of Saintes offered “to those who paid a certain sum” for the building “the privilege of securing a relaxation of the sufferings of the purgatorial dead,” souls which, Schaff notes, Aquinas placed under “the jurisdiction of the Church on earth.” To “the abuse, to which this doctrine opened the door,” Schaff adds the popular belief that letters of indulgence freed from guilt as well as penalty, the formula a culpa et poena found in papal bulls, an element he credits H. C. Lea with bringing to light. On this reading the money that changed hands in 1517 bought what the theology of the preceding three centuries had made the indulgence promise: relief for the dead.5

Indulgences are an ancient exercise of the Church's power to remit penance; the abuses came from money attached to them

On this reading the power to grant indulgences was given by Christ and used from the earliest times, the crusade grant was one application of it among many, and the sale Luther met was an abuse that could be removed without removing the practice.

Held by: the Council of Trent (Catholic, Session XXV, 1563); William Kent (Catholic, The Catholic Encyclopedia, “Indulgences,” 1910); Paul VI (Catholic, apostolic constitution Indulgentiarum doctrina, 1967).6

Trent teaches that “the power of conferring Indulgences was granted by Christ to the Church; and she has, even in the most ancient times, used the said power,” and orders “that all evil gains for the obtaining thereof,–whence a most prolific cause of abuses amongst the Christian people has been derived,–be wholly abolished.” Paul VI's Indulgentiarum doctrina (1967) writes that the early bishops “permitted canonical penances to be replaced by other possibly easier works,” and that the conviction that pastors could free the faithful “from the vestiges of sins by applying the merits of Christ and of the saints” led gradually “to the usage of indulgences which represented a progression in the doctrine and discipline of the Church rather than a change”; it acknowledges that the practice “has at times been improperly used,” among other ways “through the collection of ‘illicit profits.’”7

Kent traces the practice through the reconciliation of the lapsed in Cyprian's time, the relaxations of penance allowed by the councils of the fourth century, the penitentials, and the Irish Synod of 807, where a fast might be “redeemed” by a psalter or a denarius to the poor: “the beginning of the so-called ‘redemptions’ which soon passed into general usage.” Pilgrimage to Rome and Jerusalem followed, and after Clermont “Similar indulgences were granted throughout the five centuries following.”8

The abuses, on Kent's account, entered through almsgiving made a condition of the grant, for “the building of churches, the endowment of hospitals, or the organization of a crusade.” In such purposes “there is nothing essentially evil,” but there was “the danger that the payment might be regarded as the price of the indulgence.” The remedy was reform: Trent abolished “the name and method” of the quaestores and directed that indulgences be published by the bishops and “bestowed gratuitously.” Kent holds that for the dead the Church, having no jurisdiction over them, makes the indulgence available “by way of suffrage (per modum suffragii),” petitioning God to accept the works offered. On this reading the indulgence remains what it was at the start, the Church's remission of the penalty due to forgiven sin, with the money taken away.9

The crusade indulgence, once it could be gained by paying for a substitute, became the channel of the later trade

On this reading the decisive step was Innocent III's provision of 1213–1215 that let those who paid share the crusader's indulgence, from which a line of crusade finance runs to the indulgence of 1515–1517.

Held by: Thomas F. Madden (historian of the Crusades, The New Concise History of the Crusades, 2005).10

It was also decreed that those who paid to outfit and supply a crusader could share in the crusading indulgence. This not only increased the number of those able to make the journey but also extended to women, the elderly, and the sick spiritual benefits previously available only to fighting men. No one could see it then, but in this provision was planted the seed that in three centuries' time would grow into the weed of abuses that ultimately led to the Protestant Reformation.

Madden locates the turn not in Urban's grant to those who went but in the concession that the same reward could be had by paying.11 The chain runs as follows. G. G. Coulton calls Urban's grant (1095) “the first definite instance of a plenary Indulgence.” The reward was soon applied beyond Jerusalem: Urban urged Catalans in 1089 to restore Tarragona in lieu of going east; Paschal II granted the Balearic campaign (1113–1115) “the same remission of sins as to the defenders of the eastern church.”12 Quia maior (1213) opened the vow to “anyone who wishes,” to be “commuted, redeemed, or deferred,” with the remission promised to those who redeemed it by a payment proportioned to their means; Ad liberandam made it conciliar law, with a share for donors “according to the quality of their aid.” J. A. Brundage reads the blanket admission as setting aside the earlier case-by-case screening, including a wife's consent.13 Under Gregory IX (1227–1241) the cash redemption of vows became a standing part of crusade finance, administered through the friars and applied to the Baltic, the Stedinger, heretics and the papacy's political enemies; C. T. Maier calls it a “regular feature of all thirteenth-century crusading bulls.”14 The St. Peter's indulgence of Leo X (1515), preached in Germany by Johann Tetzel under an arrangement that secretly divided the proceeds between Rome and the bankers of Archbishop Albert of Mainz, is on this reading the same instrument at the end of three centuries: a spiritual reward that money could obtain.15
