John Chrysostom on purgatory and prayer for the dead

Dates: c. 392 · Wikipedia
Summary: Chrysostom (c. 347–407), presbyter of Antioch and later bishop of Constantinople, commends prayer, alms, and the Eucharistic offering for the dead, urging that "some refreshment may thereby ensue to them," and holds that the Apostles ordered that "remembrance should be made of the dead in the dreadful Mysteries." In his homilies on 1 Corinthians he reads the fire of 1 Corinthians 3:13–15 as the fire of judgment, glossing "he shall be saved" as a dark hint that the man "shall remain forever in punishment."

Claims & evidence

(a) Chrysostom: the commemoration of the departed is made "not in vain"

…nor do we in vain make mention of the departed in the course of the divine mysteries, and approach God in their behalf, beseeching the Lamb Who is before us, Who taketh away the sin of the world;—not in vain, but that some refreshment may thereby ensue to them. Not in vain cloth he that standeth by the altar cry out when the tremendous mysteries are celebrated, "For all that have fallen asleep in Christ, and for those who perform commemorations in their behalf."

— Homilies on First Corinthians 41 §8, historicalchristian.faith. Chrysostom urges the living to aid the dead "not by tears, but by prayers and supplications and alms and offerings," insisting the commemoration at the altar is "not in vain… but that some refreshment may thereby ensue to them." He presents prayer for the departed as apostolic practice that brings them benefit.

In the same passage he grounds the practice in a scriptural syllogism drawn from the patriarch Job, who offered sacrifice for his children (Job 1:5):

For if the children of Job were purged by the sacrifice of their father, why dost thou doubt that when we too offer for the departed, some consolation arises to them? since God is wont to grant the petitions of those who ask for others.

— Homilies on First Corinthians 41 §8, historicalchristian.faith. He reasons from Job's sacrifice for his children to the offering for the departed, on the ground that "God is wont to grant the petitions of those who ask for others."

(a′) The fuller exhortation: aid the dead by prayer, oblation, and alms, and the catechumens by alms

His most expansive statement of the practice comes in the homily on Philippians 1:24, where he presses the living to "think of some assistance" for the departed:

Let us weep for these; let us assist them according to our power; let us think of some assistance for them, small though it be, yet still let us assist them. How and in what way? By praying and entreating others to make prayers for them, by continually giving to the poor on their behalf… Not in vain did the Apostles order that remembrance should be made of the dead in the dreadful Mysteries. They know that great gain resulteth to them, great benefit; for when the whole people stands with uplifted hands, a priestly assembly, and that awful Sacrifice lies displayed, how shall we not prevail with God by our entreaties for them? And this we do for those who have departed in faith, whilst the catechumens are not thought worthy even of this consolation, but are deprived of all means of help save one. And what is this? We may give to the poor on their behalf. This deed in a certain way refreshes them.

— Homilies on Philippians 3 §3 (on Phil 1:24), historicalchristian.faith. Here prayer, the Eucharistic "Sacrifice," and alms are all said to "prevail with God" for those who died in faith, on the Apostles' order. Chrysostom also holds that catechumens who died unbaptized, though "not thought worthy" of the oblation, can still be "refreshed" by alms given on their behalf. The benefit he names is "great gain," "great benefit," "consolation," and "refreshment."

(b) Chrysostom reads the fire of 1 Corinthians 3:15 as the penalty of judgment

And so here in saying, "he shall be saved," he hath but darkly hinted at the intensity of the penalty: as if he had said, "But himself shall remain forever in punishment."

— Homily on First Corinthians 9, historicalchristian.faith. Chrysostom reads "saved" as understatement: the fire is the penalty of the day of judgment, and "shall be saved" hints "darkly" that the man "shall remain forever in punishment." He sets out a two-fold loss: "lo, here is one punishment… lo, again, here is a second. And his meaning is, 'He himself shall not perish in the same way as his works… but he shall abide' "— abide, that is, in the fire. He reads the verse of the eschatological judgment.

Reception and debate

  • Chrysostom commends prayer, alms, and oblation for the dead in the homilies on 1 Corinthians and Philippians, and in the homily on 1 Corinthians 3 reads the fire of that passage as the penalty of the last judgment. Compare Cyril of Jerusalem on prayer for the departed at the Eucharist.
  • Other witnesses on this timeline read the fire of 1 Corinthians 3:13–15 differently: Origen and Gregory of Nyssa speak of a medicinal fire, and Ambrosiaster and Thomas Aquinas of a fire by which those who are saved are cleansed.
  • For his fuller account of the soul's lot immediately after death — conscious, escorted by angels, fixed in Abraham's bosom or in torment across an impassable gulf — see his intermediate-state page. See also the purgatory timeline.
