Aerius of Sebaste on purgatory and prayer for the dead

Dates: c. 355 · Wikipedia
Summary: The one ancient figure on this timeline recorded as rejecting prayer and offering for the dead. A presbyter of Sebaste in Pontus, Aerius held that naming the departed in the liturgy was pointless and even harmful — if the prayers of the living could benefit the dead, no one would need to live holily. Epiphanius listed the objection as the seventy-fifth heresy of his Panarion and answered it; Augustine's De Haeresibus also records it.

Claims & evidence

Aerius's objection: prayer and offering for the dead are useless and breed moral laxity

Why do you mention the names of the dead after their deaths (i.e., in the liturgy)? … If the prayer of the people here has benefited the people there, no one should practice piety or perform good works! He should get some friends any way he wants, either by bribery or by asking friends on his death bed, and they should pray that he may not suffer in the next life, or be held to account for his heinous sins.

— Aerius, as quoted by Epiphanius, Panarion 75 (Against the Aerians), §3.5 (Frank Williams trans.), archive.org. Aerius reasons that if post-mortem help were available, personal holiness would be dispensable, since one could arrange for prayers and offerings instead of repenting. Epiphanius records that Aerius rejected prayer for the dead along with the appointed fasts and the keeping of Easter, treating the established custom as no part of the apostolic faith.

Epiphanius's reply, and Augustine's entry on the Aerians

… the church has received it, and there was a world-wide agreement before Aerius and his Aerians.

— Epiphanius, Panarion 75 §6.4, archive.org. Epiphanius answers that naming the departed confesses that they "live with the Lord" and that the prayer for them is "useful … even if it does not force back the whole of guilty charges laid to them" (§8), and he appeals to "a world-wide agreement before Aerius." Augustine lists the Aerians on the same ground (De Haeresibus 53: Aerius "said that the sacrifice ought not to be offered for the dead"); the article on Aerius (signed E.V.) in Wace and Piercy's Dictionary of Christian Biography (1911) writes that Augustine here "merely epitomises Epiphanius," so that the ancient record of Aerius's teaching rests on Epiphanius's account.1

Reception and debate

  • Aerius is the one figure on this timeline recorded as rejecting the practice. How his objection and Epiphanius's answer have been read — as a departure from a received, world-wide custom, as evidence that the Fathers held the custom without agreement on its effect, or as a principled dissent — and whether Epiphanius's report of him as "a thoroughgoing Arian" (Panarion 75.1.3) bears on the question, is set out at Was prayer for the dead uncontested in the ancient Church?
  • Aerius's own words survive only through Epiphanius's report, written against him; no work of his is extant. The quotation above is given as Epiphanius reproduces it. His floruit is fixed by his break with Eustathius, bishop of Sebaste (made bishop c. 355), under whom Aerius had been a presbyter and a candidate for the same see.
  • The next timeline entry is Epiphanius of Salamis (c. 375); neighbouring entries include Cyril of Jerusalem and the Liturgy of Saint Mark before him and Ambrose and the Eastern liturgies after, on the purgatory timeline.
  • A second tenet, on a different doctrine. The same Panarion 75 that records Aerius's rejection of prayer for the dead also preserves a second Aerian tenet — that a presbyter and a bishop are one and the same order. That tenet is treated on his ministerial-priesthood page, beside Jerome. Both tenets are known only through Epiphanius.
  • A related objection a half-century on. Vigilantius of Calagurris (c. 406), a presbyter in Gaul, objected to prayer to the saints and the veneration of relics and was answered by Jerome; his words, too, survive in the work of the writer who answered him.
