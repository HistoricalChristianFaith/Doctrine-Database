Cyprian of Carthage on purgatory and prayer for the dead

Dates: c. 253 · Wikipedia
Summary: Cyprian, defending the Church's practice of restoring penitents to communion, draws a sharp contrast between the martyr who is "crowned" at once and the believer who must be "cleansed and long purged by fire"; whether that cleansing is after death or in the Church's penitential discipline in this life is disputed. His letters also presuppose the routine offering of the Eucharistic sacrifice for the departed.

Claims & evidence

The penitent "cleansed and long purged by fire," and the martyr "at once crowned"

It is one thing to stand for pardon, another thing to attain to glory: it is one thing, when cast into prison, not to go out thence until one has paid the uttermost farthing; another thing at once to receive the wages of faith and courage. It is one thing, tortured by long suffering for sins, to be cleansed and long purged by fire; another to have purged all sins by suffering. It is one thing, in fine, to be in suspense till the sentence of God at the day of judgment; another to be at once crowned by the Lord.

— Epistle 51 (To Antonianus), §20, source. Arguing that pardon granted to the penitent does not cheapen the martyr's crown, Cyprian sets the restored penitent, "tortured by long suffering for sins, to be cleansed and long purged by fire" and held "in suspense till the sentence of God at the day of judgment," beside the martyr who has "purged all sins by suffering" and is "at once crowned by the Lord." Whether the passage describes purification after death or the penitential discipline of the lapsed in this life is disputed (see below).

The Eucharistic sacrifice is normally offered for the departed

if any one should do this, no offering should be made for him, nor any sacrifice be celebrated for his repose. For he does not deserve to be named at the altar of God in the prayer of the priests, who has wished to call away the priests and ministers from the altar.

— Epistle 65 (To the Clergy and People of Furni), §2, source. Geminius Victor had named a presbyter as guardian in his will, against the Church's rule. As a penalty Cyprian directs that "no offering should be made for him, nor any sacrifice be celebrated for his repose," and that he not be "named at the altar of God in the prayer of the priests." Withholding the offering and the naming of the dead "in the prayer of the priests" is imposed as a penalty, which presupposes that they were otherwise made.

Reception and debate

  • Cyprian's "uttermost farthing" is the farthing of Matthew 5:25–26, which Tertullian also reads of a reckoning after death. Here Cyprian uses it beside the image of being "cleansed and long purged by fire."
  • How §20 has been read. Joseph Ratzinger (Catholic), in Eschatology: Death and Eternal Life, reads the passage of purification after death: “The saying found in Matthew 5:26 offered Cyprian an occasion for thinking through a possible continuation of penance in the afterlife,” so that “the penitential way of purification exists not only in this world but in the world to come” (pp. 215–233, excerpted in Church Life Journal, 2023, churchlifejournal.nd.edu). Trent Horn (Catholic Answers, 2021) reads it the same way. Gavin Ortlund (Protestant), as quoted by Horn, reads it of this life: “he’s not talking about the afterlife. He’s talking about the penitential discipline of the church in this life”; Horn names a 1960 article by Pierre Jay as the earliest modern statement of that reading he found ⚠ unverified (catholic.com).
  • Epistle 65 bears on prayer and oblation for the dead through a penalty: Cyprian withholds the sacrifice for one who broke the Church's rule. Edward Hanna, in the Catholic Encyclopedia article “Purgatory” (1911), cites this letter for the “settled custom of the Church” of prayer for the dead (newadvent.org). Epiphanius of Salamis later defends the practice against Aerius; see also Was prayer for the dead uncontested in the ancient Church? and the purgatory timeline.
