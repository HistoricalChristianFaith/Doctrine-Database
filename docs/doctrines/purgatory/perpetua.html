Perpetua on purgatory and prayer for the dead

Dates: c. 203 · Wikipedia
Summary: The Passion of Perpetua and Felicity records the prison diary of Vibia Perpetua, a young North African catechumen martyred at Carthage c. 203. In it she recounts praying for her dead brother Dinocrates — who had died at seven of a disfiguring facial cancer — and seeing him in two successive visions: first in a "gloomy place," parched and unable to reach water, then, after her continued prayer, "with a clean body well clad," refreshed, the wound healed to a scar, drinking freely. She concludes that "he was translated from the place of punishment." The narrative records a Christian praying for a dead member of her family and, in her vision, the prayer availing; Vincentius Victor and Augustine of Hippo later disputed what the case shows (see below).

Claims & evidence

Perpetua prays for her dead brother and sees him suffering in a gloomy place

After a few days, whilst we were all praying, on a sudden, in the middle of our prayer, there came to me a word, and I named Dinocrates; and I was amazed that that name had never come into my mind until then, and I was grieved as I remembered his misfortune. And I felt myself immediately to be worthy, and to be called on to ask on his behalf. And for him I began earnestly to make supplication, and to cry with groaning to the Lord. … I saw Dinocrates going out from a gloomy place, where also there were several others, and he was parched and very thirsty, with a filthy countenance and pallid colour, and the wound on his face which he had when he died. … And moreover, in the same place where Dinocrates was, there was a pool full of water, having its brink higher than was the stature of the boy; and Dinocrates raised himself up as if to drink. And I was grieved that, although that pool held water, still, on account of the height to its brink, he could not drink. And I was aroused, and knew that my brother was in suffering. But I trusted that my prayer would bring help to his suffering; and I prayed for him every day … groaning and weeping that he might be granted to me.

— The Passion of the Holy Martyrs Perpetua and Felicitas 2.3 (ANF translation), newadvent.org. Dinocrates "had been my brother after the flesh, seven years of age, who died miserably with disease." Perpetua intercedes for him without argument for the practice, trusting "that my prayer would bring help to his suffering."

After her continued prayer, she sees him refreshed and "translated from the place of punishment"

Then, on the day on which we remained in fetters, this was shown to me. I saw that that place which I had formerly observed to be in gloom was now bright; and Dinocrates, with a clean body well clad, was finding refreshment. And where there had been a wound, I saw a scar; and that pool which I had before seen, I saw now with its margin lowered even to the boy's navel. And one drew water from the pool incessantly, and upon its brink was a goblet filled with water; and Dinocrates drew near and began to drink from it, and the goblet did not fail. And when he was satisfied, he went away from the water to play joyously, after the manner of children, and I awoke. Then I understood that he was translated from the place of punishment.

— The Passion of the Holy Martyrs Perpetua and Felicitas 2.4 (ANF translation), newadvent.org. The change of state — from thirst, filth, and an unreachable pool to refreshment, a healed scar, and water freely drunk — follows Perpetua's daily prayer for him, and she understands it as his translation "from the place of punishment."

Reception and debate

  • The Passion presents its visionary chapters as Perpetua's own first-person diary, completed by an editor after her death. The introductory notice to the Ante-Nicene Fathers translation states, "I think Tertullian was the editor of the story, not its author," and reports that "some have maintained that, whoever the writer was, he was a Montanist" (ANF 3, introductory notice); compare Tertullian, her fellow North African of the same years.
  • The account and its use. The help Perpetua wins for Dinocrates is described as a passage from a place of suffering and thirst to refreshment in answer to prayer; the narrative does not name a mechanism. Compare Tertullian's widow who requests "refreshment for him meanwhile." Vincentius Victor, as Augustine reports him, argued from the case of Dinocrates that "even to the unbaptized may be given the remission of sins and an abode with the blessed" (On the Soul and Its Origin II.14, historicalchristian.faith). Augustine replied that neither Perpetua "nor whoever it was that wrote the account, say that the boy, who had died at the age of seven years, died without baptism" (I.12, historicalchristian.faith).
  • The motif of refreshment (refrigerium) for the departed in the interim before the resurrection also appears in Tertullian and the early liturgies; see Cyril of Jerusalem a century and a half later making the petition at the altar. See also the purgatory timeline.
