The Liturgy of Saint James on purgatory and prayer for the dead

Dates: c. 400 · Wikipedia
Summary: The Liturgy of Saint James, the historic anaphora of Jerusalem and Antioch, offers the Eucharistic sacrifice "for the rest of the souls that have fallen asleep aforetime" and prays God to "give rest" to all the faithful departed "from righteous Abel unto this day." It is an Eastern witness to intercession for the dead within the offering of the sacrifice.

Claims & evidence

The offering is made for the rest of the souls who have fallen asleep

… and grant that our offering may be acceptable, sanctified by the Holy Spirit, as a propitiation for our transgressions and the errors of the people; and for the rest of the souls that have fallen asleep aforetime.

— The Divine Liturgy of James, source. The Eucharistic offering is explicitly described as availing not only "for our transgressions and the errors of the people" but "for the rest of the souls that have fallen asleep aforetime" — the sacrifice presented on behalf of the dead.

A commemoration that God give rest to all the faithful departed

Remember, O Lord God, the spirits and all flesh, of whom we have made mention, and of whom we have not made mention, who are of the true faith, from righteous Abel unto this day: unto them do Thou give rest there in the land of the living, in Thy kingdom, in the joy of paradise, in the bosom of Abraham, and of Isaac, and of Jacob, our holy fathers; whence pain, and grief, and lamentation have fled.

— The Divine Liturgy of James, source. The great intercession names "the spirits and all flesh … from righteous Abel unto this day" and asks God to "give rest" to them "in the bosom of Abraham" — the Abraham's-bosom imagery of Luke 16, here the object of the prayer for the dead (see the intermediate-state timeline).

Reception and debate

  • Dating: the developed Greek text is later than its core; it is placed here c. 400 as a Jerusalem/Antiochene witness. ⚠ unverified: that the anaphora is fourth-century in substance and that its intercessions are close kin to those Cyril of Jerusalem describes for the Jerusalem rite c. 350. Compare Cyril of Jerusalem, whose Mystagogic Catecheses describe a supplication for the departed at the Eucharist.
  • The other early anaphoras on the timeline are the Liturgy of Addai and Mari (Syriac) and the Liturgy of Saint Mark (Alexandrian). On how such witnesses bear on the history of the practice, see Was prayer for the dead uncontested in the ancient Church?
  • On the fire of 1 Corinthians 3, a separate strand of the history, see Origen, John Chrysostom, and Thomas Aquinas.
