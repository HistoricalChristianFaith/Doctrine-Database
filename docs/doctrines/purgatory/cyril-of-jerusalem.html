Cyril of Jerusalem on purgatory and prayer for the dead

Dates: c. 350 · Wikipedia
Summary: Cyril (c. 313–386), bishop of Jerusalem, explains to the newly baptized why the Church prays for the dead. In his Mystagogic on the Eucharist he describes the Church commemorating the departed over the consecrated gifts, "believing that it will be a very great benefit to the souls for whom the supplication is put up." He defends the practice with the parable of a king petitioned on behalf of offenders: in offering Christ for sinners who have fallen asleep, the Church entreats God's mercy for them. The Mystagogic Catecheses are cited here under their conventional ascription to Cyril; whether they are his or his successor John's is disputed (see Reception and debate).

Claims & evidence

The Eucharistic supplication is "a very great benefit to the souls for whom" it is offered

Then we commemorate also those who have fallen asleep before us, first Patriarchs, Prophets, Apostles, Martyrs, that at their prayers and intercessions God would receive our petition. Then on behalf also of the Holy Fathers and Bishops who have fallen asleep before us, and in a word of all who in past years have fallen asleep among us, believing that it will be a very great benefit to the souls, for whom the supplication is put up, while that holy and most awful sacrifice is set forth.

— Catechetical Lecture 23 (Mystagogic 5) §9, newadvent.org. Over "that holy and most awful sacrifice" the departed are named; Cyril states that the Church does so "believing that it will be a very great benefit to the souls" remembered.

As a king is petitioned for offenders, so the Church offers Christ for the sinful dead

For if a king were to banish certain who had given him offence, and then those who belong to them should weave a crown and offer it to him on behalf of those under punishment, would he not grant a remission of their penalties? In the same way we, when we offer to Him our supplications for those who have fallen asleep, though they be sinners, weave no crown, but offer up Christ sacrificed for our sins, propitiating our merciful God for them as well as for ourselves.

— Catechetical Lecture 23 (Mystagogic 5) §10, newadvent.org. Cyril takes up the objection — "what is a soul profited… if it be commemorated in the prayer?" — and answers with the king-and-crown illustration: the Eucharistic offering of Christ "propitiat[es] our merciful God" for the departed, "though they be sinners."

Reception and debate

  • Authorship of the Mystagogic Catecheses. Some manuscripts name John, Cyril's successor as bishop of Jerusalem (c. 386), as the author of the five Mystagogic Lectures; Edwin Hamilton Gifford (NPNF 2.7, 1894) reports that Andrew Rivet (1640) and Edmund Aubertin (1654) urged this against Cyril, and himself follows Touttée in ascribing all the lectures to Cyril (NPNF 2.7, Introduction, ch. XI). James Ussher (Church of Ireland), in An Answer to a Jesuit (1624), cites the passage as by "Cyril, or rather John, Bishop of Jerusalem" (1835 Cambridge ed., p. 237). Anders-Christian Jacobsen (2019) reports Edward Yarnold (2000) and Alexis Doval (2001) as holding that Cyril wrote both the catechetical and the mystagogical lectures, the latter late in his episcopate, and Juliette Day (2007, 2011) as holding that Cyril cannot be their author ("Cyril of Jerusalem," Aarhus University).
  • Cyril speaks of the benefit to the departed as coming from the Church's offering of Christ. Edward Hanna, in the Catholic Encyclopedia article “Purgatory” (1911), quotes this passage (Mystagogic 5.9) for “the Apostolic practice of praying for the dead which passed into the liturgy of the Church,” among his patristic witnesses to purgatory (newadvent.org). Mark of Ephesus, replying to the Latin report on purgatory at the Council of Ferrara–Florence, wrote that the departed “are without doubt helped by the Liturgies and prayers and almsgiving performed for them,” while deliverance “thanks to a certain purgatorial suffering and temporal fire” he did not find “in the prayers and hymns for the dead, or in the words of the teachers” (silouanthompson.net). James Ussher (Church of Ireland, 1624) cites the objection Cyril answers, that he "knew many who said thus: What profit doth the soul get that goeth out of this world, either with sins or not with sins, if you make mention of it in prayer?", among his witnesses that "Whether the dead received profit by the prayers of the living, was still a question in the Church" (An Answer to a Jesuit, 1835 Cambridge ed., p. 237). On how the ancient record of prayer for the dead has been read, see Was prayer for the dead uncontested in the ancient Church?
