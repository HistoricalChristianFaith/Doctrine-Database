Josephus on Og of Bashan — the giant king and his iron bed

Dates: c. AD 93 (the Antiquities of the Jews 4.5.3; Josephus b. c. AD 37, d. c. 100) · Wikipedia
Summary: Josephus retells Deuteronomy’s notice as a portrait of a man of exceptional stature, and reads his size from the iron bed at Rabbath.

Claims & evidence

Og’s stature, and his iron bed at Rabbath

Now Og had very few equals, either in the largeness of his body, or handsomeness of his appearance. He was also a man of great activity in the use of his hands, so that his actions were not unequal to the vast largeness and handsome appearance of his body. And men could easily guess at his strength and magnitude when they took his bed at Rabbath, the royal city of the Ammonites; its structure was of iron, its breadth four cubits, and its length a cubit more than double thereto.

— Josephus, Antiquities of the Jews 4.5.3, trans. William Whiston, source. Josephus takes the “bedstead of iron” of Deuteronomy 3:11 as literal furniture and its dimensions as the measure of the man: the breadth “four cubits” and the length “a cubit more than double thereto” (= nine cubits) reproduce the biblical figures exactly, and he locates the relic at Rabbath (Rabbath-Ammon), just as Deuteronomy does. Josephus makes no mention of the Rephaim or of any Flood-survival, and his Og is a very large and able man.1

Reception and debate

  • Josephus reads the iron bed as the measure of Og himself. Maria Lindquist compares the bed instead with the cultic bed of Marduk at Babylon (⚠ unverified; see the Og of Bashan timeline).
  • Josephus’s Og is human and heroic — large, handsome, dexterous; the rabbinic Og is an antediluvian Watcher-descendant of far greater size (see Og in Jewish tradition).
