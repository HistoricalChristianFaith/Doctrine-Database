Og in Jewish tradition — the antediluvian who outrode the Flood

Dates: traditions from c. AD 200, redacted c. 500–800 (Targum Pseudo-Jonathan; the Babylonian Talmud; Pirqei de-Rabbi Eliezer) · Wikipedia
Summary: Where Scripture measures only Og’s bed, the rabbinic sources make him a survivor-giant — an antediluvian, brother of Sihon and grandson of the fallen Watcher Shamhazai, who outrode the Flood on the ark and, at the last, tried to crush the camp of Israel under an uprooted mountain.

Claims & evidence

An antediluvian, and brother of Sihon

Now, Sihon and Og were brothers, as the Master said: Sihon and Og were sons of Ahijah, son of Shamhazai. … And Rabbi Yoḥanan said that the term “one that was saved” is referring to Og, who was saved from the punishment of the generation of the flood.

— Babylonian Talmud, Niddah 61a, William Davidson translation, Sefaria. Og is made an antediluvian and a grandson of Shamhazai — the chief of the fallen Watchers of the 1 Enoch tradition — and Rabbi Yoḥanan reads the fugitive “one that escaped” who tells Abram of Lot’s capture (Genesis 14:13) as Og, whom this tradition makes the giant who alone survived the Flood.1

How he survived: riding the ark, fed by Noah

And Og came, who had been spared from the giants that died in the deluge, and had ridden protected upon the top of the ark, and sustained with food by Noah… And he arose and came, upon the eve of the day of the Pascha, and found him making the unleavened cakes.

— Targum Pseudo-Jonathan on Genesis 14:13, Etheridge translation, Sefaria. The same legend is told at more length in Pirqei de-Rabbi Eliezer:2

…except Og, king of Bashan, who sat down on a piece of wood under the gutter of the ark. He swore to Noah and to his sons that he would be their servant for ever. What did Noah do? He bored an aperture in the ark, and he put (through it) his food daily for him, and he also was left, as it is said, “For only Og, king of Bashan, remained of the remnant of the giants” (Deut. 3:11).

— Pirqei de-Rabbi Eliezer 23, Sefaria. Here Deuteronomy 3:11’s “only Og… remained” is read not of the conquest but of the Flood, as the text that he alone of the giants came through. The Talmud raises a physical difficulty: Zevachim 113b asks “how did Og… who according to tradition was of the generation of the flood, stand, i.e., survive the boiling water?” and answers with a miracle, “the water on the sides of the ark cooled.”3

The uprooted mountain, and a giant measured in cubits

…Og said: How large is the camp of Israel? It is three parasangs. I will go and uproot a mountain three parasangs long and I will hurl it upon them and kill them. He went, uprooted a mountain three parasangs long, and brought it on his head. And The Holy One, Blessed be He, brought grasshoppers upon it and they pierced the peak of the mountain and it fell on his neck. Og wanted to remove it from his head; his teeth were extended to one side of his head and to the other and he was unable to remove it… How tall was Moses? He was ten cubits tall. He took an axe ten cubits long, jumped up ten cubits, and struck Og in the ankle and killed him.

— Babylonian Talmud, Berakhot 54b, William Davidson translation, Sefaria. Where Scripture gives the measure of his bed, the aggadah measures the giant: a man so vast that a ten-cubit Moses, leaping ten cubits with a ten-cubit axe, can reach only his ankle. Reception and debate

  • The legend sets a giant “remnant” (Deuteronomy 3:11) beside a deluge that killed “all flesh.” Pirqei de-Rabbi Eliezer cites Genesis 7:23 just before the passage quoted above — “And Noah only was left, and they that were with him in the ark” — and makes Og the exception; Zevachim 113b accounts for his survival by a miracle.5
  • Making Og a descendant of Shamhazai the Watcher ties him to the Genesis-6 / 1 Enoch account of the Nephilim, traced on the Nephilim timeline.
  • Josephus (see Josephus on Og) keeps the giant human and heroic; these rabbinic sources make him an antediluvian of far greater size.
