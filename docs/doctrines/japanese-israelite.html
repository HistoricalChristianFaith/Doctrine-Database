The Japanese-Israelite theory: a lost tribe in the East?

The Japanese-Israelite theory (Japanese Nichiyu dōsoron, “the common ancestry of the Japanese and the Jews”) holds that the Japanese — or a priestly or imperial class within them — descend from the Ten Lost Tribes of Israel, carried east after the fall of Samaria until they reached the islands of Japan. Proponents rest the case on two kinds of resemblance: ritual parallels between Shinto practice and the worship of ancient Israel (a portable shrine likened to the Ark of the Covenant, a mountain festival read as the binding of Isaac, priestly linen and salt purifications), and linguistic ones — Japanese words and names derived from Hebrew by sound-resemblance, the same folk-etymology method that drives the Anglo-Saxon version told on the British Israelism timeline. This page weighs that narrower historical claim of literal Israelite descent, not the reality or dignity of the Japanese people or the sincerity of the Japanese Christians who have most often held it.1

Related doctrines: The Lost Tribes of Israel — see also British Israelism (the Anglo-Saxons as the Lost Tribes) · the Beta Israel of Ethiopia (a lost tribe of Dan?) · the Pashtun Bani Israel (an Afghan lost tribe?) · the Bene Israel of India (a lost tribe of Israel?)

This page tells the story of that identification as a reception-history timeline: not a survey of a belief held by many ancient witnesses, but a chronology of the datable sources that advanced, transmitted, or answered the theory, earliest first. Its “witnesses” are therefore dated texts — a recanting Jesuit, a Scottish missionary’s privately printed book, a Waseda professor’s Nestorian thesis, a Yale-trained enthusiast, a Holiness bishop, a philo-Semitic revival movement, an Israeli engineer’s word-lists, a modern pastor’s website, and the historians and geneticists who close the file — each footnoted to its source.

Two facts frame the whole timeline and set it apart from its family. First, unlike the ancient and indigenous Pashtun tradition, the Japanese theory is modern and externally introduced: it was first worked out in the 1870s not by a Japanese or a Jew but by a Scot, and it entered Japan through the Western missionary channel before Japanese Christians took it up — the same colonial-era pattern the historian Tudor Parfitt traces across the globe. Second, it sits inside a peculiar double history of Japanese feeling about Jews: the philo-Semitic enthusiasm that produced the “common ancestry” idea grew up alongside, and sometimes tipped into, the Protocols of the Elders of Zion antisemitism that spread in Japan from the 1920s — two opposite fantasies flourishing in a country with almost no Jews. The core descent claim is weighed adversarially in the Arguments below and comes out unsupported as history: it has no footing in documented history, historical linguistics, or genetics, which place Japanese origins squarely in East Asia.

Timeline

A Jesuit ties the Lost Tribes to the East — then recants (1608)

The earliest thread runs back to João Rodrigues (1561–1634), the Portuguese Jesuit interpreter who spent decades in Japan. In his História da Igreja do Japão (c. 1608) he floated the idea that an East-Asian people descended from the Lost Tribes — though he applied it to the Chinese, not the Japanese, arguing that Confucius and Laozi had drawn on Judaism. He is a precursor rather than a proponent of the Japan theory: he abandoned the notion, concluding instead that Japan had been peopled by ordinary migration from the Asian mainland (from Zhejiang and by way of Korea). His false start is worth recording only because it shows the lost-tribes-in-the-East impulse already stirring in the early modern Catholic mission, two-and-a-half centuries before anyone fixed it on Japan.2

Nicholas McLeod launches the Japan theory (c. 1875–1878)

The theory in its Japanese form was the work of Nicholas McLeod (a Scot of the Isle of Skye, active in Japan in the 1870s–80s), a former herring-trader turned missionary. In a privately printed book — Epitome of the Ancient History of Japan (Nagasaki; the cover title of the fuller edition reads Japan and the Lost Tribes of Israel), with a companion volume of Illustrations issued at Kyoto — he argued that Japan’s aristocratic and priestly “holy class” descended from the deported tribes. He identified Japan’s legendary early king “Osee” (whom he placed c. 730 BC) with Hoshea, the last king of Israel (d. 722 BC), matched the founding emperor Jimmu to Moses, and filled his pages with parallels between Shinto and Israelite ritual — reading the Shinto shrine as “divided into a holy and a most holy place” answering to the Temple, and its priests’ linen dress, bonnet, and breeches as those of Israel’s ancient priesthood. The publication year is variously given (a small first edition c. 1875, the fuller Epitome c. 1878, the illustrated volume c. 1877–1879), but the substance is settled: this is where the Japanese-Israelite theory begins, and — as Parfitt stresses — it “was not the brainchild of a Japanese or a Jew but of a Scot.”3

P. Y. Saeki makes the Hata clan a “Jewish-Nestorian” tribe (1908)

The theory entered Japanese scholarship through Peter Yoshirō Saeki (1871–1965), a Waseda University professor and the leading Japanese authority on the Nestorian (Church of the East) mission in China. In a 1908 book on the Chinese Nestorians he added an appendix, “Regarding Uzumasa,” proposing that the Hata clan (秦氏) — immigrants who reached Japan from Korea around the third century — were a Jewish tribe that had converted to Nestorian Christianity in Central Asia before travelling east. His key prop was a piece of wordplay: the Kyoto district of Uzumasa, where the Hata settled, he parsed as concealing “Ishu” (Jesus) and “masa” (messiah). (The name in fact has a documented origin unrelated to any of this: Emperor Yūryaku granted it to the clan in 471 in recognition of its work in silk production.) Saeki also produced genuine scholarship on the subject — his Nestorian Monument in China (1916) and The Nestorian Documents and Relics in China (1937) remain cited — but it was this thesis that, in the words of the historian Ben-Ami Shillony, “spread the theory about ‘the common ancestry of the Japanese and the Jews’ in Japan, a theory endorsed by some Christian groups.”4

Oyabe Zen’ichirō traces the emperor to the tribe of Gad (1929)

The most ambitious pre-war statement came from Oyabe Zen’ichirō (1867–1941), a Yale-trained scholar who had lived among the Ainu. His Nihon oyobi Nihon kokumin no kigen (“The Origin of Japan and the Japanese,” 1929) traced the Japanese to the Hebrews and made the imperial line itself descend from the tribe of Gad — reading the title mikado as mi (“honourable”) + Gad. He argued that the portable mikoshi shrine derived from the Ark of the Covenant, and marshalled the ritual parallels that would become standard. The book ran to a sixth edition within months and was presented to the Imperial Household — a measure of how respectable the idea had briefly become in an age of surging Japanese nationalism.5

Bishop Juji Nakada gives it a Christian-Zionist turn (1933)

Juji Nakada (1870–1939), co-founder and first bishop of the Japan Holiness Church, folded the common-ancestry idea into a Christian-Zionist theology. In Japan in the Bible (Tokyo, 1933) and related preaching he held that Japan had a providential destiny bound up with Israel’s restoration — the Japanese, as kin of the ancient Israelites, being divinely appointed to a role in the last-days gathering of the Jews. Nakada shows how quickly the theory passed from antiquarian speculation into the pulpit of a large Japanese Protestant body, where the question was no longer merely historical but prophetic.6

Ikurō Teshima founds the Makuya movement (1948)

The theory found its most enduring institutional home in the Makuya (“Tabernacle”) movement, founded in May 1948 by Ikurō Teshima (1910–1973), a businessman shaped by Uchimura Kanzō’s “Non-church” Christianity who took the Hebrew name Abraham. Teshima read the near-simultaneous births of his movement and the modern State of Israel (the same month) as a single act of Providence; Makuya teaches a historical bond between the Japanese and ancient Israel through the dispersion of the Lost Tribes, and expresses it concretely — members have worn vests marking descent from the seafaring tribe of Zebulun, made pilgrimages to Jerusalem, and sent young people to Israeli kibbutzim, and Teshima led Japan’s first pro-Israel demonstration during the 1973 war. The movement belongs to a wider wave of Japanese philo-Semitism in the same years: the Kyoto Hebraist Abraham (Setsuzō) Kotsuji (1899–1973), descended from Shinto priests, who rescued Jewish refugees in the war and formally converted to Judaism in Jerusalem in 1959; and, at the popular level, the 1971 bestseller The Japanese and the Jews by “Isaiah Ben-Dasan” (the pen-name of Yamamoto Shichihei), which made the comparison of the two peoples a national talking-point.7

Joseph Eidelberg builds the linguistic case (1980)

The fullest linguistic version came from Joseph Eidelberg (1916–1985), an Israeli engineer and former army officer who, after spending about a year living at a Shinto shrine, published The Japanese and the Ten Lost Tribes of Israel (1980; Japanese translation 1984, which sold tens of thousands of copies and spawned a television series). Eidelberg derived hundreds of Japanese words, songs, and names from Hebrew: Yamato from a Hebrew phrase for “the people of God,” the imperial style Sumera Mikoto as “His Majesty of Samaria,” and much else, tracing a migration-route for the tribes from Samaria across Asia to the archipelago. His work — extended in the posthumous The Biblical Hebrew Origin of the Japanese People (2005) — became the reference-text for the modern movement, even as academic reviewers judged many of his etymological comparisons far too far-fetched to bear weight.8

Arimasa Kubo carries it online (1990s–present)

The theory’s most widely read contemporary vehicle is the work of Arimasa Kubo (b. 1955), a Tokyo pastor and head of Remnant Publishing, whose essay “Israelites Came to Ancient Japan” (first published in 1999, then freely circulated on his website) has been reposted across the internet and translated widely. Kubo’s signature argument is the Ontohsai festival at the Suwa Taisha shrine in Nagano, which he reads as a re-enactment of the binding of Isaac (Genesis 22), pointing to the local place-name Moriya as an echo of Moriah; he adds the standard parallels — the omikoshi as the Ark, the yamabushi’s forehead box as Jewish phylacteries, the two-chamber shrine as the Tabernacle. “Apparently,” he writes, “no other country but Japan has a festival illustrating the biblical story of Abraham and Isaac.” Kubo, who has translated Eidelberg and the rabbi Marvin Tokayer into Japanese, represents the theory’s move from print into the global online marketplace of ideas.9

The historians read it as a Western projection (1991–2002)

Modern scholarship treats the theory as a construction rather than a lineage. Ben-Ami Shillony, in The Jews and the Japanese: The Successful Outsiders (1991), documents both the philo-Semitic enthusiasm behind it and its strange coexistence with antisemitism; David Goodman and Masanori Miyazawa, in Jews in the Japanese Mind (1995), trace how Japanese Christians, having redefined themselves as the “real” Israel, could in the same culture absorb the Protocols of the Elders of Zion (introduced to Japan in the 1920s) — a paranoia that culminated in the Aum Shinrikyō cult’s antisemitic tract on the eve of its 1995 sarin attack; and Jacob Kovalio’s The Russian Protocols of Zion in Japan (2009) anatomizes that “Jewish peril” discourse in a country with virtually no Jews. Above all Tudor Parfitt, in The Lost Tribes of Israel: The History of a Myth (2002), devotes a chapter to Japan and sets it within his general thesis: the ascription of an Israelite past to distant peoples “forms a consistent feature of the Western colonial enterprise,” introduced, in Japan as elsewhere, “by Western agents” — the same verdict this wiki records for the Anglo-Saxon, Ethiopian, and Afghan versions on their timelines.10

The genetics point to East Asia (2019–2021)

The last witnesses are the population-genetic studies, which place Japanese origins firmly in East Asia. On the now-standard account — extended to a “tripartite” model by Cooke and colleagues in Science Advances (2021) — the Japanese descend from the indigenous Jōmon hunter-gatherers (a lineage deeply diverged from the mainland some 15,000–20,000 years ago), the Yayoi rice-farmers who arrived from the Korean peninsula and Northeast Asia, and a further East-Asian influx in the Kofun period. The Y-chromosome record tells the same story: Japanese paternal lineages are dominated by the Jōmon marker haplogroup D (the D-M55 line, all but unique to Japan) and the Yayoi haplogroup O (shared across East and Southeast Asia), while the Near-Eastern “Semitic” haplogroup J that would mark a Levantine migration is essentially absent. As the writer Jon Entine summarizes, the DNA evidence excludes any significant genetic link between Japanese and Jews; the Japanese language, likewise, is traced by linguists to the Korean peninsula, not the Levant.11

Arguments

Are the Japanese descended from the Ten Lost Tribes of Israel? (assessment: unsupported)

The core identification — the claim the whole theory stands on, taken in its strongest form and weighed against the documentary, linguistic, and genetic evidence. It comes out unsupported. Unlike the ancient, indigenous Pashtun tradition (rated weak), the Japanese version is a modern construction, worked out in the 1870s by a Scottish missionary and carried into Japan through the Western mission before Japanese Christians adopted it; it rests on resemblance-hunting rather than on any transmitted memory of Israelite origin, and the genetics place Japanese ancestry unambiguously in East Asia, with the Levantine paternal marker absent. It is rated unsupported, alongside the Anglo-Saxon version, because — once the ritual look-alikes and Hebrew etymologies are set aside — there is no evidential footing left for it to stand on.12

Were the Hata clan a Jewish-Nestorian tribe? (assessment: unsupported)

The subset variant — the narrower, more testable form of the descent claim, advanced by P. Y. Saeki (1908) and Arimasa Kubo: that a single documented immigrant clan, the Hata, were a Jewish tribe converted to Nestorian Christianity in Central Asia who carried it east along the Silk Road into Japan — the one place the theory offers a concrete migration channel rather than a bare resemblance. Weighed on its own it comes out unsupported: the Nestorian overlay is ruled out by chronology (the Church of the East did not exist until after 431 and reached even China only c. 635, whereas the Hata are dated to the fourth–fifth centuries and reached Japan before any Christian presence there), and its props each dissolve — Uzumasa is a documented 471 silk-tax title, the Konoshima triangular torii an eighteenth-century rebuild, Kōryū-ji a Buddhist temple — while the Hata’s documented origin is the Korean peninsula.13

Do Shinto rituals preserve ancient Israelite worship? (assessment: unsupported)

The ritual proof — the distinct argument, resting on cultic resemblance rather than tradition, that Shinto practice preserves the worship of the Ark and the Temple: the omikoshi as the Ark of the Covenant, the Suwa Taisha Ontohsai festival as the binding of Isaac, the yamabushi’s forehead box as phylacteries, priestly linen and salt purifications, and the hexagram on shrine lamps as the Star of David. Weighed on its own it comes out unsupported: the parallels are selected out of two rich ritual systems, few are diagnostic, and the most striking (the Ontohsai) rests on a folk-etymology (Moriya = Moriah) and dissolves on closer inspection. The resemblances are the kind that resemblance-hunting will always find; they do not demonstrate a shared origin.14

Do Japanese words and names derive from Hebrew? (assessment: unsupported)

The philological proof — the claim that Japanese words, place-names, and songs preserve Hebrew (Yamato from a name of God, Sumera Mikoto from “Samaria,” mikado from the tribe of Gad, samurai from Samaria, a folk song read as a Hebrew psalm). Weighed on its own it comes out unsupported: it is the same sound-alike method that gave British Israelism “Saxon” = “Isaac’s sons,” and it fails the elementary test of historical linguistics — regular, systematic sound-correspondences between the languages — that alone can establish kinship. Japanese and Hebrew belong to unrelated families, the kana writing invoked in some claims dates only to the ninth century AD, and the matches are chosen after the fact.15
