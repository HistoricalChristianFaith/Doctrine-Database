The medieval reception: the Acts of Pilate, Nero’s magicians, and the flying sorcerers

Dates: c. AD 400 – 13th century — a reception stratum. Its earliest members are the Christian apocryphal Acts (the Gospel of Nicodemus / Acts of Pilate, Greek form c. 4th–5th c.; the Acts of Peter and Paul, c. 5th–6th c.); its Jewish strand runs through the medieval midrashim and the Zohar (c. 13th c.); the Latin West carries the pair, as “Jamnes and Mambres,” into the age’s necromantic imagination.
Summary: Long after the Fathers had finished asking where Paul got the names, the two magicians went on living in the church’s apocryphal tales and the synagogue’s legends. Christian writers press them into new service — Nicodemus warns Pilate not to treat Jesus as Pharaoh treated Moses, and the apostles warn Nero that Simon Magus will end as Jannes and Jambres did. Jewish tradition, meanwhile, keeps embroidering their fate: they join the mixed multitude, instigate the golden calf, and finally — in the Zohar — fly into the air to escape the avenging sword only to be brought down and slain by the power of the Ineffable Name. And in the Latin West the pair, now fixed as “Jamnes and Mambres,” become bywords of the master necromancer, their book copied out at the edge of Anglo-Saxon England and their legend feeding, in the long run, the Faust saga.

Claims & evidence

The Gospel of Nicodemus (Acts of Pilate): a test of divine origin (Greek form c. 4th–5th c.)

The most widely read of the Christian apocrypha to name the pair is the Gospel of Nicodemus, also called the Acts of Pilate — a narrative of Jesus’ trial and descent to Hades that circulated across the medieval West in Latin and the vernaculars. In its fifth chapter Nicodemus rises in the council to plead for Jesus, and reaches for Pharaoh’s magicians to make his case:

Just as happened also when God sent Moses into Egypt, and Pharaoh king of Egypt told him to do a miracle, and he did it. Then Pharaoh had also two magicians, Jannes and Jambres; and they also did miracles by the use of magic art, but not such as Moses did.

— Gospel of Nicodemus (First Greek Form) 5, in the Roberts–Donaldson translation. Nicodemus’ argument is a test of origin: what is truly from God endures, and what is mere magic collapses — the same principle Gamaliel states in Acts 5:38–39. He applies it to Jesus (“if indeed what he does be from God, it will stand; but if from man, it will be destroyed”). The move turns the magicians into a fixed exemplum: they are the standing case of counterfeit power that mimics God’s messenger and then fails — exactly the reading the Fathers drew from 2 Timothy 3:8–9. That a Greek Christian narrative could name “Jannes and Jambres” without a word of explanation shows how thoroughly the names had entered common Christian memory.1

The Acts of Peter and Paul: as they deceived Pharaoh, so Simon Magus deceives Nero (c. 5th–6th c.)

A second apocryphon puts the pair to sharper polemical use. In the Acts of the Holy Apostles Peter and Paul (the Pseudo-Marcellus Passion), Peter and Paul stand before Nero to unmask the magician Simon Magus, who has won the emperor’s favour. Paul warns the court by the precedent of Exodus:

As the Egyptians Jannes and Jambres led Pharaoh and his army astray until they were swallowed up in the sea, so also he, through the instruction of his father the devil, persuades men to do many evils to themselves.

— Acts of Peter and Paul 34 (Ante-Nicene Fathers translation). Two things are worth marking. First, the apocryphon draws the same line the Apostolic Constitutions drew — Jannes and Jambres as the Old-Testament type of the New-Testament deceiver — only here the antitype is not Annas and Caiaphas but Simon Magus, the arch-sorcerer of Christian legend. Second, and by a striking accident of imagery, the story ends with Simon flying over Rome and then crashing to his death at Peter’s prayer — the very motif Jewish legend would attach to Jannes and Jambres themselves (below). The magician who takes to the air and is brought down became a shared folk-image of the sorcerer overreached.2

The Jewish legends of their fate: mixed multitude, golden calf, and the flight from Phinehas’ sword

While Christian writers used the pair as an exemplum, Jewish tradition went on filling in their biography — above all its ending, which the sources give in strikingly different ways. The medieval midrash Yelammedenu counts them “among the mixed multitude that went up with Israel from Egypt” and has them aid in the making of the golden calf; other strands make them proselytes who left Egypt at the Exodus, or the sons of Balaam (the “two youths” of Numbers 22:22). Their death is told at least three ways: they were drowned in the Red Sea; or put to death in the slaughter after the golden calf; or — the most vivid version, in the Zohar — they escaped by sorcery only to be caught by the divine Name:

They flew up into the air before the sword of Phinehas and made themselves invisible, until, by the power of the Ineffable Name, they were caught and slain.

— Zohar, Balak 194 (cf. Targum Pseudo-Jonathan to Numbers 31:8). The Talmud preserves a further scrap of their sparring with Moses: when he worked his first signs, “Jannes and Jambres” scoffed that he was bringing magic into Egypt of all places — “Dost thou wish to introduce magic into Egypt?” (Menaḥot 85a), Egypt being the very home of the art. These divergent fate-legends — convert or reprobate, drowned or burned or struck down in mid-air — are not harmonised here; how legend disposed of the two magicians is one of the genuinely contested questions this tradition leaves open.3

The Latin West: “Jamnes and Mambres” and the necromancer’s book

In the Latin-speaking church the second magician’s name settled into the form Mambres (whence the couplet “Jamnes and Mambres”), and it is under that name that the pair passed into the medieval literature of magic. The lost Book of Jannes and Jambres was still being copied at the edge of Anglo-Saxon England: its necromancy scene — Jambres raising his dead brother’s shade — survives on an eleventh-century Latin-and-Old-English leaf (London, British Library, Cotton Tiberius B.v), and the legend, in Albert Pietersma’s summary of its reach, was told “from the Syriac east to the Latin west and from Egypt’s deserts to King Alfred’s court,” standing in the long run as a backdrop to the Faust saga of European literature. So the two figures the Torah never named ended the Middle Ages as archetypes of the learned sorcerer who commands the dead — the dark counterpart, on this timeline, to Balaam the pagan seer overruled to bless.4

Notes

  • Why this is reception, not evidence for the men. None of these witnesses adds historical knowledge of Pharaoh’s magicians; each shows what later ages did with a pair of famous names — a Christian courtroom exemplum, an anti-Simon-Magus polemic, a Jewish martyrology-in-reverse, a necromancer’s pedigree. The block is placed near the end of the timeline for that reason: it is the afterlife of the legend, dated by its earliest members (c. 4th–5th c.), not a fresh source for the Exodus contest.
  • The flying magician. That the Zohar has Jannes and Jambres fly and fall, while the Acts of Peter and Paul has Simon Magus fly and fall, is not a borrowing in either direction but a shared folk-image: the sorcerer whose art lifts him above the crowd until a higher power brings him down. It marks how completely the pair had become the type of the master magician in both traditions.
  • The fate question is open. The contradictory endings — conversion, Red Sea, golden-calf slaughter, the flight from Phinehas — cannot all be true; they are the raw material of the “how did legend dispose of them?” question, weighed on its own rather than settled in this survey.
