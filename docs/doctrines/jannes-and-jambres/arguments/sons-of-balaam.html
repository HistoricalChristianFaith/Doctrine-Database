Were Jannes and Jambres the sons of Balaam?

Disputed reading · on Who Were Jannes and Jambres? · witnesses: the Targums, the Book of Jannes and Jambres, the medieval reception

Related readings: Where do the names “Jannes and Jambres” come from? · What became of Jannes and Jambres, according to the traditions about them? · Did a “book of Balaam” ever exist?

The passage

But the anger of the Lord was provoked, because he would go (that he might) curse them; and the angel of the Lord stood in the way to be an adversary to him. But he sat upon his ass, and his two young men, Jannes and Jambres, were with him.

— Targum Pseudo-Jonathan, Numbers 22:22 (J. W. Etheridge’s translation), source.1

And when they had gone Pharaoh sent for Balaam the magician and to Jannes and Jambres his sons, and to all the magicians and conjurors and counsellors which belonged to the king, and they all came and sat before the king.

— Sefer ha-Yashar (the mediaeval “Book of Jasher”), ch. 79, source.2

The question

Several Jewish sources join Pharaoh’s magicians to Balaam son of Beor, the seer hired to curse Israel in Numbers 22–24: as his attendants, or as his sons. The question is whether the two magicians belonged to Balaam’s house, as these sources tell it, or whether the kinship is a later joining of two traditions as the legend grew. It bears on how the legend of Jannes and Jambres took shape and on how the Exodus and Balaam stories were read together. The Targum’s wording is itself read in two ways: as Balaam’s “two youths” or servants by Kaufmann Kohler (Jewish Encyclopedia, 1906), and as a witness to the magicians as “the sons of Balaam” by the Encyclopaedia Judaica.3

Balaam’s sons and household

In this reading, as the sources that tell it have it, the two magicians belong to Balaam’s own house: his “young men” at his side in the Targum, his sons in the apocryphon and Sefer ha-Yashar.

Held by: Targum Pseudo-Jonathan (Numbers 22:22); the Book of Jannes and Jambres (P. Michigan fragments, composed c. 1st–3rd c.); Yalkut Shimoni (Exodus 168, 176); Sefer ha-Yashar (ch. 79).4

The link is attested in two streams of tradition, Aramaic and Greek. Targum Pseudo-Jonathan sets Jannes and Jambres at Balaam’s side on the road to curse Israel, and the Book of Jannes and Jambres, which Albert Pietersma dates “perhaps as early as the first century C.E.,” preserves in two P. Michigan fragments part of a genealogy that makes them Balaam’s sons. The Encyclopaedia Judaica lists the Targum and the Yalkut together as sources naming them “the sons of Balaam.” The pairing joins the Pentateuch’s two episodes of foreign magic set against God’s messenger, the rod-contest before Pharaoh and the hired curse at the edge of Canaan, into one family of sorcerers. Sefer ha-Yashar tells the story with Balaam himself at Pharaoh’s court: Pharaoh “sent for Balaam the magician and to Jannes and Jambres his sons,” and “Balaam the son of Beor the magician answered the king.” In that telling father and sons stand together at Pharaoh’s court. Louis Ginzberg’s Legends of the Jews gathers these strands into one narrative of Moses in Egypt. On this reading the genealogy gathers into one house the opponents whom Israel met at the Exodus and at the end of the wilderness journey.5

A later linking of two traditions

In this reading the magicians and Balaam were brought together as the legend grew, and the kinship belongs to its later layers.

Held by: A. J. Maclean (Hastings’ Dictionary of the Bible, 1909); the International Standard Bible Encyclopedia (1915).6

The ISBE describes the Jewish traditions about the pair as “full of contradictions and impossibilities and anachronisms,” and notes that the sons of Balaam “were said to have withstood Moses 40 years previously at the court of Pharaoh”; Maclean writes that “Later Jewish fancy ran wild on these names; according to some they were Balaam’s sons.” Kohler renders the Targum’s word as “two youths” (Aramaic ‘ulemoi), the word the King James Version translates “his two servants” in the Hebrew of Numbers 22:22. The earliest sources that name the magicians have no Balaam connection: the Damascus Document (c. 100 BC), 2 Timothy, Pliny, Apuleius, Numenius and the Fathers who asked where Paul found the names. Among the Targums, only Pseudo-Jonathan names the pair; Onkelos keeps the anonymous magicians of the Hebrew. The Talmud’s “Yoḥana and Mamre” (Menaḥot 85a) oppose Moses with no paternity given. In the biblical narrative the magicians are grown men at the Exodus, and Balaam’s journey comes near the end of the forty years in the wilderness, from “Aram, out of the mountains of the east”; Sefer ha-Yashar places Balaam himself in Egypt at Pharaoh’s court. On this reading the pairing records how storytellers drew the Torah’s two great foreign opponents of Moses and Israel into one circle, first as master and attendants, then as father and sons.7
