What became of Jannes and Jambres? Converts, drowned, or slain at the calf?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: weak as history. The tradition gives the pair at least four incompatible ends — they repented and turned proselyte; they drowned with Pharaoh’s host in the Red Sea; they instigated the golden calf and were slain in its aftermath; they flew up before Phinehas’ sword and were struck down by the Ineffable Name — and the endings cannot all be true. Each is late, extra-biblical haggadah with no independent line back to the event, and the one datum Scripture supplies (2 Timothy 3:9) fixes only their exposure, not any death. The earliest and most distinctive strand — the penitence that titles the lost apocryphon — is honestly reported as the oldest, but it too is legend, not recovered fact.

The claim

But they shall proceed no further: for their folly shall be manifest unto all men, as theirs also was.

— 2 Timothy 3:9 (KJV). This is the whole of what the canonical text says about the outcome: the magicians’ folly was made manifest — their sham was exposed, exactly as it is in Exodus 8:18–19, where their art fails at the lice and they confess “the finger of God.” Paul asserts failure, not a fate. Yet the later tradition could not leave two such famous villains without an ending, and it supplied several — incompatible ones. This page catalogues the rival fates, steelmans each in turn, and asks whether any has a claim on history, or whether all are downstream legend.1

Who advances it

No single proponent advances “the fate” of Jannes and Jambres; rather, distinct streams of Jewish and Christian legend each supply a different ending, and modern reference works simply gather them. The Jewish Encyclopedia (1906) and the Encyclopaedia Judaica note flatly that “the traditions of their death are… given in a varying fashion” — drowned in the Red Sea, put to death after the golden calf, or slain by the power of the Name. The conversion strand is oldest, carried by the lost Book of Jannes and Jambres — known to the Latin West precisely as the Paenitentia Iamnis et Mambre, “the Penitence of Jannes and Mambres” — and edited in Albert Pietersma’s Apocryphon of Jannes and Jambres the Magicians (Brill, 1994). The golden-calf strand is midrashic (Tanhuma and Yelammedenu, Ki Tissa); the flight-and-slaying is the Zohar’s (Balak 194). None of these knows, or answers, the others.2

The rival fates, steelmanned

(1) Repentance and conversion — the oldest strand. The single most distinctive ending is that one or both magicians repented. It is the earliest we can date: the apocryphon composed in Roman Egypt (perhaps as early as the first century) is remembered by its very title as the “Penitence,” and in Pietersma’s reconstruction Jannes falls gravely ill and confesses that “Moses’s power is of divine origin” before he dies. The instinct is not arbitrary: it is the natural extension of the magicians’ own words in Exodus 8:19 (“This is the finger of God”), and a Latin Father, Ambrosiaster, independently knew the plot — that the magicians “became humble and confessed” the God of Moses. A related rabbinic strand makes them (or the group they belong to) proselytes among the mixed multitude who left Egypt at the Exodus. As the oldest and best-attested strand this has the strongest claim to represent what the earliest tellers said — but note carefully what that establishes: it is evidence about the tradition, not about two historical sorcerers.3

(2) Death in the Red Sea. A second strand drowns them with Pharaoh’s army. The Acts of Peter and Paul has Paul warn Nero that “the Egyptians Jannes and Jambres led Pharaoh and his army astray until they were swallowed up in the sea” — making the pair perish in the very catastrophe they engineered. This ending has a certain narrative economy: it ties the magicians’ doom to the great judgement of Exodus 14 and reads their exposure in 2 Timothy as issuing, at last, in destruction. Notably it stands in tension with strand (1): the apocryphon itself has the host drown while Jambres, warned by the dying Jannes, survives the sea — so even the “drowning” motif is told inconsistently within the tradition.4

(3) Instigators of the golden calf, slain in its aftermath. A third strand keeps them alive past the sea to make them ringleaders of Israel’s worst apostasy. The Midrash Tanhuma and Yelammedenu (Ki Tissa) count them “among the mixed multitude that went up with Israel from Egypt” and have them aid — some tellings, incite — the making of the calf (Exodus 32); the death-traditions then have them “put to death after the making of the golden calf, or during the slaughter connected with it” (the Levites’ purge of Exodus 32:26–28). This version has the appeal of moral symmetry — the men who counterfeited signs against Moses end by seducing Israel into a counterfeit god, and fall under the same judgement — and it links the pair to the “mixed multitude” that Jewish tradition blamed for the calf. But it is flatly incompatible with both strand (1) (converts) and strand (2) (drowned at the sea, before Sinai).5

(4) The flight from Phinehas’ sword. The most vivid ending is the Zohar’s. There the pair — now folded into the story of Balaam, whose sons or servants the later tradition made them — “flew up into the air before the sword of Phinehas and made themselves invisible, until, by the power of the Ineffable Name, they were caught and slain” (Zohar, Balak 194; cf. Targum Pseudo-Jonathan to Numbers 31:8). This places their death not at the Exodus at all but forty years later, in the Midianite war in which Balaam falls — a placement that only works once the “sons of Balaam” identification is in force, and it shares the “flying sorcerer brought down” folk-image that Christian legend independently attached to Simon Magus. It is the latest of the four and the most obviously mythographic.6

Adversarial assessment

What holds. Two modest things. First, that the tradition did supply the magicians with these fates, and that the penitence strand is the earliest datable of them, is well-grounded — it is documented in the apocryphon’s title, its reconstructed plot, and Ambrosiaster’s independent knowledge of that plot. Second, every strand honestly develops something latent in the biblical text: the confession of Exodus 8:19 (strand 1), the drowning of Exodus 14 (strand 2), the calf and its purge (strand 3), and Balaam’s end (strand 4). As history of the legend, the material is real and datable.

What is overstated or unprovable. But if the question is which fate actually befell the magicians, three defeaters converge:

(1) The endings are mutually exclusive. One cannot both repent-and-live and drown at the sea; one cannot both drown at the sea (before Sinai) and be slain at the golden calf (after it), still less survive to fly before Phinehas’ sword forty years on. These are not variant details of one event but rival plots. Their sheer irreconcilability is itself the strongest sign that none is a memory: a preserved fact does not fork four ways.

(2) Every strand is late and downstream. The earliest, the Penitence, is a first-century-at-soonest apocryphon — already a century past the events’ supposed date and dependent on the naming tradition that Exodus itself lacks; the calf and flight strands are medieval midrash and the thirteenth-century Zohar. None draws on a source earlier than the legend it elaborates. The chronology, too, runs against the later strands: they require the magicians to be grown sorcerers at the Exodus and still active at the golden calf — and, in strand (4), alive at the Midianite war forty years later — the same wilderness-chronology strain that sinks the sons-of-Balaam reading.

(3) Scripture supplies no death at all. 2 Timothy 3:9 commits only to manifestation — “their folly shall be manifest unto all men” — and Exodus stops at the confession of the finger of God. The concrete deaths are precisely the blank that later piety rushed to fill, in incompatible ways. That the fillings disagree is not a puzzle to be harmonised but the expected signature of haggadic elaboration around a canonical silence.

Granting a genuine miracle-contest behind Exodus does not change this: even if one takes the rod-and-plague duel as real history (this wiki does not discount a claim merely because it requires the supernatural), that concession touches the contest, not the magicians’ deaths — on which the primary text is silent and the secondary tradition self-contradictory. Possibility is not proof, and here the evidence for any specific end is late, dependent, and divided against itself.7

Assessment

Weak — as a recovery of what actually became of Pharaoh’s magicians. The four endings the tradition offers (repentance and conversion; drowning in the Red Sea; instigation of the golden calf and death in its purge; flight before Phinehas’ sword and slaying by the Ineffable Name) are mutually exclusive, each late and downstream of the legend it elaborates, and none anchored in a source that reaches the event; the canonical text fixes only that their “folly was manifest,” not any death. The honest positive finding is narrower and worth stating plainly: the penitence strand is the earliest and most distinctive of the four — it titles the lost apocryphon, a Latin Father knew its plot, and it grows directly from the magicians’ own “finger of God” in Exodus 8:19 — so if one asks not “what happened?” but “what did the tradition first say happened?” the answer is a story of repentance, not of drowning or slaughter. That is a fact about the tradition’s development, not about two historical men. The full witness detail lives on the Book and medieval-reception pages; this page weighs whether any of the endings they record has a claim on history — and finds none does.
