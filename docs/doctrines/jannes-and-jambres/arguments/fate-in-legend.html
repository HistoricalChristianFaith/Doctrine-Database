What became of Jannes and Jambres, according to the traditions about them?

Disputed reading · on Who Were Jannes and Jambres? · witnesses: the Book of Jannes and Jambres, Ambrosiaster, the medieval reception

Related readings: Were Jannes and Jambres the sons of Balaam? · Did 2 Timothy 3:8 take the names from the Book of Jannes and Jambres? · Where do the names “Jannes and Jambres” come from?

The passage

But they shall proceed no further: for their folly shall be manifest unto all men, as theirs also was.

— 2 Timothy 3:9 (KJV), source.1

The question

Exodus last mentions the magicians when their art fails at the plague of lice and they say to Pharaoh, “This is the finger of God”; 2 Timothy says that their folly was made manifest. Neither narrates their end. Later Jewish and Christian sources give them several different endings, and the International Standard Bible Encyclopedia (1915) notes that “the traditions of their death are also given in a varying fashion.” The question is what each tradition held became of the pair, and how the endings relate to one another. It bears on how the legend grew and on how each community used the figures. The four endings are set out below in the order of their earliest datable witness.2

Repentance and confession

In this telling the magicians, or at least Jannes, came to confess the God of Moses.

Held by: the Book of Jannes and Jambres (Paenitentia Iamnis et Mambre, composed c. 1st–3rd c.); Ambrosiaster (Commentary on 2 Timothy, c. 384).3

The apocryphon was known in the Latin West as the Penitence of Jannes and Mambres. In Albert Pietersma’s reconstruction Jannes falls gravely ill after the contest and confesses that “Moses’s power is of divine origin” before he dies. Ambrosiaster tells the same turn: “when the virtue of Moses grew in his works, they became humble and confessed with sorrow the God who worked in Moses’ wounds.” In Exodus 8:19 the magicians themselves tell Pharaoh, “This is the finger of God.” A related tradition, reported by the ISBE, holds that they “became proselytes” and “left Egypt at the Exodus, among the mixed multitude.” On this telling the story of Pharaoh’s sorcerers ends in acknowledgement of the God they had opposed.

Drowned in the Red Sea

In this telling the magicians perished with Pharaoh’s army in the sea.

Held by: Jewish tradition reported by A. J. Maclean (1909) and the ISBE (1915); the Acts of the Holy Apostles Peter and Paul 34 (c. 5th–6th c.).4

In the Acts of Peter and Paul Paul warns Nero against Simon Magus: “As the Egyptians Jannes and Jambres led Pharaoh and his army astray until they were swallowed up in the sea.” Hastings’ Dictionary of the Apostolic Church (1916) takes the drowning as Pharaoh’s, “who was drowned in the Red Sea through listening to Jannes and Jambres.” A. J. Maclean reports Jewish tradition that “they were drowned in the Red Sea,” and the ISBE that “they were said to have been drowned in the Red Sea.” This ending ties their fate to the judgement of Exodus 14, the destruction of the army they had served. In Pietersma’s reconstruction of the apocryphon the host drowns while Jambres, warned by the dying Jannes, survives. On this telling the men who deceived Pharaoh shared the end of his host.5

Instigators of the golden calf, put to death after it

In this telling the magicians left Egypt with Israel and led the people into the worship of the golden calf.

Held by: Midrash Tanhuma, Ki Tissa 19; Midrash Yelammedenu, Ki Tissa.6

Kaufmann Kohler reports that according to Yelammedenu “they were among ‘the mixed multitude that went up with Israel from Egypt’ (Ex. xii. 38) and aided in the making of the golden calf,” and the Encyclopaedia Judaica cites Tanhuma for their part in the calf “after joining the mixed multitude.” The ISBE reports that “they are reported to have instigated Aaron to make the golden calf” and were “put to death after the making of the golden calf”; Maclean, that they were put to death “for inciting Aaron to make the Golden Calf.” This ending links the pair to the mixed multitude that Jewish tradition associated with the calf, and to the Levites’ purge in Exodus 32:26–28. On this telling the men who counterfeited signs against Moses in Egypt went on to lead Israel to a counterfeit god in the wilderness.7

The flight before Phinehas

In this telling the magicians fled through the air from Phinehas’s sword and were brought down by the divine Name.

Held by: the Zohar, Balak 194 (c. 13th c.); cf. Targum Pseudo-Jonathan on Numbers 31:8.8

The Zohar tells that “they flew up into the air before the sword of Phinehas and made themselves invisible, until, by the power of the Ineffable Name, they were caught and slain.” The setting is the war against Midian in Numbers 31, in which Balaam is killed; other sources make the magicians Balaam’s sons or attendants (see “Were Jannes and Jambres the sons of Balaam?”). The ISBE reports a death “during the slaughter connected with the name of Phinehas.” The image of a sorcerer who takes to the air and is brought down by a higher power is also told of Simon Magus in the Acts of Peter and Paul. On this telling the magicians’ career ends where Balaam’s does, at the close of the wilderness years.9
