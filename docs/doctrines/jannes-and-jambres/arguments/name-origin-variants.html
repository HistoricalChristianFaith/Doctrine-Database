Where do the names “Jannes and Jambres” come from — and why does Jambres become Mambres?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: plausible — that the names are a genuine, pre-Christian Jewish onomastic tradition and not a late or arbitrary invention holds up (sound on that core: the pair is named a century and a half before 2 Timothy, and the Western “Mambres” is a regular Jambres → Mambres shift); but the specific etymologies (Jannes ~ Yoḥana/John; Jambres ~ Aramaic “to oppose”/mamre) remain contested, with a rival Egyptian derivation still argued and the earliest sources naming only one of the pair · the reading surveyed in modern scholarship (Pietersma; the DDD; the reference-work tradition)

The claim

JANNES AND JAMBRES (more correctly Mambres… also… Yoḥanai and Mamre): Names of two legendary wizards of Pharaoh “who withstood Moses” (II Tim. iii. 8)…

— Jewish Encyclopedia (1906), “Jannes and Jambres.” The interpretation weighed here is the mainstream one behind that entry: the names of Pharaoh’s magicians belong to a real, early, Semitic-Jewish onomastic tradition — forms with regular by-development and a knowable sense (“Yoḥana,” a by-form of John; “Mamre/Mambres,” the opposer or rebel) — from which Paul, in 2 Timothy 3:8, simply cites a pair his readers already knew, rather than coining or borrowing them at random. The Latin West’s Mambres for Jambres is a variant within that tradition, not evidence against it.1

Who advances it

This is the settled reading of the philologists and reference works rather than any single popular proponent. Albert Pietersma’s edition of the lost Book of Jannes and Jambres (Brill, 1994) and his and R. T. Lutz’s entry in the Dictionary of Deities and Demons in the Bible set out the Semitic onomastics; the Anchor Bible Dictionary, the Jewish Encyclopedia (1906), and Encyclopaedia Judaica carry the same account, alongside the older Egyptian-derivation line of Heinrich Ewald, F. J. Lauth, and Jacob Freudenthal. Behind them stand the primary attestations themselves — the Greek of 2 Timothy, the Hebrew of the Damascus Document, the Aramaic Targum Pseudo-Jonathan, the Latin of Pliny, Apuleius, the Vulgate, and the Gelasian Decree, and the rabbinic tradition — the full spread of which is gathered on this page’s modern-scholarship survey.2

The evidence for

(1) The tradition is demonstrably early and Jewish. The names are not a Christian or a late invention. The Damascus Document (CD V.17–19), composed about 100 BC, already names one of the pair — “Belial raised up Yannes and his brother” — a century and a half before 2 Timothy, and in the same dualistic key. By the time Paul writes he can drop the pair into a letter with no gloss, as common knowledge; and within a generation the pagan Roman naturalist Pliny and, a little later, Apuleius cite “Jannes” as a byword among the mages. A name attested across Qumran Hebrew, Pauline Greek, and Latin literature inside roughly two centuries is a living tradition, not an ad hoc coinage.

(2) The first name has a stable Semitic form and a plausible sense. “Jannes” renders a Hebrew/Aramaic Yoḥana (the Damascus Document’s yḥnh; the Talmud’s “Yoḥana”) — a recognised by-form of Yoḥanan/John — and that same root shows through every language the name travels in (Greek Iannes, Latin Iannes/Iohannes). Its persistence across scripts is exactly what a real onomastic tradition, rather than a random label, would produce.

(3) The second name’s variation is regular, not chaotic. The Latin West writes Mambres for Jambres — the Vulgate manuscripts of 2 Timothy 3:8, the Gelasian Decree’s title “Liber qui appellatur Paenitentia Iamne et Mambre,” Ambrosiaster, and the whole mediaeval “Jamnes and Mambres” necromancer legend. An initial y-/j- shifting to m- is a known nasal interchange, and the rabbinic pairing “Yoḥana and Mamre” (Menaḥot 85a) shows the Mamre form already inside the Jewish tradition — so Jambres and Mambres are two developments of one name, not two names. Read this way, the proposed sense “the opposer / the rebel” (Aramaic root “to oppose,” participle mamre) fits the figure the tradition made him, standing against Moses.3

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The load-bearing claim — that “Jannes and Jambres” comes from a real, pre-Christian, Semitic-Jewish tradition that Paul cites rather than invents — is on firm ground. The pre-Pauline Damascus Document naming settles the dating; the cross-lingual stability of the first name and the regular Jambres → Mambres alternation settle that we are dealing with the transmission of fixed names, not a scatter of guesses. On that core the reading is sound.

What is contested or overstated. Three qualifications keep the fuller claim — “stable names with regular linguistic development and a knowable meaning” — from rising to probable:

(1) The specific etymologies are not settled. The Yoḥana/John derivation of “Jannes” is widely accepted but is an identification of form, not a demonstration of what the name-giver meant; the “he who deceives” sense sometimes attached to it is a guess. For “Jambres”/“Mambres” the “to oppose / rebel” reading is attractive precisely because it fits the story — which is also its weakness: a meaning read back from a figure’s role can be folk-etymology rather than the name’s origin. The reference works themselves catalogue rival and “equally untenable” proposals (Steiner, Geiger, Jastrow, Levy), which is the mark of an unresolved question.

(2) A live rival: an Egyptian derivation. A serious line (Ewald, Lauth, Freudenthal) has argued the names are Egyptian, fitting the pair’s Egyptian setting rather than a Hebrew root. This is a minority view and is weakest for the first name (which clusters so tightly on the Yoḥanan/John root across languages), but it is not refuted, and it keeps the etymological question genuinely open — the more so for the mobile second name.

(3) The earliest sources name only one. The Damascus Document gives “Yannes and his brother”; Pliny and Apuleius give “Jannes” alone. The fixed couplet “Jannes-and-Jambres” is therefore itself a development — the anonymous brother was named later — which both supports the “living, growing tradition” picture and cautions against treating the second name as primitive. It is exactly the second, later-crystallised name that is textually the most unstable (Jambres / Jamberes / Mambres), and whose etymology is least secure.4

Assessment

Plausible — with its core claim sound. That the names of Pharaoh’s magicians descend from a genuine, pre-Christian Jewish onomastic tradition — a tradition old enough that Paul cites it without explanation and wide enough to reach pagan Rome — is well-grounded: the Damascus Document’s pre-Pauline naming and the cross-lingual stability of “Jannes” put that beyond serious doubt, and the Latin Mambres is a regular variant of Jambres, not a second name. What does not reach probability is the stronger form of the claim — that the names have a settled, regular etymology with a known meaning. The Yoḥana/John identification of the first name is likely but is a matching of form rather than a proof of sense; the “opposer / rebel” reading of the second is attractive largely because it suits the role, a live rival Egyptian derivation remains unrefuted, and the earliest witnesses name only one magician — so the fixed couplet, and the second name’s etymology, are themselves late and unstable. The honest verdict, then, is split: sound that the tradition is early, Jewish, and non-arbitrary; contested on where exactly the names come from and what they mean.
