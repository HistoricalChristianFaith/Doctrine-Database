Where do the names “Jannes and Jambres” come from, and why does Jambres also appear as Mambres?

Disputed reading · on Who Were Jannes and Jambres? · witnesses: the Damascus Document, the pagan writers, the Targums, modern scholarship

Related readings: Were Jannes and Jambres the sons of Balaam? · Did 2 Timothy 3:8 take the names from the Book of Jannes and Jambres? · What became of Jannes and Jambres, according to the traditions about them?

The passage

Now as Jannes and Jambres withstood Moses, so do these also resist the truth: men of corrupt minds, reprobate concerning the faith.

— 2 Timothy 3:8 (KJV), source.1

For in times past Moses and Aaron stood in the power of the Prince of Lights and Belial raised up Yannes and his brother in his cunning when seeking to do evil to Israel the first time.

— Damascus Document, col. V, lines 17–19 (c. 100 BC), Edward Cook’s translation, source.2

The question

Exodus never names Pharaoh’s magicians. The Damascus Document names one, “Yannes and his brother” (Hebrew yḥnh); 2 Timothy names both; Pliny and Apuleius name Jannes alone; the Babylonian Talmud gives “Yoḥana” and “Mamre”; and Latin sources and some manuscripts write “Mambres” for Jambres.3 The question is what language and what meaning the names come from, and how the forms Jambres and Mambres are related. It bears on how old the legend is and where it took shape. Kaufmann Kohler’s Jewish Encyclopedia article (1906) reports that “various etymologies have been proposed,” and the two main lines are set out below.

Names of Egyptian origin

In this reading the names of the two Egyptian magicians come from the Egyptian language.

Held by: Heinrich Ewald (Geschichte); F. J. Lauth (Moses der Hebräer); Jacob Freudenthal (Alexander Polyhistor, 1875) ⚠ unverified.4

Kohler records that Ewald, Lauth and Freudenthal “believe them to have been derived from the Egyptian.” The setting supports looking to Egypt. Exodus calls the pair ḥarṭummîm, a word the International Standard Bible Encyclopedia (1915) explains as “sacred scribes who were skilled in the sacred writing, that is in the hieroglyphics; they were a variety of Egyptian priests.” The earliest pagan writer to tell their story, the second-century Platonist Numenius, calls them “Egyptian sacred scribes” (hierogrammateis), and Freudenthal traced Numenius’s account back to Artapanus, a Jewish historian writing in Egypt. On this reading a legend about Egyptian temple-scribes, told in Egypt, carries names taken from Egypt, and the Greek, Hebrew and Latin forms are adaptations of those names into each language.5

Names from a Hebrew and Aramaic tradition

In this reading the names come from Jewish Hebrew and Aramaic usage: Jannes from a form of Yoḥana/John, and Jambres or Mambres from a root meaning “to oppose” or “to rebel.”

Held by: Kaufmann Kohler (Jewish Encyclopedia, 1906); A. J. Maclean (Hastings’ Dictionary of the Bible, 1909); the International Standard Bible Encyclopedia (1915); A. Pietersma and R. T. Lutz (Dictionary of Deities and Demons in the Bible, 1999) ⚠ unverified.6

Maclean puts it most directly: “‘Jannes’ is probably a corruption of ‘Johannes’ (John); ‘Jambres’ is almost certainly derived from a Semitic root meaning ‘to oppose’ (imperfect tense), the participle of which would give ‘Mambres.’” The ISBE gives the meaning of either form as “he who opposes.” Kohler identifies “Mambre” with a Hebrew word for “the rebel,” following Levy, and tentatively connects “Jannes” with the name Yoḥane, borne in Talmudic tradition by a dreaded sorceress and given to Keturah. The first name holds steady across languages: Hebrew yḥnh in the Damascus Document, Greek Iannes, Pliny’s Ianne, Apuleius’s Iohannes and the Talmud’s Yoḥana. On this reading Jambres and Mambres are two forms of one name, an imperfect and a participle of the same verb, and the Talmud’s “Mamre” shows the m-form already inside Jewish tradition. The tradition is Jewish and early. The Damascus Document names Yannes about 100 BC, 2 Timothy uses the pair without explanation, and the names reached pagan Latin writers within a generation. On this reading the earliest witnesses name only the first magician because his name was fixed first, and the second, “the opposer,” was attached to “his brother” afterwards.7
