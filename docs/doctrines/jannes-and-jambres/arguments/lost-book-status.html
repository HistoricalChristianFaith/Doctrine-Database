Did 2 Timothy 3:8 take the names from the lost Book of Jannes and Jambres?

Disputed reading · on Who Were Jannes and Jambres? · witnesses: the Book of Jannes and Jambres, the Fathers on Paul’s source

Related readings: Where do the names “Jannes and Jambres” come from? · What became of Jannes and Jambres, according to the traditions about them? · Did a “book of Balaam” ever exist?

The passage

Now as Jannes and Jambres withstood Moses, so do these also resist the truth: men of corrupt minds, reprobate concerning the faith.

— 2 Timothy 3:8 (KJV), source.1

“As Jannes and Mambres resisted Moses” is not found in the public books, but in the secret book which is entitled the Book of Jannes and Mambres.

— Origen, Commentary on Matthew, Commentariorum Series (on Matt 27:9), c. 250; see the Book detail page.2

The question

Exodus leaves Pharaoh’s magicians unnamed, and 2 Timothy names them without explanation. An apocryphon about the pair, the Book of Jannes and Jambres, was composed in Greek, and Albert Pietersma dates it “perhaps as early as the first century C.E.” The question is whether the letter drew the names from that written book or from the wider Jewish tradition about the magicians (reference works cite the Damascus Document’s “Yannes and his brother,” c. 100 BC, as an early witness to it), or, as John Chrysostom allowed, by inspiration. It bears on the date and reach of the book, and on how early Christian readers understood the letter’s use of material outside the Old Testament. The book’s standing is a separate matter: no surviving canon list includes it, and the Gelasian Decree (later fifth or sixth century) lists the “Paenitentia Iamne et Mambre” among the apocrypha.3

Taken from the written apocryphon

In this reading the letter took the names from the Book of Jannes and Jambres itself.

Held by: Origen (Commentary on Matthew, c. 250); Ambrosiaster (Commentary on 2 Timothy, c. 384); J. H. Charlesworth and D.-A. Koch (allow possible dependence; do not assert it) ⚠ unverified.4

Origen wrote about two centuries after the letter, in the Greek-Egyptian world where the book was composed and copied. When he could not find the phrase in the canonical books, he named its source: “the secret book which is entitled the Book of Jannes and Mambres.” He read other apostolic citations the same way, assigning 1 Corinthians 2:9 and the quotation in Matthew 27:9 to secret books. Ambrosiaster states that “this example is from the apocrypha,” and his summary of the story, that the magicians “became humble and confessed with sorrow the God who worked in Moses’ wounds,” matches the penitence that gives the apocryphon its Latin title, Paenitentia. Aquinas, noting that “in Exodus those magicians were not named, but here they are,” concludes that “perhaps he obtained their names from certain Jewish writings.” A book devoted to exactly these two figures existed, survives in Greek papyri from the third century on, and may date to the first century. Charlesworth and Koch are reported as allowing that 2 Timothy may depend on it. On this reading a written source with the names already fixed accounts for the letter’s naming of two men whom the Torah leaves anonymous.5

Taken from unwritten Jewish tradition, or known by inspiration

In this reading the letter’s author drew on oral Jewish tradition about the magicians, or knew their names by the Holy Spirit, and not on a particular book.

Held by: John Chrysostom (Homilies on 2 Timothy 8, c. 400); Theodore of Mopsuestia (Commentary on 2 Timothy, c. 428); Theodoret of Cyrus (Interpretation of 2 Timothy, c. 450); Theophylact of Ohrid (c. 1090).6

Chrysostom asks how the names are known when “their names are nowhere else introduced” and answers: “Either they were handed down by tradition, or it is probable that Paul knew them by inspiration.” Oecumenius and Theophylact repeat both options. Theodoret states that “Paul takes the story of Jannes and Jambres not from holy Scripture but from an unwritten Jewish tradition.” Theodore of Mopsuestia rejects the book explanation outright: “Particularly absurd is the notion that he had access to some information or writing now lost to us.” A name for one of the magicians is attested before any proposed date for the book: the Damascus Document’s “Yannes and his brother” (c. 100 BC), which reference works identify with the Exodus pair. Pliny (c. AD 77) and Apuleius (c. AD 158) use “Jannes” as a byword for a famous magician and name no book. Stephen Gero (1991) observes that “in view of the widespread occurrence of the Jannes and Jambres legend” dependence on the book “cannot be regarded as certain.” On this reading 2 Timothy names the pair as its readers already knew them, from a tradition shared by synagogue, sect and pagan writer alike.7
