The lost Book of Jannes and Jambres: did Paul quote it, and was it ever Scripture?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: two linked questions, rated apart. Did Paul (2 Timothy 3:8) draw the names from the written Book of Jannes and Jambres? — contested: real ancient testimony stands on both sides (Origen and Ambrosiaster say he did; Theodoret and Theodore of Mopsuestia say he used an unwritten tradition), and the datings are too loose to settle it. Was the book ever Scripture? — unsupported: no church canon received it, and the one catalogue that names it lists it among the rejected apocrypha — a settled negative, and the honest floor under the whole question · the descriptive detail lives on the Book of Jannes and Jambres detail page; this page adjudicates the interpretive questions

The claim

“As Jannes and Mambres resisted Moses” is not found in the public books, but in the secret book which is entitled the Book of Jannes and Mambres.

— Origen, Commentary on Matthew (on Matt 27:9), quoted and sourced on the Book detail page. The interpretation weighed here is Origen’s: that when Paul names Pharaoh’s two magicians — figures the Old Testament never names — he is not merely echoing a floating legend but citing a particular written work, the apocryphal Book of Jannes and Jambres. A second, distinct question rides on the same book and is best kept separate: given that no Old-Testament book names the pair, and that Paul may have drawn on a non-canonical source, was that book ever Scripture — did it hold, or ever come near to holding, canonical authority? The two are rated apart below: the first is genuinely contested, the second settled in the negative.1

Who advances it

The “Paul quoted the book” reading is an ancient one with a real pedigree, and the Fathers who took up 2 Timothy 3:8 divided sharply over it. Two camps formed. The written-book camp is led by Origen (c. 250), who names the liber secretus outright, and is held in the Latin West by Ambrosiaster (c. 384), who says the example is drawn “from the apocrypha” and even knows the book’s plot, and by Thomas Aquinas (c. 1270), who has Paul obtain the names “from certain Jewish writings.” The rival unwritten-tradition camp is Antiochene and Byzantine: John Chrysostom, Oecumenius, and Theophylact of Ohrid say the names came “from unwritten tradition” or by the Holy Spirit; Theodoret of Cyrus states flatly that Paul takes the story “not from holy Scripture but from an unwritten Jewish tradition”; and Theodore of Mopsuestia reaches the same conclusion by ridiculing the book-reading — “particularly absurd is the notion that [Paul] had access to some information or writing now lost to us.” The modern split runs along the same seam: J. H. Charlesworth and D.-A. Koch allow that 2 Timothy may depend on the apocryphon, while Stephen Gero cautions that, given how widespread the legend was, dependence “cannot be regarded as certain.”2

The evidence for

(1) The earliest reader who addresses the question says yes. Origen writes within roughly two centuries of Paul, in the same Greek-Egyptian milieu where the book was composed and circulated, and he had the work in front of him. When he cannot find Paul’s phrase in the canonical Scriptures, he identifies its source by name. That is not a guess about a lost book; it is a report from someone who could still read it.

(2) A real book about exactly these figures did exist, and a Latin Father knew its contents. This is no phantom source. The Book of Jannes and Jambres is edited from fragments in four languages, and its composition is dated “perhaps as early as the first century C.E.” — early enough, in principle, for Paul to have known it. Ambrosiaster does not merely repeat “from the apocrypha” as a formula: he knows the book’s story, that the magicians “became humble and confessed” that Moses’ God is real — the very “penitence” that titles the work. A Father who can summarise the plot is describing a book he (or his tradition) actually read, which makes the claim that Paul too knew it far from idle.

(3) The dependence would explain the anonymity gap. Exodus never names the magicians; Paul names them without a word of introduction. A written source into which the names were already fixed is one economical explanation of how the apostle came by a detail the Torah withholds.3

Adversarial assessment

What holds. That a written Book of Jannes and Jambres existed, that it was old, that it concerned precisely these two figures, and that a serious ancient reader (Origen) traced Paul’s phrase to it, are all sound. If the question were only “could Paul have drawn on such a book?” the answer would be yes.

What is overstated or unprovable. But Origen asserts a direct, exclusive dependence, and on that the case does not close:

(1) The names were common property well before the book, and independently of it. The Damascus Document names one of the pair (“Yannes and his brother”) about 100 BC, a century and a half before Paul and before any date proposed for the apocryphon; the pagan writers Pliny and Apuleius know “Jannes” as a stock name of famous magic. Paul could have reached for a pair of names “everybody knew” without opening any particular book — which is exactly the unwritten-tradition Fathers’ point. A widespread legend needs no single textual source.

(2) The book may be no older than Paul — or younger. Its composition is dated only loosely (“perhaps” as early as the first century, firm only before Origen c. 250). If the book postdates 2 Timothy, Paul cannot have quoted it, and the direction of borrowing reverses: the book and the apostle would both be drawing on the older shared legend the Damascus Document already attests. The datings are too soft to exclude this, which is what keeps the question genuinely open.

(3) Origen’s testimony is a systematic move, not independent confirmation. Origen makes the same identification for other New-Testament citations he cannot locate in the canon — the “eye hath not seen” of 1 Corinthians 2:9, the Zechariah-under-Jeremiah’s-name quotation of Matthew 27:9 — reading them all as apostolic borrowings from libri secreti. That habit shows he was inclined to answer “which lost book?” whenever a citation puzzled him; it does not independently establish that Paul in fact used this one. Gero’s caution is the sober verdict: given how far the legend had spread, the dependence “cannot be regarded as certain.”4

The second question: was the book ever Scripture?

This one is not contested at all. Whatever Paul did or did not do with it, the Book of Jannes and Jambres never held canonical standing in any church. No canon list — Jewish or Christian, Eastern or Western — receives it; the single ancient catalogue that mentions it does so precisely to reject it. The Decretum Gelasianum (a South-Gallic list of the later fifth or sixth century) files it among the apocrypha:

Liber qui appellatur Paenitentia Iamne et Mambre… apocryphus.

— “A book called The Penitence of Jannes and Mambres… apocryphal.” Its neighbours on that roll are the Lots of the Apostles and other writings “not to be received.” So the answer to “was it Scripture?” is a plain no, and it is worth stating flatly because it dissolves an anxiety some readers feel about the first question. Even a definite “yes” to “did Paul quote it?” would not make the book Scripture. An inspired writer citing a non-canonical source no more canonises that source than quoting a pagan poet canonises the poet — Paul quotes Epimenides and Aratus at Acts 17:28 and Menander at 1 Corinthians 15:33 without lending them any authority. The nearest parallel is Jude’s quotation of 1 Enoch as prophecy (Jude 14–15) — and there the extra-canonical citation is far firmer than anything in 2 Timothy (Jude formally quotes the book with an authority-formula, “Enoch… prophesied… saying”), yet even that did not carry 1 Enoch into the canon of any but the Ethiopian church, and its canonicity is rated weak. If a formal prophetic citation does not canonise 1 Enoch, the bare naming of two figures in 2 Timothy — with no quotation, no source-formula, and no claim to be citing a text at all — certainly does not canonise the Book of Jannes and Jambres. The citation question and the canonicity question are simply independent.5

Assessment

Contested — on whether Paul quoted the written book. The strong form of Origen’s claim (a direct, exclusive dependence of 2 Timothy 3:8 on the Book of Jannes and Jambres) is neither established nor refuted: for it stand the earliest reader who addressed the question, a real and early book on exactly these figures, and a Latin Father who knew its plot; against it stand the names’ wide currency before and apart from the book (the Damascus Document, Pliny, Apuleius), the book’s loose and possibly post-Pauline date, and Origen’s systematic habit of assigning every unlocatable citation to a “secret book.” The datings are too soft to decide it; the honest verdict is that Paul may have known the book, but more likely reached for names his readers already shared — a genuine dispute with real ancient testimony on both sides. Unsupported — on whether the book was ever Scripture. That question has a settled negative answer: no canon received it, the one list that names it rejects it as apocryphus, and — crucially — a “yes” to the citation question would not change this, since an inspired author’s use of a non-canonical source does not canonise the source (the firmer Jude/1 Enoch case does not, and 2 Timothy’s bare naming is weaker still). Keep the two apart: the interesting question is historical (where did the names come from?), not canonical (the book was never a candidate for Scripture).
