The Fathers on Paul’s source: an unwritten tradition, or the lost Book?

Dates: c. 200 – 1274 — Tertullian, On Idolatry (c. 200); the Apostolic Constitutions (c. 380); Ambrosiaster, Commentary on 2 Timothy (c. 384); John Chrysostom, Homilies on 2 Timothy 8 (c. 400); Augustine, Reply to Faustus 13 (c. 400); Theodore of Mopsuestia (c. 428); Theodoret of Cyrus (c. 450); Fulgentius of Ruspe, Letter to Scarila (c. 512); Oecumenius (c. 6th c.); Theophylact of Ohrid (c. 1090); Thomas Aquinas, Commentary on 2 Timothy (c. 1270)
Summary: When the church came to 2 Timothy 3:8, it kept tripping over the fact this whole page turns on: Paul names two magicians the Old Testament never names. The Fathers make two recurring moves. The first is a source-question — where did Paul get the names? — and here they split into two camps: an unwritten Jewish tradition (or direct inspiration), held by the Antiochene and Byzantine commentators (Chrysostom, Theodoret, Oecumenius, Theophylact, and, polemically, Theodore of Mopsuestia), against a written apocryphon named by the Latins (Ambrosiaster, Aquinas) and by Origen before them. The second move is typological: the pair become the standing type of the magic — and the heresy — that mimics the truth, flourishes briefly, and then collapses when its “folly” is exposed (Tertullian, the Apostolic Constitutions, Augustine, Fulgentius, and the exegetes on verse 9). This page gathers the patristic reception as one stratum; the underlying question of whether Paul actually quoted the lost Book of Jannes and Jambres is weighed on its own argument page (contested).

Claims & evidence

The magic that mimics — and then collapses (Tertullian, c. 200)

The earliest Christian writer to reach for the Exodus contest at all is Tertullian, who in On Idolatry sorts the kinds of magic and files Pharaoh’s sorcerers under the counterfeit that apes God’s wonders:

So, too, that other species of magic which operates by miracles, emulous even in opposition to Moses, tried God’s patience until the Gospel.

— Tertullian, On Idolatry 9. He does not name Jannes and Jambres, but the frame he sets — a “magic which operates by miracles,” a genuine wonder-working power that is nonetheless counterfeit and doomed — is the one the later Fathers fill with the two names. It is the note 2 Timothy itself strikes: the magicians “resist the truth” for a season, but their folly is finally “manifest unto all men.”1

Where did Paul get the names? The patristic debate

The question the anonymity of Exodus forces — how did Paul know names the Torah withholds? — is the one the Fathers return to most. Two answers divide them.

Camp one: an unwritten Jewish tradition (or the Holy Spirit). The Antiochene and Byzantine commentators answer that Paul drew on oral Jewish lore, or else knew the names by inspiration. John Chrysostom sets the pattern:

Who are these? The magicians in the time of Moses. But how is it their names are nowhere else introduced? Either they were handed down by tradition, or it is probable that Paul knew them by inspiration.

— Chrysostom, Homilies on 2 Timothy 8.2 Theodoret of Cyrus states the same view more flatly: “Paul takes the story of Jannes and Jambres not from holy Scripture but from an unwritten Jewish tradition.” Oecumenius and Theophylact of Ohrid, both leaning on Chrysostom, repeat the pair of options almost word for word — Oecumenius: “how does one know their names when Scripture does not say? We say that it was from unwritten tradition; it was likely from the Holy Spirit that Paul knew”; Theophylact: “Their names were taken from unwritten tradition, or probably Paul learned of them from the Holy Spirit.”3

Theodore of Mopsuestia belongs to this camp too, but reaches it by attacking the other one. He has no patience for the idea that Paul depended on a lost book:

A great deal of foolishness has been written about how Paul could have known the names of these two men who resisted Moses. Particularly absurd is the notion that he had access to some information or writing now lost to us.

— Theodore, Commentary on 2 Timothy. His scorn is itself evidence that the “lost writing” explanation was already current and worth refuting by the early fifth century.4

Camp two: a written apocryphon. The very explanation Theodore mocks is the one the Latin tradition holds. Ambrosiaster, commenting on the verse about 384, says plainly that Paul’s example is drawn from a book outside the canon:

This example is from the apocrypha; for Jannes and Mambres were brothers who were magicians or sorcerers of the Egyptians, and by the power of their magic they falsely thought they could resist the miracles of God that were performed through Moses. But when the virtue of Moses grew in his works, they became humble and confessed with sorrow the God who worked in Moses’ wounds.

— Ambrosiaster, Commentary on 2 Timothy 3:8–9. Ambrosiaster even knows the plot of the lost Book — the magicians’ humbling and deathbed confession that Moses’ God is real (the “penitence” that titles the apocryphon).5 Nearly nine centuries later Thomas Aquinas reaches the same conclusion more cautiously: “in Exodus those magicians were not named, but here they are: and perhaps he obtained their names from certain Jewish writings.” Both stand with Origen, who a century before Ambrosiaster had said outright that Paul took the names from “the secret book which is entitled the Book of Jannes and Mambres” — the fullest form of the written-source claim, treated on the Book’s own page.6

The two camps are not as far apart as they look: an “unwritten tradition” and a written Book of Jannes and Jambres are two faces of one body of extra-biblical Jewish legend — the same legend the Damascus Document, the Targums, and the pagan mages also drew on. What the Fathers disagree about is only its form at the moment Paul used it: oral lore, or a book already in circulation.

The type of heresy: “their folly shall be manifest”

Alongside the source-question runs a second, homiletic use of the pair: Jannes and Jambres as the fixed type of error that counterfeits the truth and is exposed. The Apostolic Constitutions pairs them with the Gospel’s opponents in a neat typology of the two Testaments:

The one was resisted by Jannes and Jambres, the other by Annas and Caiaphas.

— Apostolic Constitutions: Moses withstood by the magicians, Christ by the high priests — the sorcerers become the Old-Covenant figure of every later resistance to God.7 Augustine turns the same verse against the heretics of his own day; in the Reply to Faustus the Manichaean he reads Paul’s prophecy as their portrait — “As Jannes and Jambres withstood Moses, so they also resist the truth … But they shall proceed no further: for their folly shall be manifest to all men, as theirs also was” — glossing that the false teacher “who at first misled people by a promising display of superior wisdom, shall be a fool, that is, shall be seen to be a fool.” Fulgentius of Ruspe uses it the same way, ranking those “who contradict the true faith” among the reprobate Paul describes.8

The exegetes read verse 9 — “they shall proceed no further” — as the theological payoff of the whole episode. Chrysostom, Oecumenius, and Theophylact all press the point that counterfeit wonders, like counterfeit doctrine, “flourish for a time, and then are detected, and come to nought”: as Theophylact puts it, “everything that is essentially not good only appears good for a time.” Aquinas gives the motif its sharpest frame — the wicked’s will to harm is their own, but the power is only what God permits: “their folly shall be made manifest to all men, God unmasking them,” just as Pharaoh’s magicians were unmasked “because they were unable to work their signs.” The magicians who could match Moses at blood and frogs but failed at the lice are the standing proof that a mimic’s power has a ceiling.9

Notes

  • Why the debate matters. The source-question is not antiquarian. It is the patristic form of this page’s central fact — that 2 Timothy preserves a piece of extra-biblical Jewish tradition rather than a text of the Hebrew Bible — and it fed directly into how the church rated the lost Book of Jannes and Jambres. If Paul quoted an apocryphon, the apocryphon gains a footing near Scripture; if he drew on oral lore or inspiration, the Book can be shelved without loss. That is why Origen and Ambrosiaster on one side, and Theodore of Mopsuestia on the other, argue the point so pointedly — though, as the argument page works out, the stakes the Fathers felt are overdrawn: even a citation would not have made the book Scripture (an inspired author’s use of a non-canonical source does not canonise it).
  • A geographical seam. The two camps run roughly along the fault line between the Greek East and the Latin West: the Antiochene and Byzantine commentators (Chrysostom, Theodore, Theodoret, Oecumenius, Theophylact) prefer “unwritten tradition,” while the named book is the Latin and Alexandrian answer (Origen, Ambrosiaster, Aquinas). The one datum both sides concede is the anonymity of Exodus that starts the problem.
  • Ambrosiaster knows the plot. His remark that the magicians “became humble and confessed … the God who worked in Moses’ wounds” is not in Exodus at all; it is the storyline of the lost apocryphon (the “penitence” of the title). A fourth-century Latin commentator, then, still had access to the legend’s contents, whether from the book or from the tradition around it.
  • “Mambres,” not “Jambres.” The Latin Fathers — Ambrosiaster, Aquinas, the decretal ascribed to Pope Anterus — consistently give the second magician as Mambres, the Western form of the name, where the Greek tradition and Paul’s own text read Jambres. The split is a datum for the (separately weighed) question of where the names came from and why they vary.
