The Damascus Document (“Yohana and his brother”)

Dates: composed c. 100 BC (a sectarian Jewish work; the passage survives in the mediaeval Cairo Genizah manuscript A and among the Dead Sea Scrolls in the Cave 6 fragment 6Q15) · Wikipedia
Summary: A sectarian Jewish text, older than the naming in 2 Timothy 3:8, that Phillip J. Long calls “the first reference to one of the magicians by the name”; Albert Pietersma cautions that its brothers have been equated with Jannes and Jambres “without proper qualification.” In a brief dualistic aside the Damascus Document sets Moses and Aaron, raised up “by the Prince of Lights,” against “Yohana/Yannes and his brother,” whom “Belial raised up… in his cunning” when Israel was delivered “the first time.” Only one of the pair is named here, and the second brother is left anonymous; the couplet “Jannes and Jambres” appears in later sources.

What it is and how the passage survives

The Damascus Document (CD) is a rule-and-admonition text of a Second-Temple Jewish movement closely associated with the Qumran community that gathered the Dead Sea Scrolls. It first came to light in a different form: two mediaeval copies (designated manuscripts A and B) recovered by Solomon Schechter from the Cairo Genizah and published in 1910. When the Qumran caves were excavated half a century later, fragments of the same work turned up among the scrolls — multiple copies in Cave 4 (4Q266–273) and further fragments in Caves 5 (5Q12) and 6 (6Q15) — anchoring the composition itself to the late second or early first century BC. The naming passage is not a mediaeval accretion: it stands in column V of the Cairo manuscript A and is also attested in the Qumran fragment 6Q15.1

The work was composed around 100 BC, before the “Jannes and Jambres” of 2 Timothy. Phillip J. Long identifies it as “the first reference to one of the magicians by the name,” and the Encyclopaedia Judaica lists its “Jannes and his brother” among the Second Temple sources of the legend; Albert Pietersma cautions that the equation of its brothers with Jannes and Jambres is made “without proper qualification” (see below).2

Claims & evidence

The naming: “Yohana/Yannes and his brother”

For in times past Moses and Aaron stood in the power of the Prince of Lights and Belial raised up Yannes and his brother in his cunning when seeking to do evil to Israel the first time.

— Damascus Document, column V, lines 17–19 (Edward Cook’s translation, in the Wise–Abegg–Cook edition), as reproduced online. The name behind the Greek “Jannes” is the Hebrew yḥnh (Yohana / Yoḥaneh), and translators render it in a range of forms — Solomon Schechter’s 1910 editio princeps has “when Belial raised Yochaneh and his brother in his device when Israel was delivered for the first time,” while Geza Vermes, Florentino García Martínez, and Philip Davies normalise it toward “Jannes.” What every rendering agrees on is the shape of the phrase: one named magician, “and his brother.”3

The dualistic frame: the Prince of Lights against Belial

The magicians appear inside a compressed statement of the community’s cosmic dualism. Moses and Aaron do not merely outmatch the sorcerers; they “stood in the power of the Prince of Lights,” the angelic captain of the sons of light, while their opponents are the instruments of Belial, the prince of darkness, who “raised [them] up… in his cunning.” The contest of Exodus is thus read as a front in the war of the two spirits — God’s Moses and Aaron versus Belial’s pair — and it is set “the first time” Israel was delivered, at the deliverance from Egypt. In this naming the legend is Palestinian-Jewish and framed as spiritual warfare; 2 Timothy likewise casts “Jannes and Jambres” as types of those who “resist the truth.”4

A single named brother

The passage names “Yohana/Yannes” and then only “his brother”; the second magician has no name here. The pair “Jannes and Jambres” (Latin often “Jannes and Mambres”) appears in 2 Timothy, the Targums and the apocryphon. The Encyclopaedia Judaica lists the passage, as “Jannes and his brother,” among the sources of the legend; Albert Pietersma cautions that scholars have equated its two brothers with Jannes and Jambres “without proper qualification.” The passage is a primary datum for the question of where the names came from and why the second one varies.5

Notes

  • Scope of the passage. CD attests that by c. 100 BC Jewish tradition had attached a name to one of Pharaoh’s magicians and framed the pair dualistically, beyond the bare “magicians of Egypt” of Exodus and older than the naming in 2 Timothy. It does not give the second name, and its notice is brief and allusive, referring to a story rather than telling one.
  • An allegorical reading. Louis Ginzberg proposed ⚠ unverified that “Yohana/Jannes” here doubles as a coded allusion to the community’s own opponent, the Hasmonean priest-king Alexander Jannaeus, identified by him with the sect’s “Wicked Priest,” so that the passage draws Moses’ opponents and the Teacher of Righteousness’s opponents into one line.
  • Placement on the timeline. By the date of its composition (c. 100 BC) the passage is earlier than the 2 Timothy block it is placed after here. The scriptural baseline (anonymous in Exodus, named in 2 Timothy) heads the page because it frames the whole tradition and its oldest element, Exodus, is older than anything else; the Damascus Document then follows as the first extra-canonical witness on the timeline, though in strict composition-order it predates 2 Timothy.
  • Editions. Solomon Schechter, Fragments of a Zadokite Work (Cambridge, 1910), is the editio princeps; standard modern translations include Geza Vermes, The Complete Dead Sea Scrolls in English; Florentino García Martínez, The Dead Sea Scrolls Translated; and Michael Wise, Martin Abegg, and Edward Cook, The Dead Sea Scrolls: A New Translation (quoted above). Philip R. Davies, The Damascus Covenant (1983), argues the passage is original to its context.
