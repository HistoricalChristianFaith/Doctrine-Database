The Damascus Document (“Yohana and his brother”)

Dates: composed c. 100 BC (a sectarian Jewish work; the passage survives in the mediaeval Cairo Genizah manuscript A and among the Dead Sea Scrolls in the Cave 6 fragment 6Q15) · Wikipedia
Summary: The earliest datable source to put a name on one of Pharaoh’s two magicians — older than the naming in 2 Timothy 3:8. In a brief dualistic aside the Damascus Document sets Moses and Aaron, raised up “by the Prince of Lights,” against “Yohana/Yannes and his brother,” whom “Belial raised up… in his cunning” when Israel was delivered “the first time.” Only one of the pair is named here, and the second brother is left anonymous — the fixed “Jannes and Jambres” couplet is a later stage of the tradition.

What it is and where the passage sits

The Damascus Document (CD) is a rule-and-admonition text of a Second-Temple Jewish movement closely related to — very likely identical with — the Qumran community that gathered the Dead Sea Scrolls. It first came to light in a different form: two mediaeval copies (designated manuscripts A and B) recovered by Solomon Schechter from the Cairo Genizah and published in 1910. When the Qumran caves were excavated half a century later, fragments of the same work turned up among the scrolls — multiple copies in Cave 4 (4Q266–273) and further fragments in Caves 5 (5Q12) and 6 (6Q15) — anchoring the composition itself to the late second or early first century BC. The naming passage is not a mediaeval accretion: it stands in column V of the Cairo manuscript A and is also attested in the Qumran fragment 6Q15.1

That dating is the point. Because the work was composed around 100 BC, its naming of one of the Exodus magicians is older than Paul’s “Jannes and Jambres” in 2 Timothy — making the Damascus Document, and not the New Testament, the earliest surviving witness that Pharaoh’s anonymous sorcerers had by then been given names in Jewish tradition.2

Claims & evidence

It names one of the magicians — “Yohana/Yannes and his brother”

For in times past Moses and Aaron stood in the power of the Prince of Lights and Belial raised up Yannes and his brother in his cunning when seeking to do evil to Israel the first time.

— Damascus Document, column V, lines 17–19 (Edward Cook’s translation, in the Wise–Abegg–Cook edition), as reproduced online. The name behind the Greek “Jannes” is the Hebrew yḥnh (Yohana / Yoḥaneh), and translators render it in a range of forms — Solomon Schechter’s 1910 editio princeps has “when Belial raised Yochaneh and his brother in his device when Israel was delivered for the first time,” while Geza Vermes, Florentino García Martínez, and Philip Davies normalise it toward “Jannes.” What every rendering agrees on is the shape of the phrase: one named magician, “and his brother.”3

The dualistic frame: the Prince of Lights against Belial

The magicians appear inside a compressed statement of the community’s cosmic dualism. Moses and Aaron do not merely outmatch the sorcerers; they “stood in the power of the Prince of Lights,” the angelic captain of the sons of light, while their opponents are the instruments of Belial, the prince of darkness, who “raised [them] up… in his cunning.” The contest of Exodus is thus read as a front in the war of the two spirits — God’s Moses and Aaron versus Belial’s pair — and it is set “the first time” Israel was delivered, at the deliverance from Egypt. The legend is already, in this its earliest naming, both Palestinian-Jewish and framed as spiritual warfare, the same dualistic key in which the New Testament will later cast “Jannes and Jambres” as types of those who “resist the truth.”4

A single named brother — not yet “Jannes and Jambres”

The most consequential detail for the history of the names is what the passage does not say. It names “Yohana/Yannes” and then only “his brother” — the second magician has, as yet, no name at all. The fixed pair “Jannes and Jambres” (Latin often “Jannes and Mambres”) that Paul cites as common knowledge, and that the Targums and the later apocryphon carry, is therefore not the tradition’s starting point but a subsequent development: at the earliest stage recoverable, one brother is named and the other is a blank the tradition would only afterwards fill in. That makes CD a primary datum for the question of where the names came from and why the second one varies — weighed on this timeline’s name-origin argument.5

Notes

  • What it shows and does not show. CD attests that by c. 100 BC Jewish tradition had already attached a name to one of Pharaoh’s magicians and framed the pair dualistically — a full stage of legend behind the bare “magicians of Egypt” of Exodus and older than the naming in 2 Timothy. It does not yet give the second name, and its notice is brief and allusive, presupposing a story its readers already knew rather than telling one.
  • An allegorical reading. Louis Ginzberg and others proposed that “Yohana/Jannes” here doubles as a coded jab at the community’s own enemy — the Hasmonean priest-king Alexander Jannaeus, the sect’s “Wicked Priest” — so that the passage draws Moses’ opponents and the Teacher of Righteousness’s opponents into one line. On any reading, though, the surface sense presupposes the Exodus-magician legend with a name already in place; the wordplay works only because “Yohana” was already the magician’s name.
  • Where it sits on this timeline. By the date of its composition (c. 100 BC) this is the earliest datable naming of the pair — earlier than the 2 Timothy block it is placed after here. The scriptural baseline (anonymous in Exodus, named by Paul) heads the page because it frames the whole tradition and its oldest element, Exodus, is older than anything else; the Damascus Document then follows as the first extra-canonical witness, with the caveat that in strict composition-order its naming actually predates Paul’s.
  • Editions. Solomon Schechter, Fragments of a Zadokite Work (Cambridge, 1910), is the editio princeps; standard modern translations include Geza Vermes, The Complete Dead Sea Scrolls in English; Florentino García Martínez, The Dead Sea Scrolls Translated; and Michael Wise, Martin Abegg, and Edward Cook, The Dead Sea Scrolls: A New Translation (quoted above). Philip R. Davies, The Damascus Covenant (1983), argues the passage is original to its context.
