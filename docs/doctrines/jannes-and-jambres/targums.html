The Aramaic Targums on Jannes and Jambres

Dates: c. 2nd–8th century AD (a reception stratum, not a single source: the Aramaic synagogue paraphrases of the Torah, whose written redaction spans several centuries — the naming here is carried by Targum Pseudo-Jonathan, the latest and most expansive of them, though the haggadah it preserves is older) · Wikipedia
Summary: The synagogue’s Aramaic Bible does what neither Exodus nor 2 Timothy quite does: it weaves the pair back into the narrative of Exodus. Where the Hebrew leaves Pharaoh’s sorcerers anonymous, Targum Pseudo-Jonathan names them “Jannes and Jambres” at three points — interpreting Pharaoh’s dream of the lamb that outweighs Egypt (Exodus 1:15), leading the magicians who match Moses sign for sign (Exodus 7:11), and, most strikingly, riding at the side of Balaam son of Beor as his “two young men” on the road to curse Israel (Numbers 22:22). That last verse is the primary text behind the whole “sons of Balaam” thread that ties this page to Balaam’s — though the Targum itself calls them his servants, not his sons, a distinction the broader rabbinic tradition would later blur. The naming is distinctive to Pseudo-Jonathan: the restrained Onkelos and the Palestinian Neofiti leave the magicians unnamed. And the spelling is unstable even within one translation — “Jannis and Jambres,” “Janis and Jamberes,” “Jannes and Jambres” — a datum in its own right for how fluid the pair’s names remained.

Where it sits

A Targum (Aramaic for “translation”) is the running Aramaic rendering of Scripture that grew up in the synagogue once Aramaic had displaced Hebrew as the everyday language of Jewish Palestine and Babylonia; read aloud after the Hebrew, it was never a bare translation but a paraphrase that folded interpretation, expansion, and homily into the text. Three Targums render the Torah. Onkelos, the authoritative Babylonian Targum, is the most restrained and literal; Neofiti (a complete Palestinian Targum) is more expansive; and Pseudo-Jonathan (Targum Yerushalmi I) is the latest and by far the most elaborate, its final redaction usually dated to the seventh or eighth century, though it preserves much older haggadah. Only Pseudo-Jonathan names Pharaoh’s magicians: where it writes “Jannes and Jambres,” Onkelos keeps the anonymous “wise men and magicians” of the Hebrew. Because a Targum’s wording may be centuries younger than the tradition it records — and the naming of the magicians is already a century older than Paul, attested in the Damascus Document around 100 BC — this page is best read not as one dated witness but as a reception stratum: the deposit of what the synagogue said about the pair, slotted on the timeline as a body rather than pinned to a single year.1

Claims & evidence

Named at Pharaoh’s dream (Exodus 1:15)

Pseudo-Jonathan supplies a whole scene the Hebrew lacks. Before ever a plague falls, Pharaoh dreams that all Egypt lies in one pan of a balance and a single lamb in the other — and the lamb outweighs Egypt. He summons his magicians, and it is “Jannis and Jambres” who read the omen and set the slaughter of the Hebrew boys in motion:

And Pharoh told that he, being asleep, had seen in his dream, and, behold, all the land of Mizraim was placed in one scale of a balance, and a lamb, the young of a sheep, was ill the other scale; and the scale with the lamb in it overweighed. Forthwith he sent and called all the magicians of Mizraim, and imparted to them his dream. Immediately Jannis and Jambres, the chief of the magicians, opened their mouth and answered Pharoh,—A certain child is about to be born in the congregation of Israel, by whose hand will be destruction to all the land of Mizraim.

— Targum Pseudo-Jonathan, Exodus 1:15 (Etheridge; the “was ill the other scale” is an artifact of the printed text). Two things are worth noting. First, the pair are introduced not as Moses’ rivals at the rod-contest but far earlier, as the court seers who prophesy the deliverer’s birth — so the Targum makes them the very authors of the decree against the Hebrew infants that Exodus 1 reports. Second, they are “the chief of the magicians”: the tradition has by now not merely named the anonymous sorcerers of Exodus but promoted them to the head of Pharaoh’s whole guild.2

The magicians who withstood Moses (Exodus 7:11)

At the contest itself — the moment 2 Timothy has in view when it says the pair “withstood Moses” (2 Timothy 3:8) — Pseudo-Jonathan again gives the otherwise-nameless magicians the names:

But Pharoh called the hachems and magicians; and they also, Janis and Jamberes, magicians of Mizraim, did the same by their burnings of divination.

— Targum Pseudo-Jonathan, Exodus 7:11 (Etheridge). The comparison is instructive. Targum Onkelos renders the same verse without a name — “Pharaoh too called [his] wise men and magicians. The sorcerers of Egypt also did likewise with their magic art” — keeping to the anonymity of the Hebrew. The naming is Pseudo-Jonathan’s own contribution, the Aramaic paraphrase filling the very gap in Exodus that Paul had filled from the same stream of tradition.3

Balaam’s two attendants (Numbers 22:22) — the “sons of Balaam” thread

The load-bearing verse for this page’s link to Balaam is not in Exodus at all but in Numbers. Where the Hebrew of Numbers 22:22 says only that Balaam saddled his ass and “his two servants were with him,” Pseudo-Jonathan names those two servants — and they are Pharaoh’s magicians:

But the anger of the Lord was provoked, because he would go (that he might) curse them; and the angel of the Lord stood in the way to be an adversary to him. But he sat upon his ass, and his two young men, Jannes and Jambres, were with him.

— Targum Pseudo-Jonathan, Numbers 22:22 (Etheridge). This single half-verse does the work of joining the two traditions the whole wiki keeps in view: the Egyptian sorcerers who opposed Moses are made the travelling companions of the pagan seer who tried to curse Israel — the dark opponents of the Exodus and the dark opponent of the wilderness, gathered into one household. But a precise reading matters. The Targum here calls them Balaam’s “two young men” (Aramaic ‘ulemoi), the same word the King James renders “his two servants” — servants or attendants, not sons. The familiar shorthand that Jannes and Jambres were the sons of Balaam is a further step, drawn by the broader rabbinic and later tradition (the Babylonian Talmud at Menaḥot 85a names Moses’ opponents “Yoḥana and Mamre”; medieval and modern reference works then gather the strands into “sons of Balaam”). Whether that identification holds — and it runs into an obvious chronology problem, since the Exodus contest and Balaam’s journey stand forty years apart — is the question weighed on this timeline’s “were they the sons of Balaam?” argument (weak for the literal kinship); this verse is the primary text that argument adjudicates.4

Notes

  • Only Pseudo-Jonathan names them. Of the three Torah Targums, the naming of Pharaoh’s magicians is distinctive to Pseudo-Jonathan; Onkelos (the Babylonian Targum) leaves them the anonymous “wise men and magicians” of the Hebrew, as at Exodus 7:11. This fits Pseudo-Jonathan’s character as the most haggadic of the Targums — the one that most freely folds legend into the running text.
  • The spelling is genuinely unstable. Even within Etheridge’s one translation the pair are “Jannis and Jambres” (Exodus 1:15), “Janis and Jamberes” (Exodus 7:11), and “Jannes and Jambres” (Numbers 22:22); the underlying Aramaic shifts too (the second name is Yimbres in Exodus but Yemris, without the b, at Numbers 22:22). This fluidity is itself a datum for the name-origin question — the couplet was still settling long after it was fixed as a pair, and the Latin West would carry a further variant, “Mambres.”
  • Servants, then sons. The Targum’s Numbers 22:22 makes Jannes and Jambres Balaam’s attendants; the “sons of Balaam” label is a downstream hardening of the same link, not the wording of the verse. Keeping the two apart matters, because the strength of the Balaam-connection argument depends on which claim is being made — that the traditions were associated (which this verse plainly attests) is a much easier thing to grant than that the magicians were literally Balaam’s children (which the forty-year gap between Exodus and Numbers makes hard).
  • Bearing on the Balaam page. This is the firsthand primary text for the “were Jannes and Jambres sons of Balaam?” argument (rated weak for the literal kinship) that ties this timeline to Balaam’s. Balaam’s own Targum reception — the messianic “star out of Jacob” and his hardening into a wicked sorcerer — is treated on that timeline’s Targums page; the two pages share the same Pseudo-Jonathan witness seen from opposite ends.
  • Text and editions. Pseudo-Jonathan is quoted here in J. W. Etheridge’s translation (The Targums of Onkelos and Jonathan Ben Uzziel on the Pentateuch, London, 1862–65), as served by Sefaria; Onkelos in the Metsudah Chumash rendering (2009), likewise at Sefaria. On the pair across the Targums and rabbinic sources, see the article “Jannes and Jambres” in the Jewish Encyclopedia (1906).
