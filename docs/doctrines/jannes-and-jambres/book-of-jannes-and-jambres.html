The lost Book of Jannes and Jambres

Dates: composed in Greek in Roman Egypt, c. 1st–3rd century AD (Albert Pietersma: “perhaps as early as the first century C.E.”; the firm terminus ante quem is Origen’s citation c. AD 250). It survives only in fragments — Greek papyri (P. Vindobonensis, 3rd c.; P. Chester Beatty XVI, late 4th c.; P. Michigan; P. Heidelberg), a Latin-and-Old-English parchment leaf (London, BL Cotton Tiberius B.v, 11th c.), and Ethiopic fragments · Wikipedia
Summary: A whole apocryphal book once told the magicians’ story — the Book (or Apocryphon) of Jannes and Jambres, listed by the Gelasian Decree under its Latin title Paenitentia Iamnis et Mambre (“the Penitence of Jannes and Mambres”). It narrated their careers as Balaam’s sons, their defeat by Moses, Jannes’ deathbed confession that Moses’ power was “of God,” his death and descent to Hades, and Jambres’ necromancy raising his brother’s shade to describe the torments of hell. Origen held that Paul drew the names of 2 Timothy 3:8 from this “secret book”; the pagan Platonist Numenius ranked the pair among Egypt’s unrivalled magicians; and about the sixth century the church formally shelved the book among the rejected apocrypha.

What it is and how it survives

Behind the bare pair of names in 2 Timothy and the allusive notices of the Damascus Document and the Targums there once stood a full narrative: an apocryphon devoted entirely to Pharaoh’s two magicians. It was composed in Greek, most likely in Roman Egypt, and was already in circulation by the third century AD in more than one recension. No complete copy survives. What remains is a scatter of fragments in four languages: Greek papyri — P. Vindobonensis (Vienna, 3rd c., the earliest witnesses; its scribe avoids the Christian nomina sacra, pointing perhaps to a Jewish milieu), the roughly one hundred fragments of P. Chester Beatty XVI (Dublin, a single-quire codex of the late 4th c., the principal witness), P. Michigan, and P. Heidelberg; a Latin-and-Old-English parchment leaf, London, British Library Cotton Tiberius B.v (11th c., folio 87), whose eighteen lines carry the necromancy scene beneath a full-page drawing of Jambres summoning his dead brother; and Ethiopic fragments translated from the Greek, which preserve passages the Greek copies have lost.1

The standard critical edition is Albert Pietersma, The Apocryphon of Jannes and Jambres the Magicians (Leiden: Brill, 1994), the editio princeps of the Chester Beatty codex with fresh editions of the Vienna and Cotton fragments, full translation, commentary, and a survey of the ancient testimonia. Pietersma dates the composition “perhaps as early as the first century C.E.,” and no later than Origen’s citation c. AD 250; the work carries no explicitly Christian content, and its underlying Exodus-magician legend is Jewish, though the copies that reach us were transmitted by Christians. One caution follows from the state of the evidence: the fragments were long ago mounted in glass in an order that is not certainly the book’s own, so the reconstructed sequence of episodes is Pietersma’s, and reviewers have warned against pressing the narrative order too hard.2

Claims & evidence

The contest, and Jannes’ deathbed confession — the “penitence” that titles the book

As Pietersma reconstructs it, the book’s narrator is one of Pharaoh’s own officials. The brothers, warned of coming affliction, prepare against Moses; Jannes builds a walled enclosure; the two “match their feats” against Moses and Aaron in the plague-contest and are overpowered by divine force. Jannes then falls gravely ill and confesses that “Moses’s power is of divine origin” — the repentance that gives the book its Latin title, the Paenitentia. Before he dies he entrusts a secret book to Jambres with a warning not to pursue the Hebrews; the Egyptian host that ignores the like warning drowns in the Red Sea, while Jambres, heeding it, survives. This confession-and-death arc is the reason the tradition remembered the work as a story of the magicians’ fate, not merely their sorcery.3

The necromancy: Jambres raises Jannes’ shade from Hades

The book’s most striking scene, and the one best preserved, is its close. After Jannes dies violently and his mother dies after him, Jambres opens the secret book and, by necromancy under an apple tree, calls up his dead brother’s shade from Hades. The shade answers from torment, describing the sufferings of the underworld and condemning the idolaters and the damned. It is precisely this passage that the eleventh-century Cotton Tiberius leaf carries in Latin and Old English — “Jannes’s condemnation to Hell and Jambres’s summoning him through necromancy” — beneath a full-page illustration of Jambres with his magical book raising the shade. The Ethiopic fragments preserve a further stretch of the shade’s speech absent from the Greek. So the apocryphon’s enduring image was not the rod-contest of Exodus but a magician conversing with the dead.4

The genealogy: the magicians as Balaam’s sons

Two small fragments of P. Michigan preserve part of a genealogy in which Jannes and Jambres are the sons of Balaam. This is the point at which the loose association the Targum Pseudo-Jonathan makes — the magicians riding as Balaam’s “two young men” (his servants) on the road to curse Israel — hardens into literal paternity. The apocryphon is thus a primary witness for the “sons of Balaam” reading that later rabbinic and Christian sources take up, and it carries the same chronological strain: the Exodus contest and Balaam’s journey stand some forty years apart. Whether that sonship is history or a genealogical knitting-together of two famous pagan opponents of God’s messenger is weighed on this timeline’s “were they the sons of Balaam?” argument (weak for the literal kinship, though the thematic pairing is a real, old tradition); the apocryphon establishes only that the identification was current, and old.5

Origen: Paul’s names come from “the secret book”

The earliest datable reference to the book, and the source of the enduring claim that Paul quoted it, is Origen (c. AD 250). Commenting on Matthew, he explains that the apostle’s phrase is not found in the canonical Scriptures but in this apocryphon:

“As Jannes and Mambres resisted Moses” is not found in the public books, but in the secret book which is entitled the Book of Jannes and Mambres.

— Origen, Commentary on Matthew (on Matt 27:9), in the ancient Latin series (Commentariorum Series): “‘sicut Iamnes et Mambres restiterunt Moysi’ non invenitur in publicis libris, sed in libro secreto qui suprascribitur liber Iamnes et Mambres.” Origen makes the same move for other New-Testament citations he cannot locate in the canon (the “eye hath not seen” of 1 Cor 2:9, the Zechariah-under-Jeremiah’s-name quotation of Matt 27:9), reading them as apostolic borrowings from libri secreti. His notice is the hinge of the whole “did Paul quote the apocryphon?” question: he asserts a direct dependence that later readers — Ambrosiaster, and modern scholars alike — would variously affirm or qualify.6

Numenius the Platonist: the pagan witness

The legend the book carried was known outside Judaism and Christianity too. The second-century Neopythagorean philosopher Numenius of Apamea told the story, and Eusebius preserves his words in the Preparation for the Gospel:

And next in order came Jannes and Jambres, Egyptian sacred scribes, men judged to have no superiors in the practice of magic, at the time when the Jews were being driven out of Egypt. So then these were the men chosen by the people of Egypt as fit to stand beside Musaeus, who led forth the Jews, a man who was most powerful in prayer to God; and of the plagues which Musaeus brought upon Egypt, these men showed themselves able to disperse the most violent.

— Numenius, quoted in Eusebius, Praeparatio Evangelica IX.8 (E. H. Gifford’s translation). Numenius calls Moses “Musaeus” and — unlike Exodus, and in the magicians’ favour — credits the pair with power to undo the worst of the plagues. His notice (which Origen also cites, at Against Celsus IV.51) shows the pair had become stock figures of famous Egyptian magic, a reputation the Latin writers Pliny and Apuleius echo when they rank “Jannes” among history’s great mages.7

The Gelasian Decree: the church shelves the book

By the sixth century the book had a settled ecclesiastical status: rejected. The Decretum Gelasianum (De libris recipiendis et non recipiendis, “On books to be received and not received”) — a South-Gallic catalogue of the later fifth or sixth century, long ascribed to Pope Gelasius but in fact anonymous — lists it among the apocrypha:

Liber qui appellatur Paenitentia Iamne et Mambre… apocryphus.

— “A book called The Penitence of Jannes and Mambres… apocryphal,” in the Decree’s long roll of writings the church declined to receive (its neighbours on the list include the Lots of the Apostles and other rejected texts). The entry is the formal act by which the Book of Jannes and Jambres — a work Origen had treated as a real source of Paul’s — passed out of use into the class of books “not to be received,” and it is a large part of why the book was eventually lost.8

Notes

  • Composition date vs. manuscript date. Keep the two apart. The book was composed c. 1st–3rd c. AD; the copies are later — the Vienna papyrus (3rd c.) is the earliest, the Chester Beatty codex is late-4th-c., and the Cotton leaf is eleventh-century. On this timeline the block is dated by the composition, which places it after the Damascus Document (c. 100 BC) and before the fully-expansive Targum Pseudo-Jonathan.
  • Jewish substance, Christian transmission. The underlying Exodus-magician legend is Jewish (its oldest form is the account of the Hellenistic-Jewish historian Artapanus, which Numenius echoes), and the Vienna scribe’s avoidance of the Christian nomina sacra may point to a Jewish exemplar; but the surviving Greek codex uses those Christian abbreviations, so the book reaches us through Christian hands. Whether the extant text was composed by a Jew or a Christian is genuinely debated.
  • Did Paul quote this book? Origen and Ambrosiaster say yes. Modern opinion is divided: some (J. H. Charlesworth; D.-A. Koch) hold that 2 Timothy 3:8 may depend on the apocryphon, while Stephen Gero cautions that “in view of the widespread occurrence of the Jannes and Jambres legend this cannot be regarded as certain.” The safer statement is that the names belonged to a broad pre-Christian Jewish tradition; whether Paul cites this particular book or the shared legend behind it — and whether the book ever carried anything like scriptural authority — is a contested question weighed on its own, not settled here.
  • Editions and studies. Albert Pietersma, The Apocryphon of Jannes and Jambres the Magicians (Brill, 1994), is the standard edition; W. B. Henry, “Notes on Jannes and Jambres (P. Chester Beatty XVI),” ZPE 198 (2016), and Stephen Gero, “Parerga to ‘The Book of Jannes and Jambres’,” Journal for the Study of the Pseudepigrapha (1991), refine the text and its history. A more complete Ethiopic manuscript was identified by Ted Erho c. 2015, promising further recovery of the lost contents.
