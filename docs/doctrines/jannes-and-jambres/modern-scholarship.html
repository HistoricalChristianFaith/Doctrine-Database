Modern scholarship on Jannes and Jambres (a survey)

Dates: a survey of critical study, 19th century to the present — anchored by Albert Pietersma’s standard critical edition, The Apocryphon of Jannes and Jambres the Magicians (Brill, 1994), and the reference-work and commentary tradition around it.
Summary: Set against the ancient witnesses, modern scholarship grades the whole Jannes-and-Jambres tradition, and several results recur. The lost book has been reassembled and edited from fragments in half a dozen languages, and dated as early as the first century. The names themselves are traced to a Jewish onomastic tradition (Yoḥana behind “Jannes”; an Aramaic root “to oppose” behind “Jambres”/“Mambres”), with a rival Egyptian derivation still argued. The Damascus Document’s naming, the “sons of Balaam” identification, and the question of whether Paul quoted the book are each treated as open, tradition-critical problems. This survey opens those contested questions; it does not settle them — each is weighed on its own argument page, on the evidence rather than on any prior assumption.

Claims & evidence

The critical edition: a lost book reassembled (Pietersma, 1994)

The foundation of all recent work is Albert Pietersma’s The Apocryphon of Jannes and Jambres the Magicians (Leiden: Brill, 1994) — the editio princeps of the principal Greek witness, Papyrus Chester Beatty XVI, with fresh editions of the Vienna papyrus and the Cotton Tiberius leaf, a full translation and commentary, and a survey of the ancient testimonia. Pietersma reconstructs a coherent narrative from some hundred fragments, dates the composition “perhaps as early as the first century C.E.,” and shows how widely the legend travelled — recounted, he notes, in more than half a dozen languages “from the Syriac east to the Latin west and from Egypt’s deserts to King Alfred’s court.” Reviewers welcomed the edition warmly (M. A. Knibb: “an excellent commentary”) while cautioning — as Pietersma himself does — that the fragments were long ago mounted in an order that is not certainly the book’s own, so the reconstructed sequence of episodes must be held loosely. The full account of the book, its fragments, and its contents is on its own detail page.1

The names: a Jewish onomastic tradition, and a rival Egyptian derivation

The origin of the names is the most-worked philological question, and the evidence points several ways at once. The dominant reading is Semitic: “Jannes” goes back to a Hebrew/Aramaic Yoḥana (the form the Damascus Document writes yḥnh and the Talmud “Yoḥana”), a by-form of Yoḥanan/John; “Jambres” (Latin “Mambres”) is commonly derived from an Aramaic root meaning “to oppose,” whose participle would be mamre — so that the pair together read as something like “he who is gracious” and “he who opposes.” Against this a line of scholars (Ewald, Lauth, Freudenthal) has argued an Egyptian etymology, fitting the pair’s Egyptian setting. Two data constrain the question. First, the earliest witnesses name only one magician — the Damascus Document’s “Yannes and his brother,” and the Latin writers Pliny and Apuleius, who give “Jannes” alone — so the fixed couplet is evidently a later filling-out, and the second name the more mobile (Jambres / Mambres / Jamberes). Second, the first name clusters tightly around the Yoḥanan/John root across every language, which tells against a purely Egyptian origin for it. The reference works lay the options out without forcing a single answer: the entry in the Dictionary of Deities and Demons in the Bible (Pietersma), the Anchor Bible Dictionary, and Geza Vermes’ studies of the Aramaic tradition. This is the primary text behind the queued name-origin argument.2

The Damascus Document: is it really this pair?

Scholarship treats the Damascus Document’s “Yannes and his brother” (CD V.17–19) as the earliest datable naming of one of the magicians — older than 2 Timothy — but not without qualification. Pietersma notes that scholars have equated the passage’s two brothers with Jannes and Jambres “without proper qualification,” since only one name is given and the identification with the Exodus magicians is an inference; he also records the debate over whether the passage is original to its context, siding with Philip R. Davies (who argued it is original and “eminently suits its current setting”). The upshot is a careful one: the Damascus Document attests, by c. 100 BC, a Jewish tradition that named a brother-pair as opponents of Moses and Aaron in a dualistic Prince-of-Lights / Belial frame — the substrate 2 Timothy later draws on — but the leap from “Yannes and his brother” to the full couplet “Jannes and Jambres” is itself part of the tradition’s history.3

The Balaam sonship, and whether Paul quoted the book

Two further questions recur. On the Balaam connection, the tradition-critical picture is one of hardening: the Targum Pseudo-Jonathan makes the magicians Balaam’s “two young men” (his servants) at Numbers 22:22, and the later apocryphon and rabbinic sources tighten that loose association into literal paternity — a development that also inherits the chronological strain of some forty years between the Exodus contest and Balaam’s journey. On whether 2 Timothy 3:8 quotes the lost book, opinion divides: some (J. H. Charlesworth; D.-A. Koch) hold that Paul may depend on the apocryphon, following Origen and Ambrosiaster, while Stephen Gero cautions that “in view of the widespread occurrence of the Jannes and Jambres legend this cannot be regarded as certain.” The safer statement — that the names belonged to a broad pre-Christian Jewish tradition, which Paul cites without our being able to pin the exact source — is the one most scholars now make. Both questions are weighed on their own argument pages.4

Notes

  • What the survey settles, and what it opens. It settles that a real lost book stood behind the ancient namings; that the legend was old, widespread, and Jewish in origin; and that the names trace to a Semitic onomastic tradition with a mobile second member. It opens — deliberately — where the names ultimately come from, whether the Damascus Document’s brother-pair is this pair, whether the Balaam sonship is more than a genealogical knitting-together, and whether Paul quoted the book. Each is a contested question weighed on its own page.
  • Reference points. The standard edition is Pietersma (1994); the standard short treatments are the entries in the Anchor Bible Dictionary, the Dictionary of Deities and Demons in the Bible, the Jewish Encyclopedia (1906) and Encyclopaedia Judaica, and the discussion in standard commentaries on 2 Timothy (on Paul’s use of tradition). Geza Vermes and the wider Dead Sea Scrolls scholarship carry the onomastic and Damascus-Document strands.
