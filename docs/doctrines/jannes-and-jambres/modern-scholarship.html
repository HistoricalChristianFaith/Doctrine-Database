Modern scholarship on Jannes and Jambres (a survey)

Dates: a survey of critical study, 19th century to the present — anchored by Albert Pietersma’s standard critical edition, The Apocryphon of Jannes and Jambres the Magicians (Brill, 1994), and the reference-work and commentary tradition around it.
Summary: Modern scholarship has edited, dated and traced the Jannes-and-Jambres tradition, and several themes recur. The lost book has been reassembled and edited from fragments in half a dozen languages, and dated as early as the first century. The names themselves are traced to a Jewish onomastic tradition (Yoḥana behind “Jannes”; an Aramaic root “to oppose” behind “Jambres”/“Mambres”), and an Egyptian derivation has also been proposed (Ewald, Lauth, Freudenthal). The Damascus Document’s naming, the “sons of Balaam” identification, and the question of whether 2 Timothy quoted the book are discussed as tradition-historical problems, and each is set out on its own page.

Claims & evidence

The critical edition: a lost book reassembled (Pietersma, 1994)

The foundation of all recent work is Albert Pietersma’s The Apocryphon of Jannes and Jambres the Magicians (Leiden: Brill, 1994) — the editio princeps of the principal Greek witness, Papyrus Chester Beatty XVI, with fresh editions of the Vienna papyrus and the Cotton Tiberius leaf, a full translation and commentary, and a survey of the ancient testimonia. Pietersma reconstructs a coherent narrative from some hundred fragments, dates the composition “perhaps as early as the first century C.E.,” and shows how widely the legend travelled — recounted, he notes, in more than half a dozen languages “from the Syriac east to the Latin west and from Egypt’s deserts to King Alfred’s court.” Reviewers welcomed the edition warmly (M. A. Knibb: “an excellent commentary”) while cautioning — as Pietersma himself does — that the fragments were long ago mounted in an order that is not certainly the book’s own, so the reconstructed sequence of episodes must be held loosely. The full account of the book, its fragments, and its contents is on its own detail page.1

The names: a Jewish onomastic tradition, and a rival Egyptian derivation

The origin of the names is the most-worked philological question. One reading is Semitic: “Jannes” goes back to a Hebrew/Aramaic Yoḥana (the form the Damascus Document writes yḥnh and the Talmud “Yoḥana”), a by-form of Yoḥanan/John; “Jambres” (Latin “Mambres”) is commonly derived from an Aramaic root meaning “to oppose,” whose participle would be Mambres, “he who opposes” (A. J. Maclean, 1909; the International Standard Bible Encyclopedia, 1915). Kaufmann Kohler (1906) reports that Ewald, Lauth and Freudenthal derived the names from Egyptian. Two data figure in the discussion. First, the earliest witnesses name only one magician — the Damascus Document’s “Yannes and his brother,” and the Latin writers Pliny and Apuleius, who give “Jannes” alone — and the second name has the more varied forms (Jambres / Mambres / Jamberes). Second, the first name’s forms across languages (Yoḥana, Iannes, Ianne, Iohannes) cluster around the Yoḥanan/John root. The reference works lay the options out: the entry in the Dictionary of Deities and Demons in the Bible (Pietersma), the Anchor Bible Dictionary, and Geza Vermes’ studies of the Aramaic tradition. The readings are set out at “Where do the names ‘Jannes and Jambres’ come from?”2

The Damascus Document: is it really this pair?

The Damascus Document’s “Yannes and his brother” (CD V.17–19) is treated in the reference works as the earliest extant naming of one of the magicians, older than 2 Timothy, with a qualification. Pietersma notes that scholars have equated the passage’s two brothers with Jannes and Jambres “without proper qualification,” since only one name is given and the identification with the Exodus magicians is an inference; he also records the debate over whether the passage is original to its context, siding with Philip R. Davies (who argued it is original and “eminently suits its current setting”). The passage attests, by c. 100 BC, a Jewish tradition that set a named magician and his brother against Moses and Aaron in a dualistic Prince-of-Lights / Belial frame; the full couplet “Jannes and Jambres” appears in later sources.3

The Balaam sonship, and whether 2 Timothy quoted the book

Two further questions recur. On the Balaam connection, the Targum Pseudo-Jonathan makes the magicians Balaam’s “two young men” at Numbers 22:22, which Kaufmann Kohler (1906) renders “two youths” and the Encyclopaedia Judaica cites among the sources calling them his sons; the apocryphon and later rabbinic sources make them his sons. The International Standard Bible Encyclopedia (1915) calls the Jewish traditions about the pair “full of contradictions and impossibilities and anachronisms,” noting the forty years between the Exodus contest and Balaam’s journey. On whether 2 Timothy 3:8 quotes the lost book, opinion divides: some (J. H. Charlesworth; D.-A. Koch) hold that 2 Timothy may depend on the apocryphon, following Origen and Ambrosiaster, while Stephen Gero cautions that “in view of the widespread occurrence of the Jannes and Jambres legend this cannot be regarded as certain.” Both questions are set out on their own pages: “Were Jannes and Jambres the sons of Balaam?” and “Did 2 Timothy 3:8 take the names from the Book of Jannes and Jambres?”4

Notes

  • Questions in the literature. The editions and reference works surveyed here report a lost book behind the ancient namings and a widespread legend of Jewish origin. The questions they discuss include where the names come from (set out here), whether the Damascus Document’s brother-pair is this pair, how the Balaam sonship arose (set out here), and whether 2 Timothy took the names from the book.
  • Reference points. The standard edition is Pietersma (1994); the standard short treatments are the entries in the Anchor Bible Dictionary, the Dictionary of Deities and Demons in the Bible, the Jewish Encyclopedia (1906) and Encyclopaedia Judaica, and the discussion in standard commentaries on 2 Timothy (on the letter’s use of tradition). Geza Vermes and the wider Dead Sea Scrolls scholarship carry the onomastic and Damascus-Document strands.
