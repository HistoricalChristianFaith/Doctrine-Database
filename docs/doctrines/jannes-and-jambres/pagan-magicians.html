The pagan writers: “Jannes” among the great magicians

Dates: c. AD 77 – early 4th century — Pliny the Elder, Natural History 30.2.11 (c. AD 77); Apuleius of Madauros, Apologia 90 (c. AD 158); Numenius of Apamea (2nd c.), preserved by Eusebius, Praeparatio Evangelica IX.8 (early 4th c.)
Summary: The pair’s fame did not stay within Judaism and the church. Three non-Jewish, non-Christian writers name them — the Roman encyclopaedist Pliny and the orator-philosopher Apuleius rank “Jannes” among the founders and celebrities of the magic art, alongside Moses and Zoroaster; and the Platonist Numenius retells the plague-contest, crediting “Jannes and Jambres” as Egyptian sacred scribes able to blunt the worst of Moses’ plagues. Their notices show the legend of Pharaoh’s magicians had crossed the Jewish/Christian fence into the wider Greco-Roman literature of magic, and they preserve two more variants of the unstable name (Pliny’s Latin Ianne, Apuleius’ Iohannes) that bear on where the names came from.

Claims & evidence

Pliny the Elder: a Jewish “branch of magic” from Moses and Jannes (c. AD 77)

The earliest pagan writer to name Jannes is Pliny the Elder, in the potted history of magic that opens Book 30 of his Natural History (published c. AD 77). Tracing the art from Zoroaster and the Persian magi down through its later offshoots, Pliny notes a distinct Jewish school:

There is another sect, also, of adepts in the magic art, who derive their origin from Moses, Jannes, and Lotapea, Jews by birth, but many thousand years posterior to Zoroaster.

— Pliny, Natural History 30.2.11 (John Bostock’s translation). The Latin runs “est et alia magices factio a Mose et Ianne et Iotape ac Iudaeis pendens, sed multis milibus annorum post Zoroastren.” Three things are worth marking. First, this is a hostile witness turned back-handed compliment: Pliny thought all magic “detestable” and an “utter falsity,” yet he ranks Jannes with Moses and Zoroaster among the founders of a school — the measure of how famous the name had become. Second, as in the Damascus Document, only one of the pair is named: “Jannes,” not Jambres — a datum for the name-origin question, since the fixed couplet “Jannes and Jambres” is evidently a later filling-out. Third, the form of the name is again unstable: Pliny’s Latin gives Ianne (the equivalent of “Jannes”), and the third figure, “Lotapea”/Iotape, is an obscure name some scholars have read as a garbling of the divine name itself (C. C. Torrey: Iotape = ἰῶτα πῆ, i.e. Yah).1

Apuleius: “Jannes” in the roll of celebrated mages (c. AD 158)

Some eighty years later the name surfaces again, this time in a courtroom. The Platonist and novelist Apuleius of Madauros, on trial about AD 158 for allegedly using magic to win his wealthy wife, mocks the charge by rattling off the great magicians of legend — daring his accusers to call him one of them if they can show he ever profited by sorcery:

Ego ille sim Carmendas vel Damigeron vel <Moses vel Iohannes vel Apollobex vel ipse> Dardanus vel quicumque alius post Zoroastren et Hostanen inter magos celebratus est.

— Apuleius, Apologia (Pro se de magia) 90: “Let me be that Carmendas or Damigeron or Moses or Iohannes or Apollobex or Dardanus himself or whoever else has been celebrated among the mages since Zoroaster and Ostanes.” The name Pliny wrote Ianne here appears as Iohannes — rendered “Jannes” in H. E. Butler’s standard English and “John” in some online translations — another witness to the name’s instability. Apuleius at once adds that knowing such names, which he had read “in the public library, in the writings of the most famous authors,” is a scholar’s learning, not a confession of witchcraft. The point for this timeline is the offhand assurance with which he expects a Roman court to recognise “Iohannes”/Jannes as a byword for the master magician: the figure had become common cultural property.2

Numenius the Platonist, by way of Eusebius: the contest retold (2nd c.)

The third pagan notice is not a bare name in a list but a retelling of the Exodus contest itself. The second-century Neopythagorean philosopher Numenius of Apamea told the story of Moses (whom he calls “Musaeus”) and his two Egyptian opponents; his words survive because the Christian apologist Eusebius quoted them, approvingly, in the Preparation for the Gospel:

And next in order came Jannes and Jambres, Egyptian sacred scribes, men judged to have no superiors in the practice of magic, at the time when the Jews were being driven out of Egypt. So then these were the men chosen by the people of Egypt as fit to stand beside Musaeus, who led forth the Jews, a man who was most powerful in prayer to God; and of the plagues which Musaeus brought upon Egypt, these men showed themselves able to disperse the most violent.

— Numenius, in Eusebius, Praeparatio Evangelica IX.8 (E. H. Gifford’s translation). Unlike Pliny and Apuleius, Numenius names both magicians, and calls them not “founders of magic” but Egyptian “sacred scribes” (hierogrammateis) — the temple-scholar profession Exodus assigns them. And where Exodus has the magicians fail, Numenius — writing in their favour — credits them with power to undo the fiercest of Moses’ plagues. His account (which Origen also cites, at Against Celsus IV.51) most likely goes back to the Hellenistic-Jewish historian Artapanus, the same Jewish substrate behind the lost Book of Jannes and Jambres, where this passage is set out in full among the book’s ancient testimonia.3

Notes

  • What the pagan witnesses add. They do not report anything the Jewish sources do not; their value is that they report it from outside. By the late first and second centuries “Jannes” was a name a Roman naturalist, an African orator, and a Greek Platonist could each drop without explanation and be understood — the same easy familiarity 2 Timothy assumes. The legend had genuinely crossed the fence.
  • Two profiles of the pair. The pagan notices split along the same seam the Jewish tradition does. Pliny and Apuleius file “Jannes” among the celebrity mage-founders (Zoroaster, Ostanes, Dardanus, Damigeron) — the pair as archetypal magicians. Numenius keeps them within the Exodus story as Egypt’s sacred scribes — the pair as Moses’ opponents. Both profiles are older than any surviving Christian discussion of the names.
  • “Only Jannes” again. That both Latin writers name Jannes alone — as the Damascus Document names only “Yannes and his brother” — is a recurring pattern: the senior magician’s name travelled first and widest, and Jambres was attached to it later. This bears on the (separately weighed) question of where the names came from and why they vary.
  • Name-forms for the record. Pliny’s Ianne and Apuleius’ Iohannes join the Greek “Jannes,” the Damascus Document’s Hebrew yḥnh (Yohana), the Talmud’s “Yoḥana,” and the Targum’s wandering “Jannis/Janis/Jannes” as evidence that the first magician’s name clustered around a Yoḥanan/John root long before it settled into the Greek “Jannes” of Paul.
