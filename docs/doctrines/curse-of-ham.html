The Curse of Ham

The eleven verses at the close of the Flood story record that Noah planted a vineyard, drank of the wine, and lay uncovered in his tent; that Ham “saw the nakedness of his father, and told his two brethren without,” while Shem and Japheth walked backward to cover him without looking; and that Noah, awaking, pronounced a curse — “Cursed be Canaan; a servant of servants shall he be unto his brethren” (Genesis 9:20–27). From the medieval period onward some readers applied the passage to the black peoples of Africa as a divine sentence of servitude, the “curse of Ham”; in the nineteenth century American pro-slavery writers cited it as a warrant for slavery, colonial anthropology reworked its “Hamite” category, and the Latter-day Saints’ priesthood restriction was given a curse-of-Cain/Ham rationale. Other readers, among them several of the Fathers, applied it to the Canaanites alone; modern historians have traced the origins of the racial application, and in 2013 the Latter-day Saints disavowed the curse theories once used to explain their priesthood restriction.

Three features of the wording are points that later readings take up or depart from. The curse names Canaan, Ham’s son, three times, and does not name Ham. Its stated content is servitude; the verses make no mention of skin colour. And Canaan is the name the following chapter gives to the ancestor of the Canaanite peoples of the Levant (Genesis 10:15–19). How readers related these features to Ham, to his other sons, and to later peoples is the subject of this page.

This page is a reception-history timeline: not a life of Ham or Canaan but a history of how the passage was read across the centuries, set out earliest source first. Its “witnesses” are the datable readings — the biblical text and its echoes in the conquest narratives, the Fathers’ typological and historical exegesis, the rabbinic aggadah that links Ham to dark skin, the medieval Islamic “dual curse” joining colour and slavery, the American pro-slavery reading and the colonial “Hamitic hypothesis,” and the modern scholarship and church statements on the passage. Three questions run through them: what Ham did, why Canaan rather than Ham is cursed, and whether the curse reaches any people beyond the Canaanites.

Timeline

The biblical text — a curse on Canaan, and the conquest

The tradition grows from Genesis 9:20–27. Ham’s act is described briefly — he “saw the nakedness of his father, and told his two brethren without” — and his brothers respond by walking backward, faces averted, to cover their father with a garment. When Noah wakes and “knew what his younger son had done unto him,” he curses Canaan: “a servant of servants shall he be unto his brethren.” The curse names Canaan three times (9:25–27) and Ham not once; its content is servitude; and Canaan is the eponym of the Canaanite peoples of the Levant.1 Later books record the subjection of Canaanite groups to Israel — the Gibeonites made “hewers of wood and drawers of water” (Joshua 9:27), the Canaanite cities placed under tribute — and Nahum Sarna reads the curse as “the first intimation of the theme of the corruption of the Canaanites… the justification for their being dispossessed of their land.”2

Justin Martyr — the curse read as national history (c. 155)

The earliest Christian exposition on this timeline is Justin Martyr’s, in the Dialogue with Trypho. Justin holds that the curse falls on Canaan because Ham could not be cursed — “the Spirit of prophecy would not curse the son that had been by God blessed… he made the curse originate with his son” — and reads it as fulfilled in the history of nations: Shem’s line (Israel) dispossesses the Canaanites, and Japheth’s line in turn dispossesses Israel, “to the two peoples… the one people of Canaan handed over for servants.” He closes with the gospel offered to “all men everywhere, whether bond or free.” In his reading the servitude is the subjection of the Canaanite people.3

The typological reading — Noah’s drunkenness as a figure of the Passion (Cyprian, c. 250)

A parallel Christian strand read the scene sacramentally. For Cyprian the wine that overcame Noah prefigures the cup of Christ’s passion: “Noah… drank wine… and thus expressed in advance the figure of the passion of the Lord,” his exposure and covering a type of the cross despised by some and honoured by others.4 Jerome sets out the allegory — Noah “drank and was inebriated, and his thighs were laid bare… The older brothers, the Jews, came along and laughed; the younger, the Gentiles, covered up his ignominy” — and Bede gives the reading in full. In this strand the scene is read as a figure of the cross, alongside the literal-historical reading.5

The rabbinic tradition — Ham’s deed and Ham’s skin (c. 3rd–6th century)

The classical rabbinic sources add two traditions not found in the Genesis text. First, an account of Ham’s deed: the sages debated whether Ham castrated Noah or sodomized him (Sanhedrin 70a), the castration view reasoning that, having prevented a fourth son, Ham was punished through his own fourth son. Second, an account of dark skin: among three who “violated” the prohibition of intercourse on the ark — the dog, the raven, and Ham — “Ham was afflicted in that his skin turned black” (Sanhedrin 108b); Genesis Rabbah 36:7 links Ham’s darkening to darkness by wordplay. In Sanhedrin 108b the blackening is a punishment for the ark-sin, told apart from Noah’s curse of servitude.6

Ephrem the Syrian — Canaan as the offender (c. 373)

Reading Genesis in Syriac, Ephrem asks: “what sin could Canaan have committed?” He records two answers — Ham spared because blessed, and Canaan the real offender (read out of “his youngest son,” Ham being the middle son) — and leans to the second: Canaan “was cursed justly for he was not cursed in the place of another.” The servitude he reads as history: Canaan “became their slave when in the days of Joshua son of Nun, the Israelites destroyed the dwelling places of Canaan and pressed their leaders into bondage.”7

John Chrysostom — intemperance, and each “liable for the sins he has committed” (c. 388)

Preaching through Genesis at Antioch, Chrysostom reads the episode as a lesson in temperance — the vine is not evil, only its abuse — and answers the why-Canaan question in terms of individual desert. Ham was spared explicit cursing because “he had enjoyed blessing from God,” yet “did not endure less punishment,” suffering through his son; and Canaan bore the curse not merely for his father’s sin but “atoning for his own failings” — for “parents are not punished for their children, nor children for their parents, each being liable for the sins he has committed” (Ezekiel 18).8

Augustine — slavery as penal, from sin, not from nature (c. 420)

In the City of God Augustine notes that this passage is where the word “slave” first appears in Scripture, and reads it as an account of slavery as a consequence of sin: “We do not find the word ‘slave’ in any part of Scripture until righteous Noah branded the sin of his son with this name. It is a name, therefore, introduced by sin and not by nature… by nature, as God first created us, no one is the slave either of man or of sin. This servitude is, however, penal.” Slavery, for Augustine, falls under God’s just order as a penalty of sin, and the master’s pride harms him more than the servant’s lowliness harms the servant. In Against Julian he cites the same episode for descendants bearing the temporal effects of a forebear’s sin.9

Bede — Canaan’s line and Ham’s other sons (c. 720)

The Venerable Bede gathers the tradition into one commentary, laying a typological reading (Ham the mocker = the unbelieving Jews; Shem and Japheth = the Church that veils the cross) over a literal one in which Canaan is cursed because his line — the Sodomites and the Canaanites — would prove the most wicked of Ham’s descendants and be subdued when “the Israelite people, descended from the lineage of Shem, came out of Egypt.” He names Ham’s other lines and sets them apart from the curse: “from Cush came the Ethiopians, from Mizraim the Egyptians, from Phut came the Libyans… among whom there is absolutely nothing of such wickedness or vengeance as Scripture relates about the Sodomites and Canaanites.” In Bede’s reading the curse is Canaan’s, and its scope a named people.10

The Islamic transmission — the “dual curse” of colour and slavery (c. 7th–14th century)

In medieval Islamic historians and Tales of the Prophets collections, Noah’s curse falls on Ham himself and joins altered colour to enslavement: Noah prays that Ham’s “color would be altered and his descendants would be the slaves of Shem and Japheth.” David Goldenberg holds that this dual curse “evolved out of the earlier Muslim dark-skin etiologies… joined to the story of Noah’s curse of slavery” as black African slavery expanded, and notes that “the Muslim writers all put the dual curse on Ham, while the Jewish and Christian authors generally see Canaan as the one who was cursed.” Within the same tradition Ibn Khaldūn (d. 1406) rejected the Ham-curse explanation of blackness — “the curse of Noah upon his son [is found] in the Torah. No reference is made there to blackness” — and explained African complexion by climate.11

The American pro-slavery reading and the “Hamitic hypothesis” (c. 1840–1930)

In the American pro-slavery literature the passage became an opening proof-text. Thornton Stringfellow called Noah’s words “the first recorded language which was ever uttered in relation to slavery… God decreed slavery”; Josiah Priest applied the curse to “the whole Hamite race… under a peculiar liability of being enslaved,” glossing the verse “Canaan (Ham)” and so extending it from Canaan to Ham and from a named people to a race. Colonial anthropology then recast the same “Hamite” category as a superior light-skinned civilizer, the “Hamitic hypothesis” traced by Edith Sanders; Mahmood Mamdani connects its application to East Africa with the racial ideology of the 1994 Rwandan genocide.12

Modern scholarship and church statements

Historians of interpretation (Peterson, Haynes, Johnson) trace how the antebellum reading was built and used. The philologist David Goldenberg argues that there is “no etymological relationship” between the name Ham and the words for “black” or “hot,” and that the blackness and slavery etiologies were originally separate, fused as the slave trade grew. In 1995 the Southern Baptist Convention “genuinely repent[ed] of racism”; the Latter-day Saints, who had barred black members from their priesthood on a curse-of-Ham/Cain rationale, lifted the restriction in 1978 and in 2013 “disavow[ed] the theories… that black skin is a sign of divine disfavor or curse.”13
