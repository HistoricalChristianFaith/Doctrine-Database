The New Testament on James son of Alphaeus

Dates: c. 30–90 (the events, and the writing of the Gospels and Acts) · Wikipedia
Summary: The earliest extant sources on James the son of Alphaeus name him only in the four lists of the Twelve, always ninth, and record nothing else about him — no saying, deed, mission or death. The Gospels also name a “James the less,” son of a Mary, at the cross, and Paul and Acts a “James the brother of the Lord” who led the Jerusalem church. How these Jameses relate has been answered in more than one way (see the summary timeline).

Claims & evidence

Named in all four lists of the Twelve — and nowhere else

James son of Alphaeus appears in every list of the apostles and at no other point in the New Testament. In each he stands ninth, heading the third group of four (the group that also contains Thaddaeus/Jude and Simon the Zealot). His father’s name, Alphaeus, is all the personal detail the lists supply.

… James the son of Alphaeus, and Lebbaeus, whose surname was Thaddaeus; Simon the Canaanite, and Judas Iscariot…

— Matthew 10:3–4 (cf. Mark 3:18; Luke 6:15; Acts 1:13).

“James the less” at the cross

Among the women who watched the crucifixion Mark names “Mary the mother of James the less and of Joses.” The Greek is Iakōbos ho mikros — “James the little” (the KJV’s “less” renders a Latin comparative that the Greek positive does not carry). Jerome, as J. B. Lightfoot summarizes his argument, took “the less” to be “a term which implies only one besides,” and so to tell against there being three Jameses (“The Brethren of the Lord,” 1865).

There were also women looking on afar off: among whom was Mary Magdalene, and Mary the mother of James the less and of Joses, and Salome…

— Mark 15:40 (cf. Matthew 27:56). Jerome (c. 383) took this “James the little” to be the son of Alphaeus — and, through his mother Mary, to be the Lord’s brother as well; the identification and its reception are set out on the perpetual-virginity timeline.

James the brother of the Lord

The New Testament also names James the brother of the Lord, whom Paul met at Jerusalem and who presided over the council of Acts 15.

But other of the apostles saw I none, save James the Lord’s brother.

— Galatians 1:19. This James rose to lead the mother church (Acts 15:13; Acts 21:18). Matthew 13:55 names his brethren as James, Joses, Simon, and Judas; John 7:5 says “neither did his brethren believe in him”; after the Ascension “his brethren” are listed with the apostles (Acts 1:14). Lightfoot read these notices as setting the brethren apart from the Twelve: in Acts 1:14, “though with the Twelve, they are distinguished from the Twelve.” Jerome, as Lightfoot reports, met John 7:5 “by saying that James was not one of those that disbelieved” (“The Brethren of the Lord,” 1865).

Reception and debate

  • What the New Testament records of each James. The New Testament gives James son of Zebedee a story (called with John, martyred by Herod, Acts 12:2) and James the brother of the Lord a large one (Jerusalem’s leader). To James son of Alphaeus it gives only a name and a place in the lists. The accounts of his mission and death traced on the summary timeline come from later sources, several of which report of him what Hegesippus reports of James the brother of the Lord.
  • The Alphaeus of Matthew, and the Alphaeus of Levi. Mark also calls the tax-collector “Levi the son of Alphaeus” (Mark 2:14). Whether that is the same Alphaeus — making James and Levi/Matthew brothers — the New Testament never says, and none of the sources on this timeline draws that inference.
  • The three names and the identification. The New Testament names a James son of a Mary (Mark 15:40), a James the Lord’s brother (Galatians 1:19), and a James son of Alphaeus (the lists), and does not state in so many words whether any two are the same man. Jerome (c. 383) identified them, reading Galatians 1:19 as counting James among the apostles; Lightfoot (1865) concluded from the Gospel notices of the brethren that “the Hieronymian account must be abandoned.” See the summary timeline.
