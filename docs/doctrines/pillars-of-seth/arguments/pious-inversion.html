How are the pillars of Seth related to the Watchers' stone-carved astrology in Jubilees 8?

Disputed reading · on The Pillars of Seth · witness: Josephus

The passage

And the son grew, and his father taught him writing, and he went to seek for himself a place where he might seize for himself a city. And he found a writing which former (generations) had carved on the rock, and he read what was thereon, and he transcribed it and sinned owing to it; for it contained the teaching of the Watchers in accordance with which they used to observe the omens of the sun and moon and stars in all the signs of heaven. And he wrote it down and said nothing regarding it; for he was afraid to speak to Noah about it lest he should be angry with him on account of it.

— Jubilees 8:2–4 (2nd c. BC), trans. R. H. Charles, of the second Cainan.1 Josephus, two centuries later, writes that Seth's posterity "were the inventors of that peculiar sort of wisdom which is concerned with the heavenly bodies, and their order," and inscribed it on two pillars to outlast fire and flood.2

The question

Both texts tell of celestial knowledge carved on durable stone that survives the Flood and is recovered afterward; in Jubilees the knowledge is the Watchers' and its recovery is a sin, in Josephus it is the Sethites' own discovery and its survival is a rescue. What is disputed is how the two tellings are related: as one antediluvian line seen from two sides, as two opposed lines whose knowledge is told in counterpart, or as separate uses of a common motif. The answer bears on where the pillars legend sits among the ancient accounts of the origin of astronomy.

One antediluvian line: the Watchers were Seth's descendants

On this reading the "Watchers" who brought astronomy into the world and the line of Seth are the same people, so the two tellings describe one line.

Held by: Panodorus of Alexandria (fl. c. AD 395–408), as reconstructed by William Adler (Harvard Theological Review, 1983; Time Immemorial, 1989).3

The Alexandrian monk-chronographer Panodorus, whose world-chronicle survives through George Syncellus, made the Watchers the originators of antediluvian science. Before they "descended upon earth," mankind "knew not the number of the days of either the month or the year," until the Watchers "taught them concerning the orbits of the two luminaries, and their division into the twelve signs… and into three hundred and sixty degrees." Adler argues that Panodorus likely identified those Watchers with the Sethites, and the "daughters of men" with the line of Cain, following a reading of Genesis 6:1ff. found in Julius Africanus and widespread in Christian chronography; Adler notes that the Sethite identification is his inference from Syncellus, not Panodorus's explicit words. On that reconstruction the righteous discoverers and the Watcher-teachers of celestial knowledge are one antediluvian line. Later Jewish tradition performs a related merger from the other side. The second Cainan of Jubilees 8, son of Arphaxad, present in the Septuagint of Genesis 11:13 and in Luke 3:36, is folded together with the antediluvian Kenan, grandson of Seth: the Sefer ha-Yashar makes Cainan a wise king who "knew by his wisdom that God would destroy the sons of men for having sinned upon earth, and that the Lord would in the latter days bring upon them the waters of the flood," and who therefore "wrote upon tablets of stone, what was to take place in time to come, and he put them in his treasures." In both, the stone-inscribing sage of the impious telling and the line of Seth become a single figure or a single people.

Two opposed lines, their knowledge told in counterpart

On this reading the Sethite pillars are the righteous counterpart of the Watchers' stone: the same device, told once as virtue and once as sin, with the impious form attested first.

Held by: John Cassian (Conferences 8.21, c. AD 435); Nadav Sharon (TheTorah.com, 2018), on the contrast and the priority of Jubilees, as quoted here.4

Sharon states the contrast: "Unlike Jubilees, where knowledge of astrology is considered destructive and the providence of the sinful Watchers, according to Josephus, such knowledge is a good thing, and the providence of the righteous sons of Seth." He identifies the Jubilees notice as "the earliest reference to the survival of antediluvian knowledge after the flood." The device is shared in its parts: knowledge of the sun, moon and stars, committed to stone, carried across the Flood, recovered on the far side. In Josephus the inscribers are the righteous line of Seth and the survival is deliberate; in Jubilees the inscription carries the Watchers' teaching, its recovery is furtive, and Cainan "sinned owing to it." Jubilees is a work of the 2nd century BC and the Antiquities of c. AD 93–94, so the negative form is attested about two centuries before the positive one. John Cassian holds both halves in one chapter. Ham, "who had been taught these superstitions and wicked and profane arts," knowing "he could not possibly bring any handbook on these subjects into the ark," "inscribed these nefarious arts and profane devices on plates of various metals which could not be destroyed by the flood of waters, and on hard rocks, and when the flood was over he hunted for them." In the same chapter "this knowledge then of all nature the seed of Seth received through successive generations… so long as it remained separate from the wicked line." On this reading the pious and impious tellings answer each other: two lines, two moral valences, one physical device for carrying knowledge across the Flood.5

Separate uses of a common antediluvian-knowledge motif

On this reading the Jubilees stone and the Sethite pillars are independent tellings that draw on a motif common to Jewish lore, not one framed against the other.

Held by: ⚠ unverified — no named proponent located yet.

The two stories differ in their design. In Jubilees the stone is carved by "former (generations)" and found by Cainan as he seeks a city; the notice tells of a found writing, without paired media or a reckoning of fire and water. Josephus's pillars are built by their makers for that purpose, two media against two destructions, and that design is told elsewhere of righteous figures: of Seth in the Life of Adam and Eve, where "Seth made the tables" of stone and clay against water and fire, and of Enoch in the Byzantine Palaea Historica, writing "on marble (tablets) and on bricks." Both moral valences of celestial knowledge were current in Josephus's own world: his Antiquities sets the Sethite pillars (I.2.3) and the "many angels of God" who begat unjust giants (I.3.1) a page apart. On this reading Josephus could draw the virtuous Sethite archive from the same stock of antediluvian-knowledge lore that also produced the Watchers' stone, each telling developing its own valence within a shared complex.6
