Is the pillars-of-Seth legend a recasting of an Enoch tradition?

The passage

They also were the inventors of that peculiar sort of wisdom which is concerned with the heavenly bodies, and their order.

And that their inventions might not be lost before they were sufficiently known, upon Adam's prediction that the world was to be destroyed at one time by the force of fire, and at another time by the violence and quantity of water, they made two pillars, the one of brick, the other of stone: they inscribed their discoveries on them both, that in case the pillar of brick should be destroyed by the flood, the pillar of stone might remain, and exhibit those discoveries to mankind; and also inform them that there was another pillar of brick erected by them. Now this remains in the land of Siriad to this day.

— Josephus, Antiquities of the Jews I.2.3 (c. AD 93–94), of the posterity of Seth, trans. William Whiston, gutenberg.org.1

The question

Josephus gives Seth's posterity the discovery of the science of the heavens, its writing down, and its preservation on two media against fire and flood. Jewish writings older and younger than Josephus give several of the same roles to Enoch, the seventh from Adam. What is disputed is whether the Sethite story is a recasting of Enochic tradition or a Sethite telling with its own roots. The answer bears on where the legend began.

Later attributions of the pillars

The pillars passed between several antediluvian figures in later retellings. In the Latin West Peter Comestor (c. 1170) gave them to the Cainite Jubal (see Peter Comestor on the Pillars of Seth). The medieval Masonic "Old Charges" carry the two-pillars story and credit the children of Lamech: the Matthew Cooke Manuscript (c. 1410–1450), citing Ranulf Higden's Polychronicon, has them "make 2 pillars of these 2 stones, that is to say of marble and of latres," recovered after the Flood when "Pythagoras found that one, and Hermes, the philosopher, found that other." James Anderson's Constitutions of the Free-Masons (1723) writes that "godly ENOCH … erected his two large Pillars, (tho' some ascribe them to Seth) the one of Stone, and the other of Brick, whereon were engraven the Liberal Sciences, &c."; the 1738 edition and the nineteenth-century Royal Arch and George Oliver material elaborate these "pillars of Enoch." A. G. Mackey judged that in ascribing the pillars "either to Seth or to Enoch" the later editors, "equally with Anderson," had "abandon[ed] the real tradition of the old Legend, which gave them to the children of Lamech."2

Enoch's roles transferred to Seth's line

On this reading the functions Josephus gives the Sethites belong in the older tradition to Enoch, and the Sethite pillars are a recasting of Enochic lore.

Held by: R. H. Charles, as reported by M. R. James (1920), on Kedrenos's Seth material; Andrei A. Orlov (Journal for the Study of Judaism, 2001).3

Orlov states the observation: "although Josephus refers to Seth and his progeny, some features of the 'two stelae' story allude to peculiar roles and situations which Jewish lore traditionally associates with the seventh antediluvian patriarch Enoch." The thesis is not that Josephus muddled the names, but that the functions are Enoch's. Jubilees 4:17 calls Enoch "the first among men that are born on earth who learnt writing and knowledge and wisdom and who wrote down the signs of heaven according to the order of their months in a book"; the Astronomical Book of 1 Enoch (chs. 72–82) has the angel Uriel show him the courses of the luminaries and the calendar; the Qumran Book of Giants names him "Enoch the distinguished scribe." The discovery itself is credited to Enoch by Pseudo-Eupolemus, an anonymous Samaritan writer of c. 200–150 BC quoted by Eusebius, who traces "the first discovery to Enoch, and saying that he, and not the Egyptians, had first invented astrology," and adds that "Atlas is the same as Enoch." The closest formal parallel is told of Enoch himself: the Byzantine Palaea Historica has Enoch warn the giants "that the earth would either be destroyed by fire or by water," and shows him "sitting and writing on marble (tablets) and on bricks" — the paired-media, dual-catastrophe device Josephus gives the Sethites. Orlov traces this "two tablets" motif from the Book of Giants to the Palaea. In the Byzantine chronicles the transfer can be seen at work: Kedrenos's Seth is "caught away by an angel and instructed" about the Flood and the coming of the Saviour, and Charles, as James reports, regarded this as "an attempt to transfer" to Seth "the wisdom and the position which properly belonged to Enoch." On this reading the Sethite pillars are Enoch's archive under another patriarch's name.4

Sethite, Enochic and Near Eastern sage traditions side by side

On this reading the Sethite telling draws on its own older theme of a virtuous Seth and on non-Enochic stele traditions, alongside the Enochic ones, without being a recasting of any one of them.

Held by: ⚠ unverified — no named proponent located yet.

A virtuous-Seth theme stands on its own a generation before Josephus. Philo of Alexandria makes Seth the "author" of the race of "those who love virtue and piety," enlarged through Noah, Abraham and Moses, with no pillars, no astronomy and no Enoch in view. The pillar-and-stele motif has non-Enochic forms as well: Berossus has the flood-hero bury the writings of the antediluvians at Sippar and dig them up after, and the Book of Sothis attached to Manetho tells of sacred columns in the "Seriadic land" inscribed by Thoth-Hermes before the flood and re-transcribed after, a fragment preserved by George Syncellus. The two-media device is told of Seth in the Life of Adam and Eve, where "Seth made the tables" of stone and clay against water and fire. On this reading the material Josephus wove together came from more than one source: Sethite, Enochic, Babylonian and Egyptian tellings of the antediluvian sage ran in parallel and cross-fertilized. The legend's later history shows the same mobility, the pillars passing to Jubal, to Lamech's children and to Enoch, and Higden giving a second version to Zoroaster with fourteen pillars of brass and burnt tile.5 On this reading the legend is one branch of a shared antediluvian-sage complex in which Seth had a place of his own.
