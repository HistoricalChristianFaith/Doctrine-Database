Where does the fire-and-water double destruction in the pillars story come from?

Disputed reading · on The Pillars of Seth · witness: Josephus

Related readings: What is the pillar "in the land of Siriad" that Josephus says still stood in his day? · Is the pillars-of-Seth legend a recasting of an Enoch tradition? · How are the pillars of Seth related to the Watchers' stone-carved astrology in Jubilees 8? · How is the Surid pyramid legend related to the pillars-of-Seth tradition? · How are the Sethian-Gnostic Seth texts related to the pillars legend?

The kataklysmos half of the pair is the deluge of Noah's Flood; the great-year reckoning that ties the two catastrophes to planetary conjunctions belongs with Astrology and the Stars.

The passage

And that their inventions might not be lost before they were sufficiently known, upon Adam's prediction that the world was to be destroyed at one time by the force of fire, and at another time by the violence and quantity of water, they made two pillars, the one of brick, the other of stone: they inscribed their discoveries on them both, that in case the pillar of brick should be destroyed by the flood, the pillar of stone might remain, and exhibit those discoveries to mankind; and also inform them that there was another pillar of brick erected by them. Now this remains in the land of Siriad to this day.

— Josephus, Antiquities of the Jews I.2.3 (c. AD 93–94), trans. William Whiston, gutenberg.org.1

The question

The whole legend hangs on the expectation that the world would be destroyed once by fire and once by water. What is disputed is where that expectation comes from and what it is: a recurring cycle of nature, a prophecy given to the first family, a judgment on human wickedness first taught by Moses, or an old and widespread tradition that Josephus took over. The answer bears on how far the pillars story is Josephus's own construction.

Catastrophes that recur in a cosmic cycle

On this reading the world is destroyed by fire and by flood again and again, at long intervals set by the heavens.

Held by: the Egyptian priest in Plato's Timaeus (c. 360 BC); Berossus (early 3rd c. BC, as reported by Seneca); Celsus (2nd c., as quoted by Origen); Censorinus (De Die Natali, AD 238).2

In the Timaeus an aged priest of Saïs tells Solon: "There have been and there will be many and divers destructions of mankind, of which the greatest are by fire and water, and lesser ones by countless other means." He reads the Greek myth of Phaethon as the memory of "a destruction of the things on the earth by fierce fire, which recurs at long intervals," alternating with the deluges of which the Greeks remember only Deucalion's. The pattern is offered as old Egyptian priestly knowledge. Berossus gives it astronomical machinery: the earth "will burn when all the stars that now pursue separate paths converge in the constellation of Cancer," and the flood will come "when the same grouping of stars occurs in Capricorn" — the summer and winter solstitial signs, so that ekpyrosis and kataklysmos are the seasons of a great year. Ovid, writing about AD 8, has Jupiter hold back his thunderbolts as he looses the flood, recalling that the fates have fixed a coming time when sea, earth and heaven will burn. Celsus states that "after lengthened cycles of time, and the returns and conjunctions of planets, conflagrations and floods are wont to happen," the last flood being Deucalion's and a conflagration due next. Censorinus states the pair as the two seasons of the cosmic Great Year: it "has a great winter called by the Greeks the Inundation and by the Latins The Deluge; it has also a summer which the Greeks call the Conflagration of the world." On this reading the doubled catastrophe belongs to the order of the cosmos, and the knowledge of its timing belongs to the science of the stars.3

A prophecy given to the first family

On this reading the two judgments were foretold to Adam and Eve, and their children prepared for them.

Held by: Josephus (Antiquities I.2.3, c. AD 93–94); the Life of Adam and Eve (Latin Vita Adae et Evae 49–50, its underlying tradition dated to the 1st c. AD ⚠ unverified).4

Josephus grounds the pillars on "Adam's prediction that the world was to be destroyed at one time by the force of fire, and at another time by the violence and quantity of water." The Life of Adam and Eve tells the same story at greater length. The dying Eve tells her children that the archangel Michael warned of a coming judgment on the race "first by water and second by fire," and instructs them: "Make ye then tables of stone and others of clay, and write on them all my life and your father's… If by water the Lord judge our race, the tables of clay will be dissolved and the tables of stone will remain; but if by fire, the tables of stone will be broken up and the tables of clay will be baked (hard)." The text adds: "Thereupon Seth made the tables." Here the whole structure of Josephus's notice is assembled in a Jewish source: the line of Adam through Seth, a coming judgment by both water and fire, and two media chosen so that each survives the catastrophe the other cannot. The Vita names "Enoch the seventh from Adam" as the one whose prophecy the stones were later found to preserve. On this reading the double destruction is not a cycle of nature but two judgments of God announced in advance, once each, and the knowledge of them is a revelation kept by the righteous line.

Judgments on wickedness, taught first by Moses

On this reading floods and conflagrations come from human wickedness, and the Greek cycles are later misunderstandings of what Moses and the prophets taught.

Held by: Origen (Contra Celsum 4.11–12, c. AD 248).5

Celsus charged that Jews and Christians had misread the old accounts of periodic floods and conflagrations. Origen answers from chronology and from causes. Moses lived long before the Greek historians on whom Celsus relies; Origen points to Josephus's writings on Jewish antiquities as evidence of it. Moses and the prophets did not borrow their teaching about a future conflagration from the Greeks; Origen suggests it was others who misunderstood that teaching and devised recurring cycles from it. Origen sets aside whether cosmic cycles exist and places the cause of floods and conflagrations in the spread of wickedness and its removal, not in the periods of the planets. The language of God "coming down" to judge he reads figuratively, as a teacher is said to condescend to children. On this reading the fire-and-water expectation is real and ancient, its first form is the biblical one, and its meaning is moral: each destruction answers the wickedness of the world it falls on.

An ancient tradition that Josephus inherited

On this reading the double destruction is an old and widely attested belief that Josephus took over and set at the head of his story.

Held by: William Whiston (translator's note to Antiquities I.2.3, 1737).6

Whiston's note on the passage reads: "…Adam might foretell a conflagration and a deluge, which all antiquity witnesses to be an ancient tradition…" On this reading Josephus did not make up the premise his two pillars depend on; the belief that the world would be destroyed once by fire and once by water was already old and widespread when he wrote it down, and the legend's framing device, a pair of durable media hedged against a pair of catastrophes, is a shared inheritance. On this reading the comparanda gathered under the other readings attest that older tradition: Plato's Egyptian priest, Berossus and the Stoic great year, the Latin poets and encyclopedists, the dispute between Celsus and Origen over the cause rather than the fact of the alternation, and the Life of Adam and Eve, which carries the Sethite frame and the paired media. Whiston's sentence keeps two things together: the expectation is an ancient tradition, and Adam "might foretell" it. The tradition's attestation in many peoples and the story's ascription of it to the first patriarch stand side by side in his account.7
