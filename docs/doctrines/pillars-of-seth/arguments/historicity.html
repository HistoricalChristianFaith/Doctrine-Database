What is the pillar "in the land of Siriad" that Josephus says still stood in his day?

Disputed reading · on The Pillars of Seth · witness: Josephus

The passage

And that their inventions might not be lost before they were sufficiently known, upon Adam's prediction that the world was to be destroyed at one time by the force of fire, and at another time by the violence and quantity of water, they made two pillars, the one of brick, the other of stone: they inscribed their discoveries on them both, that in case the pillar of brick should be destroyed by the flood, the pillar of stone might remain, and exhibit those discoveries to mankind; and also inform them that there was another pillar of brick erected by them. Now this remains in the land of Siriad to this day.

— Josephus, Antiquities of the Jews I.2.3 (c. AD 93–94), trans. William Whiston, gutenberg.org.1

The question

Josephus closes his account of the Sethite pillars by saying that the stone pillar still stands "in the land of Siriad" (Greek Seiris / Seirida). What is disputed is what that sentence records: a surviving Sethite monument, an Egyptian royal pillar taken for Seth's, or a pillar of an Egyptian sacred tradition. The answer bears on how the legend's origins are told.

A surviving pillar of Seth's posterity

On this reading the sentence means what it says: the stone pillar raised by Seth's descendants outlasted the Flood and still stood in Josephus's day.

Held by: Josephus (Antiquities I.2.3, c. AD 93–94); repeated, as a report, in the Latin chronicle tradition by Isidore of Seville (Chronica, c. 615–626), Peter Comestor (c. 1170) and Ranulf Higden (c. 1350).2

Josephus sets the notice inside the genealogy of Seth's line, whose members "were the inventors of that peculiar sort of wisdom which is concerned with the heavenly bodies, and their order." The two pillars are built for a stated purpose: Adam had foretold one destruction by fire and one by water, so the discoveries were written on brick and on stone, so "that in case the pillar of brick should be destroyed by the flood, the pillar of stone might remain." The closing sentence reports that this design worked: the stone pillar is still there, and it still tells mankind that a second pillar of brick once stood beside it. The survival of the stone pillar is the point of the story, and Josephus gives it a place. The Latin chroniclers who carried the legend kept the sentence: Isidore writes that the stone columns "are reported to have withstood the flood and remain in Syria to this very day"; Comestor that the marble column remains in terra Syriaca; Higden that "they say" (ferunt) the stone column escaped the Flood and "still remains in Syria" (adhuc in Syria permansisse). Across a thousand years of retelling, the surviving pillar stayed part of the tradition, located in a named land.

An Egyptian royal pillar mistaken for Seth's

On this reading Josephus, or his source, took a pillar of the Egyptian king Seth or Sesostris for a pillar of Seth son of Adam.

Held by: William Whiston (translator's notes to Antiquities I.2.3, 1737; Essay towards Restoring the True Text of the Old Testament, 1722).3

Whiston's note names the error he finds: "Of Josephus's mistake here, when he took Seth the son of Adam, for Seth or Sesostris, king of Egypt, the erector of this pillar in the land of Siriad." He renders Seiris as Syria. The Greek historian Herodotus (Histories 2.102–106) reports that the conquering pharaoh Sesostris set up inscribed victory stelae in the lands he subdued, and says he saw surviving examples in Syria-Palestine.4 Whiston's note gives a second ground. He grants that "the main of this relation might be true, and Adam might foretell a conflagration and a deluge, which all antiquity witnesses to be an ancient tradition"; but a pillar from before the Flood could not have outlasted it: "it is no way credible that they could survive the deluge, which has buried all such pillars and edifices far under ground in the sediment of its waters, especially since the like pillars of the Egyptian Seth or Sesostris were extant after the flood, in the land of Siriad." On Whiston's account the legend's frame is old, and the visible pillar of Josephus's own day belongs to Egypt's post-Flood history.

A pillar in the land of the Sirius-worshippers

On this reading "Siriad" names Egypt, the land of the worshippers of the Dog-Star, and Josephus's pillar belongs with the Egyptian traditions of sacred columns inscribed before the Flood.

Held by: H. St. J. Thackeray (Loeb Josephus IV, 1930), on Seiris as Egypt; Tobias Churton (The Lost Pillars of Enoch, 2021), on Seirida as the land of the Seirios-worshippers and on Whiston's rendering.5

Thackeray's note to Antiquities I.71 takes Seiris as Egypt, the land of the worshippers of Seirios, Sirius, whose heliacal rising governed the Egyptian calendar. Churton writes that Whiston "did not realise that the Greek word 'Seirida' was used in late antiquity, not to refer to 'Syria,' but rather to denote the land of those who worshipped 'Seirios'", and identifies several sites that may have been understood in antiquity as home to an antediluvian pillar; he leaves open which way any borrowing ran between Jewish and Graeco-Egyptian tellings. Read this way, the pillar stands among Egyptian parallels. The preface of the Book of Sothis, attached to Manetho's name and preserved in George Syncellus's Chronography, speaks of sacred books copied from "the columns set up in the Seriadic land, by Thoth, the first Hermes," and re-transcribed after the deluge: the same region-name, and the same before-and-after-the-Flood logic. Berossus has the flood-hero bury the antediluvian writings at Sippar and dig them up after, and the Hermetic Kore Kosmou has Hermes hide his books "beyond the reach of destruction and of decay" and engrave teachings "on tablet and on obelisk."6 On this reading his land of Siriad is Egypt, and his pillar is one instance of a stele-lore that Babylonian, Egyptian and Hermetic writers also told.
