How is the Surid pyramid legend related to the pillars-of-Seth tradition?

Disputed reading · on The Pillars of Seth · witness: al-Maqrizi and the medieval Arab-Coptic pyramid legend

Related readings: What is the pillar "in the land of Siriad" that Josephus says still stood in his day? · Is the pillars-of-Seth legend a recasting of an Enoch tradition? · Where does the fire-and-water double destruction in the pillars story come from? · How are the pillars of Seth related to the Watchers' stone-carved astrology in Jubilees 8? · How are the Sethian-Gnostic Seth texts related to the pillars legend?

The passage

Three hundred years before the flood, Surid had a dream in which it seemed that the earth overturned… and the stars fell and collided against each other with a terrible crash… [The priests] engraved on these monuments all sayings of the wise. They wrote on every surface of the pyramids, the ceilings, foundations, and walls, all the sciences familiar to the Egyptians… the figures of the stars… the science of talismans, mathematics, architecture — all the sciences of the world.

— al-Maqrizi (d. 1442), al-Khiṭaṭ, retelling the older Coptic-Arab Surid narrative, trans. in Jason Colavito, "Al-Maqrizi on the Pyramids." King Surid ibn Salhouk, reigning "before the Flood," is warned by dreams which his priests, casting the stars, confirm: "a deluge would occur after which would appear a fire out of the constellation Leo." He builds the pyramids as an archive, engraves on them the sciences of Egypt, and sets guardian-spirits over them.1

The question

The Surid legend shares with Josephus's pillars a foreseen double catastrophe, an inscribed archive of the sciences, and its survival. What is disputed is how the two are related: as one antediluvian archive raised by one sage, in the account the legend's medieval tellers give, or as a medieval Coptic and Islamic reworking of a late-antique story of knowledge preserved from the Flood. The answer bears on how far the pillars motif travelled and in what form.

An antediluvian archive raised by the sage called Hermes, Idris and Enoch

On this reading, given by the legend's own medieval tellers, the pyramids were built before the Flood to preserve the sciences, and their builder is the same antediluvian sage whom the Hebrews call Enoch.

Held by: al-Masudi (Meadows of Gold, c. AD 947–956), for the antediluvian-preservation frame; the pseudo-Masudi Akhbār al-zamān; al-Maqrizi (d. 1442), with the authorities he reports.2

The earliest datable witness to the frame is al-Masudi, who heard at Akhmim a story of builders who acted not knowing "whether the world was to perish by fire, by a deluge, or if the sword were to exterminate its inhabitants." The Akhbār al-zamān has the king have the priests "deposit therein the secrets of their science and precepts of their wisdom." Al-Maqrizi gives the fullest form. Celestial science, read off the stars, foretells a coming catastrophe; the knowledge is inscribed on durable stone to outlast it; and the catastrophe is doubled, "a deluge… after which would appear a fire." Al-Maqrizi also reports authorities who name the builder. One strand calls him "the first Hermes, whom the Arabs call Idris," who "had been inspired with the science of the stars"; another says this first Hermes "is the same as him the Hebrews call Enoch ben Jared… who is also the same as Idris. He foresaw, from the position of the planets, the arrival of a Flood… therefore he built a large number of pyramids." On this reading the pyramids of Giza and the antediluvian inscriptions of the Jewish and Hermetic traditions belong to one history: a sage before the Flood, known to Egyptians as Hermes, to Muslims as Idris and to the Hebrews as Enoch, read the coming destruction in the heavens and set the sciences in stone so that they would survive it.3

A Coptic and Islamic reworking of the late-antique antediluvian-archive story

On this reading the Surid legend is a medieval Egyptian development of the older story of knowledge preserved from the Flood, shaped by Coptic, Hermetic and Islamic interests.

Held by: Martyn Smith (Journal of the American Research Center in Egypt, 2007); Jason Colavito ("The Antediluvian Pyramid Myth").4

Smith reads the story as one that "assigns to [the pyramids] a place in sacred history and establishes a neutral narrative ground upon which Muslims and Christians could agree," its antediluvian frame and its Hermes–Enoch builder being property of both faiths. Colavito traces the antediluvian pyramid myth from late-antique Christian and Hermetic forms through the Arab sources. The Surid story shares the motif at three joints. Its structure is the pillars story's: foreknowledge from the stars, inscription on durable stone, survival of a doubled catastrophe. Its builder is identified with the Hermes–Idris–Enoch figure, and for Colavito the Hermetic and Enochic versions "were harmonized by the Islamic equation of Enoch with the Muslim prophet Idris." Its royal name has an Egyptian root: Colavito judges "the most convincing" explanation to be that "his name is a corruption of the Suphis of Manetho," the Greek form of Khufu (Cheops), builder of the Great Pyramid, which ties the legend to the same Manethonian material that gives the Book of Sothis its columns in the Seriadic land. Colavito records an alternative derivation, the consonants of Idris reversed, a form that "played better to the national pride of the Copts," and notes older Egyptian stories associating Khufu with magic. On this reading the Surid cycle is a recognizable Egyptian-Coptic redaction of one durable, cross-confessional motif, carried across a millennium and into two further religious communities, and attached at last to the pyramids themselves.5
