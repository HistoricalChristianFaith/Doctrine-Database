George Syncellus on the Pillars of Seth

Dates: c. 810 · Wikipedia
Summary: George Syncellus (d. c. 810/811), a monk of Constantinople, compiled the Ecloga Chronographica, a universal chronicle stitched from older sources. It alone preserves the Egyptian Seiriadic columns of Thoth-Hermes (inscribed before the Flood, re-transcribed after), whose region-name matches Josephus's "land of Siriad," and, with other Christian chronographers, it transmits the Babylonian Sippar tablets that Xisouthros buried before the deluge and dug up after. M. R. James finds the passage on Seth's planets and pillars, which he quotes from George Kedrenos, "both in Syncellus and Cedrenus."

Claims & evidence

The Seiriadic columns of the Book of Sothis

…copied from the sacred books, written by Thoth, the first Hermes, in the sacred language and in hieroglyphic characters… upon the columns set up in the Seriadic land… and after the deluge… translated from the sacred language into the Greek tongue in hieroglyphic characters, and committed to writing in books… by Agathodaemon, the son of the second Hermes.

— the preface to the (pseudo-Manethonian) Book of Sothis, quoted in Syncellus's Chronography; this fragment survives only because Syncellus copied it. It tells of sacred antediluvian knowledge inscribed on stone columns, in the Seriadic land — the same region-name Josephus gives his surviving stone pillar — and recovered after the Flood. H. St. J. Thackeray reads Josephus's "Siriad" as Egypt (the land of the worshippers of Seirios, the Dog-Star); how Josephus's pillar has been read is set out on What is the pillar "in the land of Siriad" that Josephus says still stood in his day?1

Berossus's Sippar tablets

The same chronicle, with other Christian chronographers, transmits the Babylonian parallel. Quoting the priest Berossus (3rd c. BC), Syncellus reports that the flood-hero Xisouthros, warned of the coming deluge, buried "the beginning, middle, and end of all writing" at Sippar, the city of the sun, so that after the waters receded the survivors dug up the antediluvian writings and gave them back to mankind. Antediluvian knowledge hidden in durable form against a coming flood and recovered afterward is the device Josephus gives Seth's descendants, here worked by the Babylonian sages. Both the Egyptian and the Babylonian parallels reach modern readers through the Christian chronographic channel in which Syncellus stands.2

The Seth passage in Syncellus and Kedrenos

The primeval-history notices on Seth — that he named the seven planets, comprehended the courses of the heavens, and set up two pillars of stone and brick — are quoted by M. R. James from George Kedrenos, two centuries later, from "a passage both in Syncellus and Cedrenus." In the Byzantine chronicles the pillars stay with the line of Seth; in the Latin West Comestor (c. 1170) gives them to the Cainite Jubal.3

Reception and debate

  • He carries the Watchers material too. On the Nephilim timeline Syncellus is the witness who transcribes the Greek 1 Enoch "Book of the Watchers" as antediluvian history — the rebel angels who "taught" astronomy to enslave mankind. His chronicle thus holds both ancient accounts of the origin of celestial knowledge: the revelation of the Watchers and the discovery of the Sethites. His Alexandrian source Panodorus (c. AD 400) made the Watchers the originators of antediluvian astronomy and, on William Adler's reading, likely identified them with the Sethites — see How are the pillars of Seth related to the Watchers' stone-carved astrology in Jubilees 8?
  • Transmitter. Syncellus does not himself argue for the pillars legend; he transmits, in one place, the Egyptian and Babylonian parallels, the Enochic Watchers text, and the Sethite notices.
