Isidore of Seville on the Pillars of Seth

Dates: c. 620 (the Chronica Maiora, first recension c. 615, revised c. 626); Isidore c. 560–636, archbishop of Seville · Wikipedia
Summary: Isidore is the earliest Latin witness on this timeline to the two-pillars legend. His universal chronicle, the Chronica, sets Josephus's report of the antediluvians who wrote their discoveries on a brick and a stone pillar against the flood-and-fire into the standard sequence of world history — but he tells it generically, of "some men," and places it in the same breath as the Cainite line of Lamech, whose son Jubal (spelled "Tubal" in the Etymologies, III.16.1) the Etymologies names as the inventor of music "before the flood." Isidore keeps the pillars and the Cainites side by side; Peter Comestor (c. 1170) attaches the pillars to Jubal.

Claims & evidence

The two pillars against fire and water — "as Josephus reports"

In the antediluvian entries of the Chronica, at the generation of Lamech and Noah, Isidore inserts the pillars notice. He credits Josephus by name, but does not say whose descendants raised them — only "some men" who foresaw the double destruction.

In these times, as Josephus reports, some men, who knew that they were about to die by either fire or water, inscribed their discoveries on two columns made of brick and stone, so that the memory of those things which they had discovered in their wisdom might not be erased. Their stone columns are reported to have withstood the flood and remain in Syria to this very day.

— Chronica (Chronicon) §5, trans. Kenneth B. Wolf, tertullian.org. The report is Josephus's (Antiquities I.2.3), compressed to its essentials: the paired media, the fire-and-water premise, and the survival of the stone pillar "in Syria to this very day." Isidore neither names Seth nor corrects Josephus; he lodges the legend in the Latin chronicle tradition, from which it passes to the medieval Bible-histories.1

The Cainite next door: Jubal the inventor of music "before the flood"

The pillars entry sits immediately after Isidore's notice of Lamech's sons — the Cainite Jubal and Tubal-Cain — whom he credits with music and metalwork. In the Etymologies he fixes the same point in a lapidary formula that the Middle Ages copied endlessly.

In this age also, Jubal, from the line of Cain, discovered the art of music and his brother Tubal Cain was the inventor of the arts of copper and iron.

— Chronica §4, tertullian.org; compare Etymologies III.16.1: "Moyses dicit repertorem musicae artis fuisse Tubal, qui fuit de stirpe Cain ante diluvium" ("Moses says that the discoverer of the art of music was Tubal, who was of the stock of Cain before the flood"). A Cainite culture-hero, credited with an antediluvian art, stands one entry above the antediluvian pillars.2

What the Etymologies does not carry

The Etymologies does not contain the legend. Its astronomy chapters (III.24–27, on the astronomy/astrology distinction) discuss the science of the heavens without the pillars, and its music chapter gives only the Cainite-Tubal note above — no brick-and-stone columns, no fire-and-flood. The pillars legend is carried by Isidore the chronicler, not Isidore the encyclopedist; the located passage is in the Chronica.3

Reception and debate

  • In the Latin chronicles, Isidore transmits Josephus's pillars (generic "men," crediting Josephus) one entry after crediting music to the Cainite Jubal, and keeps the two apart. Peter Comestor (c. 1170) writes the pillars onto Jubal himself, and Ranulf Higden (c. 1350) carries both versions, the pillars of Josephus's line of Seth and those of the Cainite line.
  • Isidore gives the survival of the stone columns as a report: they "are reported to have withstood the flood and remain in Syria to this very day." On how Josephus's surviving pillar has been read, see What is the pillar "in the land of Siriad" that Josephus says still stood in his day?
  • The fire-and-water framing Isidore repeats is attested before Josephus (Plato, Berossus) and in the Life of Adam and Eve: see Where does the fire-and-water double destruction in the pillars story come from?
  • Isidore appears elsewhere on this wiki as a witness on astrology (the astronomy/astrology distinction), the Old Testament canon, the origin of the Septuagint, the integrity of the text, and the first language.
