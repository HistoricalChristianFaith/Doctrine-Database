Jerome on the Historicity of Noah's Flood

Dates: c. 392 · Wikipedia
Summary: Jerome reads the Flood as real, dated history, and reads it as a philologist — his chief flood text, the Hebrew Questions on Genesis (Quaestiones Hebraicae in Genesim, c. 391–393), works the narrative verse by verse against the Hebrew. He rejects a reading he says "many" hold in error: the "hundred and twenty years" of Genesis 6:3 were not a shortening of the human lifespan but a reprieve granted to that generation for repentance — a point he argues from the lifespans men still reached after the Flood (Abraham 175 years, others 200 and 300), and which he says God cut short by twenty years, bringing the Flood in the hundredth year. He treats the ark's construction, the waters returning to "the hidden veins of the abyss," and the raven and dove as a sequence of events, and — like Ambrose and Augustine — also reads figures from the narrative: the ark's three hundred cubits are the cross (the letter T), the eight souls saved the "octave" of baptism (1 Pet 3:20–21). On the disputed identity of the "sons of God" his philology inclines toward the angelic reading.

Claims & evidence

The 120 years of Genesis 6:3 as a reprieve for repentance, not a contraction of the human lifespan

Furthermore, so that it does not appear cruel on His part for not giving a place of repentance to sinners, He added: But their days shall be one hundred and twenty years. This is, they will have 120 years to do penance. Therefore, human life, as many err, is not condensed into 120 years: but to that generation, 120 years were given for penance. For we find that after the flood, Abraham lived for 175 years, and others for over 200 and 300 years. But because they refused to do penance, God did not want to wait for the appointed time: but with 20 years cut off, He brought about the flood in the 100th year designated for doing penance.

— Hebrew Questions on Genesis, on Gen 6:3, historicalchristian.faith. Jerome treats the 120 years as a span of calendar time, tested against the post-Flood ages of Abraham and others, and God's cutting the term to 100 years as a dated adjustment to a dated judgment. Jerome makes the same historical reading elsewhere, telling Daniel's reader that "prior to the Deluge one hundred and twenty years were appointed for men to come to repentance… and inasmuch as they refused to repent even within so long an interval… God did not wait for the remaining twenty years… but brought on the punishment earlier."1

Noah "righteous in his generation" — relative, not perfected, righteousness

Noah was a righteous and blameless man in his generation, pleasing to God. Specifically, it says 'in his generation' to show that his righteousness was not according to the perfected righteousness, but according to the righteousness of his generation. And this is what is said in Hebrew: Noah was a righteous man, blameless in his generations, he walked with God; that is, he followed in his footsteps.

— Hebrew Questions on Genesis, on Gen 6:9, historicalchristian.faith. Jerome weighs the force of "in his generation," measuring Noah's righteousness against the men alive with him.2

The ark's construction and the receding waters read against the Hebrew

(Verse 16.) Assembling, you shall make an ark, and you shall finish it with a cubit from above. As for the reason why it says, assembling, you shall make an ark, in Hebrew it has, 'you shall make the middle of the ark,' which Symmachus interpreted more clearly, saying 'transparent,' that is, making the ark clear, intending for a window to be understood.

— Hebrew Questions on Genesis, on Gen 6:16 (cf. 6:14, the "square wooden planks… coated with pitch in Hebrew"), historicalchristian.faith. Jerome does the same for the abating of the Flood, tracing the water back into the earth by way of Ecclesiastes: "the closed and sealed fountains were opened… all waters and torrents return to the hidden veins of the abyss" (on Gen 8:2), and tracks the sending of the raven and the dove "after forty days" (on Gen 8:6). He reads the ark and its Flood with attention to their parts and timetable, correcting the Latin against the Hebrew as he goes.3

The ark's numbers read as the cross, penance, and Christ

We read in Genesis that the ark that Noah built was three hundred cubits long, fifty cubits wide and thirty cubits high. Notice the mystical significance of the numbers. In the number fifty, penance is symbolized because the fiftieth psalm of King David is the prayer of his repentance. Three hundred contains the symbol of crucifixion. The letter T is the sign for three hundred, whence Ezekiel says, "Mark THAV on the forehead of those who moan"… we repent in the number fifty; then, through penance, we arrive at the mystery of the cross… As a matter of fact, when Jesus was baptized, according to Luke, "he was thirty years of age."

— Jerome, Homily 84 (on the ark, Gen 6:14), as gathered in Ancient Christian Commentary on Scripture: Genesis 1–11, ed. Andrew Louth (InterVarsity, 2001); the passage displays at historicalchristian.faith. Ambrose also reads the ark's three hundred cubits as the cross — the Greek tau, shaped like a cross, standing for 300 — and Jerome, like him, draws the figure from the dimensions Genesis gives.4

The eight souls saved are the "octave" of baptism (1 Peter 3:20–21)

[We see that] certain psalms are titled "for the octave." This is the day on which the synagogue comes to an end and the church is born. This is the day in the number of which eight souls were preserved in the ark of Noah, and "its counterpart, the church," says Peter, "now saves you."

— Jerome, Homily 93 (on Gen 7:13), displayed at historicalchristian.faith. Jerome takes up the New Testament's flood-typology — Noah's eight souls carried through the water as the figure of the baptism that "now saves you" (1 Pet 3:20–21). The figure is read from the eight persons and the ark of the narrative.5

The "sons of God" (Gen 6:2, 4): Jerome's philology inclines to the angelic reading

The Hebrew word Elohim, is of common gender: and both God and gods are called by this name. Hence, Aquila, in the plural number, dared to call them Sons of gods, understanding them to mean the Holy Ones or Angels… Therefore, Symmachus, following a similar interpretation, says: The sons of the powerful saw the daughters of men… And it is fitting for the name of falling ones to apply to angels and the children of the saints.

— Hebrew Questions on Genesis, on Gen 6:2 and 6:4, historicalchristian.faith. Where Chrysostom reads the "sons of God" as the Sethites and calls the angelic reading "fanciful," Jerome's survey of Aquila and Symmachus inclines the other way. His fuller treatment of this question is on the nephilim timeline; on the Flood itself both read the narrative as dated history.6

Notes

  • Jerome slots on the timeline between John Chrysostom (c. 388), the Antiochene literalist, and Augustine (c. 420). The anchor date c. 392 tracks his primary flood text, the Hebrew Questions on Genesis; the two homilies (the ark's numbers, the octave of baptism) are later, from his Bethlehem preaching.
  • Like Origen, Ambrose, and Augustine, Jerome both works through the chronology of Genesis 6–8 in the Hebrew Questions and reads figures (the ark's numbers as the cross) from the narrative. He is among the witnesses gathered on Did Christian writers before the nineteenth century all read the Flood as real history?
  • On the identity of the "sons of God" Jerome inclines to the angelic reading (via Aquila's "sons of gods" and Symmachus's "sons of the powerful"), where Chrysostom reads them as Sethites — see his nephilim detail page.
