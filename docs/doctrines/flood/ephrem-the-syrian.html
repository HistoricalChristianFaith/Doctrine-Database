Ephrem the Syrian on the Historicity of Noah's Flood

Dates: c. 373 · Wikipedia
Summary: Ephrem the Syrian (d. 373), the great Syriac poet-exegete, reads the Flood in a sober, literal-historical key — the earliest Eastern witness on this timeline, earlier than the Cappadocian and Antiochene voices that follow. In his Commentary on Genesis (section VI) he counts the flood's calendar to the day and totals Noah's time in the ark at three hundred sixty-five days, then turns that number into a chronological argument that the solar year was known before the Chaldeans and Egyptians "invented" it. He treats the ark as a real building project (asking where Noah found gopher wood, pitch, iron, and rope), gathers the animals as real beasts, has the household drink the real Flood waters, and lands the ark on Mt. Qardu (the Peshitta tradition, with Josephus and the Targums), not the Armenian "Ararat." He takes a firm Sethite reading of the "sons of God" and argues expressly against the angelic one. The figure he does draw — the predator-and-prey peace on the ark as a restored, pre-Fall state and a type of the Church — is laid lightly atop a flood he never stops treating as fact.

Claims & evidence

The "sons of God" are the line of Seth, the "daughters of men" the daughters of Cain

And the sons of God saw that the daughters of men were beautiful and they took to wife such of them as they chose. He called the sons of Seth sons of God, those who, like the sons of Seth, had been called "the righteous people of God." The beautiful daughters of men whom they saw were the daughters of Cain who adorned themselves and became a snare to the eyes of the sons of Seth.

— Commentary on Genesis VI.3, trans. Edward G. Mathews Jr., St. Ephrem the Syrian: Selected Prose Works, FOTC 91 (CUA Press, 1994), pp. 134–135, Internet Archive. Ephrem is a firm Sethite: the "sons of God" are the righteous line of Seth, the "daughters of men" the adorned daughters of Cain. In his hymns he argues expressly against the angelic reading (the translator notes Hymns on the Nativity 1.48, Hymns on Faith 46.9, Hymns against Heresies 19.1–8, and Hymns on Paradise 1.11). He is thus opposite Jerome's angelic leaning and beside Chrysostom, Cassian, and Bede — a divergence over the actors, not over the event (see the Nephilim timeline for the fuller treatment).

The hundred-and-twenty years were a real space for repentance

This generation will not live nine hundred years like the previous generations, for it is flesh and its days are filled with the deeds of flesh. Therefore, their days will be one hundred and twenty years. If they repent during this time they will be saved from the wrath that is about to come upon them. But if they do not repent, by their deeds they will call down [the wrath] upon themselves. Grace granted one hundred and twenty years for repentance to a generation that, according to Justice, was not worthy of repentance.

— Commentary on Genesis VI.4, FOTC 91, pp. 135–136, Internet Archive. Like Jerome, Chrysostom, and Bede, Ephrem reads the 120 years of Genesis 6:3 as a real reprieve granted a real generation for repentance — not, as "many err," a shortening of the human lifespan. The mercy is measured out in historical time and refused in historical time.

The ark is a real building project — where did Noah find the materials?

[God] then said to Noah, "Because the end of all flesh is before me, make yourself an ark of gopher wood: three hundred cubits in length, fifty cubits in width and thirty cubits in height and finish it to a cubit above. Make it three stories and daub it with pitch on the outside." [God] brought that difficult task to this just man in the hope that He would not have to bring the flood upon them. (2) Where was Noah to get gopher wood? pitch? iron? or rope? With the assistance of what craftsmen would he construct [the ark] and where would he procure laborers to help?… Noah began the ark in the first year that was allowed that generation for repentance and he finished it in one hundred years.

— Commentary on Genesis VI.8, FOTC 91, p. 138, Internet Archive. Ephrem reads the ark's construction as a real logistical labor — the dimensions of Genesis 6:15 taken at face value, and the practical questions (timber, pitch, iron, rope, craftsmen, a hundred years of building) pressed as they would be of any genuine structure. The hundred-year build is itself the span of the generation's reprieve: the history is coherent, dated, and workmanlike.

The flood's calendar counted to the day — the springs of the abyss, the shut door

For this reason, at the end of the seven days, in the six hundredth year of Noah's life, in the second month, on the seventeenth day of the month, the springs of the great abyss burst forth and the flood gates of heaven were opened. The Lord shut the door before Noah, lest those left behind come at the time of the floods and break down the gate of the ark. The deluge came and God blotted out all flesh. Only Noah was left and those that were with him in the ark. The springs of the abyss and the flood gates of heaven were open forty days and forty nights and the ark was afloat for one hundred fifty days.

— Commentary on Genesis VI.11, FOTC 91, p. 141, Internet Archive. The dated record is exact: the six hundredth year, the second month, the seventeenth day; the forty days of the bursting abyss and opened floodgates; the hundred and fifty days afloat. "God blotted out all flesh" — the judgment is anthropologically total, "only Noah… and those that were with him" surviving. Like Chrysostom, Ephrem reads even the shut door as considerateness (here, keeping out those who would break in), a moral note drawn from, not substituted for, the history.

The ark rests on Mt. Qardu; Noah's 365 days prove the solar year is older than the Chaldeans

But after one hundred fifty days the waters began to subside and the ark came to rest on Mt. Qardu… Therefore, Noah and those with him had been in the ark three hundred sixty-five days… Notice then that even the generation of the house of Noah employed this reckoning of three hundred sixty-five days in a year. Why then should you say that it was the Chaldeans and Egyptians who invented and developed it?

— Commentary on Genesis VI.12, FOTC 91, pp. 141–142, Internet Archive. Ephrem lands the ark on Mt. Qardu in the northern mountains (the Peshitta reading, shared with Josephus and the Targums), not the Armenian peak of the Greek and Latin tradition. Then he does something distinctively historical-apologetic: he adds the days of Genesis 7–8 to a round three hundred sixty-five and argues that Noah's household already reckoned a solar year — so the calendar cannot have been a Chaldean or Egyptian invention. This is the same move Theophilus and Josephus make: the Flood as a fixed datum from which real chronology is argued.

The peace among the animals on the ark — the one figure, laid atop the history

On that same day elephants came from the east, apes and peacocks approached from the south, other animals gathered from the west, and still others hastened to come from the north… Then, in their very presence, the lions began to enter the ark and the bulls, with no fear, hurried in right on their heels to seek shelter with the lions. The wolves and the lambs entered together and the hawks and the sparrows together with the doves and the eagles.

— Commentary on Genesis VI.9, FOTC 91, p. 139, Internet Archive. Ephrem's one sustained figure is the miraculous concord of predator and prey on the ark — wolves beside lambs, hawks beside sparrows — which his hymns read as a restored pre-Fall peace and "a type of the Church" (translator's note). It is the lightest of typological layers: drawn out of a scene he narrates as literal event (the beasts really converging from the four directions), never used to dissolve it. The history remains the floor.

Notes

  • Ephrem slots on the timeline after Origen (c. 248) and before Basil of Caesarea (c. 375) — the earliest Eastern witness here, and a Syriac one, whose literal-historical reading confirms the consensus was not merely a Greek-and-Latin phenomenon. His interest is in the narrative's coherence as history: its calendar, its logistics, its geography.
  • He is a strong witness for the argument Was patristic allegory of the Flood a denial of its history? (assessment: sound): even the great Syriac poet, whose hymns teem with symbol, expounds the Flood almost entirely as literal history and lays only the lightest figure (the ark's animal-peace as a type of the Church) atop it.
  • He anchors the Eastern side of the historic-consensus thesis (assessment: sound), and on extent his "God blotted out all flesh" reads the judgment as reaching all humanity — bearing on geographic globality vs. anthropological universality (assessment: contested).
  • On the "sons of God" his firm Sethite reading — argued explicitly against the angelic one — is treated more fully on the Nephilim timeline. His Mt. Qardu landing (over the Armenian "Ararat") and his 365-day solar-year argument mark the distinctively Syriac cast of his historical reading.
