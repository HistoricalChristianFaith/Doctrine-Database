Josephus on the Flood

Dates: c. AD 100 Summary: Flavius Josephus, retelling Genesis for a Greco-Roman readership in the Antiquities of the Jews (Book I, ch. 3), gives the Flood as sober, dated history — the earliest witness on this timeline, and a Jewish historian rather than a Christian exegete. He keeps the biblical detail intact (the ark's dimensions, the forty days' rain, the fifteen cubits, the raven and dove, the sacrifice and the bow), and does something no later witness here does: he argues the Flood's historicity from pagan corroboration, naming Berosus the Chaldean, Hieronymus the Egyptian, Mnaseas, and Nicolaus of Damascus, and reporting that the ark's remains were still shown on a mountain in Armenia.

Claims & evidence

The Flood as plain, dated history

Josephus narrates Genesis 6–9 as ordinary history, carrying the numbers and the sequence intact. The ark is a real vessel of stated proportions, stocked to preserve the animal kinds:

four stories high, three hundred cubits long, fifty cubits broad, and thirty cubits high

all sorts of living creatures, the male and his female, for the preservation of their kinds

The deluge itself he dates and measures:

When God gave the signal, and it began to rain, the water poured down forty entire days, till it became fifteen cubits higher than the earth

Noah tests the receding waters by bird, the dove returning with the token of dry land:

came back to him covered with mud, and bringing an olive branch

And after the ark grounds — "After this, the ark rested on the top of a certain mountain in Armenia; which, when Noah understood, he opened it" — Noah offers sacrifice, and God binds himself never to repeat the judgment:

the water shall no more overspread the earth

— sealed with the sign of the bow. There is no allegory here: Josephus is writing history, and the Flood is one of its events.1

The historicity argued from pagan corroboration

What sets Josephus apart from the Christian witnesses on this timeline is his method: he defends the Flood as fact by appeal to non-biblical historians who, he says, report the same event and the same ark.

Now all the writers of barbarian histories make mention of this flood, and of this ark; among whom is Berosus the Chaldean.

Alongside Berosus he names Hieronymus the Egyptian (author of a Phoenician history), Mnaseas, and Nicolaus of Damascus as further witnesses to the same tradition — marshalling the pagan record as independent testimony to the Mosaic narrative.2

The ark's remains "still shown" in Armenia

Josephus closes the section with the claim the ark-search tradition has leaned on ever since: that the vessel's remains survived and could still be seen. From Berosus he reports:

It is said there is still some part of this ship in Armenia, at the mountain of the Cordyaeans; and that some people carry off pieces of the bitumen, which they take away, and use chiefly as amulets for the averting of mischiefs.

And from Nicolaus of Damascus, a named mountain and a survivor carried on an ark:

There is a great mountain in Armenia, over Minyas, called Baris, upon which it is reported that many who fled at the time of the Deluge were saved; and that one who was carried in an ark came on shore upon the top of it; and that the remains of the timber were a great while preserved.

Josephus identifies that survivor with Noah — "This might be the man about whom Moses the legislator of the Jews wrote." The first-century report that ark-timber and bitumen were still shown in the mountains of Armenia is the earliest form of the physical-remains expectation later ark-hunters inherit; it locates the remains high in the Armenian highlands (the "mountains of Ararat" of Genesis 8:4), not in a valley below.3

Notes

  • Josephus is the earliest witness on the Flood timeline and the only non-Christian one: a first-century Jewish historian treating the deluge as an event of universal history, corroborated (he argues) by the Gentile record.
  • His report that the ark's remains were "still shown" in Armenia is the ancient anchor behind the modern ark-search claims — weighed on the Durupınar formation argument, where the identification is rated weak. Josephus places the remains on the mountains of Armenia, consistent with Genesis 8:4, not in the Tendürek valley the Durupınar case requires.
  • Josephus appears on this wiki also as a witness on the Nephilim, First Language, Old Testament Canon, Origin of the Septuagint, and Pillars of Seth timelines — all, with this one, drawn from his primeval history in Antiquities Book I.
