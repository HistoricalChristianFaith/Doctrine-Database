Isaac La Peyrère on the Historicity of Noah's Flood

Dates: c. 1655 (lived 1596–1676) · Wikipedia
Summary: La Peyrère, a French Calvinist scholar and secretary to the Prince de Condé, is the earliest witness on this timeline to deny that the Flood was universal. In Prae-Adamitae (Amsterdam, 1655; English Men before Adam, 1656) he argued that men existed before Adam — Adam being the father of the Jews only, not of all mankind — and drew from it the consequence that the Deluge judged the Jews' sins alone and overflowed only "Palestine, and the Land of the Jews," leaving the pre-Adamite Gentile nations of the wider world untouched. He denied the Flood's universality, not its reality: a real Noah and a real (local) Deluge remained. The book was condemned and publicly burned at Paris in 1656; La Peyrère was arrested, recanted before Pope Alexander VII, and converted to Catholicism (1657), and the Wikipedia article "Isaac La Peyrère" reports that he influenced Spinoza and Richard Simon.

Claims & evidence

The Flood overflowed "only Palestine, and the Land of the Jews," not the whole earth

Give us leave to discusse this last miracle of the flood of Noah, which is believ'd to have overflow'd the kingdoms of the earth with most mighty overflowings, and which I rather believe overflow'd only Palestine, and the Land of the Jews. The causes of this conjecture are chiefly, the causes of the Deluge…

— A Theological System upon that Presupposition, that Men were before Adam (in Men before Adam, London, 1656), Book 4, ch. 7, p. 239, Internet Archive. La Peyrère opens the chapter — headed "That the Flood of Noah was not upon the whole earth, but only upon the Land of the Jews. Not to destroy all men, but only the Jews" — by making the Flood turn on its cause: the sins for which it was sent were the Jews' sins, so the judgment fell only where those sinners were. He states the thesis a few pages on: "if we will take notice of the thing and not of the words, it will appear, that the Deluge came only upon the Land of the Jews, and not upon the whole world."1

Because there were men before Adam: Adam "the first and father of the Jews, not of all men"

…the design of Moses in setting down Adam, was not to mention the first of mankinde, but the Father of the Jews, whose peculiar Historie he wrote, and not the History of all Nations.

— A Theological System, Book 3, p. 218 (cf. the chapter heading, p. 135: "Adam was the first and father of the Jews, not of all men"), Internet Archive. His local Flood follows from this premise. If Adam fathered the Jews only and Gentile nations already peopled the earth, then the Flood — a punishment of the Jews — need not, and on La Peyrère's reading did not, reach them: "God did not that time send the fear of the Jews upon the Nations that dwelt in China, America, the South, or Greenland." He reads the universal-sounding language of Genesis 7 as the "high stile" of a writer describing his own land — "the high mountains of Palestine… the whole heaven, was that which covered Palestine" — since "it is ordinary to assign every Country its own heaven."2

The Deluge was "peculiar to the Jews, not universal in all Nations"

It is clear, that this Deluge was peculiar to the Jews, not universal in all Nations, by that which is written of the Dove, which Noe sent out, and she return'd to him at night.

— A Theological System, Book 4, ch. 8, p. 244, Internet Archive. His props are exegetical and physical: the dove returned with a green olive branch that a year-long, mud-burying worldwide flood could not have spared, so (he argues) it "flew over all the waters of the Deluge, and in the higher fields of Asia" found the branch on land the flood never reached; and the "natural descent of waters" from the ark's resting-place tells the same way. In his account the event is real — a real Noah, a real ark, real waters — but bounded to one region: he denies the Deluge's universality, not its reality.3

Notes

  • La Peyrère is the earliest witness on this timeline to deny the universal Flood. Whether his thesis is the beginning of a seventeenth-century break, the culmination of a Renaissance current, or an extension of older limits on the Flood's reach is set out on Was Isaac La Peyrère's local flood (1655) the earliest denial of a universal Flood?
  • His denial is of universality, not reality: he affirmed a real Noah and a real, local Deluge.
  • Earlier writers had limited the waters at particular places while holding that all humanity outside the ark perished: Augustine reports writers who exempted Mount Olympus, Cajetan spared the highest mountains, and Tostatus exempted the earthly Paradise; Rabbi Yoḥanan held that the flood did not descend on the land of Israel. La Peyrère's Flood, confined to one nation and sparing peoples descended from men before Adam, also holds that not all humanity perished. See Did the pre-modern tradition hold that the Flood covered the whole earth, or only that it destroyed all humanity?
  • A near-contemporary, Isaac Vossius (1618–1689), reached a local, non-universal flood by an independent route in his Dissertatio de vera aetate mundi (The Hague, 1659) and the writings around it — arguing from the longer Septuagint chronology and the reported antiquity of China — so that Martin Schook wrote a whole treatise (1662) against his "local and partial deluge." Eric Jorink (2008) studies the two Isaacs together in the emergence of radical biblical criticism in the Dutch Republic.
  • Reception: the book was condemned and publicly burned at Paris in 1656; its author was imprisoned some six months in the Spanish Netherlands, then recanted before Pope Alexander VII and was received into Catholic communion (1657).
