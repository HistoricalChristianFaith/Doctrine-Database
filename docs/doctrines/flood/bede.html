Bede on the Historicity of Noah's Flood

Dates: c. 735 · Wikipedia
Summary: The Venerable Bede, monk of Wearmouth–Jarrow, is the Anglo-Saxon capstone of the history-and-allegory pattern. His Commentary on Genesis (In Genesim) reads Genesis 6–8 as a real, dated event — the flood in Noah's six hundredth year, the second month "Jair, which we call May," on the seventeenth day, forty days of rain, real animals housed in real chambers — and gathers into one place the whole inherited Latin figural synthesis: Jerome's hundred-and-twenty-years reprieve, the ark's three hundred cubits as the Greek T/cross, Ambrose's ark-as-human-body, and Augustine's side-door as the wound in the Crucified's side. He weighs Origen's "geometric cubit" solution to the ark's capacity and rejects it on the evidence of Josephus, then insists the ark's preservation "was full of divine miracles" — the mark of a man treating the vessel as a real physical structure, not a fable. On the "sons of God" he takes the Sethite reading (with Chrysostom and Cassian, against Jerome's angelic leaning), and he sets the Flood inside his literal scheme of the world's ages. Every figure is laid atop a flood taken as fact.

Claims & evidence

The Flood is a real, dated event — the month named, reckoned by the Hebrew moon

The months among the Hebrews are computed only according to the course of the moon from the first to the first, and their first month, in which the Passover is commanded to be kept, and in which the world was made, is called Nisan, which we call April. The second is Jair, which we call May, on whose seventeenth day, that is, when the moon of the same month was the seventeenth, the flood came, when on that same day Noah entered the ark with all the men, animals, and birds that were to be saved through it.

— Commentary on Genesis, on Gen 7:11, source. Bede does the work of a chronologist: he identifies the Hebrew lunar months with the Roman calendar, fixes the flood's onset to the seventeenth of the second month, and tracks the forty days, the resting of the ark, and the sendings of the birds as a real sequence of real days. Like Ambrose and Chrysostom before him, he reads Genesis 7–8 as a calendar, not a myth.

The hundred-and-twenty years were a real reprieve for repentance — Jerome's reading carried forward

Moreover, lest He seem to be cruel in not giving sinners a place for repentance, He added: "And his days shall be one hundred and twenty years" (Gen. VI), that is, they will have one hundred and twenty years to repent; but because they despised repentance, God did not want to wait for the appointed time, but cutting short the span of twenty years, He brought the flood in the hundredth year appointed for repentance.

— Commentary on Genesis, on Gen 6:3, source. Bede quotes Jerome by name on the verse and takes over his exact solution: the 120 years are not a shortening of the human lifespan but a real span of clemency granted to a real generation, cut short by twenty years when they refused to repent. The reading that Jerome set against a "popular error" is by Bede's day the standard Latin exposition — a small, precise witness to the continuity of the historical reading across three and a half centuries.

The "sons of God" are the line of Seth, not angels

"Men" means the offspring of Cain, who, turned away from the view of divine will, subjected their mind solely to human affairs: "sons of God", however, refers to those who were born from the lineage of Seth, who, following the example of their father's devotion, preserved with an inviolate mind the service they owed to God… Some manuscripts have "angels of God" instead of "sons of God," which is obviously taken in the same sense. For the angels of God are rightly called righteous men, who according to the measure of their capacity strive to live an angelic life on earth.

— Commentary on Genesis, on Gen 6:1–2, source. Bede resolves the textual variant ("sons of God" / "angels of God") in favor of the Sethite reading: the "angels of God" are the holy men of Seth's line living "an angelic life on earth," not spirit-beings. He aligns with Chrysostom and Cassian, against the angelic reading to which Jerome's philology leaned — a divergence over who the actors were, not over whether the Flood occurred (see the Nephilim timeline for the wider debate).

The giants were real men of "immense bodies" — and the "fallen ones" of the Hebrew

Giants are called men with immense bodies and excessive power, of which there were also many after the flood, that is, in the times of Moses or David… It should be noted that in this place, for giants, the Hebrew reads 'fallen ones', that is, 'annasilim'; and the sense is easy and clear because they were 'fallen' men on the earth in those days, that is, adhering to earthly desires, having lost the state of uprightness devoted to God.

— Commentary on Genesis, on Gen 6:4, source. Bede glosses the Hebrew nephilim ("fallen ones," his "annasilim") both morally — men "fallen" into earthly desire — and physically, "men with immense bodies," of a kind that persisted "after the flood… in the times of Moses or David." The giants are actors in a real history, born when "the descendants of Seth chose wives from the lineage of Cain."

The ark's preservation "was full of divine miracles" — Origen's geometric cubit weighed and rejected

But it is usually asked whether the ark, with the capacity described, could have carried all the animals said to have entered it along with their food? Origen tries to solve this question with the geometric cubit… He states that the geometric cubit is equivalent to the value of six of our cubits… But it should be noted that although Moses had learned geometric cubits, the people for whom he wrote the book were ignorant of such arts… But Josephus, writing about the construction of the same tabernacle, also prohibits understanding it in this way… Thus, regarding Noah's ark, it should be observed that all that happened within it or in relation to it was full of divine miracles. For if everything were done in the usual manner of men, how could eight people daily provide food and drink and other necessities to such a multitude of birds, beasts, and reptiles?… The Lord Himself, who preserved the ark with all it contained, keeping it incorrupt… also provided how they might be fed and kept safe within the ark.

— Commentary on Genesis, on Gen 6:21, source. This is Bede at his most historical: the ark's capacity is a real physical problem, and he treats it as one. He reports Origen's solution — the "geometric cubit" of six ordinary cubits, which would make the vessel resemble "an extensive city" — and rejects it, reasoning from the tabernacle's dimensions and Josephus that Moses wrote in ordinary cubits for an ordinary readership. His own answer is not to shrink the miracle but to name it: the daily feeding of the animals, their year of quiet, the ark's incorruption and safe landing "in the mountains" were divine wonders. He weighs the mechanics of a real vessel, not the plausibility of a fable.

The judgment was anthropologically total — "all flesh" means all humans

And when God saw that the earth was corrupt: for all flesh had corrupted its way. All flesh means all humans, according to the prophet's statement: "And all flesh shall see the salvation of God" (Luke 3:6). For neither birds nor quadrupeds had corrupted their way by sinning… The waters of the flood brought upon the earth killed all flesh that was found outside the ark, but Noah and all that were in the ark were saved.

— Commentary on Genesis, on Gen 6:12 and 6:17, source. Bede reads the destruction as reaching every human being outside the ark — the anthropologically universal Flood of the mainstream tradition — with only "Noah and all that were in the ark" preserved.

The figure laid atop the history: ark = Church, flood = baptism, Noah = the builder Christ

According to this understanding, the ark clearly signifies the church, Noah signifies the Lord who builds the church with his saints, and the flood signifies the end of the age or the final judgment… According to another equally pious and catholic interpretation, the ark signifies the church, the flood signifies the water of baptism, by which the church itself is washed and sanctified in all its members, as the apostle Peter explains… Some Fathers often interpret the waves of the flood as the trials of this age, by which the holy church is daily struck but not overcome.

— Commentary on Genesis, on Gen 6:13, source. Only after working the construction of the ark as literal history does Bede raise the figures — and he offers them as alternatives ("according to this understanding… according to another equally pious and catholic interpretation"), each anchored to Scripture's own typology (1 Pet 3:20–21). The flood is at once the last judgment, the font of baptism, and the trials of the age; Noah is the Lord who builds the Church "with his saints." The letter is never displaced — it is the platform the figures stand on.

The ark's dimensions as the cross and the human body; the side-door as the wound of Christ

Hence the length of the ark is commanded to be three hundred cubits, as this number… is represented by the letter T in Greek. This letter is written in the shape of a cross… the ark, which bore the figure of this, was formed in the likeness of the human body… [the door] clearly designates that door, which was opened in the side of the Crucified Lord Savior by the soldier's lance, from which immediately flowed blood and water: through these sacraments, each of the faithful is received into the communion of the holy Church.

— Commentary on Genesis, on Gen 6:15–16, source. Every strand of the Latin synthesis is here in one commentary: Jerome's three-hundred-as-the- tau/cross, Ambrose's ark-shaped-like-the-body, and Augustine's side-door-as-the-spear-wound. Bede is the transmitter who gathers the Fathers' figures and hands them to the medieval West — and, exactly as they did, he draws all of it out of a real ark's real proportions.

The Flood set within the literal ages of the world

The full evening of this day arrived when, with the proliferation of human race's vices, the whole earth was corrupted before God and filled with iniquity, to the extent that all flesh deserved to be destroyed by the flood, except for those enclosed in the ark. On the second day, the firmament was made in the midst of the waters; and in the second age of the world, the ark, in which the remnants of the human race and the seed, so to speak, of the following ages were preserved, was placed in the midst of the waters.

— Commentary on Genesis, on the ages of the world, source. Bede — the great computist and chronographer, author of The Reckoning of Time — slots the Flood into his famous scheme of the world's six ages: it closes the first age and opens the second, "the remnants of the human race… preserved" through it as "the seed… of the following ages." The Flood is a fixed hinge in a continuous, datable human history stretching from Adam to Christ.

Notes

  • Bede slots on the timeline after John Cassian (c. 435) and before Isaac La Peyrère (1655) — the last patristic-tradition witness before the early-modern hinge. He carries the historical-and-figural reading of the Flood, unbroken, into the eighth-century Anglo-Saxon world, roughly nine centuries before the first real denial.
  • He is a compound witness for the argument Was patristic allegory of the Flood a denial of its history? (assessment: sound): Bede is at once the heaviest allegorist on the later timeline — ark = Church, flood = baptism, the dimensions read as cross and body and Trinity — and an exacting historian who reckons the flood's calendar, defends the ark's real capacity against Origen, and names its preservation a set of divine miracles. In one author the "allegorist = denier" inference collapses.
  • He also anchors the historic-consensus thesis (assessment: sound): the unbroken reading of the Flood as real history did not stop with the patristic age but was transmitted, whole, through the early-medieval West — Bede gathering Jerome, Ambrose, Augustine, and Origen into a single standard commentary that the later Middle Ages would inherit.
  • On the "sons of God" his Sethite reading joins him to Chrysostom and Cassian against Jerome's angelic leaning — the same intramural divergence traced on the Nephilim timeline, over which actors the narrative names and never over whether it happened.
