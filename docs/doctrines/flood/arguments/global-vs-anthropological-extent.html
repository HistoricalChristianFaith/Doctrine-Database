Did the pre-modern tradition hold that the Flood covered the whole earth, or only that it destroyed all humanity?

Disputed reading · on The Historicity of Noah's Flood · witness: Theophilus of Antioch

Related readings: Did Christian writers before the nineteenth century all read the Flood as real history? · Was Isaac La Peyrère's local flood (1655) the earliest denial of a universal Flood?

The passage

And the waters prevailed exceedingly upon the earth; and all the high hills, that were under the whole heaven, were covered. Fifteen cubits upward did the waters prevail; and the mountains were covered.

— Genesis 7:19–20 (KJV), historicalchristian.faith.1

The question

Two claims can be distinguished: that all human beings outside the ark died (anthropological universality), and that water covered the whole surface of the earth (geographic universality). The question is which of these pre-modern Jewish and Christian readers of Genesis 7 held, and which, if either, they treated as fixed. It bears on what the inherited reading of the Flood included before the modern debates over its extent.

Waters over the whole earth

On this reading, the Flood covered every land and every mountain, and the earliest Christian commentators said so in terms.

Held by: Theophilus of Antioch (Ad Autolycum 3.18–19, c. 185); Basil of Caesarea (Letter 260.5, c. 377); John Chrysostom (Homilies on Genesis 25.10, c. 388); Augustine (City of God, c. 420), as reported by Maas.2

Genesis says that "all the high hills, that were under the whole heaven, were covered," and that the waters stood fifteen cubits above the mountains. Theophilus of Antioch reads this against Plato, who had taught that a deluge "extended not over the whole earth, but only over the plains, and that those who fled to the highest hills saved themselves." Moses, Theophilus answers, related "no fable … that only the plains were submerged, and that those only who escaped to the mountains were saved," but that "the water overtopped every high hill 15 cubits. And thus the race of all the men that then were was destroyed."3 Here the geographic and the anthropological claims are held together, the first as the ground of the second: no height was left as a refuge, so no one survived outside the ark. Basil calls the Flood the punishment "brought on the whole earth, because sin was everywhere spread abroad," and Chrysostom says the waters "fell out of the heavens on all sides and inundated the whole world."2 Augustine knew of writers who exempted Mount Olympus from the waters and, in Maas's report, "does not agree with them." On this reading, the restriction of the waters was a minority opinion that the principal commentators either did not hold or set aside, and the plain sense of "under the whole heaven" was taken as the sense Moses taught.

All humanity destroyed; the reach of the waters left open

On this reading, the tradition fixed the death of all humanity outside the ark, while the reach of the waters was a question on which respected writers could differ.

Held by: Rabbi Yoḥanan (Babylonian Talmud, Zevachim 113a), on the land of Israel; Cajetan and Tostatus, as reported by Maas; Anthony Maas, "Deluge," Catholic Encyclopedia (Catholic, 1908).4

The two claims are distinct propositions: a flood that covered the inhabited world would destroy all human beings without covering every mountain on the globe. Maas notes that the Hebrew word rendered "earth" in the narrative "signif[ies] 'land' as well as 'earth,'" and lists writers who limited the waters while affirming a real Flood: Augustine "knows of writers who exempted the mountain Olympus from the Flood"; Cajetan "revived the opinion that the Flood did not cover Olympus and other high mountains"; Tostatus "exempts the earthly Paradise from the Deluge." He also reports that the Congregation of the Index condemned Isaac Vossius's treatise De Septuaginta Interpretibus, which held "the view that the Flood covered only the inhabited part of the earth," while some theologians held that the work was condemned on account of its "Protestant author."4 Jewish reception also knew a limit of this kind: in the Talmud Rabbi Yoḥanan holds that "the flood did not descend upon Eretz Yisrael," against Reish Lakish, who holds that it did. Maas draws the conclusion for Catholic teaching: "Neither Sacred Scripture nor universal ecclesiastical tradition, nor again scientific considerations, render it advisable to adhere to the opinion that the Flood covered the whole surface of the earth," and writers who "restrict the Deluge to certain parts of the earth's surface" do so "without incurring the blame of offending against tradition." On the death of all men outside the ark he holds the line the other way, since "the prima facie meaning of 1 Peter 3:20 sq., 2 Peter 2:4-9, and 2 Peter 3:5 sqq., refers to the death of all men not contained in the ark."5 On this reading, a writer could hold the historical, humanity-destroying Flood without holding that water stood over the whole globe, and the tradition left that second question open.

Not all humanity perished

On this reading, the Flood did not destroy every human being outside the ark, because peoples existed whom it did not reach.

Held by: Isaac La Peyrère (Prae-Adamitae, 1655; English 1656); "several Catholic writers," unnamed, reported by Maas (1908).6

La Peyrère argued that men existed before Adam, "Adam was the first and father of the Jews, not of all men," and that the Flood, a punishment of the Jews' sins, "came only upon the Land of the Jews, and not upon the whole world," so that the Gentile nations "that dwelt in China, America, the South, or Greenland" were not touched by it. He read the universal language of Genesis 7 as a writer's description of his own land: "the whole heaven, was that which covered Palestine… it is ordinary to assign every Country its own heaven."6 By the early twentieth century, Maas reports, the antiquity and distribution of certain peoples had led "several Catholic writers to regard as quite tenable the opinion" that "the Deluge did not destroy all men outside the ark." On this reading, the universal phrases of Genesis 7 and of the New Testament describe the world of the narrator and of the people judged, and the anthropological universality of the Flood is a reading of those phrases rather than a fixed point of the text.
