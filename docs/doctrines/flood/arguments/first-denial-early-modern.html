Was Isaac La Peyrère's local flood (1655) the earliest denial of a universal Flood?

Disputed reading · on The Historicity of Noah's Flood · witness: Isaac La Peyrère

Related readings: Did Christian writers before the nineteenth century all read the Flood as real history? · Did the pre-modern tradition hold that the Flood covered the whole earth, or only that it destroyed all humanity?

The passage

Give us leave to discusse this last miracle of the flood of Noah, which is believ'd to have overflow'd the kingdoms of the earth with most mighty overflowings, and which I rather believe overflow'd only Palestine, and the Land of the Jews. The causes of this conjecture are chiefly, the causes of the Deluge…

— Isaac La Peyrère, A Theological System upon that Presupposition, that Men were before Adam, Book 4, ch. 7, p. 239, in Men before Adam (London, 1656), the English translation of Prae-Adamitae (Amsterdam, 1655), Internet Archive.1

The question

La Peyrère held that the Flood was real but reached only the land of the Jews, sparing peoples descended from men before Adam. The question is where his thesis stands in the history of the Flood's interpretation: whether it is the beginning of a break with the universal reading, the culmination of an earlier current, or one instance of a limit on the Flood's reach that older writers had already set. It bears on dating when, and among whom, the universal Flood was first set aside.

A seventeenth-century break, beginning with La Peyrère

On this reading, the universal Flood was the general belief until the seventeenth century, and La Peyrère's Prae-Adamitae opened a break with it that later criticism and geology carried further.

Held by: Anthony Maas, "Deluge," Catholic Encyclopedia (Catholic, 1908), on the dating; Eric Jorink (historian, 2008), on La Peyrère and Vossius as the emergence of radical biblical criticism.2

Maas dates "the belief in the anthropological universality of the Deluge" as general before the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. Within that frame, La Peyrère (1596–1676), a French Calvinist, argued in Prae-Adamitae (Amsterdam, 1655) that Adam was the father of the Jews only, so that the Flood, sent for the Jews' sins, had to be local to leave the pre-Adamite Gentiles in being. The book was condemned and burned at Paris in 1656, and its author recanted before Pope Alexander VII.3 Four years later Isaac Vossius (1618–1689), arguing from the longer Septuagint chronology and the reported antiquity of China, held the Flood to be local to the Middle East (Dissertatio de vera aetate mundi, The Hague, 1659). Jorink studies the two Isaacs together under the heading of the emergence of radical biblical criticism in the Dutch Republic.4 Charles Lyell's Principles of Geology (1830–33) later treated the Genesis flood as at most a regional event.5 On this reading, La Peyrère's thesis marks the point at which the universal Flood was first set aside in a systematic argument; he denied its universality but not its reality, keeping a real Noah and a real, local Deluge, while denial of the Flood's historicity among Catholic writers is dated by Maas to the 1890s.

The culmination of a Renaissance current

On this reading, La Peyrère's local flood is the end-point of a sixteenth- and seventeenth-century erosion of the literal-historical Flood, rather than its origin.

Held by: Don Cameron Allen (historian, The Legend of Noah: Renaissance Rationalism in Art, Science, and Letters, 1949; repr. 1963).6

Allen's study traces the pressures that bore on the literal-historical reading of the Flood across the Renaissance: the peoples of the New World, whose descent from Noah's sons had to be explained; the problem of the ark's capacity and of the number and diversity of animal kinds; the debate over fossils; and the textual criticism of the biblical narrative. Together these formed a skeptical current in the learned literature before 1655. On this reading, La Peyrère's pre-Adamite thesis, with its local flood, is the culmination of that current rather than its origin: the Flood's universality was already under discussion among Renaissance readers, and his local flood is the systematic form those questions took in his hands.6

Older limits on the Flood's reach

On this reading, writers long before La Peyrère had already held that the Flood did not reach every land or height, so that a limited Flood has ancient and medieval precedent.

Held by: ⚠ unverified — no named proponent located yet.

The case rests on writers who limited the waters while affirming a real Flood. In the Babylonian Talmud Rabbi Yoḥanan holds that "the flood did not descend upon Eretz Yisrael," against Reish Lakish; Genesis Rabbah 33:6 has the dove's olive leaf taken from the unflooded Land. Augustine "knows of writers who exempted the mountain Olympus from the Flood" (though, Maas adds, "he himself does not agree with them"); Cajetan "revived the opinion that the Flood did not cover Olympus and other high mountains"; and Tostatus "exempts the earthly Paradise from the Deluge."7 On this reading, the question whether the Flood covered every part of the earth was open long before the seventeenth century, in Jewish and in Christian exegesis, and La Peyrère's local flood extends to whole nations a limitation that earlier writers had applied to particular lands and mountains.
