Did Christian writers before the nineteenth century all read the Flood as real history?

Disputed reading · on The Historicity of Noah's Flood · witness: Augustine of Hippo

Related readings: Did the pre-modern tradition hold that the Flood covered the whole earth, or only that it destroyed all humanity? · Was Isaac La Peyrère's local flood (1655) the earliest denial of a universal Flood?

The passage

Yet no one ought to suppose either that these things were written for no purpose, or that we should study only the historical truth, apart from any allegorical meanings; or, on the contrary, that they are only allegories, and that there were no such facts at all… we must rather believe that there was a wise purpose in their being committed to memory and to writing, and that they did happen, and have a significance, and that this significance has a prophetic reference to the church.

— Augustine, The City of God 15.27 (c. 420), trans. Marcus Dods (NPNF1 2), ccel.org.1

The question

Whether every Christian writer before the modern period read Genesis 6–9 as an account of events that happened, and how that earlier reading relates to the twentieth-century "flood geology" movement. The answer bears on whether the historical reading of the Flood is the inherited position of the churches, and on what that inherited position included. The question is often put in popular form ("who was the first saint to say the Flood did not happen?"); the readings below give the positions taken in the scholarly literature cited on this page. Augustine names none of the holders of the figure-only position he rejects, and no other source surveyed here supplies a name.

A unanimous historical reading

On this reading, Christian writers read the Flood as real history without exception until the last years of the nineteenth century.

Held by: Anthony Maas, "Deluge," Catholic Encyclopedia (Catholic, 1908), quoting Franz Zorell.2

The Catholic Encyclopedia article states that "the Bible story concerning the Flood has never been explained or understood in any but a truly historical sense by any Catholic writer," and that "the few stray discordant voices belonging to the last fifteen or twenty years are simply drowned in this unanimous chorus of Christian tradition," which places the first Catholic dissent on historicity in the 1890s.2 On this reading, the witnesses gathered on the timeline fit this description. Theophilus of Antioch (c. 185) dates the Flood inside a chronology from Adam; Origen (c. 248), who gives the ark a detailed spiritual reading in his Homilies on Genesis, defends its dimensions against Celsus; Ephrem, Basil, Chrysostom, Jerome, Cassian and Bede treat the Flood's calendar, the ark's construction or the Flood's moral causes as matters of fact; and Ambrose and Augustine draw figures of the Church and the cross from a narrative they also read "according to the letter." In the passage above Augustine sets down the rule that the events "did happen, and have a significance." On this reading, the Fathers who allegorized the Flood built the figure on the event and did not offer it in place of the event. The article also dates belief in the death of all humanity outside the ark ("the anthropological universality of the Deluge") as general until the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries; Isaac La Peyrère's Prae-Adamitae (1655), which argued for a local flood that spared other peoples, was condemned and burned at Paris in 1656.3 Read together, these place the historical reading of the Flood as the common inheritance of Christian writers, East and West, from the second century to the early-modern period.

A historical reading distinct from modern flood geology

On this reading, the pre-modern historical reading of the Flood and the twentieth-century young-earth "flood geology" are two different things, and the second has its own recent origin.

Held by: Ronald L. Numbers (historian, The Creationists, 1992; exp. ed. 2006), on the origins of flood geology; Anthony Maas, "Deluge," Catholic Encyclopedia (Catholic, 1908), on the extent of the waters as an open question.4

Numbers traces "flood geology" — a young earth, and a worldwide Flood that laid down the geological column and sorted the fossils — to the Seventh-day Adventist George McCready Price (The New Geology, 1923), and its spread among evangelicals to John C. Whitcomb and Henry M. Morris's The Genesis Flood (1961), which he credits with raising young-earth creationism "to a position of fundamentalist orthodoxy." He reports that by the early twentieth century most prominent evangelicals had accepted an old earth.4 On this reading, a writer could hold the Flood to be a real event without holding the young-earth geological account attached to it in the twentieth century. The Catholic Encyclopedia article, which affirms the unanimity of the historical reading, also notes that the Hebrew word rendered "earth" signifies "'land' as well as 'earth,'" and records writers who limited the Flood's extent: Augustine "knows of writers who exempted the mountain Olympus from the Flood" (though, the article adds, "he himself does not agree with them"), Cajetan "revived the opinion that the Flood did not cover Olympus and other high mountains," and Tostatus "exempts the earthly Paradise from the Deluge."5 On this reading, then, the historical reading of the Flood that runs through the Fathers and the scholastics carried the event and the death of humanity outside the ark, and left room on the extent of the waters; the young-earth geological reading is, in Numbers's account, a twentieth-century construction.

A figure-only reading in late antiquity

On this reading, some Christians in Augustine's day read the Flood narrative as allegory only, without the events.

Held by: ⚠ unverified — no named proponent located yet.

The case rests on Augustine's own words. In the passage above, and in the title of City of God 15.27 ("…that We Cannot Agree with Those Who Receive the Bare History, But Reject the Allegorical Interpretation, Nor with Those Who Maintain the Figurative and Not the Historical Meaning"), he rejects those who hold "that they are only allegories, and that there were no such facts at all."1 On this reading, a writer does not set out a rule against a position nobody held: the chapter attests that a figure-only reading of the Flood was current enough in the early fifth century to need answering. The reading therefore places a non-historical reading of the Flood inside Christian antiquity, as a position known to and rejected by Augustine.
