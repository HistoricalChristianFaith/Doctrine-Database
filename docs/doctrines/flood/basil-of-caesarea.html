Basil of Caesarea on the Historicity of Noah's Flood

Dates: c. 375 (On the Holy Spirit; the letter to Optimus c. 377; d. 379) · Wikipedia
Summary: Basil never wrote a treatise on the Flood — his Hexaemeron stops at the sixth day and never reaches Noah — and his references to it are incidental: in passing, in a Trinitarian treatise and an exegetical letter, the Cappadocian bishop refers to the Flood as a real, datable, worldwide event. In On the Holy Spirit he uses "what happened in Noah's time" and the destruction of Sodom as chronological markers in history, ordered by their distance from the present. In his letter to Optimus he reads the generations "from Cain to the flood" as a literal count and treats the deluge as a real punishment "brought on the whole earth, because sin was everywhere spread abroad" — contrasting that bygone cataclysm with Christ, who "takes away the sin of the world." He does not argue for the Flood; he argues from it.

Claims & evidence

The Flood as a punishment "brought on the whole earth," set in a generational chronology

There are, however, some who have gone so far as the following explanation, which does not jar with the doctrine of the Church; from Cain to the flood, they say, seven generations passed by, and the punishment was brought on the whole earth, because sin was everywhere spread abroad. But the sin of Lamech requires for its cure not a Flood, but Him Who Himself takes away the sin of the world.

— Letter 260.5 (to Optimus the bishop, c. 377), NPNF² 8, historicalchristian.faith. Answering Optimus's questions on hard verses of Genesis and Luke, Basil glosses Lamech's "seventy and sevenfold" (Gen 4:24). He reports — with approval, as a reading that "does not jar with the doctrine of the Church" — that the seven generations counted "from Cain to the flood" are matched by the sevenfold avenging, the Flood itself being the "punishment brought on the whole earth" once "sin was everywhere spread abroad." The deluge is treated as a worldwide judgment standing at a fixed point in a generational chronology (Basil goes straight on to "count the generations from Adam to the coming of Christ… according to the genealogy of Luke," finding Christ born in the seventy-seventh). The Flood's reality and its universal reach serve as the premise of the exegesis, and as the foil for the Cross — Lamech's sin needs not "a Flood, but Him Who… takes away the sin of the world."

"What happened in Noah's time" as a point of history, ordered before Sodom

For instance, what happened in Noah's time is prior to what happened to the men of Sodom, inasmuch as Noah is more remote from our own day; and, again, the events of the history of the men of Sodom are posterior, because they seem in a sense to approach nearer to our own day.

— On the Holy Spirit 6 (c. 375), NPNF² 8, historicalchristian.faith. The setting is not flood-apologetics: Basil is arguing that the Son is not "later" than the Father, and that temporal "before/after" cannot be predicated of the eternal generation. To illustrate ordinary temporal priority he cites the Flood and Sodom — "what happened in Noah's time" and "the events of the history of the men of Sodom" — events whose pastness and order he takes as known to his readers. The Flood serves here as a historical reference point in a treatise on another subject.

Notes

  • Basil's Hexaemeron, the nine homilies on the six days of creation (c. 378), never reaches the Flood — it closes with the land animals of the sixth day — so his references to the Flood come from the letter to Optimus and On the Holy Spirit, where the Flood appears not as a topic but within arguments on other subjects. (His brother Gregory of Nyssa and friend Gregory of Nazianzus were the other Cappadocian writers; they are not witnesses on this timeline.)
  • He is among the witnesses gathered for the reading that Christian writers read the Flood as history: see Did Christian writers before the nineteenth century all read the Flood as real history?
  • On extent, the Flood is the "punishment brought on the whole earth" — the phrase is cited for the worldwide reading on Did the pre-modern tradition hold that the Flood covered the whole earth, or only that it destroyed all humanity?; Basil gives it in passing, alongside the moral totality of the judgment ("sin was everywhere spread abroad"), not as a separately argued thesis.
