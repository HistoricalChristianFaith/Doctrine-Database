John Cassian on the Historicity of Noah's Flood

Dates: c. 435 · Wikipedia
Summary: The monastic teacher John Cassian, in Conference 8 (the Second Conference of Abbot Serenus), treats the Flood not as a text to allegorize but as a fixed fact of real history that carries the weight of a theodicy. He takes the Sethite reading of the "sons of God" (the fuller treatment is on the Nephilim timeline), reads their giant offspring as real men of "size… and cruelty" whose wickedness rose to a pitch at which "the world could only be purified by the flood and deluge," and defends the justice of that pre-Flood judgment against pagan and heretical sneers: those who died had the natural law implanted in them and transgressed it, so "God visited them with a righteous judgment." His incidental proof that the narrative is history is Noah himself — who could distinguish clean animals from unclean "before the law… and even before the flood" only because a real man really knew a real command.

Claims & evidence

The Flood was a real purifying judgment on a real, escalating wickedness

From these sons of Seth then and daughters of Cain, as we have said, there were born still worse children who became mighty hunters, violent and most fierce men who were termed giants by reason of the size of their bodies and their cruelty and wickedness. For these first began to harass their neighbours and to practise pillaging among men, getting their living rather by rapine than by being contented with the sweat and labour of toil, and their wickedness increased to such a pitch that the world could only be purified by the flood and deluge.

— Conference 8, ch. XXI, ccel.org. Cassian gives the Flood a moral history: the giants are real men whose violence "increased to such a pitch" that the deluge became the only remedy. The Flood is the world's purification — a real judgment answering a real escalation of sin, exactly the frame Basil and Origen assume ("the cause… we declare to be the extensive prevalence of wickedness").

Noah's real distinction of clean and unclean animals proves the narrative is history

…that the complete knowledge of the law was implanted in man at his first creation, is clearly proved from this; viz., that we know that before the law, aye, and even before the flood, all holy men observed the commands of the law without having the letter to read… How could Noah have distinguished what animals were clean and what were unclean, when the commandment of the law had not yet made a distinction, unless he had been taught by a natural knowledge?

— Conference 8, ch. XXIII, ccel.org. The point Cassian is arguing is that the moral law was written on the heart before it was written on tablets; but the proof he reaches for treats Noah as an unquestioned historical person doing a concrete historical thing — sorting the clean beasts from the unclean (Gen 7:2) at the ark. The Flood narrative is not a parable in his hands; it is the common ground of dateable fact from which he argues something else.

Those who died in the Flood were justly punished — a theodicy built on a real judgment

And therefore in the case of those who sinned before the law and even before the flood we see that God visited them with a righteous judgment, because they deserved to be punished without any excuse, for having transgressed the law of nature; nor should we fall into the blasphemous slanders of those who are ignorant of this reason, and so depreciate the God of the Old Testament… and say with a sneer: Why then did it please your God to will to promulgate the law after so many thousand years, While He suffered such long ages to pass without any law?

— Conference 8, ch. XXIV ("Of the fact that they were justly punished, who sinned before the flood"), ccel.org. Here the Flood does load-bearing theological work: against those who "depreciate the God of the Old Testament" for judging a world that had no written law, Cassian answers that the antediluvians possessed the natural law and "transgressed" it, so their destruction was "a righteous judgment." The argument only works if the judgment really happened — the historicity of the Flood is the premise the whole defense rests on, never a conclusion in doubt.

Notes

  • Cassian slots on the timeline after Augustine (c. 420) and before Bede (c. 735). Like Basil, his witness is the weightier for being incidental: he is not expounding the Flood but assuming it — as settled history — inside an argument about natural law and the justice of God.

  • His Sethite reading of the "sons of God" joins him to Chrysostom and Bede against Jerome's angelic leaning.
