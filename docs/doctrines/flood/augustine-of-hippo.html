Augustine of Hippo on the Historicity of Noah's Flood

Dates: c. 420 (City of God, Bk. 15–16) · Wikipedia
Summary: Augustine reads the Flood as history and allegory together. In the City of God he treats the sacred narrative as chronology — defending the antediluvians' 900-year lives as ordinary solar years, treating the genealogies of Seth and Cain as the literal lines of his "two cities" — and then builds the figure of the ark (Christ and the Church, the door in its side the spear-wound) on that history. In 15.27 he lays the both/and down as a rule, rejecting alike "those who receive the bare history but reject the allegorical interpretation" and "those who maintain the figurative and not the historical meaning." He names none of those who held the figure-only reading.

Claims & evidence

"The history I aim at defending" — the Genesis narrative defended as history

At present it is the history which I aim at defending, that Scripture may not be reckoned incredible when it relates that one man built a city at a time in which there seem to have been but four men upon earth, or rather indeed but three, after one brother slew the other,—to wit, the first man the father of all, and Cain himself, and his son Enoch, by whose name the city was itself called.

— The City of God, Book 15, ch. 8, source. Augustine's posture across Book 15 is that of a defender of the literal narrative against objectors who reckon it "incredible." The same chapter answers the objection that there were too few men to build a city by noting that "the writer of the sacred history does not necessarily mention all the men who might be alive at that time, but those only whom the scope of his work required him to name" — the antediluvian genealogies are abridged records of a real population, not a complete census. In these books Augustine treats Genesis 4–9 as historia.

The antediluvians' long lives as solar years

It is plain that the day then was what it now is, a space of four-and-twenty hours, determined by the lapse of day and night; the month then equal to the month now, which is defined by the rise and completion of one moon; the year then equal to the year now, which is completed by twelve lunar months, with the addition of five days and a fourth to adjust it with the course of the sun.

— The City of God, Book 15, ch. 14, trans. Marcus Dods (NPNF1 2), newadvent.org / ccel.org. Augustine is refuting those who saved Scripture's credibility by shrinking its years — supposing the antediluvian "years" were short cycles of which ten made one of ours. He rejects that reading: "those antediluvians lived more than 900 years, which were years as long as those which afterwards Abraham lived 175 of, and after him his son Isaac 180." He defends the long lives as literal, ordinary, sun-measured years.

The ark is a figure of Christ and the Church — the door in its side is the spear-wound

…this is certainly a figure of the city of God sojourning in this world; that is to say, of the church, which is rescued by the wood on which hung the Mediator of God and men… And its having a door made in the side of it certainly signified the wound which was made when the side of the Crucified was pierced with the spear; for by this those who come to Him enter; for thence flowed the sacraments by which believers are initiated. And the fact that it was ordered to be made of squared timbers, signifies the immoveable steadiness of the life of the saints.

— The City of God, Book 15, ch. 26, trans. Marcus Dods (NPNF1 2), newadvent.org / ccel.org. The chapter is titled "That the Ark Which Noah Was Ordered to Make Figures in Every Respect Christ and the Church." Augustine also reads the proportions allegorically — "the length of the human body, from the crown of the head to the sole of the foot, is six times its breadth from side to side, and ten times its depth or thickness," matching the ark's 300×50×30 to the body in which Christ came. In the next chapter he states that the events also happened.

Neither bare history without the figure, nor figure without the history

Yet no one ought to suppose either that these things were written for no purpose, or that we should study only the historical truth, apart from any allegorical meanings; or, on the contrary, that they are only allegories, and that there were no such facts at all… we must rather believe that there was a wise purpose in their being committed to memory and to writing, and that they did happen, and have a significance, and that this significance has a prophetic reference to the church.

— The City of God, Book 15, ch. 27, trans. Marcus Dods (NPNF1 2), newadvent.org / ccel.org. The chapter's full title states the rule it lays down: "that We Cannot Agree with Those Who Receive the Bare History, But Reject the Allegorical Interpretation, Nor with Those Who Maintain the Figurative and Not the Historical Meaning." Augustine rejects both positions and holds "that they did happen, and have a significance" — the events occurred and they prefigure. How far this rule describes the Christian writers of his time is a disputed reading.
