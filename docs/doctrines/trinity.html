The Trinity (the co-eternal, consubstantial Son and Spirit)

The church confesses one God in three — Father, Son, and Holy Spirit — of one being and equal in glory. But that confession was not handed down as a finished formula; it was fought into its classic shape across three centuries. The live question this timeline traces is the one the ancient controversy itself turned on: is the divinity of the Son — his being co-eternal with the Father and of the same essence (ίμοούσιος, homoousios) — a faithful drawing-out of what the apostles already worshipped, or, as Arius and later critics charged, an unscriptural novelty read back into Scripture by a council? The witnesses show a real development of language for a worship that was there from the start: they do not begin with the settled vocabulary, and the earliest of them say things a later age had to qualify.

The arc runs from the Logos-theology of the apologists (the Son as the Father’s own Word, “begotten before all creatures,” yet spoken of as “another” and even a “second God”), through Tertullian’s Latin coinage of the Trinity — “one substance… three Persons” — and Origen’s doctrine of the Son’s eternal generation (with the subordinationist tail the next generation would exploit), to the crisis: Arius’s sharp thesis that “there was when he was not,” the Son a creature made out of nothing. The Council of Nicaea (325) answered with homoousios; Athanasius spent his life defending it; and the Cappadocians supplied the grammar — one ousia, three hypostases — that the Council of Constantinople (381) crowned by confessing the full deity of the Holy Spirit. The story runs alongside the wiki’s pages on Theotokos (the same homoousios wielded against Arius), the condemnation of Origen and universal restoration (Origen’s legacy read the other way), and Tertullian’s later rigorism.

Timeline

Justin Martyr (c. 165)

Justin Martyr, the earliest witness here, already worships the Son as divine while reaching for language to say how. He calls Christ “the first-born of God” and the “first-begotten Word of God,” who “is even God.”1 This Word, he teaches, God “begat before all creatures… by an act of will,” and though the Word is “numerically distinct” from the Father — “another God” in that sense — the begetting is “not by abscission, as if the essence of the Father were divided,” like one fire kindled from another that is “not lessened.”2 The divinity is confessed; the machinery for holding it without ditheism or subordination is not yet built.

Tertullian (c. 213)

Tertullian, writing in Latin against the modalist Praxeas, coins the vocabulary the West would keep. He is the first to speak of the Trinity, “which distributes the Unity into a Trinity… the Father, the Son, and the Holy Ghost: three, however, not in condition, but in degree; not in substance, but in form… yet of one substance, and of one condition, and of one power.”3 The Persons are distinct but not divided — “as the root puts forth the tree, and the fountain the river, and the sun the ray… nor, indeed, is the Word separated from God” — “in the way of distinction, not of division.”4 His formula (in the Latin behind this translation, una substantia, tres personae) hands the later councils their central terms more than a century before Nicaea.

Origen of Alexandria (c. 248)

Origen supplies the decisive advance and the decisive ambiguity. Against any notion that the Son had a beginning, he teaches the Son’s eternal generation: God never “existed, even for a moment of time, without having generated this Wisdom… born indeed of Him… but without any beginning.”5 Yet the same theology carries a subordinationist tail: on John 1:1 he distinguishes the Father as “Very God” (Autotheos, God with the article) from the Word who is God “by being with the Father,” and elsewhere grants that the Son may be called a “second God.”6 Both halves of his legacy would be quarried in the next century — the eternal generation by the pro-Nicenes, the subordination by the Arians.

Arius (c. 321)

Arius, an Alexandrian presbyter, presses subordination into denial and forces the crisis. His own words survive as Athanasius quotes them: “God was not always a Father… The Son was not always,” “once He was not,” the Word “made out of nothing” and “alterable.”7 The Son is “not equal, no, nor one in essence” with the Father but “a beginning of things originated” — a creature, however exalted, “foreign from the Son in essence is the Father.”8 The slogan “there was when he was not” (ἐν ποτε ὅτε οὐκ ἦν) made the Son’s creaturehood a matter the church could no longer leave imprecise.

The Council of Nicaea (325)

The Council of Nicaea answered Arius with a word the Bible does not use, to guard what the Bible does teach. Its creed confesses the Son “begotten of the Father… that is, from the essence of the Father; God from God, Light from Light, Very God from Very God, begotten not made, One in essence (homoousios) with the Father,” and anathematizes those who say “Once He was not… or… He came to be from nothing.”9 Athanasius later explained that the non-scriptural terms were forced on the fathers precisely because the Arians could swallow every scriptural phrase while emptying it — so “from the essence” and “one in essence” were added to shut the door on a merely nominal divinity.10

Eusebius of Caesarea (c. 325)

Eusebius of Caesarea — the church historian, and heir of Origen through his teacher Pamphilus — stands for the large conservative party the controversy had to win over, neither Arius nor Athanasius. His Logos-theology is frankly subordinationist: the Son is a “second Being,” a “Second Lord” begotten before the ages who holds a divinity “received from the Father,” “greater than all angels, but less than the First Cause” — and, breaking with Origen’s eternal generation, “not… eternally coexistent with the Father, since… a father must exist before and precede his son.”11 Before Nicaea he had taken Arius’s part, writing to Alexander of Alexandria that Arius was misrepresented, for which the anti-Arian Synod of Antioch provisionally excommunicated him months before the council.12 Yet at Nicaea he subscribed — and wrote home to explain that he had “received without contention what no longer pained us,” once satisfied that “one in essence” did not mean “the Son subsisted from the Father in the way of division, or any severance.”13 How far that signature was conviction and how far accommodation is the classic question — his own minimizing gloss, and a later career spent defending the Son’s distinct subsistence against Marcellus rather than championing homoousios, suggest a conservative signing for the church’s peace while reading its new word as narrowly as it would bear.

Marcellus of Ancyra (c. 336)

Marcellus of Ancyra stood at the opposite extreme from Arius and Eusebius, and shows why the Nicene party had to disown a second error as much as the first. A veteran anti-Arian who had defended Nicaea, he pressed the divine unity so hard that he swung past orthodoxy into a near-modalism: the one God is a “Monad,” and there are not three hypostases — “is he not clearly saying that there is only one God, namely, himself?”14 On this scheme the Word is not a distinct person but a power that “expands” out of God as his Activity at creation and becomes Son only at the incarnation — an eternal Logos, but no eternal Son.15 Reading 1 Corinthians 15:24–28 to the letter, he taught that at the end the Word is reabsorbed and Christ’s reign therefore ends — it “began… at a certain time” and “there will be an end to Christ’s reign.”16 This scared the Eastern bishops away from homoousios, which they now suspected of hiding Sabellianism; it is the reason the Council of Constantinople (381) added to the creed the clause “of whose kingdom there shall be no end” (Luke 1:33), aimed squarely at him. His book survives only as fragments quoted — to be refuted — by his chief critic Eusebius of Caesarea.17

Athanasius of Alexandria (c. 356)

Athanasius, present at Nicaea as a deacon and later bishop of Alexandria, gave his life to homoousios through five exiles. The Son is “an offspring proper to the Father’s essence… very God, existing one in essence with the very Father… And He ever was and is and never was not.”18 His guiding logic ran from salvation: only if the Son is fully God can he make us partakers of God; a creature cannot deify creatures. So the eternity of the Son entails the eternity of the Triad — “if the Word is not with the Father from everlasting, the Triad is not everlasting.”19 He would extend the same reasoning to the Holy Spirit, whose deity the next generation dogmatized.

Basil of Caesarea (c. 375)

Basil of Caesarea, eldest of the three Cappadocians, fixed the settled grammar and carried the fight to its last frontier. Where Nicaea had left the vocabulary ambiguous, Basil distinguished the terms the councils would keep: “the distinction between ousia and hypostasis is the same as that between the general and the particular… we confess one essence… but we confess a particular hypostasis, in order that our conception of Father, Son and Holy Spirit may be without confusion” — one being in three subsistences, and to deny the three hypostases is to fall into “the error of Sabellius.”20 His On the Holy Spirit (c. 375) then presses the deity of the Spirit against the Pneumatomachi (“Spirit-fighters”): the Spirit is holy and good by nature, not by participation — “to the creature holiness was brought in from without, but to the Spirit holiness is the fulfilment of nature… not… being sanctified, but… sanctifying” — and so is not to be “sub-numbered” below the Father and the Son, for “there is one God and Father, one Only-begotten, and one Holy Ghost.”21

Gregory of Nazianzus and the Council of Constantinople (c. 380–381)

Gregory of Nazianzus, one of the three Cappadocians, supplied the settled grammar and carried the argument to its last frontier — the deity of the Spirit — while presiding (briefly) over the Council of Constantinople in 381. The one Godhead is confessed in three, distinguished “not by natures, but by properties”: the Father unbegotten, the Son begotten, the Spirit proceeding — “the Trinity in the Hypostases or Persons… the Essence of the Three is One and the same.”22 Against those who granted the Son but balked at the Spirit, he insists the Spirit “always existed, and exists, and always will exist… perfecting, not being perfected; sanctifying, not being sanctified; deifying, not being deified” — “no sooner do I conceive of the One than I am illumined by the Splendour of the Three.”23 Constantinople (381) ratified this, expanding the Nicene Creed’s bare “and in the Holy Ghost” into a confession of the Spirit as “the Lord and Giver-of-Life, who proceedeth from the Father, who with the Father and the Son together is worshipped and glorified” — the point at which the doctrine reached the shape the church has held since.24

Gregory of Nyssa (c. 385)

Gregory of Nyssa, Basil’s younger brother, became after Basil’s death the great theologian of the settlement — and answered the objection the three-hypostases grammar provoked. If Father, Son and Spirit each are God, why not “three Gods,” as Peter, James and John are three men? In On “Not Three Gods” (to Ablabius) Gregory replies that the plural attaches to the Persons, never to the one nature: “gold, even though it be cut into many figures, is one… The Father is God: the Son is God: and yet by the same proclamation God is One, because no difference either of nature or of operation is contemplated in the Godhead” — every divine operation being one, running “from the Father… through the Son… perfected in the Holy Spirit.”25 And against the Pneumatomachi who granted the Spirit every divine attribute but the name of God, he presses the inconsistency: since the Spirit shares “Good,” “Holy,” “Eternal,” “Wise” and the rest with the Father and the Son, “it is absolutely necessary either to allow to Him community in this also, or not to admit His community in the others.”26

Arguments

Recurring challenges to the creed get their own pages. Two turn on the creed itself — one on its method, one on its chief proof-text: the ancient objection that Nicaea answered Arius with a word the Bible never uses, and the perennial exegetical crux of whether John’s “the Word was God” even says what the creed claims, given the missing article. A third reaches back into the timeline itself — to the ambiguity in Origen’s Son that the next century’s rival parties each quarried:

  • Is Nicaea’s homoousios an illegitimate, unscriptural novelty? — weak
  • John 1:1c — is the Word “God” or “a god”? (the anarthrous theos) — sound
  • Was Origen’s subordination of the Son a pre-Nicene idiom — so that reading him as an Arian is anachronistic? — contested (whether his Son is a subordinate “second God” or whether his eternal generation already holds the germ of homoousios; weighed under the condemnation of Origen. The same anachronism defence is pressed for the Latin West in Tertullian’s subordination of the Son)
