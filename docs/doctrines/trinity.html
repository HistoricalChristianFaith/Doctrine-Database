The Trinity (the co-eternal, consubstantial Son and Spirit)

This timeline records how Christian writers from the second century to the fourth spoke of the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit, and how the Son’s relation to the Father came to be stated as co-eternal and of the same essence (ίμοούσιος, homoousios), the term of the Council of Nicaea (325). The fourth-century controversy turned on whether that confession states what the apostles taught and worshipped, as its defenders held, or, as its fourth-century opponents charged (the complaint Athanasius reports in De Decretis 1), adds to Scripture a term and a doctrine it does not contain.1 The witnesses are listed by date, earliest extant attestation first, with their own words.

The record runs from the Logos-theology of the apologists (the Son as the Father’s own Word, “begotten before all creatures,” and spoken of as “another” and as a “second God”), through Tertullian’s Latin Trinity — “one substance… three Persons” — and Origen’s teaching of the Son’s eternal generation, to Arius’s thesis that “there was when he was not,” the Son a creature made out of nothing. The Council of Nicaea (325) confessed the Son homoousios with the Father; Athanasius defended the term for the rest of his life; the Cappadocians distinguished one ousia from three hypostases; and the Council of Constantinople (381) confessed the Holy Spirit as worshipped and glorified with the Father and the Son. Related pages: Theotokos (the title used against Arius), the condemnation of Origen, universal restoration, and Tertullian’s later rigorism.

Timeline

Justin Martyr (c. 165)

Justin Martyr calls Christ “the first-born of God” and the “first-begotten Word of God,” who “is even God.”2 This Word, he writes, God “begat before all creatures… by an act of will,” and though the Word is “numerically distinct” from the Father — “another God” in that sense — the begetting is “not by abscission, as if the essence of the Father were divided,” like one fire kindled from another that is “not lessened.”3 The sources of this language of “another God” are disputed; see from what sources did the apologists draw the Logos as “another God”?

Tertullian (c. 213)

Tertullian, writing in Latin against the modalist Praxeas, speaks of the Trinity, “which distributes the Unity into a Trinity… the Father, the Son, and the Holy Ghost: three, however, not in condition, but in degree; not in substance, but in form… yet of one substance, and of one condition, and of one power.”4 The Persons are distinct but not divided — “as the root puts forth the tree, and the fountain the river, and the sun the ray… nor, indeed, is the Word separated from God” — “in the way of distinction, not of division.”5 The Latin behind “of one substance” is unius autem substantiae, and behind “distributes the Unity into a Trinity,” unitatem in trinitatem disponit.6 Whether his ordering of the Son after the Father is one of origin or of nature is disputed; see was Tertullian’s subordination of the Son one of order and origin, or of nature?

Origen of Alexandria (c. 248)

Origen, in De Principiis as it survives in Rufinus’s Latin translation, teaches the Son’s eternal generation: God never “existed, even for a moment of time, without having generated this Wisdom… born indeed of Him… but without any beginning.”7 On John 1:1 he distinguishes the Father as “Very God” (Autotheos, God with the article) from the Word who is God “by being with the Father,” and elsewhere grants that the Son may be called a “second God.”8 How this ranking of the Son relates to the later Arian position is disputed; see does Origen’s ranking of the Son below the Father belong to the common pre-Nicene idiom, or supply premises for the Arian position?

Arius (c. 321)

Arius, an Alexandrian presbyter, teaches that the Son had a beginning. His words survive as Athanasius quotes them: “God was not always a Father… The Son was not always,” “once He was not,” the Word “made out of nothing” and “alterable.”9 The Son is “not equal, no, nor one in essence” with the Father but “a beginning of things originated,” and “foreign from the Son in essence is the Father.”10 His slogan was “there was when he was not” (ἐν ποτε ὅτε οὐκ ἦν).

The Council of Nicaea (325)

The Council of Nicaea confesses the Son “begotten of the Father… that is, from the essence of the Father; God from God, Light from Light, Very God from Very God, begotten not made, One in essence (homoousios) with the Father,” a term not found in Scripture, and anathematizes those who say “Once He was not… or… He came to be from nothing.”11 Athanasius later wrote that the fathers adopted the non-scriptural terms because the Arians accepted every scriptural phrase while reading it in their own sense, so that “from the essence” and “one in essence” were written to exclude that reading.12

Eusebius of Caesarea (c. 325)

Eusebius of Caesarea, the church historian and pupil of Pamphilus, writes of the Son as a “second Being,” a “Second Lord” begotten before the ages who holds a divinity “received from the Father,” “greater than all angels, but less than the First Cause,” and “not… eternally coexistent with the Father, since… a father must exist before and precede his son.”13 Before Nicaea he wrote to Alexander of Alexandria that Arius had been misrepresented, and the Synod of Antioch (325) provisionally excommunicated him months before the council.14 At Nicaea he subscribed the creed, and wrote to his church that he had “received without contention what no longer pained us,” taking “one in essence” not to mean that “the Son subsisted from the Father in the way of division, or any severance.”15 In later years he wrote against Marcellus of Ancyra in defence of the Son’s distinct subsistence (see the next entry).

Marcellus of Ancyra (c. 336)

Marcellus of Ancyra, who had stood with the opponents of Arius at Nicaea, teaches that the one God is a “Monad,” and does not speak of three hypostases: “is he not clearly saying that there is only one God, namely, himself?”16 The Catholic Encyclopedia describes his Logos as one that “went out from the Father and was God’s Activity in the world” at the creation, and the New Catholic Encyclopedia reads him as “holding that the Logos became Son at the Incarnation only” — an eternal Logos, but no eternal Son.17 Reading 1 Corinthians 15:24–28, he writes that Christ’s reign “began… at a certain time” and that “there will be an end to Christ’s reign.”18 According to the same encyclopedias, his teaching made Eastern bishops suspect homoousios of Sabellianism, and the Council of Constantinople (381) added to the creed the clause “of whose kingdom there shall be no end” (Luke 1:33) against it. His book survives only in fragments quoted by his critic Eusebius of Caesarea.19

Athanasius of Alexandria (c. 356)

Athanasius, present at Nicaea as a deacon and later bishop of Alexandria, exiled five times, writes that the Son is “an offspring proper to the Father’s essence… very God, existing one in essence with the very Father… And He ever was and is and never was not.”20 He argues from salvation: a creature cannot make creatures partakers of God, so the Son who does so is God; and the eternity of the Son entails the eternity of the Triad — “if the Word is not with the Father from everlasting, the Triad is not everlasting.”21 In his Letters to Serapion (c. 359–361) he argues in the same way that the Holy Spirit is not a creature.

Basil of Caesarea (c. 375)

Basil of Caesarea, eldest of the three Cappadocians, distinguishes the terms: “the distinction between ousia and hypostasis is the same as that between the general and the particular… we confess one essence… but we confess a particular hypostasis, in order that our conception of Father, Son and Holy Spirit may be without confusion” — one being in three subsistences; to deny the three hypostases, he writes, is to fall into “the error of Sabellius.”22 His On the Holy Spirit (c. 375) argues against the Pneumatomachi (“Spirit-fighters”) that the Spirit is holy and good by nature, not by participation — “to the creature holiness was brought in from without, but to the Spirit holiness is the fulfilment of nature… not… being sanctified, but… sanctifying” — and is not to be “sub-numbered” below the Father and the Son, for “there is one God and Father, one Only-begotten, and one Holy Ghost.”23

Gregory of Nazianzus and the Council of Constantinople (c. 380–381)

Gregory of Nazianzus, one of the three Cappadocians, presided for a time over the Council of Constantinople in 381. The one Godhead is confessed in three, distinguished “not by natures, but by properties”: the Father unbegotten, the Son begotten, the Spirit proceeding — “the Trinity in the Hypostases or Persons… the Essence of the Three is One and the same.”24 Against those who confessed the Son but not the Spirit as God, he writes that the Spirit “always existed, and exists, and always will exist… perfecting, not being perfected; sanctifying, not being sanctified; deifying, not being deified” — “no sooner do I conceive of the One than I am illumined by the Splendour of the Three.”25 The Creed of Constantinople (381) expands the Nicene Creed’s “and in the Holy Ghost” into a confession of the Spirit as “the Lord and Giver-of-Life, who proceedeth from the Father, who with the Father and the Son together is worshipped and glorified.”26

Gregory of Nyssa (c. 385)

Gregory of Nyssa, Basil’s younger brother, answers the objection that if Father, Son and Spirit each are God they are “three Gods,” as Peter, James and John are three men. In On “Not Three Gods” (to Ablabius) he replies that the plural attaches to the Persons, never to the one nature: “gold, even though it be cut into many figures, is one… The Father is God: the Son is God: and yet by the same proclamation God is One, because no difference either of nature or of operation is contemplated in the Godhead” — every divine operation being one, running “from the Father… through the Son… perfected in the Holy Spirit.”27 To the Pneumatomachi, who granted the Spirit the attributes “Good,” “Holy,” “Eternal,” “Wise” and the rest but not the name of God, he writes: “it is absolutely necessary either to allow to Him community in this also, or not to admit His community in the others.”28
