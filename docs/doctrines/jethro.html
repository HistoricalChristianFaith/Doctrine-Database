Who Was Jethro?

Jethro is a Midianite priest of the Hebrew Bible, outside Israel’s covenant, who shelters the fugitive Moses, gives him a wife, and, after the exodus, blesses the LORD, offers Him sacrifice, and counsels Israel’s leader on how to govern. Moses first meets the priest’s seven daughters at the well in Midian, where he drives off the shepherds who trouble them, and is invited into the priest’s household: “Moses was content to dwell with the man: and he gave Moses Zipporah his daughter” (Exodus 2:21). It is while “Moses kept the flock of Jethro his father in law, the priest of Midian” that he comes “to the mountain of God, even to Horeb” and the burning bush (Exodus 3:1). And after the sea and Sinai, Jethro comes out to the camp, hears the whole story, and confesses: “Now I know that the LORD is greater than all gods… And Jethro… took a burnt offering and sacrifices for God” (Exodus 18:11–12). In the same chapter he watches Moses judge the people alone from morning to evening and gives the counsel that establishes Israel’s judges — “the thing that thou doest is not good… thou shalt provide out of all the people able men… and place such over them, to be rulers of thousands… and let them judge the people at all seasons” — which Moses adopts (Exodus 18:17–24).

The text gives him three names. He is Reuel when the daughters first bring Moses home (Exodus 2:18; so too Numbers 10:29, “Raguel”), Jethro through the burning-bush and counsel narratives (Exodus 3:1; 18:1), and Hobab — “the son of Raguel the Midianite, Moses’ father in law” — when Moses begs him to guide Israel through the wilderness “instead of eyes” (Numbers 10:29–32). Judges then calls the same in-law a Kenite, whose descendants settle with Judah near Arad, and traces Heber — whose wife Jael drives the tent-peg through Sisera — to “the children of Hobab the father in law of Moses” (Judges 1:16; 4:11). The rabbis read the names as one man’s titles; other interpreters read a father and a son, and modern source critics read the trace of distinct sources (see the timeline below).

The later traditions developed the figure in several directions. The rabbis made Jethro one of Pharaoh’s three counsellors, the one who refused the plan against the Hebrew boys and fled; the Torah portion in which Israel receives the Law at Sinai bears his name (Parashat Yitro), and the Talmud traces to him a line of descendants, the Rechabites and Kenites, seated in the Chamber of Hewn Stone. The Fathers read him figuratively and morally: Origen made Moses’ acceptance of Jethro’s counsel a warrant for the Church’s use of what is “said wisely by the Gentiles,” and Gregory of Nyssa, in his Life of Moses, made Jethro’s daughter, Moses’ foreign wife, the figure of the philosophy that may serve the higher life. Muslim commentators have widely identified him with the prophet Shu‘ayb, sent to the people of Midian (Qur’an 7:85–93; 11:84–95); and the Druze venerate Nabi Shu‘ayb / Jethro as their foremost prophet, whose shrine at Hittin, held by the Druze to be his tomb, is their chief shrine and pilgrimage.

This page is a biographical tradition-timeline: not a life of Jethro but a history of what was said and believed about him across the centuries, set out earliest source first. Its “witnesses” are the datable sources that report on or interpret him — the Exodus and Numbers narrative and its Judges echoes, the rabbinic traditions of his conversion and his refusal at Pharaoh’s council, the Fathers who read his counsel as a warrant for taking up pagan learning, the Islamic Shu‘ayb, and the Druze veneration — down to the modern scholars who ask whether Israel first learned the name of YHWH from Midian. Several questions about him are disputed: whether Reuel, Jethro and Hobab are one man, a father and son, or a seam between sources; whether Israel received the worship of YHWH from Midian (the “Kenite / Midianite hypothesis”); whether his confession is a convert’s or a henotheist’s; and whether he is the Shu‘ayb of the Qur’an, which has its own page under Disputed readings. Other timelines on this site follow Gentiles who confess Israel’s God — Naaman the Syrian (“there is no God in all the earth, but in Israel”) and the Queen of Sheba — and the pagan figures Balaam and Jannes and Jambres. The Mekhilta itself sets Jethro’s confession beside Naaman’s (see the rabbis on Jethro).

Timeline

The biblical portrait (Exodus 2–18, with Numbers 10 and the Judges echoes)

The biblical narrative gives a handful of scenes and several names. Moses, a fugitive from Egypt, sits down by a well in Midian and defends the seven daughters of “the priest of Midian” from the shepherds who would drive them off; brought home, he is taken into the household and married to Zipporah, who bears him Gershom, “for he said, I have been a stranger in a strange land” (Exodus 2:16–22). The priest is here called Reuel; a chapter later, keeping “the flock of Jethro his father in law, the priest of Midian,” Moses leads it “to the backside of the desert” and comes “to the mountain of God, even to Horeb” — the site of the burning bush (Exodus 3:1), where the God of Israel first speaks to Moses while he tends the priest’s flock.1

After the sea and the coming to Sinai, Jethro brings Moses’ wife and sons out to the camp, and, hearing “of all that God had done for Moses, and for Israel his people,” makes his confession: “Blessed be the LORD, who hath delivered you out of the hand of the Egyptians… Now I know that the LORD is greater than all gods.” He follows the word with an act of worship — “Jethro, Moses’ father in law, took a burnt offering and sacrifices for God” — and Aaron and all the elders come to eat bread with him “before God” (Exodus 18:9–12). Then the Midianite priest counsels Moses on government. Watching Moses judge the people alone from morning to evening, he warns, “The thing that thou doest is not good… thou wilt surely wear away,” and lays out the remedy that establishes Israel’s judges: teach the people the statutes, but “provide out of all the people able men, such as fear God, men of truth, hating covetousness,” and set them as “rulers of thousands… and let them judge the people at all seasons,” bringing only the great matters to Moses. “So Moses hearkened to the voice of his father in law, and did all that he had said” (Exodus 18:17–24).2

The narratives name the father-in-law differently. He is Reuel in Exodus 2:18, Jethro from Exodus 3:1 onward, and, when Israel breaks camp from Sinai, Hobab, the son of Raguel (Reuel) — whom Moses presses to stay as a desert guide: “Leave us not, I pray thee; forasmuch as thou knowest how we are to encamp in the wilderness, and thou mayest be to us instead of eyes” (Numbers 10:29–32). Judges then names the same in-law a Kenite: his descendants go up with Judah into the wilderness south of Arad, and Heber — whose wife Jael will kill Sisera — is reckoned “of the children of Hobab the father in law of Moses” (Judges 1:16; 4:11). Whether Reuel, Jethro, and Hobab are one man under several names, a father and a son telescoped together, or the trace of distinct sources joined in the text, is answered differently: the rabbis claim all the names for one man (below); Albert Barnes takes Hobab as Moses’ brother-in-law, reading “father-in-law” as any relation by marriage; W. F. Albright, as the Encyclopaedia Judaica reports him, takes Reuel as the clan of both Jethro and Hobab; and source critics assign the names to different Pentateuchal sources (see Modern scholarship).3

The rabbis (the Mekhilta and Sifrei, 3rd c.; the Talmud, c. 500–600; Exodus Rabbah)

The rabbinic sources pair Jethro with Balaam as a righteous gentile beside a wicked one, and four traditions carry the portrait. First, Jethro is one of Pharaoh’s three counsellors on the plan to afflict the Hebrews: “Balaam, who advised … was killed. Job, who was silent … was punished by suffering. Yitro, who ran away as a sign of protest, merited that some of his children’s children sat in the Sanhedrin in the Chamber of Hewn Stone” — a measure-for-measure legend in which each of three men is repaid for what he did at the same council (b. Sotah 11a; b. Sanhedrin 106a; Exodus Rabbah 1:9).4 Second, his confession “Now I know that the LORD is greater than all gods” (Exodus 18:11) the Mekhilta reads as the conclusion of a man who had tried every alternative: “There was no idolatry in the world that Yithro did not come across and did not worship” — in this reading the seeker who tries everything and, finding it empty, converts, the counterpart in rabbinic lore to Balaam, whose knowledge of God served his greed.5 Third, the rabbis resolve the Reuel/Jethro/Hobab question by claiming all the names for one man — the seven names of Jethro (Yether, Yithro, Chovav, Reuel, Chever, Putiel, Keni), each read as a title of merit (“Putiel — he weaned himself from idolatry”; the letter added to make “Yithro” the reward of his conversion); modern source criticism reads the same names as a seam between sources (see Modern scholarship, below).6 Fourth, the reward comes due in his line: the Kenites and Rechabites, the “families of the scribes which dwelt at Jabez” (1 Chronicles 2:55), are Jethro’s descendants, seated among the scholars in the Chamber of Hewn Stone — and the very Torah portion in which Israel receives the Law at Sinai is named for him, Parashat Yitro.7 Full detail »

The Fathers (Origen, 3rd c.; Gregory of Nyssa, Chrysostom, Augustine, 4th–5th c.; Gregory the Great, 6th c.)

The Fathers read Jethro as a figure. Origen dwells on a Gentile priest instructing the man to whom God spoke “face to face,” and draws from it a rule for Christian learning: Moses “accepted counsel from Jethro the priest of Madian … He hears not the one who speaks, but what he says. Whence also we, if perhaps now and then we discover something said wisely by the Gentiles, ought not immediately to despise what is said … we should ‘prove all things and hold fast that which is good’” — the personal form of the same motif he calls, in his Letter to Gregory, the “spoiling of the Egyptians,” the Church’s licit plunder of Greek philosophy, geometry, and astronomy for the service of Scripture.8 Gregory of Nyssa extends the reading to the pagan priest’s daughter — Zipporah, Moses’ foreign wife: “The foreign wife will follow him, for there are certain things derived from profane education which should not be rejected… moral and natural philosophy may become… a comrade, friend, and companion of life to the higher way,” though secular philosophy on its own is “barren… always in labor but never gives birth.”9

The same scene yields a second, joined reading: the delegation of the judges. Origen allegorizes the “rulers of thousands, hundreds, fifties, and tens” (Exodus 18:21) as the graded courts of the Church, kept “also in the future age” when the apostles “sit on twelve thrones.” Chrysostom marvels that the conqueror of Egypt “as a son-in-law was humbler than his father-in-law, and took advice from him, and was not indignant.” Augustine, opening On Christian Doctrine, makes the episode a proof that God teaches through human means: Moses “accepted the plan of his father-in-law, a man of an alien race … for a wise plan, in whatever mind it might originate, was to be ascribed … to Him who is the Truth, the unchangeable God.” And Gregory the Great turns it into a rule of ministry: Moses, “judged by the reproof of Jethro, who was of alien race,” is counselled to “appoint others in his stead for settling earthly strifes, and he himself should be more free to learn spiritual secrets for the instruction of the people.” In these readings the episode teaches the Church two things: to receive the truth wherever it is found, and to govern by sharing the burden.10 Full detail »

The Islamic tradition and the Druze (the Qur’an, 7th c.; the tafsir; the Druze veneration, 11th c. onward)

The Islamic and Druze traditions about the figure are living ones. The Qur’an sends the prophet Shu‘ayb (شعيب) to the people of Midian (Madyan) and to the “companions of the thicket” (Ashab al-Aykah): he preaches the worship of the one God and honest weights and measures — “O my people, worship Allāh… fulfill the measure and weight and do not deprive people of their due” — is mocked, and his people are destroyed by earthquake, shriek, and a canopy of fire (Qur’an 7:85–93; 11:84–95; 26:176–191; 29:36–37). The Qur’an does not name Shu‘ayb as Moses’ father-in-law; the commentary tradition — in which the Qisas al-Anbiya (“Stories of the Prophets”) is named, along with al-Tabari and Ibn Kathir ⚠ unverified — widely identifies the two, so that Shu‘ayb becomes the Islamic Jethro. Others, among them the Qur’an commentator Abdullah Yusuf Ali, as reported, and the Ahmadiyya writer Jalees Ahmad, hold Shu‘ayb a distinct and earlier prophet; Ibn Kathir’s genealogy makes Shu‘ayb a great-grandson of Abraham. The readings are set out on their own page.11

The Druze hold the identification as part of their faith: Nabi Shu‘ayb / Jethro is their foremost prophet and the ancestor of the whole community — a “hidden” and “true” prophet who received God’s word directly and passed it to Moses, the “revealed” prophet — and the shrine at Hittin, by the Horns of Hattin in the Lower Galilee, that they hold to be his tomb is their holiest shrine and the site of their chief annual pilgrimage, the Ziyarat al-Nabi Shu‘ayb (observed 25–28 April, an official holiday in Israel).12 Full detail »

Modern scholarship (a survey) — source-critical, epigraphic, and history-of-religions study

Modern critical study has taken up the Jethro tradition along four lines. First, the source-critical one: the father-in-law’s three names — Reuel, Jethro, Hobab, plus the Kenite of Judges — are read by source critics as a redactional seam where the Pentateuch’s sources were joined (Stephen L. Harris assigns Reuel or Hobab to J and Jethro to E ⚠ unverified; the Encyclopaedia Judaica reports “some modern scholars” assigning Hobab to J and Jethro to E), where the rabbinic seven-names reading and the father-and-son reading take them as one family. Second, the history-of-religions one: the same Midianite-priest-of-Yahweh datum anchors the Kenite/Midianite hypothesis — that Israel first received the worship of YHWH not by Jethro’s conversion but from his desert people — proposed by Ghillany (1862) and Tiele (1870s), classically formed by Budde, defended by H. H. Rowley, and given new support, in its proponents’ view, by the Egyptian “land of the Shasu of Yhw” name-rings at Soleb and Amarah West, which, on van der Toorn’s reading, place a Yahweh-connected people on the Edom–Midian fringe c. 1400 BC; Joseph Blenkinsopp in 2008 judged it “the best explanation currently available.” The objections that Israel emerged within Canaan and that Horeb lies outside attested Midianite territory are reported in the surveys cited without named holders ⚠ unverified. The wider question of the Name’s origin has its own divine-name timeline. Third, the historical-geographical one: archaeology locates Midian in north-west Arabia (the Hejaz; the painted Qurayyah “Midianite” ware; the Midianite tent-shrine at Timna), placing a Midianite priesthood in the region in the period; the surveys cited report no record of the individual outside the biblical text. Fourth, the literary one: the counsel-of-the-judges scene (Exodus 18:13–27) is read by critical commentators as an etiology of Israel’s judiciary, standing in a doublet with Deuteronomy 1:9–18, where Moses himself appoints the judges with no mention of Jethro.13 Full detail »
