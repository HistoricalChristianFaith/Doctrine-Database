Who Was Jethro?

Jethro is the great Gentile mentor of the Hebrew Bible — a Midianite priest, outside the covenant and the line of Abraham, who shelters the fugitive Moses, gives him a wife, and, when the exodus is done, blesses the LORD, offers Him sacrifice, and teaches Israel’s deliverer how to govern. Moses first meets him at the well in Midian, where he drives off the shepherds who trouble the priest’s seven daughters and is taken into the household: “Moses was content to dwell with the man: and he gave Moses Zipporah his daughter” (Exodus 2:21). It is while “Moses kept the flock of Jethro his father in law, the priest of Midian” that he comes “to the mountain of God, even to Horeb” and the burning bush (Exodus 3:1). And after the sea and Sinai, Jethro comes out to the camp, hears the whole story, and confesses: “Now I know that the LORD is greater than all gods… And Jethro… took a burnt offering and sacrifices for God” (Exodus 18:11–12). In the same chapter he watches Moses judge the people alone from morning to evening and gives the counsel that founds Israel’s judiciary — “the thing that thou doest is not good… thou shalt provide out of all the people able men… and place such over them, to be rulers of thousands… and let them judge the people at all seasons” — which Moses adopts wholesale (Exodus 18:17–24). A pagan priest thus names the God of Israel as supreme, sacrifices to Him, and reorders His people’s life — a startling role for an outsider, and the seed of everything the later tradition made of him.

He comes, however, under three names, and Scripture never quite reconciles them — the crux that shadows the whole tradition. He is Reuel when the daughters first bring Moses home (Exodus 2:18; so too Numbers 10:29, “Raguel”), Jethro through the burning-bush and counsel narratives (Exodus 3:1; 18:1), and Hobab — “the son of Raguel the Midianite, Moses’ father in law” — when Moses begs him to guide Israel through the wilderness “instead of eyes” (Numbers 10:29–32). Judges then calls the same in-law a Kenite, whose descendants settle with Judah near Arad, and traces Heber — whose wife Jael will drive the tent-peg through Sisera — to “the children of Hobab the father in law of Moses” (Judges 1:16; 4:11). One man under three names, or a father (Reuel) and son (Hobab) telescoped by the tradition, or the seam where two sources were joined — the question is left open, and it is one of the pages this timeline will weigh.

On that spare and puzzling portrait the later traditions grew in three directions at once. The rabbis made Jethro one of Pharaoh’s three counsellors who, alone, refused the plan to drown the Hebrew boys and fled — earning the Torah portion that bears his name (Parashat Yitro, the very portion in which Israel receives the Law at Sinai) and a line of honoured descendants seated in the chamber of hewn stone, the Rechabites and Kenites. The Fathers read him allegorically: Origen made Jethro’s counsel the model for the church’s right use of pagan learning — the “spoiling of the Egyptians” — and Gregory of Nyssa, in his Life of Moses, made Jethro’s wisdom the figure of the philosophy that may serve, but not rule, the soul. Islamic tradition identifies him — or a figure widely equated with him — as the prophet Shu‘ayb, sent to the people of Midian (Qur’an 7:85–93; 11:84–95); and the Druze venerate Nabi Shu‘ayb / Jethro as their foremost prophet, whose tomb near the Horns of Hattin is their chief shrine and pilgrimage.

This page is a biographical tradition-timeline: not a life of Jethro but a history of what was said and believed about him across the centuries, set out earliest source first. Its “witnesses” are the datable sources that report on or interpret him — the Exodus and Numbers narrative and its Judges echoes, the rabbinic traditions of his conversion and his refusal at Pharaoh’s council, the Fathers who read his counsel as the warrant for taking up pagan learning, the Islamic Shu‘ayb, and the Druze veneration — down to the modern scholars who ask whether Israel first learned the name of YHWH from a Midianite priest. The genuinely contested questions — is Reuel / Jethro / Hobab one man, a father and son, or a redactional seam? did Moses receive the worship of YHWH from Midian (the “Kenite / Midianite hypothesis”)? was Jethro a true convert, and is he the Shu‘ayb of the Qur’an? — are weighed on their own argument pages rather than settled by assertion. Jethro is a prime instance of a recurring biblical type, the Gentile who meets Israel’s God, and belongs with the other outsiders on this shelf who cross that threshold.

Timeline

The biblical portrait (Exodus 2–18, with Numbers 10 and the Judges echoes)

The whole later tradition grows from a handful of scenes and a tangle of names. Moses, a fugitive from Egypt, sits down by a well in Midian and defends the seven daughters of “the priest of Midian” from the shepherds who would drive them off; brought home, he is taken into the household and married to Zipporah, who bears him Gershom, “for he said, I have been a stranger in a strange land” (Exodus 2:16–22). The priest is here called Reuel; a chapter later, keeping “the flock of Jethro his father in law, the priest of Midian,” Moses leads it “to the backside of the desert” and comes “to the mountain of God, even to Horeb” — the site of the burning bush (Exodus 3:1). So the outsider priest’s pasture is where the God of Israel first speaks to Moses.1

After the sea and the coming to Sinai, Jethro brings Moses’ wife and sons out to the camp, and, hearing “of all that God had done for Moses, and for Israel his people,” makes the confession that later readers would weigh so heavily: “Blessed be the LORD, who hath delivered you out of the hand of the Egyptians… Now I know that the LORD is greater than all gods.” He follows the word with an act of worship — “Jethro, Moses’ father in law, took a burnt offering and sacrifices for God” — and Aaron and all the elders come to eat bread with him “before God” (Exodus 18:9–12). Then the Midianite priest instructs Israel’s lawgiver in the art of government. Watching Moses judge the people alone from morning to evening, he warns, “The thing that thou doest is not good… thou wilt surely wear away,” and lays out the remedy that founds Israel’s judiciary: teach the people the statutes, but “provide out of all the people able men, such as fear God, men of truth, hating covetousness,” and set them as “rulers of thousands… and let them judge the people at all seasons,” bringing only the great matters to Moses. “So Moses hearkened to the voice of his father in law, and did all that he had said” (Exodus 18:17–24). A pagan priest names the LORD supreme, sacrifices to Him, and reorders His people’s life.2

Against that portrait stands the name crux that the whole tradition inherits. The father-in-law is Reuel in Exodus 2:18, Jethro from Exodus 3:1 onward, and, when Israel breaks camp from Sinai, Hobab, the son of Raguel (Reuel) — whom Moses presses to stay as a desert guide: “Leave us not, I pray thee; forasmuch as thou knowest how we are to encamp in the wilderness, and thou mayest be to us instead of eyes” (Numbers 10:29–32). Judges then names the same in-law a Kenite: his descendants go up with Judah into the wilderness south of Arad, and Heber — whose wife Jael will kill Sisera — is reckoned “of the children of Hobab the father in law of Moses” (Judges 1:16; 4:11). Whether Reuel, Jethro, and Hobab are one man under several names, a father and a son telescoped together, or the trace of distinct sources joined in the text, is left unresolved by Scripture — and is the first of the questions this timeline weighs.3

The rabbis (the Mekhilta and Sifrei, 3rd c.; the Talmud, c. 500–600; Exodus Rabbah)

Where the rabbis made Balaam the paradigm of the wicked gentile, they made Jethro the paradigm of the righteous one, and four traditions carry the portrait. First, Jethro is one of Pharaoh’s three counsellors on the plan to afflict the Hebrews: “Balaam, who advised … was killed. Job, who was silent … was punished by suffering. Yitro, who ran away as a sign of protest, merited that some of his children’s children sat in the Sanhedrin in the Chamber of Hewn Stone” — a measure-for-measure legend that judges three men by what each does with the same evil (b. Sotah 11a; b. Sanhedrin 106a; Exodus Rabbah 1:9).4 Second, his confession “Now I know that the LORD is greater than all gods” (Exodus 18:11) the Mekhilta reads as the verdict of a man who had exhausted every alternative: “There was no idolatry in the world that Yithro did not come across and did not worship” — the archetypal seeker who tries everything and, finding it empty, converts, and so the exact positive counterpart to the rabbinic Balaam, whose true knowledge of God served only his greed.5 Third, the rabbis dissolve the Reuel/Jethro/Hobab name crux by claiming all the names for one man — the seven names of Jethro (Yether, Yithro, Chovav, Reuel, Chever, Putiel, Keni), each read as a title of merit (“Putiel — he weaned himself from idolatry”; the letter added to make “Yithro” the reward of his conversion) — the harmonizing answer this timeline weighs against the source-critical reading of the crux.6 Fourth, the reward comes due in his line: the Kenites and Rechabites, the “families of the scribes which dwelt at Jabez” (1 Chronicles 2:55), are Jethro’s descendants, seated among the scholars in the Chamber of Hewn Stone — and the very Torah portion in which Israel receives the Law at Sinai is named for him, Parashat Yitro, a gentile convert’s name over the giving of the Law.7 Full detail »
