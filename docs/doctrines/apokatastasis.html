Universal restoration (apokatastasis)

Apokatastasis (Greek for “restoration”, the word of Acts 3:21's “restitution of all things”) names the teaching that God will at the last restore all rational creatures, in some forms even the fallen angels, so that evil comes to an end and God is, in Paul's phrase, “all in all” (1 Corinthians 15:28). Its holders read 1 Corinthians 15:28 and related texts as promising that end, and hold that God's punishments heal rather than only requite. This page records who taught, qualified or opposed it, earliest extant attestation first: Clement of Alexandria and Origen; Gregory of Nyssa, Gregory of Nazianzus and Basil; Didymus, Evagrius and the Antiochenes Diodore and Theodore; the Latin writers Jerome, Ambrose and Augustine; the condemnations of 543 and 553; Maximus the Confessor, Isaac of Nineveh and Eriugena; Julian of Norwich; and, in the modern period, Schleiermacher, MacDonald, Barth, Bulgakov, Balthasar and Hart, with the historians who have debated how widely the Fathers held it. The disputed readings below set out how particular witnesses and the patristic record as a whole have been read.

Related doctrines: The living and the dead (the communion of the saints) — see also the intermediate state · purgatory and prayer for the dead · indulgences · the treasury of merit · prayer to the saints · dream-apparitions of the dead · eternal conscious torment · conditional immortality (annihilationism) · the resurrection of the body (each takes up the same question of what finally becomes of the dead; conditional immortality holds that the lost finally cease to be).

The judgements passed on Origen are traced on the condemnation of Origen, and the teaching on the soul's origin that travelled with the restoration in Origen's system on the origin of the soul; the cleansing fire after death is traced under purgatory.

Timeline

Clement of Alexandria (c. 200)

Clement, head of the Alexandrian school before Origen, holds that God's punishments are corrective, not vindictive — “God's punishments are saving and disciplinary, leading to conversion, and choosing rather the repentance than the death of a sinner”1 — and that this discipline continues after death, the wayward being chastised by “paternal admonitions… till they become ashamed and repent.”2 He does not state in these passages that all will at last be restored.

Origen of Alexandria (c. 229)

Building on 1 Corinthians 15:28, Origen argues in Peri Archōn that “the goodness of God, through His Christ, may recall all His creatures to one end, even His enemies being conquered and subdued,”3 the end answering the beginning “during the lapse of countless and unmeasured ages,” until God is “all in all.”4 He presents it under an announced caution, as a question for inquiry, and argues it at length. In a letter to friends at Alexandria he denied teaching the salvation of the devil: that, he wrote, “no man can say even if he has taken leave of his senses and is manifestly insane.”5

Basil of Caesarea (c. 375)

Basil, brother of Gregory of Nyssa, is cited for an endless punishment. In a monastic rule ascribed to him he answers those “who say that the punishment will be without end” by the parallel grammar of Matthew 25:46: “if the punishment should once end, the heavenly life would necessarily have to end too,” and he calls the notion of an end to punishment “a demonic deceit.”6 Ilaria Ramelli argues that this passage is a later interpolation. With Gregory of Nazianzus, Basil compiled the Philocalia, an anthology of Origen.

Gregory of Nyssa (c. 380)

Reading 1 Corinthians 15:28, Gregory writes of “the complete annihilation of evil,” so that “even from those evil spirits shall rise in harmony the confession of Christ's Lordship,”7 and he describes the purging fire as medicine: after “long periods of time” there is “a restoration of those who are now lying in Sin to their primal state,” and “a harmony of thanksgiving will arise from all creation.”8 He was not named in the condemnations of 543 and 553, and he is honoured as a saint. What his restoration-passages teach, whether they are all his, and how they stand to Origen's teaching and to the anathemas of 553 are disputed; see the disputed reading on Gregory of Nyssa.

Gregory of Nazianzus (c. 381)

Gregory the Theologian, listing the fires of Scripture, allows that “some may prefer… to take a more merciful view of this fire, worthily of Him That chastises”,9 without stating that view as his own. He was, with Basil, a compiler of Origen's Philocalia.

Didymus the Blind (c. 385)

Didymus, blind from childhood and for some fifty years head of the Alexandrian catechetical school, followed Origen. The commentaries recovered in the 1941 Tura papyrus find preserve his teaching of the final restoration in his own words: “aiōnios” punishment is bounded rather than everlasting, salvation alone is “hyper-aiōnios,” and even the rational creatures “who have become wicked” will “have a restoration… after rejecting the evilness that they had”; his pupil Jerome later charged him with this teaching, and also with the pre-existence of souls.10 After his death his name was joined to Origen's and Evagrius's, in the reception of the sixth-century condemnation and by name at the councils of 680–681 and 787.

Jerome (c. 388)

In the Commentary on Ephesians, written at Bethlehem during his years of admiration for Origen, Jerome let stand Origen's “restoration of all things,” when “the fallen angel will begin to revert to what he was created for.” In the Origenist controversy he became a leading Latin opponent of the doctrine, translating Origen's Peri Archōn at Pammachius's request, to “give the true sense of the Greek” and “set down the writer's words for good or for evil without bias in either direction”, as he later wrote to Avitus, and charging his own teacher Didymus with it. In a later work he wrote that the devil and the impious “perish eternally,” but that “Christians, if they be overtaken by sin, must be saved after they have been punished.” Augustine answers a similar view among those of the “tender-hearted”: deliverance promised “only to the catholics, however badly they have lived” (City of God XXI.20).11

Ambrose of Milan (c. 390)

Ambrose, bishop of Milan, who baptized Augustine, teaches in his sermons on Psalm 118 a purgative “baptism… in the vestibule of paradise”: at the gate barred to Adam by the “fiery flaming sword,” “all who desire to return to paradise must be tested through the fire” — John the Evangelist and Peter among them — and only Christ passes untouched. The fire purifies: “he who has passed through the fire enters into rest, passes from things material and worldly to things incorruptible and everlasting.”12 In these passages the fire purifies those who are saved; Ilaria Ramelli places Ambrose among the Latin writers who held a terminating, purgative punishment. The later history of a cleansing fire after death is traced under purgatory.

Diodore of Tarsus (c. 390)

Diodore, head of the school of Antioch and teacher of Chrysostom and Theodore of Mopsuestia, worked from the grammatical sense of Scripture rather than from Origen's allegory; Ilaria Ramelli counts him among the Antiochenes who held the restoration. In a treatise on the divine economy, surviving in Syriac fragments, he sets a proportionate, terminating punishment against an endless reward: there is “torment for sinners, but not everlasting, that the immortality which is prepared for them may not be worthless… immortal and unending happiness is prepared for them.”13 Most of his works were lost after his Christology was attacked in the controversies over Nestorianism; that the council of 553 also condemned him is reported by Photius ⚠ unverified.

Evagrius of Pontus (c. 395)

Evagrius, the desert teacher of contemplative prayer, sets the restoration within a cosmology of fall and return. In the Kephalaia Gnostica he teaches that all rational beings, fallen from an original unity, will be reunited in the knowledge of the Trinity — worlds dissolved, distinctions abolished, so that “in the Unity there will not be those who are leaders… but all will be gods.”14 Whether the anathemas of 553 describe this Evagrian scheme rather than Origen's own text is disputed (see the disputed reading on the anathemas); Evagrius, with Didymus, was condemned by name in the council's reception.

Theodore of Mopsuestia (c. 415)

Theodore, Diodore's pupil and the Antiochene “Interpreter”, teaches a terminating punishment and grounds it in pedagogy: punishment is a stage in the schooling of humanity toward a fixed final good. Reading the Gospel's graded measures — the “uttermost farthing,” the “many” and “few” stripes — he argues that “the penalties, being meted out according to the sins, should finally come to an end.”15 He was condemned by name at the Second Council of Constantinople (553) for his Christology, the first of the “Three Chapters”; Photius charged him with echoing Origen “in that which concerns the end of punishment.”

Augustine of Hippo (c. 420)

In City of God XXI Augustine argues against the restoration. He answers the “tender-hearted” Christians who expected the damned to be released, describes Origen as “even more indulgent” for expecting the devil freed as well, and writes that “the Church, not without reason, condemned him… especially for his theory of the ceaseless alternation of happiness and misery.”16 His theological objection is that a blessedness that can be lost again is no blessedness, so that the cyclical restoration he attributes to Origen “lost even the credit of being merciful.”

The restoration anathematized (543–553)

Justinian's edict to Menas (543) condemned anyone who holds “that the punishment of demons and impious human beings is temporary and that it will have an end at some time”;17 the fifteen anti-Origenist anathemas received at the Second Council of Constantinople (553) condemned “the fabulous pre-existence of souls” and “the monstrous restoration which follows from it,”18 and the council's eleventh canon named Origen among those it anathematized. The sixth-century controversy arose among the monks of Palestine. How far these sentences describe Origen's own teaching or a later system built on Evagrius, and whether they condemned him for a teaching not yet defined as error when he wrote, are disputed on the condemnation of Origen.

Maximus the Confessor (c. 630)

Writing after the condemnations, Maximus distinguishes three senses of apokatastasis and accepts the restoration of the soul's powers “which Gregory of Nyssa especially loves to speak of,”19 while declining to set down a further explanation — “a better and more secret explanation… we… will honour by silence.”20 What the silence withholds is disputed; see the disputed reading on Maximus.

Isaac of Nineveh (c. 680)

Isaac the Syrian, a bishop of the Church of the East, outside the Byzantine church that received the anathemas of 553, writes of Gehenna as real but not as God's vengeance: those punished there “are scourged by the scourge of love,”21 and to think that God made rational beings only to deliver them to endless affliction would be, he says, blasphemy — “the Kingdom and Gehenna are matters belonging to mercy.”22 He is venerated as a saint in the Byzantine, Oriental and Assyrian churches and, since November 2024, is listed in the Roman Martyrology (see his page).

John Scotus Eriugena (c. 860)

Eriugena's Periphyseon builds a cosmology on the return (reditus) of all things to God: “there is a general return of all things to God… the finite will be absorbed in the infinite,”23 God annihilating no nature, only substance-less evil being excluded. Read as pantheism, the work was condemned in 1210 and burned by papal order in 1225.

Julian of Norwich (c. 1393)

Julian, an English anchoress, records in her Revelations of Divine Love the assurance she says Christ gave her, “all shall be well, and all shall be well, and all manner of thing shall be well,” and in the parable of the lord and the servant she is shown a God who looks on fallen humanity with pity and “assigns no blame.” She writes that she was shown no soul in hell, that she could not see how all could then be well, and that she held to the Church's teaching that many are condemned; she places the reconciliation in a “Great Deed” that God keeps hidden until the last day.24 Ilaria Ramelli reads her as a hedged universalist; Denys Turner reads her as holding a disciplined reserve short of universalism.

Friedrich Schleiermacher (c. 1830)

Schleiermacher, a Reformed theologian, takes up the question in Protestant dogmatics. In the appendix to The Christian Faith he weighs eternal damnation against “the milder view… that through the power of redemption there will one day be a universal restoration of all souls,” and holds that this milder view has “at least… equal rights” with the received doctrine, since Scripture gives no “decisive testimony” that Christ looked to a permanently divided end.25 He grounds it in his doctrine of election — redemption and the fore-ordination to blessedness are equally wide — and holds it as a view with equal right rather than as asserted dogma. Michael McClymond calls him “the first great theologian of modern times to teach universalism.”

George MacDonald (c. 1867)

MacDonald, the Scottish preacher and novelist whom C. S. Lewis called his “master,” brought the hope to a wide English-speaking readership. In the Unspoken Sermons he reads the “consuming fire” as “the active form of Purity… that which is indeed Love, the creative energy of God,” a fire that burns away the destructible “that by that very burning” the person “may be saved”; and he treats punishment as wholly remedial — “God is not bound to punish sin; he is bound to destroy sin” — so that “no hell will be lacking which would help the just mercy of God to redeem his children.”26 He writes of the restoration of all through this purifying mercy as personal conviction rather than as a system. Lewis did not follow him into universal salvation.27

Karl Barth (c. 1942)

Barth, the Swiss Reformed theologian, sets out in Church Dogmatics II/2 a doctrine of election in Christ that embraces all, and declines to state that all will be saved. To respect the freedom of grace, he held, “we cannot venture the statement that it must and will finally be coincident with the world of man… (as in the doctrine of the so-called apokatastasis),” nor “the opposite statement that there cannot and will not be this final opening up… of the circle of election”; we are “forbidden to count on” universal reconciliation as a claim on God, yet “commanded the more definitely to hope and pray for it.”28 George Hunsinger calls this position a “reverent agnosticism”; whether it amounts to universalism is examined by Ryan Koonz.

Sergius Bulgakov (c. 1945)

Bulgakov, dean of the St Sergius Institute in Paris and an Orthodox theologian, holds in The Bride of the Lamb that “the doctrine of the eternity of torments, in the sense of infinite duration,” cannot stand, that “an eternal separation of humanity in the elect and the reprobate is clearly not the final meaning of creation,” and that hell is a purgative, transitional state on the way to a universal deification in which God is all in all — “only deification… is the only theodicy.”29 He grounds it on Gregory of Nyssa, whose “mighty No” to the eternity of hell he judges never refuted, and he states the restoration as his teaching rather than as a hope.

Hans Urs von Balthasar (c. 1988)

The Swiss Catholic theologian Balthasar argues in Dare We Hope “That All Men Be Saved”? that neither Scripture nor the Church lets anyone know, before the judgment, that a single soul is damned, so that Christians are permitted, and in charity bound, to hope that all are saved: “these statements give us a right to have hope for all men… Certainty cannot be attained, but hope can be justified.”30 He distinguishes this hope from Origen's asserted apokatastasis, which he declines to teach.

The modern historical debate (1991–2018)

Brian Daley's The Hope of the Early Church (1991) surveyed patristic eschatology as a whole; Ilaria Ramelli's The Christian Doctrine of Apokatastasis (2013) argued that a biblically grounded doctrine of universal restoration ran from the New Testament to Eriugena and was “abundantly received throughout the Patristic era”; and Michael McClymond, in a review (2015) and in The Devil's Redemption (2018), answered that there are no unambiguous universalist Fathers before Origen and that Daley's data show a large majority teaching endless punishment.31 How widely the Fathers held the restoration is set out on the disputed reading on its patristic extent.

David Bentley Hart (c. 2019)

Hart, an American Orthodox philosopher and theologian, argues in That All Shall Be Saved that the restoration is certain, not only to be hoped for: “if Christianity taken as a whole is indeed an entirely coherent and credible system of belief, then the universalist understanding of its message is the only one possible. I say that without the least hesitation or qualification.”32 His central argument is metaphysical: a God who freely creates a world in which any rational creature is eternally lost would make that loss part of what He willed, “a complete contradiction” in the doctrine of God. He appeals to Origen and Gregory of Nyssa. His book appeared a year after McClymond's.
