Who Was Enoch?

Enoch gets four verses of narrative and an epitaph unlike any other in the Genesis genealogy. Seventh in the line from Adam, he “walked with God” three hundred years, “and he was not; for God took him” (Genesis 5:24) — alone among the antediluvian patriarchs, the genealogy that tolls “and he died” over every other name falls silent at his. On that silence, and on the number of his years — three hundred and sixty-five, the days of the solar year — a large literature grew. The New Testament reads the disappearance as a bodily translation that let him escape death (Hebrews 11:5); Jude makes him a prophet, the “seventh from Adam,” and quotes words of his about the Lord coming with his holy myriads to judge the ungodly — words found not in Genesis but in the Book of Enoch, 1 Enoch (Jude 14–15). Writings bearing his name accumulated over many centuries — the Ethiopic 1 Enoch (from the 4th–3rd century BC onward), the Slavonic 2 Enoch, the Hebrew 3 Enoch (c. 5th–6th century AD) in which the patriarch is exalted into the angel Metatron — and made him the recipient of the secrets of heaven, the calendar, and the coming judgment. Was he a mere name in a list, spared death by a miracle? An author, and is the book that bears his name Scripture, given that Jude cites it? One of the “two witnesses” who never died and will return to face Antichrist? Or the seer swept up to walk among the angels — or, in the latest layer, to become one?

This page is a biographical tradition-timeline: not a life of Enoch — the Genesis account of him is four verses — but a history of what was said and believed about him across the centuries, set out earliest source first. Its “witnesses” are the datable sources that report on or interpret him: the Genesis genealogy and its echoes in Hebrews, Jude, and Sirach; the vast 1 Enoch and its sequels 2 and 3 Enoch; the Book of Jubilees; the Fathers who cited or defended the Enochic writings (Tertullian among them) and those who set them aside (Jerome, Augustine); the tradition of Enoch and Elijah as the two undying witnesses of Revelation 11; and the long afterlife down to the Ethiopian Orthodox canon and modern scholarship. Much of the Enochic material — the fall of the Watchers, the origin of the Nephilim, the angels who taught forbidden arts — is traced on the Nephilim, pillars of Seth, astrology, and divine council timelines; this page keeps to the figure of Enoch and the reception of the books that bear his name. Two disputed questions — whether 1 Enoch was received as Scripture in the early churches, and how the identification of Enoch with the angel Metatron arose — are set out under Disputed readings.

Timeline

The biblical portrait (Genesis 5, Hebrews, Jude, and Sirach)

The whole later tradition grows from a single terse notice and three backward glances at it. In the antediluvian genealogy of Genesis 5, where every patriarch’s entry ends “and he died,” Enoch — the seventh from Adam — breaks the pattern: he “walked with God after he begat Methuselah three hundred years,” his days were “three hundred sixty and five years” (the count of the solar year), “and Enoch walked with God: and he was not; for God took him” (Genesis 5:24). The Hebrew “took” (laqach) and the missing death-notice together seeded the belief that Enoch did not die at all. Hebrews, Jude, and Sirach draw that belief out. The Epistle to the Hebrews makes it the second of its examples of faith: “By faith Enoch was translated that he should not see death; and was not found, because God had translated him: for before his translation he had this testimony, that he pleased God” (Hebrews 11:5).1 Jude goes further, making Enoch a prophet and quoting him by name: “And Enoch also, the seventh from Adam, prophesied of these, saying, Behold, the Lord cometh with ten thousands of his saints, to execute judgment upon all” (Jude 14–15) — words drawn not from Genesis but from the opening oracle of the Book of Enoch (1 Enoch 1:9), the New Testament’s one explicit quotation of that book, and a point in the later debate over its authority. The deuterocanonical Wisdom of Sirach twice recalls him among the great forefathers — “Enoch pleased the Lord, and was translated, being an example of repentance to all generations” and “upon the earth was no man created like Enoch; for he was taken from the earth” (Sirach 44:16; 49:14).2 Four strands are thus already present in these passages — the man who walked with God, the man translated so as not to see death, the prophet of the coming judgment, and (through Jude’s citation) the author of a book later writers debated — and later witnesses on this timeline take up one or more of them.

1 Enoch makes him the great antediluvian revealer (composed from the 4th–3rd c. BC)

The earliest and largest of the books written in his name, 1 Enoch is drawn on heavily by later writers. It is not one work but an anthology of five once-separate booklets — the Book of the Watchers (chs. 1–36), the Similitudes or Parables (37–71), the Astronomical Book (72–82), the Book of Dreams (83–90), and the Epistle of Enoch (91–105; chs. 106–108 add the Birth of Noah and a closing chapter) — composed, on Nickelsburg and VanderKam’s dating, from the 4th–3rd century BC to about the turn of the era, preserved complete only in Ethiopic (Ge’ez) but attested in Aramaic among the Dead Sea Scrolls (all but the Similitudes, the one section absent from Qumran). Beyond its Watchers-and-Nephilim narrative — the fall of the angels and the forbidden arts, traced on the Nephilim, astrology, and pillars of Seth timelines — its contribution to the figure is a portrait: Enoch the “scribe of righteousness” whose eyes God opened, who is caught up through a house of crystal and fire to stand before the throne of “the Great Glory,” is shown the courses of the heavens and a 364-day solar calendar (matching his 365 years) and the coming judgment, and returns to write it down. Jubilees, 2 and 3 Enoch, and a number of the Fathers take up this ascended seer.3 And its opening oracle, that the Lord “cometh with ten thousands of His holy ones… to execute judgement upon all” (1 Enoch 1:9), is the text the New Testament book of Jude attributes to Enoch by name — the one explicit New Testament quotation of the book. The debate over its authority is set out at “Was 1 Enoch received as Scripture in the early churches?”4

The Book of Jubilees gathers him into one portrait: first scribe, and translated priest of Eden (mid-2nd c. BC)

A generation or two after the earliest Enochic booklets, the Book of Jubilees — a retelling of Genesis revealed to Moses on Sinai, written in Hebrew about 160–150 BC and preserved complete only in Ethiopic — distils the tradition into a single orderly notice of the man (Jubilees 4:16–25). Enoch is “the first among men that are born on earth who learnt writing and knowledge and wisdom”; he wrote down the signs of heaven, the calendar, and the sabbaths of the years; he spent six jubilees “with the angels of God,” who showed him “everything which is on earth and in the heavens”; and he “testified to the Watchers, who had sinned with the daughters of men” (the Watchers myth itself is traced on the Nephilim timeline).5 Jubilees reads the terse “God took him” of Genesis 5:24 as a translation “in majesty and honour” into the Garden of Eden — which it ranks among the four holy places and calls “the holy of holies” — where Enoch does not merely rest but still works: recording the world’s judgment and, priest-like, burning “the incense of the sanctuary… before the Lord.” Its Enoch is the scribe/author, the calendar-revealer, and the man translated to a paradise where he still ministers.6

2 Enoch carries him up through the heavens and makes him a “glorious one” (1st c. AD?)

The Slavonic 2 Enoch (the Book of the Secrets of Enoch) is the fullest early “heavenly-Enoch” text. Two shining messengers carry the patriarch bodily up through the (seven, in one stratum ten) heavens — past the fallen Watchers, the “Grigori,” imprisoned in the fifth heaven (the Nephilim myth, traced there) — to the throne of God in the highest heaven. There, at the Lord’s command, the archangel Michael takes from Enoch his “earthly robe,” anoints him with holy oil, and clothes him in “the raiment of My glory,” so that the seer looks at himself and finds he has become “like one of His glorious ones… and there was no difference” from the angels — an angelic transformation of the patriarch.7 Dictated the secrets of creation, he writes them in 366 books, is sent home for thirty days to instruct his sons, and is then taken up for good in a divine darkness — the fullest early narrative of the Genesis 5:24 translation. Its date is disputed: R. H. Charles (1896) dated its composition to AD 1–50, before the Temple’s fall in AD 70, and it survives only in later Slavonic manuscripts; its closing chapters (71–72) append the marvelous birth of Melchizedek, treated on the Melchizedek timeline.8

The Fathers on the translation: Enoch as a pledge that God rewards the righteous and that death is not the end (from c. AD 96)

Alongside the disputed book, the Fathers kept a memory of the man of Genesis 5:24 and Hebrews 11:5 — mostly without reference to the Watchers lore. The earliest extant Christian comment on Enoch outside the New Testament puts him first in a roll of the righteous: Clement of Rome (c. AD 96) takes him as proof that obedience is rewarded — “found righteous in obedience, he was translated, and death was never known to happen to him.” Cyprian reads the translation as a merciful rescue “from the contagion of this world”; Chrysostom, Cosmas, and Oecumenius make it the first public pledge that death itself will be “abolished.” Because Hebrews files Enoch under faith, the commentators (Chrysostom, Bede) stress that it was believing God to be “a rewarder” that pleased Him, and that (as Tertullian and Bede press) he pleased God with neither circumcision nor the law of Moses. To the question where the translated Enoch now is and whether he is still in the body, Chrysostom, Oecumenius, and Theophylact decline to speculate — Chrysostom calls it “superfluous,” Oecumenius and Theophylact “uncertain… unknown, since Scripture says nothing.”9 Two further readings recur. Several held Enoch’s death not escaped but postponed: he and Elijah are kept alive to return, confront Antichrist, and die at last as the “two witnesses” of Revelation 11 (Tertullian, Cosmas, Bede, Aquinas — a tradition of its own, traced below). And the Latin exegetes read the bare facts typologically: Augustine makes the seventh-from-Adam patriarch (whose name means “Dedication”) a figure of the church’s deferred resurrection-rest, and Bede turns his 365 years — the solar year — and the Greek “T” of his three hundred years walking with God into signs of perseverance and the cross. This consolidated block gathers that whole reception; its head-date is its earliest voice, Clement of Rome.10

Tertullian defends the Book of Enoch as inspired Scripture, appealing to Jude (c. AD 200)

With Tertullian of Carthage the question shifts from what the books in his name say about him to whether the book that bears his name is Scripture. Building On the Apparel of Women on Enoch’s account of the fallen angels, Tertullian pauses (I.3) to defend the book’s genuineness, in the earliest extant Christian argument for it outright. He records that it “is not received by some, because it is not admitted into the Jewish canon either,” anticipates the objection that a book “published before the deluge” could not have survived the Flood, and answers it twice: Noah, Enoch’s great-grandson, could have carried its contents through in living memory; or, failing that, the Spirit could have re-inspired it afterward, as Ezra was believed to have restored the Law lost in the Babylonian sack of Jerusalem.11 His central argument is that because Enoch “in the same Scripture has preached likewise concerning the Lord,” the book “pertains to us,” and — adding a further ground — “Enoch possesses a testimony in the Apostle Jude” (Jude 14–15), so “nothing at all must be rejected by us” and “every Scripture suitable for edification is divinely inspired.” He handles it as Scripture elsewhere too, quoting “the most ancient prophet Enoch” against idolatry (On Idolatry 4, 15). Jerome and Augustine later set the book aside; whether the early churches received it as Scripture is set out at “Was 1 Enoch received as Scripture in the early churches?”12

Enoch and Elijah, the two undying witnesses reserved to face Antichrist (from c. AD 200)

Alongside the debate over his book ran a reading of the man that made Enoch an actor in the last days. The Hebrew Scriptures tell of two men “taken” with no death recorded — Enoch, of whom “God took him” (Genesis 5:24), and Elijah, caught up “by a whirlwind into heaven” (2 Kings 2:11) — and a tradition attested from c. AD 200 held that both are kept in reserve to return, preach against Antichrist, be killed, and rise: the “two witnesses” who prophesy 1,260 days in sackcloth, are slain by the beast, and are raised after “three days and an half” (Revelation 11:3–12). The earliest extant full statement is Hippolytus of Rome’s: expounding Daniel’s last week, he assigns its latter half to “the two prophets Enoch and Elias,” who “will preach 1,260 days clothed in sackcloth,” and applies to them the whole of Revelation 11 — a “two forerunners” symmetry answering to John the Baptist before Christ’s first coming (On Christ and Antichrist 43–47, c. AD 200). Tertullian gives the underlying logic: their death was not escaped but “postponed… they are reserved for the suffering of death, that by their blood they may extinguish Antichrist” (A Treatise on the Soul 50).13 The reading was then dramatized in the Coptic Apocalypse of Elijah (the two descend, are slain, rise, and at the last “pursue the son of lawlessness and kill him”) and the Gospel of Nicodemus, which pictures Enoch and Elijah already waiting in Paradise, “about to return at the coming of Antichrist… to be slain by him at Jerusalem, and to be taken up alive again into the clouds, after three days and a half” — and was set out in Adso of Montier-en-Der’s Letter on… Antichrist (c. 950), which Bernard McGinn treats as the standard medieval account. G. K. Beale and David Aune survey two further identifications of the two witnesses: Moses and Elijah, on the Transfiguration pairing, and, with Tyconius and Primasius, the Church’s testimony; this stratum narrates the Enoch-and-Elijah tradition.14

3 Enoch: the patriarch identified with the angel Metatron, the “lesser YHWH” (c. 5th–6th c. AD)

The Hebrew 3 Enoch (Sefer Hekhalot, “the Book of the Palaces”) — a Merkabah/Hekhalot text ascribed to R. Ishmael but redacted in the fifth or sixth century AD, drawing on older Enoch-Metatron traditions — makes the patriarch the angel: R. Ishmael ascends to behold the chariot and is guided by the “Prince of the Presence,” who reveals that he is none other than the translated patriarch — “Because I am Enoch, the son of Jared,” taken from the generation of the Flood “to be a witness against them.” He is enthroned on a seat “similar to the Throne of Glory,” made ruler over the whole heavenly household, robed and crowned, and given seventy names drawn from the divine Name.15 The book calls him YHWH ha-qaton, the “lesser YHWH,” “for my name is in him” (Exodus 23:21), and tells, as the Babylonian Talmud also does, the “two powers in heaven” episode: the sage Aher sees Metatron seated, suspects “two authorities,” and Metatron is smitten “with sixty rods [pulsei] of fire, so that others would not make the mistake that Aḥer made” (b. Ḥagigah 15a). How the identification of Enoch with Metatron arose is set out at “Did the Enoch–Metatron identification grow out of the earlier Enoch tradition?”; the exalted-mediator theme is traced on the divine council timeline.16

The canonical status of the Book of Enoch: left out of the Greek and Latin canons, kept in the Ethiopian, lost to the West until 1773 (3rd c. onward)

Alongside the use of the Enochic material ran the question whether the Book of Enoch itself was Scripture. On the strength of Jude’s quotation it was cited in the earliest centuries — with a Scripture-formula by the Epistle of Barnabas (c. 130), as history by Irenaeus, as prophecy by Clement of Alexandria, and defended outright by Tertullian — while Origen, who cites the book, writes that the writings bearing Enoch’s name “do not at all circulate in the Churches as divine.”17 In the fourth and fifth centuries Athanasius, Jerome, and Augustine set it aside — Athanasius’s Festal Letter 39 (367) leaves it off both the canonical and the “to be read” lists; Jerome calls it “the apocryphal book of Enoch” (and notes that Jude’s quoting it was why Jude itself “is rejected by many”); and Augustine grants that Jude vouches for Enoch’s prophesying yet rules the writings under his name out of “the purity of the canon” on account of their uncertain, “too great antiquity.” Jerome adds the argument that quoting part of a book (as Paul quotes a pagan poet at Titus 1:12) does not canonize the whole. The book then fell out of use in the Greek and Latin churches and was effectively lost to the West — known only from fragments in a few Byzantine chroniclers — until James Bruce brought complete Ge’ez manuscripts back from Ethiopia in 1773. It survives in the Old Testament canon of one communion alone, the Ethiopian (and Eritrean) Orthodox Tewahedo Church, which never rejected it.18 Whether that early esteem amounts to reception as authoritative Scripture across the early churches is set out at “Was 1 Enoch received as Scripture in the early churches?”

Modern scholarship on the tradition: the Aramaic Enoch of Qumran, the growth of the corpus, and the seventh sage (20th–21st c.)

The timeline closes with critical study of the whole tradition. One major modern development is textual: among the Dead Sea Scrolls of Cave 4 were found eleven fragmentary Aramaic manuscripts of the Enochic writings, edited in the standard editio princeps by J. T. Milik (1976). They attest that four of the five booklets — the Watchers, the Astronomical Book, the Dreams, and the Epistle — already existed in Aramaic well before the turn of the era (the oldest Watchers copy palaeographically dated to the first half of the second century BC), and they turn up a conspicuous absence: the Similitudes (chs. 37–71), the one section with the exalted “Son of Man,” is attested in no Qumran copy — a datum central to the “Son of Man” dating debate. Milik built on the silence a thesis that the Parables are a Christian composition of about AD 270; Greenfield and Stone answered it within a year, and Nickelsburg dates a Jewish Similitudes to about the turn of the era. On the make-up of the book, the Hermeneia commentary of Nickelsburg and VanderKam treats 1 Enoch as an anthology of five once-independent booklets composed in Aramaic from the 4th–3rd century BC to the turn of the era, extant complete only in Ge’ez.19 Behind the figure VanderKam and Kvanvig find the Mesopotamian seventh antediluvian sage, Enmeduranki of Sippar, the sun-god’s city. Other studies include the “Enochic Judaism” thesis (Boccaccini), which makes the corpus the deposit of a distinct Second Temple current, and the Enoch-Metatron scholarship (Orlov, and the “two streams” reading of Scholem and Alexander), set out at “Did the Enoch–Metatron identification grow out of the earlier Enoch tradition?” These scholars treat the Enochic literature as pseudepigraphic — written in Enoch’s name across later centuries — beside the four-verse Enoch of the Genesis genealogy; Tertullian, defending the book, held it to be Enoch’s own.20
