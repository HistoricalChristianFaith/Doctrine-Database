Who Was Enoch?

Enoch gets four verses of narrative and one of the strangest epitaphs in the Hebrew Bible. Seventh in the line from Adam, he “walked with God” three hundred years, “and he was not; for God took him” (Genesis 5:24) — alone among the antediluvian patriarchs, the genealogy that tolls “and he died” over every other name falls silent at his. On that silence, and on the number of his years — three hundred and sixty-five, the days of the solar year — an enormous literature grew. The New Testament reads the disappearance as a bodily translation that let him escape death (Hebrews 11:5); Jude makes him a prophet, the “seventh from Adam,” and quotes words of his about the Lord coming with his holy myriads to judge the ungodly — words found not in Genesis but in the apocryphal Book of Enoch (Jude 14–15). Around this figure Second Temple Judaism spun a whole pseudepigraphic corpus in his name — the Ethiopic 1 Enoch, the Slavonic 2 Enoch, the Hebrew 3 Enoch in which the patriarch is exalted into the angel Metatron — and made him the recipient of the secrets of heaven, the calendar, and the coming judgment. Was he a mere name in a list, spared death by a miracle? An author, and is the book that bears his name Scripture, given that Jude cites it? One of the “two witnesses” who never died and will return to face Antichrist? Or the seer swept up to walk among the angels — even, in the latest layer, to become one?

This page is a biographical tradition-timeline: not a life of Enoch — the canonical sources for that are four verses — but a history of what was said and believed about him across the centuries, set out earliest source first. Its “witnesses” are the datable sources that report on or interpret him: the Genesis genealogy and its echoes in Hebrews, Jude, and Sirach; the vast 1 Enoch and its sequels 2 and 3 Enoch; the Book of Jubilees; the Fathers who cited or defended the Enochic writings (Tertullian foremost) and those who set them aside (Jerome, Augustine); the tradition of Enoch and Elijah as the two undying witnesses of Revelation 11; and the long afterlife down to the Ethiopian Orthodox canon and modern scholarship. Much of the Enochic material — the fall of the Watchers, the origin of the Nephilim, the angels who taught forbidden arts — is weighed elsewhere on this wiki, on the Nephilim, pillars of Seth, astrology, and divine council timelines; this page hubs those threads rather than re-arguing them, and keeps to the figure of Enoch and the reception of the books that bear his name. The genuinely contested questions — is 1 Enoch inspired Scripture, given Jude’s quotation? is Enoch one of the two witnesses? did the patriarch become the angel Metatron? — are weighed on their own argument pages rather than settled by assertion.

Timeline

The biblical portrait (Genesis 5, Hebrews, Jude, and Sirach)

The whole later tradition grows from a single terse notice and three backward glances at it. In the antediluvian genealogy of Genesis 5, where every patriarch’s entry ends “and he died,” Enoch — the seventh from Adam — breaks the pattern: he “walked with God after he begat Methuselah three hundred years,” his days were “three hundred sixty and five years” (the count of the solar year), “and Enoch walked with God: and he was not; for God took him” (Genesis 5:24). The Hebrew “took” (laqach) and the missing death-notice together seeded the belief that Enoch did not die at all. The rest of the canon draws that belief out. The Epistle to the Hebrews makes it the second of its examples of faith: “By faith Enoch was translated that he should not see death; and was not found, because God had translated him: for before his translation he had this testimony, that he pleased God” (Hebrews 11:5).1 Jude goes further, making Enoch a prophet and quoting him by name: “And Enoch also, the seventh from Adam, prophesied of these, saying, Behold, the Lord cometh with ten thousands of his saints, to execute judgment upon all” (Jude 14–15) — words drawn not from Genesis but from the opening oracle of the apocryphal Book of Enoch (1 Enoch 1:9), the New Testament’s one explicit quotation of that book and the seed of the long debate over its authority. The deuterocanonical Wisdom of Sirach twice recalls him among the great forefathers — “Enoch pleased the Lord, and was translated, being an example of repentance to all generations” and “upon the earth was no man created like Enoch; for he was taken from the earth” (Sirach 44:16; 49:14).2 Four strands are thus already in the canonical portrait — the man who walked with God, the man translated so as not to see death, the prophet of the coming judgment, and (through Jude’s citation) the author whose book the church would have to weigh — and every later witness on this timeline takes up one or more of them.

1 Enoch makes him the great antediluvian revealer (composed from the 3rd c. BC)

The earliest and largest of the books written in his name, 1 Enoch is the wellspring of the whole later tradition. It is not one work but an anthology of five once-separate booklets — the Book of the Watchers (chs. 1–36), the Similitudes or Parables (37–71), the Astronomical Book (72–82), the Book of Dreams (83–90), and the Epistle of Enoch (91–108) — composed between the 3rd century BC and about the turn of the era, preserved complete only in Ethiopic (Ge’ez) but attested in Aramaic among the Dead Sea Scrolls (all but the Similitudes, the one section absent from Qumran). Beyond the Watchers-and-Nephilim myth it is famous for — the fall of the angels and the forbidden arts, weighed on the Nephilim, astrology, and pillars of Seth timelines, which this page hubs rather than repeats — its lasting bequest to the figure is a portrait: Enoch the “scribe of righteousness” whose eyes God opened, who is caught up through a house of crystal and fire to stand before the throne of “the Great Glory,” is shown the courses of the heavens and a 364-day solar calendar (matching his 365 years) and the coming judgment, and returns to write it down. This ascended seer is the Enoch every later witness — Jubilees, 2 and 3 Enoch, the Fathers — inherits.3 And its opening oracle, that the Lord “cometh with ten thousands of His holy ones… to execute judgement upon all” (1 Enoch 1:9), is the very text the New Testament book of Jude attributes to Enoch by name — the one explicit New Testament quotation of the book, and the seed of the long debate over its authority weighed at “The canonicity of 1 Enoch” (rated weak).4

The Book of Jubilees gathers him into one portrait: first scribe, and translated priest of Eden (mid-2nd c. BC)

A generation or two after the earliest Enochic booklets, the Book of Jubilees — a retelling of Genesis revealed to Moses on Sinai, written in Hebrew about 160–150 BC and preserved complete only in Ethiopic — distils the sprawling tradition into a single orderly notice of the man (Jubilees 4:16–25). Enoch is “the first among men that are born on earth who learnt writing and knowledge and wisdom”; he wrote down the signs of heaven, the calendar, and the sabbaths of the years; he spent six jubilees “with the angels of God,” who showed him “everything which is on earth and in the heavens”; and he “testified to the Watchers, who had sinned with the daughters of men” (the Watchers myth itself is hubbed to the Nephilim timeline, not re-argued here).5 Above all, Jubilees reads the terse “God took him” of Genesis 5:24 as a translation “in majesty and honour” into the Garden of Eden — which it ranks among the four holy places and calls “the holy of holies” — where Enoch does not merely rest but still works: recording the world’s judgment and, priest-like, burning “the incense of the sanctuary… before the Lord.” This fixes the three strands the later tradition runs with — Enoch the scribe/author (grounding Jude’s “prophesied”), the calendar-revealer, and the man translated to a paradise where he still ministers — and bridges the canonical “he did not die” to 2 Enoch’s heavenly ascent.6
