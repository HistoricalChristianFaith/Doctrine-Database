The Veneration of Relics

Christians have kept, enshrined, touched and kissed the bodily remains and possessions of the saints, built altars over them, and reported miracles at their shrines; others have rejected the practice as the worship of the creature. This timeline records how the practice was described, defended, opposed and legislated, earliest extant attestation first: the church of Smyrna gathering the bones of Polycarp (c. 155); Victricius of Rouen’s sermon on a gift of relics (c. 396); Jerome’s treatise against the Gallic priest Vigilantius, which quotes Vigilantius’s objections (406); Augustine on the miracles at the relics of Stephen (c. 426); the Second Council of Nicaea’s canon requiring relics at the consecration of churches (787); John Calvin’s Treatise on Relics (1543); and the Council of Trent’s decree on relics (1563).

Related doctrines: Sacred images and the veneration of the holy — see also the veneration of images and icons · the veneration of the Cross

Who holds what. In the Orthodox Church, the Serbian Orthodox theologian Justin Popović (1894–1979) writes that “By piously venerating the holy relics of the Saints, the Church reveres them as temples of the Holy Spirit, temples of the Living God, in which God dwells by Grace even after the earthly death of the Saints,” and that “the holy Liturgy is performed only on antimensia, in which are placed parts of the holy relics.” He grounds the practice in Scripture as well: “by the touch of the holy relics of the Prophet Elisea, a dead man was resurrected”; “the handkerchiefs of the holy apostle Paul healed the ill and expelled unclean spirits (Acts 19:12)”; and Peter’s “shadow healed an ill man and expelled unclean spirits (Acts 5:15-16).”1 The Orthodox dogmatic theologian Protopresbyter Michael Pomazansky (1888–1988), in his Orthodox Dogmatic Theology, grounds the honour in the body as “the temple of the Holy Spirit” (1 Cor. 6:19) and writes: “Revering holy relics, we believe not in the power or the might of the remains of the saints in themselves, but rather in the prayerful intercession of the saints whose holy relics before us arouse in our hearts a feeling of the nearness to us of the saints of God themselves, who once wore these bodies.”2

In the Catholic Church, the Council of Trent (1563) taught that the bodies of the martyrs “are to be venerated by the faithful” (see its entry). Herbert Thurston (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1911) reports “the more common opinion of theologians”, that relics are to be honoured “cultu duliae relativae, that is to say with a veneration which is not that of latria (divine worship) and which though directed primarily to the material objects of the cult—i.e., the bones, ashes, garments, etc.—does not rest in them, but looks beyond to the saints they commemorate as to its formal term” (he notes that Thomas Aquinas, Summa Theologiae III q.25 a.6, “does not seem to consider even the word adorare inappropriate”); he adds: “Neither has the Church ever pronounced that any particular relic, not even that commonly venerated as the wood of the Cross, as authentic; but she approves of honour being paid to those relics which with reasonable probability are believed to be genuine and which are invested with due ecclesiastical sanctions.” Thurston reports the scriptural argument of the Roman Catechism drawn up at Trent’s instance: “If the clothes, the kerchiefs ( Acts 19:12 ), if the shadow of the saints ( Acts 5:15 ), before they departed from this life, banished diseases and restored strength, who will have the hardihood to deny that God wonderfully works the same by the sacred ashes, the bones, and other relics of the saints?” — and, with it, “that dead body which, having been accidentally let down into the sepulchre of Eliseus, ‘when it had touched the bones of the Prophet, instantly came to life’ ( 2 Kings 13:21 , and cf. Sirach 48:14 )”.3 The Catechism of the Catholic Church (1992) names “the veneration of relics” among the forms of popular piety, which “extend the liturgical life of the Church, but do not replace it.”4 The 1983 Code of Canon Law provides that “The ancient tradition of placing relics of martyrs or other saints under a fixed altar is to be preserved, according to the norms given in the liturgical books.”5

Among the Protestant churches, Martin Luther’s Smalcald Articles (1537), part of the Lutheran Book of Concord, state: “The relics, in which there are found so many falsehoods and tomfooleries concerning the bones of dogs and horses, that even the devil has laughed at such rascalities, ought long ago to have been condemned, even though there were some good in them; and so much the more because they are without the Word of God; being neither commanded nor counseled, they are an entirely unnecessary and useless thing.”6 The Reformed Second Helvetic Confession (1566) states: “Much less do we believe that the relics of the saints are to be adored and reverenced. Those ancient saints seemed to have sufficiently honored their dead when they decently committed their remains to the earth after the spirit had ascended on high. And they thought that the most noble relics of their ancestors were their virtues, their doctrine, and their faith.”7 The Church of England’s Thirty-nine Articles (finalised 1571), Article XXII, state: “The Romish Doctrine concerning Purgatory, Pardons, Worshipping and Adoration, as well of Images as of Relics, and also Invocation of Saints, is a fond thing, vainly invented, and grounded upon no warranty of Scripture, but rather repugnant to the Word of God.”8 John Henry Newman, writing in the Church of England (Tract 90, 1841), read the Article as directed at “the Romish doctrine”, so that “the Primitive doctrine is not condemned in it”, and, appealing to the Homilies, held that “not all doctrine concerning veneration of relics is condemned in the Article before us.”9 John Calvin’s Treatise on Relics (1543) has its own entry. The Reformed church historian Philip Schaff (1819–1893) writes that “The worship of relics, like the worship of Mary and the saints, began in a sound religious feeling of reverence, of love, and of gratitude,” that “toward the middle of the fourth century the veneration of relics simultaneously with the worship of the saints, assumed a decidedly superstitious and idolatrous character,” and, of Christ, that “His true relics are the Holy Supper and His living presence in the church to the end of the world.”10 Modern academic statements of the Lutheran and Anglican positions on relics have not yet been located for this timeline.

Timeline

The Martyrdom of Polycarp (c. 155)

The letter of the church of Smyrna on the death of its bishop Polycarp (traditionally dated shortly after it; the date is discussed on the detail page) records that, when Polycarp was burned, the church gathered his bones — “more precious than the most exquisite jewels, and more purified than gold” — and laid them where the community could return each year to “celebrate the anniversary of his martyrdom.” The same letter records the fear that the Christians would “worship” the martyr, and answers: “For Him indeed, as being the Son of God, we adore; but the martyrs… we worthily love.”11

Victricius of Rouen’s theology of the relic (c. 396)

Welcoming a gift of relics from Ambrose of Milan, Victricius, bishop of Rouen, took up the objection that a saint’s power could hardly sit in a scrap of bone no bigger than a fingertip. He answered that, because the martyr is joined to the indivisible, omnipresent God, the whole saint is present in every particle: “There is nothing in relics which is not complete… a reminder of perfection, not the injustice of division.” He also writes that the martyr is what God is “by gift, not by property.”12

Jerome against Vigilantius (406)

Jerome’s treatise answers the Gallic priest Vigilantius, whom it quotes: “Why do you kiss and adore a bit of powder wrapped up in a cloth?” Vigilantius had described the cult as all but a heathen ceremony brought into the churches. Jerome replies that no one worships the martyrs (“Madman, who in the world ever adored the martyrs?”), that the honour paid them is referred to Christ whose servants they are, that emperors and the bishop of Rome carry and enshrine relics, and that the relics put demons to flight.13

Augustine and the relics of St. Stephen (c. 426)

When the relics of the protomartyr Stephen reached Roman Africa, Augustine recorded miracles at their shrines — a blind woman given sight, the dead raised, nearly seventy cures at Hippo in under two years — writing that “even now miracles are wrought… by the… relics of His saints.” He also writes: “to our martyrs we build, not temples… but monuments… it is to God, not to them, he sacrifices.”14

The Second Council of Nicaea requires relics at the consecration of churches (787)

The council that restored the veneration of images also legislated on relics. Its seventh canon decrees that relics be placed in every church consecrated without them, and that any bishop who henceforth consecrates a church without relics “shall be deposed, as a transgressor of the ecclesiastical traditions.”15

John Calvin's Treatise on Relics (1543)

Calvin argues in principle that “the desire for relics is never without superstition, and… usually the parent of idolatry”; and, as to fact, that the relics shown across Europe are largely false — enough “true Cross” to “form a whole ship's cargo,” every apostle with “more than four bodies,” rival heads of John the Baptist from Amiens to Rome.16

The Council of Trent's decree on relics (1563)

In the same last-session decree that treated the sacred images, the Council of Trent taught that “the holy bodies of holy martyrs… are to be venerated by the faithful,” and condemned those who deny it. The decree also ordered that “all superstition shall be removed, all filthy lucre be abolished,” and that new relics be approved by the bishop.17
