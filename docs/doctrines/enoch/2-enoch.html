2 Enoch: the ascent through the heavens and the making of a “glorious one”

Dates: the Slavonic apocalypse 2 Enoch (the Book of the Secrets of Enoch, “Slavonic Enoch”) is of genuinely contested date — its core is often placed in the first century AD (before the Temple’s fall in AD 70, on the strength of its interest in a functioning sacrificial cult), which would make it roughly contemporary with the New Testament; but it survives only in later Old Church Slavonic manuscripts (in a shorter and a longer recension), and its date, provenance, and even its Jewish-versus-Christian origin are disputed. It is placed on this timeline as a first-century-AD reception-stratum, after the Jewish sources of that period, with the date-uncertainty flagged · Wikipedia
Summary: 2 Enoch is the fullest early “heavenly-Enoch” text and the crucial middle term between the scribe of 1 Enoch and Jubilees and the angelified Enoch of the later Hebrew 3 Enoch. Two shining messengers carry the patriarch bodily up through the heavens — past the fallen Watchers imprisoned in the fifth heaven — to the throne of God in the highest heaven. There Enoch sees the face of the Lord, is stripped of his “earthly robe,” anointed with holy oil, and clothed in “the raiment of My glory,” so that he looks at himself and finds he has become “like one of His glorious ones… and there was no difference” from the angels. He is dictated the secrets of creation and writes them in 366 books, then is sent back to earth for thirty days to instruct his sons before being taken up for good in a divine darkness. The closing chapters (71–72) append the marvelous birth of Melchizedek, treated on the Melchizedek timeline.

The book

2 Enoch narrates, in the patriarch’s own voice, how in his 365th year two angelic men took him up through the heavens, showed him their whole furniture and their tenants, brought him before the throne of God, and sent him home to leave a testament for his children before his final assumption. It survives entire only in Old Church Slavonic, in two recensions of differing length, and is unattested at Qumran — so, unlike the older Enochic material, its date and origin rest on internal evidence and are debated (see the note below). Where 1 Enoch gives Enoch a throne-vision and Jubilees has him spend “six jubilees with the angels,” 2 Enoch expands the ascent into a guided tour of the graded heavens and, at its climax, turns the seer himself into a glorified, angel-like being — the seed of the trajectory that 3 Enoch will carry to its furthest reach, exalting Enoch into the great angel Metatron. Its treatment of the fallen Watchers is drawn from the older Enochic tradition weighed elsewhere on this wiki — on the Nephilim and astrology timelines — which this page hubs rather than repeats.1

Claims & evidence

The summons: two shining men carry Enoch up into heaven

And there appeared to me two men very tall, such as I have never seen on earth. And their faces shone like the sun, and their eyes were like burning lamps; and fire came forth from their lips… They stood at the head of my bed and called me by my name… And these men said to me: ‘Be of good cheer, Enoch, be not afraid; the everlasting God hath sent us to thee, and lo! to-day thou shalt ascend with us into heaven.’

— 2 Enoch 1:4–8, in the W. R. Morfill translation edited by R. H. Charles, The Book of the Secrets of Enoch (1896), reproduced online. The vision comes in Enoch’s old age, on his bed; the two angelic men — winged, faces like the sun — are the guides who will conduct him through the heavens. Enoch first tells his sons (“Methusal, Regim, and Gaidal”) “what wonderful things the two men had said to me,” leaving them a first instruction, and then is taken up. The book thus reads Genesis’ “God took him” not as a quiet disappearance but as a summoned, guided ascent.2

Up through the heavens — and the fallen Watchers in the fifth heaven

These are the Grigori, who, with their prince Satanail… went down on to the earth… on the side of Mount Iermon, and they saw the daughters of men, that they were fair, and took unto themselves wives… and the giants were born, and the strangely tall men, and there was much wickedness.

— 2 Enoch 18:3–5 (Morfill–Charles), online. The heavens are climbed in order (the book everywhere assumes seven, though one interpolated stratum of the manuscripts numbers up to ten — see the note below). In the fifth heaven Enoch finds the Grigori (the “Watchers,” Greek Egrēgoroi), the angels who under their prince Satanail descended on Mount Hermon, took human wives, and fathered the giants — the 1 Enoch 6–16 rebellion, which this page hubs to the Nephilim timeline rather than re-arguing. What matters for the figure is Enoch’s office within the scene: he calls the silent, standing Watchers to resume the liturgy their fallen brethren abandoned — the righteous seer set over against the apostate angels, as in 1 Enoch.3

Before the throne, Enoch is anointed and clothed and becomes “like one of His glorious ones”

I saw the vision of the face of the Lord, like iron burnt in the fire, and brought forth and emitting sparks, and it burns. So I saw the face of the Lord; but the face of the Lord cannot be told. It is wonderful and awful, and very terrible.

And the Lord said to Michael: ‘Go and take from Enoch his earthly robe, and anoint him with My holy oil, and clothe him with the raiment of My glory.’ And so Michael did as the Lord spake unto him. He anointed me and clothed me, and the appearance of that oil was more than a great light, and its anointing was like excellent dew; and its fragrance like myrrh, shining like a ray of the sun. And I gazed upon myself, and I was like one of His glorious ones. And there was no difference, and fear and trembling departed from me.

— 2 Enoch 22:1, 8–10 (Morfill–Charles), online. This is the pivot of the book and its great bequest to the tradition. Brought before the throne in the highest heaven, Enoch is bidden “rise up and stand before my face for ever,” and then, at the Lord’s command, the archangel Michael performs on him what reads as an angelic investiture: his mortal clothing is removed, he is anointed with the shining oil of God’s glory and robed in a garment of glory, and the transformation is complete — looking at himself, he finds he has become indistinguishable from the angels, “like one of His glorious ones… and there was no difference.” The seer is not merely shown heaven but changed into a heavenly being. This glorification-motif is what the later Hebrew 3 Enoch pushes to its limit when it exalts the patriarch into the angel Metatron; 2 Enoch is the middle term that makes that trajectory legible.4

The heavenly scribe who wrote 366 books

He wrote down the descriptions of all the creation which the Lord had made, and he wrote 366 books, and gave them to his sons.

— 2 Enoch 68:2 (Morfill–Charles), online. Once transformed, Enoch is set beside the archangel Vretil (Pravuil), “more wise than the other archangels,” who dictates to him for thirty days and nights “all the works of heaven and earth and sea,” and Enoch writes them out in 366 books. The scribe-of-righteousness of 1 Enoch and the first-writer of Jubilees here becomes the cosmic secretary of the whole created order — the ground of the tradition (behind Jude’s “prophesied”) that Enoch is an author whose books carry the secrets of heaven.5

Sent back to instruct his sons for thirty days, then taken up for good

And now, Enoch, I give thee a period of thirty days to work in thy house… And after thirty days, I will send My angels for thee, and they shall take thee from the earth, and from thy sons.

The Lord sent a darkness upon the earth, and there was a gloom, and it hid those men standing with Enoch. And the angels hasted and took Enoch and carried him to the highest heaven where the Lord received him, and set him before His face, and the darkness departed from the earth, and there was light.

— 2 Enoch 36:1–2 and 67:1–2 (Morfill–Charles), 36, 67. The transformed Enoch is returned to earth for a fixed span of thirty days to leave his children a testament — the long body of moral instruction that fills the middle of the book — and then is taken up permanently. He tells them the change is already irreversible: “since God has anointed me with the oil of his glory, there has been no food in me, and my soul remembers nothing of earthly pleasure, nor do I desire anything earthly” (56:2). At the appointed hour the Lord sends a darkness over the assembly, the angels carry Enoch off, and he is set before the face of the Lord for ever — the fullest early narrative reading of the terse Genesis 5:24 / Hebrews 11:5 translation.6

The appended birth of Melchizedek (chapters 71–72)

The longer recension closes, after Enoch has been taken up, with a distinct narrative unit: the marvelous birth of Melchizedek from the corpse of Sopanima, the barren wife of Noah’s brother Nir — a child born already “like one of three years old,” sealed a priest on his breast, whom the archangel Michael bears into the paradise of Eden to survive the Flood. Because Enoch is gone from the scene and the passage reads as a separate composition, it is weighed on this wiki as a witness on the Melchizedek timeline — see “2 Enoch 71–72: the marvelous birth of Melchizedek” — and is noted here only so the two Enochic strands of the book are not confused.7

Notes

  • Where it sits on this timeline — and the date problem. 2 Enoch is placed after Jubilees and the biblical portrait, in the first century AD, because its core is commonly dated then — its concern with a still-functioning sacrificial cult suggesting a date before AD 70. But the book survives only in later Slavonic manuscripts, in two recensions, is absent from Qumran, and its date and even its Jewish-versus-Christian provenance are genuinely disputed; some place the surviving form considerably later. It is therefore carried as a first-century-AD reception-stratum with the uncertainty flagged, not pinned to a firm year.
  • Seven heavens or ten? Throughout, 2 Enoch assumes a scheme of seven heavens; but one stratum of the manuscripts (and the longer recension at chapters 20–22) speaks of an eighth, ninth, and tenth heaven, naming the tenth “Aravoth” — a Hebrew term (ʻaravōt) that older Jewish tradition (b. Chagigah 12b) makes the seventh heaven. Charles judged the higher numbers a later interpolation on the underlying seven-heaven text. Either way the throne of God stands in the topmost heaven, and it is there that Enoch is transformed.
  • The middle term of the heavenly-Enoch trajectory. The book’s lasting contribution to the figure is the transformation at the throne. 1 Enoch and Jubilees give an ascended, angel-companioned seer; 2 Enoch is the first to angelify him — anointed, re-clothed, made “like one of His glorious ones” — and the Hebrew 3 Enoch completes the arc by exalting the patriarch into the angel Metatron, the “lesser YHWH.” Read in sequence, the three books trace one line from scribe to angel.
