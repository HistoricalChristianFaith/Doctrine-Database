The Book of Jubilees: Enoch, the first scribe who testified against the Watchers

Dates: composed in the mid-2nd century BC (c. 160–150 BC), in Hebrew; preserved complete only in Ethiopic (Ge’ez), with Hebrew fragments recovered among the Dead Sea Scrolls at Qumran · Wikipedia
Summary: Jubilees — a retelling of Genesis and early Exodus as revealed to Moses on Sinai, dividing history into “jubilees” of forty-nine years — gives the most orderly early portrait of Enoch after 1 Enoch, and clearly draws on the Enochic booklets. In a single dense paragraph (Jubilees 4:16–25) it fixes several strands the later tradition runs with: Enoch was the first man on earth to learn writing, knowledge, and wisdom; he wrote down the signs of heaven and the calendar; he spent six jubilees with the angels; he testified against the Watchers who sinned with the daughters of men; and he was taken from among mankind into the Garden of Eden, where he still records the world’s judgment and burns the incense of the sanctuary. This is Enoch the scribe-author (grounding Jude’s “prophesied”), the calendar-revealer, and the man translated to a paradise where he still ministers — a bridge from 1 Enoch’s throne-vision to 2 Enoch’s heavenly ascent and to the “he did not die” reading of Genesis 5:24 / Hebrews 11:5.

The book

Jubilees presents itself as the revelation an angel of the presence dictated to Moses on Mount Sinai, recounting sacred history from creation to Sinai on a rigid 364-day solar calendar and a scheme of forty-nine-year “jubilees.” Written in Hebrew in the mid-2nd century BC, it was prized at Qumran — some fifteen fragmentary copies were found there, more than of most biblical books — and survives entire only in the Ge’ez Bible of the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church, which counts it canonical.1 Its handling of the Watchers, the Nephilim, and the flood is drawn from and often abbreviates the older Enochic material weighed elsewhere on this wiki — on the Nephilim and astrology timelines — which this page hubs rather than repeats. Here what matters is the portrait Jubilees draws of the man Enoch, which it gathers into one continuous notice.

Claims & evidence

The first man on earth to learn writing, knowledge, and wisdom

And he was the first among men that are born on earth who learnt writing and knowledge and wisdom and who wrote down the signs of heaven according to the order of their months in a book, that men might know the seasons of the years according to the order of their (separate) months.

— Jubilees 4:17, in the R. H. Charles translation (online). Where 1 Enoch makes Enoch the “scribe of righteousness,” Jubilees makes the claim explicit and superlative: he is the first human being ever to learn writing at all — the inventor-scribe of civilization, the man from whom the arts of the pen descend. This is the direct ground of the New Testament’s treating Enoch as an author who could be quoted (Jude 14–15). The same claim is what the antediluvian-pillars-of-knowledge legend later attaches to Seth or to Enoch; that displacement is weighed at “Is the pillars-of-Seth legend a displaced Enoch tradition?” on the pillars of Seth timeline.2

The first to write a testimony, and the calendar-revealer

And he was the first to write a testimony, and he testified to the sons of men among the generations of the earth, and recounted the weeks of the jubilees, and made known to them the days of the years, and set in order the months and recounted the Sabbaths of the years as we made (them) known to him.

— Jubilees 4:18 (Charles), online. Enoch is the origin of the very calendar the book itself keeps: the 364-day solar reckoning, the weeks of years, the jubilee-cycles, and the sabbaths of the years. This matches the Astronomical Book of 1 Enoch (chs. 72–82), where Uriel shows Enoch the courses of the luminaries, and enrolls Enoch on one side of a live Second Temple calendar dispute — the solar reckoning shared by Jubilees and the Qumran community against the lunar. His vision reaches to the end: “what was and what will be he saw in a vision of his sleep… until the day of judgment” (4:19).3

Six jubilees with the angels of God

And he was moreover with the angels of God these six jubilees of years, and they showed him everything which is on earth and in the heavens, the rule of the sun, and he wrote down everything.

— Jubilees 4:21 (Charles), online. Jubilees reads Enoch’s “walking with God” three hundred years (Genesis 5:22) as a sojourn with the angels — six jubilees (294 years, rounded from the biblical three hundred) of angelic tutelage in which he is shown “everything which is on earth and in the heavens” and writes it down. This companionship-with-angels reading is the seed that 2 Enoch will expand into a guided ascent through the seven heavens, and that culminates, at its furthest reach, in 3 Enoch’s exaltation of Enoch into the angel Metatron.4

He testified against the Watchers who sinned with the daughters of men

And he testified to the Watchers, who had sinned with the daughters of men; for these had begun to unite themselves, so as to be defiled, with the daughters of men, and Enoch testified against (them) all.

— Jubilees 4:22 (Charles), online. The Watchers’ descent and the birth of the Nephilim are set out and weighed in full on the Nephilim timeline; this page does not re-argue them. What belongs to the figure of Enoch is the office Jubilees assigns him within that story: he is the witness for the prosecution — the man who “testified against” the Watchers — exactly the intercessor-and-judge role given him in 1 Enoch 12–16. (Notably, Jubilees 4:15 has the Watchers first descend for a good purpose, “to instruct the children of men,” before they fall — a nuance not in 1 Enoch — which makes Enoch, the God-taught scribe, the true and faithful culture-bearer against the apostate angels.)5

Taken into the Garden of Eden, where he still records judgment and burns the incense of the sanctuary

And he was taken from amongst the children of men, and we conducted him into the Garden of Eden in majesty and honour, and behold there he writeth down the condemnation and judgment of the world, and all the wickedness of the children of men.

And he burnt the incense of the sanctuary, (even) sweet spices acceptable before the Lord on the Mount.

— Jubilees 4:23, 25 (Charles), online. Here Jubilees gives its most consequential reading of Genesis 5:24. The bare “God took him” becomes a translation “in majesty and honour” into the Garden of Eden — which Jubilees elsewhere calls one of the four holy places on earth and “the holy of holies and the dwelling of the Lord” (4:26) — where Enoch is not merely at rest but still at work: he goes on recording the world’s condemnation and, priest-like, burns the incense of the sanctuary before the Lord. Enoch does not simply escape death; he is removed alive to a paradise-temple where he still ministers. This is the crucial bridge between the terse canonical “he was not, for God took him” / “translated that he should not see death” and the elaborated heavenly Enoch of the later corpus — and it feeds the tradition of Enoch as one of the undying who will return.6

Notes

  • Where it sits on this timeline. Jubilees (mid-2nd c. BC) is later than the oldest booklets of 1 Enoch (3rd c. BC) and depends on them, so it follows 1 Enoch on this timeline and precedes 2 and 3 Enoch and the Fathers. Its value is that it distils the sprawling Enochic material into one orderly biographical notice of the man.
  • It hubs the Watchers material; it does not re-argue it. The fall of the angels, the Nephilim, and the forbidden arts are weighed on the Nephilim and astrology timelines. Here Jubilees matters for the figure of Enoch: first scribe, calendar-revealer, witness against the Watchers, and translated priest of Eden.
  • The Eden-as-sanctuary reading. Jubilees’ picture of Enoch burning incense in Eden treats the garden as the first temple — a reading of Genesis 2–3 that later Jewish and Christian writers develop at length; it is why Jubilees can make the translated patriarch a still-serving priest rather than a retired saint.
