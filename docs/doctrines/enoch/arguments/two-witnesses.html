Is Enoch one of the two witnesses of Revelation 11?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — the reading that Enoch is a literal end-time witness (paired with Elijah) is the earliest and dominant one in the Church, but the text of Revelation 11 codes the two witnesses’ own signs to Moses and Elijah, not Enoch, and a strong symbolic reading treats them as the Church’s prophetic testimony rather than two returning individuals · the classic form advanced by Hippolytus of Rome

The question

Revelation 11:3–12 describes “my two witnesses” who prophesy 1,260 days in sackcloth, work signs, are killed by the beast, lie unburied “three days and an half,” then rise and ascend “to heaven in a cloud.” John never names them, and three readings are genuinely advocated. Two are literal identifications of the same two figures — (a) Enoch and Elijah, the only two men Scripture says never died, and (b) Moses and Elijah, the pair who appeared with Christ at the Transfiguration. The third is corporate/symbolic: the two witnesses are not two individuals at all but the Church’s prophetic testimony, figured by the two lampstands and olive trees. Because (a) and (b) are rival identifications of the same two witnesses, they are weighed here as one question with two candidates; the symbolic reading is folded in as a defeater to both literal candidates. The narrative of the Enoch-and-Elijah tradition itself — Hippolytus, Tertullian, the Apocalypse of Elijah, the Gospel of Nicodemus, Adso — is set out on its witness page; this page adjudicates whether it is true.

Who advances each reading

Enoch and Elijah is the earliest attested and, in the ancient Church, overwhelmingly the majority identification: Hippolytus of Rome states it fully (On Christ and Antichrist 43–47, c. AD 200), Tertullian supplies its logic (De anima 50), Victorinus of Pettau assumes it in the first Latin commentary on the Apocalypse (c. AD 260), and Oecumenius reports it in the sixth century as “an ancient tradition… in the Church.” It passes through the Apocalypse of Elijah, the Gospel of Nicodemus, and Adso of Montier-en-Der into the settled medieval account.1 Moses and Elijah has some ancient footing but is chiefly a modern-commentary favourite, resting on the Transfiguration pairing (Matthew 17:3) and on the signs of Revelation 11:6. The symbolic/corporate reading is the Tyconian–Augustinian line of Latin exegesis (Tyconius, Primasius) and is widely held by modern interpreters, who take “two” as the legal minimum of valid witnesses (Deuteronomy 19:15) and the whole scene as the Church’s witness in the last age.2

The evidence for Enoch and Elijah

The case is theological before it is exegetical, and it is strong on its own terms. Scripture names exactly two men who did not die but were “taken” — Enoch, of whom “God took him” (Genesis 5:24; Hebrews 11:5), and Elijah, who “went up by a whirlwind into heaven” (2 Kings 2:11). Since “it is appointed unto men once to die” (Hebrews 9:27), the two who escaped death are precisely the two who still owe it — so an end-time return in which they are “slain” and rise (Revelation 11:7–11) pays the debt every man owes. Tertullian draws exactly this inference: their death “was postponed… they are reserved for the suffering of death… that by their blood they may extinguish Antichrist.” Elijah’s return is independently promised (Malachi 4:5), and Enoch is the natural second, the only other deathless man — giving the “two forerunners” a symmetry with John the Baptist, Christ’s single forerunner at the first advent (Hippolytus’s argument). Oecumenius states the reasoning plainly:

Regarding another forerunner, we have heard nothing clearly anywhere, except that concerning Enoch, Genesis states that “he pleased God and was taken away.”… An ancient tradition persists in the Church: after Elijah the Tishbite, Enoch will also come, preceding the second coming of Christ and proclaiming the imminent appearance of the Antichrist.

— Oecumenius, Commentary on Revelation, on 11:3.3 The reading’s real strengths are its antiquity (attested c. AD 200, before any rival is on record), its breadth (Greek, Latin, and Coptic sources concur), and its tidy fit with the “deathless” datum and Hebrews 9:27.

The evidence against — and for Moses instead

The witnesses’ own signs point away from Enoch. The decisive weakness of the Enoch reading is that Enoch has no distinctive sign anywhere in the passage, whereas the powers John actually gives the two witnesses are the signs of Moses and Elijah:

These have power to shut heaven, that it rain not in the days of their prophecy: and have power over waters to turn them to blood, and to smite the earth with all plagues, as often as they will.

— Revelation 11:6. Shutting up heaven so that it does not rain is Elijah’s miracle (1 Kings 17:1; cf. James 5:17); turning water to blood and smiting the earth “with all plagues” is Moses’ (Exodus 7:20). The imagery is Moses-and-Elijah-coded, not Enoch-coded, and the Transfiguration — where “there appeared… Moses and Elias talking with him” (Matthew 17:3) — supplies the very pair as the Law-and-Prophets escort of the coming Christ. On the internal evidence of Revelation 11 itself, the Moses reading is the stronger of the two literal identifications; the Enoch reading owes almost nothing to the text of the vision and almost everything to the external “only two never died” syllogism.4

The Hebrews 9:27 argument is not airtight. The “every man must taste death” premise is what makes Enoch necessary, but Scripture itself contemplates an exception: those alive at the last trumpet “shall not all sleep, but we shall all be changed” (1 Corinthians 15:51). If a whole generation can be glorified without dying, the general appointment of Hebrews 9:27 already admits exceptions, and the pressure to find a future death for the two translated men is relieved. The Moses reading blunts the same argument from the other side: Moses did die (Deuteronomy 34:5), so choosing him over Enoch was always a choice for textual fit over the “deathless” tidiness — which is why proponents pressed the tradition of his disputed body and unknown grave (Jude 9) to answer it (whether Moses died or was assumed bodily is weighed at “Did Moses die, or was he assumed bodily?”).

The defeater to both: the two witnesses may not be individuals at all

Both literal readings assume the witnesses are two returning men. The text’s own gloss cuts against that assumption. John immediately identifies them: “These are the two olive trees, and the two candlesticks standing before the God of the earth” (Revelation 11:4) — a direct citation of Zechariah 4:14, where the “two anointed ones” are the priest Joshua and the prince Zerubbabel, a pair of offices upholding the community, not two deathless individuals. Even Victorinus, who holds the men-in-paradise reading, is forced to route it through Zechariah’s symbol.5 The Tyconian–Augustinian tradition drew the symbolic conclusion outright — the two witnesses are the Church testifying from the two Testaments:

As there he spoke of John, here of the two witnesses, that is, of the church that preaches and prophesies on the basis of the two Testaments.

— Primasius of Hadrumetum, Commentary on the Apocalypse, on 11:3; Tyconius likewise reads “in the two witnesses… the two Testaments by which his church is governed.”6 On this reading the whole “which two men?” debate is a category mistake: “two” is the legal minimum for a valid witness (Deuteronomy 19:15), the Moses-and-Elijah signs mark the witnesses’ testimony as Law-and-Prophets in character rather than naming two persons, and the death-and-resurrection in “the great city… where also our Lord was crucified” (Revelation 11:8) is the Church’s martyrdom and vindication. This is a live and widely-held modern reading, and it defeats both literal candidates equally.

Adversarial assessment

What holds. That the Church read Revelation’s witnesses as Enoch and Elijah, early and widely, is beyond dispute — the identification is attested by c. AD 200 and is the ancient majority. The theological instinct behind it is coherent: Enoch and Elijah are the two men who escaped death, and their end-time martyrdom would answer the general appointment of Hebrews 9:27. If the two witnesses are literal individuals at all, Enoch is a serious candidate.

What is overstated. The reading is far stronger as tradition-history than as exegesis of Revelation 11. Its whole weight rests on an inference made outside the text (“only two never died”), while the text’s own data — the Moses-and-Elijah signs of v. 6, the Zechariah-4 gloss of v. 4, the “two” of legal witness — pull toward either Moses (not Enoch) or a corporate figure (neither). The Hebrews 9:27 argument that makes Enoch necessary is itself softened by 1 Corinthians 15:51. And the antiquity of the reading does not settle its truth: it is the natural first guess once one asks “which deathless men?”, a question the vision may never have posed.

Assessment

Contested. The claim that Enoch is one of the two witnesses is genuinely disputed, with real strength and real weakness on each side. It is the earliest and dominant reading in the Church and coheres with the “deathless men / must taste death” logic — strong by the lights of reception-history and systematic theology. But it is weak by the lights of the passage’s own imagery, which codes the witnesses’ signs to Moses and Elijah and glosses them through Zechariah 4, so that the better-supported literal identification is Moses (not Enoch) and the best-supported reading of all may be the corporate one, on which no individual is named. No candidate commands the field. The honest verdict is that Enoch’s place among the two witnesses is a possible and venerable interpretation, not a secure one — a tradition to be respected, but not a result to be asserted.
