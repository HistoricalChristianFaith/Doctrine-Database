Did the Enoch–Metatron identification grow out of the earlier Enoch tradition?

Disputed reading · on Who Was Enoch? · witness: 3 Enoch (Sefer Hekhalot)

The passage

In the Hebrew 3 Enoch, R. Ishmael is carried up to the chariot-throne, and the supreme angel who guides him — the “Prince of the Presence,” Metatron, the “lesser YHWH” — discloses his own identity:

Because I am Enoch, the son of Jared. For when the generation of the flood sinned and were confounded in their deeds… the Holy One, blessed be He, took me… to be a witness against them.

— 3 Enoch 4:2–3, in Hugo Odeberg’s translation (online).1 The Babylonian Talmud tells of the same angel, without naming Enoch:

He saw the angel Mitatron, who was granted permission to sit and write the merits of Israel. He said: There is a tradition that in the world above there is no sitting; no competition; no turning one’s back before Him, i.e., all face the Divine Presence; and no lethargy. Seeing that someone other than God was seated above, he said: Perhaps, the Gemara here interjects, Heaven forbid, there are two authorities, and there is another source of power in control of the world in addition to God.… They removed Mitatron from his place in heaven and smote him with sixty rods [pulsei] of fire, so that others would not make the mistake that Aḥer made.

— b. Ḥagigah 15a (William Davidson translation); the parallel lashing is 3 Enoch 16:3–5.2

The question

3 Enoch identifies the angel Metatron with the translated patriarch Enoch. The question is how that identification arose: as the late fusion of two figures that began separately, or as the endpoint of a continuous tradition about an exalted Enoch reaching back through 2 Enoch and 1 Enoch. The answer bears on how far the Hekhalot Metatron can be read back into Second Temple Judaism. Michael Heiser, for one, groups Metatron with Melchizedek and the apocalyptic Son of Man as candidates for a “second power in heaven,” building on Alan Segal’s Two Powers in Heaven (1977).3 A related question is set out at “Did rabbinic Judaism suppress a divine-Messiah (‘two powers in heaven’) reading of Daniel 7?”

Two figures, joined late

Metatron began as a great angel in his own right, and Enoch was identified with him only later.

Held by: Gershom Scholem (Major Trends in Jewish Mysticism, 1941); Philip Alexander (“3 (Hebrew Apocalypse of) Enoch,” 1983); Yakir Paz (“Metatron is not Enoch,” 2019).4

On Scholem’s “two streams” account, two traditions about Metatron ran side by side. One identified him with Yahoel, the Name-bearing angel of the Apocalypse of Abraham, and with the archangel Michael, and knew nothing of a transfigured human; the other identified him with Enoch. The two Hebrew spellings of the name, Scholem argued, mark the two originally separate figures, and the title “lesser YHWH” reaches Metatron through Yahoel — in 3 Enoch 48D Metatron is himself called “Yahoel.” Alexander, dating the final redaction of 3 Enoch to the fifth or sixth century and finding it dependent on the Babylonian Talmud, argues that Metatron and Michael were once one angel bearing an esoteric and a common name. The Talmudic Metatron passages, such as b. Ḥagigah 15a above, present him as an angel and never as Enoch. Paz carries the case further. He derives the name from the Latin and Greek metator, “guide, forerunner,” used for the angel of the Lord who goes before Israel with God’s name in him (Exodus 23:20–21). He observes that the Babylonian Metatron is never Enoch, and that the earliest evidence joining the two appears in Palestinian sources of the seventh and eighth centuries. On his account the Enoch–Metatron identification is “neither an ancient esoteric Palestinian tradition nor a Babylonian creation, but rather a late Palestinian innovative synthesis” of two trajectories, the Babylonian Metatron and the Byzantine Enoch. On this account the identity of Enoch and Metatron belongs to the late history of the angel, joined to the patriarch in the seventh or eighth century.5

The endpoint of a continuous heavenly-Enoch tradition

The Metatron of 3 Enoch is the Enoch of the earlier books, exalted one stage further.

Held by: Andrei A. Orlov (The Enoch-Metatron Tradition, 2005).6

Read in sequence, the Enochic books trace an ascending line. 1 Enoch gives an ascended “scribe of righteousness” caught up to the throne of the Great Glory; the Similitudes close in 1 Enoch 71 by identifying Enoch himself with the enthroned “Son of Man” who judges (“You are the Son of Man who was born for righteousness”). In 2 Enoch 22 he is stripped of his “earthly robe,” anointed, and clothed in glory, until he finds himself “like one of His glorious ones… and there was no difference.” Orlov locates the beginning of the patriarch’s transformation into an angelic being in this scene. In 3 Enoch the transformation is complete: Enoch is the angel, enthroned on a seat “similar to the Throne of Glory,” given “seventy names… which the Holy One, blessed be He, took from His own name,” and called “THE LESSER YHWH.” Orlov’s central argument is that Metatron inherits the specifically Enochic offices — celestial scribe, expert in the secrets of heaven, revealer of the calendar, knower of the Name — and that he traces the celestial roles and titles of the seventh patriarch from their Mesopotamian roots through these books. On this reading the shared titles and functions mark an unbroken tradition about one figure, whose exaltation grows from book to book until the scribe who stood before the throne is himself enthroned as the Prince of the Presence.7
