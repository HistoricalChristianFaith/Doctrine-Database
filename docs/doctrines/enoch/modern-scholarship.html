Modern scholarship — what critical study makes of Enoch

Dates: a survey of modern critical scholarship (chiefly 20th–21st century) · representative works: J. T. Milik, The Books of Enoch: Aramaic Fragments of Qumrân Cave 4 (Oxford: Clarendon, 1976); George W. E. Nickelsburg & James C. VanderKam, 1 Enoch (Hermeneia, 2001–2012); James C. VanderKam, Enoch and the Growth of an Apocalyptic Tradition (1984); Gabriele Boccaccini, Beyond the Essene Hypothesis (1998); Andrei A. Orlov, The Enoch-Metatron Tradition (2005); R. H. Charles, The Book of Enoch (1912) · Milik at the Internet Archive
Summary: This page gathers what modern study — epigraphic, philological, source-critical, and reception-critical — makes of Enoch and the literature written in his name, as a survey. Six things recur across the literature: the Aramaic fragments from Qumran Cave 4 (Milik’s 1976 editio princeps) attest the antiquity of the Enochic booklets — and their silence over the Similitudes (chs. 37–71) is central to the “Son of Man” dating debate; Nickelsburg and VanderKam treat the corpus as a five-booklet anthology grown from the 4th–3rd century BC to the turn of the era; VanderKam and Kvanvig read the figure against the Mesopotamian seventh antediluvian sage, Enmeduranki of Sippar, the king initiated into divination and the secrets of heaven; Boccaccini proposes a distinct current of Second Temple religion — “Enochic Judaism” — behind these texts; Orlov traces the figure into the Enoch-Metatron tradition of the Hekhalot literature; and the book was lost to the West for a millennium until James Bruce recovered the Ge’ez manuscripts in 1773. These scholars treat the Enochic literature as pseudepigraphic — written in Enoch’s name, not by him — while the Enoch of Genesis is four verses in a genealogy, with a few glances at them in Sirach, Hebrews and Jude. The questions this survey opens — was 1 Enoch received as Scripture? how did the Enoch–Metatron identification arise? — are set out on the interpretation pages of the Enoch timeline and the Nephilim timeline.

Claims & evidence

The Aramaic Enoch from Qumran: Milik’s editio princeps and the antiquity of the booklets

One major modern development is textual. Among the Dead Sea Scrolls of Cave 4 were found eleven fragmentary Aramaic manuscripts of the Enochic writings, edited in the standard editio princeps by J. T. Milik (with Matthew Black), The Books of Enoch: Aramaic Fragments of Qumrân Cave 4 (Oxford: Clarendon, 1976). Two points are drawn from the fragments. First, they attest that four of the five booklets — the Book of the Watchers, the Astronomical Book, the Book of Dreams, and the Epistle — already existed in Aramaic well before the turn of the era; the oldest copy of the Watchers (4Q201) is dated palaeographically to the first half of the second century BC, and its composition is placed earlier still, so that the myth of the fallen angels is older than the Christian era and, on Milik’s reading, older than the received text of Genesis 6 it parallels. Second, and most discussed, is an absence: the Similitudes or Parables (chs. 37–71) — the section containing the exalted “Son of Man” and “Chosen One” figure — is the one part of 1 Enoch that turned up in no copy at Qumran.1 On that silence Milik built a thesis: that a fifth booklet, the Book of Giants, once stood where the Parables now do, and that the Parables themselves are a late, Christian composition of about AD 270. Jonas Greenfield and Michael Stone answered it within a year, arguing that the Christian dating rests on no positive evidence and that diverse Enochic collections circulated in first-century-AD Palestine; Nickelsburg dates a Jewish Similitudes to roughly the late first century BC or first century AD.2

Composition and dating: a five-booklet anthology grown across three centuries

The standard critical account of the book’s make-up is the two-volume Hermeneia commentary of George Nickelsburg and James VanderKam, which treats 1 Enoch not as one work but as an anthology of five once-independent booklets, composed in Aramaic from the 4th–3rd century BC to the turn of the era and ascribed to the seventh patriarch: the Book of the Watchers (chs. 1–36), the Similitudes/Parables (37–71), the Astronomical Book or Book of the Luminaries (72–82), the Book of Dreams (83–90, containing the Animal Apocalypse), and the Epistle of Enoch (91–105, containing the Apocalypse of Weeks), followed by the Birth of Noah (106–107) and a closing chapter (108). The whole survives complete only in an Ethiopic (Ge’ez) translation of a Greek translation of the Aramaic original. On their datings, the Astronomical Book and the core of the Watchers are the earliest layers, third century BC or earlier (chs. 6–11, the fall of the angels, older still); the Dreams and Epistle belong to the second century BC, the Animal Apocalypse datable near the Maccabean revolt; and the Similitudes are the latest booklet, turn-of-the-era. On this dating the book is among the oldest extant Jewish apocalyptic literature.3

The Mesopotamian background: Enoch and the seventh antediluvian sage

A third strand traces the figure back behind Genesis. Enoch’s distinctive marks — that he is the seventh from Adam, that he lives 365 years (the days of the solar year), that he is initiated by angels into the calendar and the secrets of heaven and returns a scribe of the coming judgment — are compared with Mesopotamian traditions about Enmeduranki, the seventh antediluvian king of Sippar, the city of the sun-god Shamash, who in the cuneiform texts is taken up into the divine assembly, shown the tablet of the gods and the techniques of divination, and returns to found the guild of diviners (the bārû). The case is made in James VanderKam’s Enoch and the Growth of an Apocalyptic Tradition (1984), with Helge Kvanvig’s Roots of Apocalyptic (1988); on their reading the biblical Enoch is a Yahwistic reflex of this seventh-sage figure, the mantic wisdom of Mesopotamia feeding the mantic apocalyptic of 1 Enoch.4

“Enochic Judaism”: a distinct current of Second Temple religion

A fourth strand asks what community stands behind the corpus. Gabriele Boccaccini’s Beyond the Essene Hypothesis (1998) argued that the Enochic writings are not stray apocrypha but the deposit of a distinct variety of Second Temple Judaism — “Enochic Judaism,” a priestly-dissident current, older than and distinct from the Zadokite/Mosaic mainstream, whose preoccupation with the fall of the Watchers and the origin of evil set it apart, and out of which (on his reconstruction) the Qumran community, and later the movements around John the Baptist and Jesus, in part grew. The thesis gave rise to the international Enoch Seminar; its “party” boundaries and genealogy of movements are debated in the essays Boccaccini collected in Enoch and Qumran Origins (2005).5

The afterlife of the figure: the Enoch-Metatron tradition

A fifth strand follows the figure from the ascended scribe of the early booklets to the great angel Metatron of the later Hebrew Hekhalot literature. Andrei Orlov’s The Enoch-Metatron Tradition (2005) is the fullest study: it traces the celestial roles and titles of the seventh patriarch from their Mesopotamian roots through 2 (Slavonic) Enoch — where, Orlov argues, the transformation of the seer into an angelic being is already under way — to the 3 Enoch (Sefer Hekhalot) in which the translated Enoch is Metatron, the “Prince of the Presence” and “lesser YHWH.” Whether that identification is one organic development or a late grafting of Enoch onto an originally independent angel is disputed: Orlov’s continuity reading stands beside the “two streams” reading of Scholem, Alexander, and Paz. Both are set out at “Did the Enoch–Metatron identification grow out of the earlier Enoch tradition?”6

Reception and recovery: lost to the West, kept only in Ethiopia, returned in 1773

The sixth strand is the book’s reception history. Cited as Scripture by some in the earliest centuries on the strength of Jude’s quotation, 1 Enoch was set outside the canon by Athanasius, Jerome, and Augustine in the fourth and fifth centuries and then fell out of use in the Greek and Latin churches, surviving in the West only as fragments in a few Byzantine chroniclers — until the Scottish traveller James Bruce brought three complete Ge’ez manuscripts back from Ethiopia in 1773, and Richard Laurence (1821) and then R. H. Charles (1893, 1912) produced the first English translations and critical editions. The book survives in the Old Testament canon of one communion alone, the Ethiopian (and Eritrean) Orthodox Tewahedo Church, which never rejected it.7 The full arc — citation, dispute, exclusion, recovery — is set out on this timeline’s canon-of-1-Enoch page, and whether the early esteem amounts to reception as Scripture is set out at “Was 1 Enoch received as Scripture in the early churches?”

Authorship: the Genesis notice, and a corpus dated later

Every strand draws a distinction between the biblical notices and the later corpus. Outside the Ethiopian (and Eritrean) Orthodox Tewahedo canon, which includes 1 Enoch, the biblical Enoch material is very small: a single notice in the antediluvian genealogy (Genesis 5:21–24), read forward by one verse of Hebrews, one prophecy-quotation in Jude, and two lines of praise in Sirach — four verses of narrative and a handful of glances at them. Nickelsburg, and VanderKam and Flint, treat the literature that made Enoch the revealer of the calendar, the seer of the throne, the scribe of judgment, and an angel as pseudepigraphic: written centuries later in his name, in the ancient convention of attributing a book to a revered ancient, on the evidence of the Aramaic palaeography, the Greek and Ethiopic transmission, and the growth of the anthology across three centuries. Tertullian, defending the book, held it to be Enoch’s own, carried through the Flood by Noah or restored by the Spirit (see his witness page). Whether the books are inspired, and how the Enoch–Metatron identification arose, are set out on the interpretation pages.8

How this survey relates to the timeline

In brief, the scholarship surveyed here supplies:

  • the Qumran Aramaic evidence (Milik) for the antiquity of the booklets and, through the absence of the Similitudes, for the Son-of-Man dating debate that bears on how the exalted-mediator figure relates to the New Testament;
  • the compositional map (Nickelsburg–VanderKam) — a five-booklet anthology grown 4th–3rd c. BC to the turn of the era — followed in the datings given across the Enoch timeline;
  • the Mesopotamian background (VanderKam, Kvanvig) behind the Enmeduranki / seventh-sage comparison;
  • the “Enochic Judaism” thesis (Boccaccini) that reads the corpus as a distinct current of Second Temple religion rather than stray apocrypha;
  • the Enoch-Metatron scholarship (Orlov, and the Scholem/Alexander line) behind the question how the Enoch–Metatron identification arose;
  • the reception and recovery history (Charles; the Bruce 1773 recovery) behind the canon-of-1-Enoch block and the canonicity question.
