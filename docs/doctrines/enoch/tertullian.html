Tertullian: the one Father who openly defends the Book of Enoch as inspired Scripture

Dates: c. AD 160–225 (Carthage); the defence is in De cultu feminarum (On the Apparel of Women) I.3, written c. AD 200 · Wikipedia
Summary: Tertullian is the first Christian writer — and, among the Fathers, very nearly the only one — to mount an explicit defence of the Book of Enoch as genuine, inspired Scripture. Building the argument of On the Apparel of Women on Enoch’s account of the fallen angels, he pauses (I.3) to answer the objection that the book cannot be authentic: he concedes it “is not received by some, because it is not admitted into the Jewish canon either,” anticipates the reply that a book “published before the deluge” could not have survived the Flood, and answers it two ways — Noah, Enoch’s great-grandson, could have carried its contents through; or, failing that, the Spirit could have re-inspired it after the Flood as Ezra was believed to have restored the Law after the Babylonian sack of Jerusalem. His decisive argument is the one still pressed today: because Jude the apostle bears witness to Enoch (Jude 14–15), and because Enoch “in the same Scripture has preached likewise concerning the Lord,” the book has a claim on Christians — “nothing at all must be rejected by us which pertains to us.” This is the sharpest ancient statement of the Jude-implies-canonicity inference, and the load-bearing patristic counterweight to the later rejection by Jerome and Augustine. Whether it establishes that the early Church received 1 Enoch as Scripture is weighed at “The canonicity of 1 Enoch” (assessment: weak).

The work

De cultu feminarum (On the Apparel of Women), an early treatise urging Christian women to modest dress, rests its whole opening argument on the Enochic tradition of the fallen angels: the ornaments and cosmetics women prize were, Tertullian says, first “disclosed” by the angels “who rushed from heaven on the daughters of men” (I.2) — the Watchers of 1 Enoch 6–8, whose descent, sin, and forbidden arts are weighed on the Nephilim and astrology timelines, which this page hubs rather than repeats. Because that argument depends on the Book of Enoch, Tertullian breaks off in chapter 3 to defend the book’s genuineness against those who reject it — and it is that defence, of the reception of the book that bears Enoch’s name, that belongs to this timeline.1

Claims & evidence

“The Scripture of Enoch… is not received by some” — and the flood-survival objection

I am aware that the Scripture of Enoch, which has assigned this order (of action) to angels, is not received by some, because it is not admitted into the Jewish canon either. I suppose they did not think that, having been published before the deluge, it could have safely survived that world-wide calamity, the abolisher of all things.

— On the Apparel of Women I.3, in the Ante-Nicene Fathers translation (read the work; also newadvent.org). Tertullian states the case against the book more clearly than any defender before him: it is disputed precisely because it stands outside the Jewish canon, and its antiquity — a work claiming to predate the Flood — is itself the ground of suspicion. He does not hide the objection; he answers it.2

Noah preserved it — or the Spirit renewed it, as Ezra restored the Law

If that is the reason (for rejecting it), let them recall to their memory that Noah, the survivor of the deluge, was the great-grandson of Enoch himself; and he, of course, had heard and remembered, from domestic renown and hereditary tradition, concerning his own great-grandfather’s “grace in the sight of God,” and concerning all his preachings; since Enoch had given no other charge to Methuselah than that he should hand on the knowledge of them to his posterity.

…he could equally have renewed it, under the Spirit’s inspiration, after it had been destroyed by the violence of the deluge, as, after the destruction of Jerusalem by the Babylonian storming of it, every document of the Jewish literature is generally agreed to have been restored through Ezra.

— On the Apparel of Women I.3 (read the work). Tertullian gives two independent answers to the Flood objection. First, a chain of custody: Enoch charged Methuselah to pass on his writings, and Noah — Enoch’s great-grandson — could have carried them through the deluge in living memory. Second, a fallback that concedes the text might well have perished: even so, the same Spirit that first inspired it could have re-inspired it afterward — exactly as Jewish tradition held Ezra to have restored the scriptures lost in the Babylonian destruction. The second answer is telling: Tertullian is prepared to grant the loss and still keep the book, because for him its authority rests on inspiration, not on an unbroken manuscript.3

“Enoch possesses a testimony in the Apostle Jude” — the decisive argument

But since Enoch in the same Scripture has preached likewise concerning the Lord, nothing at all must be rejected by us which pertains to us; and we read that “every Scripture suitable for edification is divinely inspired.” By the Jews it may now seem to have been rejected for that (very) reason, just like all the other (portions) nearly which tell of Christ… To these considerations is added the fact that Enoch possesses a testimony in the Apostle Jude.

— On the Apparel of Women I.3 (read the work). Here is the heart of the defence, and the argument every later advocate of 1 Enoch inherits. Tertullian treats the book as Scripture outright — applying to it the Pauline maxim that “every Scripture… is divinely inspired” (a paraphrase of 2 Timothy 3:16) — and grounds that standing on two things: Enoch “has preached… concerning the Lord,” so the book “pertains to us,” Christians, whatever the Jews decided; and, clinching it, “Enoch possesses a testimony in the Apostle Jude” — the New Testament citation of 1 Enoch 1:9 at Jude 14–15. He even reads the Jewish non-reception as evidence for the book: they set it aside, he claims, for the same reason they set aside “all the other” texts “which tell of Christ.” This is the Jude-implies-canonicity inference in its sharpest ancient form — the exact step later Fathers deny (Jerome answers that quoting part of a book no more canonizes it than Paul’s citation of a pagan poet canonizes him), and the sub-claim adjudicated at “The canonicity of 1 Enoch” (rated weak).4

He uses Enoch as prophetic Scripture elsewhere — against idolatry

Enoch had preceded, predicting that “the demons, and the spirits of the angelic apostates, would turn into idolatry all the elements, all the garniture of the universe, all things contained in the heaven, in the sea, in the earth, that they might be consecrated as God, in opposition to God.”… the same Enoch fore-condemns in general menace both idol-worshippers and idol-makers together.

— On Idolatry 4 (read the work; also newadvent.org). The defence in De cultu feminarum is not an isolated flourish: Tertullian actually uses Enoch as a prophet whose words carry authority, quoting the book against idolatry and calling him “the most ancient prophet Enoch” who “fore-chanted” that even doorways would fall into superstitious use (On Idolatry 15). He handles the Book of Enoch the way he handles any prophetic Scripture — citing it to settle a question of Christian practice.5

Notes

  • Where it sits on this timeline. Tertullian (c. AD 200) is the first Christian witness on this timeline, following the Jewish pseudepigrapha (1 Enoch, Jubilees, 2 Enoch) and preceding the Hebrew 3 Enoch and the later canonical-status debate. His value here is not the Watchers myth (hubbed to the Nephilim timeline) but his stance toward the book: he is the high-water mark of Christian esteem for 1 Enoch, the witness every “for” case leans on.
  • It hubs the Watchers arts; it does not re-argue them. Tertullian’s catalogue of the arts the fallen angels “traced out” — metallurgy, the properties of herbs, enchantments, “even to the interpretation of the stars” (On the Apparel of Women I.2) — tracks the Watcher-instruction list of 1 Enoch 7–8, and is weighed on the Nephilim and astrology timelines (see “Did fallen angels teach humanity astrology?”), not here.
  • The canonicity question. That Tertullian defends 1 Enoch as Scripture is the strongest single patristic datum on the “for” side; but he himself concedes the book “is not received by some,” and the later canon-defining Fathers (Jerome, Augustine, Athanasius) exclude it. Whether the early Church received it as Scripture is weighed at “The canonicity of 1 Enoch” (assessment: weak); Tertullian’s own defence is also treated, beside his rejection of the Shepherd of Hermas, on the canon timeline.
  • His contested orthodoxy. Tertullian spent his later years in the Montanist “New Prophecy,” and whether that made him a heretic — he was never tried or condemned by any synod — is weighed on the timeline “Was Tertullian a heretic?”, where he stands as a witness on some twenty of this wiki’s belief-timelines.
