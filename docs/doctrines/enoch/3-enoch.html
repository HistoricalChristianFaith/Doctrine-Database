3 Enoch: the patriarch exalted into the angel Metatron, the “lesser YHWH”

Dates: the Hebrew 3 Enoch (Sefer Hekhalot, “the Book of the Palaces”), a Merkabah/Hekhalot text ascribed to the second-century sage R. Ishmael ben Elisha but composed and redacted far later. Philip Alexander’s standard treatment dates the final redaction to the fifth or sixth century AD — its lower limit fixed by its dependence on the Babylonian Talmud, its upper by the tenth-century authors who first cite it — while noting that older Enoch-Metatron traditions are woven through it; Hugo Odeberg (1928) argued that its core (roughly chapters 3–48A) reaches back considerably earlier. It is placed on this timeline at c. 5th–6th c. AD, following Alexander’s dating of the final redaction · Wikipedia
Summary: 3 Enoch is the latest of the books bearing Enoch’s name on this timeline, after 1 Enoch, Jubilees and 2 Enoch. Here the patriarch Enoch, “taken” from the generation of the Flood, is raised above every angel and transformed into Metatron, the “Prince of the Presence” — seated on a throne like the throne of glory, made ruler over the whole heavenly household, given seventy names drawn from the divine Name, and called the “lesser YHWH.” R. Ishmael ascends to behold the chariot and is shown all this by Metatron himself, “who is Enoch, the son of Jared.” Philip Alexander and Andrei Orlov discuss the unparalleled scale of this exaltation. The book and the Babylonian Talmud both tell the “two powers in heaven” episode, in which the sage Elisha ben Abuyah (“Aher”) sees Metatron enthroned and concludes there may be a second power, whereupon Metatron is lashed “so that others would not make the mistake that Aḥer made.”

The book

3 Enoch is not an apocalypse in the older narrative mould of 1 and 2 Enoch but a Hekhalot (“palaces”) text of the Merkabah mystical school: a first-person account, set in the mouth of the priest-mystic R. Ishmael, of an ascent through the heavenly halls to behold God’s chariot-throne, guided by the great angel who reveals the secrets of the upper world. It belongs on this timeline because it identifies that guiding angel, Metatron, with the translated patriarch Enoch, and then narrates his exaltation. In 2 Enoch the seer is anointed, re-robed, and made “like one of His glorious ones”; in 3 Enoch Enoch becomes the angel Metatron, enthroned and bearing the divine Name. It survives in Hebrew and is bound up with the Babylonian Talmud’s own Metatron traditions.1

Claims & evidence

R. Ishmael ascends to the chariot, and Metatron, “Prince of the Presence,” guides him

When I ascended on high to behold the vision of the Merkaba and had entered the six Halls, one within the other…

— 3 Enoch 1:1, in Hugo Odeberg’s translation, 3 Enoch, or the Hebrew Book of Enoch (Cambridge, 1928), reproduced online. The book opens with the sage R. Ishmael taken up through the seven halls of the highest heaven to see the chariot-throne. There he is met, protected from the terrifying angelic hosts, and instructed by a supreme angel who bears the title sar ha-panim, the “Prince of the Presence” (or “of the Divine Presence”) — the angel who alone stands and serves before the face of God. Everything Ishmael reports of the upper world he is shown by this Prince, whose own astonishing identity the next chapters disclose.2

“Because I am Enoch, the son of Jared” — the angel is the translated patriarch

Because I am Enoch, the son of Jared. For when the generation of the flood sinned and were confounded in their deeds…

— 3 Enoch 4:2–3 (Odeberg), online. When R. Ishmael asks the Prince of the Presence his name and why he is called by seventy names, the angel answers that he is Enoch, seventh from Adam, whom God removed from the doomed generation of the Flood to be “a witness against them” in the high heavens — lifting him up, in their own lifetime and before their eyes, into the presence of God. In the book, the guiding angel Metatron is the patriarch of Genesis 5:24, and the terse “God took him” is read as the beginning of an ascent that ended not merely in a heavenly life but in angelic office — Enoch “assigned… for a prince and a ruler among the ministering angels.” Whether this identification grew out of the earlier portraits — scribe of righteousness in 1 Enoch, first writer and translated priest in Jubilees, glorified seer in 2 Enoch — is set out at “Did the Enoch–Metatron identification grow out of the earlier Enoch tradition?”3

Enthroned, made ruler of all, given seventy names from the Name

The exaltation is told in a sequence of scenes across chapters 4–16 and 48. God makes Metatron a throne “similar to the Throne of Glory,” sets it at the door of the seventh hall, and seats him upon it; a herald goes out through all the heavens proclaiming him God’s appointed prince, “a ruler over all the princes of my kingdoms and over all the children of heaven,” so that every angel must obey him. He is robed in a garment of glory and a royal crown set with luminous stones, given wisdom and dominion over the cosmos, and endowed with seventy names — “seventy names… which the Holy One, blessed be He, took from His own name and put upon him,” matching the seventy tongues of the world — though his King calls him simply Na‘ar, “Youth.” The man taken from earth is made vice-regent of heaven.4

“He called me THE LESSER YHWH”

And He called me THE LESSER YHWH in the presence of all His heavenly household; as it is written (Ex. xxiii. 21): ‘For my name is in him’.

— 3 Enoch 12:5 (Odeberg), online. Having crowned and enthroned Metatron, God bestows on him the title YHWH ha-qaton, “the lesser YHWH” — grounding it in the angel of Exodus 23:21, of whom God says “my name is in him.” The transformed patriarch now bears the divine Name itself. This enthroned, Name-bearing figure is the subject of the rabbinic “two powers in heaven” episode below, studied by Alan Segal (1977); Michael Heiser connects him to the “second-power” and exalted-mediator motifs traced on the divine council timeline.5

The “two powers” alarm: Aher’s error and Metatron’s lashing

Indeed, there are two Divine Powers in heaven!

— 3 Enoch 16:3 (Odeberg), online. In the episode preserved both here and in the Babylonian Talmud, the sage Elisha ben Abuyah — remembered as Aher, “the Other” — ascends to the chariot, sees Metatron seated and writing, and, since heaven was held to permit no sitting before God, concludes that there must be “two powers.” In the Talmud’s telling, seeing one other than God seated, Aher said “Perhaps… there are two authorities”; and “They removed Mitatron from his place in heaven and smote him with sixty rods [pulsei] of fire, so that others would not make the mistake that Aḥer made.” In 3 Enoch too the angel is given “sixty strokes with lashes of fire” and made to stand. How the identification of Enoch with Metatron arose is set out at “Did the Enoch–Metatron identification grow out of the earlier Enoch tradition?”6

Notes

  • Placement on the timeline and the dating. 3 Enoch is placed last among the Enochic books, in the fifth-to-sixth-century rabbinic stratum, not because its traditions are all late (many are older) but because the composition that gathers them — the Sefer Hekhalot as we have it — presupposes the Babylonian Talmud and belongs to the Hekhalot literature (Alexander, n. 1). It is placed by the date of that final redaction.
  • Enoch across the four books. 1 Enoch and Jubilees give an ascended, angel-companioned seer; 2 Enoch has him anointed and made “like one of His glorious ones”; 3 Enoch identifies the patriarch with the angel Metatron, enthroned and bearing the Name. Andrei Orlov reads the four as a single trajectory; Gershom Scholem, Philip Alexander and Yakir Paz hold that Metatron had separate origins and was joined to Enoch later (both readings, with sources, at the interpretation page). This page keeps to the figure; the Watchers-and-Nephilim material of the Enochic corpus is traced on the Nephilim and astrology timelines.
  • The Metatron question, and the “two powers” scholarship. Whether the identification Enoch = Metatron arose — and what the “two powers in heaven” episode shows about the boundaries of ancient Jewish monotheism — is treated at length in Alan Segal’s Two Powers in Heaven (1977), Daniel Boyarin’s work on Metatron, and Andrei Orlov’s The Enoch-Metatron Tradition (2005). The exalted-mediator and “second-power” themes are also part of the “unseen realm” reading traced on the divine council and serpent of Eden timelines. The derivation question is set out at “Did the Enoch–Metatron identification grow out of the earlier Enoch tradition?”
