3 Enoch: the patriarch exalted into the angel Metatron, the “lesser YHWH”

Dates: the Hebrew 3 Enoch (Sefer Hekhalot, “the Book of the Palaces”), a Merkabah/Hekhalot text ascribed to the second-century sage R. Ishmael ben Elisha but composed and redacted far later. Philip Alexander’s standard treatment dates the final redaction to the fifth or sixth century AD — its lower limit fixed by its dependence on the Babylonian Talmud, its upper by the tenth-century authors who first cite it — while noting that older Enoch-Metatron traditions are woven through it; Hugo Odeberg (1928) argued that its core (roughly chapters 3–48A) reaches back considerably earlier. It is placed on this timeline in the late, rabbinic stratum (c. 5th–6th c. AD), as the climax of the trajectory the earlier Enochic books began · Wikipedia
Summary: 3 Enoch is the furthest reach of the “heavenly-Enoch” tradition and the endpoint of the arc that runs from the ascended scribe of 1 Enoch and Jubilees through the glorified seer of 2 Enoch. Here the patriarch Enoch, “taken” from the generation of the Flood, is raised above every angel and transformed into Metatron, the “Prince of the Presence” — seated on a throne like the throne of glory, made ruler over the whole heavenly household, given seventy names drawn from the divine Name, and called the “lesser YHWH.” R. Ishmael ascends to behold the chariot and is shown all this by Metatron himself, “who is Enoch, the son of Jared.” This is the boldest exaltation of a human being in Jewish literature, and it collided head-on with rabbinic monotheism — the famous “two powers in heaven” alarm, in which the apostate sage Elisha ben Abuyah (“Aher”) sees Metatron enthroned and concludes there may be a second deity, whereupon Metatron is lashed to prove he is no rival.

The book

3 Enoch is not an apocalypse in the older narrative mould of 1 and 2 Enoch but a Hekhalot (“palaces”) text of the Merkabah mystical school: a first-person account, set in the mouth of the priest-mystic R. Ishmael, of an ascent through the heavenly halls to behold God’s chariot-throne, guided by the great angel who reveals the secrets of the upper world. Its distinctive move — the reason it belongs on this timeline rather than only in the study of Jewish mysticism — is that it identifies that guiding angel, Metatron, with the translated patriarch Enoch, and then narrates his exaltation in the most extravagant terms the tradition ever reached. Where 2 Enoch first angelified the seer — anointed, re-robed, made “like one of His glorious ones” — 3 Enoch completes the arc: Enoch does not merely become like an angel but becomes the angel, enthroned second only to God and bearing His Name. It survives in Hebrew, is bound up with the Babylonian Talmud’s own Metatron traditions, and stands at the end of a long development rather than at its head.1

Claims & evidence

R. Ishmael ascends to the chariot, and Metatron, “Prince of the Presence,” guides him

When I ascended on high to behold the vision of the Merkaba and had entered the six Halls, one within the other…

— 3 Enoch 1:1, in Hugo Odeberg’s translation, 3 Enoch, or the Hebrew Book of Enoch (Cambridge, 1928), reproduced online. The book opens with the sage R. Ishmael taken up through the seven halls of the highest heaven to see the chariot-throne. There he is met, protected from the terrifying angelic hosts, and instructed by a supreme angel who bears the title sar ha-panim, the “Prince of the Presence” (or “of the Divine Presence”) — the angel who alone stands and serves before the face of God. Everything Ishmael reports of the upper world he is shown by this Prince, whose own astonishing identity the next chapters disclose.2

“Because I am Enoch, the son of Jared” — the angel is the translated patriarch

Because I am Enoch, the son of Jared. For when the generation of the flood sinned and were confounded in their deeds…

— 3 Enoch 4:2–3 (Odeberg), online. When R. Ishmael asks the Prince of the Presence his name and why he is called by seventy names, the angel answers that he is Enoch, seventh from Adam, whom God removed from the doomed generation of the Flood to be “a witness against them” in the high heavens — lifting him up, in their own lifetime and before their eyes, into the presence of God. This is the hinge of the whole book: the guiding angel Metatron simply is the patriarch of Genesis 5:24, and the terse “God took him” is read as the beginning of an ascent that ended not merely in a heavenly life but in angelic office — Enoch “assigned… for a prince and a ruler among the ministering angels.” The whole earlier tradition — scribe of righteousness in 1 Enoch, first writer and translated priest in Jubilees, glorified seer in 2 Enoch — is here gathered up and pushed one decisive step further.3

Enthroned, made ruler of all, given seventy names from the Name

The exaltation is told in a sequence of scenes across chapters 4–16 and 48. God makes Metatron a throne “similar to the Throne of Glory,” sets it at the door of the seventh hall, and seats him upon it; a herald goes out through all the heavens proclaiming him God’s appointed prince, “a ruler over all the princes of my kingdoms and over all the children of heaven,” so that every angel must obey him. He is robed in a garment of glory and a royal crown set with luminous stones, given wisdom and dominion over the cosmos, and endowed with seventy names — “seventy names… which the Holy One, blessed be He, took from His own name and put upon him,” matching the seventy tongues of the world — though his King calls him simply Na‘ar, “Youth.” No other figure in the Hekhalot literature is elevated so far: the man taken from earth is made vice-regent of heaven.4

“He called me THE LESSER YHWH”

And He called me THE LESSER YHWH in the presence of all His heavenly household; as it is written (Ex. xxiii. 21): ‘For my name is in him’.

— 3 Enoch 12:5 (Odeberg), online. This is the most audacious line in the book. Having crowned and enthroned Metatron, God bestows on him the title YHWH ha-qaton, “the lesser YHWH” — grounding it in the angel of Exodus 23:21, of whom God says “my name is in him.” The transformed patriarch now bears the divine Name itself, a hair’s breadth from the throne of God. It is exactly this near-divinization — a second enthroned figure who carries the Name — that made Metatron the flash-point of the rabbinic “two powers in heaven” controversy, and that later readers (Heiser among them) connect to the “second-power” and exalted-mediator motifs weighed on the divine council timeline, which this page hubs rather than duplicates.5

The “two powers” alarm: Aher’s error and Metatron’s lashing

Indeed, there are two Divine Powers in heaven!

— 3 Enoch 16:3 (Odeberg), online. The exaltation carried an obvious danger for a strictly monotheistic faith, and the tradition confronts it directly. In the episode preserved both here and in the Babylonian Talmud, the sage Elisha ben Abuyah — the great apostate, remembered only as Aher, “the Other” — ascends to the chariot, sees Metatron seated and writing, and, since heaven was held to permit no sitting before God, concludes that there must be “two powers.” The Talmud tells it with the same shock: seeing one other than God enthroned, Aher said “perhaps… there are two authorities”; and to correct the error “they removed Metatron… and smote him with sixty rods of fire, so that others would not make the mistake that Aher made.” In 3 Enoch too the angel is given “sixty strokes with lashes of fire” and made to stand — a scene that at once displays how close Metatron came to looking like a rival deity and firmly subordinates him again. Whether the underlying identification (Enoch = Metatron) holds, and what the rabbis meant by policing it, is the contested question this witness sets up.6

Notes

  • Where it sits on this timeline — and the late date. 3 Enoch is placed last among the Enochic books, in the fifth-to-sixth-century rabbinic stratum, not because its traditions are all late (many are older) but because the composition that gathers them — the Sefer Hekhalot as we have it — presupposes the Babylonian Talmud and belongs to the Hekhalot literature. It is the endpoint of the development, read on this page as the climax of the arc 1 Enoch began, not as an early independent witness to the historical patriarch.
  • One line from scribe to angel. Read in sequence the four Enochic books trace a single trajectory: 1 Enoch and Jubilees give an ascended, angel-companioned seer; 2 Enoch is the first to angelify him, anointed and made “like one of His glorious ones”; 3 Enoch completes the arc by exalting the patriarch into the angel Metatron, enthroned and bearing the Name. This page keeps to that figure; the Watchers-and-Nephilim material the Enochic corpus is otherwise famous for is weighed on the Nephilim and astrology timelines, which it hubs rather than repeats.
  • The Metatron question, and the “two powers” scholarship. Whether the identification Enoch = Metatron actually holds — and what the “two powers in heaven” anxiety tells us about the boundaries of ancient Jewish monotheism — is a genuinely contested question, treated at length in Alan Segal’s Two Powers in Heaven (1977), Daniel Boyarin’s work on Metatron, and Andrei Orlov’s The Enoch-Metatron Tradition (2005). The exalted-mediator and “second-power” themes are also part of the “unseen realm” reading hubbed on the divine council and serpent of Eden timelines. This witness supplies the primary material; the adjudication belongs to those pages and to the argument that weighs the identification itself.
