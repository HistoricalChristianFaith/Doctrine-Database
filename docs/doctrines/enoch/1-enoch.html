1 Enoch: the antediluvian scribe, seer, and revealer

Dates: a composite of five originally separate booklets, composed, on Nickelsburg and VanderKam’s dating, from the 4th–3rd century BC to about the turn of the era (the earliest layers — the Astronomical Book and the Book of the Watchers — are 3rd‑century BC or older; the Similitudes are the latest, c. 1st century BC–1st century AD). It survives complete only in Ethiopic (Ge’ez), with Aramaic fragments from Qumran and a substantial Greek text behind them · Wikipedia
Summary: The oldest and largest of the books written in Enoch’s name, and the wellspring of the later tradition about him. Beyond its Watchers-and-Nephilim narrative — traced on the Nephilim timeline — its contribution to the figure of Enoch is a portrait: the “scribe of righteousness” whose eyes God opened, who is caught up through a house of crystal and fire to stand before the throne of the Great Glory, is shown the secrets of the cosmos and the coming judgment, and comes back to write them down. Jubilees, 2 and 3 Enoch, and a number of the Fathers take up this portrait. Its opening oracle (1:9) is the text the New Testament book of Jude puts in Enoch’s mouth.

The book and its five booklets

What is called 1 Enoch is not one composition but an anthology of five once-independent works, gathered under the patriarch’s name and preserved as a single book only in the Ethiopic (Ge’ez) Bible of the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church. Aramaic copies of four of the five turned up among the Dead Sea Scrolls in Cave 4 (eleven manuscripts, the oldest — of the Astronomical Book — palaeographically late-3rd or early-2nd century BC), attesting Aramaic copies of those four booklets well before the turn of the era. The five parts are: the Book of the Watchers (chs. 1–36; 3rd c. BC or earlier), whose opening oracle Jude quotes and whose chs. 6–16 tell the fall of the angels; the Book of Parables or Similitudes (chs. 37–71; c. 1st c. BC–1st c. AD), with its heavenly “Son of Man” — the one section absent from Qumran; the Astronomical Book or Book of the Luminaries (chs. 72–82; arguably the oldest layer), Enoch’s 364-day solar calendar; the Book of Dreams (chs. 83–90; c. 165 BC), containing the Animal Apocalypse; and the Epistle of Enoch (chs. 91–105, with the Apocalypse of Weeks; the Birth of Noah, chs. 106–107, and a final chapter 108 round out the book).1 The Watchers’ rebellion, the origin of the Nephilim, and the forbidden arts the angels taught are set out on the Nephilim, astrology, pillars of Seth, and divine council timelines. This page keeps to what the book makes of the man.

Claims & evidence

Enoch the righteous man whose eyes God opened, and who saw “the vision of the Holy One”

The words of the blessing of Enoch, wherewith he blessed the elect and righteous, who will be living in the day of tribulation, when all the wicked and godless are to be removed.

Enoch a righteous man, whose eyes were opened by God, saw the vision of the Holy One in the heavens, which the angels showed me, and from them I heard everything, and from them I understood as I saw…

— 1 Enoch 1:1–2, in the R. H. Charles translation (online). From its first words the book casts Enoch not as a name in a genealogy but as a seer: a righteous man granted opened eyes, shown “the vision of the Holy One,” and appointed to declare a blessing and a judgment reaching past his own generation to “a remote one which is for to come.” The whole corpus is framed as the record of what those opened eyes saw.2

“Scribe of righteousness”: Enoch as heaven’s recorder and intercessor

Fear not, Enoch, thou righteous man and scribe of righteousness: approach hither and hear my voice.

— 1 Enoch 15:1 (Charles), online. The title the book gives Enoch again and again is scribe of righteousness: he is the man sent up as an intermediary — the fallen Watchers, condemned, beg him to carry their petition before God, and God sends him back to pronounce that they have “no peace.” In the book’s portrayal Enoch stands between heaven and earth as the prophet-scribe who receives the divine sentence and records it. (The rebellion he is sent to judge is the Watchers’ myth handled in full on the Nephilim timeline; here it is the office it gives Enoch that matters.)3

The throne-vision: caught up through crystal and fire to stand before the Great Glory

And I looked and saw therein a lofty throne: its appearance was as crystal, and the wheels thereof as the shining sun, and there was the vision of cherubim. And from underneath the throne came streams of flaming fire so that I could not look thereon. And the Great Glory sat thereon, and His raiment shone more brightly than the sun and was whiter than any snow… The flaming fire was round about Him, and a great fire stood before Him, and none around could draw nigh Him: ten thousand times ten thousand (stood) before Him, yet He needed no counsellor… And until then I had been prostrate on my face, trembling: and the Lord called me with His own mouth, and said to me: ‘Come hither, Enoch, and hear my word.’

— 1 Enoch 14:19–24 (Charles), online. This is the earliest extant Jewish account of a human ascent to the divine throne-room. Its image is of the seer drawn up through a first house of crystal and a second, greater house of fire, prostrate and trembling before “the Great Glory” enthroned amid ten thousand times ten thousand attendants — and then addressed by God “with His own mouth.” From here Enoch is led on a tour of the cosmos (chs. 17–36) — the storehouses of the winds, the prison of the fallen stars, the mountain of the dead, the tree of life. 2 Enoch narrates a heavenly journey of its own, an ascent through the seven heavens.4

Enoch the calendar-revealer: the 364-day year and the courses of the luminaries

The Astronomical Book (chs. 72–82) casts Enoch as the man to whom the angel Uriel showed “the laws of the stars of heaven” — the risings and settings of the sun through its heavenly gates, the phases of the moon, and above all a 364-day solar calendar (twelve 30-day months plus four intercalary days), polemically opposed to the lunar reckoning. The book makes heaven’s calendar-revealer an antediluvian who “walked with God” three hundred and sixty-five years (Genesis 5:23) — the days of the solar year — so that the number of Enoch’s life matches the number of the days he is shown. The same solar calendar governs Jubilees and the Qumran community, one side of a Second Temple calendar dispute.5

The opening oracle Jude quotes: “Behold, He cometh with ten thousands of His holy ones”

And behold! He cometh with ten thousands of His holy ones
To execute judgement upon all,
And to destroy all the ungodly:
And to convict all flesh
Of all the works of their ungodliness which they have ungodly committed,
And of all the hard things which ungodly sinners have spoken against Him.

— 1 Enoch 1:9 (Charles), online. This is the single passage the New Testament quotes by name and attributes to Enoch: “And Enoch also, the seventh from Adam, prophesied of these, saying, Behold, the Lord cometh with ten thousands of his saints, to execute judgment upon all” (Jude 14–15). Jude’s citation raised a long-running question about the book’s authority — whether a New Testament writer’s use of 1 Enoch makes it Scripture — set out on its own page, “Was 1 Enoch received as Scripture in the early churches?”.6

Notes

  • Placement on the timeline. 1 Enoch is the earliest post-biblical witness to the Enoch tradition — older than Jubilees, 2 Enoch, and every Father — so it heads the post-biblical stretch of the timeline, immediately after the biblical portrait; it draws on and greatly expands the Genesis notice.
  • The Watchers material. The fall of the angels (chs. 6–16), the birth and spirits of the Nephilim, and the forbidden arts — metalworking, sorcery, and astrology — the Watchers taught are set out on the Nephilim, astrology, pillars of Seth (see “Is the pillars-of-Seth legend a displaced Enoch tradition?”), and divine council pages. This page keeps to the figure the book builds.
  • The seventh-from-Adam parallel. James VanderKam and Helge Kvanvig read Enoch, the seventh antediluvian patriarch, against the Mesopotamian figure of Enmeduranki — the seventh king in the Sumerian King List, the sage initiated into heavenly secrets and divination — as background to this portrait.7
  • The Similitudes and the “Son of Man.” The one booklet missing from the Qumran Aramaic finds (chs. 37–71) is also the one with the exalted, pre-existent “Son of Man” who sits on the throne of glory to judge — and in its closing chapter (71) Enoch himself is identified with, or as, that Son of Man. Its absence from Qumran and its date (late 1st c. BC–1st c. AD) are at the centre of the debate over its relation to the Gospels’ “Son of Man” (J. T. Milik’s 1976 dating of the Parables, answered by Jonas Greenfield and Michael Stone in 1977; see the modern-scholarship page). Later texts on this timeline also exalt Enoch: 2 Enoch’s angelic transformation and 3 Enoch’s identification of him with Metatron. Whether these belong to one continuous tradition is set out at “Did the Enoch–Metatron identification grow out of the earlier Enoch tradition?”
