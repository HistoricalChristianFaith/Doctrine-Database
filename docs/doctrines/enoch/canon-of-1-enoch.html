The canonical status of the Book of Enoch across the churches: early citation, exclusion from the Greek and Latin lists, and the Ethiopian canon

Dates: a consolidated reception, from the Epistle of Barnabas (c. AD 130) through Origen, Athanasius, Jerome, and Augustine to the Ethiopian Orthodox canon and James Bruce’s recovery of the Ge’ez text (1773)
Summary: This page tracks the datable judgements on the book’s authority — the timeline narration of the canon question, distinct from the memory of the man (gathered on “The Fathers on the translation”). On the strength of Jude’s quotation the Book of Enoch was cited in the earliest centuries — with Scripture-formulas by Barnabas, as history by Irenaeus, as prophecy by Clement of Alexandria, and defended outright by Tertullian — while Origen writes that the books bearing Enoch’s name do “not at all circulate in the Churches as divine,” and in the fourth and fifth centuries Athanasius, Jerome, and Augustine set it outside the canon. It then fell out of use in the Greek and Latin churches and was effectively lost to the West until Bruce brought Ge’ez manuscripts back from Ethiopia in 1773 — surviving in the Old Testament canon of one communion alone, the Ethiopian (and Eritrean) Orthodox Tewahedo Church, which never rejected it. Whether that early esteem amounts to reception as authoritative Scripture across the early churches is set out at “Was 1 Enoch received as Scripture in the early churches?”

The texts

Much of the reception concerns one fact: a New Testament author quotes the Book of Enoch by name and as prophecy. “And Enoch also, the seventh from Adam, prophesied of these, saying, Behold, the Lord cometh with ten thousands of his saints, to execute judgment upon all” (Jude 14–15) is a close rendering of the opening oracle of 1 Enoch (1:9). Tertullian reads it as apostolic endorsement of the book; Jerome and Augustine, who set the book aside, each address why an apostle cited it. What follows sets out the datable judgements in order, earliest first.

Claims & evidence

Second- and early third-century citation (2nd–early 3rd c.)

The earliest extant Christian writing after Jude to cite the Book of Enoch, the Epistle of Barnabas (c. AD 130), cites the book twice with citation formulas — “it is written” and “the Scripture saith.” It introduces an apocalyptic oracle with a naming citation — “it is written, as Enoch says” — and elsewhere quotes a passage of Enoch’s Animal Apocalypse (1 Enoch 89) as the word of “the Scripture”:

The final stumbling-block (or source of danger) approaches, concerning which it is written, as Enoch says, “For for this end the Lord has cut short the times and the days, that His Beloved may hasten; and He will come to the inheritance.”

For the Scripture saith, “And it shall come to pass in the last days, that the Lord will deliver up the sheep of His pasture, and their sheep-fold and tower, to destruction.”

— Epistle of Barnabas 4:3; 16:5 (read the work).1 Later in the second century Irenaeus of Lyons uses the Enochic tradition as history, weaving Enoch’s embassy to the fallen angels into his argument that a man may please God apart from circumcision:

Enoch, too, pleasing God, without circumcision, discharged the office of God’s legate to the angels although he was a man, and was translated, and is preserved until now as a witness of the just judgment of God.

— Against Heresies IV.16.2 (read the work) — the “legate to the angels” being drawn from the Book of Enoch, not from Genesis.2 Around AD 200 Clement of Alexandria, glossing Jude’s citation in his running comments on the Catholic Epistles (preserved in Cassiodorus’s Latin), writes that Jude “verifies the prophecy”:

“Enoch also, the seventh from Adam,” he says, “prophesied of these.” In these words he verifies the prophecy.

— Adumbrationes on Jude, from the Latin translation of Cassiodorus (read the work).3 Tertullian of Carthage defends the book outright — answering the objection that a work “published before the deluge” could not have survived the Flood, and concluding that because Enoch “in the same Scripture has preached likewise concerning the Lord… nothing at all must be rejected by us,” for “every Scripture suitable for edification is divinely inspired,” and “Enoch possesses a testimony in the Apostle Jude.” That defense is set out on his own witness page.4

Origen cites Enoch and reports that it does not circulate in the churches as divine (c. AD 230–250)

Tertullian, defending the book, records that it “is not received by some, because it is not admitted into the Jewish canon either.” Origen cites “the book of Enoch” for cosmological claims and, answering Celsus, writes:

He does not appear to have read the passages in question, nor to have been aware that the books which bear the name Enoch do not at all circulate in the Churches as divine.

— Against Celsus V.54 (read the work).5

Fourth- and fifth-century canon lists leave it out (367–c. 420)

When the canon lists are drawn up, the Book of Enoch falls outside them. Athanasius of Alexandria, whose Festal Letter 39 (367) fixes the Old and New Testament canon and adds a second tier of books “appointed by the Fathers to be read,” calls the writings beyond both lists apocryphal — and 1 Enoch appears in neither:

But the former are canonized; the latter being [merely] read… there is no mention of the apocryphal writings. But they are an invention of heretics, who write them when they choose.

— Festal Letter 39 (read the work; also newadvent.org).6 Jerome, cataloguing Jude among the seven catholic epistles, records that the very quotation of Enoch had told against the letter’s reception — and calls the source book apocryphal:

[Jude] left a short epistle which is reckoned among the seven catholic epistles, and because in it he quotes from the apocryphal book of Enoch it is rejected by many. Nevertheless by age and use it has gained authority and is reckoned among the Holy Scriptures.

— Lives of Illustrious Men 4 (read the work) — in Jerome’s report the quotation of Enoch was a ground for rejecting Jude, which “by age and use” gained authority.7 Augustine of Hippo treats the question at length. He grants that Jude says Enoch prophesied, and rules the writings under his name out of the canon, giving as his reasons their “too great antiquity” and that “they are not believed to be theirs”:

What of Enoch, the seventh from Adam? Does not the canonical epistle of the Apostle Jude declare that he prophesied? But the writings of these men could not be held as authoritative either among the Jews or us, on account of their too great antiquity… But the purity of the canon has not admitted these writings, not because the authority of these men who pleased God is rejected, but because they are not believed to be theirs.

— The City of God XVIII.38 (read the work); he says the same of “those fables about the giants… produced under his name” at City of God XV.23 (newadvent.org).8

Jerome on quotation and canonicity: the Epimenides comparison

Jerome addresses the inference that Jude’s citation canonizes its source. Commenting on Paul’s quotation of the pagan poet Epimenides (Titus 1:12), he argues that using part of a book does not adopt the whole:

Those who think that the whole book ought to be followed by the one who has made use of a part of the book, seem to me to admit the Apocryphal Book of Enoch… concerning which Judas the Apostle gave testimony in his Epistle, among the sacred Scriptures of the Church.

— Commentary on Titus 1:12 (read the work).9

Lost to the West — and recovered in 1773

After the fourth- and fifth-century exclusions the book ceased to be copied or quoted in the Greek and Latin churches and fell out of use altogether; by the early modern period Christian Europe knew the Book of Enoch only from the fragments embedded in a few Byzantine chroniclers (George Syncellus, c. 800), not as a text anyone possessed. It survived intact in one place. The complete work had been carried into Ethiopia and translated into Ge’ez by about the sixth century, and there it was copied, read, and canonized without interruption. In 1773 the Scottish traveller James Bruce returned from Abyssinia with three Ge’ez manuscripts of the whole book — the first complete Book of Enoch known in the West in more than a thousand years — from which Richard Laurence made the first English translation (1821) and R. H. Charles the standard critical edition (1906–1912).10

The one surviving canon: the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church

Alone among the churches, the Ethiopian (and Eritrean) Orthodox Tewahedo Church never set the book aside: Henok stands in its Old Testament to this day, and the Beta Israel of Ethiopia likewise preserved it. Among the Christian canons in use today, it appears in theirs alone.11
