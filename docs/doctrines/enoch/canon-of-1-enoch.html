The canonical status of the Book of Enoch across the churches: from Scripture to apocryphon, lost to the West and kept only in Ethiopia

Dates: a consolidated reception, from the Epistle of Barnabas (c. AD 130) through Origen, Athanasius, Jerome, and Augustine to the Ethiopian Orthodox canon and James Bruce’s recovery of the Ge’ez text (1773)
Summary: This page tracks the datable judgements on the book’s authority — the timeline narration of the canon question, distinct from the settled memory of the man (gathered on “The Fathers on the translation”). On the strength of Jude’s quotation the Book of Enoch stood high in the earliest centuries — cited as “Scripture” by Barnabas, used as fact by Irenaeus, ratified as prophecy by Clement of Alexandria, and defended outright by Tertullian — but its reception was contested from the start (Origen concedes it does “not at all circulate in the Churches as divine”) and was decisively set aside by the canon-defining Fathers of the fourth and fifth centuries (Athanasius, Jerome, Augustine). It then dropped out of the Greek and Latin Bibles entirely and was effectively lost to the West until Bruce brought Ge’ez manuscripts back from Ethiopia in 1773 — surviving in the Old Testament canon of one communion alone, the Ethiopian (and Eritrean) Orthodox Tewahedo Church, which never rejected it. Whether that early esteem amounts to reception as authoritative Scripture across the early Church is the adjudicated question, weighed and rated weak at “The canonicity of 1 Enoch.” This page narrates the arc; it does not re-argue the verdict.

The texts

The whole reception turns on a single fact: a New Testament author quotes the Book of Enoch by name and as prophecy. “And Enoch also, the seventh from Adam, prophesied of these, saying, Behold, the Lord cometh with ten thousands of his saints, to execute judgment upon all” (Jude 14–15) is a close rendering of the opening oracle of 1 Enoch (1:9). Every later judgement on the book — for it and against it — is in some way a reckoning with that quotation: the advocates read it as apostolic endorsement, and even the Fathers who reject the book must explain why an apostle cited it. What follows sets out the datable verdicts in order, earliest first.

Claims & evidence

The rise: an early, real esteem grounded in Jude’s quotation (2nd–early 3rd c.)

The earliest Christian writing after Jude to use Enoch, the Epistle of Barnabas (c. AD 130), cites the book twice with the formulas reserved for Scripture. It introduces an apocalyptic oracle with a naming citation — “it is written, as Enoch says” — and elsewhere quotes a passage of Enoch’s Animal Apocalypse (1 Enoch 89) as the word of “the Scripture”:

The final stumbling-block (or source of danger) approaches, concerning which it is written, as Enoch says, “For for this end the Lord has cut short the times and the days, that His Beloved may hasten; and He will come to the inheritance.”

For the Scripture saith, “And it shall come to pass in the last days, that the Lord will deliver up the sheep of His pasture, and their sheep-fold and tower, to destruction.”

— Epistle of Barnabas 4:3; 16:5 (read the work).1 Later in the second century Irenaeus of Lyons takes the Enochic tradition as plain fact, weaving Enoch’s embassy to the fallen angels into his argument that a man may please God apart from circumcision:

Enoch, too, pleasing God, without circumcision, discharged the office of God’s legate to the angels although he was a man, and was translated, and is preserved until now as a witness of the just judgment of God.

— Against Heresies IV.16.2 (read the work) — the “legate to the angels” being drawn from the Book of Enoch, not from Genesis.2 Around AD 200 Clement of Alexandria, glossing Jude’s citation in his running comments on the Catholic Epistles (preserved in Cassiodorus’s Latin), ratifies the Enochic prophecy as genuine:

“Enoch also, the seventh from Adam,” he says, “prophesied of these.” In these words he verifies the prophecy.

— Adumbrationes on Jude, from the Latin translation of Cassiodorus (read the work).3 The high-water mark comes with Tertullian of Carthage, who alone among the Fathers defends the book outright — answering the objection that a work “published before the deluge” could not have survived the Flood, and concluding that because Enoch “in the same Scripture has preached likewise concerning the Lord… nothing at all must be rejected by us,” for “every Scripture suitable for edification is divinely inspired,” and “Enoch possesses a testimony in the Apostle Jude.” That defense — the sharpest ancient form of the Jude-implies-canonicity inference — is set out on his own witness page.4

Contested from the start: Origen cites Enoch yet denies it churchly standing (c. AD 230–250)

Even at the early end that the advocates lean on, the book’s scriptural standing was disputed — and Tertullian says as much in the act of defending it, conceding it “is not received by some, because it is not admitted into the Jewish canon either.” The most telling early witness is Origen, who is content to cite “the book of Enoch” for cosmological claims, yet, correcting Celsus, states flatly that the Church does not receive it as divine:

He does not appear to have read the passages in question, nor to have been aware that the books which bear the name Enoch do not at all circulate in the Churches as divine.

— Against Celsus V.54 (read the work).5 So a leading third-century user of Enoch denies precisely the churchly, scriptural status the “received as Scripture” claim needs: the reception was mixed in its own day, not a consensus later overturned.

The fourth–fifth-century exclusion: the canon-defining Fathers set it aside (367–c. 420)

When the canon lists are drawn up, the Book of Enoch falls outside them. Athanasius of Alexandria, whose Festal Letter 39 (367) fixes the Old and New Testament canon and adds a second tier of books “appointed by the Fathers to be read,” brands everything beyond both lists apocryphal — and 1 Enoch appears in neither:

But the former are canonized; the latter being [merely] read… there is no mention of the apocryphal writings. But they are an invention of heretics, who write them when they choose.

— Festal Letter 39 (read the work; also newadvent.org).6 Jerome, cataloguing Jude among the seven catholic epistles, records that the very quotation of Enoch had told against the letter’s reception — and calls the source book plainly apocryphal:

[Jude] left a short epistle which is reckoned among the seven catholic epistles, and because in it he quotes from the apocryphal book of Enoch it is rejected by many. Nevertheless by age and use it has gained authority and is reckoned among the Holy Scriptures.

— Lives of Illustrious Men 4 (read the work) — note the reversal: it is Jude that had to earn its place despite quoting Enoch, not Enoch that rode in on Jude.7 Augustine of Hippo puts the Latin verdict most fully. He grants that Jude vouches for Enoch’s prophesying, yet rules the writings under his name out of the canon — not because Enoch is unworthy, but because their “too great antiquity” makes their authenticity unprovable:

What of Enoch, the seventh from Adam? Does not the canonical epistle of the Apostle Jude declare that he prophesied? But the writings of these men could not be held as authoritative either among the Jews or us, on account of their too great antiquity… But the purity of the canon has not admitted these writings, not because the authority of these men who pleased God is rejected, but because they are not believed to be theirs.

— The City of God XVIII.38 (read the work); he says the same of “those fables about the giants… produced under his name” at City of God XV.23 (newadvent.org).8

Quotation is not canonization: Jerome’s Epimenides reductio

The Fathers were alert to the inference the advocates would draw — that Jude’s citation canonizes its source — and answered it directly. Commenting on Paul’s quotation of the pagan poet Epimenides (Titus 1:12), Jerome presses that using part of a book does not adopt the whole:

Those who think that the whole book ought to be followed by the one who has made use of a part of the book, seem to me to admit the Apocryphal Book of Enoch… concerning which Judas the Apostle gave testimony in his Epistle, among the sacred Scriptures of the Church.

— Commentary on Titus 1:12 (read the work).9 This is the load-bearing counter to the whole “Jude cites it, therefore it is Scripture” case — the point on which the argument page’s weak verdict finally rests.

Lost to the West — and recovered in 1773

After the fourth-century verdicts the book ceased to be copied or quoted in the Greek and Latin churches and fell out of use altogether; by the early modern period Christian Europe knew the Book of Enoch only from the fragments embedded in a few Byzantine chroniclers (George Syncellus, c. 800), not as a text anyone possessed. It survived intact in one place. The complete work had been carried into Ethiopia and translated into Ge’ez by about the sixth century, and there it was copied, read, and canonized without interruption. In 1773 the Scottish traveller James Bruce returned from Abyssinia with three Ge’ez manuscripts of the whole book — the first complete Book of Enoch the West had seen in more than a thousand years — from which Richard Laurence made the first English translation (1821) and R. H. Charles the standard critical edition (1906–1912).10

The one surviving canon: the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church

Alone among the churches, the Ethiopian (and Eritrean) Orthodox Tewahedo Church never set the book aside: Henok stands in its Old Testament to this day, and the Beta Israel of Ethiopia likewise preserved it. This is a real but singular exception — and the honest measure of how isolated full canonical reception always was. A book that a New Testament apostle quoted, that Barnabas and Tertullian called Scripture, and that Origen, Athanasius, Jerome, and Augustine set outside the canon ended up in the Bible of exactly one Christian tradition.11
