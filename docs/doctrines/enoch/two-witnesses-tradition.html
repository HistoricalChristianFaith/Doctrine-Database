Enoch and Elijah, the two undying witnesses reserved to face Antichrist

Dates: a consolidated tradition, from Hippolytus of Rome (On Christ and Antichrist, c. AD 200 — the earliest extant full statement) through the Coptic Apocalypse of Elijah and the Gospel of Nicodemus down to the medieval Antichrist legend of Adso of Montier-en-Der (d. 992)
Summary: The Hebrew Scriptures tell of two men who were “taken” with no death recorded — Enoch, of whom “God took him” (Genesis 5:24), and Elijah, caught up “by a whirlwind into heaven” (2 Kings 2:11). From that pairing grew a reading, attested from c. AD 200: because they never died, both are held in reserve to return at the end of the age, preach against Antichrist, be killed by him, and rise — the “two witnesses” of Revelation 11:3–12, who prophesy 1,260 days in sackcloth, are slain by the beast, lie unburied three and a half days, and are raised. The identification is stated fully by Hippolytus (c. AD 200), grounded on Enoch’s and Elijah’s deferred death by Tertullian, dramatized in the Coptic Apocalypse of Elijah and the Gospel of Nicodemus, and set out in Adso of Montier-en-Der’s treatise, which Bernard McGinn calls the standard medieval account. This page narrates that Enoch-and-Elijah tradition as one stratum of the reception of Enoch; two other readings — that the two witnesses are Moses and Elijah (on the Transfiguration pairing), or the Church’s testimony — are noted below.

The scriptural basis

Two facts of the Hebrew canon underlie the whole tradition. First, Enoch and Elijah are the only two figures said not to have died: the seventh patriarch “walked with God: and he was not; for God took him” (Genesis 5:24; read as a translation escaping death at Hebrews 11:5), and Elijah “went up by a whirlwind into heaven” in a chariot of fire (2 Kings 2:11). Second, Malachi promises that God will “send you Elijah the prophet before the coming of the great and dreadful day of the LORD” (Malachi 4:5) — a return already expected for Elijah. Laid over the two unnamed “witnesses” of Revelation 11 — who shut heaven so it does not rain (Elijah’s sign, 1 Kings 17) and turn waters to blood and smite the earth with plagues (Moses’ signs) — these are the data on which the early writers below pair the two men.1

Claims & evidence

Hippolytus of Rome: “the two prophets Enoch and Elias” take up the last half-week (c. AD 200)

The earliest extant full statement is that of Hippolytus of Rome in his treatise On Christ and Antichrist. Expounding Daniel’s seventieth week, he assigns its latter half to two named forerunners:

By one week, therefore, he meant the last week which is to be at the end of the whole world of which week the two prophets Enoch and Elias will take up the half. For they will preach 1,260 days clothed in sackcloth, proclaiming repentance to the people and to all the nations.

— On Christ and Antichrist 43 (read the work; also newadvent.org). Hippolytus grounds the pairing on a “two advents” symmetry: just as Christ had one forerunner at his first coming, John the Baptist, so his glorious second coming has “two forerunners” (§44, 46). He then applies to them the whole passage of Revelation 11 — “And I will give power unto my two witnesses, and they shall prophesy a thousand two hundred and threescore days, clothed in sackcloth… the beast that ascendeth out of the bottomless pit shall make war against them, and shall overcome them, and kill them” — because they “will not give glory to Antichrist” (§47).2 This is the earliest extant full identification of Revelation’s witnesses as Enoch and Elijah.

Tertullian: their death was only “postponed” — reserved to extinguish Antichrist (c. AD 210)

Where Hippolytus reads the eschatology, Tertullian of Carthage supplies its logic: Enoch and Elijah did not escape death outright but had it deferred, so that they could die at the end under Antichrist. Arguing in A Treatise on the Soul that death is “the debt of nature” owed by everyone, he treats the two translated men as an apparent exception that in the end falls under the rule:

Enoch no doubt was translated, and so was Elijah; nor did they experience death: it was postponed, (and only postponed,) most certainly: they are reserved for the suffering of death, that by their blood they may extinguish Antichrist.

— A Treatise on the Soul (De anima) 50 (read the work; also newadvent.org). The chapter’s heading in the Ante-Nicene Fathers also names the point — “Even Enoch and Elijah Reserved for Death.” Tertullian’s interest is anthropological (all souls must pay death’s debt, against Menander’s promise of immortality by baptism), but in passing he states the view the later writers on this page share: the two men are alive now, and their deferred death will be their martyrdom at Antichrist’s hand.3

The Apocalypse of Elijah: they descend, are slain, rise, and return to kill the “son of lawlessness” (3rd–4th c.)

The Coptic Apocalypse of Elijah — a Christian apocalypse from Egypt, usually dated to the late third or fourth century — turns the identification into narrative. Enoch and Elijah descend from heaven to denounce the “son of lawlessness” (the Antichrist), who beheads them; they lie dead, then rise and rebuke him; and at the last they come down a final time to destroy him:

After these things Elijah and Enoch will come down and put aside the flesh of this world and put on their spiritual flesh. They will pursue the son of lawlessness and kill him, he being unable to speak. On that day he will dissolve before them like ice dissolved by fire.

— Apocalypse of Elijah (concluding section), in Anthony Alcock’s translation (PDF); overview and dating at earlyjewishwritings.com. The text closely tracks Revelation 11 — the witnesses’ preaching, martyrdom, resurrection, and the beast’s destruction — and names the two as Enoch and Elijah outright, an early full dramatization of the tradition.4

The Gospel of Nicodemus: Enoch and Elijah wait in Paradise for their return (4th–5th c.)

The Gospel of Nicodemus (the Acts of Pilate), in its account of Christ’s descent into hell, places the two men in Paradise, where the redeemed patriarchs, newly arrived, find two who had never been in hell and ask who they are:

I am Enoch, who was translated by the word of God: and this man who is with me is Elijah the Tishbite, who was translated in a fiery chariot. Here we have hitherto been, and have not tasted death, but are now about to return at the coming of Antichrist, being armed with divine signs and miracles, to engage with him in battle, and to be slain by him at Jerusalem, and to be taken up alive again into the clouds, after three days and a half.

— Gospel of Nicodemus (Acts of Pilate), ch. 20 (text). Here the tradition is set in the present tense: Enoch and Elijah are not merely destined to return but are pictured living in Paradise, waiting — the “three days and a half” drawn straight from Revelation 11:11. The scene was widely copied in the medieval West (⚠ unverified).5

Adso of Montier-en-Der makes it the standard medieval account (c. 950)

In the tenth century Adso of Montier-en-Der, in his Letter on the Origin and Time of Antichrist (Libellus de Antichristo) written for Queen Gerberga — the work Bernard McGinn treats as the standard account of Antichrist from the mid-tenth to the thirteenth century — sets Enoch and Elijah among the events of the last days:

Then shall be sent into the world the two great prophets Elias and Enoch, who shall forearm the faithful with godly weapons against the attack of the Antichrist… But after they have accomplished their preaching, the Antichrist shall rise up and slay them, and after three days they shall be raised up by the Lord.

— Adso, Libellus de Antichristo, in the translation of W. Bousset, The Antichrist Legend (trans. A. H. Keane, London, 1896) (Internet Archive). Adso compiles earlier material; through him the Enoch-and-Elijah reading passes from the Fathers into the apocalyptic literature of Latin Christendom, and it appears later in the Play of Antichrist, the Golden Legend, and medieval art (⚠ unverified).6

Notes

  • Placement on the timeline. The two-witnesses tradition belongs to the reception of Enoch as a figure: it develops the reading of Genesis 5:24 as “he did not die” into an eschatological role, without depending on the disputed Book of Enoch. It is close in date to Tertullian’s defence of the book (both c. AD 200), and its logic — death deferred, not escaped — is the one voiced on the translation of Enoch page by Cosmas, Bede, and Aquinas.
  • Other readings: Moses and Elijah, or the Church’s testimony. G. K. Beale and David Aune survey two further identifications. One takes the two witnesses to be Moses and Elijah — the pair who appear with Christ at the Transfiguration (Matthew 17:3), and whose signs (turning water to blood and smiting with plagues; shutting up heaven) map onto Revelation 11:6; Moses’ death (Deuteronomy 34:5) is read on this view alongside the tradition of his disputed body (Jude 9) and unknown grave. A corporate reading, in the Latin line of Tyconius and Primasius of Hadrumetum, takes the two witnesses as the Church preaching “on the basis of the two Testaments.”7 This page narrates the Enoch-and-Elijah stratum.
  • Attestation. The reading of Revelation’s witnesses as Enoch and Elijah is attested from Hippolytus (c. AD 200) onward, in Greek, Coptic, and Latin sources.
