Enoch and Elijah, the two undying witnesses reserved to face Antichrist

Dates: a consolidated tradition, from Hippolytus of Rome (On Christ and Antichrist, c. AD 200 — the earliest full statement) through the Coptic Apocalypse of Elijah and the Gospel of Nicodemus down to the medieval Antichrist legend of Adso of Montier-en-Der (d. 992)
Summary: Scripture names exactly two men who did not die but were “taken” — Enoch, of whom “God took him” (Genesis 5:24), and Elijah, caught up “by a whirlwind into heaven” (2 Kings 2:11). From that pairing grew an early and widespread reading: because they never died, both are held in reserve to return at the end of the age, preach against Antichrist, be killed by him, and rise — the “two witnesses” of Revelation 11:3–12, who prophesy 1,260 days in sackcloth, are slain by the beast, lie unburied three and a half days, and are raised. The identification is stated fully already by Hippolytus (c. AD 200), grounded on Enoch’s and Elijah’s deferred death by Tertullian, dramatized in the Coptic Apocalypse of Elijah and the Gospel of Nicodemus, and made the standard medieval account by Adso of Montier-en-Der. This page narrates that Enoch-and-Elijah tradition as one stratum of the reception of Enoch; the rival reading that the two witnesses are Moses and Elijah (on the Transfiguration pairing and Moses’ unique burial) is weighed separately.

The scriptural seed

Two facts of the Hebrew canon underlie the whole tradition. First, Enoch and Elijah are the only two figures said not to have died: the seventh patriarch “walked with God: and he was not; for God took him” (Genesis 5:24; read as a translation escaping death at Hebrews 11:5), and Elijah “went up by a whirlwind into heaven” in a chariot of fire (2 Kings 2:11). Second, Malachi promises that God will “send you Elijah the prophet before the coming of the great and dreadful day of the LORD” (Malachi 4:5) — a return already expected for Elijah. Laid over the two unnamed “witnesses” of Revelation 11 — who shut heaven so it does not rain (Elijah’s sign, 1 Kings 17) and turn waters to blood and smite the earth with plagues (Moses’ signs) — these data made the pairing of the two deathless men the natural early reading.1

Claims & evidence

Hippolytus of Rome: “the two prophets Enoch and Elias” take up the last half-week (c. AD 200)

The classic statement is that of Hippolytus of Rome in his treatise On Christ and Antichrist. Expounding Daniel’s seventieth week, he assigns its latter half to two named forerunners:

By one week, therefore, he meant the last week which is to be at the end of the whole world of which week the two prophets Enoch and Elias will take up the half. For they will preach 1,260 days clothed in sackcloth, proclaiming repentance to the people and to all the nations.

— On Christ and Antichrist 43 (read the work; also newadvent.org). Hippolytus grounds the pairing on a “two advents” symmetry: just as Christ had one forerunner at his first coming, John the Baptist, so his glorious second coming has “two forerunners” (§44, 46). He then applies to them the whole passage of Revelation 11 — “And I will give power unto my two witnesses, and they shall prophesy a thousand two hundred and threescore days, clothed in sackcloth… the beast that ascendeth out of the bottomless pit shall make war against them, and shall overcome them, and kill them” — because they “will not give glory to Antichrist” (§47).2 This is the earliest surviving full identification of Revelation’s witnesses as Enoch and Elijah, and the seed of the whole later Antichrist legend.

Tertullian: their death was only “postponed” — reserved to extinguish Antichrist (c. AD 210)

Where Hippolytus reads the eschatology, Tertullian of Carthage supplies its logic: Enoch and Elijah did not escape death outright but merely had it deferred, precisely so they could die at the end under Antichrist. Arguing in A Treatise on the Soul that death is “the debt of nature” owed by everyone, he treats the two translated men as the apparent exception that proves the rule:

Enoch no doubt was translated, and so was Elijah; nor did they experience death: it was postponed, (and only postponed,) most certainly: they are reserved for the suffering of death, that by their blood they may extinguish Antichrist.

— A Treatise on the Soul (De anima) 50 (read the work; also newadvent.org). The chapter’s heading in the Ante-Nicene Fathers even names the point — “Even Enoch and Elijah Reserved for Death.” Tertullian’s interest is anthropological (all souls must pay death’s debt, against the heretic Menander’s promise of immortality by baptism), but in passing he fixes the doctrine every later writer takes for granted: the two men are alive now, and their deferred death will be their martyrdom at Antichrist’s hand.3

The Apocalypse of Elijah: they descend, are slain, rise, and return to kill the “son of lawlessness” (3rd–4th c.)

The Coptic Apocalypse of Elijah — a Christian apocalypse from Egypt, usually dated to the late third or fourth century — turns the identification into narrative. Enoch and Elijah descend from heaven to denounce the “son of lawlessness” (the Antichrist), who beheads them; they lie dead, then rise and rebuke him; and at the last they come down a final time to destroy him:

After these things Elijah and Enoch will come down and put aside the flesh of this world and put on their spiritual flesh. They will pursue the son of lawlessness and kill him, he being unable to speak. On that day he will dissolve before them like ice dissolved by fire.

— Apocalypse of Elijah (concluding section), in Anthony Alcock’s translation (PDF); overview and dating at earlyjewishwritings.com. The text closely tracks Revelation 11 — the witnesses’ preaching, martyrdom, resurrection, and the beast’s destruction — and names the two as Enoch and Elijah outright, the earliest full dramatization of the tradition.4

The Gospel of Nicodemus: Enoch and Elijah wait in Paradise for their return (4th–5th c.)

The Gospel of Nicodemus (the Acts of Pilate), in its account of Christ’s descent into hell, places the two men in Paradise, where the redeemed patriarchs, newly arrived, find two who had never been in hell and ask who they are:

I am Enoch, who was translated by the word of God: and this man who is with me is Elijah the Tishbite, who was translated in a fiery chariot. Here we have hitherto been, and have not tasted death, but are now about to return at the coming of Antichrist, being armed with divine signs and miracles, to engage with him in battle, and to be slain by him at Jerusalem, and to be taken up alive again into the clouds, after three days and a half.

— Gospel of Nicodemus (Acts of Pilate), ch. 20 (text). Here the tradition is set in the present tense: Enoch and Elijah are not merely destined to return but are pictured living in Paradise, waiting — the “three days and a half” drawn straight from Revelation 11:11. This scene, widely copied in the medieval West, did as much as any argument to fix the pairing in popular belief.5

Adso of Montier-en-Der makes it the standard medieval account (c. 950)

By the tenth century the tradition was received doctrine. Adso of Montier-en-Der, in his Letter on the Origin and Time of Antichrist (Libellus de Antichristo) written for Queen Gerberga — the work that became the standard treatment of Antichrist from the mid-tenth to the thirteenth century — sets Enoch and Elijah among the fixed furniture of the last days:

Then shall be sent into the world the two great prophets Elias and Enoch, who shall forearm the faithful with godly weapons against the attack of the Antichrist… But after they have accomplished their preaching, the Antichrist shall rise up and slay them, and after three days they shall be raised up by the Lord.

— Adso, Libellus de Antichristo, in the translation of W. Bousset, The Antichrist Legend (trans. A. H. Keane, London, 1896) (Internet Archive). Adso is not an original witness but a compiler; his value here is that he shows the Enoch-and-Elijah reading passing from the Fathers into the settled apocalyptic imagination of Latin Christendom, whence it ran on through the Play of Antichrist, the Golden Legend, and medieval art.6

Notes

  • Where it sits on this timeline. The two-witnesses tradition belongs to the reception of Enoch as a figure: it develops the canonical “he did not die” into an eschatological role, without depending on the disputed Book of Enoch. It is close in date to Tertullian’s defence of the book (both c. AD 200), and its logic — death deferred, not escaped — is the same one the mainstream Fathers voice on the translation of Enoch, where Cosmas, Bede, and Aquinas repeat it.
  • The rival reading: Moses and Elijah. A competing identification takes the two witnesses to be Moses and Elijah — the pair who appear with Christ at the Transfiguration (Matthew 17:3), and whose signs (turning water to blood and smiting with plagues; shutting up heaven) map onto Revelation 11:6. Its difficulty is that Moses did die (Deuteronomy 34:5) — though the tradition of his disputed body (Jude 9) and unknown grave was pressed to answer that. Which reading better fits the text — and whether Enoch is a literal end-time witness at all, given that a strong symbolic reading takes the two as the Church’s prophetic testimony — is weighed at “Is Enoch one of the two witnesses of Revelation 11?” (rated contested). This page narrates only the Enoch-and-Elijah stratum.
  • The wiki does not discount a claim merely because it requires a miracle. That Enoch and Elijah were bodily translated and are kept alive to return is a supernatural claim, but the question here is historical — how early and how widely the church read Revelation’s witnesses as these two — and on that the attestation is early (Hippolytus, c. AD 200) and broad (Greek, Coptic, and Latin).
