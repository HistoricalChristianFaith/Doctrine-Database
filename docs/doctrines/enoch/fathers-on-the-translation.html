The Fathers on the translation of Enoch: the man who “pleased God and was not”

Dates: a consolidated reading of the mainstream church, from Clement of Rome (c. AD 96 — the earliest Christian witness) through the Greek and Latin commentators to Thomas Aquinas (d. 1274)
Summary: Set apart from the disputed Book of Enoch and its Watchers-and-Nephilim lore (weighed on the Nephilim, astrology, and pillars of Seth timelines, which this page does not repeat), the Fathers dwell steadily on the plain canonical facts about the man: he “walked with God,” “God took him” (Genesis 5:24), he was “translated that he should not see death” (Hebrews 11:5). From these few words the church built a settled devotional and doctrinal portrait, and turned it to several uses: Enoch as the first proof that God rewards the righteous and that death is not the end; Enoch as an example of the faith (not the works) that pleases God, and of a man who pleased Him without the law of Moses; the first pledge that death itself will be abolished; a caution against speculating where the translated Enoch now is and whether he is still in the body; a proof that the flesh is no excuse for failing at virtue, since he was taken up alive; the belief that his death was only postponed — he and Elijah reserved to return and die under Antichrist; and a rich typology of the seventh patriarch whose 365 years and translation figure the sabbath-rest and resurrection of the elect. This is the mainstream memory of Enoch that runs quietly alongside the louder controversy over his book.

The texts

The whole reflection grows from two verses and a backward glance. Genesis, where every antediluvian entry closes “and he died,” says instead of the seventh from Adam that “Enoch walked with God: and he was not; for God took him” (Genesis 5:24); the Epistle to the Hebrews reads that disappearance as a bodily rescue from death — “By faith Enoch was translated that he should not see death… for before his translation he had this testimony, that he pleased God” (Hebrews 11:5); and the Wisdom of Sirach adds the note the Fathers repeat, that Enoch was “an example of repentance to all generations” (Sirach 44:16). Everything below is a working-out of these.

Claims & evidence

Enoch the first proof that God rewards the righteous — “death was never known to happen to him”

The oldest Christian comment on Enoch is also the very first item in the church’s honour-roll of the righteous. Clement of Rome, writing to Corinth about AD 96, opens his catalogue of those who “perfectly ministered” to God’s glory with Enoch — the translation as the primal case that obedience is rewarded:

Let us steadfastly contemplate those who have perfectly ministered to his excellent glory. Let us take (for instance) Enoch, who, being found righteous in obedience, was translated, and death was never known to happen to him.

— First Epistle to the Corinthians 9 (read the work). This is the earliest surviving Christian reading of Genesis 5:24 — older than any of the Fathers who debate the book — and it fixes the note the rest will sound: Enoch was translated because he was found righteous, and the reward was that death never touched him.1 A century and a half later Cyprian of Carthage presses the same point pastorally, to console Christians dying in a plague — to be taken early is a mercy, as it was for Enoch:

We also find that Enoch, who pleased God, was transported, as divine Scripture testifies in Genesis and says, “And Enoch pleased God and was not seen later because God took him.” This was pleasing in the sight of God—that Enoch merited being transported from the contagion of this world.

— On the Mortality 23 (read the work). Cyprian follows the Septuagint’s “pleased God” (the wording Hebrews and Sirach also carry) and reads the translation as a rescue “from the contagion of this world” — the righteous taken early “lest… they be corrupted by familiarity with the world.”2

“By faith Enoch was translated” — and he pleased God without the law of Moses

Because Hebrews files Enoch under faith, the commentators ask what faith he can have exercised so early, and answer: the bare conviction that God exists and rewards those who seek Him. John Chrysostom, preaching through Hebrews at Antioch, makes the translation turn wholly on that belief:

How was it “by faith” that “Enoch was translated”? Because his pleasing God was the cause of his translation, and faith the cause of his pleasing Him. For if he had not known that he should receive a reward, how could he have pleased Him? “But without faith it is impossible to please” Him.

— Homily on Hebrews 22 (read the work). Chrysostom notes the striking thing: Enoch believed God rewards the righteous even though the one prior example — his uncle Abel — had been murdered and left unavenged, and though “as yet they knew nothing of a resurrection.” Faith held “the opposite of what was seen.”3 Tertullian draws out the corollary the church would need against Judaizing pressure: Enoch pleased God, and was rescued from death, though he had neither circumcision nor sabbath nor the Mosaic law — so may we:

For Enoch, too, most righteous man, uncircumcised and in-observant of the Sabbath, He translated from this world; who did not first taste death, in order that, being a candidate for eternal life, he might by this time show us that we also may, without the burden of the law of Moses, please God.

— An Answer to the Jews 2 (read the work). Bede states the same principle in his homilies: those who lived “before the giving of circumcision” pleased God “by the virtue of faith alone,” for “without faith it is impossible to please God.”4

The first pledge that death itself will be abolished

Beyond a private reward, several Fathers read Enoch’s translation as a public sign planted early in history: a guarantee that death is not final and will one day be undone. Chrysostom again:

For that the one was translated, and that the other was taken up, the Scriptures have said… For this indeed took place, I mean his translation, immediately at the beginning, the human soul thereby receiving a hope of the destruction of death, and of the overthrow of the devil’s tyranny, and that death will be done away; for he was translated, not dead, but “that he should not see death.”

— Homily on Hebrews 22 (read the work). Chrysostom even sketches the pedagogy: God let the righteous Abel be killed to show that the sentence of death is real, then “almost immediately after him… translated Enoch” alive, so that fear and hope were set side by side at the beginning.5 Cosmas Indicopleustes, the sixth-century traveller-theologian, makes Enoch a standing demonstration that death was never God’s last word:

This is Enoch on whom the sentence of death did not take effect, for he was translated by God that he should not see death… in order that thereby it might be declared to us that death shall not have power over man, but that his power over him shall be dissolved, as was exhibited in the case of the Lord Christ… This is Enoch who was translated to life, as a proof of the power of God to after generations, a power capable of warding off death from mortals.

— The Christian Topography 5 (read the work). Oecumenius’ Greek catena on Hebrews puts it in a phrase: “From him, the abolition of death was prefigured… through Abel, the approval of the sentence was shown, but through Enoch, it is abolition.”6

“But to ask these things is superfluous” — where is Enoch, and is he still in the body?

The translation invites an obvious question — if Enoch did not die, where did he go, and how does he now exist? The Fathers, strikingly, mostly refuse to speculate, and rebuke the curiosity. Chrysostom is blunt:

But many ask whither Enoch was translated, and why he was translated, and why he did not die, neither he nor Elijah, and, if they are still alive, how they live, and in what form. But to ask these things is superfluous. For that the one was translated, and that the other was taken up, the Scriptures have said; but where they are, and how they are, they have not added: For they say nothing more than is necessary.

— Homily on Hebrews 22 (read the work).7 The Byzantine commentators inherit the caution almost verbatim. Oecumenius: “he would not see death… For he was indeed removed, and still lives: moreover, where and how, it is uncertain.” Theophylact of Ohrid: “that he was translated alive and that he still lives now — we know, but where or how — is unknown, since Scripture says nothing about this.”8 Gregory of Nazianzus leaves even the why open — “Enoch was translated, but it is not yet clear whether it was because he already comprehended the Divine Nature, or in order that he might comprehend it.”9 Behind the Christian reticence stands an older Hellenistic-Jewish reading: Philo of Alexandria had already treated the “he was not found” as a philosophical translation — “the end of virtuous and holy men is not death but a translation and migration” from “a visible place, perceptible by the outward senses, into an incorporeal idea, appreciable only to the intellect.”10

Translated alive, in the flesh — so the flesh is no excuse for failing at virtue

Because Hebrews insists Enoch “should not see death,” the Fathers stress that he was taken up bodily, still clothed in flesh — and they turn that fact into an argument against every excuse that the body makes holiness impossible. Chrysostom:

Then say not thou either, “I am clothed with flesh, I cannot get the mastery, nor undertake the toils which are for the sake of virtue.” Do not accuse the Creator. For if the wearing the flesh make virtue impossible, then the fault is not ours… and Enoch also, having worn flesh, was translated, and not found. So also Elias was caught up with the flesh.

— Homilies on John 75.5 (read the work).11 For John Cassian, teaching the monks of Gaul, “walking with God” is the very pattern of the contemplative who has withdrawn his heart from the passing world, and Enoch’s bodily removal proves the pattern can be lived out to the end:

He walks with God, like Enoch. He is gone from a human way of life, from human concerns. He is no longer to be found amid the vanity of this present world. The text of Genesis relates that this actually happened to Enoch in the body: “Enoch walked with God and was not to be found because God had taken him away.”

— Conferences 3.7 (tr. Boniface Ramsey, ACW 57).12

Death only postponed: Enoch and Elijah reserved to return and die under Antichrist

One persistent qualification runs against the sunnier readings. Several Fathers held that Enoch (with Elijah, the Bible’s other man taken up without dying) did not escape death but had it deferred: the two are kept alive to return in the last days, confront Antichrist, and only then die. Tertullian states it flatly:

Enoch no doubt was translated, and so was Elijah; nor did they experience death: it was postponed, (and only postponed,) most certainly: they are reserved for the suffering of death, that by their blood they may extinguish Antichrist.

— A Treatise on the Soul 50 (read the work).13 Cosmas names the same expectation — “This is he who along with Elias will in the last days withstand the Antichrist, and refute his error, according to the ecclesiastical tradition” — and Bede calls it the settled mind of the church: “the faith of the universal Church holds” that Enoch will “return before the day of judgment… with Elijah… and then, having completed their martyrdom,” enter “immortal life,” reading them as the “two witnesses” of Revelation 11:3.14 Aquinas systematizes the whole into three deferred deaths, one for each age of salvation-history — Enoch under the law of nature, Elijah under the Mosaic law, Christ under grace:

But the death of two persons, namely, Enoch and Elijah, has been deferred… in the first state He offered the hope of evading death in the case of Enoch; in the Law in Elijah; in the time of grace in Christ… But the first two will be put to death by Antichrist.

— Commentary on Hebrews, ch. 11, lect. 2 (read the work). Aquinas also carries Sirach’s note that Enoch was “translated into paradise, that he may give repentance to the nations” — the Fathers’ standing tag for him as the archetype of penitence. This Enoch-and-Elijah expectation is a whole tradition of its own, developed across the fathers and the medieval Antichrist literature; this page records only that the reading of the translation as a postponement, not an escape, was mainstream.15

The translation as a figure: the seventh from Adam, the dedication, the solar year

Finally, the Latin exegetes read the bare facts typologically. Augustine, noting that Enoch is the seventh from Adam — and that his name in Hebrew means “Dedication” — makes the translation a figure of the resurrection-rest still owed to the whole church:

But this is that man who was translated because he pleased God, and who held in the order of the generations a remarkable place, being the seventh from Adam, a number signalized by the consecration of the Sabbath… the translation of Enoch prefigured our deferred dedication; for though it is indeed already accomplished in Christ our Head… yet there remains another dedication of the whole house… deferred till the end, when all shall rise again to die no more.

— The City of God 15.19 (newadvent.org).16 Bede gathers every number in the Genesis notice into the same key. That Enoch’s days were “three hundred sixty-five years, the number of days in a solar year, mystically signifies that throughout the whole of this world’s time, those who serve the Lord faithfully… will never fall short”; and the three hundred years he “walked with God” are written, in Greek, with the letter “T” — which “holds the figure of the cross,” so that Enoch, seventh from Adam and translated to rest, images those who “taking up their cross daily… walk with God, and aim for the entrance of paradise.”17

Notes

  • Where it sits on this timeline. This is a consolidated block, not a single dated witness: it gathers the mainstream patristic and medieval reflection on the figure of Enoch and his translation, anchored at its earliest voice, Clement of Rome (c. AD 96). It runs quietly alongside the noisier question of whether the book that bears Enoch’s name is Scripture — the concern of the Tertullian witness and of “The canonicity of 1 Enoch” (rated weak). The Fathers here rarely touch the book at all; they are reading Genesis and Hebrews.
  • It hubs the Watchers material; it does not re-argue it. The Enoch of these Fathers is the translated patriarch, not the revealer of the Watchers’ forbidden arts — that strand, and the whole 1 Enoch 6–16 rebellion, is weighed on the Nephilim, astrology, and pillars of Seth timelines.
  • The two-witnesses expectation is a tradition in its own right. That Enoch and Elijah return to die under Antichrist (Tertullian, Cosmas, Bede, Aquinas above) is developed here only as far as it bears on reading the translation as a postponement of death; whether Enoch (rather than Moses) is one of the two witnesses of Revelation 11 is a distinct question.
  • A note on the Latin editions. Quotations follow the translations cited at each note — Ante-Nicene and Nicene/Post-Nicene Fathers for Clement, Cyprian, Tertullian, Chrysostom, Augustine, and Cosmas; the Aquinas Commentary on Hebrews and the catenae of Oecumenius and Theophylact as linked; and, for Cassian, the Ancient Christian Writers rendering. Where a translation renders the Septuagint’s “pleased God” for the Hebrew “walked with God,” that follows the Greek text the Fathers actually read.
