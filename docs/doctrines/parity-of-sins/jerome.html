Jerome on the parity of sins

Dates: c. 393 (Against Jovinianus) · Wikipedia
Summary: In Book II of Against Jovinianus (393) Jerome sets out scriptural arguments for degrees of merit, guilt and reward against the Roman monk Jovinian, whom he charges with becoming “a Stoic” in “the distribution of rewards and punishments.” The fourth of the four propositions he lists from Jovinian’s book concerns one reward for all who keep their baptism; whether Jovinian also taught that all sins are equal is a disputed reading. In 416, asked by Augustine to expound James 2:10, Jerome declined to answer.

Claims & evidence

Jovinian’s four propositions, as Jerome states them

He says that “virgins, widows, and married women, who have been once passed through the layer of Christ, if they are on a par in other respects, are of equal merit.”

He endeavours to show that “they who with full assurance of faith have been born again in baptism, cannot be overthrown by the devil.”

His third point is “that there is no difference between abstinence from food, and its reception with thanksgiving.”

The fourth and last is “that there is one reward in the kingdom of heaven for all who have kept their baptismal vow.”

— Against Jovinianus I.3, source (also at New Advent). This is Jerome’s summary of the book he is answering, given before the polemic begins.

Jovinian on punishment and reward, as Jerome quotes him

He who says to his brother, ‘thou fool,’ and ‘raca,’ will be in danger of Gehenna. And the murderer and the adulterer will likewise be sent into Gehenna. In times of persecution some are burnt, some strangled, some beheaded, some flee, or die within the walls of a prison: the struggle varies in kind, but the victors’ crown is one.

— Jovinian, quoted at Against Jovinianus II.20, source (also at New Advent). The argument runs from Matthew 5:22 and the parable of the labourers’ equal penny.

Jerome’s charge: Zeno, not Christ

In sexual intercourse and full feeding he is an Epicurean; in the distribution of rewards and punishments he all at once becomes a Stoic, He exchanges Jerusalem for Citium, Judaea for Cyprus, Christ for Zeno. If we may not depart a hair’s breadth from virtue, and all sins are equal, and a man who in a fit of hunger steals a piece of bread is no less guilty than he who slays a man: you must, in your turn, be held guilty of the greatest crimes.

— Against Jovinianus II.21, source. Citium in Cyprus was Zeno’s birthplace. Jerome places the Stoic turn in “the distribution of rewards and punishments,” and “all sins are equal” follows in the same sentence; whether Jovinian taught the parity is a disputed reading (see what Jovinian taught). Elsewhere he writes, “For you admit no difference between sins” (II.31), and argues that on such terms a man who has told a lie would be punished with parricides and adulterers.

Jerome’s scriptural case for grades of guilt, reward, and rank

But what are we to think of your assertion, that because there is a division into good and bad, the good, or the bad it may be, are not distinguished one from another, and that it makes no difference whether one is a ram in the flock or a poor little sheep?

— Against Jovinianus II.22, source. Jerome’s positive case fills chapters 18–34: the thirtyfold, sixtyfold and hundredfold of the sower; “one star differeth from another star in glory”; Ezekiel’s judgment between cattle and cattle; the many mansions; the talents; and Christ’s word to Pilate that another had “the greater sin.” Augustine refers Jerome to this work in 415, calling it “that most delightful and noble dissertation.”

Twenty-three years later, he declines to expound James 2:10

But a most trying time has come upon us, in which I have found it better for me to hold my peace than to speak, so that our studies have ceased … For this reason I have not been able at the present time to give to those two books dedicated to my name — books of profound erudition, and brilliant with every charm of splendid eloquence — the answer which I would otherwise have given; not that I think anything said in them demands correction, but because I am mindful of the words of the blessed apostle in regard to the variety of men’s judgments, “Let every man be fully persuaded in his own mind.”

— Letter 134.1 (a.d. 416; numbered Letter 172 among Augustine’s), source. The “two books” are Augustine’s Letter 166 on the origin of the soul and Letter 167 on James 2:10, carried east together by the presbyter Orosius. Jerome praises them, says he finds nothing in them needing correction, and gives as his reason for not answering the “most trying time” that has come upon them.

Notes

  • Whether Jovinian himself taught the parity of sins is set out, with the readings of its holders, at did Jovinian teach that all sins are equal? Augustine repeats the charge in his letter to Jerome (415) and lists it first among Jovinian’s tenets in De haeresibus 82.
  • According to the NPNF heading of the letter, Pammachius and Jerome’s other Roman friends withheld Against Jovinianus from publication; Jerome defended it in Letter 48 to Pammachius (393 or 394), where he states the question between himself and Jovinian as “that he puts marriage on a level with virginity, while I make it inferior.”
  • Jerome is also a witness on the origin of the soul, the perpetual virginity of Mary and the Old Testament canon, and a party to two relationship timelines, Jerome and Augustine and the Origenist controversy. Jovinian’s own page is on the perpetual virginity timeline.
