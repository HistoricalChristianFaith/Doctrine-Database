Cicero on the parity of sins

Dates: c. 46 BC · Wikipedia
Summary: Cicero, a Roman orator and philosopher and not a Christian writer, reports the Stoic thesis omnia peccata paria — “all sins are equal” — in two works: among the Stoic maxims he lists in a courtroom speech (Pro Murena, 63 BC), and as the third of his Paradoxa Stoicorum (c. 46 BC), where he argues for it. Later Latin writers on this timeline — Ambrose, Jerome, Augustine, Bede and Aquinas — discuss the thesis in terms close to his, and Aquinas cites the Paradoxa by name.

Claims & evidence

The Stoic thesis in Pro Murena: killing a cock is no lesser crime than strangling one’s father

all offenses are equal; that every sin is an unpardonable crime; and that he does not commit a less crime who kills a cock if there was no need to do so, than the man who strangles his father

— Pro Murena 61, trans. C. D. Yonge, source. Cicero is defending Murena against a prosecution in which Cato the Younger, a Stoic, appeared, and he lists these among the maxims of Zeno’s followers (Latin omnia peccata esse paria).

The thesis argued in the Paradoxa

PARADOX III: THAT ALL MISDEEDS ARE IN THEMSELVES EQUAL, AND GOOD DEEDS THE SAME.

The matter it may be said is a trifle, but the crime is enormous; for crimes are not to be measured by the issue of events, but from the bad intentions of men.

— Paradoxa Stoicorum III, trans. Cyrus R. Edmonds, source. The argument runs on the intention rather than the outcome: a pilot who oversets a ship has failed alike whether her cargo was gold or straw. In the same paradox Cicero allows that killing a father “is greater by reason of the number of sins (involved in it), and is deserving of a proportionately greater punishment.”

Notes

  • The cock-and-father illustration recurs later. Ambrose describes the Novatianists as holding, “after the fashion of the Stoics,” that the man who “has stolen a common fowl, as they say” is no better off than one “who has smothered his father.” Jerome sets a stolen loaf beside a killing; Bede has a stolen chicken.
  • Aquinas cites Cicero by name and by book — “The opinion of the Stoics, which Cicero adopts in the book on Paradoxes (Paradox. iii), was that all sins are equal” — and says that from this opinion “arose the error of certain heretics.”
  • A parity principle also appears in Greek-speaking Judaism: 4 Maccabees has Eleazar say that transgression “in matters either small or great is of equal seriousness.”
