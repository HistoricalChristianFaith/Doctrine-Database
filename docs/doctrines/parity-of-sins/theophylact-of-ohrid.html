Theophylact of Ohrid on the parity of sins

Dates: c. 1100 · Wikipedia
Summary: Theophylact reads James 2:10 by love, “the head of all that is good,” and argues that if the verse meant that any single failure condemns, “no one among men would survive.” He explains James’s two examples, adultery and murder, as commandments of love of neighbour. Substantially the same exposition stands in the commentary circulating under the name of Oecumenius.

Claims & evidence

Theophylact reads the verse by love: otherwise, he says, no one would survive

Whoever sins in one thing becomes guilty of all, because he does not have perfect love, for love is the head of all that is good, and when the head is absent, the entire remaining body amounts to nothing. … If this were not so, no one among men would survive, because no one keeps all the commandments; but whoever preserved purity is sometimes overcome by anger, and whoever gives alms not infrequently has envy. Therefore he speaks not of there being no deficiency at all in the virtues, but of love — that it must not be practiced with deficiency, with partiality, but wholly.

— Commentary on James, ch. 2, source. Theophylact argues from the fact that no one keeps all the commandments, and he names partiality in love as the fault in view — the subject of James 2:1–9.

Theophylact on why “Do not commit adultery, do not murder” are chosen: the commandments of love

Note that they too are taken from the law relating to the perfection of love, for whoever loves his neighbor will neither commit adultery nor murder, because such deeds are characteristic of an enemy.

— Commentary on James, ch. 2, source. James’s two examples in v. 11 are both from the second table, and both are things done to a neighbour; Theophylact ties them to the love-command of v. 8. Hilary of Arles, in the Latin catena on James, gives the same reason: “Because they are the ones which deal most closely with loving and with hating one’s neighbors.”

Notes

  • The same exposition, at times nearly word for word, stands in the commentary on the Catholic Epistles that circulates under the name of Oecumenius — “James said this concerning love … since it would not be said in this way, no one among men would be saved” (source), and in briefer form in the Greek catena attributed to Andreas of Caesarea: “To fail in one point is to lack perfect love, for this is the source of all good deeds.”
  • In the Latin West Augustine (415) and Bede (c. 716) also read “guilty of all” by love, as the love “on which hangs the whole law.”
