Thomas Aquinas on the parity of sins

Dates: c. 1270 (Summa Theologiae I-II) · Wikipedia
Summary: Aquinas gives the parity thesis a formal article of its own (Summa Theologiae I-II q. 73 a. 2), traces it to the Stoics through Cicero, and gives his reasons for rejecting it: the Stoics treated sin as a pure privation, like death or darkness, which admits of no more and less; sin, he holds, is the other kind of privation, like sickness, which leaves something of the opposite habit standing and so admits of degrees. In the next article he orders the gravity of sins by their objects.

Claims & evidence

The thesis, its source, and the “certain heretics” Aquinas names

The opinion of the Stoics, which Cicero adopts in the book on Paradoxes (Paradox. iii), was that all sins are equal: from which opinion arose the error of certain heretics, who not only hold all sins to be equal, but also maintain that all the pains of hell are equal.

— Summa Theologiae I-II q. 73 a. 2, source. Aquinas traces the line from the Stoics through Cicero to “certain heretics,” who in his account added equal punishment to equal sin.

Aquinas: sin as a privation that admits of degrees

So far as can be gathered from the words of Cicero the Stoics arrived at their conclusion through looking at sin on the side of the privation only, in so far, to wit, as it is a departure from reason; wherefore considering simply that no privation admits of more or less, they held that all sins are equal. … There is, however, another privation which is not simple, but retains something of the opposite habit; it consists in “becoming” corrupted rather than in “being” corrupted, like sickness which is a privation of the due commensuration of the humors, yet so that something remains of that commensuration, else the animal would cease to live

— Summa Theologiae I-II q. 73 a. 2, source. Aquinas grants that some privations admit of no degrees and holds that sin is not one of them, since a sin which destroyed the order of reason entirely would destroy the act along with it — “evil, if total, destroys itself.” Augustine had met the same Stoic argument in the image of the drowning man.

Aquinas on gravity by object, with sins against God the gravest

Wherefore a sin which is about the very substance of man, e.g. murder, is graver than a sin which is about external things, e.g. theft; and graver still is a sin committed directly against God, e.g. unbelief, blasphemy, and the like

— Summa Theologiae I-II q. 73 a. 3, source. Here Aquinas gives a principle for ordering sins by gravity. Cicero’s argument in Paradoxa III had set the object aside (the cargo of gold or of straw) in favour of the lapse itself.

The proof-text: “he that hath delivered me to thee hath the greater sin”

Our Lord said to Pilate (Jn. 19:11): “He that hath delivered me to thee, hath the greater sin,” and yet it is evident that Pilate was guilty of some sin. Therefore one sin is greater than another.

— Summa Theologiae I-II q. 73 a. 2, sed contra, source, citing John 19:11. Jerome cites the same verse in Against Jovinianus II.25.

Aquinas on “certain heretics” who held all sins, punishments and rewards equal

Likewise it refutes certain heretics who said that all sins are equal, and all punishments, and all merits, and all rewards. Therefore, he excludes this, when he says, more tolerable…, because it will be worse for certain sinners.

— Commentary on Matthew, on Matthew 10:15, source. Aquinas reads the “more tolerable… for the land of Sodom and Gomorrah” sayings (Matthew 10:15; 11:22) as a comparison that excludes equal punishment.

Notes

  • Aquinas does not name Jovinian in these places; he says “certain heretics.” Jerome and Bede name Jovinian in connection with the Stoic thesis, and Jerome ascribes to him equal reward and punishment. Whether Jovinian taught the parity of sins is set out at did Jovinian teach that all sins are equal?
  • Aquinas compares venial and mortal sin at Summa Theologiae I-II q. 88, defining venial sin as a disorder that leaves the last end intact and mortal sin as one that turns away from it (source). Bede had distinguished small sins “without which this life cannot be lived” from sins that “lead their perpetrator to eternal punishment” unless corrected.
  • Aquinas is also a witness on the origin of the soul and the nephilim.
