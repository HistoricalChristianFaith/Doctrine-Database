Cyprian on the parity of sins

Dates: c. 252 (Letter to Antonianus) · Wikipedia
Summary: The earliest Christian writer surveyed here to name the parity thesis as a philosophers’ doctrine and set it apart from Christian teaching. Writing to an African bishop about how to treat those who had lapsed under persecution, Cyprian calls the refusal to distinguish degrees of guilt the principle of “the philosophers and stoics” and argues from the physician’s care for the wounded.

Claims & evidence

Cyprian sets the Christians against the Stoic “all sins are equal”

The principle of the philosophers and stoics is different, dearest brother, who say that all sins are equal, and that a grave man ought not easily to be moved. But there is a wide difference between Christians and philosophers. And when the apostle says, “Beware, lest any man spoil you through philosophy and vain deceit,” we are to avoid those things which do not come from God’s clemency, but are begotten of the presumption of a too rigid philosophy.

— Letter 51.16 (numbered Letter 55 in the Oxford and Hartel editions), to Antonianus, source (also at New Advent). Antonianus had received letters from Novatian and was leaning to the rigorist party; Cyprian argues that the lapsed may be graded and received back according to the measure of their fall. He applies Colossians 2:8, “philosophy and vain deceit,” to the parity thesis.

The pastoral consequence: the wounded are treated as wounded, not as dead

What medical skill can he exercise who says, “I cure the sound only, who have no need of a physician?” We ought to give our assistance, our healing art, to those who are wounded; neither let us think them dead, but rather let us regard them as lying half alive

— Letter 51.16, source. Cyprian’s image is medical: as a physician treats the wounded according to their wounds, so, he argues, the church receives the lapsed according to the measure of their fall.

Notes

  • Clement had read degrees of sin out of 1 John without naming an opposing school; Cyprian names one. The pairing of the parity thesis with the Stoics recurs on this timeline in Ambrose, Jerome, Augustine and Bede.
  • He collects scriptural texts on the sin that “shall not be forgiven” in his Testimonies — “if a man sin against God, who shall pray for him?”
    — Testimonies Against the Jews III, source.
  • Cyprian is also a witness on purgatory.
