Did Jovinian teach that all sins are equal?

Disputed reading · on Are all sins equal? (the gravity of sins) · witness: Jerome

The passage

In sexual intercourse and full feeding he is an Epicurean; in the distribution of rewards and punishments he all at once becomes a Stoic, He exchanges Jerusalem for Citium, Judaea for Cyprus, Christ for Zeno. If we may not depart a hair’s breadth from virtue, and all sins are equal, and a man who in a fit of hunger steals a piece of bread is no less guilty than he who slays a man: you must, in your turn, be held guilty of the greatest crimes.

— Jerome, Against Jovinianus II.21 (393), source.1 Before the polemic begins, Jerome summarizes the four propositions he is answering:

He says that “virgins, widows, and married women, who have been once passed through the layer of Christ, if they are on a par in other respects, are of equal merit.”

He endeavours to show that “they who with full assurance of faith have been born again in baptism, cannot be overthrown by the devil.”

His third point is “that there is no difference between abstinence from food, and its reception with thanksgiving.”

The fourth and last is “that there is one reward in the kingdom of heaven for all who have kept their baptismal vow.”

— Jerome, Against Jovinianus I.3, source. Jovinian, a Roman monk, was condemned by a synod at Rome under Siricius and then by a synod at Milan under Ambrose (c. 390); his book survives only in the passages his opponents quote.2 The synod of Milan, writing to Siricius in 389, describes the teaching it condemned:

For it is a savage barking to shew no reverence to virginity, observe no rule of chastity, to seek to place every thing on a level, to abolish the different degrees of merit, and to introduce a certain meagreness in heavenly rewards, as if Christ had only one palm to bestow, and there was no copious diversity in His rewards.

— Ambrose and the synod of Milan, Letter 42.2, to Siricius, source.3

The question

Jerome’s summary of Jovinian’s book and the Milan synod’s letter report a levelling of merit and reward; the words “all sins are equal” appear in Jerome’s second book, in Augustine’s letter to Jerome (415) and his De haeresibus (428), and in later catalogues. Did Jovinian himself teach the Stoic parity of sins, or is that phrase Jerome’s description of his doctrine of one reward and one punishment? The answer decides whether Jovinian is recorded as a Christian holder of the parity thesis. The second reading’s direct statement in the academic literature has been located here only at second hand, in Hunter (2007) as quoted by Ralston.

Jovinian taught, with the Stoics, that all sins are equal

On this reading Jovinian held the equality of sins together with the equality of reward, and his opponents report it as one of his tenets.

Held by: Jerome (Against Jovinianus, 393);1 Augustine (Letter 167, 415; De haeresibus 82, 428);4,5 the Second Helvetic Confession (Reformed, 1566);6 Philip Schaff (Protestant church historian, History of the Christian Church III, 1910 printing).7,8

Jerome quotes Jovinian’s own argument before answering it:

He who says to his brother, ‘thou fool,’ and ‘raca,’ will be in danger of Gehenna. And the murderer and the adulterer will likewise be sent into Gehenna. In times of persecution some are burnt, some strangled, some beheaded, some flee, or die within the walls of a prison: the struggle varies in kind, but the victors’ crown is one.

— Jovinian, quoted at Against Jovinianus II.20, source.9 Jovinian here sets the insult beside murder and adultery under one sentence, and Jerome answers him: “For you admit no difference between sins,” so that a man who has told a lie “will be punished with parricides and adulterers” (II.31).10 Augustine, writing to Jerome in 415, calls Jovinian “a Stoic in this opinion” and credits Jerome with having refuted the equality of sins from Scripture.4 In De haeresibus, his catalogue of heresies for Quodvultdeus, he describes the Jovinianists as a sect “whom I already knew,” which arose “in our age, while we were still young men,” and he puts the parity of sins first among Jovinian’s tenets: “This man said, as did the Stoic philosophers, that all sins were equal.”5 The Second Helvetic Confession, confessing that “sins are not equal,” condemns “the Jovinians who, with the Stoics, regard all sins as equal,” in agreement with Augustine.6

Schaff reads the whole doctrine as one principle. Jovinian, he writes, “with the Stoics, denied all gradations of moral merit and demerit, consequently also all gradations of reward and punishment,” and “lost all subordinate differences of degree in the great contrast between true Christians and men of the world, between regenerate and unregenerate.”7 On this account equal reward and equal guilt are two faces of the same two-class scheme: the baptized share one crown, the lost share one Gehenna, and within each class no deed weighs more than another. The equality of sins is then the ground of the fourth proposition, and Jerome and Augustine name it as Jovinian’s own tenet.

Jovinian taught one recompense for the baptized; the Stoic parity is Jerome’s name for it

On this reading what Jovinian is recorded as teaching is the single reward of all who keep their baptism, and “all sins are equal” is the label Jerome attached to that doctrine.

Held by: David G. Hunter (Catholic theology, Marriage, Celibacy, and Heresy in Ancient Christianity, 2007, ⚠ unverified);11 Hunter (Theological Studies, 1987);12 S. Troitsky (Russian Orthodox, Orthodox Theological Encyclopedia VII, 1906);13 G. Grützmacher (Protestant church historian, New Schaff-Herzog Encyclopedia VI, repr. 1953).14

Jerome sets out the four propositions before his reply and says he will treat the arguments Jovinian adduces “respecting marriage” “in the order in which he states them”; the fourth is “that there is one reward in the kingdom of heaven for all who have kept their baptismal vow.”2 The Milan synod, writing before Jerome’s book, charges Jovinian with seeking “to abolish the different degrees of merit” and with making Christ have “only one palm to bestow.”3 In II.21 Jerome places the Stoic turn “in the distribution of rewards and punishments” and draws “all sins are equal” from it. Hunter, as quoted by Ralston, reads the charge as Jerome’s attempt at “conjur[ing] up a philosophical pedigree for Jovinian’s position.”11

The arguments Jerome quotes from Jovinian are drawn from Scripture: Matthew 5:22, the labourers’ equal penny, the two classes of sheep and goats, and Christ’s indwelling — “As, then, there are not varying degrees of Christ’s presence in us, so neither are there degrees of our abiding in Christ” (II.19).15 Hunter (1987) reads the propositions as an anti-Manichean defence of baptism, which shaped Jovinian’s “view that the sacrament of baptism, and not ascetical merit, determined one’s reward in heaven”; Jovinian “emphasized strongly that there are only two classes of people, the good and the wicked, the sheep and the goats.”12 Troitsky derives the fourth thesis from the equal worth of marriage and virginity: all the regenerate who keep the grace of baptism, married or unmarried, inherit the kingdom alike, and there will be no difference in rewards and punishments between the two classes of the righteous and sinners.13 Grützmacher likewise treats the equal recompense as “a deduction from the parity of marriage and virginity,” and notes that Jovinian’s views “are known only through the writings of his opponents.”14 On this account the levelling Jovinian taught concerns the baptized and their reward: the grace of baptism, held in common by virgin, widow and wife, admits no ranks among those who keep it, and his propositions are framed in the terms of merit and reward.
