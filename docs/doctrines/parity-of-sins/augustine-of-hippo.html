Augustine of Hippo on the parity of sins

Dates: c. 415 (Letter 167) · Wikipedia
Summary: Augustine wrote a treatise-length letter to Jerome devoted entirely to James 2:10. He treats the parity thesis as a Stoic tenet that Jerome had answered, and asks what “guilty of all” means. His answer: to break any commandment is to act against the love on which all the commandments hang, so every sinner is guilty with respect to the whole; but he acts against it more who sins more gravely, so guilt is at once universal and graded.

Claims & evidence

The question as Augustine poses it

How, then, I beseech you, are we to understand the words: “Whosoever shall keep the whole law, and yet offend in one point, he is guilty of all”? Does this affirm that the person who shall have committed theft, nay, who even shall have said to the rich man, “Sit thou here” and to the poor man, “Stand thou there,” is guilty of homicide, and adultery, and sacrilege?

— Letter 167.3, source (also at New Advent). Augustine reads the verse in its context — the rich man’s gold ring and the poor man’s vile raiment of James 2:1–9 — so that the offence in view is the seating of the rich and the poor.

Augustine names the parity of sins a Stoic tenet, answered by Jerome

The doctrine of the equality of sins, however, the Stoics alone dared to maintain in opposition to the unanimous sentiments of mankind: an absurd tenet, which in writing against Jovinianus (a Stoic in this opinion, but an Epicurean in following after and defending pleasure) you have most clearly refuted from the Holy Scriptures.

— Letter 167.4, source. The letter is addressed to Jerome, and it names Jovinian as a Stoic in this opinion. In De haeresibus 82 (428) Augustine lists the parity of sins first among Jovinian’s tenets; whether Jovinian taught it is set out at did Jovinian teach that all sins are equal?

Augustine: the unity of the virtues would not entail the parity of sins

Even though it were true that he who has one virtue has all virtues, and that he who lacks one virtue has none, this would not involve the consequence that all sins are equal; for although it is true that where there is no virtue there is nothing right, it by no means follows that among bad actions one cannot be worse than another, or that divergence from that which is right does not admit of degrees.

— Letter 167.14, source. Augustine grants for the sake of argument the inseparability of the virtues and denies that the parity of sins follows, because vices are not connected as virtues are: two of them stand against each virtue (imprudence and craftiness against prudence, cowardice and hardihood against courage). He illustrates with Catiline, whose endurance of “cold, thirst, hunger” he calls hardihood rather than courage.

Virtue admits of degrees: the cavern, not the whirlpool

The way to wisdom is therefore not like that of a man rising from the water into the air, in which, in the moment of rising above the surface of the water, he suddenly breathes freely, but, like that of a man proceeding from darkness into light, on whom more light gradually shines as he advances.

— Letter 167.13, source. The Stoic image had been a drowning man who is equally drowned an inch or a mile below the surface — hence no degrees, and no partial wisdom. Augustine sets against it a man walking out of a cave, with light and darkness in the same soul in changing proportion, and summarises: “no one lives here wholly without sin — one sins more, another less, and the best is the man who sins least.”

Augustine reads “guilty of all” by the love on which the commandments hang

Hence it is true that if a man shall keep the whole law, and yet offend in one point, he becomes guilty of all, because he does what is contrary to the love on which hangs the whole law. A man, therefore, becomes guilty of all by doing what is contrary to that on which all hang.

Why, then, may not all sins be said to be equal? May not the reason be, that the transgression of the law of love is greater in him who commits a more grievous sin, and is less in him who commits a less grievous sin?

— Letter 167.16–17, source. Guilt is universal from the object violated (the love on which all the commandments depend) and graded by the degree of the violation. Augustine then reads James’s “mercy rejoiceth over judgment” as the daily remedy for daily sins — forgive, and be forgiven.

The sin unto death: hatred of the brotherhood

The “sin,” therefore, of a brother, “unto death,” I suppose to be when, after the acknowledging of God through the grace of our Lord Jesus Christ, one fights against the brotherhood, and is set on by the fire-brands of hatred against the very grace through which he was reconciled to God. But “a sin not unto death” is when a person, not having alienated his love from his brother, yet through some infirmity of mind may have failed to exhibit the due offices of brotherhood.

— Ten Homilies on the First Epistle of John 10 (c. 407), source. Augustine sorts 1 John’s two classes of sin by love: the deadly sin turns love into hatred. He later added a caution in his Retractations — the definition holds only “if he ends this life in a perversity of mind as wicked as this,” since “we must not despair of anyone, no matter how wicked he is, while he lives.”

Notes

  • Letter 167 travelled east with Letter 166 on the origin of the soul, carried by Orosius in 415. Jerome’s reply of 416 declined to answer either: see Jerome, and the Jerome–Augustine timeline for the correspondence as a whole.
  • Bede reproduces Augustine’s reading of “guilty of all” closely, and Theophylact also reads the verse by love.
  • Augustine is also a witness on the origin of the soul, purgatory and the perpetual virginity of Mary, among others.
