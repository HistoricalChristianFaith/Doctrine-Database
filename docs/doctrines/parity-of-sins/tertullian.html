Tertullian on the parity of sins

Dates: c. 210 · Wikipedia
Summary: In On Modesty Tertullian reads 1 John 5:16–17 as a two-class division of sins — remissible and irremissible — and uses it to argue that the church may not grant peace for the graver class. He grades sins, and he draws from the grading a limit on reconciliation.

Claims & evidence

Tertullian divides sins into the remissible and the irremissible

Touching this difference, we have not only already premised certain antithetical passages of the Scriptures, on one hand retaining, on the other remitting, sins; but John, too, will teach us: “If any knoweth his brother to be sinning a sin not unto death, he shall request, and life shall be given to him;” because he is not “sinning unto death,” this will be remissible. “(There) is a sin unto death; not for this do I say that any is to request” — this will be irremissible.

— On Modesty, source. On Modesty belongs to Tertullian’s Montanist period and is written against a bishop’s edict granting reconciliation to the sexually lapsed. Tertullian holds that because John withholds even prayer from the sin unto death, the church may withhold peace.

The prohibition on prayer, read with Jeremiah

For (in making these assertions) he was looking forward to the final clause of his letter, and for that (final clause) he was laying his preliminary bases; intending to say, in the end, more manifestly: “If any knoweth his brother to be sinning a sin not unto death, he shall make request, and the Lord shall give life to him who sinneth not unto death. For there is a sin unto death: not concerning that do I say that one should make request.” He, too, (as I have been), was mindful that Jeremiah had been prohibited by God to deprecate (Him) on behalf of a people which was committing mortal sins.

— On Modesty, source. Tertullian joins John’s sin unto death to the prohibition laid on Jeremiah’s intercession (Jeremiah 7:16): some sinners, in his reading, are beyond the reach of the church’s prayer.

Notes

  • Later witnesses on this timeline also grade sins but differ from Tertullian on reconciliation. Cyprian argues that the lapsed may be graded and received back; Ambrose charges the Novatianists, who refused reconciliation to the lapsed, with reasoning like Stoics who admit no grades of sin.
  • Augustine reads “the sin unto death” as hatred of the brotherhood, and adds in his Retractations that no one is to be despaired of while he lives.
  • In On Modesty Tertullian denies that the church can forgive the “irremissible” capital sins — adultery, fornication, idolatry, murder, apostasy. How that denial relates to the discipline of his age is the subject of the disputed reading “Was Tertullian’s denial of reconciliation for adultery and apostasy continuous with earlier penitential discipline, or a departure from it?” on the “Was Tertullian a Heretic?” timeline.
  • Tertullian is also a witness on the origin of the soul and purgatory.
