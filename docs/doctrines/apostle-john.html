John, son of Zebedee

John the son of Zebedee — the younger brother of James the Great, a Galilean fisherman, and, with Peter and his brother, one of the three disciples Jesus kept nearest him — is remembered in Christian tradition as “the disciple whom Jesus loved,” the apostle entrusted with the mother of Jesus at the cross, the exile of Patmos, and the last of the Twelve to die, an old man at Ephesus under the emperor Trajan. Later writers ascribed to him a Gospel, three epistles, and the Revelation, and told of his drinking poison unharmed and of his being plunged into boiling oil at Rome and delivered. This timeline sets out what the sources report about him, earliest extant source first: the New Testament notices, the Asian tradition of his old age, the legends of his deliverances, and the questions of authorship and death. Its “witnesses” are the datable sources that report on John. Two questions on which the sources have been read differently — whether he died at a great age at Ephesus or was killed, as a report ascribed to Papias says, and whether Papias's “John the elder” was a second John — have their own pages under Disputed readings. For the shared ladder of sources on the apostles, see the apostles hub.

Timeline

The New Testament (c. 30–95)

The New Testament gives John a fuller portrait than most of the Twelve. He is a fisherman of Galilee, son of Zebedee and younger brother of James, called with his brother from the nets and surnamed with him a “son of thunder”; with Peter and James he forms the inner three admitted to the raising of Jairus's daughter, the Transfiguration, and Gethsemane. The Fourth Gospel never names him, but calls one figure “the disciple whom Jesus loved,” who leans on Jesus's breast at the supper, stands by the cross where Jesus commits his mother to him, outruns Peter to the empty tomb, and is the one whose testimony the Gospel claims to rest on. In Acts he is Peter's constant companion — at the Beautiful Gate, before the Sanhedrin, and sent down to Samaria — and Paul names him with Peter and James a “pillar” of the Jerusalem church. Revelation opens with its author, “John,” exiled “in the isle that is called Patmos, for the word of God.”1

Papias of Hierapolis, preserved by Eusebius (c. 100–130)

Papias, bishop of Hierapolis and a hearer of the elders of Asia, is the earliest post-apostolic writer on this timeline to name John. In the one surviving sentence of his preface, he twice names a John: “what … John … said” among the apostles, and then “what things Aristion and the presbyter John, the disciples of the Lord,” are still saying. Eusebius read the wording as evidence of two Johns in Asia, an apostle and an “elder”; Irenaeus calls Papias “a hearer of John.” Two much later notices, an epitome ascribed to Philip of Side (5th c.) and a passage in the chronicle ascribed to George Hamartolus (9th c.), say that Papias's second book reported that John, like his brother James, “was killed by the Jews.” How the preface and the later report have been read is set out under Disputed readings.2

Irenaeus of Lyons (c. 180)

Irenaeus, who reports that he had heard Polycarp of Smyrna and that Polycarp had heard John, writes that John “the disciple of the Lord, who also had leaned upon His breast, did himself publish a Gospel during his residence at Ephesus in Asia,” and “remained among them up to the times of Trajan.” He also preserves, on Polycarp's authority, the story of John fleeing the bath-house at Ephesus rather than share it with Cerinthus, whom John, in the story, calls “the enemy of the truth.” Trajan reigned 98–117.3

The Acts of John (c. 150–200)

The apocryphal Acts of John — part of the “Leucian” corpus of apostolic romances, with encratite and docetic features — contains the most famous scenes: John raising the dead (Drusiana), wrecking the temple of Artemis at Ephesus by a word, and at the last his own peaceful “falling asleep” in a grave he digs himself. (The poisoned-cup ordeal, often pictured with John, is not in this work — it appears in later Latin and Byzantine sources and the Golden Legend.) The work was condemned at the Second Council of Nicaea (787). On the apocryphal Acts as a class of source, see the apostles hub.4

Clement of Alexandria, preserved by Eusebius (c. 200)

Clement, writing at Alexandria, also places John's old age at Ephesus. In the story of the young robber — “a tale, which is not a mere tale” — he has John return “after the tyrant's death” from Patmos to Ephesus and there travel the surrounding country, appointing bishops and, in one town, pursuing and reclaiming a young convert who had become a brigand. Clement names no emperor, but reports the exile-and-return and the aged John overseeing the Asian churches, and in the same story has the old man ride out after the young robber, “forgetting his age.”5

Tertullian (c. 200)

Tertullian, arguing that the great churches keep the apostles' own teaching, appeals to Rome, “where the Apostle John was first plunged, unhurt, into boiling oil, and thence remitted to his island-exile.” It is the earliest extant witness to the Roman boiling-oil story (later localized at the Latin Gate) and, in the same breath, to the Patmos exile — told as an ordeal survived.6

Victorinus of Pettau (c. 280)

Victorinus, in the earliest surviving commentary on Revelation, states that John saw the Apocalypse while “condemned to the labour of the mines by Cæsar Domitian” on Patmos, and published it “when grown old” after his release. Irenaeus (c. 180) had already dated what “was seen” to “towards the end of Domitian's reign”; whether the subject of that phrase is the vision or John is disputed (see the reading of Irenaeus's “was seen”). Victorinus's testimony is treated at length on the companion timeline for the date of Revelation (where he appears as a witness in his own right); here, his Domitianic dating of the exile belongs to the chronology of John's old age at Ephesus.7

Eusebius of Caesarea (c. 325)

Eusebius gathers the tradition into a single connected account: John banished to Patmos under Domitian, released under Nerva, and living on at Ephesus until Trajan; the governor of the Asian churches; the seer of Revelation (whose authorship, however, Eusebius is willing to assign to the “other” John). He quotes Irenaeus and Clement, and preserves Polycrates of Ephesus (c. 190), who lists among Asia's buried luminaries “John, who was both a witness and a teacher, who reclined upon the bosom of the Lord … He fell asleep at Ephesus.” And, reading Papias, Eusebius argues for two Johns and “two tombs in Ephesus, each … called John's.”8

Jerome (393)

Jerome, in On Illustrious Men, condenses the Greek tradition for the Latin West: John wrote his Gospel last, at the bishops' request, against Cerinthus and the Ebionites; “in the fourteenth year … after Nero, Domitian having raised a second persecution he was banished to the island of Patmos, and wrote the Apocalypse”; released after Domitian's death, he “returned to Ephesus,” and, “continuing there until the time of the emperor Trajan, founded and built churches throughout all Asia, and, worn out by old age, died in the sixty-eighth year after our Lord's passion and was buried near the same city.”9

The Golden Legend (c. 1260)

Jacobus de Voragine's Golden Legend gathers the medieval life: John summoned to Rome and cast into a cauldron of boiling oil before the Latin Gate, from which he came out unhurt; his exile to Patmos and the writing of the Apocalypse; the poisoned-cup challenge before the temple of Diana (Aristodemus's ordeal), which he drinks unharmed; the raising of the dead; and his tranquil death in a grave dug at his word, his body afterward not found. Compiled c. 1260, it gathers the boiling oil, the poisoned cup, and the peaceful death into a single story.10

Disputed readings

How the sources have been read on John's death and on Papias's two mentions of a John.

  • Did John die of old age at Ephesus, or was he killed, as a report ascribed to Papias says? — the second-century Asian witnesses beside the later report from Papias's second book.
  • Does Papias's preface name one John twice, or two different Johns? — with its bearing on the authorship of the Johannine writings.
