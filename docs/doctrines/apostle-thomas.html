Thomas

Thomas is the apostle to whom John’s Gospel gives three speaking scenes: the loyalty that says “Let us also go, that we may die with him,” the question “how can we know the way?” and the doubt that will not believe the resurrection until it touches the wounds, ending in the confession “My Lord and my God.” His name is a by-name: Thomas (Aramaic te’oma) and its Greek gloss Didymus both mean “the Twin,” and Syriac sources call him Judas Thomas. Beyond the New Testament, sources send him east: Origen’s notice assigns him Parthia, the Acts of Thomas sends him to India to a king, Gundaphar, whose name is also known from coins, and the Saint Thomas Christians of Kerala trace their founding to him. This timeline sets out what the sources report about Thomas, earliest extant source first: the Gospel notices, Origen’s allotment of Parthia, the Acts of Thomas, the Edessene cult attested by Ephrem and the pilgrim Egeria, the medieval legend, and modern scholarship. Whether he reached India, and whether he was the twin of Jesus, are disputed; each question has its own page under Disputed readings. Timeline

The New Testament (c. 30–90)

The New Testament names Thomas in all four lists of the Twelve; John’s Gospel gives him speaking scenes. When Jesus resolves to return to Judea though the Jews there had sought to stone him, it is Thomas who rallies the rest: “Let us also go, that we may die with him.” At the Last Supper he asks “Lord, we know not whither thou goest; and how can we know the way?” — drawing out the answer “I am the way, the truth, and the life.” And after the resurrection he is the disciple who was not there, who swears he will not believe “except I shall see in his hands the print of the nails… and thrust my hand into his side,” and who, when Jesus offers him exactly that, answers “My Lord and my God” — the episode that gave the language its “doubting Thomas.” Three times John calls him Didymus, “the Twin.” Of his life after Pentecost the New Testament says nothing.1

Origen, preserved by Eusebius: Parthia (c. 230, reported c. 325)

Among the earliest extant notices of where Thomas went is Origen’s, who, in a lost commentary on Genesis quoted by Eusebius, gives the apostles’ “division of the world”: “Parthia… was allotted to Thomas as his field of labor, Scythia to Andrew, and Asia to John.” It is a geographical notice without narrative, naming the Parthian empire of Persia. Eusebius elsewhere preserves a second early strand: the Edessene legend that “after the ascension of Jesus, Judas, who was also called Thomas, sent” the apostle Thaddaeus to King Abgar of Edessa, a notice linking Thomas with the Syriac East.2

The Acts of Thomas (c. 220–240)

The Acts of Thomas is the longest of the apocryphal Acts, and the earliest extant narrative of Thomas in India. Dated to the early third century, it survives in Syriac and Greek; M. R. James reports a “consensus of opinion among Syriac scholars” that the Greek is a version from Syriac, while James, following Max Bonnet, held it arguable that the Greek Martyrdom preserves the original Greek. It opens with the apostles dividing the world by lot: India falls to Thomas, who refuses until the risen Christ sells him as a slave-carpenter to a merchant buying for the Indian king Gundaphar. Commissioned to build the king a palace, Thomas gives the money to the poor and tells him he has raised a palace in heaven instead. The tale runs on through miracles and conversions to a martyrdom: under King Misdaeus, whose wife and son Thomas had baptized, the apostle is led to a hill and run through with spears by four soldiers. Its king Gundaphar is identified by Encyclopædia Iranica with Gondophares, an Indo-Parthian ruler unknown from any other literary source and recovered in the nineteenth century from coins and an inscription. Whether the Acts preserves a memory of a mission to India is disputed.3

Ephrem the Syrian and the shrine at Edessa (4th c.)

By the fourth century Thomas had a tomb-cult in the Syriac world. Ephrem the Syrian (c. 306–373), who lived out his last decade at Edessa, sings in his hymns of the apostle martyred in India whose bones a merchant carried back to Edessa — picturing the demons dismayed that the apostle they thought slain in India had come to trouble them at Edessa. An early Syriac martyrology fixes the memory: “3 July, St. Thomas who was pierced with a lance in India. His body is at Urhai [Edessa], having been brought there by the merchant Khabin. A great festival.” And about 384 the pilgrim Egeria, on her way through Mesopotamia, “straightway repaired to the church and memorial of Saint Thomas” at Edessa, “where his body is laid entire.” The relic and its shrine at Edessa are attested from Ephrem’s time.4

The Golden Legend (c. 1260)

Jacobus de Voragine’s Golden Legend gathers the medieval Thomas: the palace built in heaven for King Gundaphorus, the raising of the king’s dead brother who had seen it, the mission to a second Indian king Mesdeus, the idol shattered, and the martyrdom by the glaive of the bishop of the idol’s temple. Jacobus draws on the Acts and the apostle-lists; the Legend was widely read in the Latin West, and its builder story is the source of Thomas’s emblem, the carpenter’s square.5

Modern scholarship

The Mar Thoma or Saint Thomas Christians of Kerala, a community attested on the Malabar coast from an early date, trace their founding to Thomas’s landing at Muziris (Cranganore) in AD 52 and his martyrdom at Mylapore, near modern Chennai, about AD 72, with his tomb at San Thome. Encyclopædia Britannica’s article on the Thomas Christians states that the apostolic advent “cannot be verified.” Sean McDowell (2015), placing each apostle’s martyrdom account on a probability scale, rates Thomas’s death in India “more probable than not,” below his rating for the deaths of Peter, Paul, and James. Encyclopædia Iranica treats Gundaphar’s historicity, and A. E. Medlycott (1905) discusses the wide ancient sense of “India.”6

Disputed readings

How the sources for Thomas’s mission and his by-name “the Twin” have been read.

  • Did Thomas evangelize India? — Origen’s Parthia, the Acts of Thomas and its King Gundaphar, the fourth-century writers, and the Saint Thomas Christians of Kerala.7
  • Was Thomas the twin brother of Jesus? — the names Thomas, Didymus and Judas Thomas, and the Acts of Thomas and the Book of Thomas the Contender.8
