Are there works of supererogation? (precepts and the counsels of perfection)

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — the underlying precept–counsel distinction is well grounded and patristically near-universal (Paul himself marks 1 Cor 7:25 as counsel, not command; the fathers read Matt 19:21's “perfection” as a step beyond the commandments the young man already kept), but the further claim the treasury needs — that a counsel, once kept, banks a surplus of merit beyond the doer's own debt, poolable and transferable to others — outruns those texts and collides with Luke 17:10 (“we are unprofitable servants”); denied at the root by the Reformers, no clear winner · advanced by Thomas Aquinas, the Council of Trent, and the Catechism; rejected by Article XIV of the Thirty-Nine Articles.

Related arguments: surplus merit — see also Congruent and condign merit: do the justified truly merit their reward? · Does the Church hold a treasury of merit it can dispense?

The claim

“Besides its precepts, the New Law also includes the evangelical counsels. The traditional distinction between God's commandments and the evangelical counsels is drawn in relation to charity… The precepts are intended to remove whatever is incompatible with charity. The aim of the counsels is to remove whatever might hinder the development of charity, even if it is not contrary to it.”

— Catechism of the Catholic Church §1973, scborromeo.org. The assertion has two joined parts: (1) Scripture distinguishes precepts (binding on all, whose neglect is sin) from counsels of perfection (voluntary — poverty, chastity, obedience — whose neglect is no sin but whose keeping is a higher good); and (2) one who follows a counsel therefore does more than is required — a work of supererogation — whose surplus merit is real, and (in the treasury doctrine) bankable and dispensable to others. Part (1) is the load-bearing evidence; part (2) is the treasury-relevant conclusion. This page rates the whole inference, steelmanned; the neighbouring page on the dispensable treasury weighs whether that surplus, once granted, may be pooled and applied.

Who advances it

The precept–counsel distinction is not a late invention: it is worked from Paul's own language and is common patristic property (see below). The schoolmen systematized it — Thomas Aquinas defines the counsels precisely (“the difference between a counsel and a commandment is that a commandment implies obligation, whereas a counsel is left to the option of the one to whom it is given”)1 — and the Council of Trent anathematized denial of the celibate counsel's superiority (Session 24, canon 10: “If any one saith, that the marriage state is to be placed above the state of virginity, or of celibacy … let him be anathema”).2 The modern Catechism restates it (§§1973–1974). Its classic Protestant rejection is Article XIV of the Thirty-Nine Articles, “Of Works of Supererogation”: such works “cannot be taught without arrogancy and impiety,” since Christ said “when ye have done all … we are unprofitable servants” (Luke 17:10).3

The evidence for

Paul marks his own counsel off from a command. On virgins he writes: “Now concerning virgins I have no commandment of the Lord: yet I give my judgment” (1 Cor 7:25). The fathers seized on exactly this seam. Chrysostom notes that having trained the Corinthians in continence, Paul now “goes forth towards what is greater, saying, ‘I have no commandment, but I esteem it to be good.’”4 Augustine builds his whole treatise On Holy Virginity on the distinction: because “whosoever obeys not a command is guilty,” and no one incurs guilt by marrying, “there is no command of the Lord concerning virgins” — virginity is counselled, not enjoined, so “it is lawful to do, but it is better not to do.”5 Origen, commenting on the same verse, draws the sharp form: “Some rules are given as commandments of God, while others are more flexible and left by God to the decision of the individual … there is no merit in doing what is obligatory, but there is in doing that which is optional.”6

Matt 19:21 sets a “perfection” above the kept commandments. The rich young man has kept the commandments from his youth; only then does Christ say, “If thou wilt be perfect, go and sell that thou hast” — a further, optional step (“if thou wilt”) beyond the binding precepts, with its own reward (“thou shalt have treasure in heaven”). The fathers read it precisely as a counsel above the commandments. Jerome, against Vigilantius, grades the levels explicitly: to sell all at once is “the first” grade, retaining property and giving from income “the second or third … which we indeed admit, only remembering that what is first is to be set before what is third or second.”7 Augustine makes the same two-tier reading pastorally: the “greater commandments” are “sell everything that you have and give to the poor and follow me,” the “lesser” the Decalogue precepts — “If you are not willing to do the greater commandments, do the lesser ones.”8 Chrysostom treats the sale as a race with “prizes” and “crowns” Christ “leaves … to his own will,” i.e. an invited surplus, not a demanded minimum.9 Taken together, the distinction between command and counsel — and the reality of a reward attaching to the counsel freely kept — is well attested, from Origen (c. 240) through the Latin and Greek fathers, long before any treasury was defined.

Adversarial assessment

What holds: the distinction, and a reward to the doer. That Scripture distinguishes binding precepts from higher, non-binding counsels is not a Roman peculiarity but the plain reading of 1 Cor 7:25 (“no commandment … but I give my judgment”) and Matt 19:21 (“if thou wilt be perfect”), and it is held across the tradition (Origen, Chrysostom, Jerome, Augustine, Aquinas). That the one who keeps a counsel receives a special reward is likewise in the texts (“treasure in heaven”). So far the interpretation is strong; a flat denial that any act can be “beyond the minimum required of all” is harder to sustain than Article XIV's rhetoric suggests.

What is overstated: the leap from “counsel” to a bankable surplus of merit. The treasury needs more than a counsel-with-reward; it needs that keeping a counsel produces merit in excess of the doer's own debt to God — a surplus that can be pooled and dispensed to others. Three strains bear on that step.

(1) The texts give the doer a reward, not the Church a fund. Matt 19:21 promises the young man his own treasure in heaven; 1 Cor 7:25–40 counsels the individual to a better state. Neither says the merit of a supererogator overflows his own need and becomes transferable property of the Church. The move from “a counsel is rewarded” to “the counsel banks a surplus for others” is a theological construction laid over the texts, not their content — and it is the very move the dispensable-treasury page finds least warranted.

(2) Luke 17:10 presses the opposite way. “When ye shall have done all those things which are commanded you, say, We are unprofitable servants: we have done that which was our duty to do.” The patristic reading is uniform and pointed: even after complete obedience the creature has put God in no one's debt. Cyprian praises the lapsed who, though holding martyrs' certificates, “never made a boast of their labour to the Lord,” citing this very verse;10 Bede makes it a definition of mature faith — “when having done all things which were commanded them, they acknowledge themselves to be imperfect”; and Theophylact: “one must not be exalted if he fulfils the commandments, for he has done nothing great,” since a servant's whole office is “to carry out all his orders.”11 On that reading no work is super-erogatory, because none exceeds what is owed to an infinite Lord — the exact premise of Article XIV.

(3) The counsel, once undertaken, arguably becomes owed. The Catholic reply to Luke 17:10 is that the verse governs precepts (“things … commanded you”) and leaves the counsels, which are not commanded, untouched — so a counsel kept is genuinely beyond duty. This is coherent and is the strongest answer. But it is not decisive: the one who vows poverty or chastity has bound himself, and thereafter owes what he vowed, so the surplus he is credited with is one he now also owes — which is why the sharper Reformers judge the “surplus” illusory even granting the distinction. And the reply concedes the key point pressed on the treasury page: it does not show the merit transfers; it only defends that it exists.

The root denial. Article XIV and the Reformed tradition reject part (2) outright, not merely its transferability: to hold that a creature can do more than God requires “cannot be taught without arrogancy and impiety,” and “all our righteousnesses are as filthy rags” (Isa 64:6). The force of this is real against the merit-surplus claim; it is weaker against the bare precept–counsel distinction, which the Article's own proof-text (Luke 17:10, about things “commanded”) does not directly reach.

Assessment

Contested. Keep the two parts apart and the verdict is clean. The precept–counsel distinction — that Scripture marks off binding commands from voluntary counsels of higher perfection — is well grounded (Paul draws it himself at 1 Cor 7:25; Matt 19:21 sets a “perfection” above the kept commandments) and is patristically near-universal from Origen onward; on that narrow claim the interpretation is strong. The works-of-supererogation claim proper — that a counsel kept banks a surplus of merit beyond the doer's own debt, the surplus the treasury draws on — outruns those texts (which reward the doer, not the Church), collides with the uniform patristic reading of Luke 17:10, and is denied at the root by the Reformers. The Catholic reply (Luke 17:10 governs precepts, not counsels) is coherent and blunts the sharpest form of the objection, but it does not establish the surplus's transferability and is itself strained by the vow-makes-it-owed point. Strong as a distinction, genuinely disputed as a doctrine of surplus merit, with no clear winner: contested. (The onward question — whether such surplus, once granted, is poolable and dispensable — is weighed on the dispensable-treasury page, also contested.)
