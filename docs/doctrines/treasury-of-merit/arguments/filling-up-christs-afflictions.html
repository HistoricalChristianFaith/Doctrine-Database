Do the saints who “fill up what is lacking in Christ’s afflictions” (Colossians 1:24) contribute merit to the Church’s treasury?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: weak — as a proof-text for a treasury of merit the verse does not hold on its best case. The reading it is cited to serve — that the saints’ sufferings bank a transferable surplus the Church dispenses to remit the temporal punishment of others — runs against the near-unanimous ancient reading, which is that Paul speaks of his own ministerial sufferings as a member of Christ’s body, not of a fund supplementing a deficient Christ. Chrysostom, Theophylact, Augustine and Ambrosiaster all read it so; Aquinas himself calls the “supplement to an insufficient passion” sense heretical. The only treasury-link is a medieval Gloss Aquinas transmits, and even that speaks of the Body’s afflictions being completed at the end of the world, not of a dispensable bank of surplus merit · advanced by the scholastic treasury tradition (the Glossa Ordinaria, transmitted by Aquinas).

Related arguments: surplus merit — see also Does the Church hold a treasury of merit it can dispense? and Are there works of supererogation? (the two joints this proof-text is adduced to support), and, on the neighbouring indulgences timeline, the power of the keys.

The claim

“afflictions are still lacking, because the treasure house of the Church’s merits is not full, and it will not be full until the end of the world.”

— the Glossa Ordinaria on Colossians 1:24, as quoted by Thomas Aquinas, Commentary on Colossians, historicalchristian.faith. The proof-text is Paul’s word to the Colossians: “Who now rejoice in my sufferings for you, and fill up that which is behind of the afflictions of Christ in my flesh for his body’s sake, which is the church” (Colossians 1:24, KJV). Read into a doctrine of the treasury, the verse is taken to teach that the sufferings of the saints are added to Christ’s own to constitute the fund of satisfaction on which the Church draws — the saints’ contribution to the treasure whose Christological core is defined in Clement VI’s Unigenitus (1343).

Who advances it

The verse is a stock proof-text of the developed treasury theology: because a treasury needs the saints’ merits as well as Christ’s, Colossians 1:24 — the one text in which an apostle says his own sufferings make up something “lacking” to Christ’s — is the natural anchor for the saints’ share. The linkage is explicit in the Glossa Ordinaria (“the treasure house of the Church’s merits is not full”), which Aquinas quotes at the close of his comment; later Catholic controversialists (Bellarmine and after) press the same text against the Reformers. The Catechism’s treasury paragraphs (§§1476–1477) keep the underlying idea — the treasury includes “the prayers and good works… of the Blessed Virgin Mary and… all the saints” — though they no longer hang it on this verse.

The evidence for

The verse really does say the members’ sufferings profit the Body. Paul does not call his afflictions merely his own: they are “the afflictions of Christ,” borne “for his body’s sake, which is the church.” The communion-of-saints premise the treasury needs — that the suffering of one member benefits the whole body — is genuinely in the text, and the fathers affirm it. Augustine makes the head-and-members point directly: Paul “said not, of my afflictions, but of Christ’s; for he was a member of Christ… he also was filling up his own share of His afflictions.”1 The scholastic step is only to generalize: if Paul’s sufferings tell for the Body, so do every saint’s, and their sum is a treasure.

Aquinas gives the reading its strongest, guarded form. He anchors it in the one mystical person: “Christ and the Church are one mystical person, whose head is Christ, and whose body is all the just… God in his predestination has arranged how much merit will exist throughout the entire Church, both in the head and in the members… And among these merits, the sufferings of the holy martyrs occupy a prominent place.” It is on the back of this that he quotes the Gloss: the afflictions are “lacking” because “the treasure house of the Church’s merits is not full, and it will not be full until the end of the world.”2 On this account the verse and the treasury cohere.

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The communion-of-saints core is sound and not peculiar to Rome: the sufferings of a member of Christ’s body are borne “for his body’s sake,” and so profit the whole. That much the fathers grant with one voice, and the treasury shares it with the whole Church. (That premise is weighed on its own on the dispensable-treasury page.)

What the proof-text will not carry. Three things the treasury reading needs are absent from the verse and denied by its earliest interpreters.

(1) The subject is Paul’s own sufferings, not a pooled surplus. The ancient reading is remarkably uniform: Paul rejoices to bear, in his own flesh, the measure of affliction that still falls to Christ’s members. Chrysostom is emphatic that this is no claim of a merit-supply: “It seems… to be a great thing he has said; but it is not of arrogancy… for he will not have the sufferings to be his own, but His.” The point is that Christ, in his body, still suffers — “what things He should have suffered, I suffer instead of Him” — the way a lieutenant takes the wounds of an absent commander “until the battle were brought to a close.”3 Theophylact repeats it almost word for word: Paul says this “not to exalt himself, but out of a desire to show that Christ even now still cares for them,” suffering in Paul’s flesh.4 Ambrosiaster reads the sufferings as the endurance a preacher meets in evangelizing.5 None of them finds a transferable stock of surplus merit; they find the head-and-members mystery.

(2) “Lacking” is the Body’s remaining afflictions, not a shortfall in Christ’s satisfaction. Aquinas flags precisely the misreading the treasury proof-text risks: “At first glance these words can be misunderstood to mean that the passion of Christ was not sufficient for our redemption, and that the sufferings of the saints were added to complete it. But this is heretical, because the blood of Christ is sufficient to redeem many worlds.” What is “lacking” is “what is lacking in the afflictions of the whole Church, of which Christ is the head” — the measure of suffering the Body has yet to pass through in history, not a deficit the saints make good.2 The Gloss’s “treasure house… not full… until the end of the world” is, on Aquinas’s own gloss, this eschatological completion of the Body’s afflictions — an accumulation over time, not a fund the Church quantifies and disburses now to remit a particular soul’s temporal debt.

(3) Nothing in the verse is transferable or dispensable. Even granting the saints’ sufferings “profit the Body,” the verse says nothing of banking one person’s excess merit, nor of the Church applying it to another, nor of remitting temporal punishment, nor of reaching the dead — every distinctive move the treasury needs. Those are supplied by the doctrine of supererogation and the power of the keys, weighed on their own pages; Colossians 1:24 adds only the communion-of-saints premise the treasury already had. The Reformation reply presses exactly this gap: read plainly, the text is about the apostle’s own share in Christ’s sufferings for the church he serves, and the fathers’ own exegesis — not merely a Protestant one — makes the “merit-bank” sense a strain the verse was never asked to bear.

Assessment

Weak. As a proof-text for a treasury of the saints’ transferable surplus merit, Colossians 1:24 fails on its best available case. Its uncontested content — that a member’s sufferings are borne “for his body’s sake” and profit the whole — is the communion-of-saints premise the treasury already shares with the whole Church, and it is granted here. But the specific machinery the verse is cited to prove (a quantified, transferable, dispensable fund that remits temporal punishment) is absent from the text and from its earliest readers, and the one thing the fathers agree it does not teach — that the saints supply something “lacking” to Christ’s sufficient passion — is the very inference the treasury proof-text invites. Aquinas himself calls that inference heretical and reads “lacking” of the Body’s afflictions across history, not of a merit-shortfall. The treasury doctrine may stand or fall on other grounds (weighed as contested on the dispensable-treasury page); it gains little from this verse.
