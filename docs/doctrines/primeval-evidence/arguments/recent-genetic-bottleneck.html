A recent eight-person bottleneck predicts a detectable genetic signature — is it there?

Presupposition: inherited from The Flood and the Nephilim in the Material Record — that Noah’s Flood happened, destroying all humanity outside the ark, and that the Nephilim were real. This page works inside that presupposition: its assessment weighs a model of the event or a trace of it, not whether it happened.

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: weak — as the genetic signature of the young literal-eight model, the Flood placed a few thousand years ago on either the Masoretic or the Septuagint reckoning. Granting that every living human descends from the eight on the ark, the genome has to bear the mark of that descent: no more than ten alleles at any autosomal locus, four maternal lineages and one paternal one at the moment the ark emptied. The mark of a recent crash is not there. Coalescence depths across the genome run to hundreds of thousands of years, and no collapse to a founding handful appears in the recent record. That does not leave the presupposition without a reading of the genome; it leaves an open puzzle about when the bottleneck happened and how the record reached its present depth, and several resolutions have been proposed. Weighed on their evidence, the one the data currently favour is an older Flood — still anthropologically universal, but placed far back in an open genealogy, at least some 180,000 years ago on the standard genetic clocks and probably earlier. It is the only proposal that keeps the whole presupposition and reads the genome on its ordinary clocks, and it pays for that in the text, which has to absorb a gap of that size. A sharply faster mutation clock is the one route that would rescue a young date; it rests on a contested reading of measured rates and is a minority view even among young-earth geneticists. Diversity front-loaded into Adam and Eve does not reach past the Flood, because five founders can carry only what five people carry. And the genealogical-ancestry model resolves the puzzle only by letting people outside the ark survive, which leaves the presupposition rather than resolving a puzzle inside it.

Related arguments: open puzzles for the presupposition — see also the predictive search for Nephilim remains · authenticated remains at Nephilim scale. On the Flood's date, which the leading resolution here turns on, see the dating of Noah's Flood; on its extent, the extent question.

What the presupposition implies for the genome

Genesis 7:13 names the passengers: Noah, his wife, his three sons, and their three wives — eight people, and the text is emphatic that they were all. If all humanity outside the ark perished, then every human being alive today descends from those eight and from no one else, and the genetic consequences are not vague. The three sons carry only their parents' alleles, so the founding pool is five genetically distinct individuals: Noah, his wife, and three daughters-in-law. That caps the whole species, at the moment the ark emptied, at ten alleles at any autosomal locus, four mitochondrial lineages (the four women), and one Y-chromosome lineage (Noah's, carried by all three sons). Everything human beings now carry beyond that ceiling must have arisen from it by mutation and recombination since.

Two of these consequences hold whatever date the Flood is given. Every living man inherits his Y chromosome from Noah, so the most recent common paternal ancestor of living men can be no older than Noah himself; and every population alive today descends from the ark's family, so the deepest genetic split among living populations must open after the Flood, not before it. The date of the Flood is therefore not free. It has to sit at least as deep as those two horizons. That is the presupposition's own arithmetic, and it is what makes the genetic record bear on it directly.

Placed a few thousand years ago, as in the young literal-eight model, the expectation becomes sharp: modern human genomes should show an abrupt, datable coalescence crash — nearly all lineages converging onto a handful of ancestral haplotypes at a horizon a few thousand years back, with a correspondingly abrupt collapse of linkage disequilibrium and allele counts across the genome. It is a risky, quantitative expectation, and the methods built for exactly this question can check it.

What the young model needs the genome to show

The young model needs the times to most recent common ancestry across many independent loci to fall within a few thousand years, and a genome-wide signature of a severe recent contraction to be recoverable. It fails if many loci coalesce far deeper than that, and it fails in particular if some locus can be shown to carry more distinct lineages, of greater age, than five founders could have transmitted in the time allowed — since that is an arithmetic contradiction rather than a probabilistic one.

It is worth saying plainly that this is not an impossible demand: severe bottlenecks are detectable and have been detected. The out-of-Africa founder effect is written across non-African genomes as reduced diversity and long haplotype blocks; the founder events in Ashkenazi, Finnish and Icelandic populations are recoverable in detail. The instrument works. The question is only what it registers at the proposed date.

The strongest case for the young signature

The mitochondrial and Y-chromosome data have the right shape, if not the right scale. All living human mitochondrial lineages do coalesce to one woman, and all Y lineages to one man — the "Mitochondrial Eve" and "Y-chromosome Adam" of the literature. That is a real and striking result and it is what the model expects qualitatively. Nathaniel Jeanson has pressed the quantitative form of it, arguing from directly measured (pedigree) mitochondrial mutation rates rather than fossil-calibrated ones, and getting a mitochondrial coalescence far younger than the conventional figure.1

And the standard rebuttal has been overstated, which the argument is entitled to point out. For years the reply of choice was that population genetics shows human numbers never fell below about ten thousand, so a founding couple or a founding eight is excluded outright. Richard Buggs, an evolutionary biologist, challenged the inference on method: the estimators involved measure long-term effective population size and assume relative stability over many generations, and are therefore poorly suited to detecting a short, sharp crash followed by rapid recovery. The exchange that followed was long and public, and it ended with the critic largely vindicated on the narrow point — Joshua Swamidass's analysis put the boundary at the "time to most recent four alleles" (TMR4A), a median of roughly 500,000 years, meaning a bottleneck of two is excluded by genetics only within that window and not before. BioLogos subsequently revised its position, and the articles making the stronger claim were withdrawn. The argument's complaint that it was met with more confidence than the data supported is justified.2

A second overreach on the same side is worth recording, because it went the same way. Francisco Ayala argued in 1995 from the extreme polymorphism of the immune-system HLA-DRB1 locus that its lineages coalesce some 60 million years ago, before the ape–monkey split, and that human ancestral populations therefore can never have been smaller than a few thousand. That argument was answered within genetics: Bergström, Josefsson, Erlich and Gyllensten reported in 1998 that the DRB1 alleles are of recent origin, undercutting the old trans-species coalescence dates the minimum-population inference rested on. A page that leaned on Ayala here would be leaning on a prop that has already broken.3

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The universal mitochondrial and Y coalescences are real. The methodological critique of the "never below 10,000" claim is correct and was conceded. The window in which a two-person bottleneck is genetically excluded is bounded, and the boundary is around half a million years, not the whole of human history. Anyone repeating the older, harder claim is repeating something that has been withdrawn.

But every one of those concessions is about a date the young model does not want. This is the decisive point and it should not be softened. TMR4A of roughly 500,000 years is not a licence for a recent bottleneck; it is the opposite. It says that if a founding pair existed they must be placed before that horizon, because any more recent date would require them to have carried more alleles than two people can carry. Eight passengers relax the constraint — ten alleles rather than four, four maternal lineages rather than one — and the corresponding boundary for ten alleles is younger, but still on the order of a couple of hundred thousand years (see the deep-date resolution below), against a young model that places its founders a few thousand years ago. The concession the argument won runs directly against the young date.2

The mitochondrial and Y coalescences are also far too old for a young date. Applying the same methods to both, the Y-chromosome common ancestor falls at roughly 120,000–156,000 years and the mitochondrial one at 99,000–148,000; the rare, deep-rooting A00 Y lineage discovered in 2013 pushes the Y root further back — first estimated at 338,000 years, and revised on reanalysis to about 208,000 (95% interval 164,000–260,000). None of these lands near a few thousand.4 The pedigree-mutation-rate argument that would compress them is the pivot of the young case and it is where the case is weakest. Pedigree rates measured over one or two generations include transient heteroplasmies that do not fix, and applying them without correction across thousands of generations underestimates ages — the discrepancy between pedigree and phylogenetic mitochondrial rates is a known problem in the field with a known direction. The two independent calibrations that avoid fossils altogether agree with the conventional clock rather than the compressed one: an Icelandic study measured the Y-chromosome point-mutation rate directly across 1,365 father-to-son transmissions in deep genealogies and obtained 8.71 × 10−10 per site per year, the same order as the 0.82 × 10−9 on which the conventional Y dates above rest; and a mitochondrial clock calibrated on ten securely dated ancient modern-human genomes spanning 40,000 years reproduced the earlier divergence times.5 And even if the mitochondrial clock were recalibrated, mitochondria are one non-recombining locus. The autosomal genome contains tens of thousands of independent histories, and it is those, not mtDNA, that carry the arithmetic ceiling.

The one dramatic bottleneck recently reported is the wrong bottleneck, and it is disputed. Honesty requires both halves. Wangjie Hu and colleagues inferred in Science in 2023 a severe ancestral crash to roughly 1,280 breeding individuals lasting some 117,000 years, at c. 930,000–813,000 years ago. That is a striking result and it is sometimes reached for as support. It is not support for a young eight: it is about a thousand times older than the young model's date and a hundred times larger than its founding group — a different event of a different kind. And it may not be an event at all. Yuxin Deng, Rasmus Nielsen and colleagues argue it is likely a statistical artifact, noting that no comparable bottleneck appears in non-African populations, and a second group reproduced the signal with the original method but not with an independent method on the same data. An argument that cites Hu is citing something contested, and contested in a direction that does not help it.6

And the one popular bottleneck of roughly the right magnitude did not survive scrutiny either — a case worth recalling as calibration. Before the Hu result, the go-to "near-extinction" datum was the Toba super-eruption of about 74,000 years ago in Sumatra, the largest explosive eruption of the Quaternary, which Stanley Ambrose proposed in 1998 had thrown the world into a volcanic winter and crashed the human population to a few thousand — low enough, he argued, to drive rapid racial differentiation. For a while this was cited as the human bottleneck, and it is instructive here precisely because it failed on evidence the young model's own defenders would grant is fair. Archaeologically, Michael Petraglia and colleagues recovered Middle Palaeolithic tool assemblages in continuity above and below the Toba ash at Jwalapuram in southern India — people living on both sides of the layer, not extinguished by it. Environmentally, subdecadal phytolith and charcoal records from Lake Malawi show no volcanic winter and no vegetation collapse in East Africa after the eruption. Genetically, no bottleneck coincident with 74 kya can be recovered at all; the genuine Late Pleistocene contraction is the later out-of-Africa founder effect, a different event of a different date. The Toba bottleneck is now widely rejected. The lesson for this page: a proposed crash to a few thousand at 74,000 years is already more than ten times too old and far too shallow for a Holocene eight, and even that claim did not hold up — which is what it looks like when a bottleneck signature is searched for and is not there.7

The undisputed part is the negative, and the negative is what the page's question asks about. Across all of this dispute — the ten-thousand claim, the HLA claim, the TMR4A boundary, the Hu bottleneck and its critics — nobody on any side of the mainstream literature reports a crash to a founding handful within the last several thousand years. That is not a gap in the search; it is the one thing the search agrees on.

What this does and does not touch. Inside the presupposition, the result is not that the bottleneck did not happen — on the presupposition it did, because every living human descends from the ark. The result is that its signature does not sit where the young model puts it. That turns the question from whether to when and by what mechanism the present depth of human variation arose from five founders, and it is the subject of the next section. It says nothing about the antiquity of the flood tradition itself, which rests on the cuneiform record, not on genomes.

Resolutions proposed inside the presupposition

The tension is real and it is stated above without softening: five founders, and a genome whose lineages coalesce far deeper than a few thousand years. Four families of resolution have been seriously advanced. They are weighed here on their evidence, with one question put to each: does it resolve the puzzle while keeping the whole presupposition — a Flood that left no human survivors outside the ark — or does it resolve it only by giving part of that up?

A Septuagint date: older, but not older enough

The Septuagint's long chronology moves the Flood from about 2348 BC to about 3300 BC, and this wiki weighs it at the Septuagint long chronology. Genetically it buys roughly a thousand years against a discrepancy measured in hundreds of thousands; it changes nothing in the coalescence arithmetic above. Ancient DNA sharpens the point. Genome-wide data from forty-four ancient Near Easterners spanning roughly 12,000 to 1,400 BC — from Natufian hunter-gatherers to Bronze Age farmers — show strongly differentiated local populations, the first farmers of the southern Levant and of the Zagros each descending from local hunter-gatherers and only later mixing with one another. Those samples straddle both the Masoretic and the Septuagint horizons in the very region the narrative concerns, and show continuity across them rather than replacement by a single family's descendants.8 That reading depends on the radiocarbon dates of the samples, whose security is weighed at the calibration spine and are these witnesses securely dated? — but on those dates, a Septuagint Flood fares no better genetically than a Masoretic one.

An older Flood in an open genealogy

The textual licence. In 1890 William Henry Green argued that the genealogies of Genesis 5 and 11, like later biblical genealogies, may skip generations, and concluded that "the Scriptures furnish no data for a chronological computation prior to the life of Abraham." His proposal, backed by B. B. Warfield, became widely accepted among conservative scholars. It has been answered on the text: Jeremy Sexton argues that even if the genealogies skip names, each begetting age still fixes the interval to the birth of the named descendant, so that "we must accept the chronological intent of Gen 5 and 11." The licence is real but disputed, and it was never argued for gaps of the size the genome would ask of it.9

The proposal. The fullest version is that of Reasons to Believe, an old-earth ministry that holds the Flood was geographically local but anthropologically universal — confined to the region humanity then occupied, but destroying, in Hugh Ross's words, "the entire 'world' of ungodly people." Ross dates it to "about 50,000 +/- 40,000 years ago," reasoning that it must precede the human settlement of Europe, Asia and Australia, and so "most probably earlier than 45,000 years ago."10 That is the right kind of resolution: it keeps the presupposition whole and reads the genome on its ordinary clocks.

How far back it has to go. The genome sets the floor, and it sits deeper than Ross's window. Applying the same analysis to the ark's ten possible alleles, Swamidass reports a genome-wide median "time to most recent ten alleles" (TMR10A) of about 180,000 years, and concludes that "apart from miraculous intervention or interbreeding between Noah's lineage and others," it rules out a Noahic bottleneck more recent than that. He reports that Reasons to Believe used the TMR10A figure to refine its model and "places Noah at about 180,000 years ago"; Ross's own published dates — 50,000 ± 40,000 in 2021, "earlier than 45,000" in 2023 — sit inside the window the ten-allele floor excludes.11 The presupposition's own two horizons point the same way or deeper. Since the paternal common ancestor of living men can be no older than Noah, the Flood must be at least as old as the Y root — about 120,000–156,000 years on the main tree and about 208,000 once the A00 lineage is included.4 And since every living population descends from the ark, the Flood must also precede the deepest split among living humans, which ancient southern African genomes place between 350,000 and 260,000 years ago.12 On the standard clocks, then, an anthropologically universal Flood belongs somewhere before about 200,000–260,000 years ago.

Who counts as humanity. One further question sets the depth, and the presupposition leaves it open. Neanderthals and Denisovans interbred with the ancestors of living people, and the population split between them and modern humans is estimated at 553,000–589,000 years ago (550,000–765,000 by a second method), with the authors cautioning that such split times are "at the best approximate."13 If they were human and all humanity outside the ark perished, they too descend from the ark, and the Flood moves past half a million years. The two sides that hold a universal Flood answer differently. Reasons to Believe treats Neanderthals as non-image-bearing hominins and concedes that interbreeding with them "does stand as a failed prediction" of its model, requiring revision; a young-earth paper in the Answers Research Journal instead counts Neanderthals and Denisovans as Noah's descendants, arguing that their Y chromosomes "descended from Noah's Y chromosome in the line of Ham" — which keeps them human but needs the compressed clock discussed below.14

How it fares. This is the resolution the genetic evidence currently favours: it is the only one that keeps the whole presupposition and accepts the coalescence data as measured. Its costs are not genetic but textual and internal. It needs a gap between Noah and Abraham of the order of two hundred thousand years, perhaps more than half a million — roughly a hundredfold beyond what the written genealogies record, and far past what Green's gaps were argued for. And it outruns its own leading proponents, whose published date sits well inside the window the genome excludes. As a resolution it is coherent and not ruled out; it is not yet a worked-out model that its advocates have aligned with the genetic floor.

Diversity created into the founders

The proposal. A second family of resolutions holds that the first humans were not genetically uniform. John Sanford, Robert Carter and colleagues argue that Adam and Eve were created "massively heterozygous" with designed diversity, and simulate two such founders yielding allele-frequency distributions of the modern kind.15 Ola Hössjer and Ann Gauger model a single founding couple against the allele frequency spectrum and linkage disequilibrium of present-day genomes, and report that a couple with "primordial diversity" fits the data at about 500,000 years ago (about two million without it), suggesting that refinements might bring it to "100kya, or more recently"; they note that a more recent couple produced the largest mismatch in the rare-variant end of the spectrum.16 Anjeanette Roberts of Reasons to Believe has proposed a "mosaic Eve," created with gametes of differing genotypes, while conceding that "current computational modeling doesn't support a founding couple (or a bottleneck of two) at an intermediate date."17

How it fares here. These proposals answer a question about Adam and Eve, and they answer it seriously — Hössjer and Gauger's result is part of why the old "never below 10,000" claim fell. But they do not reach past the Flood. However rich the diversity created into the first couple, the ark carried five genomes, and five people transmit at most ten alleles at a locus, four mitochondrial lineages and one Y chromosome. Everything beyond that ceiling in living people must have arisen after the Flood, which is why Swamidass's ark-specific figure is the ten-allele one and not the four-allele one. Created diversity can explain the frequencies among the founders' alleles; it cannot supply the post-Flood depth. Even on its own terms, the best fit Hössjer and Gauger report is half a million years old, not a few thousand. The one extension that would make front-loading reach the Flood — the ark's founders themselves given diversity beyond what five people normally carry — is logically possible, and this wiki does not rule it out because it would be a miracle. But no text reports such an act and no proponent has modelled it, and a founding group so endowed no longer predicts a genetic signature at all; it explains why there is none. That move is weighed in general at mature creation and apparent age. Possibility is not proof.

A faster mutation clock

The proposal. The only resolution that keeps a young date is to compress the clock. Nathaniel Jeanson and Ashley Holland argue that pedigree-based Y-chromosome mutation rates from high-coverage sequencing are roughly ten to seventeen times higher than the rates used in mainstream studies — about three mutations per generation — and that at that rate the human Y tree records "a 4,500-Year History for Human Paternal Inheritance," with the major branches laid down in the first generations after the Flood; Jeanson pairs this with his mitochondrial clock.18 It is the strongest form the young model can take, because it makes risky predictions about branch structure and population history, and Jeanson holds that they are being borne out.

How it fares. The rate is the whole case, and it is contested on two sides. Against it, the largest direct measurement — 1,365 father-to-son transmissions in Icelandic deep pedigrees — yields a rate consistent with the conventional clock, and the ancient-DNA mitochondrial calibration agrees with it independently of fossils.5 Jeanson answers that those measurements rest on low-coverage sequencing that misses real mutations; that reply is a live technical claim, but it has been made in a young-earth journal and has not persuaded the field. Within young-earth genetics itself, Robert Carter has questioned the constancy the clock assumes, arguing that "patriarchal drive is a strong mutational force" that would make branches grow at different rates, so that dates read off them "could easily be inflated"; Jeanson has replied at length.18 And a fast Y clock is not enough on its own: the same compression has to account for the autosomal ten-allele depth, the mitochondrial calibration on dated ancient genomes, and the ancient Near Eastern population continuity noted above, each of which leans on a different method. As a resolution it is coherent and still argued; on present evidence it is weak, resting on one disputed reading of the measured rates.

Genealogical rather than genetic ancestry

The proposal. S. Joshua Swamidass's The Genealogical Adam and Eve (2019) distinguishes genealogical ancestry from genetic ancestry: a couple living a few thousand years ago in the Near East can be a genealogical ancestor of everyone alive without being anyone's sole genetic source, because their descendants intermarried with people outside their line. It is a genuine and carefully argued result for Adam and Eve, and it has been offered as a way to keep a recent historical pair without a recent genetic bottleneck.

How it fares here. It does not resolve the puzzle inside this presupposition; it departs from it. The genealogical model works only because a population outside the line survives and interbreeds with it. An anthropologically universal Flood removes that population: if everyone outside the ark perished, the ark's eight are everyone's genealogical ancestors and everyone's genetic ancestors, and the distinction collapses onto the same five genomes. Swamidass draws the consequence himself. His own exception to the ten-allele floor is "interbreeding between Noah's lineage and others,"11 and in the book he is reported to conclude that, since human ancestry never dips to five people in the last hundred thousand years, the Flood was regional and "did not destroy the people outside the garden" ⚠ unverified.19 That is a coherent position, and the wiki weighs the question of the Flood's human extent at global vs. anthropological extent. But it is a Flood with human survivors outside the ark, and so it is a departure from the presupposition this page holds, not a resolution of the puzzle within it.

Assessment

Weak — as the genetic signature of the young literal-eight model. Granting the presupposition, the model's genetics are admirably precise: five distinct founders, ten alleles per autosomal locus, four maternal lineages, one paternal, a few thousand years ago, on the Masoretic or the Septuagint reckoning alike. What the genome shows instead is coalescence depths two orders of magnitude older, Y and mitochondrial roots far older than the young date, and no recent crash to a founding handful anywhere in the data.

Two things must be conceded on the way, and neither changes the verdict. The "human numbers never fell below ten thousand" reply was overstated, was challenged on sound methodological grounds by Buggs, and was withdrawn; Ayala's HLA argument for a minimum population was answered within genetics in 1998. But the boundaries that replaced them — TMR4A at about half a million years for a couple, TMR10A at about 180,000 for the ark's five — exclude a small founding group in exactly the window the young model needs. And the one severe bottleneck genetics has lately reported is a thousand times too old, a hundred times too large, and disputed as an artifact besides.

That leaves a genuine open puzzle inside the presupposition, not a closed one. Of the proposed resolutions, the evidence currently favours an older, anthropologically universal Flood placed deep in an open genealogy: it alone keeps the whole presupposition and reads the genome as measured, at the price of a Flood at least some 200,000 years old on the standard clocks and a genealogical gap of matching size, which the text's critics contest and which its own leading advocates have not yet adopted. A faster mutation clock is the only way to keep a young date and is weak on present evidence. Created diversity in Adam and Eve is a serious answer for the first couple but does not reach past the Flood without a further, unattested act. The genealogical-ancestry model resolves nothing here, because it needs the survivors outside the ark that the presupposition excludes.

One caveat is owed on the other side and is stated rather than buried: molecular-clock dating is the most assumption-laden method this page leans on, resting on a mutation rate, a generation time and a demographic model, with correspondingly wide intervals. That is weighed at are these witnesses securely dated? — where the openness is conceded and found not to rescue the young date, since the disputed figure runs two to three orders of magnitude in the wrong direction. It is also why the depth the older-Flood resolution needs is stated as an order of magnitude rather than a date.
