A recent single global Flood predicts a synchronous worldwide sediment horizon — is there one?

Presupposition: inherited from The Flood and the Nephilim in the Material Record — that Noah’s Flood happened, covering the whole earth and destroying all humanity outside the ark, and that the Nephilim were real. This page works inside that presupposition: its assessment weighs a model of the event or a trace of it, not whether it happened.

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: weak — as a prediction that a recent, single-date Flood covering the whole earth left one synchronous worldwide sediment horizon. Granting that the waters covered the whole earth, the rocks should hold their deposit, and the young model's prediction of one thin synchronous horizon is a checkable one, open to tools that demonstrably work: the one genuinely global catastrophe geologists have looked for in this way, the end-Cretaceous impact, was found exactly as predicted, as a thin synchronous layer traceable on every continent. No such Flood horizon is there. That leaves an open puzzle inside the presupposition rather than a settled one, and several resolutions have been proposed. The strongest is flood geology's reading of the whole sedimentary column, from the Great Unconformity up through Sloss's six continent-scale megasequences, as the Flood's deposit rather than a layer within it. It engages the record at full scale and has done real work, but it does not yet discharge the tension: its onset surface is contested as diachronous, its matching of megasequences between continents borrows the correlation framework whose time-values it rejects, its answers to the column's markers of long quiet intervals are partial, and its advocates do not agree where the deposit ends. Catastrophic plate tectonics supplies an engine but carries an acknowledged heat problem. An older Flood date does not help, because a Flood that covered the whole earth needs a worldwide deposit at whatever date it is placed. A regional Flood would remove the puzzle, but only by leaving the presupposition.

Related arguments: open puzzles for the presupposition — see also single-point post-Flood faunal radiation · a recent eight-person bottleneck · the predictive search for Nephilim remains. On how the tradition itself has read the Flood's extent, see global vs. anthropological extent on the historicity timeline.

The prediction

The presupposition holds that the Flood covered the whole earth. The model of that event that makes the sharpest physical prediction is the young single-date one — a single deluge within the last several thousand years, on the Masoretic or the Septuagint reckoning, which destroyed the land life of the earth and then drained. Such an event moves an enormous quantity of sediment and drops it. It should therefore have left one contemporaneous deposit, recognisable on every continent, occupying the same position in every sequence: a worldwide horizon with the same event on both sides of it — a world before, a world after.

This is a genuinely risky prediction, and it is the reason this page exists. It is not a vague expectation that "there should be lots of sedimentary rock." There are enormous quantities of sedimentary rock, and no model is distinguished by pointing at it. The prediction has teeth because it specifies synchrony: not many flood deposits at many dates, which is what a planet with rivers produces anyway, but one deposit at one date everywhere. The open date does not soften it. A Flood that covered the whole earth at any date must have left its deposit somewhere in the record; the date decides only where to look, a point taken up among the resolutions below.

What would count against the prediction — committed in advance

The prediction fails if the sedimentary record turns out to be a set of local sequences deposited at many different times, correlated to each other by fossils, radiometric dates and magnetic reversals rather than by a shared event surface, and containing positive features that require long quiet intervals interleaved with deposition. It fails specifically if there are formations that cannot be produced quickly, sitting in the middle of the interval the Flood is supposed to occupy.

It would be borne out — and this is the part that makes the prediction fair — if a single thin, chemically or mineralogically distinctive layer could be traced worldwide at the same stratigraphic position. That is not an impossible standard invented to be failed. It is precisely what Luis and Walter Alvarez and their colleagues found in 1980 when they measured iridium across the Cretaceous–Tertiary boundary in Italy, Denmark and New Zealand and got anomalies of roughly 30, 160 and 20 times background at exactly the same horizon, subsequently traced worldwide and matched to the Chicxulub crater. A single global catastrophe leaves a single global layer, the layer is findable, and the method that finds it is in routine use.1

The strongest case for

The megasequences are real, and they are the best evidence the model has. In 1963 Laurence Sloss divided the North American sedimentary cover into six great packages — Sauk, Tippecanoe, Kaskaskia, Absaroka, Zuni, Tejas — each hundreds of feet thick, each bounded above and below by an unconformity, and each recording seawater advancing across the continental interior and then withdrawing. This is mainstream stratigraphy, not a creationist construct, and it does describe water covering continental interiors on a scale no modern flood approaches. Since 2013 Timothy Clarey's Column Project at the Institute for Creation Research has extended the mapping using oil-well logs, outcrops and seismic data on several continents, and reads all six megasequences as successive surges of the Flood — the Sauk its beginning, the top of the Tejas its end.2

And the model has done real work against a specific counter-argument. The Coconino Sandstone of the Grand Canyon was for decades the textbook demonstration that at least one thick unit in the canyon's sequence is a fossil desert — wind-blown dunes, impossible under water, sitting in the middle of what the Flood must have deposited in weeks. John Whitmore and Paul Garner have contested that reading in detail: cross-bed dips averaging near 20° rather than the 30-plus of desert dunes, poor to moderate sorting with subangular grains, surviving mica flakes that wind transport tends to destroy, and sand injectites at the base. Whether or not the reinterpretation carries — and mainstream responses have been sharp, Lorence Collins arguing that an average dip taken across two hundred sites is not a meaningful statistic — the exchange shows the position engaging its hardest counter-evidence rather than avoiding it.3

Adversarial assessment

What holds. Continental flooding on a scale unlike anything modern is a genuine feature of the record; the megasequence packages exist and are bounded by unconformities; the late Cretaceous really was a maximum of continental inundation. Anyone answering the argument by denying that seas have repeatedly covered continental interiors is answering something else.

The packages are not synchronous, and that is the whole prediction. The megasequences are separated by unconformities that are the model's evidence and its problem at once. Higher-order sequences nested inside them show sea level rising and falling many times over, with the predictable internal development — transgressive lags, maximum flooding surfaces, prograding wedges — that sequence stratigraphy was built to describe and that petroleum geology depends on to find hydrocarbons. A model that reads the whole stack as one year's surges has to treat every one of those internal cycles as noise. And the boundaries themselves do not line up between continents: they are correlated by biostratigraphy and radiometric dating, which is to say by the very framework the model rejects. Reading a Sauk in Africa as the same event as a Sauk in North America requires the correlation tools whose results are being denied.4

Formations exist that require long quiet intervals in the middle of the supposed Flood year. This is the decisive class of evidence, because it is positive rather than merely absent. At Joggins in Nova Scotia, upright fossil tree trunks stand rooted in dozens of stacked horizons: each is a forest floor that grew, was buried, and was grown over again, and stacking them takes as many growth intervals as there are horizons. Reef complexes are built organism by organism at measurable rates. Thick evaporite sequences require repeated evaporation of standing water, not its arrival. Varved lake sediments preserve annual couplets in counts far exceeding a few thousand. Each of these sits inside the interval the Flood is asked to have deposited, and each requires the water to have been absent, or still, for a long time at that exact point in the sequence.5

The Coconino dispute, even resolved generously, does not reach the prediction. Suppose Whitmore and Garner are right that the unit is subaqueous. That removes one obstacle; it supplies no synchronous global horizon. And the intra-creationist literature has itself questioned whether the dip-angle criterion carries the weight placed on it. Winning a local depositional argument is not the same as passing the test the model set for itself.3

The longest continuous record runs across every proposed date. The EPICA Dome C ice core preserves a continuous climate record through eight glacial cycles, some 740,000 years on its glaciological timescale, with no flood layer in it.6 Flood geology does not read that ice as crossing the Flood: it places the growth of the polar ice sheets after the Flood, in an Ice Age the Flood itself set off, and holds that the long Antarctic ages rest on an assumed accumulation rate that need not have held.7 On that reading the core says nothing about the Flood layer and a good deal about the time since it, and whether its record can be compressed into a few thousand years is weighed at the calibration spine. On any timescale that keeps the core's own dating, a Flood covering the whole earth more recently than the ice began to accumulate would have had to cover the sheet itself and leave its trace in it, and none is recorded.

A note on method, since it matters here more than anywhere on this timeline. None of the above rests on ruling miracles out. The objection is narrower and harder than that: the model itself predicts a particular physical trace, the search for that trace is the model's own proposed test, and the trace is absent while a different and incompatible pattern is present. An appeal to miracle at this point does not rescue the prediction — it withdraws it. A Flood that leaves no signature because God arranged that it leave none is not making the claim this argument was advanced to make, and cannot be credited with the evidence it then declines to produce. Possibility is not proof, and a prediction that cannot fail is not a prediction.

What the puzzle does and does not reach. It presses on the whole-earth extent the presupposition holds: the deposit such a Flood requires has not been identified as one synchronous worldwide body, and the resolutions below are weighed on whether they can identify it. It does not touch the antiquity of the flood tradition, which is secured by the cuneiform record independently of any geology. And it does not touch the theological claim of a divine judgement in history, which this page has no instruments to weigh.

Resolutions proposed inside the presupposition

The tension is stated above without softening: a Flood that covered the whole earth should have left a worldwide deposit, and the thin synchronous horizon the young model predicts is not there. Several resolutions have been seriously advanced, and the presupposition's open date implies one more. Each is weighed here on its evidence, with one question put to each: does it resolve the puzzle while keeping the whole presupposition — a Flood that covered the whole earth — or does it resolve it only by giving part of that up?

The column itself as the Flood's deposit

The proposal. The most developed answer rejects the premise that the Flood should be a thin layer at all. On the flood-geology reading, the Flood's deposit is the bulk of the fossil-bearing sedimentary record. It begins at the Great Unconformity, the erosion surface on which layered sediments sit sharply on older crystalline basement — in the Grand Canyon, the contact that Answers in Genesis's profile of Andrew Snelling calls the "contact line between pre-flood rocks and flood deposits" — and it rises through the megasequences above.8 Clarey's compilation now runs to "over 3,000 stratigraphic columns" across North America, South America, Asia, Africa and Europe, and he reads them as a progressive Flood: limited continental coverage in the earliest three megasequences, far more in the later three, and a high point "near the end of the Cretaceous," which he identifies as "Day 150 in the Flood year."9 On this view the prediction was misframed. A year-long deluge that reshaped the continents would leave not a centimetre of distinctive clay but kilometres of rock, and the worldwide surface to look for is its base.

What it gets right. The Great Unconformity is a real feature of continental geology on a scale that wants explaining, and mainstream geology has not settled its origin. Keller and colleagues wrote in 2019 that "a conclusive explanation for the formation and global extent of the Great Unconformity has remained elusive," and proposed Neoproterozoic glacial erosion "totaling a global average of 3–5 vertical kilometers."10 A creationist reading that treats the surface as one great erosional event is engaging a genuine open problem, not inventing one.

How it fares. The resolution moves the synchrony question rather than answering it, and its worldwide surface is where synchrony is now most directly disputed. Flowers and colleagues, from thermochronology in Colorado, find that the bulk of the erosion there predates the glaciations, and "suggest that there are multiple, regionally diachronous Great Unconformities that are tectonic in origin."11 The sediments lying on the surface are dated to ages "ranging from the Ediacaran to lower Ordovician," a point pressed in reply to Clarey and Snelling, who treat those sandstones as one contemporaneous sand body.12 Above the base, the megasequences are matched between continents by the same biostratigraphic and radiometric correlation already noted, so their synchrony across oceans is borrowed from the framework whose time-values the model rejects.4

The reading also has to place inside the Flood year every marker of a long quiet interval listed above, and its advocates have proposed answers. For the upright trees at Joggins, John Morris suggested that pre-Flood forests "may have actually grown on the water surface" as floating mats and were sunk in place, comparing the Spirit Lake log mat at Mount St Helens; he grants that "a fuller understanding awaits more research." A 2019 survey in the Answers Research Journal treats stacked fossil forests as a standing challenge for creationist research, proposes criteria for telling transported forests from rooted ones, and assigns some forests to catastrophes after the Flood instead.13 For thick salt beds, the proposal is precipitation from superheated brines released by the "fountains of the great deep" of Genesis 7:11, drawing on Martin Hovland's hydrothermal salt model; Morris concedes that "there is no modern analog for this either." Hovland's mechanism is published geology, but a minority view: an unsigned note on the site of Saltwork Consultants, the salt geologist John Warren's consultancy, observes that such salt "is not a true evaporite" and that the model "has not been widely accepted by the geological community."14 These are the right kind of answers — specific, physical, checkable — and they are partial. Each addresses one class of marker, several are offered as possibilities, and the reefs, varves and buried soils each need answers of their own.

As a resolution this is the strongest on offer. It is the only one that keeps the whole presupposition and works with the record at full scale, and its megasequence mapping is real work. It does not yet discharge the tension, because the two things it needs — a synchronous onset surface and a single year for everything above it — are exactly the points the evidence currently disputes.

Catastrophic plate tectonics as the engine

The proposal. A Flood over the whole earth needs a mechanism for putting the continents under water, and John Baumgardner's catastrophic plate tectonics supplies one. He proposed a "thermal runaway" of the mantle's cold upper boundary layer, in which ocean lithosphere sank rapidly and the present ocean floor — "all the basalt that comprises the upper five kilometers or so of today's igneous ocean crust" — was produced "within the year of the Flood."15 Hot, buoyant new sea floor raises sea level, and the water is driven across the continents.

How it fares. It is a serious geophysical model, and it has been contested inside creationism as well as outside it; creation.com has hosted a forum on it with Baumgardner for and Michael Oard against. Its acknowledged difficulty is heat. Making a new ocean floor in a year releases energy Baumgardner calculated at some 1028 joules, and he wrote in 1986, as quoted by Humphreys, that "all creationist Creation or Flood models I know of have serious problems with heat disposal." The cooling mechanism Russell Humphreys proposed, in which the excess heat is absorbed through cosmic expansion, has been criticised as unworkable.16 None of this is settled by holding a miracle impossible; a God who sends a Flood can remove its heat. But at that point the engine is no longer a physical explanation of the record. And even granted in full, it supplies a mechanism for flooding, not evidence that the deposit is synchronous, so it does not touch the puzzle this page states.

Where the Flood ends

The proposal. Flood geologists broadly agree that the Flood's deposit begins at or near the Great Unconformity. They disagree widely about where it stops. One view puts the Flood/post-Flood boundary "at or around the Cretaceous/Paleogene (K-Pg) boundary," following John Whitmore and Paul Garner's multi-criteria method, and Marcus Ross, testing the alternative against North American mammal fossils, concluded that the boundary lies at the K-Pg and that a placement near the start of the Ice Age does not hold.17 The other view puts it near the top of the Pliocene. Clarey and D. J. Werner argue that Paleogene and Neogene marine strata surround Turkey, so that "the Flood/post-Flood boundary must be above the level of these marine layers."18 The choice decides how much of the record the Flood has to account for, and how much is left to the centuries after it.

How it fares. Each placement relieves one difficulty by transferring another, and the arguments against each come from the other camp. A boundary at the K-Pg leaves the whole Cenozoic — on the conventional timescale some sixty-six million years of sediment — to be laid down after the Flood, within a few centuries; Clarey objects that its advocates "have not offered a testable, viable method" for getting large animals to the separated continents in that window.19 A boundary near the top of the Pliocene eases the post-Flood timetable but puts much more of the record, and more of its quiet-interval markers, inside the Flood year. Chad Arment, arguing from marsupial fossils and the biblical kinds, concludes that "the Neogene- Quaternary boundary is not viable within a biblical framework," while allowing that the boundary "may in some, many, or all places be at or near the K-Pg boundary."20 A boundary that may lie at different levels in different places is a reasonable working position. But it means the deposit the presupposition requires has, on its own advocates' account, no agreed top surface — which is the synchrony problem again, at the other end of the column.

An older Flood date

The presupposition leaves the date open, and on the genetic puzzle an older date is the best fit to the genetic evidence. Here it does not help. The Septuagint's long chronology moves the Flood from about 2348 BC to about 3300 BC (weighed at the Septuagint long chronology), which leaves it inside the span of the continuous records — tree rings, lake varves, layer-counted ice — whose security is weighed at the calibration spine. Moving the date back by hundreds of thousands of years, to meet the genetic floor of about 260,000–350,000 years, carries a Flood that covered the whole earth into the span of the Dome C ice, where it would have had to leave its mark and has not.6 The difficulty is not which number is chosen. A Flood that covered the whole earth needs a worldwide deposit at whatever date it is placed, and the kind of synchronous worldwide marker geologists do recognise on the conventional timescale is a thin event bed like the impact clay, not a flood deposit.1 Flood geology does find a candidate deposit, but it finds it by setting the conventional timescale aside, so the date question folds back into the first resolution above.

A regional Flood

The puzzle disappears if the waters did not cover the whole earth. A Flood confined to one region predicts no worldwide layer, and the Mesopotamian floodplain evidence bearing on such an event is weighed at the Ur flood deposit; the tradition's own debate over the Flood's extent is traced at global vs. anthropological extent. But a regional Flood gives up the whole-earth extent this page holds, so it is a departure from the presupposition, not a resolution of the puzzle within it.

A trace removed or concealed

It can also be held that God removed or concealed the Flood's physical signature, or made the later record with an appearance of age. The wiki does not rule this out because it would be a miracle. But it does not resolve the puzzle; it holds that the record cannot speak to it, and a record that cannot speak cannot be entered on either side. The move is weighed in general at mature creation and apparent age.

Assessment

Weak — as a prediction that a recent, single-date Flood covering the whole earth left one synchronous worldwide sediment horizon. The prediction was well framed and checkable, with tools that have found exactly this kind of horizon when it existed: the end-Cretaceous iridium anomaly is a synchronous worldwide layer, and it was predicted, looked for, and found. The Flood horizon is not there. What the record holds instead is many local sequences correlated by fossils, isotopes and magnetic reversals, carrying positive markers of long quiet intervals — rooted forest floors stacked dozens deep at Joggins, reefs, evaporites, varves — distributed through the very interval a single Flood year would have to occupy, and above them some 740,000 years of continuous ice at Dome C.

The honest credit to the argument is the megasequences: continent-scale packages bounded by unconformities, recording water advancing over cratons, are a real large-scale pattern and the model is not inventing them. What the model cannot get from them is synchrony between continents or compression into one year, and those are the two things the prediction required.

That leaves a genuine open puzzle inside the presupposition. Of the resolutions proposed, the strongest is flood geology's reading of the whole column, from the Great Unconformity up, as the Flood's deposit: it keeps the whole presupposition and engages the record at full scale, but its onset surface is disputed as diachronous, its correlations between continents borrow the framework it rejects, its answers to the quiet-interval markers are partial, and its advocates cannot yet agree where the deposit ends. Catastrophic plate tectonics supplies an engine with an acknowledged heat problem and no evidence of synchrony. An older date does not help, since a Flood that covered the whole earth needs its deposit at whatever date it is placed. A regional Flood removes the puzzle only by leaving the presupposition, and an appeal to a concealed trace takes the record out of the question rather than answering it. None of the resolutions yet discharges the tension.
