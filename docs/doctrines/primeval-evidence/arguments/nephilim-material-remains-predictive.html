A widespread, physically gigantic Nephilim population predicts authenticated remains — are there any?

Presupposition: inherited from The Flood and the Nephilim in the Material Record — that Noah’s Flood happened, covering the whole earth and destroying all humanity outside the ark, and that the Nephilim were real. This page works inside that presupposition: its assessment weighs a model of the event or a trace of it, not whether it happened.

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: unsupported — as a prediction of the widespread-physical-giants model; of the resolutions proposed, the evidence currently favours the one on which the post-Flood giants were tall but not outsized and their clans few and local. Holding, as this wiki does, that the Nephilim were real, the model weighed here is one particular picture of them: a widespread population of physically outsized people across the historical Levant. That picture makes a material prediction — such a people leaves bones — and the search that would have found them has been run. Excavated human skeletal remains from the Near East and Europe between 10,000 and 1000 BC have been assembled into a modelled corpus of some six thousand individuals with measured long bones, and the resulting stature surface is an ordinary human one — means in the mid-160s of centimetres, moving by a few centimetres across millennia, with no outlier population anywhere on it. The traditions are abundant; for this model, the remains are absent. That is a genuine puzzle inside the presupposition, and it is stated plainly here rather than softened. This is the predictive counterpart to the retrospective question of whether the claimed giant remains are authentic, and it is the page that weighs the resolutions: modest stature or a warrior category, the Flood's destruction followed by a few local clans, preservation bias, the spiritual significance of the Nephilim, and suppressed remains.

Related arguments: open puzzles for the presupposition — see also a synchronous worldwide sediment horizon · single-point post-Flood faunal radiation · a recent eight-person bottleneck. On whether any claimed giant remains are authentic, see authenticated remains at Nephilim scale under the material trace of the Nephilim. On the warrior-category reading weighed among the resolutions, see the post-Flood "giants" as a warrior category.

The prediction

On the presupposition, the Nephilim were real. The model weighed on this page adds a specific picture of them — a physical one, a population of people substantially larger than ordinary humans, present in the Levant across a datable historical period. Numbers 13 has them as tribes with names and towns; Deuteronomy 2 supplies two more with local names for each; Joshua 11 leaves a remnant in three named Philistine cities. On this reading it is not a single prodigy. It is peoples, in places, over centuries.

Peoples in places over centuries die and are buried where they lived. The prediction is therefore osteological and specific: the excavated skeletal record of the southern Levant and its neighbours should contain measurable individuals well outside the normal human range, and it should contain them as a population — a cluster, a shifted distribution, an outlier group — not as isolated pathological cases. Skeletons are what archaeology recovers most reliably, femora survive when almost nothing else does, and stature is one of the easiest things to estimate from a femur. If this picture of the Nephilim is right, the bones are the place it should be easiest to see.

What the model commits to

The model is in difficulty if the measured stature record of the relevant regions and periods is an ordinary unimodal human distribution with no outlier population, and if the individually tall specimens that do exist turn out to be isolated endocrine pathologies rather than members of a large-statured group.

It would be borne out by a single well-provenanced burial population with a mean stature far above the human norm, or by a run of individuals from one region and period clustering well outside it. That standard is met routinely for subtler claims: skeletal populations are regularly distinguished by a few centimetres of mean stature, by degrees of sexual dimorphism, by growth-arrest lines. An instrument that resolves a three-centimetre shift would not miss a population averaging a metre more.

The strongest case for

The picture has modern advocates, and they argue for a people, not a prodigy. Its fullest modern statement is Jim Vieira and Hugh Newman's: "more than 1000 accounts of seven-foot and taller skeletons" reported "from coast to coast," giant men who "once lived and roamed in the territories of their forefathers," with "the Nephilim of the Bible Lands" among the origins they canvass.1 Their field is North America rather than the Levant, and their figures run to seven, eight or nine feet rather than the register of the conquest narratives; but the claim is the one this page tests — a widespread population of outsized people, whose bones the ground should hold.

The textual claim is concrete, not merely rhetorical, and it names findable places. The roster does not gesture at a legendary age; it puts the Anakim at Hebron and the remnant "in Gaza, in Gath, and in Ashdod," gives Og's bed dimensions and a city where it stood, and dates the encounters within a narrative of conquest. A claim that specific is a claim archaeology can go and check, which is more than most traditions offer.2

The name may have external attestation. Egyptian Middle Kingdom execration texts curse a Canaanite group read as ʾiy-ʿanaq, "the people of Anaq," under three named rulers — potentially an independent attestation of the Anakim some eight centuries before the biblical texts. The identification is argued rather than settled, and even granted it attests a people-name and not a stature; nothing Egyptian calls them large. But it is a real datum and the argument is entitled to it.2

And authenticated gigantism does exist in the skeletal record. This concession is usually withheld by sceptics and should not be. The Fidenae skeleton T.30 from the Roman imperial period stands about 202 cm and was still growing when he died in his teens; a Polish individual reaches 215.5 cm; Robert Wadlow, documented in life, reached 2.72 m. Extremely tall human beings are real, they are recoverable archaeologically, and their remains are recognisable when found. The prediction is not asking for the impossible.3

What the skeletal record shows

What holds. The textual claim is locatable; the Anaq execration reading is a live if unsettled datum; and gigantism is genuinely present in the osteological record, so the search is well posed. Nothing here turns on declaring the question silly.

The search has been run at scale, and the corpus is ordinary. This is the page's central finding. A Bayesian spatiotemporal analysis of human stature in the Near East and Europe from c. 10,000 to 1000 BC assembled 6,098 published prehistoric skeletons with measured long bones, estimated stature by three methods, corrected for mismeasurement and for site-level effects, and produced stature isolines and regional trend curves across the whole period. The result is a normal human surface: European male means around 174 cm in the Early Upper Palaeolithic falling to about 164 cm in the Mesolithic and recovering to about 167 cm by the Bronze Age, with a comparable and modestly rising Near Eastern curve. The variation the study resolves is of the order of a few centimetres. No outlier population appears anywhere on the map or the timeline.4

And in the Philistine coastland, beside the cities where the text leaves the survivors, the bones exist and are short. Joshua leaves the Anakim remnant in Gaza, Gath and Ashdod. Ashkelon, the Philistine city on the coast between Gaza and Ashdod, has the first Philistine cemetery ever excavated, and it gives a mean male stature of 154.6 cm across the eight males (of sixteen adults complete enough to be measured directly rather than estimated by regression), out of some 231 individuals — a stressed population, below the regional mean. Ashkelon is not one of the three named towns, and the cemetery is later than the conquest narrative, so this is a regional datum rather than a direct check on the remnant; but it is the nearest well-measured population to it, and it is ordinary.5

The authenticated giants are the wrong kind of evidence for this model, and the distinction is the crux. Fidenae T.30, the Polish individual and Wadlow are each a documented endocrine pathology in a single person — pituitary hypertrophy, with its costs: Wadlow walked in braces and died at twenty-two of an infection from a brace sore. Extreme stature of this pathological kind has never been shown to run in a family, let alone to characterise a people. The model needs a heritable population trait; the record supplies a rare sporadic disorder. These are not the same phenomenon at different frequencies, they are different phenomena.3

The honest caveats, stated rather than suppressed. Two limits qualify the finding without dissolving the puzzle. Near Eastern coverage is thinner than European: in the Copper and Bronze Age Near East skeletal remains were often studied for pathology rather than metrics, so the Levantine sample is sparser than one would like. And the corpus is biased toward elite burials, which in stratified societies may skew the sample upward — a bias that runs in the model's favour and still produces nothing. Neither caveat can absorb the discrepancy for a widespread population: the shortfall between an ordinary human distribution and a people of the register the roster describes, spread across the region, is not a sampling artifact. Whether it can be absorbed for a smaller, localised population is a different question, taken up below.4

Where the puzzle sits. Holding, as this wiki does, that the Nephilim were real, the tension is this: the texts give the post-Flood giant clans names, towns and a datable setting, and the ground — sampled at a scale that resolves centimetres — shows no population of outsized people anywhere in the region or period. The absence is a finding about the widespread-physical-population picture, stated plainly. It does not by itself say which other picture of the Nephilim is right; that is what the resolutions below are for.

Resolutions proposed inside the presupposition

Five resolutions are on offer among those who hold the Nephilim to be real. Four have named modern advocates; the fourth is less an answer anyone has pressed against the missing bones than the oldest reading of the Nephilim brought to bear on them, and is weighed here as proposed. Each is weighed on its evidence. None is dismissed for appealing to the supernatural, and none is credited merely because it would remove the difficulty; the question in each case is how much of the actual record it explains, and at what cost.

1. The stature was real but modest — or "giant" names a warrior category

The resolution. The giants were genuinely tall, not monstrous: men around two metres among neighbours averaging well under 170 cm, remembered as giants because by any ordinary standard they were. Michael Heiser, who held the supernatural reading of Genesis 6:1–4, put it bluntly: "I personally don't believe that the biblical giants were much over seven feet tall," and he dismissed the ten-to-thirty-foot figures of popular giant literature outright.6 The textual base for this is the best-attested reading of the one giant whose height the Bible gives: Goliath stands "four cubits and a span" — about two metres — in the Qumran Samuel scroll 4QSama, the Old Greek and Josephus, against a Hebrew "six" attested only in the medieval codices; this wiki rates the priority of the shorter reading sound. Clyde Billington, defending the larger figure, reads both numbers as one man of roughly eight feet in different cubits — still inside the range of documented human stature.7 The companion form of the resolution reads the conquest-era vocabulary as a category of formidable warrior-peoples rather than a measurement: the clans are described in comparatives ("great, and many, and tall"), the one verse that makes them sons of the Nephilim sits in a report the narrative calls "evil," and Og's nine-cubit bed reads best as a royal-monumental object, not a body-measure — a reading this wiki rates sound, as it does the monumental reading of Og's bed. That form keeps the Nephilim of Genesis 6 real and reclassifies only the post-Flood clans: the Anakim, Rephaim and their neighbours become formidable Canaanite peoples described in the idiom the tradition had for giants, not a surviving line.

How it fares. Best of the five. It changes the prediction rather than excusing its failure: a lineage of men near two metres is a heritable variation within the normal human range — the kind of between-population stature difference that genetics already documents in prehistoric Europe — not the pituitary disorder the record shows to be sporadic and costly.4 Such a lineage, if it was a few clans rather than a people spread across the region, would not shift a continental stature surface, and its skeletons would look like tall human skeletons, not like a new kind of being. Its cost is real and should be named: it gives up the outsized register — Og at four metres, observers "as grasshoppers" — as a literal measurement, and reads those as the comparatives of frightened spies and the language of royal monuments; and in its warrior-category form it also gives up the lineage of Numbers 13:33, reading the spies' "sons of Anak, which come of the giants" as their rhetoric rather than the narrator's genealogy. Those who hold the larger figures literally will regard that as a concession too far. On the evidence presently in the ground, though, it is the resolution that asks least of the record.

2. The antediluvian Nephilim perished in the Flood, and the later clans were few and local

The resolution. Two parts. For the generation before the Flood, the text itself supplies the explanation: all flesh outside the ark died. Young-earth writers extend the point to the whole pre-Flood population, arguing that drowned bodies "tend to bloat, and therefore become lighter and float to the surface," to be scavenged rather than buried, and that there are "reasonable explanations, consistent with Scripture, for the seeming lack of human fossils in Flood rocks."8 For the post-Flood clans, the text makes them local and finite: Anakim at Hebron and in the hill country, Rephaim in Bashan under Og, Emim and Zamzummim in Moab and Ammon, and a remnant left in three Philistine towns after Joshua "cut off the Anakims."2 A few clans over a few centuries, in a handful of regions, are a small fraction of the population of the Bronze Age Levant — and the regions in question are scarcely sampled. Heiser stressed that only a small fraction of the Holy Land — by his estimate under ten per cent — has been excavated with any thoroughness.6 Bashan is a case in point: in the Golan, its western part, more than 5,200 megalithic burials have been mapped, yet the excavators of the Shamir dolmen field report that "the number of modern excavations performed is limited," that grave-robbing in antiquity helps explain why most excavated dolmens yielded few finds, and that bone in the Golan dolmens is generally poorly preserved in the moist conditions; even their well-preserved chamber held a heavily fragmented secondary burial of at least three individuals, with no stature reported.9

How it fares. The two parts fare differently. The first depends on where the Flood sits. On a chronology that puts every dated burial after the Flood, the antediluvian Nephilim are simply outside the skeletal record and the corpus says nothing about them. On the Septuagint horizon of c. 3300–3000 BC — the reckoning this wiki rates arguably the stronger reckoning (though weak as a fixed date) — the corpus's Neolithic and Chalcolithic skeletons, if their dates hold, fall on the pre-Flood side, and they too are ordinary; the antediluvian Nephilim would then have to have been few, or not buried among the ordinary dead. That is coherent, but it is an added assumption rather than something the evidence supplies (the security of those dates is weighed on dating security). The second part does real work. A small, localised population genuinely would not register in a corpus of thousands drawn from two continents, and the places the text names are among the least-excavated and worst-preserving in the region. Its cost is that it is no longer the model this page weighs: "widespread" has been exchanged for "local," and a local model makes a weaker prediction — a handful of burials at Hebron, in Bashan or at Gath — that the present record can neither confirm nor exclude. It also leaves open how the post-Flood clans came to carry the Nephilim name at all, a separate problem weighed under the post-Flood Nephilim problem. Paired with the first resolution, it accounts for the null; on its own, with outsized stature retained, it does less, because a people of three or four metres living across several regions for several centuries would still sit uneasily with the ordinary burials that have been measured nearby, Ashkelon's among them.

3. Preservation, recovery and identification bias

The resolution. The record is thin and skewed: the Near Eastern sample is sparser than the European, Bronze Age Levantine remains were often studied for pathology rather than measured, and many burials survive only as fragments.4 Giant remains, on this view, may simply not have survived, not been recovered, or not been recognised for what they were. Heiser made the point against those who demand the bones: skeletal remains from the biblical period are few — "they didn't embalm, and less than 10% of the Holy Land has been excavated by any standard of thoroughness."6

How it fares. Real as a description of the sample, weak as a standalone explanation. Taphonomy does not discriminate by stature: soil that dissolves a giant's femur dissolves everyone's, and the ordinary dead survive in their thousands. The one directional bias the stature study identifies, toward elite burials, would skew the sample upward, not down. And identification bias runs the wrong way: unusually long skeletons are what excavators notice first — the Fidenae giant was singled out from an unusually long tomb, and long burials are exactly what field reports single out (the Bureau of Ethnology's mound reports give examples, set out on the claimed-remains page).3 Bias explains why the Levantine sample is thin, and so strengthens the second resolution; it supplies no mechanism that removes tall bodies specifically.

4. The Nephilim's significance is chiefly spiritual

The resolution. What matters about the Nephilim, on the oldest reading of them, is not their skeletons but what survived them. In 1 Enoch, "the giants, who are produced from the spirits and flesh, shall be called evil spirits upon the earth … Evil spirits have proceeded from their bodies," and those spirits "afflict, oppress, destroy" and "rise up against the children of men."10 The second- and third-century Fathers widely took the unclean spirits to be these disembodied giants — a reading this wiki rates contested — and Heiser defended it in modern scholarship: "the disembodied spirits released at the death of the giants" are "the demons known in Second Temple texts and the New Testament Gospels."10 Brought to bear on the missing bones, this reading proposes that the material trace is the wrong place to look for the doctrine's weight: the Nephilim matter as the origin of the demonic, and their bones, wherever they lie, are incidental.

How it fares. A fair point about significance, and an anciently attested one. But it is not an explanation of the absence. The same text gives the giants flesh and bodies from which the spirits proceed, so on its own terms there were bodies to bury. The spiritual reading is compatible with the null without predicting it; it relocates the question's importance rather than answering it, and pairs with the first two resolutions rather than replacing them.

5. The remains were found and suppressed

The resolution. Authentic giant skeletons were recovered, chiefly from North American mounds, and then lost or hidden — the thesis Jim Vieira and Hugh Newman argue at book length.1

How it fares. Weakest of the five. The long burials it starts from, the hoaxes that travel with it and the structure of the suppression claim itself are weighed in full on the claimed-remains page, which finds the field lengths to be tall individuals below Wadlow's measured height, not giants. The point specific to this model is geographic: the reports are American, not Levantine, so even if every one of them were vindicated they could not be the conquest-era clans whose absence from the Levantine record is the puzzle here.

Where the resolutions leave the puzzle

Taken together, the first two resolutions account for the post-Flood record: if the post-Flood giants were genuinely tall men rather than outsized beings, and were a few clans in a few poorly sampled places, an ordinary regional stature surface is what one would expect to find, and the absence of a population signal from the conquest-era Levant is no longer a difficulty. Preservation bias helps the second resolution along without standing on its own; the spiritual reading is compatible but does not explain; the suppression thesis does not survive contact with its own sources. Three things remain genuinely open. One is the pre-Flood residue: on the Septuagint horizon the corpus's Neolithic and Chalcolithic skeletons are antediluvian and ordinary, and accommodating that still takes the added assumption that the antediluvian Nephilim were few or not buried among the ordinary dead. Another is the narrower, local prediction — whether a tall clan will ever surface in the burials of Hebron, Bashan or Gath. The last is whether those who read the outsized figures literally can find a resolution that keeps them.

A note on method

Nothing here rests on holding a supernatural Nephilim origin impossible. A being of miraculous parentage still has a skeleton, still dies, and is still buried in ground that gets excavated — which is precisely why this picture of the Nephilim was able to make a physical prediction in the first place. The difficulty is not metaphysical; it is that the ground has been checked. An appeal to miracle at this point would have to explain not the existence of the giants but the disappearance of every one of their bodies from a record that reliably preserves everyone else's, which is a heavier claim than the one it was called in to save — and the resolutions that actually absorb the evidence need no such appeal.

Assessment

Unsupported — as a prediction of the widespread-physical-giants model. The prediction was the right one to make and was well posed: peoples living in named places over centuries leave burials, femora survive, and stature is straightforwardly recoverable from them. The search has been run at a scale that cannot be dismissed — 6,098 measured prehistoric skeletons across the Near East and Europe from 10,000 to 1000 BC, modelled into stature surfaces that resolve differences of a few centimetres — and it returns an ordinary human distribution with no outlier population anywhere in it. In the Philistine coastland beside the cities where the text leaves the remnant, the excavated cemetery at Ashkelon yields a mean male stature of 154.6 cm. Authenticated gigantism is in the record and is conceded without reservation — Fidenae T.30 at about 202 cm, a Polish individual at 215.5 cm, Wadlow at 2.72 m — but every case is a pituitary disorder in a single person, none approaches the register the roster describes, and none is heritable.

Holding, as this wiki does, that the Nephilim were real, that leaves a genuine puzzle, and the resolutions do not fare equally. The evidence currently favours the reading on which the post-Flood giants were tall but within the human range — Goliath at "four cubits and a span," "giant" as the name of a formidable warrior-people — and were a few clans in poorly sampled regions that would leave no mark on a continental stature record. Preservation bias reinforces that reading but cannot replace it; the spiritual significance of the Nephilim is real but does not explain where the bodies went; the suppression thesis fails on its own documents. That accounts for the post-Flood clans; for the generation before the Flood, on the Septuagint horizon, the ordinary Neolithic and Chalcolithic skeletons still need the added assumption that the antediluvian Nephilim were few or not buried among the ordinary dead, and that residue stays open. What the record removes is the picture of a widespread people of outsized bodies. The textual questions the Nephilim raise — the post-Flood pedigree, Og's bed, Goliath's height — are weighed on their own pages.
