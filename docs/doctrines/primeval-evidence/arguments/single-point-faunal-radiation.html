A global Flood predicts single-point post-Flood faunal radiation — does biogeography show it?

Presupposition: inherited from The Flood and the Nephilim in the Material Record — that Noah’s Flood happened, covering the whole earth and destroying all humanity outside the ark, and that the Nephilim were real. This page works inside that presupposition: its assessment weighs a model of the event or a trace of it, not whether it happened.

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: weak — as a prediction that the land fauna radiates from the ark's landing place and left its routes marked by fossils. Granting that the waters covered the whole earth, every land animal now living descends from those that walked off one vessel at one place, and the prediction is unusually clean: the living distribution should be a radiation from that place, and the fossil record should preserve the routes. What biogeography shows instead is a world partitioned into faunal provinces whose boundaries track ancient geography rather than distance from any single point, and the intermediate fossils a recent overland radiation would have left are absent from its routes. That leaves an open puzzle inside the presupposition, and several resolutions have been proposed. The strongest is the one that places the end of the Flood's deposit early, at or near the Cretaceous–Paleogene boundary, and reads the Cenozoic marsupial fossils of South America, Antarctica and Australia as the post-Flood route itself; it is the only proposal that puts fossils on a route, but the route it finds is the one ancient continental geography predicts, it gives no mechanism for the Antarctic leg, and it has to fit the whole Cenozoic record into the centuries after the Flood. Rafting and Ice Age land bridges are real mechanisms that each cover only part of the pattern; rapid diversification within created kinds lowers the number of travellers without supplying their routes; and divine direction of the dispersal, now proposed by some of the model's own advocates, explains why the pattern cannot be checked rather than meeting it. None yet discharges the tension. A regional Flood would remove it, but only by leaving the presupposition.

The prediction

On the text's own account, a Flood that covered the whole earth destroyed every air-breathing land creature outside the ark. Genesis grounds the ark "upon the mountains of Ararat," and the animals go out of it. That makes a single release point for the entire terrestrial fauna of the earth. The prediction follows directly and admits of no vagueness: the present distribution of land animals should be a radiation from one point in eastern Anatolia. Diversity should broadly decrease with distance and difficulty from that point; the animals of remote regions should be a subset of those nearer; and, because dispersal takes generations and generations leave dead animals, the routes should be marked by fossils — a trail of the dispersing kinds strung out between Ararat and wherever they ended up.

That last clause is what makes this a real test rather than a story. Living distributions alone can be explained by enough conjectured migrations. A migration that actually happened leaves remains along the way. Which rocks should hold those remains depends on where the Flood's own deposit ends — a question taken up among the resolutions below — but on any placement the route has to be recorded somewhere above it.

What would count against the prediction — committed in advance

The prediction fails if the world partitions into faunal provinces whose boundaries correspond to ancient continental configurations rather than to distance from Anatolia; if whole major groups are confined to regions they could only have reached by crossing terrain where they are entirely unrecorded, living or fossil; and if sharp faunal breaks occur across trivial distances where no barrier to a recent walking migration existed.

It would be confirmed by concentric structure — a radiation signature — and above all by the intermediate fossils. On the young model, with the dispersal a few thousand years ago, finding Australian marsupials, or their ancestral kinds, in the Neolithic and later deposits of Iran, India and Southeast Asia would confirm it decisively. The absence is not an argument from silence in the weak sense: these are among the most intensively excavated regions on earth, and the deposits of the relevant few thousand years are precisely the ones archaeology digs through routinely.

The strongest case for

The dispersal mechanisms are real, and this must be granted without hedging. Over-water rafting on floating vegetation is not a creationist invention; it is the accepted explanation for how monkeys and rodents reached South America and how lemurs reached Madagascar, and it is observed in the modern world. Kurt Wise and Matthew Croxton set it out as a post-Flood mechanism in 2003, and Michael Oard developed the case that log mats — vast rafts of timber left floating after a global inundation — would have been available in unprecedented quantity precisely in the period required, carrying passengers across water in a window that later closed. Land bridges are equally real: lowered sea level during glaciation exposed Beringia, Sundaland and the Sahul shelf, and the model's ice-age reconstruction places that low stand immediately after the Flood, which is exactly when it is needed.1

And the model has thought about the fossil evidence rather than ignoring it. Chad Arment's treatment of the marsupial fossil record in the Answers Research Journal engages the problem directly, using the genera found on both sides of each proposed Flood/post-Flood boundary to test where the boundary can lie, and treating the Cretaceous metatherians as outside the marsupial kinds — a genuine attempt to say what the model predicts stratigraphically rather than only where the animals ended up. Complicating data are cited by its own advocates, including the Tertiary Thylacinus material from Papua New Guinea and the report of Mesozoic monotremes in South America, both of which sit awkwardly for anyone assuming marsupials were always where they now are.2

Adversarial assessment

What holds. Rafting, land bridges and long-distance dispersal are genuine and well-attested; biogeography is not a discipline that denies animals cross oceans. The model is right that some distributions require over-water transport. And it is right that the fossil record of marsupials outside Australia is more complicated than a simple story allows.

The provinces track ancient geography, not distance from a point. Alfred Russel Wallace established in 1876 that the world's land fauna divides into regions with sharp boundaries, and the boundaries are not concentric about anywhere. Marsupials, living and fossil, are confined to Australasia and the Americas — two regions on opposite sides of the planet, joined in deep time through Antarctica and by nothing at all in the Holocene. Lemurs are confined to Madagascar. Most tellingly, the Wallace Line runs between Bali and Lombok, islands some twenty miles apart, and the fauna changes across it completely: a barrier that means everything to an animal that must cross deep water in a strait that has never been dry, and nothing whatever to an animal walking from Anatolia, which by that point has already crossed continents.3

The route fossils are missing where a recent dispersal needs them. If marsupials walked or rafted from Ararat to Australia within the last few thousand years, they were in Iran, in India, and in Southeast Asia on the way, in numbers, for generations. Nothing of the kind is recorded — no Holocene marsupial anywhere along the route, in regions whose Holocene deposits are exhaustively excavated. The same holds for the reverse trip the model also requires, since Australia's marsupials must first have reached the ark. A migration that leaves no trace over half the world in either direction is not a migration for which evidence has yet to be found; on the young model it is the specific prediction that was made, checked, and not met. (Placing the dispersal deeper in the rocks changes where the route should be looked for, and is weighed below.)

The intra-creationist critique is sharper than anything external, and it should be reported as such. Answering a critic in the same journal, Arment argues that a single dispersal route would have offered every marsupial the same ecological conditions, that "it would be beyond silly to argue that the different marsupials used a multitude of different routes to Australia," and that "there is no such route within Asia that can be identified as either placental-resistant, or marsupial-centric" — wherever marsupials could go, placentals could go, and placentals did not go. The koala case is put bluntly: it eats eucalyptus leaves, and "there is no pathway of eucalypt trees from the Middle East to Australia." Nor did the marsupials travel as a bound community: of the marsupial predators, "not a single one of these predators required marsupial prey," so nothing kept the carnivores and their present prey moving together.4

And some of the model's advocates have conceded the natural case. Oard's 2022 treatment allows that "God was possibly directing their dispersal"; a subsequent contributor argues there are no natural explanations available and attributes post-Flood biogeography to direct divine intervention. This is honest of them and it is decisive here. The concession does not make the position false — this wiki does not discount a claim merely because it requires a miracle, and a God who parts a sea can move a koala. What it does is withdraw the prediction. An argument that begins "the model predicts a radiation pattern, and here it is" cannot end "the pattern is absent because the dispersal was miraculously arranged" and still count as evidence. A creation.com contributor made the corresponding methodological point from inside the camp: by claiming their natural mechanisms explain biogeography better, creationists invite the counter that deep time supplies more and better mechanisms of the same kind.4

A note on method. Nothing here rests on holding a miracle impossible. The objection is that the distribution of animals was offered as physical evidence for the model, and physical evidence has to be able to come out the other way. Possibility is not proof. If the dispersal was supernaturally directed, then biogeography is silent about it in both directions and cannot be entered in the model's favour either.

What the puzzle does and does not reach. It presses on the whole-earth extent the presupposition holds, since only a Flood that emptied every continent of its land animals requires every one of them to have come from the ark. It does not touch the antiquity of the flood tradition, which stands on the cuneiform record. For the human analogue of this same release-point logic — whether one dispersing people left a radiation of cities — see the near-simultaneous rise of civilizations.

Resolutions proposed inside the presupposition

The tension is stated above without softening: one release point, and a living and fossil distribution that does not radiate from it. The resolutions seriously advanced are weighed here on their evidence, with one question put to each: does it resolve the puzzle while keeping the whole presupposition — a Flood that covered the whole earth, so that every land animal descends from the ark — or does it resolve it only by giving part of that up?

Rafting on post-Flood log mats

The proposal. Wise and Croxton proposed that animals rafted across the post-Flood oceans on mats of logs and vegetation, observing that the places where ocean currents meet the continents correspond with recognised centres of endemism; Oard developed the log-mat case at length.1 It is the right kind of answer, because it names a mechanism that operates today and could, in principle, sort animals by ocean current rather than by distance from Ararat.

How it fares. The sharpest limits again come from inside the model. Timothy Clarey notes that Wise and Croxton "assumed floating log mats and trees may have remained afloat for several centuries or longer after the Flood ended," that "these assumptions were not based on empirical evidence," and that the Mount St Helens logs on which the estimate rests had a flotation half-life of about seventy-five years; post-Flood storms "would have also likely torn the floating mats apart repeatedly." He grants that insects, small animals and plants could have dispersed this way, but finds rafting "inadequate for transport of the larger mammals such as elephants, camels, and horses," and adds that "both a male and female would be required to float to the same locations to populate any distant continent."5 Rafting explains some island faunas well; it does not explain why marsupials and not placentals reached Australia, and it leaves the route fossils of the walking leg where they were.

Ice Age land bridges

The proposal. Clarey's own answer is that lowered sea level in a post-Flood Ice Age opened land bridges between the continents, a window he times to the first centuries after the Flood and the dispersion from Babel: "God provided the land bridges and removed the bridges via the Ice Age."5 That gives large mammals a dry route that rafts could not.

How it fares. The land bridges are real, but they do not reach where the problem is. The Wallace Line follows a strait that stayed water even at the lowest sea levels, and the fauna changes completely across it.3 A land bridge open to marsupials is open to placentals too, which is Arment's objection that no Asian corridor can be identified as "placental-resistant, or marsupial-centric."4 And it places the dispersal late, in the Pleistocene and after, where the route fossils are most clearly absent. Clarey meets that directly — kangaroos may have migrated elsewhere "but have since died off," or their fossils "may have been buried elsewhere and have since eroded away or have not yet been exposed"; "fossils and/or the lack of fossils may not reveal the whole story."5 That is possible, but it explains the absence of the route rather than showing it.

An early end to the Flood: the route recorded in the Cenozoic

The proposal. Where the Flood's deposit ends decides where the route should lie (the debate is set out at a synchronous worldwide sediment horizon). If the boundary is at or near the Cretaceous–Paleogene, as Marcus Ross has argued from North American mammal fossils, then the whole Cenozoic record is post-Flood and the dispersal should be written in it.5 Arment's marsupial study finds that late boundaries face "a serious challenge in the fossil record," because dozens of genera occur on both sides of them on the same continent, while for an early boundary "there is no data here that contradicts that."2 On his reconstruction the marsupials' "diversification from within one or a handful of baraminic lineages began in South America," and "their lineages extended across Antarctica, before arriving in Australia."4 Eocene metatherians are known from Antarctica, so on this reading the route fossils exist and lie where the route ran.2

How it fares. This is the strongest resolution on offer, because it is the only one that puts fossils on a route, and it uses the record as it stands rather than explaining its silence. Its costs are real. The route it finds — South America, Antarctica, Australia — is the one the ancient southern supercontinent predicts on the conventional reading, so the same fossils that serve it serve the deep-time account equally well and do not discriminate between them. It treats the Northern-Hemisphere metatherians as other kinds, so the leg from Ararat to South America remains unrecorded and needs an ocean crossing of its own. It gives no mechanism or timing for an Antarctic connection after the Flood. And it has to fit the whole Cenozoic record — on the conventional timescale some sixty-six million years of sediment and faunal succession — into the centuries after the Flood. Late-boundary advocates object on exactly that ground: Clarey holds that early-boundary advocates "have not offered a testable, viable method for the migration of large animals to the newly separated continents after the Flood."5 The tension is not dissolved but moved, from the fossils to the timescale of the rocks that hold them.

Rapid diversification within created kinds

The proposal. Baraminology holds that the ark carried kinds, not species, and that species arose within kinds after the Flood. The method by which Answers in Genesis researchers set out to count the ark's kinds treats hybridisation as the clearest evidence that creatures descend from one ark kind; Todd Wood describes the emerging picture as "rapid initial diversification after the Flood followed by a period of relative stasis," what others call "hyperspeciation."6 If only a handful of marsupial kinds travelled, and the many living genera arose on arrival, far fewer animals had to make the journey and far fewer would have died along the way.

How it fares. It is a genuine mitigation of the route-fossil problem, since fewer travellers leave fewer remains, and it fits the observation that much marsupial diversity is local to Australia. But it does not supply the routes, and it does not explain why the provinces track ancient continental geography. It also cuts against the late boundary: Arment argues that a boundary near the top of the Pliocene puts multiple genera of one unclean kind on both sides of the Flood, against the single pair Genesis 7:2 allows, and concludes that "the Neogene-Quaternary boundary is not viable within a biblical framework."7 Rapid diversification makes the bookkeeping lighter; it leaves the geography where it was.

Divine direction of the dispersal

Some of the model's advocates now attribute the pattern to God's direction of the animals, as set out above. The wiki does not rule this out, and it is coherent: a dispersal God directed could produce any distribution at all. But for that reason it does not resolve the puzzle; it holds that biogeography cannot speak to it, and so retires the prediction rather than meeting it.4 The general form of the move is weighed at mature creation and apparent age.

An older Flood date

The presupposition leaves the date open, but an older date does not help here. On the Septuagint's reckoning, about 3300 BC rather than about 2348 BC (weighed at the Septuagint long chronology), the route fossils should still lie in the recent deposits where they are absent. A date hundreds of thousands of years earlier gives more time for dispersal but still asks for a route in the Pleistocene record, which shows the same provinces. The marsupial pattern follows the break-up of continents that conventional dating places tens of millions of years back, so no date within human history brings the radiation into view; only setting the conventional timescale aside does, which is the early-boundary resolution above.

A regional Flood

The puzzle disappears if the waters did not cover the whole earth. On a Flood confined to one region, the fauna of the lands it never reached never left home, no radiation from Ararat is predicted, and the observed provinces are what such a Flood would lead one to expect; the tradition's own debate over the Flood's extent is traced at global vs. anthropological extent. But that gives up the whole-earth extent this page holds, so it is a departure from the presupposition, not a resolution of the puzzle within it.

Assessment

Weak — as a prediction that the land fauna radiates from the ark's landing place and left its routes marked by fossils. The prediction was clean and checkable: one release point, therefore a radiation, therefore route fossils. Biogeography instead partitions the world into provinces whose boundaries follow ancient continental configurations — marsupials in Australasia and the Americas, lemurs in Madagascar, a total faunal change across twenty miles of deep water at the Wallace Line — and the intermediate fossils that any recent overland radiation would have strewn between Anatolia and Australia are absent from the most heavily excavated ground on earth.

The mechanisms the model invokes are genuine and are granted: rafting on vegetation mats and land bridges at low sea level really do move animals across water. What they do not produce is the observed pattern, and the model's own literature has said so — that log mats cannot carry large mammals, that no corridor selective for marsupials over placentals can be identified, that no route supplies a koala with eucalypts.

That leaves a genuine open puzzle inside the presupposition. Of the resolutions proposed, the strongest is an early end to the Flood's deposit, at or near the Cretaceous–Paleogene, with the Cenozoic marsupial fossils of South America, Antarctica and Australia read as the post-Flood route: it alone puts fossils on a route, but the route is the one ancient geography predicts, its first leg and its Antarctic crossing are unaccounted for, and it moves the tension onto the timescale of the Cenozoic rocks, where it is weighed on the sediment page. Rafting and Ice Age land bridges each cover part of the pattern and not the selective whole. Rapid diversification within kinds lightens the load without supplying the routes. Divine direction is available and not refuted here, but it retires the prediction rather than satisfying it. An older date does not reach the problem, and a regional Flood resolves it only by leaving the presupposition. None of the resolutions yet discharges the tension.
