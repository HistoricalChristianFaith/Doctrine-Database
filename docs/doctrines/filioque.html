The Filioque (the procession of the Holy Spirit — “and the Son”)

The creed the whole church confesses says the Holy Spirit “proceedeth from the Father” (John 15:26). The Latin West, over centuries, came to recite it with two more words — Filioque, “and the Son” — so that the Spirit proceeds “from the Father and the Son.” The Greek East never added them, and by the ninth century was calling the addition a heresy and an illegal tampering with an ecumenical creed. This one clause became the sharpest doctrinal fault line between the two halves of Christendom, cited in the mutual excommunications of 1054 and unresolved at every reunion attempt since. The live question this timeline traces is twofold: is double procession a faithful drawing-out of what Scripture and the early fathers taught about the Spirit’s relation to the Son, or a Latin novelty that confuses the persons of the Godhead — and, quite apart from the doctrine, was the West ever entitled to put it into the creed unilaterally?

The witnesses show a real development, and one in which the two traditions did not begin as far apart as they ended. The earliest language on both sides is a shared instinct that the Spirit is somehow “of the Son” and reaches the world through him: Tertullian already says the Spirit is “from the Father through the Son,” and the Greek Cappadocians — Basil, Gregory of Nazianzus, Gregory of Nyssa — order the Godhead Father → Son → Spirit, the Spirit coming forth from the Father alone but through the Son who stands “between.” Even Cyril of Alexandria, a Greek, calls the Spirit “proper to the Son” and poured out “through the Son” — language the Latins would later quarry. The decisive Latin step is Augustine, who taught that the Spirit proceeds principaliter (principally) from the Father, yet also from the Son, as the mutual love and bond of the two.

From doctrine the story turns to the creed. In Visigothic Spain the words entered the recited creed at Toledo, to seal the kingdom’s victory over Arianism; the East, meanwhile, fixed its own synthesis in John of Damascus — from the Father, through the Son, but never “from the Son,” “for the Father alone is cause.” Between the two, Maximus the Confessor (c. 645) had already offered the classic reconciliation — that the Latin “and the Son” makes the Son not a second cause of the Spirit but the one through whom the Spirit comes forth — the bridge text both sides would cite ever after. The Frankish court of Charlemagne pressed the addition on a reluctant Rome (Pope Leo III agreed with the doctrine but hung the creed without the words on silver shields in St. Peter’s); Photios of Constantinople answered with the classic Eastern refutation, that the Filioque puts two causes into the Trinity and destroys the Father’s monarchy; and the last serious attempt at reunion, the Council of Florence (1439), defined double procession “as from one principle and a single spiration” — a union that collapsed when one Greek bishop, Mark of Ephesus, refused to sign. Timeline

Tertullian (c. 213)

Tertullian, writing in Latin against the modalist Praxeas, gives the West its earliest procession-language — and it already routes the Spirit through the Son. He believes “the Spirit to proceed from no other source than from the Father through the Son.”1 In his chain of analogies the Spirit stands “third from God and the Son; just as the fruit of the tree is third from the root… or as the apex of the ray is third from the sun” — the Trinity “flowing down from the Father through intertwined and connected steps.”2 This is not yet the developed Filioque, but it is the seed the Latin tradition would grow: the Son is somehow on the path by which the Spirit comes.

Basil of Caesarea (c. 375)

Basil, eldest of the Cappadocians, sets the Eastern grammar: an ordered Godhead in which the Father is sole first cause and the Son the one through whom he acts. There is “the original cause of all things… the Father; of the creative cause, the Son; of the perfecting cause, the Spirit,” so that the knowledge of God runs “from One Spirit through the One Son to the One Father, and conversely… from the Father through the Only-begotten to the Spirit.”3 The Spirit is conjoined “to the one Father through the one Son” — a “through the Son” (dia tou Huiou) the East would insist is the whole of what the Son contributes, and the West would read as an early witness to its own doctrine.

Gregory of Nazianzus (c. 380)

Gregory of Nazianzus, “the Theologian,” draws the sharp line the East would hold to: the Spirit’s distinguishing mark is Procession, and its source is named as the Father. The Father is “of no one”; the Son is begotten “of the Father”; and “the Holy Ghost is truly Spirit, coming forth from the Father indeed, but not after the manner of the Son, for it is not by Generation but by Procession.”4 The person’s origin is stated as “from the Father” and defined against the Son’s begetting, with no mention of the Son as a source of the Spirit’s being — the point later Eastern polemic would press.

Gregory of Nyssa (c. 385)

Gregory of Nyssa supplies the subtlest Cappadocian formula, and the one that comes nearest the Latin instinct while stopping short of it: the Son stands between the Father and the Spirit as a mediating cause, yet the Father remains the one Cause. One Person is “the Cause, and another is of the Cause; and again in that which is of the Cause we recognize another distinction. For one is directly from the first Cause, and another by that which is directly from the first Cause,” so that “the interposition of the Son… does not shut out the Spirit from His relation by way of nature to the Father.”5 The Spirit is caused, mediately, by way of the Son — but of the Father.

Augustine of Hippo (c. 419)

Augustine, in On the Trinity, takes the decisive Latin step and gives double procession its classic form. The Spirit is “neither of the Father alone, nor of the Son alone, but of both,” the “mutual love wherewith the Father and the Son reciprocally love one another” — and God the Father is “He from whom… the Holy Spirit principally proceeds. And therefore I have added the word principally, because we find that the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Son also.”6 The Father, by begetting the Son, gave him “that the same Holy Spirit should proceed from Him” too, so that the Spirit proceeds from the Father “principally… yet in common from both.”7 The word principaliter is the hinge of the whole later dispute: it lets the Latins hold both that the Spirit is from the Son and that the Father is still the primal source.

Cyril of Alexandria (c. 429)

Cyril, patriarch of Alexandria and the great Greek Christologist, uses language both sides would claim. Commenting on John, he writes that “albeit the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father, yet He comes through the Son, and is His Own; for all things are through the Son from the Father,” the Spirit “shed forth indeed from the Father, but through the Son Himself conveyed to the creature.”8 That the Spirit is “proper to” and “of the Son” the Latins read as double procession in Greek dress; that Cyril keeps the Father as the one from whom the Spirit “proceeds” and the Son as the one “through” whom he is given the Greeks read as their own dia tou Huiou. The ambiguity is the argument in miniature.

The Third Council of Toledo (589)

At Toledo the Spirit’s procession left the theologians’ pages and entered the recited creed. Convened by King Reccared to bring the Visigoths out of Arianism, the council had the Constantinopolitan Creed sung at the Eucharist in a form that reads “Credo in Spiritum Sanctum qui ex Patre Filioque procedit” — “who proceeds from the Father and the Son” — the first clear creedal trace of the addition, made to seal the Son’s co-equal divinity against the Arians.9 (Whether the words stood in the authentic acts of 589 or were entrenched by the later Toledan councils is disputed; either way the doctrine and its Spanish, anti-Arian motive are secure.) Those who first sang it very likely did not know they were adding anything to an ecumenical text — but the seed of the great controversy had been planted in the West’s liturgy.

Maximus the Confessor (c. 645)

Maximus the Confessor, a Greek monk and the last great theologian of the still-undivided church, wrote the one patristic text both sides would later invoke as the key to reconciliation. When Constantinople faulted a synodal letter of Pope Martin I for saying the Spirit “proceeds also from the Son,” Maximus answered in his Letter to Marinus (c. 645–646) that the Romans “have not made the Son the cause of the Spirit — they know in fact that the Father is the only cause of the Son and the Spirit, the one by begetting and the other by procession; but [they use this expression] in order to manifest the Spirit’s coming-forth (proienai) through him.”10 The Latin Filioque and the Greek dia tou Huiou, he argued, need not conflict: both confess the Father as sole cause (aitia), and both let the Spirit come through the Son. It became the single most-cited bridge text in the whole controversy — adduced at Florence and in modern Catholic–Orthodox dialogue — though the procession section has itself been questioned as a possible later interpolation, and, on the East’s own terms, reconciles “from the Son” only as manifestation, not as a second origin.

John of Damascus (c. 743)

John of Damascus, the last of the Greek fathers, fixes the Eastern doctrine in the form it has held ever since — and it is a deliberate wall against the Filioque. The Spirit “proceedeth from the Father’s essence… not having been generated but simply proceeding,” “proceeding from the Father and communicated through the Son.”11 He then states the distinction the whole East would rest on: “we do not speak of the Spirit as from the Son: but yet we call Him the Spirit of the Son… we speak also of the Spirit of the Son, not as through proceeding from Him, but as proceeding through Him from the Father. For the Father alone is cause.”12 “Of the Son” and “through the Son”: yes; “from the Son” as a source: never.

Charlemagne, the Council of Aachen (809), and Pope Leo III

The controversy became a public quarrel under Charlemagne. When Greek monks in Jerusalem accused Frankish monks of heresy for chanting the creed with the Filioque (c. 808), Charlemagne’s Council of Aachen (809) affirmed the doctrine and sent envoys to press Rome to insert the words into the creed itself. Pope Leo III refused: he agreed with the doctrine but held that the ecumenical councils had “forbidden any addition to be made to their formulary,” and to preserve the original text he set up in St. Peter’s two silver shields engraved with the creed — one in Latin, one in Greek — without the addition.13 Rome would not chant the interpolated creed until 1014, under Benedict VIII. The West had split its own mind: the doctrine was received, the creedal addition resisted by the very see that later became its champion.

Photios of Constantinople (c. 867–886)

Photios, patriarch of Constantinople, wrote the refutation that framed the Eastern case for the next thousand years. In his Mystagogy of the Holy Spirit he argues from John 15:26 that the Spirit proceeds from the Father alone, and that the second ecumenical council “directly dogmatised that the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father”; the Filioque, by contrast, splits the one source in two: “if two causes are discerned in the divine, sovereign, and transcendent Trinity… then where is the much-hymned, divine majesty of the Monarchy?”14 In his encyclical of 867 he had already denounced the addition as grounds for “a thousand anathemas.” With Photios the dispute is no longer about a devotional phrase but about the structure of the Godhead and the legitimacy of touching the creed.

The Council of Florence (1439)

The last great reunion attempt, the Council of Florence, tried to dissolve the quarrel by declaring the two languages equivalent. Its Bull of Union with the Greeks, Laetentur Caeli (6 July 1439), defined that the Holy Spirit “proceeds from both eternally as from one principle and a single spiration,” and that when the fathers say the Spirit proceeds “from the Father through the Son,” this signifies the Son too — “according to the Greeks indeed as cause, and according to the Latins as principle.”15 One principle, not two, was the Latin answer to Photios. The Greek delegation signed — all but one. Mark of Ephesus (Mark Eugenikos) refused, and his lone dissent was enough: the union was repudiated in the East almost at once, and the fault line the timeline opened with has never closed.16

Arguments

The controversy folds two separable disputes into one, and both are weighed below. Whether double procession is itself true — the doctrine, apart from the creed — is the question the two grouped arguments take up. Whether the West was ever entitled to put the words into an ecumenical creed unilaterally — a question not of the doctrine but of authority and procedure — is weighed by the standalone argument that follows them.

Related arguments — is double procession sound?

The Eastern case against the doctrine runs on two prongs, and they bite at different levels: an exegetical one (does the Gospel text state the Spirit’s origin in a way that shuts out the Son?) and a systematic one (does adding the Son wreck the Father’s unique standing as sole cause of the Godhead?). Each is weighed on its own page.

Does John 15:26’s “who proceedeth from the Father” exclude procession from the Son? (assessment: contested)

The exegetical crux. The verse teaches the Spirit’s procession “from the Father,” and the origin-verb ekporeuetai is nowhere predicated of the Son — so the Filioque is at best an inference, not a quoted text. But the verse lacks the word “alone,” and the same discourse gives the Son “all things that the Father hath” (John 16:15): strong against reading double procession out of the verse, weak as a bar against inferring it from the wider witness.17

Does the Filioque introduce “two causes” and destroy the Father’s monarchy? (assessment: contested)

The systematic objection, independent of any proof-text: Photios’s charge that a Spirit proceeding also from the Son posits a second cause (duo aitia) in the Godhead and so destroys the monarchy of the Father, “the Father alone is cause.” The crude form is met by the Latin “one principle and a single spiration” (the Son spirating only by power received from the Father, who stays sole ultimate cause) — but Lossky’s deeper worry, that a shared principle grounds unity in the essence rather than the Father’s person, survives it.18

Was the West entitled to add the Filioque to the creed unilaterally? (the canonical question) (assessment: contested)

The procedural question, which concedes the doctrine and contests only the manner of the change: could Western synods, and finally Rome, insert two words into the ecumenical creed with no ecumenical council and no Eastern consent? On the bare facts the East is right — the addition was unilateral, and the West’s own Pope Leo III refused the creedal insertion “on the express ground that the General Councils had forbidden any addition to be made to their formulary.” The strongest Latin replies (that Ephesus canon 7 barred a rival “other faith,” not a clarifying expansion; that the East itself recites a creed Constantinople expanded in 381) answer the objection’s letter but not its core — that expansion was conciliar, which is the difference the East is pressing. Left unresolved is the papal-vs.-conciliar question the canon presupposes rather than settles — though even Rome now recites the creed in its un-interpolated Greek form alongside the Orthodox.19
