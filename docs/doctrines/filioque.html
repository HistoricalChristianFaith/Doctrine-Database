The Filioque (the procession of the Holy Spirit — “and the Son”)

The Niceno-Constantinopolitan Creed confesses the Holy Spirit “who proceedeth from the Father,” the wording of John 15:26. From the late sixth or the seventh century (589 in the received acts of the Third Council of Toledo; 653 on the dating of A. E. Burn, Knut Schäferdiek and Shawn S. Smith) churches of the Latin West came to recite it with one more word, Filioque, “and the Son,” so that the Spirit proceeds “from the Father and the Son”; the Greek churches did not adopt the addition. This timeline records the dated witnesses to two related questions: how the Spirit’s procession stands to the Son, and whether the words could be added to the creed.

Among the earlier witnesses, Tertullian writes that the Spirit proceeds “from the Father through the Son”; the Cappadocians Basil, Gregory of Nazianzus and Gregory of Nyssa write that the Spirit is from the Father and is joined to him through the Son; Augustine teaches that the Spirit proceeds principaliter (principally) from the Father and also from the Son; and Cyril of Alexandria calls the Spirit the Son’s “Own” and poured out “through the Son.”

The later entries concern the creed and the controversy over it: the creed with the addition in the received acts of Toledo (589); Maximus the Confessor’s letter (c. 645) explaining the Roman usage to the Greeks; John of Damascus’s “through the Son” and “the Father alone is cause”; the Frankish request to add the words at Rome and Pope Leo III’s refusal (809–810); Photios of Constantinople’s objections to the doctrine and to the addition; and the Council of Florence (1439), which defined procession “as from one principle and a single spiration” and which Mark of Ephesus refused to sign. Timeline

Tertullian (c. 213)

Tertullian, writing in Latin against the modalist Praxeas, states that he believes “the Spirit to proceed from no other source than from the Father through the Son.”1 In his chain of analogies the Spirit stands “third from God and the Son; just as the fruit of the tree is third from the root… or as the apex of the ray is third from the sun” — the Trinity “flowing down from the Father through intertwined and connected steps.”2

Basil of Caesarea (c. 375)

Basil, in On the Holy Spirit, describes God’s action toward creation as from the Father, through the Son, perfected in the Spirit: “the original cause of all things… the Father; of the creative cause, the Son; of the perfecting cause, the Spirit”; and he writes that the knowledge of God runs “from One Spirit through the One Son to the One Father, and conversely… from the Father through the Only-begotten to the Spirit.”3 The Spirit is “conjoined… to the one Father through the one Son” (dia tou Huiou, “through the Son”).

Gregory of Nazianzus (c. 380)

Gregory of Nazianzus, “the Theologian,” distinguishes the Persons by their origin: the Spirit’s mark is Procession, and its source is named as the Father. The Father is “of no one”; the Son is begotten “of the Father”; and “the Holy Ghost is truly Spirit, coming forth from the Father indeed, but not after the manner of the Son, for it is not by Generation but by Procession.”4 The Spirit’s origin is stated as “from the Father” and defined against the Son’s begetting.4

Gregory of Nyssa (c. 385)

Gregory of Nyssa, writing to Ablabius, distinguishes the Persons by cause and caused, with the Son standing between the Father and the Spirit. One Person is “the Cause, and another is of the Cause; and again in that which is of the Cause we recognize another distinction. For one is directly from the first Cause, and another by that which is directly from the first Cause,” so that “the interposition of the Son… does not shut out the Spirit from His relation by way of nature to the Father.”5

Augustine of Hippo (c. 419)

Augustine, in On the Trinity, teaches that the Spirit proceeds from the Father and from the Son. The Spirit is “neither of the Father alone, nor of the Son alone, but of both,” the “mutual love wherewith the Father and the Son reciprocally love one another” — and God the Father is “He from whom… the Holy Spirit principally proceeds. And therefore I have added the word principally, because we find that the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Son also.”6 The Father, by begetting the Son, gave him “that the same Holy Spirit should proceed from Him” too, so that the Spirit proceeds from the Father “principally… yet in common from both.”7

Cyril of Alexandria (c. 429)

Cyril, patriarch of Alexandria, commenting on John, writes that “albeit the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father, yet He comes through the Son, and is His Own; for all things are through the Son from the Father,” the Spirit “shed forth indeed from the Father, but through the Son Himself conveyed to the creature.”8 Cyril ties the verb “proceeds” to the Father and calls the Spirit the Son’s “Own,” given “through the Son.”8

The Third Council of Toledo (589)

In the received acts of Toledo the procession clause appears in a recited creed. Convened by King Reccared to bring the Visigoths out of Arianism, the council had the Constantinopolitan Creed sung at the Eucharist in a form that reads “Credo in Spiritum Sanctum qui ex Patre Filioque procedit” — “who proceeds from the Father and the Son”; Philip Schaff called it “the first clear trace of the Filioque in the Nicene Creed,” made “to seal the triumph of orthodoxy over Arianism.”9 A. E. Burn, Knut Schäferdiek and Shawn S. Smith regard the creed-words in the acts of 589 as a likely later interpolation and place the first secure creedal appearance at the Eighth Council of Toledo (653). Henry R. Percival held that “the Spanish Church was unaware that it had added to or changed the Constantinopolitan creed.”9

Maximus the Confessor (c. 645)

Maximus the Confessor, a Greek monk then in the West, wrote to Marinus of Cyprus about the Roman usage. When Constantinople faulted a Roman synodal letter (the translator’s bracket names Martin I; the translation’s reviser, Peter Gilbert, allows “perhaps, Pope Theodore I”) for saying the Spirit “proceeds also from the Son,” Maximus answered in his Letter to Marinus (c. 645–646) that the Romans “have not made the Son the cause of the Spirit — they know in fact that the Father is the only cause of the Son and the Spirit, the one by begetting and the other by procession; but [they use this expression] in order to manifest the Spirit’s coming-forth (proienai) through him.”10 The Latin Filioque and the Greek dia tou Huiou, he argued, need not conflict: both confess the Father as sole cause (aitia), and both let the Spirit come through the Son. The Pontifical Council for Promoting Christian Unity (1995) reads the letter thus: “According to St. Maximus, echoing Rome, the Filioque does not concern the ekporeusis of the Spirit issued from the Father as source of the Trinity, but manifests his proienai (processio) in the consubstantial communion of the Father and the Son.” A. Edward Siecienski (2006) reads it as “a clear explication of Maximus’s own trinitarian thinking and the consensus patrum as it existed in the seventh century (i.e., the procession of the Holy Spirit from the Father and his eternal flowing forth through the Son)”; ⚠ unverified: his later book (2010) is also reported to read it as reconciling “from the Son” only in the sense of proienai, not a double origin. The authenticity of the procession section has also been questioned (⚠ unverified: questioning scholars not yet identified).10

John of Damascus (c. 743)

John of Damascus, in An Exact Exposition of the Orthodox Faith, writes that the Spirit “proceedeth from the Father’s essence… not having been generated but simply proceeding,” “proceeding from the Father and communicated through the Son.”11 He then distinguishes: “we do not speak of the Spirit as from the Son: but yet we call Him the Spirit of the Son… we speak also of the Spirit of the Son, not as through proceeding from Him, but as proceeding through Him from the Father. For the Father alone is cause.”12 He affirms “of the Son” and “through the Son” and declines to speak of the Spirit “as from the Son.”12

Charlemagne, the Council of Aachen (809), and Pope Leo III

The controversy became a public quarrel under Charlemagne. When Greek monks in Jerusalem accused Frankish monks of heresy for chanting the creed with the Filioque (c. 808), Charlemagne’s Council of Aachen (809) affirmed the doctrine and sent envoys to press Rome to insert the words into the creed itself. Pope Leo III declined to add them. Henry R. Percival (1899) reports that he opposed the insertion “on the express ground that the General Councils had forbidden any addition to be made to their formulary”; Anthony Maas (1909) that he “approved of the doctrine conveyed by the Filioque, but gave the advice to omit the expression in the Creed”; the Pontifical Council for Promoting Christian Unity (1995) that he acted “in his anxiety to preserve unity with the Orient in the confession of faith.” He set up in St. Peter’s two silver shields engraved with the creed — one in Latin, one in Greek — without the addition.13 The creed with the Filioque was first sung at a papal Mass in 1014, under Benedict VIII.13

Photios of Constantinople (c. 867–886)

Photios, patriarch of Constantinople, wrote against both the doctrine and the addition. In his Mystagogy of the Holy Spirit he argues from John 15:26 that the Spirit proceeds from the Father alone, and that the second ecumenical council “directly dogmatised that the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father”; against the Filioque he asks: “if two causes are discerned in the divine, sovereign, and transcendent Trinity… then where is the much-hymned, divine majesty of the Monarchy?”14 In his encyclical of 867 he had already denounced the addition as grounds for “a thousand anathemas.” Whether the councils’ ban on “a different faith” forbade the addition is set out among the disputed readings.

The Council of Florence (1439)

The Council of Florence, meeting with Greek representatives, set out the Greek and Latin formulas as one faith. Its Bull of Union with the Greeks, Laetentur Caeli (6 July 1439), defined that the Holy Spirit “proceeds from both eternally as from one principle and a single spiration,” and that when the fathers say the Spirit proceeds “from the Father through the Son,” this signifies the Son too — “according to the Greeks indeed as cause, and according to the Latins as principle.” It also defined that “and from the Son” “was licitly and reasonably added to the creed.”15 Mark of Ephesus (Mark Eugenikos) refused to sign.16 The North American Orthodox–Catholic Theological Consultation (2003) records that the decisions of Florence were never received by a representative body in the East and were formally rejected in Constantinople in 1484.17

Disputed readings

How the conciliar ban on “a different faith” has been read in relation to the addition of the words to the creed.

  • Did the councils’ ban on “a different faith” (Ephesus, canon 7) forbid adding the Filioque to the creed? — turns on whether “different” means any addition or a contrary creed, and on who may add to or explain the common creed.18
