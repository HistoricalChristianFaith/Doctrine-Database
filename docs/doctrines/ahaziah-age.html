Ahaziah's Age: 22 or 42? (2 Kings 8:26 vs. 2 Chronicles 22:2)

Related doctrines: Hard passages — see also Who Killed Goliath? (2 Samuel 21:19) · How Tall Was Goliath? (six or four cubits) · Solomon's Stalls: 40,000 or 4,000? (1 Kings 4:26 vs. 2 Chronicles 9:25) · What Jesus Wrote on the Ground (John 8:6) · The Two Genealogies of Jesus (Matthew 1 vs. Luke 3) · The Death of Judas (Matthew 27 vs. Acts 1) · Naming the Wrong Prophet (Mark 1:2; Matthew 27:9) · Baptized for the Dead (1 Corinthians 15:29)

Two books of the Old Testament give the age of Ahaziah of Judah at his accession, and they disagree by twenty years. The Masoretic Hebrew of 2 Kings 8:26 makes him "two and twenty years old was Ahaziah when he began to reign."1 The parallel notice at 2 Chronicles 22:2 — the Chronicler retelling the same reign — makes him "forty and two years old… when he began to reign."2 Both notices agree that he reigned one year in Jerusalem and that his mother was Athaliah; only the age differs, by exactly twenty. This is a plain text-versus-text disagreement between two biblical books, of the same textual-criticism family as Solomon's forty-thousand-or-four-thousand stalls and the two slayers of Goliath.

Here, unlike Solomon's stalls, one of the two numbers is not merely improbable but impossible. The Chronicler himself has just reported that Ahaziah's father Jehoram "was thirty and two years old when he began to reign, and he reigned in Jerusalem eight years" (2 Chronicles 21:20 = 2 Kings 8:17) — so Jehoram died at forty. A forty-two-year-old Ahaziah would therefore be two years older than his own father.3 The "forty and two" cannot be Ahaziah's age, and no appeal to the miraculous rescues it: this is not a claim the supernatural could make true but a straightforward arithmetical contradiction inside the text.

The external witnesses converge on the smaller figure. The oldest translation of Chronicles, the Septuagint, does not carry "forty-two": its manuscripts read "twenty" (eíkosi), agreeing nearly with the "two-and-twenty" of Kings; the Syriac and Arabic versions read "twenty-two" outright; and 2 Kings 8:26 reads "twenty-two" in both the Hebrew and the Greek. Only the Masoretic Hebrew of Chronicles stands alone with "forty-two."4 So the mainstream verdict — carried by conservative and critical commentators alike — is that the "forty-two" of Chronicles is a scribal corruption of an original "twenty-two," the figure preserved by Kings and by the versions. The corruption is most naturally located in the way the numeral was written: whether in the later alphabetic numeral-letters, where "twenty" (כ) could be misread as "forty" (מ), as Keil & Delitzsch propose, or in the older stroke-and-hook notation of Ezra's day, as Gleason Archer proposes, a "twenty"-sign was misread as "forty."5

This page gathers the witnesses earliest first — the book of Kings that first gives "twenty-two," the Chronicler whose Masoretic text now reads "forty-two," the ancient versions that never had it, and the harmonizers and text-critics who reopened the count — and leaves the two sub-claims (is "forty-two" a scribal slip? does it instead count the years of a dynasty?) to be weighed under Arguments.

Timeline

The book of Kings (c. 560 BC)

The earliest witness to "twenty-two" is the book of Kings itself. 2 Kings 8:26 opens Ahaziah's brief reign: "Two and twenty years old was Ahaziah when he began to reign; and he reigned one year in Jerusalem."6 This is the older of the two accounts and the Chronicler's written source; its "twenty-two" is internally consistent with the same book's notice that Ahaziah's father died at forty (2 Kings 8:17), and it is the reading the ancient versions carry through to Chronicles.

The Chronicler (c. 400 BC)

Retelling Solomon's line from Kings, the Chronicler reaches Ahaziah at 2 Chronicles 22:2 — and the received Hebrew here reads "forty and two years old."7 On the mainstream reading his text originally matched his source's "twenty-two," and a later copyist inflated the numeral; the impossibility is telling, for the Chronicler is the same writer who two verses earlier has Jehoram die at forty, so he cannot have meant to make the son older than the father. Either way the "forty-two" is a slip in the transmission of Chronicles, not a rival tradition about Ahaziah's birth.

The ancient versions — Septuagint, Syriac, and Arabic (c. 150 BC onward)

The old translations never carry "forty-two." In the Septuagint's 2 Paralipomenon 22:2 Ahaziah is "twenty years old" (ὦn eἴkosi ἐtῶn), agreeing nearly with the Kings figure; the Syriac and the Arabic read "twenty-two" outright.8 The disagreement, then, is not between two ancient traditions but between the Masoretic Hebrew of Chronicles and everything else — Kings, the Greek, the Syriac, the Arabic. The "forty-two" is a peculiarity of one line of the Hebrew, and the versions preserve the number that was there before it crept in.

Matthew Poole (1685)

With the early-modern annotators the harmonizing begins. Matthew Poole, in his Annotations upon the Holy Bible, lays out both routes. He allows that the number may be a copyist's slip, but presses first the harmonization that keeps "forty-two" as written by referring it not to Ahaziah's age but to the age of the dynasty from which his mother came: "Omri, who reigned six years… Ahab his son reigned twenty-two years… Ahaziah his son two years… Joram his son twelve years; all which, put together, make up exactly these forty-two years."9 The first move is the classic "different thing being counted" harmonization — the years of the house of Omri, not the years of Ahaziah; the second concedes the textual reading that later criticism would prefer. It is weighed under Arguments.

Keil & Delitzsch (1872)

The nineteenth-century commentary that set the conservative-critical consensus states the verdict without hedging. Keil & Delitzsch, on 2 Chronicles 22:2, judge the figure a plain transcription error: "The number 42 is an orthographical error for 22… 2 Kings 8:26."10 They locate the slip in the Hebrew numeral-letters — "twenty" (כ) written or read as "forty" (מ) — and John Gill, on the same verse, reaches the same conclusion: "it seems best to acknowledge a mistake of the copier, which might easily be made through a similarity of the numeral letters, forty two, for twenty two." The camp that held Chronicles original in the two-slayers crux here holds Chronicles corrupt.

Gleason Archer (1982)

Twentieth-century conservative apologetics adopts the textual explanation as its own. Gleason Archer, in his Encyclopedia of Bible Difficulties, treats the pair as a model case of a numerical copying-error: the correct age is the twenty-two of Kings, and the "forty-two" of Chronicles arose in the older system of number-notation, where a smudged or flaked decade-mark could shift a figure by ten or by a factor of ten.11 Archer frames the slip not as a wound to Scripture but as the ordinary hazard of hand-copied numerals — recoverable precisely because the parallel in Kings, and the ancient versions, preserve the un-inflated figure.

Arguments

The sub-claims that cut across the timeline — not "who reported which number, and when," but "why does Chronicles read forty-two?" — are weighed adversarially on their own pages, each with an assessment of the reading it serves. The shared floor here is that everyone agrees a forty-two-year-old Ahaziah is impossible, since it would make him older than his father; what is disputed is whether the fix is textual (a corrupted numeral, the original being "twenty-two") or harmonistic (the "forty-two" counts something other than Ahaziah's age, so the letter of the text stands).

Related arguments — reconciling the number

Everyone grants that Ahaziah cannot literally have been forty-two; the two advocated fixes divide over where the trouble lies. One is textual — the "forty-two" is a copyist's inflation of an original "twenty-two," so only the numeral is wrong and the two books never disagreed about history. The other is harmonistic — the "forty-two" is a real figure that counts not Ahaziah's age but the years of the royal house of Omri (or, in a variant, the age of his mother Athaliah), so both texts stand as written. Each is weighed on its own page.

Is the "forty-two" of 2 Chronicles 22:2 a scribal corruption of an original "twenty-two" (preserved by 2 Kings 8:26)? — sound

The mainstream verdict: the arithmetical impossibility (a son older than his father), the surviving "twenty-two" of Kings, the unanimous witness of the ancient versions (the Septuagint's "twenty," the Syriac and Arabic "twenty-two"), and a credible numeral-confusion mechanism all converge on an inflated numeral in the Masoretic text of Chronicles. It runs cleaner than its Solomon's-stalls cousin: here the older Greek does not split — it too supports the smaller figure — so the original reading is not in real doubt (sound).

Does the "forty-two" count the years of the house of Omri rather than Ahaziah's age? — weak

The older harmonizer's route (Poole, Jamieson–Fausset–Brown, and Jewish reckonings after them): the "forty-two" is not Ahaziah's personal age but the summed reign-years of the Omride dynasty into which his mother married, so the verse stands as written. But the Hebrew plainly says Ahaziah "was a son of forty-two years," the same idiom Kings uses for his personal age; no such dynastic total is signposted in the text; the arithmetic is assembled after the fact to reach forty-two; and the reading has to explain away the unanimous versional witness to "twenty/twenty-two" that marks the number as corrupt (weak).
