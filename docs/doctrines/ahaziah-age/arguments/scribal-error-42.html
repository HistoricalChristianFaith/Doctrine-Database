Is the "forty-two" of 2 Chronicles 22:2 a scribal corruption of an original "twenty-two"?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: sound that the "forty-two" is a corrupted numeral and 2 Kings 8:26 preserves the original "twenty-two" — the arithmetic, the parallel, and the unanimous versions converge, and (unlike the Solomon's-stalls cousin) the early witnesses do not split · advanced by Keil & Delitzsch, Gleason Archer, and virtually all modern commentators.

Related arguments: reconciling the number — see also Does the "forty-two" count the years of the house of Omri, not Ahaziah's age?

The claim

"The number 42 is an orthographical error for 22… 2 Kings 8:26."

— Keil & Delitzsch, on 2 Chronicles 22:2, link. The interpretation: the "forty and two years" of 2 Chronicles 22:2 is not a real figure but a transcription error that inflated an original "twenty-two" — the age Kings gives, the age the ancient versions carry, and the only age consistent with Ahaziah's father dying at forty — so the two biblical books do not actually disagree about history, only about a corrupted numeral in the transmitted text of Chronicles.1

Who advances it

It is the mainstream verdict, conservative and critical alike. Keil & Delitzsch (1872) call it an orthographical error in the numeral-letters; John Gill "acknowledge[s] a mistake of the copier… through a similarity of the numeral letters"; Gleason Archer (1982) grounds it in the older stroke-and-hook number-notation; and Barnes, the Cambridge Bible, the Pulpit Commentary, and Ellicott concur that the figure "cannot be correct" and read "twenty-two."2 It stands against the older harmonization — associated with Matthew Poole and Jamieson–Fausset–Brown — that keeps "forty-two" by making it count the years of a dynasty.

The evidence for

The arithmetical impossibility. This is the load-bearing point, and it is decisive in a way the Solomon's-stalls disproportion is not. Ahaziah's father Jehoram became king at thirty-two and reigned eight years (2 Chronicles 21:20 = 2 Kings 8:17), dying at forty. A son of forty-two would be two years older than his own father — not merely improbable but impossible. The "forty-two" therefore cannot be Ahaziah's age, whatever else it might be.3

A surviving un-inflated witness. 2 Kings 8:26 — the older account and the Chronicler's source — reads "two and twenty years old," with the same one-year reign and the same mother, Athaliah. The smaller number is not a conjecture but an attested biblical reading, and the one internally consistent with the father's death at forty.4

The unanimous versions. The old translations of Chronicles never carry "forty-two": the Septuagint reads "twenty" (eἴkosi), the Syriac and Arabic "twenty-two." Together with the Hebrew and Greek of Kings, every independent line of the tradition gives a figure in the low twenties; only the Masoretic Hebrew of Chronicles stands alone with "forty-two." That distribution is the fingerprint of a corruption confined to one line, not a genuine ancient variant.5

A credible mechanism. Copied numerals drift, and here two plausible routes converge on the same result: a confusion of the alphabetic numeral-letters (twenty, כ, for forty, מ), as Keil & Delitzsch propose, or a lost or added decade-mark in the older stroke-and-hook notation, as Archer proposes. The two theories disagree about how the slip happened but agree that a low-twenties figure became "forty-two."6

Adversarial assessment

What holds. That "forty-two" is a corruption and "twenty-two" the original is as close to certain as these numeral cruxes get. The arithmetic makes "forty-two" impossible as an age; the parallel in Kings and the unanimous versions preserve the smaller figure; and a credible mechanism is available. The two biblical books are not in real historical conflict; the difficulty lies in the transmitted numeral of one of them.

Cleaner than its Solomon's-stalls cousin. On Solomon's forty thousand stalls the case for the smaller number is only sound-with-caveats, because the oldest Greek is divided and the exact original stays open. Here neither limit applies: no witness outside the Masoretic Hebrew of Chronicles carries "forty-two," and the smaller figure is not just better-proportioned but the only possible one. So the assessment is firmer — the original reading is not genuinely in doubt.

The standing rival is weaker. The chief alternative — that "forty-two" counts the reign-years of the house of Omri, not Ahaziah's age — is genuinely advocated (Poole, Jamieson–Fausset–Brown) but does not survive the text: the Hebrew says Ahaziah "was a son of forty-two years," the ordinary idiom for a person's own age; the verse signposts no dynastic total; and the reading must still explain away the unanimous versional witness to the low-twenties figure. It is weighed on its own page and rated weak.7

What is a genuine (minor) uncertainty. It is the fact of corruption and the low-twenties figure that are settled, not the precise original digit: the Septuagint reads "twenty," Kings and the Syriac "twenty-two." That is a slip within the smaller number, not a reopening of "forty-two." And the exact copying mechanism is a reconstruction — the received text spells the number out in words — but the conclusion does not rest on any one mechanism; it rests on the impossibility and the versions.8

Assessment

Sound. On its load-bearing claim — that the "forty-two" of 2 Chronicles 22:2 is a corrupted numeral and 2 Kings 8:26 preserves the original "twenty-two" — the reading is near-certain: the arithmetic makes "forty-two" impossible, the parallel and the unanimous ancient versions preserve the smaller figure, and a credible mechanism is available. The competing dynasty-reckoning harmonization does not hold. Unlike the Solomon's-stalls crux, the early external witnesses do not split, so the original reading is not in real doubt; the only residual uncertainty is whether the original digit was "twenty" or "twenty-two," a question that leaves the verdict against "forty-two" untouched. Rate the scribal-error reading sound.
