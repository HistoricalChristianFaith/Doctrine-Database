Matthew Poole on Ahaziah's age

Dates: c. 1685 (the Annotations upon the Holy Bible, published posthumously) · Wikipedia
Summary: The early-modern annotator who lays out both routes. Poole presses first the harmonization that keeps "forty-two" as written — reading it as the summed reign-years of the house of Omri, not Ahaziah's age — and secondarily concedes that the number may be a copyist's slip.

Claims & evidence

The "forty-two" as the years of the house of Omri

"Omri, who reigned six years… Ahab his son reigned twenty-two years… Ahaziah his son two years… Joram his son twelve years; all which, put together, make up exactly these forty-two years."

— Annotations upon the Holy Bible, on 2 Chronicles 22:2. Poole reads the "forty-two" not as Ahaziah's personal age but as the age of "that royal race and family from which by his mother he was descended" — the house of Omri — whose reigns, added up, reach forty-two years by the time Ahaziah takes the throne. On this reading 2 Kings 8:26 gives Ahaziah's age (twenty-two) and 2 Chronicles 22:2 the age of the dynasty, so both texts stand as written.

The copyist-error option, conceded

Poole does not rest the whole weight on the dynasty-reckoning. Like his near-contemporary John Gill on the same verse — "it seems best to acknowledge a mistake of the copier, which might easily be made through a similarity of the numeral letters, forty two, for twenty two" — he allows that the figure may simply be a transcription slip. The two options he canvasses are exactly the two later criticism would sort out: harmonize the letter of the text, or emend the numeral.

Notes

  • The classic "different thing being counted" move. Poole's dynasty-reckoning is the same shape as the "stables-versus-stalls" harmonization on Solomon's stalls: rather than admit an error, it keeps both numbers by having them count different things (there, buildings vs. partitions; here, a person's years vs. a dynasty's). Whether it holds is weighed on the dynasty-reckoning argument page and rated weak — the Hebrew says Ahaziah "was a son of forty-two years," the plain idiom for his own age.
  • A witness to the difficulty's antiquity. That a careful seventeenth-century harmonizer reached for an elaborate dynastic sum shows the problem was felt long before modern text-criticism — and that the copyist-error reading was already on the table as the alternative. Jamieson–Fausset–Brown would later carry the same dynasty-reckoning; Keil & Delitzsch and Archer the copyist-error verdict.
