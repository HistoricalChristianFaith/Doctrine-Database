Matthew Poole on Ahaziah's age

Dates: c. 1685 (the Annotations upon the Holy Bible, published posthumously) · Wikipedia
Summary: Poole gives two answers to the difficulty. The first keeps "forty-two" as written, reading it as the summed reign-years of the house of Omri (or, as an alternative, Athaliah's age) rather than Ahaziah's age; the second reports those who "acknowledge an error in the transcribers" and sets out the versional evidence for it.

Claims & evidence

The "forty-two" as the years of the house of Omri

"Omri, who reigned six years… Ahab his son reigned twenty-two years… Ahaziah his son two years… Joram his son twelve years; all which, put together, make up exactly these forty-two years."

— Annotations upon the Holy Bible, on 2 Chronicles 22:2. Poole reads the "forty-two" not as Ahaziah's personal age but as the age of "that royal race and family from which by his mother he was descended" — the house of Omri — whose reigns, added up, reach forty-two years by the time Ahaziah takes the throne. He first allows that the phrase may instead give "the age of his mother Athaliah." On this reading 2 Kings 8:26 gives Ahaziah's age (twenty-two) and 2 Chronicles 22:2 the age of the dynasty, so both texts stand as written.

The copyist-error answer

"Some acknowledge an error in the transcribers of the present Hebrew copies, in which language the numeral letters for twenty-two and forty-two are so like, that they might easily be mistaken."

— Annotations upon the Holy Bible, on 2 Chronicles 22:2, "Answ. 2." Poole cites for this answer "divers ancient Greek copies," the Syriac and Arabic, and the Antioch Syriac copy transcribed for Archbishop Ussher, and holds that it does not "overthrow the authority of the sacred text," since "the question here is not whether this text be true, but which is the true reading of the text." John Gill, on the same verse, takes this answer: "it seems best to acknowledge a mistake of the copier, which might easily be made through a similarity of the numeral letters, forty two, for twenty two."

Reception and debate

  • Comparison with Solomon's stalls. Poole's dynasty-reckoning has the same shape as the "stables-versus-stalls" harmonization on Solomon's stalls: it keeps both numbers by having them count different things (there, buildings vs. partitions; here, a person's years vs. a dynasty's). Gill, on the same verse, objects to the dynastic reckoning: "how impertinent must the use of such a date be in the account of the reign of a king of Judah? all that can be said is, his mother was of that family, which is a trifling reason for such an unusual method of reckoning." The readings are set out on the disputed reading page.
  • Other holders of the two answers. Gill reports the dynastic reckoning from "the Jewish chronology," footnoting Seder Olam Rabba and Gersonides; Jamieson–Fausset–Brown give the same reckoning ("the forty-second year of the kingdom of his mother's family"), citing Lightfoot. Keil & Delitzsch and Archer hold the copyist-error reading.
