Olaus Magnus on Dragons, Unicorns & Mythical Beasts

Dates: c. 1555 (the Historia de Gentibus Septentrionalibus, Rome; Olaus Magnus, 1490–1557) · Wikipedia
Summary: The exiled Swedish archbishop and cartographer supplies this timeline's one sea-serpent witness: in his great compendium of the northern peoples (1555) he reports a monstrous Norwegian sea-serpent as sober regional natural history, on the sworn agreement of the coast's mariners — then folds in a portent-superstition that keeps the report straddling the line between natural history and legend.

Claims & evidence

A 200-foot Norwegian sea-serpent, offered as natural history on the mariners' testimony

They who in Works of Navigation, on the Coasts of Norway, employ themselves in fishing or Merchandise, do all agree in this strange story, that there is a Serpent there which is of a vast magnitude, namely 200 foot long, and moreover 20 foot thick; and is wont to live in Rocks and Caves toward the Sea-coast about Berge: which will go alone from his holes in a clear night, in Summer, and devour Calves, Lambs, and Hogs, or else he goes into the Sea to feed on Polypus, Locusts, and all sorts of Sea-Crabs. He hath commonly hair hanging from his neck a Cubit long, and sharp Scales, and is black, and he hath flaming shining eyes.

— Historia de Gentibus Septentrionalibus (Rome, 1555), Book XXI, ch. 43, in the first English translation, A Compendious History of the Goths, Swedes, & Vandals, and Other Northern Nations (London: J. Streater, 1658), Book 21, ch. 43; reproduced from the 1658 Streater translation in Joseph Nigg, "Olaus Magnus' Sea Serpent" (The Public Domain Review). The original 1658 edition is digitized in Early English Books Online (Univ. of Michigan). Olaus offers the creature not as a figure of Scripture nor as an avowed fable but as the settled testimony of Norway's fishermen and merchants — the same register of first-person natural history in which Marco Polo had catalogued the "great serpents" of Yunnan and in which Athanasius Kircher would, a century later, defend even the winged dragon. The vast dimensions ("200 foot long") are the mark of the report-into-legend inflation the page diagnoses elsewhere: a real, alarming sea-animal — a decomposing basking-shark carcass, an oarfish, a line of surfacing porpoises — imperfectly seen and vividly retold into a single "great Serpent."

The sighting as a political omen — where the report shades into legend

This Snake disquiets the Shippers, and he puts up his head on high like a pillar, and catcheth away men, and he devours them; and this hapneth not, but it signifies some wonderful change of the Kingdom near at hand; namely that the Princes shall die, or be banished; or some Tumultuous Wars shall presently follow.

— Compendious History of the Goths, Swedes, & Vandals (1658 trans.), Book 21, ch. 43, continuing; reproduced in Nigg, "Olaus Magnus' Sea Serpent" (The Public Domain Review). Here the account leaves plain zoology behind: the serpent's appearance is read as a prodigy foretelling the death or exile of princes and the outbreak of war — the 16th-century portent-lore that the wiki's "sift legend from fact" readers (Jerome, John of Damascus) were working to strip out. It is this omen-frame, laid over an otherwise naturalistic report, that keeps Olaus's sea-serpent half in the doctrine's legend strand rather than squarely with the sober sightings of Sozomen and Marco Polo.

Notes

  • Why he sits on the literal / natural-history strand. Olaus does not interpret a verse or knowingly retell a fable; he transmits what he takes to be a real animal known to the Norwegian coast, guaranteed by the collective testimony of its seafarers. That places him with Herodotus, Ammianus, John of Damascus, Marco Polo, and Kircher — the stream of writers who catalogue great serpents as fact — and he extends that stream, for the first time on this page, into the sea, filling the "sea-serpent reports" the doctrine's summary and legend section both name as in scope.
  • Report or legend? Honestly, both. The zoological core (dimensions aside) is a natural-history report of the kind that so often has a real animal behind it; but the portent-conclusion, and the frankly impossible "200 foot" length, pull it toward the legend pole shared by the Beowulf fire-drake and the Tombstone winged monster. He is best read as the sea-going hinge between the two strands.
  • The Carta Marina. Olaus's sea-monsters are best known from the great map of the North he engraved at Venice in 1539 (the Carta Marina); the 1555 Historia is the prose commentary that supplied the map's many creatures with their descriptions. The sea-serpent (the "Soe Orm") is among the most-copied of them, passing into the maps and compendia of Ortelius, Münster, and Konrad Gesner.
