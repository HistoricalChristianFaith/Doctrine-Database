Olaus Magnus on Dragons, Unicorns & Mythical Beasts

Dates: c. 1555 (the Historia de Gentibus Septentrionalibus, Rome; Olaus Magnus, 1490–1557) · Wikipedia
Summary: The exiled Swedish archbishop and cartographer supplies this timeline's one sea-serpent witness: in his compendium of the northern peoples (1555) he reports a monstrous Norwegian sea-serpent as regional natural history, on the agreement of the coast's mariners, and then reads its appearance as a portent.

Claims & evidence

A 200-foot Norwegian sea-serpent, offered as natural history on the mariners' testimony

They who in Works of Navigation, on the Coasts of Norway, employ themselves in fishing or Merchandise, do all agree in this strange story, that there is a Serpent there which is of a vast magnitude, namely 200 foot long, and moreover 20 foot thick; and is wont to live in Rocks and Caves toward the Sea-coast about Berge: which will go alone from his holes in a clear night, in Summer, and devour Calves, Lambs, and Hogs, or else he goes into the Sea to feed on Polypus, Locusts, and all sorts of Sea-Crabs. He hath commonly hair hanging from his neck a Cubit long, and sharp Scales, and is black, and he hath flaming shining eyes.

— Historia de Gentibus Septentrionalibus (Rome, 1555), Book XXI, ch. 43, in the first English translation, A Compendious History of the Goths, Swedes, & Vandals, and Other Northern Nations (London: J. Streater, 1658), Book 21, ch. 43; reproduced from the 1658 Streater translation in Joseph Nigg, "Olaus Magnus' Sea Serpent" (The Public Domain Review). The original 1658 edition is digitized in Early English Books Online (Univ. of Michigan). Olaus offers the creature not as a figure of Scripture nor as an avowed fable but as the settled testimony of Norway's fishermen and merchants — the same register of first-person natural history in which Marco Polo had catalogued the "great serpents" of Yunnan and the register Athanasius Kircher also uses (1665) for the winged dragon.

The sighting as a political omen

This Snake disquiets the Shippers, and he puts up his head on high like a pillar, and catcheth away men, and he devours them; and this hapneth not, but it signifies some wonderful change of the Kingdom near at hand; namely that the Princes shall die, or be banished; or some Tumultuous Wars shall presently follow.

— Compendious History of the Goths, Swedes, & Vandals (1658 trans.), Book 21, ch. 43, continuing; reproduced in Nigg, "Olaus Magnus' Sea Serpent" (The Public Domain Review). Here the account turns from zoology to portent: the serpent's appearance is read as a prodigy foretelling the death or exile of princes and the outbreak of war, an omen-frame laid over a naturalistic report.

Notes

  • Placement on the timeline. Olaus does not interpret a verse or present a fable; he transmits what he takes to be a real animal known to the Norwegian coast, on the collective testimony of its seafarers. He reports the serpent as natural history, like Herodotus, Ammianus, John of Damascus, Marco Polo, and Kircher, and he is the page's one witness to a sea-serpent.
  • Report and portent. The account joins a natural-history report, given on the coast's testimony and with its 200-foot length, to a reading of the serpent's appearance as an omen of political change.
  • The Carta Marina. Olaus's sea-monsters are best known from the great map of the North he engraved at Venice in 1539 (the Carta Marina); the 1555 Historia is the prose commentary that supplied the map's many creatures with their descriptions. The sea-serpent (the "Soe Orm") is among the most-copied of them, passing into the maps and compendia of Ortelius, Münster, and Konrad Gesner.
