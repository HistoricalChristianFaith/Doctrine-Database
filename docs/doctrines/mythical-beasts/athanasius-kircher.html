Athanasius Kircher on dragons and mythical beasts

Dates: 1601–1680 · Mundus Subterraneus published 1665 · Wikipedia
Summary: The Jesuit polymath Athanasius Kircher, in the "De Draconibus" chapter of his vast survey of the underground world Mundus Subterraneus (Amsterdam, 1665), treats dragons as real, cataloguable creatures of natural history. He holds the existence of enormous serpents to be "entirely certain," defends even the disputed winged dragons against the scholars who call them imaginary, and adduces museum specimens and named naturalists' reports as evidence. He is the latest witness on the belief-timeline, writing in the 1660s.

Claims & evidence

The existence of enormous serpents is "entirely certain" — they are found everywhere

The existence of dragons, or rather serpents, of enormous size is entirely certain, for they are found everywhere, and most especially in Ethiopia, India, and in other places in the tropical zones under the present rule of various European maritime powers.

— Athanasius Kircher, Mundus Subterraneus (Amsterdam, 1665), "De Draconibus," trans. Darius Matthias Klein, "A Natural History of Dragons" (christianlatin.blogspot.com); original Latin edition scanned at archive.org (Tomus II). Kircher writes in the middle of the scientific revolution — a contemporary of Boyle and Newton, and himself an experimenter and geologist. On dragons he reads with the page's literal / natural-history strand: the great serpents are fauna of the hot zones, their existence for him a settled fact rather than a figure of Scripture or a piece of legend.

Winged dragons are real — the doubters are contradicted by histories and eyewitnesses alike

With regards to the winged dragons there is no agreement among the Authors, who for the most part deemed them to be imaginary. But they are contradicted both histories from all eras and eyewitness accounts as well. Winged dragons - small, large, and enormous - have occurred in every era in all parts of the globe; see Cardinal Barbarini, Book VII, Chapter 23, concerning winged dragons, which he himself claimed to have seen in the museum of Gulselm Asulica the Parisian. Here also we must make mention of the authoritative account of Bellon, who tells that he saw the complete bodies of winged dragons which he found in Egypt after a zealous search.

— Kircher, Mundus Subterraneus, "De Draconibus," trans. Klein, christianlatin.blogspot.com. Where, by his own account, most authors had judged the winged dragon imaginary, Kircher answers them with "histories from all eras and eyewitness accounts." His witness is the French naturalist Pierre Belon (Bellon, 1517–1564), who reported finding in Egypt the complete bodies of winged dragons — Kircher relays that one had "a distended belly, two feet, two wings anatomically similar to those of bats, and the tail of a serpent," a description that closely resembles Herodotus's bat-winged Arabian flying serpent. It is the same appeal to remains and eyewitnesses that Herodotus made ("I saw the back-bones and ribs of serpents") and that Sozomen and Marco Polo made for their beasts.

A stuffed dragon may still be seen in the Barberini museum at Rome

A similar dragon can still be seen at Rome in the Museum of Cardinal Francis Barbarini…

— Kircher, Mundus Subterraneus, "De Draconibus," trans. Klein, christianlatin.blogspot.com. Kircher points to a preserved specimen — a dragon stuffed and mounted in the collection of Cardinal Francesco Barberini, some fifteen palms in length, with two rows of teeth — as physical evidence of the kind.

Notes

  • Where he sits on the timeline. Kircher (1665) is the latest witness on the belief-timeline and, with Olaus Magnus (1555), one of its two early-modern voices. He belongs to the literal / natural-history strand — Herodotus, Ammianus, Augustine, Julian, Sozomen, John of Damascus, Marco Polo — reading the dragon as a real animal rather than an allegory of the devil or a passage of Scripture. He holds the literal-dragon view in the 1660s and supports it with specimens and eyewitness reports.
  • The "De Draconibus" digression. Most of Mundus Subterraneus treats geology, hydrology and volcanoes, and it is one of the first works of speleology. In the dragon chapter Kircher relays second- and third-hand narratives (the dragon of Rhodes slain by the knight Deodato of Gozon; a Swiss dragon episode) alongside measured specimens.
  • Translations differ. A widely circulated English wording of this winged-dragon passage ("Of winged dragons, dispute has only arisen between authors… Winged dragons — small, great, and greatest — have been produced in all times in every land") renders the same passage differently; Darius Matthias Klein's translation is quoted above. Kircher's "winged dragons" gather together Belon's Egyptian specimens, museum mounts, and classical reports.
