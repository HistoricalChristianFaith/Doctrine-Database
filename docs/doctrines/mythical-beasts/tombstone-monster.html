The Tombstone winged monster (1890)

Dates: the alleged sighting Sunday, April 20, 1890; reported six days later in the Tombstone Epitaph (Tombstone, Arizona Territory), Saturday, April 26, 1890, p. 3 · Library of Congress scan
Summary: A frontier newspaper item — famous ever since — in which two ranchers on horseback with Winchesters, the paper reports, chased and shot a winged, alligator-like "monster" on the desert between the Whetstone and Huachuca mountains, giving it dimensions of a 160-foot wingspan, an eight-foot head, and eyes like a dinner plate. No follow-up, specimen, photograph, or second witness is recorded, and the account is treated as a period tall tale by later writers on the case.1 It is set on this page among the dragons of legend, at the modern "living-pterosaur"/thunderbird end of the story.

The account

The article as it ran in the Epitaph

Under the headline "Found on the Desert" and the subhead "A Strange Winged Monster Discovered and Killed on the Huachuca Desert," the paper opens with the discovery:

A winged monster, resembling a huge alligator with an extremely elongated tail and an immense pair of wings, was found on the desert between the Whetstone and Huachuca mountains last Sunday by two ranchers who were returning home from the Huachucas. The creature was evidently greatly exhausted by a long flight and when discovered was able to fly but a short distance at a time. After the first shock of wild amazement had passed the two men, who were on horseback and armed with Winchester rifles, regained sufficient courage to pursue the monster and after an exciting chase of several miles succeeded in getting near enough to open fire with their rifles and wounding it. The creature then turned on the men, but owing to its exhausted condition they were able to keep out of its way and after a few well directed shots the monster partly rolled over and remained motionless.

— Tombstone Epitaph, April 26, 1890, p. 3, "Found on the Desert." The paper then gives the dead beast's measurements: it "measured about ninety-two feet in length and the greatest diameter was about fifty inches," had "only two feet" set just ahead of the wings, a head "about eight feet long, the jaws being thickly set with strong, sharp teeth," and eyes "as large as a dinner plate" that "protruded about half way from the head." One partly-folded wing, straightened out, gave "a measurement of seventy-eight feet, making the total length from tip to tip about 160 feet"; the wings were "composed of a thick and nearly transparent membrane and were devoid of feathers or hair, as was the entire body."2

The article's closing promise of proof

The report ends by staging its own verification — a wing-tip souvenir, a trip to town for supplies, and a plan to skin the carcass and ship the hide to the experts:

The men cut off a small portion of the tip of one wing and took it home with them. Late last night one of them arrived in this city for supplies and to make the necessary preparations to skin the creature, when the hide will be sent east for examination by the eminent scientists of the day.

— Tombstone Epitaph, April 26, 1890, p. 3.3 No follow-up article appeared in the Epitaph or any other paper, no hide is recorded as sent east or received by any scientist, and no second witness came forward.

Notes

  • Why it sits off the timeline. The witnesses on the belief-timeline offer their beasts either as fact (natural history, an eyewitness report they stood behind) or as a reading of Scripture. The Epitaph item is an anonymous newspaper story that its own paper never followed up. It is placed in the "dragon in legend" section of the summary alongside Beowulf's fire-drake, not among the datable witnesses.
  • The apocryphal "thunderbird photograph." The 1890 article contains no photograph — the Epitaph could not print photographs in 1890 — yet a durable legend holds that a picture once ran of the dead creature "nailed to a barn" with a row of men before it. That claim first surfaces only in 1963, in pulp magazine articles by Jack Pearl (Saga) and H. M. Cranmer (Fate); the Epitaph's own staff searched their archives in the 1960s and found nothing, and cryptozoologist Karl Shuker, who has tracked the case for decades, reports that every "thunderbird photo" circulated since has been exposed as a modern hoax (one a Revell pterosaur model kit, another a manipulation that left the original deer's hooves in the frame). Shuker argues that the remembered "photo" is a false memory, not a lost document.4
