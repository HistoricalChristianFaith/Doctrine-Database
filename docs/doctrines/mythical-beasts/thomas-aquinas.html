Thomas Aquinas on the rhinoceros, the elephant-and-whale, and the one-horned beast

Dates: c. 1274 (the biblical commentaries; Aquinas c. 1225–1274) · Wikipedia
Summary: Thomas Aquinas, the Dominican master whose commentaries fix the high-scholastic reading of Scripture, handles the great beasts along all three of this doctrine's axes at once — the second witness on the timeline, after Jerome, to do so — and does it by a disciplined method of stacking the senses in order: the literal creature first, on the best natural science he has (Aristotle, Pliny, Isidore), then the figure it serves set atop it. On the literal plane he is a sober naturalist: the re'em is a real rhinoceros, Job's Behemoth and Leviathan the two largest real animals (the elephant and the whale), and Isaiah's "flying" serpent flies "not because it has wings, but because it leaps." On the allegorical plane the same Job set-piece becomes "the metaphor of an elephant and a whale" for the devil, and the unicorn of the Psalms a figure of pride — the proud rulers of the Jews. And on the christological seam Ambrose had opened, the single horn carries the reading of the one God and the singular generation of Christ. He is the timeline's fullest medieval reader, the systematizer of a layered-sense method that Julian and Augustine had used less formally.

Claims & evidence

The literal creatures — rhinoceros, elephant, whale (Job 39:9; 40:15; 41:1)

At the descriptive base Aquinas is a plain zoologist working from the animal-science of his day. On God's question about the re'em (Job 39:9) he identifies a real, un-tameable beast:

He is compared to the rhinoceros or unicorn among wild animals, a very strong and fierce four-footed animal with one horn in the middle of his forehead. This animal, because of his ferocity, cannot be as easily domesticated as the ox.

— Commentary on Job, on Job 39:9; Job 39:9.1 Job's two great set-piece beasts he takes, at the literal level, for the two largest animals on land and in the sea — and cites Aristotle's History of Animals, Pliny, and Isidore to place them:

Among all land animals, the elephant excels in size and strength, and among aquatic animals the whale. So the Lord describes the devil using the metaphor of an elephant and a whale. Thus the name Behemoth, which means "animal," is referred to the elephant, which among other land animals… has a certain preeminence because of the size of his body. The name Leviathan, which means "their addition," is referred to the large whales which have an increase in size over every other genus of animal.

— Commentary on Job, on Job 40:15 (Behemoth); Job 40:15.2 On the Leviathan he adds the same naturalist's apparatus: "As Pliny says, 'he is the size of four acres,' and Isidore says, that 'they have bodies the size of mountains'… this animal is called balena from the Greek 'balein,' which means to send forth, because it sends forth water higher than the other animals" (Job 41:1).3 This literal identification — Behemoth the elephant, Leviathan the whale — is a distinct medieval alternative to the modern commentary's hippopotamus-and-crocodile, and both are weighed on the identity-of-Job's-two-beasts argument.

The "flying" serpent that does not fly (Isa 30:6)

On Isaiah's oracle of the beasts of the southern trade-road — the lion, the viper, and the "fiery flying serpent" — Aquinas denies the wings outright, reading the "flight" as the creature's springing:

the flying basilisk, not because it has wings, but because it leaps on the face of men, because of its swiftness to do evil.

— Commentary on Isaiah, on Isa 30:6; Isa 30:6.4 This is the exegetical tradition's cleanest statement that Scripture's "flying serpent" is a real leaping snake, not a winged reptile — a load-bearing datum on the fiery-flying-serpent argument, where the wings are shown to be metaphor or emblem rather than literal flight.

The beasts as the devil — the figure set atop the fact (Job 40–41)

Aquinas does not let the literal identification dissolve the figure, nor the figure erase the creature: he layers them, and states the method. The whole Job set-piece, having been described as elephant and whale, is at its deeper sense a portrait of Satan:

Perhaps it could seem that the Lord in the literal sense intended to express the characteristics of elephant and whale because of the size in which they surpass the rest of animals. But the properties of these animals are described as a metaphor of something else. This is clear because after he has described the characteristics pertaining to this figure, truth is added. After he has described the properties of the Behemoth, that is, the elephant, as if explaining the truth he adds, "He is the first of the ways of God." When he has explained the properties of the Leviathan, that is the whale, he says, "He is the king over all the sons of pride."

— Commentary on Job, on Job 40:15.5 So "the Lord describes the devil using the metaphor of an elephant and a whale," and, on Leviathan's un-catchability (Job 41:1), the un-hookable whale figures the enemy no human power can take away from his malice.6 It is the devil-allegory of Athanasius, Cyril, and Gregory the Great — but held expressly alongside the literal creature under a stated genre rule, the both/and of Julian of Eclanum and Augustine given its most systematic form.

The unicorn as pride, and the powers going down to death (Ps 22:21; Isa 34:7)

Where the one-horned beast appears in the Psalter and the prophets as an image of an enemy, Aquinas reads it for pride. On "save my lowliness from the horns of the unicorns" (Ps 22:21):

Here he describes them as proud; and it refers to the chief priests and scribes, who are compared to the unicorn, which signifies pride. And this is indicated by the fact that it has one horn on its head, and is of such pride that it in no way endures subjection, but immediately dies when captured… And by this are signified the rulers of the Jews, who uniquely gloried in their knowledge of God.

— Exposition on the Psalms, on Ps 22:21; Ps 22:21.7 On Edom's doom (Isa 34:7) the same beast figures the great: "the unicorns, a fierce animal… with one horn, and thus it signifies the powerful and monarchs, shall go down, into death."8 The one horn is here read not of Christ but of the proud who exalt themselves — the opposite valence from the christological strand below, and a reminder that the medieval unicorn was a many-sided figure.

The christological one horn — the one God, the singular generation (Ps 29:6)

On "the beloved one, as the offspring of unicorns" (Ps 29:6) Aquinas turns the single horn to Christ, along the very monotheism-axis Ambrose had worked on Joseph's blessing (Deut 33:17):

And the Jews are called unicorns inasmuch as they glory in the worship of one God. Or he is called a unicorn because according to his eternal generation he is without a mother, and according to his temporal generation he was a son without a father.

— Exposition on the Psalms, on Ps 29:6; Ps 29:6.9 The single horn signifies at once the one God the Jews confess and the singularity of Christ's twofold generation — Ambrose's "one Word, one God" reading carried on and given a christological point, the strand tracked on the re'em / unicorn argument.

Notes

  • All three axes, systematically layered. Like Jerome, Aquinas holds the literal, allegorical, and christological readings together; unlike Jerome he does so by an explicit method — state the literal creature on the best available science, then add the figure the text's own "truth" clauses signal, without letting either cancel the other. He is the timeline's clearest model of the medieval fourfold-sense discipline applied to the beasts.
  • A distinctive literal identity. His Behemoth-the-elephant and Leviathan-the-whale is not the modern default (the hippopotamus and the crocodile of Bochart, Pope, and Hartley) but a real medieval rival, resting on "the largest land animal" and "the largest sea animal" rather than on the reed-and-river habitat the modern reading turns on — a datum kept on the identity argument.
  • The flying serpent, decided. His single sentence on Isa 30:6 — "not because it has wings, but because it leaps" — is the most-cited patristic-through-medieval denial that Scripture's "flying serpent" is a winged animal, and is weighed against the young-earth "surviving pterosaur" reading on the fiery-flying-serpent argument.
  • The desolation-beasts, read as demons. On the strange creatures that inherit fallen Babylon and Edom (Isa 13:21–22; 34:14) Aquinas takes the plainest demonic line — "demons, monsters of demons, which appear most in deserts… lamia, having the feet of a horse and the other members of a woman" — the developed form of the versional reading, weighed against the modern literal wild-goat / night-bird case on the desolation-oracle beasts argument (assessment: contested).
  • Why he belongs, and why he was late. Aquinas was invoked across the argument pages — the re'em identity, the fiery flying serpent, and the identity of Job's two beasts all lean on his glosses — but held no timeline slot of his own. As a load-bearing medieval voice commenting firsthand on Job 39–41, Isaiah 30–34, and the Psalms, he was a conspicuous omission, now seated at c. 1274, just before Marco Polo.
  • Cross-links. On the two Job beasts compare Ephrem ("a land animal"), Gregory the Great (pure devil-allegory), and Julian of Eclanum (the literal creature affirmed first); on the unicorn, Tertullian, Ambrose, and the Physiologus; on the flying serpent, Jerome's draco volans and Herodotus's winged Arabian snakes. Aquinas also appears on this wiki as a witness elsewhere — on the Nephilim, the Real Presence, the perpetual virginity of Mary, and purgatory, among others.
