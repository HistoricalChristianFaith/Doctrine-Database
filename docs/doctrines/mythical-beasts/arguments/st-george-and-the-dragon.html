Is St George's dragon-slaying a historical event?

Disputed reading · on Dragons, Unicorns & Mythical Beasts · witness: the Golden Legend of Jacobus de Voragine (c. 1260)

Related readings: Did the early Christian writers read Scripture's dragons, leviathan and behemoth as real animals?

The passage

… by this city was a stagne or a pond like a sea, wherein was a dragon which envenomed all the country… [The lot having fallen on the king's daughter, George meets her at the pond.] …he smote him with his spear and hurt him sore and threw him to the ground. And after said to the maid: Deliver to me your girdle, and bind it about the neck of the dragon and be not afeard. When she had done so the dragon followed her as it had been a meek beast… [George to the people:] Ne doubt ye no thing, without more, believe ye in God, Jesu Christ, and do ye to be baptized and I shall slay the dragon… Then were there well fifteen thousand men baptized, without women and children, and the king did do make a church there of our Lady and of Saint George.

— Jacobus de Voragine, The Golden Legend (Legenda aurea, c. 1260), "The Life of S. George," in William Caxton's English (1483).1

The question

A cult of the martyr George is attested at Lydda (Diospolis) in Palestine from the fourth century, and a Greek inscription of the 360s from Shaqqa in the Hauran honours "the saints and triumphant martyrs George and companions."2 Disputed is whether the dragon-fight told of him in the Golden Legend records an event in the martyr's life or a later legend attached to his cult. A modern academic defence of the episode's historicity was not located for this page; the first reading below is given from the medieval texts that narrate it.

An episode in the life of the martyr

On this reading the rescue at Silene is one of George's deeds, told in the lives of the saint as part of his history.

Held by: Jacobus de Voragine (Dominican, archbishop of Genoa; Golden Legend, c. 1260), who narrates it within the life; Vincent of Beauvais (Speculum historiale, mid-thirteenth century).3

The Golden Legend tells the episode as history, set in a named place, "a city which is said Silene" in "the province of Libya," with a king, a lake, a lot cast among the townspeople, and an aftermath that can be counted: fifteen thousand men baptized, a church built "of our Lady and of Saint George," and a fountain that the text says still flows there. It places the rescue in sequence with George's preaching and martyrdom under Dacian, as one life. Voragine works as a compiler who reports where his sources differ: he notes that George's legend "is numbered among other scriptures apocryphal" because "his martyrdom hath no certain relation," and sets the calendar of Bede (martyrdom in Persia at Diospolis) beside other accounts.4 The episode stands in an older line of Christian reports of holy men overcoming dragons told as history: the fifth-century church historian Sozomen relays, as local record vouched for by the inhabitants, that Bishop Donatus of Euroea killed a man-eating dragon with the sign of the cross, its carcass hauled away by eight yoke of oxen.5 Through Caxton's 1483 printing the Silene narrative became the standard English life of the patron of England, honoured in the Order of the Garter, and on this reading the scene is received as the crowning act of a real saint, a victory won by prayer and the cross that led a city to baptism.6

A later legend joined to an early martyr cult

On this reading the martyr is early, while the dragon-fight is a story that came to be told of him many centuries after his death.

Held by: Charles Clermont-Ganneau ("Horus et saint Georges," 1876); Hippolyte Delehaye (Bollandist, Les légendes grecques des saints militaires, 1909); Samantha Riches (St George: Hero, Martyr and Myth, 2000); Christopher Walter (The Warrior Saints in Byzantine Art and Tradition, 2003).7

The case rests on the order in which the sources appear. The oldest Passions of George, with their repeated tortures and resurrections, contain no dragon, and the early cult at Diospolis and the evidence of the fourth to eighth centuries know none; Delehaye studied these Passions, and Riches describes a martyr-cult that preceded the dragon story. The Decretum Gelasianum (early sixth century) lists the Passio of George among writings "not received," while the saint himself remains venerated.8 Walter dates the earliest narrative of the dragon-rescue to an eleventh-century Georgian text and the earliest images to eleventh-century Cappadocia, with the dragon-and-maiden motif told earlier of Theodore Tiro and passing to George in that century, then through the Byzantine world in the twelfth and into the Latin West in the thirteenth.9 Clermont-Ganneau pointed to geography: the story of Perseus and Andromeda, a princess exposed to a sea-monster and saved by a hero, was set by classical authors at Joppa (Jaffa), a few miles from George's cult-centre at Lydda, and he read the dragon-slayer's role as passing onto George within that small region.10 On this reading the scene is a symbol rather than a chronicle, and Riches traces the medieval readings of it in which the dragon stands for paganism and the devil, the maiden for the Church or the soul in danger, and the rider for the faith that converts a city.11
