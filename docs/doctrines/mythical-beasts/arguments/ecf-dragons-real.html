Did the early Christian writers read Scripture's dragons, leviathan and behemoth as real animals?

Disputed reading · on Dragons, Unicorns & Mythical Beasts · witnesses: Ephrem the Syrian, Augustine, John of Damascus

Related readings: Is St George's dragon-slaying a historical event?

The passage

The Behemoth is a dragon, that is, a land animal, just as the Leviathan is an aquatic sea animal.

— Ephrem the Syrian, Commentary on Job 40.15, in the Ancient Christian Commentary on Scripture, OT VI: Job (2006), Job 40:15.1

"All that is under heaven belongs to me. And I will not be silent." These words indicate the devil, whose prodigious nature is described in these two beasts, who is destined to be conquered by the power of Christ when the fullness of time will come.

— Ephrem the Syrian, Commentary on Job, on Job 41, in the same volume.2

"Dragons" live about the water, come out from caverns, fly through the air; the air is set in motion by them: "dragons" are a huge kind of living creatures, greater there are not upon the earth. Therefore with them he begins, "Dragons and all abysses."

— Augustine, Exposition on Psalm 148, on Ps 148:7, NPNF1 vol. 8, New Advent.3

The question

Early Christian writers comment on Scripture's great beasts both as creatures of land and sea and as figures of the devil, sometimes in the same work. Disputed is how that body of comment should be characterized as a whole, and whether the Fathers' "dragons" can be identified with the animals now called dinosaurs. The dinosaur reading is advanced in popular creationist writing; academic literature defending it was not located for this page.

The Fathers described real animals, the creatures now called dinosaurs

On this reading the Fathers took dragons, behemoth and leviathan to be real, if rarely seen, animals, and their descriptions match dinosaurs living within the biblical timeframe.

Held by: Timofey Alferov (young-earth creationist; "Dragons: animals … not apparitions," Creation 22.3, 2000); the essay "The Early Christians Believed in Dragons and Dinosaurs" (Catholicism for the Modern World, Medium; ⚠ unverified).4

The case starts from John of Damascus's treatise On Dragons and Ghosts, which Alferov translated into English. John sets real dragons against folk fiction: "dragons exist but they are serpents borne of other serpents," and "It is said they grow up more than thirty cubits." For Alferov, John's purpose "was to persuade his readers that dragons were real living creatures, and not mythical beings like ghosts, werewolves, faceless bogey-creatures and so on. Dragons, he wrote, were absolutely real animals, though sometimes big and terrifying, which at that time were only rarely seen by humans."5 Other Fathers speak the same way. Ephrem calls behemoth "a land animal" and leviathan "an aquatic sea animal"; Augustine calls dragons "a huge kind of living creatures, greater there are not upon the earth"; Julian of Eclanum calls the literal account of Job's beast "the true description of the beast according to the faithful [understanding] of the story," to be believed "because of the power of the Creator"; and Sozomen reports a dragon killed in Epirus whose carcass "did not appear inferior in size to the noted serpents of India."6 The features the reports give, horns, crests and great size, recall known dinosaurs: "When we look at accounts of dragons in history, the similarity of many of them to various types of dinosaurs is apparent. Some dinosaurs are well known to have had bony protrusions and horns on their head, and some had spikes on their back and/or tail, as is commonly seen in depictions of dragons." Alferov explains the fabulous features as later growth: "as time went on and these creatures became ever rarer, fables sprang up about them, and mythical features were added to the accounts. It was to counter these that John of Damascus wrote." The patristic witness, read this way, is "one more piece of evidence for the co-existence of people and dinosaurs, not millions of years ago, but during the biblical timeframe of history."7 On this reading the Fathers' natural-history language is a record of real animals within human memory, and the dragon of the later fables is a remembered creature overlaid with legend.

The Fathers read the beasts in two registers, and their "dragon" was a great serpent

On this reading the Fathers read the beasts both as animals and as figures of the devil, by verse and context, and the animal they meant by "dragon" was a very large snake.

Held by: ⚠ unverified — no named proponent located yet.

The case rests on reading each Father's comments together. Ephrem, who calls behemoth "a land animal" on Job 40:15, reads the same two beasts on Job 41 as the devil, "destined to be conquered by the power of Christ." Augustine, who calls dragons the largest living creatures on Psalm 148, reads "the heads of dragons" on Psalm 74 as "demons' pride," broken in Baptism, and the one great dragon as "the devil himself." Julian affirms the literal creature first and then grants a reading "to a plane of higher intellect," the beast as an image of "the strength of spiritual iniquity."8 Beside these stands a line that reads the beasts as the devil throughout: Athanasius's leviathan "drawn with a hook by the Saviour," Basil's "crooked serpent" as "the author of sin," Gregory the Great's Behemoth and Leviathan as "the ancient enemy," and Bede's "dragon" as one of the devil's names.9 Where the Fathers describe the animal, they describe a serpent. John of Damascus calls dragons "serpents borne of other serpents" and says they alone among serpents "do not emit poison"; the Greek drakōn is the ordinary word for a large snake, and Daniel Ogden reads the classical set-piece drakontes, the Bagradas monster among them, as literary figures worked up from real large snakes. Henry Yule identifies the clawed "serpents" of Marco Polo's Yunnan as crocodiles.10 On this reading the Fathers reason from a text or a report to a known large serpent, and the patristic testimony records a literal sense and a figurative sense held side by side, the literal sense naming a great serpent of the known world.
