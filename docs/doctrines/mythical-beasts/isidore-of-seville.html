Isidore of Seville on dragons and the unicorn

Dates: c. 636 (the Etymologies, Book XII, "De animalibus" — the draco at XII.4.4–5, the rhinoceros/monoceron at XII.2.12–13; the work was compiled c. 615–630 and left unfinished at Isidore's death); Isidore c. 560–636, archbishop of Seville · Wikipedia
Summary: Isidore is the great encyclopedist of the early medieval West, and in the animal book of his Etymologies he files the dragon and the unicorn as plain natural history — entries in a zoology, not figures of Scripture to be interpreted. His draco is the standard medieval dragon: the largest of all serpents, harmless by venom but deadly by its coils, that lurks for the elephant and strangles it. His one-horned monoceron carries the Physiologus's virgin-capture, but Isidore reports it as a fact about the beast — "as those who have written about the natures of animals claim" — stripped of the christological allegory the Physiologus and Gregory gave it. Because the Etymologies was the most-copied reference book of the age, these two notices are the channel through which the ancient beast-lore reached the Latin bestiaries.

Claims & evidence

The dragon is the greatest of serpents — a real animal that kills by its coils, not its venom

Isidore's draco is offered as zoology, not allegory: a chapter-entry among the serpents, described the way he describes any creature. It is the largest of all snakes, crested and small-mouthed, its power lodged not in its teeth but in its tail; and, uniquely among serpents, it does its killing not by poison but by strength — it crushes and suffocates.

The dragon [draco] is the largest of all the snakes, or of all the animals on earth. The Greeks call it "drákon," whence the term is borrowed into Latin so that we say draco. It is often drawn out of caves and soars aloft, and disturbs the air. It is crested, and has a small mouth and narrow pipes through which it draws breath and sticks out its tongue. It has its strength not in its teeth but in its tail, and it causes injury more by its lashing tail than with its jaws.

Also, it does not harm with poison; poison is not needed for this animal to kill, because it kills whatever it wraps itself around. Even the elephant with his huge body is not safe from the dragon, for it lurks around the paths along which the elephants are accustomed to walk, and wraps around their legs in coils and kills them by suffocating them. It is born in Ethiopia and India in the fiery intensity of perpetual heat.

— Etymologies XII.4.4–5 ("De serpentibus"), trans. Barney, Lewis, Beach, and Berghof. This is the dragon exactly as John of Damascus had defended it a century later — a real, if outsized, serpent whose only venom-less killing is by constriction — and the very portrait (strangler of elephants, strength in the tail) that the medieval bestiaries would copy from Isidore verbatim. Isidore reads no Scripture here and allegorizes nothing: the draco is simply the greatest of the animals God made.1

The rhinoceros is the one-horned monoceron, "the unicorn" — caught by a virgin, told as zoology

Isidore identifies the biblical-versional one-horn with the real rhinoceros, glossing the Greek name and equating it with the monoceron. His unicorn is a fierce, elephant-fighting beast with a single four-foot horn — and he passes on the Physiologus's virgin-capture, but as a datum of animal nature ("as those who have written about the natures of animals claim"), with none of the Christ-and-the-Virgin allegory the Physiologus and Gregory the Great had wrapped around it.

The rhinoceros [rhinoceron] is named with a Greek word; in Latin it means "horn on the nose." This is also the monoceron, that is, the unicorn [unicornus], because it has a single four-foot horn in the middle of its forehead, so sharp and strong that it tosses in the air or impales whatever it attacks. It often fights with the elephant and throws it to the ground after wounding it in the belly.

It has such strength that it can be captured by no hunter's ability, but, as those who have written about the natures of animals claim, if a virgin girl is set before a unicorn, as the beast approaches, she may open her lap and it will lay its head there with all ferocity put aside, and thus lulled and disarmed it may be captured.

— Etymologies XII.2.12–13 ("De bestiis"), trans. Barney, Lewis, Beach, and Berghof. Isidore does two things the re'em / unicorn argument turns on: he pins the versional "one horn" to a real animal, the rhinoceros (his μονóκερως is "horn on the nose," the natural-history one-horn, not a fairy-tale horse); and he transmits the Physiologus's virgin-and-unicorn as zoology — the same scene Gregory told as a figure of Christ made flesh in the Virgin, here reported flatly as how the beast is caught. It is this naturalized notice, in the age's standard reference book, that carries the one-horn into the Latin bestiaries.2

Notes

  • Isidore's register on this page is the literal / natural-history one: he catalogues the beasts among the serpents and the wild animals, not among the figures of Scripture. Where Gregory the Great — his near-contemporary — read Job's beasts as pure allegory of the devil, and the Physiologus read the unicorn as Christ, Isidore hands both creatures on shorn of allegory, as entries in a zoology. He is the bridge from John of Damascus's real-animal dragon to the bestiary tradition and, through it, to travelers' reports like Marco Polo's.
  • The virgin-capture is the tell. In the Physiologus it is an allegory whose whole point is the Incarnation; in Isidore it is a bare fact of the animal's nature, credited to "those who have written about the natures of animals." That naturalizing of a Christian allegory into medieval "zoology" is exactly how the emblem became a fixture of the bestiaries and of the later heraldic and tapestry unicorn.
  • Isidore appears on this wiki also as a witness on the astrology, Old Testament canon, pillars of Seth, Septuagint origin, and rabbinic corruption timelines — in each case as the encyclopedist whose Etymologies fixed a tradition for the Latin Middle Ages.
