Jerome on unicorns, flying serpents & the night-monsters of the prophets

Dates: c. 408–410 (the Commentary on Isaiah; Jerome c. 347–420) · Wikipedia
Summary: Jerome is the translator who fixed the Latin vocabulary for nearly every beast on this page — unicornis and rhinoceros for the re'em, draco volans for the "flying serpent," lamia (the Hebrew Lilith) and the pilosi (satyrs) for the night-creatures of the oracles — and he comments directly on the key verses. He reads them along several axes at once. As a Hebraist he insists on the plain lexical sense (saraph is "the burner"; the re'em keeps its one horn even from the Hebraica veritas). As a critic of pagan story he repeatedly flags the fabulous beasts — the onocentaurs, sirens, and the lamia — as things "the fables of the Gentiles and the creations of poets describe," and warns against reading Scripture "contrary to the nature of things." And as an allegorist he takes the whole menagerie of fallen Babylon and Edom "in the likeness of angels or demons," the flying serpents becoming evil rulers and "various heresies." He is thus the tradition's most complete single witness on this doctrine: the man who names the beasts, sifts the fables, and allegorizes the residue.

Claims & evidence

The translator who fixed the Latin names of the beasts

Jerome's Vulgate is where the Western reader met these creatures. He rendered the Hebrew re'em now as rhinoceros, now as unicornis ("one-horned"), following the Greek monokeros — and, going back to the Hebraica veritas, he kept the one-horned beast rather than quietly substituting a plain wild ox. In his Commentary on Habakkuk (on Hab 3:4) he equates the two outright:

no horned animal is considered unclean in Leviticus, and it also signifies the unicorn in the Psalms (Psalm 49 and 91), or ẑινοκερότα [rhinocerota]; and that: In you we will scatter our enemies with a horn (Psalm 44, 7).

— Commentary on Habakkuk, on Hab 3:4 ("horns were in his hands").1 That the age's foremost Hebraist, most bent on following the Hebrew, kept the one-horned beast is a sign the versions' monokeros was not felt as an obvious error to correct — a point the re'em-identity argument and the "fabulous beast" argument both turn on. For the "fiery flying serpent" he coined the Latin draco volans; for Isaiah's night-fauna he supplied lamia, pilosi, and onocentauri — the vocabulary the whole later bestiary inherited.

The plain lexical sense — the "burner" and the flying serpent (Isa 14:29; 30:6)

because we have translated it as absorbing the winged creature, and it is written in Hebrew as Saraph Mopheth, which can be interpreted as a flying serpent… so that you may understand a flying dragon [draco volans].

— Commentary on Isaiah, on Isa 14:29; Isa 14:29.2 On Isaiah 30:6–7 — "there will also be venomous snakes and flying serpents" — he again works from the Hebrew, noting that "for the flying ruler, which the Seventy translated as flying asps, in Hebrew it is called ἐμπρηστὴς [emprēstēs] and flying, which we can call the burner, and in Hebrew it is called Saraph."3 The reading is soberly zoological at the lexical level: the beast is a real venomous serpent whose name means "the burning one," listed in the concrete bestiary of the southern road — the plain sense on which the fiery-flying-serpent argument rests before it turns figurative.

Sifting the pagan fables — the satyrs, sirens, onocentaurs, and lamia of the oracles (Isa 13:21; 34:14)

…beasts will rest there, and their houses will be filled with dragons: and ostriches will dwell there, and shaggy creatures will dance there; and owls will respond there in its houses: and sirens in the temples of pleasure.

— Commentary on Isaiah, on Isa 13:21–22 (the beasts of fallen Babylon); Isa 13:21.4 Jerome treats the strange creatures of Babylon's ruin partly as a problem in translation and partly as pagan story to be named and set aside. The onocentauri "whom only the LXX translated" he charges with "imitating the fables of the Gentiles, who say that they were hippocentaurs"; the sirens he glosses as "signifying either some sort of animals or demons, according to the error of the Gentiles, sweetly singing and deceiving people."5 On the parallel desolation of Edom (Isaiah 34:14) he identifies the most famous of these monsters and traces it to legend:

…onocentaurs, and shaggy figures, and lamia, which the fables of the Gentiles and the creations of poets describe… the lamia, which is called Lilith in Hebrew… which some Hebrews suspect to be the Fury.

— Commentary on Isaiah, on Isa 34:14; Isa 34:14.6 This is the "sift legend from fact" instinct the doctrine's intro names, applied by the very man who furnished the Latin words: Jerome will translate lamia, but he tells the reader plainly that it belongs to "the fables of the Gentiles and the creations of poets." He makes the caution a rule elsewhere in the same commentary, refusing on Isaiah 30 to think "that Holy Scripture truly speaks… contrary to the nature of things."7

The allegorical residue — the monsters as demons and heresies

…let the beasts and dragons rest there, and the ostriches, and the shaggy ones, and the screech owls, and the sirens, all of which beasts we understand in the likeness of angels or demons, and those to whom we are delivered for punishment.

— Commentary on Isaiah, on Isa 13:22.8 Having named and sifted the beasts, Jerome reads the residue as figure. On Isaiah 34:14 he separates the two senses expressly: "Let these things be said according to the Hebrew and historical explanation. Moreover, those who follow the allegory, expelling the people of the Jews under the names of beasts and monsters, affirm that they will dwell in Jerusalem, serving idols and various superstitions."9 And the flying serpent of Isaiah 14:29, plain-sensed above, becomes a figure of evil powers: "I believe that these feathered serpents are those who exalt and rise up against the knowledge of God… unless they also seek the protection of evil works, and invent various heresies."10

Notes

  • All three axes in one interpreter. Where Ephrem and Augustine hold the literal and the allegorical together, Jerome adds a third register the doctrine's intro names but no other witness supplies: the deliberate sifting of pagan legend. He translates the lamia and the onocentaur, but tells the reader they are "the fables of the Gentiles and the creations of poets" — a philologist's caution against mistaking the poets' monsters for creatures of the natural order.
  • The night-creatures otherwise unrepresented. The satyrs (Isa 13:21) and the Lilith/lamia (Isa 34:14) appear in this doctrine's opening survey of Scripture's "satyrs and night-creatures," but no other witness on the timeline treats them. Jerome is the page's voice for that whole family of beasts — and the source of the very names by which the Latin West knew them. Whether those seʻirim and the Lilith/lamia denote real desert animals, demons, or the uncanny population of a ruin is weighed on its own page, the desolation-oracle beasts argument (assessment: contested).
  • Why he was absent, and why he belongs. Jerome is cited across the argument pages — the re'em identity, the "fabulous beast", and the fiery flying serpent all lean on his renderings and his commentary — but he had held no timeline slot of his own. As the translator who fixed the Latin names and commented firsthand on Deut 33:17, Job 40–41, and the Isaian fauna, he is a conspicuous omission now repaired.
  • Cross-links. On the re'em compare Tertullian, Ambrose, and the Physiologus (the christological one-horn); on the flying serpent, Herodotus and Ammianus (the classical winged-serpent natural history) and Gregory the Great (the same figure allegorized). Jerome also appears on this wiki as a witness elsewhere — on infant baptism, the birthplace of Christ, and the Balaam and Old Testament canon timelines among others.
