The Origin of the Soul

Where does an individual human soul come from? Genesis says that God formed the first man of dust and “breathed into his nostrils the breath of life”; the witnesses on this timeline differ over what happens at each later conception. Three answers were given, and they are the axes of this timeline. Traducianism (from tradux, the layered vine-shoot by which a vine is propagated): the soul comes from the parents' souls as the body comes from their bodies, so that the whole race is one stock descending from Adam. Creationism: God makes each soul afresh out of nothing and joins it to the body He has formed in the womb. Pre-existence: souls were all made long ago, and enter bodies later — on Origen's version, because they cooled from their first contemplation of God and fell.1

Writers on the question tied it to three others: how original sin reaches a newborn — through a soul inherited from Adam, or through a fresh soul joined to inherited flesh; what to say about infants who die unbaptised, the stake Augustine names; and whether the diversity of human lots is the residue of choices made before birth, the stake Origen names. The question drew a papal letter (498), an imperial anathema (543), and a scholastic censure of heresy (c. 1268).2

Among the witnesses here, Tertullian argues for traducianism; Origen proposes pre-existence, which Didymus also taught at Alexandria and Justinian's edict of 543 anathematized; Lactantius, Jerome, Cassian, Gennadius, Anastasius II and Aquinas hold creationism; Rufinus and Augustine decline to decide, Augustine wavering, in Philip Schaff's words, “because the Scriptures do not expressly decide.” Gregory of Nyssa and John of Damascus hold that soul and body begin together; C. Dubray (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1912) lists Gregory among those who “seem to prefer” generationism, reports that creationism “is held as certain by the Scholastics,” and writes that, “although there are no strict definitions condemning Generationism as heretical, it is certainly opposed to the doctrine of the Church, and could not be held without temerity.” What follows are the datable witnesses, earliest first.3

Timeline

Tertullian (c. 220)

Tertullian's Treatise on the Soul is the earliest extant sustained argument for traducianism, and Schaff and Dubray name him its author. Body and soul, he holds, are conceived together and neither precedes the other: “both are conceived, and formed, and perfectly simultaneously, as well as born together; and… not a moment's interval occurs in their conception.” The soul is therefore a sprout from a root — it “has been derived from Adam as its root, and has been propagated among his posterity by means of woman” — so that “from the one (primeval) man comes the entire outflow and redundance of men's souls.”4 His chief evidence is observational: we resemble our parents in disposition, not only in feature, and that likeness must be propagated like any other. He holds the soul to have body and form; Schaff writes that “with Tertullian this theory was connected with a materializing view of the soul.”5

Origen of Alexandria (c. 253)

Origen gives the third option, and writes in the preface to De Principiis that the church's rule of faith has not settled “whether it is derived from the seed by a process of traducianism… or whether it has any other beginning.” Into that gap he puts pre-existence. On his account rational minds were made equal; freedom made them unequal; and a mind that cooled from the divine warmth became a soul — he derives psychē from psychesthai, to grow cold — and was placed in a body proportioned to what it had become.6 He presents it as an account of God's justice: the inequality of human lots is not the Creator's arbitrariness but the residue of prior choice, so that even Jacob supplanting Esau in the womb was loved “according to the deserts of his previous life.” Origen himself twice marks the whole discussion as exploratory rather than dogmatic.7

Lactantius (c. 325)

Lactantius gives the earliest Latin argument for creationism among these witnesses. What the soul is, he writes, “is not yet agreed upon by philosophers, and perhaps will never be agreed upon.” On its origin he gives an answer. Souls, he argues, come from neither parent nor from both, “because nothing can depart from a slight and incomprehensible subject”; therefore “the manner of the production of souls belongs entirely to God alone.” The parents' whole contribution is the matter of the body; conception, moulding, and the breathing-in of life are God's.8 He agrees with Tertullian on timing against the philosophers — the soul is present from conception, not breathed in at birth — while denying his mechanism.9

Didymus the Blind (c. 380)

Didymus, blind from childhood and for half a century the head of the catechetical school of Alexandria, is the fourth-century witness here to Origen's third option. Reading the Lord's “Adam, where are you?” he takes the fallen soul to have been “free from every place through incorporeality” and to have “come into a place” only once it “became attached to the body” — a reading of Genesis 3 along the lines of Origen's account of the soul's descent.10 His pupil Jerome, who extolled “the purity of his faith in the Trinity” but withdrew from him where he “erred in trusting to Origen,” reports a treatise of Didymus holding that the pre-existent souls “had not sinned much, and therefore it was enough punishment for them just to have touched their bodily prisons.” Ilaria Ramelli (2013) reads his teaching as Origen's version — rational creatures with incorruptible bodies, not a pre-existence of bare souls; Daria Szymańska-Kuta (2015) notes that the commentary ascribes the sharpest form of the scheme to unnamed “some authors.” He died about 398; his name was joined to Origen's and Evagrius's in the conciliar definitions of 680–681 and 787.11

Gregory of Nyssa (c. 395)

Gregory of Nyssa sets out two opinions — souls before bodies, or bodies before souls — and rejects both: “the doctrine of both, then, is equally to be rejected; but I think that we ought to direct our own doctrine in the way of truth between these theories.” Against pre-existence he presses that it slides into the pagan transmigration tales, fit for “the chatter of frogs or jackdaws”; against the literalist reading of Genesis 2:7 that makes the clay figure first, he answers that man is one, and so “the beginning of his existence is one, common to both parts,” lest a man be “antecedent and posterior to himself.”12 His positive image is the seed that already contains the whole plant potentially: what is sent forth from a living body to be the occasion of life cannot itself be lifeless. Like Tertullian he holds that soul and body begin together; he does not make the soul corporeal. How he stands on the soul's source is read differently, as his page sets out.13

Rufinus of Aquileia (c. 400)

Rufinus, defending himself before Pope Anastasius I against the charge of Origenism, sets out the three-way division — soul infused with the seed (he names Tertullian and Lactantius), or made new daily, or made long ago and planted in bodies (“the opinion of Origen”) — and then refuses to choose: “I declare in the presence of God that, after reading each of these opinions, I am up to the present moment unable to hold any of them as certain and absolute.”14 Rufinus holds that the church delivers as an article of faith only that God is the creator of souls as well as of bodies; Jerome, mid-quarrel, treated the refusal as evasion.15 (Lactantius's own chapter on the question denies that souls come from the parents.)16

Jerome (c. 411)

Jerome holds creationism. Writing to Marcellinus and Anapsychia shortly after the sack of Rome he lays out the same taxonomy in five branches and marks his own view by the proof-text he attaches to it: are souls “daily created by God and sent into bodies, according to what is written in the gospel, My Father works hitherto, and I work”? The rival he names is Tertullian's, which “Tertullian, Apollinaris, and the majority of the Western divines conjecture… so that as the body is the offspring of body, the soul is the offspring of soul, and exists on conditions similar to those regulating the existence of the inferior animals” — Jerome's objection being that traducianism likens man to the beasts.17 He states it elsewhere too. On Ecclesiastes 12:7, since the spirit returns to God who gave it, “there is enough to smile at in those who think that spirits are produced with bodies, not from God, but are made from the parent's body”; on Zechariah 12:1 God is “the same Creator of all souls.” Yet in the letter itself he declined to expound the matter and sent his correspondents to Augustine instead — “that holy man and learned bishop Augustine, who will be able to teach you on this subject viva voce” — whose own letter to Jerome on the question followed in 415.18

Augustine of Hippo (c. 415)

Augustine left the question undecided. In On Free Will he had already enumerated four possibilities — souls propagated from Adam's; each newly made; pre-existing souls sent into bodies; pre-existing souls entering of their own accord — and deliberately argued in a way that would hold on any of them. He did not decide it in later works. In 415 he sent Jerome a whole treatise as a letter (Letter 166), carried east by the priest Orosius, confessing “many are perplexed by questions concerning the soul, and I confess that I myself am of this number,” and pressing one difficulty above all: if God makes each soul new and sinless, whence the guilt for which an unbaptised infant is condemned?19 Jerome's reply (416) did not take it up. In 419–420, against Vincentius Victor, Augustine was still saying the same thing — “I have no certain knowledge how it came into my body; for it was not I who gave it to myself” — while explicitly offering to accept creationism from anyone who could reconcile it with original sin and infant condemnation. His last word on the subject, in the Retractations, was that as to the soul's origin “I did not know then, and I still do not know.”20

John Cassian (c. 435)

Cassian argues for creationism from Hebrews 12:9, which distinguishes “fathers of our flesh” from “the Father of spirits.” What could show more clearly, he asks, “that he laid down that men were the fathers of our flesh, but always taught that God alone was the Father of souls”? The mechanism is denied outright — “spirit does not beget spirit just as soul cannot procreate soul” — and God “makes them out of nothing whenever He pleases.”21 He makes the argument in passing, inside a conference on demons.22

Gennadius of Massilia (c. 470)

Gennadius's handbook of church dogmas states creationism as the church's teaching and names both rejected alternatives in one breath: souls were not all made at the beginning among the other intellectual natures, “as Origen imagines,” nor sown with the bodies through intercourse, “as the Luciferians, Cyril, and certain presumptuous Latins affirm”; rather the body alone is sown, and “the body being now formed, the soul is created and infused.”23 The book circulated for centuries under Augustine's name.24

Pope Anastasius II (498)

Anastasius II wrote to the bishops of Gaul against people there who reasoned that “just as the parents transmit bodies to the human race from material dregs, so also they bestow the vital principle of the living souls.” Against them he argues from the same verse Jerome used — the Father's work has “not ceased even today” — and concludes that God “imparts souls.”25 Dubray (1912) cites it as condemning generationism and writes that, “although there are no strict definitions condemning Generationism as heretical, it is certainly opposed to the doctrine of the Church, and could not be held without temerity.” Denzinger prints the letter in excerpt; Thiel (1868) edits the Latin.26

Justinian I (543)

Justinian's edict against Origen opens with the pre-existence of souls and anathematizes Origen's mechanism: whoever says that human souls pre-existed, “that they had previously been spirits and holy powers, but that, satiated with the vision of God, they had turned to evil, and in this way the divine love in them had died out… and they had therefore been condemned to punishment in bodies, shall be anathema.”27 A fuller set of fifteen anathemas, opening with “the fabulous pre-existence of souls,” is transmitted with the Fifth Ecumenical Council of 553, though its relation to the council's own acts is disputed.28

John of Damascus (c. 749)

John of Damascus writes in the Exact Exposition of the Orthodox Faith that the reasoning soul God “bestowed upon him by His own inbreathing”; and, naming Origen: “body and soul were formed at one and the same time, not first the one and then the other, as Origen so senselessly supposes.”29 The sentence restates Gregory of Nyssa's simultaneity; the one error it names is pre-existence.30

Thomas Aquinas (c. 1270)

Aquinas argues metaphysically rather than from a proof-text. He holds that since the intellectual soul has an operation independent of the body it is subsistent; a subsistent immaterial thing cannot be produced by a power working in matter, so it “cannot be caused through generation, but only through creation by God.” Therefore “to hold that the intellectual soul is caused by the begetter, is nothing else than to hold the soul to be non-subsistent and consequently to perish with the body. It is therefore heretical to say that the intellectual soul is transmitted with the semen.”31 Tertullian had held a soul with body and form together with its transmission; Aquinas holds an incorporeal soul and rejects its transmission. The charge of heresy is his own theological censure; Dubray reports that creationism “is held as certain by the Scholastics,” with Hugh of Saint Victor and Alexander of Hales proposing it “merely as more probable.”32
