The Divine Council and the Sons of God

The Hebrew Bible repeatedly pictures Yahweh amid an assembly of "gods" and "sons of God": "God stands in the divine assembly; among the gods (elohim) he holds judgment" (Psalm 82:1); "the sons of God came to present themselves before the Lord" (Job 1:6); the Most High apportions the nations "according to the number of the sons of God" (Deuteronomy 32:8, in the reading of a Qumran fragment; the Masoretic Text has "sons of Israel"). One question runs under all of it: are these elohim and "sons of God" real, created, lesser divine beings — a heavenly council under the one God — or is the language about human beings (Israel, its judges, believers by adoption) and mere idols?

The earliest extant witnesses on this timeline are Second-Temple texts: the Book of Enoch, and the Greek Septuagint at the level of its own wording, describe a populated heaven of real "sons of God," an assembly of divine beings among whom God judges and to whom the nations were parcelled out. The Fathers read this language in several ways. Justin, Irenaeus, Athanasius, Chrysostom, Jerome, and Augustine read the elohim of Psalm 82 as men, made gods by grace and adoption — Irenaeus c. 180, Athanasius and Jerome in their writings against the Arians. Athenagoras (c. 177) describes a “multitude of angels… appointed to their several posts” over the elements and the world, with a rebel “prince of matter” among them. Clement of Alexandria, Origen, Eusebius, and Pseudo-Dionysius read Deuteronomy 32:8 (with 4:19) of every nation set under a presiding heavenly power, Israel the Lord's own portion, and take those powers to be created angels under providence; Eusebius tells the allotment as a history of angelic guardianship, a permitted star-cult, its corruption by a rebel power, and Christ's reclamation of the nations. Justin, Tertullian, and Lactantius, with the Septuagint's Psalm 96:5, identify the gods of the nations with the fallen angels of Genesis 6 and the demons sprung from them, real powers who “make themselves gods.” Pseudo-Dionysius' Celestial Hierarchy (c. 500) sorts the biblical host into nine ranks in three triads. In the modern era the Semitic-languages scholar Michael Heiser argues that the elohim of Psalm 82 and the "sons of God" of Deuteronomy 32:8 are real, created divine beings, Yahweh's "divine council," and makes that reading the frame of a "supernatural worldview" into which, on his account, the Nephilim, the Flood, and the modern "aliens" fit. This timeline traces these readings, earliest witness first.

Related doctrines: The unseen realm — see also the Serpent of Eden (the nachash). Heiser reads the angelic interpretation of Genesis 6, traced on the Nephilim timeline (a different concept-family, which shares him as a witness), within the divine-council frame. Origen and Heiser place the Deuteronomy 32:8 apportionment of the nations at Babel, the event whose confusion of tongues is the subject of the first language of mankind; the Nimrod timeline traces the traditions that connect him with its tower.

Timeline

The Book of Enoch (c. 300–100 BC)

The Book of Enoch describes a populated heaven of real "sons of God": the Watchers are "children of the heaven" who descend (1 Enoch 6–7), and around the throne of the "Lord of Spirits" stand myriads and four ranked "presences" (1 Enoch 39–40) — an assembly of divine beings.1 Outside the canon of most churches (it is Scripture in the Ethiopian church), it is quoted as prophecy in Jude 14–15.2

The Septuagint (c. 250–100 BC)

The wording of the Septuagint gives a divine-council cosmology: Deuteronomy 32:8 reads that God set the nations' bounds "according to the number of the angels of God" (a Qumran fragment: "sons of God"), where the received Hebrew has "sons of Israel"; which wording is earlier is disputed (see Disputed readings).3 It renders Psalm 82(81):1 as "God stands in the assembly of gods," and Psalm 96(95):5 as "all the gods of the nations are demons" (where the Hebrew has "idols"), naming the pagan gods as demons.4

Justin Martyr (c. 160)

Justin Martyr writes on both questions. He affirms real spiritual powers behind the nations — angels appointed over men and “all things under heaven,” whose transgression begat the demons of pagan cult — and in the Dialogue lets stand Trypho's citation that "the gods of the nations… are idols of demons, and not gods."5 And he reads Psalm 82 of men: the "gods" and "children of the Most High" who "die like men" are human beings made in God's image for incorruption, who forfeited it as Adam did.6

Athenagoras of Athens (c. 177)

Athenagoras, pleading the Christians' cause before Marcus Aurelius, describes an administered heaven. Christians, he says, “recognise also a multitude of angels and ministers, whom God the Maker and Framer of the world distributed and appointed to their several posts by His Logos, to occupy themselves about the elements, and the heavens, and the world” — a hierarchy of created powers set over the parts of creation, “so that God may have the universal and general providence of the whole, while the particular parts are provided for by the angels appointed over them.”7 But some angels “outraged… the government entrusted to them,” chief among them “this ruler of matter and its various forms” — the “prince of matter,” a created power now “hostile to God” who “exercises a control and management contrary to the good that is in God.” In his account the domain of the appointed powers is the elements and the world at large; he does not speak of the nations.8

Irenaeus of Lyons (c. 180)

Irenaeus writes on both questions. Reading Psalm 82 he takes its "gods" to be "the Father and the Son, and those who have received the adoption — but these are the Church," so the elohim are men deified by adoptive grace, not a council of created spirits.9 On the nations he agrees with Justin: "the gods of the heathen are idols of demons" — barred from being gods "in every sense," but real demonic powers behind the idols.10

Tertullian of Carthage (c. 197)

Tertullian states that the gods of the nations are the fallen angels and their demon-offspring. Scripture teaches "how from certain angels, who fell of their own free-will, there sprang a more wicked demon-brood," which feeds on the "flesh-fumes and blood" of sacrifice.11 These demons "make themselves gods," so behind the whole pagan pantheon is "but a single race — the race of demons"; in On Idolatry he adds that the same "deserters from God, the lovers of women" invented astrology.12

Clement of Alexandria (c. 215)

Clement of Alexandria gives the earliest extant patristic statement on this timeline that the angels are set over the nations: "by an ancient and divine order the angels are distributed among the nations," while believers are "the Lord's portion" — the two halves of Deuteronomy 32:8–9.13 He pairs it with Deuteronomy 4:19: God gave the sun, moon, and stars to the nations to worship as a permitted first step toward the Maker. For Clement these are created, ministering angels under the one Word, not rival gods.14

Origen of Alexandria (c. 248)

Origen reads Deuteronomy 32:8 — the peoples divided "according to the number of the angels of God," Israel the Lord's own portion — as a real heavenly administration of the nations. Answering the pagan Celsus, who held that "the various quarters of the earth were… allotted to different superintending spirits," he sets out his own view of "the original distribution of the various quarters of the earth among different superintending spirits."15 Those powers are created angels under God's ordering providence, not an assembly of rival "gods," and he reads the allotment as a penalty laid on the nations after Babel.16

Lactantius (c. 310)

Lactantius sets out the "pagan gods = fallen angels' offspring = demons" thesis. God sent angels to guard mankind; seduced by the devil, they were "polluted… by intercourse with women," and their mixed-nature offspring became "two kinds of demons; one of heaven, the other of the earth."17 These earthbound demons "wish themselves to appear as [men's] guardians, that they themselves may be worshipped," so men "worship the wise demons as gods of the earth"; under exorcism they "utter their own names — those which are adored in the temples," on his argument the gods of the nations and the demons are one and the same, as Tertullian also argues.18

Eusebius of Caesarea (c. 320)

Eusebius of Caesarea sets out the allotment of the nations at length. In the Proof of the Gospel IV he quotes Deuteronomy 32:8–9 in full — the nations "distributed among the invisible guardians of the nations, that is the angels," Israel handed to the Word (Christ) as His portion — and makes the Deuteronomy 4:19 pairing explicit: the guardian angels let their peoples worship "the sun and moon and stars," for "the Lord thy God has divided them for all the nations."19 He then narrates the arrangement's corruption — a rebel "great Daemon" who "obliterated the boundaries of the nations," turning the permitted star-cult into idolatry until the Word reclaims all the nations — an original angelic ordering followed by an idolatrous turn.20

Athanasius of Alexandria (c. 340)

Athanasius reads Psalm 82's "God stands in the congregation of gods" of creatures "adopted and deified through the Word" — gods by grace, not by nature — in a discourse against the Arians, setting the Son's natural divinity apart from them. The Son alone is "very God from the very God"; the others are "gods" only by participation in him.21

John Chrysostom (c. 390)

John Chrysostom, the Antiochene preacher, reads Psalm 82's "gods" as men, not a council of created spirits. Expounding Christ's use of the verse at John 10:34–36, he glosses "ye are gods" of "those who have received this honor by grace" — deified humans set off against the Son, "who hath this by nature"; and on Psalm 82:6 itself he takes "Ye are gods, and all of you are children of the Most High" to mean that "the man can become God, and a child of God" by grace. He does not take the "gods" as human judges.22

Jerome (c. 400)

Jerome reads Psalm 82 against Arius and Eunomius: "That we are gods, we are so not by nature, but by grace… I made man, that out of men gods might be made." He explicitly rejects taking the "gods" as an exalted class of kings or princes — they are all humanity, deified by adoptive grace.23

Augustine of Hippo (c. 415)

Augustine denies that Psalm 82's "gods" are heavenly beings: "Far however be it from us to understand by these Gods the gods of the Gentiles, or idols, or any creature in heaven or earth except men." The "gods" are the people of Israel, sons of the Most High by adoptive grace, who "die like men" and fall through pride "like… the devil."24

Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite (c. 500)

In On the Celestial Hierarchy Pseudo-Dionysius sorts the heavenly beings of Scripture — "sons of God," "holy ones," the "host of heaven" — into a fixed system: the heavenly beings "designated… as nine… divided into three threefold Orders" (Seraphim–Cherubim–Thrones, then Dominions–Powers–Authorities, then Principalities–Archangels–Angels), each rank passing the divine light down to the one below.25 On the nations he reads the Deuteronomy 32:8 allotment — "the Most High established borders of nations according to number of Angels of God," Michael over Israel — like Origen, as created angels under one Providence, denying that "certain other gods, preside over the other nations."26

Michael S. Heiser (c. 2015)

Michael S. Heiser argues that the elohim of Psalm 82 are real, created, lesser divine beings — Yahweh's heavenly council — sentenced to lose their immortality for misgoverning the nations, and "the plural elohim in Psalm 82 cannot be humans."27 He distinguishes Yahweh by nature, not category: "Yahweh was an elohim — but no other elohim was Yahweh." With Deuteronomy 32:8 he builds a "Deuteronomy 32 worldview" in which the nations were disinherited to rebel elohim at Babel — the frame behind his Nephilim and unseen-realm work.28
