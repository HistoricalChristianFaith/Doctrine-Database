The Apostolic Constitutions on the deacon administering the cup to the faithful

Dates: c. 380 (compiled in Syria, probably around Antioch, in the last quarter of the fourth century) · Wikipedia
Summary: The Apostolic Constitutions — the largest surviving collection of early church law and liturgy, put together in Syria about 380 — give, in Book VIII, the fullest early order of service for distributing communion, and it is plainly under both kinds. After the whole congregation has come forward "in order" — clergy, then the women, then the children, then "all the people" — the bishop hands each communicant the consecrated Bread with the words "The body of Christ," and the deacon then gives the Cup with the words "The blood of Christ, the cup of life," each communicant answering "Amen." This is direct, rubrical evidence that the laity drank the chalice in the fourth-century East, and the earliest surviving text to name the deacon as its minister — filling the Eastern/Syrian span between Cyril of Jerusalem (c. 350) and the Council in Trullo (692).

Claims & evidence

The bishop gives the Body, the deacon gives the Cup — "The blood of Christ, the cup of life" (Book VIII, ch. 13)

And after that, let the bishop partake, then the presbyters, and deacons, and sub-deacons, and the readers, and the singers, and the ascetics; and then of the women, the deaconesses, and the virgins, and the widows; then the children; and then all the people in order, with reverence and godly fear, without tumult. And let the bishop give the oblation, saying, The body of Christ; and let him that receiveth say, Amen. And let the deacon take the cup; and when he gives it, say, The blood of Christ, the cup of life; and let him that drinketh say, Amen.

— Constitutions of the Holy Apostles, Book VIII, ch. 13, source (cf. New Advent). This is a liturgical rubric, not an argument: it simply lays out how communion is administered, and the ordinary sequence it prescribes is the whole church receiving both the Bread and the Cup. Two details are notable. First, the recipients are explicitly the entire assembly — after the ranks of clergy come "the women," "the children," and "all the people," none of whom are exempted from the Cup. Second, the two species are given by two different ministers: the bishop gives the Body and the deacon the Cup — the earliest surviving text to fix the deacon as the chalice's minister, a division of labour that would remain standard in the East.

"Let every rank by itself partake of the Lord's body and precious blood in order" (Book II, ch. 57)

After this let the sacrifice follow, the people standing, and praying silently; and when the oblation has been made, let every rank by itself partake of the Lord's body and precious blood in order, and approach with reverence and holy fear, as to the body of their king. Let the women approach with their heads covered, as is becoming the order of women.

— Constitutions of the Holy Apostles, Book II, ch. 57, source (cf. New Advent). The earlier and independently compiled Book II, describing the shape of the Sunday assembly, says the same in passing: "every rank" — laymen and laywomen no less than clergy — partakes "of the Lord's body and precious blood." Both species are named for the whole body of the faithful, confirming that the two-minister distribution formula of Book VIII is describing the reception of the ordinary communicant, not the clergy's communion alone.

Notes

  • What the text is, and its date. The Apostolic Constitutions are a pseudonymous compilation — cast as the decrees of the apostles handed down through Clement of Rome, but in fact assembled by a single Syrian editor who reworked earlier sources (the Didascalia behind Books I–VI, the Didache behind Book VII, and a developed church order, the so-called Apostolic Tradition material, behind Book VIII). Scholars date the compilation to roughly 375–380, in or near Antioch. The eucharistic liturgy of Book VIII — the "Clementine liturgy" — is the oldest complete eucharistic rite to survive, and its distribution rubric is the value here: whatever its sources' varied dates, the compiler's own late-fourth-century church administered communion to the laity under both kinds as a matter of settled routine.
  • The interpolation question does not touch the point. The compiler's hand has long been suspected of an Arianizing or subordinationist theology, and the collection was formally set aside by canon 2 of the Council in Trullo (692) precisely because "heretical" matter had been "of old time" interpolated into it. But that reservation concerns the doctrinal cast of the prayers, not the shape of the rite; no one has disputed that the fourth-century Syrian church gave the cup to the faithful, and the surrounding witnesses — Cyril in the East, Ambrose and Augustine in the West — show exactly the same practice with no controversy attached.
  • Where it sits on this timeline. The Constitutions reinforce the both-kinds norm on the Eastern/Syrian side between Cyril of Jerusalem's Jerusalem catechesis (c. 350) and the Council in Trullo and the later communion-spoon (692 onward). Where Cyril describes reception from the communicant's side (receiving the Body in the cupped hand, then drawing near to "the Cup of His Blood"), Book VIII gives the minister's side — the very words said over each species and who says them — the two accounts together painting the fourth-century Eastern rite in full. It is the practice the medieval Latin West would later reverse (see Aquinas), and which the East, faithful to rubrics like this one, never withdrew.
