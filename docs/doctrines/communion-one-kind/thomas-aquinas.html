Thomas Aquinas on concomitance and withholding the chalice

Dates: c. 1274 · Wikipedia
Summary: Aquinas is the theological hinge of this timeline. In the treatise on the Eucharist in the Summa Theologiae (Tertia Pars) he gives classic scholastic form to the doctrine of concomitance — that because the risen Christ's body, blood, soul, and divinity are inseparably joined, the whole living Christ is present under each species, so one who receives the consecrated bread alone receives Christ entire (III, q. 76, aa. 1–2). On that basis he defends, as a "prudent custom," the growing practice of not offering the chalice to the laity for fear the Blood be spilt (III, q. 80, a. 12) — while insisting the priest who consecrates must always receive both. This is the reasoning on which the medieval West could judge withholding the cup no deprivation of grace; the timeline block here reports the position, and the adversarial weighing of the concomitance case belongs among this doctrine's arguments.

Claims & evidence

Concomitance: the whole Christ present under each species

Aquinas distinguishes two modes in which anything of Christ is present in the sacrament. What the words of consecration directly effect — the body, under the bread; the blood, under the wine — is present "by the power of the sacrament"; everything really united to it (for the risen Christ, that is all the rest of him) follows "from natural concomitance":

It is absolutely necessary to confess according to Catholic faith that the entire Christ is in this sacrament. Yet we must know that there is something of Christ in this sacrament in a twofold manner: first, as it were, by the power of the sacrament; secondly, from natural concomitance.

— Summa Theologiae III, q. 76, a. 1.1 Because the risen Lord's blood, soul, and Godhead are never again separated from his body, wherever the body is present the rest is present too — so the whole Christ is under the bread, and the whole Christ under the wine alike:

it must be held most certainly that the whole Christ is under each sacramental species yet not alike in each. For the body of Christ is indeed present under the species of bread by the power of the sacrament, while the blood is there from real concomitance… and under the species of wine the blood is present by the power of the sacrament, and His body by real concomitance, as is also His soul and Godhead: because now Christ's blood is not separated from His body, as it was at the time of His Passion and death.

— Summa Theologiae III, q. 76, a. 2.2 The qualification "not alike in each" preserves the sign-value of the two species — the separate consecration represents the shedding of the blood in the Passion — while the reality received is one and the same whole Christ.

The prudence of withholding the cup from the laity

On this foundation Aquinas takes up the question "Whether it is lawful to receive the body of Christ without the blood?" (III, q. 80, a. 12). He first notes the practice as already established — "It is the custom of many churches for the body of Christ to be given to the communicant without His blood"3 — and then distinguishes the priest, who consecrates and must complete the whole sacrifice, from the communicant. For the people, reverence and caution govern, and the risk of spilling the Blood makes withholding the cup a prudent custom:

on the part of the recipient the greatest reverence and caution are called for, lest anything happen which is unworthy of so great a mystery. Now this could especially happen in receiving the blood, for, if incautiously handled, it might easily be spilt. And because the multitude of the Christian people increased, in which there are old, young, and children, some of whom have not enough discretion to observe due caution in using this sacrament, on that account it is a prudent custom in some churches for the blood not to be offered to the reception of the people, but to be received by the priest alone.

— Summa Theologiae III, q. 80, a. 12.4 Because of concomitance, nothing is lost to the layman who receives the host alone; the priest receives the Blood on behalf of all:

the body can be received by the people without the blood: nor is this detrimental to the sacrament. Because the priest both offers and consumes the blood on behalf of all; and Christ is fully contained under either species.

— Summa Theologiae III, q. 80, a. 12, ad 3.5 Whether this practical reason — the fear of spilling — is a sufficient warrant for the particular remedy of withdrawing the cup (as against a less-drastic remedy for the same danger, the Eastern communion-spoon) is weighed at "Was the danger of spilling the Precious Blood a sufficient warrant for withdrawing the cup?" (assessed weak as a warrant).

Gelasius answered: the old canon spoke of priests

The objector confronts Aquinas with exactly the text this timeline records earlier — Pope Gelasius's ruling that those who take the Body alone and abstain from the chalice must "either receive the entire sacrament, or… be withheld from the sacrament altogether," since "the dividing of one and the same mystery cannot happen without a great sacrilege." Aquinas does not deny the canon; he restricts its scope to the clergy:

Pope Gelasius is speaking of priests, who, as they consecrate the entire sacrament, ought to communicate in the entire sacrament. For, as we read in the (Twelfth) Council of Toledo, "What kind of a sacrifice is that, wherein not even the sacrificer is known to have a share?"

— Summa Theologiae III, q. 80, a. 12, ad 1.6 This priest/layman distinction is the pivot on which the later councils would answer the Hussite and Reformation appeal to Gelasius: the requirement of both kinds binds the one who offers the sacrifice, not every communicant.

Notes

  • The rationale, not the practice's origin. Aquinas did not invent withholding the cup — he reports it as an existing "custom of many churches" and a "prudent custom in some churches," spreading across the Latin West over the twelfth and thirteenth centuries (amid the reed/fistula and intinction debates). What he supplies is the mature theological account — concomitance — that lets the practice be judged, on the Church's own terms, no loss of grace to the laity.
  • Priest and people are treated differently on purpose. For Aquinas the perfection of the sacrament lies in its consecration, not in the manner of the faithful's reception; so the priest must receive both species (completing the sacrifice), while the people may receive one. This is the seed of the distinction the Council of Trent (Session XXI, 1562) would define.
  • Where the weighing lives. Whether concomitance actually justifies withholding a cup the Lord handed to the disciples with "Drink ye all of it" (Matthew 26:27) is a contested question adjudicated among this doctrine's arguments, not on this timeline block, which reports Aquinas's position.
  • Provenance. Questions 76 and 80 stand in the Tertia Pars proper, the part Aquinas wrote himself. The translation quoted is that of the Fathers of the English Dominican Province.
