The Didache on communion under both kinds

Dates: c. 100 (the Didache, or Teaching of the Twelve Apostles, a Syrian/Palestinian church order usually dated late first–early second century) · Wikipedia
Summary: The earliest surviving Christian liturgical text gives thanksgivings over both the cup and the broken bread — the two elements named together, in that order — and admits only the baptized to partake. It is the oldest witness on this timeline that the assembly's thanksgiving meal was celebrated under both kinds, and it carries no hint of reception under one kind alone.

Claims & evidence

The thanksgiving is offered over both the cup and the broken bread (Didache 9)

Now concerning the Thanksgiving (Eucharist), thus give thanks. First, concerning the cup: We thank thee, our Father, for the holy vine of David Thy servant, which Thou madest known to us through Jesus Thy Servant; to Thee be the glory for ever. And concerning the broken bread: We thank Thee, our Father, for the life and knowledge which Thou modest known to us through Jesus Thy Servant … But let no one eat or drink of your Thanksgiving (Eucharist), but they who have been baptized into the name of the Lord.

— Didache 9, source (cf. New Advent). Both species appear in the one rite: a blessing "concerning the cup" and a blessing "concerning the broken bread," and the closing rubric guards the whole against the unbaptized — "let no one eat or drink," naming both actions. Whatever the precise character of the meal (see Notes), the earliest church-order we possess describes the faithful as receiving under both bread and cup, with no allowance for one alone.

Notes

  • The Didache lists the cup before the bread — the reverse of the Pauline and later liturgical order (bread then cup, 1 Corinthians 11:23–25) — a primitive feature it shares with the "cup first" reading found in some manuscripts of Luke 22:17–20. This bears on the antiquity of the text, not on the number of kinds: both elements are present either way.
  • Scholars debate whether Didache 9–10 describes the full eucharist or an agape (fellowship-meal) with eucharistic overtones, since the chapters lack the words of institution and the death-of-the-Lord language that Justin and Cyprian supply. Even on the more minimal reading, the point that matters here is untouched: the church's earliest table-prayers know a cup and a bread given to the baptized, not a bread alone. The realist and sacrificial weight of the rite is treated on the Real Presence and ministerial priesthood timelines, where the Didache also appears.
  • No text of this period contemplates withholding the cup. Communion under one kind enters the record only later and only as a practical exception — the sacrament carried to the sick or reserved at home — as the Cyprian of Carthage block below shows.
