The Didache on communion under both kinds

Dates: c. 100 (the Didache, or Teaching of the Twelve Apostles, a Syrian/Palestinian church order usually dated late first–early second century) · Wikipedia
Summary: The earliest surviving Christian liturgical text gives thanksgivings over both the cup and the broken bread — the two elements named together, in that order — and admits only the baptized to partake. It is the earliest extant witness on this timeline to a thanksgiving celebrated under both kinds.

Claims & evidence

The thanksgiving is offered over both the cup and the broken bread (Didache 9)

Now concerning the Thanksgiving (Eucharist), thus give thanks. First, concerning the cup: We thank thee, our Father, for the holy vine of David Thy servant, which Thou madest known to us through Jesus Thy Servant; to Thee be the glory for ever. And concerning the broken bread: We thank Thee, our Father, for the life and knowledge which Thou modest known to us through Jesus Thy Servant … But let no one eat or drink of your Thanksgiving (Eucharist), but they who have been baptized into the name of the Lord.

— Didache 9, source (cf. New Advent). Both species appear in the one rite: a blessing "concerning the cup" and a blessing "concerning the broken bread," and the closing rubric guards the whole against the unbaptized — "let no one eat or drink," naming both actions. Whatever the precise character of the meal (see Notes), this early church-order describes the baptized as receiving under both bread and cup.

Notes

  • The Didache lists the cup before the bread — the reverse of the Pauline and later liturgical order (bread then cup, 1 Corinthians 11:23–25) — a primitive feature it shares with the "cup first" reading found in some manuscripts of Luke 22:17–20. This bears on the antiquity of the text, not on the number of kinds: both elements are present either way.
  • Didache 9–10 lacks the words of institution and the death-of-the-Lord language that Justin and Cyprian supply, and whether it describes the full eucharist or an agape (fellowship-meal) with eucharistic overtones is debated (⚠ unverified: no proponents of either view are cited here). On either reading the prayers name a cup and a bread given to the baptized. The realist and sacrificial weight of the rite is treated on the Real Presence and ministerial priesthood timelines, where the Didache also appears.
  • No text of this period on this timeline speaks of withholding the cup. The earliest instances of reception under one kind recorded here are practical ones — the sacrament reserved at home — in Cyprian of Carthage.
