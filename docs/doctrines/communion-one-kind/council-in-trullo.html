The Council in Trullo & the communion-spoon — lay communion in the Byzantine East

Dates: the Quinisext Council in Trullo, 692; the communion-spoon (labis) generalizing from the late 7th to the 11th century · Wikipedia
Summary: In the Christian East the laity continued to receive both kinds, while the mechanism of reception changed. In 692 the Council in Trullo still assumes lay reception of the Body in the hand (canon 101), as Cyril of Jerusalem had described three centuries earlier. Over the following centuries the Byzantine rite merged the two species: the consecrated Bread was placed into the chalice and the sops administered together with the Blood by a communion-spoon (the labis), standard in Constantinople by the eleventh century and to this day. The Byzantine rite thus gives the laity both the Body and the Blood by a different route than the Western common cup. For the Western instruments — the drinking-reed (fistula), and intinction, which Latin synods forbade — see the fistula and the West's rejected intinction.

Claims & evidence

Canon 101 (692): the laity receive the Body into cross-formed hands — no vessel between

Wherefore, if any one wishes to be a participator of the immaculate Body in the time of the Synaxis, and to offer himself for the communion, let him draw near, arranging his hands in the form of a cross, and so let him receive the communion of grace. But such as, instead of their hands, make vessels of gold or other materials for the reception of the divine gift, and by these receive the immaculate communion, we by no means allow to come, as preferring inanimate and inferior matter to the image of God.

— Council in Trullo, canon 101, "The Canons of the Council in Trullo" (NPNF2 14). Canon 101 directs that the communicant receive the Body in cross-formed hands, as Cyril describes at Jerusalem c. 350.

The communion-spoon: both species merged, administered together by intinction

In the Byzantine rite its use is restricted to serving communion to the lesser clergy and laity under both species together via intinction; the consecrated bread is immersed in the consecrated wine, then the sops are served by means of a spoon.

— Robert F. Taft, "Byzantine Communion Spoons: A Review of the Evidence," Dumbarton Oaks Papers 50 (1996): 209–238, at 210 (also JSTOR). On Taft's description the spoon delivers both kinds at once: the Body is put into the chalice and given with the Blood.

The dating: emergent in the late seventh century, standard by the eleventh

[Humbert of Silva Candida gives] clear testimony that by the middle of the eleventh century the Byzantines in the Great Church gave communion to the laity the way they do today, with a spoon, by intinction.

— Taft, "Byzantine Communion Spoons," at 226; the earliest explicit witness to spoon-and-intinction is Anastasius of Sinai (d. after 700) in the Antiochene sphere (p. 220), and "from the twelfth century on, the communion spoon begins to appear commonly in the euchology rubrics" (p. 231). On Taft's evidence the change of mechanism was gradual, roughly the seventh through eleventh centuries.

Notes

  • The mode of reception. In the Eastern churches the laity have continued to receive both kinds; the manner of reception changed: the Byzantine rite moved from receiving both species separately and in the hand (canon 101, 692) to receiving them together by the spoon (by the eleventh century). The churches that kept the oldest mode — both species separately, in the hand — are the Coptic, Ethiopian, and Assyrian (East Syrian), per Taft (p. 210). In each of these churches the laity receive the Blood.
  • The Chrysostom legend. A late tradition credits John Chrysostom with introducing the spoon; Taft attributes it to "overly imaginative older authors" (p. 211) and finds no documentary support for it, dating the spoon from the seventh century onward.
  • Reception and debate. Fr. Chad Arneson, writing from an Orthodox standpoint (2026), holds that the Orthodox practice of giving both species together by the spoon "satisfies the exact requirement of Pope St. Gelasius I" that the sacrament not be divided (see What does Gelasius's Comperimus forbid?).
