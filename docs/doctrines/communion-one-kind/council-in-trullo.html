The Council in Trullo & the communion-spoon — the East keeps the cup

Dates: the Quinisext Council in Trullo, 692; the communion-spoon (labis) generalizing from the late 7th to the 11th century · Wikipedia
Summary: While the medieval West drifted toward withdrawing the chalice, the Christian East kept giving the laity both kinds — but changed the mechanism. In 692 the Council in Trullo still assumes lay reception of the Body in the hand (canon 101), as Cyril of Jerusalem had described three centuries earlier. Over the following centuries the Byzantine rite merged the two species: the consecrated Bread was placed into the chalice and the sops administered together with the Blood by a communion-spoon (the labis), standard in Constantinople by the eleventh century and to this day. The East thus gives the laity both the Body and the Blood by a different route than the Western common cup — the standing counter-case that the West's withdrawal was a Latin development, not the church's universal mind. For the Latin mirror of this engineering — the drinking-reed (fistula) and the intinction the West possessed but forbade — see the fistula and the West's rejected intinction.

Claims & evidence

Canon 101 (692): the laity receive the Body into cross-formed hands — no vessel between

Wherefore, if any one wishes to be a participator of the immaculate Body in the time of the Synaxis, and to offer himself for the communion, let him draw near, arranging his hands in the form of a cross, and so let him receive the communion of grace. But such as, instead of their hands, make vessels of gold or other materials for the reception of the divine gift, and by these receive the immaculate communion, we by no means allow to come, as preferring inanimate and inferior matter to the image of God.

— Council in Trullo, canon 101, "The Canons of the Council in Trullo" (NPNF2 14). As late as 692 the Eastern lay communicant still received the consecrated Body directly into the cupped, cross-formed hands — the very posture Cyril taught at Jerusalem c. 350 — and then the Cup. The canon legislates how the laity receive, not whether they receive the Blood: there is no hint here of reserving the chalice to the clergy. The Eastern discipline at this date is both kinds, received separately, in the hand and from the cup.

The communion-spoon: both species merged, administered together by intinction

In the Byzantine rite its use is restricted to serving communion to the lesser clergy and laity under both species together via intinction; the consecrated bread is immersed in the consecrated wine, then the sops are served by means of a spoon.

— Robert F. Taft, "Byzantine Communion Spoons: A Review of the Evidence," Dumbarton Oaks Papers 50 (1996): 209–238, at 210 (also JSTOR). This is the crucial point for this timeline: the spoon does not replace the cup — it delivers both kinds at once. The Body is put into the chalice and given with the Blood, so the Byzantine layman receives exactly what the priest does, the whole sacrament under both species. The East solved the practical fear of spilling the Blood — the same fear the West cited to reserve the chalice — not by taking the cup away but by merging the species so the Blood could not be dropped.

The dating: emergent in the late seventh century, standard by the eleventh

[Humbert of Silva Candida gives] clear testimony that by the middle of the eleventh century the Byzantines in the Great Church gave communion to the laity the way they do today, with a spoon, by intinction.

— Taft, "Byzantine Communion Spoons," at 226; the earliest explicit witness to spoon-and-intinction is Anastasius of Sinai (d. after 700) in the Antiochene sphere (p. 220), and "from the twelfth century on, the communion spoon begins to appear commonly in the euchology rubrics" (p. 231). The change of mechanism was therefore gradual — roughly the seventh through eleventh centuries — and precisely tracks the era in which the Latin West was moving the opposite way, toward the host alone. Where the West narrowed the sacrament for the laity, the East re-engineered its delivery while keeping it whole.

Notes

  • Both kinds, never withdrawn — but the mode did change. The precise claim is that the East never withdrew the cup (both kinds) from the laity, as the medieval Latin West did; bread-alone lay communion never became Eastern practice. That does not mean the manner of reception never changed: the Byzantine rite moved from receiving both species separately and in the hand (canon 101, 692) to receiving them together by the spoon (by the eleventh century). The churches that kept the oldest mode — both species separately, in the hand — are the Coptic, Ethiopian, and Assyrian (East Syrian), per Taft (p. 210). What unites all of them, and separates the whole East from the medieval West, is that the laity always received the Blood.
  • The Chrysostom legend. A late tradition credits John Chrysostom with introducing the spoon; Taft dismisses it as the invention of "overly imaginative older authors" (p. 211) with no documentary support. The spoon is a genuine later development of the seventh century onward, not an apostolic or fourth-century institution.
  • Why it belongs on this timeline — the concomitance counter-example. The East affirms the whole Christ present in each particle of the sacrament as fully as the West's doctrine of concomitance does — Orthodox communicants receive by spoon precisely because each sop is the whole Christ. Yet that same conviction did not lead the East to withhold the cup. So concomitance, by itself, cannot have compelled the Western withdrawal: a church can hold that Christ is received entire under either kind and still give the laity both. The East is thus the live counter-example to the inference "concomitance → drop the cup," which any assessment of the concomitance argument on this timeline must reckon with.
