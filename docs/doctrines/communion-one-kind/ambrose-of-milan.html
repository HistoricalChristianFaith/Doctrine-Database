Ambrose of Milan on the faithful drinking the consecrated cup

Dates: c. 390 (the mystagogic catecheses On the Mysteries and On the Sacraments, delivered to the newly baptized during his episcopate, 374–397) · Wikipedia
Summary: Instructing the newly baptized at Milan in the week after their baptism, Ambrose walks them through the eucharist they have just received for the first time — and it is plainly under both kinds. He tells them that "Blood flowed from Christ" for them and "satiates you for eternity," that after the consecration the cup "is called Blood" and the communicant answers "Amen," and that the Church bids her children "Eat … and drink." Ambrose is thus a fourth-century witness from the Latin West that lay reception of the chalice was the assumed norm — the very practice the medieval West would later reverse — filling the Western spine between Cyril of Jerusalem in the East and the later Roman witnesses Leo and Gelasius.

Claims & evidence

"For you Blood flowed from Christ … the Blood satiates you for eternity" (On the Mysteries §§48–49)

For them water flowed from the rock, for you Blood flowed from Christ; water satisfied them for a time, the Blood satiates you for eternity. The Jew drinks and thirsts again, you after drinking will be beyond the power of thirsting; that was in a shadow, this is in truth.

— On the Mysteries (De mysteriis), §§48–49, source (cf. New Advent). The whole treatise is addressed to the neophytes — the lay men and women just baptized at the Easter vigil — and the second person throughout ("for you Blood flowed," "you after drinking") is the ordinary communicant, not the clergy. Ambrose takes for granted that they drink the consecrated cup: the contrast he draws between the water the Israelites drank from the rock and the Blood "you" now drink only works if the faithful before him receive the chalice.

After the consecration the cup "is called Blood," and the communicant answers "Amen" (On the Mysteries §54)

The Lord Jesus Himself proclaims: "This is My Body." Before the blessing of the heavenly words another nature is spoken of, after the consecration the Body is signified. He Himself speaks of His Blood. Before the consecration it has another name, after it is called Blood. And you say, Amen, that is, It is true. Let the heart within confess what the mouth utters, let the soul feel what the voice speaks.

— On the Mysteries, §54, source (cf. New Advent). Ambrose describes the reception dialogue as the neophyte experiences it: the minister names what is given — the Body, and then the Blood — and the communicant answers "Amen." His parallel companion work On the Sacraments spells the cup half out explicitly, having the newly baptized told "The Blood of Christ" and answer "Amen" as they drink, and quoting the institution's "Take, and drink from this all of you; for this is my blood" over the chalice.* The point either way is the same: at Milan the laity received, and drank, the consecrated wine.

The Church bids her children "Eat … and drink and be inebriated" (On the Mysteries §58)

Wherefore, too, the Church, beholding so great grace, exhorts her sons and her friends to come together to the sacraments, saying: "Eat, my friends, and drink and be inebriated, my brother."

— On the Mysteries, §58, source (cf. New Advent). The invitation to the sacrament is a double one — eat and drink — held out to the whole body of the faithful ("her sons and her friends"), not to the celebrant alone.

Notes

  • What the passages establish. Ambrose is not arguing about the cup — no one in his day disputed it — he is simply describing the rite he administers, and that rite gives the laity the Blood. Taken with Cyril of Jerusalem's Eastern account a generation earlier, he shows both halves of the fourth-century church, Greek and Latin, communicating the faithful under both kinds. This is the pre-withdrawal Western practice on which the whole later controversy leans: the medieval reservation of the chalice to the priest (see Aquinas) is a change from what Ambrose, Augustine, Leo, and Gelasius all take for granted.
  • *On On the Sacraments. The reception dialogue at the cup — the communicant told "The Blood of Christ" and answering "Amen" as they drink, and the words of institution "drink from this all of you" said over the chalice — is given more fully in Ambrose's companion catechesis On the Sacraments (De sacramentis) IV–V, the shorter and probably stenographic twin of On the Mysteries. The clean quotations above are drawn from On the Mysteries, whose standard English is more reliable.
  • Ambrose appears on this wiki elsewhere as well (among the witnesses to the descent of Christ, and on the perpetual virginity of Mary); the eucharistic catecheses cited here belong to his teaching of the newly baptized late in his Milanese episcopate.
