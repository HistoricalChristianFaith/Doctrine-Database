Pope Gelasius I — Comperimus: the whole sacrament, or none at all

Dates: c. 494 (the decretal Comperimus, to the bishops Majoricus and John; Gelasius reigned 492–496) · Wikipedia
Summary: In a fragment of a letter to two bishops — preserved as a canon in Gratian's Decretum (De consecratione, D.2 c.12) under the rubric "the priest ought not to receive the Body of Christ without His Blood" — Gelasius rules that those who take only the consecrated Bread and abstain from the chalice must "either receive the sacraments in their entirety, or be repelled from them entirely, because the division of one and the same mystery cannot occur without grave sacrilege." As with Leo the Great half a century earlier, the target is the Manichaeans, who shunned the wine; and as with Leo, the ruling assumes reception under both kinds as the norm whose division is a scandal. This canon became one of the chief proof-texts of the Hussite and Reformation demand for the lay chalice.

Claims & evidence

Whole sacrament or none — dividing the mystery is "grave sacrilege" (Comperimus)

Item Gelasius Papa Maiorico et Iohanni Episcopis. Conperimus autem, quod quidam sumpta tantummodo sacri corporis portione a calice sacrati cruoris abstineant. Qui proculdubio (quoniam nescio qua superstitione docentur astringi) aut integra sacramenta percipiant, aut ab integris arceantur, quia diuisio unius eiusdemque misterii sine grandi sacrilegio non potest prouenire.

— Gelasius, decretal Comperimus (to bishops Majoricus and John), preserved in Gratian, Decretum, De consecratione D.2 c.12, source (Bayerische Staatsbibliothek digital edition). In English: "We have learned that certain persons, having received a portion of the sacred Body alone, abstain from the cup of the sacred Blood. Since these are taught to be bound by I know not what superstition, let them without doubt either receive the sacraments in their entirety, or be repelled from them entirely, because the division of one and the same mystery cannot occur without grave sacrilege." The ruling treats reception of the Body without the Blood as an offence to be corrected, not a permitted economy — the cup is integral to "the sacraments in their entirety."

Notes

  • The Manichaean context. Later tradition (so the Catholic Encyclopedia) explains the decretal as aimed at detecting Manichaeans, who — present in numbers at Rome and feigning Catholicism — held wine to be impure and so refused the chalice; requiring both kinds flushed them out. On this reading the canon's immediate purpose is disciplinary and polemical, matching Leo's sermon. See Catholic Encyclopedia, "Pope St. Gelasius I."
  • What it settles, and what it does not. The canon is strong evidence that in the fifth century the Roman church regarded reception under both kinds as the norm and dividing the sacrament as sacrilege. Whether it also decides the later question — is the medieval Western withholding of the cup a reversal of binding ancient discipline, or the lawful adjustment of a custom? — is contested. The Augsburg Confession (1530) cited Comperimus for the lay chalice ("Gelasius commands that the sacrament not be divided"); the Catholic reply answered that Gelasius here addresses priests concelebrating, or a specific heretical abuse, so the canon does not establish that both kinds are required of all by divine command. That dispute — the "reversal of ancient discipline" argument pressed at Constance and Trent — is weighed on this timeline in its own right; this page only fixes what the text plainly says.
  • The companion witness is Leo the Great (c. 445), whose Sermon 42 made the same point homiletically: the Manichaeans "altogether refuse to drink the Blood of our Redemption," and are thereby detected.
