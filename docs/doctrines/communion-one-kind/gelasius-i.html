Pope Gelasius I — Comperimus: the whole sacrament, or none at all

Dates: c. 494 (the decretal Comperimus, to the bishops Majoricus and John; Gelasius reigned 492–496) · Wikipedia
Summary: In a fragment of a letter to two bishops — preserved as a canon in Gratian's Decretum (De consecratione, D.2 c.12) under the rubric "the priest ought not to receive the Body of Christ without His Blood" — Gelasius rules that those who take only the consecrated Bread and abstain from the chalice must "either receive the sacraments in their entirety, or be repelled from them entirely, because the division of one and the same mystery cannot occur without grave sacrilege." The decree does not name the persons; John F. X. Murphy (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1909) identifies them as Manichaeans, who shunned the wine, as in the sermon of Leo the Great half a century earlier. Jakoubek of Stříbro (1414) and the Augsburg Confession (1530) cited the canon for the lay chalice; what it forbids is disputed (see Notes).

Claims & evidence

Whole sacrament or none — dividing the mystery is "grave sacrilege" (Comperimus)

Item Gelasius Papa Maiorico et Iohanni Episcopis. Conperimus autem, quod quidam sumpta tantummodo sacri corporis portione a calice sacrati cruoris abstineant. Qui proculdubio (quoniam nescio qua superstitione docentur astringi) aut integra sacramenta percipiant, aut ab integris arceantur, quia diuisio unius eiusdemque misterii sine grandi sacrilegio non potest prouenire.

— Gelasius, decretal Comperimus (to bishops Majoricus and John), preserved in Gratian, Decretum, De consecratione D.2 c.12, source (Bayerische Staatsbibliothek digital edition). In English: "We have learned that certain persons, having received a portion of the sacred Body alone, abstain from the cup of the sacred Blood. Since these are taught to be bound by I know not what superstition, let them without doubt either receive the sacraments in their entirety, or be repelled from them entirely, because the division of one and the same mystery cannot occur without grave sacrilege." The ruling orders those who take the Body and abstain from the cup to receive both or neither.

Notes

  • Murphy’s identification of the persons. John F. X. Murphy (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1909) explains the decretal as aimed at detecting Manichaeans, who — present in numbers at Rome and feigning Catholicism — held wine to be impure and so refused the chalice; requiring both kinds flushed them out. On this reading the canon's immediate purpose is disciplinary and polemical, matching Leo's sermon. See Catholic Encyclopedia, "Pope St. Gelasius I."
  • Reception and debate. Gratian files the canon under a rubric about the priest, and Thomas Aquinas (c. 1274) read it of priests, "who, as they consecrate the entire sacrament, ought to communicate in the entire sacrament." Jakoubek of Stříbro (1414) cited it for the lay chalice, the Augsburg Confession (1530) wrote that "Pope Gelasius commands that the Sacrament be not divided," Calvin (1559) quoted it in the same cause, and Fr. Chad Arneson (Orthodox, 2026) reads it as condemning "the physical act of division itself" ("Was Pope St. Gelasius a Hussite?"). Robert Bellarmine (⚠ unverified, as reported by Johann Adam Scherzer, Anti-Bellarmine), Patrick Toner (1908) and John F. X. Murphy (1909) read it as a measure against the Manichaean refusal that passes no judgement on communion under one kind, as does James Gibbons (1876). These readings are set out with their proponents at What does Gelasius's Comperimus forbid?
  • The companion witness is Leo the Great (c. 445), whose Sermon 42 describes the same behaviour: the Manichaeans "altogether refuse to drink the Blood of our Redemption," and are thereby detected.
