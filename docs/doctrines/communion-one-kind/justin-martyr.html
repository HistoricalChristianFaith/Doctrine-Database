Justin Martyr on communion under both kinds

Dates: c. 155 (the First Apology, addressed to the emperor Antoninus Pius) · Wikipedia
Summary: Describing the Sunday assembly for a pagan readership, Justin gives the earliest detailed account of a Christian eucharist. The deacons "give to each of those present to partake of the bread and wine mixed with water," and to the absent "they carry away a portion" — both elements distributed to all who are present, and both carried out to those who could not attend. It is the earliest witness on this timeline that reception under both kinds, including the reserved sacrament carried to the absent, was the ordinary Roman practice of the mid-second century.

Claims & evidence

The deacons give everyone present both the bread and the cup, and carry both to the absent (First Apology 65)

Then there is brought to the president of the brethren bread and a cup of wine mixed with water; and he taking them, gives praise and glory to the Father of the universe, through the name of the Son and of the Holy Ghost … And when the president has given thanks, and all the people have expressed their assent, those who are called by us deacons give to each of those present to partake of the bread and wine mixed with water over which the thanksgiving was pronounced, and to those who are absent they carry away a portion.

— First Apology 65, source (cf. New Advent). Writing to explain Christian worship to outsiders, Justin has no reason to shade the description: every communicant present receives the bread and the mingled cup alike. The clause about the absent is the first attested instance of the reserved sacrament — a portion consecrated in the assembly and carried out by the deacons — and here it is carried under both species ("a portion" of the bread-and-cup rite), not the host alone.

The Sunday synaxis distributes to each and sends to the absent by the deacons (First Apology 67)

And on the day called Sunday, all who live in cities or in the country gather together to one place … when our prayer is ended, bread and wine and water are brought, and the president in like manner offers prayers and thanksgivings, according to his ability, and the people assent, saying Amen; and there is a distribution to each, and a participation of that over which thanks have been given, and to those who are absent a portion is sent by the deacons.

— First Apology 67, source (cf. New Advent). The weekly account repeats the pattern of chapter 65: "bread and wine and water are brought," a distribution "to each," and a portion "sent by the deacons" to the absent. The consistency across two descriptions makes both-kinds reception the settled shape of the rite as Justin knew it.

Notes

  • The value of the witness lies in its audience: this is an apology addressed to the emperor, not in-house catechesis, so Justin describes plainly what any inquirer could have observed. That the deacons distribute both the bread and the cup to all present, and carry a portion to the absent, is offered as simple fact about how Christians worship.
  • Justin's "to those who are absent … they carry away a portion" is the seed of a practice that would later narrow toward one kind — the sacrament taken to the sick, or kept in the home — but in Justin it is still explicitly the whole rite, bread and cup. On the reserved sacrament kept at home in the next century, and the first genuine one-kind exceptions, see the Cyprian of Carthage block below.
  • Justin's realist and sacrificial language (the food "is the flesh and blood of that Jesus who was made flesh"; the "pure sacrifice" of Malachi) is treated on the Real Presence and ministerial priesthood timelines, where he also appears.
