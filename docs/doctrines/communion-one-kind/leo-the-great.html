Leo the Great on the cup as a test of orthodoxy (against the Manichaeans)

Dates: c. 445 (Sermon 42, On Lent, 4, preached during his pontificate, 440–461) · Wikipedia
Summary: Preaching at Rome, Leo tells his congregation how to unmask the Manichaeans who hid among the faithful. Because the Manichaeans held all matter — wine included — to be evil, they would take the consecrated Bread but refuse the chalice; and it is precisely by that refusal, Leo says, that they betray themselves and are to be driven out "by priestly authority." The passage is a fifth-century Roman witness that reception under both kinds was the assumed norm — so much so that declining the cup marked a man as a heretic. It is one of the two texts (with Pope Gelasius I) that the Hussites and Reformers would later press to argue the medieval withdrawal of the cup reversed ancient Roman discipline.

Claims & evidence

The Manichaeans betray themselves by receiving the Body but refusing the Blood (Sermon 42.5)

And since to conceal their infidelity they dare to be present at our meetings, at the Communion of the Mysteries they bring themselves sometimes, in order to ensure their concealment, to receive Christ's Body with unworthy lips, though they altogether refuse to drink the Blood of our Redemption. And this we make known to you, holy brethren, that men of this sort may be detected by you by these signs, and that they whose impious pretences have been discovered may be driven from the society of the saints by priestly authority.

— Sermon 42 (On Lent, 4), §5, source (cf. New Advent). The logic of Leo's detection test presupposes the ordinary practice: every genuine communicant drinks the cup, so a man who takes the Bread but consistently avoids the chalice stands out. Withholding the cup is here not a Catholic option but the tell-tale of a heretic — the reverse of the medieval Western discipline that would later make one-kind reception the lay norm.

Why the Manichaeans shunned the cup: matter, and wine, held to be evil (Sermon 42.4)

For they condemn the creature's nature to the Creator's injury, and maintain that they are defiled by eating those things of which they contend the devil, not God, is the author: although absolutely nothing that exists is evil, nor is anything in nature included in the actually bad. For the good Creator made all things good … Of which whatever is granted to man for food and drink, is holy and clean after its kind.

— Sermon 42 (On Lent, 4), §4, source (cf. New Advent). Leo's refutation of Manichaean dualism supplies the reason behind the refusal of the chalice: holding all matter (and wine especially) to be the devil's work, the sect could not bring itself to drink the consecrated wine. The abstention was thus a doctrinal act, not mere squeamishness — which is exactly why Leo could treat it as a reliable sign.

Notes

  • What the passage does and does not establish. It shows plainly that at fifth-century Rome the faithful communicated under both kinds and that abstaining from the cup was so anomalous it flagged a heretic. It does not, by itself, decide whether the later Western practice of withholding the cup from the laity is a "reversal of ancient discipline" or a legitimate change of a disciplinary custom: Leo is condemning a heretical refusal grounded in a false doctrine of matter, not laying down that both kinds are required of all by divine command. That distinction is the hinge of the argument the Utraquists and Reformers built on this text and Gelasius's, and the reply the councils of Constance and Trent returned — weighed in its own right at Did the medieval withdrawal of the cup reverse ancient Roman discipline?
  • Leo also appears on this wiki as a witness elsewhere (the Tome to Flavian on the two natures of Christ). His Sermon 42 belongs to a series of Lenten sermons preached across his pontificate; the Manichaean crisis at Rome he addresses here is the same one that prompted his investigations of the sect in the mid-440s.
  • The companion witness is Pope Gelasius I (c. 494), whose decretal Comperimus made the same point as a canon: those who take only the Bread and abstain from the cup must "either receive the sacraments in their entirety, or be repelled from them entirely."
