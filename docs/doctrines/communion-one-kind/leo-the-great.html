Leo the Great on refusal of the cup as a sign of the Manichaeans

Dates: c. 445 (Sermon 42, On Lent, 4, preached during his pontificate, 440–461) · Wikipedia
Summary: Preaching at Rome, Leo tells his congregation how to unmask the Manichaeans who hid among the faithful. Because the Manichaeans held all matter — wine included — to be evil, they would take the consecrated Bread but refuse the chalice; and it is by this, among other "signs," Leo says, that they betray themselves and are to be driven out "by priestly authority." The passage is read in the later debate both (Chad Arneson, 2026) as a witness to lay reception of the cup and (Patrick Toner, 1908) as evidence that some received under one kind, and alongside the decree of Pope Gelasius I; how it bears on lay communion under one kind is disputed (see What does Gelasius's Comperimus forbid?).

Claims & evidence

The Manichaeans betray themselves by receiving the Body but refusing the Blood (Sermon 42.5)

And since to conceal their infidelity they dare to be present at our meetings, at the Communion of the Mysteries they bring themselves sometimes, in order to ensure their concealment, to receive Christ's Body with unworthy lips, though they altogether refuse to drink the Blood of our Redemption. And this we make known to you, holy brethren, that men of this sort may be detected by you by these signs, and that they whose impious pretences have been discovered may be driven from the society of the saints by priestly authority.

— Sermon 42 (On Lent, 4), §5, source (cf. New Advent). Leo gives the refusal of the Blood as one of the "signs" by which the hidden Manichaeans may be detected and excluded. Patrick Toner (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1908) holds that such concealment "would have been impossible, unless it was customary at the time for at least some of the faithful to receive under one kind alone," and that the exclusion of those detected "implies no reprobation on the merits of Communion under one kind" ("Communion under Both Kinds"). Fr. Chad Arneson, writing from an Orthodox standpoint (2026), reads the sermon as a witness to the Roman norm: "For Leo, as for Gelasius after him, refusal of the Chalice was a conspicuous anomaly that stood out against the universal Roman practice of lay chalice reception" ("Was Pope St. Gelasius a Hussite?").

Why the Manichaeans shunned the cup: matter, and wine, held to be evil (Sermon 42.4)

For they condemn the creature's nature to the Creator's injury, and maintain that they are defiled by eating those things of which they contend the devil, not God, is the author: although absolutely nothing that exists is evil, nor is anything in nature included in the actually bad. For the good Creator made all things good … Of which whatever is granted to man for food and drink, is holy and clean after its kind.

— Sermon 42 (On Lent, 4), §4, source (cf. New Advent). Leo's account of Manichaean teaching gives the reason for the refusal of the chalice: the sect held the things it abstained from to be of the devil's making, and so would not drink the consecrated wine. On Leo's account the abstention followed from the sect's teaching about matter.

Notes

  • Reception and debate. Leo's sermon is read together with Gelasius's Comperimus in the later debate over the lay cup. Patrick Toner (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1908) reads both texts, and John F. X. Murphy (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1909) reads Gelasius's decree, as measures against the Manichaean refusal that pass no judgement on communion under one kind; Fr. Chad Arneson (Orthodox, 2026) reads the sermon as a witness to the Roman norm of lay chalice reception; the readings of Gelasius's decree as a general rule against dividing the sacrament, and as a rule for the celebrating priest, are set out with their proponents at What does Gelasius's Comperimus forbid?
  • Leo also appears on this wiki as a witness elsewhere (the Tome to Flavian on the two natures of Christ). His Sermon 42 belongs to a series of Lenten sermons preached across his pontificate; the Manichaean crisis at Rome he addresses here is the same one that prompted his investigations of the sect in the mid-440s.
  • The companion witness is Pope Gelasius I (c. 494), whose decretal Comperimus describes the same behaviour in a canon: those who take only the Bread and abstain from the cup must "either receive the sacraments in their entirety, or be repelled from them entirely."
