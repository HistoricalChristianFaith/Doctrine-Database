Martin Luther and the restoration of the cup to the laity

Dates: c. 1520 (The Babylonian Captivity of the Church) · Martin Luther, 10 November 1483–18 February 1546 · Wikipedia
Summary: In The Babylonian Captivity of the Church (October 1520) Luther named communion under one kind the first of the sacrament's three "captivities" under Rome. He argued from the Gospel accounts of the institution that Christ commanded both kinds for all — "Drink ye all of it" — so that withholding the cup from the laity is an act no church, pope, or council has power to impose. With Luther the medieval one-kind custom passed from a discipline the councils had merely defended into one the Reformation churches set out to reverse: the Lutheran, Reformed, and Anglican bodies restored the lay chalice as a matter of Gospel obedience, and the point was written into their confessions and liturgies.

Claims & evidence

Communion under one kind as the "first captivity" (1520)

Luther opens his treatment of the Eucharist by declaring the withdrawal of the cup the sacrament's first bondage: "The first captivity of this sacrament, therefore, concerns its substance or completeness, of which we have been deprived by the despotism of Rome" (The Babylonian Captivity of the Church, trans. A. T. W. Steinhaeuser). By "substance or completeness" he means the whole institution — bread and cup — of which, on his reading, the laity had been robbed. The three "captivities" he names in turn are the withholding of the cup, the doctrine of transubstantiation, and the mass offered as a sacrifice; the lay chalice comes first.

The argument from the institution: "Drink ye all of it"

Luther's case rests on the words of institution as the evangelists report them. Christ's command over the cup, he insists, is addressed to all and is a command, not a permission: "For if all are to drink, and the words cannot be understood as addressed to the priests alone, then it is certainly an impious act to withhold the cup from laymen who desire it" (Babylonian Captivity). He presses a grammatical point the defenders of one kind had to answer: the note of universality is fastened to the cup, not the bread. "Mark likewise does not say, 'They all ate from it,' but, 'They all drank from it.' Both Matthew and Mark attach the note of universality to the cup, not to the bread, as though the Spirit saw this schism coming, by which some would be forbidden to partake of the cup, which Christ desired should be common to all" (Babylonian Captivity; the proof-text is Matthew 26:27, "Drink ye all of it").

No power in pope or council to deny the cup

From this Luther draws the conclusion that fixes the Reformation position: the manner of the sacrament is not the church's to alter against Christ's command. "I conclude, then, that it is wicked and despotic to deny both kinds to the laity, and that this is not in the power of any angel, much less of any pope or council" (Babylonian Captivity). The sacrament, he adds, belongs to the whole people and not to the ministers who dispense it: "The sacrament does not belong to the priests, but to all, and the priests are not lords but ministers, in duty bound to administer both kinds to those who desire them" (Babylonian Captivity). This is the exact hinge that the Council of Constance (1415) had turned the other way — Constance conceding that Christ instituted both kinds and that the early church received them, yet ruling that the later one-kind custom "should be held as a law"; Luther denies the church any such authority over a dominical command.

The confessional and liturgical restatements

The point Luther made in 1520 was quickly written into the Reformation's public standards. The Augsburg Confession (1530), Article XXII, "Of Both Kinds in the Sacrament," makes exactly his argument: "To the laity are given Both Kinds in the Sacrament of the Lord's Supper, because this usage has the commandment of the Lord in Matt. 26:27, 'Drink ye all of it,' where Christ has manifestly commanded concerning the cup that all should drink. And lest any man should craftily say that this refers only to priests, Paul in 1 Cor. 11 recites an example from which it appears that the whole congregation did use both kinds" (Book of Concord, Augsburg Confession XXII). Calvin likewise treated the withdrawal of the cup as a corruption to be undone, devoting a section of the Institutes to communion in one kind as "an invention of Satan" (Institutes of the Christian Religion IV.17.47–48, CCEL). In England the change was made by statute and rubric: the Sacrament Act 1547 (the Act "against such as shall unreverently speak against the Sacrament … and for the receiving thereof under both kinds") directed that the people be communicated "under both the kinds of bread and wine" (Sacrament Act 1547), and the 1549 Book of Common Prayer embedded both-kinds reception in the English communion rite (Book of Common Prayer, 1549).

Notes

  • What Luther establishes on this timeline. Luther is the pivot from defence to reversal. Where Jakoubek and the Utraquists had won a local, negotiated concession of the cup (the Compactata, later revoked), the Reformation made both-kinds reception a settled mark of whole church-communions, and it has remained so in the Protestant world since.
  • Where the adversarial weighing belongs. Luther's block is deliberately descriptive: it states his case, not its verdict. Whether "Drink ye all of it" binds the laity to the cup by divine command — and whether the church nonetheless has authority to regulate the manner of reception — are the two questions the councils (Constance and, later, Trent) answered by distinguishing what Christ instituted from what the faithful are bound to receive. Both are weighed among this timeline's arguments in their own right; Luther is here as the proponent, not the judge.
  • The immediate forerunner on the timeline is the Hussite demand of Jakoubek of Stříbro and Jan Hus, whose chalice-banner Luther's Catholic opponents were quick to throw in his face — Johann Eck had already branded him "a Hussite" at the Leipzig Disputation of 1519.
