The Council of Trent and the definition of communion under one kind

Dates: c. 1562 (Session 21, 16 July 1562, under Pope Pius IV) · the Council of Trent sat 1545–1563 · Wikipedia
Summary: Answering the Reformers, the Council of Trent's twenty-first session (16 July 1562) issued the Doctrine on Communion under Both Kinds and on the Communion of Little Children. It defined, with attached canons of anathema, that the laity and non-celebrating clergy are not bound by any divine precept to receive under both kinds; that by concomitance the whole and entire Christ is received under either species alone, so that one who receives the host is defrauded of no grace necessary to salvation; and that the Church has power, the substance of the sacraments untouched, to regulate the manner of their administration. It answered Luther's reading of "Drink ye all of it," and restated the ruling of Constance (1415).

Claims & evidence

No divine precept binds the laity or non-celebrating clergy to both kinds (Chapter I; Canon 1)

Against the Reformers' claim that "Drink ye all of it" is a command binding every communicant, Trent's first chapter teaches that "laymen, and clerics when not consecrating, are not obliged, by any divine precept, to receive the sacrament of the Eucharist under both species" (Session 21, ch. I). The matching canon fastens an anathema to the contrary: "If any one saith, that, by the precept of God, or, by necessity of salvation, all and each of the faithful of Christ ought to receive both species of the most holy sacrament not consecrating; let him be anathema" (Session 21, Canon 1). The distinction is the one Luther had denied: Trent grants that Christ instituted both kinds, but distinguishes what he instituted from what the faithful are bound to receive.

The Church's power to regulate the manner of administration (Chapter II; Canon 2)

The second chapter states the principle that the church may govern the manner of the sacraments so long as their substance is untouched: "it hath ever had in the Church this power, that, in the dispensation of the sacraments, their substance being untouched, it may ordain, or change, what things soever it may judge most expedient, for the profit of those who receive, or for the veneration of the said sacraments, according to the difference of circumstances, times, and places" (Session 21, ch. II). On that basis the church "induced by weighty and just reasons" approved communion of the laity under the one species of bread. The canon anathematizes the denial: "If any one saith, that the holy Catholic Church was not induced, by just causes and reasons, to communicate, under the species of bread only, laymen, and also clerics when not consecrating; let him be anathema" (Session 21, Canon 2).

Concomitance: the whole Christ received under either species (Chapter III; Canon 3)

Trent ratifies the scholastic doctrine of concomitance given classic form by Aquinas. The third chapter teaches that "Christ whole and entire and a true sacrament are received under either species alone; and that therefore, as regards the fruit thereof, they, who receive one species alone, are not defrauded of any grace necessary to salvation" (Session 21, ch. III). Its canon: "If any one denieth, that Christ whole and entire — the fountain and author of all graces — is received under the one species of bread; let him be anathema" (Session 21, Canon 3).

Little children not bound to sacramental communion (Chapter IV; Canon 4)

A fourth chapter treats the communion of infants — a practice attested as early as Cyprian, whose deacon gives an infant the cup: "little children, who have not attained to the use of reason, are not by any necessity obliged to the sacramental communion" (Session 21, ch. IV), with a canon anathematizing the claim "that the communion of the Eucharist is necessary for little children, before they have arrived at years of discretion" (Session 21, Canon 4).

The chalice referred to the pope: the 1564 concession and its withdrawal

Session 21 deliberately left one question open, referring it to the pope: whether the urgent petition of the Emperor Ferdinand I to allow the cup in his dominions should be granted. By a brief of 16 April 1564 Pope Pius IV granted the petition, with restrictions, to the subjects of the emperor and of Duke Albrecht of Bavaria; the Archbishop of Prague, Antonín Brus, was to empower certain priests to give both kinds to the laity who desired it, on their profession of Christ's presence entire in each species (Wikipedia, "Antonín Brus of Mohelnice"). Patrick Toner records that Pius IV "authorized some German bishops to permit it in their dioceses," and that "owing to the inconveniences that were found to result, this concession was withdrawn in the following year" (Catholic Encyclopedia, "Communion under Both Kinds", 1908); James MacCaffrey writes that it "remained practically a dead-letter, and later on was withdrawn" (History of the Catholic Church from the Renaissance to the French Revolution, vol. I, ch. IV).

Notes

  • Placement on the timeline. Trent defines three points: concomitance (Christ whole under either kind), the absence of a divine precept binding the laity to both kinds, and the church's authority to regulate the manner of reception. Where Constance (1415) had defined the one-kind custom against Jakoubek and stated that Scripture and the early church knew both kinds, Trent answers Luther, distinguishing what Christ instituted from what the faithful are bound to receive.
  • Trent appears elsewhere on this wiki as a witness on other timelines — on the invocation of the saints and sacred images (Session 25), the ministerial priesthood and sacrament of order (Session 23), and the Real Presence and transubstantiation (Session 13).
  • The concession referred to Pius IV (1564) concerns the same question of a grant of the cup raised at Basel a century earlier, and is treated here rather than on the Basel page because it flows directly from Session 21's decision to remit the matter to the pope.
