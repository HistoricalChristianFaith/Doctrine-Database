The Council of Trent and the definition of communion under one kind

Dates: c. 1562 (Session 21, 16 July 1562, under Pope Pius IV) · the Council of Trent sat 1545–1563 · Wikipedia
Summary: Answering the Reformers, the Council of Trent's twenty-first session (16 July 1562) issued the Doctrine on Communion under Both Kinds and on the Communion of Little Children, the sharpest statement of the Catholic side of this whole controversy. It defined, with attached canons of anathema, that the laity and non-celebrating clergy are not bound by any divine precept to receive under both kinds; that by concomitance the whole and entire Christ is received under either species alone, so that one who receives the host is defrauded of no grace necessary to salvation; and that the Church has power, the substance of the sacraments untouched, to regulate the manner of their administration. This is Trent's direct answer to Luther's "Drink ye all of it" and to Constance's open question of the church's authority over a dominical practice.

Claims & evidence

No divine precept binds the laity or non-celebrating clergy to both kinds (Chapter I; Canon 1)

Against the Reformers' claim that "Drink ye all of it" is a command binding every communicant, Trent's first chapter teaches that "laymen, and clerics when not consecrating, are not obliged, by any divine precept, to receive the sacrament of the Eucharist under both species" (Session 21, ch. I). The matching canon fastens an anathema to the contrary: "If any one saith, that, by the precept of God, or, by necessity of salvation, all and each of the faithful of Christ ought to receive both species of the most holy sacrament not consecrating; let him be anathema" (Session 21, Canon 1). The distinction is exactly the one Luther had denied: Trent grants that Christ instituted both kinds, but distinguishes what he instituted from what the faithful are bound to receive.

The Church's power to regulate the manner of administration (Chapter II; Canon 2)

The second chapter states the principle Constance had left implicit — that the church may govern the manner of the sacraments so long as their substance is untouched: "it hath ever had in the Church this power, that, in the dispensation of the sacraments, their substance being untouched, it may ordain, or change, what things soever it may judge most expedient, for the profit of those who receive, or for the veneration of the said sacraments, according to the difference of circumstances, times, and places" (Session 21, ch. II). On that basis the church "induced by weighty and just reasons" approved communion of the laity under the one species of bread. The canon anathematizes the denial: "If any one saith, that the holy Catholic Church was not induced, by just causes and reasons, to communicate, under the species of bread only, laymen, and also clerics when not consecrating; let him be anathema" (Session 21, Canon 2).

Concomitance: the whole Christ received under either species (Chapter III; Canon 3)

Trent ratifies the scholastic doctrine of concomitance given classic form by Aquinas — the theological hinge on which withholding the cup is judged no deprivation. The third chapter teaches that "Christ whole and entire and a true sacrament are received under either species alone; and that therefore, as regards the fruit thereof, they, who receive one species alone, are not defrauded of any grace necessary to salvation" (Session 21, ch. III). Its canon: "If any one denieth, that Christ whole and entire — the fountain and author of all graces — is received under the one species of bread; let him be anathema" (Session 21, Canon 3).

Little children not bound to sacramental communion (Chapter IV; Canon 4)

A fourth chapter closed the ancient practice — attested as early as Cyprian, who gives infants the cup — of communicating infants: "little children, who have not attained to the use of reason, are not by any necessity obliged to the sacramental communion" (Session 21, ch. IV), with a canon anathematizing the claim "that the communion of the Eucharist is necessary for little children, before they have arrived at years of discretion" (Session 21, Canon 4).

The chalice referred to the pope: the 1564 concession and its withdrawal

Session 21 deliberately left one question open, referring it to the pope: whether the urgent petition of the Emperor Ferdinand I to allow the cup in his dominions should be granted. On 16 April 1564 Pope Pius IV empowered the bishops of much of the Empire — Austria and Bohemia among them — to permit the laity the chalice in their dioceses on stated conditions, and Mass was celebrated sub utraque at Prague cathedral under Archbishop Antonín Brus. But the indult, like the Compactata before it, proved temporary: the permission was gradually withdrawn diocese by diocese, and was last suppressed in Bohemia in 1621 (Catholic Encyclopedia, "Communion under Both Kinds"). As at Basel, the episode shows the practice treated by Rome itself as disciplinary and negotiable — grantable and revocable — never as beyond the church's power to change.

Notes

  • What Trent establishes on this timeline. Trent is the sharpest and most formal statement of the Catholic side of the three arguments this timeline turns on: the concomitance reading (Christ whole under either kind), the no-divine-precept reading of "Drink ye all of it," and the church-authority to regulate the manner of reception. Where Constance (1415) had defined the one-kind custom against Jakoubek and conceded that Scripture and the early church knew both kinds, Trent supplies the full dogmatic defence against Luther — distinguishing what Christ instituted from what the faithful are bound to receive.
  • Where the adversarial weighing belongs. This block is deliberately descriptive: it states Trent's definitions, not their verdict. Whether "Drink ye all of it" binds the laity by divine command, and whether the church may nonetheless regulate the manner of reception, are weighed among this timeline's arguments in their own right.
  • Trent appears elsewhere on this wiki as a witness on other timelines — on the invocation of the saints and sacred images (Session 25), the ministerial priesthood and sacrament of order (Session 23), and the Real Presence and transubstantiation (Session 13).
  • The concession referred to Pius IV (1564) reopens the same disciplinary question settled and then unsettled at Basel a century earlier — the cup granted and then revoked — and is treated here rather than on the Basel page because it flows directly from Session 21's decision to remit the matter to the pope.
