Jan Hus and "the sacrament of the chalice" (letter from Constance, 1415)

Dates: 21 June 1415 (the letter to Havlík, written from prison at Constance) · Jan Hus, c. 1372–6 July 1415 · Wikipedia
Summary: Jan Hus did not begin Utraquism — that was Jakoubek of Stříbro, while Hus was already away at the Council of Constance. But from his cell, six days after the council condemned the lay chalice (15 June 1415) and fifteen days before he was burned, Hus wrote to a Prague priest who had opposed Jakoubek, urging him not to resist "the sacrament of the chalice of the Lord." The letter is Hus's clearest word on the cup: at first he had held only that Scripture and the ancient church "appeared favourable" to it; the council's decree — which he took to condemn Christ's own institution as heresy — moved him to positive endorsement. His martyrdom then fused his name to the chalice that became the emblem of the movement he had launched.

Claims & evidence

The letter to Havlík (Gallus): "Do not oppose the sacrament of the chalice" (21 June 1415)

Beloved brother Gallus, preacher of the word of Christ! Do not oppose the sacrament of the chalice of the Lord which Christ established through Himself and through His apostle; for no word of Scripture is opposed to it, only custom which, I ween, sprang from negligence; for we must not follow custom, but Christ's example and the truth. Already has the council, alleging custom, condemned the use of the chalice at the communion of laymen as a heresy, and he who practises it is to be punished as a heretic unless he comes to his right mind … What wickedness! Behold, they condemn Christ's enactment as heresy! I therefore beg thee in the name of God no longer to oppose Jacobellus, lest dissension arise among the faithful … Be then, dearest, prepared to suffer when administering communion in the two kinds.

— Jan Hus, letter to Gallus (in Bohemian, Havlík), preacher at the Bethlehem Chapel, 21 June 1415, in Count Lützow, The Life and Times of Master John Hus, ch. 8. "Jacobellus" is Jakoubek of Stříbro; Havlík was among the Prague priests who had at first opposed his restoration of the cup. Hus grounds the lay chalice on Christ's institution ("through Himself and through His apostle" — the Lord's own "Drink ye all of it" and Paul's rehearsal of it in 1 Corinthians 11), reads the one-kind rule as mere custom "sprung from negligence," and reverses the council's charge: it is the council, not the Utraquists, that condemns "Christ's enactment as heresy."

From cautious to positive: the effect of the decree

Hus's position developed. In an earlier, undated letter to friends at Constance he "expresses no positive opinion, but writes that Scripture and the custom of the primitive church appear favourable to utraquism"; only after the decree of 15 June "established a new dogma with regard to a matter on which freedom of opinion had previously existed" did he "express himself more positively," as in the letter to Havlík (Lützow, Life and Times, ch. 8). The chalice thus became a Hussite banner partly by the council's own doing: by making one-kind reception a matter of binding law and its denial a heresy, Constance turned what Hus had treated as an open question into a confessional line.

Notes

  • Hus approved; Jakoubek authored. The strong Utraquist thesis — that both kinds are of divine necessity, and one-kind communion a sacrilege — belongs to Jakoubek, developed chiefly after 1415; Hus's contribution is the endorsement in this letter and the weight of his martyrdom two weeks later. It is a common simplification to credit Hus with founding communion in both kinds; the record has him blessing a practice another man began while he was in chains.
  • What the letter shows. Hus frames the dispute exactly where Constance did — around custom versus institution — but draws the opposite conclusion: because Christ instituted both kinds, custom cannot bind against it ("we must not follow custom, but Christ's example and the truth"). That is the "Drink ye all of it" argument and the challenge to the church's authority to alter a dominical practice, both weighed among this timeline's arguments in their own right.
  • Hus appears on this wiki chiefly as a witness to the wider Bohemian reform; here his single, decisive word on the chalice is what the timeline records. He was condemned and burned at Constance on 6 July 1415, and on 23 June the same council ordered his writings burnt.
