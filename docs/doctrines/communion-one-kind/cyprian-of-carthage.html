Cyprian of Carthage on communion under both kinds — and the earliest one-kind exceptions

Dates: c. 251–253 (On the Lapsed, c. 251; the letter to Caecilius, Epistle 62, c. 253) · Wikipedia
Summary: Cyprian is the fullest early witness on both sides of this timeline's opening. In the letter to Caecilius he insists the cup must hold wine — against those who used water alone — and takes for granted that the laity drink it: "how can we shed our blood for Christ, who blush to drink the blood of Christ?" He treats both-kinds reception as the norm. On the Lapsed also preserves the earliest concrete instances on this timeline of reception outside the full assembly's ordinary rite: the consecrated bread reserved at home in a private box, and communion given to an infant (under the cup).

Claims & evidence

The laity drink the cup: "the brotherhood" and the Blood (Epistle 62.15–16)

Therefore thus the brotherhood is beginning even to be kept back from the passion of Christ in persecutions, by learning in the offerings to be disturbed concerning His blood and His blood-shedding … But how can we shed our blood for Christ, who blush to drink the blood of Christ? … But when we sup, we cannot call the people together to our banquet, so as to celebrate the truth of the sacrament in the presence of all the brotherhood.

— Epistle 62.15–16 (to Caecilius; numbered 63 in some editions), source (cf. New Advent). Cyprian's argument assumes that the laity receive the cup: he rebukes those who would offer water alone, or offer only in the evening out of "fear … lest by the taste of wine [one] should be redolent of the blood of Christ," because that timidity teaches "the brotherhood" to shrink from Christ's blood in the very act meant to steel them for martyrdom. To "drink the blood of Christ" is, in this letter, what the assembled people do.

Wine, not water, must be in the cup, or "the blood of Christ is not offered" (Epistle 62.2, 9)

[S]ince some, either by ignorance or simplicity in sanctifying the cup of the Lord, and in ministering to the people, do not do that which Jesus Christ, our Lord and God, the founder and teacher of this sacrifice, did and taught … Whence it appears that the blood of Christ is not offered if there be no wine in the cup, nor the Lord's sacrifice celebrated with a legitimate consecration unless our oblation and sacrifice respond to His passion.

— Epistle 62.2, 9, source (cf. New Advent). The letter is directed against the "Aquarians," who used water only. Cyprian insists that the cup contain true wine, as the blood Christ named at the Supper, in a letter concerned with "ministering to the people." (The letter's teaching on the elements is treated on the Real Presence timeline, where it also appears.)

Earliest one-kind exception (a): the consecrated bread reserved at home (On the Lapsed 26)

And another woman, when she tried with unworthy hands to open her box, in which was the holy (body) of the Lord, was deterred by fire rising from it from daring to touch it. And when one, who himself was defiled, dared with the rest to receive secretly a part of the sacrifice celebrated by the priest; he could not eat nor handle the holy of the Lord, but found in his hands when opened that he had a cinder.

— On the Lapsed 26, source (cf. New Advent). The "box" (Latin arca) in which a Christian kept "the holy (body) of the Lord" at home is the earliest clear attestation of the reserved sacrament kept privately — and it was reserved under the species of bread alone. Home reservation of the host allowed a believer to commune on days without a public liturgy. Cyprian records the custom without objection; his point is the sacrilege of a lapsed Christian handling it unworthily, not the practice of reserving it.

Earliest one-kind exception (b): communion given to an infant — under the cup (On the Lapsed 25)

[T]he deacon began to offer the cup to those present, and when, as the rest received it, its turn approached, the little child, by the instinct of the divine majesty, turned away its face, compressed its mouth with resisting lips, and refused the cup. Still the deacon persisted, and, although against her efforts, forced on her some of the sacrament of the cup. Then there followed a sobbing and vomiting.

— On the Lapsed 25, source (cf. New Advent). The episode belongs chiefly to the communion of infants: in third-century Carthage the deacon offered the cup to those present, an infant among them, "as the rest received it," in the public assembly.

Notes

  • Placement on the timeline. Cyprian assumes the laity drink the cup (against the water-users) and gives it to infants; the reception apart from the full two-kind synaxis that he mentions is the private reservation of the bread at home. Later Catholic writers cite such instances: Patrick Toner (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1908) surveys home communion and other one-kind usages of the early centuries and concludes that "the Church never regarded Communion under both kinds as a matter of Divine precept" ("Communion under Both Kinds").
  • Cyprian's arca-reservation is echoed by other third- and fourth-century notices of the host kept at home (Tertullian and, later, Basil, Ep. 93, and Jerome, Ep. 48.15), and by the sacrament carried to the sick, usually under the species of bread (so Toner, "Communion under Both Kinds").
  • The letter to Caecilius is numbered Epistle 62 in the Ante-Nicene Fathers and Epistle 63 in the Migne/CSEL sequence commonly cited today; both refer to the same letter on the sacrament of the cup. Cyprian also appears on the Real Presence and ministerial priesthood timelines.
