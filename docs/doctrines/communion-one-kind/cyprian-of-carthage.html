Cyprian of Carthage on communion under both kinds — and the earliest one-kind exceptions

Dates: c. 251–253 (On the Lapsed, c. 251; the letter to Caecilius, Epistle 62, c. 253) · Wikipedia
Summary: Cyprian is the fullest early witness on both sides of this timeline's opening. In the letter to Caecilius he insists the cup must hold wine — against those who used water alone — and takes for granted that the laity drink it: "how can we shed our blood for Christ, who blush to drink the blood of Christ?" Both-kinds reception is his unquestioned norm. Yet On the Lapsed also preserves the earliest concrete exceptions to reception in the full assembly: the consecrated bread reserved at home in a private box, and communion given to an infant. These are edge-cases, not the rule — but they are where reception apart from the ordinary two-kind synaxis first appears in the record.

Claims & evidence

The laity drink the cup — the whole "brotherhood" partakes of the Blood (Epistle 62.15–16)

Therefore thus the brotherhood is beginning even to be kept back from the passion of Christ in persecutions, by learning in the offerings to be disturbed concerning His blood and His blood-shedding … But how can we shed our blood for Christ, who blush to drink the blood of Christ? … But when we sup, we cannot call the people together to our banquet, so as to celebrate the truth of the sacrament in the presence of all the brotherhood.

— Epistle 62.15–16 (to Caecilius; numbered 63 in some editions), source (cf. New Advent). The argument only works if the laity do receive the cup: Cyprian rebukes those who would offer water alone, or offer only in the evening out of "fear … lest by the taste of wine [one] should be redolent of the blood of Christ," because that timidity teaches "the brotherhood" to shrink from Christ's blood in the very act meant to steel them for martyrdom. To "drink the blood of Christ" is what the whole assembled people do; withholding the cup is not even a live option for him.

Wine, not water, must be in the cup, or "the blood of Christ is not offered" (Epistle 62.2, 9)

[S]ince some, either by ignorance or simplicity in sanctifying the cup of the Lord, and in ministering to the people, do not do that which Jesus Christ, our Lord and God, the founder and teacher of this sacrifice, did and taught … Whence it appears that the blood of Christ is not offered if there be no wine in the cup, nor the Lord's sacrifice celebrated with a legitimate consecration unless our oblation and sacrifice respond to His passion.

— Epistle 62.2, 9, source (cf. New Advent). The letter is directed against the "Aquarians," who used water only. Cyprian's insistence that the cup contain true wine — because it is the blood Christ named at the Supper — is a strong incidental witness that the cup was a real and necessary part of what "the people" received. (The identity of the elements with Christ's body and blood is weighed on the Real Presence timeline, where this letter also appears.)

Earliest one-kind exception (a): the consecrated bread reserved at home (On the Lapsed 26)

And another woman, when she tried with unworthy hands to open her box, in which was the holy (body) of the Lord, was deterred by fire rising from it from daring to touch it. And when one, who himself was defiled, dared with the rest to receive secretly a part of the sacrifice celebrated by the priest; he could not eat nor handle the holy of the Lord, but found in his hands when opened that he had a cinder.

— On the Lapsed 26, source (cf. New Advent). The "box" (Latin arca) in which a Christian kept "the holy (body) of the Lord" at home is the earliest clear attestation of the reserved sacrament kept privately — and it was reserved under the species of bread alone. Home reservation of the host, so that a believer could commune on days without a public liturgy, is one of the practical roots of reception under one kind: the bread travels and keeps as the cup does not. Cyprian records the custom without objection; his point is the sacrilege of a lapsed Christian handling it unworthily, not the practice of reserving it.

Earliest one-kind exception (b): communion given to an infant — under the cup (On the Lapsed 25)

[T]he deacon began to offer the cup to those present, and when, as the rest received it, its turn approached, the little child, by the instinct of the divine majesty, turned away its face, compressed its mouth with resisting lips, and refused the cup. Still the deacon persisted, and, although against her efforts, forced on her some of the sacrament of the cup. Then there followed a sobbing and vomiting.

— On the Lapsed 25, source (cf. New Advent). The episode belongs to the communion of infants more than to the withdrawal of the cup, but it is telling in both directions: in third-century Carthage even infants were given the chalice — a datum against any notion that reserving the cup from the laity was ancient or universal. Note that the child here received the cup precisely as "the rest received it," in the public assembly — the both-kinds norm reaching down to the youngest communicant.

Notes

  • Both witnesses cut the same way for this timeline. Cyprian assumes the laity drink the cup (against the water-users) and even gives it to infants, so both-kinds reception is plainly the norm; the only reception apart from the full two-kind synaxis he mentions is the private reservation of the bread at home — an accommodation of circumstance, not a doctrine of withholding. The medieval Western case for one kind would later lean on exactly this kind of practical precedent (the host that keeps and travels), which is why the earliest instances matter here.
  • Cyprian's arca-reservation is echoed by other third- and fourth-century notices of the host kept at home (Tertullian and, later, Jerome and Basil), and by the sacrament carried to the sick under the species of bread. These are the practical narrowings the summary intro flags — one kind at the edges of church life, long before any theology of withholding the chalice.
  • The letter to Caecilius is numbered Epistle 62 in the Ante-Nicene Fathers and Epistle 63 in the Migne/CSEL sequence commonly cited today; both refer to the same letter on the sacrament of the cup. Cyprian also appears on the Real Presence and ministerial priesthood timelines.
