Cyril of Jerusalem on receiving both kinds in the East

Dates: c. 350 (the Mystagogical Catecheses — the five lectures to the newly baptized that close the Catechetical Lectures — traditionally assigned to Cyril's Jerusalem episcopate around 350; the date and authorship are debated, some assigning them to his successor John of Jerusalem and a decade or two later) · Wikipedia
Summary: Cyril's step-by-step instruction to the newly baptized on how to receive the Eucharist is the fullest early Eastern account of lay reception, and it is emphatically under both kinds: the communicant receives the Body in the hollowed palm, then draws near to the Cup of His Blood. It stands here as the head of the Eastern counter-case — the tradition that, unlike the medieval Latin West, never withdrew the chalice from the laity but eventually merged the two species into administration by the communion-spoon (see the Council in Trullo & the communion-spoon below).

Claims & evidence

Receive the Body in the hollowed hand, then draw near to the Cup of His Blood (Mystagogic 5)

In approaching therefore, come not with thy wrists extended, or thy fingers spread; but make thy left hand a throne for the right, as for that which is to receive a King. And having hollowed thy palm, receive the Body of Christ, saying over it, Amen. … Then after thou hast partaken of the Body of Christ, draw near also to the Cup of His Blood; not stretching forth thine hands, but bending, and saying with an air of worship and reverence, Amen, hallow thyself by partaking also of the Blood of Christ.

— Catechetical Lecture 23 (On the Mysteries V), §§21–22, source (cf. New Advent). The rite Cyril describes is plainly under both kinds and given to every communicant: first the consecrated Body, laid in the cupped palm, and then a separate approach to "the Cup of His Blood." He treats the reverent handling of the Blood not as a reason to withhold it from the laity — the West's later rationale — but as a reason for the faithful to receive it with the greatest care: while "the moisture is still upon thy lips," he adds, "touch it with thine hands, and hallow thine eyes and brow and the other organs of sense."

Guard against losing any portion — the same care later urged in the West against spilling the Blood

And having hollowed thy palm, receive the Body of Christ, saying over it, Amen. … partake of it; giving heed lest thou lose any portion thereof; for whatever thou losest, is evidently a loss to thee as it were from one of thine own members. For tell me, if any one gave thee grains of gold, wouldest thou not hold them with all carefulness, being on thy guard against losing any of them, and suffering loss?

— Catechetical Lecture 23, §21, source. The same reverent anxiety about the Precious Blood that the Latin West would eventually turn into an argument for reserving the chalice to the priest is here directed the opposite way: it makes lay reception more careful, not lay reception impossible. This is the crux of the Eastern data-point on this timeline — the fear of profanation did not compel the East to take the cup away.

Notes

  • Dating and authorship. The catechetical lectures proper are usually dated to c. 348–350, when Cyril was a presbyter or newly a bishop; the five Mystagogical Catecheses that follow (Lectures 19–23) are transmitted with them but their date and even their authorship are contested — some manuscripts and scholars assign them to Cyril's successor John of Jerusalem, and to the 380s. Either way they witness the fourth-century Jerusalem rite, and the point that matters here — the laity receiving both the Body and the Cup — is not in dispute.
  • The Eastern counter-case. Cyril heads a continuous Eastern tradition of communicating the laity under both kinds that runs unbroken to the present day. The mechanism changed — from receiving the Body in the hand and the Cup by mouth (as here, and as the Council in Trullo canon 101 still assumes in 692) to the later merger of the species, the consecrated Bread placed in the chalice and administered together with the Blood by the communion-spoon (the labis). But the East never did what the medieval West did: withdraw the cup. Because the whole controversy on this timeline turns on the West's withdrawal, the East stands as the standing witness that withdrawal was a Latin development, not the church's universal mind — and, against the later argument from concomitance, that holding the whole Christ present under each species did not, of itself, require dropping the chalice.
