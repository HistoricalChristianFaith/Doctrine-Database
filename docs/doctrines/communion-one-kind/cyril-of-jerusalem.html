Cyril of Jerusalem on receiving both kinds in the East

Dates: c. 350 (the Mystagogical Catecheses — the five lectures to the newly baptized that close the Catechetical Lectures — traditionally assigned to Cyril's Jerusalem episcopate around 350; the date and authorship are debated, some assigning them to his successor John of Jerusalem and a decade or two later) · Wikipedia
Summary: Cyril's step-by-step instruction to the newly baptized on how to receive the Eucharist is the fullest early Eastern account of lay reception, and it is under both kinds: the communicant receives the Body in the hollowed palm, then draws near to the Cup of His Blood. It is the earliest Eastern witness on this timeline; the Eastern churches have continued to give the laity both kinds, the Byzantine rite later giving them together by the communion-spoon (see the Council in Trullo & the communion-spoon below).

Claims & evidence

Receive the Body in the hollowed hand, then draw near to the Cup of His Blood (Mystagogic 5)

In approaching therefore, come not with thy wrists extended, or thy fingers spread; but make thy left hand a throne for the right, as for that which is to receive a King. And having hollowed thy palm, receive the Body of Christ, saying over it, Amen. … Then after thou hast partaken of the Body of Christ, draw near also to the Cup of His Blood; not stretching forth thine hands, but bending, and saying with an air of worship and reverence, Amen, hallow thyself by partaking also of the Blood of Christ.

— Catechetical Lecture 23 (On the Mysteries V), §§21–22, source (cf. New Advent). The rite Cyril describes is under both kinds and given to each communicant: first the consecrated Body, laid in the cupped palm, and then a separate approach to "the Cup of His Blood." Of the Blood he adds that, while "the moisture is still upon thy lips," the communicant should "touch it with thine hands, and hallow thine eyes and brow and the other organs of sense."

Care against losing any portion of the Body (Mystagogic 5)

And having hollowed thy palm, receive the Body of Christ, saying over it, Amen. … partake of it; giving heed lest thou lose any portion thereof; for whatever thou losest, is evidently a loss to thee as it were from one of thine own members. For tell me, if any one gave thee grains of gold, wouldest thou not hold them with all carefulness, being on thy guard against losing any of them, and suffering loss?

— Catechetical Lecture 23, §21, source. Cyril's caution here concerns the consecrated Body received in the hand: the communicant is to guard it "with all carefulness," as one would grains of gold. The same lecture then directs the communicant to the Cup (above).

Notes

  • Dating and authorship. The catechetical lectures proper are usually dated to c. 348–350, when Cyril was a presbyter or newly a bishop; the five Mystagogical Catecheses that follow (Lectures 19–23) are transmitted with them but their date and authorship are debated — some manuscripts and scholars assign them to Cyril's successor John of Jerusalem, and to the 380s (see Alexis James Doval, Cyril of Jerusalem, Mystagogue, 2001, cited on the timeline). On either attribution they describe the fourth-century Jerusalem rite, with the laity receiving both the Body and the Cup.
  • Placement on the timeline. Cyril is the earliest of the Eastern witnesses here; the Eastern churches have continued to communicate the laity under both kinds. The mechanism changed — from receiving the Body in the hand and the Cup by mouth (as here, and as the Council in Trullo canon 101 still assumes in 692) to the later merger of the species, the consecrated Bread placed in the chalice and administered together with the Blood by the communion-spoon (the labis). In the Byzantine rite, as Robert Taft documents (see the Trullo page), the laity continued to receive the Blood.
