Jakoubek of Stříbro and the restoration of the cup to the laity

Dates: c. 1414 (the restoration of the lay chalice at Prague) · Jakoubek of Stříbro, c. 1372–9 August 1429 · Wikipedia
Summary: In 1414 Jakoubek of Stříbro (Latin Jacobellus de Misa, "Jacob of Mies"), master of the University of Prague and soon successor to Jan Hus as preacher at the Bethlehem Chapel, began giving the faithful the consecrated cup as well as the bread — the first deliberate restoration of lay communion under both kinds after centuries of the Western one-kind custom. He grounded it in Christ's institution and in the practice of the ancient church, and pressed it further than Hus himself had: that communion sub utraque specie ("under both kinds") is a dominical command binding on all. From his initiative the whole reform movement took its emblem and its name — the Utraquists or Calixtines (from Latin calix, cup), the chalice on their banners.

Claims & evidence

The restoration of the lay chalice at Prague (1414)

According to the contemporary Hussite chronicler Vavřinec of Březová (Laurence of Březová), Jakoubek began administering the chalice to the laity in Prague in the autumn of 1414 — his congregation and then, in short order, other Prague parishes receiving the consecrated wine as well as the host, after centuries in which the cup had been reserved to the celebrating priest (Wikipedia, "Jacob of Mies"). The innovation spread rapidly and became a rallying point of the reform: it was Jakoubek, not Hus, whom the Council of Constance would name as the instigator of the practice — see Hieromonk Patapios, "Sub Utraque Specie: The Arguments of John Hus and Jacoubek of Stříbro in Defence of Giving Communion to the Laity under Both Kinds," Journal of Theological Studies 53.2 (2002): 503–522.

The origin of the Bohemian chalice: Peter of Dresden and the Janovite theory

Who first set the Prague restoration in motion has been disputed since the fifteenth century. The oldest tradition credits Master Peter of Dresden — a German schoolmaster who, driven from Saxony (reportedly for spreading Waldensian ideas), took refuge at Prague and ran a small school at the house of the Black Rose (Černá Růže) — as the man who drew Jakoubek to the cup. The nineteenth-century Cyclopedia of Biblical, Theological and Ecclesiastical Literature preserves the strong form: Peter "attracted to himself one of his friends called Jacobel, with whom he published his opinions … to his influence … is to be attributed in large measure the origin of that discussion in respect to the communion of the cup which almost revolutionized Bohemia" (McClintock & Strong, "Peter (Pierre) of Dresden," quoting Gillett, Huss and the Hussites). Peter is not to be confused with the English Wycliffite Peter Payne (a later, distinct figure), nor with Nicholas of Dresden (d. 1417), the younger of the two Dresden masters at the Black Rose, who actually composed genuine Utraquist treatises and cooperated in the 1414 introduction of the lay chalice.

Modern scholarship treats the "Peter of Dresden inspired Jakoubek" story as later embellishment rather than established origin. Helena Krmíčková, surveying the rival theories of the chalice's renewal, sets the Dresden theory aside as "subsidiary": Peter, "the protagonist of the fifteenth-century chronicler," was in modern retellings superseded by Nicholas — yet neither man can hold "the palm of primacy," since their Utraquist writings postdate their arrival at Prague and came after Jakoubek's own, so neither was the "conveyor of the chalice from abroad." What "really remains," on her account, is Jakoubek himself and the eucharistic teaching of Matthias of Janov (the "Master of Paris"), the advocate of frequent lay communion on whom Jakoubek verifiably drew from 1412 — the genetic root of his chalice (Helena Krmíčková, "The Janovite Theory and the Renewal of the Lay Chalice," Bohemian Reformation and Religious Practice 3 (2000): 63–68). So while a Dresden schoolmaster stood in the early Utraquist milieu, the timeline turns on Jakoubek's deliberate act of 1414, not on Peter of Dresden as its author; the honest verdict is that the intellectual lineage runs Janov → Jakoubek, with the Dresden circle as company rather than cause.

The case for the cup: Christ's institution and the ancient church

Jakoubek argued the lay chalice from Scripture and history. His chief proof-text was the Lord's own words in John 6:53 — "Except ye eat the flesh of the Son of man, and drink his blood, ye have no life in you" — which he read as making reception of the cup, not merely the bread, necessary for the faithful; and he pressed Christ's institution "Drink ye all of it" (Matthew 26:27). To this he added the historical argument that the church had communicated the laity under both kinds for many centuries, and that ancient authorities favoured the practice — so that the one-kind custom, far from apostolic, was a later narrowing (Patapios, "Sub Utraque Specie"). Where the earlier timeline witnesses (Leo, Gelasius) had treated refusing the cup as the mark of a heretic, Jakoubek turned the point into a positive demand: to withhold the cup is to divide the sacrament Christ gave whole.

Further than Hus: the cup as a command, not merely a liberty

The step Jakoubek took was sharper than Hus's. Hus, at Constance, thought Scripture and the primitive church "appeared favourable" to the cup but at first advanced no positive demand; the argument that communion under both kinds is of divine necessity — and that one-kind reception is therefore a sacrilege depriving the laity — was made largely by Jakoubek, and chiefly after the council's condemnation hardened the lines (Patapios, "Sub Utraque Specie"). Hus's role was approval, not authorship: from his cell he wrote to a Prague priest urging him not to oppose "the sacrament of the chalice" and to stand with Jakoubek (see Jan Hus).

Notes

  • What the restoration establishes. Jakoubek's initiative is the hinge on which the medieval one-kind custom passed from settled discipline to open church-dividing controversy. Communion under both kinds became the first of the Four Articles of Prague (1420), the platform of the whole Hussite movement, and the chalice its badge; the Utraquist church would keep the lay cup in Bohemia for two centuries.
  • Whether the demand holds. Jakoubek's strong claim — that both kinds are required of all by divine command, so that Rome's one-kind custom is sacrilege — is exactly what the Council of Constance (1415) and later Trent (1562) answered by distinguishing what Christ instituted from what the faithful are bound to receive, granting the former while denying the latter. His two scriptural props are weighed among this timeline's arguments in their own right: the "Drink ye all of it" command (rated contested — strong as exegesis, overstated as a precept of necessity) and his chief proof-text, "except ye … drink his blood, ye have no life in you" (John 6:53). It is his pressing of that verse into a proof of necessity that most overreaches: read as making the cup severally necessary to salvation it is rated weak, since the same discourse promises life to eating the bread alone (John 6:51, 57–58) — so the eating and the drinking are not two independently required conditions. The wider thesis Jakoubek built on these props — that communion under both kinds is de necessitate salutis, so that Rome's one-kind custom imperils the laity's souls — is weighed as such at Is communion under both kinds necessary for salvation? and likewise rated weak (concomitance severs the inference; the "universal ancient practice" was never in fact exceptionless — the host was reserved alone for the sick and the absent without anyone alleging damnation; and the hard form was not even the settled Utraquist position, being Jakoubek's and the Taborites' emphasis rather than the moderate Calixtine consensus). The separate question of the church's authority to change the dominical practice is likewise weighed on its own.
  • The companion witness is Jan Hus, whose approving letter from Constance (21 June 1415) is on the timeline immediately after the council's decree that provoked it.
