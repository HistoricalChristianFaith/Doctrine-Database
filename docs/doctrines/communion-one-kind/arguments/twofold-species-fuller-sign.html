Is reception under both kinds a fuller sacramental sign that communion under one kind lacks?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: sound — as a sign claim, narrowly scoped: the consecrated bread and wine set forth Christ's body and blood separately, and reception under both kinds is the fuller sacramental sign of the covenant sealed in the blood — a real good the one-kind communicant forgoes even granting that no grace is lost. This is conceded by every party, Rome's own liturgical law now commending "the fuller sign." But its force is limited: it establishes a shortfall of sign, not of grace (that is concomitance) and not a breach of command or a necessity (those are weighed elsewhere) · advanced by the Reformers (Calvin, Luther) and, in a milder form, by the Second Vatican Council and current Roman liturgical law.

The interpretation to weigh

Set apart from the two obligation claims for the lay chalice — that Christ commanded it ("Drink ye all of it") and that it is necessary to salvation (John 6:53, rated weak) — stands a quieter and sturdier claim about the sign. In the eucharist the bread and the cup are consecrated separately, the body "given" under one form and the blood "shed" under the other; the two species apart sacramentally set forth Christ's blood poured out from his body — his death, the sacrifice — and seal the New Covenant "in my blood." Reception under both kinds is therefore the fuller sacramental sign: the communicant who both eats and drinks receives the sign whole, while the one who receives the host alone receives the same whole Christ (concomitance grants this) but a lesser sign. This page weighs that claim on its own ground — is both-kinds reception the fuller sign, and does one-kind communion genuinely lack it? — deliberately kept apart from whether the shortfall is a loss of grace, a disobeyed command, or a peril to salvation. Its distinctive feature is that it is admitted on both sides of the historic dispute: the Reformers pressed it, and the Catholic Church now urges the cup for precisely this reason.

The claim

Holy Communion has a fuller form as a sign when it takes place under both kinds. For in this form the sign of the Eucharistic banquet is more clearly evident and clearer expression is given to the divine will by which the new and eternal Covenant is ratified in the Blood of the Lord, as also the connection between the Eucharistic banquet and the eschatological banquet in the Kingdom of the Father.

— General Instruction of the Roman Missal, §281, USCCB text. The strongest form of the both-kinds case, stated by the very church that withheld the cup: reception under both species is the fuller sign, something one-kind communion genuinely lacks — not that it lacks any grace (which the next paragraph, §282, expressly reaffirms with Trent).1

Who advances it

The argument runs down both channels of the controversy. On the Reformation side, once "Drink ye all of it" restored the cup, the Reformers read the withheld chalice as a mutilation of the sign: Calvin charged that Rome had "robbed" the greater part of God's people of "half of the Supper" and made the cup "the peculiar possession of a few shaven and anointed" priests, so that the divinely instituted sign was maimed (Institutes IV.17.47).2 On the Catholic side the same point is now written into liturgical law: the Second Vatican Council reopened the cup because both-kinds reception is "the fuller sign" (Sacrosanctum Concilium §55), and the General Instruction §281 states the theology.3 Its theological root is older than either camp and shared by both: the Council of Trent's own doctrine of the Mass grounds the sacrifice in the separate species — the twofold consecration representing the bloody separation of body and blood on the cross (Sess. 22, ch. I) — the very sign-value Aquinas guarded in the qualifier that the whole Christ is "not alike in each" species (Summa III q. 76, a. 2).4

The evidence for

The words of institution divide the sign in two. Christ did not hand the disciples one consecrated thing but two, each with its own word: the bread "This is my body, which is given for you," the cup "This cup is the new testament in my blood, which is shed for you" (Luke 22:19–20; cf. Matthew 26:26–28, 1 Corinthians 11:24–26). Body given and blood shed, set forth apart, is the sacramental picture of a death — the blood separated from the body. Reception that answers the institution answers it under both forms; the sign is halved when one is withheld.5

The Catholic theology of the Mass rests on the separated species. This is not a Protestant premise. Trent teaches that at the Supper Christ left the Church a "visible sacrifice … whereby that bloody sacrifice, once to be accomplished on the cross, might be represented," himself "immolated in an unbloody manner" — and the representation is the double consecration, the body under bread and the blood under wine shown apart (Sess. 22, ch. I). If the separate species signify the blood poured out, then to receive both is to receive that sign in full.6

The church that withheld the cup now concedes the point. The decisive evidence is that the argument no longer divides the confessions. Rome's own liturgical law calls both-kinds reception the "fuller sign" and urges it (SC §55; GIRM §281), while carefully reaffirming — in the same breath — that no grace is lost under one kind. That is exactly the shape of a sign claim that survives concomitance: the Catholic Church grants the fuller sign precisely because concomitance has already answered the grace worry, so nothing but the sign is left to commend.3

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The core is sound and, unusually, uncontested across the divide. The species are consecrated separately and each carries its own word of institution; the separate species do signify the blood shed apart from the body (Catholic sacrificial theology says so first); and reception under both kinds is therefore a fuller sacramental sign than reception under one — a genuine good the one-kind communicant forgoes. Crucially, this holds even granting concomitance entirely: concomitance concerns what is received (the whole Christ, whole grace), and is simply silent on what is signified. The two claims do not collide, which is why the same church can affirm both — no grace is lost, and the sign is nonetheless fuller under both kinds. That the withholding church itself now commends the fuller sign is the strongest possible attestation that the good is real.

What is overstated or imprecise. Two cautions keep the claim from being made to prove more than it does. First, the sign that lay reception under both kinds most securely adds is the sign of the banquet — the completeness of eating and drinking, the covenant "ratified in the Blood," the foretaste of the eschatological cup — which is how Rome itself frames it (GIRM §281), not, strictly, the sign of the sacrifice. The sacrificial sign of the separated species is accomplished in the double consecration at the altar, which the priest performs and consumes under both kinds at every Mass, whether or not the laity drink; so a proponent who rests the whole case on "one-kind reception loses the sign of Calvary" overstates — the Calvary-sign is set forth at the altar regardless, and what the laity's dual reception adds is the fuller participatory and banquet sign. The claim is strongest stated as Rome and the eschatological-banquet framing state it, not as "the Mass loses its sacrificial sign." Second, a fuller sign is a real good, but a good is not yet an obligation: that the cup is the fuller sign does not establish that withholding it is sinful, that grace is defrauded, or that the laity are bound to it — those are the separate (and weaker) claims of necessity and command. The sign argument's honesty is precisely that it stops short of them; pressed into an obligation it borrows force it does not have.

Assessment

Sound — as a sign claim, narrowly scoped. That reception under both kinds is a fuller sacramental sign, and that communion under one kind genuinely lacks it, is well-grounded and effectively uncontested: the species are consecrated and worded separately, the separated species signify the blood poured out (Catholic sacrificial theology teaches this first), and the church that withdrew the cup now urges it back for exactly this reason (SC §55; GIRM §281). The point holds even granting concomitance, which answers what is received and is silent on what is signified — so this is the mildest and the sturdiest form the both-kinds case takes, admitted by its opponents. Two precisions keep it honest: the fuller sign lay reception adds is most exactly the sign of the eucharistic banquet and the covenant blood (GIRM's own framing), since the sacrificial sign of the separated species is already set forth in the priest's double consecration; and a fuller sign is a real good, not an obligation — it does not by itself make the cup commanded or necessary (weighed at "Drink ye all of it" and John 6:53). Within those bounds the interpretation is sound.
