What does Pope Gelasius’s decree Comperimus (c. 494) forbid when it calls dividing the sacrament “grave sacrilege”?

Disputed reading · on Communion under one kind · witness: Pope Gelasius I (with Leo the Great)

The passage

Item Gelasius Papa Maiorico et Iohanni Episcopis. Conperimus autem, quod quidam sumpta tantummodo sacri corporis portione a calice sacrati cruoris abstineant. Qui proculdubio (quoniam nescio qua superstitione docentur astringi) aut integra sacramenta percipiant, aut ab integris arceantur, quia diuisio unius eiusdemque misterii sine grandi sacrilegio non potest prouenire.

— Gelasius I, decretal Comperimus (to bishops Majoricus and John), in Gratian, Decretum, De consecratione D.2 c.12, source.1 In English: “We have learned that certain persons, having received a portion of the sacred Body alone, abstain from the cup of the sacred Blood. Since these are taught to be bound by I know not what superstition, let them without doubt either receive the sacraments in their entirety, or be repelled from them entirely, because the division of one and the same mystery cannot occur without grave sacrilege.”

Half a century earlier Leo the Great had described the same behaviour at Rome:

at the Communion of the Mysteries they bring themselves sometimes, in order to ensure their concealment, to receive Christ's Body with unworthy lips, though they altogether refuse to drink the Blood of our Redemption.

— Leo the Great, Sermon 42 (On Lent, 4), §5, of the Manichaeans, source.2

The question

Gelasius orders those who take the consecrated Body but abstain from the cup to receive the whole sacrament or none. What the decree forbids is disputed: a rule binding the celebrating priest, a general rule for every communicant that the later Latin practice of lay communion under the bread alone set aside, or a measure against a Manichaean refusal of the cup that passes no judgement on communion under one kind as such. The answer bears on how the Utraquist, Reformation, Catholic and Orthodox accounts of the medieval change each read the fifth-century Roman record. Modern academic literature was not located for three of the positions below: the priestly reading, whose latest named holder located is the Roman Confutation (1530); the Protestant form of the general reading, whose latest academic holder located is Philip Schaff (1867); and the Orthodox form of the general reading, sourced to a popular essay. For the Manichaean reading the latest academic holders located are Patrick Toner (1908) and John F. X. Murphy (1909).

A rule for the priest who consecrates

In brief: the decree states what the celebrant owes the sacrifice; the one who consecrates both kinds must receive both.

Held by: Gratian’s Decretum (c. 1140), which files the canon under a rubric about the priest;3 Thomas Aquinas, Summa Theologiae III, q. 80, a. 12 (Catholic, c. 1274);4 and the Roman Confutation, Article XXII (Catholic, 1530).5

Gratian placed the decree under the heading Corpus Christi sine eius sanguine sacerdos non debet accipere, “the priest ought not to receive the Body of Christ without His Blood,” so that the canon reached the medieval schools already framed as a rule for the celebrant. Aquinas, meeting it as an objection to the custom of giving the laity the Body alone, read it the same way: “Pope Gelasius is speaking of priests, who, as they consecrate the entire sacrament, ought to communicate in the entire sacrament. For, as we read in the (Twelfth) Council of Toledo, ‘What kind of a sacrifice is that, wherein not even the sacrificer is known to have a share?’” His reasoning distinguishes the sacrament from its reception. “On the part of the sacrament it is proper for both the body and the blood to be received, since the perfection of the sacrament lies in both,” and since “it is the priest’s duty both to consecrate and finish the sacrament, he ought on no account to receive Christ’s body without the blood.” On the part of the recipients, “the greatest reverence and caution are called for,” and “the perfection of this sacrament does not lie in the use of the faithful, but in the consecration of the matter.” When the Augsburg Confession cited the canon for the lay cup, the Roman Confutation answered with the same reading: “if they examine the document they will find that Gelasius speaks of priests, and not of laymen.” Read this way, the integrity Gelasius requires is the integrity of the sacrifice, which the priest completes on behalf of all, while the lay communicant who receives the host alone receives Christ entire under that species and divides nothing of the mystery.

A general rule against dividing the sacrament, which the later Latin custom set aside

In brief: Gelasius forbade dividing the sacrament as such, so the later Latin norm of lay communion under the bread alone departed from a Roman rule.

Held by: the Utraquist Jakoubek of Stříbro (1414);6,7 the Augsburg Confession (Lutheran, 1530);8 the church historian Philip Schaff (Reformed, 1867);9 and Fr. Chad Arneson, writing from an Orthodox standpoint (2026).10

The case rests on the reason the decree gives. Gelasius does not say that those who hold wine evil sin; he says that “the division of one and the same mystery cannot occur without grave sacrilege,” a statement about the sacrament itself, and the persons he disciplines are communicants who took the Body and left the cup. Leo’s sermon is read on this side as a witness to the Roman norm the decree enforced; in Arneson’s words, “For Leo, as for Gelasius after him, refusal of the Chalice was a conspicuous anomaly that stood out against the universal Roman practice of lay chalice reception.” When Jakoubek began giving the chalice to the laity at Prague in 1414, he justified it in his quaestio Quia heu in templis with citations from Gratian, among them Gelasius’s Comperimus. The Council of Constance (1415), in the decree that made lay communion under one kind binding, stated that “this sacrament was received by the faithful under both kinds in the early church.”11 The Augsburg Confession set the canon among its witnesses to the ancient usage: “Pope Gelasius commands that the Sacrament be not divided (dist. II., De Consecratione, cap. Comperimus). Only custom, not so ancient, has it otherwise.” Calvin cited the decree for both kinds as “an inviolable law.”12 Schaff read it as “a remarkable testimony against what he pronounces the ‘sacrilege’ of withholding the cup from the laity.” Arneson, citing the canonists Theodore Balsamon and Matthew Blastares, grants the Manichaean background but holds that “a heretical context does not alter the objective logic of a sacramental principle”: on his reading Gelasius “condemned the physical act of division itself as inherently sacrilegious.” On this reading the rule binds wherever the sacrament is given, and Arneson holds that the Orthodox practice of giving both species together by the spoon meets Gelasius’s requirement that the faithful receive the sacraments in their entirety, as the Eastern churches have continued to do.

A measure against a Manichaean refusal of the cup

In brief: Gelasius enforced the cup to expose Manichaeans who refused wine as evil; the decree disciplined that refusal and did not judge communion under one kind as such.

Held by: Robert Bellarmine, De Eucharistia IV.26 (Catholic, in the Disputationes de Controversiis, 1586–1593) ⚠ unverified;13 Patrick Toner, “Communion under Both Kinds” (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1908);14 John F. X. Murphy, “Pope St. Gelasius I” (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1909).15,16

The decree answers the situation Leo had described: Manichaeans at Rome, holding wine to be the devil’s work, took the Body to stay hidden and refused the Blood. Bellarmine, as Scherzer reports him, identifies Gelasius’s “certain persons” as these Manichaeans. Murphy counts the decree among the occasions when Gelasius “knew when to make exceptions or modifications,” and explains it as “the only effective way of detecting the Manichæans, who, though present in Rome in large numbers, sought to divert attention from their hidden propaganda by feigning Catholicism. As they held wine to be impure and essentially sinful, they would refuse the chalice and thus be recognized.” The decree names the cause in its own words, a “superstition” by which these persons were “taught to be bound.” Toner reads Leo’s account as showing that such concealment “would have been impossible, unless it was customary at the time for at least some of the faithful to receive under one kind alone,” and holds that the exclusion of those detected “implies no reprobation on the merits of Communion under one kind; and the same is true of the decree attributed by Gratian to Pope Gelasius.” James Gibbons (1876) describes Gelasius as making a reception under both kinds that had been optional into a law, for this reason, a law that “continued in force for several ages” and fell into disuse toward the thirteenth century;17 Murphy likewise writes that “with the change of conditions, the old normal method of receiving Holy Communion under the form of bread alone returned into vogue.” In Gibbons’s account the authority that enforced the cup against the Manichees in the fifth century withdrew it against the Calixtines in the fifteenth. On this reading the decree belongs to discipline, which Toner, quoting Trent, describes as within the Church’s authority to set and to change, Christ being received whole under either kind, as Trent defined.
