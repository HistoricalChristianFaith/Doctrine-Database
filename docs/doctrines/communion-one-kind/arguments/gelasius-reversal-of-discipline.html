Did the medieval withdrawal of the cup reverse ancient Roman discipline? (Gelasius, Leo)

Type: historical argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — the factual core is solid and even conceded by the councils: fifth-century Rome required the laity to drink the cup, and Pope Gelasius I (c. 494) called dividing the sacrament "grave sacrilege," so the later Latin one-kind practice is a genuine change, not a continuation; but the strong thesis — that this change reverses a binding discipline and so convicts the medieval practice out of Rome's own mouth — turns on whether Gelasius's ruling, occasioned by crypto-Manichaeans who spurned wine as evil, reaches a reverent disciplinary reservation of the cup, and on the separate question of the church's authority to alter an instituted form; strong by the lights of the identical act and Gelasius's general wording, weaker by the lights of the decretal's heretical occasion and the discipline-vs-doctrine distinction, with no clear winner · advanced by the Utraquists and by the Reformers (the Augsburg Confession, Art. XXII); the limiting reading advanced by Thomas Aquinas and the Council of Trent.

Related arguments: this is the historical prong of the church-authority question — see also May the church withdraw the cup? (the normative prong), on which it leans, and the dominical-command and concomitance arguments.

The claim

Indeed, Pope Gelasius commands that the Sacrament be not divided (dist. II., De Consecratione, cap. Comperimus). … And this usage has long remained in the Church, nor is it known when, or by whose authority, it was changed.

— Augsburg Confession (1530), Art. XXII, "Of Both Kinds in the Sacrament," Book of Concord.1 The historical thesis pressed here — first by the Hussite Utraquists, then by the Reformers — is that the medieval Western withdrawal of the lay chalice did not merely innovate but reversed the discipline an ancient Roman pope had enforced: what Gelasius called "grave sacrilege" the medieval church made the law. If so, one-kind communion cannot be "the constant mind of the church"; Rome overturned its own earlier ruling.

Who advances it

The argument is the historical companion of the scriptural case for the lay chalice. Its two witnesses — Leo the Great (c. 445) and Gelasius I (c. 494) — were brought forward by the Bohemian Utraquists in defence of the cup restored at Prague in 1414, and the Reformers made them a standing proof-text: the Augsburg Confession (1530) cites Gelasius by name and adds Cyprian ("Cyprian in some places testifies that the blood was given to the people"), concluding that both-kinds reception "has long remained in the Church, nor is it known when, or by whose authority, it was changed" (quoted above). The point is deliberately not scriptural but ecclesiastical-historical: it argues from Rome's own canon law, not from "Drink ye all of it."

The evidence for

Rome once enforced the cup on the laity. Both witnesses are Roman and pre-medieval. Preaching at Rome c. 445, Leo teaches his people to unmask the crypto-Manichaeans precisely because they "receive Christ's Body with unworthy lips, though they altogether refuse to drink the Blood of our Redemption" — a detection test that only works if every genuine communicant drinks the cup.2 A half-century later Gelasius turned the same judgement into a canon (the decretal Comperimus, later gathered into Gratian's Decretum): those who "having received a portion of the sacred Body alone, abstain from the cup of the sacred Blood" must "either receive the sacraments in their entirety, or be repelled from them entirely, because the division of one and the same mystery cannot occur without grave sacrilege" (De consecratione D.2 c.12).3

The stated principle is general, and the condemned act is identical. The load-bearing strength of the thesis is that Gelasius does not phrase his rule as "those who hold matter evil sin," but as a rule about the act: dividing the one mystery is what "cannot occur without grave sacrilege." The medieval Latin discipline made that very act — the laity receiving the Body while abstaining from the cup — the ordinary norm. On its face the general principle reaches the later practice, whatever the difference of motive.

The change is conceded, not merely asserted. The reversal thesis does not have to prove that both-kinds was the ancient norm against a denial — the councils grant it. Constance (1415) concedes "that Christ instituted this venerable sacrament … under the forms of both bread and wine," and "that this sacrament was received by the faithful under both kinds in the early church," ruling one-kind lawful only "notwithstanding this."4 So the bare historical fact — that a change occurred, from an instituted-and-primitive both-kinds norm to a one-kind norm — is common ground; the dispute is only over its force.

The Catholic limiting reading

The decretal's occasion was heresy, not devout laity. The received explanation (so the Catholic Encyclopedia) is that Comperimus, like Leo's sermon, was aimed at detecting Manichaeans — present in numbers at Rome, feigning Catholicism, and refusing the chalice because they held wine and all matter to be intrinsically evil.5 On this reading the "sacrilege" is a heretical refusal grounded in a false doctrine of matter — a contempt for the creature and so for the Creator — not a reverent reservation of the cup by lawful authority that affirms the wine's goodness and the whole Christ received under either kind. A refusal in contempt and a reservation in reverence are, on this account, different acts, and only the first is what Gelasius struck.

Discipline changed, not doctrine. The medieval church never denied that the sacrament was instituted under both kinds; it denied only that the laity are bound to receive under both. What was reversed, on the Catholic side, is a disciplinary custom, which the church holds itself competent to order — the very question weighed at "May the church withdraw the cup?" Reversing a reformable discipline is not self-contradiction if the discipline was never immutable.

Aquinas's restriction to priests. Faced with the canon directly, Aquinas read it of the clergy: "Pope Gelasius is speaking of priests, who, as they consecrate the entire sacrament, ought to communicate in the entire sacrament" — the rubric under which Gratian filed it ("the priest ought not to receive the Body of Christ without His Blood") lends this some support.6

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The narrow historical claim is true and uncontested: in the fifth century the Roman church required the laity to drink the cup, treated abstaining from it as anomalous enough to flag a heretic, and — in Gelasius — called dividing the sacrament "grave sacrilege"; the medieval West then made one-kind reception the lay norm. That is a real reversal of practice, and the councils themselves concede the starting point. The Reformers' factual premise, that Rome once enforced what it later forbade, is sound.

How far the Manichaean framing narrows Gelasius. It narrows the occasion but not cleanly the principle. It is fair that the decretal was written against crypto-Manichaeans and that a canon is read in light of the mischief it addresses; a devout layman obeying a reverent reservation is not doing what a Manichaean did in contempt of Christ's blood. But two things blunt the limiting reading. First, Gelasius grounds the sacrilege in the act of dividing "one and the same mystery," not in the heretic's motive, so on the wording the principle overshoots its occasion. Second, the persons Gelasius disciplined were laymen, which is exactly why Aquinas's confinement of the ruling to priests reads against the text; the rubric in Gratian supplies a home for that reading, but the decretal's own scenario is lay reception, not concelebration. So the tradition is not univocal: Rome's fifth-century voice and its medieval practice point opposite ways, and the reconciliation requires a motive-and-office distinction the bare text does not supply.

What is overstated. The strong form of the thesis — that Gelasius by himself convicts the medieval practice of sacrilege and settles the whole controversy — outruns what a single occasional decretal can bear. Whether reversing an admitted dominical-and-apostolic practice is illegitimate is not decided by proving the reversal happened; it is the authority question, weighed on its own page. The Gelasian evidence is the sharpest single lever against the "constant mind" claim, not a knockdown: it shows the mind of the church was not constant, which is a premise the Catholic side already grants and answers with its doctrine of reformable discipline.

Assessment

Contested. That the medieval withdrawal reversed an earlier Roman discipline is, as a matter of fact, well-established and even conceded — fifth-century Rome required the cup and called dividing the sacrament sacrilege, and the later Latin church made one-kind the law. Where the thesis stops being plain fact and becomes disputed is its force: the Catholic side answers that Gelasius struck a heretical refusal of the cup rooted in a false doctrine of matter, not a reverent disciplinary reservation, and that what changed was reformable discipline, not the confession that both kinds is instituted. That limiting reading narrows the decretal's occasion honestly but does not cleanly narrow its general wording, and it leans on restricting to priests a ruling whose own subjects were laymen — so the reversal thesis keeps real bite as the strongest historical lever against a claimed "constant mind." Strong by the lights of the identical act and Gelasius's own words, weaker by the lights of the heretical occasion and the discipline-vs-doctrine distinction, and finally resting on the unsettled prior question of the church's authority to alter an instituted form — hence contested. (See the normative prong, on which this historical prong leans.)
