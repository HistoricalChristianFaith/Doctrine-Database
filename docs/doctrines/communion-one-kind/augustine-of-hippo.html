Augustine of Hippo on the faithful drinking the consecrated cup

Dates: c. 410 (preaching during his episcopate at Hippo, 395/6–430) · Wikipedia
Summary: Preaching to his North-African congregation, Augustine describes the converts of Pentecost — the very crowd that had cried for Christ's blood — coming to the Lord's table and, "in faith," drinking that Blood. Like Ambrose a generation earlier, he is not defending the lay chalice but simply assuming it: the whole body of the faithful receives the cup. He is a fifth-century witness from the Latin West that reception under both kinds was the ordinary practice — the norm the medieval West would later reverse — standing alongside Leo and Gelasius at Rome.

Claims & evidence

The converted people "came to the Lord's table, and in faith drank that Blood" (Sermon 27 on the New Testament §4)

They were converted from among this people of the Jews; were converted, and baptized. They came to the Lord's table, and in faith drank that Blood, which in their fury they had shed.

— Sermon 27 on the New Testament [77 in the Benedictine numbering], §4, on Matthew 15:21–28 (the Canaanite woman), source (cf. New Advent). The striking figure — the crowd that clamoured for Christ's blood now drinking it at the altar — is possible only because, for Augustine, the ordinary converted layman drinks the cup. He is not marking out a clerical privilege but describing what the baptized multitude does; the reception of the consecrated Blood is simply what coming "to the Lord's table" means.

Notes

  • What the passage establishes. As with Ambrose, the value of the text is that it is incidental: Augustine is preaching on the forgiveness of even Christ's killers, and reaches for the eucharist as an illustration precisely because his hearers all know that the faithful drink the Blood. That unspoken assumption is the pre-withdrawal Western practice on which the whole later controversy turns — the change made by the high-medieval Latin Church (see Aquinas) is a departure from what Augustine took for granted.
  • Elsewhere in Augustine. The same assumption runs through his eucharistic preaching to the newly baptized: in Sermon 272, showing the neophytes what lies on the altar, he says plainly "the bread is the body of Christ, the cup is the blood of Christ," and has them answer "Amen" as they receive; and in his sermons on John 6 he speaks throughout of the faithful eating the flesh and drinking the blood. Nowhere does he contemplate the laity receiving the host alone.
  • Augustine appears on this wiki as a witness on many doctrines; the eucharistic teaching cited here is representative of his preaching across his episcopate.
