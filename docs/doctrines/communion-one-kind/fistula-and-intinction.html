The Latin fistula and the West's rejected intinction — instruments of lay reception of the cup

Dates: the fistula (drinking-reed) in general Western use for lay communion to about the twelfth century, retained in the papal Mass thereafter; intinction condemned by Western synods from Braga (675) to Westminster (1175); the cup withdrawn from the laity across the thirteenth century · Wikipedia
Summary: In the medieval West the laity's reception of the cup was served by the fistula, a silver drinking-reed or tube through which the faithful sipped the Blood without touching the rim, described in an older liturgical handbook as used "to prevent even accidental irreverence." Intinction (dipping the host in the wine to give both together), which the Byzantine rite adopted with the communion-spoon, was forbidden by a series of Latin synods. Over the thirteenth century the wine was withdrawn from the laity, and the fistula survived in the papal liturgy. This block covers the Latin practice between Gelasius and Aquinas.

Claims & evidence

The fistula: a drinking-reed that let the laity sip the Blood without spilling it

The fistula (also known as calamo, cannula, arundo, calamus, pipa, pugillaris, sipho or sumptorium) is a metal drinking straw used in the Roman Rite to receive the Blood of Christ in Holy Communion … used for the communion of the laity during Mass.

— "Fistula (liturgical object)." Under its shifting names — fistula, calamus, pugillaris, siphon, canna — the reed was, in the words of an older liturgical manual, "an ordinary accompaniment of the chalice when the laity received Communion under both species to prevent even accidental irreverence." [note] A metal tube (silver for the laity, gold for the pope) through which the communicant drew the consecrated wine, or by which it was dispensed into the mouth like a pipette, it allowed the layman to receive the cup while guarding against spilling — an instrument comparable to the Byzantine communion-spoon, a contrast Robert Taft draws (see the timeline's note).

Its general Western use — and its retreat to the papal Mass

The use of the fistula was a much more general practice that could be found throughout the Western Church up until about the 12th century (when communion under one kind was becoming normative) … It was most famously retained in the papal liturgy, where a golden straw called the fistula … was utilized by the pope for communing from the chalice.

— "Curiosities of the Ancient Papal Mass: The Fistula," Liturgical Arts Journal (2020). The reed was no papal curiosity at first: it was named in the Roman ordines and spread with the Carolingian rite across Italy, France, Germany, England, and Poland, and into the Cistercian and Carthusian houses, precisely as the ordinary means of giving the laity the Blood. [note] It survived in the papal Mass (and, at Saint-Denis, into the eighteenth century) after the lay cup fell away in the thirteenth century. The reformed rite permits it — "the Blood of the Lord may be consumed either by drinking from the chalice directly, or by intinction, or by means of a tube or a spoon" (General Instruction of the Roman Missal, no. 245). [note]

Intinction available and used — then forbidden, synod after synod

The practice of the intinctio Panis … was already forbidden by the Council of Braga in 675 … It was condemned once more by the Council of Clermont (1095) under the presidency of Urban II, but with the limitation "nisi per necessitatem et per cautelam" … Its prohibition is renewed as late as 1175 by a Council of London or Westminster.

— Catholic Encyclopedia, "Communion under Both Kinds." Intinction — dipping the consecrated host in the consecrated wine so that both kinds are given together — is the practice the Byzantine rite adopted with the communion-spoon (see the Trullo page). In the Latin West it was used and repeatedly forbidden: forbidden at Braga (675), condemned again at Clermont (1095) save "by necessity and as a precaution," restricted by Paschal II to "small children and the wholly infirm who cannot swallow the bread" (parvulis ac omnino infirmis qui panem absorbere non possunt), and prohibited once more at a Council of London or Westminster in 1175.

The cup withdrawn: intinction suppressed, then the wine itself abolished for the laity

The final suppression of intinctio was followed in the thirteenth century by the gradual abolition for the laity of Communion under the species of wine … The Council of Lambeth (1281) directs that wine is to be received by the priest alone, and non-consecrated wine is to be received by the faithful.

— Catholic Encyclopedia, "Communion under Both Kinds." On this account the suppression of intinction was followed by the gradual abolition of the lay cup; by the time of Aquinas (d. 1274) reserving the chalice to the priest was "a prudent custom in some churches," and the Council of Lambeth in 1281 directed that the laity receive only unconsecrated wine (an ablution to help swallow the host), the consecrated Blood being the priest's alone.

Notes

  • The older manual quoted. The description of the tube as "an ordinary accompaniment of the chalice when the laity received Communion under both species to prevent even accidental irreverence … called tube, calamus, fistula, canna, virgula, siphon and pugillaris" is from an older liturgical handbook, The Mass and Vestments of the Catholic Church (John Walsh), p. 485 — a convenient summary of the instrument's names and purpose.
  • The fistula's spread. On the reed's presence in the Roman ordines and its diffusion across the Carolingian West (Italy, France, Germany, England, Poland) and the Cistercian and Carthusian orders, see "Fistula (liturgical object)" and the survey in the Liturgical Arts Journal, "Eucharistic Utensils" (2020). Among all the Western eucharistic implements, the fistula was the only one to survive in ceremonial use into the twentieth century — in the papal Mass.
  • The reed still permitted. General Instruction of the Roman Missal, no. 245, retains the tube (with intinction and the spoon) as a lawful mode of giving the Blood — so these mechanisms remain in the Latin rite's law.
  • Placement on the timeline. This block is the Latin counterpart of the Trullo block: the Byzantine rite gave both species together by the spoon, while Latin synods forbade intinction and the lay cup fell out of use in the thirteenth century.
