The Latin fistula and the West's rejected intinction — the cup engineered, then dropped

Dates: the fistula (drinking-reed) in general Western use for lay communion to about the twelfth century, retained in the papal Mass thereafter; intinction condemned by Western synods from Braga (675) to Westminster (1175); the cup withdrawn from the laity across the thirteenth century · Wikipedia
Summary: The medieval West faced the very fear the East did — spilling the Precious Blood from the common cup — and it, too, engineered a mechanical answer that kept the chalice for the laity: the fistula, a silver drinking-reed or tube through which the faithful sipped the Blood without touching the rim. It even possessed the East's own remedy of intinction (dipping the host in the wine to give both together). Yet where the East merged the species and kept both kinds, the Latin West did the opposite: it repeatedly forbade intinction by synod after synod, let the reed fall out of ordinary use, and over the thirteenth century withdrew the wine from the laity altogether — keeping the fistula only in the papal liturgy as a relic of the older practice. This block fills the mechanical history between Gelasius and Aquinas: the West had the tools to retain the cup and chose to drop it instead.

Claims & evidence

The fistula: a drinking-reed that let the laity sip the Blood without spilling it

The fistula (also known as calamo, cannula, arundo, calamus, pipa, pugillaris, sipho or sumptorium) is a metal drinking straw used in the Roman Rite to receive the Blood of Christ in Holy Communion … used for the communion of the laity during Mass.

— "Fistula (liturgical object)." Under its shifting names — fistula, calamus, pugillaris, siphon, canna — the reed was, in the words of an older liturgical manual, "an ordinary accompaniment of the chalice when the laity received Communion under both species to prevent even accidental irreverence." [note] A metal tube (silver for the laity, gold for the pope) through which the communicant drew the consecrated wine, or by which it was dispensed into the mouth like a pipette, it met the spilling fear head-on while keeping the cup in the layman's own reception — the Latin parallel to the Byzantine communion-spoon, arrived at independently and for the same reverential motive.

Its general Western use — and its retreat to the papal Mass

The use of the fistula was a much more general practice that could be found throughout the Western Church up until about the 12th century (when communion under one kind was becoming normative) … It was most famously retained in the papal liturgy, where a golden straw called the fistula … was utilized by the pope for communing from the chalice.

— "Curiosities of the Ancient Papal Mass: The Fistula," Liturgical Arts Journal (2020). The reed was no papal curiosity at first: it was named in the Roman ordines and spread with the Carolingian rite across Italy, France, Germany, England, and Poland, and into the Cistercian and Carthusian houses, precisely as the ordinary means of giving the laity the Blood. [note] That it survived only in the papal Mass (and, at Saint-Denis, into the eighteenth century) is the whole point of this timeline: as the lay cup fell away in the thirteenth century, the instrument that had preserved it became a fossil of the older discipline. Even the reformed rite still permits it — "the Blood of the Lord may be consumed either by drinking from the chalice directly, or by intinction, or by means of a tube or a spoon" (General Instruction of the Roman Missal, no. 245) [note] — the West's spoon-and-reed clause, kept but long unused.

Intinction available and used — then forbidden, synod after synod

The practice of the intinctio Panis … was already forbidden by the Council of Braga in 675 … It was condemned once more by the Council of Clermont (1095) under the presidency of Urban II, but with the limitation "nisi per necessitatem et per cautelam" … Its prohibition is renewed as late as 1175 by a Council of London or Westminster.

— Catholic Encyclopedia, "Communion under Both Kinds." This is the decisive contrast with the East. Intinction — dipping the consecrated host in the consecrated wine so that both kinds are given together — is exactly the remedy the Byzantine rite embraced and made permanent by the communion-spoon. The Latin West knew it, used it (as a convenience for the sick and, in places, for the healthy laity), and then rejected it: forbidden at Braga (675), condemned again at Clermont (1095) save "by necessity and as a precaution," restricted by Paschal II to "small children and the wholly infirm who cannot swallow the bread" (parvulis ac omnino infirmis qui panem absorbere non possunt), and prohibited once more at a Council of London or Westminster in 1175. The remedy that let the East keep the cup, the West legislated away.

The cup withdrawn: intinction suppressed, then the wine itself abolished for the laity

The final suppression of intinctio was followed in the thirteenth century by the gradual abolition for the laity of Communion under the species of wine … The Council of Lambeth (1281) directs that wine is to be received by the priest alone, and non-consecrated wine is to be received by the faithful.

— Catholic Encyclopedia, "Communion under Both Kinds." The sequence is the heart of the matter. Having closed off intinction — the one path that would have kept both kinds in the Eastern manner — the West let the common cup and the fistula lapse together, until by the time of Aquinas (d. 1274) reserving the chalice to the priest was becoming the norm, and the Council of Lambeth in 1281 could direct that the laity receive only unconsecrated wine (an ablution to help swallow the host), the consecrated Blood being the priest's alone. The withdrawal was therefore not forced by any impossibility of giving the laity the Blood safely: the West had two workable mechanisms — the reed and intinction — and set both aside.

Notes

  • The older manual quoted. The description of the tube as "an ordinary accompaniment of the chalice when the laity received Communion under both species to prevent even accidental irreverence … called tube, calamus, fistula, canna, virgula, siphon and pugillaris" is from a nineteenth-century handbook, The Mass and Vestments of the Catholic Church (John Walsh), p. 485 — a convenient summary of the instrument's names and purpose.
  • The fistula's spread. On the reed's presence in the Roman ordines and its diffusion across the Carolingian West (Italy, France, Germany, England, Poland) and the Cistercian and Carthusian orders, see "Fistula (liturgical object)" and the survey in the Liturgical Arts Journal, "Eucharistic Utensils" (2020). Among all the Western eucharistic implements, the fistula was the only one to survive in ceremonial use into the twentieth century — in the papal Mass.
  • The reed still permitted. General Instruction of the Roman Missal, no. 245, retains the tube (with intinction and the spoon) as a lawful mode of giving the Blood — so the Latin rite has never formally abolished the very mechanisms whose disuse accompanied the withdrawal of the cup; it simply stopped using them.
  • Why it belongs on this timeline — the West chose to drop rather than merge. This block is the Latin mirror of the Trullo block. The two churches met the identical practical fear — losing a drop of the Blood — with the identical kind of engineering answer (a special utensil; the option of merging the species). The East kept the cup by merging (spoon-and-intinction); the West, having the same tools, forbade intinction and let the reed and the cup lapse. The withdrawal of the chalice was thus a Latin choice, not a technical necessity — which is why the practical "danger of spilling" defense is weighed as insufficient on its own terms (see the spilling-danger argument), and why concomitance and the church's authority, not the spill-risk, are where the councils actually rested the case.
