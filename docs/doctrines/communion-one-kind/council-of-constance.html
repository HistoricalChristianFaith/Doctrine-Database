The Council of Constance and the decree Cum in nonnullis (1415)

Dates: 15 June 1415 (Session 13) · Wikipedia
Summary: Confronted with the Bohemian restoration of the lay chalice by Jakoubek of Stříbro, the Council of Constance, in its thirteenth session, issued the decree known from its opening words as Cum in nonnullis — the first conciliar definition of communion under one kind for the laity. Its structure is the crux of every later dispute: it concedes that Christ instituted the sacrament under both kinds and administered it to the apostles so, and that the early church received under both kinds — and then rules, "notwithstanding this," that the church's custom of giving the laity the host alone is to be held as law, that calling it sacrilegious or illicit is erroneous, and that those who obstinately assert the contrary are to be punished as heretics. It is the disciplinary answer to Jakoubek's demand, and the charter later Reformers would attack.

Claims & evidence

The condemnation of the Bohemian practice (Cum in nonnullis)

Certain people, in some parts of the world, have rashly dared to assert that the christian people ought to receive the holy sacrament of the eucharist under the forms of both bread and wine. They communicate the laity everywhere not only under the form of bread but also under that of wine … contrary to the church's custom which has been laudably and sensibly approved, from the church's head downwards, but which they damnably try to repudiate as sacrilegious.

— Council of Constance, Session 13 (15 June 1415), decree Cum in nonnullis, source (Decrees of the Ecumenical Councils, trans. Norman Tanner). The manuscript rubric names the target directly: "Condemnation of communion under both kinds, recently revived among the Bohemians by Jakoubek of Stříbro."

The concession: institution and the early church under both kinds

… although Christ instituted this venerable sacrament after a meal and ministered it to his apostles under the forms of both bread and wine, nevertheless and notwithstanding this, the praiseworthy authority of the sacred canons and the approved custom of the church have and do retain … Moreover, just as this custom was sensibly introduced … so with similar or even greater reason was it possible to introduce and sensibly observe the custom that, although this sacrament was received by the faithful under both kinds in the early church, nevertheless later it was received under both kinds only by those confecting it, and by the laity only under the form of bread.

— ibid., source. This is the decree's remarkable candour: it does not deny the Utraquist's history but grants it — Christ instituted both kinds, the apostles received both, the primitive church communicated the laity under both — and rests the whole case on the church's authority to introduce, "for good reasons," a later custom. The theological warrant for the change is concomitance (the whole Christ "truly contained under both the form of bread and the form of wine"), the doctrine Aquinas had supplied.

The custom held as law; dissent punished as heresy

… since this custom was introduced for good reasons by the church and holy fathers, and has been observed for a very long time, it should be held as a law which nobody may repudiate or alter at will without the church's permission. To say that the observance of this custom or law is sacrilegious or illicit must be regarded as erroneous. Those who stubbornly assert the opposite of the aforesaid are to be confined as heretics and severely punished …

— ibid., source. A following clause forbids any priest, "under pain of excommunication," to communicate the people under both kinds. The reception under one kind is thus declared not merely permitted but obligatory for the laity, and its denial made a punishable heresy — the very charge under which Jan Hus would be burned three weeks later (6 July 1415), and under which the Bohemian Utraquists would be fought for a century.

Notes

  • What the decree does and does not settle. It settles the church's discipline: one-kind communion for the laity is lawful and binding, and asserting the contrary is heresy. It does not claim that Christ instituted the sacrament under one kind — it explicitly says the opposite — and so it locates the whole question on the ground of authority to change a dominical practice, not of Scripture's plain sense. That is exactly the seam the Utraquists and Reformers pressed: if Christ said "Drink ye all of it" and the early church obeyed, on whose warrant may the church countermand it? The decree's answer — that a custom introduced "for good reasons" and long observed "should be held as a law" — is weighed among this timeline's arguments in its own right.
  • Relation to the older Roman witnesses. The Hussites cited Leo the Great and Gelasius I — who had made refusing the cup the tell of a heretic — to argue Rome once required the chalice. Constance's reply is implicit in its structure: those texts condemned a heretical refusal grounded in a false doctrine of matter (the Manichaeans), not a disciplinary custom of the church; and the church that once required the cup may, on the same authority, now withhold it.
  • The decree was confirmed by Pope Martin V and its teaching reaffirmed and expanded at the Council of Trent (Session XXI, 1562), which anathematized the claim that reception under both kinds is required of all the faithful by divine command.
