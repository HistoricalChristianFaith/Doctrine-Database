The Council of Constance and the decree Cum in nonnullis (1415)

Dates: 15 June 1415 (Session 13) · Wikipedia
Summary: Confronted with the administration of the cup to the Bohemian laity by Jakoubek of Stříbro, the Council of Constance, in its thirteenth session, issued the decree known from its opening words as Cum in nonnullis on communion under one kind for the laity. It grants that Christ instituted the sacrament under both kinds and administered it to the apostles so, and that the early church received under both kinds — and then rules, "notwithstanding this," that the church's custom of giving the laity the host alone is to be held as law, that calling it sacrilegious or illicit is erroneous, and that those who obstinately assert the contrary are to be punished as heretics. It answered Jakoubek's practice.

Claims & evidence

The condemnation of the Bohemian practice (Cum in nonnullis)

Certain people, in some parts of the world, have rashly dared to assert that the christian people ought to receive the holy sacrament of the eucharist under the forms of both bread and wine. They communicate the laity everywhere not only under the form of bread but also under that of wine … contrary to the church's custom which has been laudably and sensibly approved, from the church's head downwards, but which they damnably try to repudiate as sacrilegious.

— Council of Constance, Session 13 (15 June 1415), decree Cum in nonnullis, source (Decrees of the Ecumenical Councils, trans. Norman Tanner). The heading printed with the decree in Tanner's edition names the target: "Condemnation of communion under both kinds, recently revived among the Bohemians by Jakoubek of Stříbro."

The decree's statements on the institution and the early church

… although Christ instituted this venerable sacrament after a meal and ministered it to his apostles under the forms of both bread and wine, nevertheless and notwithstanding this, the praiseworthy authority of the sacred canons and the approved custom of the church have and do retain … Moreover, just as this custom was sensibly introduced … so with similar or even greater reason was it possible to introduce and sensibly observe the custom that, although this sacrament was received by the faithful under both kinds in the early church, nevertheless later it was received under both kinds only by those confecting it, and by the laity only under the form of bread.

— ibid., source. The decree grants that Christ instituted the sacrament under both kinds, that he ministered it so to the apostles, and that the early church communicated the faithful under both, and grounds the later custom in "the praiseworthy authority of the sacred canons and the approved custom of the church," a custom introduced "for good reasons." It adds that "the whole body and blood of Christ are truly contained under both the form of bread and the form of wine," the teaching Aquinas had expounded as concomitance.

The custom held as law; dissent punished as heresy

… since this custom was introduced for good reasons by the church and holy fathers, and has been observed for a very long time, it should be held as a law which nobody may repudiate or alter at will without the church's permission. To say that the observance of this custom or law is sacrilegious or illicit must be regarded as erroneous. Those who stubbornly assert the opposite of the aforesaid are to be confined as heretics and severely punished …

— ibid., source. A following clause forbids any priest, "under pain of excommunication," to communicate the people under both kinds. The reception under one kind is thus declared binding for the laity, and stubborn assertion of the contrary made punishable as heresy. The council's sentence on Jan Hus three weeks later (6 July 1415) does not mention the chalice; it condemns him for teaching and defending the condemned errors of John Wyclif (Session 15, "Sentence against John Hus").

Notes

  • Reception and debate. The decree declares one-kind communion for the laity lawful and binding, and stubbornly asserting the contrary is heresy; it states that Christ instituted the sacrament under both kinds. Luther (1520) answered that denying both kinds to the laity "is not in the power of any angel, much less of any pope or council." Patrick Toner (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1908) reads Constance and Trent as introducing "no new legislation on the subject; both were content with declaring that the existing custom had already acquired the force of law" ("Communion under Both Kinds").
  • Relation to the older Roman witnesses. Jakoubek cited Gelasius I's decree for the lay chalice; the decree of Constance does not mention Gelasius or Leo the Great. The readings of Gelasius's decree are set out at What does Gelasius's Comperimus forbid?
  • In the bull Inter cunctas (22 February 1418), Pope Martin V ordered suspected Wycliffites and Hussites to be asked whether they believe that "the custom of giving communion to lay persons under the species of bread only," being "approved by the sacred Council of CONSTANCE, must be preserved" (Denzinger 668, trans. Deferrari; on Martin V as the bull's author, Catholic Encyclopedia, "Council of Constance", which also records that how far Martin V confirmed the decrees of Constance is disputed). The Council of Trent (Session XXI, 1562) restated the custom and anathematized the claim that reception under both kinds is required of all the faithful by divine command.
