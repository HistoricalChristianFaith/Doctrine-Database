The Council of Basel and the Compactata (1433–1436)

Dates: Prague Compacts accepted 30 November 1433; promulgated at Jihlava (Iglau) 5 July 1436 · Wikipedia
Summary: To end the Hussite wars, the Council of Basel negotiated with the moderate Utraquists (Calixtines) the agreement known as the Compactata — the Compacts of Basel, or of Prague. Its first article conceded communion under both kinds to the laity of Bohemia and Moravia. This is a formal grant of the lay chalice by a Western council, made as a concession, with the doctrinal proviso that the whole Christ is present under each kind, and declared null by Pope Pius II in 1462. Where Constance had forbidden the lay cup, Basel granted it; Patrick Toner (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1908) records that the concession "had never been approved by any pope" ("Communion under Both Kinds").

Claims & evidence

The first Compactatum grants the laity communion under both kinds

In Bohemia and Moravia, communion under both kinds is to be given to all adults who desire it …

— the first of the four Compactata of Basle, as given in the Catholic Encyclopedia, "Hussites". The Council, "chiefly bent on safeguarding the dogma," consented to this and the other disciplinary articles; the concession of the cup was thus made without conceding the Utraquist claim that both kinds are of divine necessity. Having ratified the Compacts on 15 January 1437, the council acknowledged "only on 23 December" that communion under both kinds was not heretical (Wikipedia, "Compacts of Basel," citing Joachim W. Stieber, 1978, p. 163); the Constance decree had held that those who stubbornly asserted the contrary of its ruling were to be confined as heretics.

The doctrinal proviso: the whole Christ under each kind

Communion in both kinds was granted to all who desired it, but with the understanding that Christ was entirely present in each kind.

— Philip Schaff (ed.), New Schaff–Herzog Encyclopedia, on the Hussite Wars and the Compactata of Prague. As the concession was framed, "the cup was granted to the laity; at the same time the teaching of the Church as to the Real Presence of Christ under each form was insisted upon" — priests administering the cup were to instruct the people that receiving both kinds is permitted as a concession, not because either single kind is deficient. The grant of the chalice thus carried with it the teaching, expounded by Aquinas as concomitance, that Christ is present entire under each kind.

Weakened by "subtle clauses"

[The agreement] followed the Four Articles of Prague but weakened them with subtle clauses (e.g., the council granted the Czechs the Communion in both kinds but under vaguely defined conditions).

— Encyclopædia Britannica, "Prague Compactata." The compromise wording left the conditions vaguely defined, and Catholic and Utraquist readings of the key first article diverged — the Czechs took it as a permanent charter of the lay chalice, while the council came to insist it had extended only to those Hussites then living. Pius II later declared the grant null (below).

Pius II declares the Compactata null and void (1462)

[Pope Pius II] … refused to acknowledge the Utraquist rite, and declared the Compactata null and void, 31 March, 1462.

— Catholic Encyclopedia, "Hussites." Pius II — who as Aeneas Silvius Piccolomini had known the Hussites first-hand at Basel and as legate to Prague — "declared the Compactata null and void, prohibited communion in both kinds," and recognized George of Poděbrady as king only on condition of "unconditional harmony with the Roman Church." His legal ground was that the concession had been meant only for the Hussites then living and had never been extended by the church to their heirs. Utraquist worship continued in Bohemia: the same article records that in 1485 King Wladislaw "granted equal liberty and rights to both parties." Toner describes the concession as "positively revoked in 1462 by the Nuncio Fantini on the order of Pius II" ("Communion under Both Kinds").

Notes

  • Placement on the timeline. Basel granted the lay cup to the Bohemian Utraquists as a concession, while affirming that Christ is present entire under each kind; it did not teach that the cup is necessary.
  • Relation to Constance. Constance forbade the lay cup against Jakoubek's practice in Bohemia; Basel granted it to the Utraquists there as part of the settlement of the Hussite wars. Neither council taught that both kinds are commanded by Christ for all.
  • Forward to Trent and beyond. The concession question the Compactata opened was reopened at the Council of Trent (Session XXI, 1562), which reserved the decision to the pope; Pius IV then granted the cup in parts of the Empire in 1564, a concession Toner records as "withdrawn in the following year." (Those later developments are treated with Trent and the modern chapter of this timeline, not here.)
