The Council of Basel and the Compactata (1433–1436)

Dates: Prague Compacts accepted 30 November 1433; promulgated at Jihlava (Iglau) 5 July 1436 · Wikipedia
Summary: To end the Hussite wars, the Council of Basel negotiated with the moderate Utraquists (Calixtines) the agreement known as the Compactata — the Compacts of Basel, or of Prague. Its first article conceded communion under both kinds to the laity of Bohemia and Moravia. This is a rare formal grant of the lay chalice by a Western council — but granted as a disciplinary concession, hedged with the doctrinal proviso that the whole Christ is present under each kind, and later repudiated by Rome. It is the mirror image of Constance: where Constance had forbidden the cup, Basel restored it — on terms that conceded nothing of the theology behind concomitance. That the same church could both withhold and grant the chalice shows the practice was held (even by Rome) to be within the church's power to regulate, not fixed by divine command.

Claims & evidence

The first Compactatum grants the laity communion under both kinds

In Bohemia and Moravia, communion under both kinds is to be given to all adults who desire it …

— the first of the four Compactata of Basle, as given in the Catholic Encyclopedia, "Hussites". The Council, "chiefly bent on safeguarding the dogma," consented to this and the other disciplinary articles; the concession of the cup was thus made without conceding the Utraquist claim that both kinds are of divine necessity. The council had already, on 23 December 1436, acknowledged that communion under both kinds is not heretical — a striking counterpoint to the Constance decree that had made demanding it a punishable heresy.

The doctrinal proviso: the whole Christ under each kind

Communion in both kinds was granted to all who desired it, but with the understanding that Christ was entirely present in each kind.

— Philip Schaff (ed.), New Schaff–Herzog Encyclopedia, on the Hussite Wars and the Compactata of Prague. As the concession was framed, "the cup was granted to the laity; at the same time the teaching of the Church as to the Real Presence of Christ under each form was insisted upon" — priests administering the cup were to instruct the people that receiving both kinds is permitted as a concession, not because either single kind is deficient. The very grant of the chalice thus carried the concomitance theology of Aquinas forward intact: the cup is given because the faithful wish it, not because the host alone left them lacking Christ.

Weakened by "subtle clauses"

[The agreement] followed the Four Articles of Prague but weakened them with subtle clauses (e.g., the council granted the Czechs the Communion in both kinds but under vaguely defined conditions).

— Encyclopædia Britannica, "Prague Compactata." The compromise wording was deliberately ambiguous, and Catholic and Utraquist readings of the key first article diverged sharply from the outset — the Czechs took it as a permanent charter of the lay chalice, while the council came to insist it had extended only to those Hussites then living. That latent conflict of interpretation is what let Rome later declare the whole grant lapsed.

Pius II declares the Compactata null and void (1462)

[Pope Pius II] … refused to acknowledge the Utraquist rite, and declared the Compactata null and void, 31 March, 1462.

— Catholic Encyclopedia, "Hussites." Pius II — who as Aeneas Silvius Piccolomini had known the Hussites first-hand at Basel and as legate to Prague — "declared the Compactata null and void, prohibited communion in both kinds," and recognized George of Poděbrady as king only on condition of "unconditional harmony with the Roman Church." His legal ground was that the concession had been meant only for the Hussites then living and had never been extended by the church to their heirs. The revocation was, in practice, largely ignored in Bohemia, where the chalice remained the enduring mark of the Utraquists; but it re-established Rome's position that the cup had been a revocable indulgence, not a right.

Notes

  • Why it belongs on this timeline. The evidential weight of the Compactata is not that Rome ever taught the cup to be necessary — Basel granted it precisely while denying that — but that the concession was formally possible. Constance withheld the chalice and Basel restored it, both as exercises of the same disciplinary authority over "the manner of reception." The episode is the strongest medieval demonstration that withholding the cup was understood, even at Rome, as a matter of church discipline that could be relaxed, not a fixed point of divine law.
  • Relation to Constance. The two councils are usually read as opposites, but their premise is identical: the church may bind or loose the laity's cup as it judges fit. Constance bound it against Jakoubek's Bohemia; Basel loosed it back to that same Bohemia to buy peace. Neither council conceded the Utraquist thesis that both kinds are commanded by Christ for all — that claim Constance condemned and Basel's proviso quietly sidestepped.
  • Forward to Trent and beyond. The concession question the Compactata opened was reopened at the Council of Trent (Session XXI, 1562), which reserved the decision to the pope; Pius IV then granted the cup to parts of Germany in 1564, only for the indult to be withdrawn again within a decade — the same pattern of a revocable grant. (Those later developments are treated with Trent and the modern chapter of this timeline, not here.)
