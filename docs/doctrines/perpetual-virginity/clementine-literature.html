The Clementine Literature on the perpetual virginity of Mary

Dates: c. 220 (the Pseudo-Clementine romance — the Greek Clementine Homilies and the Latin Recognitions, both descended from a lost Jewish-Christian “Basic Writing” of the earlier third century that drew in turn on second-century Ebionite sources: the Kerygmata Petrou, and — underlying Recognitions i.27–71 — the Ascents of James. The prefixed Epistle of Clement to James belongs to the same cycle. The extant Homilies and Rufinus’s Latin Recognitions are fourth-century redactions, but the material is older: J. B. Lightfoot dated the Homilies “not late in the second century” and the Ascents of James “very early”) · Wikipedia
Summary: The Pseudo-Clementine writings come from a Jewish-Christian circle (Lightfoot calls it “a peculiar phase of Ebionism”) in which James the Lord’s brother is the leading figure — “bishop of bishops” ruling Jerusalem, the standard against which all preaching must be measured — and, in Lightfoot’s words, “distinguished from and in some respects exalted above the Twelve.” The Recognitions (resting, on Lightfoot’s account, on the older Ascents of James) has “James the son of Alphaeus” dispute among the Twelve while “James, the chief of the bishops,” presides over them and receives their report, so that two men named James appear in one scene. With Hegesippus and the Apostolic Constitutions, Lightfoot cites this distinction on the “James not one of the Twelve” sub-question, against the Hieronymian identification of the Lord’s brother with James the son of Alphaeus; the Epiphanian and Helvidian readings both keep the two men distinct. The passages quoted here say nothing of Mary’s virginity, and Ebionites are reported by Eusebius to have differed among themselves on the virgin birth.

Claims & evidence

James the Lord’s brother is “bishop of bishops” who rules Jerusalem

The letter that stands at the head of the Homilies, the Epistle of Clement to James, opens by addressing James not as one apostle among others but as the ruler over all the churches:

Clement to James, the lord, and the bishop of bishops, who rules Jerusalem, the holy church of the Hebrews, and the churches everywhere excellently founded by the providence of God, with the elders and deacons, and the rest of the brethren, peace be always.

— Epistle of Clement to James 1.1 James is styled “the lord… and the bishop of bishops” — not “apostle” — and is addressed as ruling “the churches everywhere.”

James, “called the brother of my Lord,” is the standard by which all teaching is tested

In the eleventh Homily, Peter warns the churches to trust no teacher who cannot show that his preaching agrees with James’s — the head of the Jerusalem church made the measure of teaching:

…remember to shun apostle or teacher or prophet who does not first accurately compare his preaching with that of James, who was called the brother of my Lord, and to whom was entrusted to administer the church of the Hebrews in Jerusalem,—and that even though he come to you with witnesses.

— Clementine Homilies xi.35.2 James is “called the brother of my Lord” — phrasing also found in Hegesippus — and is set over “apostle or teacher or prophet” alike. Lightfoot noted that the expression itself “has been variously interpreted as favouring all three hypotheses… and is indecisive in itself.”5

Two Jameses in the Recognitions — one of the Twelve, one presiding

In its account of a seven-day disputation in the Temple — a section Lightfoot describes as “founded on the Ascents of James, another very early Ebionite writing” — the twelve apostles each speak “at the request of James,” and among those twelve disputants is James the son of Alphaeus:

After him James the son of Alphaeus gave an address to the people, with the view of showing that we are not to believe on Jesus on the ground that the prophets foretold concerning Him, but rather that we are to believe the prophets, that they were really prophets…

— Recognitions i.59.3 Presiding over the conference is another James — identified by Lightfoot as the Lord’s brother — to whom the Twelve report and whom the high priest addresses as “the chief of the bishops”:

…when we twelve apostles… had come together… each one of us, at the request of James, stated briefly, in the hearing of the people, what we had done in every place.

…the chief of the priests asked of James, the chief of the bishops, that the discourse concerning Christ should not be drawn but from the Scriptures.

— Recognitions i.44, i.68.4 The scene holds two men named James in view at once: one is numbered with the Twelve and disputes as their colleague; the other, “the chief of the bishops,” receives the Twelve’s account.

The distinction in the debate over the brethren

Lightfoot set the Clementine literature in his catena for this feature. Of the Homilies and the prefixed epistle he observed that James is “styled not Apostle, but Bishop of Bishops, and seems to be distinguished from and in some respects exalted above the Twelve”; and of the Recognitions, resting on the Ascents of James, that “the distinction thus implied in the Homilies is explicitly stated… while James the son of Alphaeus is distinctly mentioned among the Twelve as one of the disputants (i.59).”5 Jerome’s Hieronymian reading identifies the Lord’s brother James with James the son of Alphaeus, and Lightfoot lists the Clementine literature among the authorities that keep the two men apart, with Hegesippus and the Apostolic Constitutions. Both the Epiphanian and Helvidian readings leave James outside the Twelve. Whether the cousin-theory had witnesses before Jerome is itself disputed; see the disputed reading on the cousin theory’s antiquity.

Dating and provenance

  • The Ascents of James and the dating. The extant Homilies (Greek) and Recognitions (surviving whole only in Rufinus’s Latin) are fourth-century works, but both descend from a common third-century “Basic Writing” that itself incorporated older Jewish-Christian sources. The Temple-disputation of Recognitions i.27–71, from which the two-Jameses distinction comes, is identified with the Ascents of James (Anabathmoi Iakobou), an Ebionite writing Lightfoot called “very early”; a later-second-century date for it is also given ⚠ unverified. On those datings the material reflects a second-century Jewish-Christian tradition preserved in later redactions.
  • Ebionite provenance and the virgin birth. Lightfoot describes the Homilies as written “to support a peculiar phase of Ebionism.”5 Eusebius reports two kinds of Ebionites: some held Jesus to be “the fruit of the intercourse of a man with Mary,” while others “did not deny that the Lord was born of a virgin and of the Holy Spirit.”6 The Clementine passages quoted here are not statements about Mary’s virginity; they are cited for one point — that they name James the Lord’s brother apart from James the son of Alphaeus.
  • Relation to the three theories. Lightfoot’s conspectus lists “Clementine Homilies (?)” — with a query — and the “Ascents of James” under “James the Just not one of the Twelve,” a heading he enters as “A. or B.”: compatible with the Epiphanian or the Helvidian view, not with the Hieronymian identification.5
