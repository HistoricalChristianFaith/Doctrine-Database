Theophylact of Ohrid on the perpetual virginity of Mary

Dates: c. 1075 (his Gospel and Pauline commentaries, largely epitomized from Chrysostom, written during his archiepiscopate at Ohrid; Theophylact c. 1050 – after 1107, Byzantine archbishop of Ohrid) · Wikipedia
Summary: Theophylact, an eleventh-century Byzantine exegete, combines elements of the two readings that hold Mary ever-virgin. Commenting on both Matthew 13:55 and Galatians 1:19 he fuses the Epiphanian and Hieronymian views by the Mosaic law of the levirate: Clopas died childless, and his brother Joseph “raised up seed” to him by the widow, so the brethren were Joseph’s natural sons (and thus the Lord’s reputed brothers) and Clopas’s legal sons (and thus the Lord’s cousins) at once — Joseph the natural father, Clopas the legal. On either side of the account they are not children of the Virgin. J. B. Lightfoot records that the suggestion “found but little favour.”

Claims & evidence

James both reputed brother and cousin — the levirate fusion (Galatians 1:19)

Expounding Paul’s “James the Lord’s brother” (Galatians 1:19), Theophylact denies a fleshly brotherhood and then explains James’s descent from Clopas by the levirate — Joseph begetting children to his dead brother by the widow:

And indeed, if he had wanted to distinguish him, he would have called him the son of Cleopas. For he was not a brother of the Lord according to the flesh, but was only considered so. How then was he the son of Cleopas, listen: Cleopas and Joseph were brothers; when Cleopas died childless, Joseph raised up seed for him and begot him and his other brothers, and Mary, whom, although she was the daughter of Cleopas, the Gospel calls the sister of the Mother of the Lord, since Joseph maintained toward the Most Holy Virgin the care of a father rather than the affection of a husband.

— Commentary on Galatians, on Galatians 1:19.1 The account combines the two readings. From the Hieronymian side he keeps “Mary of Clopas” as the Virgin’s reputed sister and James as a son of Clopas; from the Epiphanian side he keeps Joseph as the man who actually begat the brethren, so that they are the Lord’s brothers only as sharing Joseph’s house and reputed fatherhood. The levirate law (Deuteronomy 25:5–10) joins the two: the child a dead brother’s heir in law, the living brother’s son in fact. The last clause — Joseph “maintained toward the Most Holy Virgin the care of a father rather than the affection of a husband” — affirms Mary’s virginity in the same sentence that gives Joseph children.

The brethren as Joseph’s children by Clopas’s widow (Matthew 13:55)

The same construction appears in his comment on the Nazareth pericope, where the townsfolk name the Lord’s “brethren” and “sisters” (Matthew 13:55–56):

The Lord had brothers and sisters, the children of Joseph which he begat by the wife of his brother Cleopas. For when Cleopas died childless, Joseph took his wife in accordance with the law and had six children by her, four boys and two girls, Mary, who was called the daughter of Cleopas, in accordance with the law, and Salome.

— Commentary on Matthew, on Matthew 13:55.2 Here the mechanism is spelled out: Clopas “died childless,” Joseph “took his wife in accordance with the law” — the levirate marriage — and by her “had six children.” The daughter Mary is “called the daughter of Cleopas, in accordance with the law” (i.e. reckoned to the legal, deceased father) though begotten by Joseph. J. B. Lightfoot summarized the double parentage from Theophylact: “James… was the Lord’s reputed brother as the son of Joseph and the Lord’s cousin as the son of Clopas; the one was his natural, and the other his legal father.”3

Reception and context

  • Relation to the three theories. Lightfoot lists Theophylact under a separate heading, “Levirate,” in his conspectus.3 With the Epiphanians he makes the brethren Joseph’s children, not Mary’s; with the Hieronymians he ties them to Clopas and “Mary of Clopas,” the Virgin’s reputed sister, making them the Lord’s cousins. The levirate joins the two. Chrysostom and Theodoret, earlier on this timeline, also draw on more than one reading, without a levirate account.
  • Reception. Lightfoot records the theory’s reception: “This novel suggestion however found but little favour, and the Eastern Churches continued to distinguish between James the Lord’s brother and James the son of Alphaeus. The Greek, Syrian, and Coptic Calendars assign a separate day to each.”3
  • Another account in his corpus. Theophylact’s preface to the four Gospels gives the “former wife” account — Joseph “had seven children by his previous wife—four sons, and three daughters” — with no levirate, and a different count (seven, not the six of the Matthew comment).4
  • Mary’s virginity in each account. In both the levirate account and the preface’s “former wife” account, the brethren are not children of the Virgin.
