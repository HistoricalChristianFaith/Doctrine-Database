Bernard of Clairvaux on the perpetual virginity of Mary

Dates: c. 1153 (the timeline slot; Bernard, 1090/91 – 20 August 1153, the Cistercian abbot of Clairvaux, called Doctor Mellifluus and later a Doctor of the Church — his four homilies In laudibus Virginis Matris, on the Gospel words Missus est Gabriel (“the ‘Missus est’ homilies”), were among his earliest works, written c. 1120–1125) · Wikipedia
Summary: In the fourth of his homilies on the Annunciation, Bernard reads Mary’s reply to Gabriel — “How shall this be done, because I know not man?” (Luke 1:34) — as the question of a woman already bound by a vow of virginity. He dramatizes the tension the vow creates: put in the mouth of a betrothed woman just told she will bear a son, the question voices her fear that God’s wonder might require her to break the vow, and her resolve to obey either way. In this way he addresses, within the vow reading, the question how a betrothed woman could have vowed virginity.

Claims & evidence

Mary’s question presupposes a vow to “know no man” (Luke 1:34)

Bernard takes Mary’s perplexity not as doubt of the fact but as inquiry into the manner — and behind the inquiry he sets a vow already sworn. He imagines her interior reasoning at the angel’s word:

She doubts not the fact, but only inquires about the manner of its accomplishment. She says not “Will it be done?” but “How will this be done?” … As if she would say: “Since my Lord knows, and my conscience bears me witness, that His handmaid has made a vow to know no man, by what law shall it please Him to work this wonder? If I must break my vow that I may bring forth such a Son, I rejoice on account of the Son, but I grieve because of my vow. Nevertheless, His will be done. If, however, as a Virgin I may bring forth this Son … then I shall know that He hath had regard to the humility of His handmaid.”

— Homilies in Praise of the Virgin Mother (on Missus est), Homily IV.1 In Bernard’s reading Mary is already under a standing obligation of virginity: for a betrothed woman expecting an ordinary marriage, on his account, the promise of a son would raise no question. He presents her question as a conflict of goods — the promised Son on one side, the vow on the other.

The betrothal and the vow in Bernard’s homily

Bernard makes the question how a woman betrothed to Joseph could have vowed never to know a man the drama of the scene. His imagined Mary reckons openly with the possibility that the miracle might cost her the vow — “If I must break my vow … I rejoice on account of the Son, but I grieve because of my vow” — and submits regardless: “Nevertheless, His will be done.” The passage turns on the alternative that the miracle spares the vow: if she may bring forth the Son as a Virgin, her resolve is honoured, not annulled.1 In Bernard’s homily the tension is resolved by the virgin birth itself, by which a vowed virgin becomes a mother without ceasing to be a virgin.

Reception and context

  • The vow-of-virginity reading. Bernard’s meditation is a firsthand instance of the vow-of-virginity reading of Luke 1:34 — that Mary’s question implies she had already resolved never to “know” a man. The reading also appears on this timeline in Gregory of Nyssa, Augustine, Bede, and, in the following century, Aquinas.
  • The form of the vow. Among the witnesses on this timeline, Bernard stages the question the vow raises — that a betrothed woman’s vow of virginity would seem to bar the motherhood now promised — and answers it from within Mary’s imagined reasoning: “if I must break my vow…” He speaks of a vow already made (“His handmaid has made a vow to know no man”), where Bede speaks of a propositum, a settled purpose. Whether a vow of virginity was possible for a betrothed woman in Second-Temple Judaism is a further historical question that Bernard’s homily does not take up.
  • Relation to the three theories. Like Bede and John of Damascus, Bernard here affirms the virginity itself without taking up the identity of the “brethren of the Lord,” so this passage does not place him with the Epiphanians, Hieronymians, or Helvidians. The homily builds an extended meditation on the vow-reading of Luke 1:34.
  • The homilies. Bernard himself wished these four sermons called De laudibus Virginis Matris (“In Praise of the Virgin Mother”), though they are universally known by their opening lemma, Missus est (“[The angel Gabriel] was sent,” Luke 1:26). They were written early in his career.
