Helvidius on the perpetual virginity of Mary

Dates: c. 383 (a layman resident at Rome under Pope Damasus; his treatise on the brethren of the Lord appeared about 383, and Jerome’s reply, The Perpetual Virginity of Blessed Mary, Against Helvidius, followed the same year. Nothing else of his life is recorded; his own treatise is lost, and, as the standard editors note, “the only contemporary notice preserved of Helvidius is that by Jerome”) · Wikipedia
Summary: Helvidius is the eponym of the Helvidian reading of the brethren — that the “brethren of the Lord” were sons of Joseph and Mary, born after Jesus, so that Mary was not ever-virgin. He argued it at Rome about 383 from the example of the Lord’s own mother, who (he held) had gone on to bear children to her husband; the editors of the English translation of Jerome’s reply summarize the outcome of his views as “that virginity was ranked below matrimony.” His treatise does not survive: it is known only where Jerome quotes it to answer it, so his own words come to us at second hand and are marked ⚠ unverified. Jerome preserves the shape of his case — the proof-texts from Matthew (“knew her not till…”; “her first-born son”) and the appeal to earlier writers, Tertullian and Victorinus of Pettau. Lightfoot reports that “contemporary writers speak of the Helvidians as a party,” and J. B. Lightfoot files him in his conspectus under “sons of Joseph and Mary,” at the head of the Helvidian column beside Tertullian and the Antidicomarianites. On Lightfoot’s reckoning, Jerome’s answer to him is the earliest statement of the Hieronymian cousins-theory.

Claims & evidence

Helvidius’ thesis: the brethren are sons of Joseph and Mary

Helvidius’ thesis survives only in the treatise Jerome wrote against it. Jerome opens by naming his opponent and the occasion:

I was requested by certain of the brethren not long ago to reply to a pamphlet written by one Helvidius. I have deferred doing so, not because it is a difficult matter to maintain the truth and refute an ignorant boor…

— Jerome, Against Helvidius §1.1 The pamphlet’s contention, as the treatise reports it throughout, was that the Gospel “sisters” and “brethren” of the Lord showed that the Virgin “had subsequent issue” — children born to her and Joseph after Jesus — and that therefore virginity could claim no superiority over marriage. One fragment Jerome quotes in Helvidius’ own words holds that the two titles fall on the same people:

The same names are repeated by the Evangelists in another place, and the same persons are there brethren of the Lord and sons of Mary.

— Helvidius, as quoted in Jerome, Against Helvidius §15.2 Because his treatise is lost and every line of it reaches us only through the pen of the man answering it, Helvidius’ own words are marked ⚠ unverified; what is verified is that Jerome reports and answers this Helvidian position.

The proof-texts — “knew her not till” and “first-born son”

Helvidius rested the reading on two phrases in Matthew’s infancy narrative, and Jerome answers each in turn. From Matthew 1:25 — Joseph “knew her not till she had brought forth her son” — Helvidius argued that a man who “knew her not until” a point in time must have known her afterward, and that “before they came together” (Matthew 1:18) implies they did come together in the end:

the Evangelist would not have said before they came together if they were not to come together, for no one would use the phrase “before he dined” of a man who was not going to dine.

— the argument as Jerome states it, Against Helvidius §4.3 From the second phrase — Mary “brought forth her first-born son” (Luke 2:7) — Helvidius pressed a further inference:

From this he endeavours to show that the term first-born is inapplicable except to a person who has brothers, just as he is called only begotten who is the only son of his parents.

— Jerome, Against Helvidius §10.4 Jerome replies that Scripture uses “till” without implying a change after (as God says “I am with you till the end of the world”), and “first-born” is a legal term for the child that “openeth the womb,” applicable to an only child as much as to the eldest of many. The arguments themselves, as Jerome reports them, are Helvidius’.

The appeal to Tertullian and Victorinus, and the ranking of virginity

Helvidius cited two older Latin writers for the reading. The editors of the standard English edition summarize:

Helvidius maintained that the mention in the Gospels of the “sisters” and “brethren” of our Lord was proof that the Blessed Virgin had subsequent issue, and he supported his opinion by the writings of Tertullian and Victorinus. The outcome of his views was that virginity was ranked below matrimony.

— introduction to Jerome, Against Helvidius (Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, 2nd ser., vol. 6).5 On Tertullian Jerome replies that he was “not a member of the Church”; Victorinus of Pettau he claims for his own side, holding that Victorinus had meant kinsmen, not sons of Mary. (Tertullian’s own position is the subject of the disputed reading “Did Tertullian hold that Mary bore children after Jesus?”) Jerome devotes the treatise’s last chapters (§§19–22) to defending the superiority of the virgin state.5

Lightfoot on the Helvidians and on Jerome’s reply

J. B. Lightfoot gathers the notices of Helvidius’ following and of writers who held the same view in the same years:

Somewhere about the year 380 Helvidius, who resided in Rome, published a treatise in which he maintained that the Lord’s brethren were sons of Joseph and Mary. He seems to have succeeded in convincing a considerable number of persons, for contemporary writers speak of the Helvidians as a party. These views were moreover advocated by Bonosus, bishop of Sardica in Illyria, about the same time, and apparently also by Jovinianus a monk probably of Milan.

— J. B. Lightfoot, “The Brethren of the Lord,” §22.6 In the conspectus that closes his dissertation, Lightfoot sorts the witnesses under the reading each supports, and sets Helvidius in the column that bears his name:

A. Sons of Joseph and Mary: Tertullian; Helvidius; Bonosus; Jovinianus (?); Antidicomarianites.

— Lightfoot, conspectus of authorities.6 In reply to this treatise Jerome set out the Hieronymian cousins-theory, rather than the Epiphanian account (in which “brother” stands for Joseph’s sons by a former wife). Lightfoot judged that “no decisive instance can be produced of a writer holding Jerome’s view, before it was propounded by Jerome himself” in this book.6 Whether the cousin kinship is attested before Jerome is disputed (Florentine Bechtel; Josef Blinzler and John McHugh, as reported by Richard Bauckham); see “Did the cousin reading have witnesses before Jerome, and did Jerome later let it go?”.

Reception and context

  • Lost treatise, transmitted by an opponent. Helvidius wrote a book; not a sentence of it survives except as embedded in Jerome’s reply, which quotes it in order to answer it. Because the claim rests only on an opponent’s report, Helvidius’ teaching in his own voice is marked ⚠ unverified. What is attested is that Jerome answered this position, preserving its proof-texts (Matthew 1:18, 1:25; Luke 2:7) and its appeal to Tertullian and Victorinus.
  • Lightfoot on the infancy proof-texts. Lightfoot reads the “till” of Matthew 1:25 as “intended simply to show that Jesus was not begotten in the course of nature,” so that “while it denies any previous intercourse with her husband, it neither asserts nor implies any subsequent intercourse”; and “firstborn” as conveying to a Jew “not the birth of other children, but the special consecration of this one” (“Every male that openeth the womb,” Luke 2:23). He grants that “if the Evangelists had considered the perpetual virginity of the Lord’s mother a matter of such paramount importance as it was held to be in the fourth and following centuries, they would have avoided expressions which are at least ambiguous and might be taken to imply the contrary; but these expressions are not in themselves fatal to such a belief.”6
  • Dating. Lightfoot dates the treatise itself “about the year 380” (§22); the standard editors and Jerome’s own reply place the exchange “about a.d. 383,” when both men were at Rome under Damasus. The timeline slots Helvidius at c. 383 with the reply, just after the Apostolic Constitutions and immediately before Jerome.
  • Relation to the three theories. Helvidius is the Helvidian namesake — the brethren are sons of Joseph and Mary, and the perpetual virginity is denied. He held the same tenets as the Arabian Antidicomarianites, and Lightfoot files Tertullian in the same column; Augustine equated Helvidians and Antidicomarianites, though Lightfoot cautioned that “considerations of time and place alike resist this identification.” The Epiphanian witnesses (Origen, Eusebius, Hilary, Epiphanius) hold a different reading, and Jerome set out the Hieronymian cousins-theory in reply to him.
