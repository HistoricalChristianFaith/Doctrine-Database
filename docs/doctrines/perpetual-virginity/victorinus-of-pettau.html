Victorinus of Pettau on the perpetual virginity of Mary

Dates: c. 300 (Victorinus, bishop of Poetovio in Pannonia — Pettau, the modern Ptuj — the earliest Latin commentator on Scripture whose work survives, martyred under Diocletian c. 303–304) · Wikipedia
Summary: Victorinus was claimed by both sides of the later Roman controversy. Helvidius quoted him for the view that the brethren were Mary’s own sons; Jerome denied that reading and claimed him for the kinship sense. His own comment on the brethren does not survive, and his two extant works speak of Mary’s virginity in the conception without saying who the brethren were. J. B. Lightfoot classed him as Uncertain.

Claims & evidence

Helvidius’s appeal and Jerome’s reply

When Helvidius argued at Rome (c. 383) that the Lord’s brethren were Mary’s later-born children, he closed by producing two older authorities for his side: Tertullian and Victorinus of Pettau. Jerome, replying, answered the appeal to Tertullian by saying that he “did not belong to the Church,” and disputed the appeal to Victorinus, saying the bishop had meant “brother” in the sense of kinship:

Feeling himself to be a smatterer, he there produces Tertullian as a witness and quotes the words of Victorinus bishop of Petavium. Of Tertullian I say no more than that he did not belong to the Church. But as regards Victorinus, I assert what has already been proved from the Gospel—that he spoke of the brethren of the Lord not as being sons of Mary, but brethren in the sense I have explained, that is to say, brethren in point of kinship not by nature.

— Jerome, Against Helvidius 19 (§17 in Lightfoot’s citation).1 Helvidius cited Victorinus for the Helvidian reading, and Jerome denied that sense and claimed Victorinus for his own. The passage does not give Victorinus’s words: Jerome reports that Helvidius “quotes the words of Victorinus” but does not reproduce them himself.

Victorinus’s surviving works on Mary

The work of Victorinus that Helvidius and Jerome cited is lost, and his surviving works do not take up the identity of the brethren. Both speak of Mary’s virginity in the conception — the Commentary on the Apocalypse says the Son “has at this time taken a beginning from the Virgin,” and On the Creation of the World that “the Holy Spirit overflowed the Virgin Mary” — but neither says whether she bore other children, or whose sons the “brethren” were.

…He was, because with the Father He made all things, and has at this time taken a beginning from the Virgin; He is to come, because assuredly He will come to judgment.

— Victorinus, Commentary on the Apocalypse of the Blessed John (on Rev. 1:8).2 His statement on the brethren is known only at second hand, through the reports of Helvidius and Jerome, who read it in opposite senses; his own position on the point is ⚠ unverified. His extant works affirm the virginal conception and do not address the perpetual virginity or the brethren.

Lightfoot’s classification

J. B. Lightfoot (Anglican, 1865) drew an inference from the double claim:

Victorinus Petavionensis (about 300) was claimed by Helvidius as a witness in his own favour. Jerome denied this and put in a counterclaim. It may perhaps be inferred from this circumstance that Victorinus did little more than repeat the statements of the evangelists respecting the Lord’s brethren.

— J. B. Lightfoot, “The Brethren of the Lord,” §12.3 On Lightfoot’s inference, Victorinus named the brethren as the Gospels do, without deciding whose sons they were. Lightfoot files him under none of the three readings: in his conspectus of the early authorities Victorinus of Pettau stands under the heading Uncertain.3

Reception and context

  • Not the later Victorinus. This is Victorinus of Pettau (Poetovio), the martyr-bishop and exegete of c. 300 — not Marius Victorinus the Philosopher (c. 360), the Roman rhetorician and convert whose commentary on Galatians is a separate witness: taking the “but” of Galatians 1:19 as opposition rather than exception, he states that James the Lord’s brother was not one of the Twelve — “Cum autem fratrem dixit, apostolum negavit” (“when he called him a brother, he denied he was an apostle”). Lightfoot files the Philosopher, unlike the bishop, among the among the definite “James not one of the Twelve” witnessesldquo;James not one of the Twelveamong the definite “James not one of the Twelve” witnessesrdquo; witnesses.4
  • Relation to the three readings. Victorinus’s extant works do not choose among the Helvidian, Epiphanian, and Hieronymian readings, and his lost comment is claimed by Helvidius for the first and by Jerome for the kinship sense. In the same conspectus Lightfoot lists Hegesippus, the Clementine Homilies (with a query), and the Apostolic Constitutions among the authorities for “James the Just not one of the Twelve.”3
  • Jerome’s report and Lightfoot’s reading of it. Jerome reports Victorinus’s sense as brethren “in point of kinship not by nature”; he does not report that Victorinus identified the brethren as the sons of the Virgin’s sister, the identification Jerome himself made, and he grounds his reading of Victorinus on “what has already been proved from the Gospel.”1 Lightfoot took the exchange as part of his case that “Jerome claims no traditional support for his theory”: “Jerome in reply is obliged to concede him Tertullian… but denies him Victorinus,” and “no decisive instance can be produced of a writer holding Jerome’s view, before it was propounded by Jerome himself.”5 Whether the cousin reading had witnesses before Jerome is disputed; see “Did the cousin reading have witnesses before Jerome, and did Jerome later let it go?”
