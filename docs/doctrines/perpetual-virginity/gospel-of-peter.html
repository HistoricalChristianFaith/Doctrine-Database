The Gospel of Peter on the perpetual virginity of Mary

Dates: c. 150 (an apocryphal gospel “highly esteemed by the Docetae of the second century,” in J. B. Lightfoot’s words; already in circulation when Serapion of Antioch found it at Rhossus late in the second century, so “about the middle of the second century at the latest, and probably somewhat earlier” in J. B. Lightfoot’s dating) · Wikipedia
Summary: Alongside the Protevangelium of James, the Gospel of Peter is one of the two earliest named sources of the Epiphanian reading — that the “brethren of the Lord” were sons of Joseph by a former wife, so that Mary bore no child but Jesus. Nothing of the Gospel of Peter’s own text on this point survives: the single fragment recovered in modern times is a Passion narrative that says nothing of the brethren. The attribution rests wholly on Origen, who names “the Gospel according to Peter… or the Book of James” as the tradition on which holders of the Epiphanian account relied. This witness is therefore mediated: Origen’s report is firsthand and quotable; the Gospel of Peter’s wording is lost.

Claims & evidence

The testimony survives only through Origen’s report (Comm. Matt. x.17)

Commenting on “Is not this the carpenter’s son? is not his mother called Mary? and his brethren…” (Matthew 13:55), Origen († c. 253) records the two texts to which holders of the perpetual virginity appealed — the Gospel of Peter being the first he names:

But some say, basing it on a tradition in the Gospel according to Peter, as it is entitled, or “The Book of James,” that the brethren of Jesus were sons of Joseph by a former wife, whom he married before Mary. Now those who say so wish to preserve the honour of Mary in virginity to the end… And I think it in harmony with reason that Jesus was the first-fruit among men of the purity which consists in chastity, and Mary among women; for it were not pious to ascribe to any other than to her the first-fruit of virginity.

— Origen, Commentary on Matthew x.17, trans. John Patrick (Ante-Nicene Fathers, vol. 9).1 On Origen’s report, the “former wife” account was already attached to named texts in his day, texts Lightfoot dates to the second century. Origen sets that account against the view that would not preserve Mary’s virginity “to the end”; he does not mention a cousins account, which Jerome set out in Against Helvidius (c. 383). ⚠ unverified — the claim that the Gospel of Peter itself contained this cannot be checked against the text of that gospel, which does not survive on the point; it rests on Origen’s report (and Lightfoot’s relaying of it).

Dating and circulation of the Gospel of Peter

Lightfoot describes the gospel as esteemed by the Docetae; late in the second century Serapion, bishop of Antioch, found it in use at Rhossus in Cilicia and at first allowed it, withdrawing his sanction only when he judged some of its language to favour heresy — the episode preserved in a fragment of Serapion’s letter quoted by Eusebius.2 Because Serapion “speaks of this apocryphal work as if it had been long in circulation,” Lightfoot dates it “about the middle of the second century at the latest, and probably somewhat earlier” — contemporary with, or a little before, the Protevangelium, which he dates “probably not later than the middle of the second century.” Lightfoot places it at the head of his list of Epiphanian authorities.3

Reception and context

  • What survives, and what does not. The only substantial portion of the Gospel of Peter known today is the Akhmîm fragment, recovered from an Egyptian grave in 1886–87 — an account of the Passion and resurrection. It contains nothing about the infancy, about Joseph, or about the brethren of the Lord. Origen’s notice is the only surviving report that the gospel addressed the question, and his words cannot be checked against the source he cites.4
  • Named in one breath with the Protevangelium. Origen offers the two texts as alternatives (“the Gospel according to Peter… or the Book of James”), and Lightfoot lists them first and second among the Epiphanian authorities. The Protevangelium’s Joseph-the-widower narrative survives in full, and the two are read together here (see the Protevangelium detail page).
  • Relation to the three theories. With the Protevangelium, this is among the earliest attestations named for the Epiphanian view, which holds Mary’s perpetual virginity and makes Joseph the father of an earlier family. Origen’s report of it does not mention a cousins account (the Hieronymian view) and sets it against the reading that the brethren were Mary’s own later-born children (the Helvidian view). Whether this early attestation preserves historical memory of Jesus’ family is taken up at the disputed reading “Does the second-century stepbrother tradition preserve historical memory of Jesus’ family?”
