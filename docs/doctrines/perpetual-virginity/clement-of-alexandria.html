Clement of Alexandria on the perpetual virginity of Mary

Dates: c. 200 (the Alexandrian teacher flourished c. 190–202, when the Paedagogus and Stromateis were composed; he died c. 215. The Hypotyposeis, from which the note on Jude survives, belongs to this maturity) · Wikipedia
Summary: Clement’s note on Jude is the earliest extant statement of the Epiphanian solution by a named father in his own voice — that the “brethren of the Lord” were sons of Joseph, older step-relations of Jesus, not children of Mary. Commenting on the Epistle of Jude, he explains why its author calls himself “brother of James” rather than brother of the Lord: Jude was “one of the sons of Joseph,” and out of reverence forbore to claim the nearer title. The remark survives only in a Latin abridgement of his lost Hypotyposeis made by Cassiodorus in the sixth century. J. B. Lightfoot argued that the statement about Jude’s relationship must be Clement’s own, since Cassiodorus, a Latin churchman, would have held the Hieronymian view; the reliability of the Latin is discussed on a separate page. Of a second extract, preserved by Eusebius — Clement’s remark that “there were two Jameses” — Lightfoot wrote that Clement “neither states nor implies that there were two only.” Lightfoot called the note on Jude “explicit” and placed Clement after the Gospel of Peter and Protevangelium in his roll of the “sons of Joseph by a former wife” authorities.

Claims & evidence

Jude was “one of the sons of Joseph” — the brethren are Joseph’s, not Mary’s

In his Adumbrationes — brief notes on the Catholic Epistles, surviving in Cassiodorus’ Latin — Clement opens his comments on Jude by explaining the epistle’s self-designation. Why does Jude, kin to the Lord, style himself only “brother of James”? Because, Clement answers, he was a son of Joseph, and modesty kept him from claiming the Lord as his brother:

Jude, who wrote the Catholic Epistle, the brother of the sons of Joseph, and very religious, whilst knowing the near relationship of the Lord, yet did not say that he himself was His brother. But what said he? “Jude, a servant of Jesus Christ,”—of Him as Lord; but “the brother of James.” For this is true; he was His brother, (the son) of Joseph.

— Clement of Alexandria, Comments on the Epistle of Jude.1 The reasoning is Epiphanian: Jude and James are “the sons of Joseph,” brothers of the Lord in the household sense but not His mother’s children, so that Jude can rightly be called “brother of James” and only in a looser way “brother of the Lord.” Lightfoot renders the same passage “Jude… being one of the sons of Joseph and [the Lord’s] brother… nevertheless did not say he was His brother,” and calls the testimony “explicit” — Clement “puts forward the Epiphanian solution.”2

The “two Jameses” extract in Eusebius

Beside the Latin note stands a Greek fragment Eusebius drew from the sixth and seventh books of the same Hypotyposeis: Clement there says that after the resurrection the Lord gave the gnosis to James the Just, John, and Peter, and adds, “Now there are two Jameses: one called the Just, who was thrown down from the pinnacle of the temple… and another who was beheaded.”3 Because Clement counts “two” — James the Just (the Lord’s brother) and James the son of Zebedee — and omits James the son of Alphaeus, the fragment can be read as favouring the Hieronymian view, which identifies the Lord’s brother James with the apostle son of Alphaeus; Lightfoot addresses that reading without naming its holders (⚠ unverified). Lightfoot’s answer: “Clement says that there were two of the name of James, but he neither states nor implies that there were two only. His sole object was to distinguish the son of Zebedee from the Lord’s brother; and the son of Alphaeus… did not occur to his mind when he penned this sentence.” There is, he adds, “nothing which contradicts the Latin extract,” and he takes the statement on Jude, that the brethren are sons of Joseph, as Clement’s view.2

“Found, when examined, to be a virgin” — the virginity in the birth (Stromateis VII.16)

Clement’s other passage on Mary’s virginity concerns not the brethren but the manner of the birth. In the seventh book of the Stromateis — extant in Greek, not in Cassiodorus’ Latin — he reports, as an image for how Scripture yields truth while remaining “virgin” and uncorrupted, that Mary was held to have remained a virgin through the birth itself:

But, as appears, many even down to our own time regard Mary, on account of the birth of her child, as having been in the puerperal state, although she was not. For some say that, after she brought forth, she was found, when examined, to be a virgin.

… “And she brought forth, and yet brought not forth,” says the Scripture; as having conceived of herself, and not from conjunction.

— Clement of Alexandria, Stromateis VII.16.4 This is the virginitas in partu — Mary’s virginity unimpaired through the delivery (“found, when examined, to be a virgin”) — a claim distinct from the post partum perpetual virginity (no marital relations, no later children) that the note on Jude carries. Clement reports the belief as held by “many even down to our own time,” c. 200, at Alexandria, and introduces it with “as appears” and “some say.” The passage concerns the birth, not the identity of the brethren.

Text and reception

  • The textual-reliability question. Because the note on Jude reaches us only through Cassiodorus’ Latin, from which he says he removed what he thought unsafe, the reliability of Clement’s witness has been questioned; Thorncrown Ministries (2017), quoting a caution in Juniper Carol’s edited Mariology (1957) that was written there by Philip J. Donnelly, S.J., attach that caveat to the Stromateis VII.16 passage above. Donnelly attaches the caution to the Latin Adumbrationes, holds that there is “no indication that in the matter of Mary’s virginity there was any emendation of Clement’s own words,” and cites the Stromata passage separately from the Greek.4 The question is set out at the disputed reading on Clement’s textual reliability.
  • The statement is Clement’s, not Cassiodorus’. The note on Jude reaches us only through the Adumbrationes, a Latin abridgement that Cassiodorus (c. 485–585) says he made of Clement’s notes on the Catholic Epistles, “purging” what he thought unsafe. That raises the question whether the Epiphanian remark could be the translator’s. Lightfoot holds that it cannot: “the statement about the relationship of Jude must be Clement’s own and cannot have been inserted by Cassiodorus, since Cassiodorus in common with the Latin Church would naturally hold the Hieronymian hypothesis” — that is, would have made the brethren cousins, not Joseph’s sons. On Lightfoot’s argument, a Latin editor of that view would have had reason to remove such a line, not to add it.2
  • Placement on the timeline. Clement stands in Lightfoot’s conspectus of the “sons of Joseph by a former wife” authorities after the Gospel of Peter and the Protevangelium, ahead of Origen, Eusebius, Gregory of Nyssa, Epiphanius, and the Eastern churches.2 His note is the earliest extant statement of the reading by a named father, as against an anonymous apocryphon, in his own voice. He wrote about a decade before Tertullian, whom Lightfoot files among those who made the brethren sons of Joseph and Mary (a classification that is disputed; see the disputed reading on Tertullian). Origen, also of Alexandria, later gives the Epiphanian reading and attaches it to the Gospel of Peter and the Book of James.
  • Character of the note. Clement’s note records that a teacher of the Alexandrian church, c. 200, took the brethren to be Joseph’s sons. He gives no argument from the Gospel text, only the identification, which he shares with the second-century infancy apocrypha. Whether that shared tradition preserves historical memory of Jesus’ family is a separate question; see the disputed reading on the stepbrother tradition.
