Thomas Aquinas on the perpetual virginity of Mary

Dates: c. 1270 (the Summa Theologiae, Third Part, q. 28, written c. 1271–1273; the Lectura super Matthaeum reported c. 1269–1270; Thomas Aquinas, 1225 – 1274, Dominican friar and doctor of the Latin schools) · Wikipedia
Summary: Aquinas treats the perpetual virginity in scholastic form. In the Summa Theologiae he devotes a whole question (III, q. 28) to Mary’s virginity and, in its third article, rejects the Helvidian reading — “without any hesitation we must abhor the error of Helvidius” — and gives four reasons: it derogates from Christ’s perfection as the Only-begotten, insults the Holy Ghost whose “shrine” was the virginal womb, dishonours Mary’s dignity, and imputes gross presumption to Joseph. A fourth article (a. 4) asks whether Mary vowed her virginity, and reads Luke 1:34 as implying a vow, first conditional and later absolute, taken in view of the betrothal. On the identity of the “brethren” he is Hieronymian: following Jerome he makes them the Lord’s cousins, not Joseph’s sons by a former wife, and holds that Joseph too “remained a virgin” — holding the virginity of Mary and of Joseph alike.

Claims & evidence

Four reasons against Helvidius (Summa III, q. 28, a. 3)

The article asks “whether Christ’s Mother remained a virgin after His birth,” and Aquinas answers against Helvidius with four arguments — from Christ, from the Holy Ghost, from Mary, and from Joseph:

Without any hesitation we must abhor the error of Helvidius, who dared to assert that Christ’s Mother, after His Birth, was carnally known by Joseph, and bore other children. For, in the first place, this is derogatory to Christ’s perfection: for as He is in His Godhead the Only-Begotten of the Father, being thus His Son in every respect perfect, so it was becoming that He should be the Only-begotten son of His Mother, as being her perfect offspring.

Secondly, this error is an insult to the Holy Ghost, whose “shrine” was the virginal womb… wherein He had formed the flesh of Christ: wherefore it was unbecoming that it should be desecrated by intercourse with man.

Thirdly, this is derogatory to the dignity and holiness of God’s Mother: for thus she would seem to be most ungrateful, were she not content with such a Son; and were she, of her own accord, by carnal intercourse to forfeit that virginity which had been miraculously preserved in her.

Fourthly, it would be tantamount to an imputation of extreme presumption in Joseph, to assume that he attempted to violate her whom by the angel’s revelation he knew to have conceived by the Holy Ghost.

We must therefore simply assert that the Mother of God, as she was a virgin in conceiving Him and a virgin in giving Him birth, so she remained a virgin ever afterwards.

— Summa Theologiae, Third Part, q. 28, a. 3.1 The four heads are Christ’s only-begottenness (the argument John of Damascus had drawn from “firstborn”), the sanctity of the womb-as-temple, Mary’s dignity, and Joseph’s reverence; the last two are moral rather than exegetical arguments.

The “till” and “firstborn” of Matthew 1:25

Within the same article Aquinas answers the two texts urged against the doctrine, following Jerome. To “knew her not till she had brought forth” he answers, with Jerome, that the Scriptural “until” sometimes marks a fixed term and sometimes an indefinite one:

Jerome, however, grants that this is to be understood of knowledge by intercourse; but he observes that “before” or “until” has a twofold sense in Scripture. For sometimes it indicates a fixed time… On the other hand, it sometimes indicates an indefinite time, as in Psalm 122:2: “Our eyes are unto the Lord our God, until He have mercy on us”; from which it is not to be gathered that our eyes are turned from God as soon as His mercy has been obtained… the evangelist says that the Mother of God was not known by her husband until she gave birth, that we may be given to understand that still less did he know her afterwards.

— Summa Theologiae, Third Part, q. 28, a. 3.2 His Commentary on Matthew presses the same point at greater length and repeats Jerome’s a fortiori against Helvidius (“Elvidius”): “if… a warning in sleep was strong enough to keep him from knowing Mary, how much more the knowledge of the angels and the adoration of the shepherds and the Magi?” — and reads “firstborn” as Jerome did, of “all those whom others did not precede,” not of a first among later siblings.3 The same answer is given on this timeline by Chrysostom, Chromatius, and John of Damascus.

The brethren as cousins — the Hieronymian reading

On the identity of the “brethren of the Lord,” Aquinas follows Jerome against the Epiphanian “former wife” account, and does so explicitly. Still in q. 28, a. 3 he weighs the two options and adopts the cousins-reading:

Some, as Jerome says on Matthew 12:49–50, “suppose that the brethren of the Lord were Joseph’s sons by another wife. But we understand the brethren of the Lord to be not sons of Joseph, but cousins of the Saviour, the sons of Mary, His Mother’s sister.” For “Scripture speaks of brethren in four senses”… Wherefore the brethren of the Lord are so called, not by birth, as being born of the same mother; but by relationship, as being blood-relations of His. But Joseph, as Jerome says, is rather to be believed to have remained a virgin, “since he is not said to have had another wife,” and “a holy man does not live otherwise than chastely.”

— Summa Theologiae, Third Part, q. 28, a. 3.4 His Commentary on Matthew repeats the judgment on the Nazareth pericope in his own voice: of “his brethren James and Joseph, Simon and Jude,” he writes, “Helvidius understood these to be sons of Mary. But this is false; rather they were his cousins. Or they are called brethren because they were of the kindred of Joseph” — citing Abraham’s calling Lot, his nephew, “brother” (Genesis 13:8) for the loose kinship-sense of the word.5

Whether the Mother of God took a vow of virginity (Summa III, q. 28, a. 4)

A separate article asks whether Mary’s reply to Gabriel — “How shall this be, seeing I know not a man?” (Luke 1:34) — presupposes a vow. Aquinas answers yes, distinguishing two stages in view of the betrothal. His sed contra is Augustine:

Augustine says (De Sanct. Virg. iv): “Mary answered the announcing angel: How shall this be done, because I know not man? She would not have said this unless she had already vowed her virginity to God.”

The body affirms that it was fitting for Mary’s virginity to be “consecrated to God by vow,” yet refuses an absolute vow sworn before the espousals — because while the Law was in force men and women were bound to the duty of leaving a posterity:

It was therefore fitting that her virginity should be consecrated to God by vow… the Mother of God is not believed to have taken an absolute vow of virginity, before being espoused to Joseph, although she desired to do so, yet yielding her own will to God’s judgment. Afterwards, however, having taken a husband, according as the custom of the time required, together with him she took a vow of virginity.

— Summa Theologiae, Third Part, q. 28, a. 4.6 The Reply to Objection 1 sharpens the sequence: the vow was at first conditional — “the Mother of God did not vow virginity absolutely, but under the condition that it were pleasing to God” — and only later made unconditional: “When, however, she knew that it was acceptable to God, she made the vow absolute, before the angel’s Annunciation.”7 On this construction the vow was conditional before the espousals, because the Law seemed to require leaving a posterity, and absolute only once Mary knew it was acceptable to God, taken together with Joseph. John Calvin later rejected the vow reading of Luke 1:34 as “unfounded and altogether absurd”; see his entry.

Reception and context

  • Classification: Hieronymian. By making the brethren cousins (not step-brothers) and holding that Joseph “remained a virgin” too, Aquinas takes the Hieronymian position, as Jerome and Augustine had before him in the West. He reports the Epiphanian “former wife” account (“Some… suppose…”) and sets it aside.
  • Placement on the timeline. Aquinas is the timeline’s scholastic witness. Where Theophylact, two centuries earlier, had combined the two readings that hold Mary ever-virgin, Aquinas adopts the Hieronymian one and defends it by ordered argument.
  • The four-reasons form. The argument against Helvidius bears on the perpetual virginity as a whole, not on any one proof-text: it does not turn on the grammar of “until” or on who the brethren were, but on the fittingness (convenientia) of a virginity outlasting the birth, argued from the perfection of Christ, the sanctity of the Spirit’s dwelling, and the honour due to Mary and Joseph. It is a theological rather than an exegetical argument.
