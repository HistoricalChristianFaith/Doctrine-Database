The Antidicomarianites on the perpetual virginity of Mary

Dates: c. 370 (an obscure sect of the Arabian churches in the latter half of the fourth century, active before Epiphanius wrote his pastoral letter against them — incorporated as Panarion (Haereses) 78 into his catalogue of heresies, itself finished about 377. No writing of the sect survives; the group is known only from Epiphanius, whose name for it means “opponents of Mary,” and from later notices dependent on him) · Wikipedia
Summary: The Antidicomarianites were a fourth-century Arabian sect that held the Helvidian reading of the brethren — that after Jesus’ birth Mary bore other children to her husband Joseph, so that she was not ever-virgin. They are the one party, as against individual writers, that the timeline names for the denial of the perpetual virginity in the East. They left no book: what is known of them comes from Epiphanius of Salamis, who wrote against them and gave them their name (one of the “terms of ridicule invented by their enemies,” in Lightfoot’s phrase), so the sect’s own voice survives only in the report of its opponent — and their own words are, accordingly, ⚠ unverified. J. B. Lightfoot files them in his conspectus under the “sons of Joseph and Mary” heading, beside Tertullian and Helvidius; a reaction the other way, in the same region, produced the Collyridians, who paid divine honours to the Virgin.

Claims & evidence

A party holding that Mary bore children to Joseph after Jesus

The sect’s tenet is stated only by the writer who answered it. In the letter that became Panarion 78, Epiphanius reports the claim he means to answer — that the Lord’s mother did not remain a virgin but lived as Joseph’s wife after the Nativity:

For I have heard from someone that certain persons are venturing to say that she had marital relations after the Savior’s birth. And I am not surprised. The ignorance of persons who do not know the sacred scriptures well and have not consulted histories, always turns them to one thing after another, and distracts anyone who wants to track down something about the truth out of his own head. To begin with, when the Virgin was entrusted to Joseph — lots having compelled her to take this step — she was not entrusted to him for marriage, since he was a widower. He was called her husband because of the Law, but it plainly follows from the Jewish tradition that the Virgin was not entrusted to him for matrimony.

— Epiphanius, Panarion 78.7.1 (on Matthew 13:55 // Mark 6:3).1 The “certain persons” are the Antidicomarianites, and their position — “she had marital relations after the Savior’s birth” — is the Helvidian position on the perpetual virginity. Epiphanius’ own answer is the Epiphanian solution: Joseph was already a widower with children when the Virgin was “entrusted” to him, so that he was her husband only “because of the Law” and the brethren were his sons by the former marriage. Because the sect wrote nothing that survives, this summary by its opponent is the only evidence, and the Antidicomarianites’ teaching in their own words remains ⚠ unverified.

Known only through Epiphanius — and the Collyridian reaction

J. B. Lightfoot’s notice gathers what little is recoverable, and pairs the sect with its mirror-image opposite in the same Arabian churches:

The Antidicomarianites, an obscure Arabian sect in the latter half of the fourth century, maintained that the Lord’s mother bore children to her husband Joseph. These opinions seem to have produced a reaction, or to have been themselves reactionary, for we read about the same time of a sect called Collyridians, likewise in Arabia, who going to the opposite extreme paid divine honours to the Virgin.

— J. B. Lightfoot, “The Brethren of the Lord,” §20.2 Epiphanius came forward against them, Lightfoot adds, “not very long before Jerome wrote in defence of the perpetual virginity… against the Helvidians at Rome,” denouncing them in the “elaborate pastoral letter” he later folded into the Panarion; and Lightfoot notes that, discussing the question at length there, Epiphanius “never once alludes to the Hieronymian account,” the cousins-theory set out by Jerome in Against Helvidius (c. 383).2

Lightfoot’s classification

In the conspectus that closes his dissertation, Lightfoot sorts every witness under the reading he took it to support, and sets the Antidicomarianites with the deniers of the perpetual virginity — the Helvidian column:

A. Sons of Joseph and Mary: Tertullian; Helvidius; Bonosus; Jovinianus (?); Antidicomarianites.

— Lightfoot, conspectus of authorities.3 Augustine, cataloguing heresies a generation later, treated the Antidicomarianites (he spells it Antidicomaritae) as the same party as Jerome’s Helvidians: “They held the same tenets, it is true,” Lightfoot allows — but he holds that “there seems to have been otherwise no connexion between the two,” and that “considerations of time and place alike resist this identification.” Augustine thus treats the Roman Helvidius and the Arabian sect as one party; Lightfoot treats them as two, reaching the same conclusion independently.3

Sources and context

  • Attestation through an opponent. The sect produced no surviving text; every particular — its region, its date, its name — comes from Epiphanius, who wrote against it. The label “Antidicomarianites” (“opponents of Mary”) is, as Lightfoot notes, one of the “terms of ridicule invented by their enemies.” The sect’s teaching in its own voice is therefore marked ⚠ unverified; what is attested firsthand is that Epiphanius reports and answers this Helvidian position.
  • Relation to the three theories. As Epiphanius reports them, the Antidicomarianites held the Helvidian reading — the brethren sons of Joseph and Mary — and so denied the perpetual virginity. On the timeline they stand beside the Epiphanian witnesses Origen, Eusebius, and Hilary; Lightfoot files Tertullian in the same column as the sect, a classification that is disputed (see the disputed reading on Tertullian). Epiphanius’ letter against them sets out the Epiphanian solution at length (“in which he explains his views at length,” in Lightfoot’s words).2
  • Not the Collyridians. The same passage names a contrary Arabian sect, the Collyridians (Panarion 79), women who offered cakes (kollyrides) to Mary as to a goddess. Epiphanius writes against both — the one, in his view, for dishonouring the Virgin, the other for deifying her; the Collyridians bear on the honours paid to Mary, not on the brethren question, and are noted here only because Lightfoot pairs the two as reaction and counter-reaction.
