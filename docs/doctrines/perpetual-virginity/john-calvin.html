John Calvin on the perpetual virginity of Mary

Dates: c. 1562 (Sermon on Matthew 1:22–25, pub. 1562; treated also in the Harmony of the Evangelists; John Calvin, 1509 – 1564, the Genevan Reformer) · Wikipedia
Summary: A second magisterial Reformer on the timeline, whose statements are more guarded than Luther’s. Expounding Matthew 1:25 — both in his Harmony of the Evangelists and in a Geneva sermon on Matthew 1:22–25 — Calvin denies that the text decides the question against the perpetual virginity. The two Helvidian inferences — that “first-born” implies later-born children and that Joseph “knew her not till” she gave birth implies he knew her after — he rejects: the “first-born” marks only that Jesus was born of a virgin, and the “till” is “limited to that very time,” the evangelist reporting nothing whatever of what followed. From the pulpit he is sharper, calling the suggestion that Mary “had other children… folly” (in an English rendering of the sermon whose translator is not named). On Matthew 13:55 he takes “brothers” “agreeably to the Hebrew idiom, to denote any relatives whatever” and says that “Helvidius displayed excessive ignorance in concluding that Mary must have had many sons, because Christ’s brothers are sometimes mentioned”; his commentary on John 7:3 glosses the Hebrew usage of “brethren” as including “all cousins and other relations.” He is not filed here under any of the three readings of the brethren.

Claims & evidence

“No just and well-grounded inference” — the Harmony on Matthew 1:25

In the Harmony of the Evangelists Calvin names Helvidius’ construction of the verse and sets it aside by denying that the text speaks to the later question. “First-born” establishes the virgin birth and nothing more; the “till” fixes only the time before the birth:

And knew her not. This passage afforded the pretext for great disturbances, which were introduced into the Church, at a former period, by Helvidius. The inference he drew from it was, that Mary remained a virgin no longer than till her first birth, and that afterwards she had other children by her husband. Jerome, on the other hand, earnestly and copiously defended Mary’s perpetual virginity. Let us rest satisfied with this, that no just and well-grounded inference can be drawn from these words of the Evangelist, as to what took place after the birth of Christ. He is called first-born; but it is for the sole purpose of informing us that he was born of a virgin. It is said that Joseph knew her not till she had brought forth her first-born son: but this is limited to that very time. What took place afterwards, the historian does not inform us… Certainly, no man will ever raise a question on this subject, except from curiosity; and no man will obstinately keep up the argument, except from an extreme fondness for disputation.

— Harmony of the Evangelists, on Matthew 1:25.1 In these words Calvin does not assert the perpetual virginity as Aquinas or Jerome do. He says the Evangelist leaves the later question open and reproves those who press it from this verse: he rejects reading later children out of the text, and does not read the perpetual virginity into it.

“What folly this is!” — the 1562 sermon on Matthew 1:22–25

Preaching on the same passage, Calvin speaks more sharply than in the commentary. He rebukes those who take Matthew 1:25 to mean Mary bore other children, and again explains “first-born” as a term of precedence that implies no second child:

There have been certain folk who have wished to suggest… that the Virgin Mary had other children than the Son of God, and that Joseph then dwelt with her later; but what folly this is! For the gospel writer did not wish to record what happened afterwards; he simply wished to make clear Joseph’s obedience… And besides this Our Lord Jesus Christ is called the first-born. This is not because there was a second or a third, but because the gospel writer is paying regard to the precedence. Scripture speaks thus of naming the first-born whether or not there was any question of the second.

— Sermon on Matthew 1:22–25 (1562); English rendering ⚠ unverified.2 The two arguments — that the Scriptural “till” states a terminus without implying a change after it, and that “first-born” denotes precedence, not the first of several — are also the two Jerome makes against Helvidius.

“Any relatives whatever” — the Harmony on Matthew 13:55

Commenting on the Nazarenes’ question at Matthew 13:55, “Is not this the carpenter’s son?”, Calvin turns to the brothers named in the verse:

The word brothers, we have formerly mentioned, is employed, agreeably to the Hebrew idiom, to denote any relatives whatever; and, accordingly, Helvidius displayed excessive ignorance in concluding that Mary must have had many sons, because Christ’s brothers are sometimes mentioned.

— Harmony of the Evangelists, on Matthew 13:55.3 The translator’s note at this place refers the reader to Jerome’s reply to Helvidius and to Calvin’s earlier allusion to their controversy at Matthew 1:25. Here Calvin addresses the brethren directly: the word, he says, does not by itself show that Mary had other sons. He does not say whose sons the brethren were.

“Unfounded and altogether absurd” — the Harmony on Luke 1:34 (against the vow)

Calvin also takes up Luke 1:34 — Mary’s “How shall this be, seeing I know not a man?” Where Augustine, Gregory of Nyssa and, later, Aquinas read her question as the trace of a vow of virginity, Calvin rejects that inference, on the grounds that a standing vow of continence would make the betrothal itself an act of bad faith, and that it rests on an institution he says did not exist:

The conjecture which some have drawn from these words, that she had formed a vow of perpetual virginity, is unfounded and altogether absurd. She would, in that case, have committed treachery by allowing herself to be united to a husband, and would have poured contempt on the holy covenant of marriage… Besides, it is an idle and unfounded supposition that a monastic life existed among the Jews.

He gives his own account of Mary’s question: wonder at what has been announced:

the greatness or rather majesty of the subject made so powerful an impression on the virgin, that all her senses were bound and locked up in astonishment… this question was not so contrary to faith, because it arose rather from admiration than from distrust.

— Harmony of the Evangelists, on Luke 1:34.4 Calvin thus rejects the vow reading of Luke 1:34 while also rejecting the Helvidian reading of Matthew 1:25.

“Under the word brethren” — the commentary on John 7:3

Commenting on “his brethren” at John 7:3, he takes the word in its extended Hebrew sense:

Under the word brethren the Hebrews include all cousins and other relations, whatever may be the degree of affinity.

— Commentary on the Gospel of John, on John 7:3.5 The note states the lexical premise of the cousin-reading of adelphos — that “brother” in Hebrew usage ranges over cousins and kin — in a note on the Lord’s “brethren”; the same premise is used by the cousin (Hieronymian) reading. He states it as a gloss and does not say whose sons the brethren were, where Luther writes that “‘brothers’ really mean ‘cousins’.”

Reception and context

  • Calvin and the three readings. Calvin’s Matthean texts answer the Helvidian inference from Matthew 1:25 and from the brothers of Matthew 13:55 without saying whose sons the “brethren of the Lord” were; in the Harmony he holds that “no just and well-grounded inference can be drawn” from Matthew 1:25 “as to what took place after the birth of Christ,” and on Matthew 13:55 that “brothers” denotes “any relatives whatever.” His commentary on John 7:3 notes that “under the word brethren the Hebrews include all cousins and other relations,” a lexical premise the cousin (Hieronymian) reading also uses; he does not there name the brethren’s parents or choose between the cousin and the Epiphanian (Joseph’s sons by a former wife) accounts. He is not filed here under any of the three readings.
  • The cautious stance. Calvin is more guarded than Luther or Aquinas. Where Luther calls the Helvidian reading “babble… without justification” and Aquinas “abhor[s] the error of Helvidius,” Calvin’s point is that the text does not decide the question — “no just and well-grounded inference can be drawn… as to what took place after the birth of Christ.” He says no one will raise the question “except from curiosity,” or keep up the argument “except from an extreme fondness for disputation,” and from the pulpit calls the suggestion that Mary had other children “folly.” His texts reject the Helvidian inference from Matthew 1:25 and do not rest the perpetual virginity on that verse.
  • Calvin among the Reformers. Luther and Huldrych Zwingli, also on this timeline, affirm the perpetual virginity; Calvin’s texts reject the Helvidian inference and, in the sermon, the suggestion of later children.
