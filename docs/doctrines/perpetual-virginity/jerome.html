Jerome on the perpetual virginity of Mary

Dates: c. 383 (the date of the treatise; Jerome, c. 347–420, was then a young man at Rome under Pope Damasus, and wrote The Perpetual Virginity of Blessed Mary, Against Helvidius in reply to Helvidius the same year) · Wikipedia
Summary: Jerome is the eponym of the Hieronymian reading of the brethren — that the “brethren of the Lord” were His maternal cousins, sons of “Mary of Clopas” the Virgin’s sister, so that “brother” means kinsman and the perpetual virginity of Mary and Joseph alike is preserved. He set out the theory in c. 383 in his reply to Helvidius, who had argued the brethren were sons of Joseph and Mary; rather than the Epiphanian solution (that they were Joseph’s sons by a former wife), Jerome gave a third reading that left both Mary and Joseph ever-virgin. He built it on the wide use of “brother” in Scripture and on identifying the “Mary the mother of James and Joses” of the passion narratives with the “Mary of Clopas” whom John sets beside the Virgin at the cross and calls her sister. J. B. Lightfoot, who named the view after him, filed Jerome as its earliest witness; whether the reading had earlier roots, and whether Jerome later let it go, is disputed (see the reading on its antiquity). Lightfoot also records that, supported by Augustine, it “won its way to general acceptance in the Latin Church.”

Claims & evidence

The cousins-theory — brethren as sons of “Mary of Clopas,” the Virgin’s sister

Jerome’s hypothesis rests on an identification of the several Marys named at the cross and tomb. John 19:25 sets beside the Virgin “his mother’s sister, Mary the wife of Clopas”; the Synoptic passion-narratives name “Mary the mother of James the less and of Joses.” Jerome takes these to be one woman — the Virgin’s sister — and so makes her sons James and Joses the Lord’s cousins, not His brothers:

The only conclusion is that the Mary who is described as the mother of James the less was the wife of Alphaeus and sister of Mary the Lord’s mother, the one who is called by John the Evangelist “Mary of Clopas,” whether after her father, or kindred, or for some other reason.

— Jerome, Against Helvidius §15.1 He then states the reading:

Now here we have the explanation of what I am endeavouring to show, how it is that the sons of Mary, the sister of our Lord’s mother, who though not formerly believers afterwards did believe, can be called brethren of the Lord.

— Jerome, Against Helvidius §16.1 Jerome does not press this identification to certainty: he grants he cannot say why the woman is called “of Clopas,” and declines to insist — “contentiosum funem non traho,” he writes (§15), ‘I do not pull the rope of contention’ — on whether “Mary the wife of Clopas and Mary the mother of James and Joses were different women, provided it is clearly understood that Mary the mother of James and Joses was not the same person as the Lord’s mother.” Lightfoot cites this hesitation in his critique (see below).

“Four kinds of brethren” — the philological argument

To license “brother” = “cousin,” Jerome lays out the range of the word in Scripture, distinguishing four senses (in his Latin: natura, gente, cognatione, affectu):

In Holy Scripture there are four kinds of brethren—by nature, race, kindred, love. Instances of brethren by nature are Esau and Jacob, the twelve patriarchs, Andrew and Peter, James and John.

— Jerome, Against Helvidius §16.1 The brethren of the Lord, he argues, belong to the third class, kinship — as Scripture calls a nephew a “brother”:

Certainly Lot was not Abraham’s brother, but the son of Abraham’s brother Aram.

— Jerome, Against Helvidius §16 (citing Genesis 13:8; he adds Jacob called “brother” of his uncle Laban, Genesis 29:15).1 Augustine later uses the Lot parallel in the same way. Hegesippus, earlier, uses the Greek word for cousin (anepsios) of Symeon son of Clopas while calling James and Jude the Lord’s brothers.

The virginity of Joseph too

Where the Epiphanian solution kept Joseph a widower and father of an earlier family, Jerome’s reading gives Joseph no prior children, and he asserts the virginity not of Mary only but of Joseph:

You say that Mary did not continue a virgin: I claim still more, that Joseph himself on account of Mary was a virgin, so that from a virgin wedlock a virgin son was born.

— Jerome, Against Helvidius §21.2 Lightfoot writes that “as he boasted, he asserted the virginity not of Mary only but of Joseph also,” and argues that this feature would have commended the theory to earlier ascetic writers such as Epiphanius, had they known it.

Jerome’s appeal to earlier writers

Helvidius had claimed the older authority of Tertullian and Victorinus of Pettau — and, as Lightfoot observed, Jerome “is obliged to concede him Tertullian, whose authority he invalidates as ‘not a member of the Church,’ but denies him Victorinus” (Jerome reads Victorinus’ brethren as kin “not by nature”). Jerome replies by setting Tertullian aside and naming fathers who, he says, “held these same views”:

Of Tertullian I say no more than that he did not belong to the Church.

Might I not array against you the whole series of ancient writers? Ignatius, Polycarp, Irenaeus, Justin Martyr, and many other apostolic and eloquent men, who against Ebion, Theodotus of Byzantium, and Valentinus, held these same views, and wrote volumes replete with wisdom.

— Jerome, Against Helvidius §19.3 Lightfoot reads these names as authorities for the virginity, not for the cousins-identification: “in support of his own account of the relationship he cannot, or at least does not, name a single writer; he simply offers it as a critical deduction from the statements of Scripture.” Whether the cousin kinship is attested before Jerome is disputed (Florentine Bechtel; Josef Blinzler and John McHugh, as reported by Richard Bauckham); see “Did the cousin reading have witnesses before Jerome, and did Jerome later let it go?”.

Later restatements

Nine years later, Jerome restated the reading in his catalogue of Christian writers:

James, who is called the brother of the Lord, surnamed the Just, the son of Joseph by another wife, as some think, but, as appears to me, the son of Mary sister of the mother of our Lord of whom John makes mention in his book, after our Lord’s passion at once ordained by the apostles bishop of Jerusalem…

— Jerome, Lives of Illustrious Men 2 (392).4 Earlier, expounding Galatians 1:19, he had declined to re-argue the point:

I remember that while I was in Rome, I published a book on the perpetual virginity of Saint Mary under the urging of the brothers. In this matter, I had to argue for a long time about those who are called the brothers of the Lord. Therefore, we should be satisfied with whatever we have written. Now let it suffice that, because of his outstanding character and incomparable faith and wisdom, he was called the Lord’s brother, not in an ordinary sense.

— Jerome, Commentary on Galatians 1:19 (c. 387).5 Lightfoot read the works in chronological order — vague in 387, firm again in 392 and in the Commentary on Matthew (398), but in the Letter to Hedibia (c. 406–7) treating the “keystone” identification “with such indifference” — and asked whether Jerome, long resident in Bethlehem and steeped in the Palestinian tradition, had at last relaxed his hold on his own hypothesis (see Notes).

Jerome on the shut gate of Ezekiel 44

Besides the argument about the brethren, Jerome twice reads the closed east gate of Ezekiel’s temple vision — Ezekiel 44:1–2, “This gate shall be shut… because the Lord, the God of Israel, hath entered in by it” — as a positive figure of Mary, whose womb the Lord alone opened and which stayed closed thereafter. Expounding the vision at length, he records the Marian reading (“Some understand beautifully”) and turns it against those who make her the mother of other children, tying it back to his own reply to Helvidius:

Some understand beautifully that the closed gate through which only the Lord God of Israel enters, and the leader to whom the gate is closed, to be the Virgin Mary, who both before childbirth and after childbirth remained a virgin… to confound those who claim that after the birth of the Savior, she had other sons by Joseph, based on the occasion of his brothers who are mentioned in the Gospel. I know that I wrote a small book in my youth, against Helvidius, the heretic of that time, in Rome.

— Jerome, Commentary on Ezekiel 13 (on 44:1–3).6 He puts the same typology to polemical use against the Pelagians, where the shut gate “prefigures” the virgin womb that Christ alone opened and which closed again behind Him:

…when Christ, as the firstborn, opened the virgin’s womb (Exod. xiii. 2). The heretics refused to acknowledge the mystery, which was prefigured by the Eastern door of the Temple (Ezek. xliv. 2), which closed again when once the High Priest had gone through it.

— Jerome, Against the Pelagians 2.4.7 The patristic use of Ezekiel 44:2 is taken up at the disputed reading on the consecrated-womb and shut-gate typology. In the Commentary on Ezekiel Jerome applies the figure to the post partum question, against those who “claim that after the birth of the Savior, she had other sons by Joseph.”

Reception and context

  • The theory as Jerome stated it, and later additions. Jerome himself advanced only that the brethren were sons of “Mary of Clopas” the Virgin’s sister. Two further identifications usually reckoned part of the “Hieronymian” view — that Clopas is Alphaeus (making the brethren the apostles James and Jude “of Alphaeus”/“of James” among the Twelve) — Jerome does not advance; Lightfoot finds the Clopas–Alphaeus identification “first, I believe, in Chrysostom” and then in Theodoret. Lightfoot notes that Jerome “confesses plainly that he does not know why Mary is called” of Clopas. The fuller construction found in later writers adds three identifications (Clopas = Alphaeus; “Jude of James” as brother not son; “James the little” as “the less”).8
  • John 19:25 and the maternal reading. Jerome’s cousinhood is a cousinhood on the mother’s side, and it rests on reading John 19:25 (“his mother’s sister, Mary the wife of Clopas”) as a single apposition — so that the verse names three women and “Mary of Clopas” is the Virgin’s own sister. Lightfoot judged that identification “most improbable, for it gives two sisters both called Mary,” and noted that the Peshito, which inserts a conjunction, and the punctuation of the Jerusalem Syriac lectionary read four women, leaving the Virgin’s sister unnamed — an arrangement the Greek, he writes, “admits, even if it does not favour.” Jerome did not insist on the identification — declining to insist “whether Mary the wife of Clopas and Mary the mother of James and Joses were different women” (§15) and, in the Letter to Hedibia, treating them as distinct (below).8
  • Lightfoot’s critique. J. B. Lightfoot — whose 1865 dissertation named all three views — rejected the Hieronymian reading. Two of his counts: that “Jerome claims no traditional support for his theory… he cannot, or at least does not, name a single writer,” so that “no decisive instance can be produced of a writer holding Jerome’s view, before it was propounded by Jerome himself”; and that “Jerome does not hold his theory staunchly and consistently.” On Lightfoot’s reading of the sources, writers who discuss the question before Jerome — Origen, the “two Hilaries,” Epiphanius — show no knowledge of the cousins-reading, which Epiphanius, jealous for Joseph’s honour, “would have hailed with delight.” Lightfoot files Jerome in column C of his conspectus, “Sons of the Virgin’s Sister” (with Pelagius, Augustine, and the Western service-books), and concludes that its difficulties “must lead to its rejection, if any hypothesis less burdened with difficulties can be found.”8 Whether the cousin kinship is attested before Jerome is disputed (Florentine Bechtel; Josef Blinzler and John McHugh, as reported by Richard Bauckham); see “Did the cousin reading have witnesses before Jerome, and did Jerome later let it go?”.
  • The Letter to Hedibia. In the late Letter to Hedibia (c. 406–7), untangling the resurrection appearances, Jerome enumerates the Marys of the Gospels and now sets the Virgin’s sister apart from the mother of James and Joses as two different women: “the Gospel makes mention of four women called Mary: the first is the mother of our Lord, and the second is Mary, wife of Cleophas and aunt of Jesus Christ, being His mother’s sister, and the third is Mary, mother of James and Joses, and the fourth is Mary Magdalene. Some, however, confused the mother of James and Joses with the aunt of Jesus Christ.”9 The identification made in Against Helvidius §15 — aunt (“Mary of Clopas”) = “Mary the mother of James and Joses” — he here lists as two distinct persons, and assigns the identification to unnamed others (“some… confused”). Lightfoot remarks that this identification, “of which he here speaks with such indifference, was the keystone of his own theory.”8
  • Dating. The treatise against Helvidius is dated “about a.d. 383” by its standard editors and by Jerome’s own reply; Lightfoot puts it “in the year 382 (or 383).” The timeline slots Jerome at c. 383, immediately after Helvidius, whose treatise it answers.
  • Relation to the three theories. Jerome is the Hieronymian namesake. His reading differs from both the Helvidian reading (Helvidius, Tertullian, the Antidicomarianites) and the Epiphanian — the “sons of Joseph by a former wife” of Origen, Eusebius, Hilary, Ambrosiaster, and Epiphanius. In his Commentary on Matthew (398), as Lightfoot reports, he describes holders of the Epiphanian view as “following the ravings of the apocryphal writings.”8
