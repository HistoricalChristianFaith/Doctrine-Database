Tertullian on the perpetual virginity of Mary

Dates: c. 210 (the North-African advocate flourished c. 197–220; the works that bear on the brethren cluster in that maturity — Against Marcion iv and On the Flesh of Christ c. 206–212, the Montanist On Monogamy a little later, c. 213–217) · Wikipedia
Summary: J. B. Lightfoot set Tertullian at the head of the patristic writers he reckoned Helvidian — holding that the “brethren of the Lord” were the sons of Joseph and Mary, born after Jesus. Tertullian never states this as a thesis; the question arises from his anti-docetic polemic. Pressing the reality of Christ’s human birth against Marcion and Apelles, he insists that the mother and brethren who “stood without” were really the Lord’s kin. Where he speaks of Mary’s virginity, he affirms it before the birth: she conceived a virgin but “was a wife when she brought forth her son,” under the law of “opening the womb.” Helvidius appealed to his authority, and Jerome replied that Tertullian was “not a man of the Church.” Lightfoot judged it “highly probable” — though “not absolutely certain” — that Tertullian held the Helvidian view; Josef Blinzler and John McHugh (⚠ unverified) are cited as reading the same texts as bearing on the manner of the birth rather than on the brethren’s parentage. The question is set out at “Did Tertullian hold that Mary bore children after Jesus?”

Claims & evidence

The mother and brethren who “stood without” (Against Marcion iv.19; On the Flesh of Christ 7)

Marcion and Apelles seized on Christ’s words — “Who is my mother, and who are my brethren?” (Matthew 12:48) — as proof that He was never really born, having (they said) no true mother or brethren at all. Tertullian answers that the very announcement presupposes their existence, and that Christ did not deny His kin but only ranked faith above them. His argument requires that they be real relations: in Against Marcion he writes,

We, for our part, say in reply, first, that it could not possibly have been told Him that His mother and His brethren stood without, desiring to see Him, if He had had no mother and no brethren… If, therefore, He made them “His mother and His brethren” who were not so, how could He deny them these relationships who really had them? Surely only on the condition of their deserts, and not by any disavowal of His near relatives.

— Tertullian, Against Marcion iv.19.1 He makes the same case in On the Flesh of Christ, where he notes that the crowd knew the family perfectly well — “His supposed father, Joseph the carpenter, and His mother Mary, and His brethren, and His sisters, were very well known to them” — and insists that “nobody would have told Him that His mother and brethren were standing outside, if he were not certain both that He had a mother and brethren.”2 Tertullian never says in so many words that the brethren were Mary’s own children; but, as Lightfoot observed, “his argument seems to imply that the Lord’s brethren were His brothers in the same sense in which Mary was His mother”.3

“A virgin when she conceived… a wife when she brought forth” (On the Flesh of Christ 23; On Monogamy 8)

Where Tertullian speaks directly of Mary’s virginity, he ties it to the conception and calls her a wife at the birth. Against opponents who played on the paradox “a virgin and yet not a virgin,” he draws the line himself:

She who bare (really) bare; and although she was a virgin when she conceived, she was a wife when she brought forth her son. Now, as a wife, she was under the very law of “opening the womb”… Indeed she ought rather to be called not a virgin than a virgin, becoming a mother at a leap, as it were, before she was a wife.

— Tertullian, On the Flesh of Christ 23.4 On this passage Mary’s womb was “opened” in the ordinary way of childbirth, which excludes a virginity in partu; whether, taken with the passages above, it also bears on a virginity after the birth is the disputed question noted above. He returns to Mary in the Montanist tract On Monogamy, praising Mary as the model of the once-married: “it was a virgin, about to marry once for all after her delivery, who gave birth to Christ, in order that each title of sanctity might be fulfilled in Christ’s parentage, by means of a mother who was both virgin, and wife of one husband.”5 Mary is a virgin before the delivery and a true wife — a univira, wife of one husband — after it. In this passage Tertullian praises Mary as both virgin and wife of one husband.

Helvidius’s appeal and Jerome’s reply

When Helvidius argued at Rome (c. 383) that Mary bore other children, he appealed to Tertullian as a predecessor. Jerome, replying, answered the appeal by setting Tertullian aside: “de Tertulliano nihil amplius dico quam ecclesiae hominem non fuisse” (“concerning Tertullian I say nothing more than that he was not a man of the Church”).6 In the same reply Jerome names Ignatius, Polycarp, Irenaeus and Justin on his own side. Lightfoot concludes: “It is therefore highly probable that he held the Helvidian view. Such an admission from one who was so strenuous an advocate of asceticism is worthy of notice.”3

Reception and context

  • Lightfoot’s qualification. Lightfoot notes that Tertullian nowhere says the brethren were Mary’s sons; the Helvidian conclusion is drawn from his anti-Marcionite argument (adv. Marc. iv.19, de Carn. Christ. 7), while “the more distinct passages” concern Mary’s virginity only (de Carn. Christ. 23, de Monog. 8). He grants that the usual assumption — that Tertullian held the brethren to be sons of Joseph and Mary — “though probable, is not absolutely certain,” since Jerome’s dispute with Helvidius turned on the virginity of the mother rather than on the precise relationship of the brethren.3
  • Tertullian’s context. Lightfoot calls Tertullian “so strenuous an advocate of asceticism.” The passages on Mary come from Christological polemic: against Marcion he presses that Christ’s birth, mother, and brethren were real, and against those who played on the paradox “a virgin and not a virgin” he presses that Mary’s childbearing was a real birth. On Monogamy belongs to his Montanist period; his relation to the “New Prophecy” is the subject of the timeline “Was Tertullian a heretic?”
  • Position in Lightfoot’s table. Tertullian heads Lightfoot’s conspectus of the first theory — the “sons of Joseph and Mary” column — ahead of Helvidius, Bonosus, and the Antidicomarianites.3 He is the earliest named witness on this timeline whom Lightfoot places on the Helvidian side, writing in the same century in which the Protevangelium’s Epiphanian account circulated. Whether he positively held the Helvidian reading of the brethren is the subject of a disputed reading.
