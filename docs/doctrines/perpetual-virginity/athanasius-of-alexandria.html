Athanasius of Alexandria on the perpetual virginity of Mary

Dates: c. 360 (Athanasius of Alexandria, c. 296–373, bishop of Alexandria, opponent of the Arians and defender of the Nicene homoousion. His Four Discourses (Orations) Against the Arians are conventionally assigned to the fruitful years of his third exile among the Egyptian monks, c. 356–360 — though a minority of scholars, following Charles Kannengiesser, argue for a date around 339–343) · Wikipedia
Summary: Athanasius is not discussing the brethren-question — he is defending the reality of Christ’s flesh against the Arians. In the course of that argument he calls the Lord’s mother “Mary Ever-Virgin” (Greek aēiparthenos, ἀειπάρθενος), using the title in passing, without arguing for it. The passage is dated to the mid-fourth century, a generation before Helvidius wrote at Rome (c. 383).

Claims & evidence

“Mary Ever-Virgin” in an anti-Arian argument

The setting is Christological, not Mariological. In Oration II Athanasius is pressing the Arians on the reality and purpose of the Incarnation: only if the Word is truly God and truly took human flesh could He deify what He assumed and bring man into the Father’s presence. He turns the point into a double challenge — those who deny the Son’s true deity may as well deny His true flesh, since both are needed for our salvation — and it is here, naming the flesh’s source, that the title falls:

And as we had not been delivered from sin and the curse, unless it had been by nature human flesh, which the Word put on… so also the man had not been deified, unless the Word who became flesh had been by nature from the Father and true and proper to Him… Therefore let those who deny that the Son is from the Father by nature and proper to His Essence, deny also that He took true human flesh of Mary Ever-Virgin; for in neither case had it been of profit to us men, whether the Word were not true and naturally Son of God, or the flesh not true which He assumed. But surely He took true flesh, though Valentinus rave; yea the Word was by nature Very God, though Ariomaniacs rave.

— Athanasius, Four Discourses Against the Arians, Discourse II, §70.1 The reality of the flesh is the point argued (against Valentinus on one side and the Arians on the other); Mary’s perpetual virginity is not argued. The epithet “Ever-Virgin” occurs in passing, as a title used without comment.

The title in its setting

Athanasius does not set out to show that Mary remained a virgin after Christ’s birth; he uses “Ever-Virgin” as her designation in an argument about something else. He wrote at Alexandria, where Clement and Origen had earlier written on Mary’s virginity, some decades before Helvidius and Jerome debated the question in the Latin West (c. 383).

The brethren question

Athanasius uses the title but gives no account of the Lord’s brethren in this passage, so it bears on the perpetual virginity itself and not on the choice among the three theories of the brethren. He does not tell us whether he took the brethren to be Joseph’s sons by a former marriage (the Epiphanian view) or cousins (the Hieronymian); he calls the Virgin ever-virgin and is silent on the genealogy.

Text and dating

  • Setting of the passage. The passage belongs to a Christological argument about the true, saving reality of Christ’s flesh, framed against Valentinus (“though Valentinus rave”) and the Arians (“though Ariomaniacs rave”). The Marian title is incidental to that purpose.
  • The Greek title. The NPNF renders aēiparthenos (ἀειπάρθενος) “Ever-Virgin”; the same compound epithet appears in later Greek conciliar and liturgical texts ⚠ unverified.
  • Date. The Orations are usually placed in Athanasius’ third exile (c. 356–360), when he withdrew to the Egyptian desert and wrote a cluster of major works; Robertson noted they seem from the language of II.1 to have been “issued at intervals.” A minority (Kannengiesser) argues for an earlier date, c. 339–343. Either date falls before the Latin controversy of the 380s.2
