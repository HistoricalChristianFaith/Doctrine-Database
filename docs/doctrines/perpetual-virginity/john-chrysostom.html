John Chrysostom on the perpetual virginity of Mary

Dates: c. 398 (the anchor here is his latest word on the question, the Commentary on Galatians, finished before he left Antioch for Constantinople in 398; his Homilies on Matthew, which give the earlier Epiphanian reading, belong to the same Antiochene presbyterate, c. 390. Chrysostom c. 347–407) · Wikipedia
Summary: J. B. Lightfoot reads Chrysostom as marking a transition: he observed that Chrysostom “wrote both before and after Jerome’s treatise had become generally known, and his expositions of the New Testament mark a period of transition.” In the homilies on the earlier books he is Epiphanian: expounding Matthew 1:25 he defends Mary’s perpetual virginity and explains that the “brethren” are so called only in the same reputed sense in which Joseph was “supposed to be husband of Mary” — no blood-kin of Mary at all — and elsewhere he keeps James the Lord’s brother a figure apart, one of the unbelieving brethren of John 7:5 who became the first bishop of Jerusalem. In the Commentary on Galatians (on 1:19) he calls James “the son of Cleophas, as the Evangelist does,” which Lightfoot reads as accepting Jerome’s cousins-identification and completing it by taking Clopas for Alphaeus. Lightfoot accordingly enters him under both readings: the Epiphanian in the earlier works, the Hieronymian in the later.

Claims & evidence

Early and Epiphanian: the brethren “reputed,” Mary ever-virgin (on Matthew 1:25)

In the fifth of his Homilies on Matthew (delivered at Antioch, c. 390), Chrysostom reaches the verse — Joseph “knew her not till she had brought forth her first-born son” — and, like Hilary before him, reads it as guarding the perpetual virginity rather than denying it. The word “till,” he argues, fixes only what was true before the birth and leaves the sequel to inference; and he takes the sequel to be that Mary bore no one else:

He hath here used the word “till,” not that thou shouldest suspect that afterwards he did know her, but to inform thee that before the birth the Virgin was wholly untouched by man… that which both was seen to be a consequence of the former statement, and was acknowledged, this in its turn he leaves for thee to perceive; namely, that not even after this, she having so become a mother… could that righteous man ever have endured to know her. For if he had known her, and had kept her in the place of a wife, how is it that our Lord commits her, as unprotected, and having no one, to His disciple, and commands him to take her to his own home?

He then takes up the objection — if Mary bore no other children, in what sense are the “brethren” his brethren? — and answers it in Epiphanian terms: the title is a reputed one, parallel to the reputed fatherhood of Joseph, and no assertion of a common mother:

How then, one may say, are James and the others called His brethren? In the same kind of way as Joseph himself was supposed to be husband of Mary. For many were the veils provided, that the birth, being such as it was, might be for a time screened. Wherefore even John so called them, saying, “For neither did His brethren believe in Him.”

— Chrysostom, Homilies on the Gospel of St Matthew V.5–6 (on Matthew 1:22–25).1 He goes on to trace what became of those unbelieving brethren: James “was so admired as even to be the first to be entrusted with the bishop’s office,” the man to whom Paul reported at Jerusalem — the brethren who “made light of this Man living” being turned by the resurrection into those who “consented even to be slain for His sake.” Lightfoot cites this homily for Chrysostom’s earlier view: the brethren were “at one time… unbeliever[s]” with the rest, the resurrection their turning-point, and they “were called the Lord’s brethren, as Joseph himself was reputed the husband of Mary” — adding that “hitherto he betrays no knowledge of the Hieronymian account.”

The same Epiphanian reading in the homilies on John and 1 Corinthians

The reading recurs across the Antiochene homilies. Expounding John 7:5 (“For neither did his brethren believe in him”), Chrysostom distinguishes the unbelieving kinsmen from the Twelve and names what one of them became — James, first bishop of Jerusalem:

Of those who said these things, one became first Bishop of Jerusalem, the blessed James, of whom Paul saith, “Other of the Apostles saw I none, save James, the Lord’s brother” (Gal. i. 19); and Judas also is said to have been a marvelous man… superiority among kindred is wont somehow to be envied by such as are not alike exalted. But who are those that they call disciples here? The crowd that followed Him, not the twelve.

— Chrysostom, Homilies on the Gospel of St John XLVIII.2 (on John 7:1–5).2 And commenting on 1 Corinthians 9:5, where Paul ranges “the brethren of the Lord” alongside “the rest of the Apostles… and Cephas,” Chrysostom comments on the verse without identifying the brethren with the Twelve:

“Have we no right to lead about a wife that is a believer, even as the rest of the Apostles, and the brethren of the Lord, and Cephas?” Observe his skilfulness. The leader of the choir stands last in his arrangement… But neither does he mention Peter alone, but all of them.

— Chrysostom, Homilies on the First Epistle to the Corinthians XXI.6 (on 1 Corinthians 9:4–5).3 None of these passages mentions Jerome’s cousins-theory; like Eusebius and Gregory of Nyssa, they keep the Lord’s brother distinct from the Twelve.

Late and Hieronymian: James “the son of Cleophas” (on Galatians 1:19)

The Commentary on Galatians reads differently. Expounding Paul’s “other of the Apostles saw I none, save James, the Lord’s brother” (Galatians 1:19), Chrysostom pauses on why Paul chose that title, and in doing so says that James could have been named instead “the son of Cleophas, as the Evangelist does”:

Had he only wished to point out whom he meant, he might have shown this by another appellation, and called him the son of Cleophas, as the Evangelist does. But as he considered that he had a share in the august titles of the Apostles, he exalts himself by honoring James; and this he does by calling him “the Lord’s brother,” although he was not by birth His brother, but only so reputed.

— Chrysostom, Commentary on the Epistle to the Galatians, on 1:19.4 The phrase “not by birth His brother, but only so reputed” repeats the formula of the homilies; the passage also calls James the Lord’s brother the “son of Cleophas” of John 19:25 — the identification on which Jerome’s reading rests. Lightfoot notes that he speaks of James here “as if he were an apostle,” and that by naming him “son of Cleophas” (i.e. the James “son of Alphaeus” of the apostolic lists, on the Aramaic equation Clopas = Alphaeus) appears, as Lightfoot put it, to have “accepted the hypothesis of Jerome and to have completed it by the identification of Clopas with Alphaeus.” Lightfoot noted that this stretch of the exposition “is somewhat confused, and it is difficult to resist the suspicion that it has been interpolated” ; he also writes that the identification of Clopas with Alphaeus “occurs first, I believe, in Chrysostom” and after him in Theodoret, “who is more explicit” on the same verse.5

Reception and context

  • Relation to the three theories. Lightfoot reads Chrysostom’s works as spanning two readings: Epiphanian in the Antiochene homilies (on Matthew, John, and 1 Corinthians), Hieronymian in the Commentary on Galatians. Lightfoot registered the split by entering “[Chrysostom]” — in brackets — in both columns of his closing conspectus: under B, “Sons of Joseph by a former wife,” and under C, “Sons of the Virgin’s Sister.” He is the only name so doubled. Neither group of texts adopts the Helvidian reading: in both, Mary is held to have borne no child but Jesus.
  • A stray Epiphanian fragment. Lightfoot flags a comment on 1 Corinthians 9:4–7 attributed to Chrysostom in Cramer’s Catena — but “not found in the Homilies” — that is more explicit still: it calls the brethren “those reputed to be his brethren… the sons of Joseph,” so styled “on account of Joseph’s betrothal to the Theotokos,” and names them James bishop of Jerusalem, Joseph, Simon, and Judah. The same note recurs almost verbatim in the Oecumenian catena and in Theophylact. Its authorship is uncertain; it is reported here on Lightfoot’s authority.6
  • Dating. Both bodies of work belong to Chrysostom’s years as a presbyter at Antioch (386–398), before his elevation to Constantinople. The Homilies on Matthew are usually placed c. 390; the Commentary on Galatians is his latest word on the point and cannot be later than 398. He is slotted at c. 398 — after Augustine’s early Commentary on Galatians (c. 394) and before Pelagius (c. 406) — so that the Galatians identification can be read beside the other witnesses of the 390s and 400s. (On other timelines Chrysostom is anchored c. 395 by his Antioch homilies; here the later Galatians work sets the slot.)
