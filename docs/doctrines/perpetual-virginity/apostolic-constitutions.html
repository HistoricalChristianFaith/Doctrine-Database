The Apostolic Constitutions on the perpetual virginity of Mary

Dates: c. 380 (the Constitutions of the Holy Apostles, a large Church order compiled in Syria in the later fourth century out of older material — the third-century Didascalia Apostolorum, the Didache, and other strata — and pseudonymously issued in the name of the twelve apostles through Clement of Rome. J. B. Lightfoot judged that “the main part… may perhaps be regarded as a work of the third century, though they received considerable additions in later ages”) · Wikipedia
Summary: The Apostolic Constitutions repeatedly name two men called James separately: James the son of Alphaeus, one of the Twelve, and James the brother of the Lord, bishop of Jerusalem. Because the second is counted in addition to the Twelve — a supernumerary apostle like Paul, so that the roster runs to fourteen — the work bears on the “James not one of the Twelve” sub-question. Lightfoot and the nineteenth-century Ante-Nicene Fathers editor cite the distinction against the Hieronymian reading, which identifies the Lord’s brother with James the son of Alphaeus; the Epiphanian and Helvidian readings both keep the two men distinct.

Claims & evidence

James the Lord’s brother listed after the Twelve, beside Paul

Closing the sixth book, the compiler has the apostles subscribe the whole work by name. The list runs through the Twelve — including “James the son of Alphaeus” — and then, past Matthias, adds two more: James the Lord’s brother and Paul.

On whose account also we, who are now assembled in one place,—Peter and Andrew; James and John, sons of Zebedee; Philip and Bartholomew; Thomas and Matthew; James the son of Alphaeus, and Lebbaeus who is surnamed Thaddaeus; and Simon the Canaanite, and Matthias, who instead of Judas was numbered with us; and James the brother of the Lord and bishop of Jerusalem, and Paul the teacher of the Gentiles, the chosen vessel, having all met together, have written to you this Catholic doctrine.

— Constitutions of the Holy Apostles vi.14.1 James the son of Alphaeus stands among the Twelve; James “the brother of the Lord and bishop of Jerusalem” is named separately at the end, and Paul — “the chosen vessel” — is set beside him. The list gives fourteen names in all.

The Twelve, then Paul, then James the Lord’s brother — the reckoning in Book II

The same threefold reckoning appears in the body of the work. Enumerating the messengers God sent in each age, Book II ends with the Christian apostolate:

…those after His passion by us, the twelve apostles, and Paul the chosen vessel. We therefore, who have been vouchsafed the favour of being the witnesses of His appearance, together with James the brother of our Lord, and the other seventy-two disciples, and his seven deacons, have heard from the mouth of…

— Constitutions of the Holy Apostles ii.55.2 Here “the twelve apostles, and Paul” are the preachers; “James the brother of our Lord” is joined to them alongside the seventy-two disciples and the seven deacons — ranked with the wider circle, not counted inside the Twelve.

James the brother at the head of the list of bishops

The enumeration of the bishops ordained by the apostles opens with James, as the first bishop of Jerusalem, followed by successors named as his kin:

Now concerning those bishops which have been ordained in our lifetime, we let you know that they are these:—James the bishop of Jerusalem, the brother of our Lord; upon whose death the second was Simeon the son of Cleopas; after whom the third was Judas the son of James.

— Constitutions of the Holy Apostles vii.46.3 The nineteenth-century editor of the Ante-Nicene Fathers English text commented on this feature: “An incidental proof of the early origin of this compilation is furnished by the clear distinction it makes between James the son of Alphaeus and James the brother of our Lord. The theory of Jerome, which identifies them, was later.”3 And in the eighth book the ordination-canons are delivered by “we, the twelve apostles of the Lord, who are now together… there being present with us Paul the chosen vessel, our fellow-apostle, and James the bishop” — once more the Twelve, then Paul, then James.4

The distinction in the debate over the brethren

Lightfoot placed the work in his catena for this feature: the Apostolic Constitutions, he wrote, “distinguish James the Lord’s brother from James the son of Alphaeus, making him, like St. Paul, a supernumerary apostle, and thus counting fourteen in all.”5 Jerome’s Hieronymian solution identifies the Lord’s brother James with James the son of Alphaeus, so making the “brethren” the Lord’s cousins and placing James among the Twelve; Lightfoot and the ANF editor read the Constitutions’ separation of the two men as a witness against that identification, the editor dating Jerome’s theory later than the compilation. Both the Epiphanian (the brethren are Joseph’s sons by a former wife) and the Helvidian (they are Mary’s own later children) readings keep James the Lord’s brother outside the Twelve. Whether the cousin-theory had witnesses before Jerome is itself disputed; see the disputed reading on the cousin theory’s antiquity.

Text and context

  • Two Jameses within one book. The same eighth book that names “James the bishop” also gives, under its own heading, “The Constitution of James the Son of Alphaeus” (“And I James, the son of Alphaeus, make a constitution in regard to confessors…”), and, in a variant reading preserved in some manuscripts, records that “James, the brother of the Lord, has been killed with stones by the Jews in Jerusalem” — the martyred bishop, named separately from the son of Alphaeus who issues a constitution.6
  • Date and character. The Constitutions are a pseudonymous compilation issued in the apostles’ names; Lightfoot regarded the main part as perhaps third-century, with later additions. The compiler does not argue the distinction between the two Jameses; the lists simply name them separately. The timeline slot (c. 380) precedes Jerome’s Against Helvidius (c. 383).
  • Relation to the three theories. Lightfoot groups the Apostolic Constitutions with Hegesippus under “James the Just not one of the Twelve,” a heading he enters as “A. or B.” — compatible with either the Epiphanian or the Helvidian view and not with the Hieronymian identification. The passages above neither affirm nor deny the perpetual virginity itself; they name the Lord’s brother James separately from the Twelve.5
