John Wesley on the perpetual virginity of Mary

Dates: c. 1749 (A Letter to a Roman Catholic, Dublin, 18 July 1749; John Wesley, 1703 – 1791, the Anglican priest and founder of Methodism) · Wikipedia
Summary: An eighteenth-century Protestant witness, writing two centuries after the Reformers. In A Letter to a Roman Catholic — an irenic appeal written at Dublin in 1749 to cool the sectarian hatred between Protestants and Catholics in Ireland — Wesley sets out a creed of “the faith of a true Protestant,” and in it confesses that Christ was “born of the blessed Virgin Mary, who, as well after as before she brought Him forth, continued a pure and unspotted virgin.” Like Zwingli before him, his statement affirms the virginity itself and never takes up the identity of the “brethren of the Lord” (as Luther and Calvin elsewhere do), so he is not filed under any of the readings of the brethren.

Claims & evidence

“As well after as before… a pure and unspotted virgin” — the 1749 Letter

Section 7 of the Letter is a compact confession of the person and work of Christ, cast in the idiom of the ancient creeds. Within it, professing the Incarnation, Wesley affirms Mary’s virginity after the birth as well as before — the perpetual virginity in its plain sense:

I believe that He was made man, joining the human nature with the divine in one person; being conceived by the singular operation of the Holy Ghost, and born of the blessed Virgin Mary, who, as well after as before she brought Him forth, continued a pure and unspotted virgin.

— A Letter to a Roman Catholic, §7 (Dublin, 18 July 1749).1 The words “as well after as before” affirm that Mary’s virginity continued after the Nativity, the point Helvidius denied. Wesley states it as an article of the common faith he invites his Catholic reader to recognize as shared ground.

The setting: an irenic creed of shared belief

The confession is part of an appeal for peace rather than a treatise on Mary. Writing amid the sectarian bitterness of Ireland, Wesley lays out what “a true Protestant” believes precisely in order to show a Roman Catholic how much the two hold in common, urging that both “resolve… to help each other on in whatever we are agreed leads to the kingdom” and give over “this endless jangling about opinions.”2 In that framing the perpetual virginity appears among the beliefs Wesley sets out as held in common by Protestant and Catholic.

Reception and context

  • Classification: not filed. As with Zwingli, the statement affirms the virginity itself and never says who the “brethren of the Lord” were, so Wesley is not classed Epiphanian (Joseph’s sons by a former wife) or Hieronymian (cousins) — unlike Luther, who elsewhere adopts the cousin-reading, and Calvin, who glosses “brethren” as including cousins. His witness bears on the virginity, not the brethren-question.
  • Other Protestant witnesses on this timeline. Before Wesley, Zwingli (1522) and Luther (1523) affirm the doctrine and Calvin (1562) rejects the Helvidian inference from Matthew 1:25; after him, the Anglican J. B. Lightfoot (1865) favours the Epiphanian reading. Their statements figure in the dispute over when the doctrine was held and defined; see “Is the perpetual virginity a late development?”
  • Wesley on another Scriptural “until.” In his Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament Wesley reads the “till he should pay all that was due” of Matthew 18:34 as “without all hope of release, for this he could never do” — an “until” whose term is never reached.3 The verse figures in the modern debate over the “till” (heōs hou) of Matthew 1:25: Eric Svendsen (Protestant, doctoral thesis, 2001) counts it among the uses that mark a terminus, the torture ceasing, by implication, once payment is made.4
