Origen on the perpetual virginity of Mary

Dates: c. 248 (the Alexandrian, then Caesarean, master lived c. 185–253/4; his Commentary on Matthew, from which the main passage comes, belongs to the last years at Caesarea, c. 246–248) · Wikipedia
Summary: Origen, in J. B. Lightfoot’s words, “declares himself very distinctly in favour of the Epiphanian view” — that the “brethren of the Lord” were sons of Joseph by a former wife, married before Mary, so that they are no children of Mary and her perpetual virginity stands. He attaches the view to two named second-century texts, the Gospel according to Peter and the Book of James (the Protevangelium), and grounds it in a theological fitness of his own: as Jesus was the first-fruit of chastity among men, so Mary was the first-fruit of virginity among women. His is the earliest extant text to name those works as sources of the reading. Lightfoot took the passage to show both that Origen held the Epiphanian view and that he was “wholly unaware of the Hieronymian” (the cousins-reading found in Jerome c. 383), since the only alternative Origen mentions is the denial of the perpetual virginity.

Claims & evidence

The brethren were Joseph’s sons by a former wife — on the Gospel of Peter and the Book of James

Commenting on “Is not this the carpenter’s son? Is not his mother called Mary? and his brethren James, and Joseph, and Simon, and Judas?” (Matthew 13:55), Origen sets out the tradition and endorses it in his own voice:

Some say, basing it on a tradition in the Gospel according to Peter, as it is entitled, or The Book of James, that the brethren of Jesus were sons of Joseph by a former wife, whom he married before Mary. Those who say so wish to preserve the honour of Mary in virginity to the end, so that that body of hers which was appointed to minister to the Word… might not know intercourse with a man after that the Holy Ghost came into her. And I think it in harmony with reason that Jesus was the first-fruit among men of the purity which consists in chastity, and Mary among women; for it were not pious to ascribe to any other than to her the first-fruit of virginity.

— Origen, Commentary on Matthew, Book X.17 (on Matthew 13:55).1 The reading is the Epiphanian one: the brethren are Joseph’s children by an earlier marriage, step-relations of Jesus, and Mary bore no child but her Son. The closing clause — “I think it in harmony with reason” — is Origen speaking for himself, not merely reporting others; Lightfoot notes that a reader who overlooks it (as J. H. Mill did) misses “Origen’s real meaning” and mistakes his own settled judgement for a bare report of “a minority… founded on apocryphal traditions.”2

“The Lord’s brother” can also mark character, not only kinship

Elsewhere Origen glosses the same title in a looser register. Explaining why Paul calls James “the Lord’s brother” (Galatians 1:19), he says Paul so names him “not so much on account of his kinsmanship or their companionship together, as on account of his character and language.”2 Lightfoot judges this gloss “not inconsistent with the explicit statement” of the Commentary on Matthew: on the Epiphanian reading the brotherhood is already a household relation rather than a blood-tie to Mary.

The consecrated womb and the new tomb — a typology of fitness

Lightfoot (1865) held that the belief was not “suggested solely by the false asceticism of the early Church” but owed as much to “another sentiment which the fathers fantastically expressed by a comparison between the conception and the burial of our Lord.”2 Origen states such a comparison. Answering Celsus, he reasons from the three Evangelists’ care to note that the sepulchre was new and unused (John 19:41; Luke 23:53) that a fitness bound the virgin birth to the virgin tomb:

…it became him, who was unlike other dead people… and who was, so to speak, a new dead man, to be laid in a new and clean tomb, in order that, as his birth was purer than any other (because he was born not in the way of ordinary generation but of a virgin), his burial also might have the purity symbolically indicated in his body being deposited in a sepulcher that was new… quarried and hewn out of one rock, united together in all its parts.

— Origen, Against Celsus ii.69.3 The birth “of a virgin” and the burial in a tomb “wherein was never man yet laid” are set in parallel. Whether such typology grounds the perpetual virginity is the subject of a disputed reading. Origen reads Ezekiel’s gate, too, as one “made from sensible material and always closed,” through which “the Lord God… enters and departs” (Homilies on Ezekiel 14); later writers on this timeline, such as Ambrose, apply the shut gate to Mary.3

Reception and context

  • The earliest named sources of the Epiphanian view. Origen’s is the earliest extant text to attach the reading to specific works — the Gospel according to Peter and the Book of James (the Protevangelium of James). Because the Gospel of Peter’s own words on the brethren do not survive, its testimony on this point reaches us only through Origen; his report is the basis for placing that gospel on the timeline (see the summary’s entry for it). His own endorsement is preserved in full in his Commentary on Matthew. The same passage also figures on the canon timeline, read there for how Origen handles the “Book of James” when he adduces its tradition (“some say, basing it on a tradition”).
  • Lightfoot on Origen and the cousins-reading. Lightfoot draws a second conclusion from the passage: it “shows not only that Origen himself favoured the Epiphanian view… but that he was wholly unaware of the Hieronymian, the only alternative which presented itself being the denial of the perpetual virginity.” On Lightfoot’s argument, a writer who canvasses the question and names only two options — brethren as Joseph’s earlier sons, or as Mary’s later children — indicates that the third reading, the maternal cousins of “Mary of Clopas,” was not current before Jerome set it out c. 383. Lightfoot lists the same silence in the two Hilaries and Epiphanius in support of his view that the Hieronymian reading is the latest of the three.2 Whether the cousin kinship is attested before Jerome is disputed (Florentine Bechtel; Josef Blinzler and John McHugh, as reported by Richard Bauckham); see “Did the cousin reading have witnesses before Jerome, and did Jerome later let it go?”.
  • Position in Lightfoot’s table. Origen stands fourth in Lightfoot’s roll of the “sons of Joseph by a former wife” authorities — after the Gospel of Peter, the Protevangelium, and Clement, and ahead of Eusebius, Gregory of Nyssa, Epiphanius, and the Eastern churches.2 As Clement’s successor at Alexandria he attests the reading there in the mid-third century, with both named sources and a theological reason (the “first-fruit of virginity”).
  • The grounds Origen gives. Origen supports the reading by a tradition he takes from the Gospel according to Peter and the Book of James and by an argument from fitness (the “first-fruit of virginity”); he does not argue it here from the text of the Gospels. Whether the second-century stepbrother tradition preserves historical memory of Jesus’ family is the subject of a disputed reading.
