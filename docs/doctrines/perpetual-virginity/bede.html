The Venerable Bede on the perpetual virginity of Mary

Dates: c. 735 (the timeline slot; the Northumbrian monk of Wearmouth–Jarrow, c. 672/673 – 735, called “the Venerable” — his Commentary on Luke (In Lucae evangelium expositio), dedicated to Bishop Acca of Hexham, was written earlier in his career, c. 709–716) · Wikipedia
Summary: Commenting on Mary’s reply to Gabriel — “How shall this be, seeing I know not a man?” (Luke 1:34) — Bede reads the question as her reverent disclosure of a settled purpose of virginity: she had “decided to lead a virginal life,” and asks not for a sign, as Zachariah did, but only after the manner of obedience. He gives, firsthand, the Latin vow-of-virginity reading of Luke 1:34 also found in Augustine.

Claims & evidence

Mary’s question discloses a settled purpose of virginity (Luke 1:34)

Expounding the Annunciation, Bede takes Mary’s perplexity not as ignorance of how children are conceived but as the reverent unveiling of a resolve already formed — to remain a virgin:

But Mary said to the angel: How will this be, since I do not know a man? She reverently expressed the purpose of her mind, that is, that she had decided to lead a virginal life. Because she was the first among women to devote herself to such great virtue, she rightfully deserved, by unique merit, to excel in blessedness above other women. How, she said, will this be? She did not say: How will I know this; but, How will this be, she said, since I do not know a man. She inquired about the order of obedience to which she should submit, not asking for a sign to believe. For it did not befit the virgin chosen to bear God to exist in doubtful mistrust but in cautious prudence, since man could not easily know the mystery that was hidden in God through the ages. Therefore, because she had read, Behold a virgin shall conceive, and bear a son, but had not read how it could happen, rightfully believing in what she had read, she asked the angel about what she did not find in the prophet (Isaiah VII).

— Commentary on Luke (In Lucae evangelium expositio), Book I, on Luke 1:34.1 The Latin reads: Propositum suae mentis reverenter exposuit, vitam videlicet virginalem se ducere decrevisse — “She reverently explained the purpose of her mind, namely that she had decided to lead a virginal life.”2 Bede does not argue the perpetual virginity here; he treats Mary’s virginity as a propositum, a standing purpose.

Mary’s question set against Zachariah’s

Bede’s reading turns on a contrast between the two Annunciation scenes of Luke 1. Zachariah, told he would father the Baptist, asked Whereby shall I know this? (Luke 1:18) and was struck dumb for unbelief; Mary asks How shall this be? For Bede the one demands a sign, the other only inquires after the way it will come to pass:

She did not say: How will I know this; but, How will this be, she said, since I do not know a man. She inquired about the order of obedience to which she should submit, not asking for a sign to believe.

— Commentary on Luke, on Luke 1:34.1 Having read Isaiah’s a virgin shall conceive (Isaiah 7:14) but nowhere read how a virgin could conceive, she “rightfully believing in what she had read, asked the angel about what she did not find in the prophet.” For Bede the question therefore presupposes, rather than doubts, her virginity: it is the puzzle of a woman who does not expect to “know a man,” wondering by what order the promised conception will be worked.

Reception and context

  • The vow-of-virginity reading. Bede’s gloss is a firsthand instance of the vow-of-virginity reading of Luke 1:34 — that Mary’s question implies she had already resolved to remain a virgin. He states it as a settled purpose or resolve of virginity (propositum…vitam virginalem se ducere decrevisse), not as a formal, already-sworn vow such as Bernard of Clairvaux later describes. The reading also appears on this timeline in Gregory of Nyssa and Augustine, and in Aquinas.
  • Relation to the three theories. Like John of Damascus, Bede here affirms the virginity itself without taking up the identity of the “brethren of the Lord,” so this passage does not place him with the Epiphanians, Hieronymians, or Helvidians.
  • Text and transmission. Bede’s Luke commentary draws largely on Ambrose, Augustine, Gregory the Great, and Jerome, and was used in Carolingian and medieval exegesis, including the Glossa Ordinaria; the vow-reading of Luke 1:34 is described as the common opinion of the schools from the thirteenth century ⚠ unverified.
