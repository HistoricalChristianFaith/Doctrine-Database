Ephraem the Syrian on the perpetual virginity of Mary

Dates: c. 363 (Ephraem — Ephrem the Syrian, c. 306–373 — deacon, hymnographer, and biblical commentator of the Syriac church; a native of Nisibis, he removed to Edessa after the city was ceded to Persia in 363 and there produced his major exegetical work, including the Commentary on the Acts of the Apostles drawn on here. The commentary cannot be dated closely within his Edessene decade [363–373]; it is anchored to c. 363 for the timeline) · Wikipedia
Summary: Ephraem holds the Epiphanian account of the brethren — they are sons of Joseph by a former marriage, no children of Mary — and so witnesses the perpetual virginity by the stepbrother reading. What sets him apart from the timeline’s other Epiphanians is a second move: in his Commentary on Acts (on the apostle-roll of Acts 1:13) he folds two of the brethren into the Twelve, identifying the apostle “Judas of James” with Jude the Lord’s brother (author of the Epistle of Jude, and Matthew and Mark’s Lebbaeus/Thaddaeus) and the apostle Simon the Zealot with Simon the Lord’s brother — while keeping both distinct from the apostle Judas Thomas. He is a fourth-century Syrian witness who differs from the “brethren distinct from the Twelve” position found in Hegesippus, the Clementines, Marius Victorinus, Cyril of Jerusalem, and the Apostolic Constitutions — on the Epiphanian basis (Joseph’s sons), not the Hieronymian (cousins), and for Jude and Simon only, not James.

Claims & evidence

Ephraem on Acts 1:13: the brethren as Joseph’s sons and brothers of the Lord

Ephraem’s Commentary on the Acts of the Apostles does not survive in the original Syriac; it is preserved in an Armenian version, from which J. Rendel Harris printed extracts (with an English rendering) in his 1894 Four Lectures on the Western Text, and which F. C. Conybeare later translated in full into Latin for the Beginnings of Christianity. Commenting on the list of the Twelve gathered in the upper room (Acts 1:13), Ephraem stops on Simon and Jude and identifies each as a son of Joseph and a brother of the Lord:

But Simeon (Shmawon) the Zealot is by Matthew and Mark called Simon the Cananaean. Perhaps in the Hebrew tongue he is called Zealot. And it is averred by many that he is son of Joseph father of the Lord, and brother of the Lord. Moreover Judas (brother) of Jacob, was brother of the same Simon and son of Joseph, who also was brother of the Lord. This one wrote the Catholic epistle which in his name is called the epistle of Judas, in which at the beginning out of humility instead of calling himself brother of the Lord, he writes brother of Jacob. And hence it is clear that he is the same whom Matthew and Mark call Lebaeus and Thadaeus, so that they and Luke do not respectively name different persons, but only one and the same person by different names.

— Ephraem the Syrian, Commentary on Acts, on Acts 1:13 (Armenian), trans. J. Rendel Harris, Four Lectures on the Western Text of the New Testament (1894), pp. 37–38.1 Two things stand together in this passage. First, the brethren are “son of Joseph father of the Lord, and brother of the Lord” — the Epiphanian account, in which “brother of the Lord” rests on Joseph’s (reputed) fatherhood and implies no blood-tie to Mary. Ephraem does not suggest these are Mary’s own later children; and the reasoning he gives for Jude’s self-styling — that Jude, out of humility, wrote “brother of James” rather than claim the nearer title “brother of the Lord” — is the reasoning Clement of Alexandria also gives. Second, the two brethren are here apostles: Simon the Zealot and “Judas of James” are both members of the Twelve, and Ephraem harmonizes the apostle-lists so that Luke’s “Judas of James,” Matthew’s Lebbaeus, and Mark’s Thaddaeus are “one and the same person.”

“Judas of James” read as Jude the brother of James — and kept distinct from Judas Thomas

Ephraem’s harmonization turns on reading the ambiguous apostolic name “Judas of James” (Ioudas Iakobou, Luke 6:16; Acts 1:13) as “Judas the brother of James” — the self-designation of the author of the Epistle of Jude (Jude 1) — rather than “Judas the son of James.” Having made that identification, he continues past the passage above to distinguish this apostle from a different Thaddaeus (one of the Seventy, associated with the Abgar legend) and to keep him separate again from the apostle Judas Thomas, whom the East Syrian church cherished under that name:

And no wonder if in Hebrew there was a plenty of double names and multiple names, whence the ambiguity in question of the Evangelists as to Thadaeus and Judas is one of name only, not of persons. For of the first set chosen by Christ not one perished, but only Judas the traitor. It is certain then that the other Thadaeus who was with Abgar was of the Seventy… For Thadaeus, one of the Seventy, died in Armenia in the region Artazon; but Judas of Jacob who in Matthew and Mark is Thadaeus, one of the Twelve, died in Ormi in Armenia. Thus the agreement of the Evangelists as to the names of the Apostles is confirmed.

— Ephraem the Syrian, Commentary on Acts, on Acts 1:13–17 (continuing).1 Richard Bauckham summarizes the whole: Ephraem here “identifies the apostle Judas of James (understood as Judas the brother of James) with Judas the Lord’s brother, but neither with Thomas… He also identifies the apostle Simon the Zealot with Simon the Lord’s brother.”2 The last point bears on a separate question, whether Jude the Lord’s brother was the apostle Thomas, Jesus’ twin. Ephraem’s own Syriac tradition used the name “Judas Thomas”; in this commentary Ephraem places Jude the Lord’s brother among the Twelve as “Judas of James”/Thaddaeus and keeps Judas Thomas a different man (see the disputed reading “Was the apostle Judas Thomas, called Jesus’ twin in Syriac tradition, the Lord’s brother Jude?”).

Ephraem and the brethren’s place among the Twelve

On the doctrine itself, Ephraem gives the Epiphanian account: the brethren are Joseph’s sons, and he does not present them as Mary’s children. His placing of Jude and Simon inside the Twelve differs from the witnesses on this timeline who set the Lord’s brethren apart from the Twelve — Hegesippus, the Clementine literature, Marius Victorinus, Cyril of Jerusalem, and the Apostolic Constitutions. Three features of his account bear on how it is used in the debate:

  • A fourth-century Syrian attestation. Ephraem (d. 373) reckons two of the four named brethren among the Twelve, before Jerome’s Against Helvidius (c. 383), and introduces the Simon identification as a view “averred by many.”
  • James is not included. Ephraem does not place James the Lord’s brother among the Twelve — the identification the Hieronymian reading makes with James son of Alphaeus. His Jude and Simon are “brothers of the Lord” and sons of Joseph, not cousins.
  • The reading of “Judas of James.” The identification rests on reading “Judas of James” as “brother” rather than “son” of James. J. B. Lightfoot, discussing the same identification within Jerome’s theory, calls “the son” “the obvious rendering,” “supported by two of the earliest versions, the Peshito Syriac and the Thebaic, while two others, the Old Latin and Memphitic, leave the ellipsis unsupplied and thus preserve the ambiguity of the original.”3 Ephraem reads it as “brother,” matching the self-designation of the Epistle of Jude (“brother of James,” Jude 1), and harmonizes Luke’s “Judas of James” with Matthew’s Lebbaeus and Mark’s Thaddaeus as “one and the same person.”

Reception and context

  • Text and transmission. The Syriac original of Ephraem’s Acts commentary is lost. It survives in an Armenian translation (edited by N. Akinian, Vienna, 1921); Harris’s 1894 Four Lectures printed excerpts in English in an appendix devoted to recovering Ephraem’s Acts text, and F. C. Conybeare gave a full Latin translation in The Beginnings of Christianity, Part I, vol. III (1926), pp. 373–453. The quotations here follow Harris’s English of the Armenian.
  • “Averred by many.” Ephraem introduces the Simon identification with “it is averred by many,” reporting a current view rather than coining one, and states the Jude identification in his own voice as its consequence (“Moreover Judas… was brother of the same Simon… brother of the Lord”). Both rest on the Epiphanian premise that the brethren are Joseph’s sons.
  • Relation to the three theories. Ephraem belongs with the “sons of Joseph by a former wife” authorities (Clement, Origen, Eusebius, Hilary, Epiphanius) on the doctrine, but differs from them on the apostleship of the brethren: among the Epiphanian witnesses on this timeline, he alone reckons Jude and Simon members of the Twelve. J. B. Lightfoot, whose 1865 conspectus classifies most of the other witnesses on this timeline, does not cite Ephraem’s Acts commentary (Harris first put the Armenian text within reach in 1894).
