Eusebius of Caesarea on the perpetual virginity of Mary

Dates: c. 324 (the Church History in its final edition; Eusebius, bishop of Caesarea in Palestine and the “Father of Church History,” was present at Nicaea in 325 and died c. 340) · Wikipedia
Summary: Eusebius is an early fourth-century witness to the Epiphanian reading — that the “brethren of the Lord” are sons of Joseph and no blood-kin of Mary. Two features of his Church History are read that way. He explains that James was called the Lord’s brother because he was a son of Joseph, and Joseph was the reputed father of Christ — reputed brotherhood tracking reputed fatherhood, so that James is Joseph’s son, not Mary’s (H.E. ii.1). And he consistently reckons James the Lord’s brother a supernumerary apostle like Paul, standing beside the Twelve rather than among them (H.E. i.12; vii.19), whereas the Hieronymian reading identifies him with James the son of Alphaeus, one of the Twelve. J. B. Lightfoot filed him in his catena of the “sons of Joseph by a former wife” authorities, immediately after Origen.

Claims & evidence

James is “called the brother of the Lord” because Joseph was his reputed father

Opening his second book on the course the apostles took after the Ascension, Eusebius names James the Just as Jerusalem’s first bishop, and stops to explain how a man who was Joseph’s son came to be styled the Lord’s brother at all:

Then James, whom the ancients surnamed the Just on account of the excellence of his virtue, is recorded to have been the first to be made bishop of the church of Jerusalem. This James was called the brother of the Lord because he was known as a son of Joseph, and Joseph was supposed to be the father of Christ, because the Virgin, being betrothed to him, “was found with child by the Holy Ghost before they came together,” as the account of the holy Gospels shows.

— Church History ii.1.2.1 In the passage James is a son of Joseph, called the brother of the Lord because Joseph was the reputed father of Christ; the passage asserts no blood-tie to Mary. Lightfoot took the phrase “most naturally… to refer to the reputed brotherhood of James, as a consequence of the reputed fatherhood of Joseph, and thus to favour the Epiphanian view,” and noted that the verb “cannot… imply any doubt on the part of Eusebius about the parentage of James, for the whole drift of the passage is plainly against this.”2 The same “so-called” idiom recurs whenever Eusebius touches the relationship — James “one of the so-called brethren of the Saviour” (i.12), James “called a brother of Christ” (vii.19).

James as a supernumerary apostle, like Paul

Cataloguing the apostles and the wider circle of the seventy, Eusebius comes to the resurrection appearances Paul lists in 1 Corinthians 15, and uses them to mark James off as an apostle added to the Twelve, on the same footing as Paul himself:

Afterwards he says he appeared unto James, who was one of the so-called brethren of the Saviour. But, since in addition to these, there were many others who were called apostles, in imitation of the Twelve, as was Paul himself, he adds: “Afterward he appeared to all the apostles.”

— Church History i.12.4–5.3 Lightfoot reads Eusebius as “representing him as a supernumerary apostle like St. Paul” — an apostle in the wider sense, over and above the Twelve.4 Eusebius again sets James beside “the apostles” when he describes the relic shown at Jerusalem in his own day:

The chair of James, who first received the episcopate of the church at Jerusalem from the Saviour himself and the apostles, and who, as the divine records show, was called a brother of Christ, has been preserved until now…

— Church History vii.19.1.5 Here James “received the episcopate… from the Saviour himself and the apostles” — named beside “the apostles” as their appointee, and again “called” a brother of Christ. Lightfoot cites i.12 and vii.19 for Eusebius’ distinguishing James the Lord’s brother from the Twelve, a distinction the Hieronymian reading, which identifies him with James the son of Alphaeus, does not make; the Epiphanian and Helvidian readings both keep him distinct. On the parentage question, the ii.1 explanation, which makes James a son of Joseph, is the passage Lightfoot pairs with these (“Accordingly in another passage…”).

The “five sons of Anna” — a Syriac tract transmitted under Eusebius’ name

The Church History makes James a son of Joseph but does not there name a former marriage; nor does Eusebius’ Commentary on Isaiah, which again marks James the Lord’s brother off from the Twelve as a supernumerary apostle like Paul but says nothing of Joseph’s earlier wife or children.6 The “former wife” is spelled out and named in a Syriac tract that has travelled under Eusebius’ name, On the Star. Narrating the Magi and the Star of Bethlehem, it lists among those who fled Herod “Joseph and Mary and Our Lord with them, and the five sons of Hannah (Anna) the first wife of Joseph” — the brethren as Joseph’s children by an earlier marriage. J. B. Lightfoot quoted the line from William Wright’s translation (p. 17), noting that the account professes to rest on “a document dating a.d. 119”; the modern critical catalogue of the piece (e-Clavis: Christian Apocrypha) likewise records that On the Star has “the five sons of Joseph’s first wife (here named Hannah)” fleeing Herod’s threats.7

The tract survives in a single Syriac manuscript (British Library Add. 17142, fifth–sixth century), and the e-Clavis entry classes it as pseudonymous — “Pseudo-Eusebius,” written “in the name of Eusebius of Caesarea” rather than by him — one of a cluster of early Syriac legends of the Star and the Magi; its professed source “dating a.d. 119” is the tract’s own claim. On that classification On the Star is a separate Syriac witness to the Epiphanian tradition, naming Joseph’s first wife Hannah, and Eusebius’ own reading is drawn here from the Church History and Commentary on Isaiah set out above.8

Eusebius and the Palestinian tradition

Eusebius collected and preserved older Palestinian tradition — Hegesippus’ account of the Lord’s kindred survives through his quotation — and his own account agrees with the Epiphanian reading: the brethren are Joseph’s sons, James is called the Lord’s brother by a reputed and not a natural relationship, and he ranks among the apostles as a supernumerary beside Paul. Lightfoot accordingly set “Eusebius” in his conspectus of the “sons of Joseph by a former wife” authorities, next after Origen and before Hilary and Gregory of Nyssa — and observed that both Eusebius and Epiphanius “derived their information mainly from Hegesippus” and “gave this account of the Lord’s brethren,” which “materially strengthens the position” Lightfoot takes on Hegesippus, that his testimony favours the Epiphanian view.9 The Church History (c. 324) is some sixty years earlier than Jerome’s statement of the cousins-theory in Against Helvidius (c. 383), and does not mention that theory.

Reception and context

  • The Clement “two Jameses” extract he quotes. In the same chapter (H.E. ii.1.4) Eusebius quotes Clement of Alexandria: “there were two Jameses: one called the Just, who was thrown from the pinnacle of the temple… and another who was beheaded.” Lightfoot reports that, “owing to an extract preserved in Eusebius,” Clement’s authority “is generally claimed for the Hieronymian view” (as though Clement recognised only two men of the name, identifying the Lord’s brother with James of the Twelve). Lightfoot reads it otherwise: Clement “says that there were two of the name of James, but he neither states nor implies that there were two only,” his “sole object was to distinguish the son of Zebedee from the Lord’s brother.”10 The words are Clement’s, quoted by Eusebius in the same chapter in which he states his own explanation at ii.1.2.
  • Relation to the three theories. The H.E. ii.1 explanation is Epiphanian in substance (James a son of Joseph, no blood-tie to Mary); the i.12 and vii.19 “supernumerary apostle” passages, like Hegesippus and the Apostolic Constitutions, keep James apart from the Twelve, which the Hieronymian reading does not, and do not by themselves distinguish the Epiphanian from the Helvidian reading. Lightfoot files Eusebius with the Epiphanian authorities. In the passages quoted here Mary is “the Virgin” who conceived by the Holy Ghost, and James is “known as a son of Joseph.”
