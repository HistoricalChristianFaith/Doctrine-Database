The Protevangelium of James on the perpetual virginity of Mary

Dates: c. 150 (an infancy narrative whose colophon names James as its writer; dated by J. B. Lightfoot “probably not later than the middle of the second century,” and the earliest extant text to give an account of Mary’s parentage, childhood, and betrothal) · Wikipedia
Summary: The Protevangelium is the earliest extant text to give the Epiphanian account of “the brethren of the Lord.” It portrays Joseph as an elderly widower who already has sons of his own when he is betrothed to Mary, and it narrates a test of Mary’s virginity after the birth. J. B. Lightfoot called it “a purely fictitious but very early narrative”; Richard Bauckham holds that the beliefs it combines do not originate with it. It attests that the belief in Mary’s perpetual virginity, and the account of the “brethren” as Joseph’s children by a former marriage, were current by the date it was written.

Claims & evidence

Joseph is an old man with sons of his own when he is betrothed to Mary (§9)

When the lot falls on Joseph to take the Virgin into his keeping, he protests that he is too old and already a father — the detail on which the Epiphanian reading rests:

And the priest said to Joseph, Thou hast been chosen by lot to take into thy keeping the virgin of the Lord. But Joseph refused, saying: I have children, and I am an old man, and she is a young girl. I am afraid lest I become a laughing-stock to the sons of Israel.

— The Protevangelium of James §9, trans. Alexander Walker (Ante-Nicene Fathers, vol. 8).1 On this account Joseph is Mary’s guardian, not in the ordinary sense her husband; the “brethren of the Lord” are his sons by an earlier wife, older step-brothers of Jesus and no blood-kin of Mary, and Mary bore no other child. Later Epiphanian writers on this timeline, down to Epiphanius, give the same account of Joseph’s earlier family.

The narrative gives Joseph sons who travel with the family, and never a daughter (§17–18)

The detail is carried through consistently. On the road to Bethlehem for the enrolment, Joseph reckons his sons among those to be registered, and it is a son who leads the ass:

And Joseph said: I shall enrol my sons, but what shall I do with this maiden? How shall I enrol her? As my wife? I am ashamed. As my daughter then? But all the sons of Israel know that she is not my daughter… And he saddled the ass, and set her upon it; and his son led it, and Joseph followed.

— The Protevangelium of James §17.2 At the cave, Joseph “found a cave there, and led her into it; and leaving his two sons beside her, he went out to seek a midwife.”3 Lightfoot notes that the work gives Joseph sons but “no daughters”; later apocryphal narratives, in his account, name Joseph’s daughters or his wife.

A midwife and Salome test the virginity after the birth (§§19–20)

The narrative affirms Mary’s virginity not only in the conception but through the birth itself. Joseph fetches a Hebrew midwife, who reaches the cave to find it filled with a great light and the newborn already at the breast; coming out, she meets a second woman, Salome, and tells her the marvel — “a virgin has brought forth — a thing which her nature admits not of.” Salome demands a bodily test, in words that echo doubting Thomas (John 20:24–29):

Then said Salome: As the Lord my God liveth, unless I thrust in my finger, and search the parts, I will not believe that a virgin has brought forth. And the midwife went in, and said to Mary: Show thyself; for no small controversy has arisen about thee. And Salome put in her finger, and cried out, and said: Woe is me for mine iniquity and mine unbelief, because I have tempted the living God; and, behold, my hand is dropping off as if burned with fire.

— The Protevangelium of James §§19–20.4 Salome’s hand is restored when, at an angel’s word, she takes up the infant — the sign healed by touching the very body whose virgin birth she had doubted. The episode is the earliest extant narrative of the virginitas in partu: the belief that Mary’s virginity was preserved through the act of childbirth, her body left physically intact by the delivery. Richard Bauckham observes that in the Protevangelium the stepbrother account of the “brethren” is bound up with two further beliefs found nowhere in the New Testament — the Davidic descent of Mary (10:1) and this miraculous birth (19:2–3), “whereby Mary’s virginal condition was unimpaired by childbearing” — three notions that all serve to glorify Mary’s virginity, though, he judges, it is “unlikely that any of these three beliefs originate with the Protevangelium of James.” The in partu strand is distinct from, but interwoven with, the brethren-question.5

In the late fourth century the virginitas in partu became the point of a controversy of its own. Where the Helvidian party denied the virginity after the birth (the “brethren” as Mary’s later sons), the monk Jovinian granted the virginal conception but denied the virginity in the birth — “She was a virgin when she conceived, but not a virgin when she brought forth” — and Ambrose, drafting the Council of Milan’s reply to Pope Siricius, met him with the shut “gate” of Ezekiel 44: “for as a virgin she both conceived and brought forth.” The in partu virginity is distinguished in Latin usage from the ante partum (the conception) and the post partum (no later children); the Salome scene of the Protevangelium is the earliest extant narrative of it.

The colophon presents “James” the writer as already grown at Herod’s death (§25)

The book closes by claiming James the Lord’s brother as its author, writing as an adult in the aftermath of Herod’s death:

And I James that wrote this history in Jerusalem, a commotion having arisen when Herod died, withdrew myself to the wilderness until the commotion in Jerusalem ceased, glorifying the Lord God, who had given me the gift and the wisdom to write this history.

— The Protevangelium of James §25.6 Lightfoot, who dates the work to the second century, notes that it represents “James the writer of the account apparently as grown up at the time of Herod’s death” — that is, as an older step-brother rather than a younger child of Mary, consistent with the account of Joseph’s sons in §9.7

Reception and context

  • Character and date of the work. Lightfoot calls the Protevangelium “a purely fictitious but very early narrative, dating probably not later than the middle of the second century.” Bauckham holds that the beliefs it combines are older than the book itself. On either view it attests that by about the middle of the second century there were Christians who held Mary to be perpetually virgin and who explained the Lord’s brethren as Joseph’s sons by a former marriage. Whether that stepbrother account preserves historical memory of Jesus’ family is the subject of a disputed reading.7
  • Who is Salome? She steps into the story at 19:3 with no introduction — not even “a woman named Salome,” simply “Salome.” Bauckham takes this to mean the writer expected his readers to know her, and argues she is Salome the daughter of Joseph, one of the sisters of Jesus (whom the same family tradition, and later Epiphanius, name Mary and Salome), travelling with her father and brothers to Bethlehem; Morton Smith identified her instead with Salome the disciple of Jesus (Mark 15:40). Later texts identify her differently: the Latin Gospel of Pseudo-Matthew makes her a second midwife beside “Zelomi,” and two Coptic narratives drop the doubting-and-healing and make the one midwife Salome a lifelong follower of Jesus, joining Salome the midwife to Salome the disciple — which Bauckham calls “a late development in the tradition.” Whether the sister-names Mary and Salome preserve early family tradition is the subject of a disputed reading.5
  • Later texts with the same account. Lightfoot traces the same Joseph-the-widower account, with “various modifications, in some cases naming Joseph’s daughters or his wife,” through the Gospel of Pseudo-Matthew, the Gospel of the Nativity of Mary, the History of Joseph the Carpenter, the Gospel of Thomas (the infancy gospel), and the Arabic Gospel of the Infancy, which he describes as “following in this track.”7
  • Relation to the three theories. This is the earliest extant text of the Epiphanian reading, which holds Mary perpetually virgin and Joseph the father of an earlier family. It does not mention the cousins-reading set out by Jerome c. 383; Lightfoot argued that these narratives “would scarcely have taken this form, if the Hieronymian view of the Lord’s brethren had been received or even known when they were written.” It is incompatible with the Helvidian reading that the brethren were Mary’s own later-born children.7
