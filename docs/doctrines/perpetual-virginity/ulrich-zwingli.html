Huldrych Zwingli on the perpetual virginity of Mary

Dates: c. 1522 (Eine Predigt von der ewig reinen Magd Maria — “A Sermon on the Ever-Virgin Maid Mary” — preached at Zürich 17 September 1522, printed 1524; Huldrych [Ulrich] Zwingli, 1484 – 1531, reformer of Zürich) · Wikipedia
Summary: A Reformation witness from Zürich. Preaching on the Virgin Mary in September 1522, the year before Luther’s That Jesus Christ Was Born a Jew, Huldrych Zwingli confesses that “this pure virgin… remained, in the birth and after it, a pure and unsullied virgin, for eternity.” The sermon speaks of the virginity itself and does not take up the identity of the “brethren of the Lord,” so he is not filed under any of the readings of the brethren.

Claims & evidence

Mary “a pure and unsullied virgin, for eternity”

Zwingli devoted a whole sermon to the mother of Christ, and in it professes the perpetual virginity. He first disclaims ever having taught anything to the contrary, then confesses the doctrine “according to the word of holy gospel”:

I have never thought, still less taught, or declared publicly, anything concerning the subject of the ever Virgin Mary, Mother of our salvation, which could be considered dishonorable, impious, unworthy or evil… I believe with all my heart according to the word of holy gospel that this pure virgin bore for us the Son of God and that she remained, in the birth and after it, a pure and unsullied virgin, for eternity.

— Eine Predigt von der ewig reinen Magd Maria (Zürich, 1522).1 The confession has three parts: Mary bore the Son of God as a virgin, remained a virgin “in the birth” (the virginitas in partu), and a virgin “after it” — the last being the point Helvidius denied. Zwingli states that he believes it “according to the word of holy gospel.”

Reception and context

  • Classification: not filed. The sermon affirms the perpetual virginity itself but does not address who the “brethren of the Lord” were, so Zwingli is not classed Epiphanian (Joseph’s sons by a former wife) or Hieronymian (cousins) — unlike Luther, who elsewhere adopts the cousin-reading, and Calvin, who glosses “brethren” as including cousins. His witness bears on the virginity, not the brethren-question.
  • Other Protestant witnesses on this timeline. Zwingli’s sermon (1522) is dated before Luther’s That Jesus Christ Was Born a Jew (1523) and Calvin’s sermon on Matthew 1:22–25 (published 1562); later comes John Wesley’s Letter to a Roman Catholic (1749). Their statements figure in the dispute over when the doctrine was held and defined; see “Is the perpetual virginity a late development?”
  • Scope of the sermon. The sermon is cited here as a witness to Mary ever-virgin; it is not cited on this timeline for the immaculate conception or the assumption. Zwingli rejected the veneration of saints from the beginning of his Zürich ministry (1519).2
