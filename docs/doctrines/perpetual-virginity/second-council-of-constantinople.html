The Second Council of Constantinople on the perpetual virginity of Mary

Dates: 553 (the fifth ecumenical council, convened at Constantinople by the emperor Justinian I and presided over by Patriarch Eutychius. Its business was the “Three Chapters” controversy and the reception of Chalcedon’s two-natures Christology; among its fourteen anathemas (the Capitula) is the confession quoted here) · Wikipedia
Summary: The council’s second anathema names Mary “ever-virgin.” Anathematizing those who deny the Word’s two nativities, the council names the mother of the second nativity “Mother of God and always a virgin” (aeiparthenos) — as a title within a Christological anathema rather than as a matter argued. Like John of Damascus, it attests the virginity itself and says nothing to decide the identity of the Lord’s brethren.

Claims & evidence

“Mother of God and always a virgin”

The council’s second capitulum anathematizes anyone who will not confess the two nativities of the Word — the eternal generation from the Father and the temporal birth from Mary. In naming the second birth it calls Mary aeiparthenos, “always a virgin”:

If anyone shall not confess that the Word of God has two nativities, the one from all eternity of the Father, without time and without body; the other in these last days, coming down from heaven and being made flesh of the holy and glorious Mary, Mother of God and always a virgin, and born of her: let him be anathema.

— Second Council of Constantinople, The Capitula of the Council, Anathema 2.1 The subject of the sentence is Christological — the single subject of the two births, against those who would divide the Word from the man born of Mary. The title by which it names her is not only “virgin” (of the conception) but always a virgin, the Greek epithet aeiparthenos; the canon states it without argument.

What the canon addresses

The canon confesses the perpetual virginity and, by placing it in the title aeiparthenos (“ever-virgin”), extends it beyond the conception to the whole of Mary’s life. It offers no account of the “brethren of the Lord,” so it does not address the choice between the Epiphanian (sons of Joseph by a former marriage) and Hieronymian (cousins) readings. Like John of Damascus, Leo the Great, and the councils around it, it attests the virginity itself and does not address the genealogy of the brethren, so it is not filed under any of the readings of them.

Reception and context

  • The title in a conciliar canon. The epithet is aeiparthenos — “ever-virgin,” not just “virgin” — and it stands in one of the council’s anathemas. Its date, 553, figures in the dispute over when the doctrine was first held and defined; see “Is the perpetual virginity a late development?”
  • The Lateran Council of 649. Where this council names the title in a single clause, the Roman Lateran Council of 649, a century later, spells the triple virginity out (before, in, and after the birth). Pope John Paul II, in a General Audience of 28 January 1987, paired the two — Constantinople II (553) “speaks of Mary as ‘ever virgin,’” and the Lateran Synod (649) teaches that her virginity “remained incorrupted, and even after the birth… remained intact.”2
  • Text and edition. The English is the translation of Henry R. Percival in the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, 2nd series, vol. 14 (1900), The Seven Ecumenical Councils.
