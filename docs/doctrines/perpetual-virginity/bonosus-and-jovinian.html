Bonosus of Sardica and Jovinian on the perpetual virginity of Mary

Dates: c. 390 (two Western contemporaries of Helvidius, both condemned at the close of the fourth century and both reported to have denied an aspect of Mary’s virginity: Jovinian, a monk who taught at Rome, condemned by synods at Rome and at Milan about 390; Bonosus, bishop of Sardica in Illyria, whose case came before the synod of Capua (dated 392 by Lightfoot, 391 by the Catholic Encyclopedia). Neither man’s own writing survives entire — Jovinian’s book is known through the answers of Ambrose and Jerome, Bonosus only through conciliar notices) · Wikipedia (Jovinian) · Wikipedia (Bonosus)
Summary: J. B. Lightfoot names two further Western voices raised against the perpetual virginity in the same decade as Helvidius, and files both in the Helvidian column of his conspectus — but they did not hold identical positions, and Lightfoot marks the second with a query. Bonosus, bishop of Sardica, is reported to have held the Helvidian reading, that the Lord’s mother bore children to Joseph after Jesus; his own words do not survive, so his teaching in his own voice is ⚠ unverified, known from Lightfoot and from a contemporary letter on his case, which records that he was “justly blamed concerning the sons of Mary.” Jovinian, a monk condemned at Rome and Milan about 390, is better attested, and his position differs: his central campaign was against the exaltation of virginity over marriage — “a virgin is no better as such than a wife” — and his teaching about Mary was not the Helvidian claim of later children but a denial of her virginity in the birth itself (in partu): “She was a virgin when she conceived, but not a virgin when she brought forth.” That formula, which differs from Helvidius’, is preserved firsthand in the synodal letter of the Council of Milan, drawn up (in its editor’s judgment) probably by Ambrose, and Jovinian’s wider case was answered at length by Jerome in the two books Against Jovinianus.

Claims & evidence

Bonosus of Sardica — the Helvidian reading and the synod of Capua

Bonosus left no book, and what survives of his teaching on the brethren is only what a later hand reports. Lightfoot gathers him with Helvidius as one who advocated the same reading — that the “brethren of the Lord” were the true sons of Joseph and Mary:

Somewhere about the year 380 Helvidius, who resided in Rome, published a treatise in which he maintained that the Lord’s brethren were sons of Joseph and Mary. He seems to have succeeded in convincing a considerable number of persons, for contemporary writers speak of the Helvidians as a party. These views were moreover advocated by Bonosus, bishop of Sardica in Illyria, about the same time, and apparently also by Jovinianus a monk probably of Milan. The former was condemned by a synod assembled at Capua (AD 392), and the latter by synods held at Rome and at Milan (about AD 390).

— J. B. Lightfoot, “The Brethren of the Lord,” §22.1 Because Bonosus’ own statement of the position is lost, his teaching on the brethren in his own words remains ⚠ unverified. The case itself is recorded in a letter preserved among Ambrose’s letters and ascribed by its editor, following Tillemont, probably to Siricius of Rome. It records that the synod of Capua had assigned the judgment to “the Macedonian Bishops, who, with the Bishop of Thessalonica, should judge of his acts and writings,” and writes to those bishops:

Assuredly we cannot deny that he is justly blamed concerning the sons of Mary, and that your Holiness deservedly repudiated the opinion that from the same Virgin womb, of which according to the flesh Christ was born, other offspring was produced.

— “Letter on the case of Bonosus,” §§1, 3 (A.D. 392 or 393).2 Lightfoot describes Bonosus as “condemned by a synod assembled at Capua”; the letter’s editor records that the synod “decided that it should be referred to the Bishops of Macedonia.”2

Jovinian — not later children, but virginity denied in the birth (in partu)

The synod that condemned Jovinian distinguished his position on Mary from the Helvidian denial. As they report it, his claim was not that Mary bore other children afterward, but that she was not a virgin in the act of giving birth — that the birth was an ordinary parturition which opened the womb. The synod of Milan’s reply to Pope Siricius, drawn up (in its editor’s judgment) “in all probability” by Ambrose himself, quotes the Jovinianist formula and answers it:

How great is the madness of their dismal barkings, that the same persons should say that Christ could not be born of a virgin, and yet assert that women, after having given birth to human pledges, remain virgins?… But from their perverse ways they are induced to say ‘She was a virgin when she conceived, but not a virgin when she brought forth.’ Could she then conceive as a virgin, and yet not be able to bring forth as a virgin, when conception always precedes, and birth follows?

— Ambrose (with Sabinus, Bassianus, and the rest), Letter 42.4, the reply of the Council of Milan to Siricius (c. 390).3 Ambrose answers it with the proof-text he also uses in his treatise De Institutione Virginis (c. 392) — the “gate of the sanctuary… which remains shut” of Ezekiel 44: “Is not Mary this gate…? therefore it shall be shut after birth; for as a virgin she both conceived and brought forth.” The editor of the letter observes that the council “argue in especial detail against his argument with regard to the Virgin Mary, which differs from that of Helvidius and other assailants of the” ever-virgin (ἀεὶ παρθένος) — so that Jovinian’s Mariology is a denial of the virginity in partu, a distinct moment from the post partum virginity Helvidius and Bonosus denied.

The wider campaign — “a virgin is no better than a wife”

Mary was only one front. Jovinian’s book advanced four propositions, of which the first — the levelling of virginity with marriage — was the engine of the rest. Jerome, who answered the whole in two books, sets it out in Jovinian’s own terms:

He says that “virgins, widows, and married women, who have been once passed through the layer of Christ, if they are on a par in other respects, are of equal merit.”

— Jerome, Against Jovinianus i.3.4 Jerome states what he took to be Jovinian’s aim: “his object in proclaiming the excellence of marriage was only to disparage virginity. For when the less is put upon a level with the greater, the lower profits by comparison, but the higher suffers wrong.” The prefatory note to the English translation records that Jovinian also “held the birth of our Lord to have been by a ‘true parturition’”: his Marian claim concerned the birth, not later children.4

Lightfoot’s classification

In the conspectus that closes his dissertation, Lightfoot sorts every witness under the reading he took it to support, and places both men in the “sons of Joseph and Mary” column with Tertullian, Helvidius, and the Antidicomarianites — but appends a query to Jovinian’s name:

A. Sons of Joseph and Mary: Tertullian; Helvidius; Bonosus; Jovinianus (?); Antidicomarianites.

— Lightfoot, conspectus of authorities.5 The parenthetical “(?)” marks Lightfoot’s uncertainty about Jovinian; as Ambrose’s synodal letter reports it, Jovinian’s denial of the virginity fell on the birth, not on later childbearing. Bonosus is filed there without a query; for him only the reports survive.

Context and reception

  • One controversy, four fronts. Jovinian’s book advanced four propositions, which Jerome enumerates at Against Jovinianus i.3 (the first quoted above), and the controversy appears on several timelines. The first — that baptized virgins, widows and wives are of equal merit — is the levelling of virginity with marriage, answered by Jerome on the remarriage timeline and, on the Marian front, here. The fourth — “one reward in the kingdom of heaven for all who have kept their baptismal vow” — is the equal-recompense thesis from which Jerome drew the charge that Jovinian taught the Stoic parity of sins; see did Jovinian teach that all sins are equal? The second (the baptized cannot be overthrown by the devil) and third (no difference between fasting and thankful eating) complete the list. Jerome presents all four as serving one aim, the disparagement of virginity.
  • Three moments of the virginity. The doctrine classically distinguishes Mary’s virginity before the birth (ante partum, the virginal conception), in the birth (in partu, that the birth left her physically intact), and after the birth (post partum, that she bore no other children). The Helvidian party — Helvidius, Bonosus, the Antidicomarianites — denied the post partum virginity by making the “brethren” Mary’s later sons. Jovinian denied the in partu virginity, granting the virginal conception but insisting the delivery was ordinary. Ambrose’s synodal letter and Lightfoot’s query both keep the two positions apart. On this timeline the earliest extant narrative of the virginity in partu is the Salome episode of the Protevangelium of James (§§19–20, c. 150), where a doubting Salome tests Mary’s virginity after the birth and her hand withers; Ambrose defends the same point with the shut “gate” of Ezekiel 44.
  • Attestation is uneven. Bonosus’ view in his own words is ⚠ unverified: no text of his survives, only Lightfoot’s notice and the letter on his case. Jovinian’s Marian claim, by contrast, is verified at first hand — his very formula (“a virgin when she conceived, but not a virgin when she brought forth”) is quoted in Ambrose’s synodal letter — and his four propositions are preserved verbatim by Jerome. His own book is lost; his opponents quote him.
  • Bonosus and the “Bonosians.” Bonosus of Sardica gave his name to a later sect, the Bonosiaci, whom the sources associate both with his denial of the perpetual virginity and with an adoptionist Christology; the two charges are variously reported, and some of the later notices may conflate him with other controversialists of the same name. The earliest record is the letter on his case, which blames him “concerning the sons of Mary”; Lightfoot likewise associates him with the Helvidian reading. The letter’s editor adds that the Bonosians “were accused of Photinianism” and that “the Helvidian doctrines of which this letter speaks are the most clearly ascertained of their errors.”2
  • Placement on the timeline. These two are dated between Helvidius and Jerome (both c. 383) and Ambrose’s De Institutione Virginis (c. 392). Ambrose’s treatise answers those who denied the perpetual virginity; that it was written against the circle of Bonosus and Jovinian in particular is ⚠ unverified. The synodal letter quoted here is Ambrose’s earlier answer to Jovinian. Jerome’s Against Jovinianus (393) answered Jovinian’s book, as his Against Helvidius (383) had answered Helvidius.
