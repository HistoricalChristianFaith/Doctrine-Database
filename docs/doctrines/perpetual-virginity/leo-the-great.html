Leo the Great on the perpetual virginity of Mary

Dates: c. 450 (Leo I, “the Great,” c. 400–461, a Roman deacon who became bishop of Rome in 440 and held the see until his death. He is best known for the dogmatic Tome (Letter 28, 449) read at the Council of Chalcedon in 451, which set out the two natures in the one person of Christ; a large body of his Christmas, Epiphany, and Passion sermons survives. The Christmas sermon quoted here belongs to his episcopate and is conventionally placed in the 440s) · Wikipedia
Summary: Preaching at Rome on the Feast of the Nativity, Leo the Great states the perpetual virginity in its full threefold reach — “a Virgin conceived, a Virgin bare, and a Virgin she remained” — and rests it on the Christology: because the One born was God as well as man, His birth kept “the primal virginity of the Mother” intact. The formula matches Augustine’s virgo concepit, virgo peperit, virgo permansit, and Leo states it in the years around Chalcedon (451). Like Peter Chrysologus, Leporius, Athanasius, and Chromatius, he attests the perpetual virginity itself and does not address the identity of the Lord’s brethren.

Claims & evidence

“A Virgin conceived, a Virgin bare, and a Virgin she remained”

Explaining what he calls the “new character of the birth of Christ,” Leo sets Christ’s origin apart from ordinary human generation: the Holy Spirit came upon Mary and the power of the Most High overshadowed her (Luke 1:35), so that “the origin is different but the nature like: not by intercourse with man but by the power of God was it brought about.” On that ground he states Mary’s virginity through all three moments:

The origin is different but the nature like: not by intercourse with man but by the power of God was it brought about: for a Virgin conceived, a Virgin bare, and a Virgin she remained.

— Leo the Great, Sermon 22 (On the Feast of the Nativity, II), §2.1 The three verbs name the three moments: virginity ante partum (in conceiving), in partu (in bearing), and post partum (remaining so). Leo immediately points the reader away from the biology of the birth and toward its author — “Consider here not the condition of her that bare but the will of Him that was born” — a move Peter Chrysologus also makes when he refuses to liken Mary’s childbearing to that of “other women.”

The virginity grounded in the Christology

Leo presents Mary’s intact virginity as a consequence of who her Son is. Because He came “to do away with, not to endure, our pollutions,” it “behoved Him to be born by a new order, who brought to men’s bodies the new gift of unsullied purity”; and so “the uncorrupt nature of Him that was born had to guard the primal virginity of the Mother, and the infused power of the Divine Spirit had to preserve in spotlessness and holiness” her womb.2 On Leo’s account the purity of the Son preserves the virginity of the Mother. His dogmatic Tome, read at Chalcedon in 451, likewise has Christ “born of the Holy Spirit and the Virgin Mary” with “the reality of His mother’s virginity” unimpaired.3

What the sermon addresses

Leo affirms the perpetual virginity, including the virginity in partu and post partum, but offers no account of the “brethren of the Lord.” His witness bears on the virginity itself and not on the choice between the Epiphanian (sons of Joseph by a former marriage) and Hieronymian (cousins) readings of the brethren. Like Peter Chrysologus, Athanasius, Didymus, Chromatius, and Leporius, he attests that Mary was and remained ever-virgin and does not address the genealogy of the brethren.

Reception and context

  • Leo and Chalcedon. Leo’s Tome (449) was read at the Council of Chalcedon (451) and acclaimed there, the acts of the council recording the bishops’ cry “Peter has spoken thus through Leo.”4 His Nativity sermons date from the same years.
  • The threefold formula. “Virgin in conceiving, in bearing, and remaining” is the Western triad virginitas ante partum, in partu, post partum. Augustine’s Christmas sermons state it as virgo concepit, virgo peperit, virgo permansit (Sermon 186); Leo at Rome and his contemporary Peter Chrysologus at Ravenna both use the formula in the decades around Chalcedon.
  • Text and edition. The English is the translation of Charles Lett Feltoe in the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, 2nd series, vol. 12 (1895). The Latin sermons (Tractatus septem et nonaginta) are critically edited by A. Chavasse in the Corpus Christianorum, Series Latina 138, 138A (Brepols, 1973).
