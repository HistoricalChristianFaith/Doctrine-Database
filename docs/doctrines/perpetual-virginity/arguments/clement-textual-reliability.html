Does the Latin transmission of Clement of Alexandria’s notes affect the weight of his witness?

Disputed reading · on the perpetual virginity of Mary · witness: Clement of Alexandria

Related readings: Did Tertullian hold that Mary bore children after Jesus?

The passage

Jude, who wrote the Catholic Epistle, the brother of the sons of Joseph, and very religious, whilst knowing the near relationship of the Lord, yet did not say that he himself was His brother. But what said he? “Jude, a servant of Jesus Christ,”—of Him as Lord; but “the brother of James.” For this is true; he was His brother, (the son) of Joseph.

— Clement of Alexandria, Comments on the Epistle of Jude, from the Latin translation of Cassiodorus (the Adumbrationes), source.1

But, as appears, many even down to our own time regard Mary, on account of the birth of her child, as having been in the puerperal state, although she was not. For some say that, after she brought forth, she was found, when examined, to be a virgin.

— Clement of Alexandria, Stromata VII.16, source.2

The question

Clement’s statements bearing on Mary’s virginity survive in two forms: the Stromata, and the Adumbrationes, Latin notes on the Catholic Epistles from his Hypotyposeis, which Cassiodorus in the sixth century had translated with what he judged offensive matter removed. What is disputed is how far that Latin transmission limits the weight of Clement’s witness, and whether the caution applies to the Stromata passage as well. The second reading below is stated in a popular Protestant publication; its academic literature was not located.

The Latin notes keep Clement’s own statements on Mary’s virginity

On this reading the Latin adaptation does not touch what Clement says about the brethren and Mary’s virginity, and the Stromata passage is a separate text.

Held by: J. B. Lightfoot (Anglican, “The Brethren of the Lord,” 1865); Philip J. Donnelly, S.J. (Catholic, in Juniper B. Carol, ed., Mariology, vol. 2, 1957).3

Lightfoot identifies the Adumbrationes with the notes Cassiodorus describes, and holds that the brethren-statement in the note on Jude survived the editing as Clement wrote it: “the statement about the relationship of Jude must be Clement’s own and cannot have been inserted by Cassiodorus, since Cassiodorus in common with the Latin Church would naturally hold the Hieronymian hypothesis.” The note makes Jude “the brother of the sons of Joseph,” the stepbrother account that the Latin editor did not hold.4

Donnelly, writing in a Catholic Mariology, grants a general caution about the Latin while holding that it does not reach Mary’s virginity: “We cannot rely absolutely on this text, since it is a translated adaptation, with the expressed intention of expurgating anything that might be offensive… On the other hand, there is no indication that in the matter of Mary’s virginity there was any emendation of Clement’s own words.” His caution is attached to the Adumbrationes; he cites the Stromata passage separately, from the Greek of Migne’s Patrologia Graeca.3 On this reading Clement’s two texts together report that in Egypt at the end of the second century Mary was held to have remained a virgin in the birth, and the brethren to be Joseph’s sons.

The Latin transmission makes Clement’s witness uncertain

On this reading Clement’s apparent support for the perpetual virginity reaches us through an adaptation made to remove offensive matter, so it cannot be relied on as his own.

Held by: Thorncrown Ministries (“Roman Catholics and Their Queen, Part 4,” 2017), citing the caution in Carol’s Mariology.5

Thorncrown writes that Clement “initially appears to support Mary’s perpetual virginity,” but that “we cannot completely trust the document because the Greek original is lost, and the Latin version dates to the 6th century and was written specifically to correct offending sentiments.” Cassiodorus describes his purpose: he had Clement’s notes translated so that, with certain stumbling-blocks removed (exclusis quibusdam offendiculis), his teaching, purified, might be drawn on more safely.6 A text edited for doctrinal safety by a sixth-century churchman, with its Greek lost, is on this reading a witness at one remove, whose wording on Mary may be the editor’s. Thorncrown sets Clement among the “unreliable and questionable early sources” and counts “the only remaining early support” for the doctrine as “from the latter part of the 4th century and later,” beginning with Hilary of Poitiers, Athanasius and Epiphanius.5
