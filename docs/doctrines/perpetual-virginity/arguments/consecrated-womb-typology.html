Did the belief rest on a typology of the consecrated womb, the new tomb, and the shut gate of Ezekiel 44:2, as well as on esteem for virginity?

Disputed reading · on the perpetual virginity of Mary · witness: Origen

Related readings: Did belief in the perpetual virginity arise in Gnostic circles or elsewhere in early Christianity? · Is the perpetual virginity a late development — when was it first held and first defined?

The passage

…it is not altogether correct to represent this belief as suggested solely by the false asceticism of the early Church which exalted virginity at the expense of married life. It appears in fact to be due quite as much to another sentiment which the fathers fantastically expressed by a comparison between the conception and the burial of our Lord. As after death His body was placed in a sepulcher ‘wherein never man before was laid,’ so it seemed fitting that the womb consecrated by His presence should not thenceforth have borne any offspring of man. It may be added also, that the Epiphanian view prevailed especially in Palestine where there was less disposition than elsewhere to depreciate married life, and prevailed too at a time when extreme ascetic views had not yet mastered the Church at large.

— J. B. Lightfoot, “The Brethren of the Lord” (1865), in the discussion of the Epiphanian theory, source.1

…it became him, who was unlike other dead people… and who was, so to speak, a new dead man, to be laid in a new and clean tomb, in order that, as his birth was purer than any other (because he was born not in the way of ordinary generation but of a virgin), his burial also might have the purity symbolically indicated in his body being deposited in a sepulcher that was new… quarried and hewn out of one rock, united together in all its parts.

— Origen, Against Celsus ii.69, on the new tomb “wherein was never man yet laid” (John 19:41; Luke 23:53), source.2

The question

Origen sets the virgin birth beside the new tomb, and from the third century the fathers read the shut gate of Ezekiel 44:2 (“This gate shall be shut… because the Lord, the God of Israel, hath entered in by it”) as a figure of Mary’s virginity, a typological argument for the doctrine made by Jerome, Ambrose, Augustine, Theodoret and John of Damascus among others.3 What is disputed is the historical motive behind the early belief in Mary’s lifelong virginity: whether it arose from a sense of what befitted the mother of the Lord, or chiefly from the esteem for virginity that grew in the ascetic movement. Others hold that the belief was received from the apostolic church: John Paul II teaches that “the Church has always professed her belief in the perpetual virginity of Mary,” and Pomazansky reads the Gospels “as the ancient Christians also understood it”;4 that question is treated at Is the perpetual virginity a late development?

A belief resting on what befitted the womb that bore Christ

On this reading the belief was held because the womb consecrated by the Lord was thought fit to bear no other child, a sense the fathers expressed through the figure of the new tomb.

Held by: Origen (Commentary on Matthew, c. 248); J. B. Lightfoot (Anglican, “The Brethren of the Lord,” 1865); Michael Pomazansky (Orthodox, Orthodox Dogmatic Theology, Eng. 1994); Pope John Paul II (Catholic, General Audience, 28 August 1996).5

Origen reports that those who held the stepbrother account “wish to preserve the honour of Mary in virginity to the end, so that that body of hers which was appointed to minister to the Word… might not know intercourse with a man after that the Holy Ghost came into her,” and in Against Celsus he sets Christ’s birth “of a virgin” beside his burial in a new tomb.6,2 Lightfoot names this sentiment as a cause of the belief “quite as much” as asceticism: “it seemed fitting that the womb consecrated by His presence should not thenceforth have borne any offspring of man.” He adds two historical observations: the Epiphanian view “prevailed especially in Palestine where there was less disposition than elsewhere to depreciate married life,” and it prevailed “at a time when extreme ascetic views had not yet mastered the Church at large.”1

Pomazansky binds the ever-virginity to the Incarnation itself: “Two dogmas concerning the Mother of God are bound up, in closest fashion, with the dogma of God the Words becoming man. They are: a) Her Ever-virginity, and b) Her name of Theotokos.” John Paul II grounds it in the same motherhood: “Her prerogative is the consequence of her divine motherhood which totally consecrated her to Christ’s mission of redemption.”4 On this reading the belief follows from the conviction that Mary’s motherhood of the Lord set her apart for him alone.

A belief that grew with the ascetic esteem for virginity

On this reading the belief in Mary’s lifelong virginity was carried and extended by the early Christian exaltation of virginity, which reached its height in the fourth-century monastic movement.

Held by: Philip Schaff (Protestant church historian, History of the Christian Church, vol. 3); the Encyclopedia of Early Christianity (ed. Everett Ferguson, 2nd ed., 1999), as quoted on Wikipedia.7

Schaff traces the extension of Mary’s virginity to her whole life to the monastic estimate of virginity: “the rise of monasticism supplied the development of Mariology a further motive in the enhanced estimate of virginity, without which no true holiness could be conceived. Hence the virginity of Mary, which is unquestioned for the part of her life before the birth of Christ, came to be extended to her whole life, and her marriage with the aged Joseph to be regarded as a mere protectorate, and, therefore, only a nominal marriage.” He judged the belief “so essential to the whole ascetic system, that it became from this time an article of the Catholic faith.”7 The Encyclopedia of Early Christianity counts “the growing acceptance of belief in the perpetual virginity of Mary” as one of “two gauges of the antisexual tendency of early Christian theology,” beside “the campaign against second marriage,” and holds that the belief, “stimulated by the second-century Protevangelium of James, served to bolster the ascetic movement of the fourth century.”8 On this reading the belief’s growth from the second century to its fourth-century prominence tracks the rising value placed on virginity over marriage.
