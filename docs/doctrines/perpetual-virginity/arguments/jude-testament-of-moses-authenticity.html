Does Jude 9’s allusion to the Testament of Moses bear on the letter’s authorship?

Disputed reading · on the perpetual virginity of Mary · witness: the Epistle of Jude

Related readings: Does the Epistle of Jude’s pesher-style exegesis bear on its authorship by Jude the Lord’s brother? · Does the letter’s title “Master” (despotēs) for Christ, beside the relatives’ name desposynoi, bear on its authorship? · Does the Enochic structure of the Lukan genealogy link it to Jude’s circle, and does that bear on the letter’s authorship?

The passage

Yet Michael the archangel, when contending with the devil he disputed about the body of Moses, durst not bring against him a railing accusation, but said, The Lord rebuke thee.

— Jude 9 (KJV), source.1

Whereas angels, which are greater in power and might, bring not railing accusation against them before the Lord.

— 2 Peter 2:11 (KJV), source.1

The question

Jude 9 alludes, without explanation, to a story of the archangel Michael disputing with the devil over Moses’ body; the parallel in 2 Peter 2:11 omits the story. Clement of Alexandria and other early writers named the source as the Assumption of Moses; Richard Bauckham assigns Jude’s version to the lost ending of the Testament of Moses, a Palestinian Jewish work. What is disputed is whether Jude’s easy use of this source places the letter in the Palestinian circle of Jude the Lord’s brother, marks a Jewish-Christian author of a later generation, or rests on a source too uncertain to bear on authorship. Raymond E. Brown (1997) reported scholarly “debate about Jas and Jude, with the majority favoring pseudonymity.”2

A Jewish-Christian author of a later generation, writing in Jude’s name

On this reading the use of a Moses apocryphon shows a Jewish-Christian author steeped in apocalyptic tradition, and the letter’s outlook places him after the apostles.

Held by: Werner Georg Kümmel (Protestant, Introduction to the New Testament; German 17th ed. 1973); Bart D. Ehrman (Forgery and Counterforgery, 2013); Jörg Frey (commentary on Jude and 2 Peter, 2015), as reported by reviewers.3

Kümmel reads verse 9 as evidence of the author’s formation: “The author was presumably a Jewish Christian,” one who knew the “Ascension of Moses (9) and the Enoch Apocalypse (14), and the Jewish legends (9, 11).” Such knowledge, on this reading, fits a Jewish-Christian teacher wherever and whenever he wrote; the date is fixed by the author’s stance toward the apostles. He “speaks of the apostles like a pupil from a time long afterward” (v. 17), and the letter’s appeal to a deposit of faith and to collected predictions “points to a late phase of primitive Christianity,” so that “Jude must be considered a pseudonymous writing.” Ehrman likewise finds the apostles “referred to as living in the past” (Jude 17–18) and the book “produced relatively late in the first century.” Frey, as his reviewers report, argues against Bauckham that the identification with the Lord’s brother is “extremely improbable” and dates the letter roughly to 100–120. On this reading the Moses legend belonged to the stock of tradition that Jewish Christians carried into the second generation, and the writer drew on it as naturally as on Enoch.

A Palestinian source, known in the circle of the Lord’s brother

On this reading Jude 9 draws on a Palestinian Jewish work that the wider early church did not know, which places the letter in the Palestinian Jewish-Christian circles of the Lord’s family.

Held by: Richard Bauckham (Anglican, Jude and the Relatives of Jesus in the Early Church, 1990); Gene L. Green (Jude and 2 Peter, 2008).4

“Clearly Jude is here referring to a story well-known to him and presumably also to his readers,” Bauckham writes. From later witnesses he reconstructs the story and assigns it to the lost ending of the Testament of Moses, a Palestinian Jewish work of the early first century; only this version turns on the devil’s slander (blasphēmia), “and it is that catchword… which Jude picks up (vv 8, 10).”5 The Alexandrian fathers who cite “the Assumption of Moses” for Jude 9 knew, on his account, a different and later second-century story, while “the Testament is never referred to by name in any document of the early church outside the lists of apocryphal books.”6 When 2 Peter rewrites Jude 9 it drops the story, which Bauckham attributes to “his and his readers’ ignorance of the story,” marking a different milieu.7 He rests the dependence on Jude 9 alone, concluding “with Laperrousaz… against Charles” that further allusions are “not proven.”8 His chapter closes:

That Jude knows and can assume his readers’ familiarity with the Testament of Moses suggests, therefore, a different milieu from those from which most extant early Christian literature comes. This Palestinian Jewish work would have [been] best known in the Palestinian Jewish Christian circles from which an authentic letter of Jude the Lord’s brother would come.

Green agrees that “Jude’s use of the Testament of Moses (v. 9 and comments) assumes that the readers understood the allusion,” a writing “best known in the Palestinian Jewish Christian circles,” and accepts the letter as Jude’s own.9 On this reading the unexplained allusion is the mark of an author writing to readers who shared his Palestinian sources.

The source of Jude 9 cannot be fixed on the surviving texts

On this reading no surviving text contains Jude’s story, so its source, and any conclusion drawn from it about the letter’s origin, remains a reconstruction.

Held by: James T. Dennison Jr. (Kerux, 1995).10

Dennison writes that “any suggestion that Jude is citing the Testament or Assumption of Moses in v. 9 must reckon with the fact that we, as yet, have no definitive text of that apocryphal work. Hence Bauckham’s (and many other’s) reading must remain speculative.” The surviving Testament of Moses is an incomplete Latin text in a sixth-century Milan manuscript, and the dispute over Moses’ body appears in no extant copy of it; the story is known from later witnesses, among them the Palaea Historica, the Slavonic Life of Moses and catena fragments, and the early writers who named a source for Jude 9, Clement of Alexandria among them, named the Assumption of Moses.5,2 On this reading Jude’s source is an open question, known only through the reconstruction of a lost ending, and the allusion is read for what the letter itself says.
