Do the names Mary and Salome given to Jesus’ sisters preserve early family tradition?

Disputed reading · on the perpetual virginity of Mary · witness: Epiphanius of Salamis, with the Gospel of Philip and the Protevangelium of James

Related readings: Does the second-century stepbrother tradition preserve historical memory of Jesus’ family?

The passage

had a son named Joses—then Simeon after him, then Judah, and two daughters, one named Mary and one, Salome

— Epiphanius (c. 377), of Joseph’s children by his first wife, Panarion 78.8.1, trans. Frank Williams, source.1

There were three who always walked with the Lord: Mary, his mother, and her sister, and Magdalene, the one who was called his companion. His sister and his mother and his companion were each a Mary.

— Gospel of Philip 59:6–11, trans. Wesley W. Isenberg, source.2

The question

The Gospels record that Jesus had sisters — “are not his sisters here with us?” (Mark 6:3 ∥ Matthew 13:56) — but do not name them. Epiphanius names them Mary and Salome, as Joseph’s daughters by a former wife (Panarion 78.8.1; 78.9.6; in Ancoratus 60.1 the names appear as Anna and Salome).1 What is disputed is whether those names reach back, through earlier texts, to the memory of Palestinian Jewish-Christian circles, or are first securely attested with Epiphanius in the fourth century. Sisters appear on every account of the Lord’s brethren (as Mary’s daughters, as Joseph’s, or as kinswomen), so the dispute concerns the age and source of the family tradition that Epiphanius reports, not the sisters’ parentage.

The names preserve Palestinian Jewish-Christian memory

On this reading the names Mary and Salome were carried by tradition from the Palestinian circles that knew Jesus’ family, and are attested before Epiphanius.

Held by: Richard Bauckham (Anglican, Jude and the Relatives of Jesus in the Early Church, 1990; “The Relatives of Jesus,” Themelios).3

Bauckham finds two witnesses earlier than Epiphanius. The Valentinian Gospel of Philip, probably of the third century, is consistent only if the “her sister” of its first clause is read as “his sister,” as the second clause has it; so “the original reference must have been to Jesus’ sister Mary.”2 The Protevangelium of James (late second century) brings “Salome” into the Nativity (19:3) with no introduction, which “requires that the writer expected his readers to know who she was”; “the best explanation is that she is Salome the daughter of Joseph.”4

His case for Palestinian origin is onomastic. Of 247 Palestinian Jewish women of 330 B.C.–A.D. 200 whose names are known, 61 were called Salome and 58 Mary, together 47.7%; in Mayer’s wider list there are “at least 21 women named Mary living in the diaspora, but only two women named Salome… Salome seems to have been a peculiarly Palestinian name.”5 A second-century Gentile Christian inventing names “may well have called one sister Mary, but is unlikely to have called the other Salome,” and “if the tradition is therefore to be traced back to Palestinian Jewish Christian circles, the chances of its being an accurate historical tradition are relatively high.” He adds that the names “must antedate their identification with the women of Mark 15:40,” and that the later writers who repeat them depend on Epiphanius, so that the names stayed within one branch of tradition.4 On this reading the sisters “were also known figures in the early church”: “if, as we suggested, the names of sisters of Jesus—Mary and Salome—were correctly preserved in tradition,” they played some part, more modest than their brothers’, in the early Christian movement.3

The names are first securely given by Epiphanius

On this reading the earlier texts are too uncertain to carry the names behind Epiphanius, whose fourth-century list is the earliest account that yields a set of names for the sisters.

Held by: D. P. Curtin (editor-in-chief of the Scriptorium Project, “The Sisters of Christ,” Public Orthodoxy, 2026).6

Curtin, writing from the Epiphanian account of the family, which he takes as “the position held universally by early Byzantine sources,” notes how little the earliest record says of the sisters: they “are never alluded to in the works of Paul, Ignatius, or any of the ante-Nicene Fathers.” Of the Gnostic witness to a sister named Mary he writes that such texts “stop short of offering substantial details” and that “this testimony must also be treated with caution, as Gnostic texts are of dubious provenance and are often centuries removed from the life of Christ.” J. B. Lightfoot read the Protevangelium as giving Joseph “sons of his own (§ 9…) but no daughters (§ 17…)”, and reported that Epiphanius “alleges the authority of Scripture” for the names Mary and Salome.7

“It is only in the 4th century,” Curtin writes, “that Epiphanius of Salamis gives them proper names, citing two or potentially three sisters: Maria, Anna, and Salome. These names are all extremely common given names for Jewish women of the first century, and Epiphanius provides no additional details.” Later sources diverge: the History of Joseph the Carpenter names the sisters Assia and Lydia, and the Salome of the tomb narrative is identified in various sources as the mother of the sons of Zebedee, as an aunt of the Lord, or as his sister. From this he concludes that “the names given by Epiphanius are, therefore, the earliest account that can render a reasonable set of names for the sisters,” and that “what space they truly occupied in the early church, between familial reality and devotional memory, is likely lost to history.”6 On this reading the sisters’ existence rests on the Gospels, and their names on Epiphanius’s fourth-century report.
