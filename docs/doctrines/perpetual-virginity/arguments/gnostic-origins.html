Did belief in the perpetual virginity arise in Gnostic circles or elsewhere in early Christianity?

Disputed reading · on the perpetual virginity of Mary · witness: the Protevangelium of James

Related readings: Is the perpetual virginity a late development — when was it first held and first defined? · Did the belief rest on a typology of the consecrated womb, the new tomb, and the shut gate of Ezekiel 44:2, as well as on esteem for virginity? · Does the second-century stepbrother tradition preserve historical memory of Jesus’ family?

The passage

“Are you aware that such doctrines as perpetual virginity and bodily assumption have their origin in gnosticism, not Christianity, and have no foundation in apostolic doctrine or practice?”

— James White, “Reposting a Top Ten List on Roman Catholicism” (Alpha and Omega Ministries).1

The question

The earliest extant text that guards Mary’s virginity after the birth is the Protevangelium of James (c. 150); Richard Bauckham places it among six second-century works that are “either certainly or quite probably of Syrian origin,” forming “a complex of traditions which dates from the very beginning of the second century in Syria.”2 In the same century Irenaeus reports a Valentinian teaching that the Saviour “passed through Mary just as water through a tube.”3 What is disputed is the milieu in which the belief in Mary’s lifelong virginity arose: the church at large from its beginnings, an ascetic current that prized virginity, or Gnostic circles. For the third reading below, academic literature was not located; it is stated here by a popular apologist.

A belief of the early church, held against the Gnostics

On this reading the belief belonged to the church from its early generations and was held by the very writers who opposed the Gnostic teachers.

Held by: Jerome (Against Helvidius, c. 383); Florentine Bechtel (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1907); Michael Pomazansky (Orthodox, Orthodox Dogmatic Theology, Eng. 1994); Pope John Paul II (Catholic, General Audience, 28 August 1996).4

Jerome answered Helvidius by appealing to the writers of the second century who had opposed the Gnostic and adoptionist teachers: “Might I not array against you the whole series of ancient writers? Ignatius, Polycarp, Irenaeus, Justin Martyr, and many other apostolic and eloquent men, who against Ebion, Theodotus of Byzantium, and Valentinus, held these same views.”5 Bechtel writes of “the great antiquity of the belief in the perpetual virginity of Mary,” and Pomazansky reads the Gospels’ brothers and sisters of Jesus as kin, “as the ancient Christians also understood it.” John Paul II taught that “the Church has always professed her belief in the perpetual virginity of Mary,” and that “the early Christians expressed this conviction of faith in the Greek term aeiparthenos.”4 On this reading the belief belonged to the church of the writers who opposed the Gnostic teachers, and the later title aeiparthenos names a conviction those early Christians already held.

A belief elaborated in an ascetic milieu

On this reading the belief took shape and spread with the early Christian esteem for virginity, which reached its height in the fourth-century ascetic movement.

Held by: Philip Schaff (Protestant church historian, History of the Christian Church, vol. 3); the Encyclopedia of Early Christianity (ed. Everett Ferguson, 2nd ed., 1999), as quoted on Wikipedia.6

Schaff traces the belief’s extension to Mary’s whole life to the monastic estimate of virginity: “the rise of monasticism supplied the development of Mariology a further motive in the enhanced estimate of virginity, without which no true holiness could be conceived. Hence the virginity of Mary, which is unquestioned for the part of her life before the birth of Christ, came to be extended to her whole life.” He judged the belief “so essential to the whole ascetic system, that it became from this time an article of the Catholic faith.”6 The Encyclopedia of Early Christianity counts the belief among “two gauges of the antisexual tendency of early Christian theology”: “the campaign against second marriage and the growing acceptance of belief in the perpetual virginity of Mary.” It adds that “the theory of Mary’s perpetual virginity, stimulated by the second-century Protevangelium of James, served to bolster the ascetic movement of the fourth century.”7 On this reading the belief’s second-century literary form and its fourth-century rise to a binding article both belong to the history of Christian asceticism.

A belief with its origin in Gnosticism

On this reading the perpetual virginity has no apostolic foundation and entered Christianity from outside it, from Gnostic sources.

Held by: James White (Reformed apologist, Alpha and Omega Ministries; Mary—Another Redeemer?, 1998).1

White sets the belief beside the Bodily Assumption as doctrines that “have their origin in gnosticism, not Christianity, and have no foundation in apostolic doctrine or practice.” The same list asks whether the reader has “considered how completely unbiblical and a-historical is the entire complex of doctrines and dogmas related to Mary,” and whether he believes “the Apostles taught that Mary was immaculately conceived, and that she was a perpetual virgin.” In his longer treatments he argues that the doctrine “came from works outside the New Testament,” chiefly the Protoevangelium of James, carried by an ascetic devaluing of marriage.8 On this reading the belief’s earliest carriers are second-century works outside the New Testament, and its source lies outside the apostolic teaching.
