Did the cousin reading have witnesses before Jerome, and did Jerome later let it go?

Disputed reading · on the perpetual virginity of Mary · witness: Jerome

Related readings: Does Hegesippus call Symeon “a second cousin” of the Lord, or “the second [bishop]” (Eusebius, Church History iv.22.4)? · Does the report that James the Lord’s brother and the apostle James son of Alphaeus were both stoned at Jerusalem bear on whether they were one man? · Does the second-century stepbrother tradition preserve historical memory of Jesus’ family?

The passage

Maria mater Domini: Maria Cleophae, sive Alphei uxor, quae fuit mater Jacobi episcopi et apostoli et Symonis et Thadei et cujusdam Joseph: Maria Salome uxor Zebedei mater Joannis evangelistae et Jacobi: Maria Magdalene: istae quatuor in Evangelio reperiuntur.

— the opening of a Latin extract headed with the name Papias, in a Bodleian manuscript (no. 2397) of 1302 or 1303, printed by Grabe (Spicilegium II, p. 34) and Routh (Reliquiae Sacrae I, p. 16). It makes the brethren James, Simon, Thaddaeus and Joseph sons of Mary of Clopas, called the wife of Alphaeus.1

But at this stage I do not wish to argue for or against the supposition that Mary the wife of Clopas and Mary the mother of James and Joses were different women, provided it is clearly understood that Mary the mother of James and Joses was not the same person as the Lord’s mother.

— Jerome, Against Helvidius §15 (c. 383), source.2

The question

Jerome’s reply to Helvidius (c. 383) is the earliest extant work that argues the brethren were the Lord’s cousins. What is disputed is whether the reading had witnesses before him, in a fragment ascribed to Papias or in the second-century Palestinian tradition reported by Hegesippus, and what Jerome’s later works show about his own hold on it: he restated the reading in 392 and 398, and in the Letter to Hedibia (c. 406–7) lists Mary of Clopas and the mother of James and Joses as distinct women.3 For the second reading below, no proponent’s treatment of the Letter to Hedibia was located.

The cousin reading begins with Jerome

On this reading the cousin account was Jerome’s own deduction from Scripture, with no earlier writer behind it, and Jerome later held it less firmly.

Held by: J. B. Lightfoot (Anglican, “The Brethren of the Lord,” 1865); James White (Reformed apologist, Mary—Another Redeemer?, 1998).4

Lightfoot rests the case on Jerome’s own procedure. For the virginity Jerome names authorities at once — “Ignatius, Polycarp, Irenaeus, Justin Martyr, and many other apostolic and eloquent men” — but for the cousin relationship, Lightfoot writes, “he cannot, or at least does not, name a single writer; he simply offers it as a critical deduction from the statements of Scripture.”5 Lightfoot’s survey of the earlier writers finds Origen, Hilary of Poitiers, the Ambrosiaster and Epiphanius taking the stepbrother reading or answering the Helvidian, and concludes: “No decisive instance can be produced of a writer holding Jerome’s view, before it was propounded by Jerome himself.” He adds the apocryphal infancy narratives, which uniformly make Joseph a widower with sons; such accounts “would scarcely have taken this form, if the Hieronymian view of the Lord’s brethren had been received or even known when they were written.”6

On the “Papias” extract, Lightfoot holds that its sentence on “Maria uxor Alphei” “is taken from Jerome (adv. Helvid.) almost word for word,” that Jerome, who had Papias’s writings, “could not have failed to refer to testimony which was so important,” and that the passage “was written by a mediaeval namesake of the Bishop of Hierapolis, Papias the author of the ‘Elementarium,’ who lived in the 11th century.” A modern collation arranged on Stephen Carlson’s edition lists it among the spurious references as “Papias of Lombardy, On the Four Maries.”7 Lightfoot also finds that “Jerome does not hold his theory staunchly and consistently”: he would not fight for the pivot of the identification in §15, and in the Letter to Hedibia named the two Marys as distinct, so that Lightfoot asks whether Jerome “first relaxed his hold of and finally relinquished his own hypothesis.”8

The cousin kinship is attested before Jerome

On this reading the kinship Jerome described, a family of Clopas from which the Lord’s kinsmen came, is already present in earlier witnesses, so that Jerome stated in argument a relationship older than his treatise.

Held by: Grabe (Spicilegium) and Mill, on the “Papias” extract, as reported by Lightfoot; Florentine Bechtel (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1907), on Hegesippus; Josef Blinzler (Die Brüder und Schwestern Jesu, 1967) and John McHugh (The Mother of Jesus in the New Testament, 1975), as reported by Bauckham.9

The extract headed “Papias” makes the brethren sons of Mary of Clopas and the Lord’s maternal aunt. Grabe printed it in his Spicilegium as Papias’s, setting aside only its final sentence as the copyist’s addition (“addidit is qui descripsit ex suo”); Lightfoot reports that “the authenticity of this fragment is accepted by Mill” and that it was “frequently quoted at the head of the patristic authorities, as favouring the view of Jerome.”10

The second-century Palestinian witness Hegesippus supplies the family. He records that Clopas was a brother of Joseph and that Symeon son of Clopas, who succeeded James as bishop of Jerusalem, was the Lord’s cousin. Bechtel builds on this: “Hegesippus calls Clopas the brother of St. Joseph,” and Simon of the brethren is “identified by most commentators with the Symeon, or Simon, who, according to Hegesippus, was a son of Clopas,” so that “he also is a cousin.” With Mary of Clopas taken as the mother of James and Joseph, he concludes: “James, Joseph, and Jude are undoubtedly His cousins.”11 Blinzler and McHugh, as Bauckham reports, read Hegesippus’s description of Symeon as “a second (i.e. another) cousin of the Lord” “with the implication that his predecessor James… was also a cousin of the Lord.” Their form of the reading derives the cousinship from the Clopas family of the Jerusalem church as Hegesippus knew it, a source two centuries older than Jerome’s treatise.12
