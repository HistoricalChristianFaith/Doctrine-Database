Is the perpetual virginity a late development — when was it first held and first defined?

Disputed reading · on the perpetual virginity of Mary · witness: Basil of Caesarea, the Second Council of Constantinople and the Lateran Synod of 649

Related readings: Did belief in the perpetual virginity arise in Gnostic circles or elsewhere in early Christianity? · Did the belief rest on a typology of the consecrated womb, the new tomb, and the shut gate of Ezekiel 44:2, as well as on esteem for virginity? · Does the second-century stepbrother tradition preserve historical memory of Jesus’ family? · Did the cousin reading have witnesses before Jerome, and did Jerome later let it go?

The passage

That would not have affected the teaching of our religion at all, because Mary’s virginity was necessary until the service of the Incarnation… But since the lovers of Christ do not allow themselves to hear that the Mother of God… ceased at a given moment to be a virgin, we consider their testimony to be sufficient.

— Basil of Caesarea (c. 377), On the Holy Generation of Christ §5, trans. Thomas Buffer in Luigi Gambero, source.1

holy Mary, ever-virgin and immaculate… [who] gave birth to Him without corruption, her virginity remaining equally inviolate after the birth

— Lateran Synod under Pope Martin I (649), Canon 3, condemning anyone who does not so confess her, source.2

The question

The earliest extant writings outside the New Testament that state Mary’s lifelong virginity are of the second century; the acts of the Second Council of Constantinople (553) style her Aeiparthenos (“ever-virgin”), and the Lateran Synod of 649 anathematized the denial of her virginity before, during and after the birth.2 Among the Reformers, Luther and Zwingli also held it, and Calvin wrote that “no just and well-grounded inference can be drawn from these words of the Evangelist, as to what took place after the birth of Christ.”3 What is disputed is what the interval between the earliest attestation and the formal definitions shows about the belief’s origin: whether it was received from the apostolic church and defined when it was denied, or grew up after the apostles and was then made binding. The question is often raised together with two later Marian definitions, the Immaculate Conception (1854) and the Assumption (1950);4 the corresponding question for the first of these is treated at the Immaculate Conception’s development.

A belief that grew after the apostles and was later made binding

On this reading Mary’s lifelong virginity is not the teaching of the New Testament or of the earliest church but a belief that grew with the esteem for virginity and was made an article of faith from the late fourth century on.

Held by: Helvidius (Rome, before 383); Philip Schaff (Protestant church historian, History of the Christian Church, vol. 3); Eric Svendsen (Protestant, Evangelical Reformed; Who Is My Mother?, PhD thesis, 2001); James White (Reformed apologist, “Top Ten List on Roman Catholicism”).5,6

Helvidius, writing at Rome before 383, rested his case on Matthew 1:25 (“knew her not till”), on Jesus as Mary’s “firstborn,” and on the Gospel passages that name the Lord’s brothers and sisters, and he closed by citing two earlier church writers for his view: Jerome reports that “he there produces Tertullian as a witness and quotes the words of Victorinus bishop of Petavium.”7

Schaff judged that “the perpetua virginitas of Mary has less support from Scripture than the opposite theory,” and traced its growth to the ascetic movement: “the rise of monasticism supplied the development of Mariology a further motive in the enhanced estimate of virginity… Hence the virginity of Mary, which is unquestioned for the part of her life before the birth of Christ, came to be extended to her whole life.” He dates its elevation to dogma to Jerome’s generation: “it became from this time an article of the Catholic faith, and the denial of it was anathematized as blasphemous heresy.”8 Svendsen writes: “The notion that the brothers of Jesus were in fact something other than true siblings developed over time in the post-apostolic church.”9 Witherington, as Matthew Levering reports him, links the belief with “the increasing asceticism of the early medieval church” and holds that “the burden of proof” rests on those who argue against the Helvidian view.6

White presses the point against the Marian dogmas as a set, within a sola scriptura frame: “Do you really believe that dogmas defined nearly 2,000 years after the birth of Christ represent the actual teachings of the Apostles? … how do you explain how it is you must believe these things de fide, by faith, when generations of Christians lived and died without ever even having heard of such things?” His list names the perpetual virginity beside the Immaculate Conception and the Assumption.5 On this reading the conciliar formulas of 553 and 649, and the later requirement of assent, are the steps by which a devotion of the post-apostolic church became binding doctrine.

A belief held from the earliest church and defined when it was denied

On this reading Mary’s lifelong virginity was received in the church from the beginning, is attested in writings of the second century, and was defined by councils only after it was contested in the late fourth century.

Held by: Jerome (Against Helvidius, c. 383); Florentine Bechtel (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1907); Pope John Paul II (Catholic, General Audience, 28 August 1996); José M. Pedrozo (The Thomist, 1999) ⚠ unverified.10

Jerome answered Helvidius’s appeal to earlier writers with a longer roll of his own: “Might I not array against you the whole series of ancient writers? Ignatius, Polycarp, Irenaeus, Justin Martyr, and many other apostolic and eloquent men, who against Ebion, Theodotus of Byzantium, and Valentinus, held these same views.”7 Bechtel likewise writes of “the great antiquity of the belief in the perpetual virginity of Mary,” and John Paul II taught that “the Church has always professed her belief in the perpetual virginity of Mary”: “the most ancient texts, when referring to the conception of Jesus, call Mary simply ‘virgin,’” and “the early Christians expressed this conviction of faith in the Greek term aeiparthenos.”10

Jerome, Bechtel and John Paul II rest this reading on the early record. The Protevangelium of James (c. 150) makes Joseph an aged widower and guards Mary’s virginity after the birth; the Gospel of Peter (c. 150) presupposes the same household; Origen (c. 248) reports the stepbrother account and adopts it; and by the later fourth century Athanasius and Epiphanius use Aeiparthenos as a title.11 Basil (c. 377) holds the belief on the testimony of “the lovers of Christ.”1 Pedrozo, as Matthew Levering reports him, asks: “Before the fourth century, exactly who was a supporter of the Helvidian position? There is not one single explicit witness in favor of it.”10

On this reading a doctrine is characteristically defined when it is denied. Helvidius and Jovinian contested Mary’s virginity in the late fourth century, and the formulas of 553 and 649 followed. Donnelly, who finds up to the end of the second century “only fragmentary indications,” grounds the belief in revelation: he holds that Mary’s virginity after the birth “is not contained explicitly in Scripture,” but that “the Church under the guidance of the Holy Spirit grew in its vital awareness that all the elements of Mary’s perpetual virginity were revealed truths and, therefore, were capable of solemn dogmatic definition.”10 Armstrong’s answer to White is that “development is not creation,” and the analogy he draws is to the homoousion fixed at Nicaea (325) and the two natures defined at Chalcedon (451), formulas framed centuries after the apostles for a faith in Christ’s deity that their holders regard as apostolic.12 On this reading the dates of 553 and 649 record when the belief was challenged and answered, and the second-century writings record that it was already held.

Ever-virginity confessed from the Gospel and in the Church’s worship

On this Orthodox reading Mary’s ever-virginity is bound up with the Incarnation itself, testified in the Gospel narrative, so understood by the ancient Christians, and confessed in the councils and the daily liturgy.

Held by: Michael Pomazansky (Orthodox, Orthodox Dogmatic Theology, Eng. trans. 1994); Thomas Hopko (Orthodox, The Orthodox Faith, vol. 1, Orthodox Church in America).13

Pomazansky sets the ever-virginity beside the title Theotokos: “Two dogmas concerning the Mother of God are bound up, in closest fashion, with the dogma of God the Words becoming man. They are: a) Her Ever-virginity, and b) Her name of Theotokos.” He finds it in the Gospel itself: “The Ever-virginity of the Mother of God is testified by Her own words, handed down in the Gospel,” in the Magnificat (Luke 1:46–49), and in Luke’s notices that Mary “kept all these things, and pondered them in her heart.” Reading the brothers and sisters of Jesus against the journey to Jerusalem at twelve and the entrusting of Mary to John at the cross, he concludes: “Evidently, as the ancient Christians also understood it, the Evangelists speak either of ‘half’ brothers and sisters or of cousins.”13

Hopko writes that the Lord’s “brethren” “according to Orthodox doctrine were not children born of Mary who is confessed as ‘ever-virgin,’ but were either cousins or children of Joseph.” He places the confession in the Church’s worship as well as its councils: he quotes the anathema of the Second Council of Constantinople on anyone who will not call “the holy, glorious and ever-virgin Mary, the Theotokos” by that name, and the hymn of the emperor Justinian, “still sung in the Orthodox Church at every divine liturgy”: “Only-begotten Son and Word of God, Who for our salvation willed to be incarnate of the holy Theotokos and ever-virgin Mary.”14 On this reading the conciliar title and the liturgical hymn name what the Church confesses in its reading of the Gospels about the one from whom the Word became flesh.
