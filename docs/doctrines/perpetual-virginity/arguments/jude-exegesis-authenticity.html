Does the Epistle of Jude’s pesher-style exegesis bear on its authorship by Jude the Lord’s brother?

Disputed reading · on the perpetual virginity of Mary · witness: the Epistle of Jude

Related readings: Does Jude 9’s allusion to the Testament of Moses bear on the letter’s authorship? · Does the letter’s title “Master” (despotēs) for Christ, beside the relatives’ name desposynoi, bear on its authorship? · Does the Enochic structure of the Lukan genealogy link it to Jude’s circle, and does that bear on the letter’s authorship?

The passage

14 And Enoch also, the seventh from Adam, prophesied of these, saying, Behold, the Lord cometh with ten thousands of his saints, 15 To execute judgment upon all, and to convince all that are ungodly among them of all their ungodly deeds which they have ungodly committed, and of all their hard speeches which ungodly sinners have spoken against him. 16 These are murmurers, complainers, walking after their own lusts; and their mouth speaketh great swelling words, having men's persons in admiration because of advantage.

— Jude 14–16 (KJV), source.1

The question

The author of the letter calls himself “Jude, the servant of Jesus Christ, and brother of James” (Jude 1). Richard Bauckham argued that Jude 4–19 is formal scriptural commentary in the manner of the Qumran pesharim: a series of “texts” (as here, the prophecy of Enoch, vv. 14–15), each followed by an interpretation beginning “these are” (v. 16). What is disputed is whether that learned Jewish exegesis places the letter in the lifetime and circle of Jude the Lord’s brother, or fits a Jewish-Christian author of a later generation writing in his name. Raymond E. Brown (1997) reported scholarly “debate about Jas and Jude, with the majority favoring pseudonymity,” and Bart Ehrman (2013) “a healthy split.”2 For the brethren question the letter matters as a possible writing of one of the Lord’s kin; Bauckham, who leaves the choice between the Helvidian and Epiphanian readings open, writes that it “will make no difference” to his account of the brothers’ role in the early church.3

A Jewish-Christian teacher of a later generation, writing in Jude’s name

On this reading the letter’s exegesis shows a learned Jewish-Christian author, and its outlook places him after the apostolic generation, writing under the name of the Lord’s brother.

Held by: Werner Georg Kümmel (Protestant, Introduction to the New Testament; German 17th ed. 1973); Bart D. Ehrman (Forgery and Counterforgery, 2013); Jörg Frey (commentary on Jude and 2 Peter, 2015), as reported by reviewers.4

Kümmel grants the letter’s Jewish formation: “The author was presumably a Jewish Christian,” one who knew the “Ascension of Moses (9) and the Enoch Apocalypse (14), and the Jewish legends (9, 11).” What sets the date, on this reading, is how the author places himself. He “speaks of the apostles like a pupil from a time long afterward” (v. 17); he assumes “a faith once for all delivered to the saints” (v. 3); and against the false teachers “he adduces in similar manner Jewish and early Christian predictions (14 f, 17).” Kümmel adds that “the cultivated Greek language as well as the citations from a Greek translation of the Enoch Apocalypse do not well suit a Galilean,” and concludes that “Jude must be considered a pseudonymous writing.”

Ehrman reads verses 17–18 the same way: the book “gives every indication of being produced relatively late in the first century, after the ‘age of the apostles.’ The apostles themselves are referred to as living in the past.” Frey, according to his reviewers, argues against Bauckham that the identification of the author with the brother of Jesus is “extremely improbable,” marshals verses 17 and 3b against an early date, and places the letter roughly between 100 and 120. On this reading the exegetical skill Bauckham describes belonged to the living inheritance of Jewish Christianity, which a teacher of the next generation shared, and the name of Jude, brother of James, lent his letter the authority of the Lord’s family.

The exegesis of a Palestinian circle, consistent with the Lord’s brother as author

On this reading the letter’s formal, Qumran-like exegesis places it in early Palestinian Jewish Christianity, during the apostles’ lifetime, where Jude the Lord’s brother belonged.

Held by: Richard Bauckham (Anglican, Jude and the Relatives of Jesus in the Early Church, 1990); Gene L. Green (Jude and 2 Peter, 2008).5

Bauckham argues that the central section, vv. 4–19, is “a very carefully composed piece of scriptural commentary” on four “texts”: three Old Testament types (vv. 5–7), a woe on three more (v. 11), the prophecy of 1 Enoch 1:9 (vv. 14–15), and a prophecy of the apostles (vv. 17–18), each followed by an interpretation, as in the thematic pesharim of Qumran (4QFlorilegium, 11QMelchizedek).6 The interpretations open with houtoi, “these are” (vv. 8, 10, 12, 16, 19), like the “demonstrative explanations” of the pesharim; catchwords bind the sections; and the quotation of Enoch is shaped to the interpretation, Jude supplying kyrios so that the verse speaks of the coming of Christ.7

Bauckham places this exegesis early and in Palestine. Jude probably knew 1 Enoch “in Aramaic,” the language of the Qumran fragments, reflecting “some circles of apocalyptic Jewish Christianity in Palestine during the New Testament period.” He reads verses 17–18 as written “during the lifetime of the apostles,” looking back “simply to the time when apostles founded the churches to which he is writing.” “Like the authors of the pesharim, he was skilled in the exegetical methods of his time and place.”8 His chapter closes:

The letter of Jude contains probably the most elaborate passage of formal exegesis in the manner of the Qumran pesharim to be found in the New Testament… Such exegesis must have flourished especially in the early Palestinian church, where Jewish Christian leaders, in order to justify, to explain and to develop their faith in Jesus the Messiah, studied and expounded the Scriptures with the exegetical techniques available to them in their religious culture. Such exegetical work and the skilled exegetes who produced it will have played a major part in the early development of Christian theology. Evidently Jude the Lord’s brother was one such skilled exegete.

Green draws the same inference: “Jude’s midrashic exegesis of the OT also locates the epistle squarely within the sphere of Palestinian Judaism,” and, finding no compelling argument against it, he accepts the letter as “a genuine composition written by Jude, albeit likely with some scribal assistance.”9 On this reading the learned commentary is the work of one of the Jewish-Christian leaders of the Palestinian church, among whom the Lord’s brothers held their place.
