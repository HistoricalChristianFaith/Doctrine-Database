Was the apostle Judas Thomas, called Jesus’ twin in Syriac tradition, the Lord’s brother Jude?

Disputed reading · on the perpetual virginity of Mary · witness: the Acts of Thomas and the Book of Thomas, with Ephraem the Syrian

Related readings: What does Hegesippus’s report of Jude’s grandsons show about the relationship called “brother of the Lord”? · Does the second-century stepbrother tradition preserve historical memory of Jesus’ family? · Do the names Mary and Salome given to Jesus’ sisters preserve early family tradition?

The passage

But the Lord said to him: I am not Judas which is also called Thomas but I am his brother.

…for I know that thou art the twin brother of the Christ and always abolishest our nature…

— Acts of Thomas §§ 11 and 31, trans. M. R. James, source.1

Now, since it has been said that you are my twin and true companion, examine yourself, and learn who you are, in what way you exist, and how you will come to be. Since you will be called my brother, it is not fitting that you be ignorant of yourself.

— Book of Thomas the Contender 138, trans. John D. Turner, source.1

The question

In the East Syrian tradition of Edessa the apostle Thomas is called Judas Thomas, and “Thomas” (like the Greek Didymus, John 11:16) means “twin”; two works from that region make him the twin brother of Jesus. Mark 6:3 names a Judas among the brothers of Jesus. What is disputed is whether the Syriac Judas Thomas is that Jude, so that one of the brethren would be presented as Jesus’ twin and a son of Mary, or whether Judas was the apostle’s own name and the twinship with Jesus belongs to the theology of the Thomas literature.

Judas Thomas, Jesus’ twin, is Jude the Lord’s brother

On this reading the apostle remembered in Syria as Judas Thomas, “the twin,” is the Judas whom Mark lists among the brothers of Jesus.

Held by: the Acts of Thomas and the Book of Thomas (East Syrian, c. 200); Helmut Koester (1965) and Bentley Layton (Yale, The Gnostic Scriptures, 1987), as reported by Richard Bauckham.2

The name is attested early and across the Edessene literature. The Gospel of Thomas opens with “the secret sayings which the living Jesus spoke and which Didymos Judas Thomas wrote down”; the Abgar legend that Eusebius translated from the Syriac begins, “After the ascension of Jesus, Judas, who was also called Thomas, sent to him Thaddeus”; and Bauckham lists the same name in the Curetonian Syriac of John 14:22, the Doctrine of Addai and Ephraem.3 Two works of the region draw out what the surname says. The Acts of Thomas has a demon address him as “the twin brother of the Christ,” and has Jesus, appearing in his likeness, say, “I am not Judas which is also called Thomas but I am his brother”; in the Book of Thomas the Saviour calls him “my twin and true companion” and says, “you will be called my brother.”

A brother of Jesus named Judas is known from the Gospels (Mark 6:3). A Judas who was Jesus’ twin would be that Judas, and Bauckham summarizes the tradition’s logic in these terms: the surname “is taken to indicate that the apostle Judas Thomas was the twin brother of Jesus… and so presumably the same person as Jude the Lord’s brother.” Layton names the apostle of the Gospel of Thomas “Jude (St. Thomas), Jesus’ twin,” and writes that “Jude’s personal authority was especially high in the regional church that used the Gospel of Thomas.”2 On this reading the Syrian church preserved, under the apostle’s surname, the memory of a brother of Jesus who led its mission, and the authority that region gave “Judas Thomas” is the authority of the Lord’s own kin. Koester, as Bauckham reports, connected the Letter of Jude with the same figure, suggesting that it was written in Syria under the name of Jude the brother of Jesus because Judas Thomas was a recognized authority there.

Judas was Thomas’s own name, and the twinship is spiritual

On this reading the apostle’s personal name was Judas and “Thomas” his surname, and the Thomas books made him Jesus’ twin as a figure of spiritual likeness, not as one of the brethren.

Held by: Ephraem the Syrian (4th century, as summarized by Bauckham); Herbert Thurston (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1912); Richard Bauckham (Anglican, Jude and the Relatives of Jesus, 1990); the Orthodox Church in America, Lives of the Saints.4

The Syriac church that most used the name kept Judas Thomas and Jude apart. Ephraem, Bauckham writes, “distinguishes Judas Thomas from both the apostle Judas of James and Jude the Lord’s brother, but regards the last two as the same person,” and in several sources that use the name “there is no hint of his identification with the Lord’s brother.” The explicit twin-of-Jesus identification “is confined to the Acts of Thomas and the Book of Thomas, though it may well be presupposed in the Gospel of Thomas,” and outside East Syria appears “only in much later works under the influence of the Acts of Thomas.”5

In those works, Bauckham argues, “the identification of Thomas as the twin brother of Jesus serves a highly theological purpose”: Thomas is “the apostle who knows Jesus best and most resembles him,” and “the main impetus to this identification came from the theory of spiritual twinship with Jesus.” He adds that “it is hard to imagine how the idea of a twin brother of Jesus could have been taken seriously in circles where the tradition of the virgin birth was current,” and that “the east Syrian tradition that his personal name was Judas is entirely plausible”: a Judas surnamed “Twin” to distinguish him from the other Judases, whose twin “need not have been prominent in the early church.” Nor is he called by the title that marks the author of Jude: “he is never called ‘Judas, the brother of James.’”6 Thurston treats the twinship as a feature of the legend of the Acts. The Orthodox calendar keeps the apostle Thomas (6 October) and the apostle Jude, “the son of Righteous Joseph the Betrothed… by his first wife” (19 June), as two saints.4
