What does Hegesippus’s report of Jude’s grandsons show about the relationship called “brother of the Lord”?

Disputed reading · on the perpetual virginity of Mary · witness: Hegesippus

Related readings: Does Hegesippus call Symeon “a second cousin” of the Lord, or “the second [bishop]” (Eusebius, Church History iv.22.4)? · Does the second-century stepbrother tradition preserve historical memory of Jesus’ family? · Do the names Mary and Salome given to Jesus’ sisters preserve early family tradition?

The passage

Of the family of the Lord there were still living the grandchildren of Jude, who is said to have been the Lord’s brother according to the flesh. Information was given that they belonged to the family of David, and they were brought to the Emperor Domitian by the Evocatus… And he asked them if they were descendants of David, and they confessed that they were. Then he asked them how much property they had, or how much money they owned. And both of them answered that they had only nine thousand denarii, half of which belonged to each of them… this property did not consist of silver, but of a piece of land which contained only thirty-nine acres, and from which they raised their taxes and supported themselves by their own labor. Then they showed their hands, exhibiting the hardness of their bodies and the callousness produced upon their hands by continuous toil as evidence of their own labor.

— Hegesippus, in Eusebius, Church History iii.20.1–5, source.1

The question

The Gospels name Jude among the four “brethren of the Lord” (Mark 6:3; Matthew 13:55). Hegesippus (c. 175), as Eusebius quotes him, reports that two grandsons of Jude, “said to have been the Lord’s brother according to the flesh,” were brought before Domitian, and that afterwards “they ruled the churches because they were witnesses and were also relatives of the Lord”; Eusebius later says they were “descended from one of the so-called brothers of the Saviour, whose name was Judas,” and an ancient epitome of Hegesippus names them Zoker and James.1,2,3 What is disputed is what kind of kinship to Jesus the notice attests for Jude and his line.

A remembered family line, distinct from the Lord’s cousins

On this reading the notice preserves a real family memory: Jude, the Lord’s brother, married and left grandsons whose farm and leadership Palestinian Christians remembered, and Hegesippus keeps the brethren distinct from the Lord’s cousin Symeon.

Held by: Richard Bauckham (Anglican, Jude and the Relatives of Jesus in the Early Church, 1990); Eric Svendsen (Protestant, Evangelical Reformed; Who Is My Mother?, PhD thesis, 2001).4,5

Bauckham separates the frame of the story from its core. The link to a Domitianic persecution “seems historically gratuitous,” and the grandsons’ speech about the kingdom is “stereotyped, almost credal.” What remains he treats as family memory: “That Zoker and James were poor farmers is no doubt accurately remembered, and the size of their smallholding (39 plethra, valued at 9000 denarii) is so precisely stated that perhaps this too is accurate tradition”; the farm was “no doubt the inherited family property”; and the leadership of the Lord’s relatives “is unlikely to be Hegesippus’ creation.”4 He ties the family’s travels, reported by Julius Africanus from a Palestinian Jewish-Christian source, to “the same missionary travels which are presupposed as well-known by Paul in 1 Corinthians 9:5,” where the brothers of the Lord travel with believing wives,6 and concludes that “we must now see the family of Jesus as Davidides, conscious, through family tradition, of the hopes of Davidic restoration which had been cherished in their line since Zerubbabel.”7

In the same fragments Hegesippus calls Symeon son of Clopas the Lord’s cousin and keeps for James the title “brother of the Lord”; Bauckham reads him as one who “remains content with the traditional term.” Bauckham also holds that the evidence does not decide between the brethren as Mary’s sons and as Joseph’s sons by an earlier marriage.8 Svendsen, who takes the brethren to be Mary’s sons, uses Hegesippus “to show that he knew of the difference between ‘brother’ and ‘cousin’,” and quotes R. Ruether: “His phrase, ‘his brother after the flesh,’ decisively points to the Jerusalem tradition that these brothers were known as siblings of Jesus.”5 On this reading Jude’s grandsons show the brethren as a household with its own descendants, remembered in Palestine as the Lord’s brothers and named apart from the cousin line of Clopas.

The Lord’s kinsmen, among whom “brother” covers a cousin

On this reading the notice attests that Jude and his descendants were the Lord’s blood relatives, and the title “brother” names a near kinsman, a cousin, in the usage of Scripture.

Held by: José M. Pedrozo (The Thomist, 1999) ⚠ unverified.9

On this reading Hegesippus’s own phrasing marks the kinship as one of blood, “according to the flesh,” and his phrase for Jude, “said to have been the Lord’s brother,” with Eusebius’s “one of the so-called brothers of the Saviour,” reports the name by which Jude was known. Pedrozo, as Matthew Levering reports him, points out that both Hegesippus and Eusebius describe Judas “not simply as Jesus’ ‘brother according to the flesh’ but as someone who is ‘said to have been’ Jesus’ ‘brother according to the flesh,’” and reads Symeon’s election to succeed James as that of the son of Clopas, “a second cousin”.9 The grandsons lead the churches “as relatives of the Lord,” the same family standing that Hegesippus gives Symeon son of Clopas, whom he calls the Saviour’s cousin.2 The cousin reading identifies Jude among the Clopas kindred: Bechtel, writing for the Catholic Encyclopedia, holds that “James, Joseph, and Jude are undoubtedly His cousins,” and that in Scripture “brethren” “is often loosely extended to all near, or even distant, relatives.”10 Read so, the farm, the Davidic descent and the grandsons’ leadership belong to the Lord’s wider family, of which Jude was a married member with a line of his own.
