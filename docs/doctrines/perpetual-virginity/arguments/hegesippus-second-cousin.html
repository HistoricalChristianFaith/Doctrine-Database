Does Hegesippus call Symeon “a second cousin” of the Lord, or “the second [bishop]” (Eusebius, Church History iv.22.4)?

Disputed reading · on the perpetual virginity of Mary · witness: Hegesippus

Related readings: Did the cousin reading have witnesses before Jerome, and did Jerome later let it go? · Does the report that James the Lord’s brother and the apostle James son of Alphaeus were both stoned at Jerusalem bear on whether they were one man? · What does Hegesippus’s report of Jude’s grandsons show about the relationship called “brother of the Lord”?

The passage

And after James the Just had suffered martyrdom, as the Lord had also on the same account, Symeon, the son of the Lord’s uncle, Clopas, was appointed the next bishop. All proposed him as second bishop because he was a cousin of the Lord.

— Hegesippus, quoted by Eusebius, Church History iv.22.4, trans. A. C. McGiffert, source. The Greek of the second sentence ends …Symēōn ho tou Klōpa kathistatai episkopos, hon proethento pantes, onta anepsion tou kyriou deuteron.1

The question

The final word deuteron (“second”) is masculine accusative singular, and can agree either with the expressed anepsion (“cousin”) or with an understood episkopon (“bishop”). Taken with “cousin,” it makes Symeon “another cousin” of the Lord, which implies that James, his predecessor, was a cousin too, as the Hieronymian reading of the brethren holds; taken with “bishop,” it numbers Symeon in the Jerusalem succession, and Hegesippus’s terms for the two men stay distinct. Elsewhere in the fragments Eusebius preserves, Hegesippus calls James “the brother of the Lord” and Jude one “said to have been the Lord’s brother according to the flesh,” and Eusebius reports from him that Symeon “was a cousin, as they say, of the Saviour. For Hegesippus records that Clopas was a brother of Joseph.”2

“Second” numbers Symeon as the second bishop of Jerusalem

On this reading Hegesippus says that all put Symeon forward as James’s successor, the second bishop, because he was the Lord’s cousin, and keeps the title “brother of the Lord” for James.

Held by: J. B. Lightfoot (Anglican, “The Brethren of the Lord,” 1865); A. C. McGiffert (translator, Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, 1890); Richard Bauckham (Anglican, Jude and the Relatives of Jesus in the Early Church, 1990).3

Lightfoot renders the sentence “his paternal uncle’s child Symeon the son of Clopas is next made bishop, who was put forward by all as the second in succession, being cousin of the Lord,” and holds that “this rendering alone seems intelligible.” Against taking palin and deuteron as “another child of his uncle, another cousin,” he argues from the Greek article: “To this rendering the presence of the definite article alone seems fatal (‘ho ek tou theiou’ not ‘heteros tōn ek tou theiou’); but indeed the whole passage appears to be framed so as to distinguish the relationships of the two persons.” He adds that Eusebius and Epiphanius “must have interpreted the words” the same way, and that across the fragments “Symeon is never once called the Lord’s brother, while James is always so designated.”4

Bauckham reads the clause as a report of an appointment to office: Symeon kathistatai episkopos, “is appointed bishop,” and the passage “describes the succession in the episcopal see of Jerusalem,” so that “dēuteron should be taken, not with anepsion, but with episkopon understood.” He renders: “Symeon the son of Clopas, was next appointed bishop, whom, since he was a cousin of the Lord, they all put forward as the second [bishop].”5 He finds the same elliptical “second” in Eusebius’s own account of the succession, where the word bishop is left to be understood: “At this time Ignatius was known as the second bishop of Antioch, Evodius having been the first. Symeon likewise was at that time the second ruler of the church of Jerusalem, the brother of our Saviour having been the first.”6 Had Hegesippus meant “another cousin,” Bauckham writes, “his whole sentence is an awkward way of saying it.” On this reading Hegesippus “remains content with the traditional term” for James, and distinguishes the two men’s kinship: Symeon was put forward as the second bishop because he was the Lord’s cousin, and James is the brother of the Lord.5

“Second” makes Symeon another cousin of the Lord, after James

On this reading Hegesippus calls Symeon a second cousin of the Lord in the sense of “another” cousin, so that James, the first bishop, was the Lord’s cousin as well.

Held by: Mill (cited by Lightfoot, 1865); Kirsopp Lake (translator, Loeb Ecclesiastical History, 1926); Josef Blinzler (Die Brüder und Schwestern Jesu, 1967) and John McHugh (The Mother of Jesus in the New Testament, 1975), as reported by Bauckham.7

On this reading the two accusatives belong together. Anepsion tou kyriou deuteron stand side by side and agree in case, gender and number, and “a second one of the same kind” is a sense the ordinal can bear. The sentence opens with palin, “again”: in the rendering Lightfoot reports, Hegesippus says that once more one of the Lord’s uncle’s family was made bishop, “another child of his uncle, another cousin.”4 The “again” and the “second” then point back to James, named at the start of the sentence, as the first such cousin. Lake’s Loeb translation takes the word this way: “whom they all proposed because he was another cousin of the Lord.”8

Blinzler and McHugh read the clause, as Bauckham reports, as calling Symeon “a second (i.e. another) cousin of the Lord” “with the implication that his predecessor James… was also a cousin of the Lord.”7 José M. Pedrozo (The Thomist, 1999), as Matthew Levering reports him, argues the same way: Hegesippus “describes Symeon as being elected to replace James, because Symeon, as the son of Clopas, is a second cousin,” “second to the first, James.”9 Read so, Hegesippus attests a single family of Clopas from which both early bishops of Jerusalem came, and his “brother of the Lord” for James is the customary title by which the Jerusalem church knew a man who was by kinship the Lord’s cousin. The second-century Palestinian witness then describes the same kindred that the cousin reading finds in the Gospels, where the brethren James and Joses are sons of a Mary standing at the cross, and where John names a “Mary of Clopas” there.
