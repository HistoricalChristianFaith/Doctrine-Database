Did Tertullian hold that Mary bore children after Jesus?

Disputed reading · on the perpetual virginity of Mary · witness: Tertullian

Related readings: Does the second-century stepbrother tradition preserve historical memory of Jesus’ family? · Is the perpetual virginity a late development?

The passage

although she was a virgin when she conceived, she was a wife when she brought forth her son. Now, as a wife, she was under the very law of ‘opening the womb’… becoming a mother at a leap, as it were, before she was a wife.

— Tertullian (c. 210), On the Flesh of Christ 23, trans. Peter Holmes, source.1

if… He made them ‘His mother and His brethren’ who were not so, how could He deny them these relationships who really had them?

— Tertullian, Against Marcion iv.19, trans. Peter Holmes, source.2

it was a virgin, about to marry once for all after her delivery, who gave birth to Christ, in order that each title of sanctity might be fulfilled in Christ’s parentage, by means of a mother who was both virgin, and wife of one husband.

— Tertullian, On Monogamy 8, trans. S. Thelwall, source.3

The question

Writing against Marcion and Apelles, Tertullian insists that Christ had a real mother and real brethren, and he reads Mary as a virgin in conceiving but “a wife when she brought forth”; Lightfoot, Schaff and Quasten read On the Flesh of Christ 23 as denying a virginity preserved in the birth itself (the virginitas in partu). He nowhere states whose children the brethren were. What is disputed is whether these texts show that he held them to be Mary’s sons born after Jesus (the Helvidian view), or speak only of the manner of the birth and the reality of Christ’s kin. Helvidius cited him for his view at Rome c. 383, and Jerome answered: “Of Tertullian I say no more than that he did not belong to the Church.”4 Richard Bauckham makes the date of the earliest positive attestation of the Helvidian view turn on this question.5 On Monogamy belongs to Tertullian’s Montanist period; the later judgements on his standing are traced on the timeline “Was Tertullian a heretic?”.

Tertullian held that the brethren were Mary’s sons

On this reading Tertullian’s argument for Christ’s real humanity treats the brethren as Mary’s children in the same sense in which she is Christ’s mother, and his picture of Mary as a true wife after the birth leaves no place for stepbrothers.

Held by: Helvidius (c. 383, as reported by Jerome); J. B. Lightfoot (Anglican, “The Brethren of the Lord,” 1865); Philip Schaff (Protestant, History of the Christian Church); Johannes Quasten (Catholic, Patrology, vol. 2) ⚠ unverified.6

Lightfoot set Tertullian first among the authorities for the “sons of Joseph and Mary.” Though Tertullian “does not directly state it, his argument seems to imply that the Lord’s brethren were His brothers in the same sense in which Mary was His mother (adv. Marc. iv. 19, de Carn. Christ. 7). It is therefore highly probable that he held the Helvidian view. Such an admission from one who was so strenuous an advocate of asceticism is worthy of notice.”7 On this reading the reply to Marcion turns on the brethren being real relations of the same order as the mother, and the plain sense of “brother” is the sense the polemic leans on. Eric Svendsen reads the rhetorical questions of Against Marcion 4.19 as presupposing “that these brothers are not merely cousins” and “that these brothers are not siblings only in an adopted sense,” and concludes: “Both the Hieronymian view and the Epiphanian view are therefore precluded in Tertullian.”12 In On Monogamy he makes Mary a virgin “about to marry once for all after her delivery,” a true univira.

Quasten reads the same texts together: Tertullian, defending Christ’s real body “born of the very substance of Mary,” “denies the virginity of Mary in partu and post partum,” and “understands the ‘brethren of Jesus’ as children of Mary according to the flesh,” because “the claim of a perduring virginity seemed to him a most welcome confirmation” of the Docetists’ belief “that Christ had no real human body.”8 Schaff writes that the brother-theory “was held by Tertullian,” and that Helvidius and Jovinian argued from “earlier church teachers, like Tertullian.”9 The reception is read the same way: Helvidius claimed Tertullian, and Jerome, in Lightfoot’s words, “is obliged to concede him Tertullian, whose authority he invalidates as ‘not a member of the Church,’ but denies him Victorinus.”7 On this reading Tertullian, c. 210, is the earliest extant named writer for the view that Mary bore other children.

Tertullian’s statements concern the birth, not the brethren’s parentage

On this reading Tertullian denies only the miraculous manner of the birth and insists that Christ’s kin were real, without saying whose sons the brethren were.

Held by: Josef Blinzler (Die Brüder und Schwestern Jesu, 1967), John McHugh (The Mother of Jesus in the New Testament, 1975) and José M. Pedrozo (The Thomist, 1999), as cited by others ⚠ unverified.10

On this reading the places where Tertullian speaks directly of Mary’s virginity, On the Flesh of Christ 23 and On Monogamy 8, answer heretics who used the paradox “a virgin and not a virgin” to deny a real human birth; their subject is the manner of the childbearing. That a womb was “opened” in bearing a first child says nothing, of itself, about a second. Lightfoot, who holds the first reading, grants that “the more distinct passages in the extant writings of Tertullian relate to the virginity only,” that the usual assumption about Tertullian, “though probable, is not absolutely certain,” and that in Jerome’s exchange with Helvidius “the point at issue… is not the particular opinion of Helvidius respecting the Lord’s brethren, but the virginity of the Lord’s mother.”7

McHugh, as Svendsen reports him, denies that Tertullian took the brethren for Mary’s sons, on the ground that these “brothers” “are never called ‘sons of Mary’” in his writings.12 Pedrozo, as Matthew Levering reports him, writes that Tertullian “never asserts explicitly that Mary and Joseph had children of their own” and that throughout his corpus “Tertullian simply affirms that Mary was a virgin mother.”11 The anti-Marcionite argument needs the brethren to be genuine kin of the flesh, and on this reading real kinship is all it requires; the texts do not say whose sons the brethren were, and the step from “real brethren” to “Mary’s sons” is drawn from the ordinary sense of “brother,” not from a sentence of Tertullian’s. Jerome’s refusal of Tertullian’s authority answers Helvidius on the virginity of the mother, which on this reading is the point Tertullian contested. Read so, Tertullian is a witness, c. 210, to the denial of the virginity in childbirth, and he leaves the parentage of the brethren unstated.
