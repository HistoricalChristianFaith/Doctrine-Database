Does the report that James the Lord’s brother and the apostle James son of Alphaeus were both stoned at Jerusalem bear on whether they were one man?

Disputed reading · on the perpetual virginity of Mary · witness: Hegesippus, with Josephus and the Pseudo-Hippolytan On the Twelve Apostles

Related readings: Did the cousin reading have witnesses before Jerome, and did Jerome later let it go? · Does Hegesippus call Symeon “a second cousin” of the Lord, or “the second [bishop]” (Eusebius, Church History iv.22.4)?

The passage

James the son of Alphæus, when preaching in Jerusalem, was stoned to death by the Jews, and was buried there beside the temple.

— Pseudo-Hippolytus, On the Twelve Apostles § 9, source.1

…assembled the sanhedrim of judges, and brought before them the brother of Jesus, who was called Christ, whose name was James, and some others, [or, some of his companions]; and when he had formed an accusation against them as breakers of the law, he delivered them to be stoned.

— Josephus, Antiquities of the Jews XX.9.1, on the high priest Ananus, c. 62, trans. William Whiston, source.2

The question

The apostle-catalogue On the Twelve Apostles, which circulates under Hippolytus’s name, reports that the apostle James son of Alphaeus (Matthew 10:3) was stoned in Jerusalem and buried beside the temple; Josephus reports the stoning of James “the brother of Jesus,” and Hegesippus that the Lord’s brother was stoned at the temple and buried “by the temple.” What is disputed is whether the two notices record one man’s death, and so count toward identifying the Lord’s brother with the apostle, as the Hieronymian reading of the brethren does, or record two men. Benedict XVI (2006) described the identity of the two Jameses as “disputed” among experts.3 The use of the shared death as evidence for the identification was located only in a popular Catholic question-and-answer column and in an anonymous ancient apostle-list; no academic statement of it was found.

One James, remembered under two names

On this reading the stoning at Jerusalem and the burial by the temple are one event, told of one man who was both the apostle son of Alphaeus and the Lord’s brother.

Held by: the anonymous List of the Apostles (“Anonymous I”), as quoted by Tony Burke (2023); Charles Grondin (Catholic, Catholic Answers, 2017), who reports the argument and leaves the identification open.4

The two notices agree on four points: the name James, the city, the manner of death, and the place of burial. Josephus has James “the brother of Jesus” condemned and stoned at Jerusalem in about 62; Hegesippus tells the same death at the temple and ends, “they buried him on the spot, by the temple, and his monument still remains by the temple”;5 the apostle-catalogue has James son of Alphaeus stoned at Jerusalem “by the Jews” and “buried there beside the temple.” Grondin sets the Hippolytan and Josephan notices side by side: “Hippolytus writes of James, the son of Alphaeus, being stoned to death while preaching in Jerusalem. Josephus also wrote that James, the brother of Jesus was stoned to death in Jerusalem. Some believe these to be the same person.”

An ancient list draws the same conclusion in its own wording. As Burke quotes it, the List of the Apostles reads: “James, son of Alphaeus, called the Just, was stoned by the Jews in Jerusalem and is buried there near the temple.” “The Just” is the name Hegesippus gives the Lord’s brother: “He has been called the Just by all from the time of our Saviour to the present day.”5 Burke also reports that the Coptic and Ethiopian churches tell the apostle’s martyrdom as a stoning in Jerusalem, with burial beside the temple, and keep it as his feast.4 Read so, the apostle-catalogues and the martyrdom of James the Just preserve one memory, and the patronymic “son of Alphaeus” and the title “the Lord’s brother” are two ways of naming the same leader of the Jerusalem church. Grondin places the argument beside the others given for the identification, among them that Alphaeus and Clopas were one person, so that James son of Alphaeus is the son of the “Mary of Clopas” who stood at the cross (John 19:25).

Two Jameses, with two death traditions

On this reading the apostle’s stoning is a notice in a late catalogue that itself counts the Lord’s brother as a different man, and the apostle son of Alphaeus has a separate memory of his death.

Held by: J. B. Lightfoot (Anglican, “The Brethren of the Lord,” 1865); the Orthodox Church in America, Lives of the Saints (commemorations of 9 and 23 October).6

The catalogue that reports the apostle’s stoning has a companion, On the Seventy Apostles, which lists “James the Lord’s brother, bishop of Jerusalem” among the Seventy. The same corpus thus registers two figures named James, one among the Twelve and one among the Seventy. The Ante-Nicene Fathers editors preface both pieces “[Probably spurious, but yet antique.]”1 Lightfoot, who counted James the Just “not one of the Twelve,” read the stoning notice as a transfer: of the son of Alphaeus, “He is thus confused or identified with James the Lord’s brother. But this blundering treatise was certainly not written by the bishop of Portus.”7

Other tradition gives the apostle a different end. Burke reports that Nicetas the Paphlagonian has James son of Alphaeus preach in Eleutheropolis, Gaza and Tyre and die “by crucifixion in Ostracine (Egypt),” as Nicephorus Callistus later repeats, and observes that the apostle “rarely appears as a character distinct from James the Just.”4 The Orthodox commemorations keep the two apart. James son of Alphaeus, “the brother of the holy Evangelist Matthew,” “finished his apostolic work In the Egyptian city of Ostrachina, where he was crucified by the pagans”; James the brother of the Lord was thrown from the temple roof and stoned at Jerusalem; and “The Church distinguishes between the Holy Apostle James the Brother of God, Saint James the son of Zebedee (April 30), and Saint James the son of Alphaeus (October 9).”6 Hegesippus’s account of the Lord’s brother is a sequence of fall, stoning and a fuller’s club: “they went up and threw down the just man… they began to stone him, for he was not killed by the fall.”5 On this reading the stoning at the temple belongs to James the Just, bishop of Jerusalem, and the apostle son of Alphaeus is remembered as a missionary who died in Egypt.
