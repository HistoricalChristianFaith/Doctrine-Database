Does the letter’s title “Master” (despotēs) for Christ, beside the relatives’ name desposynoi, bear on its authorship?

Disputed reading · on the perpetual virginity of Mary · witness: the Epistle of Jude, with Julius Africanus in Eusebius

Related readings: Does the Epistle of Jude’s pesher-style exegesis bear on its authorship by Jude the Lord’s brother? · Does Jude 9’s allusion to the Testament of Moses bear on the letter’s authorship? · Does the Enochic structure of the Lukan genealogy link it to Jude’s circle, and does that bear on the letter’s authorship?

The passage

For there are certain men crept in unawares, who were before of old ordained to this condemnation, ungodly men, turning the grace of our God into lasciviousness, and denying the only Lord God, and our Lord Jesus Christ.

— Jude 4 (KJV), source. The Greek text Bauckham follows reads ton monon despotēn kai kyrion hēmōn Iēsoun Christon, “our only Master and Lord Jesus Christ.”1

Among these are those already mentioned, called Desposyni, on account of their connection with the family of the Saviour.

— Julius Africanus, in Eusebius, Church History i.7.14, trans. McGiffert, source.1

The question

Jude 4 calls Christ despotēs, “Master” or “Sovereign,” and Julius Africanus reports that the relatives of Jesus were called desposynoi, “those who belong to the Master.” Richard Bauckham argued that the title, rare for Christ in early Christian writing, was the usage of the relatives’ own circle and so points to the letter’s origin there. What is disputed is whether that shared vocabulary bears on authorship by Jude the Lord’s brother, or whether the letter is the work of a later writer in his name. Raymond E. Brown (1997) reported scholarly “debate about Jas and Jude, with the majority favoring pseudonymity.”2 A response to the despotēs argument itself from those who hold the letter pseudonymous was not located.

A letter of the post-apostolic generation, written in Jude’s name

On this reading the letter belongs to a generation after the apostles and carries the name of Jude, brother of James, for the authority of the Lord’s family; its date and authorship are fixed by the author’s stance toward the apostles.

Held by: Werner Georg Kümmel (Protestant, Introduction to the New Testament; German 17th ed. 1973); Bart D. Ehrman (Forgery and Counterforgery, 2013); Jörg Frey (commentary on Jude and 2 Peter, 2015), as reported by reviewers. None of them, in the sources opened, discusses despotēs.3

Kümmel grants that “The author was presumably a Jewish Christian,” at home in Jewish apocalyptic writings and legends, and finds the decisive marks of date in the letter’s retrospect. The author “speaks of the apostles like a pupil from a time long afterward” (v. 17); he assumes “a faith once for all delivered to the saints” (v. 3); and he answers the false teachers with collected “Jewish and early Christian predictions (14 f, 17).” “All this points to a late phase of primitive Christianity,” and with “the cultivated Greek language” it leads him to conclude that “Jude must be considered a pseudonymous writing.” Ehrman reads the same verses: the book “gives every indication of being produced relatively late in the first century, after the ‘age of the apostles.’ The apostles themselves are referred to as living in the past.” Frey, as his reviewers report, holds the identification of the author with the brother of Jesus “extremely improbable,” argues from verses 17 and 3b against an early date, and places the letter between about 100 and 120.

The idiom of the relatives’ circle, consistent with Jude as author

On this reading the title despotēs was the spoken usage of the Palestinian circle in which the desposynoi were leaders, and Jude 4 is a direct witness to that usage.

Held by: Richard Bauckham (Anglican, Jude and the Relatives of Jesus in the Early Church, 1990; “The Relatives of Jesus,” 1996).4

Bauckham recovers four christological titles current among the relatives from evidence outside the letter: Messiah, tied to their Davidic descent; “the Lord,” presupposed by their name “the brothers of the Lord” (1 Corinthians 9:5; Galatians 1:19); “our Lord,” the Aramaic Maranatha (1 Corinthians 16:22); and “Master,” presupposed by desposynoi. “All four of these christological titles — and only these four — occur in the letter of Jude.”5 Christological despotēs is, he writes, “unique in extant early Christian literature before the late second century, with the sole exception of 2 Peter 2:1, which is dependent on Jude 4”; yet “the term desposynoi for the relatives of Jesus is only explicable if ho despotēs was commonly used to refer to Jesus in Palestinian Jewish Christianity.” Jude’s use of the title is therefore “an impressive, but neglected indication of his letter’s origin in those Palestinian Christian circles in which the desposynoi were leaders.”6

He reads the title royally. Desposynoi “is easier to understand if despotēs described Jesus as the messianic Ruler and desposynoi therefore attributed to his relatives the dignity of a royal family”; Jude 4 is best rendered “our only Sovereign and Lord Jesus Christ,” the two titles being “probably alternative Greek renderings of the single Aramaic expression māranā’.”7 Of the name itself he writes elsewhere: “It must be the term by which members of the family of Jesus were known in those Palestinian Jewish Christian circles in which they were revered leaders.”8 On this reading Jude 4 speaks the family’s own Greek, the language of the bilingual circle in which the brother of James was a leader.
