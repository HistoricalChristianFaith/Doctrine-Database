Does the second-century stepbrother tradition preserve historical memory of Jesus’ family?

Disputed reading · on the perpetual virginity of Mary · witness: the Protevangelium of James and the Gospel of Peter, as reported by Origen

Related readings: Do the names Mary and Salome given to Jesus’ sisters preserve early family tradition? · Does Hegesippus call Symeon “a second cousin” of the Lord, or “the second [bishop]”? · Did the cousin reading have witnesses before Jerome, and did Jerome later let it go? · Does the report that James the Lord’s brother and the apostle James son of Alphaeus were both stoned at Jerusalem bear on whether they were one man? · Is the perpetual virginity a late development?

The passage

But some say, basing it on a tradition in the Gospel according to Peter, as it is entitled, or “The Book of James,” that the brethren of Jesus were sons of Joseph by a former wife, whom he married before Mary. Now those who say so wish to preserve the honour of Mary in virginity to the end, so that that body of hers which was appointed to minister to the Word which said, “The Holy Ghost shall come upon thee, and the power of the Most High shall overshadow thee,” might not know intercourse with a man after that the Holy Ghost came into her and the power from on high overshadowed her. And I think it in harmony with reason that Jesus was the first-fruit among men of the purity which consists in chastity, and Mary among women; for it were not pious to ascribe to any other than to her the first-fruit of virginity.

— Origen (c. 248), Commentary on Matthew X.17, on Matthew 13:55–56, source.1

The question

The account that the “brethren of the Lord” were Joseph’s sons by an earlier marriage appears around the middle of the second century in the Protevangelium of James (9:2; 17:1–2; 18:1), in the Gospel of Peter as Origen reports it, and in the Infancy Gospel of Thomas (16); it was taken up by Clement, Origen, Eusebius and Epiphanius, and it is, in Richard Bauckham’s description, “the traditional view in the Eastern Orthodox churches.”2,3 What is disputed is whether that early attestation preserves a memory of the household’s actual make-up, or records a reading of the Gospels that arose from legend or from the belief in Mary’s virginity. Bauckham also lists Hippolytus among its early holders; the apostle-catalogues under Hippolytus’s name, which the Ante-Nicene Fathers editors call “probably spurious, but yet antique,” list “James the Lord’s brother, bishop of Jerusalem” among the Seventy apart from “James the son of Alphæus” among the Twelve without saying whose sons the brethren were, and the full Epiphanian account that Nicephorus Callistus cites “as from Hippolytus of Portus” Lightfoot traced “either [to] Hippolytus the Theban” or to “some forged writings which bore the name of the older Hippolytus.”4,5

A tradition preserving the household’s real make-up

On this reading the brethren were remembered, from the first generations, as Joseph’s sons by a former wife, and the second-century writings pass on that memory, which reached Epiphanius through Palestinian channels.

Held by: Epiphanius (Panarion 29 and 78, c. 377); J. B. Lightfoot (Anglican, “The Brethren of the Lord,” 1865); John Maximovitch (Orthodox, The Orthodox Veneration of Mary, Eng. 1994); Richard Bauckham (Anglican, 1990), as a possibility he leaves open.6

Lightfoot, who calls the Protevangelium “a purely fictitious” narrative, judged the account itself to have “the highest claims to the sanction of tradition, whether the value of this sanction be great or small,” and to “represent the Palestinian view.” He held that “it is not altogether correct to represent this belief as suggested solely by the false asceticism of the early Church,” for “the Epiphanian view prevailed especially in Palestine where there was less disposition than elsewhere to depreciate married life, and prevailed too at a time when extreme ascetic views had not yet mastered the Church at large.”7

Bauckham holds that “good historical tradition certainly was still available in the early second century,” and notes that the earliest carriers assume the relationship rather than argue it: the Infancy Gospel of Thomas (16) treats it “as though the readers are already quite aware of it.” He tested candidates for a Palestinian Jewish-Christian root and found the extant fragments of Hegesippus undecided between the Helvidian and Epiphanian views,8 but judged Epiphanius’s statement in Panarion 29 that James “was Joseph’s first-born, and (thus) consecrated” to be “much more plausibly dependent on Hegesippus… than on apocryphal infancy literature”; Epiphanius credits the surrounding account of James to “Eusebius, Clement and others,” and Lightfoot likewise writes that Eusebius and Epiphanius “derived their information mainly from Hegesippus.”9 Maximovitch states the account as received knowledge: “it was known that James and others were the sons of Joseph, the Betrothed of Mary, who was a widower with children from his first wife.”10 Bauckham puts the relation between memory and doctrine this way: “it is possible that the brothers of Jesus were correctly remembered not to have been sons of Mary and that this made possible the development of the idea of Mary’s perpetual virginity as a result.”2 On this reading the second-century books carry, in a legendary setting, a fact about the household that Palestinian Christians remembered.

An apocryphal account, the brethren being the Lord’s cousins

On this reading the stepbrother account comes from the apocryphal books Origen names, and the Gospels themselves identify the brethren as sons of another Mary, the Lord’s cousins.

Held by: Jerome (Commentary on Matthew, 398); Florentine Bechtel (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1907).11

Jerome traces the account to the apocrypha and sets the cousin relationship in its place: “Some suspect that the brothers of the Lord are sons of Joseph by another wife, following the ravings of the apocrypha and inventing a certain little woman Escha. But we, as is contained in the book which we wrote Against Helvidius, understand the brothers of the Lord to be not sons of Joseph but cousins of the Savior, the children of Mary, the Lord’s maternal aunt, who is said to be the mother of James the less, and of Joses and Jude, whom in another place of the Gospel we read called the brothers of the Lord. And all Scripture shows that cousins are called brothers.”11

The source Origen gives for the account is the books themselves, “a tradition in the Gospel according to Peter… or ‘The Book of James.’”1 Bechtel, writing for the Catholic Encyclopedia, traces the Greek view to the same literature: “The majority of the Greek Fathers and Greek writers, influenced, it seems, by the legendary tales of apocryphal gospels, considered the ‘brethren’ of the Lord as sons of St. Joseph by a first marriage. The Latins, on the contrary, with few exceptions (St. Ambrose, St. Hilary, and St. Gregory of Tours among the Fathers), hold that they were the Lord’s cousins.” On this reading the Gospels supply the family directly: the James and Joses named among the brethren in Mark 6:3 are the sons of the Mary who stands at the cross in Mark 15:40, the Lord’s kinswoman, and their place in the Lord’s family is that of cousins whom Scripture, in its usual way, calls brothers.11

An account that grew from reflection on Mary’s virginity

On this reading the stepbrother account arose in second-century circles devoted to Mary’s virginity, as one of a set of beliefs that protected it, and does not record the family’s actual make-up.

Held by: Philip Schaff (Protestant church historian, History of the Christian Church, vol. 1); Eric Svendsen (Protestant, Evangelical Reformed; Who Is My Mother?, PhD thesis, 2001); Ben Witherington III (Protestant, United Methodist; commentary on Matthew) ⚠ unverified.12,13,14

Schaff grants that the account “has a strong traditional support in the apocryphal Gospels and in the Eastern church,” and that it “has the dogmatic advantage of saving the perpetual virginity.” Against it he sets the Gospel narrative: “it does not so naturally account for the constant companionship of these brethren with Mary; it assumes a former marriage of Joseph nowhere alluded to in the Gospels, and makes Joseph an old man and protector rather than husband of Mary; and finally it is not free from suspicion of an ascetic bias, as being the first step towards the dogma of the perpetual virginity.”12 Witherington, as Matthew Levering quotes him, adds the Davidic objection: “The Epiphanian view labors under the difficulty that if Joseph previously had other sons, Jesus could not legally be his firstborn or first in line for the Davidic throne, and it also depends on dubious and weak church traditions.”13 Svendsen writes: “The notion that the brothers of Jesus were in fact something other than true siblings developed over time in the post-apostolic church.” Of the three second-century carriers he writes: “Independent attestation for the Epiphanian view notwithstanding, the historical value of all three of these writings is suspect.”14

Bauckham, who leaves the question open, sets out the evidence on which this reading draws. In the Protevangelium the stepbrother idea is “combined with two other beliefs concerned with the human origins of Jesus, which are not found in the New Testament: the Davidic descent of Mary (10:1) and the miraculous birth of Jesus (19:2–3),” and “all three notions are connected with the virginity of Mary.” Read so, the tradition “looks like part of a doctrinal or hagiographical development of the theme of the virginity of Mary,” and “reflection on the idea of the virginity of Mary led to the conclusion that Jesus’ brothers and sisters could not be her children.”2,3 On this reading the brethren of the Gospels, who appear again and again with Mary, were her own children, and the stepbrother account belongs with the other second-century beliefs that guarded her virginity.
