Gregory of Nyssa on the perpetual virginity of Mary

Dates: c. 382 (a festal homily; the witness is his second oration On the Resurrection of Christ [In Christi resurrectionem ii], one of the Easter sermons he preached across his later career — the individual homily cannot be dated closely). Gregory (c. 335 – c. 395), bishop of Nyssa in Cappadocia from 372, was the younger brother of Basil of Caesarea and one of the three Cappadocian Fathers. · Wikipedia
Summary: Gregory holds the Epiphanian reading — the “brethren of the Lord” are Joseph’s sons, not Mary’s. To it he joins two identifications of his own: he identifies the “Mary the mother of James and Joses” of the Passion and resurrection narratives (Matthew 27:56; Mark 15:40) not with a second Mary but with the Theotokos herself, reckoned the boys’ step-mother because she had taken over the raising of Joseph’s children; and he reads the “James the little” of Mark 15:40 as marked off from “James the son of Alphaeus” — the “great” James, who was one of the Twelve — so that the Lord’s brother is neither Mary’s son nor one of the Twelve. J. B. Lightfoot filed Gregory in his catena of the “sons of Joseph by a former wife” authorities, next after Ambrosiaster and before Epiphanius, while judging the Marian identification “very untenable.” The resurrection homily on which the brethren-claims rest survives in Migne’s Patrologia Graeca (In Christi resurrectionem oratio ii, PG 46, col. 647–648), where the Greek text agrees with Lightfoot’s report; that oration is of disputed authenticity — one of the resurrection sermons treated as a dubium and excluded from the modern critical edition (the Gregorii Nysseni Opera) and from S. G. Hall’s translation of the Easter sermons accepted as Gregory’s — so the question is whether the sermon is Gregory’s, not whether it says what Lightfoot reported. A second strand of Gregory’s Marian teaching survives in his Homily on the Nativity of Christ: there he reads Mary’s reply to Gabriel — “How shall this be, seeing I know not a man?” (Luke 1:34) — as proof that she had already consecrated her virginity to God; this homily and Augustine are among the earliest extant statements of the “vow” reading of that verse.

Claims & evidence

The brethren are Joseph’s sons — the Epiphanian reading

Gregory’s underlying position is the one found also in Clement, Origen, Eusebius, and his fellow Cappadocians: the “brethren of the Lord” are the elder children of Joseph by a former marriage, older step-brothers of Jesus and no blood-kin of Mary, so that Mary bore Jesus alone and remained ever-virgin. J. B. Lightfoot records that Gregory “certainly adopted the Epiphanian account.”1 To it Gregory adds the two identifications set out below.

“Mary the mother of James and Joses” identified with the Theotokos

Gregory identifies the Virgin with the “other Mary” who stood by the cross and came to the tomb. Preaching on the resurrection, he takes “the Mary who is designated in the other Evangelists (besides St. John) the mother of James and Joses” to be “the mother of God and none else” — the Virgin Mary called “mother of James and Joses” not because she bore them but because, as Joseph’s wife, she “undertook the education of these her stepsons.”1 In this account the brethren remain Joseph’s and the Virgin is their step-mother. Lightfoot calls it “the very untenable position” Gregory “takes up” while he “certainly adopted the Epiphanian account.”1

“James the little” distinguished from James son of Alphaeus

Gregory couples that identification with a second inference from the Gospel wording. Mark 15:40 names among the women “Mary the mother of James the little (or ‘the less’) and of Joses.” Gregory reads the epithet as a deliberate contrast: this James is “the little” to distinguish him from James the son of Alphaeus, who was “the great” because he was numbered among the Twelve Apostles — which the Lord’s brother was not.1 This places Gregory, on the “James the Just not one of the Twelve” question, with Hegesippus, the Clementine writings, Cyril of Jerusalem, Marius Victorinus, and the Apostolic Constitutions ; the Hieronymian reading, by contrast, identifies the Lord’s brother with James son of Alphaeus. Gregory holds the two Jameses apart and keeps the Lord’s brother outside the Twelve.

The vow reading of Luke 1:34 — Mary’s consecrated virginity

Besides the resurrection homily, Gregory’s festal Homily on the Nativity of Christ (In diem natalem Salvatoris, c. 386) is a further testimony to Mary’s virginity. Expounding the Annunciation, he takes up Mary’s reply to Gabriel and takes it as evidence that her virginity was a settled resolve, consecrated to God before ever she was betrothed — not a passing circumstance a bride would expect to leave behind:

The Angel proclaims the glad tidings of the birth; but she cleaves to virginity, judging incorruption to be preferable to the appearance of the Angel, and neither does she disbelieve the Angel nor will she relinquish her resolve. “I have refrained from knowing any man,” she says: “How shall this be, seeing I know not a man?” This utterance of Mary is proof of the narrative that I have drawn from the aforementioned apocryphal work. For if she had been taken in marriage by Joseph, how would she have been surprised by him who announced that she was to bear a child, since she would herself have expected to become a mother according to the law of nature?

— Gregory of Nyssa, Homily on the Nativity of Christ, on Luke 1:34.2 Gregory states the inference: a betrothed woman who expected in the ordinary course to live with her husband would have had no cause for perplexity at the promise of a child; on his reasoning, Mary’s surprise is intelligible because she had never reckoned on “knowing” a man. Gregory names his own source for the tradition of the consecrated virgin — the “aforementioned apocryphal work,” identified with the second-century Protevangelium of James, which has Mary raised in the Temple and vowed to God from childhood — and reads Luke 1:34 as the canonical confirmation of it.

This is among the earliest extant statements of the vow reading of Luke 1:34, which appears in the same generation in Augustine (“which assuredly she would not say, unless she had before vowed herself unto God as a virgin”) and, in the thirteenth century, in Aquinas, who sets it out as a two-stage vow. The testimony is quoted from the Nativity homily directly.

Reception and context

  • Other attestations of the Marian identification. Gregory was not alone in equating “Mary the mother of James and Joses” with the Virgin: Chrysostom reasons the same way at Matthew 27:56, and the identification had already appeared in an infancy apocryphon, the History of Joseph the Carpenter (§4), from which Lightfoot suggests Gregory may have drawn it. Lightfoot adds that the same equation “was also part of the Helvidian hypothesis, where it was less out of place, and gave Jerome an easy triumph over his adversary” (Against Helvidius 12). Lightfoot judged Gregory’s identification “very untenable” while filing Gregory with the Epiphanian authorities (brethren = Joseph’s sons; the Lord’s brother not of the Twelve).3
  • Mary’s virginity in Gregory’s other writings. In the companion Easter homily preserved here, On the three days between the death and resurrection of our Lord Jesus Christ, he dwells on “the unwed mother, the fatherless flesh, the painless labor, the unblemished birth,” expounding Isaiah 7:14 of a virgin birth free of the pangs of ordinary childbearing.4 His treatise On Virginity makes the ascetic case at length, and his brother Basil also appears on this timeline as a witness to the perpetual virginity.
  • Relation to the three theories. Gregory is Epiphanian — the brethren are Joseph’s sons, no children of Mary — and, like the other Eastern witnesses cited here, does not mention the Hieronymian cousins-theory; his statement that the Lord’s brother is not James son of Alphaeus of the Twelve differs from it. His identification of the Theotokos with “the mother of James and Joses” is an identification of his own within that Epiphanian frame, not a fourth position.
