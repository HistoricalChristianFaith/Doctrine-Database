Theodoret of Cyrus on the perpetual virginity of Mary

Dates: c. 440 (his Interpretation of the Fourteen Epistles of St Paul, the Antiochene exegete’s complete Pauline commentary, written at Cyrrhus in the decade before the Council of Chalcedon; Theodoret c. 393 – c. 458/466, bishop of Cyrrhus) · Wikipedia
Summary: Theodoret, expounding Galatians 1:19, states the Hieronymian reading, naming and rejecting the Epiphanian account: James “the brother of the Lord” was not so by nature, and was not, “as some suppose, the son of Joseph by a previous marriage,” but the son of Clopas and the Lord’s cousin, his mother being “the sister of the Lord’s mother.” He supports it with the argument from the cross that Epiphanian writers also used — that the dying Christ gave Mary to John, which he would not have done had she a son of her own in James. Lightfoot files him last in his column C, “Sons of the Virgin’s Sister.”

Claims & evidence

James the cousin, not Joseph’s son by a former wife

Where earlier Eastern witnesses had made the “brethren” Joseph’s sons by a first marriage, Theodoret, commenting on Paul’s “James the Lord’s brother” (Galatians 1:19), sets that account aside by name and gives the cousins-reading instead:

He was called “the brother of the Lord” but was not so by nature. For he was not, as some suppose, the son of Joseph by a previous marriage but the son of Clopas and cousin of the Lord. For his mother was the sister of the Lord’s mother. … He was thought by others to be the Lord’s brother, both because their mothers had the same names and because the families shared one house.

— Theodoret, Commentary on Galatians 1:19.1 Two features of the note figure in the debate. First, the explicit rejection: “he was not, as some suppose, the son of Joseph by a previous marriage” rejects the Epiphanian reading — the reading a Hilary or an Epiphanius had defended. Second, the positive identification is Jerome’s in substance: James is the son of Clopas, the Lord’s cousin, born of “the sister of the Lord’s mother” — the “Mary of Clopas” of John 19:25, central to the Hieronymian reading. The apparent brotherhood Theodoret explains as a double accident of names and household: the two Marys bore the same name and the two families lived under one roof, so a cousin passed for a brother.

The argument from the entrustment at the cross

Theodoret then reaches for the argument from the cross — the same proof the Epiphanians had pressed for their reading (so Hilary: Mary would never have been given to John had she sons of her own) — and applies it to the cousins-reading:

For the sacred story of the Gospels tells us that the Blessed Virgin had no other son. For seeing her by the cross, the Lord gave her to the most divine John, but he would not have committed her to another if the blessed James, a man possessed of extreme virtue, had been her son.

— Theodoret, Commentary on Galatians 1:19.1 The argument runs: had Mary borne James, the dying Christ would have entrusted her to him, not to John (John 19:26–27). Theodoret uses it to conclude not only that Mary bore no later children but that James, though “a man possessed of extreme virtue,” was not her son at all — the same text Epiphanian writers used to place the brethren in Joseph’s first family. Richard Bauckham (Jude and the Relatives of Jesus, 1990), who himself holds the Epiphanian reading, calls the argument from the entrustment “beside the point”: “With Jesus’ death his earthly relationships to his natural family no longer count.”2

Reception and context

  • Relation to the three theories. Theodoret is filed with the Hieronymian reading — the brethren are the Lord’s cousins, sons of Clopas and of the Virgin’s sister, and Mary bore no other child — and he holds it while rejecting the Epiphanian alternative by name. Lightfoot finds the “son of Clopas” identification “first” in Chrysostom, who “incidentally speaks of James the Lord’s brother as ‘son of Clopas,’” “and after him in Theodoret who is more explicit.”3
  • “As some suppose.” Theodoret names the Epiphanian account in order to reject it, so his classification as Hieronymian rests on an explicit statement rather than on inference.
  • Dating. Theodoret’s complete commentary on the fourteen Pauline epistles is placed in the 430s–440s, before the Christological controversies of 448–451; it is slotted here at c. 440, after Pelagius (c. 406). Theodoret is the last name in Lightfoot’s column C.3 (Theodoret’s wider corpus is anchored later, c. 458; on this timeline he is dated by the Pauline commentary.)
