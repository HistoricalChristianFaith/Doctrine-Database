Martin Luther on the perpetual virginity of Mary

Dates: c. 1523 (That Jesus Christ Was Born a Jew / Daß Jesus Christus ein geborner Jude sei, 1523; Martin Luther, 1483 – 1546, Augustinian friar and Reformer) · Wikipedia
Summary: In That Jesus Christ Was Born a Jew (1523) the Reformer Martin Luther defends the perpetual virginity against the Helvidian reading of Matthew 1:18, 25, calling it “this babble of Helvidius… without justification.” He answers the two inferences — that “before they came together” and “knew her not till she brought forth her firstborn son” imply Joseph knew Mary afterward — by the idiom-argument Jerome had used: the Scriptural “until” states what held before the terminus and says nothing of after, illustrated by Pharaoh who never believed Moses though he “believed not… until he was drowned,” and by Psalm 110:1, Genesis 28:15, and Isaiah 42:4. His concern in that work is the virginity itself, not the identity of the “brethren,” which it never takes up; but his later Sermons on the Gospel of St. John (1537–39) does take the brethren up, inclining to the reading that they are cousins — the Hieronymian view — so on that text he is filed with it. Zwingli and Calvin, also on this timeline, likewise reject the Helvidian reading.

Claims & evidence

Against Helvidius on “came together” and “firstborn” (Matthew 1:18, 25)

Luther first names and rejects the Helvidian construction of the two Matthean phrases — that “before they came together she was found to be with child” implies a later coming-together, and that Joseph “knew her not until she brought forth her first-born son” implies he knew her after — as a “carnal” interpretation that misses the evangelist’s purpose:

Now this refutes also the false interpretation which some have drawn from the words of Matthew… when he says, “And Joseph knew her not until she brought forth her first-born son” [Matt. 1:25], they interpret it as though the evangelist meant to say that he knew her, but not before she had brought forth her first-born son. This was the view of Helvidius which was refuted by Jerome.

Such carnal interpretations miss the meaning and purpose of the evangelist… the words of the evangelist do not refer to anything that occurred after the birth, but only to what took place before it… Therefore, one cannot from these words [Matt. 1:18, 25] conclude that Mary, after the birth of Christ, became a wife in the usual sense; it is therefore neither to be asserted nor believed.

— That Jesus Christ Was Born a Jew (1523).1 The evangelist, Luther argues, “like the prophet Isaiah, wishes to set before our eyes this mighty wonder… that a maiden… be with child before her husband brings her home” — so the “firstborn” and “until” language secures the virginal conception and reports nothing whatever of a later marriage.

The idiom of “until” — Pharaoh, and the Old-Testament parallels

He then gives his grammatical argument: the “until” of Matthew 1:25 is “the common idiom,” which fixes what was true up to a point without implying any change after it. His first parallel is Pharaoh, who never believed Moses at all:

The form of expression used by Matthew is the common idiom, as if I were to say, “Pharaoh believed not Moses, until he was drowned in the Red Sea.” Here it does not follow that Pharaoh believed later, after he had drowned; on the contrary, it means that he never did believe. Similarly when Matthew [1:25] says that Joseph did not know Mary carnally until she had brought forth her son, it does not follow that he knew her subsequently; on the contrary, it means that he never did know her.

Elsewhere in Scripture the same manner of speech is employed. Psalm 110 [:1] reads, “God says to my Lord: ‘Sit at my right hand, till I make your enemies your footstool.’” Here it does not follow that Christ does not continue to sit there after his enemies are placed beneath his feet. Again, in Genesis 28 [:15], “I will not leave you until I have done all that of which I have spoken to you.” Here God did not leave him after the fulfillment had taken place. Again, in Isaiah 42 [:4], “He shall not be sad, nor troublesome, till he has established justice in the earth.” There are many more similar expression, so that this babble of Helvidius is without justification.

— That Jesus Christ Was Born a Jew (1523).2 The same answer on the Matthean “till” is given on this timeline by Jerome, Chrysostom, Chromatius, John of Damascus, and Aquinas. Luther’s parallels (Pharaoh; Psalm 110:1; Genesis 28:15; Isaiah 42:4) are instances of the one-word “until”; Eric Svendsen (Protestant, doctoral thesis, 2001) distinguishes such uses from the two-word heōs hou of Matthew 1:25, which he reads as marking a change after the term (see John of Damascus).

The brethren as “cousins” — the Sermons on the Gospel of St. John

Where That Jesus Christ Was Born a Jew is silent on who the “brethren of the Lord” were, Luther’s later Sermons on the Gospel of St. John (preached in Wittenberg, 1537–39) takes the question up directly. Expounding the mention of Jesus’ brothers at John 2:12, he weighs the explanations on offer — that they were Joseph’s sons by a former or a second wife (the step-brother reading) — and inclines instead to the cousin sense:

Christ… was the only Son of Mary, and the Virgin Mary bore no children besides Him… I am inclined to agree with those who declare that “brothers” really mean “cousins” here, for Holy Writ and the Jews always call cousins brothers.

— Sermons on the Gospel of St. John, on John 2:12 (1539).3 This is the Hieronymian reading of the brethren, resting on the cousin-meaning of adelphos Jerome had argued for. Luther grounds it on the claim that “Holy Writ and the Jews always call cousins brothers.” On this text he is filed, unlike in his own 1523 tract and unlike the more guarded Calvin, with the cousin (Hieronymian) reading.

Reception and context

  • Classification: Hieronymian (on the later evidence). In That Jesus Christ Was Born a Jew (1523) Luther defends the perpetual virginity itself against the Helvidian reading, without addressing who the “brethren of the Lord” were; there, like John of Damascus and Chromatius, his witness bears on the virginity alone. But the Sermons on the Gospel of St. John (1537–39) do take up the brethren, inclining to the reading that they are cousins rather than Epiphanian step-brothers (Joseph’s sons by a former wife) — which files Luther, on that text, with the Hieronymian reading.
  • Luther among the Reformers. Luther defends the perpetual virginity here in 1523; Huldrych Zwingli professed that Mary “remained… a pure and unsullied virgin, for eternity,” and John Calvin rejected the contrary inference from “firstborn” and “till” in Matthew 1:25. Their statements figure in the dispute over when the doctrine was held and defined; see “Is the perpetual virginity a late development?”
  • Scope of the 1523 tract. The tract does not take up the immaculate conception, the assumption, or the brethren, whose identity Luther addresses only later, in the John sermons above; its subject is that Mary remained ever-virgin and that Matthew 1:18, 25 do not imply otherwise.
