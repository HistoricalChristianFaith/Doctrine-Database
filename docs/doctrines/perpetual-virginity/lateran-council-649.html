The Lateran Council of 649 on the perpetual virginity of Mary

Dates: 649 (a Roman synod of some 105 bishops convened at the Basilica of St John Lateran under Pope Martin I, chiefly to condemn Monothelitism — the doctrine of a single will in Christ — and the imperial documents that had promoted it. Its twenty canons include the third, on the Mother of God, quoted here. Not counted among the ecumenical councils; a papal synod whose faith was afterward received at the Third Council of Constantinople, 680–681) · Wikipedia
Summary: A Roman synod a century after Constantinople II (553): where that council named Mary aeiparthenos in a single clause, the Lateran canon spells the triple virginity out — before, in, and after the birth — and puts denial under anathema. It addresses the virginity itself and, like the councils and Fathers around it, does not address the identity of the Lord’s brethren.

Claims & evidence

“Her virginity remaining indestructible even after His birth”

The synod’s third canon confesses Mary ever virgin and states the doctrine through all three moments — conceiving without seed, bearing incorruptibly, and remaining a virgin after the birth — condemning any who will not so confess:

If anyone does not properly and truly confess in accord with the holy Fathers, that the holy Mother of God and ever Virgin and immaculate Mary in the earliest of the ages conceived of the Holy Spirit without seed, namely, God the Word Himself specifically and truly, who was born of God the Father before all ages, and that she incorruptibly bore [Him], her virginity remaining indestructible even after His birth, let him be condemned.

— Lateran Council (649), Canon 3 (Denzinger no. 256; later editions DS 503).1 The canon’s three clauses are the standard Western triad — virginity ante partum (“conceived… without seed”), in partu (“incorruptibly bore”), and post partum (“remaining indestructible even after His birth”) — the threefold formula found in Augustine (virgo concepit, virgo peperit, virgo permansit) and in Leo the Great’s Christmas preaching. Here it is stated as a canon: denial falls under the council’s anathema.

What the canon addresses

The canon confesses the perpetual virginity in all three moments, naming the in partu (“incorruptibly bore”) and post partum (“even after His birth”) virginity. It offers no account of the “brethren of the Lord,” so it does not address the choice between the Epiphanian and Hieronymian readings. Like Constantinople II, Leo the Great, and John of Damascus, it attests the virginity itself and does not address the genealogy of the brethren, so it is not filed under any of the readings of them.

Reception and context

  • “Immaculate” in the canon. The canon calls Mary “immaculate” (Greek achrantos, unstained) as a title of her purity; the canon’s subject is her virginity. The Catholic dogma of the Immaculate Conception was defined in 1854 (Pius IX, Ineffabilis Deus).
  • Standing of the synod. The Lateran Council of 649 is not counted among the ecumenical councils; it was presided over and sanctioned by Pope Martin I, who proposed its faith as a term of communion and put its denial under anathema, and the Third Council of Constantinople (680–681) afterward received the faith it formulated.2 Its date figures in the dispute over when the doctrine was first defined; see “Is the perpetual virginity a late development?”
  • Text and edition. The English is the translation of Roy J. Deferrari in Henry Denzinger, The Sources of Catholic Dogma (Enchiridion Symbolorum), no. 256 (St Louis: B. Herder, 1957); the same canon is numbered DS 503 in the later Denzinger–Schönmetzer editions. The Greek text of the canon, of equal authority with the Latin, has aphthoros (“without corruption”) for the birth.
