Ambrose of Milan on the perpetual virginity of Mary

Dates: c. 392 (On the Instruction of the Virgin and the Perpetual Virginity of Mary, the treatise De Institutione Virginis, addressed to Eusebius of Bologna; Ambrose was bishop of Milan c. 374–397) · Wikipedia
Summary: Ambrose devotes a treatise to defending Mary’s perpetual virginity against those of his day who denied it, and when he reaches the “brethren of the Lord” he gives the Epiphanian answer — the term “brothers” is a wide one, and they “could be from Joseph, not from Mary.” He declines to pursue the identification or to argue out which relationship is meant: it is enough for his purpose that “brother” runs wider than sons of one mother. J. B. Lightfoot read that reticence as deliberate — in his view Ambrose had heard of Jerome’s cousins-theory but would not adopt it, and, valuing coöperation “against a common enemy,” declined to divide the defenders of the virginity over details. Lightfoot filed him in the “sons of Joseph by a former wife” column of his conspectus, next after Epiphanius.

Claims & evidence

A treatise answering those who denied the perpetual virginity

Ambrose’s De Institutione Virginis is a book of instruction for consecrated virgins, but its long central section (chapters V–VIII) turns aside to answer those who had begun to deny that Mary remained a virgin after Christ’s birth. He treats the denial as a scandal forced into the open:

And yet, even though everyone is called to imitate the holy virginity of Mary, there were some who denied that she remained a virgin. We have long preferred to remain silent about this great sacrilege, but because the matter has been brought into the open in such a way that even the Bishop himself was accused of her fall, we believe it should not be left unpunished.

— On the Instruction of the Virgin and the Perpetual Virginity of Mary, ch. V.1 He then works through the stock objections one by one — Mary called “woman” (John 2:4), “before they came together” (Matthew 1:18), “knew her not till” (Matthew 1:25), Joseph taking his “wife” — and answers each in defence of the ever-virginity, closing the argument with the figure of Ezekiel’s gate: Mary is the “closed gate” of Ezekiel 44:1–3, through which the Lord passed while “the locks of virginity were not opened… it shall not be opened by Joseph.”1

“Brothers” as a wide term, and the brethren as sons of Joseph

Reaching the objection from the “brethren of the Lord,” Ambrose does not grant that they were Mary’s sons, nor does he set out to prove whose sons they were. He argues instead that “brother” in Scripture reaches beyond common parentage, and offers the Epiphanian answer without pursuing it:

Moreover, the Lord himself teaches that brothers may be called the fellowship of the family and the people, when he says: I will tell your name to my brothers; in the midst of the Church I will praise you (Psalm 21:23). Paul also says: I wished myself to be an anathema for the sake of my brothers (Romans 9:3). However, the brothers could be from Joseph, not from Mary. Indeed, if someone were to pursue this further, they would find it. We did not consider it necessary to pursue this, since it is clear that the name of brotherhood is common to many.

— On the Instruction of the Virgin and the Perpetual Virginity of Mary, ch. VI.1 The reading Ambrose offers is the Epiphanian one — the brethren are Joseph’s sons, kin to Jesus through Joseph’s household, not children of Mary. He says that one who pursues the question “would find” this — which J. B. Lightfoot rendered, from the Latin, “he will find this to be the true account.”2 In Lightfoot’s rendering he then declines to go further: it is “of no importance to decide what particular relationship is implied,” and enough for his purpose that “brother” is used in an extended sense. He affirms the virginity, and leaves the precise standing of the brethren undecided.

Lightfoot’s reading of the passage and Jerome’s tract

Ambrose wrote in 392 (Lightfoot’s date), about nine years after Jerome had published the Hieronymian hypothesis (the brethren as maternal cousins, sons of Mary of Clopas). From a passage he called “perhaps intentionally obscure” Lightfoot drew a threefold inference — that Ambrose “had heard of, though possibly not read, Jerome’s tract”; that had he read it, “he did not feel inclined to abandon the view with which he was familiar” — the Epiphanian — “in favour of the novel hypothesis put forward by Jerome”; and that, “seeing the importance of coöperation against a common enemy, he was anxious not to raise dissensions among the champions of the perpetual virginity by the discussion of details”:

In a passage which is perhaps intentionally obscure he speaks to this effect: ‘The term brothers has a wide application… Doubtless they might be called brothers as sons of Joseph, not of Mary. And if any one will go into the question carefully, he will find this to be the true account. For myself I do not intend to enter upon this question: it is of no importance to decide what particular relationship is implied; it is sufficient for my purpose that the term “brethren” is used in an extended sense.’

— Lightfoot’s paraphrase and reading of the passage.2 On Lightfoot’s reading, Ambrose holds the Epiphanian view, declines to contend for it, and puts the common defence of the virginity first. Lightfoot set him in his conspectus of the “sons of Joseph by a former wife” authorities, in the same column as Gregory of Nyssa and Epiphanius, immediately after them.2

Reception and context

  • Relation to the three theories. Ambrose gives the Epiphanian answer — the brethren are sons of Joseph, not of Mary — while declining to argue the identification or to rule out other senses of “brother.” He does not take up Jerome’s cousins-theory, and Lightfoot took his refusal to “enter upon this question” as an unwillingness to adopt what Lightfoot called “the novel hypothesis put forward by Jerome.” The perpetual virginity itself he defends throughout the treatise.
  • The setting — the case of Bonosus. The same years saw the case of Bonosus of Sardica, who is reported to have taught that Mary bore other children. A letter preserved among Ambrose’s letters (ascribed by its editor, following Tillemont, probably to Siricius of Rome) records that the Synod of Capua assigned the case to the bishops of Macedonia under the bishop of Thessalonica, and that Bonosus afterwards “sent to our brother Ambrose to enquire his opinion.”3 Whether the “Bishop… accused of her fall” in chapter V refers to this case is not stated in the treatise; the connection is ⚠ unverified.
  • Dating. Lightfoot dates the work to 392; a range of c. 391–393 is also given ⚠ unverified. It is slotted at c. 392, after Jerome’s Against Helvidius (c. 383).
