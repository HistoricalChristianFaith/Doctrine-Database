British Israelism: the Anglo-Saxons as the Lost Tribes

British Israelism (or Anglo-Israelism) is the claim that the peoples of Britain — and, in some versions, the wider Germanic and English-speaking world — are the literal descendants of the Ten Lost Tribes of Israel, carried off by Assyria in 722 BC and supposed to have migrated across Europe to the British Isles. The identification is built chiefly on prophecy applied to modern nations and on a chain of philological look-alikes (“Saxon” from “Isaac's sons,” “British” from Hebrew b'rith-ish, “covenant-man”). It is a distinct thesis from the belief that Old-World peoples crossed an ocean and founded the civilizations of pre-Columbian America — the American cousin locates the tribes across the Atlantic, British Israelism locates them in Europe — but the two currents grew alongside each other, traded proof-texts, and eventually fused in the twentieth-century movements that carried both.

This page tells that four-century story as a reception-history timeline: not a survey of belief-among-many-witnesses but a chronology of the datable sources who advanced or answered the claim, earliest first. Its “witnesses” are therefore dated books, tracts, institutions, and rebuttals — an imprisoned visionary's revelation, a Scottish autodidact's lectures, an Astronomer Royal's pyramid numerology, a bank clerk's forty-seven “identifications,” an encyclopaedia's dismissal, an American minister's synthesis, a royal-patronised federation, a radio evangelist's booklet, a racist mutation, and a modern historian's autopsy of the whole idea — each footnoted to its source. Two threads run through it and are worth watching for: a scholarly counter-voice is present early and never goes away (the philological method was judged worthless by 1901 and the “lost” tribes shown to be a myth by 2002), and the affirmative case rests, at bottom, on sound-alike etymology that no historical linguist accepts.

The wiki weighs this thesis; it does not endorse it, and it treats its darkest offshoot — the antisemitic Christian Identity movement that grew out of Anglo-Israelism in twentieth-century America — strictly as a matter of documented history. Whether the core identification holds is weighed adversarially in the Arguments below and comes out unsupported: phonetic resemblance across unrelated languages is not descent, and the Assyrian deportees were assimilated, not spirited to the North Sea.

Timeline

Richard Brothers proclaims the English the hidden Hebrews (1794–1822)

The eccentric first voice is the Newfoundland-born ex-naval officer Richard Brothers, who in A Revealed Knowledge of the Prophecies and Times (1794) styled himself “Nephew of the Almighty” and a descendant of David, and prophesied that he would be revealed “Prince of the Hebrews” and lead the Jews back to Jerusalem. He held that great numbers of the English were themselves unwitting Israelites. When the promised revelation failed to arrive in November 1795 and he foretold the death of the king, he was arrested for treason and confined as criminally insane; his following collapsed. His later A Correct Account of the Invasion of England by the Saxons (1822) argued the English descend from the lost tribes and is often cited as the movement's seed — though its author was a discredited prophet.1

John Wilson gives it a system: Our Israelitish Origin (1840)

The intellectual founder of the doctrine in its modern form is the Scottish autodidact lecturer John Wilson (1799–1870), who in the 1830s toured with a series of lectures on the Israelite genealogy of the northern European peoples, collected in 1840 as Our Israelitish Origin. Wilson argued that the Ten Lost Tribes had travelled from the Near East across Europe to the British Isles, and he assigned Britain to the tribe of Ephraim. His view was inclusive: not the British alone but the Germanic peoples generally were Israel — a breadth his successors would narrow. The book put the theory “upon its present basis” and seeded the lecture-hall movement that followed.2

Charles Piazzi Smyth fuses it with the Great Pyramid (1864)

The doctrine gained an air of scientific respectability — and a lasting crank appendage — from Charles Piazzi Smyth, Astronomer Royal for Scotland, whose Our Inheritance in the Great Pyramid (1864) married British Israelism to pyramidology. Smyth held that the Great Pyramid had been built under divine guidance (by Hebrews, he thought, whom he identified with the Hyksos), that it encoded a sacred “pyramid inch” providentially preserved in the British imperial inch, and that its passages enciphered prophetic dates for the British nation. The work sold through five editions but wrecked his scientific standing; its enthusiasts were disproportionately British-Israelites, and later writers extended the pyramid-prophecy game for decades. Whether the “pyramid inch” and the passage-dates hold up is weighed separately in the Arguments below (assessment: unsupported on both).3

Edward Hine popularizes it: the “forty-seven identifications” (1871)

The doctrine's great popularizer was Edward Hine (1825–1891), a bank clerk who as a boy of fifteen had heard Wilson lecture and carried the “thought” for thirty years before taking the platform himself in 1869. His Identification of the British Nation with Lost Israel (1871), soon expanded into the Forty-Seven Identifications of the British Nation with the Lost Ten Tribes of Israel, marshalled hundreds of “scripture proofs” and became the movement's runaway bestseller; Hine claimed to have addressed some five million hearers over his career. Against Wilson he preached the exclusive view — the English-speaking peoples alone are Israel, while the Continental Germans are Assyrians — and this narrower line won out organizationally. He founded the British-Israel Identity Corporation (1880) and in 1884 carried the message to the United States, teaching that America was Manasseh and Britain Ephraim.4

The Jewish Encyclopedia dismisses the philology (1901)

A weighty scholarly answer arrived with the Jewish Encyclopedia's article “Anglo-Israelism” (1901–1906), which set out the theory's proof-texts and its etymological method — Cimmerians as “Berit-ish” men of the covenant, Saxons as “Isaac's sons” — and judged that “a religious system which seeks to justify its claims by an appeal to resemblances in words of different languages succeeds only in displaying the poverty of its proofs.” It stands here as the datable point at which the movement's central engine, its sound-alike philology, was flatly rejected by reference scholarship.5

J. H. Allen systematizes it for America: Judah's Sceptre and Joseph's Birthright (1902)

The American synthesis came from the Holiness minister John Harden Allen (1847–1930), whose Judah's Sceptre and Joseph's Birthright (1902) split the biblical inheritance in two: Judah keeps the royal “sceptre” (the Davidic line, traced to the British crown), while Joseph's sons Ephraim and Manasseh receive the national “birthright” — the British Commonwealth and the United States. Allen's book became the template for the twentieth-century American movement; its distinctive Ephraim-Britain / Manasseh-America scheme passed, largely intact, into the far more widely-read work of Herbert W. Armstrong.6

The British-Israel-World Federation: the institutional high-water mark (1919)

The movement reached its organizational peak just after the First World War. On 3 July 1919 some eighty independent Anglo-Israel associations across Britain and the Dominions federated in London as the British-Israel-World Federation, whose inaugural congress drew a reported twenty thousand and which soon enjoyed royal patronage (Princess Alice, Countess of Athlone) and its own press, Covenant Publishing (1922). This was the respectable, imperial-Protestant face of the doctrine — the version an Edwardian clergyman might hold without embarrassment — and the federation survives today.7

Herbert W. Armstrong takes it to millions (1945)

The doctrine reached its largest audience through the radio evangelist Herbert W. Armstrong, whose The United States and British Commonwealth in Prophecy (first issued 1945; repeatedly revised) was broadcast and mailed to millions through the Worldwide Church of God, The World Tomorrow radio programme, and The Plain Truth magazine. Armstrong presented the Anglo-Israel identification as a divinely revealed “master key” to prophecy — but his booklet drew heavily, in places nearly verbatim, on Allen's 1902 work, a dependence later acknowledged even by leaders of his own church, who noted that the copied text carried over “the historical errors” as well.8

The Christian-Identity mutation (1920s–1940s, documented by Barkun 1994)

Transplanted to the United States, one branch of Anglo-Israelism curdled. As the historian Michael Barkun documents in Religion and the Racist Right (1994), Howard Rand's Anglo-Saxon Federation of America (from 1928) fused the doctrine with Depression-era antisemitism, and in postwar California the preacher Wesley Swift reworked it into Christian Identity — a theology holding that white “Aryans” are the true Israel and that Jews are impostors, which became a scriptural charter for Aryan Nations, the Posse Comitatus, and allied groups. The step is logical once Anglo-Israelism's premise is granted: if the Anglo-Saxons are Israel, the actual Jewish people must be counterfeits. Most British-Israelites never took that step and repudiated the racism — the point here is historical, that the antisemitic movement grew from this root.9

Tudor Parfitt: the “lost tribes” as a myth (2002)

The modern scholarly verdict is set out by the historian Tudor Parfitt, whose The Lost Tribes of Israel: The History of a Myth (2002) treats British Israelism as one instance of a recurring Western habit — projecting Israelite descent onto peoples from Britain to the Americas to Papua New Guinea — often in the service of empire. Parfitt's point is not merely that the philology fails but that the premise itself is a construction: the deported northern tribes were absorbed into the populations around them and left no locatable “lost” nation to rediscover, in Britain or anywhere else.10

Arguments

Are the Anglo-Saxon peoples the Ten Lost Tribes of Israel? (assessment: unsupported)

The core identification — the claim on which the whole tradition stands, taken in its strongest form and weighed against the linguistic, historical, and genetic evidence. It comes out unsupported: the load-bearing “Saxon = Isaac's sons” and “British = covenant-man” etymologies are folk resemblances that collapse on contact with historical philology (Saxon is from the seax knife; British is Brythonic Celtic and predates any Hebrew contact); the migration route is unattested and the Assyrian deportees are known to have been resettled and assimilated; and the identification survives only by reading prophecy back onto modern nations. The older reference scholarship (the 1901 Jewish Encyclopedia) and the modern historical treatment (Parfitt, 2002) converge on the same verdict.11

Do the Genesis birthright and sceptre promises require an Israel outside Judaism? (assessment: weak)

The prophetic leg — the one part of the case that stands apart from the sound-alike etymologies, weighed on its own. J. H. Allen’s whole thesis (his 1902 title is Judah’s Sceptre and Joseph’s Birthright) splits the inheritance in two: Judah keeps the royal “sceptre” (Gen. 49:10), Joseph’s sons Ephraim and Manasseh the national “birthright” promised to become “a company of nations” (Gen. 35:11; 48:19) — and argues that promises this large were never fulfilled in the Jews and therefore require a great Israelite people elsewhere (Britain and America). It comes out weak. The birthright/sceptre distinction is genuinely in the text (1 Chr. 5:1–2), so this is not the empty case the philology is; but the leap from “the promises are large” to “a lost Israelite nation must exist” is a non-sequitur that presupposes its own conclusion. The “multitude of nations” idiom is worded of Abraham himself (Gen. 17:4–6) with no one demanding modern nations for it; the promises are already satisfied in the historic tribes, in the Jewish people, and (in the mainstream Christian reading) in the multinational church that inherits Abraham’s “many nations” by faith (Rom. 4; Gal. 3); and the patristic tradition reads the sceptre-text as ending Judah’s line in Christ — “until Shiloh come” — not as a royal succession in transit to the British crown.12

Does the Great Pyramid encode British-Israel prophecy? (the “pyramid inch”)

The pyramidology fusion — Piazzi Smyth's separate proof-mechanism, weighing its two sub-claims apart. It comes out unsupported on both. The metrology claim — that the Pyramid embodies a sacred “pyramid inch” providentially matched to the British inch — fails on Flinders Petrie's precise 1880–82 survey, which found the monument built in the ordinary Egyptian royal cubit (c. 20.6 in.) and no trace of any “pyramid inch”; the near-match to the British inch is a coincidence manufactured by the chosen unit. The prophecy-dating claim — that the passages, read at an inch per year, encipher dates for the British nation — is worse: its forward predictions (a “tribulation” in 1928, a crisis in 1936, and after) failed and were serially rewritten, and the whole timeline rests on the same non-existent unit.13

Did Armstrong's booklet derive from J. H. Allen's Judah's Sceptre (1902)? (assessment: sound)

The authorship question — not whether the identification is true (that is the descent argument above, unsupported) but where the text of the movement's most widely-read booklet came from. Herbert W. Armstrong presented The United States and British Commonwealth in Prophecy (1945) as a divinely revealed “master key” to prophecy; the claim weighed here is that it derives, in structure, examples, and often nearly verbatim wording, from J. H. Allen's Judah's Sceptre and Joseph's Birthright (1902), which it never names. It comes out sound: the two texts share not just a thesis but a run of particular choices — an Irish Dan-place-name list in the same order, the “not all Americans are Californians” analogy, the “race, not grace” refrain, the identical b'rith-ish etymology, the twelve-pointed crown, the Jeremiah-to-Ireland legend; Armstrong admitted reading Allen; and the head of his own church later granted that the copied text carried over even Allen's “historical errors,” before the church withdrew the booklet in 1990. The verdict concerns provenance, not truth: a borrowed argument and a false one are independent questions.14
