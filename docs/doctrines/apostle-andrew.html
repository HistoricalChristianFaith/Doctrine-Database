Andrew

Andrew was Simon Peter's brother and a fisherman of Bethsaida; John's Gospel records that he was a disciple of John the Baptist, followed Jesus when the Baptist pointed him out, and at once went to fetch his brother. The New Testament records nothing of his later mission or death. This page sets out, earliest extant source first, what the sources report about him: the Gospel notices, the early Fathers, the apocryphal Acts of Andrew, the Byzantine apostle-lists and the late-medieval Golden Legend. It is a biographical tradition-timeline: its “witnesses” are the datable sources that report on Andrew. Three questions about the tradition — whether he was crucified at Patras, the shape of his cross, and whether he founded the church of Byzantium — are set out on their own pages under Disputed readings. For the sources common to all the apostles, see the apostles hub.

Timeline

The New Testament (c. 30–90)

The New Testament names Andrew as a fisherman of Bethsaida and the brother of Simon Peter, called with him from the nets. John's Gospel adds that he was first a disciple of John the Baptist, and that when the Baptist pointed to “the Lamb of God” Andrew followed Jesus and then found Simon: “We have found the Messias … And he brought him to Jesus.” Greek tradition calls him Prōtoklētos, “the first-called.” He appears twice more in John — pointing out the boy with the loaves, and bringing word of the enquiring Greeks — and is named in all four lists of the Twelve. The New Testament records nothing of his mission or death.1

Origen, preserved by Eusebius (c. 230)

Eusebius (c. 325), in a passage he ascribes to Origen's Commentary on Genesis, gives the earliest extant datable notice of an Andrew mission field; Joseph MacRory writes that Eusebius relies “apparently, upon Origen.” In the apostles' “division of the world,” “Scythia” — identified by the editor of the English translation as the region north of the Black Sea — “was allotted to Andrew,” as Parthia went to Thomas and Asia to John. The notice names no other field and says nothing of Achaia, Patras or a martyrdom.2

The Acts of Andrew (c. 150–210)

The apocryphal Acts of Andrew — a second-century romance, surviving in fragments and in a later Greek Acts and Martyrdom — is the earliest extant source for Andrew's end: a mission in Achaia, a clash with the proconsul Aegeates at Patras over the worship of idols, a long dialogue on the “mystery of the cross,” and a death in which Andrew is “lifted on the cross” and bound by ropes — not nailed — so as to prolong his suffering. The death-narrative does not describe the cross's shape.3

Jerome (395)

Jerome, arguing in a letter to Marcella that the risen Christ is everywhere at once, lists the apostles by their fields — Christ was “with Thomas in India, with Peter at Rome, with Paul in Illyricum, with Titus in Crete, with Andrew in Achaia.” The line concerns Christ's presence, not a martyrdom; it places Andrew's ministry in Achaia, the province of the Acts' Patras account.4

The apostle-lists and the Byzantine tradition (5th–10th c.)

Philostorgius records that Andrew's relics were carried to Constantinople in 357; Joseph MacRory (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1907) writes that they were “deposited in the church of the Apostles there.” Gregory of Tours' Latin epitome of the Acts (sixth century) takes Andrew from Pontus to Patras by way of Byzantium. The Greek apostle-lists ascribed to Pseudo-Epiphanius and to Pseudo-Dorotheus of Tyre, and the related Story of Andrew, make him the founder of the church of Byzantium, who ordained Stachys (the man Paul greets in Romans 16:9) as its first bishop at Argyropolis. Tony Burke (NASSCAL) dates the Pseudo-Dorotheus compilation to the eighth century, before 810/811; Francis Dvornik (1958) places the lists at the very end of the eighth century at the earliest, notes that the seventh-century Chronicon Paschale mentions Stachys without the foundation, traces the spread of the Andrew–Stachys account in the tenth century, and follows its later use in the controversies between Constantinople and Rome.5

The Golden Legend (c. 1260)

Jacobus de Voragine's Golden Legend gives a full life of Andrew — his baptizing the proconsul's wife, his disputation with Aegeas on the mystery of the cross, and his hanging alive on the cross, “bounden by the feet and hands,” preaching to the crowds for two days; its account does not describe the cross's shape. Joseph MacRory (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1907) reports that the cross “is commonly held to have been the decussate cross, now known as St. Andrew's, though the evidence for this view seems to be no older than the fourteenth century.”6

Disputed readings

How the sources have been read on Andrew's death and on his link with Byzantium.

  • Was Andrew crucified at Patras in Achaia? — the Acts of Andrew's account beside Origen's assignment of Scythia.
  • On what shape of cross was Andrew crucified? — the Latin cross reported for Gregory of Tours' epitome of the Acts and the later X-shaped saltire.
  • Did Andrew found the church of Byzantium and ordain Stachys its first bishop? — the apostle-lists' foundation account and its dating.
