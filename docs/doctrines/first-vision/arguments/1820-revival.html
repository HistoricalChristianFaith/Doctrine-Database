The revival that dates the vision: was there an 1820 Palmyra awakening?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — the documented Palmyra church-membership revival falls in 1824–25, not 1820, but a broader regional reading of “the place where we lived” is amply attested in 1819–20; the dispute turns on the scope of the phrase and neither side lands a knockout · pressed by Wesley P. Walters, answered by Milton V. Backman.

The claim

In short, our investigation shows that the statement of Joseph Smith, Jr., cannot be true when he claims that he was stirred by an 1820 revival … It is not until the revival of 1824–25 that we find a situation that matches the conditions described in this official first vision story.

— Wesley P. Walters, “New Light on Mormon Origins from the Palmyra Revival” (1967/1969), Dialogue. The argument attacks the 1838 account's setting, not its theology: Joseph placed the vision amid “an unusual excitement on the subject of religion” in about 1820, and Walters contends the documentary record shows no such Palmyra revival then — the great local awakening came in 1824–25, after Joseph says the vision already occurred.1

Who advances it

Walters, a Presbyterian minister, published the challenge in the Bulletin of the Evangelical Theological Society (1967), reprinted in Dialogue (1969) with a faithful roundtable reply. The counter-case was carried by Milton V. Backman Jr. of Brigham Young University (Joseph Smith's First Vision, 1971/1980) and, in the same 1969 exchange and later in Rough Stone Rolling, by Richard Bushman; a further refinement is Richard Lloyd Anderson's work on an 1818 Palmyra camp meeting.

The evidence for

The datable Palmyra revival is 1824–25. Walters marshalled church-membership records and newspaper notices showing a marked Palmyra-area revival and surge of admissions in 1824–25, with no comparable 1820 event. He tied the ministers the account seems to evoke to that later date: the Methodist George Lane was not assigned to the Ontario District (which included Palmyra) until 1824, and the Presbyterian Benjamin Stockton's local prominence attaches to the aftermath of Alvin Smith's death in November 1823 — both pointing to 1824–25, not 1820.2

Adversarial assessment

What holds. Walters's documentary point is serious and has largely stood: there was no Palmyra-village, church-membership revival in 1820 on the scale the word “revival” usually names, and the strongest single match to the 1838 description — multitudes joining, the sects contending — is indeed 1824–25. This is why some critics argue the canonical setting was reconstructed around a later event Joseph did recall.

What is overstated or imprecise. Backman answered on the scope of Joseph's words. The 1838 account speaks of “an unusual excitement on the subject of religion … in the place where we lived,” not of a formal Palmyra revival in a named year; read as region-wide religious ferment, 1819–20 is well documented. Backman counted “great awakenings” in “more than fifty western New York towns or villages during the revival of 1819–20,” several — Penfield, Rochester, Lima, West Bloomfield, Junius — within about twenty-five miles of the Smith farm, and Methodist camp-meeting activity near Palmyra in the same window. Whether that regional excitement is what a fifteen-year-old would call the tumult “in the place where we lived” — or whether Joseph's own details (the multitudes joining, the ministers) require the 1824–25 event — is exactly the unresolved crux. Walters tests the phrase at the village-revival level; Backman answers it at the regional level, and the text underdetermines which is right.3

Assessment

Contested. This is a genuine, unresolved documentary dispute, strong by one measure and weak by another depending on how “unusual excitement … in the place where we lived” is scoped. If the phrase demands a Palmyra church-membership revival, Walters is largely right that 1820 does not supply one; if it means the regional awakening of the Burned-over District, Backman is right that 1819–20 does. Neither side has produced a decisive refutation of the other, and the account's own wording will not adjudicate between them. The question bears on the reliability of the 1838 setting, and it feeds the larger dispute over whether the canonical account is a faithful memory or a later reconstruction — but on its own terms it is contested, not closed.
