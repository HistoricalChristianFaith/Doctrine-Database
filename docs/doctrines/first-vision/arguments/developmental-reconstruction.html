The story that grew: do the accounts show a developed reconstruction?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — the one-directional growth of the load-bearing details is real and hard to explain away, but the reading overreaches when it treats variation as proof of invention, and the two-personage form is already present in 1835 · advanced by Grant H. Palmer and other critical historians.

Related arguments: the divergent accounts — see also the harmonizing reading.

The claim

his first vision evolved from a forgiveness epiphany to a call from God the Father and Jesus Christ to restore the true order of things.

— Grant H. Palmer, An Insider's View of Mormon Origins (Salt Lake City: Signature Books, 2002), ch. 8, archive.org. The claim is that the accounts do not merely differ in emphasis but trace a development: from a private conversion in which one divine being forgives Joseph's sins, to a public, doctrinally loaded theophany of two distinct personages who commission a restoration — a trajectory that mirrors the maturing of Joseph's own theology of the Godhead rather than a fixed memory retold.1

Who advances it

The developmental reading is pressed by critical historians of Mormon origins — Grant Palmer (a career Latter-day Saint educator writing as an insider), Dan Vogel (Joseph Smith: The Making of a Prophet, 2004), and others — and, in a milder register, is conceded in part by sympathetic scholars who grant that Joseph's telling grew richer over time. Its proof-texts are the accounts themselves, read in sequence.

The evidence for

The growth is one-directional and touches the essentials. Laid out by document date the change is not random:

  • 1832 (own hand): one personage, “the Lord,” who forgives Joseph's sins and identifies himself as the crucified Christ; the animating concern is personal salvation.
  • 1835 (journal): two personages and, uniquely, “many angels”; the second figure forgives and testifies that Jesus is the Son of God.
  • 1838 (canonical): two Personages, now explicitly the Father presenting the Son; the concern is which church to join, and the answer is that all are wrong, their creeds an abomination.

The forgiveness motif fades as the ecclesiastical commission and the two-being Godhead sharpen — the same period in which Joseph moved from a more modalist early language toward the developed doctrine of the Father and the Son as two separate, embodied persons.2

Even sympathetic scholarship registers the shift. Bushman grants that “even twelve years after the event, the First Vision's personal significance for him still overshadowed its place in the divine plan for restoring a church” — that is, that the 1832 account's center of gravity is personal forgiveness, and the church-restoring frame is the part that came to the fore later.3

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The trajectory is real, and it is the pattern most in need of explanation. The load-bearing distinctives of the canonical story — the two beings as Father and Son, the revival setting, the which-church commission, the condemnation of the creeds — do enter, cluster, and harden across the sequence, and they do so in step with the known development of Joseph's theology. A reading that simply denies any development has to explain away the plain center-of-gravity of the 1832 holograph.

What is overstated or imprecise. The strong form — that the growth proves the vision was invented or retro-fitted — outruns the evidence in two ways. First, it commits the error the harmonizers rightly flag: a genuinely experienced event, retold over years, does develop in emphasis and detail without being fictional (the three Acts tellings of Paul's Damascus vision are the standing counter-example). Growth is consistent with reconstruction, but it does not entail it. Second, the neat “one being → two beings” line is complicated by the 1832 account's own ambiguity (it names “the Lord” but does not deny a second figure) and, decisively, by the 1835 journal, which already has two personages three years before the canonical 1838 account — so the two-being form is not a late 1838 fabrication. The honest developmental claim is descriptive (the accounts grew, in a direction), not demonstrative (therefore the event did not happen).4

Assessment

Contested. As a description of the record the developmental reading is strong: the accounts do grow one-directionally, and the load-bearing distinctives enter late and together, tracking Joseph's maturing Godhead theology. But it overreaches when it converts that pattern into proof of fabrication — variation in retelling is exactly what authentic memory also produces, and the two-personage form is attested in 1835, undercutting a simple late-invention timeline. Strong by the source-critical evidence of textual growth, weaker as a disproof of the event, it stands in genuine dispute with its rival, the harmonizing reading — neither wins outright. As throughout this page, the miracle is not the issue: a real vision is not ruled out for being supernatural, and possibility is not proof either way — the leverage here is the texts, and they will not settle the question by themselves.
