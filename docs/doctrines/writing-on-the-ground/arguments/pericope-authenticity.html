Was the story of the woman taken in adultery (John 7:53–8:11) part of John's Gospel as first written?

Disputed reading · on What Jesus Wrote on the Ground (John 8:6) · witness: Augustine of Hippo

The passage

And every man went unto his own house. Jesus went unto the mount of Olives. And early in the morning he came again into the temple, and all the people came unto him; and he sat down, and taught them.

— John 7:53–8:2 (KJV), the opening verses of the passage that runs to 8:11, source.1

The question

John 7:53–8:11 is absent from many early Greek manuscripts, versions and commentators, stands in others at different places in John or in Luke, and is marked with asterisks or obeli in some copies that carry it.2 What is disputed is whether the passage belonged to John's Gospel as its author wrote it and was later removed from some copies, or was an independently circulating account later inserted into it, and whether the answer decides how the passage is read. Raymond Brown (1966) treats authorship and canonicity as distinct questions and reports that Catholics regard the story as canonical through its acceptance into the Vulgate, and that it stands in the received Byzantine text; its reception as Scripture is traced on the biblical canon timeline.3

Part of John's Gospel, removed from some copies

In brief: the passage belongs to the Gospel, and its absence from many copies comes from readers who removed it from their copies.

Held by: Augustine (De adulterinis coniugiis, c. 419); Bonaventure (Commentary on John, c. 1257); J. W. Burgon (The Causes of Corruption in the Traditional Text, 1896); Maurice A. Robinson (The New Testament in the Original Greek: Byzantine Textform, Preface, 2005).4

Augustine knew copies that lacked the passage and gave the reason: "some men of slight faith, or rather, some hostile to the true faith, fearing… that liberty to sin with impunity is granted their wives, remove from their Scriptural texts the account of our Lord's pardon of the adulteress." On this account the gap is the trace of moral anxiety among readers, not of a shorter original. The passage was read in the Latin West by c. 400: Brown records that "Ambrose and Augustine wanted it read as part of the Gospel, and Jerome included it in the Vulgate," and Tregelles that Jerome "mentions that copies existed of both kinds."5

Bonaventure records the doubt that the story was "inserted into the Gospel of John, just as the story of Susanna into Daniel," and answers that "Augustine and our expositors explain it as part of the sequence of the Gospel." For its absence from Greek copies he reports a further explanation: "Some say that it was not found in the ancient Greek books, because John added it after the Gospel was completed."6

Burgon contends that "the passage before us must be maintained to be genuine Scripture," and finds in its narrative "the impress of Divine origin." He gives two mechanisms for its loss: Augustine's, that some removed it from their copies "lest it should be pleaded in extentuation of breaches of the Seventh Commandment"; and the lectionary, the Church having chosen John 7:37–8:12 as the Pentecost lesson and, "besides omitting as inappropriate to the occasion the incident of the woman taken in adultery," passed over "the three preceding verses, thus making the severance begin, in fact, as far back as the end of chapter 7:52."7 Many Greek copies carry it: Tregelles counts the narrative in "D F G H K U, and more than 300 cursive copies, without any note of doubt or distinction." On this reading the passage stands where most Greek copies and the Latin Vulgate place it.8

An old account of Jesus, inserted into John by later copyists

In brief: the story circulated on its own as an early tradition about Jesus, and scribes later placed it in John's Gospel.

Held by: Euthymius Zigabenus (Commentary on the Four Gospels, c. 1118); S. P. Tregelles (An Account of the Printed Text of the Greek New Testament, 1854); Raymond E. Brown (The Gospel according to John (i–xii), 1966); Bruce M. Metzger (A Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament, 1971).9

Euthymius records that everything from John 7:53 to 8:12 is, in the more accurate copies, "either not found, or marked through with an obelus, because they seem illegitimate and added." The modern case rests first on the external evidence. The passage is absent from the two oldest papyri of John, P66 and P75 (both c. 200), and from Sinaiticus and Vaticanus. Whole version-families omit it: the Old Syriac, the Sahidic and older Bohairic Coptic, the Gothic, the Armenian and the older Georgian. Origen, Chrysostom and Cyril of Alexandria pass from 7:52 to 8:12 without comment.10

The oldest Greek manuscript to contain it, Codex Bezae (D, fifth century), gives it in a form Tregelles calls "utterly unlike the other copies"; other witnesses place it after John 7:36, 7:44 or 21:25, or after Luke 21:38.11 Brown finds the style "not Johannine either in vocabulary or grammar… more Lucan than Johannine." Hort notes that the copies without the story also lack "the three preliminary verses (vii 53; viii 1-2)": setting and story are absent together, as one block.12

On this reading the story is old even though its place in John is not. Metzger judges that it "has all the earmarks of historical veracity" and calls it "a piece of oral tradition which circulated in certain parts of the Western church and which was subsequently incorporated into various manuscripts at various places." Brown points to the third-century Syrian Didascalia Apostolorum, which "uses it as a presumably well-known example of our Lord's gentleness" (known, he adds, "but not necessarily as Scripture"), and to Papias's story of a woman "accused of many sins," while noting that "we cannot be certain that our story is the one meant." Didymus the Blind (c. 380) retells it from "certain gospels." The account was in circulation before copyists gave it a place in John.13

Received and read whatever its origin

In brief: the passage's first place in the text may be uncertain, but its reception and its content warrant receiving and using it.

Held by: John Calvin (Commentary on the Gospel according to John, 1553).14

Calvin grants the textual facts and holds that they do not settle the passage's use. He writes that "this passage was unknown anciently to the Greek Churches; and some conjecture that it has been brought from some other place and inserted here." He then gives three grounds for receiving it: it "has always been received by the Latin Churches," it "is found in many old Greek manuscripts," and it "contains nothing unworthy of an Apostolic Spirit." From these he concludes that "there is no reason why we should refuse to apply it to our advantage," and he goes on to expound the scene verse by verse. On this reading the long reception of the passage in the churches, joined to the character of its content, is the ground on which it is read; where it first entered the text is a further question that the reading does not need to answer before the passage is preached and applied.
