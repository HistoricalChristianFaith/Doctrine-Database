Papias of Hierapolis — the accused woman in Eusebius's report

Dates: c. 110 (the Exposition of the Sayings of the Lord, in five books; Papias's floruit is dated variously c. 100–130) · Wikipedia
Summary: Papias, bishop of Hierapolis in Phrygia, whom Irenaeus calls "a hearer of John" and Eusebius "a hearer of Aristion and the presbyter John," is the earliest writer known to have told a story of a woman accused before Jesus, two and a half centuries before Didymus. As Eusebius reports it, his form mentions no finger, no dust, and no inscription: Papias "relates another story of a woman, who was accused of many sins before the Lord, which is contained in the Gospel according to the Hebrews." Rufinus and later Eastern writers identify this story with the woman taken in adultery of John 7:53–8:11.

Claims & evidence

Eusebius's report of Papias's story (Church History 3.39.17)

Papias's own five books survive only in fragments quoted by later writers. The notice is Eusebius's, who, having just recorded what Papias transmitted about Mark and Matthew, closes his summary of Papias's contents with a single sentence about the woman:

And the same writer uses testimonies from the first Epistle of John and from that of Peter likewise. And he relates another story of a woman, who was accused of many sins before the Lord, which is contained in the Gospel according to the Hebrews. These things we have thought it necessary to observe in addition to what has been already stated.

— Eusebius of Caesarea, Church History 3.39.17 (reporting Papias, c. 110). Two features of the notice bear on this timeline. First, the charge is "many sins," not adultery, and the setting Eusebius names is the Gospel according to the Hebrews, not John. Second, the notice mentions no writing on the ground, as Didymus's Greek form two and a half centuries later also does not.1

The identification with John 7:53–8:11 (Rufinus; the Syriac and Armenian tradition)

The identification of Papias's "woman, who was accused of many sins" with the woman taken in adultery of John 8 is made by later writers; Eusebius's notice does not make it. Rufinus, translating Eusebius into Latin around 400, is the earliest extant writer to suppose the two stories the same, as the Ante-Nicene Fathers edition notes at this line. The connection hardens in the Eastern tradition: the tenth-century Arabic chronicler Agapius of Menbij reports that the Hierapolitan master who "wrote five treatises on the Gospel" spoke, "in his treatise on the Gospel of John," of "a woman who was adulterous" brought to Christ, whom he answers "Whoever of you knows that he is innocent of what she has done, let him testify against her" — and the thirteenth-century Armenian Vardan Arewelts’i likewise says "the story of that adulterous woman … was written about by a certain Papias, a student of John … Eusebius wrote about this."

And he mentions in his treatise on the Gospel of John, that in the book of John the Evangelist, he speaks of a woman who was adulterous, so when they presented her to Christ our Lord … He told the Jews who brought her to Him, "Whoever of you knows that he is innocent of what she has done, let him testify against her with what he has." So when He told them that, none of them responded with anything and they left.

— Agapius of Menbij, Universal History (10th c.), on the events of the twelfth year of Trajan. These later writers read Papias as an attestation of the pericope. Agapius's retelling reports Jesus' challenge to the accusers and their departure; neither he nor Vardan mentions writing on the ground.2

Placement on the timeline

Papias opens the summary timeline as the earliest writer known to have told a story of an accused woman brought before Jesus. The two oldest reports of such a story (Papias here, Didymus at Alexandria c. 380) both lack the writing on the ground, and Eusebius places Papias's version in the Gospel according to the Hebrews rather than John. Bart Ehrman (1988) argues that an early free-floating story of an accused woman lies behind these accounts.3

Notes

  • Whether Papias's "woman, who was accused of many sins" is the woman of the pericope adulterae is disputed. The charge Eusebius names is broader than adultery, and the home he names is the Gospel according to the Hebrews; Rufinus and the later Eastern chroniclers identify the two. Raymond Brown (1966) writes that if it is the same story, "the reference would point to early Palestinian origins; but we cannot be certain that our story is the one meant."4
  • The five-book Exposition of the Sayings of the Lord is lost; everything above is preserved at second hand (Eusebius, and the Syriac and Armenian chroniclers who depend on him). Papias is placed at c. 110 by his own report that he inquired of those who had followed the apostolic elders.
