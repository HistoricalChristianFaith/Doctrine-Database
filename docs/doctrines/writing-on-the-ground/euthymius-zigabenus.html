Euthymius Zigabenus — the Greek commentator who records the obelus

Dates: c. 1118 (the Commentary on the Four Gospels); Euthymius c. 1050–1120 · Wikipedia
Summary: Euthymius Zigabenus is the earliest extant Greek commentator to expound John 7:53–8:11. In his Commentary on the Four Gospels he notes that everything from John 7:53 to 8:12 is, "in the more accurate copies, either not found, or marked through with an obelus, because they seem illegitimate and added." Metzger names him in the line that "no Greek Church Father prior to Euthymius Zigabenus" comments on the passage. He then expounds the passage as edifying, without asking what Jesus wrote.

The critical note (Commentary on the Four Gospels, on John 7:53–8:11)

Παρὰ τοῖς ἀκριβέσιν ἀντιγράφοις ἢ οὐχ εὕρηται ἢ ὐβέλισται.

— "In the accurate copies [these things] either are not found or have been obelized." Migne's parallel Latin gives the fuller sentence: one should know that everything from this place [7:53] as far as "Again Jesus spoke to them saying, I am the light of the world" [8:12] is, "in the more accurate copies, either not found, or marked through with an obelus, because they seem illegitimate and added" (quia videntur illegitima et adjecta).1 The obelus (–, the "spit" mark Origen and the Alexandrian critics used to flag a suspect line) marks a line as suspect, and Euthymius records that copyists had so marked the pericope. He then comments on the story as spiritually useful.

Placement on the timeline, and the source of the note

Euthymius writes under Alexius Comnenus, around 1118, a compiler by trade: his Gospel commentary is largely a catena, stitched from earlier Greek exegetes (Chrysostom, Theophylact, and others). A closely parallel remark on the longer ending of Mark (16:9–20) stands in his commentary and matches, nearly word for word, Theophylact of Ohrid a generation earlier; Roger Pearse (2009) discusses the note on the pericope as derived from earlier Byzantine scholarship rather than original to Euthymius.2

Notes

  • On the summary timeline, Ambrose, Jerome, and the schoolmen ask what Jesus wrote; Euthymius records whether the verses stand in the accurate copies. For the earliest Greek writer whose own retelling of such a story survives — also without any writing detail — see Didymus the Blind (c. 380).
  • Of the Greek writers on this timeline, Didymus retells the story without the writing and Euthymius records the obelus; neither proposes a content for the writing.
  • The same Commentary on the Four Gospels makes Euthymius a witness on a second doctrine: at Luke 2:25 he is one of only two Byzantine writers to carry the Simeon-the-Just legend — that the Simeon of the Nunc Dimittis was one of the Seventy who balked at Isaiah 7:14's "virgin." See Euthymius Zigabenus on the Simeon legend, where he cites no authority, only "certain writings."
