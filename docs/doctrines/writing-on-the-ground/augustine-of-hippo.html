Augustine of Hippo — the sign, not the words

Dates: c. 417 (the Tractates on John; the companion readings span the Harmony of the Gospels c. 400 and the sermons); Augustine 354–430 · Wikipedia
Summary: Augustine reads the act of writing as a sign and does not name the words. Where Jerome reads the ground-writing as a legible list of the accusers' sins, Augustine reads the act as sign: the same finger that graved the Law on stone now writes on soil, because the Law was cut in stone "for the hard-hearted" while grace is "sown" on ground that can bear fruit. He offers a small cluster of figural readings — the accusers "written on earth, not in heaven"; the stooping as the humility of the Incarnation; the earth as fruitful soil against barren stone — but never a set of words. His scene closes not with a decoded message but with an image: the two left alone, "the wretched woman and Mercy." In a later work he gives the earliest extant explanation of why the whole episode is missing from many manuscripts: "some men of slight faith, or rather, some hostile to the true faith" removed it "from their Scriptural texts," fearing it granted their wives liberty to sin.

The writing read as sign (Tractates on John 33.5)

Augustine's fullest treatment is the thirty-third tractate on John, where the whole weight falls on Jesus as at once the Lawgiver and the Judge who will not be trapped between mercy and justice. When he reaches the finger on the ground, he does not ask what was written; he asks what the writing means — and answers with the law/grace contrast that becomes his signature reading of the scene:

What else does He signify to you when He writes with His finger on the ground? For the law was written with the finger of God; but written on stone because of the hard-hearted. The Lord now wrote on the ground, because He was seeking fruit… This is the voice of Justice: Let her, the sinner, be punished, but not by sinners: let the law be fulfilled, but not by the transgressors of the law… The two were left alone, the wretched woman and Mercy. But the Lord, having struck them through with that dart of justice, deigned not to heed their fall, but, turning away His look from them, "again He wrote with His finger on the ground."

— Tractates on John 33.5. Augustine supplies no sentence and names no proof-text for the content, and he treats the second stooping (8:8) as the averted gaze of a Judge who "deigned not to heed their fall." On his reading the meaning is carried by the gesture; later readers who also decline to name the words are traced on the summary timeline.1

Three figural readings of the ground (Harmony of the Gospels 4.10)

In the Harmony of the Gospels Augustine gathers three ways of reading why the ground — still not what the words were, but what the choice of soil signifies:

He wrote with His finger upon the ground, as if He would indicate that people of the character of these men would be written on earth, and not in heaven, as He also admonished His disciples to rejoice that their names were written in heaven! Or, it may be that He meant to convey the idea that it was by humbling Himself (which He expressed by bending down His head) that He wrought signs upon the earth; or, that the time was now come when His law should be written, not, as formerly, on the sterile stone, but on a soil which would yield fruit.

— The Harmony of the Gospels 4.10. The three readings — the accusers "written on earth, not in heaven" (Jeremiah 17:13 against Luke 10:20); the bowed head as the humility of the Incarnation working "signs upon the earth"; and the ground as fruitful soil against "sterile stone" — are the three that Thomas Aquinas sets out c. 1270, citing Augustine for the first. Augustine offers them as figures of the whole event, not as candidates for the missing text.2

"I begin with you" — the turning-away as accusation (Sermon 16A)

In a sermon on the episode Augustine reads the turning away as speech. The Redeemer, "not the condemner," turns from the accusers as if to say that if sins are to be judged, the judgment starts with the men who brought the charge:

This turning away from them is no trivial thing. Something is implied in this turning away. As if He would say: "You bring a sinner to Me, you sinners. If you think I should condemn sins, I begin with you."

— Sermon 16A. Like Jerome's "he wrote their sins," this reading turns the charge on the accusers; Augustine places it in the gesture, the averted face, rather than in words on the ground.3

Grace on the ground, the Law on stone (Sermon 272B)

A second sermon compresses the law/grace figure to its core — the finger of God is the Holy Spirit, and where that finger once wrote on stone it now writes on soil that can receive seed:

What did this indulgence signify? Grace. What did that hardness signify? The law given on stones. Therefore, the Lord was writing with His finger, but now on the ground, from which fruit could be received. However, whatever is sown on stone does not sprout, because it cannot send down roots. The finger of God, and the finger of God: the law was written by the finger of God, the finger of God, the Holy Spirit.

— Sermon 272B. The identification of the "finger of God" with the Holy Spirit (Exodus 31:18; cf. Luke 11:20) is repeated by Bede (c. 730), as is the stone/soil contrast.4

Why the passage went missing — the transmission theory (De adulterinis coniugiis 2.6–7)

Augustine is also the earliest extant writer to give a reason for the absence of the whole episode from many manuscripts. Writing on adulterous marriages a few years after the Tractates, he explains the gap as a deliberate removal by some readers from their own copies:

However, the pagan mind obviously shrinks from this comparison, so that some men of slight faith, or rather, some hostile to the true faith, fearing, as I believe, that liberty to sin with impunity is granted their wives, remove from their Scriptural texts the account of our Lord's pardon of the adulteress: as though He who said, "From now on, sin no more," granted permission to sin, or as though the woman should not have been cured by the Divine Physician by the remission of that sin, so as not to offend others who are equally unclean.

— De adulterinis coniugiis (On Adulterous Marriages) 2.6–7, c. 419 (the Latin: nonnulli modicae vel potius inimici verae fidei, credo, metuentes peccandi impunitatem dari mulieribus suis, illud quod de adulterae indulgentia Dominus fecit, auferrent de codicibus suis). Euthymius Zigabenus (c. 1118) later records the same absence from "the accurate copies" with the note that the passage seems "illegitimate and added"; Augustine treats the absence as removal from a genuine text by readers who feared it licensed sin. Whether the passage was part of John's Gospel as first written is disputed; see the disputed reading. Augustine's testimony places the passage in Latin copies, and its absence from others, by the early fifth century.5

Placement on the timeline

Augustine writes a generation after Ambrose and at about the same time as Jerome. Jerome names a content ("he wrote their sins," fixed to Jeremiah 17:13); Augustine names none and reads the act as the passage from law to grace. Both readings are catalogued together by the schoolmen — Bonaventure lists "Augustine responds that He wrote what He spoke" first among the options, and Aquinas gives Augustine's law-on-soft-earth figure as the third of his three reasons.6
