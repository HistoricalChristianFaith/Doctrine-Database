Didymus the Blind — the story in Greek, but no writing on the ground

Dates: c. 380 (the Commentary on Ecclesiastes, recovered among the Tura papyri); Didymus c. 313–398 · Wikipedia
Summary: Didymus, head of the catechetical school at Alexandria, is the earliest Greek writer whose own retelling of a story of a woman accused before Jesus survives (Papias's earlier story is known only through Eusebius's report), about a decade before Ambrose, the earliest extant Latin writer to name a content for the writing. Metzger writes that "no Greek Church Father prior to Euthymius Zigabenus" comments on the passage; Didymus retells the story without citing John. The form Didymus knows has no writing on the ground: he reports the saying "He who has not sinned, let him take a stone and cast it" and the accusers' withdrawal, and the stooping and writing of John 8:6, 8 do not appear.

What Didymus reports (Commentary on Ecclesiastes 223.6b–13a)

Commenting on Ecclesiastes 7:21–22 — "do not take to heart everything people say… for you know in your own heart that many times you yourself have cursed others" — Didymus turns to the danger of judging others and adduces the story as an illustration:

We find, therefore, in certain gospels [the following story]. A woman, it says, was condemned by the Jews for a sin and was being sent to be stoned in the place where that was customary to happen. The Savior, it says, when he saw her and observed that they were ready to stone her, said to those who were about to cast stones, "He who has not sinned, let him take a stone and cast it. If anyone is conscious in himself not to have sinned, let him take up a stone and smite her." And no one dared. Since they knew in themselves and perceived that they themselves were guilty in some things, they did not dare to strike her.

— Didymus, Commentary on Ecclesiastes, on Eccl 7:21–22, trans. Bart Ehrman.1 Three features stand out. Didymus introduces it as something found "in certain gospels" (ἐν τισιν εὐαγγελίοις) — he does not attribute it to John. The account has the woman merely "condemned… for a sin," not specifically adultery. And it turns entirely on the spoken challenge and the accusers' guilty conscience; there is no finger, no dust, no inscription. Bart Ehrman (1988) classes it as a shorter story of an intervention in an execution, akin to the story of a woman "accused of many sins" that Eusebius reports from Papias and says is contained in the Gospel according to the Hebrews, and distinct from the fuller Johannine pericope that frames the saying between two acts of writing (see below).

Placement on the timeline

The timeline records what Jesus was thought to have written on the ground. Didymus is placed on it because his Greek form of the episode carries the reproach of the accusers without the writing gesture. Bart Ehrman (1988) reads Didymus's version as an earlier, shorter form, akin to the story Eusebius reports from Papias, and holds that the dust-writing (and much else in John 7:53–8:11) belongs to a later, conflated stage of the tradition.2

Notes

  • Didymus attests the story circulating in Greek in fourth-century Alexandria; he cites "certain gospels," not John, and does not comment on John 8 as such. See the summary timeline and, for the earliest extant Greek commentator on the Johannine text itself, Euthymius Zigabenus (c. 1118).
  • The Commentary on Ecclesiastes survives in a sixth- or seventh-century papyrus codex found among those hidden in a cave at Tura, south of Cairo, and rediscovered in 1941. The pericope sits at page 223 of that papyrus.
