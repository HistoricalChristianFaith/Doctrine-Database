Ignatius of Antioch on the Government of the Church

Dates: c. 108 (the seven letters of the middle recension, written en route to martyrdom at Rome, c. 107–110) · Wikipedia
Summary: Ignatius’s letters are the earliest extant texts to describe the monarchical (single-bishop) episcopate: one bishop set over each local church, with the presbytery (the college of elders) and the deacons ranked beneath him in a threefold order. He urges obedience to the bishop as obedience to God and Christ, and makes the bishop the visible centre of the church’s unity — “do nothing without the bishop.” The letters date about a decade after 1 Clement.

Claims & evidence

One bishop in each church, with a presbytery and deacons (Magnesians 6; Trallians 3)

… I exhort you to study to do all things with a divine harmony, while your bishop presides in the place of God, and your presbyters in the place of the assembly of the apostles, along with your deacons, who are most dear to me, and are entrusted with the ministry of Jesus Christ…

— Epistle to the Magnesians, ch. 6, source (Roberts–Donaldson translation, Ante-Nicene Fathers; cf. New Advent).1 The three orders are named and ranked in a single sentence: the bishop presiding “in the place of God,” the presbyters as the apostolic council, and the deacons under both. Ignatius reads them typologically — the earthly order mirrors a heavenly one — and the structure he addresses has a single presiding bishop. He states the same rank order elsewhere:

In like manner, let all reverence the deacons as an appointment of Jesus Christ, and the bishop as Jesus Christ, who is the Son of the Father, and the presbyters as the sanhedrim of God, and assembly of the apostles. Apart from these, there is no Church.

— Epistle to the Trallians, ch. 3, source (cf. New Advent).2 “Apart from these, there is no Church” ties what counts as a church to the three orders. Ignatius names the bishops of four of the churches he addresses (Damas at Magnesia, Polybius at Tralles, Onesimus at Ephesus, Polycarp at Smyrna).

Obedience to the bishop is obedience to God and Christ (Magnesians 13; Ephesians 6; Smyrnaeans 9)

Be ye subject to the bishop, and to one another, as Jesus Christ to the Father, according to the flesh, and the apostles to Christ, and to the Father, and to the Spirit; that so there may be a union both fleshly and spiritual.

— Epistle to the Magnesians, ch. 13, source.1 Submission to the bishop is patterned on Christ’s submission to the Father: to obey the one is to obey the other. Ignatius extends the point — the bishop is to be looked on as the Lord himself:

… we should look upon the bishop even as we would upon the Lord Himself.

— Epistle to the Ephesians, ch. 6, source (cf. New Advent).3 And to the Smyrnaeans he draws the negative corollary: to bypass the bishop is to serve the devil.

He who honours the bishop has been honoured by God; he who does anything without the knowledge of the bishop, does [in reality] serve the devil.

— Epistle to the Smyrnaeans, ch. 9, source (cf. New Advent).4 The letters are exhortations written to churches Ignatius warns against division by Judaizing and docetic teachers, and in them the bishop is the safeguard of unity: nothing that touches the church is to be done without him.

The bishop as the centre of church unity — “do nothing without the bishop” (Trallians 2; Smyrnaeans 8)

For, since ye are subject to the bishop as to Jesus Christ… It is therefore necessary that, as ye indeed do, so without the bishop ye should do nothing, but should also be subject to the presbytery, as to the apostle of Jesus Christ… It is fitting also that the deacons, as being [the ministers] of the mysteries of Jesus Christ, should in every respect be pleasing to all.

— Epistle to the Trallians, ch. 2, source.2 The refrain across the letters — “do nothing without the bishop” — ties every act of the church back to the one office. To the Smyrnaeans he makes the bishop the visible sign of the church’s catholicity:

See that ye all follow the bishop, even as Jesus Christ does the Father, and the presbytery as ye would the apostles… Let no man do anything connected with the Church without the bishop… Wherever the bishop shall appear, there let the multitude [of the people] also be; even as, wherever Jesus Christ is, there is the Catholic Church.

— Epistle to the Smyrnaeans, ch. 8, source.4 The same passage goes on to bind the Eucharist, baptism, and the love-feast to the bishop’s presidency — that sacramental side of the ministry is traced separately on his Ministerial Priesthood detail page, to keep the who-governs question here from repeating the who-confects-the-sacraments question there. For church order, the passage gathers the congregation visibly around the one bishop.

Reception and debate

  • Placement on the timeline. Ignatius’s letters are the earliest extant to describe a single bishop in each church, distinct from and set over the presbytery, with the deacons a third order beneath both. 1 Clement (c. 96) speaks of the officers at Corinth in terms of “the episcopate” and as “presbyters”; how the two witnesses relate is taken up in Is the threefold ministry apostolic in origin, or a second-century development? and Were “bishop” and “presbyter” originally one office?
  • The questions his letters raise. Whether Ignatius’s three orders are a development beyond the New Testament ministry (where episkopos and presbyteros are used of the same men in Acts 20:17/28 and Titus 1:5–7) or the making-explicit of an apostolic order is taken up in the threefold-ministry question above. Whether the emphasis of his exhortations reflects an established office or one he was urging on the churches is taken up in Do Ignatius’s letters describe an established episcopate, or urge a new one? Both are under Disputed readings.
  • Data cited on the spread of the single-bishop form. (1) Alone among the seven, his letter to Rome greets no bishop of Rome and urges no obedience to one — the only office-title in it is his own, “the bishop of Syria.”5 (2) Writing a little later, Polycarp addresses the Philippians as “Polycarp, and the presbyters with him” and instructs them about presbyters and deacons but names no single bishop at Philippi.6 Both are cited in Was the single-bishop episcopate in place everywhere by c. 110, or did it emerge unevenly?
  • The middle recension. The quotes above are from the seven letters of the “middle recension,” not from the later “long recension” or the additional epistles transmitted under Ignatius’s name, whose episcopal language is fuller. The Roberts–Donaldson edition prints the shorter text first in each chapter, and it is that text quoted here.
  • Same author, elsewhere on this wiki. Ignatius also appears as a witness on the Ministerial Priesthood (the bishop’s Eucharistic presidency, and the tradition, reported there from John Chrysostom’s Eulogy on Saint Ignatius (c. 386–397) and later writers, of his ordination by the apostles at Antioch), the Real Presence, and the Intermediate State timelines.
