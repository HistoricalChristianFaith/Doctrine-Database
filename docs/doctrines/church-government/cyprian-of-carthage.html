Cyprian of Carthage on the Government of the Church

Dates: c. 251–256 (On the Unity of the Church c. 251; Epistle 54 to Cornelius c. 252; Epistle 67 to the Spanish churches c. 254; the Seventh Council of Carthage 256) · Cyprian, c. 200–258; bishop of Carthage from c. 248, martyred under Valerian · Wikipedia
Summary: Cyprian writes on church order across several works of the 250s. He makes the bishop the centre of the local church’s unity. He writes that the episcopate is one, “each part of which is held by each one for the whole,” and at the African council of 256 says that none of the bishops sets himself up as “a bishop of bishops.” On how a man comes to office he writes that bishops are chosen in the presence of the people, by their suffrage and testimony, with the sentence of the assembled bishops, and that the people have “the power either of choosing worthy priests, or of rejecting unworthy ones.” This page keeps to church order (who governs, how bishops relate, how they are chosen); the priestly and Eucharistic side of Cyprian’s ministry — the bishop as the one who offers the sacrifice — is on his Ministerial Priesthood page.

Claims & evidence

“The episcopate is one” (On the Unity 4–5)

And this unity we ought firmly to hold and assert, especially those of us that are bishops who preside in the Church, that we may also prove the episcopate itself to be one and undivided… The episcopate is one, each part of which is held by each one for the whole.

— On the Unity of the Church, ch. 5, source (Roberts–Donaldson translation, Ante-Nicene Fathers; cf. New Advent).1 The Latin runs episcopatus unus est, cuius a singulis in solidum pars tenetur — the whole episcopate is held in solidum (jointly and severally, as Roman law held co-heirs each liable for the whole) by every bishop. Cyprian’s images are of one light with many rays, one tree with many branches, one spring with many streams: the bishops are many, the episcopate one. In ch. 4 he grounds this in the commission to Peter; the received text reads: “the rest of the apostles were also the same as was Peter, endowed with a like partnership both of honour and power; but the beginning proceeds from unity.” (See the note below on the two recensions of ch. 4.)

“Neither does any of us set himself up as a bishop of bishops” (Seventh Council of Carthage, 256)

It remains, that upon this same matter each of us should bring forward what we think, judging no man, nor rejecting any one from the right of communion, if he should think differently from us. For neither does any of us set himself up as a bishop of bishops, nor by tyrannical terror does any compel his colleague to the necessity of obedience; since every bishop, according to the allowance of his liberty and power, has his own proper right of judgment, and can no more be judged by another than he himself can judge another. But let us all wait for the judgment of our Lord Jesus Christ…

— Cyprian’s opening address to the Seventh Council of Carthage (the Sentences of the Eighty-Seven Bishops, 1 September 256), source (Roberts–Donaldson translation, Ante-Nicene Fathers).2 The council’s eighty-seven African bishops each gave a sentence on the rebaptism question, and Cyprian opened it on the principle that every bishop “has his own proper right of judgment.” The occasion was his dispute with Stephen of Rome over rebaptizing heretics. What the address bears on Roman primacy is taken up on the primacy of Rome timeline.

Bishops chosen in the presence of the people (Epistle 67, to the churches of Spain)

…a people obedient to the Lord’s precepts, and fearing God, ought to separate themselves from a sinful prelate… especially since they themselves have the power either of choosing worthy priests, or of rejecting unworthy ones. Which very thing, too, we observe to come from divine authority, that the priest should be chosen in the presence of the people under the eyes of all, and should be approved worthy and suitable by public judgment and testimony… the ordination, which shall have been examined by the suffrage and judgment of all, may be just and legitimate.

— Epistle 67 (al. 68), §§ 3–4, to Felix and the peoples of Legio, Asturica, and Emerita, on the deposed bishops Basilides and Martial (c. 254), source (Roberts–Donaldson translation, Ante-Nicene Fathers).3 In the letter the congregations have “the power either of choosing worthy priests, or of rejecting unworthy ones,” and no ordination is “just and legitimate” unless examined by “the suffrage and judgment of all.” He calls this the “divine tradition and apostolic observance… maintained among us, and almost throughout all the provinces” (§ 5), and gives the working procedure: the neighbouring bishops of the province assemble with the people, “and the bishop should be chosen in the presence of the people… so that, by the suffrage of the whole brotherhood, and by the sentence of the bishops who had assembled… the episcopate was conferred.” The procedure names two consents — the people’s and the assembled bishops’. He names the same two to Cornelius: no one should set himself up as judge “after the divine judgment, after the suffrage of the people, after the consent of the co-bishops” (Epistle 54, § 5).4

Reception and debate

  • Passages cited in the polity debate. Cyprian’s exaltation of the bishop and his one episcopate, his “neither does any of us set himself up as a bishop of bishops,” his conciliar practice, and the people’s power to choose and reject are each cited by different parties to the polity debate. The questions on which they are cited are taken up in Was a minister made by the people’s election, or by ordained conferral? under Disputed readings, and, for Roman primacy, on the primacy of Rome timeline.
  • The two recensions of On the Unity ch. 4. Chapter 4 survives in two forms. The one quoted above is the “received” text (Textus Receptus). A second recension — the “Primacy Text” — adds lines absent here: “the primacy is given to Peter… [that there might be] one Church of Christ and one See,” and “he who deserts the chair of Peter… does he trust that he is in the Church?” The Ante-Nicene Fathers editors print the received text and flag those additions as interpolations; Maurice Bévenot and others argue that both are Cyprian’s own — two authorial editions — and read the Primacy Text, in context, as making Peter a symbol of unity rather than a jurisdictional head ⚠ unverified. Which recension one quotes figures in the Roman-primacy debate, taken up on the primacy of Rome timeline. This page quotes the received text and notes the variant.
  • The Basilides–Martial affair. Epistle 67 concerns two Spanish bishops who had lapsed under persecution and were deposed and replaced by their own congregations acting with neighbouring bishops. When Basilides went to Stephen of Rome and sought to be reinstated, Cyprian and his African colleagues wrote that Stephen, “placed at a distance, and ignorant of what had been done,” had been deceived, and that this could not “rescind an ordination rightly perfected.”3
  • The priestly / sacramental side is traced elsewhere. Cyprian’s teaching that the bishop is a priest (sacerdos) who offers the Eucharistic sacrifice in the place of Christ — the sacramental rather than the governing aspect of the office — is on his Ministerial Priesthood page. This page keeps to the who-governs / how-are-bishops-related-and-chosen question.
  • Same author, elsewhere on this wiki. Cyprian also appears on the ministerial priesthood, infant baptism, prayer to the saints, the real presence, the Old Testament canon, and other timelines.
