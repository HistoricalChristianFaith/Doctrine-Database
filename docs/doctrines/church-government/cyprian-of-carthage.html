Cyprian of Carthage on the Government of the Church

Dates: c. 251–256 (On the Unity of the Church c. 251; Epistle 54 to Cornelius c. 252; Epistle 67 to the Spanish churches c. 254; the Seventh Council of Carthage 256) · Cyprian, c. 200–258; bishop of Carthage from c. 248 and martyr under Valerian, the leading Latin churchman of the mid-third century · Wikipedia
Summary: Cyprian supplies the most developed third-century theology of church order — and a double-edged one. On the office he is strongly episcopal: the bishop is the hinge of unity, and “there is no Church” without him. But on the relations between bishops he is conciliar and egalitarian: the episcopate is one, held collegially, each bishop holding the whole “for the whole,” with no “bishop of bishops” over the rest — an implicit check on any single see’s jurisdiction, worked out in the African synods. And on how a man comes to office he is congregation-involving: bishops are rightly chosen in the presence of the people, by their suffrage and testimony, the people retaining the power to choose worthy men and reject unworthy ones. This page keeps to church order (who governs, how bishops relate, how they are chosen); the priestly and Eucharistic side of Cyprian’s ministry — the bishop as the one who offers the sacrifice — is on his Ministerial Priesthood page.

Claims & evidence

The episcopate is one, held collegially — each bishop holds the whole “for the whole” (On the Unity 4–5)

And this unity we ought firmly to hold and assert, especially those of us that are bishops who preside in the Church, that we may also prove the episcopate itself to be one and undivided… The episcopate is one, each part of which is held by each one for the whole.

— On the Unity of the Church, ch. 5, source (Roberts–Donaldson translation, Ante-Nicene Fathers; cf. New Advent).1 The Latin runs episcopatus unus est, cuius a singulis in solidum pars tenetur — the whole episcopate is held in solidum (jointly and severally, as Roman law held co-heirs each liable for the whole) by every bishop. Cyprian’s images are of one light with many rays, one tree with many branches, one spring with many streams: the bishops are many, the episcopate one. In ch. 4 he grounds this in the commission to Peter — but on the received text his point is equality, not Roman supremacy: “the rest of the apostles were also the same as was Peter, endowed with a like partnership both of honour and power; but the beginning proceeds from unity.” Peter is the symbol of the one episcopate, not a superior over the others. (See the note below on the two recensions of ch. 4.)

No bishop is a “bishop of bishops”: each has his own right of judgment; matters are settled in council (Seventh Council of Carthage, 256)

It remains, that upon this same matter each of us should bring forward what we think, judging no man, nor rejecting any one from the right of communion, if he should think differently from us. For neither does any of us set himself up as a bishop of bishops, nor by tyrannical terror does any compel his colleague to the necessity of obedience; since every bishop, according to the allowance of his liberty and power, has his own proper right of judgment, and can no more be judged by another than he himself can judge another. But let us all wait for the judgment of our Lord Jesus Christ…

— Cyprian’s opening address to the Seventh Council of Carthage (the Sentences of the Eighty-Seven Bishops, 1 September 256), source (Roberts–Donaldson translation, Ante-Nicene Fathers).2 This is the sharpest ancient denial of a monarchical papacy from a bishop of Cyprian’s stature. He convened eighty-seven African bishops to decide the rebaptism question in council — each casting his own sentence — and framed the whole proceeding on the principle that no bishop can bind another’s judgment. The context is his running quarrel with Stephen of Rome over rebaptizing heretics: Cyprian held the Roman bishop’s ruling did not bind the African churches. The office of bishop is exalted; the relations between bishops are those of equal colleagues in one college (collegium), unity secured by concord and council rather than by subordination to a single see.

Bishops are rightly chosen in the presence of the people, by their suffrage and testimony (Epistle 67, to the churches of Spain)

…a people obedient to the Lord’s precepts, and fearing God, ought to separate themselves from a sinful prelate… especially since they themselves have the power either of choosing worthy priests, or of rejecting unworthy ones. Which very thing, too, we observe to come from divine authority, that the priest should be chosen in the presence of the people under the eyes of all, and should be approved worthy and suitable by public judgment and testimony… the ordination, which shall have been examined by the suffrage and judgment of all, may be just and legitimate.

— Epistle 67 (al. 68), §§ 3–4, to Felix and the peoples of Legio, Asturica, and Emerita, on the deposed bishops Basilides and Martial (c. 254), source (Roberts–Donaldson translation, Ante-Nicene Fathers).3 Cyprian grants the congregations a real, active role: they have “the power either of choosing worthy priests, or of rejecting unworthy ones,” and no ordination is “just and legitimate” unless examined by “the suffrage and judgment of all.” He calls this the “divine tradition and apostolic observance… maintained among us, and almost throughout all the provinces” (§ 5), and gives the working procedure: the neighbouring bishops of the province assemble with the people, “and the bishop should be chosen in the presence of the people… so that, by the suffrage of the whole brotherhood, and by the sentence of the bishops who had assembled… the episcopate was conferred.” Election joins two consents — the people’s and the provincial college’s. He states the same double consent to Cornelius: no one should set himself up as judge “after the divine judgment, after the suffrage of the people, after the consent of the co-bishops” (Epistle 54, § 5).4

Notes

  • What it establishes for polity — a genuinely double-edged witness. Cyprian is claimed by every side, and honestly so. High-church readers cite his exaltation of the bishop and his one episcopate; opponents of the papacy cite “no bishop of bishops” and the autonomy of each see; Presbyterian and congregational readers cite the conciliar decision-making and the people’s power to choose and reject. All three are on the page. He is episcopal on the office, conciliar-egalitarian against a single ruling see, and congregation-involving on election — a combination later polities pulled apart. The contested inferences (does “no bishop of bishops” refute Roman primacy? does the people’s suffrage support congregational or presbyterian election against sacramental ordination?) are weighed adversarially in the summary’s Arguments section, not asserted here.
  • The two recensions of On the Unity ch. 4. Chapter 4 survives in two forms. The one quoted above — the “received” text (Textus Receptus) — makes Peter the emblem of the one episcopate that all bishops share equally. A second, more Rome-exalting recension — the “Primacy Text” — adds lines absent here: “the primacy is given to Peter… [that there might be] one Church of Christ and one See,” and “he who deserts the chair of Peter… does he trust that he is in the Church?” The Ante-Nicene Fathers editors print the received text and flag those additions as interpolations; modern scholarship (Maurice Bévenot and others) argues instead that both are Cyprian’s own — two authorial editions — and that even the Primacy Text, read in context, makes Peter a symbol of unity rather than a jurisdictional head. Which recension one quotes matters for the Roman-primacy debate; the question of what the Primacy Text proves is weighed on the primacy argument (see the summary’s Arguments), not settled here. This page quotes the received text and flags the variant so the reader knows a more papal-sounding version exists.
  • Cyprian’s own practice matches his theory. The Basilides–Martial affair of Epistle 67 is a live test case: two Spanish bishops who had lapsed under persecution were deposed and replaced by their own congregations acting with neighbouring bishops. When Basilides appealed over their heads to Stephen of Rome and got himself provisionally reinstated, Cyprian and his African colleagues ruled the appeal void — a distant bishop “ignorant of what had been done” could not rescind “an ordination rightly perfected” by the people and the provincial bishops. Practice and principle agree: authority rests in the local church and its provincial college, not in a supra-episcopal see.
  • The priestly / sacramental side is traced elsewhere. Cyprian’s teaching that the bishop is a priest (sacerdos) who offers the Eucharistic sacrifice in the place of Christ — the sacramental rather than the governing aspect of the office — is on his Ministerial Priesthood page. This page keeps to the who-governs / how-are-bishops-related-and-chosen question.
  • Same author, elsewhere on this wiki. Cyprian also appears on the ministerial priesthood, infant baptism, prayer to the saints, the real presence, the Old Testament canon, and other timelines.
