Hippolytus of Rome on the Government of the Church

Dates: c. 215 AD (the Apostolic Tradition) · Hippolytus, c. 170 – c. 235; presbyter (and rival bishop) at Rome · Wikipedia
Summary: The Apostolic Tradition is the earliest surviving ordination manual, and it settles in a rubric what the New Testament leaves as a crux: who confers office by the laying on of hands. The people elect; the bishop is made by other bishops (the presbyters standing by in silence); a presbyter is made by the bishop, the presbyters merely joining their hands; and a deacon the bishop ordains alone. The presbyter, it says outright, “has no power to give… a presbyter does not ordain the clergy.” The document thus grades the three orders by their part in the rite of conferral itself.

Note on attribution: this church-order, long ascribed to Hippolytus of Rome and dated c. 215, survives only in later Latin, Coptic, and other versions, and a number of modern scholars regard it as a composite text of uncertain authorship. The dating and Roman provenance are debated; what is not in doubt is that it transmits very early liturgical usage. It is cited here from Burton Scott Easton’s 1934 English edition. The Eucharistic/sacerdotal side of these same prayers — the bishop consecrated as “high priest” to offer the Church’s gifts — is treated on Hippolytus’s ministerial-priesthood page; here the angle is the conferral of office and rank.

Claims & evidence

The people choose, but the bishops ordain: a bishop is made by other bishops (Apostolic Tradition 2)

The rite keeps two acts distinct. The congregation chooses the man — but the ordaining, the laying on of hands, is reserved to the assembled bishops, with the local presbyters present but silent:

Let the bishop be ordained after he has been chosen by all the people. When he has been named and shall please all, let him, with the presbytery and such bishops as may be present, assemble with the people on a Sunday. While all give their consent, the bishops shall lay their hands upon him, and the presbytery shall stand by in silence.

— Apostolic Tradition, ch. 2, source (Burton Scott Easton, The Apostolic Tradition of Hippolytus, Cambridge, 1934).1 Election and ordination are two moments, not one: “chosen by all the people” is the popular act; “the bishops shall lay their hands upon him” is the ministerial act, and it belongs to the bishops, not the electing people and not the watching presbyters. This is the earliest liturgical text to distinguish the congregation’s choosing from the ministry’s conferring — the very seam every later polity divides over.

A presbyter “does not ordain the clergy”; a deacon the bishop ordains alone (Apostolic Tradition 8–9)

At a presbyter’s ordination the bishop lays on his hand “while the presbyters touch him,” but the manual is careful to say what that co-laying does and does not mean: the presbyters share a like Spirit, so they join in, yet the power to ordain is not theirs:

… the presbyter has only the power to receive; but he has no power to give. For this reason a presbyter does not ordain the clergy; but at the ordination of a presbyter he seals while the bishop ordains.

The deacon is graded lower still, and the reason given is again a rule about who lays hands:

When the deacon is ordained, this is the reason why the bishop alone shall lay his hands upon him: he is not ordained to the priesthood but to serve the bishop and to carry out the bishop’s commands… For this cause the bishop alone shall make a deacon.

— Apostolic Tradition, chs. 8–9, source (Easton translation).2 So the document answers the conferral question with a hierarchy of hands: bishops ordain a bishop; the bishop ordains a presbyter (the presbyters only “sealing”); the bishop alone ordains a deacon. The presbyter may “receive,” but he has “no power to give” — the ordaining power runs downward from the episcopate, and does not circulate laterally among the presbyters or upward from the people.

Notes

  • What it establishes for church order. The Apostolic Tradition is the first text to regulate the rite of conferral rather than merely mention it. It fixes three things later disputed: that election (the people’s) and ordination (the ministry’s) are distinct acts; that ordaining is reserved to those already in the office conferred (bishops make a bishop); and that the presbyter, though he shares the bishop’s Spirit and co-lays hands, “does not ordain the clergy.”
  • Bearing on the arguments. This is a leading witness on the argument Who confers ministerial office by the laying on of hands? — on the side that ordination is a ministerial act distinct from the congregation’s choosing. It bears too on the parity question (the parity of bishop and presbyter): Hippolytus grants bishop and presbyter a “common and like Spirit” yet still reserves ordaining to the bishop — the same reservation Jerome later concedes.
  • Authorship and date. The ascription to Hippolytus and the c. 215 dating are the traditional view; modern liturgical scholarship (Bradshaw, Johnson, Phillips and others) treats the work as a layered compilation whose original shape is hard to recover. The ordination rubrics are nonetheless agreed to be very early. On this wiki Hippolytus is anchored elsewhere at c. 204–205 for his Commentary on Daniel; the later date here reflects the church-order, not his floruit.
