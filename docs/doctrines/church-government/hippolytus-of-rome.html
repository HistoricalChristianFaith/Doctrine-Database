Hippolytus of Rome on the Government of the Church

Dates: c. 215 AD (the Apostolic Tradition) · Hippolytus, c. 170 – c. 235; presbyter (and rival bishop) at Rome · Wikipedia
Summary: The Apostolic Tradition is the earliest extant ordination manual, and its rubrics state who confers each office by the laying on of hands. The people elect; the bishop is made by other bishops (the presbyters standing by in silence); a presbyter is made by the bishop, the presbyters merely joining their hands; and a deacon the bishop ordains alone. The presbyter, it says outright, “has no power to give… a presbyter does not ordain the clergy.” The document thus grades the three orders by their part in the rite of conferral itself.

Note on attribution: this church-order, long ascribed to Hippolytus of Rome and dated c. 215, survives only in later Latin, Coptic, and other versions, and a number of modern scholars regard it as a composite text of uncertain authorship. The dating and Roman provenance are debated. It is cited here from Burton Scott Easton’s 1934 English edition. The Eucharistic/sacerdotal side of these same prayers — the bishop consecrated as “high priest” to offer the Church’s gifts — is treated on Hippolytus’s ministerial-priesthood page; here the angle is the conferral of office and rank.

Claims & evidence

The people choose and the bishops lay on hands: the making of a bishop (Apostolic Tradition 2)

The rite keeps two acts distinct. The congregation chooses the man — but the ordaining, the laying on of hands, is reserved to the assembled bishops, with the local presbyters present but silent:

Let the bishop be ordained after he has been chosen by all the people. When he has been named and shall please all, let him, with the presbytery and such bishops as may be present, assemble with the people on a Sunday. While all give their consent, the bishops shall lay their hands upon him, and the presbytery shall stand by in silence.

— Apostolic Tradition, ch. 2, source (Burton Scott Easton, The Apostolic Tradition of Hippolytus, Cambridge, 1934).1 The rubric names two moments: “chosen by all the people” and “the bishops shall lay their hands upon him,” the presbytery standing by in silence. It is the earliest extant liturgical text to set the congregation’s choosing beside the bishops’ laying on of hands; how the two acts relate is the question Was a minister made by the people’s election, or by ordained conferral?

A presbyter “does not ordain the clergy”; a deacon the bishop ordains alone (Apostolic Tradition 8–9)

At a presbyter’s ordination the bishop lays on his hand “while the presbyters touch him,” and the manual explains the presbyters’ part: they share a like Spirit and join in, but the manual does not give them the power to ordain:

… the presbyter has only the power to receive; but he has no power to give. For this reason a presbyter does not ordain the clergy; but at the ordination of a presbyter he seals while the bishop ordains.

The deacon is graded lower still, and the reason given is again a rule about who lays hands:

When the deacon is ordained, this is the reason why the bishop alone shall lay his hands upon him: he is not ordained to the priesthood but to serve the bishop and to carry out the bishop’s commands… For this cause the bishop alone shall make a deacon.

— Apostolic Tradition, chs. 8–9, source (Easton translation).2 In the manual’s rubrics, bishops ordain a bishop; the bishop ordains a presbyter, the presbyters “sealing”; and the bishop alone ordains a deacon. The presbyter may “receive,” but has “no power to give.”

Reception and debate

  • Placement on the timeline. The Apostolic Tradition is the earliest extant text to regulate the rite of conferral rather than mention it. Its rubrics treat the people’s election and the bishops’ laying on of hands as distinct acts, have bishops make a bishop, and say that the presbyter, though he shares a like Spirit and co-lays hands, “does not ordain the clergy.”
  • The manual in the disputed readings. It is cited in Was a minister made by the people’s election, or by ordained conferral? It is also set beside Jerome in Were “bishop” and “presbyter” originally one office?: the manual grants bishop and presbyter a “common and like Spirit” and reserves ordaining to the bishop, and Jerome, writing later, excepts ordination from what a presbyter does.
  • Authorship and date. The ascription to Hippolytus and the c. 215 dating are the traditional view; modern liturgical scholarship (Bradshaw, Johnson, Phillips and others) treats the work as a layered compilation whose original shape is hard to recover ⚠ unverified. Hippolytus is placed elsewhere on this wiki at c. 204–205 for his Commentary on Daniel; the later date here is that of the church-order.
