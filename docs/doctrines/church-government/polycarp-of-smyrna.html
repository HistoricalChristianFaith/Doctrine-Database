Polycarp of Smyrna on the Government of the Church

Dates: c. 125 (the Epistle to the Philippians; Polycarp himself c. 69–155, named bishop of Smyrna by Irenaeus and Tertullian, and by Irenaeus a hearer of apostles) · Wikipedia
Summary: Writing from Smyrna to the church at Philippi a generation after the apostle Paul, Polycarp gives directions for its ministry. He greets the church as “Polycarp and the presbyters with him,” instructs it about the duties of its deacons and presbyters (chs. 5–6), and addresses the case of a fallen presbyter, Valens (ch. 11); the letter names no bishop of Philippi. Irenaeus and Tertullian name Polycarp himself as bishop of Smyrna. The letter is cited in the debate over how evenly the single-bishop episcopate had spread by the early second century (see Disputed readings).

Claims & evidence

“Polycarp, and the presbyters with him” — the salutation

Polycarp, and the presbyters with him, to the Church of God sojourning at Philippi: Mercy to you, and peace from God Almighty, and from the Lord Jesus Christ, our Saviour, be multiplied.

— Epistle to the Philippians, salutation, source (Roberts–Donaldson translation, Ante-Nicene Fathers; cf. New Advent).1 The letter opens in the name of “Polycarp and the presbyters with him” (the ANF note offers “Polycarp, and those who with him are presbyters”). When it turns to the ministry at Philippi, the officers it addresses are presbyters and deacons.

The duties of the deacons and the presbyters (chs. 5–6)

In like manner should the deacons be blameless before the face of His righteousness, as being the servants of God and Christ, and not of men. They must not be slanderers, double-tongued, or lovers of money, but temperate in all things, compassionate, industrious, walking according to the truth of the Lord, who was the servant of all… Wherefore, it is needful to abstain from all these things, being subject to the presbyters and deacons, as unto God and Christ.

And let the presbyters be compassionate and merciful to all, bringing back those that wander, visiting all the sick, and not neglecting the widow, the orphan, or the poor… abstaining from all wrath, respect of persons, and unjust judgment; keeping far off from all covetousness, not quickly crediting [an evil report] against any one, not severe in judgment, as knowing that we are all under a debt of sin.

— Epistle to the Philippians, chs. 5–6, source.1 Polycarp addresses two offices, presbyters and deacons; Paul’s letter to the same city greets “the bishops and deacons” (Philippians 1:1). The congregation is to be “subject to the presbyters and deacons, as unto God and Christ”; in Ignatius’s letters a like formula of obedience is directed to the bishop (Magnesians 13). The deacons are servants of God and Christ, and the presbyters are charged with the straying, the sick, the widow, the orphan, and the poor.

The discipline of Valens, a fallen presbyter (ch. 11)

I am greatly grieved for Valens, who was once a presbyter among you, because he so little understands the place that was given him [in the Church]… For if a man cannot govern himself in such matters, how shall he enjoin them on others?… And be ye then moderate in regard to this matter, and “do not count such as enemies,” but call them back as suffering and straying members, that ye may save your whole body.

— Epistle to the Philippians, ch. 11, source.2 The occasion of much of the letter is a discipline case: Valens, “once a presbyter among you,” had fallen into avarice and lost “the place that was given him.” The office Valens held is the presbyterate, and Polycarp urges the whole church to deal with him moderately and to “call them back… that ye may save your whole body”.

Reception and debate

  • The letter in the polity debate. The letter’s naming of presbyters and deacons at Philippi, without a bishop, is cited in Was the single-bishop episcopate in place everywhere by c. 110, or did it emerge unevenly? and in Is the threefold ministry apostolic in origin, or a second-century development? under Disputed readings, where the readings of its silence are set out with their holders.
  • Polycarp’s own office. Irenaeus says Polycarp was “by apostles in Asia, appointed bishop of the Church in Smyrna,” and Tertullian that the church of Smyrna “records that Polycarp was placed therein by John”; the manuscript title of this letter styles him “Polycarp, Bishop of Smyrna.”4 (On Polycarp in the episcopal succession-lists, see Irenaeus and Tertullian.)
  • The date, and the Ignatius connection. Chapter 13, in which Polycarp forwards “the Epistles of Ignatius… which we have by us” at the Philippians’ request and asks for “any more certain information” about his fate, implies a date shortly after Ignatius passed through Smyrna on his way to martyrdom at Rome (c. 108–117); chapter 9 holds up “the blessed Ignatius” as an example of endurance already in his “due place in the presence of the Lord”, which P. N. Harrison (1936) explains by dividing the letter into two, an early covering note (ch. 13) and a later main letter (chs. 1–12).3 Estimates for the letter range from c. 110 to c. 135; it is placed here at c. 125, after Ignatius (c. 108) and before Irenaeus (c. 180).
  • The translation. Quotations are from the Roberts–Donaldson rendering (Ante-Nicene Fathers), the received Greek text (chs. 1–9) with the Latin version for the closing chapters; chapter numbers follow the standard division. Polycarp also appears on this wiki as a witness on the intermediate state and the Old Testament canon, from the same letter.
