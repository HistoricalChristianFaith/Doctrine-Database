Polycarp of Smyrna on the Government of the Church

Dates: c. 125 (the Epistle to the Philippians; Polycarp himself c. 69–155, bishop of Smyrna and a disciple of the apostle John) · Wikipedia
Summary: Writing from Smyrna to the church at Philippi a generation after the apostle Paul, Polycarp gives directions for its ministry — and the ministry he addresses is a college of presbyters and deacons. He greets the church as “Polycarp and the presbyters with him,” instructs it about the duties of its deacons and presbyters (chs. 5–6), and disciplines a fallen presbyter, Valens (ch. 11) — yet he names no single bishop of Philippi anywhere in the letter. Polycarp is himself the bishop of Smyrna, so the omission is not that he does not know the office; it is that the church he writes to is still ordered, on the face of this letter, by presbyters and deacons and not by a monarch-bishop — a two-order ministry surviving at Philippi at the very moment Ignatius was urging the single bishop on the churches of Asia. That makes this letter a key counter-datum for the developmental reading of church order (weighed in the summary’s Arguments), not a settled verdict against the antiquity of the episcopate.

Claims & evidence

“Polycarp, and the presbyters with him” — a corporate address, no bishop of Philippi named

Polycarp, and the presbyters with him, to the Church of God sojourning at Philippi: Mercy to you, and peace from God Almighty, and from the Lord Jesus Christ, our Saviour, be multiplied.

— Epistle to the Philippians, salutation, source (Roberts–Donaldson translation, Ante-Nicene Fathers; cf. New Advent).1 The letter does not open in Polycarp’s name alone but in the name of “Polycarp and the presbyters with him” (the ANF note offers “Polycarp, and those who with him are presbyters”) — a corporate salutation from Smyrna’s presbytery, not a bishop writing over his own single signature. And when he turns to the ministry at Philippi, the officers he addresses are, throughout, presbyters and deacons: the single ruling bishop who dominates Ignatius’s letters to the nearby churches of Asia is nowhere named at Philippi, nor is any instruction given to obey one.

The duties of the deacons and the presbyters — a two-order local ministry (chs. 5–6)

In like manner should the deacons be blameless before the face of His righteousness, as being the servants of God and Christ, and not of men. They must not be slanderers, double-tongued, or lovers of money, but temperate in all things, compassionate, industrious, walking according to the truth of the Lord, who was the servant of all… Wherefore, it is needful to abstain from all these things, being subject to the presbyters and deacons, as unto God and Christ.

And let the presbyters be compassionate and merciful to all, bringing back those that wander, visiting all the sick, and not neglecting the widow, the orphan, or the poor… abstaining from all wrath, respect of persons, and unjust judgment; keeping far off from all covetousness, not quickly crediting [an evil report] against any one, not severe in judgment, as knowing that we are all under a debt of sin.

— Epistle to the Philippians, chs. 5–6, source.1 Polycarp lays out the church’s order in the two offices Paul had greeted at this very city — “the bishops and deacons” of Philippians 1:1, here named as “presbyters and deacons” (the same two-order ministry, the first office under its other title). The whole congregation is to be “subject to the presbyters and deacons, as unto God and Christ” — the formula of obedience Ignatius reserves for the bishop is here directed to the plural presbyters and the deacons together. There is no third rank set over them; the deacons are the servants of the community, the presbyters its pastors and shepherds of the straying, the sick, and the poor. It is a plural, local, two-order ministry — the same shape as the Didache and 1 Clement, and unlike the ranked threefold order of Ignatius.

The discipline of Valens, a fallen presbyter (ch. 11)

I am greatly grieved for Valens, who was once a presbyter among you, because he so little understands the place that was given him [in the Church]… For if a man cannot govern himself in such matters, how shall he enjoin them on others?… And be ye then moderate in regard to this matter, and “do not count such as enemies,” but call them back as suffering and straying members, that ye may save your whole body.

— Epistle to the Philippians, ch. 11, source.2 The occasion of much of the letter is a discipline case: Valens, “once a presbyter among you,” had fallen into avarice and lost “the place that was given him.” The datum is doubly telling for church order. First, the office in question is again the presbyterate — the ministry Philippi knows — not an episcopate. Second, the handling of the case is congregational and pastoral, not the act of a single ruling bishop: Polycarp does not tell the Philippians to refer the matter to their bishop but urges the whole church to deal with Valens moderately and to “call them back… that ye may save your whole body.” Office and discipline at Philippi run through the presbytery and the congregation, not through a monarch-bishop.

Notes

  • What it establishes for polity — and what it does not. The letter is firsthand evidence that a two-order ministry of presbyters and deacons was still the order of the Philippian church around c. 125, a generation after Paul and at the very time Ignatius was pressing the single bishop on the churches of Asia. That is a real counter-datum to the claim that the monarchical episcopate was uniform and universal in the early second century. But it does not by itself prove the episcopate un-apostolic: an argument from silence cannot exclude a presiding elder at Philippi whom Polycarp simply had no occasion to single out, and Polycarp himself holds the very office at Smyrna. The letter shows the episcopate’s uneven, region-by-region emergence, which is exactly what the developmental reading predicts and a delivered-whole apostolic blueprint does not — so its interpretive weight is carried in the summary’s Arguments (see Is the threefold ministry apostolic, or a second-century development?), where it is a datum in the debate, not a verdict in it.
  • Polycarp is himself a bishop — which sharpens the point rather than blunting it. Irenaeus and Tertullian both name Polycarp as bishop of Smyrna, set there (they say) by the apostle John, and the manuscript title of this very letter styles him “Polycarp, Bishop of Smyrna.” So the absence of a Philippian bishop is not ignorance of the office on Polycarp’s part: it is that Smyrna had its bishop while Philippi, on the evidence of this letter, was still governed by its presbyters and deacons — the two cities at different stages of the same development. (On Polycarp as a link in the episcopal succession-lists, see Irenaeus and Tertullian.)
  • The date, and the Ignatius connection. The letter dates itself by Ignatius: Polycarp forwards “the Epistles of Ignatius… which we have by us” at the Philippians’ request (ch. 13), holds up “the blessed Ignatius” as an example of endurance already in his “due place in the presence of the Lord” (ch. 9), and asks for “any more certain information” about his fate (ch. 13) — so it is written from Smyrna a short time after Ignatius passed through on his way to martyrdom at Rome (c. 108 –117).3 Estimates for the letter range from c. 110 to c. 135 (some scholars, following P. N. Harrison, divide it into an early covering note and a later main letter); it is anchored here at c. 125, its place on the timeline — after Ignatius (c. 108), before Irenaeus (c. 180) — being unaffected by the exact year.
  • The translation. Quotations are from the Roberts–Donaldson rendering (Ante-Nicene Fathers), the received Greek text (chs. 1–9) with the Latin version for the closing chapters; chapter numbers follow the standard division. Polycarp also appears on this wiki as a witness on the intermediate state and the Old Testament canon, from the same letter.
