Is the threefold ministry apostolic in origin, or a second-century development?

Disputed reading · on The Government of the Church · witness: Ignatius of Antioch

Related readings: Was the single-bishop episcopate in place everywhere by c. 110, or did it emerge unevenly? · Do Ignatius's letters describe an established episcopate, or urge a new one? · Were “bishop” and “presbyter” originally one office?

The passage

In like manner, let all reverence the deacons as an appointment of Jesus Christ, and the bishop as Jesus Christ, who is the Son of the Father, and the presbyters as the sanhedrim of God, and assembly of the apostles. Apart from these, there is no Church.

— Ignatius of Antioch, Epistle to the Trallians, ch. 3 (c. 108).1

And thus preaching through countries and cities, they appointed the first-fruits [of their labours], having first proved them by the Spirit, to be bishops and deacons of those who should afterwards believe.

— Clement of Rome, First Epistle to the Corinthians, ch. 42 (c. 96).2

The question

About a decade separates 1 Clement, which names apostolically appointed “bishops and deacons”, from Ignatius, who sets one bishop over a presbytery and deacons in every church he writes to. The dispute is whether that threefold order of one bishop, presbyters and deacons was handed down from the apostles, or took shape after them out of an earlier collegial ministry, and, if it developed, whether the development carries apostolic or divine sanction. Catholic teaching holds that the hierarchy of “bishops, priests, and ministers” is “by divine ordination instituted” (Council of Trent, 1563) and that “bishops by divine institution have succeeded to the place of the apostles” (Second Vatican Council, Lumen Gentium 20, 1964); the preface to the Church of England’s Ordinal (1662) states that these orders have existed “from the Apostles’ time”; Calvin counted the single bishop a “human arrangement” (1559). The answer therefore bears on how each communion reads its own ministry against the earliest record.3

A later arrangement grown out of a plural leadership

On this reading the single city bishop arose afterwards out of a plural leadership, either a body of presbyter-bishops in each church or, in Stewart’s form, a federation of congregations each with its own episkopos.

Held by: Jerome (Commentary on Titus, c. 386–387); John Calvin (Reformed, Institutes IV.4, 1559); Alistair C. Stewart (Anglican scholar, The Original Bishops, 2014) ⚠ unverified.4

Jerome states the reading on Titus 1:5: “among the ancients presbyters were the same as bishops, but that little by little, so that the seedlings of dissensions might be rooted out, the whole responsibility was conferred upon one”, so that bishops are “greater than the presbyters more by custom than by the truth of the Lord’s arrangement”.5 Calvin cites Jerome and draws the same picture of the primitive church: in each city the presbyters “selected one of their number to whom they gave the special title of bishop, lest, as usually happens, from equality dissension should arise”, a practice “introduced by human arrangement, according to the exigency of the times”.6 The New Testament supplies the starting point. Paul calls the Ephesian “elders” together (Acts 20:17) and tells the same men that the Holy Ghost has made them “overseers” (Acts 20:28); he has Titus “ordain elders in every city” because “a bishop must be blameless” (Titus 1:5–7); he greets at Philippi “the bishops and deacons”, several bishops in one city and no third rank (Philippians 1:1).7 The earliest writings after the New Testament keep that two-order shape. 1 Clement (c. 96) has the apostles appoint “bishops and deacons” and calls the deposed Corinthian officers both “bishops” and “presbyters” (chs. 42, 44), naming no single bishop of Corinth; the Didache tells a congregation to “appoint for yourselves bishops and deacons” (ch. 15) and never uses the word “presbyter”.8 Ignatius, writing about a decade later, presses the three orders on his readers (“do nothing without the bishop”), which Philip Schaff (Protestant church historian, 1889) reads as the urging of an episcopate that was “as yet a young institution, greatly needing commendation” (see the reading of Ignatius’s letters); his letter to Rome greets no bishop of Rome, and Polycarp writes to Philippi as “Polycarp and the presbyters with him” and names no bishop there.9 Modern study of house churches gives the reading its present form. Stewart, as Dan Batovici reports him, holds that “the earliest individual Christian communities had a single domestic leader, an episkopos”; that congregations in one city federated; and that “such collegiate presbyteries were the milieux in which, eventually, the position of monepiskopos would arise”. On this account the city bishop of Ignatius is the end of a second-century process whose stages differed from city to city.10

The three orders handed down from the apostles, the bishop holding the apostles' oversight

On this reading the presiding bishop continues an oversight the apostles and their delegates already held over the local presbyters; only the restriction of the title “bishop” to him came later.

Held by: Richard Hooker (Church of England, Laws of Ecclesiastical Polity VII, c. 1594); the Ordinal of the Church of England (1662); Charles Gore (Anglican, The Church and the Ministry, 1889); John Zizioulas (Orthodox, Eucharist, Bishop, Church, English trans. 2001).11

Hooker roots the episcopate in the apostolic age itself: “shall we think that James was made bishop of Jerusalem, Evodius bishop of the church of Antioch, the Angels in the churches of Asia bishops… without some like divine instigation and direction of the Holy Ghost?” He concludes that “the first institution of bishops was from heaven”.12 The Ordinal’s preface states the historical claim: “It is evident unto all men diligently reading holy Scripture and ancient Authors, that from the Apostles’ time there have been these Orders of Ministers in Christ’s Church; Bishops, Priests, and Deacons.”13 Gore builds the case from the New Testament’s own tiers. Above the local “presbyters” or “bishops” stood the apostles and “apostolic men like Timothy and Titus”, who had received by the laying-on of apostolic hands “what was essentially apostolic authority to guard the faith, to found and rule Churches, to ordain and discipline the clergy”. James, “a man of apostolic rank and authority settled in Jerusalem”, passed his office to a line of bishops. The bishops of Ignatius’s letters, “though they bear a name transferred from the presbyterate”, hold the office of representing Christ that had belonged to James, to prophets and teachers and to apostolic legates, “but never to the presbyters”; their authority “represents devolution from above and not delegation from below”, and “the transition to the localized episcopate was effected by no less an authority than that of apostles”.14 Zizioulas reaches the bishop through the eucharistic assembly. Jerusalem was headed by “the triad: James - the presbyters - those who serve”, which “may have formed the model also for the organization of the other Churches”; this triad was “the first linguistic form under which the Bishop appeared in history”, named by the office-holder’s personal name and “implicit within the collective term ‘the presbyters’”. “It follows that the office of Bishop exists even in the apostolic period, overshadowed by the institution of the Apostles.” The bishop presided at the one Eucharist, and when the apostles were gone he took their place at its table.15

A development out of the presbyterate, made under apostolic or Spirit-guided sanction

On this reading the single bishop was raised from among the presbyters in the late apostolic age or after it, and the development carries the sanction of the last apostles or the guidance of the Holy Spirit.

Held by: J. B. Lightfoot (Anglican, “The Christian Ministry”, 1868); Francis A. Sullivan (Catholic, From Apostles to Bishops, 2001) ⚠ unverified.16

Lightfoot starts from the vocabulary: if “bishop” first meant presbyter and later named the officer over the presbyters, then “the episcopate was formed not out of the apostolic order by localisation but out of the presbyteral by elevation: and the title, which originally was common to all, came at length to be appropriated to the chief among them”. He finds James at Jerusalem the earliest instance of the developed form.17 He places the change in “the last thirty years of the first century”: when St John and other surviving apostles moved to Asia Minor they would “encourage an approach in these Gentile Churches to the same organization” that had held the Jerusalem church together, giving “permanence, definiteness, stability, to an office which already existed in germ”; a college “necessarily supposes a presidency of some kind”, and an office “sanctioned by the most venerated name in Christendom” would win “wide though gradual reception”. His conclusion: “the three-fold ministry can be traced to Apostolic direction; and short of an express statement we can possess no better assurance of a Divine appointment or at least a Divine sanction”, which justifies “jealous adhesion to a polity derived from this source” without unchurching communities “differently organized”.18 Sullivan, as David Fiensy reports him, sets the development later: the monarchical episcopate “developed first in the second century, and in Antioch and western Asia Minor before other localities”; there was “no single bishop in the church of Rome in the first century”; the apostles did not appoint one bishop in each church, but “did pass on their ministry to successors”. Granting that “history alone cannot give the answers”, Sullivan argues theologically that the development was guided by the Holy Spirit: it is consistent with the apostles’ sharing of their ministry, it met the second-century need to counter heresy, and the church’s acceptance of the bishops’ teaching is “analogous to the reception” of the New Testament canon. On these grounds he holds the bishops to be the successors of the apostles.19
