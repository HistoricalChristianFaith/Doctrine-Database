Do Ignatius's letters describe an established episcopate, or urge a new one?

Disputed reading · on The Government of the Church · witness: Ignatius of Antioch

Related readings: Is the threefold ministry apostolic in origin, or a second-century development? · Was the single-bishop episcopate in place everywhere by c. 110, or did it emerge unevenly?

The passage

See that ye all follow the bishop, even as Jesus Christ does the Father… Let no man do anything connected with the Church without the bishop… Wherever the bishop shall appear, there let the multitude [of the people] also be; even as, wherever Jesus Christ is, there is the Catholic Church.

— Ignatius of Antioch, Epistle to the Smyrnaeans, ch. 8 (Roberts–Donaldson translation, shorter recension).1

… give one the title of bishop, but do all things without him. Now such persons seem to me to be not possessed of a good conscience, seeing they are not stedfastly gathered together according to the commandment.

— Ignatius of Antioch, Epistle to the Magnesians, ch. 4.2

The question

Ignatius’s seven letters, written on his way to martyrdom early in the second century, urge each church he addresses to do nothing without its one bishop, with a presbytery and deacons beside him. The dispute is whether this insistence describes an order already standing in those churches and defends it against division, or commends an order still new or resisted there; the answer bears on how early the single-bishop office is dated in Syria and Asia Minor. Kenneth Strand (1966) set out the two sides: a tendency to interpret the letters’ “urgency as evidence that the monarchical episcopate was very recent and not as yet firmly established”, and his own explanation from “the danger of divisive tendencies created by the prevalence of heresies”.3

A standing office, defended against division

On this reading Ignatius writes to churches that already have their bishops, and presses obedience to them because heresy and faction threatened their unity.

Held by: J. B. Lightfoot (Anglican, “The Christian Ministry”, 1868); Alphonse Van Hove (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1907); Kenneth A. Strand (historian, Andrews University Seminary Studies, 1966); John Zizioulas (Eastern Orthodox, Metropolitan of Pergamon, Eucharist, Bishop, Church, English translation 2001).4

Lightfoot notes that Ignatius writes to one bishop, Polycarp, mentions several others, and names the three orders together, so that “it is plain that he looks upon the episcopal system as the one recognised and authoritative form of government in all those churches with which he is most directly concerned.” He records the suggestion that Ignatius “would hardly have enforced the claims of episcopacy, unless it were an object of attack”, and answers that “still some years would be required before it could have assumed that mature and definite form which it has in his letters.”5 Proponents point to present-tense notes in the letters: the Ephesians are to run together with the will of their bishop, “which thing also ye do”, their presbytery “fitted as exactly to the bishop as the strings are to the harp”; the Trallians are told, “as ye indeed do, so without the bishop ye should do nothing”; and the bishops of Ephesus, Magnesia, Tralles and Smyrna are named as sitting incumbents. Those rebuked at Magnesia “give one the title of bishop”: on this reading they bypass a bishop they recognise.6 Van Hove writes that in the Asian communities of which Ignatius speaks “at the beginning of the second century the monarchical episcopate existed, for Ignatius does not write as though the institution were a new one.”7 Strand locates the motive in the letters’ setting: the whole Ignatian reference to church order “is set in the context of appeals to unity”, and the emphasis falls on the Asian churches troubled by docetism, while the letter to Rome, where Ignatius “shows no awareness of a like danger”, lacks it.8 Orthodox interpreters ground the office in the eucharistic assembly. Zizioulas writes: “The task of the Bishop was from the beginning principally liturgical consisting in the offering of the Divine Eucharist”; he reads Ignatius’s bishop “presiding in the place of God” together with the late first-century assembly of the Apocalypse. John Romanides (1956) argues that Ignatius’s rule that no agape be held without the bishop would be “incomprehensible and extremely fantastic if it were not presupposed that in the thought and experience of St. Ignatius each liturgical center necessitated the existence of a bishop”.9 On this reading the letters exhort churches that already have their bishops to hold to them.

An order Ignatius commends where it was new or resisted

On this reading the letters advocate a single-bishop order that was young, resisted, or held by one party, and Ignatius is its champion rather than its reporter.

Held by: Philip Schaff (Protestant church historian, History of the Christian Church, vol. II, 1889); Walter Bauer (critical historian, Orthodoxy and Heresy in Earliest Christianity, 1934); Allen Brent (historian, Ignatius of Antioch: A Martyr Bishop and the Origin of Episcopacy, 2007).10

Schaff describes the episcopate of the letters as “as yet a young institution, greatly needing commendation”, and their substance as “earnest exhortations to obey the bishop and maintain the unity of the church against the Judaistic and docetic heresies.” Their “daring superabundance of episcopalianism”, he writes, “may also be explained as a special pleading for a novelty which to the mind of the writer was essential to the very existence of the church”; his summary: “The Ignatian episcopacy, in short, is congregational, not diocesan; a new and growing institution, not a settled policy of apostolic origin.”11 Proponents point to the escalation in the letters’ language: the bishop is to be looked on “even as we would upon the Lord Himself”; one who acts “without the knowledge of the bishop” serves “the devil”; and of the three orders, “Apart from these, there is no Church”, set beside the plural presbyter-bishops of 1 Clement and the Didache.12 Some at Magnesia act without their bishop, and the church there must be urged “not to treat your bishop too familiarly on account of his youth, but to yield him all reverence”.13 The letter to Rome, as Schaff notes, says “not a word” about bishops beyond Ignatius’s own title, “the bishop of Syria”.14 Bauer reads the letters as aspiration: “Ignatius is less concerned with depicting the actual situation than with portraying the ideal”, since “his approach takes the form of admonition rather than of description”, and he knew Magnesia and Tralles chiefly from their own bishops’ reports. On Bauer’s account the single bishop allowed a party to lead a divided church, so that “orthodoxy can hope to take the helm even where it constitutes only a minority of the whole group”.15 Brent, who defends the letters’ authenticity, presents Ignatius as “a controversial figure arising in the context of a church at war with itself” who “constructs out of the conflicting models of church order available to him one founded on a single bishop that he commends to Christian communities through which he passes in chains as a condemned martyr prisoner.”16 On this reading Ignatius is the advocate of the single-bishop order in the churches he passes through.
