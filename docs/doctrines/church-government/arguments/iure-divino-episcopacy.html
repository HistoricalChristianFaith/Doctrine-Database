Is episcopacy of divine right (iure divino), or an alterable arrangement?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — that government by bishops is apostolic, primitive, and divinely approved (the moderate form, Hooker’s) is well-grounded; that it binds by strict divine right as a distinct order the church may not do without, so that a non-episcopal body is no true church (the hard form, Bancroft’s and Laud’s), does not hold. Between the sound moderate reading and the weak strict one, “episcopacy is iure divino” as a total account of church government is genuinely disputed, with no clear winner · advanced by Richard Hooker and Richard Bancroft, against the adiaphorism of John Whitgift

The claim

Wherefore let us not fear to be herein bold and peremptory, that if any thing in the Church’s government, surely the first institution of bishops was from heaven, was even of God, the Holy Ghost was the author of it.

— Richard Hooker, Of the Laws of Ecclesiastical Polity, Book VII, ch. 5 (Keble VII.5.10), Keble ed.1 The interpretation this page weighs is that the government of the church by bishops — a distinct order set over the presbyters, with the sole power to ordain — is not a mere human convenience but is of divine right (iure divino), given to the church by God and not at the church’s free disposal to lay aside. It runs in two strengths that must be kept apart, and they earn very different verdicts.

Who advances it

The question was forged in the Elizabethan and early-Stuart debates over the English church. Its moderate form is Hooker’s (Laws, Book VII, 1590s): the episcopate is apostolic and of divine institution, yet not so absolutely necessary that its absence unchurches — in cases of necessity ordination without a bishop may hold, and he will “not simply without exception” require an unbroken episcopal succession for a valid ministry. Its hard form is Bancroft’s (Paul’s Cross, 1589) and, after him, Hadrian Saravia, Thomas Bilson, and — at its height — Archbishop Laud: bishops are a distinct order superior to presbyters, and to deny it is the ancient Aerian heresy. Both stand against the adiaphorist position of Whitgift (against Cartwright, 1574), that no one polity is prescribed in Scripture, so church government is a thing indifferent the magistrate may order — and against the presbyterian claim (Thomas Cartwright) that a non-episcopal discipline is the divinely-fixed form. This page steelmans the divine-right reading in Hooker’s nuanced version and rates it; the rival adiaphorist and presbyterian readings are the standing counter-positions it must answer.2

The evidence for

A trans-congregational oversight already stands over the local elders in the New Testament. Even granting that episkopos and presbyteros named one local office (which the parity arguments establish), the apostolic writings show a real tier of oversight above the local presbyter-bishops: the apostles themselves; James presiding at Jerusalem; and the delegates Timothy and Titus, personally empowered to appoint elders city by city, to receive charges against them, and to lay on hands (Titus 1:5; 1 Timothy 5:19–22). The presiding function the bishop later gathers into one office is therefore not an invention but the settling of something apostolic — the point at which the developmental thesis leaves the substance-apostolic reading standing.3

The single bishop is attested remarkably early and became all but universal. By Ignatius (c. 108) the threefold order — one bishop, a presbytery, deacons — is everywhere presupposed and made constitutive of the church; within a century it is the undisputed order of the whole church, East and West, and the succession-lists of Irenaeus and Tertullian trace the bishops of the great sees back to the apostles. That a form so early, so universal, and so continuous should be a mere human accident is, on its face, unlikely; Hooker’s inference — that the raising of bishops to quell schism was itself under “divine instigation and direction of the Holy Ghost” — is the theological reading of that record.4

The moderate form concedes the defeaters and survives them. Hooker does not need the New Testament to command a distinct third order; he grants the parity of the two titles and rests the case on the office’s apostolic origin, its Spirit-guided universality, and its retention of the reserved power to ordain. That is why his is the strong version: it is the reading the parity of episkopos and presbyteros does not touch.

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The moderate claim is well-grounded. Government by bishops is apostolic in root (a real trans-congregational oversight stands in the New Testament), primitive (settled by Ignatius’s day and universal within a century), and, on any reading friendly to providence, divinely approved — the church’s catholic and God-blessed order for a millennium and a half. This overlaps two readings already rated here: the substance-apostolic half of the threefold-ministry question (that the presiding function is apostolic even if the title and structure developed) and the doctrinal-continuity form of apostolic succession (rated sound). Hooker’s divine-institution claim, scoped to the office’s first institution and balanced by his own acknowledgement of custom, is a defensible theological gloss on a real historical fact.

What is overstated (flag on Bancroft’s page, and on Hooker’s where he leans hard). The strict form — episcopacy as a distinct order by God’s express command, whose absence unchurches a body or invalidates its ministry — does not hold, defeated on four fronts:

(1) No distinct third order is commanded in the New Testament. The two local titles interchange within single passages (Acts 20:17, 28; Titus 1:5–7; Philippians 1:1) — the philological core the office-terms and Jerome arguments rate sound. A presiding oversight existed; a divinely-mandated order of bishops set by name over presbyters is not laid down. Bancroft answers this case not on its merits but by branding it the Aerian heresy — a label, not a refutation.5

(2) The monarchical bishop emerged unevenly, not as a blueprint delivered whole. The developmental record shows the single bishop still absent from Rome and Philippi in Ignatius’s own day, urged rather than assumed, and reached by different regions at different times. A form that arrives piecemeal across a century reads awkwardly as a fixed divine constitution imposed from the first — the very reason that question is rated contested.

(3) The adiaphorist reply stands: Scripture prescribes no one perpetual polity. Whitgift’s case — “I find no one certain and perfect kind of government prescribed or commanded in the scriptures” — is not refuted by the antiquity of episcopacy; antiquity and universality show the church’s choice and providential blessing, not a command binding on pain of being no church. And the Church of England’s own practice belied the strict claim: through the Elizabethan and Jacobean period it received the non-episcopally-ordained ministers of the foreign Reformed churches as true ministers — conduct incompatible with holding episcopal ordination strictly necessary to a valid ministry.6

(4) The doctrine’s ablest defender stops short of the strict form. Hooker himself will not “simply without exception” require episcopal succession, and allows “very just and sufficient reason to allow ordination made without a bishop” in necessity. When the strongest advocate concedes the church is not un-made by the office’s absence, the hard iure divino claim has already been surrendered from within.1

Assessment

Contested. The interpretation splits cleanly by strength. In its moderate form — episcopacy is apostolic in root, primitive, all but universal from the second century, and divinely approved, the church’s best and God-blessed order — the reading is well-grounded, and coincides with the substance-apostolic and doctrinal-continuity readings rated elsewhere on this timeline. In its strict form — a distinct order by divine command whose absence unchurches — it is weak: no such order is commanded in the New Testament (the parity of episkopos and presbyteros is sound), the office emerged unevenly rather than as a delivered blueprint (the threefold-ministry question is contested), the adiaphorist reply that Scripture fixes no one perpetual polity stands, and the establishment’s own reception of non-episcopal Reformed ministers — and Hooker’s own necessity-concession — refuse the church-unmaking edge. Between a sound moderate reading and a weak strict one, “episcopacy is iure divino” as a total account of church government is genuinely disputed, strong by the antiquity-and-practice lights and weak by the New-Testament-parity and uneven-emergence lights, with no clear winner. Trust the moderate claim; flag, on Bancroft’s page, the strict version (and the Aerian-heresy move that substitutes a label for an answer). This is the episcopal reading of church government; it stands as the rival to the adiaphorist and presbyterian readings, which each carry real force — the three best read together as competing total accounts of the same evidence.
