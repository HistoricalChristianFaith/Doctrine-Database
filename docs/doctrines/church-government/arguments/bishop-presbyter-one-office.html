Were “bishop” and “presbyter” originally one office?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: sound — that in the New Testament and earliest church episkopos (“bishop/overseer”) and presbyteros (“presbyter/elder”) named one and the same office is near-consensus among historians of the ministry: the two titles interchange within single passages (Acts 20:17, 28; Titus 1:5–7), Paul greets “bishops” in the plural in one city (Philippians 1:1), and Jerome, the West’s own great biblical scholar, states the identity flatly. What does not follow from the terminological identity alone — and is the proponent’s overreach to flag — is the further inference that the single presiding bishop is therefore a merely human arrangement without the Lord’s warrant: that a distinct trans-congregational oversight (exercised apostolically by the apostles, James at Jerusalem, Timothy and Titus) existed above the local presbyter-bishops is untouched by the fact that the two local titles were one. That second, larger claim is weighed on its own in this doctrine’s Arguments · advanced by Jerome

Related arguments: the parity of bishop and presbyter — see also Do the New Testament office-terms settle a biblical polity?

The claim

Therefore, the presbyter is the same as the bishop, and before, by the instigation of the devil, competitions arose in religion and it was said among the people, “I am of Paul,” “I am of Apollo,” “I am of Cephas,” the governance of the church was conducted by the joint counsel of the presbyterate. But when each person began to consider those whom he had baptised as his own rather than Christ’s, it was decided throughout the world that one chosen from among the presbyters should be placed in charge of the others and have care of the general well-being of the church, so that the seeds of schism might be rooted out.

— Jerome, Commentary on Titus, on Titus 1:5–7 (c. 386–387), full text.1 Jerome restates the case, with the same proof-texts, in Letter 146 to Evangelus: the passages “clearly prove a bishop and a presbyter to be the same,” the presiding bishop was chosen only “subsequently… to remedy schism,” and — the hinge — “what function, excepting ordination, belongs to a bishop that does not also belong to a presbyter?”2 The asserted claim has two layers that must be kept apart: a lexical/historical layer (the two words named one office in the apostolic churches) and an inferential layer (therefore the later single bishop set “over the rest” is a churchly custom, not a divine institution). This page weighs the first; the second is a distinct question, handled separately below.

Who advances it

The identity of the two titles is Jerome’s (c. 347–420), pressed in his Commentary on Titus and Letter 146; his fuller detail treatment — including the eucharistic dignity he takes for granted even while levelling the two ranks — is on his ministerial priesthood page. He was not the first to make the equation: Aerius of Sebaste (c. 355) had already argued “a bishop is no different from a presbyter… one order, and one honor and one rank” (Epiphanius, Panarion 75.3), though he broke communion over it and was catalogued a heretic, where Jerome stayed inside the Church.3 In the modern era the lexical thesis was set on its scholarly footing by J. B. Lightfoot’s classic dissertation “The Christian Ministry” (in his commentary on Philippians, 1868), and it is now the common ground of most New Testament scholarship across confessions — not a sectarian Protestant reading but a point Catholic scholars concede as well. It is a cornerstone of the presbyterian and congregational cases against episcopacy iure divino; those larger polity claims are their own arguments.4

The evidence for

The New Testament interchanges the two titles. At Miletus Paul summons “the elders [presbyterous] of the church” of Ephesus and tells those same men that the Holy Spirit has made them “overseers” [episkopous] (Acts 20:17, 28). Writing to Titus he charges him to “ordain elders [presbyterous] in every city… for a bishop [episkopon] must be blameless” — the two words used of one man within three verses (Titus 1:5–7). Paul addresses “the bishops and deacons” of Philippi (Philippians 1:1): the overseers are plural in a single city and stand in a two-order pairing with deacons, with no third “presbyter” rank between. Peter, calling himself a “fellow-elder” [sympresbyteros], exhorts the elders to “feed the flock… taking the oversight” [episkopountes] — the elder’s work described by the bishop’s verb (1 Peter 5:1–2). This is the philological core, and it is what Jerome marshals.5

The earliest sub-apostolic witness reads the same way. 1 Clement (c. 96), a decade or more before Ignatius, uses “bishop” and “presbyter” of the same Corinthian officers: the strife is over “the office of the episcopate,” to depose the men is to eject them “from the episcopate,” and those same deposed men are in the next breath “those presbyters” whom it is sin to remove (1 Clement 44). The two-terms-one-office usage of the New Testament is still in force in the oldest Christian writing outside it.6

The judgment of the ancient exegetes and of modern scholarship converges. Jerome is not reading a Protestant thesis back into Paul; expounding Titus verse by verse he found no bishop/presbyter distinction in the text and said so, as did Aerius before him and (on the lexical point) Chrysostom and Theodore of Mopsuestia after. Lightfoot’s dissertation gathered the same data into the standard modern statement, and the terminological identity in the apostolic age is today about as settled as such questions get.4

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The lexical/historical layer is sound and mainstream. In the New Testament and the earliest church episkopos and presbyteros denote one local office; the fixed, monarchical bishop — one man set over the presbyters of a city — is not yet visible in the New Testament and is best described, at least in its terminology, as a later settling. Jerome is right about this, and right that a chief motive for elevating one presbyter over the college was the checking of faction (the same reason 1 Clement and the schism at Corinth put in view).

What is overstated or imprecise (flag on Jerome’s page). Three steps in the proponent’s handling run past the evidence:

(1) The inference from “one name” to “merely human office.” That the two local titles were interchangeable does not by itself establish that no distinct presiding or oversight role existed with apostolic warrant. The New Testament already shows a tier of trans-congregational oversight above the local presbyter-bishops: the apostles themselves; James presiding at Jerusalem (Acts 15:13; Galatians 2:9); and delegates like Timothy and Titus, personally empowered by Paul to appoint presbyters city by city, to receive charges against them, and to lay on hands (Titus 1:5; 1 Timothy 5:19–22). One can grant “two names, one office” at the congregational level and still hold that a higher oversight — the seed later gathered into the monarchical bishop — was exercised from the first. Whether that development is apostolic or a merely churchly custom is a separate question, not decided by the lexical fact; the leap from the one to the other is Jerome’s (and his modern users’) overreach.7

(2) Jerome is no leveller of the office, and his own words concede the exception. His question — “what function, excepting ordination, belongs to a bishop that does not also belong to a presbyter?” — grants the bishop a real, exclusive power; he reserves ordination to the bishop, holds the bishop rightly set “over” the presbyters (“by the custom of the Church”), and never lets a layman offer. The occasion of Letter 146 is a man who exalted deacons over presbyters, and Jerome’s aim is to humble clerical pride, not to abolish episcopacy — he practised and defended episcopal ordination all his life. Reading him as a proto-congregationalist inverts him.2

(3) The Alexandrian datum is disputed, not a clinching proof. Jerome’s claim that at Alexandria “the presbyters always named as bishop one of their own number” from Mark down to Heraclas and Dionysius (mid-third century) — presbyteral consecration of the bishop — is a striking local memory, and some scholars think it preserves a real early peculiarity; but it rests on Jerome’s report (with a later echo in the Alexandrian patriarch Eutychius) and is genuinely contested. It should be weighed as a live but uncertain datum, not treated as an established fact that settles the case.8

And the terminology settled early. By Ignatius (c. 108) the threefold order — one bishop, a presbytery, deacons — is everywhere presupposed and made constitutive of the church. Whatever its origin, the single bishop is attested remarkably early; “developed” need not mean “late” or “unapostolic,” and the timeline itself shows how narrow the window between the fluid New-Testament usage and the settled order really is.9

Assessment

Sound — for the claim this page advocates: that in the New Testament and the earliest church episkopos and presbyteros named one and the same office. The lexical interchange within single passages, the plural bishops of Philippi, and the concurrence of 1 Clement, Jerome, Aerius, and modern scholarship (Lightfoot) put the terminological identity beyond serious doubt. Trust that core. Flag, on Jerome’s page, the further inference so often hung on it — that the presiding bishop is therefore a purely human custom with no apostolic warrant: that step does not follow from the lexical fact alone, since a distinct trans-congregational oversight (apostles, James, Timothy, Titus) stands above the local presbyter-bishops in the same New Testament, and Jerome himself keeps the bishop’s reserved power (ordination) and rank. Whether the monarchical episcopate is apostolic or a later, merely-churchly development — and whether it binds iure divino — is a larger and more contested question, weighed on its own in this doctrine’s Arguments, not decided here.
