Were “bishop” and “presbyter” originally one office?

Disputed reading · on The Government of the Church · witnesses: Clement of Rome; Jerome

Related readings: Is the threefold ministry apostolic in origin, or a second-century development?

The passage

A presbyter, therefore, is the same as a bishop; and before, at the devil’s instigation, factions arose in religion, and it was said among the peoples, “I am of Paul, I of Apollos, but I of Cephas” (1 Cor. 1:12), the churches were governed by the common council of the presbyters. But after each one thought that those whom he had baptized were his own and not Christ’s, it was decreed throughout the whole world that one chosen from among the presbyters should be set over the rest, to whom the whole care of the church should belong, and the seeds of schisms be removed.

— Jerome, Commentary on Titus, on Titus 1:5–7 (c. 386–387), full text.1 Jerome restates the case, with the same proof-texts, in Letter 146 to Evangelus: the passages “clearly prove a bishop and a presbyter to be the same,” the presiding bishop was chosen only “subsequently… to remedy schism,” and “what function, excepting ordination, belongs to a bishop that does not also belong to a presbyter?”2

The question

The New Testament uses presbyteros (“elder, presbyter”) and episkopos (“overseer, bishop”) of the same men at Acts 20:17, 28 and Titus 1:5–7, Paul greets “bishops” in the plural at Philippi (Philippians 1:1), and 1 Clement 44 (c. 96) calls the same Corinthian officers by both names.3,4 The dispute is what lay behind the shared names: one office, from which a single bishop was later raised; two orders not yet distinguished by name; or a presiding presbyter whose title alone later changed. Episcopal, presbyterian and congregational churches all appeal to these texts for their own order.

One office under two names, the single bishop later raised from among the presbyters

On this reading the apostolic churches were governed by a college of presbyter-bishops of one rank, and the single bishop over the presbyters was a later arrangement.

Held by: Aerius of Sebaste (presbyter, mid-fourth century, as quoted by Epiphanius); Jerome (Latin church father, Commentary on Titus, c. 387); John Calvin (Reformed, Institutes, 1559); J. B. Lightfoot (Anglican, “The Christian Ministry”, 1868).5

The case starts from Titus 1:5–7, where Titus is told to ordain presbyters “in every city” and is then told what “a bishop” must be. Jerome adds Acts 20, Philippians 1:1 and 1 Peter 5 and concludes that the churches were first “governed by the common council of the presbyters”, one presbyter being set over the rest later to remove the seeds of schism, so that bishops are “greater than the presbyters more by custom than by the truth of the Lord’s arrangement”. Aerius, a presbyter of Sebaste, is quoted by Epiphanius as saying “There is one order… and one honor and one rank”: bishop and presbyter alike lay on hands, baptize and celebrate the eucharist.6 Calvin took Jerome’s account as the history of the early episcopate: in each city the presbyters “selected one of their number to whom they gave the special title of bishop, lest, as usually happens, from equality dissension should arise”, a practice “introduced by human arrangement, according to the exigency of the times”.7 Lightfoot called the identity of the two titles “a fact now generally recognized by theologians of all shades of opinion” and drew the historical conclusion that “the episcopate was formed not out of the apostolic order by localisation but out of the presbyteral by elevation”; he held at the same time that the threefold ministry “can be traced to Apostolic direction”.8 For an older practice surviving into the third century he gathered Jerome’s report that at Alexandria, down to Heraclas and Dionysius, “the presbyters always nominated as bishop one chosen out of their own body”, Ambrosiaster’s remark that in Egypt the presbyters “seal” when the bishop is absent, and the account of the tenth-century patriarch Eutychius that the remaining presbyters laid hands on the one chosen patriarch.9 Congregational and Baptist confessions build their officers on the same texts: the Cambridge Platform (1648) speaks of “elders, who are also in scripture called bishops”, and the Second London Confession (1689) names the officers of a particular church as “bishops or elders, and deacons”.10 On this reading the New Testament knows two local offices, presbyter-bishops and deacons, and the later rank of bishop above presbyter grew out of the presidency of one presbyter among his colleagues.

The names were shared, the orders already distinct

On this reading the two words were used loosely in the apostles’ time, while a higher, episcopal oversight was already held by the apostles and the men they set over the churches.

Held by: Epiphanius of Salamis (Panarion, c. 376); John Chrysostom (Greek church father, Homily 1 on Philippians, d. 407); Thomas Aquinas (Catholic, Commentary on Philippians, d. 1274); Alphonse Van Hove (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1907).11

Epiphanius answers the texts Aerius cited (letters addressed to “presbyters and deacons” or to “bishops and deacons”) from the conditions of a new mission: “Where bishops were already consecrated he wrote to bishops and deacons, for the apostles could not establish everything at once.” The orders differ in what they do: the episcopate “generates fathers” for the church, the presbyterate produces “children through the laver of regeneration”.12 Chrysostom, at Philippians 1:1, asks whether there were “several Bishops of one city? Certainly not; but he called the Presbyters so. For then they still interchanged the titles”; Timothy was a bishop, since he is told to “lay hands hastily on no man”, and “the specific name is distinctly appropriated to each, the Bishop and the Presbyter”.13 Theodoret reads the verse the same way and finds the episcopal function in Epaphroditus, whom Paul calls the Philippians’ apostle; Theodore of Mopsuestia writes that some were “put in charge of towns, some of whole regions. These latter became the bishops of later times.”14 Aquinas puts the principle in one sentence: “in the beginning, although the orders were distinct, there were not distinct names for the orders”, the bishops answering to the Twelve and the priests to the seventy-two.15 The New Testament, on this reading, shows that higher oversight at work: James presiding at Jerusalem (Acts 15:13), Titus left to “ordain elders in every city”, Timothy receiving accusations against elders and laying on hands (1 Timothy 5:19–22).16 Richard Hooker named James at Jerusalem, Evodius at Antioch and “the Angels in the churches of Asia” as bishops and held that “the first institution of bishops was from heaven”; the preface to the Anglican Ordinal (1662) states that “from the Apostles time, there have been these Orders of Ministers in Christs Church; Bishops, Priests, and Deacons”.17,18 Van Hove grants that the two words were “to some extent” synonymous and holds that the local college of presbyter-bishops was subject to the apostles or their delegates, who were bishops in the full sense without fixed sees or a special title; when the missionaries left, the local authority of apostolic origin passed to a single bishop.19

A presiding presbyter from the first, the title “bishop” later reserved to him

On this reading one of the presbyter-bishops always presided, and what changed in the second century was chiefly the use of the title.

Held by: Ambrosiaster (Latin commentator, Commentary on Ephesians, c. 384); John Zizioulas (Orthodox, Eucharist, Bishop, Church, 1965; ET 2001); V. Rev. Lawrence Farley (Orthodox, Orthodox Church in America, Essential Orthodox Christian Beliefs: A Manual for Adult Instruction, ch. 9, 2023).20

Ambrosiaster asks, “what is a bishop, if not the first presbyter, that is, the chief priest?” He explains that Paul calls Timothy, “a presbyter created by himself, a bishop; because the first presbyters were called bishops; so that when one departed, the next might succeed him”. Later, “a council providing for it”, the choice came to rest on merit rather than order, “appointed by the judgment of many priests”.21 Lawrence Farley, writing chapter 9 of the Orthodox Church in America’s adult-instruction manual, agrees that the two words “described the same office”, citing Acts 20, 1 Timothy 3 and 5, Titus 1, Didache 15 and 1 Clement 42 and 44, and proposes that “the development was merely terminological”. From the first, “one of the bishops/presbyters must have presided at the altar”, a presider who “had (and needed) no specific or unique title” and was in most communities “probably known by his name”, as James was at Jerusalem. When persecution and the threat of schism made unity more pressing, “the title episcopos was then reserved for him”, with “no change of structure”. Farley points to Ignatius, a few years after 1 Clement, writing of presbyters “tuned to the bishop as strings to a lyre” and of “bishops appointed throughout the world”, and to the lack of any recorded protest at a new office.22 Zizioulas, citing the research of G. Konidaris, finds the same presider at Jerusalem: “the Church of Jerusalem was headed by the triad: James - the presbyters - those who serve”, the bishop being known by the office-holder’s personal name and “implicit within the collective term ‘the presbyters’ whenever there was no reason to single him out.”23 On this reading the presbyters went on governing with their president as before, and the single bishop of Ignatius is the presiding presbyter of the apostolic churches under his later title.
