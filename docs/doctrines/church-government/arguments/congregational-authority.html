Does the gathered congregation hold final authority? — the congregational thesis

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — the corporate-discipline strand is genuinely strong: the local congregation really does act as a body in discipline and in the choice of its officers (Matthew 18; 1 Corinthians 5; Acts 6), and the earliest exegetes grant the assembly a real share in the sentence. But the defining congregational claim — that final authority over discipline, doctrine, and officers rests in the whole gathered assembly, over against a presiding eldership or a trans-congregational oversight — does not follow from these texts, which under-determine it and are claimed equally by the presbyterian reading; genuinely disputed, with no clear winner · advanced by Robert Browne and the classic Congregational standards (the Cambridge Platform, the Savoy Declaration, John Owen)

Related arguments: the forms of government — see also Is episcopacy of divine right? · Is rule by a college of elders the apostolic form of government?

The claim

… power of office, that is, such as is proper to the eldership, or power of privilege, such as belongs unto the brotherhood. … This power of government in the elders, doth not any wise prejudice the power of privilege in the brotherhood; as neither the power of privilege in the brethren, doth prejudice the power of government in the elders.

— A Platform of Church Discipline (the Cambridge Platform), ch. X, 1648, full text.1 The interpretation this page weighs is the strong form of that “power of privilege”: the congregational (or independent) thesis that, in the New Testament, final authority in discipline, doctrine, and the choosing of officers rests in the whole gathered local congregation — not in a bishop, and not even in a college of elders acting over it. On the strong reading the local church is the seat of the keys (Matthew 18:17–18), the assembly acts as a body to admit, censure, and expel, and the elders lead within the congregation but do not stand above its final say.

Who advances it

The reading is the platform of the English Separatist and Independent tradition. Robert Browne (A Treatise of Reformation without Tarrying for Any, 1582) is its first blunt manifesto: a true church is a company gathered by covenant — “The Lord’s people is of the willing sort” — that holds Christ’s keys directly and may reform itself without waiting on magistrate or bishop.2 It matures into the classic Congregational standards: the New England Cambridge Platform (1648), which divides ordinary church power into the elders’ “power of office” and the brethren’s “power of privilege” — the latter reaching to “choosing their own officers” and to admission and “the public censure” of members; the Savoy Declaration (1658); and John Owen’s The True Nature of a Gospel Church (1689), the fullest exposition, which lodges all church power in Christ and its ordinary exercise in the particular congregation. The same texts are urged in every case — Matthew 18, Acts 6, Acts 15, 1 Corinthians 5 — and are read as vesting the decisive act in the assembled body.3

The evidence for

The congregation is the disciplining body. The strongest strand is not inference but the plain grammar of the discipline texts. In Matthew 18 the escalating appeal ends not with a magistrate or a single officer but with the assembly: “tell it unto the church: but if he neglect to hear the church, let him be unto thee as an heathen man and a publican,” and to that same body Christ gives the power of the keys, “Whatsoever ye shall bind on earth shall be bound in heaven” (Matthew 18:17–18). At Corinth Paul directs the sentence to the gathered church acting corporately — “when ye are gathered together… to deliver such an one unto Satan” — and closes, “put away from among yourselves that wicked person” (1 Corinthians 5:4–5, 13); the penalty is one “inflicted of many” (2 Corinthians 2:6), and its lifting is the congregation’s to grant. Even the ancient exegetes concede the assembly a real share: Chrysostom says Paul “makes them also partners in the sentence,” and Ambrosiaster that the offender was to be ejected “by common consent among themselves.”4

The congregation chooses its officers. When the Seven are appointed, the Twelve do not simply install their own men: they call “the multitude of the disciples” and bid them “look ye out among you seven men… whom we may appoint,” and “the saying pleased the whole multitude: and they chose” the Seven (Acts 6:2–5). The Didache likewise tells the whole community to “appoint for yourselves bishops and deacons” by show of hands. Election from below, on this reading, is the New Testament pattern, and the people’s power to choose implies their power to refuse.5

The whole church shares in decision. Even the great doctrinal cause of the apostolic age is settled in open assembly: the Jerusalem decree goes out from “the apostles and elders, with the whole church” (Acts 15:22), the body concurring, not merely receiving. And where a leader tries to engross authority to himself — Diotrephes, “who loveth to have the preeminence,” casting members out of the church (3 John 9–10) — the New Testament rebukes him, the congregationalist reads, precisely as a usurper of what belongs to the body.6

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The corporate-discipline element is well-grounded and should be granted without grudging. The local congregation in the New Testament is not a passive audience: it is addressed as the body that hears the charge (Matthew 18:17), gathers to pass and to lift the sentence (1 Corinthians 5:4; 2 Corinthians 2:6–8), puts away the offender “from among yourselves” (1 Corinthians 5:13), and takes a genuine part in choosing its officers (Acts 6:5). The patristic witnesses confirm rather than resist this: the people are “partners in the sentence” (Chrysostom), and the ejection is “by common consent” (Ambrosiaster). A polity that writes the congregation out of discipline and election altogether is false to the texts.

And even Cyprian, for all his episcopal theology, resolved from the first “to do nothing on my own private opinion, without your advice” — the clergy’s — “and without the consent of the people,” and held that the people have the power “of choosing worthy priests, or of rejecting unworthy ones.”7

What is overstated. The leap from “the congregation acts as a body” to “the congregation holds final authority, and no office stands over it” is where the thesis outruns its evidence. The same texts read otherwise on four points:

(1) The referent of “the church” in Matthew 18:17 is not fixed as the assembled multitude. The Greek ekklēsia can denote the local body, but the earliest readers did not take it to lodge final power in the crowd: the Glossa (from Anselm) glosses it as the church acting through its officer — excommunication “inflicted by the mouth of the Church, that is, by the Priest, and when he excommunicates, the whole Church works with him.” Against a Jewish-tribunal background the “church” addressed may well be a representative body of elders, not the whole assembly — so the load-bearing proof-text under-determines who wields the keys.8

(2) At Corinth the apostle presides over the congregational act. Paul does not refer the case to the church’s sovereign judgment; he has already “judged” it and directs the assembly to ratify his sentence — “my spirit, with the power of our Lord Jesus Christ” present when they gather (1 Corinthians 5:3–5). Chrysostom’s own gloss makes the point: Paul “sets himself at their head” so that they “no otherwise cut off the offender than as if he were present.” This is congregational agency under apostolic headship, not congregational autonomy.9

(3) A trans-congregational oversight stands above the local body in the same New Testament. The Jerusalem decree binds churches other than the one that met, and issues in the name of “the apostles and elders” (Acts 15:22–29) — the whole church concurs, but the deciding organ is the apostolic-presbyteral council, and its writ runs beyond a single congregation. The apostles themselves, James at Jerusalem, and the delegates Timothy and Titus — left to appoint elders city by city and to receive charges against them (Titus 1:5; 1 Timothy 5:19–22) — all exercise real authority over local congregations. At Acts 6 the Twelve set the terms and lay on the hands; the multitude’s choice runs within that apostolic direction, which is equally compatible with elders presiding over the choice.10

(4) The texts are claimed, on identical grounds, by the presbyterian reading — and the congregational tradition’s own standards will not go the whole way. A plurality-of-elders polity reads these same passages as lodging final authority in the eldership, not the assembly, and the vocabulary settles the offices but not the locus of authority. Tellingly, the mature Congregational documents themselves stop short of pure assembly-rule: the Cambridge Platform balances the brethren’s “power of privilege” against the elders’ “power of office,” each “not any wise” prejudicing the other, and has the elders “pronounce sentence with consent of the church” — a distributed authority, not a sovereign congregation. When the thesis’s own charter documents distribute the power they are cited to concentrate, the strong claim is conceded from within.11

Assessment

Contested. Split the thesis and both halves come clear. Its corporate-discipline core — that the gathered congregation is a real agent in discipline and in the choice of officers — is well-grounded on the texts and granted even by the fathers who most exalt the ministry; a polity that denies the congregation any part in binding, loosing, and electing is refuted. But the defining congregational claim — that final authority over discipline, doctrine, and officers rests in the whole assembly, with no office standing above it — the same texts do not establish: Matthew 18’s “church” is not fixed as the multitude, Paul presides over the Corinthian sentence, a trans-congregational oversight (apostles, James, Timothy, Titus) stands over the local body, and the Jerusalem council’s writ runs beyond one congregation. Above all, the proof-texts under-determine the question they are asked to settle: the presbyterian reading claims every one of them for the eldership, and the congregational tradition’s own standards distribute the power between elders and brethren rather than vesting it in the assembly alone. Between a sound corporate-discipline reading and a strong final-authority claim the evidence will not carry, the congregational thesis as a total account of church government is genuinely disputed, with no clear winner. Trust the congregation’s real share in discipline and election; flag the inference that this share amounts to final, unchecked authority over a presiding ministry. This is the congregational leg of the three-way dispute over the form of government; its rivals are the presbyterian reading (which lodges final authority in the college of elders) and the episcopal reading, weighed in Is episcopacy of divine right? — the three best read together as competing total accounts of the same under-determining evidence.
