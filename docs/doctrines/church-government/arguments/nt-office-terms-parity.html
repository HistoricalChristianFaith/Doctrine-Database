Do the New Testament office-terms settle a biblical polity?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: sound — for the philological core it advocates: that in the New Testament presbyteros (“elder”) and episkopos (“bishop/overseer”) name one local office, appointed in plurality in each church, with deacons (diakonoi) as the settled second order — a two-office ministry, not yet a single bishop set over the presbyters. That reading is the near-consensus common ground on which the presbyterian and congregational cases both build. What does not follow from the vocabulary alone — and is the overreach to flag — is that the terms therefore fix a complete form of government, excluding any presiding office or settling where final authority sits; the words under-determine that further question. · advanced by Jerome, J. B. Lightfoot, and the modern scholarly consensus

Related arguments: the parity of bishop and presbyter — see also Were “bishop” and “presbyter” originally one office?

The claim

It is a fact now generally recognized by theologians of all shades of opinion, that in the language of the New Testament the same officer in the Church is called indifferently ‘bishop’ (episkopos) and ‘elder’ or ‘presbyter’ (presbyteros).

— J. B. Lightfoot, “The Christian Ministry,” in St. Paul’s Epistle to the Philippians (1868), full text.1 The argument runs in three steps. One office under two names: “elder” and “overseer” are used interchangeably of the same men. Held in plurality: each city has a college of these presbyter-bishops, not a lone ruler. With a second order: the deacons stand beside them, so the apostolic ministry is two offices, not three. The strong form presses this into a polity — the biblical norm is a plural eldership, and the monarchical bishop is a later growth. This page weighs the philological base and the plurality; how far it carries into a form of government is the point the assessment tests.

Who advances it

The lexical identity is old: expounding Titus verse by verse, Jerome (c. 393) found no bishop/presbyter distinction in the text and said so — his particular statement, and its Jerome-specific limits, are weighed on its own page in this same group. He was not alone among the ancients: John Chrysostom, Theodore of Mopsuestia, Theodoret of Cyrus, and Ambrosiaster all read the New Testament’s two words as one office. In the modern era J. B. Lightfoot’s dissertation “The Christian Ministry” (1868) set the thesis on its scholarly footing, and the terminological identity in the apostolic age is now conceded across confessions — not a sectarian Protestant reading. It is the shared foundation of the presbyterian and congregational cases against episcopacy iure divino; where those two then diverge — over where final authority rests — is argued on their own pages, not decided here.2

The evidence for

The two titles interchange within single passages. At Miletus Paul summons “the elders [presbyterous] of the church” of Ephesus and tells those same men that the Holy Ghost has made them “overseers” [episkopous] (Acts 20:17, 28). Charging Titus to “ordain elders [presbyterous] in every city… for a bishop [episkopon] must be blameless,” he moves between the two words for one man within three verses (Titus 1:5–7). Peter, a “fellow-elder” [sympresbyteros], bids the elders “feed the flock… taking the oversight” [episkopountes] — the elder’s task named by the bishop’s verb (1 Peter 5:1–2).3

The office is plural and the pairing is two-fold. Paul greets “the bishops and deacons” of Philippi (Philippians 1:1): the overseers are plural in one city and stand with deacons in a two-order pairing, no third “presbyter” rank between. Elders are appointed as a college “in every church” (Acts 14:23) and “in every city” (Titus 1:5). The second office is settled and named in its own right — the qualifications for “a bishop” and then, “likewise,” for “the deacons” (1 Timothy 3:1–13), with Phoebe called a “servant [diakonos] of the church” (Romans 16:1). Two offices, each in plurality; the monarchical bishop is nowhere named.4

The ancient exegetes read it the same way. Chrysostom, reaching “the bishops and deacons” of Philippi, asks: “What is this? were there several Bishops of one city? Certainly not; but he called the Presbyters so. For then they still interchanged the titles… both the Presbyters were of old called Bishops… and the Bishops Presbyters.” Theodoret, at the same verse, agrees: “He applies the term bishops to presbyters, for at that time they had both names… it was not possible for many bishops to be shepherds to one city.” The reading is not a modern retrojection but the plain sense the Greek fathers found in the text.5

The earliest sub-apostolic witness still shows it. 1 Clement (c. 96) uses “bishop” and “presbyter” of the same Corinthian officers — the strife is over “the office of the episcopate,” yet the deposed men are “those presbyters” whom it is sin to remove (ch. 44) — and the Didache tells the congregation to “appoint… bishops and deacons” (ch. 15), the same two-order, plural, local ministry. The New Testament usage is still in force in the oldest Christian writing outside it.6

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The philological core is sound and mainstream: in the New Testament and the earliest church episkopos and presbyteros denote one local office, held by a college, with deacons as the second order. The fixed, monarchical bishop — one man set over the presbyters of a city — is not visible in the New Testament and is, at least in its terminology and settled rank, a later development. So far the argument carries.

What is overstated (fold-in defeaters). Three steps run past what the vocabulary can prove — and they are the reason a term-count does not by itself yield a polity:

(1) Interchange of the two local titles does not exclude a distinct presiding or trans-congregational oversight. The same New Testament shows a tier above the local presbyter-bishops: the apostles themselves; James presiding at Jerusalem (Acts 15:13; Galatians 2:9); and delegates like Timothy and Titus, personally empowered to appoint presbyters city by city, to receive charges against them, and to lay on hands (Titus 1:5; 1 Timothy 5:19–22). One can grant “two names, one office” at the congregational level and still hold that a higher oversight — the seed later gathered into the monarchical bishop — was exercised from the first.7

(2) Plurality is not strict parity of function. Within the one college the New Testament already differentiates: “Let the elders that rule well be counted worthy of double honour, especially they who labour in the word and doctrine” (1 Timothy 5:17) — ruling and teaching elders are distinguished, and a president among equals is not ruled out. A plural eldership is compatible with one of its number presiding; the terms do not forbid it.8

(3) The vocabulary under-determines the locus of authority — which is why rival polities both claim it. That there are two offices, not three, tells us the count of offices; it does not tell us where final authority sits. The presbyterian reading (rule by a college of elders, gathered in graded courts) and the congregational reading (final authority in the gathered local body) rest on these same verses and divide over exactly the question the office-terms leave open. To read a settled form of government straight off the word-usage is to ask the lexicon to answer a question it does not address; those two cases are weighed on their own pages, not here.9

Assessment

Sound — for the interpretation this page advocates: that in the New Testament presbyteros and episkopos name one local office, appointed in plurality, with deacons as the second order. The interchange within single passages (Acts 20:17, 28; Titus 1:5–7), the plural bishops of Philippi, the elders “in every city,” and the concurrence of Chrysostom, Theodoret, Jerome, 1 Clement, and modern scholarship put the two-office core beyond serious doubt. Trust that. Flag the further step so often hung on it — that the vocabulary therefore fixes a complete polity, excluding any presiding office and settling where authority rests: a distinct trans-congregational oversight (apostles, James, Timothy, Titus) stands above the local presbyter-bishops in the same New Testament, plurality is not strict parity (1 Timothy 5:17), and the presbyterian and congregational readings build on these identical verses and divide over what the terms leave open. The office-terms settle the offices; the form of government they under-determine — weighed separately in this doctrine’s Arguments, and whether the monarchical episcopate is nonetheless apostolic under the emergence of the episcopate.
