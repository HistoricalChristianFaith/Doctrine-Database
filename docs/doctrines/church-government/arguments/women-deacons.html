Did the early church ordain women to the diaconate?

Disputed reading · on The Government of the Church

The passage

“I commend to you Phoebe our sister, who is in the ministry of the Church which is at Cenchreae…” This passage too teaches with apostolic authority that women also are appointed in the ministry of the Church… this passage teaches two things equally: both that… women are held as ministers in the Church, and that such women ought to be taken into the ministry as have assisted many.

— Origen of Alexandria, Commentary on the Epistle to the Romans, on Romans 16:1–2 (c. 246; Rufinus’s Latin), full text.1

A woman shall not receive the laying on of hands as a deaconess under forty years of age, and then only after searching examination.

— Council of Chalcedon (451), canon 15.2

The question

Paul calls Phoebe a diakonos of the church at Cenchreae (Romans 16:1), and from the third and fourth centuries Eastern church-orders and councils regulate an order of deaconesses and a laying-on of hands for them. Two things are disputed: whether the apostolic church had a women’s diaconal office at all, and whether the later deaconess received the same ordination to the diaconate as a male deacon or was instituted to a distinct ministry. The International Theological Commission (2002) put the second question as: “Should the imposition of hands on deaconesses be considered the same as that on deacons, or is it rather on the same level as the imposition of hands on sub-deacons and lectors?”3 The answer bears on how the early ministry is described, and on present-day Catholic, Orthodox and Protestant decisions about the order.

No women’s diaconate in the apostolic order

On this reading the apostles appointed men as deacons, the women of the New Testament served in other recognized ways, and the deaconess order is a later development.

Held by: Ambrosiaster (Latin commentator, Commentary on 1 Timothy, c. 380); the Orthodox Presbyterian Church’s Committee on Women in Church Office (Reformed, report to the 55th General Assembly, 1988).4

Ambrosiaster reads the “women” of 1 Timothy 3:11 as a charge that the women of the church be “without crime, so that the Church of God may be clean”. The Cataphrygians, he writes, “maintain with vain presumption that they too ought to be ordained deaconesses; though they know that the apostles chose seven deacons. Was no woman found suitable then, when we read that there were holy women among the eleven apostles?” The apostolic choice of the Seven is for him the pattern of the diaconate, and the deaconess belongs to the Montanist churches.5 The Orthodox Presbyterian committee builds the case from the New Testament’s own usage. Of some thirty New Testament uses of diakonos it finds the official sense “clearly warranted” in only three (Philippians 1:1; 1 Timothy 3:8, 12), and for Phoebe “there is nothing in the passage that demands an official sense”. In 1 Timothy 3:11 the “women” stand inside a list of qualifications for male deacons; Paul could have written tas diakonous had he meant a separate class; and the ordered women workers of the letter are the enrolled widows of 5:9–16, with qualities like those asked of older women in Titus 2:3. The committee holds that “the most likely antecedent to the eventually emergent office of deaconess is the order of widows”, and it records the later Western councils that replaced the ordination of deaconesses with a benediction (Orange, 441) or forbade it (Epaon, 517; Orleans, 533). It concludes that the New Testament data “fix the coordinates of a trajectory pointing to the conclusion that women are not to be ordained as deacons. Nor does the New Testament make provision for a separate office of deaconess in parallel with the elders and deacons.”6 On this reading the women’s ministry the apostles ordered is the widows’ service of prayer and care, and the deaconess of the church-orders grew from it in the centuries after them.

A ministry of women distinct from the diaconal order

On this reading the ancient deaconess held a real, recognized ministry, conferred by the bishop, that was not the same order as the diaconate conferred on men.

Held by: Thomas Aquinas (Catholic, Commentary on 1 Timothy, before 1274); Aimé Georges Martimort (Catholic, Les diaconesses, 1982) ⚠ unverified; the International Theological Commission (Catholic, 2002); the Study Commission on the Women’s Diaconate under Cardinal Giuseppe Petrocchi (Catholic, report published 2025).7

Aquinas reads 1 Timothy 3:11 of deacons’ wives and adds that “in the law some women are sometimes called deaconesses, not because they have this order, but by reason of some other office they render the Church; as in Greek a minister is called a deacon.”8 The International Theological Commission reads Phoebe’s title as “servant in a very general sense”: “What seems clear is that Phoebe exercised a recognised service in the community of Cenchreae, subordinate to the ministry of the Apostle.” It traces the order to the Syrian Didascalia (c. 240), where deaconesses serve “for the service of women” and “There is no reference to the ordination of these ministers”, and it cites Epiphanius (c. 375): “There is certainly in the Church the order of deaconesses, but this does not exist to exercise the functions of a priest, nor are they to have any undertaking committed to them, but for the decency of the feminine sex at the time of baptism.”9 The Apostolic Constitutions that give the deaconess a hand-laying also set her apart: “A deaconess does not bless, nor perform anything belonging to the office of presbyters or deacons, but only is to keep the doors, and to minister to the presbyters in the baptizing of women, on account of decency”; she and the minor clergy “are the ministers to the deacons”, and in the communion and stipend orders she is listed after the deacons.10 The Commission notes that this rite “is practically the only witness” to an imposition of hands comparable to that of deacons, that the later Byzantine ordinations “were intended mainly for the superiors of monasteries of women”, and that “in the West there is no trace of any deaconesses for the first five centuries”. Its finding is that the deaconesses “were not purely and simply equivalent to the deacons”. The Petrocchi commission found in 2021 that the Church has recognized “the title of deacon/deaconess with reference to women, though attributing to it no univocal meaning”.11 On this reading the one name “deaconess” covered a real and varied ministry of women — at women’s baptisms, at the doors, in the care of women and later at the head of women’s monasteries — exercised alongside the three orders of bishop, presbyter and deacon.

Women ordained to the diaconate

On this reading women were ordained deacons by the bishop’s laying-on of hands, in a rite paralleling the ordination of men, with Phoebe as the order’s New Testament instance.

Held by: Evangelos Theodorou (Orthodox, University of Athens, 1954); Phyllis Zagano (Catholic, Hofstra University, 2003); Kevin Madigan and Carolyn Osiek (church historians, Ordained Women in the Early Church, 2005).12

Paul gives Phoebe the title diakonos and ties it to a particular church. Chrysostom reads it as rank: “he has added her rank, by mentioning her being ‘deaconess’”; Theodoret says the church at Cenchreae had “a woman acting as deaconess”; Theophylact says Paul honours her “by indicating her office, naming her a deaconess.”13 Of 1 Timothy 3:11 Chrysostom writes: “He is speaking of those who hold the rank of Deaconesses”, and Oecumenius and Theophylact agree.14 On these two verses the Lambeth bishops held that “for Women the Order of Deaconesses is the one and only Order of the Ministry which has the stamp of Apostolic approval”.15 About 112 Pliny reports two women “who were called deaconesses” (ministrae).16 The Apostolic Constitutions (c. 380) direct: “O bishop, thou shalt lay thy hands upon her in the presence of the presbytery, and of the deacons and deaconesses”, praying for her “who is to be ordained to the office of a deaconess”. Nicaea’s canon 19 counts the Paulianist deaconesses among the laity “since they have no imposition of hands”, and Chalcedon’s canon 15 names the deaconess’s laying-on of hands with cheirotonia, the term it uses of the clergy.17 Theodorou’s 1954 study, as summarized by a group of Orthodox liturgists in 2025, found the Byzantine rite given during the Divine Liturgy “at the same place in the service as the male deacon was ordained”, with two prayers and an invocation of “divine grace”, at the altar, with the orarion and communion at the altar: “All of these are marks of the higher order of clergy.”18 Zagano adds the eighth-century Barberini 336 rite, ordaining women “by the bishop within the sanctuary”, and Madigan and Osiek gather the literary, canonical and inscriptional record of women deacons East and West.19 Pelagius records that “even today, women deaconesses in the East are known to minister to their own sex in baptism or even in the ministry of the Word.”20 On this reading one diaconal ordination, given by the bishop at the altar, was conferred on women and men, and its duties differed with the people each served.
