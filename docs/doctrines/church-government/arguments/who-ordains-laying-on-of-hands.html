Who confers ministerial office by the laying on of hands?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: sound — for the interpretation this page advocates: that in the New Testament and the earliest church the conferral of ministerial office by the laying on of hands is an act of the standing ministry, distinct from the congregation’s election of the man — the people (may) choose, but those already in office ordain. The two hand-laying texts most pressed in the debate (1 Timothy 4:14, “the presbytery”; 2 Timothy 1:6, “my hands”) most plausibly describe one ordination from two sides, not two rival ordainers. What this page does not settle — and deliberately routes elsewhere — is the finer dispute over which minister ordains: a bishop as a distinct third order, or a presbytery of equals. · advanced by Hippolytus of Rome and Cyprian of Carthage

Related arguments: the making of a minister — see also Were ministers made by the people’s election, or by ordained conferral?

The claim

And the saying pleased the whole multitude: and they chose Stephen… whom they set before the apostles: and when they had prayed, they laid their hands on them.

— Acts 6:5–6 (KJV), Acts 6.1 The reading this page weighs takes the appointment of the Seven as the pattern-case of a two-act procedure that recurs across the New Testament and hardens in the church-order manuals: the community chooses (“they chose Stephen… the whole multitude”), and the ministry ordains (“the apostles… laid their hands on them”). On this reading, the laying on of hands that confers office is never in the New Testament a congregational act: it is done by an apostle (2 Timothy 1:6), by a college of elders with him (1 Timothy 4:14), by the apostles who ordained the Seven the people had elected (Acts 6:6), and by Paul and Barnabas who “ordained… elders in every church” (Acts 14:23) — a ministerial act, distinct from, and following upon, the people’s choice.

Who advances it

The distinction is the common property of the catholic and the Reformed traditions alike: episcopalians, presbyterians, and the Reformed all agree that ordination is an act of the existing ministry and not of the lay congregation — they divide only over which minister performs it. It is not a later imposition on the sources but their own procedure. The earliest ordination manual, the Apostolic Tradition (c. 215), states it as a rubric — a bishop is “chosen by all the people,” but “the bishops shall lay their hands upon him, and the presbytery shall stand by in silence” — and grades every order by its part in the rite: a presbyter “has no power to give… a presbyter does not ordain the clergy,” and a deacon “the bishop alone shall make.”2 Cyprian (c. 250s) enacts the same split from the other side: the people “have the power either of choosing worthy priests, or of rejecting unworthy ones,” yet a valid ordination still requires “the sentence of the bishops who had assembled” — the people’s suffrage and the bishops’ ordaining act together.3 The fuller fourth-century rites of the Apostolic Constitutions VIII regulate it in canon-law form.

The evidence for

The New Testament keeps election and ordination as two acts. In Acts 6 the multitude chooses the Seven and the apostles lay hands on them; the choosing is the people’s, the laying-on the apostles’. Paul reminds Timothy of “the gift… given thee… with the laying on of the hands of the presbytery” (1 Timothy 4:14) and of the gift “which is in thee by the putting on of my hands” (2 Timothy 1:6); either way the hands are a minister’s. Paul and Barnabas “ordained them elders in every church” (Acts 14:23), and Titus is left in Crete precisely so that he might “ordain elders in every city” (Titus 1:5) — the ordaining is delegated to an apostolic man, not referred back to each congregation. And the solemn warning “lay hands suddenly on no man” (1 Timothy 5:22) is addressed to Timothy as the ordainer, holding him answerable for whom he admits.1

The Fathers read the hand-laying texts as ministerial ordination, not congregational vote. Chrysostom takes the “presbytery” of 1 Timothy 4:14 to mean the ordaining ministry, not a lay assembly: “He speaks not here of Presbyters, but of Bishops. For Presbyters cannot be supposed to have ordained a Bishop”; Theophylact echoes it — “That is, of bishops. For it was not priests who ordained a bishop” — and Theodore of Mopsuestia reads “the council of presbyters” as the assembled ordaining bishops.4 Whatever one makes of that particular gloss (see below), it shows the ancient exegetes reading the passage as an act performed by ministers upon a candidate — never as the congregation conferring office on itself.

The church-order manuals reserve the ordaining act to the ministry, and grade it. The Apostolic Tradition distinguishes the people’s choosing from the bishops’ laying-on of hands, and rules that a presbyter “does not ordain the clergy.” The Apostolic Constitutions VIII make it canon law — “Let a bishop be ordained by three or two bishops” (VIII.27) — and draw the line in a single clause: “A bishop… lays on hands, ordains…. A presbyter… lays on hands, but does not ordain” (VIII.28).5 The third-century Syrian Didascalia already makes the bishop the near-monarchical ordainer of his church. Across the whole early literature the conferral of office is a rite the ministry performs; the congregation’s role, where it has one, is to elect and to consent.

Adversarial assessment

What holds. That ordination is a ministerial act, distinct from the people’s election, is well-grounded and close to a cross-confessional consensus. The New Testament never presents the laying on of hands that confers office as a congregational deed; it is always an apostle’s, an apostolic delegate’s, or a college of elders’ act, and the earliest manuals regulate it as such. The two-act pattern — the community chooses, the ministry ordains — is visible already in Acts 6 and is exactly what Hippolytus and Cyprian codify.

What is overstated or imprecise, on more than one side (fold-in defeaters).

(1) The congregationalist reading of cheirotoneō proves less than it is asked to. That the Seven were “chosen” by the multitude (Acts 6:5), that the Didache tells a church to “appoint [cheirotonēsate] for yourselves bishops and deacons” (ch. 15), and that Paul and Barnabas “cheirotonēsantes” elders (Acts 14:23) do show a real congregational stake in selecting ministers — a genuine check the episcopal tradition too often forgot. But cheirotoneō by New Testament usage means “appoint” (its literal “stretch out the hand, vote” sense is not required), and in Acts 14:23 its subject is Paul and Barnabas, not the crowd; Chrysostom reads that verse as an ordination done with prayer and fasting, not a show of hands.4 So election by the people is well attested, but the texts do not make the congregation’s vote the ordaining act; even in the Didache someone must still be appointed, and Acts 6 has the apostles, not the assembly, lay on hands.

(2) The two hand-laying texts are most likely one ordination from two sides, not two rival ordainers. 1 Timothy 4:14 credits “the presbytery” and 2 Timothy 1:6 credits “my hands” — and the polity debate has long pulled them apart, the presbyterian pressing the first (a presbytery ordains), the episcopalian the second (the apostle ordains, the presbytery assisting). The economical reading takes them as one event described twice: Paul presiding and the college of elders associating itself — precisely the shape Hippolytus later prescribes, where the bishop lays on hands “while the presbyters touch him.” On that reading neither “presbytery alone” nor “apostle alone” is proven, and Chrysostom’s gloss that “the presbytery” simply means bishops is itself an interpretive move, not a demonstration. So the texts establish that ordination is ministerial; they do not, by themselves, fix the ordainer’s rank.

(3) Acts 13:3 is a commissioning, not an ordination to office. When the Antioch church “laid their hands” on Barnabas and Saul, the two were already prophets and teachers being sent to a particular work (Acts 13:1–3); the gesture blesses and dispatches a mission rather than conferring an office not yet held. It is therefore weak evidence either for congregational ordination or for any particular ordainer, and is best not pressed for the conferral question at all.

Where that leaves it. The advocated claim — conferral of office by laying on of hands is a ministerial act, distinct from the people’s election — stands. The step this page will not take is from that claim to a complete polity: whether the ordainer must be a bishop as a distinct third order (so that a presbytery cannot validly ordain), or whether bishop and presbyter are one order and a presbytery suffices, is the separate and genuinely contested question weighed under the parity of bishop and presbyter and the emergence of the episcopate. Jerome frames the seam exactly: he holds “the presbyter is the same as the bishop,” yet still concedes the one reserved episcopal act — “what function, excepting ordination, belongs to a bishop that does not also belong to a presbyter?”6 That Jerome can affirm parity and still reserve ordination to the bishop shows why the conferral question and the parity question are distinct: this page settles the first, and hands the second to those two arguments.

Assessment

Sound — for the interpretation this page advocates: that the conferral of ministerial office by the laying on of hands is an act of the standing ministry, distinct from the congregation’s election, and that the two New Testament hand-laying texts describe one such ministerial ordination rather than two rival ordainers. Trust that core (Acts 6:5–6; 1 Timothy 4:14 with 2 Timothy 1:6; Acts 14:23; Titus 1:5; the church-order manuals from Hippolytus to the Apostolic Constitutions). Flag three overreaches: that the congregation’s cheirotoneō makes its vote the ordaining act (it marks election, not ordination); that either 1 Timothy 4:14 or 2 Timothy 1:6 alone fixes the ordainer’s rank (they most likely report one event from two sides); and that Acts 13:3 bears on the conferral of office at all (it commissions men already in office). The remaining live dispute — which minister ordains, a bishop as a distinct order or a presbytery of equals — is not adjudicated here but on the parity of bishop and presbyter and the emergence of the episcopate.
