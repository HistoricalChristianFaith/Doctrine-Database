Was a minister made by the people’s election, or by ordained conferral?

Disputed reading · on The Government of the Church · witnesses: the Didache, Cyprian of Carthage, Hippolytus of Rome

The passage

And the saying pleased the whole multitude: and they chose Stephen… whom they set before the apostles: and when they had prayed, they laid their hands on them.

— Acts 6:5–6 (KJV).1

And when they had ordained them elders in every church, and had prayed with fasting, they commended them to the Lord, on whom they believed.

— Acts 14:23 (KJV); the verb rendered “ordained” is cheirotonēsantes.2

Appoint, therefore, for yourselves, bishops and deacons worthy of the Lord, men meek, and not lovers of money, and truthful and proved; for they also render to you the service of prophets and teachers.

— Didache 15.1 (the verb rendered “appoint” is cheirotonēsate).3

The question

The New Testament and the early church orders describe two acts in the making of a minister: a choice in which the congregation takes part, and a setting apart with prayer and the laying on of hands. The communions differ over which act makes the minister, and so over whether the power to make ministers lies with the congregation, a presbytery or the bishops; the verb cheirotoneō in Acts 14:23 and the Didache, which can mean “elect by show of hands” or “appoint”, is read both ways. David F. Wright (1985) wrote that “Most of the limited New Testament texts relevant to the question are susceptible of divergent interpretations”.4

Office conferred by ordination from those already ordained, with the people’s testimony and consent

On this reading the people may choose and attest a candidate, but the office is conferred by the ordination that the apostles and their successors perform.

Held by: John Chrysostom (Greek father, Homilies on Acts, c. 400); the Council of Trent (Catholic, Session 23, 1563); Hubert Ahaus (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1911); Kallistos Ware (Eastern Orthodox, The Orthodox Church).5

The case begins from Acts 6, where the choosing and the ordaining are done by different hands. Chrysostom, who stresses that “again it is the people that choose”, also writes: “The fixing the number, and the ordaining them… rested with them: but the choice of the men they make over to the people”; the apostles “might indeed themselves have made the election… but nevertheless, they desire the testimony of the people”.6 In Acts 14:23 the subject of cheirotonēsantes is Paul and Barnabas; Chrysostom reads the verse as ordinations “accompanied with fastings”, and Theophylact infers that “Paul and Barnabas held the rank of bishop, because they ordained not only deacons but also elders”.7 Titus is left in Crete to “ordain elders in every city”, and Timothy is told to “Lay hands suddenly on no man”.8 Matthias is chosen by lot, which Chrysostom reads as God's designation: “They said not, Choose, but, Show the chosen one”.9 The church orders keep the two acts apart: in the Apostolic Tradition a bishop is “chosen by all the people”, but “the bishops shall lay their hands upon him”, and the Apostolic Constitutions rule that a presbyter “lays on hands, but does not ordain”.10 Cyprian joins “the suffrage of the whole brotherhood” to “the sentence of the bishops who had assembled”, by which “the episcopate was conferred upon him”.11 Ahaus summarizes the early practice: “Though clergy and laity had a voice in the election of the candidates, the ultimate and definite determination rested with the bishops”, the election being “in the nature of a testimony of fitness”.12 Trent teaches that in ordination “neither the consent, nor vocation, nor authority, whether of the people, or of any civil power or magistrate whatsoever, is required in such wise as that, without this, the ordination is invalid”.13 Ware states the Orthodox form: “Only a bishop has power to ordain”, and “An ordination, while performed by the bishop, also requires the consent of the whole people of God”, voiced in the acclamation “Axios!” (“He is worthy!”).14 On this reading the people's voice is the church's witness to a candidate's fitness, and office is given by those who have themselves received it.

The church’s call and election make the minister; ordination confirms and installs

On this reading the right to call and elect ministers belongs to the church, and ordination publicly confirms the one it has chosen and puts him into his office.

Held by: the Lutheran theologians assembled at Smalcald (Treatise on the Power and Primacy of the Pope, 1537); the Cambridge Platform (New England Congregational, 1648); Edwin Hatch (Anglican historian, Bampton Lectures, 1880), on the appointive character of early ordination.15

The case starts from the vocabulary. The Didache tells a congregation to “Appoint, therefore, for yourselves, bishops and deacons” with cheirotonēsate, the verb of voting by a show of hands;3 in 2 Corinthians 8:19 the same verb names a brother “chosen of the churches”, which Chrysostom glosses “there was a vote of the whole people taken”.16 In Acts 6 the initiative runs through the assembly: “it is the people that draw them, not the Apostles that lead them”.6 Cyprian holds that the people “have the power either of choosing worthy priests, or of rejecting unworthy ones”.11 The Smalcald theologians draw the principle: “it is necessary for the Church to retain the authority to call, elect, and ordain ministers”, and the church's custom bears it out: “For formerly the people elected pastors and bishops”, a neighbouring bishop then confirming the one elected by the laying on of hands, “and ordination was nothing else than such a ratification”.17 The Cambridge Platform states it as polity: “This ordination we account nothing else, but the solemn putting of a man into his place and office in the church, whereunto he had right before by election”; “Ordination doth not constitute an officer, nor give him the essentials of his office”, for “Paul and Barnabas were officers before that imposition of hands, Acts, xiii. 3”, and where a church has no elders, imposition of hands “may be performed by some of the brethren orderly chosen by the church thereunto”.18 Hatch finds the same pattern in the early record: the elements of appointment to church office were “nomination, election, approval, and the declaration of election by a competent officer”, the same as for civil office, so that “ordination meant appointment and admission to office”; the imposition of hands “was not a universal, and that consequently it could not have been a necessary, element in ordination”.19 On this reading the church that calls is the bearer of the ministry Christ gave it, and ordination is the solemn public form of the call it has made.

A lawful call joins the people’s election to ordination by the pastors

On this reading the people's election and the pastors' or elders' ordination are both required parts of one lawful call, and neither is sufficient without the other.

Held by: John Calvin (Reformed, Institutes, 1559); the Westminster Form of Presbyterial Church-Government (Presbyterian, 1645); John Owen (Congregational, The True Nature of a Gospel Church, 1689); the Second London Confession (Particular Baptist, 1689).20

Calvin reads Acts 14:23 as an election presided over by the apostles: “They therefore selected (creabant) two; but the whole body, as was the custom of the Greeks in elections, declared by a show of hands which of the two they wished to have”. Ministers are therefore “legitimately called according to the word of God, when those who may have seemed fit are elected on the consent and approbation of the people”, with other pastors presiding “lest any error should be committed by the general body”. Ordination takes “the last place in the call”: the apostles' uniform use of the laying on of hands “ought to be regarded by us in the light of a precept”, and “it was not the whole people, but only pastors, who laid hands on ministers”.21 The Westminster Form joins the two acts in procedure: members of the congregation appear before the presbytery “to give their consent and approbation to such a man to be their minister”, and “if… the people give their consent, then the presbytery shall proceed to ordination”, every minister being ordained “by imposition of hands, and prayer, with fasting, by those preaching presbyters to whom it doth belong”.22 Owen makes the two parts explicit: “The call of persons unto the pastoral office in the church consists of two parts, — first, Election; secondly, Ordination”; the election belongs “unto the body of the people, and their authoritative designation or ordination unto the elders”. The election “doth not actually and immediately instate the person chosen in the office”, and of the two acts he writes that “neither of them without the other hath any at all”.23 The Second London Confession sets out the same order as “The way appointed by Christ”: a minister is “chosen thereunto by the common suffrage of the church itself; and solemnly set apart by fasting and prayer, with imposition of hands of the eldership of the church, if there be any before constituted therein”.24 On this reading Acts 6 and Acts 14 give the pattern for every age: the people choose, those already in office ordain, and the call is complete when both have acted.
