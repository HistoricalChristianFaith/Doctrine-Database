Were ministers made by the people’s election, or by ordained conferral?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — a real strand of congregational participation and consent in the making of ministers is well-attested and well-grounded (the people genuinely chose, and cheirotoneō genuinely carries an election sense); but the strong claim this page weighs — that popular election was the decisive, constitutive act, ordination merely ratifying a choice the church had already made — outruns the same texts, hanging heavily on the disputed sense of cheirotoneō and on reading consent as decisive election. Between a sound participation core and a decisive-election claim the evidence will not carry, genuinely disputed (contested). · advanced by the Didache and Cyprian of Carthage (ancient), and by Calvin and the Separatist tradition (early modern)

Related arguments: the making of a minister — see also Who confers ministerial office by the laying on of hands?

The claim

We see, then, that ministers are legitimately called according to the word of God, when those who may have seemed fit are elected on the consent and approbation of the people. Other pastors, however, ought to preside over the election, lest any error should be committed by the general body either through levity, or bad passion, or tumult.

— John Calvin, Institutes of the Christian Religion IV.3.15, CCEL.1 The interpretation this page weighs is that in the New Testament, and in the earliest practice, a minister was made by the church’s own choice — the people elect the man, and the ordination that follows ratifies and installs a decision already taken — against the rival reading that office is conferred from above by an ordaining authority (a bishop, or a college of elders), the people merely nominating, consenting, or receiving. The two readings agree that ordination is an act of the standing ministry (that is settled on the companion who-ordains page); they divide over which act — the people’s election or the ministry’s conferral — is the constitutive one.

Who advances it

The popular-election reading has a genuine ancient root and a sharpened Reformation form. In the sub-apostolic church the Didache tells a congregation to “elect [cheirotonēsate] for yourselves bishops and deacons” (ch. 15), and Cyprian, a century and a half later, defends the people’s “power either of choosing worthy priests, or of rejecting unworthy ones” as a “divine tradition and apostolic observance” (Epistle 67).2 The Reformers pressed these data into a principle: Calvin holds that “no man is legitimately called… without the consent and approbation of the people,” reads the elders “ordained” in Acts 14:23 as chosen “by suffrage” (the Greek show of hands), and cites Matthias and the Seven as elected “at the sight and approval of the people”; and the Separatist and Congregationalist tradition after him — Robert Browne, the Cambridge Platform, John Owen — made the congregation’s call the effectual act, ordination being only its solemn recognition.1 The reading is not a Reformation novelty imposed on the sources but a genuine strand within them; the question is how far that strand reaches.

The evidence for

The New Testament gives the people a real part in choosing ministers. When the Seven are appointed the initiative and the choice run through the assembly: the apostles bid the multitude “look ye out among you seven men of honest report” (Acts 6:3), “and the saying pleased the whole multitude: and they chose Stephen” (Acts 6:5). Chrysostom presses the point twice over — “again it is the people that choose,” and, at the presentation, “it is the people that draw them, not the Apostles that lead them” — and reads the whole procedure as a deliberate handing of the decision to the congregation: “the choice of the men they make over to the people, that they might not seem to act from favor.”3

The appointing vocabulary can carry a genuine election sense. The verb behind “appoint” in Acts 14:23 and the Didache, cheirotoneō, literally means “to stretch out the hand” — to vote by a show of hands — and the New Testament itself uses it plainly of a church election: the brother sent with Titus was “chosen [cheirotonētheis] of the churches to travel with us” (2 Corinthians 8:19), which Chrysostom glosses without hesitation, “he was chosen by the churches, and there was a vote of the whole people taken.”4 That the same verb names the appointment of elders (Acts 14:23) and of the Didache’s bishops and deacons is the lexical backbone of the reading: where the church “stretches out its hand,” it elects.

The Fathers preserved the people’s suffrage well into the settled episcopate. The earliest ordination manual keeps the people’s choice as the first act — a bishop is “chosen by all the people” (Apostolic Tradition 2) — and Cyprian makes it a matter of “divine authority” that “the priest should be chosen in the presence of the people… approved worthy and suitable by public judgment and testimony,” the people retaining the power to reject an unworthy candidate (Epistle 67; the same suffrage named to Cornelius in Epistle 54).2 So a congregational voice in the making of ministers is not a marginal or short-lived custom but a well-documented and “almost universal” practice of the ante-Nicene church.

Adversarial assessment

What holds. That the people had a real, active share in choosing their ministers is well-grounded and should not be soft-pedalled: Acts 6 puts the choice of the Seven in the congregation’s hands, cheirotoneō demonstrably carries an election sense in the New Testament’s own usage (2 Corinthians 8:19), and the Fathers guarded the people’s suffrage and their veto over an unworthy man for centuries. Any picture of early ministry as office simply handed down from above upon a passive laity is too thin for the sources — a genuine check the later church too often forgot, and the Reformers rightly recovered.

What is overstated (fold-in defeaters).

(1) The vocabulary will not bear the weight of decisive election. That cheirotoneō can mean “elect” (2 Corinthians 8:19) does not fix its force in Acts 14:23, where by New Testament usage it has largely bleached to “appoint” and where its grammatical subject is Paul and Barnabas, not the congregation: they “cheirotonēsantes… elders in every church.” Chrysostom reads that verse as an ordination done “with prayer and fasting,” and Theophylact takes it to prove that “Paul and Barnabas held the rank of bishop, because they ordained” — neither hears a show of hands.5 Calvin’s inference from the word to a congregational suffrage in Acts 14:23 is exactly the step the grammar resists; the verb marks that someone is appointed, not that the assembly’s vote is the appointing act. (The mirror overreach — that cheirotoneō proves the congregation performs the ordaining — is rated from the other side on the who-ordains page.)

(2) Matthias is chosen by lot, not by vote. The one apostolic appointment narrated at length runs against the model: the assembly nominates two, but the choice between them is referred to God and settled by lot — “they gave forth their lots; and the lot fell upon Matthias” (Acts 1:26). Chrysostom is emphatic that the electing is God’s, not the people’s: they prayed “Show whether of these two thou hast chosen… not, Choose, but, Show the chosen one,” for “it is He who has made the choice.”6 Calvin cites Matthias for popular election, but the text makes the people’s part nomination and the decisive act a divine designation — the opposite of election ratified by ordination.

(3) Even in Acts 6 the constitutive acts stay with the ministry. Chrysostom, who insists the people choose, is equally clear on what they do not do: “The fixing the number, and the ordaining them… rested with them [the apostles]: but the choice of the men they make over to the people.” The apostles set the criteria (“full of the Holy Ghost and wisdom”), fixed the number at seven, and performed the ordaining laying-on of hands; and the people’s role, on Chrysostom’s reading, is the testimony the apostles chose to seek — “they might indeed themselves have made the election… but nevertheless they desire the testimony of the people” — a granted participation, not an inherent decisive right.3 So the strongest election text keeps conferral, criteria, and number in the ministry’s hands.

(4) An ordaining authority stands over the local choice. Titus is left in Crete precisely so that he might “ordain elders in every city” (Titus 1:5), and Timothy is charged “lay hands suddenly on no man” (1 Timothy 5:22) — each held answerable, as an apostolic delegate, for whom he admits. The making of ministers in the Pastoral Epistles is entrusted to a trans-congregational officer, not referred back to each assembly for a decisive vote.7

(5) Consent is not the same as decisive election — and the Fathers couple the two. Cyprian, the richest witness to the people’s power, does not make their suffrage self-sufficient: the bishop is chosen “by the suffrage of the whole brotherhood, and by the sentence of the bishops who had assembled… [so that] the episcopate was conferred” — the people’s consent and the provincial college’s act together, the verb of conferral reserved to the bishops.2 Hippolytus keeps the same order: chosen by all the people, but the bishops lay on hands. What the record firmly attests, then, is congregational participation and consent — a datum every conferral-from-above model can absorb (each of them gives the people a real voice). The distinctively congregational thesis — that election is the effectual act and ordination its mere ratification — is precisely what these coupled procedures leave open.

Where that leaves it. The participation strand is sound on its own; the strong thesis — decisive popular election, ordination ratifying — is not carried by texts that keep the choosing, the criteria, and the conferral in different hands, and that name Matthias’s appointment a divine lot. The reading is genuinely disputed, and its strength depends on a lexical claim (cheirotoneō as suffrage in Acts 14:23) that the grammar and the ancient exegetes resist. The nearer questions this page does not settle — whether the ordaining act is a congregational or a ministerial deed (it is ministerial: who ordains?), and whether the gathered congregation holds final authority in its whole life (congregational authority) — are weighed on their own pages; this one rates only the election-versus-conferral seam in the making of a minister.

Assessment

Contested — for the interpretation this page weighs: that a minister is made by the people’s election, ordination merely ratifying a decision already taken. Trust the participation core: the congregation genuinely shared in choosing ministers (Acts 6:3–5; the people’s suffrage in Cyprian and the Apostolic Tradition), and cheirotoneō demonstrably carries an election sense in the New Testament’s own usage (2 Corinthians 8:19) — a real congregational voice the later church often forgot. Flag the overreach: that popular election was the decisive, constitutive act. It hangs on reading cheirotoneō as a congregational vote in Acts 14:23 (where its subject is Paul and Barnabas, and the ancient exegetes hear an ordination), it is embarrassed by Matthias’s choice-by-lot, and it must treat the people’s testimony and consent — which the sources everywhere couple with the ministry’s conferral (Acts 6’s apostles “ordaining them”; Cyprian’s “sentence of the bishops”; Titus “to ordain elders”) — as if it were decisive election. Between a well-grounded participation strand and a decisive-election claim the same under-determining texts will not carry, no clear winner.
