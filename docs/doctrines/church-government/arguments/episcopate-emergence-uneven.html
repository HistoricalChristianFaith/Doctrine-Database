Was the single-bishop episcopate in place everywhere by c. 110, or did it emerge unevenly?

Disputed reading · on The Government of the Church · witnesses: Ignatius of Antioch, Polycarp of Smyrna, the Shepherd of Hermas, Irenaeus of Lyons

Related readings: Is the threefold ministry apostolic in origin, or a second-century development? · Do Ignatius's letters describe an established episcopate, or urge a new one?

The passage

You will write therefore two books, and you will send the one to Clemens and the other to Grapte. And Clemens will send his to foreign countries, for permission has been granted to him to do so. And Grapte will admonish the widows and the orphans. But you will read the words in this city, along with the presbyters who preside over the Church.

— The Shepherd of Hermas, Vision 2.4.3 (Rome, c. 140–155).1

Polycarp, and the presbyters with him, to the Church of God sojourning at Philippi…

— Polycarp of Smyrna, Epistle to the Philippians, salutation (c. 125); the letter later bids the church be “subject to the presbyters and deacons, as unto God and Christ” (ch. 5).2

The blessed apostles, then, having founded and built up the Church, committed into the hands of Linus the office of the episcopate. Of this Linus, Paul makes mention in the Epistles to Timothy. To him succeeded Anacletus; and after him, in the third place from the apostles, Clement was allotted the bishopric.

— Irenaeus of Lyons, Against Heresies III.3.3 (c. 180).3

The question

Ignatius of Antioch (c. 108) writes to churches in Asia Minor that each have one named bishop with presbyters and deacons, while Roman and Philippian texts of c. 96–155 speak of presbyters and of “bishops” in the plural, and Irenaeus (c. 180) lists single bishops of Rome back to the apostles. The dispute is whether a single bishop headed each church from the apostolic period, the plural language naming the college around him, or whether the single-bishop order was reached at different times in different churches. The dates of two of the witnesses are themselves disputed: the Shepherd of Hermas is dated across c. 100–160, and Polycarp’s letter across c. 110–135, P. N. Harrison (1936) dividing it into two letters.4

Single bishops in the churches from the apostles’ time, Rome among them

On this reading each church was headed by one bishop from the apostolic period, and where the early sources speak only of presbyters the bishop stood among them, unnamed.

Held by: Irenaeus (Against Heresies, c. 180); Alphonse Van Hove (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, “Bishop”, 1907); John Zizioulas (Orthodox, Eucharist, Bishop, Church, 1965; ET 2001).5

Irenaeus gives the Roman church as a line of single bishops from Linus, Anacletus and Clement onward. Hegesippus, as Eusebius quotes him, names the Roman bishops from Anicetus to Eleutherus and writes: “In every succession, and in every city that is held which is preached by the law and the prophets and the Lord.”6 Van Hove takes these lists as the central evidence: “If the monarchical episcopate began only in the middle of the second century it impossible to comprehend how at the end of second century the episcopal lists of several important bishoprics giving the succession of bishops as far back as the first century were generally known and admitted.” He adds that nowhere in the later second century “is the least trace to be found of a change of organization” that would have removed a college’s authority “without leaving in the contemporary documents the least trace of a protest.” The collegial texts, on his account, show “a college of presbyters or of bishops which administered several churches, but which had a president who was no other than the monarchic bishop”; in Asia “Ignatius does not write as though the institution were a new one.”7 Stanislaus Dunin-Borkowski (Catholic, 1910), who grants that in some places the presbyterate long remained the highest local authority, holds that “The mere fact that the ancient sources speak of colleges of presbyters, without any mention of a monarchical bishop at their head, does not warrant the immediate conclusion that there was no such bishop”, and that “To explain the Epistles of St. Ignatius, one must assume that the separation of the titles bishop and presbyter took place in many localities as early as 70-80”, and reads the Clement of Hermas’s vision as “evidently such an episkopos in Rome”.8 Zizioulas, drawing on G. Konidaris, holds that the bishop first appeared “known only by the personal name of the office-holder and implicit within the collective term ‘the presbyters’ whenever there was no reason to single him out”, so that “the office of Bishop exists even in the apostolic period”.9 He also holds that each city kept one eucharistic assembly, “Nowhere in the sources… do we find more than one ‘household Church’ in each city”, and that “Throughout the first three centuries and, so far as we can tell, in all regions, the principle of the unity of each Church in one Eucharistic assembly under one Bishop was faithfully observed.”10 On this reading the presbyteral language of 1 Clement, Hermas and Polycarp names the college gathered around the bishop, and the bishop-lists record the men who presided over it.

A single bishop reached by different churches at different times

On this reading the one-bishop order was established early at Antioch and in Asia Minor but was reached later in other churches, Rome and Philippi among them.

Held by: J. B. Lightfoot (Anglican, “The Christian Ministry”, 1868); Peter Lampe (historian of Roman Christianity, From Paul to Valentinus, 1987; ET 2003); Francis A. Sullivan (Catholic, From Apostles to Bishops, 2001).11

Lightfoot held that “the episcopate was formed not out of the apostolic order by localisation but out of the presbyteral by elevation”, and that “Asia Minor too was the nurse, if not the mother, of episcopacy in the Gentile Churches”: developed in St John’s community, it “could hardly have grown up without his sanction”. There “the evidence for the early and wide extension of episcopacy… may be considered irrefragable”, while “As we turn from Asia Minor to Macedonia and Greece, the evidence becomes fainter and scantier.”12 Ignatius’s Asian letters greet a bishop with his presbytery and deacons in each church: “Let no man do anything connected with the Church without the bishop” (Smyrnaeans 8). His letter to the Romans honours a church “worthy of God” and “which presides over love”, and the one bishop it names is Ignatius himself, “the bishop of Syria”.13 Lightfoot notes that “in this epistle alone there is no allusion to the episcopal office as existing among his correspondents”; that 1 Clement uses “bishop” and “presbyter” of the same officers (chs. 42, 44), Clement being “rather the chief of the presbyters than the chief over the presbyters”; and that from Polycarp’s letter “episcopacy did not exist at all among the Philippians at this time, or existed only in an elementary form, so that the bishop was a mere president of the presbyteral council.”14 Lampe places the Roman evidence in its social setting: “The fractionation in Rome favored a collegial presbyterial system of governance and prevented for a long time, until the second half of the second century, the development of a monarchical episcopacy in the city.” He dates the first monarchical bishop of the whole city to Victor (c. 189–199) and reads Hegesippus as concerned with a succession of correct teaching.15 Sullivan, as his reviewer David Fiensy summarizes him, concludes that “The monarchical episcopate developed first in the second century, and in Antioch and western Asia Minor before other localities” and that “There was no single bishop in the church of Rome in the first century.”16 On this reading the sources of one generation form a map of different stages: a single bishop at Antioch and in Asia c. 108, a presbyteral college at Rome and Philippi in the same decades, and the plural “bishops and deacons” of the Didache (ch. 15).17
