Is rule by a college of elders the apostolic form of government? — the presbyterian thesis

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — the plurality-of-elders pattern is genuinely well-grounded: the New Testament appoints elders in the plural in every church, and even the fathers who most exalt the bishop concede the apostolic congregations were led by a college of presbyters, not a lone monarch. But the further, specifically presbyterian claims — strict parity that excludes any presiding office, a distinct order of lay “ruling elders,” and graded assemblies (session → presbytery → synod) as the divinely-fixed, unalterable polity — outrun the same texts, which the episcopal and congregational readings claim on identical grounds; genuinely disputed, with no clear winner · advanced by John Calvin and the classic Reformed standards (the Second Helvetic Confession, 1566; the Westminster Form of Presbyterial Church-Government, 1645)

Related arguments: the forms of government — see also Is episcopacy of divine right? · Does the gathered congregation hold final authority?

The claim

The scripture doth hold out a presbytery in a church. … A presbytery consisteth of ministers of the word, and such other publick officers as are agreeable to and warranted by the word of God to be church-governors, to join with the ministers in the government of the church. … It is lawful, and agreeable to the word of God, that the church be governed by several sorts of assemblies, which are congregational, classical, and synodical.

— The Form of Presbyterial Church-Government (Westminster Assembly, 1645), full text.1 The interpretation this page weighs is the strong form of that platform: that in the New Testament every church was governed by a plurality (college) of elders of one order, so that collegial elder-rule — not a single presiding bishop, and not the gathered congregation — is the apostolic form of government, extended upward through graded assemblies (the local session of elders, the presbytery/classis over many congregations, the provincial and national synod) on the model of the Jerusalem council.

Who advances it

The reading is the platform of the Reformed and Presbyterian tradition, and its ancient charter is Jerome, who held that before the rise of the monarchical bishop “the churches were governed by a common council of the presbyters” (communi presbyterorum consilio), the presiding bishop raised over the rest only later, “as a remedy against schism” (Commentary on Titus 1:5; Letter 146).2 John Calvin gives the reading its systematic form: reading “bishop,” “presbyter,” and “pastor” as scriptural synonyms, he holds that “from the beginning… each church had its senate, composed of pious, grave, and venerable men,” and that the New Testament “governors” (Romans 12:8; 1 Corinthians 12:28) were “seniors selected from the people to unite with the bishops in pronouncing censures and exercising discipline” — the seed of the distinct ruling elder (Institutes IV.3–4).3 From Calvin’s Geneva the polity is confessionalized — the Second Helvetic Confession (1566), the French and Scottish disciplines, and above all the Westminster Form of Presbyterial Church-Government (1645), which lays down the presbytery, the ruling elder, and the graded assemblies as the scriptural pattern. The same handful of texts is urged in every case — Acts 14:23; Titus 1:5; Philippians 1:1; 1 Timothy 5:17; 1 Timothy 4:14; Acts 15 — and read as fixing collegial elder-rule as the divinely-warranted form.4

The evidence for

Plurality is the fixed New-Testament pattern. Wherever the apostolic writings show elders being appointed, they are appointed in the plural, congregation by congregation. Paul and Barnabas “ordained them elders [presbyterous, plural] in every church” (Acts 14:23); Titus is left “to ordain elders [plural] in every city” (Titus 1:5); Paul greets the one church of Philippi with its “bishops [plural] and deacons” (Philippians 1:1); and it is “the elders of the church” of Ephesus, in the plural, whom he summons and then calls “overseers” (Acts 20:17, 28). No apostolic congregation is anywhere shown under a single presiding officer; the college is the norm. Even Chrysostom, reading Philippians 1:1, concedes the point outright: “were there several Bishops of one city? Certainly not; but he called the Presbyters so” — a plurality of presbyter-bishops in the one church.5

The eldership is collegial and non-monarchical, even under an apostle’s hand. When Peter addresses the churches’ leaders he numbers himself among them, not over them: “The elders which are among you I exhort, who am also an elder… Feed the flock of God which is among you, taking the oversight thereof [episkopountes]… Neither as being lords over God’s heritage” (1 Peter 5:1–3) — the oversight (episkopē) is a function the college of elders exercises, not the title of a man set over them. The same picture stands a generation later at Corinth, where 1 Clement knows only “the presbyters set over” the flock, names no single bishop, and treats the deposing of presbyters as the whole sin — the collegial eldership Rome itself still assumes c. 96.6

The eldership both rules and (some of it) teaches. Paul writes, “Let the elders that rule well be counted worthy of double honour, especially they who labour in the word and doctrine” (1 Timothy 5:17) — read by the presbyterian as marking two kinds of elder within the one college, those who rule and those who also labour in the word, so that the ruling office is a standing part of the ministry, not merely the pastor’s teaching. The lists of gifts confirm a distinct governing charism beside the teaching one: “he that ruleth, with diligence” (Romans 12:8) and “governments” (kybernēseis, 1 Corinthians 12:28). On this Calvin builds the ruling elder — the “seniors selected from the people” who join the pastors in government and discipline.7

Ordination and government run through the college, and upward through synods. Timothy’s gift was given “with the laying on of the hands of the presbytery [presbyterion]” (1 Timothy 4:14) — a standing college of elders ordaining, on the presbyterian reading, not a single bishop. And the New Testament’s one great cause is settled not in a single congregation but in an assembly whose decrees bind others: the Jerusalem council of “the apostles and elders” issues a sentence (Acts 15:22–29) that Paul then “delivered them… to keep, that were ordained of the apostles and elders which were at Jerusalem” as he “went through the cities” (Acts 16:4) — taken as the apostolic warrant for graded assemblies above the local session, the presbytery and synod that make the whole church one government.8

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The plurality-of-elders pattern is well-grounded and should be granted without grudging. Every New-Testament church whose order is described is led by a college of presbyter-bishops, appointed in the plural; the two titles interchange; and the fathers nearest the apostles — 1 Clement’s presiding presbytery, Chrysostom’s frank “he called the Presbyters” bishops, Jerome’s “common council of the presbyters” — read the evidence the same way. A polity that reads a lone monarch-bishop back into every apostolic congregation is false to the texts. This is the same sound core the parity arguments carry, rated sound on the office-terms and Jerome pages.

What is overstated. The leap from “the elders governed as a college” to “strict parity, a distinct ruling-elder order, and graded synods are the divinely-fixed, unalterable polity” is where the thesis outruns its evidence. The same texts read otherwise on five points:

(1) Plurality of elders is not the same as strict parity. A college admits a president — a primus inter pares — without ceasing to be a college; a plurality of elders and a presiding officer over them are perfectly compatible. This is exactly the seam the episcopalian exploits: James manifestly presides at the Jerusalem council and frames its decree (Acts 15:13–21), the “angel” of each church is addressed in the singular in Revelation 2–3, and within a decade Ignatius everywhere presupposes a single bishop amid the presbytery. The plural-elders data establish a college; they do not by themselves exclude a presiding office, which is the whole burden of the parity claim.9

(2) 1 Timothy 5:17 may grade the one eldership, not found two orders. “Especially they who labour in the word and doctrine” reads at least as naturally as marking two emphases within one presbyteral office — all elders rule, some also preach — as founding a separate order of lay ruling elders. The ancient exegetes take it of the one clergy: Chrysostom reads it of “priests” who rule and of whom some “labour in the word,” and the distinct, non-teaching “ruling elder” is hard to find in the sources before the Reformation revived it. So the classic proof for the lay ruling elder is a possible reading, not a compelled one.10

(3) A trans-congregational, quasi-episcopal authority stands over the elders in the same New Testament. The apostles, and their delegates Timothy and Titus, exercise an authority above the local college: Titus is told to appoint elders city by city (Titus 1:5) and Timothy to receive charges against them and not to “lay hands suddenly on any man” (1 Timothy 5:19–22) — a personal oversight the plural elders do not themselves hold. The presbyterian reading must absorb this standing oversight; and it is precisely the datum on which the episcopal reading builds its presiding office and the substance-apostolic account of the ministry.11

(4) The two structural proofs are the weakest links. “The laying on of the hands of the presbytery” (1 Timothy 4:14) need not mean a college of presbyters ordaining: Theodore of Mopsuestia reads the presbyterion there not as ordaining presbyters but as the honorific “council” of the apostolic company present when Paul ordained — the parallel text has Paul say the gift came “by the putting on of my hands” (2 Timothy 1:6) — and Chrysostom flatly denies presbyters could ordain a superior: “Presbyters would not have laid hands on a Bishop.” And Acts 15 is an apostolic council wielding the apostles’ own, unrepeatable authority — not self-evidently a template for a standing presbytery: reading it as the charter of graded synods is the very overreach flagged on the conciliar-governance page (which rates the conciliar pattern sound precisely by not making Acts 15 a council-only polity). The ordainer question itself is routed to Who confers office by the laying on of hands?12

(5) The proof-texts under-determine presbyterian vs. congregational vs. episcopal. Every one of these verses is claimed, on identical grounds, by the rival readings: the congregational tradition lodges final authority in the gathered assembly (Matthew 18; 1 Corinthians 5), the episcopal in the bishop, and the presbyterian in the college of elders — and the New-Testament office-vocabulary settles the offices (one order, held in plurality, with deacons) but not the locus of final authority. So the plurality texts fix a collegial ministry; they do not, of themselves, decide the form of government.13

Assessment

Contested. Split the thesis and both halves come clear. Its plurality-of-elders core — that a college of presbyter-bishops, not a lone monarch, governed every New-Testament congregation — is well-grounded on the texts and granted even by the fathers who most exalt the bishop; a polity that denies the apostolic church its collegial eldership is refuted, and that core is rated sound on the parity pages. But the defining presbyterian claims — strict parity that excludes any presiding office, a distinct order of lay ruling elders, and graded assemblies (session → presbytery → synod) as the divinely-warranted, unalterable form — the same texts do not establish: a plurality admits a president (James at Jerusalem, the single “angel” of each church, Ignatius’s bishop within a decade), 1 Timothy 5:17 may grade one office rather than found two, a trans-congregational oversight (the apostles, Timothy, Titus) stands over the elders, and Acts 15 furnishes a precedent that presupposes the apostles’ own unrepeatable authority rather than a standing synod. Above all the proof-texts under-determine the very question they are asked to settle: the episcopal and congregational readings claim every one of them. Between a sound plurality core and an iure divino synodal superstructure the evidence will not carry, the presbyterian thesis as a total account of church government is genuinely disputed, with no clear winner. Trust the collegial eldership of the apostolic church; flag the leap from plurality to strict parity, a separate ruling-elder order, and graded synods as the one form Scripture commands. This is the presbyterian leg of the three-way dispute over the form of government; its rivals are the episcopal reading, weighed in Is episcopacy of divine right?, and the congregational reading, weighed in Does the gathered congregation hold final authority? — the three best read together as competing total accounts of the same under-determining evidence.
