John Calvin on the Government of the Church

Dates: c. 1509–1564 (the church-order passages below from the Institutes of the Christian Religion, Book IV, chs. 3–4, in the definitive 1559 edition) · Wikipedia
Summary: The mature Reformed formulation of presbyterian church order, presented as a recovery of the New Testament and early-church pattern. Reading “bishop,” “presbyter,” and “pastor” as scriptural synonyms, Calvin orders each church by a college of elders of one order — pastors and teachers who preach, beside “governors” (the ruling elder) chosen from the people for discipline — with deacons for the poor; holds that a minister is lawfully called by the people’s consent; and judges the single presiding bishop set over the presbyters a matter of the church’s custom, not of divine right. His is the systematic counter-pole to episcopacy iure divino. The advocacy is weighed adversarially in Is rule by a college of elders the apostolic form of government? and Were ministers made by the people’s election, or by ordained conferral? (Calvin also appears on this wiki as a witness for infant baptism.)

Claims & evidence

“Bishop,” “presbyter,” and “pastor” name one office

In giving the name of bishops, presbyters, and pastors, indiscriminately to those who govern churches, I have done it on the authority of Scripture, which uses the words as synonymous.

— Institutes IV.3.8.1 Calvin takes as settled the parity reading of the New Testament vocabulary — the interchange of episkopos and presbyteros in Acts 20:17, 28, Titus 1:5–7, and Philippians 1:1 — and builds his whole account of church order on it: since the titles are one, the office is one, held in a plurality of elders, not divided into a bishop set by God over the presbyters.

The offices: pastors and teachers, governing elders, and deacons

By these governors I understand seniors selected from the people to unite with the bishops in pronouncing censures and exercising discipline. … From the beginning, therefore, each church had its senate, composed of pious, grave, and venerable men, in whose hands was lodged the power of correcting faults.

— Institutes IV.3.8.2 Calvin distinguishes, within the one presbyteral order, two kinds of elder: the pastors and teachers who preach and teach the word (the perpetual offices with which the church “never can dispense”), and the “governors” — lay elders chosen from the people to join the ministers in discipline, the seed of the Reformed ruling elder, drawn from “he that ruleth, with diligence” (Romans 12:8) and “governments” (1 Corinthians 12:28). Beside these stand the deacons, whose office he reads as the care of the poor (Acts 6; Romans 12:8). So the government of the church is committed to a college, not to a monarch.3

A minister is lawfully called by the consent of the people

We see, then, that ministers are legitimately called according to the word of God, when those who may have seemed fit are elected on the consent and approbation of the people.

— Institutes IV.3.15.4 Calvin holds together the two acts the ancient church kept together: the people choose the man, and other pastors “preside over the election, lest any error should be committed” — consent from below, order from the standing ministry. This is the ground on which the Reformed tradition rests the people’s share in the making of a minister, weighed in Were ministers made by the people’s election, or by ordained conferral?

The presiding bishop is the church’s custom, not divine right

All, therefore, to whom the office of teaching was committed, they called presbyters, and in each city these presbyters selected one of their number to whom they gave the special title of bishop, lest, as usually happens, from equality dissension should arise.

— Institutes IV.4.2, on the government of the primitive church before the papacy.5 For Calvin the ancient bishop was a president among equals — not “so superior in honour and dignity as to have dominion over his colleagues,” but one who, as in an assembly, brings matters forward and executes what is decreed by common consent. He grounds the point in Jerome: the bishop stands above the presbyter by the custom of the church rather than by the Lord’s appointment, the office introduced “by human arrangement, according to the exigency of the times” as a remedy against schism. The single bishop is therefore a thing the church may have, not one God commanded — the exact denial of episcopacy iure divino, weighed in Is episcopacy of divine right?

Notes

  • Calvin gives the presbyterian reading its systematic form, but its ancient charter is Jerome, on whom he leans throughout IV.4 for the identity of bishop and presbyter and the merely-customary rise of the presiding bishop. The parity core Calvin assumes is rated sound on the office-terms and Jerome pages.
  • From Calvin’s Geneva the polity was confessionalized and extended upward into graded assemblies (the local session of elders, the presbytery/classis over many congregations, the provincial and national synod) on the model of the Jerusalem council — the Second Helvetic Confession (1566, ch. 18) and, most fully, the Westminster Form of Presbyterial Church-Government (1645). That synodal superstructure, beyond what the Institutes itself lays down, is part of the thesis weighed in Is rule by a college of elders the apostolic form of government?
  • Calvin’s adversary across the Channel was the adiaphorist case of Whitgift (Scripture commands no one perpetual polity) and, later, the iure divino episcopacy of Bancroft and Hooker; the presbyterian side of that quarrel in England was carried by Thomas Cartwright, who pressed exactly Calvin’s pattern of pastors, doctors, elders, and deacons as the divinely-fixed discipline.
