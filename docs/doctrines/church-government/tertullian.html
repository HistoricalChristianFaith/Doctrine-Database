Tertullian on the Government of the Church

Dates: c. 200 (The Prescription Against Heretics, written c. 200–203, in his “catholic” period) · Wikipedia
Summary: Tertullian, writing at Carthage, gives the succession argument a legal form. Borrowing the Roman lawyer’s praescriptio — an objection that stops a suit before its merits are heard — he tells the heretics they have no standing even to argue Scripture: only the churches the apostles founded, and the churches whose faith agrees with them, hold the apostolic deposit. He challenges the heretics to “unfold the roll of their bishops” back to an apostle, and in the same chapter counts churches with no apostolic founder “as not less apostolic because they are akin in doctrine.”

Claims & evidence

The apostolic churches and the tradition of the faith (Prescription 20–21)

They then in like manner founded churches in every city, from which all the other churches, one after another, derived the tradition of the faith, and the seeds of doctrine, and are every day deriving them, that they may become churches… Therefore the churches, although they are so many and so great, comprise but the one primitive church, [founded] by the apostles, from which they all [spring]. In this way all are primitive, and all are apostolic, whilst they are all proved to be one.

— The Prescription Against Heretics, ch. 20, source (Roberts–Donaldson translation, Ante-Nicene Fathers; cf. New Advent).1 The apostles founded churches; those churches are the “moulds and original sources of the faith,” and any doctrine is true only insofar as it “agrees with the apostolic churches” (ch. 21). Tertullian states the criterion in one line: “We hold communion with the apostolic churches because our doctrine is in no respect different from theirs. This is our witness of truth.”

“Let them unfold the roll of their bishops” (Prescription 32)

Let them produce the original records of their churches; let them unfold the roll of their bishops, running down in due succession from the beginning in such a manner that [their first bishop] shall be able to show for his ordainer and predecessor some one of the apostles or of apostolic men… For this is the manner in which the apostolic churches transmit their registers: as the church of Smyrna, which records that Polycarp was placed therein by John; as also the church of Rome, which makes Clement to have been ordained in like manner by Peter.

— The Prescription Against Heretics, ch. 32, source (cf. New Advent).2 In Tertullian’s argument the heretics can produce no such roll, and so have no standing. His specimen “registers” are two — Polycarp placed by John at Smyrna, Clement ordained by Peter at Rome; in Irenaeus’s Roman list (c. 180) Clement is third after Linus and Anacletus.

Churches without an apostolic founder, “akin in doctrine” (Prescription 32)

…those churches, who, although they derive not their founder from apostles or apostolic men (as being of much later date, for they are in fact being founded daily), yet, since they agree in the same faith, they are accounted as not less apostolic because they are akin in doctrine.

— The Prescription Against Heretics, ch. 32, source.2 In the same chapter, churches “being founded daily,” with no line back to an apostle, are accounted “not less apostolic” because “they agree in the same faith.”

Notes

  • The passage in the polity debate. Prescription 32 couples a challenge to produce a roll of bishops from an apostle with the statement that churches “founded daily,” with no apostolic founder, are “not less apostolic… because they are akin in doctrine.” Both halves are cited in the debate over what apostolic succession is and does.
  • The two Roman lists. Tertullian has Clement ordained by Peter; Irenaeus, writing some twenty years earlier, puts two bishops (Linus, Anacletus) between the apostles and Clement. The difference between the two earliest Roman lists is cited in Was the single-bishop episcopate in place everywhere by c. 110, or did it emerge unevenly? under Disputed readings.
  • The date of the work. The Prescription belongs to Tertullian’s earlier years (c. 200–203), before his association with the Montanist movement; his later writings and their reception are traced on the reception-history timeline on Tertullian.
  • The sacramental side is traced elsewhere. Tertullian’s teaching on the ministry that offers the Eucharist and administers baptism — the priestly rather than the governing aspect — is on his Ministerial Priesthood page. This page keeps to the who-governs / how-is-the-faith-guaranteed question.
  • Same author, elsewhere on this wiki. Tertullian also appears on the canon, ministerial priesthood, Baptist successionism (a modern chain-of-churches thesis), and other timelines.
