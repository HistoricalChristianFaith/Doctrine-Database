Tertullian on the Government of the Church

Dates: c. 200 (The Prescription Against Heretics, written c. 200–203, in his “catholic” period) · Wikipedia
Summary: Tertullian takes up Irenaeus’s succession argument and turns it into a legal one. Borrowing the Roman lawyer’s praescriptio — an objection that stops a suit before its merits are heard — he tells the heretics they have no standing even to argue Scripture: only the churches the apostles actually founded, and whose faith agrees with those churches, hold the apostolic deposit. His famous challenge is that heretics “unroll the roll of their bishops” back to an apostle. But in the same chapter he makes plain that the real test is doctrine: churches with no apostolic founder at all are “not less apostolic because they are akin in doctrine.” His witness is a cornerstone of the episcopal succession argument — and its own best evidence that the point was doctrinal continuity, not a mechanical pedigree.

Claims & evidence

The apostolic churches are the depositories of the faith; true doctrine is what agrees with them (Prescription 20–21)

They then in like manner founded churches in every city, from which all the other churches, one after another, derived the tradition of the faith, and the seeds of doctrine, and are every day deriving them, that they may become churches… Therefore the churches, although they are so many and so great, comprise but the one primitive church, [founded] by the apostles, from which they all [spring]. In this way all are primitive, and all are apostolic, whilst they are all proved to be one.

— The Prescription Against Heretics, ch. 20, source (Roberts–Donaldson translation, Ante-Nicene Fathers; cf. New Advent).1 The apostles founded churches; those churches are the “moulds and original sources of the faith,” and any doctrine is true only insofar as it “agrees with the apostolic churches” (ch. 21). Tertullian names the criterion in one line: “We hold communion with the apostolic churches because our doctrine is in no respect different from theirs. This is our witness of truth.” The continuity that matters is continuity of teaching, publicly checkable against the churches the apostles planted.

“Let them unroll the roll of their bishops” — the challenge to trace a line back to an apostle (Prescription 32)

Let them produce the original records of their churches; let them unfold the roll of their bishops, running down in due succession from the beginning in such a manner that [their first bishop] shall be able to show for his ordainer and predecessor some one of the apostles or of apostolic men… For this is the manner in which the apostolic churches transmit their registers: as the church of Smyrna, which records that Polycarp was placed therein by John; as also the church of Rome, which makes Clement to have been ordained in like manner by Peter.

— The Prescription Against Heretics, ch. 32, source (cf. New Advent).2 This is the sharpest early statement of the traceable-episcopate test, and the one later writers quote: the Gnostics can produce no such roll, and are thereby non-suited. Note that Tertullian’s specimen “registers” are only two names deep — Polycarp from John, Clement from Peter — and that his Roman datum (Clement ordained by Peter) does not match Irenaeus’s list, where Clement is third after Linus and Anacletus. The rolls are being pressed into service against heretics, not compiled as exact archives.

But the real test is doctrine: churches with no apostolic founder are “not less apostolic” if they keep the faith (Prescription 32)

…those churches, who, although they derive not their founder from apostles or apostolic men (as being of much later date, for they are in fact being founded daily), yet, since they agree in the same faith, they are accounted as not less apostolic because they are akin in doctrine.

— The Prescription Against Heretics, ch. 32, source.2 This is the hinge, and it is often overlooked by those who cite the “roll of bishops” as if pedigree were everything. Tertullian himself relativises the pedigree: a church founded only yesterday, with no line back to an apostle, is “not less apostolic” provided it “agrees in the same faith.” Apostolicity, for him, is finally a matter of doctrine — the succession-roll is powerful evidence of that agreement, not an independent guarantee that could stand where the doctrine failed. The traceable episcopate is a means to the end of doctrinal continuity, not the thing itself.

Notes

  • What it establishes for polity. Tertullian gives the succession argument its classic forensic form: the heretics cannot “unfold the roll of their bishops” back to an apostle, so they are barred at the threshold. It is a staple proof-text for the episcopal reading of authority. But his own qualifier — churches “founded daily,” with no apostolic founder, are “not less apostolic… because they are akin in doctrine” — shows that in c. 200 the argument was still about the content of the faith, publicly traceable, and not yet a doctrine of tactile or jurisdictional transmission. Whether the later, heavier claim follows is weighed in the summary’s Arguments section.
  • The Roman rolls already disagree. Tertullian has Clement ordained directly by Peter; Irenaeus, writing twenty years earlier, puts two bishops (Linus, Anacletus) between Peter and Clement. The discrepancy in the two earliest Roman “lists” is a datum for the argument that the single-line episcopal register is partly a retrojection onto what began as a college of presbyter-bishops — folded into the succession argument on the summary.
  • His “catholic” period. The Prescription is a work of Tertullian’s earlier, pre-Montanist years (c. 200–203); it argues for the authority of the mainstream churches without reservation. His later, Montanist-leaning writings sit in tension with parts of it — a shift traced on the reception-history timeline on Tertullian. The succession argument here belongs to the “catholic” Tertullian and was never retracted.
  • The sacramental side is traced elsewhere. Tertullian’s teaching on the ministry that offers the Eucharist and administers baptism — the priestly rather than the governing aspect — is on his Ministerial Priesthood page. This page keeps to the who-governs / how-is-the-faith-guaranteed question.
  • Same author, elsewhere on this wiki. Tertullian also appears on the canon, ministerial priesthood, Baptist successionism (whose modern chain-of-churches thesis inverts the argument he makes here), and other timelines.
