The Didache on the Government of the Church

Dates: c. 100 AD (the Didache, or Teaching of the Twelve Apostles, an anonymous church-order manual; date debated, c. 50–150, and its church-order section is widely held to preserve a very early stratum) · Wikipedia
Summary: The earliest surviving church-order manual — a manual of practice, not a person — and so a uniquely direct window onto how an early congregation was ordered. It shows a ministry in transition: a still-living order of itinerant, charismatic apostles, prophets, and teachers (chs. 11–13) set beside a settled local ministry of bishops and deacons whom the congregation is told to elect for itself (ch. 15). It names no single presiding bishop and no presbyters — the offices are plural and local, appointed by show of hands, and the appointed officers are said to “render the service of prophets and teachers.” For the developmental reading of polity this is the pivotal witness to the stage between the fluid ministry of the New Testament and 1 Clement and the settled threefold order that appears a little later in Ignatius.

Claims & evidence

“Appoint for yourselves bishops and deacons” — a plural, elected, local ministry (Didache 15)

Appoint, therefore, for yourselves, bishops and deacons worthy of the Lord, men meek, and not lovers of money, and truthful and proved; for they also render to you the service of prophets and teachers. Despise them not therefore, for they are your honoured ones, together with the prophets and teachers.

— Didache, ch. 15, source (Roberts–Donaldson translation, Ante-Nicene Fathers; cf. New Advent).1 Three features of this instruction bear directly on church order. First, the officers are named in the plural and as a pair — “bishops and deacons” (episkopous kai diakonous), the same two-order grouping Paul greets at Philippi (Philippians 1:1) and Clement describes at Rome (1 Clement 42). There is no third, higher rank; no single ruling bishop is named; and, notably, the word presbyter never appears in the manual at all. Second, the congregation chooses its own officers: the verb is cheirotoneō, “elect” or “appoint by show of hands,” the New-Testament sense (Acts 14:23; 2 Corinthians 8:19) — election by the community, not yet ordination by the laying-on of hands.1 Third, the appointed officers are commended precisely because “they also render to you the service of prophets and teachers” — the local, resident ministry is being urged as an equal-in-honour alternative to the itinerant charismatics whose day was passing.

The older order still living — itinerant apostles, prophets, and teachers (Didache 11–13)

Behind the appointed bishops and deacons the manual presupposes an earlier, charismatic ministry that is still very much present: travelling apostles (in the wider, post-Twelve sense of missionary messengers), prophets who “speak in the Spirit,” and teachers. The manual’s worry is how to tell the true from the false among them:

But concerning the apostles and prophets, according to the decree of the Gospel, thus do. Let every apostle that cometh to you be received as the Lord. But he shall not remain except one day; but if there be need, also the next; but if he remain three days, he is a false prophet. And when the apostle goeth away, let him take nothing but bread until he lodgeth; but if he ask money, he is a false prophet.

— Didache, ch. 11, source.2 The prophets, where they “will settle among you,” are to be supported from the community’s first-fruits, “for they are your high priests” (ch. 13) — a charismatic ministry with the community’s highest honour, ranked above the local officers, not under them.2 This is the ministry the New Testament also knows (“first apostles, second prophets, third teachers,” 1 Corinthians 12:28; Ephesians 4:11): mobile, Spirit-given, and unappointed by any local election.

A ministry caught mid-transition — charismatic giving way to appointed (Didache 13–15)

Read together, chs. 11–15 catch a single congregation at the hinge between two kinds of ministry. The itinerant prophets and teachers are still honoured — still the “high priests” who receive the first-fruits — but they are becoming scarce (“but if ye have not a prophet, give it to the poor,” ch. 13:4) and increasingly hard to police against imposters (chs. 11–12). Into that gap the manual directs the community to elect its own resident bishops and deacons, and to honour them as it honours the prophets, “for they also render to you the service of prophets and teachers” (ch. 15:1–2). The older commentary on the text noticed the same movement: chs. 11–15 “point to a movement from an itinerant and extraordinary ministry to a more settled one.”1 The Didache thus documents, from the inside, the shift from a charismatic to an appointed leadership — not by argument but by the practical instructions of a congregation living through it.

Notes

  • What it establishes for polity — and what it doesn’t. The Didache attests a local, plural, two-order ministry — bishops and deacons — elected by the congregation and set alongside (not yet over or under, in any fixed rank) a residual charismatic order of prophets and teachers. It does not attest a single ruling bishop, and it never uses the word presbyter at all. The threefold, monarchical order — one bishop over a college of presbyters over the deacons — appears clearly only a little later, in Ignatius. Whether the Didache’s silence on the monarch-bishop and the presbytery reflects an earlier stage of the ministry or merely a different region at the same date is exactly the question weighed in the Arguments — it is a datum for that debate, not a verdict in it.
  • Election, not laying-on of hands. That the officers are chosen by cheirotoneō (“elect / appoint by show of hands”) in its New-Testament sense — rather than the later technical sense of “ordain” by imposition of hands — is one of the marks of the manual’s early date, and a datum for the election-versus-ordination question — weighed on its own merits under were ministers made by the people’s election, or by ordained conferral?, not here.
  • The contested date. The Didache has no author and no fixed date: proposals range from c. 50 to c. 150, and it is plainly a composite — its moral opening is a Christian recension of the Jewish “Two Ways,” and its church-order and liturgical sections are widely held to preserve a very early stratum. It is anchored here at c. 100 (the conventional midpoint), and the church-order material it reports is best read as reflecting a stage of ministry before the settled threefold order — which is why it sits on this timeline between 1 Clement (c. 96) and Ignatius (c. 108), whatever the precise year of its final compilation.
  • “High priests” and the offering. That the prophets are called the community’s “high priests” (ch. 13:3) and that the Lord’s-Day assembly is called a “sacrifice” (ch. 14, citing Malachi 1:11) belongs to the who-offers question rather than the who-governs one, and is traced under the ministerial priesthood; this page keeps to church order.
  • The translation. Quotations are from the Roberts–Donaldson rendering (Ante-Nicene Fathers), following the text of the Codex Hierosolymitanus (Bryennios, 1873), the sole complete Greek manuscript; chapter and verse numbers follow the standard division.
