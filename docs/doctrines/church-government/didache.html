The Didache on the Government of the Church

Dates: c. 100 AD (the Didache, or Teaching of the Twelve Apostles, an anonymous church-order manual; date debated, c. 50–150; the Ante-Nicene Fathers notes take its church-order chapters to “point to an early date”) · Wikipedia
Summary: The earliest extant church-order manual — a manual of practice, not a person. It sets an itinerant order of apostles, prophets, and teachers (chs. 11–13) beside a local ministry of bishops and deacons whom the congregation is told to appoint for itself (ch. 15). It names no single presiding bishop and does not use the word presbyter; the officers are plural and local, and are said to “render the service of prophets and teachers.” The Ante-Nicene Fathers notes read chs. 11–15 as pointing “to a movement from an itinerant and extraordinary ministry to a more settled one.” On this timeline it stands between 1 Clement (c. 96) and Ignatius (c. 108).

Claims & evidence

“Appoint for yourselves bishops and deacons” (Didache 15)

Appoint, therefore, for yourselves, bishops and deacons worthy of the Lord, men meek, and not lovers of money, and truthful and proved; for they also render to you the service of prophets and teachers. Despise them not therefore, for they are your honoured ones, together with the prophets and teachers.

— Didache, ch. 15, source (Roberts–Donaldson translation, Ante-Nicene Fathers; cf. New Advent).1 Three features of this instruction figure in the polity debate. First, the officers are named in the plural and as a pair — “bishops and deacons” (episkopous kai diakonous), the same pairing Paul greets at Philippi (Philippians 1:1) and Clement names in 1 Clement 42. The manual names no single ruling bishop, and the word presbyter does not appear in it. Second, the congregation appoints its own officers: the verb is cheirotoneō, which the Ante-Nicene Fathers note renders in its New-Testament sense (Acts 14:23; 2 Corinthians 8:19), “elect” or “appoint (by show of hands),” not “ordain.”1 Third, the appointed officers are commended because “they also render to you the service of prophets and teachers,” and are to be honoured “together with the prophets and teachers.”

Itinerant apostles, prophets, and teachers (Didache 11–13)

Beside the appointed bishops and deacons the manual addresses an itinerant ministry: travelling apostles (in the wider, post-Twelve sense of missionary messengers), prophets who “speak in the Spirit,” and teachers. The manual’s worry is how to tell the true from the false among them:

But concerning the apostles and prophets, according to the decree of the Gospel, thus do. Let every apostle that cometh to you be received as the Lord. But he shall not remain except one day; but if there be need, also the next; but if he remain three days, he is a false prophet. And when the apostle goeth away, let him take nothing but bread until he lodgeth; but if he ask money, he is a false prophet.

— Didache, ch. 11, source.2 The prophets, where they “will settle among you,” are to be supported from the community’s first-fruits, “for they are your high priests” (ch. 13).2 The same triad appears in the New Testament (“first apostles, second prophets, third teachers,” 1 Corinthians 12:28; cf. Ephesians 4:11).

The two ministries side by side (Didache 11–15)

In chs. 11–15 the itinerant prophets and teachers receive the first-fruits as “high priests”; the manual provides for a community without one (“but if ye have not a prophet, give it to the poor,” ch. 13:4) and sets tests against impostors (chs. 11–12); and it directs the community to appoint its own resident bishops and deacons and to honour them with the prophets, “for they also render to you the service of prophets and teachers” (ch. 15:1–2). The Ante-Nicene Fathers notes read these chapters as pointing “to a movement from an itinerant and extraordinary ministry to a more settled one.”1

Reception and debate

  • The manual in the polity debate. The Ante-Nicene Fathers note to ch. 15 observes that the distinction between bishops and presbyters “does not appear, and yet it is found in Ignatius.” Whether the manual’s silence on a single bishop and on presbyters reflects an earlier stage of the ministry or a different region at the same date is taken up in Was the single-bishop episcopate in place everywhere by c. 110, or did it emerge unevenly? and Is the threefold ministry apostolic in origin, or a second-century development? under Disputed readings.
  • Appointment by cheirotoneō. The Ante-Nicene Fathers note counts the use of cheirotoneō in its New-Testament sense (“elect” or “appoint (by show of hands)”) among the marks of an early date. The verse is cited in the question Was a minister made by the people’s election, or by ordained conferral?
  • The date. The Didache has no author and no fixed date: proposals range from c. 50 to c. 150. It is a composite — its moral opening is a Christian recension of the Jewish “Two Ways.” It is placed here at c. 100, the conventional midpoint of that range, between 1 Clement (c. 96) and Ignatius (c. 108).
  • “High priests” and the offering. That the prophets are called the community’s “high priests” (ch. 13:3) and that the Lord’s-Day assembly is called a “sacrifice” (ch. 14, citing Malachi 1:11) belongs to the who-offers question rather than the who-governs one, and is traced under the ministerial priesthood; this page keeps to church order.
  • The translation. Quotations are from the Roberts–Donaldson rendering (Ante-Nicene Fathers), following the text of the Codex Hierosolymitanus (Bryennios, 1873), the sole complete Greek manuscript; chapter and verse numbers follow the standard division.
