Clement of Rome on the Government of the Church

Dates: c. 96 AD (1 Clement, written c. 95–96; Clement is named third bishop of Rome after the apostles in Irenaeus’s list, Against Heresies III.3; d. c. 99) · Wikipedia
Summary: The earliest extant post‑New‑Testament church document, and so the earliest extant sub‑apostolic text on church order. Writing to Corinth to end a revolt that had, in his judgement, wrongly deposed its presbyters, Clement describes a ministry of bishops and deacons appointed by the apostles and arranged to be continued by an orderly succession. In the same letter he speaks of the ejected officers both in terms of “the episcopate” and as “presbyters,” names no single ruling bishop at Corinth, and grounds an appointment in “the consent of the whole Church.” Its chapter 44 is cited in the debate over whether bishop and presbyter were one office and in the debate over apostolic succession. Ignatius’s letters, about a decade later, describe a single bishop with a presbytery and deacons.

Claims & evidence

The occasion: the deposing of presbyters at Corinth (1 Clement 1, 47, 54, 57)

The occasion of the whole letter is that a faction at Corinth has removed its presbyters from office. Before the schism, Clement recalls, the Corinthians had been “obedient to those who had the rule over you, and giving all fitting honour to the presbyters among you” (ch. 1). His remedy is submission to that same order:

… only let the flock of Christ live on terms of peace with the presbyters set over it.

— First Epistle to the Corinthians, ch. 54, source (Roberts–Donaldson translation, Ante-Nicene Fathers; cf. New Advent).1 And to the ringleaders directly:

Ye therefore, who laid the foundation of this sedition, submit yourselves to the presbyters, and receive correction so as to repent, bending the knees of your hearts.

— First Epistle to the Corinthians, ch. 57, source.1 The officers at Corinth are spoken of in the plural — “the presbyters” “set over” the flock — and the wrong Clement writes to correct is the deposing of some of their number. The letter names no single presiding bishop of Corinth; the authority it invokes against the sedition is the presbyters and, behind them, the apostolic appointment from which they hold office.

The apostles appointed “bishops and deacons” (1 Clement 42)

… And thus preaching through countries and cities, they appointed the first-fruits [of their labours], having first proved them by the Spirit, to be bishops and deacons of those who should afterwards believe. Nor was this any new thing, since indeed many ages before it was written concerning bishops and deacons. For thus saith the Scripture in a certain place, “I will appoint their bishops in righteousness, and their deacons in faith.”

— First Epistle to the Corinthians, ch. 42, source.2 The order Clement names here is a pair, “bishops and deacons” (episkopoi kai diakonoi) — the same pair Paul greets at Philippi (Philippians 1:1). He traces their authorization in a descending line — God → Christ → apostles → bishops and deacons — and presents it as no innovation but the fulfilment of Scripture (a loose citation of Isaiah 60:17 LXX). The passage names no office between the apostles and this pair. The offering-and-order side of this passage — the divinely fixed “where and by whom” of the church’s ministry — is traced on his Ministerial Priesthood detail page, to keep the who‑governs question here from repeating the who‑offers question there.

“Episcopate” and “presbyters” in one passage (1 Clement 44)

Our apostles also knew, through our Lord Jesus Christ, that there would be strife on account of the office of the episcopate. For this reason, therefore, inasmuch as they had obtained a perfect fore-knowledge of this, they appointed those [ministers] already mentioned, and afterwards gave instructions, that when these should fall asleep, other approved men should succeed them in their ministry. We are of opinion, therefore, that those appointed by them, or afterwards by other eminent men, with the consent of the whole Church… cannot be justly dismissed from the ministry. For our sin will not be small, if we eject from the episcopate those who have blamelessly and holily fulfilled its duties. Blessed are those presbyters who, having finished their course before now, have obtained a fruitful and perfect departure… But we see that ye have removed some men of excellent behaviour from the ministry, which they fulfilled blamelessly and with honour.

— First Epistle to the Corinthians, ch. 44, source.3 In this paragraph the strife is over “the office of the episcopate” (episkopē); to depose the officers is to “eject from the episcopate”; and the next sentence speaks of “those presbyters” (presbyteroi) who have finished their course. The New Testament passages set beside it in the polity debate are Acts 20:17, 28, where the elders of Ephesus are told the Spirit made them episkopoi, and Titus 1:5–7, where the qualifications of a presbyteros run on under the heading of the episkopos. Whether the two words named one office is taken up in Were “bishop” and “presbyter” originally one office? under Disputed readings.

How a man comes to office — appointment, succession, and “the consent of the whole Church” (1 Clement 44)

The same passage is the earliest description of how the ministry is filled and continued. The apostles, foreseeing the coming strife, “appointed those [ministers] already mentioned, and afterwards gave instructions, that when these should fall asleep, other approved men should succeed them in their ministry.” Valid holders are therefore those “appointed by them, or afterwards by other eminent men, with the consent of the whole Church”. Clement names apostolic appointment, that consent, and blameless service (those who have “served the flock of Christ in a humble, peaceable, and disinterested spirit, and have for a long time possessed the good opinion of all”) as the grounds on which such men “cannot be justly dismissed from the ministry.”3 The passage names both an orderly succession from the apostles and “the consent of the whole Church” in a lawful appointment; the first is cited in the debate over apostolic succession, the second in the question Was a minister made by the people’s election, or by ordained conferral? In Clement’s argument, those so appointed may not be removed by the congregation that consented to them.

Reception and debate

  • Features of the letter cited in the polity debate. The pairing “bishops and deacons” appointed by the apostles (ch. 42); the officers at Corinth spoken of in the plural as “the presbyters” (chs. 54, 57); no named bishop of Corinth; “episcopate” and “presbyters” in one paragraph; and an apostolically arranged succession joined to “the consent of the whole Church” (ch. 44). Ignatius’s letters, about a decade later, describe a single bishop with a presbytery and deacons; how the two witnesses relate is taken up in Is the threefold ministry apostolic in origin, or a second-century development? under Disputed readings.
  • Rome writing to Corinth. Whether the Roman church’s letter into another city’s dispute bears on a Roman primacy is taken up on the Primacy of Rome timeline. The letter is issued in the name of “the Church of God which sojourns at Rome.”
  • The translation. Quotations are from the Roberts–Donaldson rendering (Ante-Nicene Fathers), which follows the long-known text; the fuller text recovered in the Codex Hierosolymitanus (1873) and Latin/Coptic/Syriac witnesses does not alter these passages. Chapter numbers follow the standard division.
  • Same author, elsewhere on this wiki. Clement also appears as a witness on the Ministerial Priesthood (the divinely ordered, succeeding ministry that presents the church’s offering) and the Old Testament canon (his use of Wisdom and Judith).
