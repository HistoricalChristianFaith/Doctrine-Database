Clement of Rome on the Government of the Church

Dates: c. 96 AD (1 Clement, written c. 95–96; Clement floruit as bishop of Rome, d. c. 99) · Wikipedia
Summary: The earliest post‑New‑Testament church document, and so the earliest sub‑apostolic datum on church order. Writing to Corinth to end a revolt that had wrongfully deposed its presbyters, Clement describes a ministry of bishops and deacons appointed by the apostles, and arranged to be continued by an orderly succession — yet across the same letter he calls the very officers who were ejected “bishops” (episkopoi) and “presbyters” (presbyteroi) interchangeably, names no single ruling bishop at Corinth, and grounds an appointment in “the consent of the whole Church.” For the parity (presbyterian/congregational) reading of polity this is the anchor witness; for the episcopal reading it is the seed of apostolic succession — a decade before Ignatius gives the first clear picture of a single bishop set over the presbytery.

Claims & evidence

The scandal is the deposing of presbyters — keep peace with “the presbyters set over” the flock (1 Clement 1, 47, 54, 57)

The occasion of the whole letter is that a faction at Corinth has removed its presbyters from office. Before the schism, Clement recalls, the Corinthians had been “obedient to those who had the rule over you, and giving all fitting honour to the presbyters among you” (ch. 1). His remedy is submission to that same order:

… only let the flock of Christ live on terms of peace with the presbyters set over it.

— First Epistle to the Corinthians, ch. 54, source (Roberts–Donaldson translation, Ante-Nicene Fathers; cf. New Advent).1 And to the ringleaders directly:

Ye therefore, who laid the foundation of this sedition, submit yourselves to the presbyters, and receive correction so as to repent, bending the knees of your hearts.

— First Epistle to the Corinthians, ch. 57, source.1 The governing office at Corinth is thus a plurality — “the presbyters,” a college of them, “set over” the flock — and the sin Clement writes to correct is the deposing of some of their number. No single presiding bishop of Corinth is named or appealed to anywhere in the letter; the authority he invokes against the sedition is the presbyteral college and, behind it, the apostolic appointment that constituted it.

The apostles appointed “bishops and deacons” — a two‑order ministry (1 Clement 42)

… And thus preaching through countries and cities, they appointed the first-fruits [of their labours], having first proved them by the Spirit, to be bishops and deacons of those who should afterwards believe. Nor was this any new thing, since indeed many ages before it was written concerning bishops and deacons. For thus saith the Scripture in a certain place, “I will appoint their bishops in righteousness, and their deacons in faith.”

— First Epistle to the Corinthians, ch. 42, source.2 The order Clement names has two ranks, not three: “bishops and deacons” (episkopoi kai diakonoi) — the same pair Paul greets at Philippi (Philippians 1:1). He traces their authorization in a descending line — God → Christ → apostles → bishops and deacons — and reads it as no innovation but the fulfilment of Scripture (a loose citation of Isaiah 60:17 LXX). There is no third, higher office between the apostles and this pair; the “bishops” here are the local overseers, and (as the next section shows) the same men Clement elsewhere calls presbyters. The offering-and-order side of this passage — the divinely fixed “where and by whom” of the church’s ministry — is traced on his Ministerial Priesthood detail page, to keep the who‑governs question here from repeating the who‑offers question there.

“Bishop” and “presbyter” name one office — the parity datum (1 Clement 44)

Our apostles also knew, through our Lord Jesus Christ, that there would be strife on account of the office of the episcopate. For this reason, therefore, inasmuch as they had obtained a perfect fore-knowledge of this, they appointed those [ministers] already mentioned, and afterwards gave instructions, that when these should fall asleep, other approved men should succeed them in their ministry. We are of opinion, therefore, that those appointed by them, or afterwards by other eminent men, with the consent of the whole Church… cannot be justly dismissed from the ministry. For our sin will not be small, if we eject from the episcopate those who have blamelessly and holily fulfilled its duties. Blessed are those presbyters who, having finished their course before now, have obtained a fruitful and perfect departure… But we see that ye have removed some men of excellent behaviour from the ministry, which they fulfilled blamelessly and with honour.

— First Epistle to the Corinthians, ch. 44, source.3 This one paragraph uses both terms of the same men. The strife is over “the office of the episcopate” (episkopē); to depose the officers is to “eject from the episcopate”; and in the very next breath those same deposed officers are “those presbyters” (presbyteroi) whom it is sin to remove. Clement plainly treats episkopos and presbyteros as two names for one order — exactly the fluid usage of the New Testament, where the elders of Ephesus are told the Spirit made them episkopoi (Acts 20:17, 28) and Paul lists the qualifications of a presbyteros under the heading of the episkopos (Titus 1:5–7). This is the earliest post-biblical text adduced for the reading that bishop and presbyter were originally one office — weighed adversarially in the summary’s Arguments section.

How a man comes to office — appointment, succession, and “the consent of the whole Church” (1 Clement 44)

The same passage is the earliest description of how the ministry is filled and continued. The apostles, foreseeing the coming strife, “appointed those [ministers] already mentioned, and afterwards gave instructions, that when these should fall asleep, other approved men should succeed them in their ministry.” Valid holders are therefore those “appointed by them, or afterwards by other eminent men, with the consent of the whole Church” — and precisely because they hold by that orderly appointment and succession, they “cannot be justly dismissed from the ministry.”3 Two things stand together here that later readings pull apart: office passes by an orderly succession from the apostles (the plank the apostolic-succession argument rests on), and a lawful appointment carries “the consent of the whole Church” (the plank the congregational/election argument rests on). Clement holds both without felt tension — the congregation consents, the office is nonetheless a transmitted and irrevocable charge, not a mandate the assembly may revoke at will.

Notes

  • What it establishes for polity — and what it doesn’t. 1 Clement firmly attests, c. 96, a two‑order local ministry — bishops/presbyters (one office under two names) and deacons — appointed by the apostles and continued by an orderly succession with the church’s consent. It does not attest a single ruling bishop over the presbytery: no monarch-bishop of Corinth is named or appealed to, the governing body is “the presbyters” in the plural, and the terms episkopos and presbyteros are used interchangeably of the same men. The threefold, monarchical order appears clearly only a decade later, in Ignatius.
  • The pivot of two arguments. Because ch. 44 both grounds the ministry in an apostolically arranged succession and uses bishop/presbyter interchangeably, the same passage anchors both the parity reading (bishop = presbyter) and the apostolic-succession reading — which is why every side of the polity debate cites it. The two sub-claims are weighed on their own merits in the summary’s Arguments section, not here.
  • Rome writing to Corinth. That the Roman church wrote to intervene in another city’s dispute is sometimes read as the first assertion of a Roman primacy. Whether it shows primacy or merely the fraternal concern of one apostolic church for another is a distinct question, weighed where Roman primacy is treated — not on this timeline. What the letter shows for church order is the shape of the ministry it presupposes at Corinth, quoted above; the letter is issued in the name of “the Church of God which sojourns at Rome,” not over the name of Clement as a monarch-bishop.
  • The translation. Quotations are from the Roberts–Donaldson rendering (Ante-Nicene Fathers), which follows the long-known text; the fuller text recovered in the Codex Hierosolymitanus (1873) and Latin/Coptic/Syriac witnesses does not alter these passages. Chapter numbers follow the standard division.
  • Same author, elsewhere on this wiki. Clement also appears as a witness on the Ministerial Priesthood (the divinely ordered, succeeding ministry that presents the church’s offering) and the Old Testament canon (his use of Wisdom and Judith).
