Richard Hooker on the Government of the Church

Dates: c. 1554–1600 (the passages below from Of the Laws of Ecclesiastical Polity, Book VII, written in the 1590s and published posthumously) · Wikipedia
Summary: An Anglican defence of episcopacy. Hooker holds that the office of bishop is apostolic and of divine institution (“the first institution of bishops was from heaven, was even of God”), a distinct order with the sole power to ordain and to govern presbyters; yet he refuses to make episcopacy so absolutely necessary that its absence unchurches, allowing that in cases of necessity ordination without a bishop may hold.

Claims & evidence

A bishop is a distinct order — with the power to ordain and to govern presbyters

A Bishop is a minister of God, unto whom with permanent continuance there is given not only power of administering the Word and Sacraments, which power other Presbyters have; but also a further power to ordain ecclesiastical persons, and a power of chiefty in government over Presbyters as well as Laymen, a power to be by way of jurisdiction a Pastor even to Pastors themselves.

— Of the Laws of Ecclesiastical Polity, Book VII, ch. 2 (Keble VII.2.3), Keble ed., Works vol. 3 (facsimile at Project Canterbury).1 For Hooker the power to ordain and the jurisdiction over presbyters (the bishop’s chiefty) mark the bishop as a rank above the presbyter, not a presiding equal; the parity readings deny that rank.

Episcopacy is of divine institution — “from heaven, was even of God”

And shall we think that James was made bishop of Jerusalem, Evodius bishop of the church of Antioch, the Angels in the churches of Asia bishops, that bishops every where were appointed to take away factions, contentions, and schisms, without some like divine instigation and direction of the Holy Ghost? Wherefore let us not fear to be herein bold and peremptory, that if any thing in the Church’s government, surely the first institution of bishops was from heaven, was even of God, the Holy Ghost was the author of it.

— Of the Laws of Ecclesiastical Polity, Book VII, ch. 5 (Keble VII.5.10), Keble ed., Works vol. 3.2 Hooker argues that the raising of bishops to quell schism was itself Spirit-guided, so that the office is a divine institution, not a bare human convenience. He scopes the claim to the first institution, and elsewhere in Book VII he balances it, allowing that bishops hold their place partly “by the force of custom” and that the Church “hath power” to take from them.

Yet not absolutely necessary: in necessity, ordination without a bishop may hold

The whole Church visible being the true original subject of all power, it hath not ordinarily allowed any other than bishops alone to ordain: howbeit, as the ordinary course is ordinarily in all things to be observed, so it may be in some cases not unnecessary that we decline from the ordinary ways… there may be sometimes very just and sufficient reason to allow ordination made without a bishop.

— Of the Laws of Ecclesiastical Polity, Book VII, ch. 14 (Keble VII.14.11), Keble ed., Works vol. 3.3 He presses the concession further in the same chapter: “where the church must needs have some ordained, and neither hath nor can have possibly a bishop to ordain; in case of such necessity, the ordinary institution of God hath given oftentimes, and may give, place. And therefore we are not simply without exception to urge a lineal descent of power from the Apostles by continued succession of bishops in every effectual ordination.” On this ground Hooker declines to unchurch the foreign Reformed communions that lacked bishops; here his position differs from the stricter claims of Bancroft and Laud.

Reception and debate

  • Book VII was published only posthumously (first printed 1662, more than sixty years after Hooker’s death), so how fully it represents his own final wording has been questioned (⚠ unverified).
  • Of the three Elizabethan witnesses on this timeline, Hooker claims more for episcopacy than Whitgift, who made polity a thing indifferent (adiaphora) the magistrate may order, and allows more exception than Bancroft, who likened the denial of the bishop’s superiority to the teaching of Aerius.
