Richard Hooker on the Government of the Church

Dates: c. 1554–1600 (the passages below from Of the Laws of Ecclesiastical Polity, Book VII, written in the 1590s and published posthumously) · Wikipedia
Summary: The classic Anglican defence of episcopacy — and a deliberately moderated one. Hooker holds that the office of bishop is apostolic and of divine institution (“the first institution of bishops was from heaven, was even of God”), a distinct order with the sole power to ordain and to govern presbyters; yet he refuses to make episcopacy so absolutely necessary that its absence unchurches, conceding that in cases of necessity ordination without a bishop may hold. His is a iure divino that concedes more than Bancroft’s. The interpretation he serves is weighed in Is episcopacy of divine right?

Claims & evidence

A bishop is a distinct order — with the power to ordain and to govern presbyters

A Bishop is a minister of God, unto whom with permanent continuance there is given not only power of administering the Word and Sacraments, which power other Presbyters have; but also a further power to ordain ecclesiastical persons, and a power of chiefty in government over Presbyters as well as Laymen, a power to be by way of jurisdiction a Pastor even to Pastors themselves.

— Of the Laws of Ecclesiastical Polity, Book VII, ch. 2 (Keble VII.2.3), Keble ed., Works vol. 3 (facsimile at Project Canterbury).1 The bishop’s chiefty is precisely what the parity readings deny: for Hooker the power to ordain and the jurisdiction over presbyters mark the bishop as a rank above the presbyter, not a mere presiding equal.

Episcopacy is of divine institution — “from heaven, was even of God”

And shall we think that James was made bishop of Jerusalem, Evodius bishop of the church of Antioch, the Angels in the churches of Asia bishops, that bishops every where were appointed to take away factions, contentions, and schisms, without some like divine instigation and direction of the Holy Ghost? Wherefore let us not fear to be herein bold and peremptory, that if any thing in the Church’s government, surely the first institution of bishops was from heaven, was even of God, the Holy Ghost was the author of it.

— Of the Laws of Ecclesiastical Polity, Book VII, ch. 5 (Keble VII.5.10), Keble ed., Works vol. 3.2 This is Hooker’s strongest claim, and the flag his argument for episcopacy plants: the raising of bishops to quell schism was itself Spirit-guided, so the office is not a bare human convenience but a divine institution. Note, though, its careful scope — it is the first institution he calls divine, and elsewhere in Book VII he balances it, allowing that bishops hold their place partly “by the force of custom” and that the Church “hath power” to take from them.

Yet not absolutely necessary: in necessity, ordination without a bishop may hold

The whole Church visible being the true original subject of all power, it hath not ordinarily allowed any other than bishops alone to ordain: howbeit, as the ordinary course is ordinarily in all things to be observed, so it may be in some cases not unnecessary that we decline from the ordinary ways… there may be sometimes very just and sufficient reason to allow ordination made without a bishop.

— Of the Laws of Ecclesiastical Polity, Book VII, ch. 14 (Keble VII.14.11), Keble ed., Works vol. 3.3 He presses the concession further in the same chapter: “where the church must needs have some ordained, and neither hath nor can have possibly a bishop to ordain; in case of such necessity, the ordinary institution of God hath given oftentimes, and may give, place. And therefore we are not simply without exception to urge a lineal descent of power from the Apostles by continued succession of bishops in every effectual ordination.” This is the hinge of Hooker’s moderation — the ground on which he will not un-church the foreign Reformed communions that lacked bishops, and the reason his iure divino stops short of Bancroft’s and Laud’s harder claim.

Notes

  • Book VII was published only posthumously (first printed 1662, more than sixty years after Hooker’s death), so how fully it represents his own settled and final wording is debated; the substance, however, is continuous with the moderate conformism of the Books he saw through the press.
  • Hooker’s reading is the steelman weighed in Is episcopacy of divine right (iure divino)? — strong precisely because it concedes the defeaters (the New Testament parity of bishop and presbyter, the uneven emergence of the monarchical bishop) that sink the stricter form.
  • He stands between the two other Elizabethan witnesses on this timeline: harder than Whitgift, who made polity a thing indifferent (adiaphora) the magistrate may order, and softer than Bancroft, who branded the denial of the bishop’s superiority the ancient Aerian heresy.
  • On the New Testament parity Hooker’s divine-institution claim must answer, see Jerome’s thesis and the office-terms argument.
