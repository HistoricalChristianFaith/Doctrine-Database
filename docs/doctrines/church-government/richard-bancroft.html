Richard Bancroft on the Government of the Church

Dates: c. 1544–1610 (the sermon below preached at Paul’s Cross, 9 February 1588/9) · Wikipedia
Summary: The hardest of the Elizabethan defences of episcopacy. In his Paul’s Cross sermon Bancroft grounds the threefold ministry — bishops, priests, deacons — on the practice of the church “ever since the apostles times,” and brands the presbyterian denial of any difference between bishop and presbyter the ancient Aerian heresy. The sermon has long been celebrated (and, more recently, contested) as the first English assertion of episcopacy by divine right. His reading is weighed in Is episcopacy of divine right?

Claims & evidence

The threefold ministry stands “ever since the apostles times”

…you must know that the church of God ever since the apostles times, hath distributed the ecclesiasticall ministerie principallie into these three parts, Bishops, Priests, and Deacons: according as it is contained in the apology of the church of England…

— A Sermon Preached at Paules Crosse the 9. of Februarie… 1588, EEBO text (STC 1346).1 Bancroft’s positive case rests on the office’s antiquity and apostolic origin: the settled threefold order is not a late accretion but the church’s ministry from the apostles onward.

To deny the difference between bishop and presbyter is the Aerian heresy

…he affirmed himselfe (being but a priest) to be equall in honour and dignitie with Eustathius a Bishop, and that there was no difference by the word of God betwixt a priest and a B. He used for proofe of these his assertions the very same arguments which nowe are used of those that maintaine his opinions: as that the apostles sometimes writing to priests and deacons, & somtimes to Bb. and deacons, should therby signifie, that a B. and a priest is all one. Which is an assertion (saith Epiphanius) Stultitiae plena: full of follie.

— A Sermon Preached at Paules Crosse, EEBO text.2 The parity argument from the New Testament’s interchange of titles (Philippians 1:1; Titus 1:5–7) — the very case Jerome and modern scholarship make — Bancroft answers not on its merits but by tarring it with the name of Aerius of Sebaste, the fourth-century presbyter Epiphanius catalogued a heretic for pressing exactly this equality and breaking communion over it.

The bishops’ superiority over the clergy defended against Marprelate

…Martin… taking upon him with Aerius to proove an equality in the ministerie, and that there ought to be no difference betwixt a B. and a priest, commeth at last to these wordes; There was never anie but Antichristian popes and popelings that ever claimed this authoritie (he meaneth the superioritie which Bb. have over the clergie) especially when the matter was gainsaid, &c.

— A Sermon Preached at Paules Crosse, EEBO text.2 Against the anonymous Marprelate tracts Bancroft defends outright “the superioritie which Bb. have over the clergie” as no Antichristian usurpation but the church’s apostolic order.

Notes

  • The “first jure divino” fame is itself contested. The sermon’s reputation as the first Anglican assertion of the divine right of bishops does not come from its own text so much as from its reception: it was Bancroft’s opponents (notably Sir Francis Knollys, who charged him with teaching that bishops held their superiority “of God rather than the queen,” a threat to the Royal Supremacy) and later writers (Strype in the eighteenth century, Daniel Neal’s History of the Puritans) who fixed the label on it. W. D. J. Cargill Thompson’s reconsideration argues that the sermon in fact asserts the antiquity and apostolic origin of episcopacy and the Aerian-heresy charge, but stops short of a strict “bishops are a distinct order by God’s express command” thesis.3
  • The harder iure divino claim — and its (contested) reconciliation with the Royal Supremacy — is generally located slightly later, in Hadrian Saravia and Thomas Bilson in the early 1590s, Bancroft’s own Survay of the Pretended Holy Discipline (1593), and its hardening under Archbishop Laud.3
  • Bancroft is the hard pole of this timeline’s three Elizabethan witnesses: sharper than Hooker, whose iure divino concedes ordination without a bishop in necessity, and the direct antagonist of the position Whitgift defended, that polity is a thing indifferent. His overstatement — treating the parity case as merely a revived heresy rather than answering its proof-texts — is weighed on the argument page.
