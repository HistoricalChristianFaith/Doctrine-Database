Richard Bancroft on the Government of the Church

Dates: c. 1544–1610 (the sermon below preached at Paul’s Cross, 9 February 1588/9) · Wikipedia
Summary: An Elizabethan defence of episcopacy. In his Paul’s Cross sermon Bancroft grounds the threefold ministry — bishops, priests, deacons — on the practice of the church “ever since the apostles times,” and identifies the presbyterian denial of any difference between bishop and presbyter with the teaching of Aerius, whom Epiphanius listed among the heresies. McClintock and Strong called Bancroft “the first Anglican divine who publicly maintained the divine right of bishops”; W. D. J. Cargill Thompson (1969) reads the sermon as asserting the antiquity and apostolic origin of episcopacy without a strict divine-right thesis (see the notes below).

Claims & evidence

The threefold ministry stands “ever since the apostles times”

…you must know that the church of God ever since the apostles times, hath distributed the ecclesiasticall ministerie principallie into these three parts, Bishops, Priests, and Deacons: according as it is contained in the apology of the church of England…

— A Sermon Preached at Paules Crosse the 9. of Februarie… 1588, EEBO text (STC 1346).1 Bancroft rests his case on the office’s antiquity and apostolic origin: on his account the threefold order has been the church’s ministry from the apostles onward.

Bancroft likens the parity argument to the teaching of Aerius

…he affirmed himselfe (being but a priest) to be equall in honour and dignitie with Eustathius a Bishop, and that there was no difference by the word of God betwixt a priest and a B. He used for proofe of these his assertions the very same arguments which nowe are used of those that maintaine his opinions: as that the apostles sometimes writing to priests and deacons, & somtimes to Bb. and deacons, should therby signifie, that a B. and a priest is all one. Which is an assertion (saith Epiphanius) Stultitiae plena: full of follie.

— A Sermon Preached at Paules Crosse, EEBO text.2 Bancroft answers the parity argument from the New Testament’s interchange of titles (Philippians 1:1; Titus 1:5–7), which is also argued from Jerome, by identifying it with the teaching of Aerius of Sebaste, the fourth-century presbyter whom Epiphanius catalogued among the heresies for teaching this equality and breaking communion over it.

The bishops’ superiority over the clergy defended against Marprelate

…Martin… taking upon him with Aerius to proove an equality in the ministerie, and that there ought to be no difference betwixt a B. and a priest, commeth at last to these wordes; There was never anie but Antichristian popes and popelings that ever claimed this authoritie (he meaneth the superioritie which Bb. have over the clergie) especially when the matter was gainsaid, &c.

— A Sermon Preached at Paules Crosse, EEBO text.2 Against the anonymous Marprelate tracts Bancroft defends “the superioritie which Bb. have over the clergie” as the church’s apostolic order, not an Antichristian usurpation.

Reception and debate

  • The sermon’s reputation. W. D. J. Cargill Thompson argues that the sermon’s reputation as the first Anglican assertion of the divine right of bishops comes less from its own text than from its reception: on his account it was Bancroft’s opponents (notably Sir Francis Knollys, who charged him with teaching that bishops held their superiority “of God rather than the queen,” a threat to the Royal Supremacy) and later writers (Strype in the eighteenth century, Daniel Neal’s History of the Puritans) who fixed the label on it. He reads the sermon as asserting the antiquity and apostolic origin of episcopacy and the Aerian charge, stopping short of a strict “bishops are a distinct order by God’s express command” thesis.3
  • Stricter iure divino statements, and the question of their reconciliation with the Royal Supremacy, are located in the studies cited slightly later: in Hadrian Saravia and Thomas Bilson in the early 1590s, Bancroft’s own Survay of the Pretended Holy Discipline (1593), and under Archbishop Laud.3
  • The timeline’s other Elizabethan witnesses: Hooker (Laws, Book VII, written in the 1590s) holds that “the first institution of bishops was from heaven” while allowing ordination without a bishop in necessity; Whitgift held polity a thing indifferent.
