The Jerusalem Council (Acts 15) on the Government of the Church

Dates: c. 49 (the apostolic conference at Jerusalem, as narrated in Acts 15) · Wikipedia
Summary: The earliest recorded church council, and the New Testament’s own picture of a question of doctrine and practice referred to “the apostles and elders” at Jerusalem and decided “with the whole church,” after deliberation and dispute, in a common decree (“it seemed good to the Holy Ghost, and to us”). Chrysostom reads James, who speaks last, as bishop, “invested with the chief rule.” Cyril of Jerusalem and Cyprian cite its decree as the pattern for a council’s Spirit-guided sentence.

Claims & evidence

The question referred to the apostles and elders at Jerusalem

When the circumcision controversy divided Antioch, the church sent a delegation “to the apostles and elders” at Jerusalem, who “came together for to consider of this matter” and decided it in open assembly after “much disputing”:

they determined that Paul and Barnabas, and certain other of them, should go up to Jerusalem unto the apostles and elders about this question… And the apostles and elders came together for to consider of this matter.

— Acts 15:2, 6 (KJV), Acts 15:2, 15:6.1 The body addressed is a plurality — “the apostles and elders” (hoi apostoloi kai hoi presbyteroi) — and the decision is reached after deliberation, with Peter and James each speaking in the assembly (Acts 15:7–21).

Chrysostom, in his homily on these verses, reads James, who speaks after Peter and Paul, as the bishop who held “the chief rule” in the assembly:

This (James) was bishop, as they say, and therefore he speaks last… No word speaks John here, no word the other Apostles, but held their peace, for James was invested with the chief rule, and think it no hardship.

— John Chrysostom, Homily 33 on Acts (on Acts 15:12–13), source.2

The whole church joins in, and the decree is issued in the plural

Then pleased it the apostles and elders, with the whole church, to send chosen men of their own company to Antioch… And they wrote letters by them after this manner; The apostles and elders and brethren send greeting unto the brethren which are of the Gentiles…

— Acts 15:22–23 (KJV), Acts 15:22–23.1 The decree goes out corporately — “the apostles and elders and brethren” — and “the whole church” concurs in the sending. Leaders acting with the consent of the congregation also appear in 1 Clement and in Cyprian’s rule that a bishop is chosen “by the suffrage of the whole brotherhood, and by the sentence of the bishops who had assembled.”

“It seemed good to the Holy Ghost, and to us”

For it seemed good to the Holy Ghost, and to us, to lay upon you no greater burden than these necessary things…

— Acts 15:28 (KJV), Acts 15:28.1 Chrysostom comments on the double subject: in his reading the council does not level itself with God, yet it binds its own judgment to the Spirit’s and issues “simply a command: for it was the Spirit’s legislating”:

“For it seemed good to the Holy Ghost and to us” — it is not man’s doing, it says… not making themselves equal to Him — they are not so mad. But why does it put this so? Why did they add, “And to us,”… The one, “To the Holy Ghost,” that they may not deem it to be of man; the other, “To us,” that they may be taught that they also themselves admit the Gentiles.

— John Chrysostom, Homily 33 on Acts, source.3 Cyril of Jerusalem draws the same lesson — the decree, “though… written by men who were apostles,… was from the Holy Spirit and universal” — and Cyprian, in the thick of the third-century baptismal controversy, cites Acts 15:28 as the pattern for a council’s Spirit-guided common sentence.4

Reception and debate

  • Placement on the timeline. Acts 15 is a New Testament text, earlier than the sub-apostolic witnesses (1 Clement, the Didache, Ignatius), and so stands first on this timeline as the earliest narrative of a church controversy decided in council.
  • Features of the account cited in the polity debate. The deciding body at Jerusalem included the apostles as well as the elders, and “the whole church” concurred in the sending; later councils, such as Nicaea, were assemblies of bishops.
  • Cross-links. Decision by synod appears later on this timeline in the African councils of Cyprian (“no bishop of bishops”) and in the provincial-synod canons of Nicaea (canons 4–5). Where final authority sits in a corporate body — congregation, presbytery, or bishops — is among the questions taken up under Disputed readings.
