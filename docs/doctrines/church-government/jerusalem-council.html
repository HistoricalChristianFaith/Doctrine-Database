The Jerusalem Council (Acts 15) on the Government of the Church

Dates: c. 49 (the apostolic conference at Jerusalem, as narrated in Acts 15) · Wikipedia
Summary: The earliest recorded church council, and the New Testament’s own picture of a question of doctrine and practice settled not by a single ruler but corporately — by “the apostles and elders… with the whole church,” deliberating, disputing, and issuing a common decree (“it seemed good to the Holy Ghost, and to us”). It is the scriptural charter every later conciliar and synodal polity appeals to; whether that charter establishes council-government as the apostolic form, or merely furnishes a precedent that presupposes the apostles’ own authority, is weighed in the conciliar-governance argument.

Claims & evidence

A disputed question is referred to a corporate body, not to one man

When the circumcision controversy divided Antioch, the church did not appeal to a single presiding officer but sent a delegation “to the apostles and elders” at Jerusalem, who “came together for to consider of this matter” and decided it in open assembly after “much disputing”:

they determined that Paul and Barnabas, and certain other of them, should go up to Jerusalem unto the apostles and elders about this question… And the apostles and elders came together for to consider of this matter.

— Acts 15:2, 6 (KJV), Acts 15:2, 15:6.1 The governing body is a plurality — “the apostles and elders” (hoi apostoloi kai hoi presbyteroi) — and the decision is reached by deliberation among them, Peter and James each speaking as members of the assembly rather than James pronouncing as a monarch-bishop over it.

The whole church joins in, and the decree is issued in the plural

Then pleased it the apostles and elders, with the whole church, to send chosen men of their own company to Antioch… And they wrote letters by them after this manner; The apostles and elders and brethren send greeting unto the brethren which are of the Gentiles…

— Acts 15:22–23 (KJV), Acts 15:22–23.1 The decree goes out corporately — “the apostles and elders and brethren” — and “the whole church” concurs in the sending, the same coupling of a governing college with the consent of the congregation that reappears in 1 Clement and in Cyprian’s rule that a bishop is chosen “by the suffrage of the whole brotherhood, and by the sentence of the bishops who had assembled.”

“It seemed good to the Holy Ghost, and to us”

For it seemed good to the Holy Ghost, and to us, to lay upon you no greater burden than these necessary things…

— Acts 15:28 (KJV), Acts 15:28.1 This is the phrase later councils made their own. Chrysostom reads the double subject carefully: the council does not level itself with God, yet it binds its own judgment to the Spirit’s and issues “simply a command: for it was the Spirit’s legislating”:

“For it seemed good to the Holy Ghost and to us” — it is not man’s doing, it says… not making themselves equal to Him — they are not so mad. But why does it put this so? Why did they add, “And to us,”… The one, “To the Holy Ghost,” that they may not deem it to be of man; the other, “To us,” that they may be taught that they also themselves admit the Gentiles.

— John Chrysostom, Homily 33 on Acts, source.2 Cyril of Jerusalem draws the same lesson — the decree, “though… written by men who were apostles,… was from the Holy Spirit and universal” — and Cyprian, in the thick of the third-century baptismal controversy, cites Acts 15:28 as the pattern for a council’s Spirit-guided common sentence.3

Notes

  • Why it sits at the head of this timeline. Acts 15 is New Testament — earlier than the sub-apostolic witnesses (1 Clement, the Didache, Ignatius) and prior to any of the developed forms of government. It is placed first not as one more father’s opinion but as the scriptural datum they all reason from: the church’s memory of the apostles themselves deciding a controversy in council.
  • What it does and does not settle. The chapter plainly shows corporate, deliberative decision as an apostolic practice. What it does not settle by itself is whether council-government is the form of church order or a supplement to it: the deciding body at Jerusalem included the apostles, whose personal authority is not repeatable, and later councils are assemblies of bishops, so conciliarism presupposes rather than replaces the standing ministry. That adjudication is made on the conciliar-governance argument page.
  • Cross-links. The corporate decree of Acts 15 is the seed of the collegial episcopate of Cyprian (the African synods; “no bishop of bishops”) and of the provincial-synod machinery legislated at Nicaea (canons 4–5). The question of where final authority sits in a corporate body — congregation, presbytery, or bishops — is the seam this timeline’s Arguments work.
