Irenaeus of Lyons on the Government of the Church

Dates: c. 180 (Against Heresies, esp. Book III, written c. 175–185) · Wikipedia
Summary: Irenaeus, bishop of Lyons, sets out the succession argument against the Gnostic teachers. Against those who claimed a secret tradition passed privately from the apostles, he answers that the apostolic teaching is public and traceable: one can name the bishops the apostles installed in the churches and follow the list down to the present, and Rome’s list is his specimen. In his argument the succession carries doctrinal continuity — the churches that can show a public teaching-line from the apostles hold the faith the apostles taught — and he also says that a successor who falls away would be “the direst calamity,” and that presbyters in the succession are to be followed where conduct is blameless and speech “unadulterated and incorrupt.”

Claims & evidence

The tradition of the apostles and the succession of bishops (Against Heresies III.3.1)

It is within the power of all, therefore, in every Church, who may wish to see the truth, to contemplate clearly the tradition of the apostles manifested throughout the whole world; and we are in a position to reckon up those who were by the apostles instituted bishops in the Churches, and [to demonstrate] the succession of these men to our own times; those who neither taught nor knew of anything like what these [heretics] rave about.

— Against Heresies, Book III, ch. 3.1, source (Roberts–Donaldson translation, Ante-Nicene Fathers; cf. New Advent).1 The argument answers the Gnostic claim to a hidden tradition: if the apostles had kept back secret mysteries, Irenaeus reasons, they would have entrusted them to the men to whom they handed over the churches — so what those public successors teach is the apostolic deposit, and it contains nothing of what the heretics “rave about.”

In the same passage he says that a successor is a boon if he discharges his functions honestly:

… whom also they were leaving behind as their successors, delivering up their own place of government to these men; which men, if they discharged their functions honestly, would be a great boon [to the Church], but if they should fall away, the direst calamity.

— Against Heresies, Book III, ch. 3.1, source.1 In Irenaeus’s words a successor who should “fall away” would be “the direst calamity.”

The Roman list, Polycarp at Smyrna, and John at Ephesus (Against Heresies III.3.2–4)

The blessed apostles, then, having founded and built up the Church, committed into the hands of Linus the office of the episcopate… To him succeeded Anacletus; and after him, in the third place from the apostles, Clement was allotted the bishopric… To this Clement there succeeded Evaristus… [down to] Eleutherius [who] does now, in the twelfth place from the apostles, hold the inheritance of the episcopate. In this order, and by this succession, the ecclesiastical tradition from the apostles, and the preaching of the truth, have come down to us.

— Against Heresies, Book III, ch. 3.3, source.2 Rather than “reckon up the successions of all the Churches” — “very tedious” — Irenaeus offers Rome as his specimen (III.3.2), then Smyrna and Ephesus (III.3.4): Polycarp “was… by apostles in Asia, appointed bishop of the Church in Smyrna,” whom Irenaeus himself “saw in my early youth”; and the church at Ephesus, “founded by Paul, and having John remaining among them… until the times of Trajan, is a true witness of the tradition of the apostles.” The lists serve the point of III.3.1: in Irenaeus’s argument the teaching can be checked, and the line so checked teaches nothing of the Gnostics’ doctrine. (What Irenaeus means by Rome’s “pre-eminent authority” is taken up on the primacy of Rome timeline.)

Obedience to the presbyters who have the succession (Against Heresies IV.26.2, 5)

Wherefore it is incumbent to obey the presbyters who are in the Church,—those who, as I have shown, possess the succession from the apostles; those who, together with the succession of the episcopate, have received the certain gift of truth (charisma veritatis certum), according to the good pleasure of the Father.

— Against Heresies, Book IV, ch. 26.2, source (cf. New Advent).3 Two features of the passage figure in the polity debate. First, the officers who “possess the succession” are here called presbyters, who hold it “together with the succession of the episcopate” — usage set beside that of 1 Clement and the New Testament in the question Were “bishop” and “presbyter” originally one office? Second, a few lines on Irenaeus says the truth is to be learned from those in the succession “among whom exists that which is sound and blameless in conduct, as well as that which is unadulterated and incorrupt in speech,” and he warns against those “who are believed to be presbyters by many, but serve their own lusts.”3

Reception and debate

  • Placement on the timeline. Against Heresies III.3 is the earliest extant extended argument that the apostolic faith is shown by a public, traceable succession of teachers from the apostles. In it the succession is tied to continuity of public teaching, and the faithfulness of the successors is part of the argument (III.3.1; IV.26.2–5).
  • Presbyter or bishop? In III.3 Irenaeus speaks of a succession of bishops and lists the Roman bishops singly; in IV.26 those who possess the succession are called presbyters. How the Roman list relates to the plural leadership of the earlier Roman texts (1 Clement, Hermas) is taken up in Was the single-bishop episcopate in place everywhere by c. 110, or did it emerge unevenly? and Is the threefold ministry apostolic in origin, or a second-century development? under Disputed readings.
  • The sacramental side is traced elsewhere. Irenaeus’s teaching on the Eucharist and the ministry that offers it — the priestly rather than the governing aspect — is on his Ministerial Priesthood page. This page keeps to the who-governs / how-is-the-faith-guaranteed question.
  • Same author, elsewhere on this wiki. Irenaeus also appears on the canon, ministerial priesthood, Real Presence, and other timelines. The modern Baptist successionism claim traces a chain of dissenting congregations where Irenaeus traces a chain of episcopal sees.
