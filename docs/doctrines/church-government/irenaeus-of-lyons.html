Irenaeus of Lyons on the Government of the Church

Dates: c. 180 (Against Heresies, esp. Book III, written c. 175–185) · Wikipedia
Summary: Irenaeus is the father who forged the succession argument into a weapon against the Gnostics. Against heretics who claimed a secret tradition passed privately from the apostles, he answers that the real apostolic teaching is public and traceable: any church can name the bishops the apostles installed and follow the list down to the present. For him the point of the succession is doctrinal continuity — the churches that can show an unbroken public teaching-line hold the faith the apostles taught; the Gnostics, who can show no such line, are exposed as innovators. His witness is foundational for the episcopal reading of authority; but read closely he grounds the guarantee in fidelity, not in the mere fact of ordination.

Claims & evidence

The apostolic tradition is public and traceable — anyone can reckon up the succession of bishops (Against Heresies III.3.1)

It is within the power of all, therefore, in every Church, who may wish to see the truth, to contemplate clearly the tradition of the apostles manifested throughout the whole world; and we are in a position to reckon up those who were by the apostles instituted bishops in the Churches, and [to demonstrate] the succession of these men to our own times; those who neither taught nor knew of anything like what these [heretics] rave about.

— Against Heresies, Book III, ch. 3.1, source (Roberts–Donaldson translation, Ante-Nicene Fathers; cf. New Advent).1 The argument is aimed squarely at the Gnostic claim to a hidden tradition: if the apostles had kept back secret mysteries, Irenaeus reasons, they would have entrusted them precisely to the men to whom they handed over the churches — so what those public successors teach is the apostolic deposit, and it contains nothing of what the heretics “rave about.” The succession is thus an epistemic test of doctrine, not yet a theory of sacramental grace: the true faith is the one you can trace out in the open.

He states in the same breath the honest limit of the argument — a successor is a boon only if faithful:

… whom also they were leaving behind as their successors, delivering up their own place of government to these men; which men, if they discharged their functions honestly, would be a great boon [to the Church], but if they should fall away, the direst calamity.

— Against Heresies, Book III, ch. 3.1, source.1 The office does not sanctify its holder automatically; a bishop who “falls away” is a calamity, not a guarantee. That qualification is decisive for how much weight the succession can bear.

The Roman list, Polycarp at Smyrna, and John at Ephesus — specimens of the traceable line (Against Heresies III.3.2–4)

The blessed apostles, then, having founded and built up the Church, committed into the hands of Linus the office of the episcopate… To him succeeded Anacletus; and after him, in the third place from the apostles, Clement was allotted the bishopric… To this Clement there succeeded Evaristus… [down to] Eleutherius [who] does now, in the twelfth place from the apostles, hold the inheritance of the episcopate. In this order, and by this succession, the ecclesiastical tradition from the apostles, and the preaching of the truth, have come down to us.

— Against Heresies, Book III, ch. 3.3, source.2 Rather than “reckon up the successions of all the Churches” — “very tedious” — Irenaeus offers Rome as his specimen (III.3.2), then Smyrna and Ephesus (III.3.4): Polycarp “was… by apostles in Asia, appointed bishop of the Church in Smyrna,” whom Irenaeus himself “saw in my early youth”; and the church at Ephesus, “founded by Paul, and having John remaining among them… until the times of Trajan, is a true witness of the tradition of the apostles.” The lists are marshalled to make the same point as III.3.1: the teaching is checkable, and the checked line teaches no Gnosticism. (What Irenaeus means by Rome’s “pre-eminent authority” — whether primacy of jurisdiction or of witness — is a separate question, weighed on the primacy-of-Rome timeline, not here.)

Obey the presbyters who have the succession — because they keep the faith sound (Against Heresies IV.26.2, 5)

Wherefore it is incumbent to obey the presbyters who are in the Church,—those who, as I have shown, possess the succession from the apostles; those who, together with the succession of the episcopate, have received the certain gift of truth (charisma veritatis certum), according to the good pleasure of the Father.

— Against Heresies, Book IV, ch. 26.2, source (cf. New Advent).3 Two things are worth marking. First, the officers who “possess the succession” are here called presbyters, and the succession is “of the presbytery” as much as “of the episcopate” — the same fluidity of title found in 1 Clement and the New Testament, a generation after Ignatius’s sharp threefold order. Second, Irenaeus at once conditions the obedience on fidelity: a few lines on he says such presbyters are to be followed only where there is “that which is sound and blameless in conduct… unadulterated and incorrupt in speech,” and elsewhere he warns that those “who are believed to be presbyters by many, but serve their own lusts” are to be shunned. The “gift of truth” travels with the office, but it is guarded by the office-holder’s keeping of the apostolic teaching, not conferred as an automatic charism.3

Notes

  • What it establishes for polity. Irenaeus gives the earliest fully-developed argument (not just a picture) that the apostolic faith is guaranteed by a public, traceable succession of teachers from the apostles. Against the Gnostic appeal to secret tradition this is a strong move: the churches can name their bishops back to the apostles; the heretics cannot. It is the seed of every later doctrine of apostolic succession — but in Irenaeus the succession serves doctrine (continuity of public teaching), and he never treats it as a mechanical or sacramental transmission that makes a see orthodox regardless of what it teaches.
  • Presbyter or bishop? In III.3 Irenaeus speaks of a “succession of bishops” and lists the Roman bishops singly; in IV.26 the very same succession is a succession of presbyters. The overlap of titles — and the fact that the earliest “list” is best read as the leading names of a presbyter-college retrojected into a single line — is folded into the argument over the succession on the summary’s Arguments section, and bears on whether the monarchical episcopate the list presupposes is apostolic or a later drawing-together.
  • The sacramental side is traced elsewhere. Irenaeus’s teaching on the Eucharist and the ministry that offers it — the priestly rather than the governing aspect — is on his Ministerial Priesthood page. This page keeps to the who-governs / how-is-the-faith-guaranteed question.
  • Same author, elsewhere on this wiki. Irenaeus also appears on the canon, ministerial priesthood, Real Presence, and other timelines. His succession argument is also the taproot the modern Baptist successionism claim inverts — substituting a chain of dissenting congregations for the chain of episcopal sees.
