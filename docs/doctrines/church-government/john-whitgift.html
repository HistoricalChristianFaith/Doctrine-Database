John Whitgift on the Government of the Church

Dates: c. 1530–1604 (the passages below from The Defence of the Answer to the Admonition, 1574) · Wikipedia
Summary: The founding English statement of the adiaphorist case. Against Thomas Cartwright in the Admonition Controversy, Whitgift — later Archbishop of Canterbury — held that Scripture prescribes no one form of church government as perpetually binding: polity is a thing indifferent (adiaphora) which the Christian magistrate may order for the good of the church, so that episcopacy is retained by the realm’s authority and the church’s antiquity, not by strict divine command. His is the counter-pole to episcopacy iure divino, weighed in Is episcopacy of divine right?

Claims & evidence

No one form of government is prescribed in Scripture as necessary or unalterable

I confess that in a church collected together in one place, and at liberty, government is necessary in the second kind of necessity; but that any one kind of government is so necessary that without it the church cannot be saved, or that it may not be altered into some other kind thought to be more expedient, I utterly deny; and the reasons that move me so to do be these: The first is, because I find no one certain and perfect kind of government prescribed or commanded in the scriptures to the church of Christ; which no doubt should have been done, if it had been a matter necessary unto the salvation of the church.

— The Defence of the Answer to the Admonition (1574), Tractate II, Works of John Whitgift (Parker Society), vol. 1, p. 184.1 This is the hinge of Whitgift’s whole case: because no single polity is commanded in Scripture, no polity is necessary to salvation, and any may be “altered into some other kind thought to be more expedient.”

Scripture does not set down one perpetual form for all times and places

…I deny that the scriptures do express particularly everything that is to be done in the church… or that it doth set down any one certain form and kind of government of the church, to be perpetual for all times, persons, and places, without alteration…

— The Defence of the Answer to the Admonition (1574), Tractate II, Works, vol. 1, p. 191.2 Where the presbyterian platform read a fixed discipline off the New Testament, Whitgift finds the text deliberately silent on the “certain form,” leaving it to the church and the godly prince to settle.

Against Cartwright’s claim that the presbyterian discipline is divinely fixed

The whole Defence answers Thomas Cartwright (c. 1535–1603), who held that Scripture — above all the Calvinist pattern of pastors, doctors, elders, and deacons — prescribes one form of discipline, perpetually binding, which the church may not alter. Whitgift’s repeated “I utterly deny… that it may not be altered” is the direct rebuttal of that thesis. Cartwright’s positive position — the founding English statement of presbyterian iure divino — belongs with the presbyterian case; its standard scholarly treatment is A. F. Scott Pearson’s.3

Notes

  • Whitgift’s adiaphorism was the Elizabethan establishment’s initial rationale for its bishops — order, antiquity, and the royal law, not strict divine right. The divine-right argument was added afterward by Bancroft and, in moderated form, Hooker; that later turn is exactly what makes the iure divino question live.
  • His position is the counter-pole the argument page folds in as the standing adiaphorist defeater: if no one polity is commanded, then episcopacy cannot bind iure divino in the strict, church-unmaking sense — a point the Church of England’s own practice of receiving non-episcopally-ordained foreign Reformed ministers reinforced.
  • The parity of bishop and presbyter that Cartwright and the presbyterians pressed against him is weighed on its own in Jerome’s thesis and the office-terms argument.
