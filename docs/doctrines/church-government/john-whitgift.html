John Whitgift on the Government of the Church

Dates: c. 1530–1604 (the passages below from The Defence of the Answer to the Admonition, 1574) · Wikipedia
Summary: An Elizabethan statement of the adiaphorist case. Against Thomas Cartwright in the Admonition Controversy, Whitgift — later Archbishop of Canterbury — held that Scripture prescribes no one form of church government as perpetually binding: polity is a thing indifferent (adiaphora) which the Christian magistrate may order for the good of the church, so that episcopacy is retained by the realm’s authority and the church’s antiquity, not by strict divine command.

Claims & evidence

No one form of government is prescribed in Scripture as necessary or unalterable

I confess that in a church collected together in one place, and at liberty, government is necessary in the second kind of necessity; but that any one kind of government is so necessary that without it the church cannot be saved, or that it may not be altered into some other kind thought to be more expedient, I utterly deny; and the reasons that move me so to do be these: The first is, because I find no one certain and perfect kind of government prescribed or commanded in the scriptures to the church of Christ; which no doubt should have been done, if it had been a matter necessary unto the salvation of the church.

— The Defence of the Answer to the Admonition (1574), Tractate II, Works of John Whitgift (Parker Society), vol. 1, p. 184.1 Whitgift reasons that because no single polity is commanded in Scripture, no polity is necessary to salvation, and any may be “altered into some other kind thought to be more expedient.”

Scripture does not set down one perpetual form for all times and places

…I deny that the scriptures do express particularly everything that is to be done in the church… or that it doth set down any one certain form and kind of government of the church, to be perpetual for all times, persons, and places, without alteration…

— The Defence of the Answer to the Admonition (1574), Tractate II, Works, vol. 1, p. 191.2 Where the presbyterian platform read a fixed discipline off the New Testament, Whitgift holds that the text is silent on the “certain form,” leaving it to the church and the godly prince to settle.

Against Cartwright’s claim that the presbyterian discipline is divinely fixed

The whole Defence answers Thomas Cartwright (c. 1535–1603), who held that Scripture — above all the Calvinist pattern of pastors, doctors, elders, and deacons — prescribes one form of discipline, perpetually binding, which the church may not alter. Whitgift’s repeated “I utterly deny… that it may not be altered” is his answer to that thesis. Cartwright’s own position, that the presbyterian discipline is of divine right, is treated by A. F. Scott Pearson and Peter Lake.3

Reception and debate

  • Whitgift defends the Elizabethan bishops on grounds of order, antiquity, and the royal law, not strict divine right. Bancroft’s Paul’s Cross sermon of 1589 has been associated with the divine-right claim for episcopacy (McClintock and Strong), an association W. D. J. Cargill Thompson (1969) reconsiders (see the Bancroft page and its notes); Hooker argued a form of it (c. 1594).
  • On Whitgift’s premise that no one polity is commanded, episcopacy does not bind iure divino in a sense that would unchurch non-episcopal churches. The Church of England in this period received foreign Reformed ministers who had not been ordained by bishops (⚠ unverified).
  • The parity of bishop and presbyter that Cartwright and the presbyterians pressed against him is the subject of the disputed reading Were “bishop” and “presbyter” originally one office?
