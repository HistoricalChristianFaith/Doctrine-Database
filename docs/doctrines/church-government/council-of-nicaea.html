The Council of Nicaea on the Government of the Church

Dates: AD 325 (the first ecumenical council, convened by Constantine; its disciplinary canons of church order) · Wikipedia
Summary: Beyond the Arian question, Nicaea legislated the shape of church government that had grown up in the provinces. Its canons make the standing organ of church order not the single bishop alone but the provincial synod under its metropolitan: a bishop is consecrated by the bishops of his province and confirmed by the metropolitan (canon 4); those bishops must assemble in synod twice a year to try disputed cases (canon 5); and the great sees of Alexandria, Rome, and Antioch keep their customary spheres over the provinces around them (canon 6). This is the earliest ecumenical charter of the metropolitan (and, in seed, patriarchal) system — the corporate, conciliar polity of the ancient church set down as law. (For the same council’s canon on the eucharistic ordering of the clergy, see the ministerial-priesthood page.)

Claims & evidence

A bishop is made by the province’s bishops, confirmed by the metropolitan (Canon 4)

It is by all means proper that a bishop should be appointed by all the bishops in the province; but should this be difficult, either on account of urgent necessity or because of distance, three at least should meet together, and the suffrages of the absent [bishops] also being given and communicated in writing, then the ordination should take place. But in every province the ratification of what is done should be left to the Metropolitan.

— Canons of Nicaea, Canon 4, source.1 No bishop is made by one man’s act: the ordaining authority is the college of the province’s bishops, and the metropolitan does not appoint but ratifies what the college has done — a corporate procedure directly continuous with Cyprian’s rule (a generation earlier) that ordination is “by the sentence of the bishops who had assembled.”

Provincial synods twice a year (Canon 5)

…in every province synods shall be held twice a year, in order that when all the bishops of the province are assembled together, such questions may by them be thoroughly examined…

— Canons of Nicaea, Canon 5, source.1 The council fixes a standing calendar of synods — one before Lent, one in autumn — making the regular assembly of the bishops, not the decree of any single see, the ordinary court of appeal for disputed cases (here, whether an excommunication was justly imposed). Corporate government is not left to emergencies; it is scheduled.

The customary spheres of Alexandria, Rome, and Antioch (Canon 6)

Let the ancient customs in Egypt, Libya and Pentapolis prevail, that the Bishop of Alexandria have jurisdiction in all these, since the like is customary for the Bishop of Rome also. Likewise in Antioch and the other provinces, let the Churches retain their privileges.

— Canons of Nicaea, Canon 6, source.2 The canon ratifies a settled, plural geography of oversight: three great sees each hold a customary sphere, and Rome’s is named as one instance of the same “custom,” not as a rule above the others — the germ of the later Eastern pentarchy. The council knows regional pre-eminence but no single universal head; it governs the church as a federation of provinces under their metropolitans, the greatest sees first among them.

Notes

  • What it establishes for church government. By 325 the corporate, conciliar polity is not a theory but the enacted law of the whole church: bishops are made by their colleagues, synods sit on a fixed calendar, and even the greatest sees hold spheres, not a monarchy. This is the mature institutional form of the pattern seeded in Acts 15 and pressed by Cyprian — government by the assembled bishops.
  • East and West diverge from here. The metropolitan-and-synod structure of canons 4–6 became the enduring shape of the Eastern church (provincial synods and, later, the pentarchy of five patriarchates). The Western reading of canon 6 — Rome’s “customary” sphere construed as a primacy of jurisdiction — is the seam where the two polities part, and is weighed with the primacy question, not here. Canon 6 names Rome only as a parallel case of provincial custom.
  • A council as a witness. As on the ministerial-priesthood timeline, a council is treated here as a single timeline “witness” dated to its sitting (AD 325) — the latest voice on this timeline, and the point at which the conciliar polity becomes ecumenical law.
