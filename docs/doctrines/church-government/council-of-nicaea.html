The Council of Nicaea on the Government of the Church

Dates: AD 325 (the first ecumenical council, convened by Constantine; its disciplinary canons of church order) · Wikipedia
Summary: Beside the Arian question, Nicaea issued disciplinary canons on church government in the provinces. Its canons give a standing role to the provincial synod under its metropolitan: a bishop is consecrated by the bishops of his province and confirmed by the metropolitan (canon 4); those bishops must assemble in synod twice a year to try disputed cases (canon 5); and the great sees of Alexandria, Rome, and Antioch keep their customary spheres over the provinces around them (canon 6). These are the earliest extant canons of an ecumenical council on the metropolitan system. (For the same council’s canon on the eucharistic ordering of the clergy, see the ministerial-priesthood page.)

Claims & evidence

A bishop is made by the province’s bishops, confirmed by the metropolitan (Canon 4)

It is by all means proper that a bishop should be appointed by all the bishops in the province; but should this be difficult, either on account of urgent necessity or because of distance, three at least should meet together, and the suffrages of the absent [bishops] also being given and communicated in writing, then the ordination should take place. But in every province the ratification of what is done should be left to the Metropolitan.

— Canons of Nicaea, Canon 4, source.1 In the canon the bishops of the province appoint, at least three meeting together, and the metropolitan ratifies what is done. Cyprian, in the 250s, had described a bishop chosen “by the sentence of the bishops who had assembled.”

Provincial synods twice a year (Canon 5)

…in every province synods shall be held twice a year, in order that when all the bishops of the province are assembled together, such questions may by them be thoroughly examined…

— Canons of Nicaea, Canon 5, source.1 The council fixes a standing calendar of synods — one before Lent, one in autumn — at which the assembled bishops of the province examine disputed cases (here, whether an excommunication was justly imposed).

The customary spheres of Alexandria, Rome, and Antioch (Canon 6)

Let the ancient customs in Egypt, Libya and Pentapolis prevail, that the Bishop of Alexandria have jurisdiction in all these, since the like is customary for the Bishop of Rome also. Likewise in Antioch and the other provinces, let the Churches retain their privileges.

— Canons of Nicaea, Canon 6, source.2 The canon confirms the “ancient customs” of Alexandria’s jurisdiction, citing the like custom of the Bishop of Rome, and the privileges of Antioch and the other provinces. How Rome’s mention in the canon is to be read is taken up on the primacy of Rome timeline.

Reception and debate

  • Features of the canons cited in the polity debate. By the canons of 325, bishops are appointed by the bishops of their province and ratified by the metropolitan, provincial synods sit twice a year, and the sees of Alexandria, Rome, and Antioch keep customary spheres. Corporate decision by synod appears earlier on this timeline in Acts 15 and in Cyprian’s African councils.
  • Canon 6 in East and West. The metropolitan-and-synod structure of canons 4–6 is set beside the later patriarchal structure of the Eastern churches, and canon 6’s mention of Rome’s “customary” sphere figures in the debate over Roman primacy; both are taken up on the primacy of Rome timeline.
  • A council as a witness. As on the ministerial-priesthood timeline, a council is treated here as a single timeline “witness” dated to its sitting (AD 325).
