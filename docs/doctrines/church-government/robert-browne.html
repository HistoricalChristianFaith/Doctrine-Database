Robert Browne on the Government of the Church

Dates: c. 1550–1633 (the church-order argument below from A Treatise of Reformation without Tarrying for Any, Middelburg, 1582) · Wikipedia
Summary: A manifesto of English Separatism, stating the congregational position in the Reformation-era quarrel over the form of church government. Against both the bishops’ establishment and the presbyterians’ hope of a reform enacted by Parliament, Browne argues that a true church is a company gathered by covenant under Christ’s rule — a willing, self-governing body that holds Christ’s keys directly and may reform itself at once, “without tarrying for any” magistrate or bishop.

Claims & evidence

The church is a covenanted company of the willing

The Lord’s people is of the willing sort.

— A Treatise of Reformation without Tarrying for Any (1582).1 For Browne a true church is not the whole baptized nation under its bishops but a gathered company bound to God and to one another by a voluntary covenant — those who “call for the covenant” and hold fast to the Lord, joined by conscience rather than compulsion. Because the church is constituted by that willing covenant and not by the magistrate’s writ, the power to order its own life belongs to the company itself.

Reformation need not wait on the magistrate or the bishop

The tract’s title states its thesis: Reformation without Tarrying for Any. Browne’s quarrel is as much with the cautious puritans and presbyterians — who “say the time is not yet come to build the Lord’s house; they must tarry for the magistrates and for parliaments to do it” — as with the prelates. The kingdom of Christ, he answers, “is not by force, neither by an army or strength, as are the kingdoms of the world”; a covenanted congregation may and must reform itself now, gathering out of the corrupt establishment and setting its own house in order under Christ, without waiting for prince or Parliament to license the change.2

The keys and the discipline belong to the congregation

Because the gathered church holds Christ’s commission directly, it holds the keys — the power to admit, to censure, and to expel — and the choice of its own officers, as functions of the body and not of an external authority. Browne’s charge against the conforming ministry is precisely that they had surrendered this spiritual discipline to the civil power instead of exercising it within the congregation. The later Congregational standards — the Cambridge Platform, the Savoy Declaration, and John Owen — also lodge the keys and the discipline in the covenanted assembly, in their own formulations.3

Reception and debate

  • Browne’s name gave the early nickname “Brownists” to the Separatists and Congregationalists; two men, Elias Thacker and John Coppin, were hanged at Bury St Edmunds in 1583 for distributing his tracts. Browne himself later made his peace with the established church; the Separatist and Independent churches continued to hold the congregational principle he had stated.
  • Where Calvin gives the presbyterian pole (final authority in a college of elders) and Hooker and Bancroft the episcopal (a bishop set over the presbyters), Browne states the congregational position — final authority in the gathered assembly itself. All three appeal to the same New Testament texts.
  • The 1582 text survives in the original spelling in early editions (the Middelburg printing of Richard Schilders); the wording quoted here follows a modernized transcription, and the single blockquote is kept to that one line.
