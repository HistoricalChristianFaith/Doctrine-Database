John Owen and the Congregational Platforms on the Government of the Church

Dates: John Owen, 1616–1683 (the church-order passages below from The True Nature of a Gospel Church and its Government, published posthumously 1689; with the two corporate Congregational standards he crowns — the New-England Cambridge Platform of 1648 and the Savoy Declaration of 1658, of which Owen was a principal author) · Wikipedia
Summary: The mature, systematic statement of congregational (Independent) church order, a century after Browne first stated the principle blunt. Owen lodges all church power in Christ, and its ordinary seat and exercise in the particular congregation of officers and members; the Cambridge Platform and the Savoy Declaration confessionalize the same order — each local church complete in itself, its officers chosen by the church’s own suffrage, its discipline its own, synods advisory and not juridical. This is the congregational counter-pole to Calvin’s presbyterian elder-rule and to episcopacy iure divino; its reading of the New Testament is weighed adversarially in Does the gathered congregation hold final authority?

Claims & evidence

All church power is in Christ, its ordinary seat in the church itself

This power in actu primo, or fundamentally, is in the church itself; in actu secundo, or its exercise, in them that are especially called thereunto.

— John Owen, The True Nature of a Gospel Church, ch. “Of the polity, rule, or discipline of the church in general.”1 Owen’s whole account turns on this distinction: the authority Christ grants is fundamentally (in actu primo) in the gathered church as a body, though its exercise (in actu secundo) is committed to the officers the church calls. The rule is real, but ministerial and derived — “The first, original grant of all church power and privileges is made unto believers as such” — so office is not a power standing over the congregation from without but the ordered exercise, by its called officers, of a power seated in the body under Christ.2

The Cambridge Platform: the elders’ “power of office,” the brethren’s “power of privilege”

This power of government in the elders, doth not any wise prejudice the power of privilege in the brotherhood; as neither the power of privilege in the brethren, doth prejudice the power of government in the elders.

— A Platform of Church Discipline (the Cambridge Platform), ch. X, 1648.3 The New-England synod of 1648 divides ordinary church power into two: the “power of office… proper to the eldership” and the “power of privilege… belong[ing] unto the brotherhood.” The brethren’s power reaches to “choosing their own officers, whether elders or deacons… in admission of their own members,” and to the church’s “public censure” of an offender “whether by admonition or excommunication” — the local church complete in itself. Yet the same chapter has the elders “pronounce sentence with consent of the church”: a distributed authority, elders and brethren each with a part neither may override, not the sovereign, officer-less assembly of the strongest congregational rhetoric.4

The Savoy Declaration: each church the seat of Christ’s power, its officers chosen by its own suffrage

The Congregational platform Owen himself helped frame states the same order confessionally. The particular churches “intrusted with power from him” are “each of them… the seat of that power which he is pleased to communicate to his saints or subjects in this world” (§ V); and the way “for the calling of any person… unto the office of pastor, teacher, or elder in a church, is, that he be chosen thereunto by the common suffrage of the church itself, and solemnly set apart by fasting and prayer” (§ XI). So each congregation is complete for the administration of Christ’s ordinances and the calling of its own officers, and the wider assemblies of churches (synods) are for counsel and communion, not a superior court of jurisdiction over the particular church.5

Notes

  • Owen states the mature form of the principle Robert Browne first stated in 1582: the covenanted local congregation, under Christ, holds the keys and orders its own life. Where the Independents differ from the earlier Separatists is in temper, not in polity — Owen was Cromwell’s chaplain, Dean of Christ Church, and vice-chancellor of Oxford, arguing the congregational way from within the Puritan mainstream rather than from exile.
  • The two standards folded in here are to congregationalism what the Second Helvetic Confession (1566) and the Westminster Form of Presbyterial Church-Government (1645) are to Calvin’s presbyterian order — the corporate, confessional expression of the polity. The Savoy Declaration (1658) is the Independents’ revision of the Westminster Confession, appended to which is the “Institution of Churches” platform of congregational order.
  • That the congregational reading’s own charter documents distribute power between elders and brethren — rather than vesting it all in the assembly — is itself weighed as a caution against the strong “final authority” claim in Does the gathered congregation hold final authority? (assessment: contested). The rival readings are presbyterian elder-rule and episcopacy of divine right.
