The Shepherd of Hermas on the Government of the Church

Dates: c. 140–155 (the Shepherd, written at Rome; the Muratorian Fragment places its composition while Pius was bishop of Rome, though some of the Visions may preserve earlier material) · Wikipedia
Summary: A long apocalypse written at Rome in the mid-second century, a generation after 1 Clement and some decades after Ignatius’s letters, which in passing describes the Roman church’s leaders. Hermas is to read his book to the congregation “along with the presbyters who preside over the Church” (Vision 2.4.3), and Clement is named as the one who sends the church’s writings “to foreign countries.” When Hermas lists the church’s faithful leaders, he speaks of “apostles, bishops, teachers, and deacons” (Vision 3.5.1) and of “bishops given to hospitality” (Similitude 9.27), in the plural; the book names no single bishop of Rome. It is cited in the debate over when the single-bishop episcopate took hold at Rome (see Disputed readings).

Claims & evidence

“The presbyters who preside over the Church” and Clement (Vision 2.4.3)

You will write therefore two books, and you will send the one to Clemens and the other to Grapte. And Clemens will send his to foreign countries, for permission has been granted to him to do so. And Grapte will admonish the widows and the orphans. But you will read the words in this city, along with the presbyters who preside over the Church.

— Visions 2.4.3, source (Roberts–Donaldson translation, Ante-Nicene Fathers vol. 2; also at by_father).1 Two features of the passage figure in the polity debate. First, those who preside are named in the plural — “the presbyters who preside over the Church” (presbyteroi, proistamenoi, “presiding”). Second, Clement appears with a task — sending the church’s writings “to foreign countries” — set beside Grapte, who admonishes the widows and orphans. Irenaeus’s list, written c. 180, names Clement third bishop of Rome after the apostles (see Irenaeus).

“Apostles, bishops, teachers, and deacons” (Vision 3.5.1)

Those square white stones which fitted exactly into each other, are apostles, bishops, teachers, and deacons, who have lived in godly purity, and have acted as bishops and teachers and deacons chastely and reverently to the elect of God. Some of them have fallen asleep, and some still remain alive.

— Visions 3.5.1, source (also at by_father).2 In the vision of the tower — the church built of living stones — the well-fitted foundation stones are the church’s leaders, named in a single breath: “apostles, bishops, teachers, and deacons.” “Bishops” here is plural, listed with apostles, teachers, and deacons. The book does not state how the “bishops” of this passage relate to the presiding presbyters of Vision 2; whether they are the same leaders under two names, as with the usage cited from 1 Clement 44 and the New Testament (Acts 20:17, 28; Titus 1:5–7; Philippians 1:1), is part of the question Were “bishop” and “presbyter” originally one office?

“Bishops given to hospitality” (Similitude 9.27)

they who believed were the following: bishops given to hospitality, who always gladly received into their houses the servants of God, without dissimulation. And the bishops never failed to protect, by their service, the widows, and those who were in want, and always maintained a holy conversation.

— Similitudes 9.27, source (also at by_father).3 The ninth similitude, retelling the tower-vision, again names the office in the plural — “bishops given to hospitality” — and describes it by its work: receiving the travelling servants of God into their homes, and protecting the widows and the destitute.

Reception and debate

  • The book in the polity debate. The Shepherd is a devotional apocalypse, not a church-order manual. Its plural “presbyters who preside” and plural “bishops,” and its silence on a single bishop of Rome, are cited in Was the single-bishop episcopate in place everywhere by c. 110, or did it emerge unevenly? and Is the threefold ministry apostolic in origin, or a second-century development? under Disputed readings, where the readings of that silence are set out with their holders.
  • Clement and the Roman bishop-lists. Hermas has Clement sending the church’s writings to the churches abroad (Vision 2.4.3); 1 Clement is written in the name of the Roman church. Irenaeus (c. 180) gives a single line of Roman bishops — Linus, Anacletus, Clement — from the apostles. How the Roman list relates to the collective language of these Roman texts is taken up in the episcopate-emergence question above; what Rome’s letter to Corinth bears on Roman primacy is taken up on the primacy of Rome timeline.
  • The date. The Muratorian Fragment states that “Hermas wrote the Shepherd very recently, in our times, in the city of Rome, while bishop Pius, his brother, was occupying the [episcopal] chair of the church of the city of Rome” — that is, during the episcopate of Pius I, conventionally c. 140–155.4 The work is composite; an earlier date has been proposed for some of the Visions (see the overview cited in the note). The completed book is placed here at c. 140–155 with the Fragment, after Polycarp (c. 125) and before Irenaeus (c. 180).
  • The translation. Quotations are from the Roberts–Donaldson rendering (Ante-Nicene Fathers vol. 2), by the standard Vision/Similitude division; the Muratorian Fragment is given in Bruce Metzger’s translation.
