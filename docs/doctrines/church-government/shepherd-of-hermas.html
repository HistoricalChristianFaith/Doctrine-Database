The Shepherd of Hermas on the Government of the Church

Dates: c. 140–155 (the Shepherd, written at Rome; the Muratorian Fragment places its composition while Pius was bishop of Rome, though some of the Visions may preserve earlier material) · Wikipedia
Summary: A long, popular apocalypse written at Rome in the mid-second century — a generation after 1 Clement and at the very time Ignatius’s single-bishop order was hardening in Asia — and its incidental picture of the Roman church’s order is strikingly collective. Hermas is to read his book to the congregation “along with the presbyters who preside over the Church” (Vision 2.4.3): Rome is still governed by a college of presbyters, and Clement is named not as a monarch-bishop but as the officer who corresponds with the churches abroad. When Hermas lists the church’s builders and its faithful leaders, he speaks of “apostles, bishops, teachers, and deacons” (Vision 3.5.1) and of “bishops given to hospitality” (Similitude 9.27) — bishops in the plural, his offices functional and overlapping, with no single ruling bishop of Rome anywhere in view. Coming from Rome, c. 140–155, this is a load-bearing counter-datum for the uneven, late-at-Rome emergence of the monarchical episcopate: the polity Ignatius urged on Asia around 108 has not, on the evidence of this Roman book, reached Rome itself half a century later. Its interpretive weight is carried in the summary’s Arguments, not as a verdict against the antiquity of the office.

Claims & evidence

“The presbyters who preside over the Church” — and Clement, the officer who writes abroad (Vision 2.4.3)

You will write therefore two books, and you will send the one to Clemens and the other to Grapte. And Clemens will send his to foreign countries, for permission has been granted to him to do so. And Grapte will admonish the widows and the orphans. But you will read the words in this city, along with the presbyters who preside over the Church.

— Visions 2.4.3, source (Roberts–Donaldson translation, Ante-Nicene Fathers vol. 2; also at by_father).1 Two things stand out for Roman polity. First, the governing body Hermas is to read to is a plurality — “the presbyters who preside over the Church” (presbyteroi, in the plural, who proistamenoi, “preside”) — not a single bishop over them. This is the same presiding college of elders that 1 Clement presupposes at Rome and Corinth a generation earlier, unchanged. Second, Clement himself — whom later bishop-lists would enrol as an early monarch-bishop of Rome — appears here in a functional role: the one charged with sending the church’s writings “to foreign countries,” Rome’s correspondent with the churches abroad, set beside Grapte, who cares for the widows and orphans. The offices are described by what they do, and the man who “presides” over the reading is not one bishop but the presbyters together.

“Apostles, bishops, teachers, and deacons” — bishops in the plural (Vision 3.5.1)

Those square white stones which fitted exactly into each other, are apostles, bishops, teachers, and deacons, who have lived in godly purity, and have acted as bishops and teachers and deacons chastely and reverently to the elect of God. Some of them have fallen asleep, and some still remain alive.

— Visions 3.5.1, source (also at by_father).2 In the vision of the tower — the church built of living stones — the well-fitted foundation stones are the church’s leaders, named in a single breath: “apostles, bishops, teachers, and deacons.” The point for church order is the plural: Hermas knows “bishops” as a body, not a bishop as a single presiding officer, and he ranges the word alongside “teachers” and “deacons” as one of several overlapping ministries, not as a distinct order set over the rest. He does not distinguish “bishop” from “presbyter” at all — the presiding elders of Vision 2 and the plural bishops here are, on the face of the book, the same collective leadership under its two New-Testament names, the “two-terms-one-office” usage of 1 Clement 44 and the New Testament (Acts 20:17, 28; Titus 1:5–7; Philippians 1:1).

“Bishops given to hospitality” — the plural again, a ministry of service (Similitude 9.27)

they who believed were the following: bishops given to hospitality, who always gladly received into their houses the servants of God, without dissimulation. And the bishops never failed to protect, by their service, the widows, and those who were in want, and always maintained a holy conversation.

— Similitudes 9.27, source (also at by_father).3 The long ninth similitude, retelling the tower-vision, again reaches for the office in the plural — “bishops given to hospitality” — and again describes it by its function: receiving the travelling servants of God into their homes, sheltering the widows and the destitute. This is oversight as practical service and pastoral care, a body of bishops doing the diaconal work of hospitality and relief, not a single ruler exercising jurisdiction. Across all three passages Hermas’s terms are consistently collective and consistently functional — presiding presbyters, plural bishops, teachers, deacons — the picture of a Roman church still ordered by a college, not a monarch-bishop.

Notes

  • What it establishes for polity — and what it does not. The Shepherd is firsthand evidence that, at Rome around the middle of the second century, the church was still spoken of as governed by a college of presiding presbyters and plural “bishops,” with the two titles undifferentiated — the same two-order, collective ministry that 1 Clement attests for Rome c. 96. That is a real counter-datum to the claim that the monarchical episcopate was uniform and universal by the early-to-mid second century: it had not, on this evidence, taken hold at Rome itself even after Ignatius had urged it on the churches of Asia (c. 108) and Polycarp held it at Smyrna. But the book does not by itself prove the episcopate un-apostolic. It is a devotional apocalypse, not a church-order manual, and its offhand, functional vocabulary neither excludes a leading presbyter at Rome whom Hermas had no occasion to single out nor settles the question of what stood over the local college. What it shows is the episcopate’s uneven, region-by-region emergence — exactly what the developmental reading predicts and a delivered-whole apostolic blueprint does not — so its interpretive weight is carried in the summary’s Arguments (see Is the threefold ministry apostolic, or a second-century development?), where it is a datum in the debate, not a verdict in it.
  • The Clement datum, and Rome’s own polity. Hermas has Clement corresponding with the churches abroad on Rome’s behalf (Vision 2.4.3) — a functional role that matches the letter-writing church of 1 Clement, whose author is never named and which speaks throughout in the plural voice of the Roman presbytery. On the face of these two Roman witnesses, mid-second-century Rome had no monarch-bishop but a presiding college whose spokesman handled foreign correspondence — a bearing on the later question of Rome’s primacy that is weighed with that question, not here. (Later bishop-lists, from Irenaeus on, would retroject a single line of Roman bishops — Linus, Anacletus, Clement — back over this collegial period; whether that line is an exact archive or a retrojection onto a college is weighed in the summary’s Arguments under apostolic succession.)
  • The date. The Muratorian Fragment states that “Hermas wrote the Shepherd very recently, in our times, in the city of Rome, while bishop Pius, his brother, was occupying the [episcopal] chair of the church of the city of Rome” — that is, during the episcopate of Pius I, conventionally c. 140–155.4 The work is composite and was likely written in stages; some scholars date the earliest Visions nearer the turn of the century, but the completed book is anchored here at c. 140–155 with the Fragment, its place on the timeline — after Polycarp (c. 125), before Irenaeus (c. 180) — unaffected by the exact year. (The Fragment’s notice is also a small irony: it reports that Rome now has a “bishop” in the singular, Pius, at the very moment Hermas’s own book still speaks of the church’s leaders only in the collective — a hint that the monarchical title was settling at Rome across just these decades.)
  • The translation. Quotations are from the Roberts–Donaldson rendering (Ante-Nicene Fathers vol. 2), by the standard Vision/Similitude division; the Muratorian Fragment is given in Bruce Metzger’s translation.
