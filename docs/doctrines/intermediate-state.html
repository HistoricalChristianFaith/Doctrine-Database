The Intermediate State (the soul between death and resurrection)

What becomes of a person between death and the general resurrection? Scripture speaks of the dead in Sheol / Hades, of "Abraham's bosom" and a place of "torments" divided by a "great gulf" (Luke 16), of the penitent thief "with me in paradise" that very day (Luke 23:43), of the believer "absent from the body … present with the Lord" (2 Cor 5:8), and of the sinning angels cast into Tartarus (2 Pet 2:4). The witnesses below draw on these texts and answer two questions differently:

  1. Is the soul conscious in the interval? Does it live, perceive, rest, or suffer at once — or does it "sleep" until the resurrection?
  2. Is the place structured? Is Hades a single realm with compartments (Abraham's bosom / a place of torment / the angelic prison of Tartarus), or are there simply two final destinies, heaven and hell, entered immediately?

Several second- and third-century writers on this timeline (Justin, Irenaeus, Tertullian, Hippolytus) describe the souls of the dead kept in Hades, or in "the invisible place allotted to them by God," until the resurrection, the righteous at rest and the wicked in torment; Melito, Origen, Cyril of Jerusalem and others describe Christ's descent to the dead and the release of those held there. The Syriac writers Aphrahat and Ephrem describe the dead as sleeping, or as waiting on the borders of Paradise, until body and soul are reunited. Among the Latin witnesses, Gregory the Great, Aquinas and Benedict XII (1336) describe a judgement at death, a purgation for some (traced on the purgatory and prayer-for-the-dead timeline), the limbus patrum emptied at Christ's descent, and the vision of God for the purified before the general judgement. Among the Orthodox witnesses after John of Damascus, Mark of Ephesus at Ferrara–Florence (1438) affirms that the departed are helped by prayer while declining a purgatorial fire, and the Synod of Jerusalem (1672) teaches that souls go at once to rest or torment, neither complete before the resurrection. Among the Reformation witnesses, Calvin and the Westminster Confession (1646) affirm the soul's conscious survival and acknowledge two places only for souls separated from their bodies, while Luther and the Formula of Concord (1577) confess Christ's victorious descent into hell and decline to define its manner. Among recent witnesses, the Catechism of the Catholic Church (1992) restates the descent and the deliverance of the just; the Anglican scholar N. T. Wright describes a conscious "paradise" before the resurrection; and the Reformed pastor Doug Wilson describes a Hades divided between Paradise and Tartarus. How two texts bearing on the descent have been read is set out under Disputed readings below.

Timeline

1 Enoch (c. 300 BC)

1 Enoch gives a detailed map of a structured intermediate state: the spirits of the dead are gathered into "hollow places" in a great western mountain and separated into divisions — the righteous by "the bright spring of water," the sinners apart — "to receive them till the day of their judgement."1

2 Maccabees (c. 124 BC)

2 Maccabees is the earliest witness on this timeline to an offering for the dead: Judas Maccabeus, finding idolatrous tokens on his fallen soldiers, sends a sin offering to Jerusalem for them and "made a reconciliation for the dead, that they might be delivered from sin" — reasoning that, but for the resurrection, it would be "superfluous and vain to pray for the dead." The passage is also discussed on the purgatory and prayer-for-the-dead timeline.2

The Wisdom of Solomon (c. 50 BC)

The Wisdom of Solomon, an Alexandrian Jewish wisdom book roughly contemporary with Philo, speaks of post-mortem rest for the righteous: "the souls of the righteous are in the hand of God, and there shall no torment touch them"; though "in the sight of the unwise they seemed to die … they are in peace," "their hope full of immortality."3

The New Testament (c. AD 60–90)

In the New Testament, Jesus' account of the rich man and Lazarus sets "Abraham's bosom" against a place of "torments," with "a great gulf fixed" so that none may pass between;4 he promises the thief, "To day shalt thou be with me in paradise" (Luke 23:43); and Paul is "willing rather to be absent from the body, and to be present with the Lord" (2 Cor 5:8), "having a desire to depart, and to be with Christ" (Phil 1:23).5

Ignatius of Antioch (c. 107)

Ignatius, writing on his way to martyrdom, says that the prophets, who as Christ's disciples "did wait for Him as their Teacher," were at his coming "raised … from the dead."6

Polycarp of Smyrna (c. 125)

Polycarp, a disciple of John, confesses that God raised Christ "having loosed the bands of the grave," quoting Acts 2:24, whose "Western" text reads "loosed the pains of Hades."7

The Odes of Solomon (c. 125, date disputed)

The Odes of Solomon, an anonymous early Christian hymnbook, give in Ode 42 a first-person descent: "Sheol saw me and was shattered, and Death ejected me and many with me"; the dead cry out to be brought "out from the bonds of darkness," and the descending Christ makes "a congregation of living among his dead" and leads them out.8

The Shepherd of Hermas (c. 150)

The Shepherd of Hermas describes a preaching to the dead: the apostles and teachers, "after falling asleep," "preached … to those who were asleep" and gave them "the seal," so that those who "had previously fallen asleep descended dead, but rose up again alive" (cf. 1 Pet 3:19; 4:6).9

Justin Martyr (c. 165)

Justin holds that "the souls of the pious remain in a better place, while those of the unjust and wicked are in a worse, waiting for the time of judgment," and says that those who hold that the souls of the dead "are taken to heaven" at once, denying the resurrection, one should "not imagine … are Christians."10

Melito of Sardis (c. 170)

Melito of Sardis, bishop and paschal preacher, puts the harrowing of Hades on the lips of the risen Christ in his Easter homily On Pascha: "I am the one who destroyed death, and triumphed over the enemy, and trampled Hades under foot, and bound the strong one, and carried off man to the heights of heaven."11

Clement of Alexandria (c. 200)

Clement writes of a divided Hades — "who in his senses can suppose the souls of the righteous and those of sinners in the same condemnation?" — and of Christ's descent as an act of preaching: "the Lord preached the Gospel to those in Hades," and the apostles after him, so that the righteous who died before the Incarnation might hear, repent, and be saved.12

Irenaeus (c. 202)

Irenaeus teaches that the souls of the departed "go away into the invisible place allotted to them by God, and there remain until the resurrection" — for Christ himself "dwelt in the place where the dead were" three days, so the disciples ought not to suppose they will fly straight above the heavens.13

Tertullian (c. 220)

Tertullian, in A Treatise on the Soul, makes Hades "a vast deep space in the interior of the earth" that holds every soul until the resurrection — the faithful refreshed in Abraham's bosom, the wicked in torment, all conscious in the interval — with the martyrs the sole exception: "The sole key to unlock Paradise is your own life's blood."14

Hippolytus of Rome (c. 235)

Hippolytus, in Against Plato, on the Cause of the Universe, describes one Hades detaining all souls until judgment, but divided — the righteous "conducted in the light toward the right … to a locality full of light" — "we call it by the name Abraham's bosom" — the unrighteous "dragged toward the left," with "a deep and vast abyss" set between that none can cross.15

Origen of Alexandria (c. 240)

Origen counts the descent, with the Incarnation, as one of the Word's two "shoes"; by it Christ "release[d] those who were held there" in the underworld and "led captivity captive" (Eph 4:8), reopening to the penitent thief — and "all the saints for whom he had descended" — the paradise Adam had closed.16

Eusebius of Caesarea (c. 324)

Eusebius, in the Church History, includes the narrative of King Abgar of Edessa, in which the apostle Thaddaeus undertakes to preach to the city how Christ "descended into Hades, and burst the bars which from eternity had not been broken, and raised the dead; for he descended alone, but rose with many, and thus ascended to his Father."17

Aphrahat the Persian Sage (c. 345)

Aphrahat, the Syriac writer of the Demonstrations, describes the dead as sleeping until the judgment: "when men fall asleep, they sleep this slumber without knowing good from evil … until the Judge come," so that "as yet no one has received his reward" — neither the righteous their kingdom nor the wicked their torment. He also writes that the heavenly "spirit which the righteous receive … goes to our Lord until the time of the Resurrection," while the buried man sleeps.18

Cyril of Jerusalem (c. 350)

Cyril of Jerusalem, catechizing at the Holy Sepulchre, teaches that Christ "went down into the regions beneath the earth, that thence also He might redeem the righteous" — David, Samuel, the prophets, and John the Baptist — and "sent forth His prisoners out of the pit wherein is no water" (Zech 9:11).19

Athanasius of Alexandria (c. 371)

Athanasius, writing on the integrity of Christ's body, says that the body "was laid in a grave, when the Word had left it, yet was not parted from it, to preach, as Peter says, also to the spirits in prison" (1 Pet 3:19). (The "gate-keepers of hell" line in his De Synodis 8 occurs in a creed of Sirmium that he quotes there.)20

Ephrem the Syrian (c. 373)

Ephrem the Syrian, Syriac hymnographer, writes that the departed do not enter Paradise at death, for "the soul cannot have perception of Paradise without its mate, the body." So the souls of the just wait "in the delightful mansions on the borders of Paradise … awaiting there the bodies they love," entering the Garden only at the resurrection, when body and soul are reunited and go in together; where Aphrahat speaks of sleep, Ephrem's souls reside. His Nisibene Hymns also describe the descent: at Christ's death "Death opened the gates of Sheol, and there shone from it the splendour of the face of our Lord."21

Ambrose of Milan (c. 380)

The early Latin Easter hymn Aurora Lucis Rutilat, traditionally ascribed to Ambrose, sings the harrowing: "Hell is writhing in despair," and the risen "Earth's great king … leads His saints to life and light."22

Gregory of Nyssa (c. 395)

Gregory of Nyssa, in the dialogue with Macrina On the Soul and the Resurrection, reads Abraham's bosom as a condition of rest rather than a locality — "the name of Abraham's bosom is given to that good situation of the soul" — and argues that the simple, uncompounded soul consciously survives the body's dissolution, keeping watch over its own elements until the resurrection.23

John Chrysostom (c. 395)

Chrysostom teaches that the soul is conscious the instant it leaves the body: the righteous are carried off by angels "as a crowned victor" to Abraham's bosom (so Lazarus), the gulf between the states "fixed, because it cannot be loosened, moved, or shaken," and the faithful "depart … to be with Christ."24

An Ancient Homily on Holy Saturday (c. 403, date disputed)

An anonymous ancient homily for Holy Saturday, transmitted under the name of Epiphanius of Salamis (d. 403) and dated by André Vaillant (1958) to the end of the seventh century, gives the descent a dramatic voice: "there is a great silence on earth today … because the King is asleep … God has died in the flesh and hell trembles with fear." The descended Christ seeks Adam "as for a lost sheep," takes him by the hand — "Awake, O sleeper, and rise from the dead" — and leads the imprisoned dead out of Hades to enthronement in heaven.25

Augustine of Hippo (c. 420)

Augustine holds that "during the time … which intervenes between a man's death and the final resurrection, the soul dwells in a hidden retreat, where it enjoys rest or suffers affliction" according to its earthly desert,26 and that the dead "are benefited by the piety of their living friends" through prayer, alms, and the Eucharistic sacrifice; he entertains, tentatively, a purgatorial fire; the fallen angels meanwhile are kept in Tartarus for judgment.27

Cyril of Alexandria (c. 444)

Cyril of Alexandria, in his Commentary on Luke, counts the harrowing among the works that show Christ to be God: he "utterly abolished death … and spoiled hell, and overthrew the tyranny of the enemy … and opened the gates above to the dwellers upon earth, and united earth to heaven."28

Leo the Great (c. 447)

Pope Leo I, writing against the Priscillianists, states the descent: "when Christ descended to the realms below, his flesh rested in the tomb" — the soul's descent and the body's burial and resurrection held together, grounded in "thou wilt not leave my soul in Hades" (Ps 16:10).29

The Gospel of Nicodemus (c. 450, date disputed)

The Gospel of Nicodemus, an apocryphal work whose second part is the Descent into Hell, tells the harrowing as an extended narrative: the patriarchs and prophets waiting in "the shadow of death," John the Baptist heralding "the dayspring … coming from on high unto us that sit in darkness," and the descending Christ shattering the gates of brass — "Remove, O princes, your gates … and the King of glory shall come in" (Ps 24) — to take "the right hand of Adam" and lead "all the saints" up out of Hades into paradise. The narrative passed into medieval Western liturgy and drama and is associated with the Anastasis icon ⚠ unverified.30

Gregory the Dialogist (c. 594)

Gregory the Great reads Abraham's bosom as "the secret rest of the fathers" before the last judgment, holds that each soul is judged at death and enters rest or punishment at once, and writes of a cleansing fire: "there must be a … fire before judgment, because of some minor faults that may remain to be purged away."31

Bede (c. 735)

Bede calls Abraham's bosom "the rest of the blessed … to which they are received after this life," and his Vision of Drythelm (Ecclesiastical History V.12) maps distinct intermediate regions — a flowery resting-place "into which the souls of those are received who depart the body in good works, but … are not so perfect as to deserve to be immediately admitted into the kingdom of heaven."32

John of Damascus (c. 749)

John of Damascus describes the soul as "a living essence, simple, incorporeal … immortal, reasoning and intelligent," and teaches that Christ's deified soul "descended into Hades" and "freed those who had been bound for ages."33

Thomas Aquinas (c. 1274)

Aquinas, in the Supplement to the Summa (compiled from his commentary on the Sentences), sets out the abodes of souls: heaven, hell, purgatory (for "those who die in grace with venial sins"), the limbus patrum — the OT "Abraham's bosom," emptied when "Christ descended into hell [and] delivered the holy Fathers from thence" — and the limbus infantum, the limbo of children (unbaptized infants kept from the vision of God but spared all sensible punishment; see also the infant baptism timeline). Since the descent the fully-cleansed pass straight to the vision of God, others to purgatory.34

Benedict XII (c. 1336)

Benedict XII, in the constitution Benedictus Deus, after the controversy over John XXII's teaching on when the blessed see God, defined that the fully-purified souls, immediately after death and any needed purgation, "already before they take up their bodies again and before the general judgment," see "the divine essence with an intuitive vision and even face to face."35

Mark of Ephesus (c. 1438)

Mark of Ephesus, answering the Latin report on purgatory at the Council of Ferrara–Florence, affirms that "those reposed in faith are without doubt helped by the Liturgies and prayers and almsgiving performed for them," but finds a "purgatorial suffering and temporal fire" neither in the Scriptures nor in the prayers for the dead. Of souls that departed with faults unrepented or unexpiated, some "must be cleansed after the departure from the body," and those with graver sins "are kept in Hades, but not in order to remain forever in fire and torment, but as it were in prison and confinement under guard."36

Martin Luther (c. 1533)

Luther, in his Easter sermon at the castle of Torgau (1533), preaches a real, victorious descent: "hell has been torn apart for us through Christ, and … the devil's kingdom and power have been destroyed, which is why Christ died, was buried, and descended." He declines to speculate on the manner — "let him only stick to the words … in a simple way."37

John Calvin (c. 1542)

Calvin, in Psychopannychia, defends conscious survival against those he calls "sleepers," whom he identifies as some Anabaptists — "the soul, after the death of the body, still survives, endued with sense and intellect" — and rejects the limbus patrum, which, "though this fable has the countenance of great authors … is nothing but a fable," and purgatory likewise.38

The Formula of Concord (1577)

The Formula of Concord, the Lutheran confession of 1577, appeals in its Solid Declaration (Art. IX) to Luther's Torgau sermon: "we simply believe that the entire person, God and man, after the burial descended into hell, conquered the devil, destroyed the power of hell, and took from the devil all his might" — and "how this occurred we should … reserve until the other world."39

The Westminster Confession (1646)

The Westminster Confession (ch. XXXII) states the Reformed position: the souls of men "neither die nor sleep" but "immediately return to God"; the righteous "are received into the highest heavens" and the wicked "cast into hell"; and "besides these two places for souls separated from their bodies, the Scripture acknowledgeth none."40

The Synod of Jerusalem (1672)

The Synod of Jerusalem (Eastern Orthodox), in Decree XVIII of the Confession of Dositheus, teaches that "the souls of those that have fallen asleep are either at rest or in torment," departing "immediately either to joy, or to sorrow and lamentation; though confessedly neither their enjoyment, nor condemnation are complete" until the common resurrection. Some who repented without "bringing forth any fruits of repentance" depart into Hades and are delivered, before the common resurrection, through the prayers of the priests, the good works of their relatives, and "especially the unbloody Sacrifice."41

The Catechism of the Catholic Church (1992)

The Catechism of the Catholic Church restates the descent (§§632–635): Christ "descended there as Saviour, proclaiming the Good News to the spirits imprisoned there" (1 Pet 3:19) and delivered "these holy souls, who awaited their Saviour in Abraham's bosom"; he "did not descend into hell to deliver the damned … but to free the just who had gone before him."42

N. T. Wright (c. 2008)

N. T. Wright, in Surprised by Hope, describes a conscious intermediate state — "paradise," a "blissful garden where God's people rest prior to the resurrection," a state of "restful happiness" with Christ — and places the Christian hope in bodily resurrection, "life after life after death," which he distinguishes from "going to heaven when you die."43

Doug Wilson (c. 2021)

Doug Wilson, a Reformed/CREC pastor, describes a two-compartment Hades: "There was a chasm between Tartarus on the one side, and Paradise on the other … The lowest pit of Hades … was called Tartarus, and that is where the angels were locked up." He reads Christ's descent (1 Pet 3:18–20) as heralding victory — not the gospel — to those imprisoned angels.44

Disputed readings

How two texts bearing on Christ's descent to the dead have been read.

The descent to the dead

Two readings concern the descent: the history of the creed clause that names it, and a prophecy of it that Justin and Irenaeus quote as Scripture.

  • Is the creed clause “he descended into hell” an original article of the Apostles’ Creed, and should it be retained? — on the clause’s history from the Old Roman Symbol to the eighth-century received text, and the exchange between Wayne Grudem (1991) and David Scaer (1992).45
  • Was the descent-prophecy that Justin and Irenaeus quote as Scripture (“The Lord God remembered His dead people of Israel”) a passage removed from the Jewish text of the prophets? — on Justin, Dialogue 72, and Irenaeus, Against Heresies III.20.4.46
