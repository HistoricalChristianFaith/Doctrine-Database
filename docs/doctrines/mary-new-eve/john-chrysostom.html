John Chrysostom on Mary as the New Eve

Dates: c. 390 (John Chrysostom, c. 349–407, presbyter at Antioch; the Nativity homily quoted here is transmitted among his works, its authorship questioned; the year is a placement) · Wikipedia
Summary: In the securely attributed works surveyed here Chrysostom most often pairs Eve not with Mary but with fallen humanity or with virgins in general. A Nativity homily transmitted among his works gives the full New-Eve antithesis, and names Mary: the Devil deceived the virgin Eve, so Gabriel brought good news to the virgin Mary; Eve’s word bred death, Mary bore the Word who is life; Eve’s word pointed to the tree that cast Adam from Paradise, Mary’s Word to the Cross that led Adam back in. The homily stands as a Greek witness between Ambrose (c. 389) and Augustine (c. 396).

Claims & evidence

Virgin Eve deceived / virgin Mary evangelized; the tree answered by the Cross

And why is He born from a virgin, and keeps her virginity undefiled? Because, long ago, the Devil deceived Eve while a virgin; for this reason Gabriel shared the good news with Mary while a virgin. But Eve, when she was deceived, on the one hand, gave birth to a word [that was the] cause of death. But, in contrast, after Mary was told the good news, she bore the Word in flesh, the agent of our eternal life. Eve’s word pointed out a tree, through which she thrust Adam from Paradise. But the Word from the virgin pointed out the Cross, through which He led the bandit (representing Adam) into Paradise.

— Second Homily on the Birthday of Our Savior. The passage gives the New-Eve structure and names Mary throughout: the two virgins (Eve, Mary) addressed by two spirits (the Devil, Gabriel); the two “words” (Eve’s word of death, the Word Mary bears); and the two trees (the tree of the fall, the Cross), with Adam expelled through the one and the penitent thief — “representing Adam” — brought in through the other. The reversal is the same one Justin and Irenaeus drew, worked out here in a matched, four-fold antithesis.

The Virgin “paying off the debt owed… on Eve’s behalf”

… for this reason today the virgin gave birth without a husband, paying off the debt owed to men on Eve’s behalf.

— Second Homily on the Birthday of Our Savior. Earlier the same sermon frames Mary’s childbearing as the settling of a debt Eve incurred: as Adam produced a woman without a wife, so now a virgin bears a man without a husband, and womankind repays what it owed — Mary discharging, on Eve’s behalf, the debt of the first woman.

Reception and debate

  • Eve elsewhere in Chrysostom. In the securely attributed works surveyed here — the homilies On Matthew (including the nativity homilies), On Romans (including Homily 10 on Adam and Christ in Romans 5), On John — Chrysostom keeps Eve paired with fallen humanity or with women generally, not with Mary. In the same homilies he comments critically on Mary’s requests to her Son: at Cana “she desired both to do them a favor, and through her Son to render herself more conspicuous” (Homilies on John 21), and when she and his brethren sought him while he taught (Matthew 12:46–50), “that which she had essayed to do, was of superfluous vanity” (Homilies on Matthew 44). The homily on John adds that the rebuke at Cana shows “that He held her in high honor, for by His displeasure He showed that He reverenced her greatly.” Among the works surveyed here, the explicit Eve–Mary parallel appears only in this Nativity homily.
  • On the homily’s authorship. The sermon is transmitted among Chrysostom’s works and preached in his manner, but it is a different piece from his securely-attested Christmas sermon On the Day of the Birth of Our Saviour (which argues the December 25 date and lacks this passage), and the authenticity of the several Nativity homilies under his name is questioned ⚠ unverified. The passage is quoted here as transmitted; whether Chrysostom himself or another preacher composed it is an open question. It is placed here as a Greek witness of the late fourth or early fifth century in the Antiochene tradition.
  • The form is the Greek “word/hearing” antithesis — the serpent’s word to Eve answered by Gabriel’s word to Mary — that runs from Justin and Irenaeus through Cyril of Jerusalem and on to Proclus, here joined to the tree/Cross pairing that Ambrose was drawing in Latin at the same date.
