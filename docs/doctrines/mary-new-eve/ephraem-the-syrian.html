Ephraem the Syrian on Mary as the New Eve

Dates: c. 370 (Ephraem — Ephrem the Syrian, c. 306–373 — deacon of Edessa and the foremost Syriac hymnographer; the Hymns on the Nativity belong to his Edessene decade [363–373] and cannot be dated closely within it, so they are anchored to c. 370 for the timeline) · Wikipedia
Summary: In the Hymns on the Nativity of Christ in the Flesh the Eve–Mary parallel becomes song. Ephraem sets “Eve our mother” against “Eve’s daughter” the Virgin, weaving the antithesis into the wider Adam–Christ and virgin-earth typology; and he pictures Eve herself lifting her eyes from Sheol to rejoice that “the Son of her daughter… came down to raise up the mother of His mother.” His hymns are the earliest Syriac witness on this timeline.

Claims & evidence

Eve our mother and Eve’s daughter: the Virgin bears the Adam who is Head over the heavens

Travail Adam on the woman brought, that from him had come forth. She to-day her travail ransomed, who to her a Saviour bare! To Eve our mother a man gave birth, who himself had had no birth. How much more should Eve’s daughter be believed to have borne a Child without a man! The virgin earth, she bare that Adam that was head over the earth! The Virgin bare to-day the Adam that was Head over the Heavens.

— Hymns on the Nativity, Hymn I, source. Ephraem folds the Eve–Mary antithesis into the older Pauline frame: as the virgin earth from which the first Adam was formed prefigures the Virgin from whom the second Adam is born, so “Eve our mother” — through whom travail came — is answered by “Eve’s daughter,” the Virgin who bears the Saviour that ransoms Eve’s travail. The parallel is drawn in defence of the virgin birth: the surrounding stanza reasons from the miracle of the first creation to the greater miracle of the second.

Eve lifts her eyes from Sheol: the Son of her daughter raises up the mother of His mother

Eve lifted up her eyes from Sheol and rejoiced in that day, because the Son of her daughter as a medicine of life came down to raise up the mother of His mother. Blessed Babe, that bruised the head of the Serpent that smote her!

— Hymns on the Nativity, Hymn VIII, source. Here the typology is redemptive: Mary’s Son is “a medicine of life” who reverses the death Eve brought, and Ephraem ties the reversal to Genesis 3:15 — the “Blessed Babe” who bruised the Serpent’s head. In the hymn it is the “Blessed Babe” who bruises the serpent’s head, as in Irenaeus it is the one “born of a woman”; Mary is the daughter of Eve through whom the child is given. How Genesis 3:15 has been read of Mary is set out on the interpretation page.

Reception and debate

  • Where Justin, Irenaeus and Tertullian argue the parallel in prose, Ephraem sings it in Syriac hymnody. He appears also on the Immaculate Conception timeline, where his language about Mary and sin is discussed.
  • Ephraem’s prose Commentary on Genesis reads 3:15 of the enmity between the serpent and the woman “and her children,” whom the serpent had “subjected to death”; it does not identify the woman there as Mary. The Eve–Mary antithesis appears in the hymns, in connection with the virgin birth.
  • Ephraem appears on this wiki also as a witness on the perpetual virginity of Mary.
