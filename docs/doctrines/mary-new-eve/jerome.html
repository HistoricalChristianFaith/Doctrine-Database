Jerome on Mary as the New Eve

Dates: c. 384 (the antithesis is coined in Letter 22 to Eustochium, Jerome’s great treatise-letter on virginity, written at Rome in 384; Jerome c. 347–420) · Wikipedia
Summary: Jerome gives the Eve–Mary antithesis in a terse Latin form — mors per Evam, vita per Mariam, “death came through Eve, but life has come through Mary.” He sets it inside an exhortation to consecrated virginity: because life came into the world through the Virgin Mary, the gift of virginity is now bestowed most richly upon women, “seeing that it has had its beginning from a woman.” He ties the reversal to the older virgin-Eve motif — Eve was a virgin in paradise and married only after the fall — that Justin, Tertullian, and Epiphanius also use.

Claims & evidence

“Death came through Eve, but life has come through Mary”

But now that a virgin has conceived in the womb and has borne to us a child of which the prophet says that “Government shall be upon his shoulder, and his name shall be called the mighty God, the everlasting Father,” now the chain of the curse is broken. Death came through Eve, but life has come through Mary. And thus the gift of virginity has been bestowed most richly upon women, seeing that it has had its beginning from a woman.

— Letter 22 (to Eustochium), §21, trans. W. H. Fremantle, source. The Latin reads mors per Evam, vita per Mariam; in 1964 Lumen Gentium 56 attributed the saying “death through Eve, life through Mary” to the fathers. In Jerome the curse is broken because the Virgin bore “the mighty God,” and he turns the point at once to his ascetical purpose: the Incarnation through a woman is what has now exalted virginity among women.

Eve a virgin in paradise, wedded only after the fall

In paradise Eve was a virgin, and it was only after the coats of skins that she began her married life.

— Letter 22 (to Eustochium), §19, trans. W. H. Fremantle, source. Here Jerome joins the Eve–Mary reversal to the virgin-Eve motif found also in Justin, Tertullian, and Epiphanius: because Eve fell while still a virgin (marrying only after being clothed in the “coats of skins” of Genesis 3:21), the disobedient virgin is properly answered by the obedient Virgin. Virginity, for Jerome, is the paradisal state to which Mary restores women — which is why the antithesis serves his exhortation to Eustochium to keep her virginity.

Reception and debate

  • Letter 22 is an exhortation to consecrated virginity, and Jerome draws from “life through Mary” the conclusion that “the gift of virginity has been bestowed most richly upon women.” The setting is ascetical, where the earliest witnesses on this timeline use the parallel in argument against Trypho, the Gnostics and Marcion.
  • On Genesis 3:15, Jerome’s Hebrew Questions on Genesis renders the clause “He will crush your head” and says this “is better in Hebrew”; how that bears on the Latin ipsa (“she”) is set out on the interpretation page.
  • Jerome appears on this wiki also as a witness on the perpetual virginity of Mary (his Against Helvidius, c. 383), among other timelines.
