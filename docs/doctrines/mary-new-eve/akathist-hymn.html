The Akathist Hymn on Mary as the New Eve

Dates: c. 500 (an anonymous Greek kontakion; its date and author are disputed; see “Dating” below) · Wikipedia
Summary: The Akathist Hymn to the Theotokos — still sung in the Byzantine rite through Lent — gives the Eve–Mary reversal in liturgical verse, in a salutation: greeting the Virgin at the Annunciation, the archangel hails her as “the recall of fallen Adam… the redemption of the tears of Eve.” The hymn does not argue the typology; it sings it as praise.

Claims & evidence

The Virgin reverses the fall — Adam recalled, Eve’s tears redeemed

In the first oikos (stanza) of the hymn the archangel Gabriel, sent to greet the Theotokos, addresses her with a chain of salutations each opening “Hail” (or “Rejoice”; Greek Chaire). Among the first of them the fall is undone member by member — the curse annulled, Adam recalled, Eve’s tears redeemed:

Hail, O you through whom joy will shine forth; Hail, O you through whom the curse will disappear! … Hail, O Restoration of the fallen Adam; Hail, O Redemption of the tears of Eve!

— The Akathist Hymn to the Most Holy Theotokos, Ikos 1, source.1 The Greek makes the Eve wordplay plain:

… Χαῖρε, δι᾿ ἧς ἡ χαρὰ ἐκλάμψει· χαῖρε, δι᾿ ἧς ἡ ἀρὰ ἐκλείψει. Χαῖρε, τοῦ πεσόντος Ἀδὰμ ἡ ἀνάκλησις· χαῖρε, τῶν δακρύων τῆς Εὔας ἡ λύτρωσις.

— the original Greek of Ikos 1.2 The paired antitheses are those drawn by the earlier witnesses on this timeline: the curse (ara, Genesis 3) undone by the Virgin through whom joy (chara) dawns; the fallen Adam “recalled” (anaklesis) as Christ the new Adam takes flesh in her; and Eve, whose sentence was sorrow and tears (Genesis 3:16), “redeemed” (lytrosis) in the second Eve. The hymn nowhere reasons to the parallel; it states it in praise.

Reception and debate

  • A liturgical witness. Justin, Irenaeus and Tertullian state the Eve–Mary parallel inside polemic about how Christ became man; the Akathist states it in a hymn sung by a congregation.
  • Dating. The Akathist is anonymous, and its date and author are disputed. Egon Wellesz placed it in the sixth or seventh century and named Romanos the Melodist (fl. c. 540) its author; the Byzantine Catholic Archeparchy of Pittsburgh likewise calls Romanos “probably the author.”4 Leena Mari Peltomaa dates the hymn to the first half of the fifth century, arguing that its Christology reflects the Council of Ephesus (431) and shows no trace of Chalcedon (451); Mary Cunningham accepts the theology but, weighing the hymn’s highly developed poetic form, leans to the late fifth or early sixth century. The c. 500 placed here is a midpoint across that range.3
  • The proem. The prologue “To thee, the Champion Leader” (Têi Hypermachôi) hails Mary as defender of the city. The EWTN introduction to the hymn ties it to the deliverance of Constantinople in 626, when Patriarch Sergius chose the hymn as a thanksgiving, “adding the prelude in which is an allusion to Mary’s part in the remarkable victory”; the Byzantine Catholic Archeparchy of Pittsburgh writes that “starting with the seventh century the original introductory stanza was replaced by the present one.” On these accounts the proem is not part of the hymn body that carries the Eve typology quoted above.5
  • The same reversal appears in Proclus of Constantinople’s sermon in the Theotokos debate (c. 430) and in John of Damascus (c. 730).
