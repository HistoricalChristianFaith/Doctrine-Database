Who crushes the serpent’s head in Genesis 3:15? (the Vulgate ipsa and the Hebrew hû)

Disputed reading · on Mary as the New Eve · witnesses: Irenaeus; Jerome; Ephraem the Syrian

Related readings: Does the second-century Eve–Mary parallel assign Mary an active part of her own in the reversal of the fall?

The passage

וְאֵיבָ֣ה ׀ אָשִׁ֗ית בֵּֽינְךָ֙ וּבֵ֣ין הָֽאִשָּׁ֔ה וּבֵ֥ין זַרְעֲךָ֖ וּבֵ֣ין זַרְעָ֑הּ ה֚וּא יְשׁוּפְךָ֣ רֹ֔אשׁ וְאַתָּ֖ה תְּשׁוּפֶ֥נּוּ עָקֵֽב׃

And I will put enmity between thee and the woman, and between thy seed and her seed; they shall bruise thy head, and thou shalt bruise their heel.

— Genesis 3:15, Hebrew (Masoretic) text, with the Jewish Publication Society translation (1917).1

And I will put enmity between thee and the woman and between thy seed and her seed, he shall watch against thy head, and thou shalt watch against his heel.

— Genesis 3:15, Septuagint, trans. L. C. L. Brenton (1851).2

Inimicitias ponam inter te et mulierem, et semen tuum et semen illius : ipsa conteret caput tuum, et tu insidiaberis calcaneo ejus.

I will put enmities between thee and the woman, and thy seed and her seed: she shall crush thy head, and thou shalt lie in wait for her heel.

— Genesis 3:15, Latin Vulgate (Clementine text), with the Douay–Rheims translation.1

The question

In Genesis 3:15 the Hebrew pronoun before “shall crush” is hû, masculine, and the Septuagint reads autos, “he”; the Latin Vulgate as it circulated in the Middle Ages read ipsa conteret caput tuum, “she shall crush your head,” and the 1979 Nova Vulgata reads the neuter ipsum.2 The dispute is over who is said to defeat the serpent — the woman’s offspring, Christ; the woman herself, read as Mary; or no single figure — and so over how far the verse bears on Mary. Pius IX’s bull Ineffabilis Deus (1854), defining the Immaculate Conception, drew on the verse.3 No modern Catholic exegete holding the second reading below, and no modern Eastern Orthodox exegete of the verse, was located for this page.

The woman’s seed, Christ, crushes the serpent’s head

On this reading the conqueror promised in the verse is the woman’s offspring, Christ; holders differ over whether “the woman” is also to be read as Mary.

Held by: Irenaeus (Against Heresies V.21.1, c. 180); Jerome (Hebrew Questions on Genesis, late fourth century); John Calvin (Reformed, Commentary on Genesis, sixteenth century); John Paul II (Catholic, general audience, 1996).4

The Hebrew hû and the verb are masculine singular, agreeing with the masculine zera‘, “seed”; the Septuagint keeps the masculine autos even where its own noun sperma is neuter.2 Irenaeus reads the seed as Christ: “He who should be born of a woman, [namely] from the Virgin, after the likeness of Adam, was preached as keeping watch for the head of the serpent”; Ephraem hails “the Blessed Babe, that bruised the head of the Serpent.” Jerome, translating from the Hebrew, writes: “It is better in Hebrew: He will crush your head, and you will crush his heel.”5 Calvin, noting the Latin, writes that “The feminine gender has crept in instead of the masculine or neuter,” and reads the seed as “the posterity of the woman generally” until “we must necessarily come to one head, that we may find to whom the victory belongs.” C. John Collins argues that “the singular pronoun hû’ in Genesis 3:15 is quite consistent with the pattern where a single individual is in view.”6

Several holders read the woman of the verse as Mary while giving the victory to her Son. Luther finds in “the woman” “a general reference to all individual women, and yet… a direct reference to one individual woman only, to Mary and to her Seed.”7 The 1910 Catholic Encyclopedia states: “The conqueror from the seed of the woman, who should crush the serpent’s head, is Christ; the woman at enmity with the serpent is Mary.” John Paul II: “Exegetes now agree in recognizing that the text of Genesis, according to the original Hebrew, does not attribute action against the serpent directly to the woman, but to her offspring. Nevertheless, the text gives great prominence to the role she will play in the struggle against the tempter.”8 On this reading the verse promises a victory won by one born of the woman, and whatever honour it gives the woman comes through that birth.

Mary herself crushes the serpent’s head, with and through her Son

On this reading the woman of the verse is Mary, and the victory over the serpent is hers as well as her Son’s.

Held by: Bernard of Clairvaux (twelfth century, as quoted by Bonaventure); Bonaventure (Collationes de Septem Donis, thirteenth century); Pius IX (Catholic, Ineffabilis Deus, 1854); Tenny Thomas (Malankara Orthodox, “List of Typology for the Theotokos”).9

The enmity is set “between the serpent and the woman”: the woman is a named party to the promise, and on the New-Eve reading she finds her fulfilment in Mary as the second Adam finds his in Christ. The feminine Latin text is old: Ambrose reads “She shall watch for your head,” and Bede comments on “She will crush your head.”10 Bonaventure reads that text of Mary, citing Bernard: “The Lord said to the serpent: I will put enmities between you and the woman, and your seed and her seed; she shall crush your head, and you shall lie in wait for her heel. … Bernard says of the glorious Virgin: ‘She crushed the head of the ancient serpent, when she reduced to nothing every suggestion of the evil one.’”9 On this reading her victory is her complete refusal of the tempter whom Eve heeded.

Pius IX joins the victory to her Son’s: as Christ fastened the decree against us to the cross, “so the most holy Virgin, united with him by a most intimate and indissoluble bond, was, with him and through him, eternally at enmity with the evil serpent, and most completely triumphed over him, and thus crushed his head with her immaculate foot.” He reports the Fathers as reading the promise “I will put enmities between you and the woman” as “Unmistakable evidence that she crushed the poisonous head of the serpent.”3 Tenny Thomas, whose page renders the verse “He shall bruise your head, And you shall bruise His heel,” reads it as prefiguring “the Virgin Mary’s mission: in opposition to the serpent (the ‘enmity’); in union with the Messiah-Redeemer (her ‘seed’) fighting and crushing the head of the serpent; in contrast with Eve, seduced and conquered by the serpent.”11 On this reading an enmity that is total admits no share in the serpent’s power, and the woman who never yielded to him treads on his head together with the Son she bore.

The verse describes a lasting enmity between the woman’s offspring and the serpent’s, not one conqueror

On this reading “seed” is collective on both sides, and the verse belongs to the curse on the serpent: a standing hostility between humankind and snakes.

Held by: Hans Peter Rüger (Old Testament, Tübingen, “On Some Versions of Genesis 3.15, Ancient and Modern,” 1976); Claus Westermann (Genesis 1–11, English 1984) ⚠ unverified.12

Rüger holds that “‘seed’ is evidently a collective noun, which as such cannot have any direct or indirect reference to Christ and/or Mary, and secondly, Gen. 3.15 forms part of a curse, and therefore cannot be a promise or a prophecy by implication.” The verbs point the same way: “the repeated attempts of men and snakes to kill each other are indicated by means of the iterative imperfects ‘it bruises you … and you bruise it’.” On the Latin he concludes that “the original Vulgate equivalent of Hebrew [hû] was ‘he’ (ipse) rather than ‘she’ (ipsa).”12 Westermann, as Collins reports him, “emphatically denies any possibility of Messianic promise or protevangelium here, first because he takes zera’ as collective.”6 The Aramaic Targum Pseudo-Jonathan also reads the seed collectively, of “the sons of the woman,” who strike the serpent’s head “when the sons of the woman keep the commandments of the law,” though it adds a remedy “in the days of the King Meshiha.”13 On this reading the verse explains, as part of the sentence on the serpent, why people and snakes are at war in every generation.
