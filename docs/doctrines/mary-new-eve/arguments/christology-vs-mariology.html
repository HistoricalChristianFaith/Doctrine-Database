Does the second-century Eve–Mary parallel assign Mary an active part of her own in the reversal of the fall?

Disputed reading · on Mary as the New Eve · witnesses: Justin Martyr; Irenaeus; Tertullian

Related readings: Does Irenaeus’s advocata (Against Heresies V.19.1) present Mary as Eve’s intercessor?

The passage

…so also did Mary, having a man betrothed [to her], and being nevertheless a virgin, by yielding obedience, become the cause of salvation, both to herself and the whole human race. … And thus also it was that the knot of Eve’s disobedience was loosed by the obedience of Mary.

— Irenaeus, Against Heresies III.22.4, source.1

The question

Justin (c. 160), Irenaeus (c. 180) and Tertullian (c. 210) each set Mary’s obedience at the Annunciation against Eve’s disobedience, and each does so within an argument about how Christ became man — against Trypho, the Gnostics and Marcion. The question is whether the parallel is a corollary of Christ’s recapitulation of the fall, with Mary as the counter-term the argument needs, or whether it also gives Mary an active, personal share of her own in the reversal; the answer bears on how later Marian teaching is related to these texts. Several interpreters, among them Georges Florovsky and J. A. Ross MacKenzie, affirm something of both, as noted under each reading. Beyond Harnack (1885–1890), no more recent Protestant scholar who holds the second reading without also granting Mary an active role was located for this page.

The parallel gives Mary an active, personal role in salvation

On this reading the earliest fathers, while arguing about Christ, assign Mary a real and free part of her own in undoing Eve’s fall.

Held by: John Henry Newman (Catholic, Letter to Pusey, 1866); Herbert Thurston (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1912); Georges Florovsky (Russian Orthodox, “The Ever-Virgin Mother of God,” 1949); John Paul II (Catholic, Redemptoris Mater, 1987).2

Irenaeus says that the virgin, “by yielding obedience, become the cause of salvation, both to herself and the whole human race,” and he calls her the advocata of Eve, “virginal disobedience having been balanced in the opposite scale by virginal obedience.”3 The antithesis works because Mary’s free assent answers Eve’s free refusal term for term: on the scale she is an agent, not only a vessel. Newman calls the parallel “the great rudimental teaching of Antiquity from its earliest date” concerning Mary — “She is the Second Eve” — and draws from the agreement of a Palestinian, an Asian-Gallic and an African father the mark of an apostolic tradition.4 Thurston writes that Irenaeus “has deserved to be called the first theologian of the Virgin Mother” for drawing out the parallel.5

Florovsky places the parallel inside Irenaeus’s recapitulation — “an elaborate conception, organically connected with his basic idea of the recapitulation” — and draws from it that, because salvation came by the assumption of true human nature, “man had to have his active share in the mystery”: Mary’s was “freedom of obedience, not of initiative” and yet “freedom of co-operation (cf. St. Irenaeus, Adv. Haeres., 3, 21, 8: ‘Mary cooperating with the economy’).”6 The Second Vatican Council (1964) states that “the holy Fathers see her as used by God not merely in a passive way, but as freely cooperating in the work of human salvation through faith and obedience,” and John Paul II, citing Justin, Irenaeus and Tertullian, calls Mary’s faith “in a certain sense the counterpoise to the disobedience and disbelief embodied in the sin of our first parents.”7 On this reading the second-century texts, in making their point about Christ, set out a Marian teaching: Mary’s act of faith is the hinge of the reversal, and later reflection on her draws on that role.

The parallel is a corollary of Christ’s recapitulation of the fall

On this reading the parallel is one panel of the second Adam’s undoing of the fall, and Mary appears as the counter-term the argument for a real Incarnation needs.

Held by: Adolf von Harnack (Lutheran, History of Dogma, 1885–1890); J. A. Ross MacKenzie (Reformed, “The Theme of Eve and Mary in the Early Christian Church”; on the recapitulation frame, also granting Mary an active role).8

Every early statement of the parallel is made inside a case about Christ. Justin frames it in the Dialogue with Trypho to show that “the disobedience which proceeded from the serpent” is destroyed “in the same manner in which it derived its origin” — an argument about the manner of the Incarnation and the reversal of Adam’s fall.9 Irenaeus sets the antithesis inside his doctrine of recapitulation, in which Christ re-heads the whole human story, so that the race bound to death by a virgin is “rescued by a virgin.”3 Tertullian uses it against Marcion to show that Christ took real flesh from a real woman: “that what had been reduced to ruin by this sex might by the selfsame sex be recovered to salvation. As Eve had believed the serpent, so Mary believed the angel.”10 In all three the conclusion argued for is the reality and saving effect of the Incarnation.

Harnack reads the parallel as an outgrowth of Irenaeus’s Christology: “his recapitulation theory also suggested to him a parallel between Adam and Eve on the one hand and Christ and Mary on the other, which included the birth from the virgin.” Of the “very extravagant expressions with regard to Mary” in Irenaeus and Tertullian he writes: “These, however, have no doctrinal significance,” while granting that “the later Mariolatry has one of its roots in the parallel between Eve and Mary.”8 Luigi Gambero (Catholic), on the setting only, describes Irenaeus deploying the contrast “in a completely Christocentric context” to safeguard the reality of the redemptive Incarnation.11 On this reading the Marian language serves the argument about Christ: Mary is the virgin through whose belief the Word takes flesh, the term that answers Eve so that the second Adam may answer the first.
