Augustine of Hippo on Mary as the New Eve

Dates: c. 396 (the terse antithesis — “through a woman death, through a woman life” — in De agone Christiano 22.24, an instructional treatise of the late 390s; restated more fully c. 410–418 in Sermon 51; Augustine 354–430) · Wikipedia
Summary: Augustine folds the Eve–Mary antithesis into his defence of the goodness of the body and the female sex against the Manichaeans: as death entered the world through a woman (Eve, corrupted by the deceiver), so life came through a woman (Mary, who bore the Saviour). The contrast sets Eve, deceived and disobedient, against Mary, uncorrupted and believing, and is used to defend the reality of the Incarnation and the dignity of women against the Manichaean disdain for birth from a woman.

Claims & evidence

“Through a woman came death; through a woman, life: through Eve, ruin; through Mary, salvation”

Through a woman came death; through a woman, life: through Eve, ruin; through Mary, salvation. The former, corrupted, followed the deceiver; the latter, uncorrupted, gave birth to the Saviour.

— The Christian Combat (De agone Christiano) 22.24, trans. Robert P. Russell, in Christian Instruction; Admonition and Grace; The Christian Combat; Faith, Hope and Charity (Fathers of the Church 2; CUA Press, 1947). Written for unschooled believers in the late 390s, the treatise gives the antithesis in two balanced members — the same mors / vita, Eva / Maria pairing found in Jerome’s Letter 22 (384). The two women are set against each other by what each did: Eve “corrupted, followed the deceiver,” Mary “uncorrupted, gave birth to the Saviour.”

“Through a woman came the poison, through a woman came the salvation” — against the Manichaeans

Christ wished to be born of a virgin. Through a woman came the poison, through a woman came the salvation.

By deceiving, poison was offered to man through a woman: by repairing, salvation is offered to man through a woman. Let a woman compensate for the sin of the man deceived through her, by giving birth to Christ. Hence, also, women first announced the resurrection of God to the Apostles. A woman announced death to her husband in paradise: and women announced salvation to men in the Church.

— Sermon 51 (on the harmony of the genealogies), §§2–3, source. Here Augustine is answering the Manichaean taunt “ut quid elegerit feminam?” — why should Christ deign to be born of a woman at all? — and answers it with the Eve–Mary reversal: because the first man “was deceived by a woman,” women would else “despair about themselves,” so Christ was born of a woman that “in no sex of human creation should there be despair about Himself.” The sex through which the poison came is the sex through which the antidote comes; the “woman… in paradise” who announced death is answered by the women who announce the resurrection.

Reception and debate

  • The setting of Sermon 51. Sermon 51 deploys the parallel inside a defence of the Incarnation against Manichaean disdain for birth and the body; the point of “salvation… through a woman” is that the female sex is God’s good creature and no bar to the Saviour, “so that in no sex of human creation should there be despair.” As with Justin, Irenaeus, and Tertullian, the parallel stands within argument against an opposing party; here it serves the goodness of the body and the standing of women. On the scholarly setting and the disputed date (the majority place the sermon c. 410–412, Verbraken c. 417/418), see Anthony Dupont, “‘Ut quid elegerit feminam?’: Manichaeans Questioning the Usefulness of Mary in the Perspective of Augustine’s Sermon 51.”
  • Augustine on Mary and sin. Augustine appears also on the Immaculate Conception timeline, where his statement — “the holy Virgin Mary, concerning whom I wish to raise no question when it touches the subject of sins, out of honour to the Lord” (On Nature and Grace 36) — and its bearing on her conception are treated. The New-Eve passages quoted here concern Mary’s believing obedience.
