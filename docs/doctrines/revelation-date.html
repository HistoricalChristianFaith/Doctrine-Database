The Date of Revelation (Neronian or Domitianic?)

When was the book of Revelation written? Two datings are held. The late (Domitianic) date, c. AD 95–96, rests chiefly on a sentence of Irenaeus (c. 180), which its holders read as dating the vision: "For that was seen no very long time since, but almost in our day, towards the end of Domitian's reign."1 It is the dating of the commentaries of David Aune (1997) and Craig Koester (2014), among others.2 The early (Neronian) date, c. AD 64–70 (often narrowed to the year of the four emperors, 68–69), is argued by J. A. T. Robinson (1976) and Kenneth Gentry (1989), who point to internal signs that the temple was still standing when John wrote; for preterist interpreters, among them Gentry, it allows the "great tribulation" of Revelation to be read as the Roman war and the destruction of Jerusalem in AD 70.3

The debate draws on two bodies of evidence. Of the ancient witnesses on this page, Irenaeus, Victorinus, Eusebius and Jerome name Domitian, Clement of Alexandria names no emperor, Epiphanius names Claudius, and a Syriac heading to the book names Nero. Within the book, early-date advocates point to the measured temple of Revelation 11, the seven kings of Revelation 17 and the number 666; late-date advocates point to the imperial cult, the Nero-redivivus legend and the condition of the seven churches. The witnesses follow, earliest first; each of these questions is set out with its proponents under Disputed readings.

Timeline

Irenaeus (c. 180)

Arguing that the number of the Antichrist's name would have been revealed had it been needful, Irenaeus writes that it "would have been announced by him who beheld the apocalyptic vision. For that was seen no very long time since, but almost in our day, towards the end of Domitian's reign" — the earliest extant external notice bearing on when Revelation was seen. Whether the subject of "was seen" is the vision or John himself is disputed; see the interpretation page.4

Clement of Alexandria (c. 200)

Telling the story of the apostle John and the young robber, Clement places John's return from Patmos to Ephesus "on the tyrant's death." He names no emperor; Eusebius quotes the passage in his Church History (3.23). Whether Clement is an independent witness to the Domitianic dating is disputed; see the interpretation page.5

Victorinus of Pettau (c. 300)

In the earliest extant commentary on Revelation, Victorinus states that when John saw the Apocalypse he was on Patmos, "condemned to the labour of the mines by Cæsar Domitian," and that he published it after Domitian's death.6

Eusebius of Caesarea (c. 324)

Eusebius sets out a single chronology: John banished to Patmos under Domitian, released after Domitian's death under Nerva, and living on at Ephesus until Trajan. He quotes Irenaeus's "almost in our own generation, at the end of the reign of Domitian" in support.7

Epiphanius of Salamis (c. 375)

Writing against the Alogi, Epiphanius twice dates John's prophecy on Patmos to the reign of Claudius Caesar (41–54), and places John's writing of the Gospel "past ninety," after his return from Patmos. Kenneth Gentry proposes that Epiphanius meant Nero, whose full name was Nero Claudius Caesar; whether the notice witnesses an early date is set out on the interpretation page.8

Jerome (c. 393)

Jerome writes that John was banished to Patmos and wrote the Apocalypse "in the fourteenth year… after Nero" — under Domitian, in a "second persecution" — and that, freed at Domitian's death, he returned to Ephesus and lived until Trajan.9

The Syriac subscription (6th century)

The heading prefixed to the Apocalypse in the Syriac tradition names Nero, not Domitian, as the emperor who banished John: "written in Patmos, whither John was sent by Nero Caesar." Mark Hitchcock calls it "the first clear, accepted, unambiguous witness to the Neronic date" (see the Syriac subscription). It is a heading carried in the later Syriac versions that included Revelation and appears in the printed Syriac Apocalypse (L. de Dieu's 1627 edition, whence the London and Paris Polyglots); the Peshitta did not carry the book.10

Moses Stuart and the nineteenth-century early-date scholars (1845)

Moses Stuart argued in 1845 for a date c. AD 68, before Jerusalem fell; in England the Cambridge scholars Westcott, Lightfoot, and Hort, and F. W. Farrar, dated the book to the years around Nero's death.11

J. A. T. Robinson (1976)

An Anglican bishop and New Testament scholar, not a preterist, who argued that the whole New Testament, Revelation included, was written before AD 70. Robinson dated the Apocalypse to c. AD 68, read the seven kings against the emperors of 68–69, and held the Domitianic persecution behind the late date to be historically insubstantial.12

Adela Yarbro Collins and Leonard Thompson (1984–1990)

Two scholars who keep the Domitianic date while arguing against the picture of a savage empire-wide "Domitianic persecution": Yarbro Collins locates the book's setting in a perceived crisis and the pressure of the imperial cult, and Thompson argues that Domitian's "reign of terror" was largely a construct of hostile later sources.13

Kenneth L. Gentry Jr. (1989)

A book-length case for the early date. Gentry, writing as a postmillennial preterist, argues Revelation was written c. AD 65–66, under Nero and before Jerusalem's fall, resting the case on internal evidence — the temple still standing (Rev 11:1–2), the "sixth king" as Nero (Rev 17:10) — and reading the subject of Irenaeus's "was seen" as John himself rather than the vision.14

The late-date commentaries (1997–2014)

The reference commentaries of David Aune, G. K. Beale, and Craig Koester each set out both cases and date the book c. AD 95–96, under Domitian; Aune does so by way of a two-edition theory, with a first edition as early as c. AD 70 incorporating older material.15

Disputed readings

Particular pieces of evidence for the book's date, each set out with its rival readings on its own page.

Irenaeus and the later Fathers

How Irenaeus's sentence of c. 180, and the later Fathers who name Domitian, have been read.

  • Does Irenaeus (5.30.3) date the seeing of Revelation to Domitian's reign? (the subject of "was seen": the vision, or John)16
  • Do the later Fathers' Domitianic dates confirm Irenaeus independently, or derive from him?17

Internal features cited for the early date

Features of the book's text that early-date advocates read as fixing a setting before AD 70.

  • Does the temple "still standing" in Revelation 11 require a pre-70 date?18
  • Do the "seven kings" of Revelation 17:9–11 date the book to Nero's reign? (which emperor the count begins from)19
  • Does 666 = "Nero Caesar" (Revelation 13:18) date Revelation to Nero's reign?20

Internal features cited for the late date

Features of the book's world that late-date advocates read as fitting the reign of Domitian.

  • Does Revelation's emperor-worship setting fit Domitian's imperial cult, dating the book to the 90s?21
  • Does the Nero-redivivus legend (Revelation 13:3; 17:8,11) require a post-Nero, Domitianic date?22
  • Does the settled condition of the seven churches (Laodicea's wealth) require a late date?23

Ancient notices naming another emperor

The two ancient notices that name an emperor other than Domitian for John's exile.

  • Does the Syriac subscription naming Nero evidence an early (Neronian) date for Revelation?24

  17. See the interpretation page Do the later Fathers' Domitianic dates confirm Irenaeus independently, or derive from him? Moses Stuart, Milton Terry, Thomas Randell and Kenneth Gentry hold that the Fathers who name Domitian depend on Irenaeus: Eusebius (Church History 3.18.3) quotes Irenaeus as his proof, and Jerome repeats the dating (Randell: they "do not strengthen what they merely repeat"). Clement attests the exile and return without naming an emperor, and Victorinus gives a detail, labour in the mines, that Irenaeus does not. ↩

  18. See the interpretation page Does the temple "still standing" in Revelation 11 require a pre-70 date? Kenneth Gentry reads the measured temple of Rev 11:1–2, 8 as the Jerusalem temple still standing, and so dates the book before 70. G. K. Beale reads it symbolically, noting that Revelation uses naós elsewhere only of the heavenly temple (cf. 21:22, "no temple"); Wellhausen and Aune read the passage as a pre-70 oracle incorporated into a book completed later. Robinson and Yarbro Collins also read the passage as pre-70 in outlook; Yarbro Collins dates the book late. ↩

  19. See the interpretation page Do the "seven kings" of Revelation 17:9–11 date the book to Nero's reign? Counted from Julius Caesar, the reigning sixth king of Rev 17:10 is Nero and the short-lived seventh Galba (68–69), the count Kenneth Gentry adopts; counted from Augustus the sixth is Galba, and with the year-of-four usurpers omitted it is Vespasian. Ancient enumerations of the emperors differ (Suetonius, Josephus, 4 Ezra, the Sibylline Oracles). G. K. Beale reads the seven as a number of completeness and the heads as Danielic figures of world-power. Richard Bauckham, The Climax of Prophecy (T&T Clark, 1993), 384–452, reads the beast as Nero throughout, takes the "eighth… of the seven" as the Nero-redivivus legend, and dates the book under Domitian. ↩

  20. See the interpretation page Does 666 = "Nero Caesar" (Revelation 13:18) date Revelation to Nero's reign? By Hebrew gematria נרון קסר (Neron Qesar) = 666, and the Latin Nero Caesar (dropping the final nun) = 616, the variant attested in P115 and Codex Ephraemi (Rev 13:18). The identification with Nero is held by Kenneth Gentry, who reads it as dating the book to Nero's reign, and by Richard Bauckham (The Climax of Prophecy, T&T Clark, 1993, 384–452), David Aune and Craig Koester, who read the beast in the light of the Nero-redivivus legend and date the book under Domitian. ↩

  21. See the interpretation page Does Revelation's emperor-worship setting fit Domitian's imperial cult, dating the book to the 90s? Colin Hemer, Steven Friesen and the commentaries of Aune, Beale and Koester read Revelation's coerced emperor worship (Rev 13:11–18; 14:9–11) against the provincial imperial cult of Asia, including the temple of the Sebastoi dedicated at Ephesus c. AD 89/90 (Steven J. Friesen, Imperial Cults and the Apocalypse of John, Oxford, 2001). Yarbro Collins and Thompson argue against a systematic Domitianic persecution, and Brian W. Jones (The Emperor Domitian, 1992) holds that dominus et deus (Suetonius, Domitian 13) was likely never a decreed cult. ↩

  22. See the interpretation page Does the Nero-redivivus legend (Revelation 13:3; 17:8,11) require a post-Nero, Domitianic date? Richard Bauckham, David Aune and Craig Koester read the healed death-wound of Rev 13:3 and the beast that "was, and is not, and is to come… the eighth, and is of the seven" (17:8, 17:11) as the Nero-redivivus legend in a developed form, and date the book c. 95. The expectation of Nero's return is attested from 69 — a false Nero (Tacitus, Hist. 2.8), edicts circulated as if he lived (Suetonius, Nero 57) — and Augustine (City of God 20.19) records the speculation c. 425. Kenneth Gentry reads the wound as the empire's own death and revival in the civil war of 68–69. ↩

  23. See the interpretation page Does the settled condition of the seven churches (Laodicea's wealth) require a late date? Colin Hemer reads the situation of the churches in Revelation 2–3 as fitting the reign of Domitian; Leon Morris and Kim Riddlebarger read Laodicea's boast at Rev 3:17 against the earthquake of AD 60 as requiring a later date. Tacitus (Annals 14.27) records that the city recovered "with no help from us," which Kenneth Gentry reads as a swift recovery putting its wealth within reach in the mid-60s; Robert Mounce, who dates the book late, reads the "wealth" of 3:17 as spiritual rather than economic. The Ephesus (2:4) and Smyrna arguments are set out on the same page. ↩

  24. See the interpretation page Does the Syriac subscription naming Nero evidence an early (Neronian) date for Revelation? The Syriac heading of the Apocalypse names Nero as the emperor who banished John — Moses Stuart quotes the title page directly (A Commentary on the Apocalypse, 1845, 1:267): "written in Patmos, whither John was sent by Nero Caesar" (a fuller form, "…into which he was thrown by Nero Caesar," circulates through the early-date literature). Revelation entered Syriac in the Philoxenian (507) and Harklean (616) versions. Mark Hitchcock, who dates the book late, calls the heading "the first clear, accepted, unambiguous witness to the Neronic date" and dates it to the sixth and seventh centuries; F. J. A. Hort, who inclined to the early date on internal grounds, wrote that "if external evidence alone could decide, there would be a clear preponderance for Domitian" (both as quoted at Evidence Unseen). With Epiphanius it is one of the two ancient notices on this page that name an emperor other than Domitian. ↩
