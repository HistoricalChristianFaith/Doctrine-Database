The Great Apostasy (did the church wholly fall, needing a restoration?)

The Great Apostasy thesis holds that the church founded by the apostles did not merely err here and there but defected wholesale after their death — losing valid authority, ordinances, and true doctrine — so that no legitimate church survived until it was restored in a later age. It is the premise of the Latter-day Saint Restoration; Roger Williams’s Seeker position (below) is an earlier total-apostasy position. In its Latter-day Saint (Mormon) form the young Joseph Smith is told in his First Vision account to join none of the churches, “for they were all wrong,” their creeds “an abomination” and their professors “all corrupt,” and the claim is the foundation on which the Restoration is built.1 Catholic and Orthodox teaching holds that the church has continued without a break since the apostles; the Lutheran and Reformed confessions hold that a church has always remained on earth, though corrupted in places; these readings are set out, with their sources, on the interpretation page. The thesis is a historical claim about the patristic and medieval centuries, which other timelines here record witness by witness (the canon, the Eucharist, baptism, church government, the communion of the saints, and more).

Two questions are at stake: (a) whether there were real corruptions and innovations in the post-apostolic church, and (b) whether the church entirely apostatized, losing all valid authority and ceasing to exist. Luther and the Reformed confessions answer the first yes and the second no; Latter-day Saint teaching answers both yes, calling its own church “‘restored,’ not ‘reformed,’ Christianity”; Catholic and Orthodox teaching answers the second no (see the interpretation page for sources). The timeline below records the question earliest extant witness first: Paul’s warning of “a falling away” (c. 51) and the promises of the church’s permanence in the same writings; Irenaeus’s appeal to a succession “from the apostles until now” (c. 180); Vincent of Lérins’s statement that the faith “has continued one and the same… and so will continue for ever” (c. 434); Luther’s papal “Antichrist” alongside his affirmation of a church surviving under the papacy (1528–37); Roger Williams’s Seeker position awaiting new apostles (c. 1639); Joseph Smith’s Restoration (1820–30); James E. Talmage’s systematic case (1909); and the modern historians of doctrine Newman, Harnack, Kelly and Pelikan (1845–1971).

Timeline

The New Testament (c. 50–95)

The apostolic writings contain both the texts cited for the thesis and those cited against it. Paul foretells that “that day shall not come, except there come a falling away first, and that man of sin be revealed” (2 Thessalonians 2:3), warns the Ephesian elders that “after my departing shall grievous wolves enter in among you” and that “of your own selves shall men arise, speaking perverse things” (Acts 20:29–30), and writes that “in the latter times some shall depart from the faith” (1 Timothy 4:1).2 Restorationist writers, Talmage among them (below), cite these texts as foretelling the apostasy. Writers who hold that the church continued cite Christ’s promises in the same writings: he builds his church so that “the gates of hell shall not prevail against it” (Matthew 16:18) and pledges “lo, I am with you alway, even unto the end of the world” (Matthew 28:20).3 Whether these texts foretell a total falling-away is disputed; see Disputed readings.

Irenaeus of Lyons (c. 180)

Irenaeus does not address the thesis, which had not yet been proposed. Arguing against Gnostics who claimed a secret apostolic tradition, Irenaeus answers that the true faith has been kept in public view, unbroken, through the succession of teachers in every apostolic church: the succession is “most abundant proof that there is one and the same vivifying faith, which has been preserved in the Church from the apostles until now, and handed down in truth” (Against Heresies III.3.3), while the systems he opposes are recent: “prior to Valentinus, those who follow Valentinus had no existence” (III.4.3).4 His words are cited for the continuity reading on the interpretation page.

Vincent of Lérins (c. 434)

The Gallic monk states the test known as the “Vincentian canon,” that we hold “what has been believed everywhere, always, and by all” (Commonitory ch. 2). Vincent makes antiquity one of its three marks and describes the church as the guardian of the deposit that “never changes… does not lose her own” (ch. 23), holding a doctrine that “has continued one and the same through the several successions of ages… and so will continue for ever” (ch. 24).5

Martin Luther (1528–1537)

Luther applies the apostasy texts to the papacy while holding that the church survived under it. He names the papacy the Man of Sin of 2 Thessalonians 2 — “the Pope is the very Antichrist, who has exalted himself above, and opposed himself against Christ” (Smalcald Articles II.4).6 He also holds that the true church had not ceased to exist: under the papacy, he writes, “there are the true holy Scriptures, true baptism, the true sacrament of the altar, the true keys to the forgiveness of sins, the true office of the ministry… I contend that in the papacy there is true Christianity, even the right kind of Christianity.”7

Roger Williams (c. 1639)

The founder of Rhode Island came to hold a total position. Williams regarded established Christendom’s church, baptism, and worship as “a whole forme of Antichristian worship” and false (Christenings Make Not Christians, 1645).8 Having helped gather a Baptist congregation at Providence, he withdrew from it too. Cotton Mather, an opponent, reports that he judged there was “none upon earth that could administer baptism” “for the want of a called administrator”; Robert T. Handy summarizes the “Seeker” position he then took as holding that the apostasy since Constantine had engulfed Christendom and that the true church could not be discerned “until God raised up new apostles.”9

Joseph Smith (1820–1830)

Latter-day Saint teaching rests the Restoration on a total apostasy. Joseph Smith’s First Vision account has him told to join no church, “for they were all wrong… all their creeds were an abomination… those professors were all corrupt” (Joseph Smith—History 1:19).10 The Doctrine and Covenants frames the age as one of universal straying — men “have strayed from mine ordinances, and have broken mine everlasting covenant” — a loss the newly organized body is raised to repair as “the only true and living church upon the face of the whole earth” (D&C 1:15, 30).11 The loss it describes is of priesthood authority as well as doctrine, restored in Latter-day Saint teaching by angelic ordination.

James E. Talmage (1909)

The systematic Latter-day Saint statement is Talmage’s The Great Apostasy, which gathers the New-Testament prophecies and the patristic-to-medieval innovations into a single case. It states the thesis: the primitive church “lost its power, authority, and graces as a divine institution, and degenerated into an earthly organization only” (Preface), so that “succession in the priesthood was broken; and… the Church, as an earthly organization operating under divine direction and having authority to officiate in spiritual ordinances, ceased to exist” (ch. II).12 His two-part case, the scriptural prediction and the historical rupture, is recorded on the interpretation page.13

The historians of doctrine (1845–1971)

John Henry Newman argued that Christian teaching legitimately develops — “to live is to change, and to be perfect is to have changed often” — and set out seven “notes” to tell a genuine development from a corruption.14 Adolf von Harnack described the Gnostic systems as “the acute secularising or hellenising of Christianity” and the Catholic system as “a gradual process of the same kind.” J. N. D. Kelly writes of the ante-Nicene centuries that the broad outline of revealed truth was “respected as a sacrosanct inheritance from the apostles” while “its theological explication was to a large extent left unfettered,” and Jaroslav Pelikan titled his history The Christian Tradition: A History of the Development of Doctrine.15
