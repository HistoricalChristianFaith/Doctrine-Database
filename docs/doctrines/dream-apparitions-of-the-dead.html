Dream-apparitions of the dead (do the departed themselves appear to the living?)

A grieving son dreams of his dead father, who tells him where a cancelled debt-note lies hidden — and on waking he finds it exactly there. A dead man appears in the night and points to where his unburied body rests, begging burial. A murdered relative shows in a dream how he died. Stories of this kind are recorded by the writers below, and they raise a doctrinal question: when the dead appear to the living — in a dream, a vision, or a waking apparition — and disclose something true, is the figure the actual soul of the departed, conscious, aware of the living, and acting on their behalf? Or is it an image — a representation the mind receives, produced (on Augustine's account) by angelic agency under God's permission, or (on Tertullian's and Chrysostom's) by a demon counterfeiting the dead — while the departed themselves are unaware of it?

The question is not whether a true message can come in a dream (Scripture narrates divinely sent dreams), but specifically whose act the apparition is. The writers below distinguish two groups: the ordinary dead, who on the accounts of Tertullian, Chrysostom and Augustine do not, of their own nature, involve themselves in the affairs of the living; and the saints and martyrs, who on Augustine's account aid the living "by a Divine power" and on Aquinas's may appear "when they will" (see prayer to the saints). On Tertullian's, Chrysostom's and Augustine's accounts, the awareness the folk stories attribute to the ordinary dead belongs, if to anyone, to the saints. Tertullian and Augustine accept that such apparitions occur and assign them to an agent other than the dead; Chrysostom holds that the dead do not return at all; Gregory the Great relates apparitions in which the dead appear as themselves; Aquinas cites both Augustine and Gregory. The same question of agency — is a given dream the work of God, of a demon, or of the body? — is traced on the neighbouring timeline the discernment of dreams, where the figure in the dream need not be a dead person at all.

Timeline

Tertullian (c. 210)

Tertullian, in A Treatise on the Soul, gives the earliest extant sustained Christian treatment, and denies that the dead return: no soul escapes Hades before the resurrection (Luke 16's fixed gulf), so what necromancy and tomb-oracles "bring up" is a demonic counterfeit — "God forbid… that we should suppose that the soul of any saint, much less of a prophet, can be dragged out of (its resting-place in Hades) by a demon." He also compares dreams of the dead with dreams of the living: "we admit apparitions of dead persons in dreams to be not more really true than those of living persons… things are not true because they appear to be so, but because they are fully proved to be so."1

John Chrysostom (c. 388)

John Chrysostom, preaching at Antioch in the Discourses on the Rich Man and Lazarus, holds that the dead do not appear at all, because they are not here to appear. At death every soul — "not the souls of the just only, but also those of sinners" — is "forthwith led away" and does "not still linger here"; and God has deliberately "closed the portals" so that none "return to tell us the things that take place there," lest the tempter exploit an open traffic with the dead. He concludes that a departed figure seen in sleep is the deceiver's mask, not the departed: "the forms of the departed… have appeared in dreams, and have deceived and ruined many." A spirit that announces "I am the spirit of such and such a monk" is likewise to be disbelieved, "since evil spirits say so to deceive."2

Augustine of Hippo (c. 421)

Augustine wrote On Care to Be Had for the Dead (De cura pro mortuis gerenda), answering Paulinus of Nola's question whether burial near a martyr's tomb profits the dead; among the cases he discusses is one in which a dead father appeared in his son's dream to resolve a debt (§13). He argues that the image of a dead man in a dream is no more the man's soul than the image of a living friend, who is likewise seen in dreams "while they themselves know not that they do appear." On his account the departed are ordinarily unconscious of it; true dream-visions are worked "by angelical operations… permitted from above," false ones by demonic deception. He argues from his own case: if the dead attended the living, his mother Monica — "who by land and sea followed me" — "would no night fail to visit" him. He also allows that by special divine dispensation a soul may be sent (as Samuel to Saul, Moses at the Transfiguration), and that the martyrs aid the living — "by a Divine power," never "by their proper nature."3

Gregory the Great (c. 593)

Gregory the Dialogist, in Dialogues Book IV, relates deathbed and apparition stories in which the dead appear as themselves, conscious of their own state and soliciting the living's prayers: the deacon Paschasius, found doing service in the hot baths, confesses he is "appointed to this place of punishment" and begs, "pray unto our Lord for me"; and Gregory draws a general moral — "the spirits of them that be dead desire it of the living, and give certain tokens." In Gregory's telling the apparition is the departed person, asking the living for help.4

Thomas Aquinas (c. 1274)

Aquinas, in the Summa's Supplement, cites both Augustine and Gregory. "According to the natural course, the separated souls… are utterly cut off from communication with the living," he writes, following Augustine; but "according to the disposition of Divine providence separated souls sometimes come forth from their abode and appear to men," citing both Augustine's martyr Felix and "the fourth book of the Dialogues." And he distinguishes the saints from the damned: "the saints can appear when they will to the living, but not the damned" — the damned only when permitted.5
