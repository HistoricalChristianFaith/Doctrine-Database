Caesarius of Arles on the anointing of the sick

Dates: c. 520 · Wikipedia
Summary: Preaching to a laity tempted to run to charmers and amulets when sick, Caesarius sets the church's remedy against the sorcerer's: the sick should hurry to the church, receive the Eucharist, be anointed by the presbyters with blessed oil, and be prayed over — gaining both bodily health and the forgiveness of sins.

Claims & evidence

To the church, not the sorcerer: Eucharist, anointing, and prayer

Caesarius folds James 5:14–15 into a single pastoral prescription for the sick Christian — and promises the double effect the apostle names:

Whenever some illness comes upon a man, he should hurry back to the church. Let him receive the body and blood of Christ, be anointed by the presbyters with consecrated oil and ask them and the deacons to pray over him in Christ's name. If he does this, he will receive not only bodily health but also the forgiveness of his sins.

— Caesarius of Arles, Sermon 19.5, trans. Mary Magdeleine Mueller (Fathers of the Church, vol. 31).1

Notes

  • Still a rite of recovery. Caesarius promises "bodily health" first and forgiveness with it — anointing as the Christian's alternative to pagan healing-magic, not a preparation for death. His sermon is strong evidence that in sixth-century Gaul the practice remained what James described, aimed at the sick person's cure. Cf. the narrowing thesis.
  • The double effect — body and soul — runs through the whole tradition, from Chrysostom to Bede and beyond.
