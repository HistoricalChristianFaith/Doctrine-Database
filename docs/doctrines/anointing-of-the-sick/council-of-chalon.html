The Council of Chalon-sur-Saône (813) on the anointing of the sick

Dates: 813
Summary: One of Charlemagne's five reform councils of 813, Chalon makes the anointing of the sick a standing ecclesiastical duty: on the authority of James 5:14–15 — "with which the decrees of the fathers also agree" — the sick "ought to be anointed by priests" with oil blessed by bishops. The rite is still framed as a healing medicine "of soul and body," but its observance is now legislated, and the anointing is placed firmly in priestly hands.

Claims & evidence

The sick "ought to be anointed by priests" — a duty grounded in James 5

Canon 48 quotes James 5:14–15 in full and turns the apostle's counsel into an obligation, binding oil-and-prayer to the priesthood and to the bishop's blessing of the oil:

Secundum beati Iacobi apostoli documentum, cui etiam decreta patrum consonant, infirmi oleo, quod ab episcopis benedicitur, a presbyteris ungui debent. Sic enim ait: Infirmatur aliquis in vobis? Inducat presbyteros ecclesiae, et orent super eum, unguentes eum oleo in nomine Domini, et oratio fidei salvabit infirmum, et allevabit eum Dominus; et si in peccatis sit, dimittentur ei. Non est itaque parvipendenda huiuscemodi medicina, quae animae corporisque medetur languoribus.

— Council of Chalon-sur-Saône (813), canon 48, source.1 "According to the teaching of the blessed apostle James, with which the decrees of the fathers also agree, the sick ought to be anointed by priests with oil that is blessed by bishops. For he says: 'Is any sick among you? Let him bring in the priests of the church, and let them pray over him, anointing him with oil in the name of the Lord; and the prayer of faith shall save the sick man, and the Lord shall raise him up; and if he be in sins, they shall be forgiven him.' Not to be lightly esteemed, therefore, is a medicine of this kind, which heals the ailments of both soul and body."

Notes

  • Still healing, but now mandated — and priest-only. Chalon has not narrowed the rite to the dying: it calls unction a "medicine… of soul and body," the language of recovery. What is new is the register — a conciliar duty ("ungui debent") rather than a described custom — and the actor: where Innocent I (416) and Bede (c. 710) still allowed lay anointing "in one's own… necessity," Chalon assigns the anointing to presbyteri. Together with Theodulf's parish ordo, it marks the Carolingian consolidation the narrowing thesis points to: the same reform decade that folds unction into a penitential deathbed sequence also makes its observance a standing ecclesiastical obligation.
  • The numbering. The canon is headed XLVIII (48) in Werminghoff's critical edition; a few secondary works miscite it as canon 47.
