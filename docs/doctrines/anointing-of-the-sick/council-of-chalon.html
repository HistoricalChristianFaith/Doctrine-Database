The Council of Chalon-sur-Saône (813) on the anointing of the sick

Dates: 813
Summary: One of Charlemagne's five reform councils of 813, Chalon makes the anointing of the sick a standing ecclesiastical duty: on the authority of James 5:14–15 — "with which the decrees of the fathers also agree" — the sick "ought to be anointed by priests" with oil blessed by bishops. The canon calls the rite a medicine "of soul and body," makes its observance a legislated duty, and assigns the anointing to priests.

Claims & evidence

The sick "ought to be anointed by priests" — a duty grounded in James 5

Canon 48 quotes James 5:14–15 in full and states an obligation, binding oil-and-prayer to the priesthood and to the bishop's blessing of the oil:

Secundum beati Iacobi apostoli documentum, cui etiam decreta patrum consonant, infirmi oleo, quod ab episcopis benedicitur, a presbyteris ungui debent. Sic enim ait: Infirmatur aliquis in vobis? Inducat presbyteros ecclesiae, et orent super eum, unguentes eum oleo in nomine Domini, et oratio fidei salvabit infirmum, et allevabit eum Dominus; et si in peccatis sit, dimittentur ei. Non est itaque parvipendenda huiuscemodi medicina, quae animae corporisque medetur languoribus.

— Council of Chalon-sur-Saône (813), canon 48, source.1 "According to the teaching of the blessed apostle James, with which the decrees of the fathers also agree, the sick ought to be anointed by priests with oil that is blessed by bishops. For he says: 'Is any sick among you? Let him bring in the priests of the church, and let them pray over him, anointing him with oil in the name of the Lord; and the prayer of faith shall save the sick man, and the Lord shall raise him up; and if he be in sins, they shall be forgiven him.' Not to be lightly esteemed, therefore, is a medicine of this kind, which heals the ailments of both soul and body."

Notes

  • Reception and debate. Chalon calls unction a "medicine… of soul and body," frames it as a conciliar duty ("ungui debent"), and assigns it to presbyteri. F. W. Puller (Anglican, 1904) calls the canon "the first clear instance that I have been able to discover of a synodical injunction being given, requiring that the oil should be administered to the sick by the presbyters."2 Whether the canon narrowed the rite that Innocent I (416) and Bede (c. 710) describe, under which all Christians might use the blessed oil "in their own… necessity," is disputed; see Was the lay anointing permitted by Innocent I (416) the same rite as the presbyters' anointing of James 5:14? How the Carolingian texts bear on the later reservation of the anointing to the dying is also disputed; see How is the medieval reservation of the anointing of James 5:14 to the dying to be read?
  • The numbering. The canon is headed XLVIII (48) in Werminghoff's critical edition; a few secondary works miscite it as canon 47.
