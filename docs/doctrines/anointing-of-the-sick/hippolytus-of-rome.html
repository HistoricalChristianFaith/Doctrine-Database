Hippolytus of Rome on the anointing of the sick

Dates: c. 215 · Wikipedia
Summary: The church order known as the Apostolic Tradition preserves the earliest surviving prayer for the blessing of oil: offered like the bread and wine, the oil is consecrated so that it may "bestow comfort on all who taste it and health on all who use it" — the liturgical seed of the anointing of the sick.

Claims & evidence

Oil blessed to give health to those who use it

Among the offerings brought to the bishop, oil receives its own thanksgiving, modelled on the eucharistic blessing:

If anyone offers oil, he shall give thanks as at the offering of the bread and wine, though not with the same words but in the same general manner, saying: That sanctifying this oil, O God, wherewith thou didst anoint kings, priests and prophets, thou wouldest grant health to them who use it and partake of it, so that it may bestow comfort on all who taste it and health on all who use it.

— Hippolytus (attrib.), The Apostolic Tradition, ch. 5 ("Of the Offering of Oil"), trans. Burton Scott Easton, source.1

Notes

  • The blessing before the rite. The prayer says nothing of a set ceremony for the sick; it blesses oil for general use — tasting and anointing — "for health." But it establishes the two features that run through the whole tradition: oil consecrated by the church's ministry, aimed at the health of those who use it. That the oil could be tasted as well as applied confirms how far the early practice stood from the later deathbed rite. Bears on the narrowing thesis.
  • On attribution and dating. The Apostolic Tradition is transmitted anonymously and reconstructed from Latin and oriental versions; it is conventionally ascribed to Hippolytus of Rome (c. 215), though that attribution and the text's unity are debated. The chapter number for the oil-blessing varies by edition (ch. 5 in Easton and Dix).
