Hilary of Arles on the anointing of the sick

Dates: c. 430 · Wikipedia
Summary: Hilary's brief gloss on James reads the "sickness" of v. 14 as the sickness of sin, and the oil as a symbol of the "grace of mercy" — an allegorizing strand that pulls the passage toward spiritual healing and forgiveness rather than bodily cure.

Claims & evidence

The sickness is sin; the oil, the grace of mercy

Where the plain sense speaks of a bodily illness, Hilary's short tractate turns the whole picture inward — and notes that the custom of anointing continued in his own day:

This sickness is the sickness of sin. If someone is struck down by his own thoughts, then he should pray on his own, but if he has committed some sin he should ask for the church's prayers. The custom mentioned here is followed even today, for the grace of mercy is symbolized by oil.

— Hilary of Arles, Introductory Tractate on the Letter of James, on James 5:14, trans. in Ancient Christian Commentary on Scripture: James, 1–2 Peter, 1–3 John, Jude, ed. Gerald Bray.1 On v. 15 he adds that the "prayer of faith" is "the consensus of the whole church," citing the Gospel promise that whatever is asked in Christ's name shall be done.2

Notes

  • The spiritual reading. Hilary is a compact early witness to the interpretive move that makes James 5:14 primarily about the forgiveness of sins — feeding the later sacramental reading — even as he still reports anointing as a live "custom." The oil-as-mercy symbolism reappears in the medieval theology of the rite.
  • On sourcing. Hilary's Tractatus survives in fragments; the wording above is as printed in the Ancient Christian Commentary series, which draws on the critical edition of the pseudo-Hilarian James material. No open online copy of the excerpt was located.
