Innocent I on the anointing of the sick

Dates: 416 · Wikipedia
Summary: Answering a bishop's questions, Innocent I gives the earliest surviving authoritative gloss on James 5:14: the oil is chrism blessed by the bishop; not only priests but all Christians may use it to anoint themselves or their household in need; but it is a "kind of sacrament" and so may not be given to those under public penance.

Claims & evidence

The oil is bishop-blessed chrism, and laity may anoint with it

Decentius of Gubbio had asked how James 5:14 was to be understood. Innocent replies that the passage concerns "the sick faithful," that the oil is chrism the bishop has consecrated, and that its use in anointing is open to priests and laity alike — the "elders" are named only because bishops cannot visit every sick person:

There is no doubt that this [passage of James] ought to be interpreted or understood of the sick faithful, who can be anointed with the holy oil of chrism, which, prepared by a bishop, it is permitted not only to priests but also to all Christians to use for anointing in their own necessity or in that of their [people].… For the reason priests are named [by James] is that bishops, hindered by other occupations, cannot go to all the sick.

— Innocent I, Epistle 25 (to Decentius of Gubbio), ch. 8 (19 March 416), trans. Roy J. Deferrari, The Sources of Catholic Dogma (Denzinger 99), source; Latin in Patrologia Latina 20:559–560.1

It is "a kind of sacrament" — and so is denied to penitents

The one restriction Innocent draws is not a restriction to the dying but the exclusion of penitents — and his reason is that the anointing is itself a sacrament, and penitents are barred from the sacraments:

For this cannot be administered to penitents, because it is a kind of sacrament. For how is it thought that one kind [of sacrament] can be granted to those to whom the rest of the sacraments are denied?

— Innocent I, Epistle 25, ch. 8 (Latin: "Nam paenitentibus istud infundi non potest, quia genus est sacramenti…"), Latin text (MGH).2

Notes

  • A healing rite with lay administration. Innocent's ruling assumes anointing is ordinary pastoral care for "the sick faithful," administrable even by laypeople with the blessed oil — a scope flatly wider than the later reservation of the rite to those in danger of death. He supplies decisive early evidence for the narrowing thesis — and, as the earliest voice permitting lay anointing, is the linchpin witness for the companion minister-narrowing thesis (the later reservation of the anointing to priests alone). He is quoted to both effects three centuries later by Bede.
  • “A kind of sacrament.” His phrase genus sacramenti is among the earliest to count the anointing as a sacrament in some sense — grist for the later sacramental reading — though it long predates the closed list of seven and the Tridentine definition. He is cited on the seven sacraments and on confirmation for parallel rulings on who may minister a rite.
