Thomas Aquinas on the anointing of the sick (extreme unction)

Dates: c. 1274 · Wikipedia
Summary: Aquinas gives the fullest scholastic treatment of the rite as extreme unction — the deathbed sacrament that Bonaventure defined and the Council of Florence would legislate. In the treatise on Extreme Unction (Summa Theologiae Supplement, qq. 29–33) he fixes its matter (olive oil blessed by a bishop), its form (a prayer of petition), its principal effect (the remission of the "remnants of sin," and bodily health only "if requisite" for the healing of the soul), its minister (a priest, never a layman or deacon), and its recipients (only the sick who are in danger of death). The healing anointing of James 5 has become, first of all, the last rite of the dying.

Claims & evidence

A true sacrament, instituted by Christ, its matter olive oil

Aquinas ranks extreme unction as a full sacrament (not a mere sacramental), because it reaches the sacraments' principal effect — the healing of the disease of sin:

the sacraments are neither more nor less than spiritual remedies. Now Extreme Unction is a spiritual remedy, since it avails for the remission of sins, according to James 5:15. Therefore it is a sacrament.… Since then Extreme Unction reaches to this effect, as is clear from the words of James, and is not ordained to any other sacrament as an accessory thereto, it is evident that Extreme Unction is not a sacramental but a sacrament.

— Summa Theologiae Suppl. q. 29, a. 1.1 On institution, he takes the more probable of the two scholastic opinions — that Christ instituted it but reserved its promulgation to the apostles — and answers the objection that the Gospels are silent by pointing, as Bonaventure and Florence would, to Mark 6:13:

others hold that Christ Himself instituted all the sacraments, but that He Himself published some, which present greater difficulty to our belief, while he reserved some to be published by the apostles, such as Extreme Unction and Confirmation.… As a matter of fact… an anointing done by the apostles is mentioned in the Gospel (Mk. 6:13) where it is said that they "anointed the sick with oil."

— Summa Theologiae Suppl. q. 29, a. 3.2 The matter is olive oil, appointed by James and consecrated by a bishop — "since oil is, above all, the name of the liquid extract of olives… it follows that olive oil is the matter which should be employed in this sacrament."3

Its form: a prayer of petition, not an assertion

Unlike "This is My body" or "I baptize thee," extreme unction's form is deprecatory — a petition — because its effect is not guaranteed to follow, and because James ascribes its power to prayer:

The form of this sacrament is expressed by way of a petition, as appears from the words of James, and from the custom of the Roman Church, who uses no other than words of supplication in conferring this sacrament.… the effect of this sacrament is not such that it always results from the minister's prayer… Consequently the form of this sacrament cannot be expressed in the indicative mood.

— Summa Theologiae Suppl. q. 29, a. 8. The words in use, he notes, run: "Through this holy unction, and His most tender mercy, may the Lord pardon thee whatever sins thou hast committed, by sight," etc.4

Its principal effect: the remission of the "remnants of sin"

Aquinas places extreme unction third in a series of spiritual medicines — baptism the regeneration, penance the resurrection, unction the final cure — and locates its proper effect not in the guilt of mortal sin (penance's work) but in the residual weakness that sin leaves behind:

just as Baptism is a spiritual regeneration, and Penance, a spiritual resurrection, so Extreme Unction is a spiritual healing or cure.… this sacrament is not an antidote to those defects which deprive man of spiritual life, namely. original and mortal sin, but is a remedy for such defects as weaken man spiritually… Consequently we must say that the principal effect of this sacrament is the remission of sin, as to its remnants, and, consequently, even as to its guilt, if it find it.

— Summa Theologiae Suppl. q. 30, a. 1.5

Bodily health, only "if requisite" for the soul

Bodily cure survives — the James text and Mark 6:13 both promise it — but demoted to a secondary, conditional effect that follows only when it serves the spiritual healing:

Extreme Unction causes a bodily healing, not by a natural property of the matter, but by the Divine power which works reasonably. And since reasonable working never produces a secondary effect, except in so far as it is required for the principal effect, it follows that a bodily healing does not always ensue from this sacrament, but only when it is requisite for the spiritual healing.

— Summa Theologiae Suppl. q. 30, a. 2.6

The minister: a priest — not a layman, not a deacon

Because the sacrament confers grace and the remission of sin, its minister must be a priest. A layman cannot give it ("laymen have not the power to forgive sins"), nor can a deacon, who "has the power to cleanse but not to enlighten"; against the parallel with confirmation, no bishop is required either — "it can be given by ordinary priests":

The minister of this sacrament has to be brought in to the recipient, as is clear from James 5:14. Now a bishop cannot go to all the sick people of his diocese.… those sacraments are reserved to a bishop's dispensation, which place the recipient in a state of perfection above others. But this is not the case with this sacrament, for it is given to all. Consequently it can be given by ordinary priests.

— Summa Theologiae Suppl. q. 31, aa. 1–3.7

For the sick in danger of death — the reservation stated

The narrowing is sharpest here: the sacrament is refused to the healthy (even the healthy about to be beheaded), to children, and to the merely-ailing, and reserved to those whose sickness threatens death:

This sacrament is the last remedy that the Church can give, since it is an immediate preparation for glory. Therefore it ought to be given to those only, who are so sick as to be in a state of departure from this life, through their sickness being of such a nature as to cause death, the danger of which is to be feared.

— Summa Theologiae Suppl. q. 32, a. 2 (cf. a. 1: it is not for those "who are in good health"; a. 4: not for children).8

No character — and therefore repeatable

Unlike baptism, confirmation, and orders, extreme unction imprints no permanent character, since it deputes a man to no sacred duty but is "for no other purpose than a remedy." From this Aquinas draws its repeatability — against the objection that nothing comes after what is "extreme":

A character is not imprinted except in those sacraments whereby man is deputed to some sacred duty. Now this sacrament is for no other purpose than a remedy… Therefore it does not imprint a character.

— Summa Theologiae Suppl. q. 30, a. 3. And so, since "health of body and soul… can be lost after it has been effected," the sacrament "can, without disparagement thereto, be repeated" — the name "extreme" meaning only that it is given when "death men think to be nigh," not that it can be received but once.9

Notes

  • The definitive statement of the deathbed rite. Aquinas systematizes what Bonaventure had defined a generation earlier, and supplies the full theological apparatus — matter, form, effect, minister, recipients, repetition — that the Council of Florence (1439) would then legislate almost point for point ("the fifth sacrament is extreme unction, whose matter is the olive oil blessed by the bishop… given only to the sick of whose death there is fear… the minister … is the priest"). See the narrowing thesis.
  • Where he keeps the older horizon. The healing effect of James 5:15 and Mark 6:13 is not denied — Aquinas affirms that "unction has its effect now in healing bodies" — but it is subordinated: bodily cure follows "only when it is requisite for the spiritual healing." This is the demotion the healing reading (Cajetan, and later the Reformers) would resist; see the healing reading and the sacramental reading.
  • Provenance. The treatise on Extreme Unction stands in the Supplement to the Summa, compiled after Aquinas's death (1274) from his earlier Commentary on the Sentences; it transmits his teaching, though he did not write out the Summa that far himself (the Tertia Pars breaks off at the sacrament of penance). The translation quoted is that of the Fathers of the English Dominican Province.
  • His near-contemporary Peter Lombard had already numbered extreme unction among the seven sacraments, holding it "instituted by the apostles" — the opinion Aquinas cites and corrects here (Suppl. q. 29, a. 3).
