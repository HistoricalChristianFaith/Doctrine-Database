Origen on the anointing of the sick

Dates: c. 245 · Wikipedia
Summary: In his second homily on Leviticus, Origen lists the seven ways sins are remitted in the gospel; the seventh — "hard and laborious" — is the remission of sins through penance, and he anchors it directly in James 5:14, the sick calling the elders to anoint and pray. The earliest surviving reading of James 5 as a channel of the forgiveness of sins.

Claims & evidence

The seventh remission of sins — penance — is grounded in James 5:14

After baptism, martyrdom, almsgiving, mutual forgiveness, converting a sinner, and abundant love, Origen names a seventh, harder path to the remission of sins, and cites James for it:

There is still a seventh also, though hard and laborious — the remission of sins through penance, when the sinner washes "his bed with tears" and his "tears" become to him "bread day and night," when he is not ashamed to make known his sin to the priest of the Lord and to seek the remedy… In which is fulfilled also that which the Apostle James says: "but if anyone is sick, let him call the elders of the church, and let them lay hands on him, anointing him with oil in the name of the Lord. And the prayer of faith will save the sick man, and if he be in sins, they will be remitted to him."

— Origen, Homilies on Leviticus, Homily 2.4 (surviving in Rufinus's Latin), source.1

Notes

  • James 5 as remission, not the deathbed. Origen reads James 5:14 as the scriptural warrant for sin-forgiving penance — confession to the priest and the seeking of a remedy — not as a preparation for dying. His witness feeds two threads at once: the sacramental reading of James 5 (the anointing carries forgiveness) and, by its silence on any deathbed reservation, the narrowing thesis.
  • Anointing and penance intertwined. That Origen files James 5:14 under penance shows how closely the forgiveness of sins and the anointing of the sick were bound together in the early centuries — the same overlap that reappears in Bede (confession to the elders as the condition of forgiveness) and that the schoolmen would later have to separate into two distinct sacraments.
