Bonaventure on the anointing of the sick

Dates: c. 1257 · Wikipedia
Summary: In the Breviloquium, Bonaventure gives the classic high-scholastic definition of the rite as extreme unction — "the Sacrament of those departing from this life," preparing the dying for "perfect health," which secondarily remits venial sins and may restore bodily health "if it be expedient." The healing rite has become, first of all, a rite for the deathbed.

Claims & evidence

The sacrament of those departing this life

Bonaventure orders the ends of the sacrament with the deathbed first and bodily cure last and conditional:

Extreme unction is the Sacrament of those departing from this life, preparing and disposing them for perfect health; it avails also for the removal of venial sins and for the recovery of present health, if it be expedient for the sick person.

— Bonaventure, Breviloquium, Part VI (on the sacraments), source.1

Its effects, and why it can still heal the body

He explains the double working — devotion and the remission of the "dross of sins" — and why, for those whom it is still expedient to live, it "frequently also alleviates from disease," grounding it in James 5:15:

This Sacrament has efficacy both for arousing devotion and for remitting venial sins and for more easily removing the dross of sins.… hence it is that this Sacrament, both by invigorating the soul in good and by unburdening it from evil, frequently also alleviates from disease. And this is what blessed James says, that the prayer of faith shall save the sick person, and if he be in sins, they shall be forgiven him.

— Bonaventure, Breviloquium, Part VI, source.2

Notes

  • The narrowing, stated. Bonaventure crystallizes the medieval turn: the sacrament's primary end is now to prepare the dying for glory ("perfect health"), with bodily recovery demoted to a conditional side-effect ("if it be expedient"). The very name — extreme unction, the "last" anointing — encodes the shift that Chrysostom, Innocent I, Caesarius, and Bede had not made. See the narrowing thesis.
  • Institution read out of Mark 6:13 — an overreach. Elsewhere in Part VI Bonaventure grounds the sacrament's dominical origin in the Gospel healing mission: "Christ only initiated and intimated these two Sacraments… Extreme unction he initiated by sending the disciples to heal, who anointed the sick with oil" (Mark 6:13). His own careful "only initiated and intimated" concedes that Mark 6:13 — a pre-Passion charismatic healing mission — cannot bear the full weight of "instituted by Christ"; the inference is weighed on the dominical-institution argument (assessed weak).
  • His contemporary Peter Lombard had already listed extreme unction among the seven; Bonaventure supplies its theological rationale.
