The Barberini Euchologion (c. 790) — the Byzantine oil-of-the-sick service

Dates: c. 790
Summary: Codex Vaticanus Barberini gr. 336, written in Byzantine southern Italy in the later eighth century, is the oldest surviving Byzantine Euchologion (service-book). It preserves the earliest datable form of the Eastern rite of anointing — the euchelaion (“prayer-oil”, the “Office of the Holy Oil”) — a service for the healing of the sick that, unlike the contemporaneous Latin rite, never contracted into a last anointing for the dying. It supplies the “East never narrowed” control case in the rite's own liturgical texts rather than in a later summary.

Claims & evidence

The oldest Byzantine service-book already has a full oil-of-the-sick rite

Barberini gr. 336 is “the oldest and, without any doubt, the most famous manuscript containing the Byzantine Euchologion,” copied in the Greek-speaking south of Italy from a Constantinopolitan model in the second half of the eighth century (commonly dated c. 780–795).1 Among its rites of the sacraments it carries a set of prayers for the anointing of the sick: in Paul Meyendorff's reconstruction the manuscript has five such prayers — three prayers over the sick person and two for the blessing of the oil, the oil evidently consecrated at the Eucharistic Liturgy immediately after the consecration of the bread and wine, exactly as the third-century blessing over oil had been offered alongside the offering.2

A rite of healing — oil blessed “unto the healing… relief from every ailment”

The euchelaion's whole horizon is recovery. The prayer over the oil asks God to make it a medicine of health, in the line that descends from Serapion's fourth-century prayer to the living Byzantine service:

Do Thou sanctify this Oil unto the healing of those who shall be anointed therewith; unto relief from every ailment…

and the anointing prayer itself — said over the sick person at each anointing — petitions bodily and spiritual cure together, never preparation for death:

O Holy Father, Physician of souls and bodies, Who did send Thine Only Begotten Son, our Lord Jesus Christ, Who heals every infirmity and delivers from death: Heal also Thy servant [Name]…

God addressed as the “Physician of souls and bodies,” the whole prayer bent on healing — the same double register (health of body, forgiveness of the soul) that the earliest Eastern and Latin witnesses share.3

The seven-priest form — and the well anointed alongside the sick

Where the Latin rite was being handed to a single priest at the deathbed, the Byzantine service grew in the opposite direction: the concelebrating presbyters who blessed the oil increased “from one to as many as seven priests, as is cited in the eighth-century Codex Barberini,” giving the rite its classic name heptapapadon akolouthia, the “seven-priest service.” By the eleventh century the prayers were fixed at seven (with seven Epistle and seven Gospel readings and seven anointings), and — the decisive contrast with the West — “sources from this period attest to the fact that healthy persons (including the celebrants) were anointed along with the sick as a preparation for receiving the Eucharist.” A rite so far from a deathbed sacrament that the well received it.4

Never “Extreme Unction”

The Eastern church drew the explicit contrast the timeline turns on: the euchelaion “has never been regarded by the Orthodox Church as ‘Extreme Unction’, intended only for the dying,” but is “available for all who suffer from any physical or mental illness.” The same apostolic rite of James 5:14 that the medieval West reserved to those in danger of death remained, in the East, a healing of the sick.5

Notes

  • Why this is the “East never narrowed” anchor. The narrowing thesis needs a control case: a branch of the church that kept the apostolic rite as a healing anointing so the Latin contraction shows up as a specifically Western development, not something intrinsic to the sacrament. The euchelaion is that control case, and the Barberini Euchologion — a datable service-book from the very century in which the Carolingian West was welding unction to the deathbed (Theodulf, c. 810; the Council of Chalon, 813) — lets it rest on the rite's own liturgical text rather than on a later report. The Greek commentators Oecumenius (c. 990) and Theophylact (c. 1107) read the same James 5 passage the same healing way.
  • A datable book, dated by the book. Like the councils on this timeline, this witness is a document rather than a person; it is slotted by the date of the manuscript (c. 790), though the practice it records is older — the euchelaion descends in an unbroken line from Serapion's fourth-century oil prayer, which the Barberini prayers echo.
