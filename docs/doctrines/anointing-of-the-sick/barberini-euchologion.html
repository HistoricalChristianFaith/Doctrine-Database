The Barberini Euchologion (c. 790) — the Byzantine oil-of-the-sick service

Dates: c. 790
Summary: Codex Vaticanus Barberini gr. 336, written in Byzantine southern Italy in the later eighth century, is the oldest surviving Byzantine Euchologion (service-book). It preserves the earliest datable form of the Eastern rite of anointing — the euchelaion (“prayer-oil”, the “Office of the Holy Oil”), a service of prayers over the sick and for the blessing of the oil.

Claims & evidence

The oldest Byzantine service-book and its oil-of-the-sick prayers

Barberini gr. 336 is “the oldest and, without any doubt, the most famous manuscript containing the Byzantine Euchologion,” copied in the Greek-speaking south of Italy from a Constantinopolitan model in the second half of the eighth century (commonly dated c. 780–795).1 Among its rites of the sacraments it carries a set of prayers for the anointing of the sick: in Paul Meyendorff's reconstruction the manuscript has five such prayers — three prayers over the sick person and two for the blessing of the oil — and no rubrics.2

A rite of healing — oil blessed “unto the healing… relief from every ailment”

The prayer over the oil in the received Byzantine service asks God to make it a medicine of health:

Do Thou sanctify this Oil unto the healing of those who shall be anointed therewith; unto relief from every ailment…

and the anointing prayer, said over the sick person at each anointing, petitions for healing:

O Holy Father, Physician of souls and bodies, Who did send Thine Only Begotten Son, our Lord Jesus Christ, Who heals every infirmity and delivers from death: Heal also Thy servant [Name]…

These prayers are cited from the received form of the service, which addresses God as the “Physician of souls and bodies.”3

The seven-priest form — and the well anointed alongside the sick

Meyendorff, as Job Getcha reports him, cites Theodulf of Orléans for the Greek rite of the unction of the sick performed by three priests. The next witness he cites is an eleventh-century manuscript, Paris Coislin 213, in which the seven-priest service appears: the rite is performed by seven priests on seven consecutive days during the Divine Liturgy; a twelfth-century manuscript, Sinai gr. 973, describes a celebration by seven (or three or two) priests over seven days. The present form of the rite, with seven Epistle and seven Gospel readings, appears in the fourteenth century.2 The service is known as the heptapapadon akolouthia, the “seven-priest service” (⚠ unverified). According to the survey cited, “by the eleventh century the number of prayers was fixed at seven,” and “sources from this period attest to the fact that healthy persons (including the celebrants) were anointed along with the sick as a preparation for receiving the Eucharist.”4

An Orthodox account of the rite

Kallistos Ware (Orthodox), as quoted, writes that the euchelaion “has never been regarded by the Orthodox Church as ‘Extreme Unction’, intended only for the dying,” but is “available for all who suffer from any physical or mental illness.”5

Notes

  • Reception and debate. How the Byzantine rite bears on the Latin reservation of the anointing to the dying is read differently by Protestant, Catholic and Orthodox writers; see How is the medieval reservation of the anointing of James 5:14 to the dying to be read? The Greek commentators Oecumenius (c. 990) and Theophylact (c. 1107) gloss James 5:14 by the apostles' anointing of the sick in Mark 6:13.
