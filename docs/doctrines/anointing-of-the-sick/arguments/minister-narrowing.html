Was the lay anointing permitted by Innocent I (416) the same rite as the presbyters' anointing of James 5:14?

Disputed reading · on Anointing of the Sick · witness: Innocent I

Related readings: How is the medieval reservation of the anointing of James 5:14 to the dying to be read? · Do the early witnesses attach the forgiveness of James 5:15 to the anointing or to penance?

The passage

There is no doubt that this [passage of James] ought to be interpreted or understood of the sick faithful, who can be anointed with the holy oil of chrism, which, prepared by a bishop, it is permitted not only to priests but also to all Christians to use for anointing in their own necessity or in that of their [people].… For the reason priests are named [by James] is that bishops, hindered by other occupations, cannot go to all the sick.

— Innocent I, Epistle 25 (to Decentius of Gubbio), ch. 8 (19 March 416).1

the proper ministers of this sacrament are the Presbyters of the Church; by which name are to be understood, in that place, not the elders by age, or the foremost in dignity amongst the people, but, either bishops, or priests by bishops rightly ordained

— Council of Trent, Session 14 (1551), Doctrine on the Sacrament of Extreme Unction, ch. 3.2

The question

Innocent I permits "all Christians" to use the bishop-blessed oil "for anointing in their own necessity or in that of their [people]," and Bede (c. 710) repeats the permission; the Council of Chalon (813) rules that the sick "ought to be anointed by priests," and Florence (1439) and Trent (1551) define the priest as the minister. Current Catholic law provides that "Every priest and a priest alone validly administers the anointing of the sick."3 The dispute is whether the lay use Innocent and Bede describe was the rite of James itself, later reserved to priests; a devotional use of the oil distinct from that rite, which presbyters alone administered; or the application of a sacramental oil whose consecration was the church's indispensable act.

One anointing, given by clergy and laity alike, later reserved to presbyters

On this reading Innocent and Bede describe the one anointing of James, which lay people as well as presbyters administered until the ninth century reserved it to priests.

Held by: John Calvin (Reformed, Institutes, 1559); F. W. Puller (Anglican, The Anointing of the Sick in Scripture and Tradition, 1904); Paul Turner (Catholic liturgist, Worship, 2015; on Innocent's letter only).4

Calvin reads Innocent's letter as a ruling about the anointing itself: Innocent "ordained, that not elders only, but all Christians, should use oil in anointing, in their own necessity, or in that of their friends."5 Puller argues from the wording of the letter. Innocent "evidently drew no distinction between the Unction administered by lay people and the Unction administered by priests. If he had drawn such a distinction, he must have guarded his words from misapprehension." The permission covers anointing "in the need of the members of their household," which shows, Puller holds, that the laity were given "the active use of the oil," not only its reception. Across the early sources, "neither Pope Innocent nor any other Father of the first eight centuries has ever hinted at this distinction. They all speak of only one kind of anointing; and Innocent, Bede, and others assure us that the right to apply the Unction belonged equally to priests and lay-folk." He adds the Life of Geneviève of Paris, in which the saint anoints the sick and the possessed with oil a bishop had blessed.6 Puller then dates the reservation. Chalon's canon 48 (813) is "the first clear instance that I have been able to discover of a synodical injunction being given, requiring that the oil should be administered to the sick by the presbyters," and "probably during the earlier part of the ninth century the custom was gradually growing up of limiting the administration of the Unction to the presbyters, instead of allowing the lay people to anoint themselves and their children and their servants." He connects the change with the rise of the view that the unction itself imparts the remission of sins, which he finds in the ninth-century councils of Chalon and Pavia.7 On this reading the oil blessed by the bishop was applied to the sick by presbyters and by the faithful in one practice, and the Carolingian legislation and the later councils assigned that practice to priests alone.

A devotional use of the oil, distinct from the rite the presbyters administer

On this reading James's rite has always been administered by presbyters, and the laity's use of the blessed oil was a separate devotional practice.

Held by: P. Toner (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1909); the Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith (Catholic, commentary on its Note on the Minister of the Sacrament of the Anointing of the Sick, 2005).8

The case begins with James's "presbyters." Toner holds that tous presbyterous "would naturally admit no other meaning" than the ordained clergy, "but the addition of tes ekklesias excludes the possibility of doubt (cf. Acts 20:17)"; Trent defines the "proper ministers" accordingly. Innocent's letter, on this reading, teaches that "the 'sacrament' enjoined by St. James was to be administered to the sick faithful who were not doing canonical penance; that priests, and a fortiori bishops, can administer it; but that the oil must be blessed by the bishop." The lay use he permits is something else: "it is clear enough from the text of St. James and from the way in which Pope Innocent explains the mention of priests in the text, that this could not have been considered by him to be identical with the Jacobean rite, but to be at most a pious use of the oil allowable for devotional, and possibly for charismatic, purposes." Toner adds that "charismatic and other unctions of the sick, even with consecrated oil, distinct from the Jacobean unction, were practiced in the early ages," and concludes: "This lay unction was merely what we call today a sacramental."9 The 2005 commentary reads Innocent as answering whether bishops as well as presbyters may anoint: he "rejected this restriction, stating that presbyters are ministers of the Sacrament and Bishops are too." It holds that his letter, "like other testimonies of the first millennium (Caesarius of Arles, the Venerable Bede), provides no proof of the possibility of introducing ministers who are not priests," and traces the same rule through Gratian's Decretum (c. 1140), a decretal of Alexander III and Florence's "the minister of this Sacrament is the priest," to the teaching that the minister "est omnis et solus sacerdos," a doctrine it describes as "definitive tenenda."10 On this reading the presbyteral ministry runs unbroken from James's "presbyters of the church" through Innocent's "priests" to Florence and Trent, with the faithful's private use of the oil standing beside it.

The application of a sacramental oil whose consecration was the church's act

On this reading the early church regarded the bishop-blessed oil itself as the sacrament, so that its application to the sick, by presbyters or by lay people, followed from its consecration.

Held by: J. P. McClain and J. M. Donohue (Catholic, New Catholic Encyclopedia, "Anointing of the Sick").11

McClain and Donohue start from the place of the bishop: "It was always the rule, although one does find exceptions, that the oil for this sacrament had to be consecrated by a bishop. It is important to note that the intervention of the Church was indispensable in the blessing of this oil." From this they read the lay practice: "The oil itself appears to have been regarded as a permanent sacrament, in much the same way as the Eucharist today, and so its confection could be separated from the administration of the sacrament. If this is so, the practice of lay anointing is easily understood. The use of the oil, either by drinking it or applying it to the sick was a religious gesture that invoked the healing power of God over whatever ailment, evil, or sin afflicted the person." They cite the Life of Geneviève, who "was accustomed to anoint with blessed oil the sick for whom she cared" and was distressed when the vessel was empty "because there was no bishop within reach to bless it." Donohue's companion article on the liturgy adds that "in addition to presbyteral anointing, lay people took the blessed oil home and either drank it or rubbed themselves with it as needed." On the change, "the practice of lay anointing gradually fell into disuse from about the beginning of the 8th century in the Frankish kingdom and probably at an earlier date in Celtic lands," and "the ninth-century Carolingian reforms confirmed the trend toward clericalization, reserving anointing exclusively to priests."12 On this reading the bishop's consecration was the fixed point of the early rite, and the oil it made holy served the sick in the hands of presbyters and faithful alike.
