Does James's disputed canonical standing undercut the sacrament? — the antilegomena objection

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: weak — the interpretation weighed here is not about what James 5:14 means (that is the sacramental vs. healing question) but about the standing of the book it comes from: that a grace-conferring sacrament binding on the whole church cannot be securely founded on the single verse of an epistle whose apostolic authorship and canonical status were long contested — James was among Eusebius's antilegomena ("disputed"), little cited by the earliest fathers, and Luther later doubted its apostolicity and ranked it "an epistle of straw." On its best case the objection does not hold. The "disputed" tag records slow and uneven early reception (few citations), not a doctrinal verdict or a denial of inspiration — Eusebius himself transmits James as "recognized by many" and "read publicly in very many churches" — and the dispute was closed across East and West by the late fourth century (Athanasius 367; Hippo/Carthage), a full millennium before the sacrament was defined (Florence 1439, Trent 1551), by which time Trent had already reaffirmed the book's canonicity (1546). The Christian East, whose euchelaion is the richest anointing rite, never doubted James at all. And the move proves too much: the Reformers' own healing reading rests on the same verse (Luther used James 5 even as he disparaged the book), and the church grades no received book's authority a tier lower — so once canonical, James is Scripture without an asterisk. A real but narrower worry survives — that a whole sacrament rests on a single text — but that is the one-proof-text concern weighed on the sacramental reading, not a canonicity defect (weak) · advanced by Luther and later Protestant controversialists, answered by Trent and the Catholic and Orthodox traditions.

See also: Does James 5:14 institute a grace-conferring sacrament? and the healing reading — the two readings of the verse this objection tries to undercut at the root; and the wiki's full treatment of James's canonical history on the New Testament canon timeline (this page does not re-litigate the canon — it asks only whether the book's once-disputed status undoes the sacrament).

The claim

Many assert, and with great probability, that this epistle was not written by the Apostle James, and is not worthy of the apostolic spirit; although, whosesoever it is, it has obtained authority by usage. But even if it were written by the Apostle James, I should say that it was not lawful for an apostle to institute a sacrament by his own authority; that is, to give a divine promise with a sign annexed to it. To do this belonged to Christ alone.

— Martin Luther, The Babylonian Captivity of the Church (1520), on the sacrament of extreme unction.1 The whole rite of anointing rests on one proof-text, James 5:14–15 (the Gospel notice in Mark 6:13 being, even on Trent's account, only where it is "insinuated"). The objection targets that foundation directly: James stood among the books whose apostolic authority the ancient church disputed (Eusebius's antilegomena), and a sacrament — a grace-conferring instrument of the whole church — demands a more secure scriptural warrant than a single verse in a book whose canonical standing was, for centuries, not settled. This is distinct from the exegetical dispute over what the verse says; it attacks the proof-text's right to bear the weight at all.

Who advances it

Luther makes both halves of it in The Babylonian Captivity: the epistle is probably "not written by the Apostle James," and even if it were, an apostle could not on his own authority institute a sacrament — a promise-with-sign belongs "to Christ alone." Two years later his 1522 Preface to the New Testament ranked James below "the true and noblest books" and called it, by comparison with them, "an epistle of straw," faulting it for "nothing of the nature of the Gospel about it."2 The historical premise behind the move is real and older than Luther: Eusebius classes James among the "disputed" writings and notes that "not many of the ancients have mentioned it."3 Later Protestant controversialists press the two together into a foundation-level objection: the one sacrament whose sole scriptural charter is James 5 is the sacrament whose charter comes from the shakiest-attested book in the New Testament.

The evidence for

James really was an antilegomenon. This is not Reformation invention. Eusebius twice records the doubt — in the account of James the Just, "it is to be observed that it is disputed; at least, not many of the ancients have mentioned it," and in the formal catalogue of Scripture, "among the disputed writings… are extant the so-called epistle of James… also that of Jude… and the second epistle of Peter."3 James is absent from the Muratorian Fragment's list and is little quoted in the West before the fourth century; the doubt was genuine and lasted a long time. The sacrament rests on this one book alone. Unlike baptism or the Eucharist — each attested across several undisputed Gospels and Paul — anointing-of-the-sick has, for its institution, a single verse (James 5:14–15) in a single letter, with Mark 6:13 conceded to be only an "insinuation." A foundation that narrow is more exposed to a defect in its one source than a broadly-attested doctrine would be. Luther's second premise is weighty in its own right. Even granting the book, the claim that an apostle may institute a sacrament — couple a divine promise to an outward sign, binding the church for all time — is a real theological question; if that power belongs to Christ alone, then James 5, whoever wrote it, could at most record a practice, not found a sacrament.

Adversarial assessment

"Disputed" is a report on reception, not a rejection. Eusebius's antilegomena are precisely the books "which are nevertheless recognized by many"; of James he adds that it, "with the rest, [has] been read publicly in very many churches."3 The category marks slow and uneven early circulation — few surviving citations, a general (Jewish-Christian) audience with no founding-apostle church to champion it — not a doctrinal charge against the book or a denial of its inspiration. Eusebius himself hands it on as churchly Scripture. To read "disputed" as "dubious and therefore unfit to found doctrine" mistakes a note about attestation for a verdict about authority.

The dispute was closed a millennium before the sacrament was defined. James was firmly canonical across East and West from the later fourth century: Athanasius's Festal Letter 39 (367) lists it among the twenty-seven, and the African councils of Hippo (393) and Carthage (397) ratified the same canon.4 Extreme unction was not defined as one of the sacraments until the Council of Florence (1439) and the Council of Trent (1551) — and Trent had already, in 1546, formally listed James among the canonical Scriptures received "with equal devotion and reverence."5 So at the moment the church that defined the sacrament defined it, the canonicity of James was not in question in that church at all. A doubt that had been dead for a thousand years cannot destabilize a definition made after it closed.

The East — home of the richest anointing rite — never doubted James. The euchelaion, the seven-priest Office of the Holy Oil that anoints even the well, is the fullest development of James 5 anywhere in Christendom; and the Greek and Syriac churches received James without hesitation (it stands among the three Catholic Epistles the early Syriac Peshitta did include). The tradition with the most elaborate practice built on James 5 is the one that never treated the book as disputed.4

The objection proves too much — it cuts the hand that wields it. The Reformers' own healing reading of the same passage — "the prayer of faith shall save the sick, and the Lord shall raise him up" — rests on James 5:14. Luther disparaged the epistle and still appealed to it; Calvin built his case for anointing-as-recovery on it. If James is too insecure to found the Catholic sacrament, it is equally too insecure to found the Protestant reading — and a weapon that destroys both readings of the verse, and with them every doctrine that leans on an antilegomenon (2 Peter, Jude, and, in the West, Hebrews), is not a discriminating argument against this rite. The church grades no received book a tier below the rest: once James is in the canon, its single verse carries the same authority as any other, and "it was once disputed" no more voids James 5:14 than it voids James 2 on faith and works.

Luther's "epistle of straw" is a value-judgment, not a canonical datum. The phrase is comparative and theological — he measures James against "the true and noblest books" and finds it thin on his own justification-by- faith touchstone (James's stress on works). It is not a finding that the early church rejected the book, and it cannot be smuggled in as if it were evidence for the antilegomena premise; Luther kept James in his New Testament and went on quoting it. The ancient "disputed" status and Luther's sixteenth-century preference are two different things, and neither, on inspection, is a canonical disqualification.

What genuinely survives is a different argument. Luther's second premise — that only Christ, not an apostle, may institute a sacrament — and the observation that the whole rite hangs on a single verse are real points. But they are not canonicity points: they concede James is Scripture and ask instead whether that one scriptural verse institutes a sacrament. That is exactly the question weighed on the sacramental reading (rated contested) and the dominical-institution page (rated weak). The distinctively canonical objection — "the book itself is too disputed to found anything" — is the part that fails.

Assessment

Weak. As an argument that the sacrament's foundation is undone by James's contested canonical standing, it does not hold. The "disputed" classification reports thin early attestation, not a doctrinal rejection or a denial of inspiration — Eusebius passes James on as read in very many churches; the dispute was settled across the whole church by the late fourth century and formally reaffirmed at Trent, a millennium before and again just before the sacrament was defined; the East that developed the fullest anointing rite never doubted the book; and the objection recoils on the Reformers' own reading of the same verse and on every doctrine resting on an once-disputed book. Luther's "epistle of straw" is a theological value-judgment, not a canonical verdict, and cannot do the work the objection needs. What remains live is not about the book's standing but about whether its one verse institutes a sacrament — the question already carried by the sacramental and institution pages. On its own terms, the canonicity objection is weak.
