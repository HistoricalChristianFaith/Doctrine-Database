Does Mark 6:13 institute the sacrament, or record a healing mission? — the dominical-institution argument

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: weak — the interpretation weighed here is that Mark 6:13, where the Twelve "anointed with oil many that were sick, and healed them," furnishes the dominical institution of the sacrament of anointing — the sacrament "instituted by Christ… insinuated indeed in Mark" (Trent). Read on its best case the claim does not hold: Mark 6:13 records a pre-Passion charismatic healing mission — the apostles sent out to preach repentance, cast out demons, and heal (Mark 6:7–13) — with no word of institution, no promise of grace or the forgiveness of sins, and no note of permanence; the anointing is a means of bodily cure, oil being the era's ordinary medicine. Even the councils that cite it hedge to "insinuated" / "alluded to" and rest the real weight of institution on James, so Mark 6:13 is offered as an inference, not a datum — Bonaventure grants that Christ "only initiated and intimated" the rite. What the verse does establish — the antiquity of apostolic anointing-of-the-sick and the ritual antecedent the Greek fathers rightly tie to James 5:14 — is real, but that is the continuity of a rite, not the institution of a grace-conferring sacrament (weak) · advanced by Bonaventure and the Council of Trent, denied by the Reformers.

See also: Does James 5:14 institute a grace-conferring sacrament? (the sacramental reading) — the sacramentality question this institution claim is meant to undergird — and the healing reading.

The claim

Before the sending of the Holy Spirit, there was neither the full gift of the Holy Spirit for confirmation and public confession of the name of Christ, nor the plenary anointing of the mind for departure; therefore Christ only initiated and intimated these two Sacraments, namely confirmation and extreme unction. Extreme unction he initiated by sending the disciples to heal, who anointed the sick with oil.

— Bonaventure, Breviloquium VI, on extreme unction, glossing Mark 6:13.1 The assertion is that Mark 6:13 — the only Gospel notice that the apostles anointed the sick with oil — is the point at which Christ himself instituted (or at least first "insinuated") the sacrament later promulgated by James, so that extreme unction has a dominical and not merely apostolic warrant.

Who advances it

The scholastics fixed the reading — Bonaventure locates the sacrament's initiation precisely in Christ's sending of the Twelve to anoint (Mark 6:13), its later promulgation in James 5:14 — and the Council of Trent made it dogma: extreme unction was "instituted by Christ our Lord, as truly and properly a sacrament of the new law, insinuated indeed in Mark, but… promulgated to the faithful by James the Apostle."2 The verb is carefully weak on the Gospel side: Trent says insinuated (adumbrated), not instituted here, and the modern Catechism repeats the hedge — the sacrament "is alluded to indeed by Mark, but is recommended to the faithful and promulgated by James."2 The Reformers denied the inference outright, reading Mark 6:13 as part of a temporary miracle-working commission that does not institute a standing rite of the church — the same ground on which they read James 5:14 of the recovery of the sick.3

The evidence for

It is the one Gospel record of apostolic anointing. Mark alone reports that the Twelve "anointed with oil many that were sick, and healed them" (Mark 6:13), and they do so under Christ's own commission (Mark 6:7). That the church's later anointing-of-the-sick has a root in an act the incarnate Lord commanded is not nothing: the practice is not a post-apostolic invention but goes back to a mission Jesus sent. The Greek fathers tie the two texts together. Commenting on Mark 6:13, Theophylact notes that "only Mark speaks of this, and also James… in his Catholic Epistle," reading the Gospel act and the epistle's rite as one continuous ordinance; the catena tradition (Victor of Antioch, in the Catena Aurea) makes the same pairing.4 Bede reads it as apostolic warrant for the standing custom. On Mark 6:13, Bede takes the verse to prove "that it is an ancient custom of the holy Church that persons… afflicted with any disease whatever, should be anointed with oil consecrated by priestly blessing" — the Gospel mission read as the seedbed of the ongoing rite.4

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The ritual continuity is genuine. Mark 6:13 really is the earliest notice of apostolic anointing of the sick with oil, done by men Christ sent, and the Greek exegetes are right that James 5:14 describes the same act carried on in the church. As evidence for the antiquity of the rite — that anointing the sick is a practice reaching back into the Gospel period, not a medieval novelty — the verse tells, and the flat charge that the anointing is "a human figment" cannot stand against it.

What is overstated. (1) It is a healing mission, not an institution. Mark 6:7–13 is a single commissioning: the Twelve are sent, before the Passion, to preach repentance, cast out demons, and heal, and the anointing is one instrument of that cure ("healed them"). Nothing in the passage institutes a rite — no institution formula, no matter-and-form, no promise of grace or the forgiveness of sins, no word that it is to continue. To read the institution of a New-Covenant sacrament out of it is an inference laid on the text, not a datum in it. (2) The oil is medicine. Anointing with oil was the ordinary therapy of the ancient world (so the Samaritan pours oil and wine on the traveller's wounds, Luke 10:34); Mark's "anointed… and healed them" reads first as bodily treatment joined to the healing power given the apostles, not as the conferral of sacramental grace.5 (3) Even the proponents hedge. Trent says insinuated, the Catechism alluded to, and Bonaventure that Christ "only initiated and intimated" it — each resting the actual institution and promulgation on James, not on Mark. That careful wording is itself the concession that Mark 6:13 alone cannot bear "instituted by Christ." (4) Continuity is not institution. The link the Greek fathers draw between Mark 6:13 and James 5:14 establishes that the same practice runs from the Gospel into the apostolic church — the antiquity of the rite. It does not establish that Christ thereby founded a permanent grace-conferring sacrament; those are distinct claims, and the sacramental one must be argued from James, where it is itself contested.

Assessment

Weak. Rate the interpretation on its best case. As the grounding for the sacrament's dominical institution — the load-bearing sense in which Trent adduces it — Mark 6:13 does not hold: it records a pre-Passion charismatic healing mission, with the anointing a means of bodily cure, and carries no institution, no promise of grace or forgiveness, and no note of permanence. The councils' own hedging — "insinuated," "alluded to," Bonaventure's "only initiated and intimated" — concedes that the verse is offered as an argument, not a datum, with the real weight of institution placed on James. What the verse genuinely supports is more modest and more defensible: the antiquity and continuity of apostolic anointing-of-the-sick, the ritual antecedent the Greek fathers fairly tie to James 5:14. That continuity claim is sound; the institution claim built on it is not (weak). Whether James promulgates a true sacrament is weighed on the sacramental reading; whether the rite is aimed at recovery, on the healing reading.
