Is James 5:14 a prayer for bodily healing rather than a deathbed sacrament? — the healing reading

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: plausible — the interpretation weighed here is that James 5:14–15 describes a prayer, with anointing, for the recovery of the sick — "the prayer of faith shall save the sick, and the Lord shall raise him up" — not a rite for the dying, so that the medieval extreme unction inverts the apostle's plain sense. It is coherent and strongly grounded: "raise up" reads as restoration to health, and the earliest practice (oil blessed "for health," lay anointing "in their own… necessity") aimed at cure. But it is short of the whole truth — the text itself adds "if he have committed sins, they shall be forgiven," and the ritual character (elders, blessed oil, prayer in the Lord's name) is more than a private prayer — and Calvin's further claim that the healing charism has ceased, voiding the rite, overreaches beyond what James says. Coherent and not ruled out, but overstated in its strong form (plausible) · advanced by Martin Luther, John Calvin, and (as a Catholic dissenter) Cardinal Cajetan.

Related arguments: the anointing of James 5:14 — see also Does James 5:14 institute a grace-conferring sacrament? (the sacramental reading)

The claim

James tells the sick to call the elders, who anoint and pray, "and the prayer of faith shall save the sick, and the Lord shall raise him up." On the healing reading, "save" (sōsei) and "raise up" (egerei) mean bodily recovery: the passage prescribes prayer for the cure of a sick believer, with anointing as its accompanying sign. It is therefore not a sacrament for those at the point of death; the church that reserves the anointing to the dying — renaming it extreme unction — has stood the apostle's meaning on its head.

Who advances it

Luther presses the plain grammar against Rome directly — James commands anointing so the sick man "may be healed and raised up, that is, may not die, and that the unction may not be extreme":

The Apostle commands the use of anointing and prayer for the very purpose that the sick man may be healed and raised up, that is, may not die, and that the unction may not be extreme.… They say, on the contrary, that unction should not be administered except to those on the point of departing, that is, that they may not be healed and raised up.

— Luther, The Babylonian Captivity of the Church (1520), "Of the Sacrament of Extreme Unction."1 Calvin adds a further, cessationist claim: the "prayer of faith" that healed belonged to the temporary gift of healing, "which the Lord was pleased to give for a time," and which "immediately ceased" — so that to keep anointing as a standing sacrament is "mere hypocritical stage-play," and the priests "besmear, with their oil, not the sick, but half-dead carcasses… in extremis," precisely the reverse of what James intended.2 Strikingly, the reading is not only Protestant: the Dominican cardinal Cajetan (Thomas de Vio), a loyal Catholic and Aquinas's great commentator, held that James's words refer to an anointing for the recovery of the sick and cannot be read of the sacrament of extreme unction — a concession noted (and attacked) by his own Catholic contemporary Ambrosius Catharinus, and later admitted by Bellarmine to be all but unique among Catholics.3

The evidence for

The verbs point to recovery. "The Lord shall raise him up" (egerei) most naturally means restoration from the sickbed; "save the sick" reads as make-well, not usher-into-glory. A rite whose success is the sick person not dying is ill-named "the last anointing." The earliest practice aims at health. The oldest witnesses bless oil "for health" to be tasted as well as applied (the Apostolic Tradition; Serapion), and Innocent I permits any Christian to anoint "in their own… necessity" — a rite for the sick as such, not for the dying (see the narrowing thesis). Even a cardinal concedes it. That Cajetan, with no Protestant motive, reached the same exegetical conclusion shows the healing reading is driven by the text, not merely by polemic against Rome.

Adversarial assessment

What holds. As an account of the passage's primary horizon, the healing reading is strong. The grammar favours recovery, the earliest attested practice is therapeutic and even lay-administered, and its cross-confessional support (Cajetan) shows it is not special pleading. On the narrow point — that James's rite is aimed at the sick for their healing, not at the dying — it is essentially correct, and the deathbed-only development is a later contraction the church itself later reversed.

What is overstated. (1) The forgiveness clause. James does not stop at bodily cure: "if he have committed sins, they shall be forgiven him." From Origen on, the tradition read the passage as touching the remission of sins too, so a purely-somatic reading understates the text's own words. (2) The ritual character. James prescribes not private prayer but a churchly act — the elders called, anointing "in the name of the Lord" — which is more than a bare petition for recovery and gives the sacramental reading a genuine foothold. (3) Calvin's cessationism overreaches. The claim that the healing gift "immediately ceased," so that any continuing anointing is a "stage-play," goes well beyond what James asserts; it rests on a separate, contestable thesis about the cessation of charisms, and it sits awkwardly against the unbroken Eastern euchelaion, which never claimed to work only by first-century miracle. The healing horizon of James can be affirmed without the cessationist rider — and is stronger for dropping it.

Assessment

Plausible. Rate the interpretation, not the polemic. That James 5:14 concerns a prayer, with anointing, for the recovery of the sick — and so is not, in its plain sense, a rite for the dying — is coherent, grammatically well-founded, matched by the earliest practice, and conceded even by a Catholic cardinal. On that core point it is strong. It falls short of the whole reading, however, because the text itself joins the forgiveness of sins to the healing and prescribes a churchly rite, not a private prayer, and because Calvin's cessationist form (the gift has ceased, so the rite is void) claims more than James gives. Coherent and not ruled out, but overstated in its strong form (plausible). The rival — that James promulgates a grace-conferring sacrament — is weighed on its own page; that the rite narrowed from healing to the deathbed and back is the narrowing thesis.
