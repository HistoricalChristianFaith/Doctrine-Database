Was extreme unction a medieval narrowing of an apostolic healing rite? — the narrowing thesis

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: sound — the historical thesis weighed here is that the rite of anointing began as a healing anointing of the sick and was later contracted into extreme unction, a last rite reserved for the dying, before being deliberately re-broadened in the twentieth century. The healing → dying → healing arc is documented from the primary sources themselves — oil blessed "for health" and lay anointing "in their own… necessity" in the early centuries; "given only to the sick of whose death there is fear" at Florence; and, at Vatican II, Rome's own statement that the sacrament "is not… for those only who are at the point of death" and is "more fittingly" called "anointing of the sick." A well-grounded, near-consensus historical judgment that the Catholic Church itself has ratified (sound).

The claim

The rite that James 5:14 describes — the elders called to anoint and pray so that "the Lord shall raise up" the sick person — was for the first millennium a rite of healing, given to the sick for their recovery. The medieval West gradually reserved it to those in danger of death and renamed it extreme unction ("the last anointing"), so that its primary end became preparation for glory rather than cure. The twentieth-century reform reversed the contraction, restoring both the wider scope and the older name. The narrowing is thus a real historical development, not the unchanging practice of the church.

Who advances it

The thesis is the common judgment of liturgical history, but it is not merely a modern or Protestant claim: the Catholic Church stated it in its own voice at the Second Vatican Council, which declared that the sacrament "may also and more fittingly be called 'anointing of the sick,'" and "is not a sacrament for those only who are at the point of death."1 The Reformers had pressed the same historical point polemically (see the healing reading); Vatican II conceded its factual core.

The evidence for

The early rite is for health, and even lay-administered. The oldest witnesses bless oil "for health" to be tasted as well as applied (Apostolic Tradition; Serapion, "health and soundness of soul, body, spirit"), and the earliest authoritative gloss, Innocent I (416), permits the anointing "not only to priests but also to all Christians… in their own necessity or in that of their [people]" — a breadth of administration impossible to square with a rite reserved for the dying.2 Caesarius promises the sick "not only bodily health but also the forgiveness of his sins," and Bede (c. 710) still describes the sick "anointed with consecrated oil by the presbyters, and… healed with accompanying prayer," repeating Innocent's permission of lay anointing.3 The Carolingian reform is where the turn is legislated. The five centuries between Bede and the scholastics are not blank: in the ninth-century Frankish reform, Theodulf of Orléans's parish ordo binds unction to penance and viaticum in a single deathbed sequence — "that no one die without the viaticum," with "first let penance be given to the sick person" — and the Council of Chalon (813) makes anointing a standing duty assigned to presbyteri, where Innocent and Bede had still allowed lay anointing. The rite is still called a healing "medicine… of soul and body," but its setting is now penitential and priest-only — the mechanism of the narrowing, which Frederick Paxton traces through the reform books reserving anointing to priests and turning a healing rite into "a sacrament for the dying."4 The medieval turn to the deathbed. By the high middle ages the accent has moved: Bonaventure defines it as "the Sacrament of those departing from this life," with bodily recovery only "if it be expedient," and the very name extreme unction encodes the change. The Council of Florence (1439) makes it law — "this sacrament should be given only to the sick of whose death there is fear" — and Trent (1551) dogmatizes the deathbed emphasis ("especially to those who lie in such danger as to seem to be about to depart this life").5 The East never narrowed. The Greek euchelaion remained a rite for the sick (and even the well), as Theophylact attests — a control case showing the contraction was a specifically Latin development, not intrinsic to the rite.6 Rome's own reversal. Vatican II's re-broadening, and its judgment that "anointing of the sick" is the "more fitting" name, is the church correcting the narrowing from within — the strongest possible confirmation that a narrowing had occurred.7

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The documentary arc is not in serious dispute. The horizon of the rite in the sources demonstrably moves from healing (with lay administration) to a priest-only deathbed sacrament and back again; each stage is attested by primary texts, and the endpoints (Florence's "only… in danger of death"; Vatican II's "not for those only who are at the point of death") are almost verbatim opposites. That the Catholic Church itself made and then undid the contraction removes the main way the thesis could be resisted.

What would overstate it. Two cautions keep the thesis honest. (1) The narrowing was a shift of emphasis, not a total substitution: the forgiveness-of-sins and preparation-for-death dimensions were present from the start (Origen files James 5 under penance; Serapion's prayer already asks "remission of sins"; Innocent calls it "a kind of sacrament"), so the early rite was never purely somatic, and the medieval rite never wholly abandoned healing ("if it be expedient"). The change is real but is a change of center of gravity, not the invention of an unrelated rite. (2) The thesis is a claim about the rite's scope and emphasis, and does not by itself settle the separate question whether James 5:14 institutes a sacrament at all — that is weighed on the sacramental and healing pages. Held to its actual claim — a documented drift from healing to dying, later reversed — the thesis is secure.

Assessment

Sound. The narrowing thesis is a well-grounded, near-consensus historical judgment, confirmed by the sources at every stage and ratified by the Catholic Church's own reversal at Vatican II. Kept to its proper claim — that the rite's dominant purpose contracted from the healing of the sick to the preparation of the dying, and was then deliberately re-broadened — it holds up (sound). It overreaches only if pressed into "the early church knew nothing of forgiveness or of death in this rite" (false — both were present from the outset) or made to decide, by itself, whether the anointing is a sacrament (a distinct question). Neither overreach is needed for the thesis, and neither is asserted here.
