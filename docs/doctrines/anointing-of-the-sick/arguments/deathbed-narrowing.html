How is the medieval reservation of the anointing of James 5:14 to the dying to be read?

Disputed reading · on Anointing of the Sick · witnesses: Bede, the Council of Florence, the Second Vatican Council

Related readings: Was the lay anointing permitted by Innocent I (416) the same rite as the presbyters' anointing of James 5:14? · Do the early witnesses attach the forgiveness of James 5:15 to the anointing or to penance?

The passage

And let them pray over him, anointing him, etc. We read that the apostles did this in the Gospel, and now the custom of the Church holds that the sick are anointed with consecrated oil by the presbyters, and are healed with accompanying prayer.

— Bede, Commentary on the Catholic Epistles, on James 5:14 (c. 710).1

The fifth sacrament is extreme unction, whose matter is the olive oil blessed by the bishop. This sacrament should be given only to the sick of whose death there is fear… The minister of this sacrament is the priest. Now the effect is the healing of the mind and, moreover, in so far as it is expedient, of the body itself also.

— Council of Florence, Decree for the Armenians (1439).2

"Extreme unction," which may also and more fittingly be called "anointing of the sick," is not a sacrament for those only who are at the point of death. Hence, as soon as any one of the faithful begins to be in danger of death from sickness or old age, the fitting time for him to receive this sacrament has certainly already arrived.

— Second Vatican Council, Sacrosanctum Concilium §73 (1963).3

The question

The early prayers over the oil ask for health, and Bede describes the sick anointed and "healed with accompanying prayer"; by the Council of Florence (1439) the Latin anointing was to be given "only to the sick of whose death there is fear", and the Second Vatican Council (1963) gave it the name "anointing of the sick" and set its time at the onset of danger from sickness or old age. P. Toner (Catholic, 1909) dates the name "Extreme Unction" as technical in the West to "towards the end of the twelfth century".4 The dispute is whether the medieval reservation to the dying departed from the rite James prescribed, was a change of practice within one continuous sacrament, or was a Western development that the Eastern rite did not share.

A rite for healing the sick, made into a rite for the dying

On this reading James prescribed anointing and prayer so that the sick might recover, and the medieval West turned that rite into a preparation for death.

Held by: Martin Luther (Lutheran, The Babylonian Captivity of the Church, 1520); John Calvin (Reformed, Institutes, 1559); F. W. Puller (Anglican, The Anointing of the Sick in Scripture and Tradition, 1904); Gary S. Shogren (evangelical New Testament scholar, Evangelical Quarterly, 1989).5

The case starts from James's promise, "The prayer of faith shall save the sick, and the Lord shall raise him up." Luther reads it as the purpose of the rite: the Apostle "commands the use of anointing and prayer for the very purpose that the sick man may be healed and raised up, that is, may not die, and that the unction may not be extreme." He points to "the prayers which are used even at this day during the ceremony of anointing, and in which we ask that the sick man may be restored," and describes the change as making "out of a daily or free anointing… an extreme unction."6 Calvin sets the same contrast: "James would have all the sick to be anointed," where the rite of his day was given in extremis.7 Puller traced the change through the sources. In the first seven centuries he could "find no trace… of sick people being anointed for the remission of their sins, or for the removal of the reliquiae of sin, or to impart to them grace enabling them to die happily or courageously"; but "as soon as we come to the ninth century, the custom changes," unction becoming "a regular accompaniment of the reception of the Eucharistic viaticum," so that "in the ninth and following centuries Unction is chiefly regarded as a preparation for death." He reads Theodulf's capitulary as clergy "learning for the first time how to administer the 'Extreme Unction' of the later Middle Ages."8 Shogren, writing as an exegete, reads "save" and "raise up" in James as bodily recovery and notes that "the illness is not necessarily life-threatening"; for him the passage expresses "James' expectation of healing."9 On this reading the oil blessed "for health" in the Apostolic Tradition and by Serapion, and Innocent's and Bede's anointing of "the sick faithful," show James's rite as it was first kept: a prayer of the church for the recovery of its sick.

One sacrament for the seriously ill, whose practice came to centre on the dying

On this reading the anointing has always been one sacrament for the gravely ill, with spiritual and bodily effects, and its medieval concentration on the dying was a change of practice within it.

Held by: the Council of Trent (Catholic, Session 14, 1551); P. Toner (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1909); Paul F. Palmer (Catholic, Theological Studies, 1958); Paul VI (Catholic, Sacram unctionem infirmorum, 1972).10

Trent declares that the unction "is to be applied to the sick, but to those especially who lie in such danger as to seem to be about to depart this life." Toner reads this as meaning that the sacrament "is not for the dying alone, but for all the faithful who are seriously ill with any sickness as involves danger of death," and finds grave illness already in James, whose sick man summons the priests: "presumably the sick person cannot go to them." On the history, Toner holds that "down to the twelfth century in the Western Church the practice was to give the unction freely to all (except public penitents) who were suffering from any serious illness," and that in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries "a change of practice took place, and the sacrament came to be regarded by many as intended only for the dying." He assigns three causes: clerical fees, popular superstitions about the anointed, and the Scotist teaching (with Bonaventure) that the sacrament's chief effect was the final remission of venial sins. "Church authority earnestly tried to correct" the first two, he writes, and the Scotist teaching "was never generally admitted in the schools."11 Palmer, who reports reaching Antoine Chavasse's conclusion independently, finds "no certain evidence" before the Carolingian reform that unction was regarded as a preparation for death, and writes that "the practice of anointing dying Christians is a legitimate development of the earlier practice of anointing the sick," and a capitulary ascribed to Charlemagne gives "the first clear suggestion that unction along with viaticum is to be regarded as a sacrament of the dying."12 Paul VI presents one tradition: "From ancient times testimonies of the Anointing of the Sick are found in the Church's Tradition, particularly her liturgical Tradition, both in the East and in the West," from Innocent I's letter through Florence, Trent and Vatican II.13 On this reading the twelfth-century schools held two views within the one sacrament, one treating it as "a sacrament of the sick," the other as "a Sacrament of the dying," and the reform of 1963–1972 restored the earlier time of reception.14

The mystery of healing, kept in the East and not an "extreme unction"

On this reading the anointing is the Church's mystery of healing for the sick, which the Orthodox Church has kept as such, and its restriction to the dying is a Latin development.

Held by: Thomas Hopko (Orthodox, The Orthodox Faith, vol. II, 1972); Kallistos Ware (Orthodox, The Orthodox Church, ⚠ unverified); Paul Meyendorff (Orthodox, The Anointing of the Sick, 2009, ⚠ unverified).15

Hopko calls the sacrament "the Church's specific prayer for healing." For the dying the Church has other prayers: "In the case where a person is obviously in the final moments of his earthly life, the Church has special prayers for the 'separation of soul and body.' Thus, it is clear that the sacrament of holy unction is for the sick-both the physically and mentally sick-and is not reserved for the moment of death." He concludes: "Holy unction is the sacrament of the spiritual, physical, and mental healing of a sick person whatever the nature or the gravity of the illness may be."16 Ware, as quoted, writes that the sacrament "has never been regarded by the Orthodox Church as 'Extreme Unction', intended only for the dying, but it is available for all who suffer from any physical or mental illness," and describes the Holy Week celebration at which "everyone present is invited to approach for anointing, whether physically ill or not."17 The rite's own prayers carry the same horizon: the oil is sanctified "unto the healing of those who shall be anointed therewith," and the sick are anointed with the prayer to the "Physician of souls and bodies," in the received service.18 Meyendorff, as his reviewer Job Getcha reports, finds five prayers for the sick and the oil in the earliest Byzantine euchologion, Barberini gr. 336, and traces the rite from there through the seven-priest services of Paris Coislin 213 (11th c.) and Sinai gr. 973 (12th c.), and is "critical of the rite's transformation into an 'extreme unction' during the time of Metropolitan Peter Moghila (17th c.) under Latin scholastic influence."19 On this reading the euchelaion, served by several priests with the gathered faithful, keeps the double petition of James and of the earliest prayers over the oil: healing of body and soul for the sick, whatever their illness.
