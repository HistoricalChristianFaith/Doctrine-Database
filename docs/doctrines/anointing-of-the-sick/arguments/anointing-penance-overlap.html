Do the early witnesses attach the forgiveness of James 5:15 to the anointing or to penance?

Disputed reading · on Anointing of the Sick · witnesses: Origen, John Chrysostom, Bede

Related readings: How is the medieval reservation of the anointing of James 5:14 to the dying to be read? · Was the lay anointing permitted by Innocent I (416) the same rite as the presbyters' anointing of James 5:14? · see also the penance & confession timeline.

The passage

There is still a seventh also, though hard and laborious — the remission of sins through penance, when the sinner washes "his bed with tears" and his "tears" become to him "bread day and night," when he is not ashamed to make known his sin to the priest of the Lord and to seek the remedy… In which is fulfilled also that which the Apostle James says: "but if anyone is sick, let him call the elders of the church, and let them lay hands on him, anointing him with oil in the name of the Lord. And the prayer of faith will save the sick man, and if he be in sins, they will be remitted to him."

— Origen, Homilies on Leviticus 2.4 (c. 245).1

If therefore the sick are in sins, and they have confessed these to the elders of the Church, and have endeavored with a perfect heart to abandon and correct them, they will be forgiven. For sins cannot be forgiven without the confession of amendment.

— Bede, Commentary on the Catholic Epistles, on James 5:15 (c. 710).2

The question

James attaches to the elders' prayer and anointing both the raising up of the sick and the promise "if he have committed sins, they shall be forgiven him." Origen (c. 245) cites the passage under the remission of sins through penance, Chrysostom (c. 390) cites it for the priests' authority to forgive sins after baptism, and Bede (c. 710) makes the forgiveness turn on confession to the elders. In the ordo printed under the name of Theodulf penance precedes the anointing, and Aquinas (Summa, Supplement, q. 30, a. 1) assigns each rite its own principal effect, penance "a spiritual resurrection" and extreme unction "a spiritual healing or cure".3 The dispute is whether the early witnesses ascribe the promised forgiveness to the priests' reconciliation of penitents, to the anointing as a rite that itself remits sins, or to one mystery of healing that accompanies confession. For the first reading, modern academic literature later than Puller (1904) was not located in openly accessible sources.

The forgiveness belongs to penance and absolution; the anointing to bodily healing

On this reading the early witnesses refer James's promise of forgiveness to penance and the priests' absolution, and the anointing to the healing of the body.

Held by: F. W. Puller (Anglican, The Anointing of the Sick in Scripture and Tradition, 1904).4

Puller reads James's exhortation in two parts, the unction and prayer for the sick and then the forgiveness of those "in the state of sin," and finds the early writers reading it the same way. Origen quotes the whole passage because the bearing of its last clause "would not be perceived" otherwise, but "he evidently interprets the passage as I do, and attributes the promised remission of sins to the Absolution of the priests"; numbering seven ways of remission, "he finds no place among the seven for the Unction of the sick." Chrysostom, proving that priests forgive post-baptismal sins, "understands the remission of sins, spoken of by S. James, to be imparted by the priests in the sacrament of Penance," since "in hundreds of passages Baptism and Penance are coupled together as the great channels of forgiveness." Bede, for whom the oil serves "the imparting of physical health," likewise "attributes the remission of sins, about which S. James writes, to the sacrament of Penance and not to the Unction," and directs that "the uncleanness of a more grievous leprosy" be disclosed to the priest.5 Innocent's permission for lay people to anoint points the same way for Puller: "It is quite inconceivable that Innocent can have meant that laymen and lay-women had the power of imparting the remission of deadly sins to themselves by anointing themselves with consecrated oil."6 He dates the view that the unction itself remits sins to the ninth century: Chalon (813) calls it a medicine for the ailments of soul and body, and the Council of Pavia (850) calls it a mystery "by means of which, if it is earnestly asked for in faith, both sins are remitted, and as a consequence bodily health is restored."7 On this reading the early church kept two remedies side by side, the keys for the sinner and the blessed oil with prayer for the sick, and the first eight centuries read James 5:15 within that pairing.

The anointing is a complement to penance that itself remits sins

On this reading the early witnesses treat the anointing as a rite that remits sins or completes their remission, given with penance and alongside it.

Held by: P. Toner (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1909); Paul F. Palmer (Catholic, Theological Studies, 1958; on the remission of sins by the anointing only).8

Toner reads the forgiveness as an effect of the unction in James itself: "All three clauses are connected in the very same way with the unction, 'and the prayer of faith. . .and the Lord. . .and if he be in sins. . .', so that the remission of sins is just as clearly stated to be an effect of the unction as the saving and raising up." Origen, on this reading, "evidently means to speak of the whole rite, unction and all, and to assert that it is performed as a means of remitting sin for the sick"; since canonical penance was granted only once, it is "much more reasonable" to suppose "that Origen looked upon the rite of unction as a supplement to penance, intended for the sick or dying who either had never undergone canonical penance, or after penance might have contracted new sins." John Cassian, with Origen's list before him, gives "the Jacobean rite" "an independent place." For Chrysostom the unction, "so much more akin to baptism in the simplicity of its ritual character," "supplied a much apter illustration of the priestly power of remitting post-baptismal sins than the judicial process of penance."9 Bede's stress on confession is explained by the practice of his time: "the unction was regarded and administered as a complement of the Sacrament of Penance, and… no formal question had yet been raised about their respective independent effects," and Bede "does not deny that the unction also may be efficacious in remitting them, or at least in completing their remission, or in remitting the lighter daily sins which need not be confessed." A century later Amalarius of Metz, with Bede's passage before him, writes that Christ's grace, "which is signified by the unction of oil, has destroyed his arms, which are daily sins."10 Paul F. Palmer reads Serapion's prayer over the oil as listing all the sacrament's effects: "the rite of anointing is a means of grace and the remission of sins." Of Origen he writes: "We are willing to grant the possibility that Origen has a dying penitent in mind and that even at this early date the practice of reconciling and anointing dying penitents was known."11 On this reading the anointing was, in Toner's words, "independently efficacious to remit sins or complete their remission," a complement to absolution for the sick whose penance had been cut short by illness.9

Healing and forgiveness together in one mystery that accompanies confession

On this reading the anointing gives healing of body and soul together, forgiveness included, and goes with confession without replacing it.

Held by: Thomas Hopko (Orthodox, The Orthodox Faith, vol. II, 1972); Fr Anthony Alevizopoulos (Greek Orthodox priest, "The Mysterion of Holy Unction," text as reprinted 2013); Kallistos Ware (Orthodox, The Orthodox Church, ⚠ unverified).12

Hopko reads James 5:14–16 as one text, from the elders' anointing to "Therefore, confess your sins to one another and pray for one another, that you may be healed," and states: "The express purpose of the sacrament of holy unction is healing and forgiveness."13 Alevizopoulos reads the passage's promise as bodily and spiritual at once: "It will not only be for physical healing, but spiritual healing as well." The forgiveness it names "does not mean the replacement of the Mystery (Sacrament) of Holy Confession, since Holy Unction goes together with Confession." He counts Chrysostom among the witnesses to the mystery: "At another point he adds that priests have authority to forgive sins, not only when they regenerate us but also later. This Holy Father of the Church supports this authority with St. James 5:14." Ware, as quoted, explains the pairing: the sacrament "has a double purpose: not only bodily healing but the forgiveness of sins. The two things go together, for the human being is a unity of body and soul and there can therefore be no sharp and rigid distinction between the bodily and spiritual ills."14 On this reading the early witnesses who join forgiveness, confession and the elders' anointing describe the practice the Orthodox Church keeps, in which unction "is usually preceded by Confession and is usually concluded with the Mystery of Communion," and the priest prays at the anointing: "O Holy Father, Physician of our souls and bodies, have mercy, forgive and save Thy servant."15
