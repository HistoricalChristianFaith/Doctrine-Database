Was the forgiveness of James 5:14–15 originally a penitential act, later split from the anointing? — the anointing–penance overlap

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: sound — the historical thesis weighed here is that James's forgiveness clause (“if he have committed sins, they shall be forgiven him,” James 5:15) gave the anointing a genuine sin-remitting dimension, so that in the early church the anointing of the sick and the forgiveness of sins were bound tightly together, and only later were prised apart into two discrete sacraments — penance for sin, extreme unction for the dying. The overlap is documented from the sources themselves (Origen files James 5 under penance; Chrysostom reads it as priests forgiving sins after baptism; Bede makes the forgiveness “turn on confession to the elders”; the Carolingian rite welds unction to penance and viaticum), so the historical claim holds up (sound). Two things keep it from proving more than it does: an institutional distinction between the anointing and the order of penitents already exists by 416 (Innocent I bars penitents from the anointing), and the history is dogmatically neutral — it does not by itself settle whether either rite is a sacrament.

The claim

James 5:14–15 does not describe a rite for sin and a separate rite for sickness. It names one action — the elders called to pray over and anoint the sick — and attaches to it two promises at once: “the Lord shall raise him up; and if he have committed sins, they shall be forgiven him.” The thesis is that the earliest church took the forgiveness clause seriously as forgiveness, reading the anointing as a channel of the remission of sins and folding a dimension of confession-and-pardon into it, so that anointing and penance were never cleanly separable in the first millennium. The clean modern picture — two distinct sacraments, one for sin and one for the sick or dying — is a later development of the medieval West, not a datum of the text.

Who advances it

The point is common ground in the history of the liturgy rather than a partisan claim, and it cuts both ways confessionally. Protestant polemicists press it to argue that James describes no distinct sacrament of “extreme unction” at all (see the healing reading); Catholic and Orthodox liturgical historians grant the same intertwining and read it as an originally-undifferentiated matrix from which two sacraments were later, legitimately, distinguished (development of doctrine). The history is not in dispute between them; its dogmatic weight is.

The evidence for

The forgiveness clause was read as forgiveness — penitentially — from the third century. The earliest surviving reading of James 5 makes it a text about the remission of sins: Origen (c. 245) lists seven ways sins are remitted in the gospel and files James 5:14 under the seventh — “the remission of sins through penance… when [the sinner] is not ashamed to make known his sin to the priest of the Lord and to seek the remedy” — expressly binding the elders' anointing to confession and the seeking of a remedy.1 A century and a half later Chrysostom (c. 390) cites the same verses to prove that priests “not only at the time of regeneration, but afterwards also… have authority to forgive sins” — the anointing read as an exercise of the priestly power over sin, held together with the bodily recovery.2 The prayer-books say the same: Serapion's oil-prayer asks for “remission of sins” alongside bodily health, and Caesarius promises the anointed sick “not only bodily health but also the forgiveness of his sins.”3 Bede makes the forgiveness turn explicitly on confession. On v. 15, Bede (c. 710) ties the promised pardon to a penitential condition: “if… the sick… have confessed these [sins] to the elders of the Church, and have endeavored with a perfect heart to abandon and correct them, they will be forgiven. For sins cannot be forgiven without the confession of amendment.” The anointing here presupposes confession and amendment — the very substance of penance.4 The Carolingian rite welds the two into one deathbed sequence. The mechanism of separation-by-fusion is visible in the ninth century: Theodulf of Orléans's parish ordo binds unction, penance, and the viaticum together as one deathbed duty and orders that “first let penance be given to the sick person,” the anointing set inside a penitential rite.5 The clean two-sacrament division is a high-medieval settlement. Only with the twelfth-century closing of the list at seven sacraments, and the scholastic treatises of Bonaventure and Aquinas, does extreme unction become a sacrament formally distinct from penance, its proper effect the remission of the “remnants of sin” that penance leaves — a division the earlier witnesses never draw so sharply.6

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The forgiveness dimension of the anointing is not read into James by later apologists; it is in the verse (“they shall be forgiven him”) and was taken as forgiveness by the earliest readers we have. That the same fathers who expound the anointing also expound it as a remission of sins — Origen under the heading “penance,” Chrysostom as the priest's power over sin, Bede as forgiveness conditioned on confession — shows that the sin-forgiving and the sick-healing aspects were not held in separate compartments. And the Carolingian welding of unction to penance and viaticum, followed by the scholastic partition into two discrete sacraments, is a documented sequence. The strong version of the modern textbook picture — that Scripture gives one sacrament for sin and another, unrelated, for the dying — is anachronistic when pushed back onto James or the early church.

What would overstate it. Two cautions. (1) “Not cleanly separate” is not “wholly undifferentiated.” A distinct penitential discipline — the public, graded order of penitents — already existed alongside the anointing, and the two were institutionally distinguished by the early fifth century: Innocent I (416) rules that the anointing “cannot be administered to penitents, because it is a kind of sacrament,” those enrolled in the order of penitents being barred from the sacraments generally.7 Forgiveness was thus a grace that both the anointing and the penitential discipline conveyed — overlapping in effect — not a single rite that was only later cut in two. What the schoolmen did was formalize and demarcate, not invent from nothing. (2) The history is dogmatically neutral. That the two rites grew from an intertwined matrix does not by itself decide whether James institutes a permanent sacrament (weighed on the sacramental and healing pages) or how far priestly absolution effects forgiveness (weighed on penance's power of the keys). A Catholic can read the same data as a legitimate development of two sacraments latent in one apostolic practice; a Protestant can read it as evidence that neither “extreme unction” nor a juridical sacrament of penance is a discrete New-Testament institution. The overlap is a fact about the history of the rites, not a lever that tips the sacramental question either way.

Assessment

Sound. Kept to its actual claim — that James 5:15's forgiveness clause was read penitentially from the earliest evidence, that the anointing carried a genuine sin-remitting dimension bound up with confession, and that the clean division into two distinct sacraments is a later, chiefly Latin and scholastic development — the thesis is well-grounded and close to a consensus of liturgical history (sound). It overreaches only if pressed into “there was one undifferentiated rite until the schoolmen split it” (the order of penitents was already distinguished from the anointing by 416) or made to settle, by itself, whether either rite is a sacrament (a separate question, weighed elsewhere). Neither overreach is needed for the thesis, and neither is asserted here.
