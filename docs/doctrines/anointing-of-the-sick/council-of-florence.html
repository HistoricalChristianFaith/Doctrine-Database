The Council of Florence on extreme unction

Dates: 1439 · Wikipedia
Summary: The Decree for the Armenians gives the sacrament its first ecumenical definition and its sharpest narrowing: extreme unction's matter is olive oil blessed by a bishop, its minister is the priest, and it "should be given only to the sick of whose death there is fear" — the healing rite of James now formally reserved to the dying.

Claims & evidence

Given only to the sick whose death is feared

Listing extreme unction as the fifth of seven sacraments, the decree fixes its matter, minister, effect, and — decisively — the restriction to those in danger of death:

The fifth sacrament is extreme unction, whose matter is the olive oil blessed by the bishop. This sacrament should be given only to the sick of whose death there is fear… The minister of this sacrament is the priest. Now the effect is the healing of the mind and, moreover, in so far as it is expedient, of the body itself also.

— Council of Florence, Decree for the Armenians (Exsultate Deo), 1439 (Denzinger 700), trans. Roy J. Deferrari, The Sources of Catholic Dogma, source.1 The decree also prescribes anointing the several senses "on the eyes because of sight, on the ears because of hearing…" with the form "Through this holy anointing and his most kind mercy may the Lord forgive you whatever [you have sinned]," making forgiveness the leading effect.

Notes

  • The narrowing made law. Where Innocent I, Caesarius, and Bede gave the anointing to "the sick faithful" for recovery — even by laity — Florence restricts it to the priest and to those in danger of death, and orders bodily healing behind healing of the soul. It is a hinge document of both narrowings — the recipient-narrowing ("only… the sick of whose death there is fear"), later reversed at Vatican II, and the minister-narrowing ("The minister of this sacrament is the priest"), which was not.
