The Council of Trent on extreme unction

Dates: 1551 · Wikipedia
Summary: Session 14 (1551), appended to its decree on penance, defines extreme unction as a true and proper sacrament instituted by Christ (hinted in Mark, promulgated by James), whose ministers are ordained priests (the "elders" of James), to be administered to the sick "but especially to those who lie in such danger as to seem to be about to depart this life" — and anathematizes the denial that it is a sacrament.

Claims & evidence

A true sacrament, instituted by Christ and promulgated by James

Now, this sacred unction of the sick was instituted by Christ our Lord, as truly and properly a sacrament of the new law, insinuated indeed in Mark, but recommended and promulgated to the faithful by James the Apostle, and brother of the Lord.

— Council of Trent, Session 14 (1551), Doctrine on the Sacrament of Extreme Unction, ch. 1, trans. J. Waterworth, source.1

The "elders" are priests — and the rite is for those near death

Trent reads James's "presbyters" as ordained priests, not the elderly or the leading laity — the dogmatic endpoint of the minister-narrowing thesis — and locates the proper time of the sacrament at the danger of death:

the proper ministers of this sacrament are the Presbyters of the Church; by which name are to be understood, in that place, not the elders by age, or the foremost in dignity amongst the people, but, either bishops, or priests by bishops rightly ordained… And it is also declared, that this unction is to be applied to the sick, but to those especially who lie in such danger as to seem to be about to depart this life.

— Council of Trent, Session 14, chs. 1 and 3 (Waterworth), source.2

Anathema on denying the sacrament

If any one saith, that Extreme Unction is not truly and properly a sacrament, instituted by Christ our Lord, and promulgated by the blessed apostle James; but is only a rite received from the Fathers, or a human figment; let him be anathema.

— Council of Trent, Session 14, Canon 1 (Waterworth), source.3

Notes

  • The Reformers' denial answered. Trent's canon aims at the Protestant reading — that James 5 is a prayer for bodily healing, not a sacrament for the dying (see the healing reading) — and at Luther's charge that extreme unction was a human invention. Trent affirms the sacramental reading in its strongest form.
  • "Insinuated indeed in Mark" is the weak link. Trent's careful verb concedes as much: the institution is insinuated (adumbrated) in Mark, but promulgated by James — the real weight rests on the epistle. Mark 6:13, where the Twelve on a pre-Passion mission "anointed with oil many that were sick, and healed them," is a charismatic healing mission, not obviously the institution of a New-Covenant sacrament; whether it can ground "instituted by Christ" is weighed on the dominical-institution argument (assessed weak).
  • The narrowing dogmatized. The "especially those about to depart this life" of ch. 3 confirms in dogma the deathbed emphasis that Florence had fixed — the endpoint of the narrowing thesis, later relaxed at Vatican II. Note that Trent says "especially," stopping short of Florence's flat "only" — leaving room the twentieth century would reopen.
  • Trent's parallel decree on penance in the same session is treated on the penance timeline.
