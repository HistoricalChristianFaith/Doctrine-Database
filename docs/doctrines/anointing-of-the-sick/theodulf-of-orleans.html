Theodulf of Orléans on the anointing of the sick

Dates: c. 810 (bishop of Orléans c. 798–821) · Wikipedia
Summary: Theodulf's second diocesan statute gives the earliest Frankish parish ordo for the unction of the sick — and welds it to penance and the viaticum, ordering that penance be given first and that "no one die without the viaticum." Here the healing anointing of James 5 is being folded, at the parish level, into a sequenced deathbed rite: the Carolingian mechanism of the Latin narrowing caught in the act.

Claims & evidence

Unction yoked to penance and the viaticum — "that no one die without the viaticum"

Instructing his parish priests, Theodulf treats unction, penance, and the viaticum as one pastoral duty of the deathbed, its governing concern that the dying not depart unprepared:

Admonendi etiam sunt sacerdotes de unctione infirmorum et poenitentia et viatico, ne aliquis sine viatico moriatur.

— Theodulf of Orléans, Second Capitulary (Capitulare ad eosdem), source.1 "Priests are also to be admonished concerning the unction of the sick, and penance, and the viaticum, that no one die without the viaticum." The three rites are named together as a single obligation — the pairing that would harden, in the West, into the deathbed complex of extreme unction.

Penance first, then anointing

Where the earliest witnesses set the anointing beside prayer for recovery, Theodulf's ordo orders the sequence penitentially — confession and penance precede the oil:

Primitus autem infirmo poenitentia detur. Deinde, si permiserit infirmitas, abluto corpore, albis vestibus induatur, et in ecclesiam deportetur…

— Theodulf of Orléans, Second Capitulary, source.1 "First let penance be given to the sick person. Then, if the illness allows, with the body washed let him be clothed in white garments and carried into the church…" — the anointing set within a penitential rite, not a simple prayer for the sick person's cure.

The anointing formula still echoes James 5:15

The words spoken over the sick person keep the language of James — the prayer of faith, the raising up, the forgiveness of sins — even as the rite around them narrows:

Ungo te in nomine Patris, et Filii, et Spiritus sancti, ut oratio fidei salvet te, et alleviet te Dominus, et si in peccatis sis, remittantur tibi.

— Theodulf of Orléans, Second Capitulary, source.1 "I anoint you in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit, that the prayer of faith may save you, and the Lord raise you up, and if you are in sins, they may be forgiven you" (cf. James 5:15) — the scriptural promise preserved verbatim inside a rite whose setting has become the deathbed.

Notes

  • The mechanism of the narrowing. Theodulf's statute is not yet the flat "only… in danger of death" of Florence (1439) — the formula still prays that the Lord "raise you up" (recovery). But by welding unction to penance and viaticum in one deathbed ordo, with penance first, it shows the Latin rite acquiring the shape that would harden into extreme unction: the mechanism the narrowing thesis points to, caught between Bede (c. 710) and the scholastics.
  • The Carolingian reform reoriented the rite toward death. On the wider consolidation of anointing into a clericalized, priest-only preparation for death — bound to reconciliation and communion as viaticum — see Frederick S. Paxton, Christianizing Death (1990), who traces the ninth-century reform books turning a healing anointing into "a sacrament for the dying."2
  • The dating is approximate. Theodulf's two episcopal statutes fall within his episcopate (c. 798–818); the Second Capitulary, which carries the anointing ordo, is placed here at c. 810, and in any case belongs to the same reform decade as the Council of Chalon (813).
  • Theodulf appears on this wiki also as a witness on the image-veneration timeline, as the chief drafter of the Libri Carolini (the Opus Caroli regis contra synodum, c. 790–793).
