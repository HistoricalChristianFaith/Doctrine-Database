Theodulf of Orléans on the anointing of the sick

Dates: c. 810 (bishop of Orléans c. 798–818; d. 821) · Wikipedia
Summary: The second diocesan statute printed under Theodulf's name gives a Frankish parish ordo for the unction of the sick, names it together with penance and the viaticum, and orders that penance be given first and that "no one die without the viaticum." The date of the rite printed under his name is disputed (see Notes).

Claims & evidence

Unction, penance and the viaticum

The capitulary printed under Theodulf's name names unction, penance and viaticum together in an admonition to parish priests:

Admonendi etiam sunt sacerdotes de unctione infirmorum et poenitentia et viatico, ne aliquis sine viatico moriatur.

— Theodulf of Orléans, Second Capitulary (Capitulare ad eosdem), source.1 "Priests are also to be admonished concerning the unction of the sick, and penance, and the viaticum, that no one die without the viaticum."

Penance first, then anointing

The ordo printed under Theodulf's name sets penance before the oil:

Primitus autem infirmo poenitentia detur. Deinde, si permiserit infirmitas, abluto corpore, albis vestibus induatur, et in ecclesiam deportetur…

— Theodulf of Orléans, Second Capitulary, source.1 "First let penance be given to the sick person. Then, if the illness allows, with the body washed let him be clothed in white garments and carried into the church…"

The anointing formula and James 5:15

The words spoken over the sick person keep the language of James — the prayer of faith, the raising up, the forgiveness of sins:

Ungo te in nomine Patris, et Filii, et Spiritus sancti, ut oratio fidei salvet te, et alleviet te Dominus, et si in peccatis sis, remittantur tibi.

— Theodulf of Orléans, Second Capitulary, source.1 "I anoint you in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit, that the prayer of faith may save you, and the Lord raise you up, and if you are in sins, they may be forgiven you" (cf. James 5:15).

Notes

  • Reception and debate. F. W. Puller (Anglican, 1904) reads the capitulary as Theodulf's instruction to his clergy, describing a rite "of which the clergy of the diocese of Orleans had hitherto been ignorant," and holds that "Theodulf's view of the Unction of the sick was that it was primarily a rite to prepare the dying for death."2 How the ordo printed under Theodulf's name bears on the later reservation of the anointing to the dying is disputed; see How is the medieval reservation of the anointing of James 5:14 to the dying to be read?
  • Paxton's account. Frederick S. Paxton, Christianizing Death (1990; ⚠ unverified, not opened), traces the ninth-century reform books binding anointing to reconciliation and communion as viaticum and reserving it to priests. J. M. Donohue (Catholic, New Catholic Encyclopedia) reports Paxton's reading of the ninth-century anointing of the five senses as "a shift in emphasis away from physical healing and toward spiritual purification," and writes in his own voice that, with the rise of private penance, the anointing "had by now become a sacrament for the dying."3,4
  • The date of the rite. Theodulf's two episcopal statutes fall within his episcopate (c. 798–818); the Second Capitulary, which carries the anointing ordo, is placed here at c. 810, in the same reform decade as the Council of Chalon (813). J. M. Donohue (Catholic, New Catholic Encyclopedia) holds that the rite, "although attributed to Theodulf (d. 821), … is probably descriptive of late tenth or early eleventh-century procedures"; Puller dates it to Theodulf's episcopate.4
  • Theodulf also appears as a witness on the image-veneration timeline, as the chief drafter of the Libri Carolini (the Opus Caroli regis contra synodum, c. 790–793).
