Bede on the anointing of the sick

Dates: c. 710 · Wikipedia
Summary: In his Commentary on the Catholic Epistles, Bede is the fullest early Latin witness to James 5:14–15 as a living rite: the sick are anointed with consecrated oil by the presbyters and healed with prayer — and, following Pope Innocent I, he records that ordinary Christians too may anoint with the oil, provided a bishop has blessed it. Still a rite of healing, not yet of the deathbed.

Claims & evidence

The church's custom: presbyters anoint the sick with consecrated oil

Bede reads the apostolic practice straight into the church of his own day — and, citing Innocent, extends the anointing (though never the consecration of the oil) to the laity:

And let them pray over him, anointing him, etc. We read that the apostles did this in the Gospel, and now the custom of the Church holds that the sick are anointed with consecrated oil by the presbyters, and are healed with accompanying prayer. Not only the presbyters, but as Pope Innocent writes, also all Christians are permitted to use the same oil to anoint in their own or their relatives' necessity, though this oil may only be consecrated by bishops.

— Bede, Commentary on the Catholic Epistles, on James 5:14, trans. David Hurst (Cistercian Studies 82), source.1

Sickness, sin, and forgiveness after confession

On v. 15 ("if he be in sins, they shall be forgiven him"), Bede links bodily illness to sin — citing Paul on the Corinthians who communicated unworthily — and makes forgiveness turn on confession to the elders and real amendment:

If therefore the sick are in sins, and they have confessed these to the elders of the Church, and have endeavored with a perfect heart to abandon and correct them, they will be forgiven. For sins cannot be forgiven without the confession of amendment.

— Bede, Commentary on the Catholic Epistles, on James 5:15, source.2

Notes

  • Healing, not the deathbed. Bede's rite is aimed at the sick person's recovery ("are healed with accompanying prayer"), and he even permits lay anointing "in their own or their relatives' necessity" — a breadth impossible to square with the later reservation of the rite to those in danger of death. He is a key witness for the narrowing thesis: the medieval extreme unction is demonstrably later than the healing-oil practice he describes. By transmitting Innocent's lay-anointing permission he is likewise a witness for the companion minister-narrowing thesis — the later reservation of the anointing to priests alone.
  • Transmitting Innocent I. Bede's "as Pope Innocent writes" is a direct debt to Innocent I's letter to Decentius (416), which he read as authorizing lay use of the episcopally-blessed oil.
