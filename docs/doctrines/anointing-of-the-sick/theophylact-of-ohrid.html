Theophylact of Ohrid on the anointing of the sick

Dates: c. 1107 · Wikipedia
Summary: The Byzantine archbishop's compact commentary on James roots the anointing directly in the Gospel practice of the apostles, who "anointed the sick with oil" (Mark 6:13) — a Greek witness that keeps the rite tied to healing and the apostolic precedent, never narrowing it to the dying.

Claims & evidence

The apostles already anointed the sick with oil

Theophylact links James 5:14 back to the Twelve's mission under Christ:

The apostles were already doing this even when the Lord still lived among men: they anointed the sick with oil (Mark 6:13).

— Theophylact of Ohrid, Commentary on James, ch. 5, on James 5:14, source.1

Notes

  • The Eastern trajectory. The Greek East never developed the reservation of the rite to those in danger of death; its euchelaion ("prayer-oil," the sacrament of holy unction) remained a rite for the sick — and, in Byzantine practice, even for the well — aimed at healing of body and soul. Theophylact, echoing Chrysostom, exemplifies that unbroken healing emphasis. This bears on the narrowing thesis: the deathbed contraction was a specifically Latin development.
  • The same Mark 6:13 anchor appears a century earlier in the Greek commentary of Oecumenius on James ("This is what the apostles did while the Lord was still among men, anointing the sick with oil and healing them") — the tradition Theophylact epitomizes.
