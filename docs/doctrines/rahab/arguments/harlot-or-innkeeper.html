Was Rahab a harlot, or an innkeeper?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: the innkeeper reading is weak as a replacement for “harlot” (the Hebrew, the Septuagint, and the New Testament are decisive); a plausible residue survives only in the weaker claim that she also kept a house open to travellers · advanced by the Targum, Josephus, and Rashi

The claim

A lady innkeeper. According to Targum Yonasson, an innkeeper who sold various kinds of foodstuff.

— Rashi on Joshua 2:1, Sefaria. The claim is that the Hebrew word describing Rahab (zonah) means, or should be read as, “innkeeper” rather than “prostitute” — that the tradition of Rahab the harlot rests on a mistranslation or a slander, and she was in fact simply the keeper of a lodging.

Who advances it

The reading is old and respectable within Judaism. Josephus (Antiquities 5.1.2, c. AD 93) already retells the episode with Rahab as the keeper of “a certain inn,” dropping the harlotry entirely. The Targum of the Prophets (Targum Jonathan) renders ishah zonah at Joshua 2:1 as pundeqita, a female innkeeper; and Rashi (c. 1075), the most influential of the medieval Jewish commentators, follows the Targum, glossing her as an innkeeper “who sold various kinds of foodstuff.” The move exploits a genuine feature of the root: the consonants z-n-h can be connected with provisioning (mazon, food) as well as with harlotry.

The evidence for

Three things give the reading traction. First, the noun zonah is not the only way Hebrew names a prostitute (the cult-prostitute is qedeshah), and an inn in the ancient Near East was often exactly the kind of establishment a woman on the margins would run — so “a woman who keeps a public house” is not a strained sense in itself. Second, the practical details fit an inn as well as a brothel: strangers can lodge there without remark, the house is built into the city wall with a window to the outside, and the king’s men expect to find travellers’ movements known to its keeper. Third, the interpretation is early and independent — Josephus and the Targum are not copying one another — which shows the “innkeeper” sense was a live option in Second-Temple and rabbinic Judaism, not a late apologetic invention.1

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The smaller, weaker form of the claim is plausible: Rahab may well have kept a house that lodged travellers, and the two roles need not exclude one another — a woman running such a house on the city wall could be both innkeeper and prostitute, and the pun on provisioning is real. Nothing in the text forbids her having an inn.

What is overstated or imprecise. As a replacement for “harlot” — the claim that she was not a prostitute at all — the reading runs against the decisive weight of the sources. The Hebrew zonah is the ordinary word for a prostitute and is nowhere else in the Hebrew Bible reduced to “innkeeper.” The Septuagint, the oldest interpretive witness, renders it pornē. And the two New Testament writers who name her both call her hē pornē, “the harlot” (Hebrews 11:31; James 2:25) — not a stray adjective but the very hinge on which their point about grace and faith turns. The pattern of the “innkeeper” sources is telling: they are precisely the ones with a motive to soften the scandal (Josephus writing Israel’s story up for a Roman readership; the rabbis honouring a proselyte matriarch). The Christian stream, running the opposite way, kept “harlot” deliberately — because a saved prostitute magnifies the grace that saved her.2

Assessment

Weak, as a replacement for “harlot.” The Hebrew zonah, the Septuagint’s pornē, and the New Testament’s twice-repeated “the harlot” settle the plain sense, and the “innkeeper” reading is a demonstrable later softening by sources with a motive to soften. What survives is only the modest, plausible residue that she may also have kept a lodging — the two roles are not exclusive — which takes nothing from the word Scripture chose and the whole tradition, Jewish and Christian alike, understood.
