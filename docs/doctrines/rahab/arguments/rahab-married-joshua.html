Did Rahab marry Joshua and become the ancestress of priests and prophets?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: weak as history — a honorific rabbinic midrash with no scriptural basis, and incompatible with the one canonical notice of Rahab’s marriage (to Salmon, not Joshua) · advanced by the Babylonian Talmud (Megillah 14b–15a)

The claim

Eight prophets, who were also priests, descended from Rahab the prostitute… Rabbi Yehuda said: So too, Huldah the prophetess was a descendant of Rahab the prostitute… For Rahab converted and married Joshua.

— Babylonian Talmud, Megillah 14b, Sefaria. The claim is that Rahab became a proselyte, married Joshua son of Nun, and through that marriage became the ancestress of a line of men who were both priests and prophets — Jeremiah, Hilkiah, Baruch, and the rest — and of Huldah the prophetess.

Who advances it

The tradition is rabbinic, fixed in the Babylonian Talmud (Megillah 14b–15a) and repeated through the midrashic and medieval literature. It belongs to the same stream of Jewish reception that softened Rahab’s harlotry into innkeeping and enrolled her among the four most beautiful women of the world: a sustained effort to draw the Canaanite outsider fully into Israel and to honour her rescued faith with a glorious posterity.

The evidence for

The tradition is not baseless as exegesis: it reads real gaps in the text. Scripture says Rahab “dwelleth in Israel even unto this day” (Joshua 6:25) but never says whom she married, leaving an opening a marriage-tradition could fill; and it was natural to pair the Gentile woman who confessed the LORD with the leader of the conquest she aided. The priest-prophet descent, likewise, develops the biblical hint that her faith was richly rewarded. As an honouring of Rahab it is coherent and old.

Adversarial assessment

What holds. As a piece of theological memory the tradition is intelligible and even fitting: it takes the Bible’s own verdict — the harlot saved by faith, dwelling in Israel — and carries it to a generous conclusion. It testifies to how completely Jewish tradition drew the outsider in.

What is overstated or imprecise. As history it fails on three counts. First, Scripture nowhere connects Rahab with Joshua, who is never said to take a wife at all. Second — and decisively — the one canonical notice of Rahab’s marriage makes her the wife of Salmon, of the tribe of Judah and the line of David and Christ (Matthew 1:5); a marriage to Joshua of the tribe of Ephraim is not a supplement to that but a rival to it, and the two cannot both be literally true. Third, the priest-prophet genealogy is a midrashic construction, not a recorded descent: it reasons backward from later priestly prophets (Jeremiah and his kin) to a distinguished ancestress, exactly the sort of honorific pedigree the rabbis liked to assign, and it is internally strained (the Talmud must reckon Huldah from both Joshua and Rahab because Joshua “did not have sons”). It is a tribute conferred on Rahab, not a datum about her life.1

Assessment

Weak, as history. The married-to-Joshua tradition has no scriptural warrant, is contradicted by the one canonical record of Rahab’s marriage (to Salmon of Judah), and rests on a genealogy built backward from later priest-prophets. It is a revealing and honourable piece of Jewish reception — the measure of how fully the tradition adopted the Canaanite convert — but a theological honour, not a fact about Rahab.
