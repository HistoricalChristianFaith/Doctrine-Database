Rahab in Jewish tradition — the innkeeper, the proselyte, and the wife of Joshua

Dates: traditions from c. AD 200 (Tannaitic/Amoraic), redacted in the Targum of the Prophets and the Babylonian Talmud by c. 500; carried on by Rashi (c. 1075) · Wikipedia
Summary: Where the Fathers kept the word “harlot” to magnify grace, the rabbis ran the other way — softening zonah into “innkeeper,” making Rahab a model proselyte, and marrying her to Joshua as the ancestress of a line of priests and prophets.

Claims & evidence

“Harlot” softened to “innkeeper” (zonah → pundeqita)

The Targum of the Prophets renders the Hebrew ishah zonah of Joshua 2:1 not “a prostitute” but pundeqita — a woman who keeps a pundak, an inn. Rashi takes it up directly:

A lady innkeeper. According to Targum Yonasson, an innkeeper who sold various kinds of foodstuff.

— Rashi on Joshua 2:1, Sefaria. The reading exploits the fact that the consonants of zonah (z-n-h) can be linked to provisioning (mazon, food) as well as to prostitution; Josephus had already assumed it two centuries earlier, calling her lodging an “inn.”1

The proselyte who married Joshua and bore priests and prophets

The Babylonian Talmud draws Rahab all the way into Israel. Where Scripture says only that she “dwelleth in Israel even unto this day” (Joshua 6:25), the Talmud supplies a marriage and a lineage:

Eight prophets, who were also priests, descended from Rahab the prostitute, and they are: Neriah; his son Baruch; Seraiah; Mahseiah; Jeremiah; his father, Hilkiah; Jeremiah’s cousin Hanamel; and Hanamel’s father, Shallum. Rabbi Yehuda said: So too, Huldah the prophetess was a descendant of Rahab the prostitute… For Rahab converted and married Joshua.

— Babylonian Talmud, Megillah 14b (William Davidson translation), Sefaria. The harlot of Jericho becomes the proselyte matriarch of a priestly-prophetic line, and (since Joshua had no sons) the ancestress from whom Huldah is reckoned on both sides.2

One of the four women of surpassing beauty

There were four women of extraordinary beauty in the world: Sarah, and Abigail, Rahab, and Esther… Rahab aroused impure thoughts by her name, i.e., the mere mention of her name would inspire lust for her.

— Babylonian Talmud, Megillah 15a (William Davidson translation), Sefaria. Even in praise the tradition keeps her allure in view — her name a byword — while enrolling her among the matriarchs and queens of Israel.3

Notes

  • The mirror image of the Christian reading. Both traditions honour Rahab, but by opposite moves. The Fathers keep “harlot” and make grace the point; the rabbis erase the harlotry (innkeeper) and make descent the point (proselyte, wife of Joshua, ancestress of prophets). Each rehabilitation says as much about the tradition doing it as about Rahab.
  • The married-Joshua tradition cannot be squared with Matthew. The one canonical notice of Rahab’s marriage makes her the wife of Salmon of Judah (Matthew 1:5), not Joshua of Ephraim — weighed on the did Rahab marry Joshua? page.
  • The innkeeper gloss feeds a real crux. Whether zonah here means “harlot” or “innkeeper” is weighed on the harlot-or-innkeeper page; the Hebrew, the Septuagint, and the New Testament all keep “harlot.”
