Rahab in Jewish tradition — the innkeeper, the proselyte, and the wife of Joshua

Dates: traditions from c. AD 200 (Tannaitic/Amoraic), redacted in the Targum of the Prophets and the Babylonian Talmud by c. 500; carried on by Rashi (c. 1075) · Wikipedia
Summary: The Targum and Rashi render zonah as “innkeeper,” and the Babylonian Talmud makes Rahab a proselyte who married Joshua and was the ancestress of a line of priests and prophets.

Claims & evidence

“Harlot” rendered “innkeeper” (zonah → pundeqita)

The Targum of the Prophets renders the Hebrew ishah zonah of Joshua 2:1 not “a prostitute” but pundeqita — a woman who keeps a pundak, an inn. Rashi takes it up directly:

A lady innkeeper. According to Targum Yonasson, an innkeeper who sold various kinds of foodstuff.

— Rashi on Joshua 2:1, Sefaria. Rashi’s Hebrew gloss describes her as selling mezonot, “foodstuffs,” a word that shares the letters z-n with zonah. Josephus, writing c. AD 93, had already called her lodging an “inn.”1

The proselyte who married Joshua and bore priests and prophets

Of Rahab, who “dwelleth in Israel even unto this day” (Joshua 6:25), the Babylonian Talmud records a marriage and a line of descendants:

Eight prophets, who were also priests, descended from Rahab the prostitute, and they are: Neriah; his son Baruch; Seraiah; Mahseiah; Jeremiah; his father, Hilkiah; Jeremiah’s cousin Hanamel; and Hanamel’s father, Shallum. Rabbi Yehuda said: So too, Huldah the prophetess was a descendant of Rahab the prostitute… For Rahab converted and married Joshua.

— Babylonian Talmud, Megillah 14b (William Davidson translation), Sefaria. The descent from Rahab is a baraita cited by Rav Eina the Elder, with Rabbi Yehuda adding Huldah; the marriage to Joshua is Rav Naḥman’s reply, joining that baraita to his own teaching that Huldah descended from Joshua. Since Joshua had no sons, the Talmud reckons Huldah from his daughters, so that she descends from both Joshua and Rahab.2

One of the four women of surpassing beauty

There were four women of extraordinary beauty in the world: Sarah, and Abigail, Rahab, and Esther… Rahab aroused impure thoughts by her name, i.e., the mere mention of her name would inspire lust for her.

— Babylonian Talmud, Megillah 15a (William Davidson translation), Sefaria. The passage places her beside Sarah, Abigail and Esther and says that her name alone aroused desire.3

Reception and debate

  • The marriage in Matthew. Matthew’s genealogy names a Rachab as the mother of Boaz by Salmon of Judah (Matthew 1:5), whom Chrysostom and Bede identify with the woman of Jericho; the Talmud names Joshua as Rahab’s husband. Both accounts are set out on the did Rahab marry Joshua? page.
  • The word in other texts. The Septuagint and the New Testament use pornē, “harlot,” for Rahab (Hebrews 11:31; James 2:25).
