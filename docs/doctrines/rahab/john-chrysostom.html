John Chrysostom on Rahab — the pearl in the mire — the harlot's confession, her faith, and Christ the Physician

Dates: c. 390–407 (homilies on Repentance, Matthew, and Hebrews; John Chrysostom b. c. 349, d. 407) · Wikipedia
Summary: Chrysostom returns to Rahab repeatedly: she is the pearl hidden in mire, her confession is faith on the lips, she prefigures the church gathered from the nations, and her place in Christ's line shows the Lord came as a Physician, not a Judge.

Claims & evidence

Rahab, the prefigurement of the church

Rahab is a prefigurement of the church, which was at one time mixed up in the prostitution of the demons and which now accepts the spies of Christ… the apostles who were sent by Jesus the true Savior… Therefore, Rahab, the prefigurement of the church, is worthy of all praise.

— John Chrysostom, Homilies on Repentance and Almsgiving 7.5.16, trans. Gus George Christo, Fathers of the Church 96 (CUA Press, 1998). Chrysostom reads Rahab as a figure of the church that once served the demons and now receives the apostles.1

The pearl in the mire

She was in a brothel, like a pearl mixed up in mire, like gold thrown in mud, the rose of piety hidden in thorns… She accepted the spies and the One whom Israel denied in the desert; Rahab preached this One in the brothel.

— John Chrysostom, Homilies on Repentance and Almsgiving 7.5.15-16, trans. Gus George Christo, Fathers of the Church 96 (CUA Press, 1998). The penitential image: the harlot is a hidden treasure, her piety wrapped in her disrepute.2

Christ came as a Physician, not a Judge

Seest thou that it was not for few nor small causes that he brought to our remembrance the whole history concerning Judah? For this end he hath mentioned Ruth also and Rahab, the one an alien, the other an harlot, that thou mayest learn that He came to do away with all our ills. For He hath come as a Physician, not as a Judge.

— John Chrysostom, Homilies on the Gospel of Matthew 3 (NPNF1 vol. 10), source. Preaching on the genealogy of Matthew 1:5, Chrysostom sees Rahab's place in Christ's line as a sign of the Lord's healing mission.3

More faithless than a harlot

It would then be disgraceful, if you should appear more faithless even than a harlot. Yet she merely heard what the men related, and forthwith believed. Whereupon the end also followed; for when all perished, she alone was preserved.

— John Chrysostom, Homilies on the Epistle to the Hebrews 27 (NPNF1 vol. 14), source. On Hebrews 11:31, Chrysostom presses Rahab's swift faith on hearing as a rebuke to slower believers.4

Notes

  • Chrysostom fuses three strands — the penitential (pearl in mire), the ecclesiological (prefigures the church), and the genealogical (Physician not Judge).
  • He keeps “harlot” sharp to make grace the point, the opposite of Josephus's innkeeper softening.
