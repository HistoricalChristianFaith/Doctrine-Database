Solomon's Stalls: 40,000 or 4,000? (1 Kings 4:26 vs. 2 Chronicles 9:25)

Related doctrines: Hard passages — see also Who Killed Goliath? (2 Samuel 21:19) · How Tall Was Goliath? (six or four cubits) · What Jesus Wrote on the Ground (John 8:6) · The Two Genealogies of Jesus (Matthew 1 vs. Luke 3) · The Death of Judas (Matthew 27 vs. Acts 1) · Baptized for the Dead (1 Corinthians 15:29)

Two books of the Old Testament count Solomon's horse-stalls, and the numbers disagree by a factor of ten. The Masoretic Hebrew of 1 Kings 4:26 gives him "forty thousand stalls of horses for his chariots, and twelve thousand horsemen."1 The parallel notice at 2 Chronicles 9:25 — the Chronicler retelling the same reign — gives "four thousand stalls for horses and chariots, and twelve thousand horsemen."2 The twelve thousand horsemen match; the stalls do not. This is not, like the height of Goliath, a single verse surviving in two manuscript families — it is a plain text-versus-text disagreement between two biblical books, of the same kind as the two slayers of Goliath in Samuel and Chronicles.

A third census fixes the scale. Both 1 Kings 10:26 and its parallel 2 Chronicles 1:14 report that Solomon had 1,400 chariots and 12,000 horsemen.3 Against that figure the smaller number is the proportionate one: 4,000 stalls supplies a full team of three horses to roughly twelve hundred chariots (with reserves), while 40,000 horses for 1,400 chariots is a stable nearly thirty times larger than the fleet it serves. So the arithmetic of Solomon's own army points to 4,000, and the mainstream verdict — carried into the text of most modern translations, the NIV printing "four thousand" and footnoting the Hebrew "forty"4 — is that the "forty thousand" of Kings is a scribal inflation of an original "four thousand," the true figure preserved by Chronicles. The suspicion is corroborated from the ground: the largest Iron-Age chariot-city stabling yet excavated, the complexes once called "Solomon's Stables" at Megiddo, held only a few hundred horses — a kingdom-wide total of four thousand is plausible for tenth-century Israel, forty thousand wildly beyond it.5

The case is not a clean sweep, and this page does not pretend otherwise. The oldest witnesses are divided: the Greek tradition carries both numbers in different places, Josephus already knew the forty thousand at the end of the first century, and no manuscript of Kings itself yet reads four. The weight for the smaller figure comes not from an unbroken external line (as it does for the shorter Goliath) but from the internal parallel with Chronicles and the arithmetic of the chariots. This page gathers the witnesses earliest first — the Chronicler who first records "four," the split Greek, Josephus siding with "forty," and the harmonizers and text-critics who reopened the count — and leaves "is the forty thousand a scribal error?" to be weighed under Arguments.

Timeline

The Chronicler (c. 400 BC)

The earliest witness to "four thousand" is the Chronicler himself. Retelling Solomon's reign from the older book of Kings, 2 Chronicles 9:25 reads "four thousand stalls for horses and chariots, and twelve thousand horsemen" — the same sentence as Kings but with the smaller, proportionate number. 6 On the mainstream reading his text preserves the figure that stood in his copy of Kings before a later hand inflated it; on the minority reading he is the one who reduced it. Either way, "four" is attested in a biblical book centuries before our oldest Hebrew codices of Kings.

The Septuagint — 3 Kingdoms (Old Greek, c. 250–150 BC)

The Greek translation of Kings does not settle the question — it doubles it. In the Septuagint's reorganized 3 Kingdoms the scattered Solomon material of Hebrew 1 Kings 4–5 is relocated into a supplement after 3 Kingdoms 2:46; there the horse-notice reads "forty thousand brood mares for his chariots" (tessarakonta chiliades), matching the Masoretic Kings.7 Yet the same Greek Bible, at 3 Kingdoms 10:26, reads "four thousand mares for his chariots, and twelve thousand horsemen" (tessares chiliades).8 So the discrepancy is already inside the Greek by the second century BC — the inflated "forty" is old, not a medieval Masoretic peculiarity, and the Greek cannot be enlisted as a united witness for "four" the way it can for the shorter Goliath.

Josephus (c. 93)

Retelling the reign in the Antiquities, Josephus follows the larger figure: "Solomon had also so great a number of chariots, that the stalls of his horses for those chariots were forty thousand; and besides these he had twelve thousand horsemen."9 Writing in Greek from a Hebrew source at the end of the first century — a thousand years before the Masoretic codices — he shows that "forty thousand" was the received text of Kings in his day. The corruption, if it is one, was already in place by the first century, and it is here that the smaller-number case parts company with the Goliath parallel, where Josephus told against the Masoretic reading rather than for it.

Matthew Poole (1685)

With the early-modern annotators the harmonizing begins in earnest. Matthew Poole, in his Annotations upon the Holy Bible, lays out both routes at once. He first proposes to reconcile the books by distinguishing the units — that Kings and Chronicles count different things, "the one signifying properly stables, of which there were 4000, the other stalls or partitions for each horse, which were 40,000" — and secondarily allows that the number may simply be a copyist's slip.10 The first move is the classic "different things being counted" harmonization; the second concedes the textual reading that later criticism would prefer. His near-contemporary John Gill records the same textual option plainly — "a mistake of the copier, of arbaim, forty, for arbah, four."

Keil & Delitzsch (1872)

The nineteenth-century commentary that set the conservative-critical consensus states the verdict without hedging. Keil & Delitzsch, on 1 Kings 4:26 (5:6 in the Hebrew versification), judge the Hebrew word for "forty" a straightforward transcription error: "ארבּעים (40) is an old copyist's error for ארבּעה (4), which we find in the parallel passage 2 Chronicles 9:25."11 On their reading Chronicles keeps the original number and Kings the slip — the same camp, and the same pair of commentators, that held Chronicles original in the two-slayers crux.

Gleason Archer (1982)

Twentieth-century conservative apologetics adopts the textual explanation as its own. Gleason Archer, in his Encyclopedia of Bible Difficulties, treats the pair as a model case of a numerical copying-error: the credible figure is the four thousand of Chronicles, whereas the Kings citation "has undoubtedly undergone multiplication by ten because of an obscurity in or misunderstanding of the Vorlage."12 Archer frames the slip not as a distortion of doctrine but as the ordinary hazard of hand-copied numerals — recoverable precisely because a second, un-inflated witness survives in Chronicles.

Raymond Dillard and the modern critics (1987)

Recent textual scholarship agrees that "forty thousand" is inflated but cautions against declaring "four thousand" the demonstrable original. Raymond Dillard, in his commentary on 2 Chronicles, notes that the number of Solomon's chariot-horses is "surrounded by a thicket of textual difficulties," the variants so tangled across Kings, Chronicles, and the Greek "that it is not possible to speak with confidence regarding the original text" — granting only that "the ratios work better in the Chronicles passage (4000/1400) than in the Kings passage (40,000/1400)."13 The honest modern position, then, is confident that the forty thousand is too large and much less confident about which exact figure the author first wrote.

Arguments

The sub-claim that cuts across the timeline — not "who counted which number, and when," but "is the forty thousand a scribal error, with the original four thousand preserved by Chronicles?" — is weighed adversarially on its own page, with an assessment of the reading it serves. The shared floor here is that everyone agrees the forty thousand is disproportionate to Solomon's 1,400 chariots; what is disputed is whether the fix is textual (a corrupted numeral) or harmonistic (Kings and Chronicles counting different units), and whether "four thousand" can be called the original text or only the better-proportioned one.

  • Is the "forty thousand" of 1 Kings 4:26 a scribal inflation of an original "four thousand" (preserved by 2 Chronicles 9:25)? — sound (that the figure is inflated and Chronicles the better witness; the "stables-vs.-stalls" harmonization does not hold, and the exact original wording stays uncertain).
