Solomon's Stalls: 40,000 or 4,000? (1 Kings 4:26 vs. 2 Chronicles 9:25)

Two books of the Old Testament count Solomon's horse-stalls, and the numbers disagree by a factor of ten. The Masoretic Hebrew of 1 Kings 4:26 gives him "forty thousand stalls of horses for his chariots, and twelve thousand horsemen."1 The parallel notice at 2 Chronicles 9:25 — the Chronicler retelling the same reign — gives "four thousand stalls for horses and chariots, and twelve thousand horsemen."2 The twelve thousand horsemen match; the stalls do not. This is not, like the height of Goliath, a single verse surviving in two manuscript families; it is a difference between two biblical books, of the same kind as the two slayers of Goliath in Samuel and Chronicles.

Both 1 Kings 10:26 and its parallel 2 Chronicles 1:14 report that Solomon had 1,400 chariots and 12,000 horsemen.3 On the archaeology, Graham Davies (1994) and Deborah Cantrell and Israel Finkelstein (2006) discuss the stable-complexes excavated at Megiddo, long called "Solomon's Stables"; Cantrell and Finkelstein reconstruct the largest single complex at about 450 horses and date the standing buildings to the eighth century BC.4

The ancient witnesses divide. The Greek of 3 Kingdoms carries both numbers in different places, Josephus gives the forty thousand at the end of the first century. No patristic discussion of the figure has been located here: Theodoret of Cyrus, whose Questions on the Third Book of Kings runs through Solomon's reign point by point, passes from the temple's cedars and bronze to the gold of Ophir and Sheba without raising the number of his horses, and Bede and Ephrem read the cedars, timber and silver of the reign figuratively. The earliest sustained discussion of the "forty thousand" surveyed here comes from the early-modern annotators below.5 This page gathers the witnesses earliest first: the Chronicler, the earliest extant attestation of "four"; the Greek, which carries both; Josephus, who gives "forty"; and the commentators from Matthew Poole to Raymond Dillard. Whether the two books count different things or one carries a copyist's error is set out under Disputed readings.

Timeline

The Chronicler (c. 400 BC)

The earliest extant attestation of "four thousand" is the Chronicler. Retelling Solomon's reign from the older book of Kings, 2 Chronicles 9:25 reads "four thousand stalls for horses and chariots, and twelve thousand horsemen," the parallel notice to 1 Kings 4:26, with the smaller number. 6 Keil & Delitzsch hold that his text preserves the figure that stood in Kings (see below).

The Septuagint — 3 Kingdoms (Old Greek, c. 250–150 BC)

The Greek translation of Kings carries both numbers. In 3 Kingdoms a supplement after 2:46 gathers Solomon material that stands in Hebrew 1 Kings 4–5; there the horse-notice reads "forty thousand brood mares for his chariots" (tessarakonta chiliades), matching the Masoretic Kings.7 The same Greek Bible, at 3 Kingdoms 10:26, reads "four thousand mares for his chariots, and twelve thousand horsemen" (tessares chiliades).8 Both figures thus stand in the Greek of Kingdoms by the second century BC.

Josephus (c. 93)

Retelling the reign in the Antiquities, Josephus gives the larger figure: "Solomon had also so great a number of chariots, that the stalls of his horses for those chariots were forty thousand; and besides these he had twelve thousand horsemen."9 Writing in Greek at the end of the first century, he reports the "forty thousand" of the Kings tradition. On Goliath's height Josephus gives the smaller figure against the Masoretic text; here his figure agrees with it.

Matthew Poole (1685)

Matthew Poole, in his Annotations upon the Holy Bible, gives two answers. His first: "Some acknowledge an error of the transcriber, writing arbahim, forty, for arbah, four, which was an easy mistake." His second distinguishes the units: "It is not exactly the same Hebrew word which is here and there, though we translate both stalls," "the one signifying properly stables, of which there were 4000, the other stalls or partitions for each horse, which were 40,000."10 John Gill, on the same verse, reports that "some think that here is a mistake of the copier, of arbaim, forty, for arbah, four," and, "without supposing this," offers reconciliations of the stall-and-stable kind, one of them after Gersonides ("Ben Gersom").

Keil & Delitzsch (1872)

Keil & Delitzsch, on 1 Kings 4:26 (5:6 in the Hebrew versification), read the Hebrew word for "forty" as a transcription error: "ארבּעים (40) is an old copyist's error for ארבּעה (4), which we find in the parallel passage 2 Chronicles 9:25."11 On their reading Chronicles keeps the original number and Kings the slip. They read Chronicles the same way in the two-slayers crux.

Gleason Archer (1982)

Gleason Archer, in his Encyclopedia of Bible Difficulties, treats the pair as an example of a numerical copying-error: he calls the four thousand of Chronicles the credible figure, and writes that the Kings citation "has undoubtedly undergone multiplication by ten because of an obscurity in or misunderstanding of the Vorlage."12

Raymond Dillard (1987)

Raymond Dillard, in his commentary on 2 Chronicles, is quoted as writing that the number of Solomon's chariot-horses is "surrounded by a thicket of textual difficulties," the variants so tangled across Kings, Chronicles, and the Greek "that it is not possible to speak with confidence regarding the original text," while granting that "the ratios work better in the Chronicles passage (4000/1400) than in the Kings passage (40,000/1400)."13
