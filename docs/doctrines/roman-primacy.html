The Primacy of Rome

Whether, and when, each church came to be governed by a single bishop over its presbyters is itself disputed (traced on the government of the church). This timeline records a further question: whether any one bishop, or any one church, held a primacy over the rest. It records the witnesses, across time, to the claim that the bishop of Rome holds a primacy over the whole church: that the see of Rome, held to be founded by Peter and to inherit the commission “thou art Peter, and upon this rock I will build my church… I will give unto thee the keys of the kingdom of heaven” (Matthew 16:18–19), is first among the churches and, as some of the witnesses below claim, exercises a jurisdiction over them by an unbroken Petrine succession. Catholic, Orthodox and Protestant readers draw on the same record and read it differently; whether a given witness attests a primacy of honour and precedence, a primacy of jurisdiction, or a priority of authority and love that some Orthodox interpreters hold to be neither, is the point at issue in several of the readings below.

The witnesses are set out earliest first, each slotted by date, with his own words where they survive: from Rome’s intervention at Corinth in 1 Clement (c. 96) and the church that “presides in love” of Ignatius (c. 108), through the potentior principalitas Irenaeus grants the Roman church (c. 180), Victor’s attempt to excommunicate the churches of Asia over the Easter date (c. 190), Tertullian’s argument that the keys of Matthew 16 were given to Peter personally and not to “every Church akin to Peter” (c. 210), Cyprian’s “chair of Peter” (c. 251), and Stephen of Rome’s attempt, resisted by Cyprian and Firmilian, to bind the churches over rebaptism by his succession from Peter (c. 256), to the conciliar canons that ranked the great sees (Nicaea 325, Sardica 343, Constantinople 381, Chalcedon 451) and Leo the Great’s teaching that the pope is Peter’s heir, speaking with Peter’s authority (c. 450).

Every side reads the same handful of texts (Matthew 16:18–19; Luke 22:32; John 21:15–17) and the same early witnesses. Where the reading of a particular witness is disputed — whether 1 Clement’s intervention attests jurisdiction or fraternal counsel, what Irenaeus’s potentior principalitas means, whether Cyprian’s “Primacy Text” is his own and what it teaches, and whether the councils record a primacy of honour or of jurisdiction — the readings are set out, with their named proponents, under Disputed readings below.

Related doctrines: Church order and government — see also the government of the church (the offices and the forms of polity — congregational, presbyterian, episcopal — among which the question of a primacy over the churches arises). The Eucharistic and sacramental side of the ministry is traced under the ministerial priesthood (who may confect the sacraments, ordination, apostolic succession as a channel of grace); congregational polity, which places no bishop over the gathered congregation, is traced on the same church-government timeline (see Robert Browne and John Owen); and the Petrine office itself — Peter among the Twelve and the tradition of his mission and death at Rome, on which the Roman claim draws (see the disputed reading Did Peter lead and die at Rome?) — sits on the apostle Peter and the apostles hub. Constantinople’s tradition of an apostolic foundation of its own — through Andrew, the brother called before Peter — is set out at Did Andrew found the church of Byzantium?

Timeline

1 Clement — the church of Rome writes to Corinth (c. 96)

The earliest witness on this timeline. Around 96 the church of Rome sent a long letter to Corinth to end a schism that had deposed its presbyters — rebuking the “sedition against its presbyters,” declaring that blamelessly serving ministers “cannot be justly dismissed,” and demanding that the ringleaders “submit yourselves to the presbyters.”1 Adrian Fortescue (Catholic, 1920), Johannes Quasten (Catholic, 1950) and Benedict XVI (2007) cite the intervention, with its warning that any who “disobey the words spoken by Him through us” incur “transgression and serious danger,” as an exercise of Roman primacy. James McCue (Catholic, 1964) reads it as an exhortation from one community to another, noting that it is issued corporately (“The Church of God which sojourns at Rome”) and that Clement’s name never appears in it.2 These and other readings are set out at the disputed reading. Ignatius of Antioch — the church that “presides over love” (c. 108)

About a decade later Ignatius of Antioch, whose other letters urge obedience to a single presiding bishop in each church, sends the Roman church the most elaborate greeting he gives any church: it is the one “which presides in the place of the region of the Romans” and “which presides over love” (prokathēmenē tēs agapēs), heaped with superlatives no other church receives.3 Alone among his seven letters it greets no bishop of Rome — the sole episcopal title in it is Ignatius’s own, “the bishop of Syria” — and Ignatius declines to command the Romans: “I do not, as Peter and Paul, issue commandments unto you. They were apostles; I am but a condemned man.”4 Whether “presides over love” gives Rome a presidency over the whole church or praises its standing and charity in its own region is disputed; the readings, with their proponents, are set out on the Ignatius page. The single presiding bishop of his other letters is traced on the government of the church. Full detail →

Irenaeus of Lyons — the church with which “every Church should agree” (c. 180)

Around 180 Irenaeus appeals to Rome by name. Refuting Gnostics who claimed a secret apostolic tradition, Irenaeus argues that the true tradition is public and traceable through each church’s bishops — and offers Rome as the sufficient specimen: “the very great, the very ancient, and universally known Church founded … by the two most glorious apostles, Peter and Paul,” with which “every Church should agree … on account of its pre-eminent authority” (Latin propter potiorem principalitatem), before giving the Roman succession-list from Linus down to the reigning Eleutherius.5 This is the earliest extant text to appeal explicitly to Rome’s standing as a touchstone of the faith. The pivotal phrase survives only in a Latin rendering of a lost Greek — the Ante-Nicene editors say they are “far from sure” the translation is right — and has been rendered “pre-eminent authority,” “more powerful origin,” and “superior origin.” Irenaeus names Peter and Paul together as the church’s founders, and has the apostles commit the episcopate to Linus. Whether the sentence asserts Roman jurisdiction or a primacy of honour, and how it relates to the same father’s admonition of Bishop Victor (c. 190, below), is disputed; see the disputed reading.6 Full detail →

Victor of Rome — the attempted excommunication of the churches of Asia (c. 190)

A decade after Irenaeus wrote, around 190, the churches of Asia and the church of Rome divided over the date of Easter, and when the Asian churches held to their older “Quartodeciman” custom, Victor of Rome attempted to cut them off from communion as heterodox, in Eusebius’s account — writing letters that “declared all the brethren there wholly excommunicate.”7 It is the earliest extant record of a bishop of Rome acting against churches beyond his own province, and of that action being openly resisted. Polycrates of Ephesus, replying to Victor and the church of Rome, refused to yield, matching Rome’s apostolic pedigree with Asia’s own (the apostles Philip and John, and Polycarp) and closing “I … am not affrighted by terrifying words. For those greater than I have said ‘We ought to obey God rather than man.’”8 Other bishops, including some who shared Victor’s Easter position, objected: “this did not please all the bishops,” and their letters “sharply rebuking Victor” are extant. Foremost was Irenaeus, who admonished Victor that he should not cut off whole churches over an ancient custom and recalled that Victor’s own predecessors had kept peace across the same disagreement. Eusebius does not record whether Victor’s sentence took effect.9 Whether the episode shows a papal prerogative asserted by Victor and recognized by Irenaeus, a breach of communion between churches, or an affair internal to the Roman church is disputed; the readings, with their proponents, are set out on the Victor page. The same Irenaeus had written of Rome’s “pre-eminent authority” about a decade before (c. 180, above). Full detail →

Tertullian — the keys read as a personal grant to Peter (c. 210)

Around 210, from Carthage, Tertullian supplies the earliest extant text to argue that the commission to Peter in Matthew 16 was given to Peter personally and does not descend to “every Church akin to Peter.” In On Modesty he rounds on a bishop’s edict remitting the sins of adultery and fornication — a bishop identified as the bishop of Rome by Fortescue and Newman (Callistus and Zephyrinus being the Roman candidates), and as “perhaps Agrippinus of Carthage” in Roger Pearse’s introduction to the treatise — mocking its author as “Pontifex Maximus — that is, the bishop of bishops.”10 He then confronts the proof-text head-on: because every clause of the grant is singular — “to thee have I given the keys … not to the Church” — the power of binding and loosing was conferred “personally upon Peter,” and those who “presume that the power … has derived to you, that is, to every Church akin to Peter” subvert “the manifest intention of the Lord”; whatever the keys convey belongs to spiritual men on the pattern of Peter, “not the Church which consists of a number of bishops.” If the edict was a Roman bishop’s, the passage is also the earliest extant denial that the commission passes to the bishop of Rome.11 Tertullian wrote On Modesty after joining the Montanist (“New Prophecy”) movement, in opposition to the mainstream (“psychic”) church and in defence of a rigorist penitential discipline. The same author, a decade earlier and before that break, had appealed warmly to Rome’s double-apostolic pedigree — “the church of Rome, which makes Clement to have been ordained … by Peter,” the “happy” church “on which apostles poured forth all their doctrine along with their blood,” Peter and Paul both.12 Full detail →

Cyprian of Carthage — the “chair of Peter” and the one episcopate (c. 251)

Around 251, in On the Unity of the Church, Cyprian of Carthage grounds the church’s unity in Christ’s singular commission to Peter — “upon this rock I will build my Church … I will give unto thee the keys” — and speaks of “the chair of Peter”; one recension of chapter 4, the so-called Primacy Text, says outright that “the primacy is given to Peter.” The same chapter, on its received text, continues: “the rest of the apostles were also the same as was Peter, endowed with a like partnership both of honour and power; but the beginning proceeds from unity.”13 In chapter 5 he writes that the episcopate is one, each part of it “held by each one for the whole.” The recension problem is disputed: the Ante-Nicene Fathers editors judged the Primacy Text an interpolation “beyond all question spurious,” while Maurice Bévenot (Jesuit, 1954) holds both versions Cyprian’s own, reversing the order of composition John Chapman had proposed.14 Which recension is Cyprian’s, and whether “the chair of Peter” places a primacy at Rome, is set out at the disputed reading. Stephen of Rome — the succession from Peter invoked, and opposed (c. 256)

Five years later the same Cyprian opposed a bishop of Rome. In the rebaptism controversy of 255–256, Stephen of Rome forbade the African and Eastern practice of baptizing afresh those who came from communities they held to be heretical, and pressed his ruling on the other churches under threat of broken communion — resting it, his opponents report, expressly on his succession from Peter: he “boasts of the place of his episcopate, and contends that he holds the succession from Peter.”15 It is the earliest extant report of a Roman bishop grounding a claim over distant churches on his descent from Peter. Cyprian answered in council, opening the Seventh Council of Carthage (256) with eighty-seven bishops on the principle that “neither does any of us set himself up as a bishop of bishops … every bishop … has his own proper right of judgment,” and held Rome’s ruling did not bind the African churches.16 Firmilian of Caesarea answered for the East, turning Stephen’s threat back on him: “while you think that all may be excommunicated by you, you have excommunicated yourself alone from all.”17 No excommunication of the African or Eastern churches is recorded as taking effect, and the dispute lapsed at Stephen’s death in 257. Stephen’s letters survive only in his opponents’ quotations: Cyprian quotes his ruling (“let nothing be innovated … which has not been handed down”), and his claim to the succession from Peter is known only from Firmilian.15 How the episode bears on Cyprian’s “chair of Peter” is set out at the disputed reading. The Council of Nicaea — Rome named beside Alexandria and Antioch (canon 6, 325)

With the fourth century the witnesses shift from individual bishops to the councils that ranked the great sees. Nicaea’s sixth canon confirms the “ancient customs” by which each chief see governs its region, and names Rome as the parallel for Alexandria’s jurisdiction: “Let the ancient customs in Egypt, Libya and Pentapolis prevail, that the Bishop of Alexandria have jurisdiction in all these, since the like is customary for the Bishop of Rome also. Likewise in Antioch and the other provinces, let the Churches retain their privileges.”18 The canon grounds the arrangement in custom and names no Petrine title for Rome. Whether it treats Rome as one great see among the metropolitans or presupposes Rome’s authority as the known measure is disputed; see the disputed reading. Full detail →

The Council of Sardica — appeal to Rome “to honour the memory of Peter” (canons 3–5, 343)

Eighteen years later a Western council supplies the earliest extant conciliar text giving Rome a defined role in the appeals of other churches. Sardica’s canons 3–5 grant that a bishop deposed by his colleagues may — “if it seem good to your charity, honour the memory of Peter the Apostle” — take his cause to Julius of Rome, who may order a retrial by the bishops of the neighbouring province and, if he sees fit, send legates to sit as judges.19 Rome later cited these canons for an appellate role. The right is proposed by the council (“if it seem good to your charity”), and Sardica was a Western synod from which the Eastern bishops withdrew; in the East the Quinisext Council (692) later set its seal on the canons “of Sardica” among those it confirmed. In the fifth century Rome cited this appeal-right as a canon of Nicaea; the African bishops, unable to find it in their Nicene copies, sent to Alexandria and Constantinople for the Nicene texts and found it to be Sardican, copied after the Nicene canons under a shared heading — the affair of Apiarius (c. 418–424), in which the African church declined the appeal-right.20 How the canons bear on Rome’s primacy is disputed; see the disputed reading. Full detail →

The First Council of Constantinople — Constantinople ranked after Rome “because Constantinople is New Rome” (canon 3, 381)

The second ecumenical council gives a civic reason for its ranking of the great sees. Its third canon gives the bishop of Constantinople “the prerogative of honour after the Bishop of Rome, because Constantinople is New Rome.”21 The canon leaves Rome first and states no ground for Rome’s own place. Seventy years later Leo the Great, writing to Anatolius of Constantinople in 451, called the canon “the written assent of certain bishops given, as you allege, 60 years ago, and never brought to the knowledge of the Apostolic See by your predecessors,” and defended against Constantinople’s elevation the inherited ranks of Alexandria and Antioch.21 How the canon bears on Rome’s primacy is disputed; see the disputed reading. Full detail →

The Council of Chalcedon — “because it was the royal city”; Leo protests and refuses to ratify (canon 28, 451)

The fourth ecumenical council extends the canon of 381. Canon 28 takes up the canon of 381, quotes it, and states its ground for both sees: “the Fathers rightly granted privileges to the throne of old Rome, because it was the royal city. And the One Hundred and Fifty most religious Bishops, actuated by the same consideration, gave equal privileges to the most holy throne of New Rome” — Constantinople now ranking next after Rome and given jurisdiction over Pontus, Asia, and Thrace.22 Pope Leo the Great — whose christological Tome the same council had acclaimed — refused to ratify canon 28, resting the order of the sees on the “unalterable” Nicene canons and declaring the bishops’ assent to it null and void: “no one may venture upon anything in opposition to the enactments of the Fathers’ canons which many long years ago in the city of Nicæa were founded upon the decrees of the Spirit.”23 How the canon and Leo’s refusal of it bear on the kind of primacy Rome held is disputed; see the disputed reading. Full detail →

Leo the Great — the pope as Peter’s living heir, “the primate of all bishops” (c. 450)

The timeline closes with Leo the Great. Preaching each year on the anniversary of his elevation, he sets out a Petrine theory of the Roman see: what Christ instituted in Peter is perpetual, so that “as that remains which Peter believed in Christ, so that remains which Christ instituted in Peter,” and Peter himself — not merely his memory — “has not abandoned the helm of the Church,” his “power lives and… authority prevails in his See.” The Roman bishop is Peter’s heir, in whom “abides the care of all the shepherds” and “whose dignity is not abated even in so unworthy an heir” — “not only the patron of this see, but also the primate of all bishops.”24 In these sermons Matthew 16 is read as founding a standing office, with the pope as its living heir and a claimed primacy over the whole episcopate; on the feast of the apostles he calls Rome “the head of the world through the blessed Peter’s holy See.”25 When Leo’s christological Tome (449) was read to the Council of Chalcedon in 451 the fathers acclaimed “Peter has spoken through Leo,” after examining it against Cyril of Alexandria.26 In the West the emperor Valentinian III’s rescript of 445 decreed that “the primacy of the Apostolic See is established by the merit of St. Peter…, by the majesty of the city of Rome, and… the authority of a holy council,” and that Rome’s sanction “shall be law.”27 The same council’s canon 28 (above) was used in the East as having force of law over the protests of Leo and his successors, as John Chapman reports, and Metropolitan Hilarion (Russian Orthodox, 2014) calls it the canon “on which the primacy of the Patriarch of Constantinople has been based.”23 How Leo’s claims relate to the conciliar record is disputed; see the disputed reading. Disputed readings

How particular witnesses on the timeline have been read on the question of Rome’s primacy.

  • Does 1 Clement’s intervention at Corinth (c. 96) attest a Roman primacy of jurisdiction? — on whether the earliest witness records an authority over another church or an admonition between churches.28
  • Does Irenaeus’s potentior principalitas (Against Heresies III.3.2) assert a Roman primacy of jurisdiction? — on a sentence that survives only in Latin translation.29
  • Does Cyprian’s On the Unity of the Church 4 (the “Primacy Text”) teach a Roman primacy of jurisdiction? — on the authorship of the Primacy Text and the meaning of “the chair of Peter.”30
  • Do the great councils (325–451) show a Roman primacy of honour among the sees, or of jurisdiction? — on the canons of Nicaea, Sardica, Constantinople and Chalcedon.31
