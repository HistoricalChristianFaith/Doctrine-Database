Did Chrysostom preach the “Again Herodias raves” sermon against the empress Eudoxia?

Disputed reading · on Theophilus of Alexandria and John Chrysostom · witness: John Chrysostom

Related readings: Was Origenism the ground of John Chrysostom's deposition at the Synod of the Oak (403)?

The passage

When John became aware of this, he delivered in the church that celebrated oration commencing with these words: ‘Again Herodias raves; again she is troubled; she dances again; and again desires to receive John's head in a charger.’ This, of course, exasperated the empress still more.

— Socrates, Church History VI.18, source. Socrates sets the sermon after Chrysostom's denunciation of the games at the dedication of Eudoxia's silver statue, when the empress moved to convene a second council against him.1

The question

Socrates and Sozomen report that John Chrysostom preached a sermon casting the empress as Herodias and himself as the Baptist, and a homily opening with that sentence is printed among his works. What is disputed is whether the sermon and its words are Chrysostom's; the answer shapes how his conflict with Eudoxia is told.

The sermon was preached as the historians report

On this reading Chrysostom, provoked by the move for a second council, preached the “Herodias” sermon against the empress.

Held by: Socrates Scholasticus (Church History VI.18, c. 439–443); Sozomen (Church History VIII.20).2

Socrates wrote at Constantinople within about thirty-five years of the events, among people who had lived through them and in a city where the “Johannite” party was a living memory; Sozomen, writing there shortly after, gives the same episode: “Herodias is again enraged; again she dances; again she seeks to have the head of John in a basin.” Both historians are favourable to Chrysostom, so the report does not come from his enemies.3

The setting is common ground. Socrates and Sozomen agree that Chrysostom denounced the games held at the dedication of the statue, that the empress took it as aimed at her, and that she moved for a second council in consequence; an earlier sermon “against the vices to which females are peculiarly prone” is likewise reported as having been carried to the palace as an attack on her. Sozomen's reservation concerns whether that earlier discourse was meant as an allusion to the empress, not whether it was preached.4 A homily opening with the very words Socrates quotes, In decollationem sancti Iohannis, circulates among Chrysostom's works and is printed in the Patrologia Graeca.5 On this reading a bishop who had twice been heard at court as attacking the empress from the pulpit spoke a third time in the sharpest terms, and two historians of the capital recorded the words.

The homily is spurious and the Herodias words belong to a later telling

On this reading the homily that carries the line is not Chrysostom's, and the celebrated words are attested only a generation after his death; the episode may be a later dramatization.

Held by: Philip Schaff (History of the Christian Church III); S. J. Voicu (“Furono chiamati giovanniti…,” 2004); Emilio Bonfiglio (John Chrysostom's Discourses on his First Exile, 2011).6

In decollationem sancti Iohannis is classed among the pseudo-Chrysostomic works: it is catalogued in the Clavis Patrum Graecorum in the block reserved for the dubia et spuria, and Schaff reports it rejected as spurious by Tillemont, Savile and Montfaucon, the last two the editors who printed it. The surviving homily is therefore a later composition that opens with the line, and the sermon may have been supplied to match the histories.7 The two historians reduce to one: Hartranft's account of Sozomen's sources makes Socrates “his guide,” and the NPNF note at this chapter names Socrates VI.18 and Palladius as its sources.8 Palladius, writing about 408 as an insider whose own case the Oak took up, reports no Herodias sermon; he records an allegation of a different word: “The treason consisted in offensive language against the queen, whom, as they alleged, he had called Jezebel.”9 Voicu argues, and Bonfiglio follows him, that the Chrysostom-against-Eudoxia confrontation was sharpened in Johannite circles after 407, when the empress, dead within months of the banishment, made a fitting villain; in Bonfiglio's words, “In decollationem is spurious and therefore this episode of Chrysostom's life may have actually never happened.”10 On this reading what the record traces to Chrysostom is pulpit language that the palace heard as an attack on the empress, and the celebrated form of the words belongs to the story as it was told a generation later.
