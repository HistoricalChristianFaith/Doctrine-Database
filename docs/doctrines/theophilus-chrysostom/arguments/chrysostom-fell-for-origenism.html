Was Origenism the ground of John Chrysostom's deposition at the Synod of the Oak (403)?

Disputed reading · on Theophilus of Alexandria and John Chrysostom · witness: Theophilus of Alexandria

Related readings: Did Chrysostom preach the “Again Herodias raves” sermon against the empress Eudoxia?

The passage

But, not to speak of his other misdeeds, he has taken the Origenists into his confidence, has advanced many of them to the priesthood, and by committing this crime has saddened with no slight grief that man of God, Epiphanius of blessed memory.

— Theophilus of Alexandria to Jerome, in Jerome, Letter 113 (a.d. 405), source. Theophilus sent Jerome, for translation into Latin, his invective against the deposed bishop of Constantinople, and Jerome translated it.1

The question

The campaign against John Chrysostom opened with the charge that he had sheltered Origenists, and the synod at the Oak deposed him in 403. What is disputed is what part Origenism played in the deposition, and so where the affair belongs in the history of doctrine.

A doctrinal case: deposed for protecting Origenists

On this reading the fall of Chrysostom was the Eastern chapter of the Origenist controversy: a bishop who received and protected men condemned as Origenists was removed for doing so.

Held by: Isaac, bishop (memorial to the Synod of the Oak, 403, as summarized by Photius); Theophilus of Alexandria (letter to Jerome, 405).2

Two of the forty-seven articles laid before the synod of the Oak concern Origenism, and Bishop Isaac's memorial names the men Chrysostom is charged with consorting with: “Ammonius, Euthymius, Eusebius, Heraclides, and Palladius.” Theophilus's letter of 405 states the ground of the whole case in one sentence, and through Jerome's Latin version it became the account the West received.

The chain of events is doctrinal at every link. Origen's books were condemned by synods at Cyprus and Alexandria; monks were expelled from Nitria as Origenists; those monks fled to Constantinople; Chrysostom received them; and that reception brought Theophilus to the capital with twenty-nine suffragans.3 Association with named Origenists was a real liability in the capital in 403: it is what Epiphanius of Salamis came to press, what he demanded Chrysostom disavow by expelling Dioscorus and his brethren and subscribing against Origen, and what he denounced publicly in the church of the Apostles when Chrysostom deferred.4 The doctrinal division underneath was genuine: Sozomen records the anthropomorphite quarrel that split the Egyptian monks into two named parties, and Elizabeth Clark's study places the Tall Brothers' circle within the Evagrian theology that the anthropomorphite monks rejected.5 On this reading a bishop who declined to subscribe a synodical condemnation and continued to house the condemned had placed himself, by the standards of 403, on one side of a live doctrinal dispute, and was deposed by the other.

An episcopal quarrel: Origenism as the occasion

On this reading the grievance behind the campaign was the lost election of 398 and the quarrel over Isidore, and the charge of Origenism was the means by which it was pressed.

Held by: Palladius of Galatia (Dialogue on the Life of St John Chrysostom, c. 408); Socrates Scholasticus (Church History VI, c. 439–443); Sozomen (Church History VIII).6

Socrates dates the plot from immediately after Chrysostom's consecration and names its cause: the failure to place Isidore in the see of Constantinople, two years before Origen enters the story. Palladius traces the quarrel with the Tall Brothers to Isidore and money: when Theophilus seized Ammonius by the neck shouting “Anathematize Origen, you heretic!”, “the only point at issue was the petition on behalf of Isidore.”7 Socrates states that Theophilus himself taught the incorporeality of God, the position he charged his opponents with, and that he “did not hesitate to pervert what he and they had rightly taught.”8

At the synod itself, Sozomen records that Theophilus “judged it expedient to make no further allusion to the works of Origen”; Socrates writes that “no mention was now made of Origen, but all were intent on urging a variety of criminations, many of which were ridiculous.” The synod deposed Chrysostom for not appearing, and referred to the emperor the one charge it said it could not try, treason for words about the empress.9 After the deposition Theophilus communicated with Dioscorus and the Tall Brothers, which Socrates gives as the reason the city turned against him; he was reconciled with the monks of Scetis, wept at Ammonius's death, and resumed reading Origen, culling the flowers of a meadow and stepping over the thorns.10

“Origenism” as a code for non-theological complaints

On this reading the label “Origenism” was the form in which rivalries of patronage, alliance and monastic networks could be prosecuted as heresy, so the affair was at once doctrinal in vocabulary and social in substance.

Held by: Elizabeth A. Clark (The Origenist Controversy: The Cultural Construction of an Early Christian Debate, 1992).11

Clark treats “Origenism” in these years as neither a straightforward doctrinal charge nor an empty one. The word functioned as a code in which complaints about patronage, rivalry, alliance and the standing of monastic networks could be pressed as heresy, and the content of the charge was reconstructed at each phase of Theophilus's campaign to suit its target. Chrysostom on this account was drawn into the Origenist orbit because the monks driven from Nitria fled to his city, and his toleration of them supplied the opening. The reading explains both why this particular accusation was the one available in 403, when the Origenist question was being fought across the Greek and Latin churches at once, and why its content could shift from the monks of Nitria to the bishop of Constantinople. In 403, on this reading, a doctrinal accusation was the usable form for a grievance whose roots lay in the social world of the bishops and monks.
