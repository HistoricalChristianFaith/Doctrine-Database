Ambrose of Milan on Who Was Job?

Dates: c. 388 (The Prayer of Job and David / De interpellatione Iob et David) · Wikipedia
Summary: The Latin West’s great bishop devotes a four-book treatise to Job and David as the two saints who show how a righteous man may plead with God under undeserved affliction. Ambrose sets Job’s suffering against the oldest scandal of faith — the just afflicted while the wicked prosper — and answers it by reading Job as “a good athlete” and “champion of Christ” whose bold complaint is not impatience but righteous speech, and who, sick on his dunghill, proves “stronger” than the healthy friends who came to condemn him. Ambrose adds nothing on Job’s land or era; his Job is the model intercessor and sufferer, the Latin bridge between Tertullian’s patient hero and Gregory’s Moralia.

Claims & evidence

Job (with David) as the saint who intercedes and pleads for human weakness

The treatise’s frame is the interpellatio — the complaint or pleading of the righteous before God. Ambrose pairs Job with David as the two who model how the faithful may cry out under affliction without falling from faith, and sets the problem of the suffering just at the centre:

Our discussion was about the intercession of the saints, and how fragile and weak the human condition is, which has no stability anywhere except in divine protection: today we must take this into consideration, as it greatly affects the common people, and even the wise are often troubled; when they see the unjust prospering, and the just frequently afflicted in this world.

— The Prayer of Job and David, Book III, ch. I. Where Job’s three friends read his suffering as punishment for hidden sin, Ambrose reads it as a permitted test — “the Lord had allowed him to be tested, so that as a champion of Christ, he could become more glorious and reach the crown through trials” — so that the friends, “with their weak understanding,” turned Job’s meritorious suffering into punishment.1

Job the “good athlete” whose bold complaint keeps faith

Ambrose’s governing image is the athlete who takes his wounds without quitting the contest — and, strikingly, he presents Job’s complaint (his long protest to God) as the athlete’s cry, not a lapse from patience:

…and even being afflicted with a severe ulcer, when he saw that his friends had not come to console him, but to magnify and exacerbate his pain, he understood that the power to tempt him had been given to him by the Lord’s adversary. And although he felt the arrows of the Lord in his body, by which he said he was being wounded, yet like a good athlete who would not yield to pain and would not refuse the challenges of the contest, he added: The Lord has begun to inflict wounds, but in the end He will not destroy me.

— The Prayer of Job and David, Book I, ch. II. The bishop quotes Job’s rawest words — “What is my strength, that I should endure?… Is my strength the strength of stones, or is my flesh bronze?” — and hears in them not blasphemy but the faith of a man who wrestles God and holds on.2

The sick man stronger than his healthy friends

The treatise’s sharpest reversal is that Job, ruined and ulcerated, out-argues the whole and comfortable men who came to accuse him — his weakness proving the stronger:

Holy Job heard these things, and like a strong athlete sat in the dung, covered in sores and painful wounds, the whole body covered in dreadful ulcers, he spoke of mysteries, not seeking his own remedies for sickness, but occupied with sacred discourses. Therefore, the words of a sick man are stronger than those who are not sick.

— The Prayer of Job and David, Book III, ch. II. Because “the holy Job could discern by the spirit how he ought to speak to each one… he was stronger than those who appeared to be healthy and whole” — the friends preached “divine judgments” but “did not know how to discern the guilty from the righteous,” condemning as unjust the very man God had pronounced righteous.3

Notes

  • Nothing on placement. Ambrose raises none of the timeline’s identity questions — Uz, Edom, Job’s era or nationality, the Job-is-Jobab equation. He assumes the man’s historicity and treats him as a spiritual exemplar; his interest is the pattern of righteous suffering and complaint, not the biography.
  • The complaint (interpellatio) as the theme. The work’s Latin title turns on interpellatio — interposition, pleading, complaint. Ambrose’s achievement is to make Job’s protest itself a form of faith: the righteous may argue with God, as Job and David did, and be justified in it. This is a distinct accent from the pure patience of Tertullian and the athlete-endurance of the Alexandrians, and it feeds the Western moral reading that Gregory would systematize.
  • Job and David paired. The four books alternate — Job (I, III) and David (II, IV) — as two witnesses to the same lesson, the great and the lowly both learning to plead with God amid the infirmity of the human condition. Only the Job books are drawn on here.
