The Septuagint appendix to Job (Job 42:17b–e)

Dates: the Old Greek of Job, c. 2nd–1st century BC (the appendix, Job 42:17a–e) · Wikipedia
Summary: The Greek translators closed their Job with a paragraph absent from the Hebrew that names the sufferer’s former name as Jobab, sets his home “on the borders of Idumea and Arabia,” runs his line back through Esau to Abraham, and slots him into the Edomite king-list of Genesis 36:33 — the earliest post-biblical attempt to give Job a genealogy, a homeland, and a date, and the fountainhead of the whole “Job was an Edomite king of the patriarchal age” tradition.

Claims & evidence

Job’s former name was Jobab, and he lived on the borders of Idumea and Arabia

Where the Hebrew ends at Job’s death (“So Job died, being old and full of days,” Job 42:17), the Old Greek adds a paragraph — conventionally numbered 42:17a–e — that first affirms Job’s resurrection and then, citing an outside source, gives his biography:

And Job died, an old man and full of days: and it is written that he will rise again with those whom the Lord raises up. This man is described in the Syriac book as living in the land of Ausis, on the borders of Idumea and Arabia: and his name before was Jobab; and having taken an Arabian wife, he begot a son whose name was Ennon. And he himself was the son of his father Zare, one of the sons of Esau, and of his mother Bosorrha, so that he was the fifth from Abraam.

— The Septuagint (Brenton), Job 42:17a–c, source. “Ausis” is the Greek rendering of Hebrew Uz, here located explicitly in the borderland between Edom (Idumea) and Arabia; the appendix pauses to name its own authority — a “Syriac” (that is, Aramaic) book — a citation formula unique in the Greek Job.1

Job is Jobab, second king of Edom — slotted into the king-list of Genesis 36

The paragraph then makes its identification explicit, reproducing the Edomite king-list of Genesis 36:31–35 and placing “Jobab, who is called Job” in it as Bela’s successor:

And these were the kings who reigned in Edom, which country he also ruled over: first, Balac, the son of Beor, and the name of his city was Dennaba: but after Balac, Jobab, who is called Job, and after him Asom, who was governor out of the country of Thaeman: and after him Adad, the son of Barad, who destroyed Madiam in the plain of Moab; and the name of his city was Gethaim. And his friends who came to him were Eliphaz, of the children of Esau, king of the Thaemanites, Baldad sovereign of the Sauchaeans, Sophar king of the Minaeans.

— The Septuagint (Brenton), Job 42:17d–e, source. The equation is exact: the Hebrew list runs Bela son of Beor → Jobab son of Zerah of Bozrah → Husham of Teman → Hadad son of Bedad “who smote Midian in the field of Moab” (Genesis 36:31–35); the Greek gives the same sequence (Balac → Jobab → Asom the Themanite → Adad son of Barad who destroyed Madiam) and folds Job into slot two. In one stroke the appendix answers all three of the questions the Hebrew leaves open — who (a great-grandson of Esau, “the fifth from Abraam”), when (the age of the Edomite kings “before there reigned any king over the children of Israel,” Genesis 36:31, i.e. the patriarchal era), and where (Uz on the Edom–Arabia border) — and it derives Job’s three friends from the same Esau genealogy, making Eliphaz the Temanite (Job 2:11) a son of Esau per Genesis 36:10–11.2

The tradition is older than the appendix: Aristeas the Exegete

The identification did not originate with the Greek Job. The Hellenistic-Jewish writer Aristeas the Exegete, in his lost Concerning the Jews — excerpted by Alexander Polyhistor (mid-1st century BC) and preserved by Eusebius — already tells the same story: that Esau married Bassara and begot Job, who dwelt in Uz “on the borders of Idumaea and Arabia,” and “was formerly called Jobab.”

Aristeas says in his book Concerning the Jews that Esau married Bassara in Edom, and begat Job… And he dwelt in the land of Ausitis on the borders of Idumaea and Arabia; and he was at first called Jobab.

— Aristeas the Exegete, in Eusebius, Praeparatio Evangelica IX.25, source. Because Aristeas is quoted only through Polyhistor and Eusebius, and because the appendix’s personal notices on Job track Aristeas so closely, scholars from Freudenthal onward have judged the Septuagint supplement to depend on this same tradition (Freudenthal thought it derived directly from Aristeas) — pushing the Job = Jobab equation back at least to the 3rd–2nd century BC, before its fixation in the Greek Bible.3

Notes

  • Two additions, not one. Modern study distinguishes 42:17a (the brief resurrection notice, “he will rise again”) from 42:17b–e (the Jobab genealogy). The resurrection line is often taken as a separate, possibly later, gloss; the genealogical appendix proper is 17b–e, which has no equivalent in the Hebrew, the Peshitta, the Qumran Job Targum (11QtgJob), or the Rabbinic Targum. Annette Yoshiko Reed’s study reads 17b–e as a deliberate re-contextualizing of Job within Israelite/Edomite history rather than a stray scribal note.4
  • A textual seam. The appendix calls Job “son” of Zare (Zerah), but Genesis 36 makes Jobab a great-grandson of Esau through Zerah, and gives Bozrah (Bosorrha) as Jobab’s city, not his mother — the Greek has turned the place-name into a parent (“his mother Bosorrha”). These slips, already noted by Schürer, mark the paragraph as an interpretive compilation stitched from Genesis 36 and Job, not an independent record.
  • Downstream. This is the first witness on the timeline after the biblical portrait; the same Edomite-king setting is taken up by the Testament of Job and assumed by later commentators. The genuinely contested questions it raises — is Job actually Jobab of Edom? when did he live? where was Uz? — are weighed on the argument pages of the Who Was Job? timeline rather than settled here.
