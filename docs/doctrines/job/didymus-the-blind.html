Didymus the Blind on Who Was Job?

Dates: c. 377 (the Commentary on Job, recovered on the Tura papyri) · Wikipedia
Summary: Didymus, head of the Alexandrian catechetical school, reads Job in the spiritual, Alexandrian key: the man is set before “his contemporaries and his successors” as a “marked pillar” of perseverance, an athlete who fights his bout knowingly, while the Satan can afflict him only by God’s permission and within God’s limits. Like Origen before him, Didymus is a witness to the athlete-of-endurance Job, not to his land or lineage.

Claims & evidence

Job set up as a “marked pillar” and athlete of perseverance

For Didymus the point of the whole trial is exemplary: Job is deliberately made a standing monument of endurance for every later generation to measure itself against.

The Lord allows even this for our best, so that Job should be presented as an expression and image of perseverance—like a marked pillar—and that he may be for his contemporaries and his successors an example of such virtue. Indeed, this did happen. From the fighter and athlete himself one can hear the words, “I know that I shall be vindicated.”

— Commentary on Job 2:6, on Job 2:6. The athlete does not merely suffer but competes with purpose: he “prays that he himself may win the crown in the competition” and “speaks like a brave athlete who warlike meets his opponent” (Job 6:8–9).1

The Satan can strike only by permission and within limits

Didymus reads the two prologue scenes as establishing a rule about all affliction — that it is neither fate nor accident but a permitted, bounded testing:

The quoted words prove that no one is tempted without God’s permission. For God says, “See, I have given everything into your hand.” But in order to show that this permission is given [only] with restrictions, it is added, “Only do not stretch out your hand against him.” Thus afflictions occur neither due to fate nor arbitrarily but due to God’s permission…

— Commentary on Job 1:11, on Job 1:11–12. The adversary is named as the real, prowling enemy of the saints — “the archvillain himself, the devil, who prowls around ‘like a roaring lion… looking for someone to devour’” — who must still ask leave before he can touch Job (Job 3:26).2

Notes

  • The Alexandrian spiritual method. Didymus reads figuratively where the Antiochene literalists refuse to: Job cursing “his day” is not a calendar day but “those things that occurred on that day,” and Job’s hope of answering God when called is read of “the soul whose thoughts are fixed on God.” His Job is thus the same figure the whole Alexandrian line hands on — the athlete and pillar of endurance — read in a spiritual register.
  • No placement material. The recovered commentary covers only Job 1–14 and says nothing of Uz, Edom, Job’s era or nationality, or the Jobab identification.
  • The Tura find. Didymus’s Commentary on Job was lost until 1941, when it turned up among the papyri discovered in a cave at Tura, south of Cairo; the quotations are cited to the verse pages where the recovered text is preserved.
