Modern scholarship — what critical study makes of Job

Dates: a survey of modern critical scholarship (chiefly 19th–21st century) · representative works: Marvin H. Pope, Job (Anchor Bible 15, 1965); Norman C. Habel, The Book of Job (Old Testament Library, 1985); David J. A. Clines, Job 1–20 (Word Biblical Commentary 17, 1989); W. G. Lambert, Babylonian Wisdom Literature (1960) · Pope at archive.org
Summary: This page gathers what modern critical study — literary, historical, philological, and comparative — makes of the book and the figure of Job, as a survey of the works cited. Four things recur in them. First, the composition: the book is read as joining an old prose folktale frame (Job 1–2; 42:7–17 — the patient Job of legend) to a later, more radical poetic core (the dialogues — the protesting Job), with the Elihu speeches (chs. 32–37) often taken as a still-later insertion. Second, the date of the book: proposals run from the Solomonic era to the post-exilic period, with the sixth–fourth century BC most common for the poem — kept distinct from the era of the figure. Third, provenance: an Edomite-wisdom setting (Uz linked to Edom; the friends from Teman and the south) against an Aramean/Hauran locale, with Job read as non-Israelite wisdom literature alongside the Mesopotamian “righteous sufferer” texts. Fourth, the figure: in these works Job is read as a legendary, paradigmatic Gentile righteous man of remote antiquity — a traditional figure, like the Noah and Daniel beside whom Ezekiel ranks him — rather than a documented historical individual. Whether Job is Jobab of Edom has its own interpretation page on the Job timeline.

Claims & evidence

The composition: an old prose frame around a radical poetic core

Modern literary study, in the commentaries cited here, reads the book of Job as composite. It falls into a prose frame — the prologue of the wager and the losses (Job 1–2) and the epilogue of the LORD’s rebuke of the friends and the doubled restoration (42:7–17) — wrapped around a long poetic core, the dialogues with the three friends, the hymn to wisdom (ch. 28), Job’s final oath, the Elihu speeches, and the whirlwind answer. On this reading the two present two different heroes. The prose Job is the proverbially patient saint who “in all this… sinned not, nor charged God foolishly” (Job 1:22) — already a fixed byword by the time Ezekiel could rank “Job” with Noah and Daniel (Ezekiel 14:14). The poetic Job is the bitter, protesting figure who arraigns God and demands a hearing. The inference drawn in these works is that the poet took up a much older, independent folktale about a patient sufferer — the legend the prologue and epilogue preserve — and set within it a radical poem that all but overturns its piety, the frame’s serene restoration sitting uneasily atop the poem’s unresolved protest. Two further seams are noted: the Elihu speeches (chs. 32–37), which introduce a speaker unannounced in the prologue and unmentioned in the epilogue and are judged a secondary insertion; and the disordered third cycle of speeches (chs. 24–27), where Job seems to voice the friends’ doctrine, often taken as textual disturbance. The book’s integrity is itself a question these works discuss (Pope’s introduction treats it under “Integrity”).1

The date of the book: kept distinct from the era of the man

When the book was written is a separate question from when its hero is imagined to have lived, and the works cited distinguish the two. The narrative sets Job in a patriarchal-looking antiquity — he offers his own sacrifices as a family priest, his wealth is reckoned in flocks and servants, and no Israel, no Law, and no Temple appear — and the works cited (Pope, Clines) read that setting as archaizing, a feature of the story, not as evidence for the composition date. On that, proposals have ranged widely: some have placed the poem in the Solomonic age of flourishing wisdom, others across the monarchy, and the date most often proposed in the works cited, on linguistic and theological grounds, is the exilic or post-exilic period, the sixth to fourth century BC — argued from its Aramaic-flavoured Hebrew, its developed treatment of the Satan, and its engagement with the problem of innocent suffering after the catastrophe of the Exile. In these works the prose frame is reckoned older than the poem it now encloses (the folktale the poet inherited), and the book is read as a layered growth rather than a single author’s single act. On this view a date for the book is a date for the tradition’s canonical literary form, not for the man.2

Provenance: Edomite wisdom, a Hauran locale, and the Mesopotamian sufferers

The book carries a non-Israelite, southern/eastern colouring, and the works cited read it as part of an international wisdom tradition. The land of Uz is elsewhere linked to Edom — Lamentations addresses “O daughter of Edom, that dwellest in the land of Uz” (Lamentations 4:21) — and Job’s friends come from the same southern arc: Eliphaz is a Temanite (Teman being a district of Edom famed for its wise men, Jeremiah 49:7), Bildad a Shuhite, Zophar a Naamathite. On this reading Job is Edomite wisdom literature, or Israelite wisdom draped in an Edomite setting — a tradition that credited Edom and “the men of the east” with proverbial sagacity. A rival localization, going back to Wetzstein and Delitzsch, places Uz rather in the Hauran east of the Jordan (an Aramean, not Edomite, locale), and the two readings pull Job’s homeland north or south of the desert accordingly. On either localization the setting is foreign to Israel, and comparative study sets beside it the Mesopotamian “righteous sufferer” texts as Job’s closest ancient parallels: the Babylonian Ludlul bēl nēmeqi (“I will praise the Lord of wisdom,” the Poem of the Righteous Sufferer), in which a pious man struck by inexplicable disease and abandonment finally is restored by Marduk; the Babylonian Theodicy, a dialogue between a sufferer and a friend over divine justice that formally parallels Job’s debate; and older Sumerian and Egyptian “sufferer” and “pessimism” compositions. Comparative study reads Job alongside these texts while noting its differences from them (its monotheism, its treatment of theodicy), within an ancient Near-Eastern literature on the suffering of the innocent.3

The figure: a legendary, paradigmatic Gentile righteous man

The fourth strand is the reading of Job the man in these works. On this view he is neither a documented historical individual nor simply an author’s free invention, but a traditional, legendary figure of remote antiquity — a Gentile paragon of righteousness whom Israel inherited and interpreted. They point to Ezekiel, who names Job in a triad of ancient righteous men, “Noah, Daniel, and Job” (Ezekiel 14:14, 20), whose merit could deliver only themselves. Noah is the flood-hero of primeval antiquity, and John Day argues that the “Daniel” here is not the sixth-century exile of the book of Daniel (a contemporary of Ezekiel) but Dan’el, the legendary righteous king of the Ugaritic Aqhat epic, renowned for judging the cause of the widow and the fatherless — a figure of the same non-Israelite, legendary stratum as Job. On this reading Ezekiel ranks Job among storied paragons of the nations, and the absence of genealogy, era and Israelite lineage is a feature of the tradition about a paradigmatic righteous Gentile of legend.4

Placement on the timeline

In brief, the works surveyed here bear on the timeline’s questions as follows:

  • the literary proposal — a patient legendary Job of the prose frame against a protesting Job of the poem — which bears on the historical man or didactic parable? question;
  • the distinction between the date of the book (a literary matter, most often placed 6th–4th c. BC) and the era of the figure, which keeps the timeline’s “when did Job live?” question about the man, not the composition;
  • the provenance proposals — Uz as Edom (Lamentations 4:21; the southern friends) versus the Hauran localization, and the Mesopotamian “righteous sufferer” parallels — that bear on “where was Uz?” and on whether Job is Jobab of Edom;
  • the reading of Job as a legendary paragon in the Ezekiel triad, alongside Noah and the Ugaritic Dan’el, which frames the historicity question as one about a traditional figure.
