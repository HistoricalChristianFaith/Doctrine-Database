Ishodad of Merv on Who Was Job?

Dates: c. 850 (the Commentary on Job) · Wikipedia
Summary: The East Syriac bishop Ishodad preserves the rationalizing Antiochene reading of Job he inherits from “the Interpreter” (Theodore of Mopsuestia): Job lived before the Law, known to God by nature; the book may be a poetic composition rather than a historical record; the heavenly council where Satan speaks “never took place in reality” but is edifying narrative; and Job’s lament is licit grief, not blasphemy. He is the timeline’s clearest witness that the “did Job exist / when / who wrote the book” questions were live in the Christian East, not only among the rabbis.

Claims & evidence

Job lived before the Law, known to God by nature

Answering why Moses never names Job, Ishodad places him in the pre-Mosaic age of natural knowledge of God — the same patriarchal horizon the Septuagint appendix had reached by a different road:

If it is true that Moses knew Job, why did he never mention him in any of his works? It is evident that [Job] lived before the law, because we know that in every time, in every place and in every person, the knowledge of God exists without the need of a human instruction, as is attested by [Job’s] friends, who composed a high doctrine on God.

— Commentary on Job 42:16, on Job 42:16–17.1

A poem, not a record: the authorship-and-historicity debate

Ishodad reports the sharp disagreement over the book’s nature. “The Interpreter” — the East Syriac title for Theodore of Mopsuestia — held it a poetic composition whose author put speeches into the mouths of Job, his friends, and even God; others held that Moses wrote it and its contents are literally true:

The Interpreter calls it “an imaginary dragon” that the author [of the book of Job] has poetically invented by himself. He has reported many statements in the name of Job, of his friends and in the name of God himself that are not appropriate to them, that appear to be unlikely… On the other hand, those who assert that this book was written by the divine Moses maintain the reality of the Behemoth.

— Commentary on Job 40:10(15), on Job 40:15. This is the Christian counterpart to the rabbinic dispute the wiki records at Bava Batra — whether Job is history or a didactic composition, and whether “Moses wrote… the book of Job.”2

The council in heaven “never took place in reality”

Consistent with that literary reading, Ishodad denies that the prologue’s scene of Satan presenting himself before God happened as narrated — it is a dramatization for the reader’s benefit:

There was never a special meeting where Satan dared to speak, to formulate questions and receive answers—nothing of the sort—but these facts are reported in a narrative style for the edification of the listeners.… They never took place in reality… but [the devil] meditated in his heart, and God, “who searches the hearts and examines the mind,” knew the devil’s malice.

— Commentary on Job 1:6, on Job 1:6. Job’s cursing of his day is likewise defended as licit grief: God “does not expect insensitivity on our part,” only that we “not abandon ourselves to blasphemous words” (Job 3:1–2).3

Notes

  • “The Interpreter” = Theodore of Mopsuestia. In the Church of the East, the Interpreter is the standard honorific for Theodore; where Ishodad cites him he is transmitting the older Antiochene reading, so this ninth-century commentary is also a window onto fifth-century exegesis now largely lost. He likewise cites Evagrius and Henana of Adiabene.
  • No Jobab, no Edom typology. Ishodad discusses when Job lived and who wrote the book, but not the Jobab/Edom genealogy or Job’s ethnicity, and offers no Christ-typology of the figure — his interest is the historical and literary question, not the devotional one.
  • Provenance. Ishodad’s commentaries survive in Syriac (ed. Ceslas Van den Eynde, Commentaire d’Išo‘dad de Merv sur l’Ancien Testament); the English is that of the catena tradition gathered in the Ancient Christian Commentary on Scripture volume on Job, cited to the verse pages where each passage is preserved.
