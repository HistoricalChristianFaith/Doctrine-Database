John Chrysostom on Who Was Job?

Dates: c. 390 (the Commentary on Job, from his Antioch years) · Wikipedia
Summary: Chrysostom’s running Commentary on Job gives the tradition its fullest pastoral portrait of the patient sufferer: Job’s grief is “a sign of victory,” not defeat; the devil, permitted to test him, unwittingly “bears witness” to his virtue; and Job’s wife, urging him to curse God, is the devil’s own instrument — “when the wife of Job speaks, a Devil is at work.” He reads Job as a righteous man who lived before the Law and even glimpsed the resurrection.

Claims & evidence

Job’s grief is a sign of victory, not defeat

Against any suspicion that Job’s mourning gestures betray weakness, Chrysostom insists they display a wisdom that never once slips into an improper word — pairing Job with the weeping Paul:

Do not believe, dear brothers, that Job’s gesture indicates a defeat. It is, above all, a sign of victory… In the same way, Job also deserves to be admired because, in spite of the emotion that pushed him to make that moving gesture, he does not speak a single inappropriate word.

— Commentary on Job 1:20, on Job 1:20–21. Job’s virtue, held before Law or fear of tribunals, is the standing rebuke to those “who assert that human nature is inclined toward evil” (Job 1:1).1

The devil bears witness to Job — and works through his wife

Chrysostom’s Satan is a real but bounded adversary who, in accusing Job, is forced to testify to him: the devil “bore witness to him from above and did not realize that he praised Job as well… the devil praised and covered him with laurels without realizing what he was doing” (Job 1:9–10). And in his homilies Chrysostom fixes the motif the tradition would keep — Job’s wife as the devil’s mouthpiece:

For when indeed the wife of Job speaks, a Devil is at work. For of such sort the advice is.

— Homily 3 on 2 Corinthians, source. In the commentary itself he reads Job’s silence before her “Curse God, and die” as piety, not love of life (Job 3:20–23).2

A righteous man before the Law — who glimpsed the resurrection

Chrysostom reads Job as living before the giving of the Law, discharging a priest’s office himself: on God’s command that Job intercede for his friends he notes, “He would have not ordered that if there had been the law, but now he becomes priest, while they bring offerings” (Job 42:8). And he credits Job with hope of bodily resurrection:

Did Job know the doctrine of resurrection? I believe so, and the doctrine concerning the resurrection of the body… “He strikes,” Job says, “and he heals.”

— Commentary on Job 19:25–26, on Job 19:26.3

Notes

  • Witness to the patience strand, not the identity debate. Chrysostom treats Job simply as a real, historical righteous man and never raises his existence as a question; he says nothing of Uz, Edom, or the Jobab identification. His weight falls on the moral portrait — the patient sufferer and his contest with the devil — that James’s “patience of Job” had already made proverbial.
  • Two works, one figure. The strongest “wife = the devil’s tool” formulation is from Chrysostom’s Homilies (here On 2 Corinthians), where he contrasts Job’s wife with the faithful obedience of Abraham; the verse-by-verse readings above are from the Commentary on Job.
