Gregory the Great on Who Was Job?

Dates: c. 540–604 (the Moralia in Job composed c. 578–595) · Wikipedia
Summary: Gregory’s Moralia in Job (“Morals on the Book of Job,” the Expositio in Iob) is a thirty-five-book commentary and the single most influential patristic treatment of Job — the foundation of medieval moral theology in the Latin West. Gregory expounds the book in three senses at once, “first historically, then in an allegorical, and lastly in a moral sense”: he takes Job as a real man of Uz, a Gentile saint who was “good among bad men”; reads the blameless sufferer as a type of Christ (the Head) and of the suffering Church (His Body); casts Job’s three friends as a figure of heretics; and hears in Job’s “I know that my Redeemer liveth” a confession of the resurrection faith. It is where the West’s devotion to the “patience of Job” is systematised.

Claims & evidence

The threefold reading — history, allegory, and morals

Gregory’s governing method is to expound each passage in three senses in turn, a plan he announces at the outset and carries through all thirty-five books: the opening verses of Job “are explained first historically, then in an allegorical, and lastly in a moral sense.” He does not dissolve the history into allegory but keeps both: Job is a real man, and the real man is also a figure. Under the Historical Interpretation he takes the setting as literal fact and reads the “land of Uz” as the measure of Job’s virtue — a Gentile saint holy in the midst of a heathen people:

There was a man in the land of Uz, whose name was Job. It is for this reason that we are told where the holy man dwelt, that the meritoriousness of his virtue might be expressed; for who knows not that Uz is a land of the Gentiles?… Let us be told then where he dwelt, that this circumstance may be reckoned to his praise, that he was good among bad men; for it is no very great praise to be good in company with the good, but to be good with the bad.

— Moralia in Job, Book I, on Job 1:1. On the same verse Gregory likens the sacred writer’s praise of Job’s soul to a narrator describing an athlete’s limbs before a wrestling-match: “because our athlete was about to combat the devil,” Scripture first “describes the members of the soul… that… we may already prognosticate also the victory to follow.”1

Job as a type of Christ — and of the suffering Church

The heart of the Moralia’s allegory is that the blameless sufferer prefigures the sinless suffering Christ, and that in his body of members he prefigures the Church. Gregory turns the etymologies of the names to this end — “Job is interpreted, ‘a mourner,’ and Uz ‘a counsellor’” — and reads the man of grief as Him “of Whom the Prophet speaks, saying, Surely He hath borne our griefs”:

Whom else then does the blessed Job express by his name, saving Him, of Whom the Prophet speaks, saying, Surely He hath borne our griefs? He dwells in the land of Uz, in that He rules the hearts of a people of wise counsels; for Paul saith, that Christ is the Wisdom of God and the Power of God.

Gregory is explicit that the one figure carries both Christ and the Church — “both the Head and the Body, i.e. both Christ and His Church, were represented by him.” Having shown “how our Head may be taken to be represented,” he passes to “how His Body, which we are, is set forth,” so that each elect soul, grieving its way “from things present to things eternal,” is a little Job in turn.

— Moralia in Job, Book I, on Job 1:1.2

Job’s three friends as a figure of heretics

The counterpart of the Christ-and-Church reading is Gregory’s treatment of Eliphaz, Bildad, and Zophar. They come with a good purpose, yet because they “fall away into sin by speaking without discretion” they “bear the likeness of heretics” who assail the Church under a friend’s guise — the pride of false teaching set against the humility of the saints:

In the Preface to this work we said that the friends of blessed Job, though they come together to him with a good purpose, yet do for this reason bear the likeness of heretics, in that they fall away into sin by speaking without discretion… Thus Holy Church, which is set in the midst of tribulation all this time of her pilgrimage… has other enemies of Christ besides to bear with, under Christ’s name. For to the increasing of her grief, heretics also meet together in dispute and strife, and they pierce her with unreasonable words like as with a kind of dart.

— Moralia in Job, Book III, on Job 2:11. Gregory presses the reading through the friends’ very names and homelands (“they came every one from his own place… the place of the wicked is pride”), making the debate of the book a drama of the Church beset by heresy.3

“I know that my Redeemer liveth” — the Gentile’s resurrection faith

Gregory reads Job’s cry in Job 19:25 as an explicit confession of the crucified and risen Christ and of the believer’s own resurrection — the more remarkable, he insists, for coming from “a believing person set in the midst of the Gentile world”:

For he who does not say, ‘Creator,’ but ‘Redeemer,’ expressly tells of Him, Who after He created all things, appeared Incarnate amongst us, that He might redeem us… The unbelievers may know that He was scourged, mocked… crucified, dead: I, with sure faith, believe Him to live after death; I confess with unreserved voice, ‘that my Redeemer liveth,’ Who died by the hands of wicked men.

From Christ’s resurrection Job is made to draw the hope of his own — “the resurrection, which He exhibited in Himself, He pledged to us; seeing that the members follow the glory of their Head” — and Gregory closes by shaming the doubter: let him “blush at the words of a believing person set in the midst of the Gentile world.”

— Moralia in Job, Book XIV, on Job 19:25.4

The pattern of patience — “Naked came I out of my mother’s womb”

Everywhere the Moralia holds Job up as the schoolbook of patience James had already made proverbial. On the loss of everything, Gregory shows the sufferer “enthroned” on the counsels of the heart even while he lies “prostrate on the earth with his clothes rent,” disarming grief by recalling that he once had nothing:

Naked came I out of my mother’s womb, and naked shall I return thither. As though he said, ‘Naked did the earth bear me, when I came upon this scene, naked it will receive me back, when I depart hence. I then who have lost what I had indeed given me, but what must yet have been abandoned, what have I parted with that was my own?’

— Moralia in Job, Book II, on Job 1:21. It is this sustained portrait — the innocent sufferer who keeps faith through undeserved loss — that made the Latin West’s “patience of Job” devotion, and gave the medieval liturgy of the dead its readings from the book.5

Notes

  • The work and its scale. The Moralia in Job runs to thirty-five books and expounds the whole book of Job verse by verse. Gregory began it while serving as papal apocrisiarius (envoy) at Constantinople, delivering it first as spoken conferences to the monks who had accompanied him, and revised it over some fifteen years (c. 578–595), completing it after he became pope in 590. He dedicated the finished work to his friend Leander, bishop of Seville, in whose company the project had begun.
  • History and allegory, not either/or. Unlike the rationalizing Antiochenes (Theodore of Mopsuestia, transmitted by Ishodad of Merv) or the strictly literal Julian of Eclanum, Gregory neither reduces Job to a poem nor confines him to the letter: he affirms the history and builds the allegory upon it. His christological typology of the figure is the opposite pole to Julian’s refusal of any Christ-typology, and it is the fullest Latin development of the type-of-Christ reading the Syriac Ephrem tradition had already reached.
  • Job’s era. Like the other Fathers on this timeline, Gregory assumes a real, ancient Job — a Gentile “good among bad men” in the land of Uz — and is uninterested in the Jobab/Edom genealogy of the Septuagint appendix; his concern is the moral and christological sense, not the placing of the man in a king-list.
  • Provenance. The English is the public-domain Library of Fathers translation (Morals on the Book of Job, 3 vols., Oxford: John Henry Parker, 1844–1850), cited by book and by the verse each passage expounds.
