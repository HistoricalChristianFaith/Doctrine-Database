Is Job of Uz the same man as Jobab, king of Edom (Genesis 36:33)?

Disputed reading · on Who Was Job? · witness: the Septuagint appendix to Job (Job 42:17b–e)

The passage

And Job died, an old man and full of days: and it is written that he will rise again with those whom the Lord raises up. This man is described in the Syriac book as living in the land of Ausis, on the borders of Idumea and Arabia: and his name before was Jobab; and having taken an Arabian wife, he begot a son whose name was Ennon. And he himself was the son of his father Zare, one of the sons of Esau, and of his mother Bosorrha, so that he was the fifth from Abraam.

And these were the kings who reigned in Edom, which country he also ruled over: first, Balac, the son of Beor, and the name of his city was Dennaba: but after Balac, Jobab, who is called Job, and after him Asom, who was governor out of the country of Thaeman: and after him Adad, the son of Barad, who destroyed Madiam in the plain of Moab; and the name of his city was Gethaim. And his friends who came to him were Eliphaz, of the children of Esau, king of the Thaemanites, Baldad sovereign of the Sauchaeans, Sophar king of the Minaeans.

— The Septuagint (Brenton), Job 42:17a–e, source; set against the Hebrew king-list, “And Bela died, and Jobab the son of Zerah of Bozrah reigned in his stead” (Genesis 36:33).1

The question

The Hebrew book introduces its hero with a homeland but no genealogy, era or office: “There was a man in the land of Uz, whose name was Job” (Job 1:1). The Old Greek closes with a paragraph absent from the Hebrew that identifies him with Jobab son of Zerah, the second king in the Edomite list of Genesis 36:31–35. At issue is whether that equation preserves a tradition about one man, or joins two figures by interpretation of Genesis 36; it bears on how the earliest readers placed Job in genealogy, era and homeland. Annette Yoshiko Reed (2001) studies the appendix as a deliberate re-contextualising of Job within Israelite and Edomite history rather than a stray scribal note.2 The holders of the identification located for this page are ancient; no modern scholar defending it was found in the sources surveyed here.

Job and Jobab are one man, an Edomite king of Esau’s line

On this reading the man of Uz and the second king of Edom are one person, and the Greek appendix transmits a written tradition about who he was.

Held by: Aristeas the Exegete (Hellenistic-Jewish historian, Concerning the Jews, c. 2nd century BC); the Old Greek appendix to Job (Job 42:17b–e, c. 2nd–1st century BC); the Testament of Job (Greek Jewish, c. 1st century BC–1st century AD).3

An early, written, and repeated tradition. Aristeas, excerpted by Alexander Polyhistor and preserved by Eusebius, says that Esau “begat Job,” who dwelt in Uz “on the borders of Idumaea and Arabia” and “was at first called Jobab.” The Greek additions to Job agree with him “almost word for word”; Jakob Freudenthal held that their author used Aristeas, while Kaufmann Kohler and Paul Wendland thought the reverse possibly more likely. The appendix itself names its authority, “the Syriac book,” presenting the identification as drawn from an older written source. The Testament of Job then has Job say, “before the Lord had named me Job, I was called Jobab… For I am of the sons of Esau.”4

Grounds in Genesis 36. According to Kohler and Wendland, Aristeas based the identification on the names Job shares with the Edomite chapter: Eliphaz recurs among the sons of Esau (Genesis 36:10–11), the appellation “Temanite” (Job 2:11) appears in Genesis 36:11 and 34, Uz is suggested by Genesis 36:28, and Zophar appears in the Greek of Genesis 36:11 and 15. In the Greek the two names are nearly one word — Iōb and Iōbab — so that “Jobab, who is called Job” reads as the longer and shorter forms of a single name.5

A setting that fits the book. A king who reigned in Edom “before there reigned any king over the children of Israel,” a grandson of Esau “fifth from Abraam,” belongs to the patriarchal age and to Edomite country. That matches the book’s own cues: Lamentations’ “daughter of Edom, that dwellest in the land of Uz,” Eliphaz the Temanite, Job’s long life and his sacrifices as his own family’s priest. The identification supplies in one move a who, a when and a where that cohere with the book’s setting.6

Job and Jobab are two figures, joined by early interpretation of Genesis 36

On this reading the equation is an early interpretive harmonisation: readers matched the hero of Job, who has no genealogy, to the one Edomite register Scripture supplies, through the names the two texts share.

Held by: Kaufmann Kohler and Paul Wendland (Jewish Encyclopedia, “Aristeas the Historian,” 1901–1906).7

The shared names are borrowings. Kohler and Wendland grant that Aristeas built the identification on Eliphaz, Teman, Uz and Zophar, but hold that “the author of Job simply borrowed the names from Genesis”: the overlap ties the book to Edomite tradition, not Job to the king Jobab. In Hebrew the hero is ’Iyyob (אִיּוֹב) and the king Yobab (יוֹבָב), names of different roots (’-y-b and y-b-b); their likeness belongs to the Greek transcription.8

A tradition appended to the book. The paragraph is absent from the Hebrew, the Peshitta, the Qumran Job Targum (11QtgJob) and the Rabbinic Targum, and it introduces its contents with a formula found nowhere else in the Greek Job: “This man is described in the Syriac book.” It reads as a tradition about Job, attached at the close of the translation.9

Two records with different contents. The Hebrew book never calls Job a king or links him to Edom’s monarchy; the Jobab of Genesis 36:33 is a succession entry with a father and a city and no narrative. The appendix’s genealogy is built from that entry: it gives “his mother Bosorrha” where Genesis gives Bozrah as Jobab’s city, a place-name taken for the mother’s name in Freudenthal’s and the Jewish Encyclopedia’s account; it calls Zare “one of the sons of Esau,” where Genesis 36:13 lists a Zerah as a son of Reuel, Esau’s son; and it grafts Jobab onto Esau’s line, where the Genesis list gives most of the kings of Edom different cities.10 Read this way, the appendix, Aristeas and the Testament are witnesses to how Second-Temple readers placed Job: an Edomite Gentile of the patriarchal age, descended from Esau, living on the Edom–Arabia frontier. The equation with Jobab is the means by which they placed him there.
