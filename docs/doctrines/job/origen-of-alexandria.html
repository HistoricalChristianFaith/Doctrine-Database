Origen of Alexandria on Who Was Job?

Dates: c. 245 (the Fragments on Job) · Wikipedia
Summary: The earliest Christian reader on this timeline, Origen leaves the questions of Job’s land and lineage untouched and fixes instead on the drama of his endurance: Job is an athlete in the stadium who wrestles the devil and, holding out to the end, wins his crown. In one striking fragment God himself recounts the wager back to the victorious sufferer — the earliest Christian statement of the “Job the champion of patience” image that the whole later devotion would carry.

Claims & evidence

Job is an athlete contending in the stadium for a crown

Origen reads Job’s cry for a hearing as the shout of a wrestler mid-contest — one who cannot be crowned until the fight is done:

“I will cry out, and there is nowhere judgment; I still contend.” This is as if Job had said, “I cried out like an athlete in the stadium, but my judgment is nowhere there. Indeed, I still fight. But if I do not bring my fight to a close, I will not get my crown.”

— Fragments on Job 13.15, 29, on Job 19:7. The athletic metaphor — suffering as a bout in the arena whose reward waits at the finish — is Origen’s governing image for the man, and it recurs across the fragments.1

God recounts the wager back to the victorious athlete

Commenting on the whirlwind speeches, Origen re-voices the heavenly wager as words spoken by God to Job at the last — the slanderer’s challenge quoted back to the man who defeated it:

A slanderer conspired against you. Sometimes he said to me, “Stretch out your hand now.” He said to me, “Touch all that he has,” and he added about you that “you would have cursed me to my face.” But, on the contrary, you were victorious. Therefore I say to you, “Clothe yourself with glory. Abase the slanderer completely. Humiliate his angels.”

— Fragments on Job 28.21, on Job 40:10–11. The reward is reserved to the one who fought bravely: “The perfect rewards of the struggles are reserved after this life to those who fought bravely… For this reason, Job faced the hardest fights; while losing his riches, he praised him who gave him these afflictions. After losing his children, he glorified him who had taken them away.”2

The ordeal purifies him — gold refined in the furnace

Where the friends see punishment for sin, Origen grants only that Job’s afflictions refine him, turning even Eliphaz’s mistaken rebuke to a “correct dogma”:

…so that he will be purified through the endurance of his afflictions. Once he is purified, he will be delivered from any extraneous element and freed from any involvement with iniquity. And like gold refined in a melting pot, he will appear to be tested.

— Fragments on Job 16.61, 69, on Job 22:22–25. Endurance, not innocence alone, is what the trial displays and perfects — the same accent the Alexandrian tradition after him (Didymus, and in a different key Chrysostom) would develop.3

Notes

  • Nothing on placement. The surviving Job fragments say nothing of Uz, Edom, Job’s era, his nationality, or the Septuagint’s Job-is-Jobab identification. Origen is a witness to the patient-athlete strand, not to the identity/historicity debate the timeline also tracks.
  • The adversary behind the beasts. In the Leviathan chapters (Job 41) Origen reads the dragon as the devil with his heretical “limbs” — heresiological exegesis that stands behind, but is distinct from, the athlete-versus-Satan reading of the man himself.
  • Transmission. Origen’s continuous commentary on Job is lost; what survives are catena excerpts (the “Fragments on Job”) preserved by later Greek compilers and gathered in modern collections such as the Ancient Christian Commentary on Scripture volume on Job. The quotations above are cited to the verse pages where each fragment is preserved.
