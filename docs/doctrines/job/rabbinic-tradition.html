The rabbinic debate over Job (b. Bava Batra 14b–16b)

Dates: the classical rabbinic stratum — opinions of tanna’im and amora’im c. 2nd–4th century AD, gathered in the Babylonian Talmud (redacted c. AD 500–600) · Wikipedia
Summary: The great discussion of Job at the foot of Bava Batra 14b–16b is the classic rabbinic reckoning with the man of Uz, and it is remarkable chiefly for how little it settles. In one place a sage declares flatly that “Job never existed and was never created… his story was a parable”; in another the rabbis line up a dozen incompatible datings for him, spanning from the patriarchs to the return from Babylon; a baraita lists him among the Gentile prophets while other tanna’im insist he was a Jew; and a separate tradition names Moses as the author of the book that bears his name. The sugya thus stages, in miniature and centuries early, every question the later tradition would spend itself on: did Job exist, when did he live, what was he (Jew or Gentile), and who wrote his book — and it answers each with a dispute rather than a verdict.

Claims & evidence

Did Job exist — or is he a parable?

The most quoted line of the whole sugya is a denial of Job’s very existence. In the give-and-take over when Job lived, the Gemara records a sage who pressed the question to its root and answered it in the negative — only to be rebutted from the opening verse of the book:

The Gemara relates that one of the Sages sat before Rabbi Shmuel bar Naḥmani and he sat and said: Job never existed and was never created; there was never such a person as Job. Rather, his story was a parable. Rabbi Shmuel bar Naḥmani said to him: In rebuttal to you, the verse states: “There was a man in the Land of Utz whose name was Job” (Job 1:1), which indicates that such a man did indeed exist.

— b. Bava Batra 15a (William Davidson Talmud, tr. Adin Steinsaltz), source. The objector will not let the rebuttal stand: if “there was a man” proves the man real, then Nathan’s “there were two men in one city” (2 Samuel 12:3) would prove the rich man and his one little ewe-lamb real too — and everyone grants that was a parable. The Gemara closes the exchange by noting that Nathan’s parable names neither man nor city, whereas Job’s verse gives both a name and a homeland: “Rather, Job was clearly a real person.” The Talmud leaves the majority on the side of a historical Job, but it is careful to preserve the dissent verbatim — the earliest surviving statement of the reading that Job is a didactic fiction, and the seed of the historicity argument that later readers, Jewish and Christian, would take up.1

When did Job live? A dozen incompatible datings

Nowhere is the tradition’s inability to place Job clearer than in the sprawl of opinions the sugya sets side by side. The man of Uz is assigned to almost every era of biblical history in turn, each anchored to a verbal echo between the book of Job and some other passage of Scripture. The Talmud collects them without choosing:

Rabbi Elazar says: Job lived in the days of the judging of the Judges… Rabbi Yehoshua ben Korḥa says: Job lived in the days of Ahasuerus… Rabbi Natan says: Job lived in the days of the kingdom of Sheba… And the Rabbis say: Job lived in the days of the kingdom of the Chaldeans in the time of Nebuchadnezzar… And some say that Job lived in the days of Jacob and that he married Dina, the daughter of Jacob.

— b. Bava Batra 15b (William Davidson Talmud, tr. Adin Steinsaltz), source. To these the preceding page adds still more: Rabbi Levi bar Laḥma holds that “Job lived in the time of Moses,” reasoning from the rare word eifo shared by Job 19:23 and Exodus 33:16; Rava counters that “Job lived at the time of the spies whom Moses sent to scout the land of Canaan”; and Rabbi Yoḥanan and Rabbi Elazar say that “Job was among those who ascended from the exile… and his house of study was in Tiberias,” placing him at the very start of the Second Temple period — against which the Gemara sets a baraita teaching that “the days of Job’s life extended from when Israel entered Egypt until they left.” From the patriarch Jacob to the Babylonian exile is the better part of fifteen centuries, and the rabbis spread Job across the whole of it. The lesson the sugya itself seems to draw is not any one date but the impossibility of fixing one.2

Was Job a Jew or a Gentile?

Entangled with the question of when is the question of what Job was. A baraita ranks him among the prophets God raised up for the nations — making him, on its face, no Israelite:

Seven prophets prophesied to the nations of the world, and they are: Balaam and his father Beor, and Job, Eliphaz the Temanite, and Bildad the Shuhite, and Zophar the Naamathite, and Elihu ben Barachel the Buzite… There was a certain pious man among the nations of the world and his name was Job, and he came into the world only to receive his reward.

— b. Bava Batra 15b (William Davidson Talmud, tr. Adin Steinsaltz), source. But the Talmud immediately reports the counter-view, and frames the whole matter as unresolved: “The matter of whether or not Job was Jewish is a dispute between tanna’im.” It then argues that most of the datings above require a Jewish Job — for after Moses died, the tradition holds, the Divine Presence no longer rested on the nations of the world (Exodus 33:16), so a prophet of Job’s stature living in Israel’s later history must have been an Israelite — with the single exception of the “some say” view that made Job a contemporary of Jacob who married Dinah. Here the wiki’s two oldest witnesses part company with the rabbis: the Septuagint appendix and the Testament of Job both make Job a Gentile son of Esau, the Edomite king Jobab — a reading the Bavli neither adopts nor mentions, though its “pious man among the nations” baraita preserves the same instinct that Job stood outside Israel.3

Who wrote the book of Job? “Moses wrote… Job”

Distinct from the question of when the man lived is the question of who wrote the book — a literary matter the Talmud settles more confidently than any other, in the great catalogue of biblical authorship that opens just before the Job discussion:

And who wrote the books of the Bible? Moses wrote his own book, i.e., the Torah, and the portion of Balaam in the Torah, and the book of Job.

— b. Bava Batra 14b (William Davidson Talmud, tr. Adin Steinsaltz), source; the same attribution is restated at 15a, where it is offered as support for Rabbi Levi bar Laḥma’s claim that Job was Moses’ contemporary — Moses could write Job’s story because he had lived alongside him. This is a claim about the book’s composition, not about the hero’s lifetime, and the two must be kept apart: one can hold that Moses wrote Job while placing the man Job in the days of the Judges or of Ahasuerus. Ranking Job with “the portion of Balaam” is itself a telling grouping — both are non-Israelite prophetic episodes the tradition credited to Moses’ pen. The question of the book’s real date and authorship (Mosaic, or the far later composition most modern scholars argue for) belongs to the reception and historicity arguments of this timeline, not to the placing of the man.4

Notes

  • Attribution of the “parable” view. The Bavli credits the denial of Job’s existence not to a named master but to “one of the Sages” who sat before Rabbi Shmuel bar Naḥmani (a third-generation Palestinian amora, late 3rd–early 4th century). Later tradition and some modern retellings attach the saying to Resh Lakish (Rabbi Shimon ben Lakish); the text as transmitted in Bava Batra keeps the speaker anonymous, and this page follows the primary text.
  • “When” vs. “who wrote.” The sugya interleaves two different questions — the era of the man and the authorship of the book — because one of its datings (Rabbi Levi bar Laḥma’s “time of Moses”) leans on the authorship tradition for support. On this timeline the two are treated separately: the man’s era is weighed with the datings above (and on the era argument page), while Mosaic authorship belongs to the historicity/reception discussion.
  • Relation to the timeline. This rabbinic stratum sits after the two Second-Temple witnesses — the Septuagint appendix and the Testament of Job — and runs parallel in time with the earliest Christian readers of Job (Origen, the Antiochenes). Its historicity dispute (“Job never existed… a parable”) and its scatter of datings feed directly into the argument pages of the Who Was Job? timeline: was Job historical or a parable? and when did he live? Whether Job really is Jobab of Edom, and where Uz lay, are weighed there rather than settled here.
