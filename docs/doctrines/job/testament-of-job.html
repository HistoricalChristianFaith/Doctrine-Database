The Testament of Job

Dates: a Greek Jewish pseudepigraphon, c. 1st century BC–1st century AD · Wikipedia
Summary: A Hellenistic-Jewish expansion of the book of Job, cast as the dying Job’s deathbed testament to his children. It inherits the Septuagint’s Job = Jobab, king-in-Edom tradition and develops it into something new: Job is a king who smashes an idol-temple after being warned it belongs to Satan, and his sufferings become a conscious, sustained duel with Satan that Job wins through endurance (hypomonē) — the very virtue James 5:11 names. The book closes with his three daughters receiving heavenly cords that let them sing in the languages of angels. It is the fullest early portrait of Job as the model of patient endurance and the key text for his afterlife in Jewish and Christian devotion.

Claims & evidence

Job is Jobab, of the sons of Esau, in the land of Ausitis (Uz)

The book opens with a Job near death gathering his children to tell them his story, and it takes up exactly the identification the Septuagint appendix had already made — that the sufferer’s former name was Jobab and that he belonged to the line of Esau in the Edom–Arabia borderland:

I was a very rich man living in the East in the land Ausitis, (Utz) and before the Lord had named me Job, I was called Jobab… For I am of the sons of Esau. My brother is Nahor, and your mother is Dinah.

— Testament of Job (tr. M. R. James, 1897), source. “Ausitis” is the Greek land of Uz, and “Jobab” the pre-conversion name — the same two moves the Old Greek made at Job 42:17b–e. The Testament then develops the family haggadically: Job’s first wife Sitis (Sitidos) shares his sufferings and dies; the children he here addresses are those of a second marriage to Dinah, made a daughter of Jacob — a detail with no warrant in the canonical book.1

Job the king smashes the idol-temple, forewarned it belongs to Satan

The Testament’s boldest innovation is why Job suffers. Where the canonical book grounds the trial in the heavenly wager over Job’s disinterested righteousness, the Testament makes Job an anti-idolatry hero in the mould of Abraham: puzzling over whether the idol his countrymen worship is truly God, he is answered by an angel who identifies the idol as Satan’s and warns that to destroy it is to invite Satan’s war — but promises victory and reward to the one who endures it:

If thou undertakest to destroy and takest away the image of Satan, he will set himself with wrath to wage war against thee… Then thou must wrestle like an athlete and resist pain, sure of thy reward, overcome trials and afflictions… And thou shalt put on a crown of amarant.

— Testament of Job (tr. M. R. James, 1897), source. Knowing in advance what it will cost him, Job accepts — “I shall from love of God endure until death all that will come upon me, and I shall not shrink back” — and then acts:

After this I rose up in the night and took fifty slaves and went to the temple of the idol and destroyed it to the ground.

— Testament of Job (tr. M. R. James, 1897), source. The whole disaster that follows is thus recast as Satan’s retaliation for this raid on his cult — Job is a chosen combatant who steps into the ring with open eyes, not the unwitting object of a bet.2

A conscious duel with Satan, won by endurance

Because Job knows his adversary, the Testament turns the story into a sustained, open contest between the two — framed throughout in the language of the athletic arena, with patience as Job’s weapon. Satan assails him in disguise (once as a beggar to whom Job’s doorkeeper, ashamed of the burnt loaf Job orders given him, offers her own bread), strips him of everything, and afflicts his body with worms — and Job endures it all until Satan himself concedes the match:

Behold, Job, I yield and give way to thee who art but flesh while I am a spirit.

— Testament of Job (tr. M. R. James, 1897), source. This is the pivot on which the whole later “patience of Job” devotion turns: the contest that in the canonical book is God’s and hidden from Job is here Job’s own, and the victory is won by hypomonē — the endurance that James 5:11 holds up (“ye have heard of the patience of Job”). The Testament is the fullest early witness to Job as the model of that virtue.3

The daughters’ heavenly cords and the songs of angels

At the end, restored, Job divides his goods among his seven sons but gives his three daughters — Hemera (“Day”), Kasia, and Amalthea’s Horn — a stranger legacy: three shimmering cords or girdles, which he says are worth more than any earthly portion:

For they were not earthly work, but celestial sparks of light flashed through them like the rays of the sun… Not only have you here sufficient to live on, but these bring you into a better world to live in, in the heavens.

— Testament of Job (tr. M. R. James, 1897), source. As each daughter puts on her cord she takes on “another heart,” no longer minding earthly things, and begins to sing in a heavenly tongue — the first in the dialect of the angels, the second “the dialect of the heavenly rulers (Archonts),” the third “the dialect of the Cherubim” —

they took these, and sang, and played on the psaltery and praised and glorified God in the holy dialect.

— Testament of Job (tr. M. R. James, 1897), source. This visionary climax — a woman’s inheritance that is spiritual, protective, and ecstatic rather than material — is the reason the Testament has loomed so large in the study of Job’s afterlife in devotion and in early Jewish mysticism.4

Notes

  • Date and provenance. The Testament is a Greek composition, most likely of Egyptian-Jewish origin, dated by scholars to somewhere between the 1st century BC and the 1st century AD; some have connected its ascetic and mystical tone (especially the daughters’ ecstatic hymns) with contemplative circles like the Therapeutae Philo describes. The closing daughters-and-cords section (conventional chapters 46–53) is sometimes judged a later or Christian-touched layer, but it is well woven into the whole.5
  • Chapter numbering. The full text linked here is M. R. James’s 1897 edition, which prints the work in twelve sections; the standard scholarly numbering (K. Kohler; R. P. Spittler in Charlesworth’s Old Testament Pseudepigrapha) runs to fifty-three chapters. On that conventional numbering the idol-temple and angel episode is chapters 2–5, Satan’s concession is chapter 27, and the daughters’ cords are chapters 46–50.
  • Relation to the timeline. The Testament sits immediately after the Septuagint appendix, whose Job = Jobab, land-of-Ausitis, son-of-Esau tradition it presupposes and expands. Its endurance-portrait is the seed of the “patience of Job” that James 5:11 already treats as a byword and that the later Fathers — above all Gregory the Great’s Moralia — would make the schoolbook of Christian patience. Whether Job really is Jobab of Edom, when he lived, and where Uz lay are weighed on the argument pages of the Who Was Job? timeline, not settled here.
