Tertullian on Who Was Job?

Dates: c. 200 (De patientia / Of Patience, ch. XIV) · Wikipedia
Summary: The earliest Christian voice on this timeline. In the crowning chapter of his treatise On Patience, the Latin apologist holds up Job as the supreme human example of endurance kept “in spirit as in flesh” — a “hero” and “instrument of God’s victory” who answered every blow with thanks to God and was rewarded “in redoubled measure.” Like Origen after him, Tertullian says nothing of Job’s land, lineage, or era; he is a witness to the patient-Job strand that James 5:11 had already made proverbial, and the first Christian to enroll the man of Uz as a model of Christian suffering.

Claims & evidence

Job as the exemplar of patience “in spirit as in flesh”

Tertullian’s De patientia closes (ch. XIV) by parading the saints of old who showed patience under violence — Isaiah sawn asunder, Stephen stoned and praying for his killers — and reserves its fullest praise for Job, whom he never names but unmistakably describes as the man tested in body and goods together:

Oh, happy also he who met all the violence of the devil by the exertion of every species of patience!—whom neither the driving away of his cattle nor those riches of his in sheep, nor the sweeping away of his children in one swoop of ruin, nor, finally, the agony of his own body in (one universal) wound, estranged from the patience and the faith which he had plighted to the Lord; whom the devil smote with all his might in vain. For by all his pains he was not drawn away from his reverence for God; but he has been set up as an example and testimony to us, for the thorough accomplishment of patience as well in spirit as in flesh, as well in mind as in body; in order that we succumb neither to damages of our worldly goods, nor to losses of those who are dearest, nor even to bodily afflictions.

— Of Patience, ch. XIV. The distinctive Tertullianic accent is twofold patience — of mind and of body at once — and Job is the proof that the two can be kept together under the worst the devil can do (Job 1–2).1

The “hero” and “instrument of God’s victory”

He then turns Job’s ordeal into a Roman triumph staged over the devil — a bier, a banner, a victor’s reward — with Job scraping his ulcers in “mighty equanimity” while God looks on and smiles:

What a bier for the devil did God erect in the person of that hero! What a banner did He rear over the enemy of His glory, when, at every bitter message, that man uttered nothing out of his mouth but thanks to God, while he denounced his wife, now quite wearied with ills, and urging him to resort to crooked remedies! How did God smile, how was the evil one cut asunder, while Job with mighty equanimity kept scraping off the unclean overflow of his own ulcer, while he sportively replaced the vermin that brake out thence, in the same caves and feeding-places of his pitted flesh! And so, when all the darts of temptations had blunted themselves against the corslet and shield of his patience, that instrument of God’s victory not only presently recovered from God the soundness of his body, but possessed in redoubled measure what he had lost.

— Of Patience, ch. XIV. The wife who urges “crooked remedies” is already read as the devil’s instrument — the reading Chrysostom and the later Greek homilists would develop — and the doubled restoration (Job 42:10–12) is folded in as the crown of the victorious sufferer.2

Notes

  • Nothing on placement. Tertullian raises none of the identity questions the timeline tracks — not Uz, not Edom, not Job’s era or nationality, not the Septuagint’s Job-is-Jobab equation. He assumes the man’s historicity and draws only a moral: Job is the school of patience.
  • An inference beyond the text. The chapter goes on to say Job “preferred” not to have his lost children restored, expecting to meet them again in the resurrection — a pious extrapolation the man of the story never voices; the standard edition flags it as Tertullian’s inference rather than anything in Scripture. It is noted here only to keep the quotation honest, not as evidence for any timeline claim.
  • The earliest Christian witness. Written c. 200, De patientia predates Origen’s Job fragments by roughly two generations and is the first Christian text on this page to make Job the pattern of endurance — the Latin seed of the devotion the Greek East (Origen, Didymus, Chrysostom) and, monumentally, Gregory’s Moralia would carry.
