Ephrem the Syrian on Who Was Job?

Dates: c. 370 (the Commentary on Job in his name; Ephrem died 373) · Wikipedia
Summary: The Syriac tradition’s earliest sustained reading of Job takes him as a real, long-lived man of Uz whose exact life-span can be reckoned from the doubled restoration — 248 years — and reads the blameless sufferer as a type of the sinless suffering Christ. It supplies the chronology strand (a number, computed from the text) that the Septuagint appendix had opened, though it never names Job Jobab or ties him to Edom.

Claims & evidence

Job lived 248 years — a life-span computed from the doubled restoration

Reading the epilogue’s “the LORD gave Job twice as much as he had before” (Job 42:10–12) as a doubling of his remaining years too, Ephrem reckons Job’s total age — and reconciles the arithmetic with the seven lost years of the ordeal:

Scripture reports God increased by half all of Job’s things, except for the children. It is evident, therefore, that the length of his life was also increased by a half. If he had lived 170 years when he was delivered from that calamity, it appears that he lived for 85 years more to the end of his life… Now, if you add 85 years to the 170 years he had lived before, you have a sum of 255. Actually Scripture declares that Job lived 248 years, therefore those 7 years that are not included in the sum were the years of his temptation. And with good reason they were not counted, because they appear to belong to a period of death more than life.

— Commentary on Job 42:16, on Job 42:16–17. The 248-year total (a Peshitta/Syriac reckoning) matches the patriarchal longevity the Septuagint appendix presupposed in slotting Job among the Edomite kings before Israel had a king — two independent attempts to give the ageless hero a fixed length of days.1

Job the man of Uz who could never be brought to blasphemy

Ephrem takes the frame narrative straightforwardly: Job is the incomparable saint of a real Uz, and the wager is God’s way of forcing the devil to concede the point by the “evidence of the facts”:

So God arranged things in order that the devil, being convinced by the evidence of the facts, might acknowledge that Job, even though he was pressed by so many afflictions, could never be brought to blasphemy.

— Commentary on Job 2:6, on Job 2:6. His endurance is faith in a God who raises the dead: on “though he slay me” Ephrem exclaims, “O admirable man, who, being destined to be killed, praises his killer… He believed that God was able to make alive those who had died” (Job 13:15).2

Job the blameless sufferer as a type of Christ

Ephrem’s most consequential move is typological: the innocent man who suffers without sin prefigures the sinless Christ, and Job’s hope of a Redeemer is read as prophecy of the Incarnation:

“For I know that my Redeemer lives and that at last he will be revealed upon the earth.” Here the blessed Job predicts the future manifestation of Emmanuel in the flesh at the end of time.

— Commentary on Job 19:25, on Job 19:25. Job’s undeserved punishment is likewise read as a figure of “Christ, as if he were guilty of sin, [who] would have suffered resolutely” (Job 9:17), and even his three friends and seven sons are allegorized into the orders of the Jewish and the Christian priesthood (Job 2:11) — a reading that anticipates, three centuries early, the christological Moralia of Gregory the Great.3

Notes

  • Authorship caveat. The Commentary on Job that carries Ephrem’s name survives in catena form, and its attribution is debated — Ephrem’s securely genuine running commentaries are those on Genesis and Exodus, and some of this Job material may be catena or Pseudo-Ephrem. The readings are cited here as the Syriac tradition transmitted under his name, not as certainly his own hand.
  • No Jobab, no Edom. Despite the Syriac setting, this commentary nowhere identifies Job with Jobab king of Edom or discusses his nationality; its contribution to the identity question is only the indirect one of the 248-year life-span, which fits a patriarchal-era dating.
  • Provenance. The quotations are the English of the catena tradition (as gathered in the Ancient Christian Commentary on Scripture volume on Job), cited to the verse pages where each is preserved.
