Julian of Eclanum on Who Was Job?

Dates: c. 425 (the Exposition on the Book of Job) · Wikipedia
Summary: Julian, the exiled Pelagian bishop, reads Job in the literal, historical manner of the Antiochene school he drew on: Job and his friends are real men in a real dispute, argued by “the rules of debating,” and even the monstrous verses are kept to “the faithful understanding of the story” before any spiritual sense is allowed. He is the deliberate foil to the allegorists — Didymus before him and, above all, Gregory the Great after.

Claims & evidence

The book as a real, historical dispute — argued by “the rules of debating”

Julian treats the speeches as an actual forensic argument whose competing claims can both be tested and, rightly qualified, both hold:

Since, according to the rules of debating, it is easier, in general, that they both lie rather than saying the truth. Here, as they speak against each other, it is nonetheless proven that the arguments of both are true… according to Elihu, [God] does not repay everybody; according to Job, he did not repay many.

— Exposition on the Book of Job 34:10–11, on Job 34:10–11. He reads the wager itself as the devil’s calculated escalation: having failed with Job’s goods, “he asserted that Job had now to be tested. The test would focus on Job’s own person” (Job 2:4–5).1

Even the monsters are kept to “the faithful understanding of the story”

Where an allegorist would leap to the mythic, Julian anchors first in the literal creature and admits a spiritual sense only as a subordinate, secondary reading — and even glosses Job’s “giants” as ordinary “wild beasts”:

This is the true description of the beast according to the faithful [understanding] of the story… However, [these words] are also addressed to a plane of higher intellect, so that we may believe through them that something else is signified as well, and therefore the strength of spiritual iniquity may be perceived more easily in the image of the bodies.

— Exposition on the Book of Job 41:25, on Job 41:34 (of Leviathan); on “Behold, giants groan under the waters” he explains that Job calls the sea-beasts “giants” only because of their “tremendous size… which we understand as ‘wild beasts’” (Job 26:5–6).2

Notes

  • The literal pole of the tradition. Julian offers no Christ-typology and no Church-allegory of Job at all — consistent with the Antiochene refusal to allegorize, and the exact counter-position to the christological reading the Syriac and later the Gregorian tradition pressed. That refusal is itself his contribution to how the figure of Job was read.
  • Silent on placement. The surviving exposition begins after the frame’s opening verses and does not treat Uz, Job’s era or nationality, or the Jobab identification; its value here is the method, the literal-historical Job.
  • The proponent. Julian was the leading Pelagian bishop of the West, deposed in 418 and condemned with the Pelagian party; his Job commentary, composed in exile and dependent on Theodore of Mopsuestia’s Antiochene exegesis, is cited here only for its reading of Job, not for the wider Pelagian controversy.
