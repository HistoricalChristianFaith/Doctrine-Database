Eusebius of Caesarea on the Biblical Canon

Dates: c. 324 (Church History 3.25, in its final edition; Eusebius was present at Nicaea in 325; d. c. 339) · Wikipedia
Summary: Eusebius gives the most deliberate early sorting of the books: the accepted (homologoumena), the disputed-but-widely-recognized (antilegomena), and the rejected (notha) — and then, set below the rejected, the gospels and acts he says "are cited by the heretics under the name of the apostles" — "the Gospels of Peter, of Thomas, of Matthias" — which he calls "the fictions of heretics" that "no one belonging to the succession of ecclesiastical writers" has deemed worthy of mention. Eusebius attended the Council of Nicaea in 325.

Claims & evidence

The threefold sorting — accepted, disputed, rejected

Since we are dealing with this subject it is proper to sum up the writings of the New Testament which have been already mentioned. First then must be put the holy quaternion of the Gospels; following them the Acts of the Apostles. … These then belong among the accepted writings. Among the disputed writings, which are nevertheless recognized by many, are extant the so-called epistle of James and that of Jude, also the second epistle of Peter, and those that are called the second and third of John … Among the rejected writings must be reckoned also the Acts of Paul, and the so-called Shepherd, and the Apocalypse of Peter, and in addition to these the extant epistle of Barnabas, and the so-called Teachings of the Apostles …

— Church History 3.25.1–4, trans. A. C. McGiffert (NPNF 2.1), historicalchristian.faith (also at New Advent).1 Eusebius ranks each book by its reception: the homologoumena accepted, the antilegomena disputed yet "recognized by many," the notha ("rejected" or spurious). The passage is cited in the debate over the Council of Nicaea (see below).

The gospels of Peter, Thomas, and Matthias, set below the rejected

But we have nevertheless felt compelled to give a catalogue of these also, distinguishing those works which according to ecclesiastical tradition are true and genuine and commonly accepted, from those others which, although not canonical but disputed, are yet at the same time known to most ecclesiastical writers—we have felt compelled to give this catalogue in order that we might be able to know both these works and those that are cited by the heretics under the name of the apostles, including, for instance, such books as the Gospels of Peter, of Thomas, of Matthias, or of any others besides them, and the Acts of Andrew and John and the other apostles, which no one belonging to the succession of ecclesiastical writers has deemed worthy of mention in his writings.

And further, the character of the style is at variance with apostolic usage, and both the thoughts and the purpose of the things that are related in them are so completely out of accord with true orthodoxy that they clearly show themselves to be the fictions of heretics. Wherefore they are not to be placed even among the rejected writings, but are all of them to be cast aside as absurd and impious.

— Church History 3.25.6–7, historicalchristian.faith (also at New Advent).2 Eusebius names the gospels of Peter, Thomas, and Matthias together and sets them, with the Acts of Andrew and John, in a class beneath the notha, "to be cast aside as absurd and impious." His grounds are reception, style, and content: these are works "no one belonging to the succession of ecclesiastical writers" has mentioned, whose style "is at variance with apostolic usage," and which he calls "the fictions of heretics." He names no council. "The Gospel of Thomas" is named without description of its contents.

Reception and debate

  • The Nicaea question. Eusebius was present at Nicaea in 325, and Church History 3.25 records books still "disputed" in his day. John D. Meade (2020) cites the silence of eyewitness attendees such as Eusebius and Athanasius about any conciliar decision on the canon: "none of the early records from the council, nor eyewitness attendees (Eusebius or Athanasius, for example), mentions any conciliar decision that established the canon."3 The readings are set out at Did the Council of Nicaea (325) decide the contents of the Bible? and Is the Synodicon Vetus’s account (c. 900) of the books sorted by a sign at the altar of Nicaea historical?
  • "Cast aside." Eusebius's terms concern the books' standing and authenticity: they "are not to be placed even among the rejected writings, but are all of them to be cast aside as absurd and impious." Whether the apocryphal gospels were afterwards suppressed is set out at Were the infancy gospels suppressed — banned, voted out, gathered up and burned?
  • The title without contents. Eusebius names "the Gospel of Thomas" without describing it, and brackets it with the gospels of Peter and Matthias as works "cited by the heretics under the name of the apostles." Which work of that name he meant — the sayings gospel or the infancy narrative — the notice does not say.
  • Four classes. Eusebius sets the gospels of Peter, Thomas, and Matthias a step lower than the notha: the Shepherd, Barnabas, and the Apocalypse of Peter are "rejected" yet, in the passage's terms, "known to most ecclesiastical writers"; the gospels of Peter, Thomas, and Matthias "are not to be placed even among the rejected writings."
