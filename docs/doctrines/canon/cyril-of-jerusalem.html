Cyril of Jerusalem on the Biblical Canon

Dates: c. 350 (Catechetical Lecture 4, the baptismal catechesis delivered at Jerusalem c. 348–350; Cyril c. 313–386) · Wikipedia · his Old Testament canon page
Summary: Catechizing the candidates for baptism, Cyril gives an Eastern canon-list and frames the boundary as a rule of reading: study the twenty-two books of the Old Testament and the recognized New, but "have nothing to do with the apocryphal writings" — "those which are not read in the Churches, these read not even by thyself." His New Testament runs to twenty-six books, without Revelation, and he grounds the list in what the Church reads and what "the Apostles, and the bishops of old time" handed down.

Claims & evidence

"Learn also diligently, and from the Church, what are the books"

Learn also diligently, and from the Church, what are the books of the Old Testament, and what those of the New. And, pray, read none of the apocryphal writings: for why dost thou, who knowest not those which are acknowledged among all, trouble thyself in vain about those which are disputed? Read the Divine Scriptures, the twenty-two books of the Old Testament, these that have been translated by the Seventy-two Interpreters.

— Catechetical Lecture 4.33, trans. E. H. Gifford (NPNF 2.7), historicalchristian.faith (also at New Advent).1 The catechumen is to learn the books "from the Church," busy himself with what is "acknowledged among all," and leave the "disputed" alone. The twenty-two-book Old Testament is the Hebrew reckoning (the same count Athanasius and Jerome give), here credited — with the Letter of Aristeas account Cyril retells in §34 — to the Seventy[-two].

"Have nothing to do with the apocryphal writings … read not even by thyself"

Of these read the two and twenty books, but have nothing to do with the apocryphal writings. Study earnestly these only which we read openly in the Church. Far wiser and more pious than thyself were the Apostles, and the bishops of old time, the presidents of the Church who handed down these books. Being therefore a child of the Church, trench thou not upon its statutes.

— Catechetical Lecture 4.35, historicalchristian.faith (also at New Advent).2 Cyril's rule is to read what "we read openly in the Church," because "the Apostles, and the bishops of old time … handed down these books"; he names no council. The apocryphal writings are to be left unread.

The New Testament: "the four Gospels only"

Then of the New Testament there are the four Gospels only, for the rest have false titles and are mischievous. The Manichaeans also wrote a Gospel according to Thomas, which being tinctured with the fragrance of the evangelic title corrupts the souls of the simple sort. Receive also the Acts of the Twelve Apostles; and in addition to these the seven Catholic Epistles of James, Peter, John, and Jude; and as a seal upon them all, and the last work of the disciples, the fourteen Epistles of Paul. But let all the rest be put aside in a secondary rank. And whatever books are not read in Churches, these read not even by thyself, as thou hast heard me say.

— Catechetical Lecture 4.36, historicalchristian.faith (also at New Advent).3 Cyril's New-Testament list runs four Gospels, Acts, seven Catholic Epistles, and fourteen of Paul — twenty-six books, without the Apocalypse; the Laodicean canon 60 gives the same twenty-six, and Athanasius's list of 367 adds Revelation. His "fourteen Epistles of Paul" count the Epistle to the Hebrews with Paul's letters — the same count given by Origen and Athanasius. Of the other gospels he names a single example — a "Gospel according to Thomas" he ascribes to the Manichaeans — the same attribution he makes more fully at Catech. 6.31 (the work of "one of the three wicked disciples of Manes").

Reception and context

  • Cyril and Eusebius. A generation after Eusebius sorted the books into accepted, disputed, and rejected (Church History 3.25, c. 324), Cyril — teaching at Jerusalem, in Eusebius's province — uses comparable categories in catechesis: the "acknowledged among all," the "disputed," and the "apocryphal" one must not read.
  • Not read. Cyril's verbs are of reading: the rest are to be "put aside in a secondary rank" and "read not even by thyself." Compare the later Western lists of books "not to be received" (Innocent I, the Decretum Gelasianum). Whether the apocryphal gospels were afterwards suppressed is set out at Were the infancy gospels suppressed — banned, voted out, gathered up and burned?
  • His "Gospel of Thomas." The "Gospel according to Thomas" Cyril names at 4.36 he ascribes to the Manichaeans, as he does more fully at Catech. 6.31 (the work of "one of the three wicked disciples of Manes"). He gives no contents for it.
  • "Twenty-two books," and the Septuagint. Cyril's Old Testament is the twenty-two-book Hebrew count (folding Ruth into Judges and Lamentations, Baruch, and the Epistle into Jeremiah), which he credits to the seventy-two translators through the Letter of Aristeas story he retells at §34. His Old-Testament list is treated on his Old Testament canon page.
