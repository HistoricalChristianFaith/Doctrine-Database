The Gelasian Decree and the Greek rejection-lists on the Biblical Canon

Dates: c. 500 (the Decretum Gelasianum, a Latin catalogue of the early sixth century; with the Greek List of Sixty Books, c. 650, and the Stichometry of Nicephorus, c. 810) · Wikipedia
Summary: The Decretum Gelasianum, a Latin catalogue dated by Ernst von Dobschütz to the first half of the sixth century, lists some sixty writings "not to be received." Among them it names "the book on the infancy of the saviour" and "the book of the nativity of the saviour and of Mary or the midwife," as entries separate from "the Gospel in the name of Thomas which the Manichaeans use." The Greek List of Sixty Books (probably seventh century) names no Thomas title and no infancy gospel among its apocrypha; the Stichometry of Nicephorus (ninth century) lists a "Gospel of Thomas" of 1,300 lines and a "Circuit of Thomas" among the New-Testament apocrypha, without naming an infancy gospel or the Protoevangelium.

Claims & evidence

The Decretum Gelasianum (c. 500): the infancy and nativity books by name

The Decretum Gelasianum de libris recipiendis et non recipiendis — On the books to be received and not to be received — carries a canon-list (part II) and then, in part V, a catalogue of rejected writings, naming some sixty apocrypha and condemned authors. Each title is followed by the tag "apocryphal" (in Pearse's translation often left in the Latin, apocryphum or apocryphus). The rubric that opens the list reads:

The remaining writings which have been compiled or been recognised by heretics or schismatics the Catholic and Apostolic Roman Church does not in any way receive; of these we have thought it right to cite below a few which have been handed down and which are to be avoided by catholics…

Among the rejected titles stand three that bear directly on the infancy material:

the Gospel in the name of Thomas which the Manichaeans use — apocryphum
…
the book on the infancy of the saviour — apocryphus
…
the book of the nativity of the saviour and of Mary or the midwife — apocryphus

— Decretum Gelasianum V, trans. Roger Pearse (checked against Hennecke–Schneemelcher), from Ernst von Dobschütz's critical edition (Texte und Untersuchungen 38.4, 1912).1 The list closes by declaring that these and similar writings, with those of a long roll of named heresiarchs, are "not merely rejected but eliminated from the whole Roman Catholic and Apostolic Church and with their authors and the followers of its authors to be damned in the inextricable shackles of anathema forever."1 The second of the three infancy-related entries is connected with the Protoevangelium of James, from its midwife-at-the-cave scene: J. K. Elliott (2005), as summarized in the article "Gospel of James," takes that work to have been known to the decree's compiler. Reidar Aasgaard (2009), as summarized in the article "Infancy Gospel of Thomas," counts the decree among the sixth- and seventh-century documents that disapprove of infancy gospels being read, while arguing that the Infancy Gospel of Thomas was likely never explicitly censored by the church.2 How these entries bear on the question whether the infancy gospels were suppressed is set out at Were the infancy gospels suppressed?

Date, authorship and wording of the decree

The decree takes its name from a tradition that it was a decretal of Pope Gelasius I (492–496); some manuscripts instead head it as the work of a Roman council under Damasus. Von Dobschütz concluded that all five chapters belong to one work, "no genuine decree or letter either of Damasus or Gelasius, but a pseudonymous literary production of the first half of the sixth century (between 519 and 553)," as F. C. Burkitt reports in his 1913 review, adding his own judgement that "v. Dobschütz has made out his case." Its first chapter quotes a work Augustine wrote about 416. Von Dobschütz placed its origin in Italy; Wilhelm Schneemelcher and Rosamond McKitterick, as reported in the article "Gelasian Decree," point to southern or Frankish Gaul.3 Faustino Arévalo, C. H. Turner and W. A. Jurgens hold that its canon-chapters preserve the acts of a Roman synod of 382; the readings are set out at Did the Roman synod of 382 under Damasus promulgate the canon-list preserved in the Decretum Gelasianum? The document's title is On the books to be received and not to be received; its rubric says the apocrypha "are to be avoided by catholics" and are not "in any way" received, and its close says they are "eliminated" and their authors anathematized. A century earlier Innocent I (405) had called books under apostolic names damnanda, "to be condemned."

The Greek catalogues (c. 650–810): a "Gospel of Thomas," but not the infancy texts by name

The East kept its own catalogues of books outside the canon. The List of Sixty Books (probably seventh-century, transmitted with the Quaestiones ascribed to Anastasius of Sinai) counts sixty canonical books, then a tier of writings "outside the sixty" (Wisdom, Sirach, 1–4 Maccabees, Esther, Judith, Tobit), then a closing list of "apocryphal" writings: Old-Testament pseudepigrapha (Adam, Enoch, Lamech and others), "the Circuits and Teachings of the Apostles," the Epistle of Barnabas, the Acts and the Revelation of Paul, the Teachings of Clement, Ignatius and Polycarp, and the Gospels "according to Barnabas" and "according to Matthias." No Thomas title, no infancy gospel and no Protoevangelium appears.4 The Stichometry of Nicephorus (appended to a chronography under the name of Patriarch Nicephorus I of Constantinople, c. 810) lists among its New-Testament apocrypha "the Circuit of Paul," "the Circuit of Peter," "the Circuit of John," "the Circuit of Thomas" (1,600 lines), "the Gospel of Thomas" (1,300 lines), the Didache, "the 32 (books) of Clement," and the writings of Ignatius, Polycarp and Hermas, each with its length in stichoi (lines). An older view, reported in the article "Infancy Gospel of Thomas," took the line-count as evidence of a longer infancy gospel later expurgated; Ursula Ulrike Kaiser (2025) and Tony Burke (2017), as summarized there, hold that the entry does not refer to the infancy gospel at all, since that work was not yet known by the title "Gospel of Thomas."5

Reception and debate

  • The vocabulary of the lists. The Latin decree speaks of books "to be avoided," not "in any way" received, and "not merely rejected but eliminated"; the Greek catalogues set their apocrypha in a list below the canonical and the "outside the sixty" books. George J. Reid (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1907) reads the Gelasian catalogue as showing that "in the Latin Church by this time, apocrypha in general, including those of Catholic origin, had fallen under the ecclesiastical ban"; Marko Marina (2025) describes the Infancy Gospel of Thomas as "marginalized and pushed aside as the institutional Church gradually took shape," the canon forming through "the gradual recognition of texts that were widely read, doctrinally useful, and apostolically grounded."6 These and other readings are set out at Were the infancy gospels suppressed?
  • Two "Thomas" entries in the Decretum. The decree registers "the Gospel in the name of Thomas which the Manichaeans use" and "the book on the infancy of the saviour" as separate entries, under different titles.
  • West and East. Of the lists surveyed here, the Western Gelasian list names infancy and nativity books outright; the List of Sixty Books names no Thomas title, and the Stichometry of Nicephorus names a "Gospel of Thomas" and a "Circuit of Thomas" but no infancy gospel by name.
  • Character of the documents. The Greek lists are catalogues appended to other works, and none of the three is transmitted as the act of a council. The Decretum is headed in some manuscripts as a decretal of Gelasius and in others as the work of a Roman council under Damasus; von Dobschütz judged it a private compilation, and others hold that its canon-chapters preserve the acts of the synod of 382 (see above).
