Did the Roman synod of 382 under Damasus promulgate the canon-list preserved in the Decretum Gelasianum?

Disputed reading · on the Biblical canon · witness: the Council of Rome

Related readings: What does the “Council of Nicaea” heading over Hippo’s canon-list in the Vallicellian codex (B.58) attest? · How did the churches come to know the New Testament canon, and what settled its disputed books?

The passage

HERE BEGINS THE COUNCIL OF ROME UNDER POPE DAMASUS "ON EXPLAINING THE FAITH"

II. Likewise it was said: Now indeed the issue of the divine scriptures must be discussed, which the universal Catholic church receives or which it is required to avoid.

— the Decretum Gelasianum, heading of the longer form and opening of its second chapter (the canon-list), trans. Roger Pearse from Ernst von Dobschütz’s edition, source.1

HERE BEGINS THE DECRETAL 'ON BOOKS TO BE RECEIVED AND NOT TO BE RECEIVED' WHICH WAS WRITTEN BY POPE GELASIUS AND SEVENTY MOST ERUDITE BISHOPS AT THE APOSTOLIC SEAT IN THE CITY OF ROME

— the heading of the shorter recension, which begins at the third chapter, in the same translation.1

The question

A list of the Old and New Testament books survives as the second chapter of the Decretum Gelasianum, a text headed in some manuscripts as a council of Rome under Damasus (pope 366–384), in others as a decretal of Gelasius (492–496) or Hormisdas, and in a few left anonymous; the text itself names no year for the council. What is disputed is whether its canon-chapters preserve the acts of the Roman synod of 382, or belong to a later work that carries Damasus’s name.2

The canon-chapters preserve the acts of the synod of 382

On this reading the first three chapters of the Decretum are a Damasan document, the record of a Roman synod of 382 at which Jerome was present, later taken into a longer compilation.

Held by: Faustino Arévalo (1794); C. H. Turner (Journal of Theological Studies, vol. 1); George J. Reid (Catholic, The Catholic Encyclopedia, 1908); W. A. Jurgens (Catholic, The Faith of the Early Fathers, 1970).3

Arévalo, the editor of Sedulius, proposed in 1794 that the first three of the Decretum’s five chapters were “really the decrees of a Roman Council held a century earlier than Gelasius, under Damasus, in 382”: “certain MSS seemed to give this earlier document separately.” Turner edited those three chapters from four manuscripts of the eighth and ninth centuries under the title “The Roman Council under Damasus,” each headed Incipit concilium urbis Romae sub Damaso papa.4

The synod itself is attested. Reid writes that Jerome “was summoned by Pope Damasus from the East… to assist at an eclectic, but not ecumenical, synod at Rome in the year 382,” and that “this Roman synod must have devoted itself specially to the matter. The result of its deliberations, presided over, no doubt, by the energetic Damasus himself, has been preserved in the document called ‘Decretum Gelasii de recipiendis et non recipiendis libris’, a compilation partly of the sixth century, but containing much material dating from the two preceding ones.” The list bears the stamp of that circle: its New Testament portion, Reid writes, “bears the marks of Jerome’s views,” and Damasus in these years commissioned Jerome’s revision of the Latin Gospels.5

Jurgens distinguishes the strata: “It is now commonly held that the part of the Gelasian Decree dealing with the accepted canon of Scripture is an authentic work of the Council of Rome of 382 A.D. and that Gelasius edited it again at the end of the fifth century, adding to it the catalog of the rejected books, the apocrypha.” On this reading a sixth-century date for the finished compilation leaves the Damasan core in place: a Roman synodal list of 382 of the books the West received.6

A single pseudonymous work of the sixth century

On this reading all five chapters, the canon-list included, belong to one anonymous composition of the sixth century, later headed with the names of Damasus and Gelasius.

Held by: Ernst von Dobschütz (critical edition, 1912); F. C. Burkitt (Journal of Theological Studies, 1913); Geoffrey Mark Hahneman (The Muratorian Fragment and the Development of the Canon, 1992).7

Von Dobschütz, examining all the manuscripts, concluded that “all five chapters belong to the same original work, which is no genuine decree or letter either of Damasus or Gelasius, but a pseudonymous literary production of the first half of the sixth century (between 519 and 553),” the shorter forms deriving from the longer. Burkitt, reviewing the edition, judged that “v. Dobschütz has made out his case”: the chapter “most directly associated with Damasus” quotes Augustine’s tractate on John, written about 416, so “the Title Incipit Concilium Vrbis Romae sub Damaso Papa de Explanatione Fidei is of no historical value.”8

Hahneman adds the testimony of Jerome, who was in Rome in 382 and wrote on the canon for decades after: “It seems highly improbable that, if Jerome, who was probably present at the council and was certainly at Rome, had ever heard of such a pronouncement about canonical books, he should nowhere have mentioned it, or that it should not have qualified his own statements on the Canon.” He notes that Nicholas I, discussing the canon in the ninth century, cites Innocent I but no decree of Damasus, and that Dionysius Exiguus began his collection of papal decretals with Damasus’s successor Siricius.9 Rosamond McKitterick places the work’s origin in Frankish Gaul, its earliest manuscript, Brussels 9850–2, dating from about 700. On this reading the list is a sixth-century witness to the books its compiler received, and the Damasan heading is part of that compilation’s presentation.10
