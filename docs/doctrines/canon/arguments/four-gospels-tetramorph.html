Does Irenaeus’s argument for four Gospels (Against Heresies 3.11.8) attest a fourfold Gospel already closed by c. 180?

Disputed reading · on the Biblical canon · witness: Irenaeus of Lyons

Related readings: How did the churches come to know the New Testament canon, and what settled its disputed books?

The passage

It is not possible that the Gospels can be either more or fewer in number than they are. For, since there are four zones of the world in which we live, and four principal winds, while the Church is scattered throughout all the world, and the "pillar and ground" of the Church is the Gospel and the spirit of life; it is fitting that she should have four pillars, breathing out immortality on every side, and vivifying men afresh. From which fact, it is evident that the Word, the Artificer of all, He that sitteth upon the cherubim, and contains all things, He who was manifested to men, has given us the Gospel under four aspects, but bound together by one Spirit.

— Irenaeus, Against Heresies 3.11.8 (c. 180), ANF 1, source.1

The question

Irenaeus goes on to match the four Gospels to the four faces of the cherubim and to “four principal covenants,” and in the next section charges those who make the Gospels “more in number” or “fewer” than four. What is disputed is whether his argument reports a fourfold collection the churches already held, or argues for one selection among gospels still in wide use in the second century. The second reading below is located here only through a reviewer’s report and a scholar’s blog; its academic literature was not opened directly.

Irenaeus transmits a fourfold Gospel already established

On this reading Irenaeus explains why the churches have four Gospels; the collection itself was received before he wrote, and the symbolism ornaments a fact rather than establishing it.

Held by: George J. Reid (Catholic, The Catholic Encyclopedia, 1908); Graham N. Stanton (“The Fourfold Gospel,” 1997); Martin Hengel (Lutheran, The Four Gospels and the One Gospel of Jesus Christ, 2000); Charles E. Hill (Who Chose the Gospels?, 2010).2

Reid writes that “from the testimony of St. Irenæus alone there can be no reasonable doubt that the Canon of the Gospel was inalterably fixed in the Catholic Church by the last quarter of the second century.” Stanton: “Irenaeus is not defending an innovation, but explaining why, unlike the heretics, the church has four gospels, no more, no less.” Hengel holds the fourfold Gospel “already presupposed rather than occasioned by Marcion”; Hill concludes that Irenaeus “was no innovator but was merely transmitting an established tradition that preceded him”; Michael Kruger adds that “Irenaeus does not write like a person advocating the scriptural status of these books for the first time.”2

The collection is attested before him. Justin Martyr (c. 155) cites the apostles’ “memoirs … which are called Gospels,” read in the Sunday assembly; his pupil Tatian wove four Gospels into one harmony, the Diatessaron; the Epistula Apostolorum, usually dated to the 140s, is read by Hill as evidence for the four decades before Irenaeus.3 When Irenaeus identifies the Gospels he does so by apostolic origin (Matthew “among the Hebrews,” Mark “the disciple and interpreter of Peter,” Luke “the companion of Paul,” John at Ephesus), and he calls them the Gospels “handed down to us from the apostles.”4 T. C. Skeat argued that the living-creatures material was drawn from an earlier source, so that both the fourfold canon and its symbolic justification are older than Irenaeus.5 On this reading the count “neither more nor fewer” works as a recognized standard Irenaeus can set against named opponents.

Irenaeus argues for one selection among many gospels

On this reading second-century Christians used many gospels, and Irenaeus is the earliest extant writer to name the four and to insist on them alone; their supremacy was settled later.

Held by: James M. Robinson, as quoted by Hill (2010); Bart D. Ehrman (Ehrman Blog, 2013–2014, on the naming of the four). ⚠ unverified: Ehrman, Robert W. Funk, Elaine Pagels and Helmut Koester as listed by Lincoln Blumell.6

Robinson, as Hill quotes him, holds that in the second century “Gospels that were later to lose out, as non-canonical, were about as common as Gospels that were later to win out, as canonical.” Blumell, reviewing Hill, groups Robinson with Ehrman, Funk, Pagels and Koester as having “argued in various forms that the four canonical Gospels did not attain a status of supremacy until the fourth century.”6

Ehrman dates the names themselves to Irenaeus’s generation: “When the Gospels of the New Testament are alluded to and quoted by authors of the early second century, they are never entitled, never named. Even Justin Martyr, writing around 150-60 CE, quotes verses from the Gospels, but does not indicate what the Gospels were named.” The naming “comes, for the first time, in the writings of the church father and heresy-hunter Irenaeus, around 180-85 CE”; in his view it happened between Justin and Irenaeus, and “did not happen earlier.”7

Irenaeus’s own pages record the variety. He writes against the Valentinians, who “boast that they possess more Gospels than there really are” and entitle “their comparatively recent writing ‘the Gospel of Truth’”; against Marcion, who kept a single Gospel; and against those who reject John’s. His case for four rests on fitness: “since God made all things in due proportion and adaptation, it was fit also that the outward aspect of the Gospel should be well arranged and harmonized.”8 On this reading the passage is advocacy by a bishop among many gospels: it records the four that his churches read and the argument he offered for confining the Gospel to them.
