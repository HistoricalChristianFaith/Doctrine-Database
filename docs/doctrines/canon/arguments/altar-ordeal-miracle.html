Is the Synodicon Vetus’s account (c. 900) of the books sorted by a sign at the altar of Nicaea historical?

Disputed reading · on the Biblical canon

Related readings: Did the Council of Nicaea (325) decide the contents of the Bible? · Were the infancy gospels suppressed — banned, voted out, gathered up and burned?

The passage

"The canonical and apocryphal books it distinguished in the following manner: in the house of God the books were placed down by the holy altar; then the council asked the Lord in prayer that the inspired works be found on top and—as in fact happened—the spurious on the bottom."

— the anonymous Synodicon Vetus, §35, on the Council of Nicaea (325), in the critical edition of John Duffy and John Parker.1

The question

The Synodicon Vetus, a Greek epitome of the church’s synods compiled after 887, reports that the Council of Nicaea distinguished the canonical from the apocryphal books by laying them at the altar and praying for a sign. What is disputed is whether this account records something that happened at Nicaea, or is a later story about the council first written down in the ninth century; it matters because the passage stands behind the modern report that the canon was settled at Nicaea by a miracle. The first reading below is stated by its ninth-century source; no modern scholar holding the account historical was located.

The Synodicon records how the council distinguished the books

On this reading §35 reports, in the words of a Byzantine compiler, the means by which the Nicene Fathers told the inspired books from the spurious: prayer at the altar, answered by a sign.

Held by: the anonymous compiler of the Synodicon Vetus (Greek, after 887), §35.2

The Synodicon Vetus presents itself as an epitome of the decisions of the councils, one chapter to each synod, its early material digested from Eusebius and the later church historians. Its chapter on Nicaea sets the sorting of the books among the acts of the council the Greek church counted first among its synods. The account is spare and specific: the books placed by the holy altar “in the house of God,” the council’s prayer that the inspired works be found on top, and the outcome “as in fact happened.” It describes a decision made by prayer and left to God, not reached by vote or decree.3

The account reached Western readers through print. A manuscript brought from the Morea in the sixteenth century by Andreas Darmasius was bought, edited and published at Strasbourg in 1601 by the Lutheran theologian Johannes Pappus (1549–1610), and from his edition the passage circulated, sometimes cited as though Pappus himself were its author.4 Voltaire related it in the Philosophical Dictionary (“Councils,” 1764) as told “in the supplement to the Council of Nice,” and Robert Taylor reported in The Diegesis (1829) that “Pappus, in his Synodicon to the council of Nice, asserts” the same story.5 On this reading the chapter preserves the form in which the ninth-century Greek church remembered Nicaea’s distinction of the canonical from the apocryphal books.

A later story about the council, first recorded in the ninth century

On this reading the altar-sign is a story attached to Nicaea long after 325, recorded first by the Synodicon and spread in modern times through Pappus’s edition.

Held by: John Duffy and John Parker (editors of the Synodicon Vetus, 1979); Roger Pearse (“The Council of Nicaea and the Bible,” 2001); John Meade (Phoenix Seminary, 2018).6

The work’s editors note that “since the story is related only by SV, it is not possible to know if it belongs to an older tradition or where our author might have come across it.” They describe a compiler who adds details found in none of his sources, such as the number of bishops at a synod, and who, “in his zeal,” was “anything but a careful researcher”; some of the synods he records they judge “doubtful or imaginary.” Pearse observes that the story “is first recorded here, in the late 9th century,” and that a companion tale of Nicaea (two dead bishops whose signatures were added to the creed in their tombs) “sounds rather like folklore of the middle ages, as indeed does the story about the apocrypha.”7

Meade sets the story against the record of the council itself: “none of the early records from the Council nor eyewitness attendees (e.g. Eusebius or Athanasius) mention any discussion over the Canon of Scripture.” The twenty canons of Nicaea concern the Arian and Melitian schisms, the lapsed, the date of Easter and clerical discipline, and Eusebius and Athanasius, both present at the council, set out their own sortings of the books after it.8 Meade traces the modern currency of the story to Pappus’s 1601 edition and its citation by Voltaire, “the reason why Dan Brown could narrate the events so colorfully.” On this reading §35 is evidence for how Nicaea was remembered in ninth-century Byzantium and for how that memory travelled into modern print.9
