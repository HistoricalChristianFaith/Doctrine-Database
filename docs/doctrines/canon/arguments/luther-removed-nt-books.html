Did Luther remove Hebrews, James, Jude, and Revelation from the New Testament?

Disputed reading · on the Biblical canon · witness: Martin Luther

Related readings: How did the churches come to know the New Testament canon, and what settled its disputed books? · Did Martin Luther remove the deuterocanonical books from the Bible, or rank and relocate them? (on the Old Testament canon)

The passage

Therefore, I will not have him in my Bible to be numbered among the true chief books, though I would not thereby prevent anyone from including or extolling him as he pleases, for there are otherwise many good sayings in him.

— Martin Luther, Preface to the Epistles of St. James and St. Jude, in the September Testament (1522), trans. Luther’s Works, vol. 35, pp. 395–399, as given at bible-researcher.com.1

Luther also took out four New Testament books — Hebrews, James, Jude, and Revelation — and put them in an appendix without page numbers as well. These were later put back into the New Testament by other Protestants.

— Jimmy Akin, "Defending the Deuterocanonicals."2

The question

Luther’s German New Testament of 1522 printed Hebrews, James, Jude and Revelation after the other twenty-three books, without numbers in its table of contents, under prefaces doubting their apostolic standing. What is disputed is what that arrangement amounts to: setting the four apart from the canonical books of the New Testament, or ranking them lower within it. The bearing is on how the Lutheran canon is described and on how Luther’s doubts stand to the ancient disputes over the same books. The form “took out … later put back” is stated here from a Catholic apologist; the academic holders below speak of the books’ canonical standing.

Luther set the four apart from the canonical books

On this reading Luther denied the four canonical standing and marked them off from the New Testament’s canonical books, though he printed them in the same volume.

Held by: George J. Reid (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1908); Johann Michael Reu (Lutheran, “Luther and the Scriptures,” 1944); J. A. O. Preus (Lutheran, 1961); Jimmy Akin (Catholic apologist), in the form quoted above.3

Reid writes that “Luther, basing his action on dogmatic reasons and the judgment of antiquity, had discarded Hebrews, James, Jude, and Apocalypse as altogether uncanonical,” and that “for over a century the followers of Luther excluded Hebrews, James, Jude, and Apocalypse.” Reu, defending Luther’s view of Scripture, argues that Luther’s sayings on James concern a book he did not count canonical: “That Hebrews, James, Jude, and Revelation were not considered canonical by Luther is also proved by the fact that in his September Testament he did not add numbers to them and that he put a space between them and the others … In the complete Bible of 1534 this is made even more pronounced by the fact that in the index of the Biblical books they are dealt with in the same way as the Old Testament Apocrypha.” Preus writes that “Luther rejected these books partly on the basis of historical precedent and partly on the basis of his own rather subjective criterion of canonicity.”4

The prefaces state the separation. The preface to Hebrews closes the numbered books: “Up to this point we have had to do with the true and certain chief books of the New Testament. The four which follow have from ancient times had a different reputation.” James is “not … the writing of an apostle,” Jude “need not be counted among the chief books,” and Revelation “neither apostolic nor prophetic”; the test is that “Whatever does not teach Christ is not yet apostolic.” Reu cites Luther’s Table Talk of 1542: “Here at Wittenberg we thrust James out of the Bible.” Akin’s “took out … put back” describes the same separation as removal and restoration. On this reading the 1522 table of contents drew the line of the canon below the twenty-third book.5

Luther kept the four in his New Testament, ranked below the chief books

On this reading the four stayed in Luther’s New Testament, translated and prefaced, and what changed was their rank and order, not their place in the Bible.

Held by: Martin Foord (Anglican, Themelios 45.2); Garrett Ham (Catholic writer, “Did Luther Remove Books from the Bible? The 1522 Testament”); Charles A. Gieschen (Lutheran, Concordia Theological Quarterly, 2015), on the distinction within the canon only.6

Foord: “In his German translation of the Bible Luther retained James among the NT books,” and “It is erroneous to claim that Luther excised James from the NT according to his individual whim.” Ham, writing as a Catholic: “Did Luther remove books from the Bible? From the New Testament, no,” and “He kept the books. He changed the test.” The 1522 Testament contains all four, and the prefaces leave the judgment open: Luther would not “prevent anyone from including or extolling him as he pleases,” and of Revelation he writes, “I leave everyone free to hold his own opinions.”7

Luther revised these judgments. The edition of 1530 dropped the sentence that James “mangles the Scriptures,” and from 1530 the short Revelation preface was replaced by a much longer one interpreting the book; Foord reports that the “epistle of straw” declaration “was removed from this preface in all editions of his German Bible after 1537.” The four were the books the early church had disputed: Eusebius counts James and Jude among the “disputed writings,” and Erasmus (1516) and Cardinal Cajetan questioned the same books in Luther’s time. Later Lutheran theology kept the ancient distinction between undisputed and disputed books inside the canon: Preus reports that Johann Gerhard held the disputed books “canonical and of apostolic authorship,” and that “The Lutherans have never made the Canon a matter of conciliar or confessional decision.” On this reading Luther ranked the four as the church’s ancient antilegomena, inside the New Testament he printed.8
