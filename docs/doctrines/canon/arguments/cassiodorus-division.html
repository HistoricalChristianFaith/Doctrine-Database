Does Cassiodorus’s naming of Nicaea among those who divided the sacred books (Institutiones I.14) attest a Nicene action on the canon?

Disputed reading · on the Biblical canon

Related readings: What does Jerome mean in writing that Judith “is found by the Nicene Council to have been counted among the number of the Sacred Scriptures”? · What does the “Council of Nicaea” heading over Hippo’s canon-list in the Vallicellian codex (B.58) attest?

The passage

"As a result, although many Fathers — St. Hilary, bishop of Poitiers, and Rufinus, a priest of Aquileia, and Epiphanius, bishop of Cyprus, and the Councils of Nicaea and Chalcedon — have said things not contradictory to one another but only different, all have, nevertheless, by their divisions, fitted the sacred books to the appropriate mysteries, as also happens in the harmonies of the Gospels where we regard the events with one faith although they differ in the way they are told."

— Cassiodorus, Institutiones I.14.3 (c. 543), trans. James W. Halporn, source.1

The question

In the chapter that closes his account of three schemes for dividing and numbering Scripture (Jerome’s, Augustine’s and the Septuagint’s), Cassiodorus names “the Councils of Nicaea and Chalcedon” with Hilary, Rufinus and Epiphanius among those who “by their divisions” fitted the sacred books to the mysteries. What is disputed is whether the sentence attests that the Council of Nicaea (325) drew up a list or division of the biblical books; it is one of three texts the Jesuit scholar Rudolph Cornely adduced for a Nicene action on the canon.

The council ranks among those who drew up a division of the books

On this reading a learned sixth-century writer, with the conciliar acts at hand, counted Nicaea among the authorities who had set out the sacred books, and so attests that the council took up the question.

Held by: Rudolph Cornely, S.J. (Catholic, Introductio specialis in historicos Veteris Testamenti libros, 1887).2

Cornely lists the Cassiodorus passage among his evidences that the synod of Nicaea enumerated the sacred books. The company the council keeps in the sentence is canon-company: Hilary, Rufinus and Epiphanius are each known for an arrangement or list of the biblical books, and Cassiodorus names the councils in the same breath and the same series. Cornely reasons that the council, like the Fathers beside it, produced a division of the books.2

In the same book of the Institutiones Cassiodorus sets out “the four accepted councils,” Nicaea first and Chalcedon fourth, and reports that he had the complete collection of letters bearing on Chalcedon translated; his own chapters on the divisions of Scripture list the books of each scheme in full.3 Cornely set this passage beside two others, Jerome’s report in his preface to Judith that the Nicene council “counted” Judith among the Scriptures and a manuscript heading that ascribes Hippo’s canon-list to “Chapter XXIV of the Nicene Council”; on his reading the three are independent traces of one fact, that the council said something about the books. On this reading Cassiodorus, two centuries on and working from the conciliar record, preserves that the Nicene Fathers had a part in setting out the sacred books.

An arrangement of received books, no proof of a Nicene enumeration

On this reading the sentence is too obscure to carry a conciliar canon-list, and the record of Nicaea does not support one.

Held by: A. E. Breen (Catholic, A General and Critical Introduction to the Study of Holy Scripture, 1897); Karl Joseph von Hefele (Catholic bishop, A History of the Councils of the Church, on a Nicene canon of Scripture generally).4

Breen, reviewing Cornely’s three props, calls the Cassiodorus passage “some obscure words” that “form no forcible proof,” and notes that Cornely took the citation from Hefele’s Conciliengeschichte rather than from the text. The chapter in which the sentence stands concerns how the books are divided and counted: Cassiodorus describes his own Septuagint codex as holding forty-four Old Testament and twenty-six New Testament books, “seventy books” in all, matched to the seventy palms of Elim, and offers his three schemes so that “they seem not to disagree but rather explain one another.”1

Hefele, discussing the Nicene canons, holds that the council issued no canon of the biblical books. Of Jerome’s Judith remark he writes, “If we conclude from these words that the Fathers of Nicaea gave a canon of the genuine books of the Bible, we certainly draw an inference which they do not sustain,” and he describes how later material came to be ascribed to Nicaea: in the early conciliar corpus “the canons of Nicaea always had the first place,” and copyists who omitted the names of the following councils let “all the later canons which followed after the true canons” be “attributed to the Council of Nicaea.” The twenty canons of Nicaea concern the Arian and Melitian schisms, the lapsed, the date of Easter and clerical discipline, and Eusebius and Athanasius, both present at the council, set out their own sortings of the books after it.5 On this reading Cassiodorus reports the place Nicaea held in sixth-century memory among the authorities on Scripture, while the record of 325 is the council’s own canons and the later lists of the men who attended it.
