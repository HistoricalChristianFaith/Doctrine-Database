What does the “Council of Nicaea” heading over Hippo’s canon-list in the Vallicellian codex (B.58) attest?

Disputed reading · on the Biblical canon · witness: the African councils

Related readings: What does Jerome mean in writing that Judith “is found by the Nicene Council to have been counted among the number of the Sacred Scriptures”? · Does Cassiodorus’s naming of Nicaea among those who divided the sacred books (Institutiones I.14) attest a Nicene action on the canon?

The passage

"There is added an ancient codex of the eighth or ninth century (Vallicell. B.58, fol. 77), which prefixes to canon 36 of the Council of Hippo, A.D. 393, this inscription: 'That besides the catholic (canonical) Scriptures nothing be read in the church. Chapter XXIV of the Nicene Council. Likewise, that besides the catholic Scriptures nothing be read in church under the name of divine Scriptures. Now the canonical Scriptures are…' etc., and there follows a catalogue exhibiting all the books of the first and second canon intermixed. Whence it is natural to conclude that the Fathers of Hippo drew their catalogue from the Nicene decrees, which Caecilian of Carthage had carried with him into Africa; nor may it be denied that the Nicene Fathers passed a decree that all the books of the wider canon be read in the church as Scriptures."

— Rudolph Cornely, S.J., Introductio specialis in historicos Veteris Testamenti libros (Paris, 1887), p. 24, in translation, source.1

The question

Canon 36 of the Council of Hippo (393), “that besides the canonical Scriptures nothing be read in the church under the name of the divine Scriptures,” with its list of books, was repeated at Carthage and transmitted in the African conciliar collections. In one codex of the eighth or ninth century the canon stands under the rubric “Chapter XXIV of the Nicene Council.” What is disputed is whether the rubric preserves a link between Hippo’s list and the decrees of Nicaea (325), or is a later heading placed over the African canon; it is one of three texts the Jesuit scholar Rudolph Cornely adduced for a Nicene action on the canon.2

Hippo drew its catalogue from the Nicene decrees

On this reading the rubric records that the African bishops took their list from the decrees of Nicaea, carried to Africa by a bishop who had attended the council, so that Nicaea itself decreed which books be read as Scripture.

Held by: Rudolph Cornely, S.J. (Catholic, Introductio specialis in historicos Veteris Testamenti libros, 1887).1

Cornely sets the codex-heading third among his evidences that the synod of Nicaea enumerated the sacred books. The heading is specific: it puts a definite chapter-number, “Chapter XXIV of the Nicene Council,” over a fully enumerated canon, so that whoever wrote it took this catalogue to be Nicene. Cornely presents the conclusion as what is “natural to conclude” (pronum est concludere) from the title.

The thread he follows runs through Carthage. Caecilian, bishop of Carthage, attended the Council of Nicaea and brought a copy of its canons back to Carthage, where the African bishops consulted it a century later.3 Nicene decrees were in African hands. Taken with Jerome’s report that the Nicene council “counted” Judith among the Scriptures and with Cassiodorus’s naming of Nicaea among those who divided the sacred books, a manuscript that heads a scriptural canon “of Nicaea” is, on Cornely’s reading, a third trace of one fact: that the council said something about the books. On this reading the African list of 393, with Tobit, Judith and two books of Maccabees among its books, descends from a Nicene decree “that all the books of the wider canon be read in the church as Scriptures.”

A later heading placed over the African canon

On this reading the catalogue is the African council’s own, and the Nicene title is one of the headings under which later conciliar material came to be filed under Nicaea’s name.

Held by: A. E. Breen (Catholic, A General and Critical Introduction to the Study of Holy Scripture, 1897); Karl Joseph von Hefele (Catholic bishop, A History of the Councils of the Church), as reported by Breen and on the mislabelling of canons generally.4

Breen allows the heading whatever force it may have, yet will not count it among the proofs: “This Canon exists but in one sole codex in the Vallicellian library, in Rome. We are not disposed to detract from what force it may have, but we do not feel warranted to refer the Council of Nice among the proofs of the Canon in the fourth century. Hefele accords no certain authority to the aforesaid Canon.” He notes that the codex’s heading runs the African canon on from a Nicene chapter-title, “the books of both canons” being mentioned beneath it.4

Hefele describes how such headings arose. The canons of various councils were gathered early into one corpus, “and in this corpus the canons of Nicaea always had the first place, on account of their importance”; when copyists omitted the names of the following councils, “all the later canons which followed after the true canons were attributed to the Council of Nicaea.” At Rome a copy held the canons of Nicaea and Sardica sub uno titulo, and Pope Zosimus cited a canon of Sardica as Nicene, “an error which he shared with many Greek authors.” In that dispute the African bishops searched their Greek and Latin copies and “the copy which Bishop Cecilian, who had himself been present at the Council of Nicaea, had brought to Carthage,” did not find the canon cited, and sent to Alexandria and Constantinople for authentic texts.5 On this reading the Vallicellian rubric belongs to that history of the collections: the African canon of 393, dated by its own council, copied under a Nicene title in one manuscript four or five centuries later.
