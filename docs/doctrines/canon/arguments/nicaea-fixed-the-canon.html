Did the Council of Nicaea (325) decide the contents of the Bible?

Disputed reading · on the Biblical canon

Related readings: Is the Synodicon Vetus’s account (c. 900) of the books sorted by a sign at the altar of Nicaea historical? · Were the infancy gospels suppressed — banned, voted out, gathered up and burned? · What does Jerome mean in writing that Judith “is found by the Nicene Council to have been counted among the number of the Sacred Scriptures”?

The passage

"The Bible is a product of man, my dear. Not of God. The Bible did not magically fall from the clouds. The Bible, as we know it today, was collated by the pagan Roman emperor Constantine the Great. In 325 AD, he decided to unify Rome under a single religion: Christianity. Constantine needed to strengthen the new Christian tradition and held a famous gathering known as the Council of Nicea… Constantine commissioned and financed a new Bible, which omitted those gospels that spoke of Christ's human traits and embellished those gospels that made him godlike. The earlier gospels were outlawed, gathered up and burned."

— Dan Brown, The Da Vinci Code (New York: Doubleday, 2003), ch. 55, spoken by the character Leigh Teabing.1

The question

The Council of Nicaea (325), convened by Constantine, issued a creed, a ruling on the date of Easter and twenty canons, and no list of biblical books survives among its acts. What is disputed is whether the council nonetheless settled which books belong to the Bible: by sorting the books itself, by a decree enumerating them, or not at all. The reading that the council selected the books, in the form given above, is stated here from a ninth-century Byzantine compiler and modern popular writers; academic literature holding that form was not located.

The council distinguished the canonical from the apocryphal books

On this reading the bishops at Nicaea, under Constantine, decided which books were inspired and which were to be set aside.

Held by: the anonymous compiler of the Synodicon Vetus (Greek, after 887), §35; Baronius (Annales ecclesiastici, 1559–1607) ⚠ unverified; Dan Brown (novelist, The Da Vinci Code, 2003).2

The Synodicon Vetus, an epitome of the councils, sets the sorting of the books among the acts of Nicaea: “The canonical and apocryphal books it distinguished in the following manner: in the house of God the books were placed down by the holy altar; then the council asked the Lord in prayer that the inspired works be found on top and—as in fact happened—the spurious on the bottom.” Baronius is reported to have recorded under the year 325 “the adoption of certain gospels, and the rejection of others as spurious,” without saying by what means. Voltaire retold the altar account in the Philosophical Dictionary (1764) from “the supplement to the Council of Nice,” and Thomas Paine described the books as “voted to be the word of God” by majority (1794), without naming a council.3

Brown’s form places the decision with Constantine. The emperor convened the council to unify a divided church, and it legislated with binding force: the creed with its homoousios, the Easter rule, the canons. After the council Constantine ordered the writings of Arius burned, on pain of death for anyone found concealing them, and some years later he ordered from Eusebius “fifty copies of the sacred Scriptures” for the churches of Constantinople, written “by professional transcribers thoroughly practiced in their art.” On this reading an emperor who presided over doctrinal decisions, ordered books destroyed and paid for the copying of Bibles is the setting in which the books were chosen, and the gospels left out were those the imperial church set aside.4

The council did not take up the canon, which formed by gradual reception

On this reading Nicaea issued no decision on the books of the Bible, and the canon took shape across the fourth century through the churches’ use and later regional lists.

Held by: Karl Joseph von Hefele (Catholic, Conciliengeschichte, 1855); A. E. Breen (Catholic, 1908), who holds that the Nicene fathers recognized the deuterocanonical books; Bart Ehrman (New Testament scholar, 2017); John D. Meade (Protestant, 2020).5

Hefele: “If we conclude from these words that the Fathers of Nicæa gave a canon of the genuine books of the Bible, we certainly draw an inference which they do not sustain.” Ehrman: “the Council of Nicaea did not debate or decide which books should belong in Scripture. We know this because we have actual records of the Council’s issues and decisions.” The twenty canons concern the Arian and Melitian schisms, the lapsed, the Easter rule and clerical discipline. Meade adds that “none of the early records from the council, nor eyewitness attendees (Eusebius or Athanasius, for example), mentions any conciliar decision that established the canon.”6

Eusebius, present at the council, sorts the books in his Church History into the acknowledged, the “disputed writings, which are nevertheless recognized by many” (James, Jude, 2 Peter, 2–3 John), and the rest. Athanasius, also present, sets out the canon in his Festal Letter 39 of 367 as an undertaking of his own, “the pattern of Luke the Evangelist”: “to set before you the books included in the Canon, and handed down, and accredited as Divine.” Breen reasons that “St. Athanasius would not have declared that it was a bold and difficult thing to fix the list of the Holy Books, had there been promulgated a catalogue of the same by a council of which he was an important factor.” Lists followed regionally: Laodicea, Hippo (393), Carthage (397).7 On this reading the canon took shape after Nicaea, in these individual and regional lists.

The council enumerated the sacred books in a decree

On this reading Nicaea issued a decree listing the sacred books, the longer canon included, which the African councils later repeated; it involved no selection by the emperor and no destruction of books.

Held by: Rudolph Cornely (Catholic, Jesuit, Historicae et criticae introductionis in U.T. libros sacros compendium, 1891).8

Cornely grants that “Num prima synodus oecumenica quidquam de canone Scripturarum statuerit, dubitatur quidem a nonnullis” (whether the first ecumenical synod laid down anything on the canon of the Scriptures is doubted by some), but holds that “argumenta historica omne prudens dubium videntur excludere” (the historical arguments seem to exclude every prudent doubt). His first witness is Jerome (c. 405), who translated Judith because “this book is found by the Nicene Council to have been counted among the number of the Sacred Scriptures”; Cornely reads this as a decree in which the synod enumerated the sacred books and received at least one deuterocanonical book among them. His second is Cassiodorus, who names the synod of Nicaea with Hilary, Rufinus and Epiphanius among those who fitted the divine books to their sacred number. His third is a codex of the eighth or ninth century (Vallicelliana B.58) that heads the canon-list of Hippo (393) “Capituli XXIV, Nicaeni Concilii.”9

From the heading Cornely concludes that the fathers of Hippo drew their catalogue from the Nicene decrees that Caecilian of Carthage had brought back to Africa, and that the Nicene fathers issued a decree on all the books of the longer canon, to be read in the church as Scripture (“neque negare licet, Nicaenos Patres decretum de omnibus amplioris canonis libris in ecclesia velut Scripturis legendis tulisse”). On this reading the three African councils of 393–419, Innocent I’s list of 405 and Augustine’s of 397 restate a Nicene enumeration of both the protocanonical and the deuterocanonical books. Each of the three witnesses is set out with its readings on its own page.10
