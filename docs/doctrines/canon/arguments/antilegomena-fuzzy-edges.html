How did the churches come to know the New Testament canon, and what settled its disputed books?

Disputed reading · on the Biblical canon · witness: Eusebius of Caesarea

Related readings: Does Irenaeus’s argument for four Gospels (Against Heresies 3.11.8) attest a fourfold Gospel already closed by c. 180?

The passage

Since we are dealing with this subject it is proper to sum up the writings of the New Testament which have been already mentioned. First then must be put the holy quaternion of the Gospels; following them the Acts of the Apostles. After this must be reckoned the epistles of Paul; next in order the extant former epistle of John, and likewise the epistle of Peter, must be maintained. After them is to be placed, if it really seem proper, the Apocalypse of John, concerning which we shall give the different opinions at the proper time. These then belong among the accepted writings. Among the disputed writings, which are nevertheless recognized by many, are extant the so-called epistle of James and that of Jude, also the second epistle of Peter, and those that are called the second and third of John, whether they belong to the evangelist or to another person of the same name.

— Eusebius of Caesarea, Church History 3.25.1–3 (c. 325), trans. A. C. McGiffert, source.1

The question

The Catholic, Protestant and Orthodox sources cited below each describe the New Testament’s books as received over generations, with several (Eusebius’s “disputed writings,” with Hebrews and the Apocalypse) debated into the fourth century, and each holds that the Church did not make the books authoritative by listing them. What they read differently is how the list came to be known and what settled it: the Church’s tradition, declared by its authority; the books’ own worth, which councils ratified; or a reception within the Church’s life whose exact number was never made a matter of dogma.

The inspired books, committed to the Church, known through its tradition and authoritatively declared

On this reading the books are canonical because God is their author and committed them to the Church, which came to know the full list through its tradition and declared it with its authority.

Held by: the First Vatican Council (Catholic, Dei Filius, 1870); George J. Reid (Catholic, The Catholic Encyclopedia, “Canon of the New Testament,” 1908); the Second Vatican Council (Catholic, Dei Verbum, 1965); Felix Just, S.J. (Catholic, “The New Testament Canon”).2

The First Vatican Council states that the Church holds the books “to be sacred and canonical not because she subsequently approved them by her authority after they had been composed by unaided human skill, nor simply because they contain revelation without error, but because, being written under the inspiration of the holy Spirit, they have God as their author, and were as such committed to the church.”3 The Second Vatican Council places the knowing of the list within tradition: “Through the same tradition the Church’s full canon of the sacred books is known.”4

Reid traces that knowing as a history: “The idea of a complete and clear-cut canon of the New Testament existing from the beginning, that is from Apostolic times, has no foundation in history.” The canon is “the result of a development, of a process at once stimulated by disputes with doubters, both within and without the Church, and retarded by certain obscurities and natural hesitations, and which did not reach its final term until the dogmatic definition of the Tridentine Council.”5 He divides that development into periods: a formation (A.D. 100–220), in which the Gospels and Pauline epistles were joined towards the end of the second century by Acts, 1 Peter, 1 John and the Apocalypse, so that “Hebrews, James, Jude, and II Peter remained hovering outside the precincts of universal canonicity, and the controversy about them and the subsequently disputed Apocalypse form the larger part of the remaining history”; then a “period of discussion” (A.D. 220–367), in which writers became conscious “of the differences between the sacred collections in divers sections of Christendom.” Reid treats the Roman synod of 382 as the moment at which a complete catalogue was set out, its New Testament portion bearing “the marks of Jerome’s views.”6

Just names the marks by which books “were eventually accepted into the NT Canon”: “Apostolic Origin,” “Universal Acceptance” (“acknowledged by all major Christian communities in the Mediterranean world (by the end of the fourth century)”), “Liturgical Use,” and “Consistent Message.”7 On this reading the disputes at the edges belong to the Church’s discernment, through its tradition, of books already given to it, which its councils declared and the Council of Trent defined.

Books recognized by their own worth, whose recognition the councils ratified

On this reading the churches acknowledged, region by region, books they already held inspired, and the synods of the late fourth century ratified a recognition that had already taken place, the edges staying soft for generations.

Held by: Bruce M. Metzger (Presbyterian, “The Formation of the New Testament Canon,” 1965; The Canon of the New Testament, 1987); F. F. Bruce (evangelical, The Canon of Scripture, 1988).8

"One thing must be emphatically stated. The New Testament books did not become authoritative for the Church because they were formally included in a canonical list; on the contrary, the Church included them in her canon because she already regarded them as divinely inspired, recognizing their innate worth and general apostolic authority, direct or indirect."

— F. F. Bruce, The Canon of Scripture, p. 22.

Metzger describes the synods’ part: “When, toward the close of the fourth century, church synods and councils began to issue pronouncements concerning the New Testament canon, they were merely ratifying the judgment of individual Christians throughout the church who had come to perceive by intuitive insight the inherent worth of the several books.”9

The boundary moved in both directions. Books once received as Scripture or near it were later set aside: Irenaeus cites the Shepherd of Hermas as “Scripture”; the Muratorian list receives the Apocalypse of Peter; Codex Sinaiticus carries Barnabas and the Shepherd, Codex Alexandrinus 1–2 Clement; and in 367 Athanasius keeps books “appointed by the Fathers to be read” outside the canon.10 Books finally received were long disputed: Hebrews in the West, where the Muratorian list omits it; the Apocalypse in the East, whose authorship Dionysius of Alexandria questioned and which Cyril of Jerusalem leaves off his list.11 On Metzger’s account Athanasius “was the first to name (in his Festal Letter for A.D. 367) exactly the twenty-seven books of the New Testament as exclusively canonical,” presenting them as books “handed down.”9 Metzger and Bruce distinguish a core of the four Gospels, Acts, the Pauline letters, 1 Peter and 1 John, undisputed across the early witnesses, from the fringe where the movement took place.12 On this reading neither individuals nor councils created the canon: the churches acknowledged writings they had already received.

A reception within the Church’s life, its exact number never made a dogma

On this reading the books were gathered gradually through the Church’s tradition and worship, and the closing of the canon is a firm principle that never fixed the number of books as dogma.

Held by: Theodore G. Stylianopoulos (Orthodox, The New Testament: An Orthodox Perspective, vol. 1, 1997).13

Stylianopoulos places the canon inside the community that received it: “When you say Hebrew Bible you say Synagogue. When you say Christian Bible you say Church.” The gradual gathering of the books “involved, as well, a dynamic process of reception and selection within the ongoing life and tradition” of the communities of faith. By the end of the second century, on the witness of Irenaeus, Tertullian and the Muratorian list, “Christians widely acknowledged twenty-two of the twenty-seven books”; “for many generations the contested books continued to be 2 Peter, Jude, 2 and 3 John, the Book of Revelation, and to a somewhat lesser degree Hebrews and James.”14

The so-called closing of the New Testament canon, he writes, “is usually dated with St. Athanasios the Great (367),” yet “the number twenty-seven is, nevertheless, not a matter of dogma. The ancient Syriac Church selected only twenty-two books for its New Testament without causing division. The Ethiopian Church embraced more than thirty. St. John of Damascus in the eighth century listed twenty-eight books.” The closing of the canon “is a firm but not rigid principle in the Orthodox Church because the classic Christian tradition never dogmatized the exact number of scriptural books and always valued many other writings and liturgical texts in addition to the canonical collection of the Scriptures.”14 On this reading the soft edges of the early centuries describe the canon’s ordinary character: a firm collection received in the Church’s life, with regional variation that did not divide the churches.
