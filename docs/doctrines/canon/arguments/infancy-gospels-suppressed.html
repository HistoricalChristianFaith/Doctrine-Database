Were the infancy gospels suppressed — banned, voted out, gathered up and burned?

Disputed reading · on the Biblical canon · witness: the Gelasian Decree and the Greek rejection-lists

Related readings: Did the Council of Nicaea (325) decide the contents of the Bible? · Is the Synodicon Vetus’s account (c. 900) of the books sorted by a sign at the altar of Nicaea historical?

The passage

"More than eighty gospels were considered for the New Testament, and yet only a relative few were chosen for inclusion — Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John among them. … Constantine commissioned and financed a new Bible, which omitted those gospels that spoke of Christ's human traits and embellished those gospels that made him godlike. The earlier gospels were outlawed, gathered up and burned."

— Dan Brown, The Da Vinci Code (New York: Doubleday, 2003), ch. 55, spoken by the character Leigh Teabing.1

The question

The infancy gospels — chiefly the Protevangelium of James, on Mary’s birth and childhood, and the Infancy Gospel of Thomas, on the boyhood of Jesus — stand outside the New Testament, and Latin church documents of the fifth and sixth centuries list books of this kind as rejected. What is disputed is how they came to stand outside it: by a ban enforced by church or emperor, by the churches’ gradual non-reception, or as books never received as Scripture yet kept in the church’s memory. The reading that they were outlawed and burned under Constantine is stated here from a novelist; academic literature holding that form was not located.

Banned in the Latin West from the fifth century, though the books survived

On this reading Western church authorities condemned the apostolic-pseudonym apocrypha, ordered them destroyed, and listed infancy books among those to be refused, and the books lived on in medieval use.

Held by: George J. Reid (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, “Apocrypha,” 1907).2

Reid writes that “no apocryphal work found official recognition in the Western Church,” and that from the Gelasian catalogue “it is evident that in the Latin Church by this time, apocrypha in general, including those of Catholic origin, had fallen under the ecclesiastical ban.” The documents name books of this kind. Innocent I (405) writes of the books “which appear under the name of Matthias or of James the Less … or under the name of Thomas” that “they are not only to be rejected but also condemned.” Leo I (447) orders that “the apocryphal scriptures, which, under the names of Apostles, form a nursery-ground for many falsehoods, are not only to be proscribed, but also taken away altogether and burnt to ashes in the fire,” and warns any bishop who “has either not forbidden the possession of apocryphal writings in men’s houses.”3

The Gelasian Decree (sixth century) lists “the Gospel in the name of James the younger” and “the book on the infancy of the saviour” among its apocrypha and closes that these and similar writings are “not merely rejected but eliminated from the whole Roman Catholic and Apostolic Church.” Reid adds the Synod of Braga (563), which anathematized anyone “who reads, approves, or defends the injurious fictions set in circulation by heretics.” He also records what followed: “in the Middle Ages these condemnations were forgotten and many of the pseudographic writings enjoyed a high degree of favour among both clerics and the laity,” while Alcuin, Bernard and Thomas Aquinas pointed out “their want of authority.” On this reading the Latin church did ban these books and ordered their destruction, and their survival records the limits of that ban.4

Excluded by gradual non-reception, and copied for centuries

On this reading the infancy gospels were never received as apostolic Scripture, were passed over by generations of church writers, and went on being copied and translated across the Christian world.

Held by: Roger Pearse (“The Council of Nicaea and the Bible,” 2001); Marko Marina (Ph.D., bartehrman.com, 2025).5

Marina: “There was no dramatic vote in a candle-lit chamber where bishops accepted some gospels and burned the rest.” He describes the Infancy Gospel of Thomas as “marginalized and pushed aside” as church leaders, uneasy with its portrait of the young Jesus, favoured writings they believed came from eyewitnesses, while “for centuries, this gospel was copied, translated, and retold across Christian communities from Egypt to Europe.” Pearse finds “almost no evidence that the council of Nicaea made any pronouncements on which books go in the Bible … or about the destruction of heretical writings.”6

The record of non-reception is early. Irenaeus (c. 180) files the Marcosians’ tale of the boy Jesus and his teacher among “an unspeakable number of apocryphal and spurious writings, which they themselves have forged.” Eusebius sets the gospels “of Peter, of Thomas, of Matthias” among books “which no one belonging to the succession of ecclesiastical writers has deemed worthy of mention in his writings.” The Western lists come centuries after the texts first circulated, and the Gelasian Decree is titled “on books to be received and not to be received.” The manuscripts record wide copying: “The Greek text of the Protevangelium exists in a daunting ca. 140 MSS,” with versions in Syriac, Coptic, Armenian, Georgian, Ethiopic, Latin, Arabic and Church Slavonic. The Infancy Gospel of Thomas was translated into Latin and Syriac and later Church Slavonic, and passed into the Latin Gospel of Pseudo-Matthew, “popular throughout the Western church.” On this reading the books’ exclusion was a long declining to read them as Scripture, alongside their continued circulation.7

Outlawed and burned in the age of Constantine and Nicaea

On this reading the gospels outside the New Testament were put down by imperial authority when Constantine’s church fixed its Bible: outlawed, collected and destroyed.

Held by: Dan Brown (novelist, The Da Vinci Code, 2003).8

Brown’s Teabing places the decision with Constantine: a new Bible “commissioned and financed” by the emperor, which kept the gospels that made Christ “godlike” and left the others to be “outlawed, gathered up and burned.” Each element has a documented counterpart in the period. Constantine convened the council of 325 and its decisions bound the church. After the council he ordered that any treatise by Arius be “consigned to the flames, in order that not only his depraved doctrine may be suppressed, but also that no memorial of him may be by any means left,” with death for anyone caught concealing such a book. A few years later he ordered “fifty copies of the sacred Scriptures” from Eusebius for the churches of Constantinople.9

The writers of the period also rejected the apostolic-pseudonym gospels by name. Eusebius, author of the Life of Constantine and the bishop asked to produce those copies, says of the gospels under the names of Peter, Thomas and Matthias that “they clearly show themselves to be the fictions of heretics,” to be “cast aside as absurd.” The documented imperial burning of Arius’s books and the later Western orders against the apocrypha show book destruction as an instrument that church and empire used. On this reading the gospels missing from the New Testament are the losers of an imperial settlement of doctrine and Scripture, and their disappearance from church use was enforced.10

A non-canonical story kept in liturgical memory

On this reading the Protevangelium was never Scripture, but its account of Mary’s childhood was kept and honoured in the feasts and art of the Orthodox and Catholic churches, read as poetry that tells something true.

Held by: Fr Lawrence Farley (Orthodox, Orthodox Church in America, 2016), writing on the Protevangelium’s place in the feast of the Entrance of the Theotokos, not on the question of suppression.11

Farley writes that the simple narrative of Mary’s entry into the Temple was adorned “in works such as the Proto-evangelium of James (i.e. a story containing a kind of prequel to the Gospel),” and that “Reading the entirety of the Proto-evangelium makes the discerning reader aware of the poetic and legendary nature of much of the writing.” He does not set the story aside on that account: “In this wonderful story one encounters devotion and love, not sober history. But no matter; truth comes in many forms, poetry as well as history. And by telling us that Mary dwelt in the Holy of Holies, the story tells us something fundamental and abidingly true about her.” The feast of the Entrance is kept on 21 November as one of the Great Feasts of the Orthodox Church; the Catholic calendar keeps the day as the memorial of the Presentation of the Blessed Virgin Mary.12

Reid, writing as a Catholic, says the Protevangelium’s first eleven chapters “are the source of various traditions current among the faithful” and show “the veneration paid to Mary at a very early age”: it first tells that Mary was the child of Joachim and Anna and was taken to the Temple at three. The work “has exercised an enormous influence on artistic imaginations throughout the centuries,” among them the mosaics of the Chora church in Constantinople. On this reading the book stood outside the canon, its story stayed within the worship of the Orthodox and Catholic churches, and their feasts of Mary’s childhood carry it still.13
