What does Jerome mean in writing that Judith “is found by the Nicene Council to have been counted among the number of the Sacred Scriptures”?

Disputed reading · on the Biblical canon · witness: Jerome

Related readings: What does the “Council of Nicaea” heading over Hippo’s canon-list in the Vallicellian codex (B.58) attest? · Does Cassiodorus’s naming of Nicaea among those who divided the sacred books (Institutiones I.14) attest a Nicene action on the canon? · Did the Council of Nicaea (325) decide the contents of the Bible?

The passage

Sed quia hunc librum Synodus Nicaena in numero sanctarum Scripturarum legitur computasse, acquievi postulationi vestrae, immo exactioni

But since the Nicene Council is considered to have counted this book among the number of sacred Scriptures, I have acquiesced to your request (or should I say demand!)

But because this book is found by the Nicene Council to have been counted among the number of the Sacred Scriptures, I have acquiesced to your request, indeed a demand

— Jerome, Preface to Judith (c. 405): the Latin of the Patrologia Latina (PL 29:37–39) with Andrew S. Jacobs’s translation, and Kevin P. Edgecomb’s translation (2006).1 The preface opens by placing Judith, among the Hebrews, “among the apocrypha” (Jacobs) or “among the Hagiographa” (Edgecomb), a book whose authority for settling disputed matters is “deemed less than sufficient.”

The question

Jerome gives the Council of Nicaea (325) as his reason for translating Judith, a book he otherwise did not rank with the canonical Scriptures, and no list of Scripture survives among the council’s acts. What is disputed is what Jerome’s sentence reports: a decree enumerating the sacred books, the council’s use of Judith as Scripture in its deliberations, a list circulating under the council’s name, or a report he relays without affirming it. The answer bears on whether any council before Hippo (393) acted on the extent of the Old Testament.

A report Jerome relays, of a book counted for reading rather than declared canonical

On this reading Jerome passes on what is “read” of the council without vouching for it, and “counted among the sacred Scriptures” means counted among the books read in church, not ranked as canonical.

Held by: William Whitaker (Protestant, Regius Professor of Divinity at Cambridge, Disputatio de Sacra Scriptura, 1588).2

Whitaker takes Jerome’s verb at its word: “Jerome’s words are, ‘We read that the synod of Nice counted this book in the number of sacred scriptures.’ But where this is read, he tells us not.” He adds that “Erasmus hath rightly noted, that Jerome does not himself affirm that this book was counted sacred scripture by the council of Nice.” His second point is a distinction of terms: “‘To be canonical scripture’ is one thing, and ‘to be counted in the number of sacred scripture’ is another thing. For those pieces which are read along with the sacred scriptures for the edification of the people, although not for confirmation of doctrines, are counted in the number of sacred scriptures.”3

Whitaker finds that distinction in Jerome’s own prefaces. In the preface to the books of Solomon Jerome writes: “As, then, the Church reads Judith, Tobit, and the books of Maccabees, but does not admit them among the canonical Scriptures, so let it read these two volumes for the edification of the people, not to give authority to doctrines of the Church”; and the Prologus Galeatus lists “Judith, and Tobias, and the Shepherd” among books that “are not in the canon.” The Judith preface itself gives the Hebrew classification and the corruption of the Chaldee copies as Jerome’s reasons for caution. Whitaker also sets the report beside the Council of Laodicea, “held a short time after that of Nice,” which left Judith off its list. On this reading the sentence attests a book counted among those read in the churches, which Jerome, in the same set of prefaces, keeps outside the canon.4

An allusion to a list of Scripture circulating under the council’s name

On this reading what Jerome had “read” was an index of the Scriptures bearing the name of Nicaea, not an act of the council of 325.

Held by: Vallarsi (“Vallarsius,” editor of Jerome’s works, Verona, 1738) ⚠ unverified, as reported by William Fitzgerald (Parker Society editor of Whitaker, 1849).5

Fitzgerald reports that “Most critics suppose that the council of Nice in some of their documents had quoted some testimony from the book of Judith: but Vallarsius thinks it more probable that Jerome alludes to some spurious index of the scriptures, forged under the name of that council. He appeals, very properly, to Cassiodorus, Instit. Divin. Lit. c. 14, to shew that such indexes existed, and passed under the names of the councils of Nice and Chalcedon.” Cassiodorus names “the Councils of Nicaea and Chalcedon” beside Hilary, Rufinus and Epiphanius among those who divided the sacred books.6

Other material circulated under Nicaea’s name in the same period. An eighth- or ninth-century codex in the Vallicelliana (B.58) heads the canon-list of the Council of Hippo (393) “Capituli XXIV. Nicaeni Concilii.” Hefele, who reads Jerome’s sentence differently (below), explains that the canons of several councils were early collected into one corpus in which “the canons of Nicæa always had the first place,” that “even at Rome there was a copy containing, sub uno titulo, the canons of Nicæa and those of Sardica,” and that later canons were in time attributed to Nicaea; he records that Pope Zosimus cited a canon of Sardica as Nicene, and quotes the Benedictines of St Maur supposing that Ambrose “may have read in his collectio of the Acts of Nicæa some rule … belonging to another synod.” On this reading Jerome’s “legitur” points to such a collection, and his sentence is evidence for what was attributed to Nicaea around 400.7

A use of Judith as Scripture in the council’s deliberations

On this reading the Nicene fathers quoted or appealed to Judith as Scripture, and so recognized it, without drawing up a list of the sacred books.

Held by: Karl Joseph von Hefele (Catholic, Conciliengeschichte, vol. 1, 1855); A. E. Breen (Catholic, A General Introduction to the Study of Holy Scripture, 2nd ed. 1908); John D. Meade (Protestant, Midwestern Baptist Theological Seminary, 2020).8

Hefele writes: “The meaning seems rather to be this: the Nicene Fathers quoted this book of Judith, that is to say, made use of it as a canonical book, and so in fact recognised it. In this way the Council of Ephesus implicitly acknowledged the Epistle to the Hebrews, by approving of the anathemas levelled by Cyril against Nestorius, in which this epistle is quoted as a book of the Bible.” The absence of such a quotation from the surviving records is no obstacle on his account: “the quotation may have been made viva voce in the Council; and this fact may have been laid hold of, and preserved in some document composed by a member of the Council.” He notes that Jerome “said only these words, ‘legitur computasse,’ that is to say, we read that the Council of Nicæa did so,” and that Gregory of Nazianzus, Amphilochius and the Council of Laodicea later left Judith out of their canons, which he reads as showing that no Nicene canon on the book existed.9

Breen holds the same: “any approbation of the book in the deliberations of the Council, would justify Jerome’s statement. We believe that the Nicene fathers recognized the deuterocanonical books as divine Scripture, but we hold that it is not sufficiently substantiated by historical data, that they drew up an official list of the Holy Scriptures.” Meade: “Jerome is probably describing discussions in which some fathers may have referred to Judith as scriptural.” On this reading the sentence records Judith’s use as Scripture at Nicaea, the ordinary way a council handled a book, and Jerome’s translation answers to that recognition.10

Testimony to a Nicene decree enumerating the sacred books

On this reading Jerome attests that the council of 325 listed the sacred books in a decree and received at least one deuterocanonical book, Judith, among them.

Held by: Rudolph Cornely (Catholic, Jesuit, Historicae et criticae introductionis in U.T. libros sacros compendium, 1891).11

Cornely calls Jerome’s words clear (“claris verbis”) and holds that they are not satisfied by a member of the synod praising the book or its being cited in the public acts: “fatendum est, synodum libros sacros in aliquo decreto enumerasse atque in eorum numerum saltem unum ex deuterocanonicis recepisse” (it must be admitted that the synod enumerated the sacred books in some decree and received at least one of the deuterocanonical books into their number). Jerome wrote some eighty years after the council and gives its action as the reason he set aside his own reluctance and translated the book.12

Cornely sets the sentence beside two further witnesses: Cassiodorus, who ranks the synod of Nicaea with Hilary, Rufinus and Epiphanius as having fitted the divine books to their sacred number “per divisiones suas”; and the Vallicellian codex that heads Hippo’s canon 36 “Capituli XXIV, Nicaeni Concilii” before a list holding both the protocanonical and deuterocanonical books. From these he concludes that the fathers of Hippo took their catalogue from the Nicene decrees that Caecilian of Carthage had brought back to Africa, and that “neque negare licet, Nicaenos Patres decretum de omnibus amplioris canonis libris in ecclesia velut Scripturis legendis tulisse” (nor may it be denied that the Nicene fathers issued a decree on all the books of the longer canon, to be read in the church as Scriptures). On this reading Jerome reports a Nicene enumeration that the African councils of 393–419 repeated.13
