Origen of Alexandria on the Biblical Canon

Dates: c. 248 (Commentary on Matthew Book X and the Homilies on Joshua from his Caesarean years, c. 240s; the Homilies on Luke somewhat earlier, c. 233–244; the Commentary on John begun c. 231; d. c. 253) · Wikipedia
Summary: Origen both enumerates the books of the New Testament and names gospels outside the four. In the Homilies on Joshua, casting the apostles as priests sounding the trumpets of Jericho, he runs through the four Gospels, the Acts, the epistles of Peter, James, and Jude, the letters of John "[and Revelation]," and Paul's "fourteen" epistles (the thirteen plus Hebrews) — the present twenty-seven books, with the Apocalypse bracketed because some manuscripts omit it (he affirms it elsewhere); Michael J. Kruger presents this as the earliest complete list of the New Testament.1 Origen also names "the Gospel according to Thomas," "according to Matthias," and "the Book of James" (the Protoevangelium), distinguishing them from the four that "the church of God has approved."

Claims & evidence

The whole New Testament enumerated — the apostles' "priestly trumpets"

But when our Lord Jesus Christ comes, whose arrival that prior son of Nun designated, he sends priests, his apostles, bearing "trumpets hammered thin," the magnificent and heavenly instruction of proclamation. Matthew first sounded the priestly trumpet in his Gospel; Mark also; Luke and John each played their own priestly trumpets. Even Peter cries out with trumpets in two of his epistles; also James and Jude. In addition, John also sounds the trumpet through his epistles [and Revelation], and Luke, as he describes the Acts of the Apostles. And now that last one comes, the one who said, "I think God displays us apostles last," and in fourteen of his epistles, thundering with trumpets, he casts down the walls of Jericho and all the devices of idolatry and dogmas of philosophers, all the way to the foundations.

— Homilies on Joshua 7.1 (on Joshua 6, the fall of Jericho), trans. B. J. Bruce (Fathers of the Church 105), as cited by Michael J. Kruger, "What is the Earliest Complete List of the Canon of the New Testament?"1 The allegory of the apostolic "trumpets" names in turn the four Gospels (Matthew, Mark, Luke, John), Acts (Luke "describes the Acts of the Apostles"), the two epistles of Peter, James, Jude, the epistles of John "[and Revelation]," and — in "that last one," Paul — fourteen epistles. Origen's fourteen are the thirteen letters bearing Paul's name together with Hebrews (whose authorship he elsewhere leaves open while keeping the letter in the count), so the total matches the modern twenty-seven, with "[and Revelation]" bracketed in the translation because some manuscripts of the homily lack it.

"The wells of the New Testament" — the concise list

Isaac, therefore, digs also new wells, nay rather Isaac's servants dig them. Isaac's servants are Matthew, Mark, Luke, John; his servants are Peter, James, Jude; the apostle Paul is his servant. These all dig the wells of the New Testament.

— Homilies on Genesis 13.2 (on Isaac's wells, Genesis 26), trans. R. E. Heine (Fathers of the Church 71), as cited at carm.org.2 A terser form of the same enumeration: the evangelists and apostles — Matthew, Mark, Luke, John, Peter, James, Jude, Paul — are the "servants" who dig "the wells of the New Testament," Origen's image for the apostolic writings.

Revelation, and the epistles "not all pronounce" genuine

And Peter, on whom the Church of Christ is built, against which the gates of hell shall not prevail left only one epistle of acknowledged genuineness. Suppose we allow that he left a second; for this is doubtful. What are we to say of him who leaned on Jesus' breast, namely, John, who left one Gospel, though confessing that he could make so many that the world would not contain them? But he wrote also the Apocalypse, being commanded to be silent and not to write the voices of the seven thunders. But he also left an epistle of very few lines. Suppose also a second and a third, since not all pronounce these to be genuine; but the two together do not amount to a hundred lines.

— Commentary on John 5.3 (on how sparingly the apostles wrote), ANF 9, historicalchristian.faith.3 Here Origen writes that John "wrote also the Apocalypse." He also reports doubts: Peter's second epistle is "doubtful," and of John's second and third "not all pronounce these to be genuine." Eusebius, c. 324, lists 2 Peter and 2–3 John among the antilegomena.

The four "approved" by the church; Thomas, Matthias, and the others "attempts"

For Matthew did not "attempt," but wrote by the Holy Spirit, as did Mark and John, and similarly Luke. However, those who wrote the Gospel according to the Egyptians and the Gospel according to the Twelve "attempted" to write. And already Basilides also dared to write his Gospel according to Basilides. "Many, therefore, have taken in hand" — for there is also the Gospel according to Thomas and the Gospel according to Matthias and several others. These are attempts; the church of God has approved only the four.

— Homilies on Luke 1 (on Luke 1:1, "Many have taken in hand"), trans. Lienhard (FOTC 94), historicalchristian.faith.4 Reading Luke's "many have taken in hand to draw up a narrative" as a criticism, Origen sorts the gospels into two classes: the four the church has "approved," written "by the Holy Spirit," and the "many" others — Thomas and Matthias among them — that were "attempted." He grounds the distinction in the church's approval; he names no decree.

A tradition from "the Book of James"

But some say, basing it on a tradition in the Gospel according to Peter, as it is entitled, or The Book of James, that the brethren of Jesus were sons of Joseph by a former wife, whom he married before Mary. Now those who say so wish to preserve the honour of Mary in virginity to the end …

— Commentary on Matthew X.17 (on Matt 13:55, "Is not this the carpenter's son?"), ANF 9, newadvent.org.5 The "Book of James" is the Protoevangelium of James; the "Gospel according to Peter" is the apocryphal gospel of that name. Origen reports the tradition they carry — that the Lord's "brethren" were Joseph's sons by an earlier marriage — as what "some say, basing it on a tradition" in those books, and notes that those who say so "wish to preserve the honour of Mary in virginity to the end." (The same passage appears on the perpetual-virginity timeline, on Origen's detail page there, at Does the second-century stepbrother tradition preserve historical memory of Jesus’ family?, and beside the Protevangelium of James itself.)

Reception and debate

  • Enumeration and exclusion. Origen gives both a positive enumeration of the books (the priestly trumpets, the wells of the New Testament) and a list of gospels which, he says, the church did not approve (the "attempts"). How such evidence is read in accounts of the canon's formation is set out at How did the churches come to know the New Testament canon, and what settled its disputed books?
  • Twenty-six or twenty-seven. Origen's Joshua list reaches the full modern count, with Revelation bracketed in the translation (some manuscripts omit it); he affirms John's authorship of the Apocalypse in the Commentary on John, and marks 2 Peter and 2–3 John as "doubtful" or not universally "genuine." His fourteen Pauline epistles include Hebrews, whose authorship he elsewhere leaves open ("who wrote the epistle, in truth God knows") while keeping the letter in the count.
  • His terms. Origen's terms on the excluded side are approval and reception: the four are what the church has "approved" and what was written "by the Holy Spirit," and the rest are "attempts" or carriers of a "tradition." Eusebius (Church History 3.25) calls such books "the fictions of heretics," and Innocent I (405) says they are "condemned."
  • The title without contents. Origen's list — "the Gospel according to Thomas … and several others" — gives no contents, so it does not say which work of that name he meant: the sayings gospel or the infancy narrative.
  • Named, not received. Origen names these books and declines to receive them; he does not speak of destroying them. Whether the apocryphal gospels were afterwards suppressed is set out at Were the infancy gospels suppressed — banned, voted out, gathered up and burned?
