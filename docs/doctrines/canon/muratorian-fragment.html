The Muratorian Fragment on the Biblical Canon

Dates: c. 170 · Wikipedia
Summary: The Muratorian Fragment is an eighty-five-line Latin catalogue of New-Testament books, mutilated at both ends, named for Ludovico Antonio Muratori, who published it in 1740 from an eighth-century codex in the Ambrosian Library at Milan. On its traditional second-century dating it is the oldest extant list of New-Testament books; A. C. Sundberg and Geoffrey Hahneman date it to the fourth century (see below). It lists the four Gospels (Matthew and Mark assumed in the broken opening), Acts, the thirteen Pauline epistles, Jude, two epistles of John, and the Apocalypse of John — some twenty-two of the twenty-seven books of the later New Testament. It does not list James, either epistle of Peter, Hebrews, or 3 John; it ranks the Wisdom of Solomon among the received writings; it accepts the Apocalypse of Peter (while noting that "some of us are not willing that the latter be read in church"); it allows the Shepherd of Hermas to be read but not publicly "among the Apostles," because it was written "very recently"; and it rejects epistles it calls "forged in Paul's name" and the writings of Valentinus, Basilides, and others.

Claims & evidence

The four Gospels — "the fourth of the Gospels is that of John"

The third book of the Gospel is that according to Luke. Luke, the well-known physician … (9) The fourth of the Gospels is that of John, [one] of the disciples. … though various elements may be taught in the individual books of the Gospels, nevertheless this makes no difference to the faith of believers, since by the one sovereign Spirit all things have been declared in all [the Gospels]: concerning the nativity, concerning the passion, concerning the resurrection, concerning life with his disciples, and concerning his twofold coming.

— lines 2–26, source (trans. B. M. Metzger).1 The fragment as extant begins in mid-sentence, on what is taken to be the close of an account of Mark; it then numbers Luke "the third book" and John "the fourth," from which the lost opening is inferred to have treated Matthew and Mark. The list narrates each Gospel's origin (Luke the physician, who "had not seen the Lord in the flesh"; John writing at his fellow-disciples' urging) and holds that, whatever "various elements" the individual books teach, "by the one sovereign Spirit all things have been declared in all." Irenaeus (c. 180) argues for four Gospels with his image of the four-faced cherubim.

Acts and the thirteen letters of Paul, "to seven churches"

Moreover, the acts of all the apostles were written in one book. … As for the Epistles of Paul … the blessed apostle Paul himself, following the example of his predecessor John, writes by name to only seven churches … To the Corinthians first, to the Ephesians second, to the Philippians third, to the Colossians fourth, to the Galatians fifth, to the Thessalonians sixth, to the Romans seventh. … [Paul also wrote] out of affection and love one to Philemon, one to Titus, and two to Timothy.

— lines 34–63, source. The fragment receives Acts ("of all the apostles … in one book") and the full thirteen-letter Pauline corpus, which it organizes by the conceit that Paul, like John in Revelation, wrote "to seven churches" — the nine community-letters reckoned as seven addressees (Corinth and Thessalonica each written twice) plus the four to individuals (Philemon, Titus, two to Timothy), "held sacred … for the regulation of ecclesiastical discipline." It does not list Hebrews, which Origen and later Eastern lists count with Paul's letters and whose authorship Jerome reports was doubted in the West.

Jude, two of John's epistles — and the Wisdom of Solomon among them

Moreover, the epistle of Jude and two of the above-mentioned (or, bearing the name of) John are counted (or, used) in the catholic [Church]; and [the book of] Wisdom, written by the friends of Solomon in his honour.

— lines 68–70, source. Of the seven Catholic Epistles the list names only Jude and "two … of John" (taken to be 1 and 2 John, or possibly 1 John with the two shorter letters reckoned as one) — James, 1 and 2 Peter, and 3 John are absent. In the same sentence it names the Wisdom of Solomon, a book of the longer Old Testament, within its New-Testament reckoning.

The Apocalypse of John — and of Peter, "though some … are not willing that it be read"

We receive only the apocalypses of John and Peter, though some of us are not willing that the latter be read in church.

— lines 71–72, source. The list receives the Apocalypse of John (Revelation), which Cyril of Jerusalem (c. 350) omits from his list. Beside it it receives the Apocalypse of Peter, which is not among the books of the later canon-lists, adding that "some of us are not willing that the latter be read in church."

The Shepherd of Hermas — read, but "after [the apostles'] time"

But Hermas wrote the Shepherd very recently, in our times, in the city of Rome, while bishop Pius, his brother, was occupying the [episcopal] chair of the church of the city of Rome. And therefore it ought indeed to be read; but it cannot be read publicly to the people in church either among the Prophets, whose number is complete, or among the Apostles, for it is after [their] time.

— lines 73–80, source. The compiler states a rule: the Shepherd of Hermas — cited as "the Scripture" by Irenaeus (c. 180) — "ought indeed to be read," but may not be read publicly in church "either among the Prophets, whose number is complete, or among the Apostles, for it is after [their] time." The ground given is date: the book was written "very recently, in our times." (The same notice supplies the fragment's chief dating clue — "very recently, in our times" under bishop Pius, who held the Roman see c. 140–154; see the note below.)

Writings not received — epistles “forged in Paul's name” and the teachers' books

There is current also [an epistle] to the Laodiceans, [and] another to the Alexandrians, [both] forged in Paul's name to [further] the heresy of Marcion, and several others which cannot be received into the catholic Church — for it is not fitting that gall be mixed with honey. … But we accept nothing whatever of Arsinous or Valentinus or Miltiades, who also composed a new book of psalms for Marcion, together with Basilides, the Asian founder of the Cataphrygians.

— lines 63–85, source. The list closes (before breaking off) with its exclusions: two epistles "forged in Paul's name" for the Marcionites — to the Laodiceans and the Alexandrians — and the writings of the Gnostic and Marcionite teachers (Valentinus, Basilides, and the rest), rejected because "it is not fitting that gall be mixed with honey." The fragment thus carries both a list of received books and a list of books not received. Later lists of books "not to be received" appear on the timeline as the rejection-lists (c. 500–810).

Context and dating

  • The dating debate. The surviving manuscript is an eighth-century Latin copy of an older text. The traditional dating — defended by Tregelles, Harnack, and Bruce Metzger — rests on the fragment's own words that the Shepherd of Hermas was written "very recently, in our times" under bishop Pius (c. 140–154), placing the list in the later second century, c. 170–200, at Rome. A. C. Sundberg (1968–73) and Geoffrey Hahneman (1992) date it instead to a fourth-century Eastern setting (c. 350–375), reading the "our times" notice as a deliberate archaism. Metzger judged Everett Ferguson's reply to Sundberg's "anomalous in the West" argument to "all but demolish" the thesis; Michael J. Kruger reports that Joseph Verheyden finds none of the late-dating arguments convincing; Eckhard Schnabel (2014) surveys the state of the question. The timeline places the fragment at c. 170, the traditional date; on the later dating it would be an early list rather than the earliest extant one.2
  • The lacuna. The fragment is mutilated at the beginning (and breaks off at the end), so its treatment of Matthew and Mark is lost; their presence is inferred from the surviving text's numbering of Luke as "the third book" and John as "the fourth."
  • Books inside and outside the list. Books not among the later canon-lists stand inside the fragment's list (the Apocalypse of Peter, the Wisdom of Solomon, the Shepherd to be read but not publicly), while several later-listed books are absent (James, 1–2 Peter, Hebrews, 3 John). How such evidence is read in accounts of the canon's formation is set out at How did the churches come to know the New Testament canon, and what settled its disputed books?
  • The fragment names Wisdom "written by the friends of Solomon in his honour." On the traditional dating the line is also an early Western witness to the Wisdom of Solomon received among the Scriptures — it is cited for that on the Old Testament canon timeline.
