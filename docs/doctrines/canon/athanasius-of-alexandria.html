Athanasius of Alexandria on the Biblical Canon

Dates: c. 367 (the thirty-ninth Festal [Easter] Letter, issued for the year 367; Athanasius c. 296/298–373) · Wikipedia · his Old Testament canon page
Summary: In the annual Easter letter of 367 the bishop of Alexandria lists the books "included in the Canon, and handed down, and accredited as Divine" — a New-Testament catalogue that matches the present twenty-seven: the four Gospels, Acts, the seven Catholic Epistles, fourteen of Paul (Hebrews counted in), and the Revelation of John. He closes the list with "let no man add to these, neither let him take ought from these," and presents the listing as his own undertaking, written "of necessity," of books "handed down." Alongside it stands a twenty-two-book Hebrew Old Testament, with Wisdom, Sirach, Esther, Judith, Tobit and others set in a second rank as "read" but not canonical.

Claims & evidence

The New Testament: a list of the present twenty-seven

Again it is not tedious to speak of the [books] of the New Testament. These are, the four Gospels, according to Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John. Afterwards, the Acts of the Apostles and Epistles (called Catholic), seven, viz. of James, one; of Peter, two; of John, three; after these, one of Jude. In addition, there are fourteen Epistles of Paul, written in this order. The first, to the Romans; then two to the Corinthians; after these, to the Galatians; next, to the Ephesians; then to the Philippians; then to the Colossians; after these, two to the Thessalonians, and that to the Hebrews; and again, two to Timothy; one to Titus; and lastly, that to Philemon. And besides, the Revelation of John.

— Festal Letter 39.5, trans. NPNF 2.4, historicalchristian.faith (also at New Advent).1 The catalogue's New Testament is the modern twenty-seven: four Gospels, Acts, seven Catholic Epistles, fourteen Paulines, and the Apocalypse, in the order he gives. Michael J. Kruger notes that Athanasius's list is commonly "mentioned as the first time this happened," and argues that Origen's Homilies on Joshua 7.1, "more than a hundred years before Athanasius," already enumerates all twenty-seven.2 His "fourteen Epistles of Paul" count the Epistle to the Hebrews with Paul's letters — the same count given by Origen and Cyril. Cyril, a generation earlier, had listed twenty-six books, without Revelation; Athanasius closes his list with "the Revelation of John."

"Let no man add to these, neither let him take ought from these"

These are fountains of salvation, that they who thirst may be satisfied with the living words they contain. In these alone is proclaimed the doctrine of godliness. Let no man add to these, neither let him take ought from these. For concerning these the Lord put to shame the Sadducees, and said, `Ye do err, not knowing the Scriptures.' And He reproved the Jews, saying, `Search the Scriptures, for these are they that testify of Me.'

— Festal Letter 39.6, historicalchristian.faith (also at New Advent).3 Athanasius forbids adding to or taking from the books listed, calling them the books in which "alone is proclaimed the doctrine of godliness." He grounds the list in what was "handed down" (39.3, below).

The frame: a list "handed down," set out "of necessity"

… it seemed good to me also, having been urged thereto by true brethren, and having learned from the beginning, to set before you the books included in the Canon, and handed down, and accredited as Divine …

— Festal Letter 39.3, historicalchristian.faith.4 Taking up "the pattern of Luke the Evangelist" against those who "have taken in hand … to reduce into order for themselves the books termed apocryphal, and to mix them up with the divinely inspired Scripture," Athanasius presents the catalogue as his own undertaking — written "of necessity" (39.7), urged by "true brethren," recording the books "handed down" from the eyewitnesses to the fathers. Athanasius had attended the Council of Nicaea (325) forty-two years earlier; how this framing bears on the question whether Nicaea took up the canon is noted below.

The Old Testament: twenty-two books, with a "read but not canonical" second rank

But for greater exactness I add this also, writing of necessity; that there are other books besides these not indeed included in the Canon, but appointed by the Fathers to be read by those who newly join us, and who wish for instruction in the word of godliness. The Wisdom of Solomon, and the Wisdom of Sirach, and Esther, and Judith, and Tobit, and that which is called the Teaching of the Apostles, and the Shepherd.

— Festal Letter 39.7, historicalchristian.faith.5 Athanasius's Old Testament is the twenty-two-book Hebrew reckoning (39.4) — the same count Cyril gives — and he names Wisdom, Sirach, Esther, Judith, and Tobit in a middle class: not "included in the Canon," but "appointed by the Fathers to be read" by catechumens, while counting Baruch and the Epistle of Jeremiah with Jeremiah within the canon. The letter thus sets out three classes: canonical, appointed to be read, and the "apocryphal," which he calls "an invention of heretics." The African councils of 393–397 list Tobit, Judith, and Esther among the canonical Scriptures. How Athanasius's middle class has been read is treated on his Old Testament canon page; this page treats the New-Testament list.

Reception and debate

  • A bishop's Paschal letter. Letter 39 is an annual Easter letter of the bishop of Alexandria, not a council's decree. How its list relates to the canon's formation is set out on the page How did the churches come to know the New Testament canon, and what settled its disputed books?
  • The Nicaea question. Athanasius attended Nicaea (325) as the deacon and aide of bishop Alexander. A. E. Breen (Catholic, 1908) reasons from Letter 39 that "St. Athanasius would not have declared that it was a bold and difficult thing to fix the list of the Holy Books, had there been promulgated a catalogue of the same by a council of which he was an important factor."6 The readings of the Nicaea question are set out at Did the Council of Nicaea (325) decide the contents of the Bible?; the letter also bears on what Jerome meant by Nicaea's counting Judith, since Athanasius places Judith among the books "appointed … to be read."
  • Read, not read, and the apocrypha. Athanasius's terms are of reception and reading: the canonical books are "handed down and accredited as Divine," the second class "appointed by the Fathers to be read," and the apocrypha called "an invention of heretics." Whether the apocryphal gospels were afterwards suppressed is set out at Were the infancy gospels suppressed?
