Jerome on the New Testament Canon

Dates: c. 394 (De viris illustribus 392–393; the Vulgate Gospels, to Damasus, 383; Letter 53 to Paulinus c. 394; Letter 129 to Dardanus c. 414; d. 420) · Wikipedia
Summary: Jerome, translator of the Latin Vulgate, revised the four Gospels for Pope Damasus (383), setting them in the order Matthew, Mark, Luke, John, and surveys the whole New Testament book by book in his letters. He also records which books were disputed: the Western doubt that Hebrews is Paul's (Tertullian ascribing it to Barnabas), the doubts over 2 Peter, James, and the two shorter epistles of John (assigned by some to "John the presbyter"), and the Greek churches' reserve over the Apocalypse. In Letter 129 (c. 414) he writes of Hebrews and the Apocalypse, "yet we receive them both," on "the authority of the ancient writers."

Claims & evidence

The four Gospels, revised and set in order — Matthew, Mark, Luke, John (Vulgate preface to Damasus, 383)

I therefore promise in this short Preface the four Gospels only, which are to be taken in the following order, Matthew, Mark, Luke, John, as they have been revised by a comparison of the Greek manuscripts. Only early ones have been used.

— Prefaces to the Vulgate Version of the New Testament, "The Four Gospels," addressed to Pope Damasus, a.d. 383, trans. W. H. Fremantle (NPNF 2.6), historicalchristian.faith.1 Commissioned by Damasus — "the command is given by you who are the supreme bishop" — Jerome's aim was to correct the corrupted Old Latin against the Greek, "to revise the old Latin, and not to make a new version." He promises "the four Gospels only," and affirms the Hebrew original of Matthew, "the first to commit to writing the Gospel of Christ." His stated labour is textual, going "back to the fountainhead."

Hebrews and its authorship (De viris illustribus 5)

The epistle which is called the Epistle to the Hebrews is not considered his, on account of its difference from the others in style and language, but it is reckoned, either according to Tertullian to be the work of Barnabas, or according to others, to be by Luke the Evangelist or Clement afterwards bishop of the church at Rome … Some read one also to the Laodiceans but it is rejected by everyone.

— Lives of Illustrious Men (De viris illustribus) 5, on Paul, trans. E. C. Richardson (NPNF 2.3), historicalchristian.faith (also at New Advent).2 After listing Paul's letters "to the Corinthians two, to the Galatians one" and the rest, Jerome sets Hebrews apart, reporting that it "is not considered his" on grounds of style and language and is ascribed instead to Barnabas (Tertullian), Luke, or Clement of Rome. The council of Carthage (397) likewise lists "one epistle … to the Hebrews" apart from Paul's "thirteen." Jerome returns to Hebrews in his letter to Dardanus (below).

2 Peter and James (De viris illustribus 1, 2)

He wrote two epistles which are called Catholic, the second of which, on account of its difference from the first in style, is considered by many not to be by him.

— Lives of Illustrious Men 1, on Simon Peter, historicalchristian.faith (New Advent). And of James:

James … wrote a single epistle, which is reckoned among the seven Catholic Epistles and even this is claimed by some to have been published by some one else under his name, and gradually, as time went on, to have gained authority.

— Lives of Illustrious Men 2, on James the brother of the Lord, historicalchristian.faith.3 Jerome reports the doubts book by book: 2 Peter doubted by many for its difference in style from 1 Peter, James claimed by some to have been published under his name and "gradually, as time went on, to have gained authority." Eusebius had listed both among the antilegomena, disputed yet "recognized by many." Jerome includes both in his survey of the New Testament in Letter 53 (below).

The two shorter Johannine epistles and "John the presbyter" (De viris illustribus 9)

He wrote also one Epistle which begins as follows "That which was from the beginning, that which we have heard, that which we have seen with our eyes and our hands handled concerning the word of life" which is esteemed of by all men who are interested in the church or in learning. The other two … are said to be the work of John the presbyter to the memory of whom another sepulchre is shown at Ephesus to the present day.

— Lives of Illustrious Men 9, on the Apostle John, historicalchristian.faith (New Advent).4 The first epistle of John is received "by all"; the two shorter ones (2 and 3 John) some assign to a separate figure, "John the presbyter," to whom a second tomb was shown at Ephesus — the authorship question that runs back through Papias and Eusebius. Jerome reports the doubt and, in the same chapter, credits the Apostle with the Apocalypse he "wrote … on which Justin Martyr and Irenaeus afterwards wrote commentaries."

The whole New Testament surveyed — Gospels, Paul "to seven churches," the Catholic Epistles, the Apocalypse (Letter 53 to Paulinus, c. 394)

The New Testament I will briefly deal with. Matthew, Mark, Luke and John are the Lord's team of four, the true cherubim or store of knowledge. … The apostle Paul writes to seven churches (for the eighth epistle—that to the Hebrews—is not generally counted in with the others). He instructs Timothy and Titus; he intercedes with Philemon for his runaway slave. … The apostles James, Peter, John, and Jude, have published seven epistles at once spiritual and to the point … The apocalypse of John has as many mysteries as words.

— Letter 53, to Paulinus of Nola, §9, trans. W. H. Fremantle (NPNF 2.6), historicalchristian.faith (also at New Advent).5 Surveying the whole Bible book by book for Paulinus, Jerome runs through the New Testament: the four Gospels as the "true cherubim" (the image of the four living creatures also used by Irenaeus), the letters of Paul "to seven churches," Timothy, Titus, and Philemon, the seven Catholic Epistles of James, Peter, John, and Jude, and the Apocalypse "of John." He notes that the epistle to the Hebrews "is not generally counted in with the others."

"We receive them both" — Hebrews and the Apocalypse (Letter 129 to Dardanus, c. 414)

It must be said by us, however, that this letter which is entitled to the Hebrews, was not only received by the churches of the Orient but by all the earlier writers of ecclesiastical Greek, as being the one received by Paul the Apostle, even though most people believe that it belongs either to Barnabas or to Clement … But even if the custom of the Latins does not include it among the canonical Scriptures, neither indeed do the Greek churches receive the Apocalypse of John with the same freedom; yet we receive them both, following not the custom of this time, but the authority of the ancient writers.

— Letter 129, to Dardanus, §3 (also at New Advent), historicalchristian.faith.6 Jerome writes that the Latins do not include Hebrews and the Greek churches do not receive the Apocalypse "with the same freedom," and that "we receive them both," following "the authority of the ancient writers who frequently use both testimonies … as if they were canonical and ecclesiastical." He names no council in the passage.

Reception and debate

  • Jerome and the synod of 382. Jerome was at Rome in 382 and became Damasus's secretary; his writings on the canon do not mention a canon-list promulgated by that synod. G. M. Hahneman (1992) adduces this silence against the ascription of the "Damasine list" to the synod; the readings are set out at Did the Roman synod of 382 under Damasus promulgate the canon-list preserved in the Decretum Gelasianum?
  • His Old-Testament canon. This page treats Jerome's New-Testament witness. His Old-Testament work — the "Hebrew verity," his ranking of Judith, Tobit, and the Maccabees "among the Apocryphal writings," and his Preface to Judith, which says the book "is found by the Nicene Council to have been counted among the number of the Sacred Scriptures" — is treated on the Old Testament canon timeline. What the Judith sentence means is set out at What does Jerome mean in writing that Judith “is found by the Nicene Council to have been counted among the number of the Sacred Scriptures”?
  • Jerome and Eusebius. Each dispute Jerome records — Hebrews, 2 Peter, James, 2–3 John, the Apocalypse — is one Eusebius had already noted among the antilegomena or as doubted (see Eusebius of Caesarea). Athanasius (367) and Innocent I (405) list all twenty-seven.
  • Revision of a received text. Jerome describes his Gospel work as an aim "to revise the old Latin, and not to make a new version." How the canon's formation in this period is read is set out at How did the churches come to know the New Testament canon, and what settled its disputed books?
