Tertullian on the Biblical Canon

Dates: c. 207 (Against Marcion IV and The Prescription Against Heretics, Carthage; d. c. 220) · Wikipedia
Summary: Writing in Carthage, Tertullian answers Marcion's single edited Gospel of Luke with four Gospels, authored by apostles (Matthew, John) and "apostolic men" (Mark, "whose interpreter Mark was" of Peter; Luke, whose Gospel "men usually ascribe to Paul"). Against the heretics he directs inquirers to the churches founded by apostles, "in which their own authentic writings are read." He calls the Shepherd of Hermas "apocryphal and false," attributes Hebrews to Barnabas, and defends the book of Enoch as Scripture, acknowledging that it "is not received by some."

Claims & evidence

The four Gospels: apostles and apostolic men, against Marcion’s Luke

We lay it down as our first position, that the evangelical Testament has apostles for its authors, to whom was assigned by the Lord Himself this office of publishing the gospel. Since, however, there are apostolic men also, they are yet not alone, but appear with apostles and after apostles … Of the apostles, therefore, John and Matthew first instil faith into us; whilst of apostolic men, Luke and Mark renew it afterwards … Now, of the authors whom we possess, Marcion seems to have singled out Luke for his mutilating process.

— Against Marcion IV.2, historicalchristian.faith (also at New Advent).1 Tertullian answers Marcion — who used one edited Gospel of Luke and “ascribes no author to his Gospel” — by assigning the Gospels to apostles (Matthew, John) and to “apostolic men” (Luke, Mark). A few chapters on he ties each of the four to an apostolic church or an apostle:

The same authority of the apostolic churches will afford evidence to the other Gospels also, which we possess equally through their means, and according to their usage — I mean the Gospels of John and Matthew — whilst that which Mark published may be affirmed to be Peter’s whose interpreter Mark was. For even Luke’s form of the Gospel men usually ascribe to Paul.

— Against Marcion IV.5, historicalchristian.faith (also at New Advent).1 Mark is “the interpreter” of Peter and Luke’s Gospel is ascribed to Paul; Tertullian argues that Marcion’s unattributed, edited Gospel is later than the four. Irenaeus had argued c. 180 that the Gospels are exactly four; how that passage has been read is set out at Does Irenaeus’s argument for four Gospels attest a fourfold Gospel already closed by c. 180?

The apostles’ “authentic writings,” read in the apostolic churches

Come now, you who would indulge a better curiosity, if you would apply it to the business of your salvation, run over the apostolic churches, in which the very thrones of the apostles are still pre-eminent in their places, in which their own authentic writings are read, uttering the voice and representing the face of each of them severally. Achaia is very near you, (in which) you find Corinth … Since you are able to cross to Asia, you get Ephesus. Since, moreover, you are close upon Italy, you have Rome, from which there comes even into our own hands the very authority (of apostles themselves).

— The Prescription Against Heretics 36, historicalchristian.faith (also at New Advent).2 Against the heretics, Tertullian directs inquirers to the churches the apostles founded — Corinth, Philippi, Thessalonica, Ephesus, Rome — where their “authentic writings” (authenticae litterae) are still read publicly. He names no list of books here; the appeal is to the churches of apostolic foundation and the writings read in them. The meaning of authenticae litterae is discussed under Reception and debate below.

Calls the Shepherd of Hermas “apocryphal and false”

But I would yield my ground to you, if the scripture of “the Shepherd,” which is the only one which favours adulterers, had deserved to find a place in the Divine canon; if it had not been habitually judged by every council of Churches (even of your own) among apocryphal and false (writings); itself adulterous, and hence a patroness of its comrades …

— On Modesty (De pudicitia) 10, historicalchristian.faith (also at New Advent).3 Irenaeus, about a generation earlier, had introduced a sentence of the Shepherd of Hermas as “Scripture.” In On Modesty Tertullian says the Shepherd has not “deserved to find a place in the Divine canon” and has been “judged by every council of Churches … among apocryphal and false” writings. He returns to the book ten chapters later, calling it “that apocryphal ‘Shepherd’ of adulterers.”

Ascribes the Epistle to the Hebrews to Barnabas

For there is extant withal an Epistle to the Hebrews under the name of Barnabas — a man sufficiently accredited by God, as being one whom Paul has stationed next to himself in the uninterrupted observance of abstinence … And, of course, the Epistle of Barnabas is more generally received among the Churches than that apocryphal “Shepherd” of adulterers.

— On Modesty (De pudicitia) 20, historicalchristian.faith (also at New Advent).3 Tertullian quotes Hebrews (6:4–8) in his case against a second repentance and names its author as Barnabas — “an Epistle to the Hebrews under the name of Barnabas” — rather than Paul, and sets it above the Shepherd as “more generally received among the Churches.”

Defends the book of Enoch as Scripture

I am aware that the Scripture of Enoch, which has assigned this order (of action) to angels, is not received by some, because it is not admitted into the Jewish canon either … But since Enoch in the same Scripture has preached likewise concerning the Lord, nothing at all must be rejected by us which pertains to us; and we read that “every Scripture suitable for edification is divinely inspired.” … To these considerations is added the fact that Enoch possesses a testimony in the Apostle Jude.

— On the Apparel of Women (De cultu feminarum) I.3, historicalchristian.faith (also at New Advent).4 Tertullian records that 1 Enoch “is not received by some, because it is not admitted into the Jewish canon either,” and defends it as Scripture on the grounds that Enoch “preached likewise concerning the Lord” and that the book “possesses a testimony in the Apostle Jude” (Jude 14–15). The wider questions are set out at Was 1 Enoch received as Scripture in the early churches? and, on the Old-Testament side, at Does Jude’s citation of 1 Enoch as prophecy show that the Old Testament canon was open in the apostolic age?

Reception and debate

  • Placement on the timeline. Tertullian (c. 207) stands between Irenaeus (c. 180) and Origen (c. 248). In the same works he rejects the Shepherd of Hermas, defends Enoch, and ascribes Hebrews to Barnabas; how the reception of the disputed books has been read is set out at How did the churches come to know the New Testament canon, and what settled its disputed books?
  • “Authentic writings.” The Latin authenticae litterae of Prescription 36 has been read in more than one way. Honoré Coppieters (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1907) considers and sets aside the sense “the original text — the Greek,” because the Greek “was still read nearly everywhere,” and concludes that the phrase means “the autographs of some Epistles of the Apostles.” S. L. Greenslade (Early Latin Theology, 1956) annotates: “‘authentic’ will scarcely mean autograph; he means unmutilated texts.” Roger Pearse (2019), comparing Tertullian’s one other use of the word (On Monogamy 11), reads the subject as “honest copies instead of heretical forgeries or corrupt translations.”5 B. M. Metzger discusses Tertullian’s canon in The Canon of the New Testament (1987).
  • Montanism and later judgements. Tertullian’s later writings defend the Montanist “New Prophecy.” How he was judged afterwards, and whether he left the catholic Church, is traced on the timeline “Was Tertullian a heretic?”
