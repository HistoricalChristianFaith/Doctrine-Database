Justin Martyr on the Biblical Canon

Dates: c. 155 · Wikipedia
Summary: Writing his First Apology at Rome around 155, Justin calls the Gospels the apostles' "memoirs … which are called Gospels," and reports that on Sundays "the memoirs of the apostles or the writings of the prophets are read" in the assembly — the apostolic records read in worship beside the prophets. He does not enumerate the books or give their number.

Claims & evidence

The Gospels are the apostles' eyewitness "memoirs"

For the apostles, in the memoirs composed by them, which are called Gospels, have thus delivered unto us what was enjoined upon them; that Jesus took bread, and when He had given thanks, said, "This do ye in remembrance of Me, this is My body;" and that, after the same manner, having taken the cup and given thanks, He said, "This is My blood;" and gave it to them alone.

— The First Apology, ch. 66, source. Justin's term for the Gospels is apomnēmoneumata, "memoirs" or "recollections" — the word a Greek reader would use for a record of a teacher's words and deeds (Xenophon's Memorabilia of Socrates). He presents them as the apostles' own record of what Jesus said and did, and adds that they "are called Gospels." Here he cites them as the authority for the words of institution at the Eucharist.

The memoirs are read in worship, beside the prophets

And on the day called Sunday, all who live in cities or in the country gather together to one place, and the memoirs of the apostles or the writings of the prophets are read, as long as time permits; then, when the reader has ceased, the president verbally instructs, and exhorts to the imitation of these good things.

— The First Apology, ch. 67, source. The apostolic "memoirs" are read aloud in the Sunday assembly alongside "the writings of the prophets," the Old-Testament Scriptures, "as long as time permits," before the president's instruction. (In his Dialogue with Trypho Justin again calls these books "the memoirs … which were drawn up by His apostles and those who followed them," tying the Gospels to apostles and apostolic men alike.)

Placement on the timeline

  • Justin follows Marcion (c. 144) on the timeline and precedes the Muratorian Fragment (dated c. 170 on the traditional dating) and Irenaeus (c. 180), who argues that the Gospels are exactly four.
  • In these passages Justin does not enumerate the Gospels, name Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John individually, or list the Pauline or Catholic epistles. He speaks of "memoirs" in the plural without giving their number, and his Gospel quotations sometimes blend or harmonize the texts.
  • The same two passages are also early evidence for Sunday worship and the Eucharist; here they are cited for what they say about the Gospel-books.
