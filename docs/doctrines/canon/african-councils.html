The African councils of Hippo and Carthage on the Biblical Canon

Dates: 393 (the Synod of Hippo, canon 36) · 397 (the Third Council of Carthage, re-enacting the Hippo list) · reaffirmed 419 (the Code of Canons of the African Church, canon 24) · Wikipedia
Summary: The North-African synods of Augustine's and Aurelius of Carthage's time — Hippo in 393, Carthage in 397, and Carthage again in 419 — each enact the rule "that besides the Canonical Scriptures nothing be read in church under the name of divine Scripture," and then list the books. Their New Testament is the present twenty-seven (Carthage setting Hebrews apart from Paul's thirteen), the earliest extant conciliar list of that New Testament on Metzger's account. Their Old Testament is the longer Septuagint list: Tobit, Judith, two books of Maccabees, and the "five books of Solomon" stand among the canonical Scriptures, books Athanasius in 367 had placed in a second rank. They are regional synods of the African church.

Claims & evidence

Hippo (393), canon 36

The acts of the Synod of Hippo of 393 are mostly lost, but its canons survive in the Breviarium Hipponense, the abstract read back into the record and adopted at Carthage in 397. Its thirty-sixth canon is, on B. M. Metzger's account, the earliest extant conciliar list of the twenty-seven-book New Testament; if the "Damasine list" is the act of the Roman synod of 382, as W. A. Jurgens and George J. Reid hold, that list is earlier (see Did the Roman synod of 382 under Damasus promulgate the canon-list preserved in the Decretum Gelasianum?). (The earlier Laodicean list of canon 60, whose genuineness is disputed, stops short of the Apocalypse; see the Council of Laodicea). After the governing rule it runs out the Old and New Testaments together:1

Item, that besides the Canonical Scriptures nothing be read in church under the name of divine Scripture.

But the Canonical Scriptures are as follows: Genesis. Exodus. Leviticus. Numbers. Deuteronomy. Joshua the Son of Nun. The Judges. Ruth. The Kings, iv. books. The Chronicles, ij. books. Job. The Psalter. The Five books of Solomon. The Twelve Books of the Prophets. Isaiah. Jeremiah. Ezechiel. Daniel. Tobit. Judith. Esther. Ezra, ij. books. Macchabees, ij. books.

The New Testament. The Gospels, iv. books. The Acts of the Apostles, j. book. The Epistles of Paul, xiv. The Epistles of Peter, the Apostle, ij. The Epistles of John the Apostle, iij. The Epistles of James the Apostle, j. The Epistle of Jude the Apostle, j. The Revelation of John, j. book.

The New-Testament total is twenty-seven — the four Gospels, Acts, fourteen Pauline letters (Hebrews counted with them, as Origen, Cyril, and Athanasius also reckon it), the seven Catholic Epistles, and the Apocalypse, which Cyril of Jerusalem had omitted in 350 and Athanasius had included in 367. It is the same New Testament as Athanasius's list.

Carthage (397), and the reaffirmation of 419

Four years later the Third Council of Carthage re-enacted the Hippo canon, in a list that differs in detail — among other things in naming "thirteen Epistles of the Apostle Paul" and then "one epistle of the same [writer] to the Hebrews," where Athanasius had counted "fourteen of Paul." Jerome records the Western doubts over the authorship of Hebrews in these same years (Jerome):2

It was also determined that besides the Canonical Scriptures nothing be read in the Church under the title of divine Scriptures. The Canonical Scriptures are these: Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, Deuteronomy, Joshua the son of Nun, Judges, Ruth, four books of Kings, two books of Paraleipomena, Job, the Psalter, five books of Solomon, the books of the twelve prophets, Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezechiel, Daniel, Tobit, Judith, Esther, two books of Esdras, two books of the Maccabees. Of the New Testament: four books of the Gospels, one book of the Acts of the Apostles, thirteen Epistles of the Apostle Paul, one epistle of the same [writer] to the Hebrews, two Epistles of the Apostle Peter, three of John, one of James, one of Jude, one book of the Apocalypse of John.

The text transmitted with the 397 council adds "Let this be made known also to our brother and fellow-priest Boniface" and "let the church over the sea be consulted to confirm this canon." The same canon was read into the record once more at the council of Carthage of 419 (the assembly whose collected canons became the Code of Canons of the African Church), where the list appears as canon 24, closing: "Let this be sent to our brother and fellow bishop, Boniface, and to the other bishops of those parts," that they may confirm the canon. Percival's notes identify this Boniface as the bishop of Rome (Boniface I, 418–422).2

Augustine's list in On Christian Doctrine

Augustine was a priest at Hippo in 393 and a bishop by 397. His own enumeration of the canon, set down in On Christian Doctrine (2.8.13, c. 397), corresponds to the conciliar lists, including Tobit, Judith, and the two books of Maccabees, and states a criterion for the disputed books — to "prefer those that are received by all the catholic churches to those which some do not receive."3

Reception and debate

  • What the councils did. Hippo and Carthage are regional African synods, and their canon regulates what is read in church "under the name of divine Scripture." How their lists relate to the canon's formation — the Church's tradition, declared by its authority; a recognition of books by their own worth, which councils ratified; or a reception within the Church's life whose exact number was never made a dogma — is set out, with its holders, on the page How did the churches come to know the New Testament canon, and what settled its disputed books?
  • The Old Testament list. Athanasius (367) had set Wisdom, Sirach, Judith, Tobit, and Esther among books "read" but "not in the canon"; the African lists place Tobit, Judith, Esther, the two books of Maccabees, and the "five books of Solomon" among the canonical Scriptures. Jerome, in these same years, ranked Judith, Tobit, and the Maccabees below the canon. The Old-Testament side of these lists is treated on the Old Testament canon timeline.
  • The "Nicaea" heading in the Vallicellian codex. An eighth/ninth-century manuscript (Vallicelliana B.58) places this African list — Hippo's canon 36 — under the heading "Chapter XXIV of the Council of Nicaea." Rudolph Cornely (1887) read the heading as evidence that Hippo drew its catalogue from the Nicene decrees; A. E. Breen (1897) and Karl Joseph von Hefele read it as a later heading placed over the African canon. The readings are set out at What does the “Council of Nicaea” heading over Hippo’s canon-list in the Vallicellian codex (B.58) attest?
