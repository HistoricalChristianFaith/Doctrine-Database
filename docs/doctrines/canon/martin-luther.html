Martin Luther on the New Testament Canon

Dates: c. 1522 (the September Testament, Wittenberg; the prefaces here are from that first edition, with the 1530 revisions to James and Revelation noted) · Wikipedia
Summary: In his 1522 German New Testament Luther translated and printed all twenty-seven books, but set four of them — Hebrews, James, Jude, and the Revelation — at the close of the volume, unnumbered in the table of contents, and introduced them as books that "from ancient times had a different reputation" from "the true and certain chief books." His prefaces measure books by apostolicity and by whether they "teach Christ." Whether this arrangement set the four apart from the canonical books (as George J. Reid, J. M. Reu, and J. A. O. Preus read it) or kept them in the New Testament ranked below the chief books (as Martin Foord and Garrett Ham read it) is set out on Did Luther remove Hebrews, James, Jude, and Revelation from the New Testament?

Claims & evidence

The 1522 September Testament: four books moved last, left unnumbered

Luther translated and printed the whole twenty-seven-book New Testament, in a new order. Where the other twenty-three books are listed in his table of contents and numbered consecutively 1–23, the last four — Hebrews, James, Jude, and Revelation — he set below them, separated by a gap and left without numbers. The reordering moved Hebrews and James from their traditional places to the back of the volume, where they still stand in Luther Bibles.1 The preface to Hebrews, which opens the group, states the principle for all four:

Up to this point we have had to do with the true and certain chief books of the New Testament. The four which follow have from ancient times had a different reputation.

— Luther, Preface to Hebrews (1522).2

The "epistle of straw," and its omission from later editions

Luther's general Preface to the New Testament (1522) set out the criterion behind the ranking: the books that "show you Christ" stand highest, and a book is measured by how much gospel it carries. Luther applied the test to James in the line that became the most quoted of the prefaces:

In a word, St. John's Gospel and his first epistle, St. Paul's epistles, especially Romans, Galatians, and Ephesians, and St. Peter's first epistle are the books that show you Christ and teach you all that is necessary and salvatory for you to know, even if you were never to see or hear any other book or doctrine. Therefore St. James' epistle is really an epistle of straw, compared to these others, for it has nothing of the nature of the gospel about it.

— Luther, Preface to the New Testament (1522).3 Martin Foord reports that the declaration "was removed from this preface in all editions of his German Bible after 1537."3

The prefaces to James and Jude

The standing preface to James and Jude lays out Luther's reasons. He doubts that James is apostolic, chiefly because (at James 2) it "ascribes justification to works" in a way he could not reconcile with Paul, and because it does not "preach Christ"; he frames the judgment as his own:

I do not regard it as the writing of an apostle; and my reasons follow.… Therefore, I will not have him in my Bible to be numbered among the true chief books, though I would not thereby prevent anyone from including or extolling him as he pleases, for there are otherwise many good sayings in him.

— Luther, Preface to the Epistles of St. James and St. Jude (1522).4 In the 1530 edition the last sentence was replaced by "Therefore, I cannot include him among the chief books, though I would not thereby prevent anyone from including or extolling him as he pleases, for there are otherwise many good sayings in him."4 He states the test in theological terms:

Whatever does not teach Christ is not yet apostolic, even though St. Peter or St. Paul does the teaching.

— Luther, Preface to James and Jude (1522).4 Of Jude he gives the grounds — that it depends on 2 Peter and cites non-canonical matter (the dispute over Moses' body, the prophecy of Enoch):

It is an extract or copy of St. Peter's second epistle, so very like it are all the words.

— Luther, Preface to James and Jude (1522).4

The preface to Revelation: 1522 and 1530

In the 1522 preface Luther wrote that he could not count Revelation as apostolic:

I miss more than one thing in this book, and it makes me consider it to be neither apostolic nor prophetic.… My spirit cannot accommodate itself to this book.

— Luther, Preface to the Revelation of St. John (1522).5 In 1530 he replaced the brief note with a long new preface that reads the Apocalypse as a prophecy of the Church's history and commends it to Christians, and the 1522 line that it was "neither apostolic nor prophetic" was dropped from his Bible from 1530 on.5

Reception and debate

  • Placement and prefaces. Hebrews, James, Jude, and Revelation were translated, printed, and bound within Luther's New Testament and remained in later editions of the Luther Bible. What the 1522 edition changed was their place (moved to the end), their numbering (left unnumbered in the contents list), and their prefaces (each stating Luther's doubts). Whether this is best described as removal or as a ranking within the canon is set out at Did Luther remove Hebrews, James, Jude, and Revelation from the New Testament?
  • Earlier and contemporary doubts. Eusebius's Church History 3.25 lists James, Jude, 2 Peter, and 2–3 John among the disputed books (Eusebius); Jerome records Western doubts over Hebrews and Greek reserve over the Apocalypse (Jerome). Erasmus, in his 1516 Greek New Testament, and Cardinal Cajetan, Luther's Catholic interlocutor at Augsburg, also questioned some of these books; see the sources cited on the disputed-reading page. How the disputed books are read in accounts of the canon's formation is set out at How did the churches come to know the New Testament canon, and what settled its disputed books?
  • Luther's criterion. Luther's prefaces rank a book by its apostolicity and by whether it teaches Christ. He doubts James chiefly for its teaching on justification and works (James 2); he doubts Hebrews partly for its passages on a second repentance (Heb. 6, 10) and its authorship. He revised his language on James and Revelation in later editions, as noted above.
  • The Council of Trent. On 8 April 1546 the Council of Trent defined the twenty-seven-book New Testament for the Catholic Church, Hebrews, James, Jude, and Revelation included.
  • Provenance. The prefaces are quoted from Luther's Works, vol. 35 (American Edition); the "epistle of straw" passage from the 1522 Preface to the New Testament (LW 35:362), the others from the prefaces to Hebrews (LW 35:394), James and Jude (LW 35:395–398), and Revelation (LW 35:398–399). The arrangement of the September Testament's table of contents is documented in the sources cited in note 1.
