The Council of Rome on the Biblical Canon

Dates: 382 (the Roman synod under Pope Damasus I; the canon-list ascribed to it — the "Damasine list" — survives only in the later Decretum Gelasianum) · Wikipedia
Summary: A synod met at Rome in 382 under Damasus, and a canon-list giving the present twenty-seven-book New Testament and the longer Septuagint Old Testament has been ascribed to it since Faustino Arévalo (1794). The list — the "Damasine list" — survives as a chapter of the Decretum Gelasianum. Arévalo, C. H. Turner, and W. A. Jurgens hold that its canon-chapters preserve the acts of the synod of 382; Ernst von Dobschütz, F. C. Burkitt, and G. M. Hahneman hold that the whole Decretum is a single pseudonymous work of the sixth century. The timeline places the list at its ascribed year, 382, and the Decretum as a whole at c. 500.

Claims & evidence

The "Damasine list" — the all-27 New Testament and the longer Old Testament

The canon-list ascribed to the Roman synod of 382 stands as the second chapter of the Decretum Gelasianum. After the Old Testament — given in the longer Septuagint form, with Wisdom, Sirach, Tobit, Judith, and the two books of Maccabees set among the books — it runs out the New Testament in full:1

Likewise the order of the Scriptures of the New Testament, which the holy and catholic Roman church supports and venerates: Four books of the Gospels: according to Matthew, according to Mark, according to Luke, according to John. Likewise the Acts of the Apostles. The letters of the apostle Paul in number fourteen: to the Romans, to the Corinthians two letters, to the Ephesians, to the Thessalonians two letters, to the Galatians, to the Philippians, to the Colossians, to Timothy two letters, to Titus, to Philemon, to the Hebrews. Likewise the Apocalypse of John. Likewise the canonical letters in number seven: of the apostle Peter two letters, of the apostle James one letter, of the apostle John one letter, of the other John the elder two letters, of the apostle Jude the Zealot one letter. Here ends the canon of the New Testament.

The New-Testament total is the present twenty-seven — the four Gospels, Acts, fourteen Pauline letters (Hebrews folded in, as Origen, Cyril, and Athanasius also reckon it), seven Catholic Epistles, and the Apocalypse. Its New Testament is the same as the list of the council of Hippo (393); its Old Testament, like the African lists, is the longer Septuagint list rather than the twenty-two-book Hebrew reckoning of Athanasius and Jerome.

The synod of 382 and the ascription of the list

A synod met at Rome in 382 under Damasus: convened in the aftermath of the Council of Constantinople (381) and occupied chiefly with the Antiochene/Meletian schism, it is the synod to which Jerome — newly arrived in Rome that year, soon Damasus's secretary — twice refers in passing. In these same years Damasus commissioned Jerome's revision of the Latin Gospels, the first installment of the Vulgate.2 Whether that synod promulgated the canon-list now ascribed to it is disputed. No fourth-century text transmits the list independently; it is preserved inside the Decretum Gelasianum. The ascription of the Decretum's first chapters to a Damasan council was proposed by Faustino Arévalo in 1794, and C. H. Turner edited them as "The Roman Council under Damasus"; the Catholic patrologist W. A. Jurgens defends them as "authentic parts of the Acts of the Council of Rome of 382." Ernst von Dobschütz, in his critical edition (1912), held all five chapters to be a single pseudonymous compilation of the first half of the sixth century, pointing to a quotation in the opening chapter from a work Augustine wrote about 416; F. C. Burkitt judged that he had "made out his case." G. M. Hahneman adds that Jerome, who was at Rome in 382, nowhere mentions a Roman conciliar canon. Placement on the timeline

  • Date of the block. The canon-lists of Athanasius (367), the African councils (393–419), and Innocent I (405) survive under their authors' names in their own documents; the Roman list survives in the Decretum Gelasianum under a heading naming Damasus. The block is placed at the year the list is ascribed to, 382.
  • The same document, two chapters. The Decretum Gelasianum that carries this canon-list (its second chapter) also carries a long catalogue of books "not to be received" (its fifth chapter), which appears on this timeline as the rejection-lists witness (c. 500–810). Von Dobschütz holds the chapters to be parts of one work; Jurgens holds that Gelasius re-edited a Damasan list, adding the catalogue of rejected books.
  • Other councils named for the canon. Claims that the Council of Nicaea took up the canon are set out at Did the Council of Nicaea (325) decide the contents of the Bible? and What does Jerome mean in writing that Judith “is found by the Nicene Council to have been counted among the number of the Sacred Scriptures”?; the Nicene heading placed over Hippo's canon 36 in one manuscript at What does the “Council of Nicaea” heading over Hippo’s canon-list in the Vallicellian codex (B.58) attest?
