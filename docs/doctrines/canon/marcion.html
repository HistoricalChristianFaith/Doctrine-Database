Marcion of Sinope on the Biblical Canon

Dates: c. 144 (his break with the church of Rome; d. c. 160) · Wikipedia
Summary: Marcion's collection is the earliest extant attestation of a fixed, closed list of Christian scriptures: a single Gospel, which his opponents describe as an edited Luke carrying no author's name, and ten letters of Paul, without the Old Testament, the other three Gospels, Acts, or the Pastoral epistles. Adolf von Harnack and Hans von Campenhausen hold that his list provoked the catholic church to define a broader canon of its own; John Barton holds that the fourfold Gospel and the Pauline collection were already forming independently. None of Marcion's own writings survive; what is known of them comes from Irenaeus, Tertullian, and other writers against him.

Claims & evidence

The single Gospel — described by his opponents as a cut-down Luke, with no author's name

Besides this, he mutilates the Gospel which is according to Luke, removing all that is written respecting the generation of the Lord, and setting aside a great deal of the teaching of the Lord … He likewise persuaded his disciples that he himself was more worthy of credit than are those apostles who have handed down the Gospel to us, furnishing them not with the Gospel, but merely a fragment of it.

— Irenaeus, Against Heresies 1.27.2, trans. A. Roberts and W. Rambaut, Ante-Nicene Fathers vol. 1 (also at New Advent).1 Writing c. 180, Irenaeus is the earliest extant witness to describe Marcion's Gospel: in his account, a copy of Luke without the nativity and much of the Lord's teaching, "merely a fragment" of the Gospel — and the only Gospel Marcion used.

Marcion, on the other hand, you must know, ascribes no author to his Gospel, as if it could not be allowed him to affix a title to that from which it was no crime (in his eyes) to subvert the very body. … Now, of the authors whom we possess, Marcion seems to have singled out Luke for his mutilating process.

— Tertullian, Against Marcion 4.2, trans. P. Holmes, Ante-Nicene Fathers vol. 3 (also at New Advent).2 Tertullian, devoting the fourth of his five books against Marcion to Marcion's Gospel, notes that it bears no evangelist's name. Against it Tertullian sets the churches' fourfold Gospel — "John and Matthew" the apostles, "Luke and Mark" the apostolic men (4.2) — and argues that the older text must be the true one: Marcion is the late "amender," and "an amender of that Gospel … first presented himself in Marcion alone" (4.4). His charge is that Marcion has only one Gospel, and that one altered.

Marcion expressly and openly used the knife, not the pen, since he made such an excision of the Scriptures as suited his own subject-matter.

— Tertullian, The Prescription Against Heretics 38, trans. P. Holmes, Ante-Nicene Fathers vol. 3 (also at New Advent).3 Tertullian contrasts the two teachers: Valentinus (he says) "seems to use the entire volume" and alters its meaning, while Marcion cut whole books and passages to leave a Scripture that "suited his own subject-matter."

The ten-letter Apostolikon

In like manner, too, he dismembered the Epistles of Paul, removing all that is said by the apostle respecting that God who made the world, to the effect that He is the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, and also those passages from the prophetical writings which the apostle quotes, in order to teach us that they announced beforehand the coming of the Lord.

— Irenaeus, Against Heresies 1.27.2.1 Beside his one Gospel Marcion set a corpus of Paul — the Apostolikon — which Irenaeus describes as cut of its references to the Creator and the prophets. As later opponents report it and the reconstructions cited below give it, this was a collection of ten letters: Galatians, 1–2 Corinthians, Romans, 1–2 Thessalonians, "Laodiceans" (his name for Ephesians), Colossians, Philippians, and Philemon. The collection did not include the three Pastoral epistles (1–2 Timothy and Titus), Hebrews, Acts, the Catholic epistles, or the Revelation. On his opponents' account Paul was for Marcion the one true apostle, the others having (he held) mixed the gospel with Judaism; his collection was a Gospel and an Apostle, eleven books in all.4

The Old Testament, and Tertullian's appeal to antiquity

Marcion of Pontus succeeded him, and developed his doctrine. … But Jesus being derived from that father who is above the God that made the world … was manifested in the form of a man to those who were in Judaea, abolishing the prophets and the law, and all the works of that God who made the world.

— Irenaeus, Against Heresies 1.27.2.1 On Irenaeus's account Marcion taught that the God of the Old Testament, the Creator, is not the Father of Jesus Christ, the higher God; his collection excluded the Hebrew Scriptures. Tertullian answers Marcion's Gospel with a criterion of antiquity:

I say that my Gospel is the true one; Marcion, that his is. I affirm that Marcion's Gospel is adulterated; Marcion, that mine is. Now what is to settle the point for us, except it be that principle of time, which rules that the authority lies with that which shall be found to be more ancient?

— Tertullian, Against Marcion 4.4.2 Adolf von Harnack held, and Hans von Campenhausen, who called Marcion "the creator of the Christian holy Scripture," developed the thesis, that this challenge led the catholic church to fix a broader canon of its own: a fourfold Gospel against Marcion's one, a full Pauline corpus (Pastorals included) against his ten, and the Old Testament retained as Christian Scripture. On their reading the later witnesses on this timeline — Justin's Gospels read aloud (c. 155), Irenaeus's argument that the Gospels are exactly four (c. 180) — are responses to Marcion. John Barton's contrary reading is given below.5

Reception and debate

  • Sources for Marcion. Marcion left no surviving writings: his Antitheses, his Gospel (the Evangelikon), and his Paul (the Apostolikon) are known only through the writers who quoted them in order to answer them — chiefly Irenaeus and Tertullian, with later notices in Epiphanius and others. Reconstructions of what his texts contained, and in what order his letters stood, rest on these reports. The reports agree in describing a two-part collection, one Gospel and ten letters, set forth as the Christian Scriptures to the exclusion of others.
  • The provocation thesis. Harnack and von Campenhausen made Marcion the catalyst of the New-Testament canon — the church defining its list in reaction to his. John Barton holds that the fourfold Gospel and a Pauline collection were already forming independently, so that Marcion accelerated and sharpened a process he did not begin; in Barton's phrase, Marcion "was responsible not for the Christians adopting a New Testament, but for their retaining the Old."5 Whether the four-Gospel core was already established before Irenaeus is set out at Does Irenaeus’s argument for four Gospels (Against Heresies 3.11.8) attest a fourfold Gospel already closed by c. 180?
  • Related witnesses. Compare Justin Martyr (c. 155), who reports the Gospels read in the Sunday assembly, and Irenaeus of Lyons (c. 180), who argues that the Gospels are exactly four and is also the source, above, for Marcion's Gospel and letters. See the doctrine overview at The Biblical Canon.
