Irenaeus of Lyons on the Biblical Canon

Dates: c. 180 (Against Heresies; bishop of Lyons, d. c. 202) · Wikipedia
Summary: Irenaeus's Against Heresies contains the earliest extant argument that the Gospels are exactly four — "It is not possible that the Gospels can be either more or fewer in number than they are" — matching them to the four living creatures (the "Tetramorph"), and narrates each evangelist's apostolic origin. Across the work he quotes from some twenty-one of the twenty-seven books of the later New Testament as authoritative. He calls 1 Clement a "most powerful letter" and introduces a sentence of the Shepherd of Hermas as "the Scripture." He files the tale of the boy Jesus and his alphabet-teacher among the Marcosians' "apocryphal and spurious writings, which they themselves have forged," and calls the Gospel of Judas "a fictitious history."

Claims & evidence

The fourfold Gospel: exactly four, "neither more nor fewer" (the Tetramorph)

It is not possible that the Gospels can be either more or fewer in number than they are. For, since there are four zones of the world in which we live, and four principal winds, while the Church is scattered throughout all the world, and the "pillar and ground" of the Church is the Gospel and the spirit of life; it is fitting that she should have four pillars, breathing out immortality on every side, and vivifying men afresh.

— Against Heresies 3.11.8, ANF 1, historicalchristian.faith1 (also newadvent.org). Irenaeus matches the four to the four faces of the cherubim (lion, calf, man, eagle) and to "four principal covenants given to the human race," concluding: "For the living creatures are quadriform, and the Gospel is quadriform." Whether the passage attests a fourfold Gospel already established before Irenaeus (as George J. Reid, Graham Stanton, Martin Hengel, and Charles E. Hill read it) or argues for one selection among many gospels (as James M. Robinson and Bart D. Ehrman read it) is set out at Does Irenaeus’s argument for four Gospels (Against Heresies 3.11.8) attest a fourfold Gospel already closed by c. 180?

The apostolic origins of the four Gospels

Matthew also issued a written Gospel among the Hebrews in their own dialect, while Peter and Paul were preaching at Rome, and laying the foundations of the Church. After their departure, Mark, the disciple and interpreter of Peter, did also hand down to us in writing what had been preached by Peter. Luke also, the companion of Paul, recorded in a book the Gospel preached by him. Afterwards, John, the disciple of the Lord, who also had leaned upon His breast, did himself publish a Gospel during his residence at Ephesus in Asia.

— Against Heresies 3.1.1, ANF 1, historicalchristian.faith2 (also newadvent.org). Irenaeus grounds each of the four in an apostle or an apostle's companion. The term Tetramorph for the four-formed Gospel is associated with this chapter and III.11.8.

Some twenty-one of the twenty-seven New-Testament books cited

Across the five books of Against Heresies, Irenaeus quotes from — and treats as authoritative — roughly twenty-one of the twenty-seven books of the later New Testament: the four Gospels, Acts, Romans, both Corinthians, Galatians, Ephesians, Philippians, Colossians, both Thessalonians, both letters to Timothy, Titus, 1 Peter, 1 John, 2 John, and the Revelation of John. He may also draw on Hebrews, James, and possibly 2 Peter, while Philemon, 3 John, and Jude go uncited.3 Several of the shorter epistles uncited here are also absent from the Muratorian list.

1 Clement: "a most powerful letter"

In the time of this Clement, no small dissension having occurred among the brethren at Corinth, the Church in Rome despatched a most powerful letter to the Corinthians, exhorting them to peace, renewing their faith, and declaring the tradition which it had lately received from the apostles … From this document, whosoever chooses to do so, may learn that He, the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, was preached by the Churches, and may also understand the apostolical tradition of the Church, since this Epistle is of older date than these men who are now propagating falsehood, and who conjure into existence another god beyond the Creator and the Maker of all existing things.

— Against Heresies 3.3.3, ANF 1, historicalchristian.faith4 (also newadvent.org). Irenaeus treats the letter known as 1 Clement — the letter of the Roman church to Corinth — as a "most powerful" document from which "the apostolical tradition of the Church" may be understood, citing it against his opponents for its antiquity. It is not among the books of the later canon-lists.

The Shepherd of Hermas introduced as "the Scripture"

Truly, then, the Scripture declared, which says, "First of all believe that there is one God, who has established all things, and completed them, and having caused that from what had no being, all things should come into existence: He who contains all things, and is Himself contained by no one."

— Against Heresies 4.20.2, ANF 1, historicalchristian.faith5 (also newadvent.org). The words Irenaeus introduces as "the Scripture" are the opening commandment of the Shepherd of Hermas (Mandate 1) — a second-century Roman work. The Muratorian list allows it to be read but not publicly "among the Apostles," and Athanasius (367) places it among the books "appointed … to be read." The Greek term behind "Scripture" (graphē) can also mean "writing," and the summary cited below reports a reading of Irenaeus as quoting an esteemed text rather than a canonical one.

The boy-Jesus alphabet tale, among the Marcosians' "apocryphal and spurious writings"

Besides the above [misrepresentations], they adduce an unspeakable number of apocryphal and spurious writings, which they themselves have forged, to bewilder the minds of foolish men, and of such as are ignorant of the Scriptures of truth. Among other things, they bring forward that false and wicked story which relates that our Lord, when He was a boy learning His letters, on the teacher saying to Him, as is usual, "Pronounce Alpha," replied [as He was bid], "Alpha." But when, again, the teacher bade Him say, "Beta," the Lord replied, "Do thou first tell me what Alpha is, and then I will tell thee what Beta is." This they expound as meaning that He alone knew the Unknown, which He revealed under its type Alpha.

— Against Heresies 1.20.1, ANF 1, historicalchristian.faith6 (also newadvent.org). The chapter heading is "The Apocryphal and Spurious Scriptures of the Marcosians." Irenaeus charges the followers of Marcus with adducing "an unspeakable number" of writings "which they themselves have forged," and sets them against "the Scriptures of truth." The story he reports is the alphabet-lesson scene also found in chapters 6 and 14 of the Infancy Gospel of Thomas.

The Gospel of Judas, "a fictitious history"

They declare that Judas the traitor was thoroughly acquainted with these things, and that he alone, knowing the truth as no others did, accomplished the mystery of the betrayal; by him all things, both earthly and heavenly, were thus thrown into confusion. They produce a fictitious history of this kind, which they style the Gospel of Judas.

— Against Heresies 1.31.1, ANF 1, historicalchristian.faith7 (also newadvent.org). Here Irenaeus names a titled book — the Gospel of Judas, in the chapter the ANF translation heads "Doctrines of the Cainites" — and calls it "a fictitious history." It is the earliest extant notice of a work of that title; a Coptic Gospel of Judas in the Codex Tchacos was published in 2006.

Reception and debate

  • A story, not a named book. On the boy-Jesus tale, Irenaeus reports an episode and gives no book title. The same alpha/beta saying also appears in the Epistle of the Apostles (ch. 4), and the summary cited here treats the saying as circulating before, and apart from, the Infancy Gospel of Thomas in its fixed form.8
  • Named and answered. Irenaeus's response to the writings he calls forged is to name and answer them; he does not speak of destroying them. Whether the apocryphal gospels were afterwards suppressed is set out at Were the infancy gospels suppressed — banned, voted out, gathered up and burned?
  • Irenaeus in the canon debate. His fourfold Gospel, his use of most of the apostolic letters, and his citations of 1 Clement and the Shepherd are read differently in accounts of the canon's formation; the readings are set out at How did the churches come to know the New Testament canon, and what settled its disputed books?
