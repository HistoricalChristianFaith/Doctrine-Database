Philip

Philip, of Bethsaida, “the city of Andrew and Peter,” was one of the first Jesus called. In John’s Gospel he fetches Nathanael, and he speaks again at the feeding of the five thousand, at the coming of the Greeks who sought Jesus, and at the Last Supper. The New Testament names him in all four lists of the Twelve and records nothing of where he went or how he died. It also names a second Philip, the deacon and evangelist of Acts, whose four prophesying daughters Luke records at Caesarea. The earliest Asian sources place a Philip and his daughters at Hierapolis, and whether that Philip was the apostle or the evangelist is disputed. This page sets out what the sources report about Philip — his home at Hierapolis, his daughters, and his death — earliest extant source first: the Gospel notices, the second-century witnesses preserved by Eusebius, the apocryphal Acts of Philip, and the excavations at Hierapolis. Its “witnesses” are the datable sources that report on Philip; the disputed questions are set out on their own pages under Disputed readings below. For the sources common to the traditions about all the apostles, see the apostles hub.

Timeline

The New Testament (c. 30–90)

In the New Testament Philip is of Bethsaida, “the city of Andrew and Peter,” one of the first Jesus calls with the word “Follow me,” and at once he finds Nathanael: “We have found him, of whom Moses in the law, and the prophets, did write.” He appears three times more, all in John — tested by Jesus before the feeding of the five thousand (“Whence shall we buy bread?”), approached by the enquiring Greeks, and answered at the Last Supper when he asks, “Lord, shew us the Father” (“he that hath seen me hath seen the Father”) — and he is named in all four lists of the Twelve. Of his mission or death the New Testament says nothing. It also names a second Philip — Philip the evangelist, one of the seven deacons, who preaches in Samaria, baptizes the Ethiopian eunuch, and settles at Caesarea with “four daughters, virgins, which did prophesy.” Whether the later Asian tradition concerns the apostle or the evangelist is disputed; see the two-Philips question.1

Papias of Hierapolis, preserved by Eusebius (c. 110–130)

Papias, bishop of Hierapolis in Phrygia, who sought out the sayings of those who had known the apostles, is the earliest extant witness to a Philip at Hierapolis. Eusebius reports that Papias “received a wonderful narrative from the daughters of Philip” — his neighbours — among them the raising of a dead man and the story of Justus Barsabas surviving a draught of poison. Eusebius calls this figure “the Apostle Philip”; which Philip is meant is disputed; see the two-Philips question.2

Polycrates of Ephesus and the Dialogue of Gaius, preserved by Eusebius (c. 190–210)

Polycrates, bishop of Ephesus, writing to Victor of Rome in the Quartodeciman controversy, lists the “great lights” buried in Asia and names first “Philip, one of the twelve apostles, who sleeps in Hierapolis, and his two aged virgin daughters, and another daughter who lived in the Holy Spirit and now rests at Ephesus.” Around 200 the Roman Dialogue of Gaius, written against the Montanists, quotes the Montanist Proclus pointing to “four prophetesses, the daughters of Philip, at Hierapolis… Their tomb is there and the tomb of their father.” Both place Philip’s home, grave, and daughters at Hierapolis, and they differ on the daughters’ number and character: Polycrates gives three, two of them buried at Hierapolis as virgins, and does not call them prophetesses; Proclus gives four prophetesses, the number and gift Acts 21:9 records of the evangelist’s daughters. Polycrates says that Philip “sleeps” (κεκοίμηται); whether the word bears on the manner of his death is discussed on the head-downward page.3

The Acts of Philip (4th–5th c.)

The apocryphal Acts of Philip, a Greek narrative in which Philip journeys with Bartholomew and his sister Mariamne (accompanied by a talking leopard and kid) to “Ophiorymos,” the “city of the serpent,” which most manuscripts identify with Hierapolis ⚠ unverified, is the earliest extant source to narrate Philip’s death. Philip converts the proconsul’s wife Nicanora, is arrested, and is executed by being hung head-downward: the soldiers, “after piercing Philip’s ankles… put the cords through his ankles, and hung him head downwards on a tree,” from which he preaches and, in some manuscripts, calls down destruction on his persecutors until Christ rebukes him. Whether the account preserves the memory of how Philip died is disputed; see the head-downward question.4

Modern archaeology at Hierapolis (2011)

On the hill above Hierapolis (modern Pamukkale, Turkey) stands the great octagonal martyrium of St Philip, a fifth-century pilgrimage shrine long known to excavators. In 2011 the Italian archaeologist Francesco D’Andria announced that, some forty metres from the martyrium, he had uncovered a fifth-century church built around a first-century Roman tomb, which he identified as Philip’s original grave, emptied when the relics were carried (he suggests) to Constantinople and then Rome.5

Disputed readings

How the sources for Philip’s residence, family, and death at Hierapolis have been read.

  • Was the Philip remembered at Hierapolis the apostle or Philip the evangelist? — Polycrates’ “one of the twelve apostles” and the four prophesying daughters Acts gives the evangelist.6
  • Was Philip crucified head-downward at Hierapolis? — the early notices of a grave and the Acts of Philip’s account of the death.7
