The Council of Nicaea (325) on the Trinity

Dates: 325 · Wikipedia
Summary: The first ecumenical council, convened by Constantine, answered Arius with a word the Bible does not use — homoousios (“one in essence,” “consubstantial”) — in order to guard what the Bible does teach: that the Son is fully and eternally God. Its creed and anathemas became the church’s standard of orthodoxy.

Claims & evidence

The creed: “Very God from Very God, begotten not made, One in essence with the Father”

And in One Lord Jesus Christ, the Son of God, begotten of the Father, Only-begotten, that is, from the essence of the Father; God from God, Light from Light, Very God from Very God, begotten not made, One in essence with the Father, by Whom all things were made, both things in heaven and things in earth

— The Nicene Creed (325), as preserved in Eusebius of Caesarea’s letter to his church and reproduced by Athanasius, trans. NPNF² vol. 4, source.

The anathemas against Arius’s slogans

And those who say, 'Once He was not,' and 'Before His generation He was not,' and 'He came to be from nothing,' or those who pretend that the Son of God is 'Of other subsistence or essence,' or 'created' or 'alterable,' or 'mutable,' the Catholic Church anathematizes.

— ibid. The anathemas quote Arius’s own phrases back at him and condemn each.

Why a non-scriptural word? Athanasius’s defence

The Council, wishing to do away with the irreligious phrases of the Arians, and to use instead the acknowledged words of the Scriptures... But the Fathers, perceiving their craft... were forced to express more distinctly the sense of the words 'from God.' Accordingly, they wrote 'from the essence of God,' in order that 'from God' might not be considered common and equal in the Son and in things originate.

— Athanasius, De Decretis (Defence of the Nicene Definition) 19, trans. NPNF² vol. 4, source. He tells the same story of “one in essence” (§20): when the Arians were caught “whispering… and winking” that vaguer words like “like” and “power” were “common to us and the Son,” the bishops “were again compelled… to re-say and re-write… that the Son is ‘one in essence’ with the Father… not merely like, but the same in likeness.”

Notes

  • The charge that homoousios is an “unscriptural novelty” — raised by Arius’s party then and by anti-creedal Christians since — is exactly the objection Athanasius answers here: the fathers preferred to stay with Scripture’s own words and adopted the technical term only because the Arians could affirm every biblical phrase while meaning something else by it. The council’s claim is that homoousios does not add to the apostolic faith but fences it. (This contested sub-question is a natural argument page for later development.)
  • The word homoousios carried baggage: it had been used by Paul of Samosata’s circle in a modalist sense and condemned at Antioch (268), which is partly why moderate Easterners (the later homoiousians, “of like essence”) hesitated over it for decades after 325. The long struggle to secure Nicaea’s meaning is what Athanasius and the Cappadocians carried through to Constantinople (381).
  • Nicaea settled the Son’s deity but said only “and in the Holy Ghost” of the Spirit; the Spirit’s consubstantial deity was left for the next generation and dogmatized at Constantinople (381).
