Gregory of Nyssa on the Trinity

Dates: c. 385 (the mature trinitarian corpus; lifespan c. 335–395) · Wikipedia
Summary: Basil’s younger brother. The Cappadocian terms — one ousia in three hypostaseis — drew the objection: if Father, Son and Spirit each are God, why not “three Gods,” as Peter, James and John are three men? Gregory’s On “Not Three Gods” (to Ablabius) answers it, and his On the Holy Trinity (to Eustathius) defends the Spirit’s full deity. This page treats those two questions; his “the Son between” procession language is treated separately on his Filioque detail page.

Claims & evidence

The tritheism dilemma stated

“…we are at first sight compelled to accept one or other of two erroneous opinions, and either to say ‘there are three Gods,’ which is unlawful, or not to acknowledge the Godhead of the Son and the Holy Spirit, which is impious and absurd.”

— On “Not Three Gods” (to Ablabius), trans. Moore & Wilson (NPNF Series 2, vol. 5), source. Gregory frames the whole treatise as an escape between two errors: tritheism on one side, and (to avoid it) demoting the Son and Spirit on the other. His task is to hold the full deity of all three and the oneness of God.

One nature, not counted into a plurality — the gold analogy

“For we say that gold, even though it be cut into many figures, is one, and is so spoken of, but we speak of many coins or many staters, without finding any multiplication of the nature of gold by the number of staters… The Father is God: the Son is God: and yet by the same proclamation God is One, because no difference either of nature or of operation is contemplated in the Godhead.”

— On “Not Three Gods” (to Ablabius), trans. Moore & Wilson (NPNF Series 2, vol. 5), source. The nature (Godhead) is not multiplied by the number of the Persons any more than gold is multiplied by the number of coins struck from it. Gregory reinforces this with an argument from operation: “every operation which extends from God to the Creation… has its origin from the Father, and proceeds through the Son, and is perfected in the Holy Spirit,” so that “whatever comes to pass… comes to pass by the action of the Three, yet what does come to pass is not three things.” From one inseparable operation he infers one nature, and so one God, not three.

The Spirit’s share in the divine attributes, and the name of Godhead

“If, then, they do not deny that the Holy Spirit has community with the Father and the Son in those attributes which, in their sense of special excellence, are piously predicated only of the Divine nature, what reason is there to pretend that He is excluded from community in this only… It is absolutely necessary either to allow to Him community in this also, or not to admit His community in the others.”

— On the Holy Trinity, and of the Godhead of the Holy Spirit (to Eustathius), trans. H. A. Wilson (NPNF Series 2, vol. 5), source. Against the Pneumatomachi, who confessed the Spirit “Good,” “Holy,” “Eternal,” “Wise,” “Righteous” and “Mighty” yet balked at “God,” Gregory charges them with inconsistency: every one of the exalted divine attributes belongs to the Spirit alike; if he shares those, there is no ground for excluding him from the one name of Godhead they all imply.

Notes

  • Gregory’s answer to the tritheism charge is that the plural attaches to the Persons, never to the nature, which, he argues, one inseparable operation shows to be single.
  • He uses the terms his brother Basil of Caesarea had distinguished (one ousia, three hypostases), alongside his friend Gregory of Nazianzus, who presided for a time at the Council of Constantinople (381). Gregory of Nyssa attended that council and was named by the emperor Theodosius as one of the bishops whose communion was to serve as the standard.
  • Gregory appears elsewhere on the wiki with the same “of the Cause… by way of the Son” formula on the Filioque timeline, where the interest is the Spirit’s procession rather than the tritheism question and the Spirit’s deity treated here.
