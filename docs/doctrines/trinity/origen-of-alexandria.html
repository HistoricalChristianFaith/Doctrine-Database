Origen on the Trinity

Dates: c. 248 (lifespan c. 185–253) · Wikipedia
Summary: Origen supplies the decisive advance — the Son’s eternal generation, so that there is no “when” the Son was not — and the decisive ambiguity — a subordinationist strand (“Very God” vs. God-by-participation; a “second God”) that the next generation would exploit. Both halves of his legacy were quarried in the Arian crisis.

Claims & evidence

The eternal generation of the Son

And who that is capable of entertaining reverential thoughts or feelings regarding God, can suppose or believe that God the Father ever existed, even for a moment of time, without having generated this Wisdom? ... Wherefore we have always held that God is the Father of His only-begotten Son, who was born indeed of Him, and derives from Him what He is, but without any beginning, not only such as may be measured by any divisions of time, but even that which the mind alone can contemplate within itself

— De Principiis I.2.2, trans. Frederick Crombie, source. He sharpens it into a warning: “Let him, then, who assigns a beginning to the Word or Wisdom of God, take care that he be not guilty of impiety against the unbegotten Father Himself, seeing he denies that He had always been a Father” (De Principiis I.2.3). And I.2.11: the Son’s existence “is derived from the Father but not in time, nor from any other beginning, except… from God Himself.” This is the doctrine later crystallized as “there was no when he was not” — the direct denial of what Arius would assert.

The subordinationist tail: “Very God” vs. God-by-participation

God on the one hand is Very God (Autotheos, God of Himself); and so the Saviour says in His prayer to the Father, 'That they may know Thee the only true God;' but that all beyond the Very God is made God by participation in His divinity, and is not to be called simply God (with the article), but rather God (without article). ... the Word of God, who was in the beginning, and who by being with God is at all times God, not possessing that of Himself, but by His being with the Father

— Commentary on John II.2, source. Elsewhere he will even grant the term “second God” — though carefully qualified: “although we may call Him a ‘second God,’ let men know that by the term ‘second God’ we mean nothing else than a virtue capable of including all other virtues” (Against Celsus V.39, source).

Notes

  • Origen’s two strands pull in opposite directions and were pressed by opposite parties. The pro-Nicenes (Athanasius, the Cappadocians) took over the eternal generation as the answer to Arius; the Arians appealed to the subordination (Son “by participation,” a “second God”) as proof the Son is a lesser being. The historical judgment is that Origen’s subordination is ontological ordering within the Godhead, not Arius’s denial of the Son’s deity — but his vocabulary was pre-Nicene and genuinely open to the harder reading.
  • This ambiguity is one root of the later condemnation of Origen; his eschatology (universal restoration) is traced on the wiki’s apokatastasis page. On the Trinity proper, the church kept his eternal generation and discarded the subordinationist residue.
  • Origen’s ho theos / theos (God with vs. without the article) distinction is the ancient form of the modern grammatical debate over John 1:1c. His grammatical observation is acute, but the gradation he built on it does not follow: the article marks the Father as the person the Word is “with,” not a lesser rank of deity in the Word. Weighed on the argument page John 1:1c — is the Word “God” or “a god”? (the anarthrous theos).
