Arius on the Trinity

Dates: c. 321 (the outbreak of the controversy; lifespan c. 256–336) · Wikipedia
Summary: An Alexandrian presbyter who taught that the Son is a creature, made out of nothing, who did not always exist; the Council of Nicaea (325) anathematized these formulas. His own words survive only as his opponents quote them; the fullest is Athanasius’s citation of Arius’s Thalia.

Claims & evidence

“There was when he was not”: the Son a creature out of nothing

'God was not always a Father;' but 'once God was alone, and not yet a Father, but afterwards He became a Father.' 'The Son was not always;' for, whereas all things were made out of nothing... the Word of God Himself was 'made out of nothing,' and 'once He was not,' and 'He was not before His origination,' but He as others 'had an origin of creation.'

— Arius’s teaching as quoted by Athanasius, Four Discourses Against the Arians I.8, trans. NPNF² vol. 4, source. Athanasius’s compact list of the tenets (I.5): “the Son was not always; the Son out of nothing; once He was not; He was not before his generation; He was created… alterable… not very God; but called so as others by participation; foreign in essence from the Father.”

Not equal, not one in essence — “foreign… in essence” from the Father

The Unbegun made the Son a beginning of things originated; and advanced Him as a Son to Himself by adoption. He has nothing proper to God in proper subsistence. For He is not equal, no, nor one in essence with Him.

— Arius, Thalia, as quoted in Athanasius, De Synodis 15, trans. NPNF² vol. 4, source. The same extract: “Foreign from the Son in essence is the Father, for He is without beginning”; and “though the Son was not, the Father was God… the Son, not being (for He existed at the will of the Father), is God Only-begotten, and He is alien from either.”

Notes

  • Arius’s slogan “there was when he was not” (Greek ἤn pote hote ouk ἤn) contradicts Origen’s teaching of eternal generation and is among the phrases the Council of Nicaea (325) anathematized (see the council’s page). In the extract quoted above he grounds the difference in the Father’s being “without beginning.”
  • Every quotation here is secondhand, preserved by Arius’s opponents (Athanasius; also Alexander of Alexandria’s encyclical), and the Thalia extracts come in Athanasius’s polemical framing. The core slogans also appear in the Nicene anathemas and in two letters in Arius’s name, which likewise survive only as quoted by later writers: the letter to Eusebius of Nicomedia (“before He was begotten, or created, or purposed, or established, He was not”), preserved by Theodoret (Ecclesiastical History I.4 in the NPNF numbering, source), and the letter of Arius and his fellow presbyters and deacons to Alexander, preserved by Athanasius (De Synodis 16, source).
  • Athanasius applied the name of Arius to later parties: he writes that Aetius “has been wrecked upon Arianism,” and that those who said “the Son is altogether unlike the Father” “were called by all Anomoeans” (De Synodis 6, 31). Those who accepted the rest of Nicaea and doubted only “the Coessential,” among whom he names Basil of Ancyra, he declines to “attack… as Ario-maniacs” and addresses “as brothers with brothers” (De Synodis 41, source).
