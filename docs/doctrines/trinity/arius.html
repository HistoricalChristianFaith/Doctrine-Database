Arius on the Trinity

Dates: c. 321 (the outbreak of the controversy; lifespan c. 256–336) · Wikipedia
Summary: An Alexandrian presbyter whose sharp thesis — that the Son is a creature, made out of nothing, who did not always exist — forced the church to define what it had always worshipped. His own words survive only as his opponents quote them; the fullest is Athanasius’s citation of Arius’s Thalia.

Claims & evidence

“There was when he was not”: the Son a creature out of nothing

'God was not always a Father;' but 'once God was alone, and not yet a Father, but afterwards He became a Father.' 'The Son was not always;' for, whereas all things were made out of nothing... the Word of God Himself was 'made out of nothing,' and 'once He was not,' and 'He was not before His origination,' but He as others 'had an origin of creation.'

— Arius’s teaching as quoted by Athanasius, Four Discourses Against the Arians I.8, trans. NPNF² vol. 4, source. Athanasius’s compact list of the tenets (I.5): “the Son was not always; the Son out of nothing; once He was not; He was not before his generation; He was created… alterable… not very God; but called so as others by participation; foreign in essence from the Father.”

Not equal, not one in essence — “foreign… in essence” from the Father

The Unbegun made the Son a beginning of things originated; and advanced Him as a Son to Himself by adoption. He has nothing proper to God in proper subsistence. For He is not equal, no, nor one in essence with Him.

— Arius, Thalia, as quoted in Athanasius, De Synodis 15, trans. NPNF² vol. 4, source. The same extract: “Foreign from the Son in essence is the Father, for He is without beginning”; and “though the Son was not, the Father was God… the Son, not being (for He existed at the will of the Father), is God Only-begotten, and He is alien from either.”

Notes

  • Arius’s slogan “there was when he was not” (Greek ἤn pote hote ouk ἤn) is the exact denial of Origen’s eternal generation and the phrase Nicaea (325) anathematized by name. His motive was to protect the Father’s uniqueness as the sole unoriginate (agennētos): if the Son is truly begotten, Arius reasoned, then the Son had a beginning and so is not God in the strict sense.
  • Every quotation here is secondhand — preserved by Arius’s adversaries (Athanasius; also Alexander of Alexandria’s encyclical). Athanasius is a hostile witness, and the Thalia extracts are polemically framed; but the core slogans are cross-attested in Arius’s own surviving letters (to Eusebius of Nicomedia and to Alexander) and in the Nicene anathemas, so the substance of the thesis is not in doubt.
  • The wiki does not endorse the reading; it records it as the position the fourth-century church defined itself against. “Arianism” became the label for the whole family of subordinationist theologies that followed, from the strict anomoians (the Son unlike the Father) to the homoiousians (the Son of like essence), whom the pro-Nicenes eventually won over.
