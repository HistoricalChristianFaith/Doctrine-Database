Basil of Caesarea on the Trinity

Dates: c. 375 (On the Holy Spirit; lifespan c. 330–379) · Wikipedia
Summary: The eldest of the three Cappadocians and the one who fixed the settled grammar of the Godhead: one being (ousia) held in three distinct subsistences (hypostaseis), the general and the particular. Where Athanasius had defended the Son’s deity, Basil carried the same fight to its last frontier — the deity of the Holy Spirit — against the Pneumatomachi (“Spirit-fighters”) who granted the Son but demoted the Spirit to a creature. His On the Holy Spirit (c. 375) argues the Spirit is holy and good by nature, not by participation, and refuses to “sub-number” him below the Father and the Son. This page treats his trinitarian grammar and the Spirit’s deity; his “from the Father through the Son” language, read by East and West for and against the Filioque, is weighed on his Filioque detail page.

Claims & evidence

One ousia, three hypostases: the general and the particular

“The distinction between ousia and hypostasis is the same as that between the general and the particular; as, for instance, between the animal and the particular man. Wherefore, in the case of the Godhead, we confess one essence or substance so as not to give a variant definition of existence, but we confess a particular hypostasis, in order that our conception of Father, Son and Holy Spirit may be without confusion and clear… there is a satisfactory preservation of the unity by the confession of the one Godhead, while in the distinction of the individual properties regarded in each there is the confession of the peculiar properties of the Persons.”

— Letter 236.6 (to Amphilochius), trans. Blomfield Jackson (NPNF Series 2, vol. 8), source. This is the Cappadocian settlement of the terminology Nicaea had left ambiguous: ousia (essence) is what the Three share, hypostasis (subsistence, “Person”) is what distinguishes them. Basil adds the sharp warning that to identify essence and hypostasis — refusing to speak of three hypostases — cannot “avoid the error of Sabellius,” the modalist collapse of the Persons into one.

The Spirit is holy and good by nature, not by participation

“He is called holy, as the Father is holy, and the Son is holy, for to the creature holiness was brought in from without, but to the Spirit holiness is the fulfilment of nature, and it is for this reason that He is described not as being sanctified, but as sanctifying. He is called good, as the Father is good, and He who was begotten of the Good is good, and to the Spirit His goodness is essence.”

— On the Holy Spirit 19.48, trans. Blomfield Jackson (NPNF Series 2, vol. 8), source. The argument against the Pneumatomachi turns on the difference between having a divine attribute by participation (as a creature is made holy from outside) and having it by nature: the Spirit sanctifies rather than being sanctified, so his holiness is essential, not derived — the mark of God, not creature.

The Spirit is not to be “sub-numbered” below the Father and the Son

“There is one God and Father, one Only-begotten, and one Holy Ghost. We proclaim each of the hypostases singly; and, when count we must, we do not let an ignorant arithmetic carry us away to the idea of a plurality of Gods… these men, in the case of the divine nature, pay reverence to number, lest they should exceed the limits of the honour due to the Paraclete.”

— On the Holy Spirit 18.44–45, trans. Blomfield Jackson (NPNF Series 2, vol. 8), source. Basil answers those who ranked the Spirit “first, second, and third” in dignity by counting him third in order: to enumerate Father, Son and Spirit is not to grade them, for the one Godhead is above number. He warns that “sub-numeration” — treating the second and third as inferior to the first — would smuggle “the polytheism of heathen error” into “the undefiled theology of Christians.”

Notes

  • Basil’s On the Holy Spirit famously stops just short of the bare word “God” for the Spirit, defending instead the doxology that glorifies the Spirit with (not merely in) the Father and the Son — a strategic reticence, since the term homoousios applied to the Spirit was still inflammatory. The full confession of the Spirit as “Lord and Giver-of-Life… worshipped and glorified” with the Father and Son came at the Council of Constantinople (381), two years after Basil’s death, carried by his friend Gregory of Nazianzus.
  • His younger brother Gregory of Nyssa took up the same grammar to answer the counter-charge it provoked — that “three hypostases” meant three Gods.
  • Basil appears elsewhere on the wiki using the same “from the Father through the Son” taxis on the Filioque timeline, where the interest is the Spirit’s procession rather than his deity and the ousia/hypostasis distinction treated here.
