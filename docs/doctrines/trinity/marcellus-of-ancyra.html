Marcellus of Ancyra on the Trinity

Dates: c. 280–374 (bishop of Ancyra; the timeline anchors him at c. 336, the years of his Against Asterius and his deposition) · Wikipedia
Summary: A bishop who stood with the opponents of Arius at Nicaea, Marcellus taught that the one God is a single Monad and rejected the language of three hypostases. Encyclopedia accounts describe a Logos that “went out from the Father” at the creation and as becoming Son only at the incarnation, and his fragments teach that Christ’s reign began in time and will end. The Synod of Constantinople (336) deposed him; Rome (341) and Serdica (343) exonerated him; and the clause “of whose kingdom there shall be no end” in the Creed of 381 is attributed in those accounts to opposition to his teaching.

Claims & evidence

Marcellus’s own book, Against Asterius (c. 335), does not survive whole. It is known from some 128 fragments quoted — hostilely — by his chief opponent, Eusebius of Caesarea, in two works written to refute him: Against Marcellus (Contra Marcellum) and On the Theology of the Church (De Ecclesiastica Theologia). The fragments are numbered in the critical editions of Klostermann and, most recently, Vinzent; the translations below follow that fragment corpus.1

The one God as a Monad, without three hypostases

By pointing to himself and saying, “I am,” is he not clearly saying that there is only one God, namely, himself?

— Against Asterius, frag. 97 (Klostermann 76), preserved in Eusebius, On the Theology of the Church 2.19, Fourth-Century Christianity. Marcellus argues from the divine Monad: God is one, and the string of Old-Testament oneness texts he marshals (Isaiah, Hosea, Malachi, the Psalms) is set against a second, really-distinct divine subject. He rejected “three hypostases” as implying three gods, and the Catholic Encyclopedia reports that this brought on him the charge of Sabellianism.2

The Logos going out from God at creation, called Son from the incarnation

The Catholic Encyclopedia (J. P. Arendzen) describes his scheme thus: “God was originally only One Personality, but at the creation of the universe the Word or Logos went out from the Father and was God’s Activity in the world. This Logos became incarnate in Christ and was thus constituted Son of God.” The New Catholic Encyclopedia (V. C. de Clercq) puts it that “while he admits the eternity of the Logos as such, he denies an eternal generation in God, holding that the Logos became Son at the Incarnation only”: an eternal Logos, but no eternal Son. Eirini Artemi (Greek Open University; Volyn Blagovisnyk, 2019), comparing him with Sabellius, sums up the difference: “if the God of Sabellianism was metamorphosed to meet the changing needs of the world, Marcellus’ God was expanded to meet the changing needs of the world.”3

Christ’s reign as beginning in time

For this reason, the prophecy says, “I was established by him as king” (Ps 2:6), as if Christ our Lord began his reign at a certain time.

— Against Asterius, frag. 100 (Klostermann 112), in Eusebius, Against Marcellus 2.3, Fourth-Century Christianity. Because the Son and his kingdom are, for Marcellus, features of the incarnate economy rather than of the eternal Godhead, the reign has a starting point.

Christ’s reign as ending (frag. 102)

Here the Apostle reveals to us a great mystery, saying that there will be an end to Christ’s reign and that this end will be at the time when all things are placed under his feet (1 Cor 15:25).

— Against Asterius, frag. 102 (Klostermann 114), in Eusebius, Against Marcellus 2.4 and On the Theology of the Church 3.17, Fourth-Century Christianity. The fragment reads 1 Corinthians 15:24–28 (“then comes the end… that God may be all in all”). The New Catholic Encyclopedia (V. C. de Clercq) reads Marcellus as teaching that at the end “both the Son and the Spirit will reenter the Godhead, and there will be the absolute Monad again.” Luke 1:33 (Gabriel of the Christ: “of his kingdom there shall be no end”) is the text the Creed of 381 later set in its clause on Christ’s kingdom (see below).4

Reception and debate

  • Placement on the timeline. Marcellus wrote against Asterius, a supporter of Arius, and was opposed in turn by Eusebius of Caesarea. The language of one ousia and three hypostases later used by the Cappadocians is the language Marcellus had rejected.
  • “Of whose kingdom there shall be no end.” The Creed of Constantinople (381) expanded the Nicene text with the clause “of whose kingdom there shall be no end” (echoing Luke 1:33), which the Catholic Encyclopedia and the New Catholic Encyclopedia connect with Marcellus’s teaching that Christ’s reign would cease (sources in n. 4).
  • Condemnation and vindication. Marcellus had stood at Nicaea (325) with the opponents of Arius, refused the Synod of Tyre’s condemnation of Athanasius (335), and wrote Against Asterius to answer a supporter of Arius. Deposed by the Eusebian Synod of Constantinople (336), he went west, and was exonerated by Pope Julius I’s Roman synod (341) and by the Western council of Serdica (343), which vindicated Marcellus and Athanasius together. The New Catholic Encyclopedia reports that “the Eastern Councils of Antioch (341) and Sardica (343), however, reaffirmed their condemnation in strong terms.” According to Fourth-Century Christianity and the Catholic Encyclopedia, the suspicion that “one in essence” covered Sabellianism, which Marcellus’s teaching drew onto the homoousian party, was one reason Eastern bishops held back from homoousios for a generation; Athanasius, at first his defender, later distanced himself.5
  • Photinus. Photinus of Sirmium, deacon to Marcellus, was condemned by the Synod of Antioch (344), by a synod at Milan (345) and by a synod at Sirmium (351), which deposed him. The Catholic Encyclopedia (John Chapman) reports that he “declared the Son of Mary to be mere man” and that in later writers such as Augustine “Photinian is the name for any who held Christ to be a mere man”; it also judges that “Photinus went so far beyond Marcellus that it is unfair to call him his follower.”6
  • The fragments survive only because his chief critic Eusebius of Caesarea quoted them to refute them.
