Marcellus of Ancyra on the Trinity

Dates: c. 280–374 (bishop of Ancyra; the timeline anchors him at c. 336, the years of his Against Asterius and his deposition) · Wikipedia
Summary: A veteran anti-Arian who stood with the strictest defenders of Nicaea, Marcellus pressed the divine unity so hard that he swung past orthodoxy into the opposite error — a near-modalism in which the Logos is an impersonal power that “expands” out of the one God at creation, becomes Son only at the incarnation, and is finally reabsorbed, so that the Son’s reign would end. He is the standing proof that “pro-Nicene” did not yet mean “orthodox,” and the reason the Creed of 381 had to add “of whose kingdom there shall be no end.”

Claims & evidence

Marcellus’s own book, Against Asterius (c. 335), does not survive whole. It is known from some 128 fragments quoted — hostilely — by his chief opponent, Eusebius of Caesarea, in two works written to refute him: Against Marcellus (Contra Marcellum) and On the Theology of the Church (De Ecclesiastica Theologia). The fragments are numbered in the critical editions of Klostermann and, most recently, Vinzent; the translations below follow that fragment corpus.1

The one God is a Monad; there are not three hypostases

By pointing to himself and saying, “I am,” is he not clearly saying that there is only one God, namely, himself?

— Against Asterius, frag. 97 (Klostermann 76), preserved in Eusebius, On the Theology of the Church 2.19, Fourth-Century Christianity. Marcellus builds his whole theology from the divine Monad: God is one “person,” and the string of Old-Testament oneness texts he marshals (Isaiah, Hosea, Malachi, the Psalms) is meant to leave no room for a second, really-distinct divine subject. Where the emerging Nicene grammar would confess one ousia in three hypostases, Marcellus heard in “three hypostases” only three gods, and refused it — a refusal that made him, to Eastern eyes, indistinguishable from Sabellius.2

The Logos “expands” out of God, and becomes Son only at the incarnation

To keep that Monad intact, Marcellus made the Word not a distinct person alongside the Father but a power (dynamis) at rest within the one God, which “went forth” as God’s active energy (energeia) at the creation of the world and again at the incarnation. On this scheme the titles “Son,” “Christ,” and “image” belong strictly to the incarnate Word: there was an eternal Logos, but no eternal Son — the Son begins when the Word takes flesh. His opponents summed the difference from Sabellius’s modalism as: where Sabellius’s God changed masks to meet the world’s needs, Marcellus’s God expanded to meet them.3

Christ’s reign began in time

For this reason, the prophecy says, “I was established by him as king” (Ps 2:6), as if Christ our Lord began his reign at a certain time.

— Against Asterius, frag. 100 (Klostermann 112), in Eusebius, Against Marcellus 2.3, Fourth-Century Christianity. Because the Son and his kingdom are, for Marcellus, features of the incarnate economy rather than of the eternal Godhead, the reign has a starting point — the opposite corner from Arius, but reached by the same refusal to let the Son be simply, eternally God.

Christ’s reign will end, and the Word be reabsorbed into God

Here the Apostle reveals to us a great mystery, saying that there will be an end to Christ’s reign and that this end will be at the time when all things are placed under his feet (1 Cor 15:25).

— Against Asterius, frag. 102 (Klostermann 114), in Eusebius, Against Marcellus 2.4 and On the Theology of the Church 3.17, Fourth-Century Christianity. Reading 1 Corinthians 15:24–28 (“then comes the end… that God may be all in all”) with rigour, Marcellus taught that at the consummation the incarnate Word hands the kingdom back and is drawn again into the divine unity — so the reign of Christ is bounded, and at the last there is the absolute Monad again. This was the single most alarming claim of the system, because Luke 1:33 (Gabriel of the Christ: “of his kingdom there shall be no end”) flatly denies it.4

Notes

  • Why he matters to the timeline. Marcellus shows that the fourth century had to steer between two ditches, not one. Against Arius and the Eusebian conservatives, the danger was subordinating the Son into a creature; against Marcellus, the danger was collapsing the Son back into the Father until the persons and the kingdom dissolve. The settled Nicene grammar — one ousia, three eternal hypostases, supplied by the Cappadocians — was framed to shut both doors: homoousios against Arius, three real hypostases against Marcellus.
  • “Of whose kingdom there shall be no end.” The Creed of Constantinople (381) expanded the Nicene text with the clause “of whose kingdom there shall be no end” (echoing Luke 1:33) precisely to exclude Marcellus’s teaching that Christ’s reign would cease. His is the name usually attached, in the histories, to that one clause.
  • “Pro-Nicene” did not yet mean “orthodox.” Marcellus was no fellow traveller of Arius: he had stood at Nicaea (325) with the strictest anti-Arians, refused the anti-Nicene Synod of Tyre’s condemnation of Athanasius (335), and wrote Against Asterius to answer an Arian apologist. For a time the leading Nicenes stood with him: deposed by the Eusebian Synod of Constantinople (336), he fled west, and was formally exonerated by Pope Julius I’s Roman synod (341) and by the Western council of Serdica (343), which vindicated Marcellus and Athanasius together. But the East never accepted this, and the suspicion Marcellus drew onto the whole homoousian cause — that “one in essence” was a cover for Sabellianism — was one reason Eastern bishops recoiled from homoousios for a generation. Athanasius himself, at first his defender, grew guarded and quietly let the association lapse.5
  • Photinus. Marcellus’s pupil Photinus of Sirmium carried the master’s premises further toward an adoptionist Christology and was condemned repeatedly (Antioch 344, Milan 345, Sirmium 351), “Photinianism” becoming a standing byword; the recoil against the disciple deepened the recoil against the teacher.
  • His chief critic Eusebius of Caesarea — the man whose graded, subordinationist Logos-theology sat at the other extreme — is the reason we have Marcellus’s words at all: the fragments survive only because Eusebius quoted them to refute them.
