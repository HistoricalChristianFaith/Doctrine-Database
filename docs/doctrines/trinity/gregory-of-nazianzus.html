Gregory of Nazianzus on the Trinity

Dates: c. 380–381 (the Constantinople orations; lifespan c. 329–390) · Wikipedia
Summary: One of the three Cappadocians and, briefly, president of the Council of Constantinople (381), Gregory supplied the settled grammar — one ousia (essence), three hypostases (persons), distinguished by their relations — and pressed the argument to its last frontier: the full deity of the Holy Spirit.

Claims & evidence

One essence, three hypostases — distinguished by properties, not natures

we... believe in the Father, the Son and the Holy Ghost, of one Substance and glory... acknowledging the Unity in the Essence and in the undivided worship, and the Trinity in the Hypostases or Persons (which term some prefer.)... For you teach that the Essence of the Three is One and the same... [the Three] are distinguished not by natures, but by properties.

— Oration 42.16 (The Last Farewell, delivered at Constantinople, 381), trans. NPNF² vol. 7, source. The distinguishing properties are the relations of origin: “The Father is Father, and is Unoriginate… the Son is Son… The Holy Ghost is truly Spirit, coming forth from the Father indeed, but not after the manner of the Son, for it is not by Generation but by Procession… There is then One God in Three, and These Three are One” (Oration 39.12, source).

The full deity of the Holy Spirit

The Holy Ghost, then, always existed, and exists, and always will exist. He neither had a beginning, nor will He have an end; but He was everlastingly ranged with and numbered with the Father and the Son... Therefore He was ever being partaken, but not partaking; perfecting, not being perfected; sanctifying, not being sanctified; deifying, not being deified

— Oration 41.9 (On Pentecost), trans. NPNF² vol. 7, source. To the “Spirit-fighters” (Pneumatomachi) who granted the Son but withheld the name “God” from the Spirit, he presses: “Confess, my friends, the Trinity to be of One Godhead; or if you will, of One Nature; and we will pray the Spirit to give you this word God” (Oration 41.8).

The Three contemplated as One: the illumination

Each God because Consubstantial; One God because of the Monarchia. No sooner do I conceive of the One than I am illumined by the Splendour of the Three; no sooner do I distinguish Them than I am carried back to the One.

— Oration 40.41 (On Holy Baptism), trans. NPNF² vol. 7, source.

Notes

  • The Cappadocian achievement was terminological as much as doctrinal: by fixing ousia (the one shared being) and hypostasis (the three distinct subsistences) as different words — where earlier usage had blurred them — they let the church say “one God in three” without contradiction, and answered the moderate homoiousians that Nicaea’s homoousios need not collapse the Persons into one.
  • Gregory delivered his celebrated Theological Orations (Orations 27–31) at Constantinople in 380 defending this whole doctrine, including the classic exchange on the Spirit (“Is the Spirit God? Certainly”). Those five orations are not among the texts hosted here; the equivalent teaching is carried by Orations 39–42, delivered in the same city and year.
  • His fellow Cappadocian Basil of Caesarea supplied the companion argument for the Spirit’s deity from worship — that the Spirit is glorified “with” the Father and the Son and is received “with the Father and the Son” at baptism, so cannot be ranked as a creature (On the Holy Spirit 68, 70). The Council of Constantinople (381) ratified the result, writing the Spirit’s deity into the creed the church still recites.
