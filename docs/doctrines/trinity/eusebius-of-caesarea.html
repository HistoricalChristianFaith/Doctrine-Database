Eusebius of Caesarea on the Trinity

Dates: c. 325 · Wikipedia
Summary: The church historian of Caesarea, pupil of Pamphilus and admirer of Origen, describes the Son as a “second Being” who holds a divinity “received from the Father” and is “less than the First Cause.” Before Nicaea he defended Arius and was provisionally excommunicated by the Synod of Antioch; at Nicaea he subscribed the creed and wrote to his church explaining the sense in which he accepted “one in essence.”

Claims & evidence

The Son as a “second Being” and “Second Lord” whose divinity is received from the Father — “greater than all angels, but less than the First Cause”

First, then, understand that here this Second Being, the Offspring of God, is addressed… the Holy Spirit in the prophet names as His own Lord a Second Being after the Lord of the Universe.

…so that strictly the First is to be reckoned God, alone being God by nature, and not receiving (divinity) from another. And the Other is to be thought of as secondary, and as holding a Divinity received from the Father, as an image of God.

We are taught in all reverence to admit an order, that One is the Most High Father and God and Lord, and God and Lord of the Second: but that the Word of God is the Second Lord, Lord of those below Him, and yet not equally with the greater.

It only remains for Him then to be God and Lord among beings, after the Almighty God of the Universe. And He would thus be the Word of God before the ages, greater than all angels, but less than the First Cause.

— The Proof of the Gospel (Demonstratio Evangelica), Book V, trans. W. J. Ferrar, historicalchristian.faith. Written before the Arian controversy, the work presents the Son as truly begotten before the ages and divine, with a divinity “received from the Father,” the Other “secondary,” below the one “First Cause.”1

The Son not co-eternal — “a father must exist before and precede his son”

For neither was He brought into being from the Unbegotten Being by way of any event, or by division, nor was He eternally coexistent with the Father, since the One is Unbegotten and the other Begotten, and one is Father and the other Son. And all would agree that a father must exist before and precede his son.

— The Proof of the Gospel, Book V, historicalchristian.faith. In the same passage he denies that the Son was brought into being “by way of any event, or by division,” and denies that he is “eternally coexistent with the Father.” Origen had taught the Son’s eternal generation, and Athanasius later wrote that the Son “ever was and is and never was not.”2

Before Nicaea he defended Arius, and an anti-Arian synod provisionally excommunicated him

A surviving fragment of Eusebius’s letter to Alexander of Alexandria takes Arius’s part, protesting that Alexander had misrepresented what Arius actually taught and urging his readmission to communion.3 For this the anti-Arian Synod of Antioch (early 325), meeting months before Nicaea under Ossius of Cordova, examined Eusebius on the point and, when he would not sign its confession, provisionally excommunicated him — along with Theodotus of Laodicea and Narcissus of Neronias — leaving them “a place for repentance” at the great council to come.4 Eusebius was the pupil of Pamphilus, himself a disciple of Origen.

At Nicaea he subscribed, and wrote to his church explaining how he understood the creed

…not in the sense of the affections of bodies, nor as if the Son subsisted from the Father in the way of division, or any severance; for that the immaterial, and intellectual, and incorporeal nature could not be the subject of any corporeal affection…

…we received without contention what no longer pained us, as soon as, on a candid examination of the sense of the words, they appeared to us to coincide with what we ourselves have professed in the faith which we have already published.

— Letter to his Church at Caesarea, subjoined to Athanasius, De Decretis, trans. NPNF² vol. 4, CCEL (Greek and a revised translation at Fourth-Century Christianity, Urk. 22). Eusebius sent his congregation the creed he had signed together with a running gloss; Socrates Scholasticus (Ecclesiastical History I.8) says he sent it “that no one might impugn his motives on account of his previous hesitation.” In the letter he explains “one in essence” as excluding any suggestion that the Son is a part of the Father or divided from him, and describes his assent as receiving “without contention what no longer pained us.” Athanasius, reproducing the letter, writes that Eusebius “had denied the day before, but afterwards subscribed,” and that he was “ashamed… and excused himself to the Church in his own way.”

Reception and debate

  • Conviction or accommodation? Athanasius presents Eusebius’s subscription as a reversal he excused “in his own way”; Eusebius’s own letter presents it as agreement reached “on a candid examination of the sense of the words.” Both accounts are quoted above.
  • Origen, Eusebius and Arius. From Origen Eusebius shares the language of a derived, “second” divinity of the Son; he denies, as quoted above, that the Son is “eternally coexistent” with the Father. Arius taught that the Son was “made out of nothing.” How such pre-Nicene ranking of the Son relates to the Arian position is disputed; see does Origen’s ranking of the Son below the Father belong to the common pre-Nicene idiom, or supply premises for the Arian position?
  • The Origenist thread. Eusebius’s master Pamphilus, imprisoned before his martyrdom, co-wrote with him an Apology for Origen; Eusebius revered Origen and defended his memory (see the condemnation-of-Origen timeline and Pamphilus’s page).
  • Later career. After Nicaea Eusebius sided with the councils that deposed Athanasius and, in Against Marcellus and On the Theology of the Church, defended the Son’s distinct subsistence against Marcellus of Ancyra. Marcellus’s own words survive because Eusebius quoted them in these works.
  • Eusebius appears elsewhere on this wiki as a witness on the intermediate-state (descent to the dead) timeline, where his Church History preserves a compact harrowing-of-Hades confession.
