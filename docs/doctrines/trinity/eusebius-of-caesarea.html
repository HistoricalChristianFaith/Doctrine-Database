Eusebius of Caesarea on the Trinity

Dates: c. 324 · Wikipedia
Summary: The church historian of Caesarea is the archetype of the large conservative party at Nicaea — neither Arius nor Athanasius, but the Origenist middle the controversy actually had to win over. His Logos-theology is frankly subordinationist (a “second Being” who receives his divinity from the Father and is “less than the First Cause”), and before Nicaea he had defended Arius; yet he subscribed the creed and wrote home to explain a signature he construed as narrowly as its words would bear.

Claims & evidence

The Son is a “second Being” and “Second Lord” whose divinity is received from the Father — “greater than all angels, but less than the First Cause”

First, then, understand that here this Second Being, the Offspring of God, is addressed… the Holy Spirit in the prophet names as His own Lord a Second Being after the Lord of the Universe.

…so that strictly the First is to be reckoned God, alone being God by nature, and not receiving (divinity) from another. And the Other is to be thought of as secondary, and as holding a Divinity received from the Father, as an image of God.

We are taught in all reverence to admit an order, that One is the Most High Father and God and Lord, and God and Lord of the Second: but that the Word of God is the Second Lord, Lord of those below Him, and yet not equally with the greater.

It only remains for Him then to be God and Lord among beings, after the Almighty God of the Universe. And He would thus be the Word of God before the ages, greater than all angels, but less than the First Cause.

— The Proof of the Gospel (Demonstratio Evangelica), Book V, trans. W. J. Ferrar, historicalchristian.faith. Written before the controversy broke, this is Eusebius’s settled theology, worked out of Origen: the Son is truly divine and truly begotten before the ages, but his deity is derivative — a divinity “received” and held “in a secondary sense,” graded below the one “First Cause.” This is exactly the pattern the Nicene “one in essence” was framed to exclude.1

The Son is not co-eternal — “a father must exist before and precede his son”

For neither was He brought into being from the Unbegotten Being by way of any event, or by division, nor was He eternally coexistent with the Father, since the One is Unbegotten and the other Begotten, and one is Father and the other Son. And all would agree that a father must exist before and precede his son.

— The Proof of the Gospel, Book V, historicalchristian.faith. This is the hinge that sets Eusebius apart from both extremes. Against Arius, he denies the Son was “brought into being… by division” or out of nothing; but against Origen’s eternal generation — the doctrine Athanasius would make load-bearing (“He ever was and… never was not”) — he denies the Son is “eternally coexistent with the Father,” because a father must “precede his son.” That single concession is the whole of the semi-Arian position: a real, pre-cosmic, divine Son who is nonetheless posterior and subordinate to the Father.2

Before Nicaea he defended Arius, and an anti-Arian synod provisionally excommunicated him

A surviving fragment of Eusebius’s letter to Alexander of Alexandria takes Arius’s part, protesting that Alexander had misrepresented what Arius actually taught and urging his readmission to communion.3 For this the strongly anti-Arian Synod of Antioch (early 325), meeting months before Nicaea under Ossius of Cordova, examined Eusebius on the point and, when he would not sign its confession, provisionally excommunicated him — along with Theodotus of Laodicea and Narcissus of Neronias — leaving them “a place for repentance” at the great council to come.4 His sympathy was less doctrinal identity with Arius than a shared Origenist school-loyalty (Eusebius was the pupil of Pamphilus, himself a disciple of Origen): the two men prized the same graded picture of Father and Son.

At Nicaea he subscribed — and wrote home to explain a signature he read as thinly as the words allowed

…not in the sense of the affections of bodies, nor as if the Son subsisted from the Father in the way of division, or any severance; for that the immaterial, and intellectual, and incorporeal nature could not be the subject of any corporeal affection…

…we received without contention what no longer pained us, as soon as, on a candid examination of the sense of the words, they appeared to us to coincide with what we ourselves have professed in the faith which we have already published.

— Letter to his Church at Caesarea, subjoined to Athanasius, De Decretis, trans. NPNF² vol. 4, CCEL (Greek and a revised translation at Fourth-Century Christianity, Urk. 22). Eusebius sent his congregation the creed he had signed together with a running gloss, so that no one “suspect his motives on account of his previous hesitation.” He accepts “one in essence” only by draining it of any suggestion that the Son is a part of the Father or divided from him — a reading the word can bear, but a minimal one — and he presents his assent as reluctant acquiescence (“without contention… what no longer pained us”) rather than as a conversion. Athanasius, reproducing the letter, notes drily that Eusebius “had denied the day before, but afterwards subscribed,” and that he was “ashamed… and excused himself to the Church in his own way.”

Notes

  • Conviction or accommodation? This is the classic question about Eusebius at Nicaea, and the honest answer is: mostly accommodation, minimally construed. He signed a word he had not used before and did not love, satisfied it could be read without “division” of the divine essence; he never made homoousios the centre of his own theology as Athanasius did, and his post-Nicene works go on speaking the old graded language. Yet it was not bare submission either: he genuinely rejected Arius’s sharpest thesis (“out of nothing,” “there was when he was not”), so the creed’s anathemas cost him nothing he wished to keep. The signature is best read as a conservative subscribing for the peace of the church while holding his subordinationism intact under the new vocabulary.
  • How his subordinationism relates to Origen’s and to Arius’s. Eusebius stands between them on the same spectrum. From Origen he inherits the “second God”/derivative-deity language and the eternal-generation impulse — but he keeps the subordination and drops the eternity, denying the Son is “eternally coexistent.” From that same soil Arius pressed subordination all the way into creaturehood (“made out of nothing”), which Eusebius would not follow. The wiki weighs the underlying reading on its own page — see is the Son subordinate to the Father?
  • The Origenist thread. Eusebius’s master Pamphilus, imprisoned before his martyrdom, co-wrote with him an Apology for Origen; Eusebius revered Origen and defended his memory (see the condemnation-of-Origen timeline and Pamphilus’s page). It was this school-loyalty, more than agreement with Arius’s formulas, that put Eusebius on the “conservative” side when the crisis broke.
  • Later career. After Nicaea Eusebius did not become a champion of its word; he sided with the councils that deposed Athanasius and, in Against Marcellus and On the Theology of the Church, defended the Son’s distinct real subsistence against Marcellus of Ancyra — the same instinct that had made him uneasy with homoousios now aimed the other way, against a theology he judged to collapse the Son into the Father.
  • Eusebius appears elsewhere on this wiki as a witness on the intermediate-state (descent to the dead) timeline, where his Church History preserves a compact harrowing-of-Hades confession.
