From what sources did the apologists draw the Logos as “another God” (Justin Martyr, Dialogue with Trypho 56, 61)?

Disputed reading · on The Trinity · witness: Justin Martyr

Related readings: Did rabbinic Judaism suppress a divine-Messiah (“two powers in heaven”) reading of Daniel 7? · Did the Enoch–Metatron identification grow out of the earlier Enoch tradition? · Did the first Christians worship Jesus, and did that worship come before and shape the doctrine of his divinity? · Does Origen’s ranking of the Son below the Father belong to the common pre-Nicene idiom, or supply premises for the Arian position?

The passage

I shall attempt to persuade you, since you have understood the Scriptures, [of the truth] of what I say, that there is, and that there is said to be, another God and Lord subject to the Maker of all things; who is also called an Angel, because He announces to men whatsoever the Maker of all things—above whom there is no other God—wishes to announce to them.

God begot before all creatures a Beginning, [who was] a certain rational power [proceeding] from Himself, who is called by the Holy Spirit, now the Glory of the Lord, now the Son, again Wisdom, again an Angel, then God, and then Lord and Logos

— Justin Martyr, Dialogue with Trypho 56 and 61, trans. ANF vol. 1, source.1 The same chapter 56 calls the one who appeared to Abraham, Jacob and Moses “distinct from Him who made all things—numerically, I mean, not [distinct] in will.” Every reading below also turns on a Jewish text about a century older, Philo of Alexandria’s comment on Genesis 9:6, which survives in Greek through Eusebius’s quotation:

For nothing mortal could be made in the likeness of the Most High God and Father of the universe, but in the likeness of the second God, who is the Word of the former.

— Philo, Questions and Answers on Genesis II.62, as quoted in Eusebius, Preparation of the Gospel VII.13, trans. E. H. Gifford, source.2

The question

Justin argues from the Jewish Scriptures that a God and Lord “numerically” distinct from the Maker of all appeared in the theophanies and is the Word begotten before all creatures, and Philo had called the Word “the second God.” Historians of doctrine dispute where this way of speaking came from: from Greek philosophy, from the Old Testament and the Gospel of John restated in philosophical terms, from the exegesis of Greek-speaking Judaism, from a scriptural proof Justin himself built out of the theophanies, from a Jewish theology of God and his Word that Christians shared, or from an older Christian exegesis of the theophanies practised in worship. The answer bears on how the Logos teaching of the second-century apologists relates both to the Jewish Scriptures and to the fourth-century creeds.

A philosophical Logos taken into Christian teaching

In brief: the apologists’ Logos, a divine power linking the transcendent God with the world, is the Logos of Greek philosophy, which Christian writers identified with Christ.

Held by: Edwin Hatch (English theologian, Oxford, Hibbert Lectures, 1888);3 Adolf von Harnack (Lutheran church historian, History of Dogma, 3rd German ed., 1894);4 George Foot Moore (Presbyterian historian of religion, Harvard, 1922).5

Moore traces the Christian search for a second divine figure in the Old Testament to “Christian theology, with its philosophical presumptions,” for which a God who appeared in human form “was necessarily such a being”: “In this assumption and to a considerable extent in their particular interpretations the Fathers had a precursor in the Jewish theologian Philo.” The rabbinic Targums supply no such figure: there memra is not “a ‘being’ of any kind or in any sense” but “a circumlocution for ‘God,’” “a phenomenon of translation, not a creature of speculation.”5

Hatch places Justin within a philosophical development. Platonic ideas and Stoic logoi had been blended and viewed “as expressions of a single Logos,” and the question “How could God create?” was answered by the doctrine that “He created by means of His Logos.” That theory “is not far distant from that which is found in the earlier Apologists”: Justin’s “seed of the Logos” in every person and the whole Logos in Christ belong to it. When Christian writers then asked about the genesis and nature of the Logos, “the influence of Greek thought is even more conspicuous than before.”3

Harnack defines dogma as “a work of the Greek spirit on the soil of the Gospel,” and insists on the last words: “The foolishness of identifying dogma and Greek philosophy never entered my mind.” The Logos title mattered because “the Logos had long been regarded by cultured men as the beginning and principle of the creation.” With it, “the theories concerning Christ are removed from Jewish and Old Testament soil, and also that of religion (in the strict sense of the word), and transplanted to the Greek one. Even in his pre-existent state Christ is an independent power existing side by side with God.” On this account the apologists’ “another God” is the Christian form of a cosmological Logos that educated Greeks had long held.4

The Word of Scripture and of John, set out in the philosophical idiom of the apologists’ readers

In brief: the Logos came into Christian teaching from the Old Testament’s word and wisdom of God through the Gospel of John; the apologists stated it in philosophical terms for pagan readers, and some features of their account come from Stoic thought.

Held by: Jules Lebreton (Catholic, Jesuit historian of Christian origins, “The Logos,” Catholic Encyclopedia, 1910).6

Lebreton reads the Old Testament’s creative word as “personified, but… not conceived of as a distinct Divine hypostasis”; in the Book of Wisdom “a parallel is established (9:1-2) between wisdom and the Word.” The Targumic Memra “appears very often to be only a paraphrase substituted by the Targumist for the name of Jahveh,” and Philo’s Logos is an impersonal intermediary. John’s Word draws on neither: “the Word is for him the Word of God, and thereby he holds with Jewish tradition, the theology of the Book of Wisdom, of the Psalms, of the Prophetical Books, and of Genesis.” “The Word of St. John is not an intermediary, but a Mediator.”

The apologists inherit that Word and develop it “partly owing to their philosophic training, but more particularly to their desire to state their faith in a way familiar to their readers”: Justin “insists strongly on the theology of the Logos in his ‘Apology’ meant for heathens, much less so in his ‘Dialogue with the Jew Tryphon.’” On the generation of the Word, Lebreton writes, they hold that it “was not created… but was born of the very Substance of the Father”; where they speak of the Word as “uttered” for creation, “Mental survivals of Stoic psychology seem to be responsible,” the Stoics having distinguished “the innate word (endiathetos) and the uttered word (prophorikos).” Greek speculation moved some writers “to consider the Word as an intermediary being between God and the world,” and so “to some of them, at least to St. Justin and Tertullian, there seemed to be in this Divinity a certain subordination.” On this account the Logos is scriptural and Johannine in origin, and the philosophical idiom is the apologists’ way of presenting it to their audience.6

Hellenistic-Jewish traditions, shared with Philo, given a personal sense

In brief: Justin’s proofs and titles for the Logos come from the scriptural exegesis of Greek-speaking Judaism, seen in Philo; the Christian element is a Logos who is a distinct person, identified with Jesus.

Held by: Erwin R. Goodenough (historian of Hellenistic Judaism, The Theology of Justin Martyr, 1923);7 Alan F. Segal (historian of religion, a self-described “believing Jew,” Two Powers in Heaven, 1977).8

Goodenough holds that Christianity “began not with speculation but with a religious experience,” and that Jewish objections to a second God were met in a Jewish setting: “It was in Hellenistic Jewish thinking that the problem was solved by the brilliant stroke of identifying Christ with the Logos.” Philo, the tragedian Ezekiel and the Targumic Memra all used the Word or an angel to explain the theophanies, though for them the interest was “the doctrine of God,” not a second being. Justin’s proof from Mamre, Bethel and the bush therefore comes from that source: he “has used material from Greek Judaism, but has given it an implication never found in any Judaism.”7

Segal finds the same exegesis among Jews whom the rabbis later opposed. The “two powers” heresy “involved interpreting scripture to say that a principal angelic or hypostatic manifestation in heaven was equivalent to God,” and the Christianity of Justin “would have been condemned by the rabbis as ‘two powers’ speculation.” In Dialogue 56, “It is a Jew, not Justin, who admits that another divine being, ‘The Lord,’ was present at the destruction of Sodom and Gomorah”; “there is no reason to doubt that such heterodox Jews existed as early as Philo. Justin only endeavored to prove that this second divinity is the Christ.” Following Goodenough, Segal writes that “both Justin and Philo should be seen as evidencing examples of the same Hellenistic Jewish traditions,” since most of Justin’s titles for the Logos are those used by Philo and other Hellenistic Jewish writers. What Segal finds new is “the sharply drawn personality of this manifestation (together with the doctrine of the incarnation)”: on this account Justin received the second divine figure from Jewish exegesis and identified it with a person, Jesus Christ.8

A scriptural proof Justin built from the theophanies, joined to the traditional two-Gods testimonia

In brief: Justin himself arranged the Old Testament theophanies into a scriptural proof that a second divine Person, the Son, appeared to the patriarchs, and joined it to an older Christian set of proof-texts for two who are called God and Lord.

Held by: Benedict Kominiak (Catholic, Benedictine, The Theophanies of the Old Testament in the Writings of St. Justin, 1948); Demetrios Trakatellis (Greek Orthodox biblical scholar, later Archbishop Demetrios of America, The Pre-Existence of Christ in the Writings of Justin Martyr, 1976); Oskar Skarsaune (church historian, MF Norwegian School of Theology, Proof from Prophecy, 1987).9

Kominiak reads Justin’s chapters on Mamre, Bethel and the bush as an exegetical construction: “Justin has arranged the theophanic texts into a scriptural proof for the plurality of Divine Persons and for the divinity of Christ. This is the first Christian attempt to construct the well-known argument from the theophanies.” Trakatellis, surveying the earlier literature, writes: “In the known texts then of the first and second centuries, there is no christological interpretation of the Old Testament theophanies except in Justin’s works”; the “coherent christological interpretation of theophanies . . . seems to be Justin’s own achievement.”

Skarsaune, working from the proof-text traditions behind the Dialogue, holds that “the argument on the theophanies is not met with prior to Justin” and that “Justin has an inventor’s pride with regard to his argument in Dial. 56–60.” In Bucur’s summary of his view, Justin fused the traditional testimonia for two Gods (Gen 19:24; Ps 110:1; Ps 45:7) with his own argument that the theophanies were christophanies, an argument “forged by Justin in the heat of his antidualistic polemic.” Skarsaune traces its course: “the section on the theophanies, Dial. 56–60, is one of Justin’s own substantial contributions to the traditional argument employed elsewhere in the Dialogue. He has probably presented it for the first time in his anti-Marcion Syntagma, briefly recalling part of it quite incidentally in 1 Apol. 63, and then coming back to the theme in a fresh way in the Dialogue.” On this account the “another God” of Dialogue 56 is the conclusion of a scriptural proof that Justin composed, first against Marcion and then in the Dialogue.9

A Jewish theology of God and his Word, which Justin attests

In brief: a theology of God and his Word or Wisdom, which its proponents call “binitarian,” was common in first- and second-century Judaism; John and Justin each draw on it, and what is distinctively Christian is its identification with Jesus.

Held by: Daniel Boyarin (historian of religion, professor of Talmudic culture, “The Gospel of the Memra,” 2001).10

Boyarin takes up Goodenough’s account but adds what Goodenough “does not emphasize enough”: “how thoroughly first-century Judaism has absorbed (or produced!) these central ‘Middle Platonic’ theological notions.” The Logos or Sophia as God’s presence and mediator “was a very widespread one in the thought-world of first-century and even second-century Judaism,” and he reads Logos theology “as a common element in much Jewish, including Christian Jewish, religious imagination,” comparing Philo’s Logos, the Targumic Memra and John’s Prologue. Justin is an independent witness. In Dialogue 61 he “knows of a midrash that reads the word ‘Beginning’ (αρχη) of Gen 1:1 as a reference to the Logos,” the kind of midrash found in the Targum and the Fourth Gospel: “We have thus in Justin precious corroborating evidence for such interpretation and such theology among Jews from which the traditions animating both the Evangelist and the apologist have drawn.” Where Hurtado treats binitarian devotion as the Christian novelty, Boyarin writes, “for me, the binitarianism is not specifically Christian, only its association with Jesus is.”10

An older Christian exegesis of the theophanies, rooted in worship, which Justin extended

In brief: Christians identified Jesus as the Lord of the Old Testament theophanies in their worship from the first decades of the movement, and Justin turned that existing exegesis to polemical use.

Held by: Larry W. Hurtado (New Testament scholar, University of Edinburgh, Lord Jesus Christ, 2003);11 Bogdan G. Bucur (Orthodox priest, Antiochian Archdiocese, patristics scholar, Theological Studies, 2014).12

Hurtado holds that “Justin did not originate the basic idea that the preincarnate Jesus could be found active in certain Old Testament passages. . . . Justin was essentially building upon a line of christological argument already available. He reflects an approach to the Old Testament that had been a feature of devotion to Jesus during the first decades of the Christian movement.”11

Bucur argues “in agreement with Larry Hurtado, that the argument from theophanies precedes its use by Justin.” He traces the exegesis into the New Testament: the Gospel of John “identifies the kyrios in Isaiah’s vision with the kyrios of Christian worship” (John 12:41 with 1:14), and the Book of Revelation “extends the thrice-holy hymn sung by Isaiah’s seraphim to the Son.” Justin’s writings and Melito’s Peri Pascha are “two separate witnesses of an older tradition.” He grants that “neither the concern with biblical theophanies, nor the binitarian tendency in reading such texts, nor the practice of ‘rewriting the Bible,’ are originally or primarily Christian,” and reads Trypho’s concession that one of Abraham’s visitors must be God as “part of an older Jewish exegetical tradition, whose trace one can discern in Philo.” What he places in early Christian worship is the identification of the Lord of the theophanies with Jesus: “The argument from theophanies did not derive from Justin’s second-century antidualistic polemics but was the extension to such purpose of a much older exegetical tradition belonging to the Christian discourse ad intra, in the context of worship and celebration. Furthermore, this approach to theophanies remained normative for the vast majority of early Christian writers before the fifth century, as well as for hymnography until the ninth century, and iconography until the fifteenth.” On this account the “another God” of Dialogue 56 is Justin’s polemical use of a reading that Christians already practised in worship.12
