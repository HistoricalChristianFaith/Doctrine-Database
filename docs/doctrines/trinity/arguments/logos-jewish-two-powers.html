Is the apologists’ Logos-as-“second God” a Hellenizing novelty, or an inheritance from Second Temple Jewish “two powers in heaven”?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: plausible — as the claim that the Logos-theology with which this timeline opens (Justin Martyr’s Word “numerically distinct… another God,” even a “second God” begotten before all creatures, who did the Old Testament theophanies) is not a Hellenizing corruption of a simpler Jewish gospel but a development from within a Second Temple Jewish matrix in which a principal divine agent — a hypostatized Word or Wisdom, the Name-bearing Angel, a “second power in heaven” — already stood beside YHWH. On this reading, binitarian/Logos-Christology grew organically out of the way Scripture was read in Jesus’ own milieu, so the divine Son the councils later confessed was not first read back into Scripture at Nicaea. The thesis pulls apart into a narrow claim and a strong claim that must be rated separately. The narrow claim — that the raw materials of Logos-Christology were Jewish, not merely Greek, and that the apologists reached for the divine Son from Scripture and its Jewish interpretation, not from Plato — is now close to a scholarly consensus (Segal, Hurtado, Bauckham, Boyarin, Hengel), and defeats the old Harnack “Hellenization of the gospel” charge. The strong claim — that there was an established, mainstream pre-Christian “two powers” / binitarian Judaism that Christianity simply inherited intact — is genuinely contested (the rabbinic reports that name “two powers” a heresy are late and reactive; the reconstruction is inferential). And neither version delivers the Nicene result: the Jewish matrix is agent-shaped and subordinationist, yielding at most a binitarian backdrop, not the co-equal homoousios Trinity — the apologists’ own “second God” language is exactly what the fourth century had to discipline. So the argument tells against the Hellenization form of the “unscriptural novelty” charge while conceding a real development beyond its Jewish seedbed · advanced by Alan F. Segal, Daniel Boyarin, Larry Hurtado.

This page is the Christian-continuity / Logos-Christology angle: where those weigh whether the second figure is in the text or in the tradition, this one weighs whether the apologists’ Logos came from that tradition. The pieces are kept distinct rather than duplicated.

The claim

The Trinity timeline opens with the apologists’ frankly striking language for the Son: Justin Martyr calls the Word “another God and Lord subject to the Maker of all things… who is also called an Angel,” “numerically distinct” from the Father though “not [distinct] in will,” and elsewhere grants he “may be called… a second God”; Origen likewise allows the Son to be a “second God” (deuteros theos).1 The oldest and hardest objection to Nicaea reads this as evidence against the creed: the divine, distinct Son is a Hellenizing intrusion — Greek philosophical speculation (the Middle-Platonic intermediary, the Stoic logos) grafted onto the simple ethical monotheism of Jesus and the earliest church, and then hardened into dogma by councils. This is the classic thesis of Adolf von Harnack: that the development of the Logos into a co-eternal divine person is the “Hellenization” of the gospel, “a work of the Greek spirit on the soil of the gospel.”2

The interpretation weighed here is the counter-thesis that has largely displaced Harnack in the scholarship: that the apologists’ Logos was not imported from pagan philosophy but inherited from Second Temple Judaism, which already knew a principal divine mediator standing next to God and sharing in his identity, name, and work — personified Wisdom (Proverbs 8; Sirach 24; Wisdom of Solomon 7), the Word/Memra of the Aramaic Targums, the Name-bearing Angel of Exodus 23:21, the enthroned “second power” the later rabbis policed as heresy, and, in Hellenistic-Jewish Alexandria, Philo’s Logos, whom he too calls a “second God” (deuteros theos). If Logos-Christology is a drawing-out of that, then the divine Son was already latent in first-century Jewish reading of Scripture, and the councils developed — rather than invented or imported — him. That directly engages the “unscriptural novelty” charge in its Hellenization form, alongside the wiki’s pages on whether homoousios is an illegitimate novelty (weak) and whether the Spirit’s deity is a fourth-century over-reading (weak).

Who advances it

The continuity thesis is held across a spectrum. Its tradition-historical foundation is Alan F. Segal, whose Two Powers in Heaven (Brill, 1977) reconstructed an early Jewish exegetical tradition — rooted in doubled-theophany and Name-bearing-angel texts — in which “a principal angelic or hypostatic manifestation in heaven was equivalent to God,” a reading only later branded the “two powers” heresy. Its boldest form is Daniel Boyarin, who argues that Logos/Memra theology was ordinary Jewish coin — a “Jewish binitarianism” — so that John’s Prologue is a Jewish midrash on Genesis 1 rather than a Hellenized corruption, and that “Judaism” and “Christianity” became separate religions only when the rabbis and the church-fathers each drew borders, the rabbis ruling “two powers” out of bounds as heresy. On the devotional side, Larry Hurtado reads the earliest binitarian worship of Jesus as a mutation within Jewish monotheism, not a Hellenizing departure from it, and Richard Bauckham, Martin Hengel, Jarl Fossum, and Charles Gieschen variously locate the roots of high Christology in Jewish mediator-, Wisdom-, and Name-traditions. A key witness to the pre-Christian existence of the vocabulary is Philo of Alexandria (d. c. 50), a Jew who never became a Christian yet already called the Logos a “second God.” Ranged against the strong form are those who read the Jewish mediator-figures as subordinate literary personifications or translation-buffers rather than distinct divine beings (the older view of George Foot Moore on the Memra), and who judge “two powers” to have been marginal and the rabbinic evidence too late to reconstruct a mainstream pre-Christian binitarianism.3

The evidence for

The mediator-figures are in the pre-Christian Jewish sources, not invented by the church. Second Temple Judaism did personify God’s Word and Wisdom and set them beside him at creation: Wisdom is “set up from everlasting… when he appointed the foundations of the earth: then I was by him” (Proverbs 8:23–30); she “came out of the mouth of the most High” (Sirach 24:3); the Aramaic Targums repeatedly make the Memra (“Word”) of the LORD the agent of creation, revelation, and salvation where the Hebrew has YHWH acting directly. Alongside these stands the Name-bearing Angel “for my name is in him” (Exodus 23:21) and the enthroned second figure “one like a son of man” who receives dominion and worship in Daniel 7:13–14. This is the same cluster of texts the ante-Nicene fathers pressed for their Christophany reading — and it is demonstrably older than Christianity. Justin does not argue the pre-existent Christ from Plato but from Genesis 19, Exodus 3, and Proverbs 8, i.e. from within a recognizably Jewish way of reading the Hebrew Bible.4

A Jew called the Logos a “second God” before any Christian did. Philo of Alexandria, commenting on Genesis 9:6 (“in the image of God he made man”), asks why Scripture speaks “as if of another God” and answers that man is made not in the likeness of the Most High Father but “in that of the second God, who is His Logos.” Philo is a devout monotheist and no Christian; yet the very phrase — deuteros theos — that critics take as Justin’s and Origen’s tell-tale Hellenizing slip is already there in a first-century Jewish exegete deriving it from Scripture. Whatever its philosophical dress, the “second God” idiom is thus a datum of Jewish theology, not a Christian novelty and not a purely pagan borrowing.5

A real early-Jewish “two powers” tradition stands behind the reading. Segal showed that before it became a heresy, “two powers in heaven” was a live way of reading the doubled-theophany and Name-angel texts, branded heretical only from the second century — largely as a reaction to Christianity (and later to Gnostic and Merkabah currents). That chronology is the crux: if the rabbis had to forbid reading a second divine power out of these passages, the reading pre-existed the prohibition. Boyarin builds the constructive case: the Memra and Wisdom traditions gave first-century Jews — including the Fourth Evangelist — a ready “binitarian” theology of God and his Word, so that the Logos hymn of John 1 reads as an intra-Jewish text rather than a Hellenized one; on this account “Christianity” and “Judaism” were not two religions one of which borrowed from Greece, but a single field later partitioned, with binitarian Logos-theology falling on the Christian side of a border the rabbis drew.6

The scholars most committed to a Jewish setting for the earliest devotion agree on the narrow point. Hurtado’s whole program is that the worship of Jesus arose as a “mutation” within Second-Temple Jewish monotheism, catalogued alongside its “principal agent” figures (Wisdom/Logos, exalted patriarchs, chief angels), not as an assimilation to Greco-Roman polytheism; Hengel had already dismantled any clean “Palestinian-Jewish vs. Hellenistic” partition, showing that first-century Judaism was itself thoroughly Hellenized, so that a “Jewish” Logos and a “Greek” one are not tidy alternatives. On any of these accounts the apologists’ Word is continuous with a Jewish theology of divine mediation, and Harnack’s picture of an alien Greek graft is the part that fails.7

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The narrow, negative claim is very strong: Logos-Christology did not have to be, and largely was not, imported from pagan philosophy, because a Jewish theology of a hypostatized Word/Wisdom and a Name-bearing second power already lay to hand, and the apologists reached for the divine Son by exegeting Jewish Scripture. Harnack’s “Hellenization of the gospel” thesis, in its strong form, is now a minority position for exactly this reason. To the degree the “unscriptural novelty” charge means “a divine, distinct Son is Greek metaphysics alien to biblical monotheism,” the continuity thesis answers it.8

“Jewish, not Greek” is a false clean split — the matrix is Hellenistic Judaism. The sharpest witness for the thesis, Philo, is himself an Alexandrian Jew whose deuteros theos is shaped by Middle-Platonic and Stoic categories; the Wisdom of Solomon is a Greek-language Jewish book steeped in Hellenistic philosophy. Hengel’s own point cuts both ways: if all first-century Judaism was Hellenized, then showing the Logos is “Jewish” does not show it is un-Hellenized. So the continuity thesis does not so much refute the presence of Greek thought as relocate it — the philosophical categories entered Christology through a Hellenistic-Jewish matrix rather than as a later pagan graft. That is a real correction to Harnack, but it is not the clean “organic Hebrew inheritance untouched by Athens” that popular restatements imply.9

The strong “binitarian Judaism” form is genuinely contested. That a mainstream pre-Christian Judaism held a “two powers” or binitarian theology — inherited by Christianity wholesale — is a bolder claim than the sources compel. The rabbinic reports that name “two powers in heaven” a heresy are Tannaitic and later, and largely reactive; Segal himself locates the crystallization of concern in the early second century. One can grant that the exegetical possibility pre-existed the prohibition without granting that a settled binitarian theology was the Jewish norm. Boyarin’s reconstruction is influential but disputed: older scholarship (George Foot Moore) read the Memra as a reverential circumlocution or translation-buffer — a way of avoiding anthropomorphic directness, not a distinct divine person — and the personified Wisdom of Proverbs 8 is widely read as a literary personification, not a hypostasis. On these readings the “matrix” supplies the vocabulary and imaginative resources Christology drew on, which is real and important, but not a ready-made second divine being waiting to be identified with Jesus.10

The matrix does not deliver Nicaea — and the apologists’ “second God” is the proof. This is the load-bearing limit, and it cuts against friend and foe alike. Even granting the fullest continuity, the Jewish mediator-figures are subordinate — a principal agent, an envoy bearing the sender’s name, a second and lesser power — and, as Hurtado stresses, they were exalted without being worshipped. The matrix yields at most a binitarian backdrop, not the co-equal, consubstantial Trinity: it grounds a Son who is “another God” and “less than the First Cause,” which is precisely the subordinationist Logos of Justin, Origen, and Eusebius that the fourth century found it had to discipline with homoousios (see this timeline’s arc from Justin’s “second God” to Nicaea’s “one in essence”). So the continuity thesis is double-edged against the novelty charge: it defeats the Hellenization form (the raw materials were Jewish) while conceding the development form (Nicene equality genuinely goes beyond the seedbed). Heiser, the modern popularizer of the “two powers” frame, is careful here — he claims a binitarian backdrop, explicitly not the Trinity taught in the Old Testament; the overstatement to flag is the popularizing slide from “the seedbed was Jewish” to “the Trinity was already there,” which reads three-ness into a matrix that at most carries two-ness, and equality into a matrix that carries subordination.11

Assessment

Plausible — as the claim that the apologists’ Logos-as-“second God” is an inheritance from a Second Temple Jewish theology of divine mediation rather than a Hellenizing novelty. The narrow form (Jewish, not merely Greek, roots; Harnack’s “Hellenization of the gospel” refuted) is close to a consensus and is arguably sound; the strong form (an established, mainstream pre-Christian “two powers” / binitarian Judaism inherited wholesale) is genuinely contested; and the honest net, weighing the interpretation as advanced, sits at plausible.

Three findings hold it there. First, the mediator-figures are demonstrably pre-Christian and Jewish — personified Wisdom, the Targumic Memra, the Name-bearing Angel, Daniel’s enthroned son of man — and a first-century Jew, Philo, already called the Logos a “second God,” so the apologists’ language is not a pagan graft. Second, “Jewish” and “Greek” are not clean alternatives: the very matrix invoked (Philo, Wisdom of Solomon) is Hellenistic Judaism, so the thesis relocates Greek thought rather than expelling it, and the boldest “binitarian Judaism” reconstruction (Boyarin) leans on late, reactive rabbinic evidence that the field disputes. Third and decisively, the matrix is subordinationist and its figures unworshipped, so it grounds only a binitarian backdrop — the apologists’ own “second God” is the subordinationist Logos Nicaea had to correct, which means the continuity thesis defeats the Hellenization version of the “unscriptural novelty” charge but concedes a genuine development at Nicaea rather than a Trinity lying ready-made in Second-Temple Judaism. The sub-claim to keep is “Logos-Christology drew on a Jewish theology of divine mediation, not on imported paganism”; the one to flag on the proponents’ side is the slide from a Jewish seedbed to a fully-formed Trinitarian (or even binitarian-ontological) doctrine already held in first-century Judaism.
