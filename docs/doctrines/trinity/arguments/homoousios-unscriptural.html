Is Nicaea’s homoousios an illegitimate, unscriptural novelty?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: weak — the narrow point is true and conceded (the word homoousios is not in Scripture), but the conclusion drawn from it — that the Nicene confession is therefore an illegitimate innovation — does not follow: an extra-biblical term can carry a biblical sense, the Antioch-268 rejection was of a different (materialist) sense, and scriptural words alone had already proven insufficient because the Arians accepted every one of them and re-read it · advanced by Arius and the later homoian party.

Related arguments: the unscriptural-novelty charge — see also Is the Spirit’s consubstantial deity a fourth-century over-reading, not scriptural?

The claim

“Why did the Fathers at Nicaea use terms not in Scripture, ‘Of the essence’ and ‘One in essence’?”

— the Arian objection as reported (and answered) by Athanasius, De Decretis 1, Defence of the Nicene Definition.1 The interpretation this serves: because homoousios (“one in essence”) and “from the essence” are words the Bible never uses, Nicaea imposed a philosophical, materialist term (“of one stuff”) that had itself been suspect — associated with Paul of Samosata and rejected by the synod that condemned him at Antioch (268) — and carried it by imperial pressure rather than the faith of the fathers. So the creed is a novelty read back into Scripture, not the apostolic faith drawn out of it.

Who advances it

The complaint is as old as the creed. Athanasius reports the Arians raising it repeatedly — that the council had no right to “introduce non-scriptural expressions” — and it became the standard banner of the homoian party after 357, whose Sirmium and Ariminum formulae proposed to ban ousia (“essence”) language from the creed altogether as unscriptural and divisive.2 The Arian and Eusebian objectors pressed two distinct points that later critics have often run together: (a) the word is extra-biblical, and (b) that same word had been condemned at Antioch a generation before — so the council was reviving a term the church had already thrown out. The “imperial fiat” charge trades on Constantine’s presence and his reported role in urging the term at the council.

The evidence for

Three things are genuinely true and give the objection its force. First, the lexical fact is simply correct: neither homoousios nor “from the essence” appears in Scripture, and its defenders granted this without a fight — Athanasius concedes “the tokens of truth are more exact as drawn from Scripture, than from other sources.”3 Second, the materialist worry was real: ousia could be heard as a lump of divine stuff divided between Father and Son (the very sense in which Paul of Samosata had deployed “coessential” to argue for “three essences”), and it was on that reading that the Antioch synod of 268 had refused the word.4 Third, the appeal to precedent is not fabricated: a prior council really had written that the Son is “not coessential with the Father,” so Nicaea did face fathers on the other side, and had to reckon with them rather than pretend they did not exist.

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The word is not in the Bible. Anyone who claims otherwise is wrong, and the honest Nicene position always conceded it: the term is a human coinage introduced to guard a scriptural teaching, not a quotation of one. The two historical facts — the materialist overtone of ousia and the earlier Antiochene refusal of “coessential” — are also real, not apologetic inventions, and they are the reason the term needed careful definition rather than bare assertion.

Where the inference fails. The argument equivocates between a true narrow claim and a false broad one. “The word is extra-biblical” does not entail “the doctrine is an illegitimate novelty” — and every step from the first to the second breaks down.

Extra-biblical wording is not the test; sense is. Athanasius’ standing reply is that one must “look at the sense more than the wording”: “religiousness is confessed by all to be lawful, even though presented in strange phrases, provided only they are used with a religious view.” The council reached for a non-scriptural term precisely because scriptural ones had failed to do the job — not from failure of Scripture but from the Arians’ skill at swallowing each biblical phrase (“from God,” “like the Father,” “true God”) and re-reading it in a sense that applied equally to creatures. When the bishops tried “from God,” the Arians said that too is said of all things (1 Cor. 8:6); so the fathers “were forced to express more distinctly the sense of the words ‘from God’” by writing “from the essence.” The novelty was in the defence, forced by a novel evasion; the thing defended was the plain sense the evaders were emptying.5

The Antioch precedent is about a different sense. Athanasius meets the strongest form of the objection — that a council of fathers had actually rejected “coessential” — head-on and does not brush it aside (“all are fathers… nor is it religious to settle that these have spoken well, and those ill”). His answer is that the two councils used the same word in two senses: the bishops who deposed the Samosatene “took Coessential in a bodily sense,” because Paul had argued that if Christ is coessential there must be “three essences,” and so they rightly denied it of that materialist meaning; the bishops at Nicaea, “reflecting that the word ‘Coessential’ has not this meaning when used of things immaterial, and especially of God,” affirmed it of that immaterial meaning. A word condemned in a sense nobody at Nicaea held is no bar to the word affirmed in a sense Antioch never addressed.6 This disarms the materialist charge in the same stroke: the council explicitly repudiated the “of one stuff, divided and portioned” reading the objection relies on.

“Imperial fiat” overstates the record. Constantine convened the council and, on Eusebius’ own account, urged the term; but the decision was a near-unanimous act of some three hundred bishops that the churches then received, defended through decades in which imperial power more often leaned the other way (the pro-Arian courts of Constantius exiled the Nicenes and pushed the homoian creeds). A doctrine carried purely by an emperor’s will does not survive its patron’s successors turning against it; this one did. That the emperor favoured the word does not make the word false, any more than later imperial hostility made it true — the term is to be weighed on whether it guards the sense of Scripture, which is a question about the sense, not the sponsor.

Assessment

Weak. As an interpretation — that homoousios is an illegitimate, unscriptural novelty — the argument fails on its best available case. Keep the one true, conceded point: the term is extra-biblical, and a doctrine ought to be able to show itself as the sense of Scripture rather than merely quote a philosophical word. That point is a demand for justification, not a refutation, and the justification was given: a non-biblical word may faithfully express a biblical sense (the mainstream verdict of the tradition, and the reason creeds since have used hypostasis, “person,” and “nature” freely), the Antioch-268 condemnation targeted a materialist sense Nicaea itself rejected, and the council’s reception through anti-Nicene reigns tells against “imperial fiat.” The serious residue is the wording-precision worry — ousia’s materialist overtone genuinely needed the disclaiming Athanasius supplied — but that is a caution about how the term must be defined, not a case against holding it. The distinct claim “the term is not in the Bible” is true; the interpretation built on it, that the Nicene faith is therefore an illegitimate innovation, does not hold.
