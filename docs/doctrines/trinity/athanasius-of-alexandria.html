Athanasius of Alexandria on the Trinity

Dates: c. 356 (the mature anti-Arian corpus; lifespan c. 296–373) · Wikipedia
Summary: Present at Nicaea as a deacon and later bishop of Alexandria (through five exiles), Athanasius is the great defender of homoousios. His argument runs from salvation: only if the Son is fully God can he make us partakers of God, so the Son must be “proper to the Father’s essence,” eternal, and one in being with him.

Claims & evidence

The Son is proper to the Father’s essence, very God, and eternal

Very Son of the Father, natural and genuine, proper to His essence... not a creature or work, but an offspring proper to the Father's essence. Wherefore He is very God, existing one in essence with the very Father... And He ever was and is and never was not. For the Father being everlasting, His Word and His Wisdom must be everlasting.

— Four Discourses Against the Arians I.9, trans. NPNF² vol. 4, source. He returns to it in I.16: “what is from the essence of the Father, and proper to Him, is entirely the Son… because He is His proper offspring from His essence”; and I.35: “the Father’s essence being unalterable, unalterable must be also the proper Offspring from it” — answering Arius’s claim that the Son is “alterable.”

The eternity of the Son entails the eternity of the Triad

if the Word is not with the Father from everlasting, the Triad is not everlasting; but a Monad was first, and afterwards by addition it became a Triad.

— Four Discourses Against the Arians I.17–18, source. The Son “always is; for, whereas the Father always is, so what is proper to His essence must always be; and this is His Word and His Wisdom” (I.29).

Notes

  • Athanasius’s master-argument is soteriological: “He was made man that we might be made God” (On the Incarnation 54). A creature cannot deify creatures; so if the Son saves and deifies, the Son must be God, not a made thing — which is why the whole weight of redemption, for Athanasius, hangs on homoousios.
  • He carried the same logic to the Holy Spirit in his Letters to Serapion (c. 359–361), arguing the Spirit too is consubstantial and not a creature — the seed of the position the Cappadocians and Constantinople (381) would dogmatize. That work is not among the texts surveyed here; the Spirit’s deity is carried on this timeline by Basil of Caesarea, Gregory of Nyssa, and Gregory of Nazianzus.
  • Athanasius appears elsewhere on the wiki using the same anti-Arian framework — e.g. on Theotokos, where “Mother of God” is deployed to affirm the child’s eternal divinity against Arius.
