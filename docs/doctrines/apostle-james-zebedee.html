James, son of Zebedee

James the son of Zebedee — called “James the Great” to distinguish him from the other apostle of that name — was a fisherman of Galilee, the elder brother of the apostle John, and, with Peter and John, one of the three disciples the Gospels place nearest to Jesus. Acts records his death: Herod Agrippa I “killed James the brother of John with the sword,” about the year 44, the only martyrdom of one of the Twelve that the New Testament narrates. Later sources add an account of his accuser's conversion (Clement of Alexandria, c. 200), a mission to Spain (the Latin apostle-lists, from the seventh century), and a tomb in Galicia venerated from the ninth century at Santiago de Compostela. This timeline sets out the datable sources that report on James, earliest extant source first. The questions on which those sources are read differently — whether his accuser was converted and died with him, whether he preached in Spain, and whether his relics lie at Compostela — each have their own page under Disputed readings. For the apostle-lists and the other sources shared by all the apostles, see the apostles hub.

Timeline

The New Testament (c. 30–90)

The New Testament gives James a fuller portrait than most of the Twelve. He is a fisherman of Galilee, the son of Zebedee and the elder brother of John, called with his brother from mending their nets; Jesus surnames the pair Boanerges, “sons of thunder.” With Peter and John he forms the inner three admitted to the raising of Jairus's daughter, the Transfiguration, and the agony in Gethsemane. He and John ask for the seats at Jesus's right and left, and are told they will “drink of the cup” he drinks — a saying Jerome (398) reads as “the cup of martyrdom,” since “only James the apostle was beheaded by Herod,” and Theophylact of Ohrid reads as foretold witness “even unto martyrdom”. Acts records that martyrdom: about the year 44 Herod Agrippa I “killed James the brother of John with the sword.” It is the only martyrdom of one of the Twelve that the New Testament narrates.1

Clement of Alexandria, preserved by Eusebius (c. 200)

Clement of Alexandria, in the lost Hypotyposes, quoted by Eusebius about a century and a quarter later, gives the earliest extant account of James's death beyond the notice in Acts: the man who had brought James to the judgment-seat was moved by his testimony and confessed himself a Christian, and the two were led off to be beheaded together — James, asked for forgiveness on the way, answering “Peace be with thee,” and kissing him. Clement told the story, Eusebius says, “as he received it from those who had lived before him”; it survives only in this quotation. Whether it preserves a memory of the martyrdom is disputed; see Was James's accuser converted and beheaded with him?2

The apostle-lists and the Breviarium Apostolorum (c. 600–700)

The division of mission fields that Eusebius reports from Origen assigns fields to Thomas, Andrew, John, Peter and Paul, and names none for James. The earliest extant attestation of a preaching in Spain is in the Latin apostle-lists, above all the Breviarium Apostolorum, which assigns each apostle a region and a resting-place. R. A. Fletcher (1984) dates it to the seventh century and describes it as a Latin translation and amplification of a Greek list whose Western editor “brought Philip to Gaul and James to Spain”; it has James preach in Spain but records that he was not buried there. So does a passage on James that Fletcher, after Bischoff, treats as an interpolation made before about 750 into the De ortu et obitu patrum ascribed to Isidore. Achille Camerlynck (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1910) reports the tradition of an apostolic see in Spain as “current in the year 700” and finds its first certain mention in the ninth century, in Notker of St Gall. Whether James preached in Spain is disputed; see Did James preach in Spain, and do his relics lie at Compostela?3

Santiago de Compostela: the tomb and the pilgrimage (9th c. onward)

Around 820–830, in the reign of Alfonso II of Asturias, a tomb in a field in Galicia was proclaimed to be that of the apostle James, and a shrine was raised over it; Fletcher places the discovery between 818 and 842, in the episcopate of Theodemir of Iria, and names the Martyrology of Usuard (c. 865) as the earliest evidence of an apostolic tomb in Spain. From this grew Santiago de Compostela, which by the twelfth century (the Codex Calixtinus) had become, with Rome and Jerusalem, one of the three great pilgrimages of Christendom, and James — Santiago Matamoros, “Moor-slayer” — the patron of the Reconquista. A companion account explained how his body came to be there: after his beheading in Jerusalem his disciples bore it by sea to Spain. Whether the relics at Compostela are his is disputed; see the same page.4

The Golden Legend (c. 1260)

Jacobus de Voragine's Golden Legend gives the fullest life — James preaching in Spain, his return to Judea, his dispute with the magician Hermogenes, the conversion of his accuser Josias on the way to execution, and the miraculous voyage of his corpse to Galicia. It gathers the martyrdom, the accuser's conversion, and the Spanish tradition into a single story.5

Disputed readings

How the sources have been read on the death of James and on his connection with Spain.

  • Was James's accuser converted and beheaded with him? — Clement of Alexandria's received story beside the notice in Acts 12.
  • Did James preach in Spain, and do his relics lie at Compostela? — the Latin apostle-lists, the ninth-century tomb, and their dating.
