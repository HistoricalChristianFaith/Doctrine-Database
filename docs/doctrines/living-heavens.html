The Living Heavens

Are the sun, moon, and stars living, ensouled, rational beings, or inanimate bodies that an angel moves? Christian writers took up the question across fifteen centuries and answered it in different ways. Scripture, Second Temple Jewish texts and Greek philosophy all supplied language of living heavens: Scripture’s “morning stars sang together” (Job 38:7), “the stars in their courses fought” (Judges 5:20), and the host of heaven that “worshippeth” God (Nehemiah 9:6); the disobedient stars bound and punished as moral agents in 1 Enoch; and the Platonist world-soul and the Stoic living cosmos. Two different claims run through the record, and the timeline keeps them apart: ensoulment, that the luminaries are living rational souls (Philo, Origen), and the angelic movers, that they are inanimate bodies moved or governed by angels or “intelligences” (Bonaventure, Aquinas, Dante; Albert speaks of “intelligences” and also of celestial souls). A third answer, in Gregory of Nyssa and Nemesius, refers the heavens’ motion to God without naming a soul or an angel as mover. The ensoulment proposition was anathematized in the Origenist condemnations of 543–553, and in the seventeenth century Kepler replaced the soul that moves the planets with a physical force. What follows are the witnesses, earliest first.

Related doctrines: Astrology and the heavens — see also Astrology and the Stars (whether the stars compel human lives — the sister question of astral fate) · The Pillars of Seth (the survival of antediluvian star-science).

Timeline

Listed earliest first: the ensouled stars of 1 Enoch and Philo; the angels set over creation in Justin, Athenagoras and Clement; Origen’s ensoulment and its sixth-century condemnation; the denials of Basil, Ambrose and John of Damascus, Augustine’s suspended judgement, and the motion referred to God in Gregory of Nyssa and Nemesius; the thirteenth-century accounts of inanimate bodies moved by angels; and Kepler’s change from a moving soul to a moving force.

1 Enoch (c. 3rd–2nd century BC)

The earliest witness on this timeline is a book, not a person. The Second Temple apocalypse 1 Enoch treats the stars themselves as moral agents: at the end of heaven and earth Enoch sees “seven stars like great burning mountains” in a “prison for the stars and the host of heaven,” punished because they “have transgressed the commandment of the Lord… because they did not come forth at their appointed times” (chs. 18, 21); and in the last days “many chiefs of the stars shall transgress the order… Yea, they shall err and take them to be gods” (ch. 80). In this picture the stars can break a commandment, incur guilt, and be bound for a term of years. The falling stars of ch. 86 figure the fallen angels, or Watchers; whether the bound stars of chs. 18 and 21 are the same beings is a disputed reading.1

Philo of Alexandria (c. 30)

The Jewish Platonist of Alexandria sets out the ensoulment of the stars in philosophical terms. Joining the philosophers’ living cosmos to Genesis, Philo teaches that the stars “are said to be animals, and animals endowed with intelligence,” the mind of each “wholly and entirely virtuous”; the heaven is full of “entire souls pervading the universe, being unadulterated and divine,” and the planets move “by their own intrinsic energy.” For Philo the star is a living, rational, sinless mind.2

Justin Martyr (c. 155)

Justin states that God administers the world He made through appointed angels. In the Second Apology he writes that God, having made the world and “arranged the heavenly elements for the increase of fruits and rotation of the seasons,” “committed the care of men and of all things under heaven to angels whom He appointed over them.” The angels are set over “things under heaven,” not expressly over the stars, and the sentence turns at once to those angels’ Genesis-6 fall.3

Athenagoras of Athens (c. 177)

Athenagoras sets angels over the heavens themselves. In his Plea for the Christians he recognizes “a multitude of angels and ministers, whom God the Maker and Framer of the world distributed and appointed to their several posts… to occupy themselves about the elements, and the heavens, and the world, and the things in it”; the angels’ office is “to exercise providence for God over the things created and ordered by Him.” In this account the heavens are placed under angelic administration.4

Clement of Alexandria (c. 200)

Clement sets angels over the stars. The Prophetic Eclogues say the stars are “spiritual bodies, that have communications with the angels set over them, and are governed by them” (LV), and that “an angel high in command is in the sun… appointed for rule over days; as the moon is for ruling over night” (LVI). In Clement’s account the star is a body, a refined one, governed from without by a ruling angel rather than by a soul of its own. Because the stars are governed, Clement adds, they “are not the cause of the production of things, but are signs” — they “do not in the least degree exert influences.”5

Origen (c. 231)

Origen sets out the ensoulment reading at length. The seventh chapter of Peri Archōn Book I asks “whether it is allowable to suppose that they are living and rational beings,” and the chapter (in Rufinus’s Latin) argues that the stars “receive commandments from God,” their ordered motion “cannot take place without a soul,” and their souls were “implanted… from without” before their shining bodies, which, being material, subject them to “vanity” (Romans 8:20) until their release. Against Celsus V restates it: the stars are “rational and virtuous beings” who “pray to the Supreme God.” The proposition was anathematized in 543 and 553 (below); whether the anathemas report what Origen himself taught is a disputed reading on the condemnation of Origen.6

Basil of Caesarea (c. 378)

Basil denies that the heavens are alive. In the Hexaemeron he writes that “the heavens are not alive because they ‘declare the glory of God,’ nor the firmament a sensible being” (III.9), and that the stars are “beings without souls”: to speak of “evil” and “benevolent” stars as if they chose is “the height of folly” about things that do not live (VI.7). The luminaries are inanimate creatures, “fashioned as a vehicle for that pure, clear, and immaterial light” (VI.2).7

Gregory of Nyssa (c. 380)

Basil’s brother takes up the same Hexaemeron from a different angle. Gregory does not ask outright whether the stars are alive, and he does not call them “beings without souls” as Basil does; but on what moves the heavens he names God, without naming a soul or an angel: “the Divine power and skill… driving around… the exceedingly rapid motion of the sphere, like a wheel” (On the Making of Man I.1). He also writes that God so wove change into the heaven that it “might not… cause the creature to be accounted as God… alien from the conception of Deity” (I.4). His stock example, the moon, is a body “in itself devoid of light… just as is the case with mirrors” (On the Soul and the Resurrection). Gregory does not address ensoulment as such; his texts deny the heaven divinity and ascribe its motion to God.8

Ambrose of Milan (c. 387)

Ambrose’s Latin Hexameron follows Basil’s and denies the luminaries’ divinity: made on the fourth day, they are junior to the life they light (“the beetle is older than the sun, the grass is older than the moon”), and are servants, not gods — “a good helper of my fertility, but not the creator… not the author,” the sun and moon “made to govern” and therefore themselves in service. For Ambrose, things made to serve are not gods.9

Nemesius of Emesa (c. 390)

Nemesius, whose treatise surveys the Greek philosophers on the soul, reports Plato’s world-soul, “the SOUL which turneth about the whole Globe,” as Plato’s opinion. In his own voice he makes God the governor of the heavens, holding that the stars keep their courses by a necessity God laid on them: “It is hee who laid that necessity upon the starres, by which they are constrained to keep the same course” (ch. 38). Reviewing Plato’s tiered providence he declines to credit the beings said to “turne the heavens about” — “we commend him not… (if any such were)” — and assigns Plato’s angels to earthly affairs, not the spheres (ch. 44). His account of stars carried by a divinely imposed necessity is comparable to Gregory of Nyssa’s ascription of the sphere’s motion to God.10

Augustine of Hippo (c. 417–421)

Augustine declines to decide. In the Enchiridion he writes, “I am not even certain… whether the sun, and the moon, and all the stars, do not form part of” the angelic society, “though many consider them merely luminous bodies, without either sensation or intelligence”; and in the City of God he will not “at once grant” Plato’s claim that the heavenly globes have “intellectual and blessed souls,” calling it “another question, which we have not undertaken to discuss.”11

Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite (c. 500)

Pseudo-Dionysius’s On the Celestial Hierarchy orders the biblical host into a fixed system of “the whole Heavenly Beings as nine… divided into three threefold Orders,” each rank passing the divine light down to the one below (“from the second, the third, and from the third, our Hierarchy”). The hierarchy concerns illumination and mediation: the treatise does not put an angel at the sun, moon, or a star or tie the orders to the physical spheres, and the luminaries enter only as metaphors (God the “Sun of Righteousness,” the “Morning Star”). Dante later matches the nine orders to the nine revolving heavens (Paradiso XXVIII).12

The Origenist anathemas condemn the ensouled stars (543, 553)

The ensoulment proposition was formally condemned. The edict Justinian sent to Menas in 543 anathematizes anyone who holds “that heaven, sun, moon, stars, and the waters above the heavens are ensouled and rational powers,” and the third of the fifteen anathemas against the Origenists (553) condemns the view “that the sun, the moon and the stars are also reasonable beings, and that they have only become what they are because they turned towards evil.”13

Gregory the Great (c. 595)

Gregory does not treat the cosmology of the stars directly, but he glosses two verses cited for living luminaries. In the Moralia in Job he reads the “morning stars” that “sang together” (Job 38:7) as the angels — “the nature of rational spirits… the Angels are, not improperly, called ‘morning stars’” (Book XXVIII) — and the “stars” of Bildad’s speech (Job 25:5) allegorically as “the souls of the several persons that live rightly,” the Elect who shine in a dark world (Book XVII). The Moralia was composed c. 578–595, in the decades after the anathemas of 543–553.14

John of Damascus (c. 743)

John’s Exposition of the Orthodox Faith states that the luminaries are “not… endowed with life. For they are inanimate and insensible” (II.7), and that they are signs of weather and change, not causes of human affairs — the soul’s habits remain “in our own hands, for it is reason that rules.” John holds the two negatives (not alive, not compelling) but does not, in this chapter, teach that angels move the stars.15

Bonaventure (c. 1250)

The Franciscan master and Aquinas’ contemporary works the same question. Asking at Sentences II, dist. 14 “whether the motion of the heaven is from its own form or from an intelligence,” he rejects the philosophers’ living, animate heaven: “this position is false and erroneous. For as Damascene says, ‘the heavens are inanimate and insensible.’” For what does move it he allows two admissible readings — the heaven moved by its own form (God supplying what it lacks), or “God moves the heaven through the mediation of a created Intelligence, that is, through the mediation of an Angel” — and inclines to the second, on the Augustinian principle that spirit presides over every body.16

Albertus Magnus (c. 1255)

Aquinas’ teacher holds that the celestial spheres are moved by separate immaterial intelligences ranked beneath God, and that their motion is the channel by which form and power descend to the world: “the whole work of nature is the work of an intelligence.” Reading Aristotle through Averroes, Albert also develops a doctrine of celestial souls, an intellectual mover-form that is not the vegetative-sensitive soul of an “animal”; he keeps the philosophers’ “intelligences” distinct from the theologians’ angels and, on the account of Adam Takahashi and the Stanford Encyclopedia, does not finally settle whether to call the movers souls or to identify them with angels. On ensoulment he differs from Aquinas, who denies the heavens an indwelling soul.17

Thomas Aquinas (c. 1270)

Asking “whether the lights of heaven are living beings,” Aquinas sets the earlier tradition side by side — Origen and Jerome for, “Basil… and Damascene” against, “Augustine leaves the matter in doubt” — and answers by distinction: the heavenly bodies are not animated by an indwelling soul, but “are moved by some intellectual substances,” that is, by angels. The old quarrel between those who call the heavens living and those who call them inanimate, he adds, “is not a difference of things but of words.” Whether Jerome belongs in his roster is a disputed reading.18

Dante Alighieri (c. 1320)

In the Paradiso Dante’s spheres are turned by Intelligences — the “blessed motors” whose virtue the star transmits “as from the artisan the hammer’s craft,” the heaven taking its image “from the Intelligence profound, which turns it” (Canto II); the “celestial Princes” who move the third heaven (Canto VIII); and the nine angelic orders matched to the nine revolving heavens (Canto XXVIII).19

Johannes Kepler — from soul to force (c. 1609)

Kepler’s account of what moves the planets changed over his career. In the Mysterium Cosmographicum (1596) he drove the planets by a moving soul (anima motrix) seated in the sun; over the next quarter-century he replaced that soul with a moving force — “My goal is to show that the heavenly machine is not a kind of divine living being but similar to a clockwork… all the manifold motions are taken care of by one single absolutely simple magnetic bodily force, as in a clockwork all motion is taken care of by a simple weight” (letter of 1605) — and told the reader of the 1621 Mysterium to strike out the word “soul” and write “force.”20

Disputed readings

How three attributions and identifications in the record have been read.

  • Did Origen teach that the sun, moon, and stars are living, ensouled rational beings? — on which turns whether the anathemas of 543 and 553 report Origen’s own teaching.21
  • Did Jerome hold that the sun, moon, and stars are living, ensouled beings, as Aquinas reports? — on which turns how many patristic witnesses are counted for ensoulment.22
  • Are 1 Enoch’s bound disobedient stars the Watcher-angels, or a distinct class of luminaries? — on which turns whether the book’s moral-agent stars are a separate witness from its Watcher story.23
