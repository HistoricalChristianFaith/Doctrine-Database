Conditional immortality (annihilationism)

What is the final fate of the lost? Three answers are distinguished in the modern debate: eternal punishment (in that debate often called eternal conscious torment), in which the lost exist forever in suffering; universal restoration, in which punishment is remedial and God at the last restores all; and conditional immortality, traced on this page, the belief that the lost finally cease to exist. Its holders teach that immortality is not a property the soul owns by nature but a gift God gives only to the saved “in Christ”, and that the wicked, after the judgment and whatever punishment God imposes, are destroyed and pass out of being. They read the Bible's language for the fate of the lost as destruction: God will “destroy both soul and body in hell” (Matthew 10:28); the fate of the wicked is the “second death” (Revelation 20:14; 21:8), to “perish” (John 3:16), to suffer “everlasting destruction” (2 Thessalonians 1:9), to be burned up like “stubble” (Malachi 4:1–3). The view is often called annihilationism (the wicked are annihilated) or conditionalism (immortality is conditional).

The page runs earliest extant attestation first: second-century writers who deny that the soul is immortal by nature, whose statements are read in different ways (Justin, Tatian, Theophilus); Arnobius of Sicca (c. 305); the Reformation-era mortalist Tyndale and the Socinians; the Victorian conditionalist writers (Edward White, Henry Constable, Pétavel); the two American church bodies that hold it in their statements of belief (the Seventh-day Adventists and the Jehovah's Witnesses); and twentieth- and twenty-first-century evangelical and Anglican writers (Atkinson, Fudge, Stott, Wenham, the 1995 Church of England Doctrine Commission report, and the “Rethinking Hell” movement).

Related doctrines: The living and the dead (the communion of the saints) — see also the intermediate state · purgatory and prayer for the dead · prayer to the saints · indulgences · the treasury of merit · dream-apparitions of the dead · eternal conscious torment · universal restoration (apokatastasis) · the resurrection of the body (what finally becomes of the dead — whether the lost are restored, tormented forever, or finally cease to be).

The three answers read a shared set of texts: “eternal” (aiōnios) punishment, the “tormented for ever and ever” of Revelation, the undying worm of Mark 9:48 and Isaiah 66:24, and the fire of Gehenna and the lake of fire. Conditionalists read that fire as consuming and ending in extinction; universalists, as remedial and ending in restoration; holders of eternal punishment, as unending.

Timeline

Second-century writers on the soul's immortality (c. 155–180)

Three second-century apologists deny that the soul, or man as created, is immortal by its own nature. Justin Martyr rejects the Platonic teaching that the soul is immortal in itself: souls live only by God's will, so that the wicked “are punished so long as God wills them to exist and to be punished,”1 and the soul “lives not as being life, but as the partaker of life.” His pupil Tatian writes: “The soul is not in itself immortal, O Greeks, but mortal… if it knows not the truth, it dies, and is dissolved with the body.”2 Theophilus of Antioch holds that God made man “neither mortal nor immortal… but… capable of both,” immortality being the reward of obedience, not an innate endowment.3 Whether these writers held conditional immortality is disputed. Pétavel (1892), LeRoy Froom (1965–66) and John Roller (2008) read them as conditionalists whose teaching was later displaced by a Platonic doctrine of the soul. Stewart Salmond (1895) reads their denial as a statement that the soul is a creature, compatible with unending punishment, pointing to Justin's “everlasting punishment” in the First Apology; Joseph Hontheim (1910) cites Justin and Irenaeus among the Fathers who “from the very earliest times, are unanimous in teaching that the wicked will be punished after death” and holds that on the eternity of that punishment “The teaching of the fathers is not less clear and decisive”; Georges Florovsky (1952) reads the denial as a statement of the soul's creaturely contingency (“‘May cease,’ however, does not mean necessarily ‘will (actually) cease’”); Thomas Farrar (2019) reads Justin's statements as one coherent view, in which the souls of the wicked “will not actually die because God wills them to be punished perpetually”; Edward Fudge, as Farrar reports him, treats Justin's statements as inconclusive. On Tatian's “death by punishment in immortality,” Roller (2008) holds that “Tatian, like his famous tutor, was a Conditionalist”; Fudge, as Farrar reports him, counts Tatian, with Athenagoras and Tertullian, among the first Christians to hold unending conscious punishment unambiguously. See the disputed reading.4

Arnobius of Sicca (c. 305)

Arnobius, a North African rhetorician who wrote his apology Against the Heathen during the Great Persecution, denies against Plato the soul's natural immortality — “that…cannot be immortal which does suffer pain” — and teaches that souls are of an “intermediate” nature, “such that they may on the one hand perish if they have not known God, and on the other be delivered from death if they have given heed to His threats and proffered favours.” The lost suffer a “real death, this which leaves nothing behind”: “souls which know not God shall be consumed in long-protracted torment with raging fire.”5 He teaches a painful punishment that ends in annihilation. Stewart Salmond (1895) calls him “the first writer of note certainly known to us” to hold this view; LeRoy Froom (1966) calls him the “Last Ante-Nicene Conditionalist Spokesman”; H. C. G. Moule records that “in the 5th cent. his treatise was banished by pope Gelasius to the index of apocryphal works” (the list's ascription to Gelasius is disputed: George McCracken, 1949, calls it a “sixth-century” decree “once ascribed to Pope Gelasius I,” and Ernst von Dobschütz, 1912 ⚠ unverified, dated it between 519 and 553). Whether he continues an earlier Christian teaching is disputed.6

William Tyndale (c. 1531)

No witness between Arnobius and the sixteenth century is entered on this timeline. At the Reformation the “mortalism” (or “soul sleep”) of the intermediate state appears in Tyndale, and the International Theological Commission notes that Luther at times “thought that these souls were in a state of sleep.”7 Answering Thomas More, Tyndale holds that the dead sleep, insensible, until the resurrection, and turns the point against purgatory: “in putting them in heaven, hell, and purgatory, [you] destroy the arguments wherewith Christ and Paul prove the resurrection.”8 Tyndale's claim concerns the intermediate state between death and resurrection (see the intermediate state), not the final annihilation of the lost.

The Socinians & the Racovian Catechism (c. 1605)

The Polish Socinians made the denial of natural immortality a confessional position. The Racovian Catechism (1605) teaches that Adam “was originally created mortal;—that is, was so constituted that he was not only by nature capable of dying, but also, if left to himself, could not but die; though he might, through the divine goodness, be for ever preserved alive.”9 The Catechism treats immortality as a gift of grace, not a property of the soul. On the fate of the wicked it still speaks of “everlasting punishment” for the unjust; the annihilationist inference is drawn in a note by its 1818 English translator, Thomas Rees.

Edward White (c. 1846) — “Life in Christ”

The Congregationalist Edward White's Life in Christ (1846; greatly enlarged 1878) argues that “life eternal is in Christ alone, and belongs exclusively to righteous men,” while “all finally impenitent sinners, persistently choosing evil, shall be ‘miserably destroyed.’”10 He distinguishes “an absolute” from “a conditional immortality” and grounds immortality in union with Christ rather than in the constitution of the soul.

Henry Constable (c. 1868)

The Anglican Henry Constable, in The Duration and Nature of Future Punishment, argues that “the second death” means literal extinction — setting his reading against those “who held that temporal death was for all that eternal non-existence, to which we hold that the second death will consign the ungodly.”11 For Constable the “destruction” and “death” the New Testament threatens are to be read at face value: the wicked are not kept alive to be tormented, but destroyed.

The confessional bodies: Seventh-day Adventism (1872) and the Watch Tower (from 1879)

Conditional immortality is set out in the statements of belief of two church bodies that arose in nineteenth-century America. Seventh-day Adventism, drawing on George Storrs's conditionalist “Six Sermons” (1842), made it a founding tenet: its first published statement of belief, the 1872 Declaration of the Fundamental Principles, already professes “the unconscious state of the dead, and the final destruction of the unrepentant wicked,” with “immortality alone through Christ, to be given to the Saints at the resurrection.”12 Ellen G. White, in The Great Controversy, traces the natural-immortality doctrine to Eden: the serpent's “Ye shall not surely die” was “the first sermon ever preached upon the immortality of the soul.”13 The doctrine stands today in the church's Fundamental Belief 26, which opens “God, who alone is immortal, will grant eternal life to His redeemed.” The Jehovah's Witnesses (Charles Taze Russell's Watch Tower movement, from 1879) hold the same two denials as defining tenets: that man has no immortal soul — “When a person dies, he ceases to exist… Not even one part of us survives the death of the body” — and that hell is the common grave, not a place of torment, so that “God has set death, not torment in a fiery hell, as the penalty for sin.”14 The Adventist scholar LeRoy Froom published a two-volume history of the teaching, The Conditionalist Faith of Our Fathers (1965–66).15

Emmanuel Pétavel (c. 1892)

The Swiss theologian Emmanuel Pétavel-Olliff's French defence of conditionalism reached English readers as The Problem of Immortality (1892). He grounds immortality in the sustaining love of God: “The love of God is our sole guarantee against the extinction which threatens contingent creatures. The believer feels the effects of God's love, and is therefore sure of his immortality.”16 On his account only the one united to God escapes the extinction that threatens a contingent creature.

Basil F. C. Atkinson (c. 1969)

The Cambridge under-librarian Basil Atkinson, in his privately printed Life and Immortality, writes: “the doom of all unrepentant sinners together with the devil, evil angels, sin and death and evil of all kinds is complete extinction and destruction out of the creation of God.”17 John Wenham recalls: “I had learnt the doctrine from Basil Atkinson… in about 1934.”18

Edward Fudge (c. 1982)

The American writer Edward Fudge published The Fire That Consumes (1982; 3rd ed. 2011), a book-length exegetical case for conditionalism, with forewords by F. F. Bruce, John W. Wenham and Richard Bauckham to its three editions. He frames the dispute in a single question: “Does Scripture teach that God will make the wicked immortal, to suffer unending conscious torment in hell? Or does the Bible teach that the wicked will finally and truly die, perish, and become extinct forever, through a destructive process that encompasses whatever degree and duration of conscious torment God might sovereignly and justly impose in each individual case?”19 Fudge argues for the second.

John Stott (c. 1988)

The Anglican evangelical John Stott gave a guarded endorsement in his 1988 dialogue with David Edwards, Essentials: A Liberal-Evangelical Dialogue. Stott writes of the emotional pressure — “emotionally, I find the concept [of eternal conscious torment] intolerable and do not understand how people can live with it without either cauterising their feelings or cracking under the strain” — but rests his case on Scripture, holding that “the ultimate annihilation of the wicked should at least be accepted as a legitimate, biblically founded alternative to their eternal conscious torment.”20

John Wenham (c. 1992)

The Anglican scholar John Wenham, Atkinson's student, argued the case at length in “The Case for Conditional Immortality” (1992; repr. in Facing Hell, 1998). He defines the position — “belief in conditional immortality is the belief that God created Man only potentially immortal… immortality being inherent in God alone” — and writes: “I believe that endless torment is a hideous and unscriptural doctrine which has been a terrible burden on the mind of the church for many centuries… I should indeed be happy if, before I die, I could help in sweeping it away.”21

The Mystery of Salvation (1995)

The Church of England's Doctrine Commission report The Mystery of Salvation (1995) describes hell as final self-exclusion ending in non-being: “Hell is not eternal torment, but it is the final and irrevocable choosing of that which is opposed to God so completely and so absolutely that the only end is total non-being.”22

Rethinking Hell (c. 2014) — the contemporary movement

The “Rethinking Hell” movement (Chris Date, Glenn Peoples, and others) and its 2014 anthology Rethinking Hell: Readings in Evangelical Conditionalism define the doctrine: “God alone possesses immortality innately and therefore any other being who is immortal… is so extrinsically, that is, as the result of a positive act of God,” so that “since the damned are not immortal and never will be, they will actually perish in hell (annihilation).”23
