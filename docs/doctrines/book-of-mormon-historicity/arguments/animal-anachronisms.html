Do the Book of Mormon's Old World animals fit the pre-Columbian record?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: weak — the Old World domesticates the text names (horses, cattle, sheep, goats, swine, asses, elephants) have no accepted place in the American faunal record for the Nephite window. The two standard rescues — loan-shifting a familiar name onto a native animal, and a small Mesoamerican stage that leaves little trace — are real phenomena and are conceded where they have force, but they strain hardest exactly where the text makes an animal do culturally specific work: a horse drawing a king's chariot is not a tapir, and elephants in the Jaredite New World have no support at all.

Related arguments: the material anachronisms — see also crops, metals & textiles · reformed Egyptian & the script

The claim

The Book of Mormon presents a real pre-Columbian civilization of Israelite descent flourishing in the Americas roughly 600 BC to AD 421 (with the Jaredites earlier still), and it repeatedly furnishes that civilization with a barnyard of Old World domesticated animals. Lehi's colony "did find upon the land of promise … both the cow and the ox, and the ass and the horse, and the goat and the wild goat" (1 Nephi 18:25); the people of Nephi keep "flocks of all manner of cattle … and goats, and wild goats, and also many horses" (Enos 1:21); King Lamoni commands his servants to "make ready his horses and chariots" so that a horse draws the king's chariot in a metal-using, literate society (Alma 18:9–12); horses and chariots recur down to the first century (3 Nephi 3:22; 3 Nephi 21:14). Earlier still, the Jaredite record adds swine, "asses," and — uniquely — "elephants and cureloms and cumoms," two of them unidentified beasts (Ether 9:18–19).1 The claim assessed here is the affirmative one a historical reading needs: that this named fauna fits — or can be made to fit — what is actually known of animals in the pre-Columbian Americas.

Who advances it

The difficulty is not manufactured by critics; it is the plain reading of the standard reference works, and it was stated at institutional length by the Smithsonian Institution, whose long-circulated "Statement Regarding the Book of Mormon" observed that "none of the principal Old World domesticated food plants or animals (except the dog) occurred in the New World in pre-Columbian times" — listing wheat, barley, cattle, pigs, horses, donkeys and camels as absent before 1492, the American horse and camel having gone extinct with the Pleistocene megafauna around 10,000 BC.2 The plant geneticist and former Latter-day Saint bishop Simon Southerton pressed the same point as a witness against historicity: Mesoamerica, the setting most Latter-day Saint scholars favour, shows "no evidence of Old World crops and animals … horse drawn wheeled vehicles" in its pre-Columbian record.3

The affirmative case — that the fauna nonetheless fits — is carried by Latter-day Saint scholarship, above all the anthropologist John L. Sorenson, whose An Ancient American Setting for the Book of Mormon (1985) and later "Once More: The Horse" set out the two reframes weighed below; his lead is followed by the apologetic organisations FairMormon (now FAIR) and the Interpreter Foundation.4 Brant A. Gardner, Book of Mormon Central (now Scripture Central), and the BYU palaeontologist Wade E. Miller have since added the strongest answers on the three hardest items, the chariot, the horse's date and the elephants, all weighed below.

The evidence for

Loan-shifting is a real and documented phenomenon. When speakers of one language meet an unfamiliar animal, they very often reach for the nearest familiar label rather than coining a new word, and the strongest form of the apologetic case builds on genuine, attested examples rather than special pleading. When the Spanish brought horses to Yucatán the Maya called them tzimin, a word they already used for the tapir (tzimin che, "forest beast"); Aztec accounts describe the conquistadors' horses as "deer which carried men on their backs." The naming runs the other way too — Greek gave the hippopotamus a "river-horse" and the Romans called the elephant a "Lucanian cow." On this reading the Nephite migrants, meeting New World fauna, could have applied inherited Semitic labels — "horse," "cow," "sheep" — to a tapir, a brocket deer, a bison, a peccary, or a llama, so that the text's "horse" need not be Equus at all.5 The tapir in particular is a six-hundred-pound quadruped with a horse-like neck and gait that some Central Americans call anteburro ("once an ass") and that Brazilian farmers have hitched to a plough — not an absurd candidate for a loan-shifted "horse."

A limited geography leaves little to find. The affirmative case does not need a hemisphere of horse-bones. On the limited-geography model that most Latter-day Saint scholars now hold, the whole narrative plays out on a Mesoamerican stage a few hundred miles across, over a few centuries, among small populations; a modest herd that never spread and left no burial in the acidic tropical soils would be genuinely hard to detect. The general principle is sound and is conceded: absence of evidence is weak evidence of absence for a small, localized population, and zooarchaeology in the wet neotropics recovers only a sliver of what once lived there.6

There is a real precedent for a fauna's later vindication, and a handful of contested finds. The apologetic case rightly presses the camel: mid-twentieth-century scholars confidently called the camel unknown in Egypt until the Greco-Roman period, and the biblical camels of Abraham an anachronism — a verdict that domesticated-camel finds later softened. If the confident negative erred there, the argument runs, it may err again. And the record is not quite empty: horse remains from Loltún Cave and Mayapán in Yucatán have been reported in what the excavators took to be pre-Columbian layers, and a few students of Pleistocene extinction allow that Equus could in principle have lingered in an American refuge later than the standard c. 10,000 BP terminus.7

The “chariot” need not be a wheeled cart. The strongest answer to the text's hardest sentence is textual. Brant Gardner observes that “chariots never appear in the context of Book of Mormon warfare,” and that its horses “never explicitly pull anything. They are never ridden.” Chariots appear only when King Lamoni prepares to receive or visit a king (Alma 18:9–12; 20:6) and among the goods carried to a stronghold (3 Nephi 3:22). On that reading the chariot is a royal conveyance of another kind: “the appropriate conveyance would be a royal litter, carried on men's shoulders rather than pulled by an animal,” like the palanquins in which Maya kings were carried at Tikal.8 The translation has a biblical precedent: the King James Version renders the Hebrew ʾappiryôn, a litter or palanquin, as “chariot” at Song of Solomon 3:9. Neal Rappleye adds that in Maya litter scenes “an animal (often a dog) is frequently depicted as traveling near the litter.” Mesoamericans also made wheeled figurines, so the wheel itself was known, though Scripture Central concedes that these are “largely dated between AD 600–1250” and that wheeled vehicles “are thus far unattested for any part of pre-Columbian America.”9

Some horse remains have been dated after the Ice Age. Wade E. Miller and Matthew Roper report a horse specimen for which “a date of 2,540 years before the present was provided by the Radiocarbon Laboratory at the University of Arizona”, about 590 BC and so in Lehi's own century. Scripture Central called the result “provisional, but promising” and said further samples would be needed.10 Miller and colleagues have since reported, in a peer-reviewed paper, post-Pleistocene horse specimens from San Luis Potosí dated by associated charcoal to between roughly 3,300 and 900 years ago (⚠ unverified: known here only from a secondary summary).11

The Jaredite elephants may be early enough. On Sorenson's chronology Ether 9:19 belongs to about 2500 BC, so the elephants “need not have lived later than 2400 B.C.” Scripture Central proposes the Columbian mammoth as the likeliest referent, and Miller and Roper note that dwarf mammoths survived on Wrangel Island until about 2000 BC.1210

Adversarial assessment

What holds. Loan-shifting is not a dodge invented for this problem; it is an ordinary linguistic mechanism with clean historical examples, and it does dispose of the crudest version of the objection ("the word 'horse' appears, therefore Equus must be found"). The limited-geography point is likewise fair: a small, non-dispersing population can slip beneath the archaeological threshold, and the camel-in-Egypt episode is a genuine cautionary tale against over-confident negatives. An honest assessment concedes all of this before weighing what remains.

The reframes fail where the text makes the animal work. Loan-shifting buys the most where the animal is a bare noun on a list and buys the least where the narrative gives it a specific cultural role — and the Book of Mormon does the latter. A "horse" that is harnessed to a "chariot" and "made ready" to convey a king (Alma 18:9–12) is not doing tapir-work or deer-work: it presupposes a draft animal broken to harness, a wheeled vehicle, and the whole traction complex that in the Old World grew up around the domesticated horse. Neither the tapir (never domesticated anywhere, solitary and not herd-trainable) nor any American deer was ever a draft animal, and no pre-Columbian Mesoamerican culture used the wheel for transport or built roads for wheeled traffic at all. The loan-shift can rename the animal but cannot supply the harness, the cart, or the road; relabel "horse" as "tapir" and the sentence stops making sense. That is the decisive strain, and it is internal to the text.

The litter reading is the best answer, and it has costs. Gardner's reading takes the strain seriously and is the strongest form of the case: the text really never shows a chariot in battle or a horse pulling one. But it has to read against the text's own pairings. Lamoni has his servants “make ready his horses and his chariots” for a journey “down to the land of Middoni” (Alma 20:6–8), and the people flee to their stronghold having “taken their horses, and their chariots” (3 Nephi 3:22). A litter carried on men's shoulders has no reason to be yoked in the narrative to horses in every passage where it appears, which is why critics argue the reading “requires doing significant violence to the text.”13 And even granted, the litter removes only the horse's work. The horse itself is still unattested in the window.

The inconsistency cuts against the loan-shift. The very passage that names elephants also names "cureloms and cumoms" — beasts the translation leaves untranslated because no English word fit (Ether 9:19). A text that transliterates the animals it has no label for, but supposedly loan-shifts "horse" and "cow" and "sheep" onto five different native species without ever signalling the substitution, is being asked to behave in two opposite ways at once. The plainer reading is that "horse" means horse and "sheep" means sheep — which is exactly the reading the faunal record cannot bear.

Elephants have no rescue at all. Whatever slack loan-shifting gives the smaller quadrupeds, Ether 9:19's elephants are a hard case: American mastodon and mammoth were gone millennia before any Jaredite date on the text's own reckoning, and there is no candidate New World animal onto which "elephant" plausibly loan-shifts. Sorenson's early date is the best answer, and it narrows the gap without closing it. The late mammoths it relies on are dwarf animals on an Arctic island off Siberia, thousands of miles from any proposed Jaredite land. On the American mainland, the young dates once cited for mastodons have mostly proved to be contaminated samples, and the youngest secure dates fall around 10,000 BC.14 The remaining move is to shorten the elephants' tenure (“present only briefly, then extinct”), which accounts for the absence of evidence without supplying any.

The pro-evidence is thin and does not carry the weight. The Loltún and Mayapán horse bones are the best physical exhibit, and they are genuinely worth citing — but their pre-Columbian attribution rests on burial depth and mineralization, not on direct dating, and where such American horse remains have been radiocarbon-tested they return Pleistocene ages, not Nephite ones. The newer dates do not yet change this. The 2,540 BP result is a single date published without the material dated or a laboratory number given. The San Luis Potosí dates come from charcoal in the same layers, because the bones held too little collagen to date directly, and charcoal in a layer does not date a bone lying in it. It is also unsettled whether those specimens are a late wild Equus or something else.11 A wild horse lingering in central Mexico would still not be a herd broken to harness. The camel-in-Egypt parallel shows a confident negative can fail; it does not show that this negative has failed, and one softened date in the Old World does not conjure a domesticated draft-horse in the New. Possibility is not proof: that horses could in principle have lingered in some refuge is not evidence that they did, still less that a literate chariot-driving civilization kept them.

Scope the failure honestly. What fails is the specific material prediction of a literal Nephite civilization stocked with Old World domesticates — horses in harness, herds of cattle and sheep and swine, elephants in Jaredite times. That is a claim about the faunal record, and on the faunal record it does not hold. It is not a verdict on the loan-shift mechanism in general (which is sound), on the limited-geography model in general (also reasonable), or on the wider faith. A different, weaker reading — that a few of these names are loose labels for a handful of local animals in a small area — survives; but that is not the barnyard the text describes, and it cannot account for the chariot-drawing horse or the elephant.

Assessment

Weak. The Old World domesticated animals the Book of Mormon names have no accepted place in the pre-Columbian American record for the relevant window: the American horse was extinct some ten millennia before the Nephite era, and no domesticated horse, cow, sheep, goat, pig or "ass" — nor any elephant — is attested for it. The two serious rescues are given their due: loan-shifting is a real mechanism with clean precedents, and a small Mesoamerican stage genuinely could hide a modest population. So are the newer answers: Gardner's royal-litter reading of the “chariot” is a real textual observation, a few post-Ice-Age horse dates have been reported, and Sorenson's early Jaredite chronology narrows the elephant gap. None yet carries the weight: the litter reading must read against the text's own pairing of horses with chariots, the horse dates are not direct dates on bone, and the nearest late mammoths lived on an Arctic island. But both break at the same joint — the text does not merely list a "horse," it harnesses one to a king's chariot in a wheel-less, road-less setting, and a tapir or a deer cannot do that work; and the elephants of Ether 9 have no candidate species to shift onto at all. The self-refuting detail is internal: the same verse that names elephants leaves "cureloms and cumoms" untranslated, showing the record could flag an unknown beast when it met one — which is precisely what the loan-shift theory needs it not to do for the horse.

Two things should be carried away rather than a flat dismissal. First, the crude form of the objection really is defeated: "the word appears, so find the bones" ignores a genuine linguistic phenomenon and a genuine detection problem, and a fair reading concedes both. Second, the failure is scoped. It falls on the model of a literal, Old-World-stocked Nephite civilization — the specific material claim weighed here — and not on the loan-shift mechanism as such, nor on the broader faith. Possibility is not proof, and here the possibility is not even well attested: the affirmative case rests on a mechanism that cannot reach the text's hardest sentences and on a few finds that direct dating has not confirmed.
