Does the limited Mesoamerican geography give the Book of Mormon a defensible setting?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — and the split runs along a disciplinary seam. Read as internal geography, the model is strong: the text's own distances are small, and confining the story to a few hundred miles of southern Mexico and Guatemala fits those distances — and the one pre-Columbian region that actually had writing, cities, and organized warfare in the window — far better than the whole hemisphere ever did. Read against the history of interpretation, it is weak: it overturns the plain hemispheric reading that Joseph Smith and the early church held, and it forces the "two-Cumorahs" problem — two hills of the same name, one in New York and one in Mesoamerica. Genuine scholars advocate each side; there is no clear winner, so this page rates the framework contested and the sibling pages that lean on it inherit that unsettledness.

The claim

The Book of Mormon narrates a thousand years of migration, city-building, and war (c. 600 BC – AD 421) without ever naming a modern place or stating a distance in miles. For most of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries Latter-day Saints read its stage as the whole New World: Lehi's colony landing in South America, the final battles at the hill Cumorah in Manchester, New York, where Joseph Smith said he recovered the plates. The claim assessed here is the scholarly alternative that replaced that reading in academic Latter-day Saint circles: John L. Sorenson's limited geography, which confines every Book of Mormon event to a few hundred miles of Mesoamerica — southern Mexico and Guatemala — and identifies the "narrow neck of land" that divides the story's two land-masses with the Isthmus of Tehuantepec.1 The question is whether that model gives the Book of Mormon a defensible real-world setting, or whether it rescues the text at a cost that defeats it.

Who advances it

The model's foundational statement is John L. Sorenson's An Ancient American Setting for the Book of Mormon (1985), the fruit of decades of work by a professional anthropologist at Brigham Young University; Sorenson expanded it in Mormon's Codex (2013), which catalogues some 420 claimed correspondences between the text and early Mesoamerican civilization.2 It is now the mainstream scholarly geography among believing Book of Mormon researchers, defended and refined by the Interpreter Foundation, the Maxwell Institute's former FARMS reviewers, and scholars such as Brant A. Gardner, who dedicated his own history-of-the-text to Sorenson.3 The Church itself takes no official position: its 2019 Gospel Topics statement declares that "the Church takes no position on the specific geographic location of Book of Mormon events" and asks members not to teach geography theories in church settings.4 A rival believing geography — Rod Meldrum's North American "Heartland" model, which keeps Cumorah in New York and reads the narrow neck as the Niagara region — exists but is a fringe position, rejected by the academic mainstream that holds the limited model.5

The evidence for

The text's own distances are small. This is the model's strongest card, and it comes from the book itself. Armies march the length of the land in days or weeks; the whole width of the narrow neck is "a day and a half's journey for a Nephite" (Alma 22:32), and a defensive line across the narrow country is "a day's journey" (Helaman 4:7). A day-and-a-half's foot journey is on the order of fifty to a hundred and fifty miles, not the thousands a hemispheric reading requires. A book that lets a single runner cross its pivotal chokepoint in thirty-six hours is describing a small region, and a limited geography honours that internal measure where the hemispheric model ignores it.6

Mesoamerica is the one region that fits the kind of civilization described. The Book of Mormon depicts a literate, urban, agricultural people keeping written records, building cities of cement and stone, and waging organized seasonal warfare across a landscape with volcanism (the cataclysm of 3 Nephi, with its darkness, burning cities, and sundered earth). Of all the pre-Columbian Americas, exactly one region in the relevant window had genuine writing systems, monumental cities, calendrical record-keeping, and large-scale organized war: Mesoamerica. Sorenson matched the text's internal directions, seasons, volcanic geology, and cultural profile to that region and identified specific correlates — highland Guatemala as the land of Nephi, the Isthmus of Tehuantepec as the narrow neck — arriving at a stage a few hundred miles long.1

As a hermeneutic it answers many "anachronism" charges at once. A small stage with a large resident population that the migrants joined lets loan-shifting explain the text's horses, cattle, and metals (native words applied to unfamiliar things); lets a founding colony vanish genetically into millions of natives; and lets the absence of a sprawling hemispheric empire be exactly what one should expect. This is why the model is not a curiosity but load-bearing: the sibling pages on the metal, animal, and DNA questions must engage it rather than dismiss it, because it is the strongest scholarly form the historicity case takes and the frame in which those charges are meant to be read.3

Adversarial assessment

What holds. On internal geography the model is genuinely well-grounded. The text really does describe short distances, and a hemispheric reading really does strain them past breaking. Mesoamerica really is the only pre-Columbian region with the writing, cities, and organized warfare the narrative assumes. The internal case is not special pleading; it is a fair reading of the book's own numbers, and it is why the model won the scholarly field it did.

It overturns the plain reading its own narrators and earliest readers held. The Book of Mormon presents its "land of promise" in language the first generations of Latter-day Saints, Joseph Smith included, took hemispherically: Lehi's seed inheriting "the whole continent," "the whole of North and South America" as the promised land, the final Nephite battles in the American northeast. Orson Pratt read Lehi landing in South America with Lamanites in the south and Nephites in the north; B. H. Roberts called "the two American continents … a promised land"; the 1834 Zelph episode had Joseph Smith naming Illinois bones as a "white Lamanite" from the Book of Mormon's own wars. The limited model relocates and shrinks the story against that reading — a real interpretive cost, not a detail.7

It forces the two-Cumorahs problem. The most concrete cost is a single hill. Joseph Smith said he recovered the plates from a hill in Manchester, New York, and generations of church leaders identified that hill with the Cumorah/Ramah where the Nephites and Jaredites fought their annihilating final battles (Mormon 6; Ether 15). But if all Book of Mormon events sit in Mesoamerica, the battle-Cumorah must lie in southern Mexico — so there are now two hills named Cumorah, the New York one merely re-named after the real one hundreds of miles south, with Moroni carrying the plates the intervening distance in a journey the record never mentions. Critics press this as the model's reductio: it saves the geography by multiplying a named landmark and silently rewriting the church's own account of where the plates came from.8

And it can function as an unfalsifiable retreat. Because the model shrinks the stage to a small, undetectable enclave inside a vast native population, it can absorb almost any negative result — no Nephite genes, no Old-World metallurgy, no horses — by placing the events just out of the reach of the test. Critics such as the geneticist Simon Southerton, and some believing scholars, read this as special pleading: an exception invoked precisely to immunize the account from the material record. Even the internal fit is contested on its own terms — the Isthmus of Tehuantepec, at roughly 120–200 miles across, is arguably too wide for a day-and-a-half foot crossing, and other readers put the narrow neck elsewhere or argue Sorenson misread Alma 22:32 as measuring the neck when it measures the bordering lands.9

Assessment

Contested — the merits split by discipline and there is no clear winner. Judged as an account of the Book of Mormon's internal geography, the limited model is strong and well-motivated: the text's own march-times and its one-and-a-half-day narrow neck describe a small region, and Mesoamerica is the single pre-Columbian setting with the writing, cities, and organized warfare the narrative takes for granted. Judged against the history of interpretation and one hard external datum, it is weak: it overturns the hemispheric reading Joseph Smith and the early church plainly held, and it forces two hills named Cumorah — one in New York where the plates were found, one in Mesoamerica where the battles must now sit — with the plates carried between them in an unrecorded journey.

Both verdicts are held by serious people. Believing scholars from the Interpreter Foundation and the former FARMS circle defend the model as sound exegesis of the book's internal geography; critics inside and outside the church reject it as a post-hoc retreat that shrinks the story until no test can reach it, and even its friendly internal fit (Tehuantepec's width, the exact sense of Alma 22:32) is disputed among its own proponents. That is the signature of a genuinely contested question — strong by the lights of internal-textual geography, weak by the lights of external history and the Cumorah datum — not a case with a settled answer. The sibling pages that invoke this framework to answer anachronism and DNA charges therefore lean on a frame that is itself unresolved: it earns its engagement, but it does not close the questions it is asked to close.
