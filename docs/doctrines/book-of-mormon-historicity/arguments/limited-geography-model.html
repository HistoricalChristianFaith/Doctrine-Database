Does the limited Mesoamerican geography give the Book of Mormon a defensible setting?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — and the split runs along a disciplinary seam. Read as internal geography, the model is strong: the text's own distances are small, and confining the story to a few hundred miles of southern Mexico and Guatemala fits those distances — and the one pre-Columbian region that actually had writing, cities, and organized warfare in the window — far better than the whole hemisphere ever did. Read against the history of interpretation, it is weak: it overturns the hemispheric reading that dominated early Latter-day Saint teaching (though the 1842 church newspaper already placed Nephite cities in Central America), and it forces the "two-Cumorahs" problem — two hills of the same name, one in New York and one in Mesoamerica, which Mormon 6:6 makes possible but early testimony did not envisage. Genuine scholars advocate each side; there is no clear winner, so this page rates the framework contested and the sibling pages that lean on it inherit that unsettledness.

The claim

The Book of Mormon narrates a thousand years of migration, city-building, and war (c. 600 BC – AD 421) without ever naming a modern place or stating a distance in miles. For most of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries Latter-day Saints read its stage as the whole New World: Lehi's colony landing in South America, the final battles at the hill Cumorah in Manchester, New York, where Joseph Smith said he recovered the plates. The claim assessed here is the scholarly alternative that replaced that reading in academic Latter-day Saint circles: John L. Sorenson's limited geography, which confines every Book of Mormon event to a few hundred miles of Mesoamerica — southern Mexico and Guatemala — and identifies the "narrow neck of land" that divides the story's two land-masses with the Isthmus of Tehuantepec.1 The question is whether that model gives the Book of Mormon a defensible real-world setting, or whether it rescues the text at a cost that defeats it.

Who advances it

The model's foundational statement is John L. Sorenson's An Ancient American Setting for the Book of Mormon (1985), the fruit of decades of work by a professional anthropologist at Brigham Young University; Sorenson expanded it in Mormon's Codex (2013), which catalogues some 420 claimed correspondences between the text and early Mesoamerican civilization.2 It is now the mainstream scholarly geography among believing Book of Mormon researchers, defended and refined by the Interpreter Foundation, the Maxwell Institute's former FARMS reviewers, and scholars such as Brant A. Gardner, who dedicated his own history-of-the-text to Sorenson.3 The Church itself takes no official position: its 2019 Gospel Topics statement declares that "the Church does not take a position on the specific geographic locations of Book of Mormon events in the ancient Americas" and urges members "not to advocate those personal theories in any setting or manner that would imply either prophetic or Church support for those theories."4 A rival believing geography — Rod Meldrum's North American "Heartland" model, which keeps Cumorah in New York and reads the narrow neck as the Niagara region — exists but is a fringe position, rejected by the academic mainstream that holds the limited model.5

The evidence for

The text's own distances are small. This is the model's strongest card, and it comes from the book itself. Armies march the length of the land in days or weeks; the whole width of the narrow neck is "a day and a half's journey for a Nephite" (Alma 22:32), and a defensive line across the narrow country is "a day's journey" (Helaman 4:7). A day-and-a-half's foot journey is on the order of fifty to a hundred and fifty miles, not the thousands a hemispheric reading requires. A book that lets a single runner cross its pivotal chokepoint in thirty-six hours is describing a small region, and a limited geography honours that internal measure where the hemispheric model ignores it.6

Mesoamerica is the one region that fits the kind of civilization described. The Book of Mormon depicts a literate, urban, agricultural people keeping written records, building cities of cement and stone, and waging organized seasonal warfare across a landscape with volcanism (the cataclysm of 3 Nephi, with its darkness, burning cities, and sundered earth). Of all the pre-Columbian Americas, exactly one region in the relevant window had genuine writing systems, monumental cities, calendrical record-keeping, and large-scale organized war: Mesoamerica. Sorenson matched the text's internal directions, seasons, volcanic geology, and cultural profile to that region and identified specific correlates — highland Guatemala as the land of Nephi, the Isthmus of Tehuantepec as the narrow neck — arriving at a stage a few hundred miles long.1

As a hermeneutic it answers many "anachronism" charges at once. A small stage with a large resident population that the migrants joined lets loan-shifting explain the text's horses, cattle, and metals (native words applied to unfamiliar things); lets a founding colony vanish genetically into millions of natives; and lets the absence of a sprawling hemispheric empire be exactly what one should expect. This is why the model is not a curiosity but load-bearing: the sibling pages on the metal, animal, and DNA questions must engage it rather than dismiss it, because it is the strongest scholarly form the historicity case takes and the frame in which those charges are meant to be read.3

It yields specific correspondences. The model's defenders do not rest on fit-in-general. The archaeologist John E. Clark, comparing the Jaredite record with the Olmec, the region's first civilization, judged that “from about 1500 BC onward their histories are remarkably parallel,” and Sorenson's Mormon's Codex works the correlation at length.7 For the destruction at Christ's death in 3 Nephi 8, with its darkness, fires and sunken cities, Latter-day Saint geologists point to volcanoes in the proposed region, such as Tacaná and San Martín, that may have erupted in the first century AD.8

The early reading was never uniform. Matthew Roper has shown that “antecedents of a limited geography go back to the 1840s” and that “it may not be entirely accurate to speak of the traditional geography even in the nineteenth century.” The church newspaper Times and Seasons, then published under Joseph Smith's editorship, greeted John Lloyd Stephens's reports of Central American ruins in 1842 by declaring that the ruins of Palenque “are among the mighty works of the Nephites:—and the mystery is solved,” and floated Quiriguá in Guatemala as Zarahemla. The church's own geography statement notes that while “it is not clear how involved Joseph Smith was in writing these editorials, he never refuted them.”9

The text itself separates the plates from the battle-hill. The two-Cumorahs objection (below) assumes that the plates Joseph Smith recovered had been buried in the hill of the final battle. The text says otherwise. Mormon writes that he “hid up in the hill Cumorah all the records which had been entrusted to me by the hand of the Lord, save it were these few plates which I gave unto my son Moroni” (Mormon 6:6). The abridgment Joseph Smith translated was the record given to Moroni, who wandered alone for decades afterward, so on the text's own account it need not have been in the battle-hill at all. As a Latter-day Saint writer puts it, “the Book of Mormon was translated from the plates given to Moroni and not those buried in the Hill Cumorah.”10

Adversarial assessment

What holds. On internal geography the model is genuinely well-grounded. The text really does describe short distances, and a hemispheric reading really does strain them past breaking. Mesoamerica really is the only pre-Columbian region with the writing, cities, and organized warfare the narrative assumes. The internal case is not special pleading; it is a fair reading of the book's own numbers, and it is why the model won the scholarly field it did.

It overturns the plain reading its own narrators and earliest readers held. The Book of Mormon presents its "land of promise" in language the first generations of Latter-day Saints, Joseph Smith included, took hemispherically: Lehi's seed inheriting "the whole continent," "the whole of North and South America" as the promised land, the final Nephite battles in the American northeast. Orson Pratt read Lehi landing in South America with Lamanites in the south and Nephites in the north; B. H. Roberts called "the two American continents … a promised land"; the 1834 Zelph episode had Joseph Smith naming Illinois bones as a "white Lamanite" from the Book of Mormon's own wars. The limited model relocates and shrinks the story against that reading — a real interpretive cost, not a detail.11 Roper's evidence softens the cost without removing it. The 1842 editorials did place Nephite cities in Central America, but they still placed the final destruction at Cumorah in New York, and they located Guatemala only loosely, “north of the Isthmus of Darien.” Even Roper calls the 1842 view “a geography that limits most Nephite activities … to Central America, with the exception of their final destruction at Cumorah.”9

It forces the two-Cumorahs problem. The most concrete cost is a single hill. Joseph Smith said he recovered the plates from a hill in Manchester, New York, and generations of church leaders identified that hill with the Cumorah/Ramah where the Nephites and Jaredites fought their annihilating final battles (Mormon 6; Ether 15). But if all Book of Mormon events sit in Mesoamerica, the battle-Cumorah must lie in southern Mexico — so there are now two hills named Cumorah, the New York one merely re-named after the real one hundreds of miles south, with Moroni carrying the plates the intervening distance in a journey the record never mentions. Critics press this as the model's reductio: it saves the geography by multiplying a named landmark and silently rewriting the church's own account of where the plates came from.12 Mormon 6:6 is the best answer to this, and it does what its defenders claim: it makes two hills possible. It does not make them required, since nothing in the verse stops Moroni from later burying his plates in the same hill, and the earliest Latter-day Saint reading of the verse went the other way. Oliver Cowdery's 1835 letter, copied into Joseph Smith's own history, reads Mormon 6:6 exactly as the defenders do and still names the New York hill as the battlefield: “here, between these hills, the entire power and national strength of both the Jaredites and Nephites were destroyed.”13

And it can function as an unfalsifiable retreat. Because the model shrinks the stage to a small, undetectable enclave inside a vast native population, it can absorb almost any negative result — no Nephite genes, no Old-World metallurgy, no horses — by placing the events just out of the reach of the test. Critics such as the geneticist Simon Southerton, and some believing scholars, read this as special pleading: an exception invoked precisely to immunize the account from the material record. Even the internal fit is contested on its own terms — the Isthmus of Tehuantepec, at roughly 120–200 miles across, is arguably too wide for a day-and-a-half foot crossing, and other readers put the narrow neck elsewhere or argue Sorenson misread Alma 22:32 as measuring the neck when it measures the bordering lands.14 The specific correspondences are real but loose. Volcanism is common across Mesoamerica, so an eruption in the right century does not single the region out, and the first-century dates proposed are broad ("ca. AD 25–72"). The Olmec parallel is a parallel of rise and fall over a millennium, not a match of named places.78

Assessment

Contested — the merits split by discipline and there is no clear winner. Judged as an account of the Book of Mormon's internal geography, the limited model is strong and well-motivated: the text's own march-times and its one-and-a-half-day narrow neck describe a small region, and Mesoamerica is the single pre-Columbian setting with the writing, cities, and organized warfare the narrative takes for granted. Judged against the history of interpretation and one hard external datum, it is weak: it overturns the hemispheric reading that dominated early Latter-day Saint teaching (though not uniformly, since the 1842 Times and Seasons already placed Nephite cities in Central America), and it forces two hills named Cumorah, one in New York where the plates were found and one in Mesoamerica where the battles must now sit. Mormon 6:6, which keeps the translated plates out of the battle-hill, makes that arrangement possible on the text's own terms; it does not answer the earliest Latter-day Saint testimony, which put the battlefield in New York.

Both verdicts are held by serious people. Believing scholars from the Interpreter Foundation and the former FARMS circle defend the model as sound exegesis of the book's internal geography; critics inside and outside the church reject it as a post-hoc retreat that shrinks the story until no test can reach it, and even its friendly internal fit (Tehuantepec's width, the exact sense of Alma 22:32) is disputed among its own proponents. That is the signature of a genuinely contested question — strong by the lights of internal-textual geography, weak by the lights of external history and the Cumorah datum — not a case with a settled answer. The sibling pages that invoke this framework to answer anachronism and DNA charges therefore lean on a frame that is itself unresolved: it earns its engagement, but it does not close the questions it is asked to close.
