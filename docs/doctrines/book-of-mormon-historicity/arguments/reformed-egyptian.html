Is the Book of Mormon's "reformed Egyptian" attested in the pre-Columbian record?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: weak — no pre-Columbian inscription anywhere in the Americas is a Semitic language written in an Egyptian-derived script, which is the specific thing the text predicts. Mesoamerica is genuinely literate — Maya, Zapotec, and Isthmian scripts are real and old — so the lazy charge "there was no writing in the New World" is false and must not be made. But every one of those systems is an indigenous invention encoding a Mesoamerican language, none is Egyptian-derived, and their decipherment turned up no Near Eastern loan. The single physical sample of the script itself, the "Anthon transcript" characters, has never been read by anyone. The small-corpus defense — one lineage's sacred record on restricted metal plates need not have left a wide trace — is coherent, but it explains the absence rather than producing evidence, and that is what costs it. The Old World precedent its defenders cite is real (Judahite scribes used Egyptian hieratic signs, and Aramaic was written in demotic) and makes the book's description of its script coherent; it does not place such a script in the Americas.

Related arguments: the material anachronisms — see also the Old World animals · crops, metals & textiles

The claim

The Book of Mormon says the Nephites kept their sacred record in a script they call "reformed Egyptian." In the closing lines of the book Moroni writes that "we have written this record according to our knowledge, in the characters which are called among us the reformed Egyptian, being handed down and altered by us, according to our manner of speech," and adds that had the plates been larger they would have written in Hebrew — but that "none other people knoweth our language."1 At the other end of the narrative, its opening verse has Lehi's son Nephi say his record is made "in the language of my father, which consists of the learning of the Jews and the language of the Egyptians."1 If a real Israelite-descended people lived in the Americas from c. 600 BC to AD 421, this yields a concrete material prediction: somewhere in the pre-Columbian New World there was a literate people writing a Semitic language in a script derived and adapted from Egyptian. That is the claim tested here — not the theology, but whether the ground has produced the script.

Who advances it

The text itself is the primary witness; the defensive case is carried by Latter-day Saint scholarship. The Interpreter Foundation and Scripture Central argue that "reformed Egyptian" is a Nephite label for a Nephite adaptation, so there is no reason to expect the exact phrase — or the exact system — to surface in Old World or New World records, and they point to attested precedents for compressing Egyptian into shorthand (hieratic, demotic, "abnormal hieratic").2 The most ambitious positive case is Brian D. Stubbs's, a working Uto-Aztecan comparativist who argues in Exploring the Explanatory Power of Semitic and Egyptian in Uto-Aztecan (2015) that the Uto-Aztecan language family carries a large stratum of Northwest Semitic and Egyptian loanwords — a linguistic, rather than epigraphic, trace of the migration the Book of Mormon describes.3 A wider popular literature adduces stray "Old World inscriptions in America" — the Los Lunas Decalogue boulder in New Mexico, the Bat Creek stone — as physical Semitic epigraphy in the pre-Columbian hemisphere.

The evidence for

Semitic languages were written with Egyptian signs in the Old World. For a Judahite family of Lehi's day, a record in “the language of the Egyptians” (1 Nephi 1:2) is not an absurd idea, and Latter-day Saint scholars have gathered the precedents. Stephen Ricks and John Tvedtnes collected the Semitic texts written in Egyptian characters. William Hamblin argues that “both the hieratic and demotic scripts could be considered ‘reformed’ or modified versions of the original hieroglyphic script,” so that “Nephite reformed Egyptian is thus a unique script” within a known family. Neal Rappleye, drawing on the Egyptologist David Calabro, argues that the Judahite use of Egyptian signs “lasted in a fuller form than only the isolated use of numbers and units of measurement.”4 The precedents they cite are established in mainstream scholarship. From the late ninth century BC Hebrew scribes borrowed Egyptian hieratic numerals and signs for measures and commodities into their alphabetic records, at Arad, Kadesh-Barnea and elsewhere. Papyrus Amherst 63 preserves Aramaic texts, among them a version of Psalm 20, written entirely in Egyptian demotic script.5

The New World really did write. The single strongest thing to concede is that the flat dismissal is false: Mesoamerica is one of the few places on earth to have invented writing independently. The Maya developed a full logosyllabic script; the Zapotecs of Monte Albán were writing from the 5th century BC; the Isthmian (Epi-Olmec) script of the La Mojarra stela and the Tuxtla statuette runs from roughly 500 BC into the first centuries AD — squarely inside the Book of Mormon's own window.6 A literate people in the right region at the right time is not a fantasy; it is the archaeological baseline. So the prediction is not absurd on its face.

There is a physical sample of the script. Before the plates were returned, Joseph Smith copied a set of characters onto a sheet that Martin Harris carried east in 1828 to Charles Anthon of Columbia — the "Anthon transcript." A related sheet of characters (the "Caractors" document) survives. Whatever their status, the claim is not that the script is wholly undocumented: a sample exists, and its defenders read the surviving lines as a genuine, compact, engraving-friendly shorthand. In 1942 the Boise attorney Ariel Crowley ran a three-part series in the Improvement Era setting the characters beside Egyptian cursive forms. Working from the “Caractors” sheet, which he took to be the slip Anthon saw, he argued that all the forms Anthon had called Chaldaic, Assyriac, Arabic and Egyptian “also occur in ‘reformed,’ that is to say, hieratic and demotic Egyptian.” He set aside the simple strokes as inconclusive and “deliberately singled out” “the most difficult, complex characters,” printing some ninety-seven of them in photographic pairs, each beside a sign photographed from Spiegelberg's Demotische Grammatik or Brugsch's hieroglyphic-demotic dictionary. A closing instalment added ten matches with the Proto-Sinaitic inscriptions of Serabit el-Khadim. His verdict was that the “Caractors” are “Egyptian in fact.”78

The small-corpus point has real force. The text does not predict a widespread public script. It describes a single royal-priestly lineage keeping a deliberately restricted sacred record, engraved on metal plates, "handed down and altered" privately over a thousand years, in a language "none other people knoweth." Metal is perishable or meltable; a hidden dynastic archive need not have left monuments. So the absence of a wide epigraphic trace is exactly what the model itself would lead one to expect — the defense is internally consistent.2

And a linguistic trace is claimed where an epigraphic one is missing. Stubbs's thesis is the serious form of the positive case: not "find the inscription" but "the loanwords are already in the living languages." He tallies well over a thousand proposed Semitic and Egyptian correspondences in Uto-Aztecan, and his standing as a Uto-Aztecan comparativist — his 2011 comparative vocabulary was welcomed by specialists — means the proposal cannot be waved off as amateur.3

Adversarial assessment

What holds. Mesoamerican literacy is real and must be granted; a script existed in the region and era the argument needs. The small-corpus reasoning is coherent and the flat "no writing in the Americas" objection is simply wrong. An honest treatment starts by conceding all of that.

The Old World precedent shows the idea is coherent, not that the script reached America. The precedents are real, and they answer the charge that “reformed Egyptian” is a nonsense category. But they are narrower than they can sound. In Judah the borrowed hieratic is, in Scripture Central's own words, “generally limited to numerals, measurements, and commodities”; no running Hebrew text written in Egyptian signs has been found, and the fuller tradition Rappleye proposes rests on one scholar's reading. Papyrus Amherst 63 is Aramaic, not Hebrew, written by Egyptian-trained scribes in Egypt on a papyrus of about the third century BC. All of it is Old World evidence: it makes the book's description of its script plausible for a family leaving Jerusalem, and says nothing about whether such a script was ever written in the Americas, which is the claim weighed here.45

But the attested scripts are the wrong scripts. The prediction is specific: an Egyptian-derived script encoding a Semitic language. Every deciphered or partly-deciphered Mesoamerican system fails both halves. Maya is a logosyllabic system built from local logograms and syllabograms, encoding a Mayan language; the decipherment that Yuri Knorozov opened and Tatiana Proskouriakoff extended — the story Michael Coe tells in Breaking the Maya Code — produced dynastic history, calendar, and ritual in a Mayan tongue, and nothing Egyptian and nothing Semitic anywhere in it.9 Zapotec and Isthmian are likewise indigenous inventions encoding Mesoamerican (Zapotecan, Zoquean) languages. No accepted pre-Columbian inscription is Egyptian-script Semitic. Literacy in the right time and place, but not the predicted kind, does not confirm the prediction; it sharpens the miss.

The one physical sample has never been read. The Anthon characters — the sole surviving specimen of "reformed Egyptian" — have yielded no decipherment under Egyptological or epigraphic scrutiny, and Anthon's own later accounts deny that he authenticated anything. The surviving sheet is not even certainly the one Anthon saw: the Joseph Smith Papers attribute the “Caractors” document to John Whitmer's hand, about 1829, and note that Anthon “described a document completely different from the document featured here.”10 Crowley's comparison, the fullest attempt to tie the characters to Egyptian, matches shapes rather than reading text. It offers no reading of a single line, and it finds the same characters echoed in South Arabian letters, cuneiform and Proto-Sinaitic as readily as in demotic. So it shows that simple pen-strokes recur across scripts, not which script these are.7 A sample nobody can read is not evidence for the script; it is the place a reader would expect evidence and finds a question mark.8

The stray "Semitic in America" inscriptions do not survive scrutiny — and honesty requires saying so. The Los Lunas Decalogue boulder mixes letter-forms from two alphabets, was cut with modern tools, sits in an implausible spot, and traces to a discoverer of dubious reputation; epigraphers judge it a modern forgery, and the Bat Creek stone has gone the same way.11 These cannot be banked as evidence for the reading; adducing them weakens the case rather than helping it.

The linguistic rescue is contested at the method, not just the conclusion. Stubbs's proposal drew a sharply mixed reception. The linguist Chris Rogers, reviewing it in the Journal of Book of Mormon Studies, faulted it for abandoning the standard bilateral comparative method — comparing whole proto-languages — and instead matching any form in any Semitic or Egyptian variety against any form in any Uto-Aztecan language, a multilateral procedure known to manufacture chance resemblances and to "prove virtually anything"; the Nahuatl specialist Magnus Pharao Hansen raised the same objection from the Nahuatl side.12 The thesis has able defenders, but it is precisely the kind of large-N resemblance-tally that historical linguistics treats with caution; it is not a settled trace and cannot carry the prediction on its own.

The small-corpus defense wins coherence and loses evidentially. This is the decisive point, and it cuts the way the evidence requires. Granting that a hidden metal archive could leave no trace is granting a possibility, not producing a datum — and possibility is not proof. The defense succeeds at explaining why the evidence is absent, which is a different and weaker thing than supplying evidence. An argument whose central move is to account for the missing corroboration has, by that very move, conceded that the corroboration is missing; it insulates the claim from disconfirmation rather than confirming it. That is an unfalsifiable rescue, and while it is not a contradiction — the model can absorb the silence — it means the material prediction comes back empty.

Scope the failure honestly. What comes out weak is the specific material prediction — an Egyptian-derived Semitic script recoverable in the pre-Columbian Americas — under the model of a literate hemispheric Nephite civilization. It is not a verdict on Latter-day Saint faith, nor a claim that the New World was pre-literate (it was not), nor a claim that a small hidden archive is logically impossible (it is not). It is the narrower finding that the ground, so far, has produced no instance of the predicted script, that the one sample of it is unread, that the auxiliary inscriptions offered for it are judged modern, and that the best linguistic substitute for the missing epigraphy is methodologically contested.

Assessment

Weak. The Book of Mormon predicts a literate people writing a Semitic language in an Egyptian-derived script somewhere in the Americas between 600 BC and AD 421. Mesoamerica was genuinely literate in that window — a fact the argument's critics too often ignore — but the scripts it produced (Maya, Zapotec, Isthmian) are indigenous inventions encoding Mesoamerican languages, none Egyptian-derived, and their decipherment surfaced no Near Eastern loan. No accepted pre-Columbian inscription is Egyptian-script Semitic; the single specimen of the script itself, the Anthon characters, has never been read; and the "Old World inscriptions in America" wheeled out to fill the gap (Los Lunas, Bat Creek) are widely judged modern forgeries. The strongest defenses — a small deliberately-restricted metal archive that need not have left a trace, and Stubbs's proposed Semitic loanword stratum in Uto-Aztecan — are the honest ones to weigh, but the first explains the absence rather than curing it (an unfalsifiable rescue that costs the argument its evidential force) and the second is contested at the level of comparative method. The Old World precedents (Hebrew hieratic numerals and signs, Aramaic in demotic) are real and show that the script the book describes is a coherent idea for a family from Jerusalem, but they are Old World evidence and cannot stand in for the missing New World trace. Possibility is conceded; proof is not delivered. The verdict is weak, and it is scoped to the material prediction, not to the faith.
