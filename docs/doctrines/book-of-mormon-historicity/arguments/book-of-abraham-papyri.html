Is the Book of Abraham a translation of the recovered papyri?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: weak — the Book of Abraham presents itself as a translation, “by his own hand, upon papyrus,” of writings of Abraham; portions of the very papyri Joseph Smith worked from resurfaced in 1966 and were identified by Egyptologists as ordinary Ptolemaic funerary texts — the Book of Breathings of a Theban priest named Hor, and Book of the Dead fragments — that name no Abraham and say nothing of his story, and Smith's published explanations of the three facsimiles do not match the Egyptological reading of the vignettes. Here, unlike the Book of Mormon, the source document is not lost but readable, so the leverage is documentary, not a metaphysical objection to revelation. The two standard rescues are weighed and neither cures the strong claim: the missing-scroll theory (the source was on the lost part of the roll) is pressed by scroll-geometry measurements (themselves disputed) and by manuscripts that pair the English text with characters from the surviving fragment (though when they were made is unknown); the catalyst theory (the papyri prompted a revelation rather than being literally rendered) rescues the possibility of inspiration but concedes the plain “translation” claim on the table and still leaves the facsimile explanations unexplained.

Scope. The Book of Abraham is a different scripture from the Book of Mormon, and this page does not weigh whether the Nephites existed. It sits among the Book of Mormon pages because it is the one place where Joseph Smith's claim to translate an ancient record can be checked against a source that survives: the gold plates are gone, but part of the Abraham papyri is in hand. Its bearing on Book of Mormon historicity is therefore indirect. It tests the translator's method, not the Nephites' history, and a verdict here is not a verdict there.

The claim

A Translation of some ancient Records, that have fallen into our hands from the catacombs of Egypt.—The writings of Abraham while he was in Egypt, called the Book of Abraham, written by his own hand, upon papyrus.

— the Book of Abraham, superscription (Pearl of Great Price).1 In 1835 Joseph Smith's church bought four Egyptian mummies and several papyrus rolls from the travelling exhibitor Michael Chandler; Smith announced that one of the rolls contained “the writings of Abraham” and another the writings of Joseph of Egypt, and set about producing a translation.2 The result, serialized in the church newspaper Times and Seasons in 1842 and canonized in the Pearl of Great Price in 1880, is presented not as a vision about Abraham but as a rendering of Abraham's own first-person record off the purchased papyrus, complete with three woodcut facsimiles reproduced from the papyri and Smith's figure-by-figure “explanations” of what they depict.1 Allowing that a genuine revelation could accompany the work, this still yields a concrete, testable claim: the text of the Book of Abraham is a translation of the Egyptian papyri Joseph Smith acquired. That is the claim weighed here.

Who advances it

The claim is first the text's own — its superscription and its facsimile captions assert a direct rendering of the papyrus. For most of the book's history the point was moot because the papyri were thought destroyed in the 1871 Chicago fire. That changed in 1966, and the defensive case since has been carried by Latter-day Saint scholarship: Hugh Nibley's mid-century essays, and more recently the Egyptologists John Gee and Kerry Muhlestein of Brigham Young University, who defend the book's antiquity while conceding that the surviving fragments are funerary. The church's own Gospel Topics essay, “Translation and Historicity of the Book of Abraham,” sets out the faithful responses in their most careful institutional form — and, tellingly, does not insist on a literal decipherment of the surviving fragments, offering instead the missing-scroll and catalyst alternatives.3

The evidence for

The rescues are not logically impossible, and the honest starting point concedes as much. Two defenses deserve a fair hearing before any verdict.

The missing-scroll (long-scroll) theory. Only eleven papyrus fragments resurfaced; the rolls Smith owned were longer, and the lost portions could in principle have carried a text he rendered as the Book of Abraham. Eyewitnesses described a “long roll,” and mounting a fragment for display destroys context; so the argument that “the surviving scraps are not the source” is not a fantasy but a real possibility about a real gap in the evidence. It is the church essay's lead response: “It is likely futile to assess Joseph's ability to translate papyri when we now have only a fraction of the papyri he had in his possession.”3

The catalyst theory. A second, more radical defense drops the literal-rendering claim altogether: the papyri were the occasion of a revelation, not its object. As the essay puts it, “Joseph's study of the papyri may have led to a revelation about key events and teachings in the life of Abraham, much as he had earlier received a revelation about the life of Moses while studying the Bible.”3 On this reading the truth of the Book of Abraham no longer stands or falls with what the fragments say, because the fragments were never being decoded in the ordinary sense.

And a reappropriation reading is available for the facsimiles. Kevin Barney offers, as an explicit hypothesis, that “the facsimiles may not have been drawn by Abraham's hand but may have been Egyptian religious vignettes that were adopted or adapted by an Egyptian Jewish redactor as illustrations of the Book of Abraham.” On that hypothesis the mismatch between Smith's captions and the Egyptological reading would reflect an ancient adaptation, not a modern error. Ancient reuse of iconography is a real phenomenon, and the church's own essay cites Barney's study; it at least engages the problem rather than ignoring it.4

The book contains ancient material Joseph Smith is not known to have had. This is the defenders' positive case, and the strongest part of it is specific. The Book of Abraham names a “plain of Olishem” near Abraham's Ur (Abraham 1:10), a place unknown to the Bible. An inscription of the Akkadian king Naram-Sin (c. 2254–2218 BC) names a northern Syrian town, Ulisum, and Stephen Smoot, John Gee, Kerry Muhlestein and John Thompson judge it “plausible and promising (though not yet definitive) evidence for the Book of Abraham's Olishem.”5 The book's story of Abraham nearly sacrificed by idolatrous priests, and other details, have parallels in ancient Jewish and Christian traditions about Abraham gathered by John Tvedtnes, Brian Hauglid and John Gee. The church's essay grants that some were “available in apocryphal books or biblical commentaries in Joseph Smith's lifetime” while “others were confined to nonbiblical traditions inaccessible or unknown to 19th-century Americans.”6 And Muhlestein notes “a papyrus from the Roman period with a person on a lion couch whom the Egyptians themselves labeled as Abraham,” showing that Egyptians could associate Abraham with exactly the kind of scene in Facsimile 1.7

The scroll and the manuscripts are less settled than critics claim. John Gee has argued that the lost portion of the Hor roll was far longer than critics allow: about 12.5 metres on his 2008 calculation, revised to about 3 metres in 2012.8 Muhlestein argues that the Kirtland Egyptian papers, in which characters from the surviving fragment stand beside passages of the Book of Abraham, are “later copies of the text of the Book of Abraham … not the original translation,” since “the Egyptian characters appear to sometimes overwrite the English.”7 The Joseph Smith Papers editors take no side but confirm the uncertainty: “the sequence of the creation” of the manuscripts “is unknown,” and “most of the Book of Abraham is not textually dependent on any of the extant Egyptian-language documents.”9

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The papyri really are longer than the surviving scraps, and a lost portion did once exist; revelation is not excluded here a priori, and possibility is genuinely conceded. Any honest treatment grants that the church does not today rest the book on a claimed decoding of the extant fragments, and that the catalyst and reappropriation moves are coherent as possibilities.

But the source document, uniquely, is not lost — it is readable, and it is a funeral text. This is the decisive difference from the Book of Mormon, whose plates are gone. In 1966 the Egyptologist Aziz Atiya recognized eleven of the Joseph Smith papyri in the Metropolitan Museum of Art; the church acquired them in 1967. Egyptologists — Klaus Baer, Richard Parker, and John Wilson independently in 1968, and later Robert Ritner in the first complete edition — identified them as a standard Book of Breathings (the “Sensen” document) made for a Theban priest named Hor, together with Book of the Dead fragments and a hypocephalus, dating to the Ptolemaic period (roughly the 3rd–1st century BC), a millennium and more after any Abraham. They name no Abraham and contain none of the book's narrative; they are mortuary spells to secure the deceased's breathing in the afterlife.10 Baer titled his translation “The Breathing Permit of Hôr: A Translation of the Apparent Source of the Book of Abraham,” and Ritner concluded flatly that “over half of the text of the Book of Abraham was invented by Smith from only two incomplete lines in the Breathing Permit of Hôr.”11

The facsimile explanations do not match the vignettes. This is independent of any scroll-length question, because the woodcuts were printed in 1842 from vignettes that are among the surviving fragments (Facsimile 1 is the recovered lion-couch scene). Facsimile 1 is a standard embalming/resurrection scene — the jackal-headed god Anubis attending a body on a lion-shaped bier — not, as Smith's caption has it, an idolatrous priest of Elkenah about to sacrifice Abraham on an altar; Facsimile 2 is a hypocephalus (an amuletic disk placed under a mummy's head), not a diagram of the astronomy of Kolob; Facsimile 3 is a judgment/presentation scene before an enthroned deity, not Abraham lecturing Pharaoh's court on astronomy. The French Egyptologist Théodule Devéria said as much as early as 1860, and even the Latter-day Saint Egyptologist John Gee concedes that “the interpretations of the Egyptologists typically do not match either those of the ancient Egyptians or Joseph Smith.”12 A caption that identifies a figure as Abraham where the iconography reads Osiris, or names a jackal-god a “priest,” is a demonstrable mismatch, not a matter of unrecoverable meaning.

The contemporaneous manuscripts pair the English with the surviving fragment. The missing-scroll theory also has to account for the “Kirtland Egyptian Papers,” a set of 1835 manuscripts — including a “Grammar and Alphabet of the Egyptian Language” — in which Smith's scribes copied characters from the surviving Sensen papyrus into the margins and set each single character against long English passages of the Book of Abraham. Muhlestein's case that the characters were added afterward is a real argument, and the Joseph Smith Papers editors leave the sequence open, so these papers do not by themselves prove that the fragment was the source. But even read as later work, they show Smith's circle associating the extant fragment with the English text, and the editors also report that “some evidence indicates that material from the Grammar and Alphabet volume was incorporated into at least one portion of the Book of Abraham text.”9 That association is what the missing-scroll defense needs to explain away.13

And the lost portion was probably too short to hold the book. The missing-scroll theory can be tested, not merely asserted. Andrew Cook and Christopher Smith reconstructed the original length of the Hor roll from the way its damage repeats across successive windings, and concluded that only a modest length of papyrus — on the order of half a metre — is actually missing from the interior, far too little to have carried a hieratic text of the Book of Abraham in addition to the Breathing Permit it demonstrably held.14 Cook and Smith judged Gee's earlier measurements to require “the papyrus to be impossibly thin”; Gee's revised figure of about 3 metres has, so far as this page has found, no published reply. The geometry therefore narrows the missing-scroll theory without closing it, and the theory's weight falls back on the documentary points: the manuscripts above and the facsimiles, which survive and do not match.8

The ancient-content parallels are real but do not reach the papyri. Ulisum is the best of them, and its own proponents call it “not yet definitive”: they concede that “there is no agreement as to the precise location of Ulisum,” and Gee's earlier verdict was that “there is nothing against the proposed identifications, [but] they are not proven either.”5 Several of the extrabiblical Abraham traditions were in print in Joseph Smith's day; the church's essay notes some were available “through the books of Jasher and Josephus” and that Smith knew those books, though “it is unknown whether he utilized them.”6 The Leiden lion-couch papyrus is disputed: Edward Ashment argues the subject of that spell is a woman, the spell a love charm, and the scene not a sacrifice.15 Above all, even granted in full, these parallels are evidence that the Book of Abraham contains some ancient-sounding material. They are not evidence that the surviving papyri, which read as a funeral text for Hor, contain it.

The catalyst theory rescues revelation by conceding the translation. Its cost is exactly its escape. If the papyri merely prompted a revelation, then the book's own superscription — “written by his own hand, upon papyrus … a translation of some ancient records” — and its facsimile captions, which purport to read specific Egyptian figures, are no longer describing what actually happened. The theory saves the possibility that God revealed something true about Abraham; it does not save the claim on the table, that the text is a translation of the recovered papyri. It relocates the question from translation to revelation rather than answering it — and it still says nothing about why the facsimile explanations misread the vignettes. That is a coherent retreat, not a vindication, and the honest thing is to score it as such rather than count the relocation as a win.

Scope the failure honestly. What comes out weak is the specific documentary claim — that the surviving Egyptian papyri are the source-text of the Book of Abraham, and that Smith's facsimile explanations recover their ancient meaning. It is not a proof that no revelation of any kind occurred, nor a claim that revelation is impossible (possibility is conceded throughout); the leverage is that here, unusually, the documents survive and can be read, and they read as a funeral scroll for a priest named Hor. The reappropriation and catalyst readings can keep a form of the faith alive, but only by abandoning the plain sense of the words on the table.

Assessment

Weak. The Book of Abraham advertises itself as a translation of the Egyptian papyri Joseph Smith bought in 1835, “written by [Abraham's] own hand, upon papyrus.” Portions of those papyri resurfaced in 1966 and are, by the unanimous reading of Egyptologists from Baer to Ritner, ordinary Ptolemaic funerary texts — the Book of Breathings of the priest Hor and Book-of-the-Dead fragments — that mention no Abraham and match none of the book's narrative, and Smith's figure-by-figure explanations of the three facsimiles contradict the standard Egyptological reading of the vignettes (a mismatch flagged as early as Devéria in 1860 and conceded even by Latter-day Saint Egyptologists). The two serious rescues do not repair the strong claim: the missing-scroll theory is pressed by scroll-geometry measurements (though Gee disputes them) and by the 1835 Kirtland manuscripts that pair the English text with characters from the surviving fragment (though when they were made is unknown), and the catalyst theory saves the possibility of revelation only by conceding the “translation” claim in its plain sense while leaving the facsimile explanations unexplained. The defenders' positive evidence (the Olishem/Ulisum identification, extrabiblical Abraham traditions, a Roman-era lion-couch scene labelled Abraham) is real and is the strongest part of their case, but it bears on whether the book holds ancient material, not on whether the recovered papyri are its source. Because the source document here is not lost but legible, the leverage is documentary rather than metaphysical: revelation is not ruled out a priori, but the specific claim that the recovered papyri are the Book of Abraham does not hold on the evidence. Weak, and scoped to that documentary claim, not to Latter-day Saint faith as such.
