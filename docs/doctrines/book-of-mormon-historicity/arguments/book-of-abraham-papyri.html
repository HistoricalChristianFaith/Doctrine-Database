Is the Book of Abraham a translation of the recovered papyri?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: weak — the Book of Abraham presents itself as a translation, “by his own hand, upon papyrus,” of writings of Abraham; portions of the very papyri Joseph Smith worked from resurfaced in 1966 and were identified by Egyptologists as ordinary Ptolemaic funerary texts — the Book of Breathings of a Theban priest named Hor, and Book of the Dead fragments — that name no Abraham and say nothing of his story, and Smith's published explanations of the three facsimiles do not match the Egyptological reading of the vignettes. Here, unlike the Book of Mormon, the source document is not lost but readable, so the leverage is documentary, not a metaphysical objection to revelation. The two standard rescues are weighed and neither cures the strong claim: the missing-scroll theory (the source was on the lost part of the roll) runs against scroll-geometry measurements and against the manuscript evidence tying the English text to characters on the surviving fragment; the catalyst theory (the papyri prompted a revelation rather than being literally rendered) rescues the possibility of inspiration but concedes the plain “translation” claim on the table and still leaves the facsimile explanations unexplained.

The claim

A Translation of some ancient Records, that have fallen into our hands from the catacombs of Egypt.—The writings of Abraham while he was in Egypt, called the Book of Abraham, written by his own hand, upon papyrus.

— the Book of Abraham, superscription (Pearl of Great Price).1 In 1835 Joseph Smith's church bought four Egyptian mummies and several papyrus rolls from the travelling exhibitor Michael Chandler; Smith announced that one of the rolls contained “the writings of Abraham” and another the writings of Joseph of Egypt, and set about producing a translation.2 The result, serialized in the church newspaper Times and Seasons in 1842 and canonized in the Pearl of Great Price in 1880, is presented not as a vision about Abraham but as a rendering of Abraham's own first-person record off the purchased papyrus, complete with three woodcut facsimiles reproduced from the papyri and Smith's figure-by-figure “explanations” of what they depict.1 Granting for exploration that a genuine revelation could accompany the work, this yields a concrete, testable claim: the text of the Book of Abraham is a translation of the Egyptian papyri Joseph Smith acquired. That is the claim weighed here.

Who advances it

The claim is first the text's own — its superscription and its facsimile captions assert a direct rendering of the papyrus. For most of the book's history the point was moot because the papyri were thought destroyed in the 1871 Chicago fire. That changed in 1966, and the defensive case since has been carried by Latter-day Saint scholarship: Hugh Nibley's mid-century essays, and more recently the Egyptologists John Gee and Kerry Muhlestein of Brigham Young University, who defend the book's antiquity while conceding that the surviving fragments are funerary. The church's own Gospel Topics essay, “Translation and Historicity of the Book of Abraham,” sets out the faithful responses in their most careful institutional form — and, tellingly, does not insist on a literal decipherment of the surviving fragments, offering instead the missing-scroll and catalyst alternatives.3

The evidence for

The rescues are not logically impossible, and the honest starting point concedes as much. Two defenses deserve a fair hearing before any verdict.

The missing-scroll (long-scroll) theory. Only eleven papyrus fragments resurfaced; the rolls Smith owned were longer, and the lost portions could in principle have carried a text he rendered as the Book of Abraham. Eyewitnesses described a “long roll,” and mounting a fragment for display destroys context; so the argument that “the surviving scraps are not the source” is not a fantasy but a real possibility about a real gap in the evidence. It is the church essay's lead response: “It is likely futile to assess Joseph's ability to translate papyri when we now have only a fraction of the papyri he had in his possession.”3

The catalyst theory. A second, more radical defense drops the literal-rendering claim altogether: the papyri were the occasion of a revelation, not its object. As the essay puts it, “Joseph's study of the papyri may have led to a revelation about key events and teachings in the life of Abraham, much as he had earlier received a revelation about the life of Moses while studying the Bible.”3 On this reading the truth of the Book of Abraham no longer stands or falls with what the fragments say, because the fragments were never being decoded in the ordinary sense.

And a reappropriation reading is available for the facsimiles. Some Latter-day Saint scholars (notably Kevin Barney) propose that a later Jewish editor took over standard Egyptian funerary vignettes and re-purposed them to illustrate Abraham — so the mismatch between Smith's captions and the Egyptological reading would reflect an ancient adaptation, not a modern error. Ancient reuse of iconography is a real phenomenon, and this at least engages the problem rather than ignoring it.

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The papyri really are longer than the surviving scraps, and a lost portion did once exist; revelation is not excluded here a priori, and possibility is genuinely conceded. Any honest treatment grants that the church does not today rest the book on a claimed decoding of the extant fragments, and that the catalyst and reappropriation moves are coherent as possibilities.

But the source document, uniquely, is not lost — it is readable, and it is a funeral text. This is the decisive difference from the Book of Mormon, whose plates are gone. In 1966 the Egyptologist Aziz Atiya recognized eleven of the Joseph Smith papyri in the Metropolitan Museum of Art; the church acquired them in 1967. Egyptologists — Klaus Baer, Richard Parker, and John Wilson independently in 1968, and later Robert Ritner in the first complete edition — identified them as a standard Book of Breathings (the “Sensen” document) made for a Theban priest named Hor, together with Book of the Dead fragments and a hypocephalus, dating to the Ptolemaic period (roughly the 3rd–1st century BC), a millennium and more after any Abraham. They name no Abraham and contain none of the book's narrative; they are mortuary spells to secure the deceased's breathing in the afterlife.4 Baer titled his translation “The Breathing Permit of Hôr: A Translation of the Apparent Source of the Book of Abraham,” and Ritner concluded flatly that “over half of the text of the Book of Abraham was invented by Smith from only two incomplete lines in the Breathing Permit of Hôr.”5

The facsimile explanations do not match the vignettes. This is independent of any scroll-length question, because the woodcuts were printed in 1842 from vignettes that are among the surviving fragments (Facsimile 1 is the recovered lion-couch scene). Facsimile 1 is a standard embalming/resurrection scene — the jackal-headed god Anubis attending a body on a lion-shaped bier — not, as Smith's caption has it, an idolatrous priest of Elkenah about to sacrifice Abraham on an altar; Facsimile 2 is a hypocephalus (an amuletic disk placed under a mummy's head), not a diagram of the astronomy of Kolob; Facsimile 3 is a judgment/presentation scene before an enthroned deity, not Abraham lecturing Pharaoh's court on astronomy. The French Egyptologist Théodule Devéria said as much as early as 1860, and even the Latter-day Saint Egyptologist John Gee concedes that “the interpretations of the Egyptologists typically do not match either those of the ancient Egyptians or Joseph Smith.”6 A caption that identifies a figure as Abraham where the iconography reads Osiris, or names a jackal-god a “priest,” is a demonstrable mismatch, not a matter of unrecoverable meaning.

The contemporaneous manuscripts tie the English to the surviving fragment. The missing-scroll theory has to explain away the “Kirtland Egyptian Papers,” a set of 1835 manuscripts — including a “Grammar and Alphabet of the Egyptian Language” — in which Smith's scribes copied characters from the surviving Sensen papyrus into the margins and set each single character against long English passages of the Book of Abraham. Whatever their exact working procedure, these documents show the translators treating the extant fragment as the source of the text, which is precisely what the missing-scroll defense needs them not to have done.7

And the lost portion was too short to hold the book. The missing-scroll theory can be tested, not merely asserted. Andrew Cook and Christopher Smith reconstructed the original length of the Hor roll from the way its damage repeats across successive windings, and concluded that only a modest length of papyrus — on the order of half a metre — is actually missing from the interior, far too little to have carried a hieratic text of the Book of Abraham in addition to the Breathing Permit it demonstrably held.8 The gap the theory needs is not the gap the physical scroll allows.

The catalyst theory rescues revelation by conceding the translation. Its cost is exactly its escape. If the papyri merely prompted a revelation, then the book's own superscription — “written by his own hand, upon papyrus … a translation of some ancient records” — and its facsimile captions, which purport to read specific Egyptian figures, are no longer describing what actually happened. The theory saves the possibility that God revealed something true about Abraham; it does not save the claim on the table, that the text is a translation of the recovered papyri. It relocates the question from translation to revelation rather than answering it — and it still says nothing about why the facsimile explanations misread the vignettes. That is a coherent retreat, not a vindication, and the honest thing is to score it as such rather than count the relocation as a win.

Scope the failure honestly. What comes out weak is the specific documentary claim — that the surviving Egyptian papyri are the source-text of the Book of Abraham, and that Smith's facsimile explanations recover their ancient meaning. It is not a proof that no revelation of any kind occurred, nor a claim that revelation is impossible (possibility is conceded throughout); the leverage is that here, unusually, the documents survive and can be read, and they read as a funeral scroll for a priest named Hor. The reappropriation and catalyst readings can keep a form of the faith alive, but only by abandoning the plain sense of the words on the table.

Assessment

Weak. The Book of Abraham advertises itself as a translation of the Egyptian papyri Joseph Smith bought in 1835, “written by [Abraham's] own hand, upon papyrus.” Portions of those papyri resurfaced in 1966 and are, by the unanimous reading of Egyptologists from Baer to Ritner, ordinary Ptolemaic funerary texts — the Book of Breathings of the priest Hor and Book-of-the-Dead fragments — that mention no Abraham and match none of the book's narrative, and Smith's figure-by-figure explanations of the three facsimiles contradict the standard Egyptological reading of the vignettes (a mismatch flagged as early as Devéria in 1860 and conceded even by Latter-day Saint Egyptologists). The two serious rescues do not repair the strong claim: the missing-scroll theory founders on scroll-geometry measurements and on the 1835 Kirtland manuscripts that tie the English text to characters on the surviving fragment, and the catalyst theory saves the possibility of revelation only by conceding the “translation” claim in its plain sense while leaving the facsimile explanations unexplained. Because the source document here is not lost but legible, the leverage is documentary rather than metaphysical: revelation is not ruled out a priori, but the specific claim that the recovered papyri are the Book of Abraham does not hold on the evidence. Weak, and scoped to that documentary claim, not to Latter-day Saint faith as such.
