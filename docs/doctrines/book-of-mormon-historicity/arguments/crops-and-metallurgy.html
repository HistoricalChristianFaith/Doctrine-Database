Do the Book of Mormon's crops, metals, and textiles fit the pre-Columbian record?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: weak — but with two honest partial fits that should be conceded before the verdict. Pre-Columbian America really did cultivate a native barley (little barley, Hordeum pusillum), so the flat "there was no New World barley" charge is wrong; and the New World really did work metal — gold, copper, tumbaga, arsenical and tin bronze — so "no metals at all" is wrong too. Granting both, the reading still fails on its load-bearing items: the text's pervasive steel and iron swords in a literate, metal-using war culture have no counterpart in a pre-Columbian record that shows no smelted ferrous industry in the relevant place and window, and the crop and textile fits hold only for the word, not cleanly for the place. Weak overall; honest about where it is closest.

Related arguments: the material anachronisms — see also the Old World animals · reformed Egyptian & the script

The claim

The Book of Mormon describes a farming, weaving, metal-working civilization. Its peoples plant "corn, and wheat, and barley," and two grains no one has identified, "neas" and "sheum" (Mosiah 9:9); barley is even the base unit of their money (Alma 11:7). They wear "fine silks" and "fine-twined linen" (Alma 1:29; Ether 9:17; Helaman 6:13). And above all they work iron and steel: Laban's sword is of "the most precious steel" (1 Nephi 4:9), Nephi hunts with "a bow, made of fine steel" (1 Nephi 16:18), the colony teaches its people "to work in all manner of wood, and of iron, and of copper, and of brass, and of steel, and of gold, and of silver, and of precious ores" (2 Nephi 5:15), the Nephites have "all manner of tools … to till the ground" of iron and steel (Jarom 1:8), and the Jaredites fight with "swords" of "steel" (Ether 7:9) and with "cimeters" — scimitars — throughout.1 The claim assessed here is that these named crops, metals, and textiles fit what the pre-Columbian Americas actually grew, wove, and forged.

Who advances it

The steelman is the limited-geography, loan-shift model most fully worked out by John L. Sorenson (An Ancient American Setting for the Book of Mormon, 1985), and carried on by the FairMormon/FAIR and Interpreter Foundation apologetic literature. Two moves do the work. First, limited geography: the events belong not to two continents but to a small Mesoamerican theatre, so the record to be matched is that of highland Guatemala and the Isthmus of Tehuantepec, not the hemisphere. Second, loan-shifting: a colonist meeting an unfamiliar plant, animal, or fabric names it with the closest word he already owns — as Europeans called the American bison a "buffalo" — so "barley," "silk," and even "steel" need not denote the Old World referent, only a native thing that struck the writer as near enough.2 On the other side stand the critics — among them the plant geneticist Simon Southerton, a former Latter-day Saint bishop — who hold that the crops, animals, and metals of the text are simply out of place in pre-Columbian America and that the limited-geography escape is inconsistent with a text that describes vast populations and continent-spanning wars.3

The evidence for

Barley is the best fit, and it is a real one. The charge that "there was no barley in the pre-Columbian New World" is false. A native American barley — little barley, Hordeum pusillum — was one of the founding crops of the Eastern Agricultural Complex, gathered and tended in the eastern woodlands from roughly the second millennium BC, with cultivated specimens recovered from Late Archaic and Early Woodland sites and, later, a domesticated form among the Hohokam of Arizona. A widely reported 1983 find of apparently domesticated (hull-less) barley at a Hohokam site near Phoenix was seized on at once as New World barley, and it holds up: little barley is a genuine, documented native domesticate.4 The text names a grain the land actually grew. As for the two mystery grains, "neas" and "sheum," they are unfalsifiable in the friendly direction — untranslated by design — and one apologetic note observes that "sheum" is a near-match for Akkadian šeʾum, "barley/grain," which at least shows the word is not gibberish.4

Metal working existed — "no metals" is wrong. The pre-Columbian Americas were emphatically not metal-less. Andean and later West Mexican smiths worked gold, silver, copper, the gold-copper alloy tumbaga, and true arsenical and tin bronze; Mesoamericans exploited iron minerals — hematite, magnetite, ilmenite — for polished mirrors as early as c. 1200 BC at San José Mogote. So a blanket "there was no metallurgy" misstates the record, and Sorenson presses this hard.5 On the steel and iron specifically, Sorenson offers a battery of reframes: Book of Mormon "steel" need not mean modern carbon steel; meteoric nickel-iron (present in Mesoamerica) has been called "a type of steel"; a Tarascan chronicle speaks of "steel" helmets; and for the Old World steel (Laban's sword, made before the migration) a carburized iron sword with a bronze haft was excavated near Jericho, dated to about the time of Josiah — Lehi's own era — so a "steel" blade in the hands of a Jerusalem noble c. 600 BC is not anachronistic at all.5

Silk, by loan-shift, has native candidates. No Old World silkworm reached the Americas, but Mesoamerica produced several fine fibers a colonist might reasonably have called "silk": cloth spun from the cocoons of wild moths (Eucheira socialis, Gloveria psidii); kapok from the ceiba tree, which a Spanish bishop compared to imported silk; silk-grass and wild-pineapple fiber; the gossamer under-fur of rabbits woven by the Aztecs; and cotton so fine that Cortés said it could not be equalled "for texture, richness of colors, and workmanship." Wild-silk textiles have in fact been recovered from a pre-Columbian burial. The word "silk" has plausible native referents.6

Adversarial assessment

What holds — concede it plainly. Two of the sub-claims survive contact with the evidence, and a page rigged to debunk would bury them. There was a pre-Columbian American barley, and there was pre-Columbian American metallurgy. Anyone who recites "no barley, no metals" as a knockdown has the facts wrong, and the loan-shift principle behind the silk argument is a real feature of how colonists name new things. These are the honest near-fits, and they are the reason this reading is "weak," not "unsupported."

But the barley fits the word, not the place. Little barley is a different species from Old World Hordeum vulgare, and — decisively for the strong model — it is an eastern and southwestern North American crop: the Eastern Agricultural Complex and the Hohokam of Arizona, not the highland-Guatemala/Isthmus theatre the limited-geography reading needs. There is no established pre-Columbian barley of any species in the Mesoamerican core where the Nephite cities are placed. So the concession is real but narrow: the text names a grain the continent grew, in the wrong part of the continent for the model that rescues everything else.4 And wheat — Old World Triticum (Mosiah 9:9) — has no native New World counterpart at all; it rides entirely on the hope that it, too, is a loan-shift for some unnamed grain.

The metallurgy concession does not reach steel swords. This is the load-bearing failure. Granting every real New World metal, the record still contains no smelted ferrous industry — no iron or steel forged into blades — in Mesoamerica in the Book of Mormon window (c. 600 BC – AD 421). West Mexican metallurgy, the region's true metalworking tradition, does not begin until about AD 600 — after the Nephite record closes — and Dorothy Hosler has shown it was introduced by sea from Andean South America and Ecuador, not developed in place and not from the Near East; its signature products are cast copper bells, needles, and tweezers, not carburized war-swords.7 Sorenson's reframes each fall short of the text. Meteoric nickel-iron is worked cold and in small ornaments — it will not furnish the mass-manufactured "tools … of iron" and armies of steel blades the text describes, and calling it "a type of steel" is a terminological courtesy, not a metallurgical fact. The Jericho sword rescues only the Old World reference (Laban's blade in Jerusalem) — which was never the hard part — and says nothing about steel in the Americas. And "it became a lost technology" is not evidence; a smelting-and-forging industry that armed nations leaves slag, furnaces, ore-heaps, and corroded blades, and none of that has surfaced. Even the most favorable apologetic surveys concede outright that "there is currently no evidence for pre-Columbian steel."7

Silk holds only as far as loan-shifting is allowed to travel. The native "silks" are genuine, and taken alone the fiber argument is the argument's second-strongest limb. But the method that saves silk is the same method that, applied to steel, dissolves the strong claim: once "steel" can mean cold-worked meteorite and "silk" can mean rabbit fur, the text is no longer making checkable material predictions — it is being read so that whatever the ground shows was what it meant. Loan-shifting is legitimate for a genuinely ambiguous word met in a new land; it becomes special pleading when it is asked to absorb a term as specific and repeated as forged steel weaponry in a literate iron-age war culture.

Scope the failure honestly. What breaks is the strong reading — a literal hemispheric (or even a robust Mesoamerican) Nephite civilization whose material culture matches the text's crops, textiles, and metals point for point. What survives is milder and worth keeping: the New World grew a barley and worked metal, so the caricature "the Book of Mormon names nothing that existed here" is false. The verdict lands where the load-bearing item lands, and the load-bearing item — steel and iron swords, tools, and armor across eight centuries — is exactly the one the record does not contain.

Assessment

Weak — and deliberately not rated lower, because two of its sub-claims are honest partial fits that must be stated as plainly as the failure. Pre-Columbian America really did cultivate a native barley (Hordeum pusillum), which retires the flat "no New World barley" charge; and it really did work metal in gold, copper, tumbaga, and bronze, which retires "no metallurgy at all." Those are the closest the reading comes, and a fair page concedes them.

But the reading fails on the item that carries it. The Book of Mormon's world is saturated with steel and iron — swords, bows, tools, armor — across a literate, metal-using, war-making civilization spanning c. 600 BC to AD 421, and the pre-Columbian record for the relevant region and window shows no smelted ferrous industry whatever: West Mexican metallurgy is post-AD 600 and Andean-derived, meteoric iron is a cold-worked ornamental material, and even friendly surveys grant that no pre-Columbian steel is attested. The barley fits the word but not the Mesoamerican place; wheat and Old World silk have no native referent except by the loan-shift that, pressed onto forged steel, becomes special pleading. So the honest result is narrow-concession-plus-decisive-failure: the caricature is wrong, and the strong claim still does not hold. As with every argument here, that scopes to the specific model — a literal Nephite civilization matching the text's material culture — not to the faith that reveres the book.
