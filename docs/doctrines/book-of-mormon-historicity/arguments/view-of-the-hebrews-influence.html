Did View of the Hebrews (1823) stand behind the Book of Mormon (1830)?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: plausible — this is the literary-origin question, kept distinct from the material-record question the rest of this doctrine weighs: not “did the events happen?” but “where did the text come from?” Ethan Smith's View of the Hebrews (Poultney, Vermont, 1823; enlarged 1825) argued from Scripture and Indian ethnography that the American natives were the lost ten tribes of Israel — and it did so seven years and a few Vermont–New York counties before the Book of Mormon appeared. That the Book of Mormon arose within the Hebraic-origin-of-the-Indians milieu View of the Hebrews popularized is not seriously in doubt; the sharper claim — that this specific book served as a direct literary source, a “ground-plan” — is coherent, was pressed by the Latter-day Saint scholar B. H. Roberts himself and by Fawn Brodie, and is not ruled out, but it falls short of demonstration. There is no documentary evidence Joseph Smith read the book; the much-cited Oliver Cowdery link is unproven; and John W. Welch has catalogued dozens of points on which the two works flatly disagree, so the parallels are consistent with a shared milieu rather than direct dependence on one title. Plausible tracks the honest middle: the environmental influence is strong, the direct-source claim is live but unproven.

The claim

Did Ethan Smith's View of the Hebrews furnish structural material for Joseph Smith's Book of Mormon? It has been pointed out in these pages that there are many things in the former book that might well have suggested many major things in the other — not a few things merely, one or two, or half dozen, but many; and it is this fact of many things of similarity and the cumulative force of them that makes them so serious a menace to Joseph Smith's story of the Book of Mormon's origin.

— B. H. Roberts, weighing the parallels in his private studies of 1921–22.1 Ethan Smith (1762–1849; no relation to Joseph) was the Congregationalist minister of Poultney, Vermont, from 1821 to 1826, and there published View of the Hebrews; or the Tribes of Israel in America (1823; a much-enlarged second edition followed in 1825). Gathering the scriptural prophecies of Israel's scattering and gathering together with the ethnographic reports of writers such as James Adair, it argued that the American Indians descended from the lost ten tribes, carried to the New World after the Assyrian deportation.2 Seven years later, and about a hundred miles west, the Book of Mormon told a story built from the same raw idea — Israelite migrants crossing the ocean to become the ancestors of the American peoples. The claim weighed here is the literary-origin thesis: that View of the Hebrews plausibly stands behind the Book of Mormon — at the least as its intellectual environment, at the most as a book Joseph Smith knew and drew on.

Who advances it

The thesis has an unusual pedigree: its most careful early statement came not from a critic but from a defender. Between 1921 and 1922 B. H. Roberts — a president of the Seventy and the church's foremost intellectual — compiled, for the eyes of the First Presidency and the Twelve, a set of studies cataloguing the parallels between the second (1825) edition of View of the Hebrews and the Book of Mormon. He listed some eighteen points of contact and judged that the earlier book could have furnished a “ground-plan” for the later one. Roberts's own faith in the Book of Mormon is debated — he continued to defend it publicly until his death in 1933 — and his studies were written as a devil's-advocate brief for the leadership rather than a confession; but the parallels he assembled became the backbone of every later version of the argument. They were not published until 1985.1

The thesis reached a wide public through Fawn Brodie, whose No Man Knows My History (1945) made Joseph Smith the author of the Book of Mormon and named View of the Hebrews as a book “he could scarcely have overlooked.” Brodie drew on Roberts's work and pressed the dependence harder than he had — while conceding, as honest treatments must, that no evidence places the book in Joseph Smith's hands.3 The link is often bolstered by geography: Ethan Smith pastored the Poultney church while members of Oliver Cowdery's family — Cowdery would become Joseph Smith's principal scribe — had ties to that same congregation, a coincidence critics read as a channel by which the book's themes could have reached the Book of Mormon's makers.4

The evidence for

The parallels are real, cumulative, and were conceded by an insider. Set side by side, the two books share a striking spine of ideas. Both narrate a migration of Israelites to the Americas for religious reasons; both split that stock into a civilized and a savage branch, with the savage destroying the civilized; both have their peoples keep written records, in one case tied to Egyptian characters; both quote long stretches of Isaiah on the scattering and restoration of Israel; both dwell on the destruction of Jerusalem, the burial and recovery of a sacred record, ancient earthworks and metallurgy, and a gospel known in the ancient New World. It is not the force of any one item but the accumulation that impressed Roberts — “not a few things merely … but many.” That the man who drew up the list was a believing general authority, writing to warn his own leadership, is itself weighty: this is not a polemicist's manufactured tally.1

The environment is not in dispute. Even critics of the direct-dependence claim grant the larger point. The notion that the Indians were Hebrews was “in the air” of Joseph Smith's New England and New York — Adair's History of the American Indians (1775), Elias Boudinot's A Star in the West (1816), a flourishing literature on the “mound-builders,” and View of the Hebrews itself as its most popular synthesis. The Book of Mormon answers the very questions that literature was asking, in the terms it was asking them; whether or not one particular book was open on the table, the Book of Mormon is unmistakably a document of that conversation. This much of the thesis is close to consensus among historians outside the church.5

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The environmental reading is strong and should be stated plainly: the Book of Mormon did not fall from a clear sky but grew in soil View of the Hebrews helped till, and the cumulative parallels Roberts listed are genuine, not invented. If the question is “did the Hebraic-origin milieu shape the Book of Mormon?” the answer is almost certainly yes.

But direct dependence on this one book is unproven — there is no chain of custody. The argument has a hole its proponents concede: no document, statement, or reminiscence places View of the Hebrews in Joseph Smith's hands before 1830, and Brodie herself admits it. The Oliver Cowdery bridge, on which much weight is often laid, does not bear it: the church-record ties are to Cowdery's stepmother and half-sisters (baptized 1818, before Ethan Smith's 1821 pastorate began), the family appears to have been living elsewhere for much of the relevant period, and — as the Latter-day Saint historian Larry Morris shows — no document links Ethan Smith to Oliver Cowdery at all. A seventeen-year-old Oliver and a sixty-three-year-old pastor sharing a small town is not a demonstrated line of transmission.4

And the two books contradict each other on their major points. This is John W. Welch's “unparallel”: if Joseph Smith had known View of the Hebrews well enough to build on it and had respected it, he should at least not have flatly contradicted it — yet he does, repeatedly. Welch lists dozens of such divergences. View of the Hebrews makes the Indians the lost ten tribes, deported after 721 BC; the Book of Mormon's Lehites leave Jerusalem about 600 BC, are of Joseph through Manasseh, and are pointedly not the northern tribes. Ethan Smith argues a single migration; the Book of Mormon has several (Jaredites from Babel, Lehites, Mulekites). View of the Hebrews has no continuous narrative, no named persons, no appearance of Christ, none of the Book of Mormon's distinctive doctrine or its thousand-year plot. Where the earlier book urges the gathering of the Indians as a present missionary duty, the Book of Mormon frames the future of the Lamanites as prophecy. The overlap is thematic and general; the disagreement is structural and specific.6

Shared milieu explains the parallels without direct copying. This is the decisive move, and it cuts both ways honestly. Because the whole family of Hebraic-origin books shared Isaiah, the lost tribes, the two-civilizations motif, the mounds, and the buried record, finding those elements in both Ethan Smith and the Book of Mormon shows common descent from the milieu, not necessarily descent of one text from the other — the same logic by which two newspapers reporting the same event share content without either copying the other. Counting generic commonplaces as “parallels” is the trap Samuel Sandmel named parallelomania: a long list of vague similarities can feel probative while proving only that both authors breathed the same air. That is why the specific-book claim cannot be pushed to certainty even though the milieu claim is secure.5

Scope the finding honestly, and do not let apologetics over-collect either. Two symmetrical cautions. Against the critic: the parallels do not establish that Joseph Smith read this book, and several of Roberts's items soften once the Book of Mormon's own text is read on its terms. Against the defender: the standard rebuttal sometimes over-reaches in the other direction — noting that many parallels ease under a limited-Mesoamerican geography, or that the differences “far outweigh” the likenesses, does not erase the environmental fact, and it is no answer to the milieu claim to refute only the crude plagiarism claim. The plagiarism charge (Joseph copied the book) is weak; the influence claim (the book, and the literature it typifies, shaped the Book of Mormon) is strong; the middle claim actually on the table — that this particular book was a direct source — is where the evidence genuinely runs out.

Assessment

Plausible. That the Book of Mormon emerged from the Hebraic-origin-of-the-Indians milieu that View of the Hebrews did more than any other book to popularize is close to certain, and the cumulative parallels catalogued by B. H. Roberts — a believing general authority, not a critic — are real enough that he called them a “serious menace” to the traditional origin story. But the sharper thesis, that this specific 1823 book was a direct literary source, is coherent and seriously advocated yet short of proof: no evidence puts the book in Joseph Smith's hands, the Oliver Cowdery channel is unproven, and John W. Welch has shown that the two works contradict one another on migration, tribe, date, structure, and doctrine — divergences that fit a shared milieu better than direct copying, and that make the generic overlaps a case study in Sandmel's “parallelomania.” The interpretation therefore rates plausible: the environmental influence is strong and undisputed, the direct-source claim is live but undemonstrated, and the crude plagiarism version is weak. This is a verdict about where the text came from, not about whether its events occurred — the material-record questions are weighed on the doctrine's other pages; the wider diffusionist tradition, on Old-World peoples in the pre-Columbian Americas.
