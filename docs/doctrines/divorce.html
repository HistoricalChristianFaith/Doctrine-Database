Divorce and remarriage — what did Jesus permit?

May a Christian divorce, and if so, may either party marry again? Jesus’ teaching on the question is recorded in four places that have been read in different ways. In the Sermon on the Mount he says, “whosoever shall put away his wife, saving for the cause of fornication, causeth her to commit adultery: and whosoever shall marry her that is divorced committeth adultery” (Matthew 5:31–32). Questioned by the Pharisees, he grounds marriage in creation — “what therefore God hath joined together, let not man put asunder” — says Moses permitted the bill of divorcement only “because of the hardness of your hearts,” and repeats the exception: “whosoever shall put away his wife, except it be for fornication, and shall marry another, committeth adultery” (Matthew 19:3–9). Mark and Luke record the same saying with no exception (Mark 10:11–12; Luke 16:18), and Paul adds a case of his own — that a believer deserted by an unbelieving spouse “is not under bondage” (1 Corinthians 7:10–16).

The later discussion turns on a few recurring questions: what “fornication” (porneia) means in Matthew’s exception, and why Matthew alone has it; whether the exception permits remarriage or only separation from bed and board; whether the marriage bond is indissoluble while both spouses live, dissoluble for adultery (and desertion) with liberty to remarry, or open to remarriage by pastoral “economy” (oikonomia); how Jesus’ words relate to the first-century dispute between the schools of Shammai and Hillel over Moses’ “some uncleanness” (Deuteronomy 24:1–4); and how the sayings relate to the prophet’s “I hate putting away” (Malachi 2:16) and the “one flesh” of Genesis 2:24. The timeline records how Christian writers, councils and churches have answered, earliest extant attestation first.

The communions answer differently today. The Catholic Church teaches that a ratified and consummated marriage “can be dissolved by no human power and by no cause, except death” (Code of Canon Law, 1983, can. 1141), the teaching the Council of Trent’s canon 7 on matrimony (1563) protected with an anathema.1 The Eastern Orthodox churches, in the account of the Orthodox historian John Meyendorff (1990), proclaim indissolubility but have “never understood indissolubility to be a legal absolute,” recognizing adultery and “situations practically equivalent to the death of one of the partners” as grounds of divorce, while remarriage is “only tolerated and subject to penitential conditions.”2 Bishop Athenagoras (Peckstadt) of Sinope (2005) writes that in the Orthodox Church “divorce and remarriage are only permitted in the context of ‘economia’, that is, out of pastoral care, out of understanding for weakness,” and gives its canonical definition as “the suspension of the absolute and strict applications of canon and church regulations in the governing and the life of the Church, without subsequently compromising the dogmatic limitations,” applied “only through the official church authorities” and “only applicable for a particular case”; he grounds it on Christ’s command to forgive sins (John 20:22–23).3 The Westminster Confession (1646) grants the innocent party divorce and remarriage for adultery and for “such willful desertion as can no way be remedied by the church, or civil magistrate.”4

Historians read the early record in more than one way. Augustinus Lehmkuhl (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1909) holds that “the synods of all centuries, and more clearly still the decrees of the popes, have constantly declared that divorce which annulled the marriage and permitted remarriage was never allowed,” and reads patristic passages that seem to allow a husband a new marriage as referring to a lighter canonical penance, or to remarriage after the dismissed wife’s death.5 Joseph Ratzinger (1972) holds that “the Fathers in East and West are from the very beginning in complete agreement on the total impossibility of the separation of a christian marriage that could lead to remarriage during the life time of the spouses,” while recognizing that beneath this teaching “there was evidently again and again in the concrete pastoral application a more elastic practice”; as prefect of the Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith he wrote in 1998: “There exists a clear consensus among the Fathers regarding the indissolubility of marriage.”6 Henri Crouzel (1970) counts Ambrosiaster the only church writer of the first five centuries who clearly permits remarriage. Pierre Nautin (1974) argues that Ambrosiaster’s view was the dominant tradition of the first three centuries; David G. Hunter (2021), with Philip Reynolds and Riccardo Bof and Conrad Leyser, holds that early Christian opinion and policy on the question were diverse.7 David Instone-Brewer (2007) holds that “the early church, and even Jewish rabbis, forgot what the ‘any cause’ divorce was,” so that “when Jesus condemned ‘divorce for “any cause,” ’ later generations thought he meant ‘divorce for any cause.’”8

Timeline

The Shepherd of Hermas (c. 140)

The earliest extant post-apostolic treatment on this timeline. Put to the angelic Shepherd, the case of a man who catches his believing wife in adultery draws a two-part ruling: if she persists unrepentant, the husband must put her away — to go on living with her is to become “a sharer in her adultery” — yet “if he put his wife away and marry another, he also commits adultery.”9 The reason he must “remain by himself” is that the door to reconciliation must stay open: should the divorced wife repent, he is bound to take her back, so “the husband ought not to marry another, when his wife has been put away” — and “man and woman are to be treated exactly in the same way.”10 Hermas thus requires separation from an unrepentant adulterous spouse and forbids a new marriage, without citing Matthew’s exception.11 David G. Hunter (2021) treats the passage as the one text of the first four centuries that clearly and unequivocally prohibits a man from remarrying after divorcing his wife for adultery.12 Joseph Ratzinger (1972) reads the Fathers of East and West as “from the very beginning in complete agreement” that a Christian marriage cannot be separated so as to permit remarriage while both spouses live.6

Justin Martyr (c. 155)

Writing to Antoninus Pius from Rome, Justin lists the divorce saying among the precepts of Christian chastity, in its form without an exception: “Whosoever shall marry her that is divorced from another husband, committeth adultery.”13 He applies it to remarriage — those who “by human law, are twice married” (the ANF editor notes he means a second spouse taken after divorce) are “in the eye of our Master” sinners.14 In the Second Apology Justin records a case: a Christian wife at Rome who — lest she “become a partaker” in her debauched husband’s vices “by sharing his table and his bed” — gave him “what you call a bill of divorce, and was separated from him.” It is the earliest datable notice on this timeline of a Christian divorcing, and the occasion of a martyrdom.15

Athenagoras of Athens (c. 177)

Pleading the Christians’ innocence before Marcus Aurelius, Athenagoras cites the divorce saying as evidence of their chastity and glosses it as a bar on both sending a wife away and remarrying: “Whosoever puts away his wife,” says the Lord, “and marries another, commits adultery” — God “not permitting a man to send her away… nor to marry again.”16 It is the earliest extant patristic quotation on this timeline of the first half of the logion (Matthew 19:9), the man who initiates the divorce; Hermas ruled the case without citing the saying and Justin quoted only its second half. Athenagoras gives the saying without “except for fornication,” and states it to a pagan court as a description of Christian practice, not as church law.17

Clement of Alexandria (c. 200)

The earliest extant witness on this timeline to quote Matthew’s exception clause. In the chapter “On Marriage” that closes the second book of the Stromata, Clement lays down that “Scripture… allows no release from the union,” adducing “Thou shalt not put away thy wife, except for the cause of fornication” — and adds that it “regards as fornication, the marriage of those separated while the other is alive.”18 He reads the exception as permitting an adulterous wife to be sent away, while a new marriage during the first spouse’s lifetime is the adultery the Lord condemns — “He that taketh a woman that has been put away… committeth adultery; and if one puts away his wife, he makes her an adulteress.” His reason is the one Hermas gave, the open door to reconciliation: the second husband is guilty too, “for did he not take her, she would return to her husband.”19

Tertullian (c. 207)

An extended early Latin treatment, and the earliest extant defence on this timeline of divorce for adultery. Answering Marcion’s claim that Christ’s ban on divorce contradicts the Creator’s Moses, Tertullian argues in Against Marcion that Christ did not abolish the Mosaic bill of divorcement (Deuteronomy 24:1) but limited it: the prohibition is conditional, aimed at the man who dismisses his wife “for the express purpose of marrying another,” so that “divorce, therefore, when justly deserved, has even in Christ a defender” — “He permits divorce when the marriage is spotted with unfaithfulness.”20 Christ, on this reading, “exculpated rather than abolished the enactment of Moses,” restoring the creation ordinance the concession had bent “because of the hardness of your hearts”; and “to marry… whilst matrimony is undissolved, is to commit adultery.”21 In the later On Monogamy he permits divorce “to no cause but one” and denies the divorced any remarriage: “to us, even if we do divorce them, even marriage will not be lawful.”22 Hunter (2021) reads Tertullian’s earlier To His Wife (II.1), which speaks of women given an opportunity of continence “by reason of a divorce or the death of a husband” who then married again, as treating remarriage after divorce like a widow’s remarriage, at least in his earliest writings.23

Origen of Alexandria (c. 246)

An extended early Greek exegesis of the divorce sayings, and the earliest extant report on this timeline of a gap between the written rule and church practice. Expounding “is it lawful to put away a wife for every cause?” (Matthew 19:3–12) in the fourteenth book of his Commentary on Matthew, Origen classes Moses’ bill of divorcement among “some laws… written not as excellent, but as by way of accommodation to the weakness” — the hardness-of-heart concession — and holds that Christ permits dissolution “for… fornication alone.”24 He then records that “some even of the rulers of the church have permitted a woman to marry, even when her husband was living… not indeed altogether without reason, for it is probable this concession was permitted in comparison with worse things.”25 Origen calls the permission “contrary to what was written,” yet allows its motive; and he asks whether a wife guilty of “poisoning, or… any form of murder” may not equally be put away — a dilemma in which “there appears to be something monstrous” either way, which he leaves unresolved.26

Lactantius (c. 305)

A Latin witness on the eve of the first Western councils, who rejects the sexual double standard of Roman law. In the sixth book of the Divine Institutes (composed c. 303–311, during and after the Great Persecution), the rhetorician Lactantius — a convert from Roman North Africa who taught at Nicomedia and later tutored Constantine’s son — treats marriage under the head of continence and sets aside the Roman law that counted only the wife an adulteress: it is not, “as is the interpretation of public law, that she alone is the adulteress who has another man, while the male is free from the charge of adultery, though he have many mistresses,” for “the divine law so joins two with equal right into a marriage, which is two in one flesh, that whoever breaks apart the joining of the body is regarded as an adulterer” — faith “must be kept with the other by each party in a marriage,” the husband bound to the continence he demands of his wife, since “it is evil to exact that which you yourself are not able to exhibit.”27 On divorce he writes: “he is an adulterer who takes a wife who has been sent away by her husband; and so is he who has, aside from the crime of adultery, put a wife away that he may take another,” for “God did not intend for that ‘one flesh’ to be separated and torn apart.”28 The clause “aside from the crime of adultery” is read in more than one way. Pierre Nautin (1974) and David Hunter (2021) read it, with the Epitome’s rule that a wife is not to be dismissed unless convicted of adultery, “so that the bond of the conjugal pact may never be dissolved, unless infidelity has broken it,” as allowing a husband who divorces an adulterous wife to remarry; Philip Reynolds (1994) doubts that Lactantius would have extended the same to a wife. Henri Crouzel’s count (1970) of Ambrosiaster as the only writer of the first five centuries who clearly permits remarriage leaves Lactantius outside it.29

The Council of Elvira (c. 306)

The earliest extant Western conciliar canons on divorce on this timeline, and the earliest here in which a council, rather than a single teacher, legislates on it. The collection attributed to a council of nineteen bishops at Elvira in southern Spain includes three canons (8, 9, 10) against the woman who leaves a husband and marries again. The mildest bars her from communion until the deserted spouse dies; the gravest — the wife who leaves “without acceptable cause” and remarries — withholds communion “even when death approaches.”30 Canon 9 addresses a baptized wife who leaves an adulterous husband: she “may not marry” another while he lives; if she does, she is barred “until her former husband dies.”31 The penalty falls on the second marriage and lifts at the first spouse’s death. Hunter (2021), following Josep Vilella (2014), holds that the canons attributed to Elvira derive from various times and places, and notes that the collection has no equivalent canons on men who remarry after divorcing an adulterous wife, which he takes to suggest that the restrictions on women did not exist in the same form for men.32 Eight years later the Gallic Council of Arles (314), under Constantine, met the case of a young husband whose wife commits adultery with a counsel rather than a penalty: “as much as is able,” he is to be counselled “not take other wives while their own wives are still living, even if the latter are adulterous.”33 Lehmkuhl (1909) reads the canon’s description of these husbands as “forbidden to marry” (prohibentur nubere) as declaring such a second marriage illicit; Hunter (2021) reads the canon as evidence of a general custom against remarriage together with the possibility of exceptions.34

Basil of Caesarea (c. 375)

A procedural treatment from the fourth-century East: legislation that treats husband and wife unequally, which Basil says rests on custom. In three canonical letters to Amphilochius of Iconium (Letters 188, 199, 217), later given conciliar force in the Byzantine church, Basil grants that “the sentence of the Lord that it is unlawful to withdraw from wedlock, save on account of fornication… applies… to men and women alike” — then adds, “Custom, however, does not so obtain.”35 Under the custom he records, a married man who falls into fornication is punished as a fornicator but not charged with adultery, and “the wife will receive the husband on his return from fornication,” whereas “the husband will expel the polluted woman from his house” — “the argument here is not easy, but the custom has so obtained.”36 A wife may not leave an adulterous husband; the man deserted by his wife is “deserving of pardon,” but the woman abandoned by her husband “ought… to remain as she is,” barred from remarriage by the Lord’s word (Matthew 5:32) — “by calling her adulteress, He excludes her from intercourse with another man.”37 Basil fixes a seven-year penance for deserting a lawful spouse, and twice says that the unequal treatment is what “custom” ordains. Lehmkuhl (1909) reads Basil’s milder words about the husband (“He is not condemned,” “He is considered excusable”) as referring to a lighter canonical penance for the husband than for the wife, not to a permission to remarry.38 John M. Rist, as quoted by David G. Hunter (2018), writes that “Basil’s name must be added to that of Ambrosiaster as a man prepared, less willingly though more fatefully, at least to tolerate a second marriage after divorce in limited circumstances”; Ratzinger (1972) describes “a likewise well known text of Basil that prescribes a longer Church penance for the second marriage and then tolerates it.”39 Bishop Athenagoras (Peckstadt) of Sinope (Orthodox, 2005) reads Basil, “speaking concerning the man who had been cheated by his wife,” as declaring “that the man is ‘pardonable’ (to be excused) should he remarry.”40

Ambrosiaster (c. 375)

The anonymous Roman commentator known as Ambrosiaster permits the husband, but not the wife, to remarry after a divorce for adultery. Expounding Paul’s divorce charge (1 Corinthians 7:10–11), he notes that Paul tells the separated woman to “remain single” but adds no such words to the man, and reads the difference as permission: “it is lawful for a man to marry another woman, if he divorces a wife who sins. For a man is not bound by the law in the same way as a woman is, for the man is the head of the woman.”41 The wife has no such right: one who leaves an adulterous or apostate husband “does not enjoy the same rights as the man, who is the superior,” and “can neither marry another man nor return to her husband.”42 On the deserting unbeliever (1 Corinthians 7:15) he holds the abandoned union no longer binding — “it is not a sin if it is abandoned because of God” — the case later called the “Pauline privilege.”43 Henri Crouzel (1970), with J. Rist, counts him the only writer of the first five centuries to permit remarriage clearly; David G. Hunter (2018) argues that his double standard reflects Roman law and his theology of male headship, and that his permission of remarriage reflects contemporary Western practice rather than departing from it.44 His commentary circulated under Ambrose’s name until modern times.

Epiphanius of Salamis (c. 375)

In section 59 of the Panarion, his catalogue of heresies, Epiphanius, bishop of Salamis in Cyprus, answers the Novatianist “Purists” (Cathari), who barred the “twice-married” from communion — “for if someone marries a second wife after baptism, they never admit him again.”45 Against them he sets out a principle of concession to weakness, and in Frank Williams’s translation includes remarriage after divorce alongside remarriage after bereavement: “if there has been a divorce for some reason — fornication, adultery or something else — and the man marries a second wife or the woman a second husband, God’s word does not censure them or bar them from the church and life, but tolerates them because of their weakness.”46 The concession is bounded: it is no licence for bigamy — “not by letting him have two wives at once while the one is still alive” — and no commendation, since the once-married is “more highly respected”; the second marriage is borne with as “the first wife a divine ordinance, the second a concession to human weakness.”47 Lehmkuhl (1909) records that Epiphanius “is especially reproached with teaching” that a husband who had put away his wife for adultery could marry another, and reads him as “speaking in reality of a second marriage after the death of the divorced wife.”48

Gregory of Nazianzus (c. 380)

Preaching on Jesus’ divorce dialogue (Matthew 19:1–12) during his brief Constantinople episcopate, Gregory protests against the sexual double standard of the civil law: a wife’s adultery was severely punished, but “if the husband commits fornication against his wife, he has no account to give” — “I do not accept this legislation; I do not approve this custom,” for “they who made the Law were men, and therefore their legislation is hard on women.”49 God’s law, he answers, is one for both sexes — grounded in creation (“one Maker of man and woman”), in the fall (“The Woman Sinned, and so did Adam… one was not found to be the stronger and the other the weaker”), and in redemption (“Christ saves both by His Passion”) — so that the “one flesh” must have “equal honour.”50 On divorce itself: “the Law grants divorce for every cause; but Christ not for every cause; but He allows only separation from the whore; and in all other things He commands patience.”51 Gregory permits separation for fornication only, and argues from the sexes’ equality before God that the same law binds both, as Lactantius had argued equality in Latin.

Ambrose of Milan (c. 385)

The bishop of Milan who baptized Augustine. Expounding Luke’s saying without an exception (Luke 16:18) in the eighth book of his Exposition of the Gospel of Luke, Ambrose sets divine law against the Roman statute: “you dismiss your wife as if it were a right… because human law does not prohibit it; but divine law does prohibit it… What God has joined together, let no man separate.”52 Remarriage while the first spouse lives is adultery — “Suppose she does marry. This is your crime of necessity; and the marriage which you imagine is adultery” — a bar he states in On Abraham: “it is not allowed for you, while your wife is alive, to take another wife.”53 He treats Moses’ bill as a hardness-of-heart concession (“Moses permitted, he says, not God commanded… he who divorces his wife, tears his flesh, divides his body”), and reads Paul’s desertion case (1 Corinthians 7:15) as freeing the abandoned believer from blame: the Apostle “neither commanded nor gave authority to anyone who wants to leave, but he removed blame from the one who is abandoned.”54 He grounds the bar on the equality of the sexes: “what is not permissible for a woman is not permissible for a man… A husband owes the same chastity to his wife as a wife owes to her husband.”55 The Ambrosiaster commentary, which permits the husband to remarry on the ground of headship, circulated under Ambrose’s name for a millennium.

John Chrysostom (c. 390)

The Antiochene preacher teaches that the bond survives the divorce. Expounding the Sermon on the Mount’s divorce saying (Matthew 5:31–32) in his Homilies on Matthew at Antioch, Chrysostom lays guilt on both men in the case: the husband who dismisses his wife “though he take not another wife, by that act alone hath made himself liable to blame, having made the first an adulteress; the latter again is become an adulterer by taking her who is another’s.”56 The second marriage is adultery because divorce does not end the marriage: “tell me not this, ‘the other hath cast her out;’ nay, for when cast out she continues to be the wife of him that expelled her,” so Christ “hath shut against her also the doors of him who was afterwards receiving her.” The one lawful ground for sending a wife away is fornication, and it permits dismissal, not remarriage.57 On Paul’s charge (1 Corinthians 7:10–11) he teaches the same: the separated wife must “remain unmarried, or be reconciled unto her husband” — never “introduce any other to be her husband.”58

Jerome (c. 394)

Across three works of the 390s — a letter of casuistry to the presbyter Amandus (c. 394), the Commentary on Matthew (398), and the eulogy of the Roman noblewoman Fabiola (399) — Jerome, the Vulgate translator, reads Matthew’s exception as permitting the dismissal of an adulterous spouse, not a new marriage while the first partner lives. To Amandus, who had asked whether a woman who left an adulterous, sodomite husband and remarried under compulsion might commune without penance, he answers: “A husband may be an adulterer or a sodomite… yet he is still her husband and, so long as he lives, she may not marry another,” and the “second husband… ought to be called not a husband but an adulterer.”59 The Commentary on Matthew reads the exception clause (Matthew 19:9) as allowing the husband to send the wife away for fornication, which severs the “one flesh”: “the husband is commanded to divorce the first wife so that he does not have a second wife while the first one is still alive.”60 Jerome argues the bar from the equality of the sexes before God: “a commandment which is given to men logically applies to women also… what is unlawful for women is equally unlawful for men.” The Fabiola eulogy applies the rule to a life Jerome admired: he calls her remarriage “a fault… at the same time… a case of necessity,” does not call it lawful, and describes her public penance “in the presence of all Rome” before her restoration to communion after the second husband’s death.61

Pope Innocent I (c. 405)

The earliest extant papal legislation on divorce on this timeline. In two decretal letters answering questions from Gallic bishops — Epistle 2 to Victricius of Rouen (404) and Epistle 6 to Exuperius of Toulouse (405) — Innocent I lays down as the rule “for everyone” that a person who remarries while the first spouse lives is an adulterer, admitting no exception for the injured party. To Exuperius he rules that of those who divorce and marry again “it is clear that both parties are adulterers,” for “those who, while the wife is living, although their marriage has been dissolved, hasten to another union cannot be other than adulterers” — resting the ruling on the divorce logion (Matthew 19:9) and extending the charge to the new spouses, so that “all such are to be kept from the communion of the faithful.”62 To Victricius he states the same principle as established discipline, under which the remarried woman is barred not only from communion but from penance until a spouse dies: it is the rule “that any woman who marries another while her husband lives is held an adulteress, and leave to do penance is not granted her unless one of the two has died.”63 Lehmkuhl (1909) cites both letters among the papal decisions that “deserve special mention.”64 The letters were later copied into Gratian’s Decretum (C. 27 q. 1 c. 10) and the collection of Ivo of Chartres ⚠ unverified. In the following decades regional councils in Gaul (below) issued canons on divorce that Hunter (2018) reads as permitting a husband’s remarriage after proven adultery.

Augustine of Hippo (c. 419)

In four works across a quarter-century — the exposition of Matthew 5:31–32 in On the Sermon on the Mount (c. 393–396), On the Good of Marriage (c. 401), On Marriage and Concupiscence (c. 419–421), and the two books On Adulterous Marriages to Pollentius (c. 419) — Augustine, bishop of Hippo, treats the marriage bond as a sacramentum, a sacred and permanent sign of the union of Christ and the Church, which therefore “can no way be dissolved but by the death of one of them.”65 He reads the exception clause as permitting the dismissal of an adulterous spouse, not a new marriage while the first lives, and applies the bar to husband and wife alike — “it is unlawful for one who leaves her husband, even when she has been put away, to be married to another, so long as her husband lives.”66 The sacramental bond, like the seal of baptism, “is lost neither by divorce nor by adultery”; Moses’ bill was a hardness-of-heart concession, so that under the gospel remarriage after divorce “is held to be guilty of adultery.”67 Answering Pollentius — who read Matthew’s exception as freeing an innocent husband to remarry — he reads Matthew together with the unqualified Mark 10:11–12 and Luke 16:18, says that the gospel words are obscure and the question not fully solved, and concludes that “any union with other persons while their first husband or wife is alive is not marriage but adultery.”68 In his Retractations (426/427) he wrote that he did not think he had “concluded the matter”; Hunter (2021) and the Orthodox bishop Peter L’Huillier (1988), as cited by Hunter, draw attention to these doubts.69 Thomas Aquinas cites him on the bond (below).

The Gallic councils: Vannes and Agde (c. 465–506)

Two regional councils of late-antique Gaul whose canons on divorce cite Matthew’s exception. The Council of Vannes in Brittany (c. 465) excommunicates the man who dismisses his wife and marries again where he acts “without having given proof of the adultery”: citing the gospel’s “except on account of fornication,” it penalizes remarriage on an unproven charge.70 The Council of Agde (506), under Caesarius of Arles, excludes from communion the layman who casts off his wife for “unlawful or others’ unions” before stating the grounds “before the comprovincial bishops” and before “she is condemned by judgment,” requiring that the adultery be adjudicated in an episcopal court first.71 David G. Hunter (2018), with Philip Reynolds, reads Vannes as “simply translat[ing] the exception clause of Matthew 19:9 into ecclesiastical law,” and Agde as evidence that a wife’s proven adultery “justified the remarriage of her husband”; on his reading the two councils stand with Ambrosiaster and show that the early Western church did not uniformly forbid remarriage after divorce.72 Lehmkuhl (1909), without naming these councils, holds that some “regulations of particular synods” permitted “unjustifiable concessions,” while “the synods of all centuries, and more clearly still the decrees of the popes” never allowed divorce that permitted remarriage.73

Justinian I (c. 535–556)

The civil law of the Christian empire. In three Novellae the emperor Justinian codified the grounds, procedure, and penalties of divorce for Roman society. Novel 22 (535) recognizes that a marriage may be undone by the parties themselves: dissolved “either by common consent… or (by agreement) for some reasonable reason when the divorce is called bona gratia, or because of some guilt or no guilt at all” — and it lets a divorced husband “immediately remarry” while binding the wife to a year’s delay, one of several points at which the law treats the sexes unequally.74 Novel 117 (542) narrows divorce to an enumerated list of “just causes” — adultery (proven by formal accusation) heading the husband’s grounds — and enacts in state law an unequal standard like the one Basil had recorded as “custom”: a wife’s adultery frees the husband, but a husband’s unchastity gives the wife a ground only when it becomes open, persistent concubinage “in the same house… or… in the same city” after “repeated” reproach; a wife who divorces without lawful cause is to be “thrown into a convent, to remain there during the remainder of her life.”75 Novel 134 (556) abolishes consensual divorce, directing that “no bill of divorce shall be sent, or if sent, shall be valid, for any cause other than those specified, nor shall the marriage be dissolved by consent,” and condemning a couple who part otherwise to lifelong monastic confinement.76 Justinian died in 565, and in 566 his successor Justin II repealed the ban and restored divorce by mutual consent (Novel 140), for “marriage is entered into through mutual affection… rightly dissolved by a contrary sentiment.”77

The Council in Trullo (692)

Meeting “in Trullo” at Constantinople under Justinian II to supply the disciplinary canons the fifth and sixth ecumenical councils had not issued, the Quinisext Council legislates on divorce in three canons. Canon 87, invoking “the holy and divine Basil” (whose canons canon 2 had ratified), rules that if a wife “appears to have departed from her husband without reason, he is deserving of pardon and she of punishment. And pardon shall be given to him that he may be in communion with the Church”; it declares that “he who leaves the wife lawfully given him, and shall take another is guilty of adultery by the sentence of the Lord,” and assigns a graded penance (“weepers for a year, hearers for two years, prostrators for three years”) rather than Elvira’s permanent exclusion.78 Canon 93 takes up the “grass widow” — the wife of a vanished or soldier husband — and, while still calling a premature remarriage adultery, grants that the case “has some excuse,” distinguishes culpable from ignorant second unions (the woman deceived “is not prevented from marrying”), and extends “pardon”; the returning soldier may “receive his own wife.”79 Canon 98 brings the betrothed woman under the same rule.80 Canon 3 describes the council’s method as blending Rome’s “exact perfection” with Constantinople’s “kindness and consideration” so “that neither gentleness fall into licence, nor severity into harshness.” Meyendorff (1990) describes the Byzantine setting of these canons: since until at least the end of the ninth century marriages were generally concluded as civil contracts, “divorce was a purely civil procedure, whereas remarriage involved penitential discipline.”81 He also describes the later dispute over the emperor Constantine VI’s divorce and remarriage (795), the “moechian” controversy, which the patriarch condoned “by economy”: “For the politically minded patriarchs, oikonomia was becoming a form of realistic accommodation with the powers-that-be. For the monks, it was an expression of divine mercy for the repentant sinner.”82 (Trullo’s handling of remarriage after bereavement is traced on the remarriage timeline.)

The Byzantine canonists: John Zonaras and Theodore Balsamon (c. 1130–1195)

The two twelfth-century Byzantine commentators on the canons, who read the canons on divorce alongside the civil law of the empire. Both repeat the rule that the woman who leaves her husband and marries another is an adulteress (Basil’s canon 9, which Trullo’s canon 87 cites, and which Basil himself traced to “custom”). They read that canon alongside Justinian’s divorce legislation (Novel 117, embedded in the Basilika at 28.7). John Zonaras, the earlier of the two, treats Basil’s double standard as “the ecclesiastical custom at that time” and notes that the imperial Novel grants a wife a ground the canon denied her, permitting “wives to dissolve marriage” for a husband’s open, unrepented adultery.83 Theodore Balsamon — who wrote c. 1177 under imperial and patriarchal commission and read canon law together with civil law — writes that Basil answered “from various writings and the custom held at that time,” but “since Novel 117 of Justinian… all but transformed everything in such a canon,” the reader is sent to the imperial statute.84 The canonists also had before them Trullo’s graded penance and “pardon” in hard cases such as the vanished husband’s wife (canon 93).85

The Latin canonists: Gratian and Peter Lombard (c. 1140–1155)

The two twelfth-century textbooks of Latin canon law and theology on marriage. Gratian’s Decretum (c. 1140) — the “harmony of discordant canons” — gathers the patristic and conciliar authorities on divorce into a single rule, stated in his own words: “whoever has dismissed his wife for cause of fornication cannot, while she lives, marry another; and if he does, he will be guilty of adultery.”86 His method sets beside these authorities the permissive commentary on 1 Corinthians 7 then circulating under Ambrose’s name (now attributed to Ambrosiaster), which grants the husband alone a right to remarry; Gratian reports that the passage “is said by forgers to have been inserted,” and, if defended, narrows it to incestuous unions and to remarriage only after the guilty spouse’s death, “since no authority permits a husband to marry another wife while the first one is still alive.”87 Peter Lombard’s Sentences (Book IV, c. 1155–58) treat marriage as a sacramentum, “a sacred sign… of the union of Christ and the Church” (Ephesians 5:31–32), made by present-tense consent (consensus de praesenti) — “the efficient cause of matrimony is consent… not of the future but of the present” — and not to be separated, the dismissed spouse bound to “remain unmarried,” the right to separate for adultery reciprocal and forfeited by the guilty party.88 The Sentences became the textbook on which masters in the schools, Aquinas among them, lectured, and the Decretum a foundation of later canon law; Erasmus later cited the “Ambrose” text.

Thomas Aquinas (c. 1270)

The Dominican theologian’s treatment of divorce, in the questions of the Summa Theologiae Supplement (compiled from his earlier Commentary on the Sentences, Bk IV). Aquinas grounds the indissolubility of marriage in the natural law and the marriage sacramentum — “the dictate of the natural law requires the latter to live together for ever inseparably… and so the indissolubility of marriage is of natural law” — from the lifelong end of marriage and from Christ’s appeal to creation, “what God hath joined together” (Matthew 19:6).89 He distinguishes two senses in which a marriage can be “dissolved”: as to the act (separation from bed and board — divortium quoad thorum), which a wife’s fornication permits (“an impediment to a previous marriage as regards the act, although the marriage tie remains”), and as to the tie (quoad vinculum), which he holds impossible for a consummated sacramental marriage — “Nothing supervenient to marriage can dissolve it: wherefore adultery does not make a marriage cease to be valid… Therefore it is unlawful for one, while the other lives, to marry again,” resting the ruling on Augustine’s “as long as they live they are bound by the marriage tie, which neither divorce nor union with another can destroy.”90 He reads Moses’ bill of divorce as a hardness-of-heart permission (Matthew 19:8) “recalled” under the New Law, applies the bar to husband and wife alike (“the same things are lawful or unlawful to the one as to the other”), and treats the case of a convert deserted by an unbelieving spouse — the “Pauline privilege” (1 Corinthians 7:15) — as a dissolution of a non-sacramental tie (“if it be not ratified, the tie is dissolved… the firmer tie always looses the weaker”).91 The term ratum et consummatum recurs in the 1983 Code of Canon Law (can. 1141, below).

Erasmus (c. 1519)

The Dutch humanist argued for divorce with remarriage in the Latin West. Erasmus, editor of the first published Greek New Testament (1516), set Matthew’s exception (Matthew 19:9) to read as an exceptive clause — “except for fornication” — and glossed it so that the adulteress, by her sin, “has stopped being his wife,” freeing the innocent husband to marry again; Davidson Razafiarivony (2024) argues that Erasmus alone printed the verse as an unambiguous exceptive clause.92 The fuller case came in a treatise-length note attached from the 1519 edition of his Annotations to Paul’s word on the widow “free to be married to whom she will” (1 Corinthians 7:39): there Erasmus argued two propositions which, in John B. Payne’s account, “were considered revolutionary and heretical by the theologians of his day” — that the church could dissolve certain marriages for grave cause, through its own authorities or judges, and that the innocent party could then remarry — urging, pastorally as well as exegetically, that to bind spouses who have come to detest one another is a danger, breeding “mutual quarrels, irremediable hatred, dread of poisoning and murder.”93 He read Paul’s desertion concession (1 Corinthians 7:15) as freeing the abandoned believer, treating adultery and desertion together. He drew on Ambrosiaster, whose commentary circulated under Ambrose’s name (Erasmus himself questioned the ascription in 1527), and cited his reading.94 On William A. Heth’s account (1982) the reading was taken up by the Reformers, among them Luther and Calvin (Zwingli and Bucer are also named in this connection, ⚠ unverified), passed into the Westminster Confession, and is known as the “Erasmian view” (the period saying “Erasmus laid the eggs, and Luther hatched them” concerns Erasmus’ relation to the Reformation generally); the Council of Trent’s canon 7 on matrimony (Session 24, 1563) anathematized the claim that the Church errs in teaching otherwise.95

Martin Luther (c. 1522)

The Wittenberg reformer denied that marriage is a sacrament — “we read nowhere that the man who marries a wife receives any grace of God” (Augustine had grounded indissolubility on the sacramentum; see above).96 In The Estate of Marriage he taught that adultery so breaks the bond that “the innocent person may remarry”: the adulterer “has in fact himself already departed and is considered as one dead,” so the other “may remarry just as though his spouse had died.”97 He reached this reluctantly — “I so greatly detest divorce that I should prefer bigamy to it” — holding that Christ “permits divorce, but for the cause of fornication only,” and “seems to permit a man to marry another woman in the stead of the one who has been put away.”98 He added desertion, reading Paul’s “not under bondage” (1 Corinthians 7:15) to “set the believing spouse free to marry again,” required that any divorce permitting remarriage be adjudicated by the civil authority, and allowed mere incompatibility only a separation without remarriage.99

John Calvin (c. 1555)

The Genevan reformer’s exegesis and legislation. In the Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists Calvin reads Matthew’s exception as a dissolution worked by the adultery itself — the unfaithful spouse “by fornication, cuts herself off, as a rotten member, from her husband, and sets him at liberty” — extends the liberty to both sexes on “an equal right” (“when, by committing adultery, he has dissolved the marriage, the wife is set at liberty”), and rejects the reading that would grant only separation and enjoin celibacy on the innocent, “very ill explained by many commentators… As if this liberty of divorce meant only not to lie with his wife.”100 A second ground he takes from Paul: the believer maliciously deserted by an unbeliever is freed, “the first and chief bond… not merely loosed, but even utterly broken through” (1 Corinthians 7:15); but spouses separated short of adultery or desertion “continue to be bound, so that they cannot take other husbands.”101 On Moses’ bill of divorce he follows Christ’s hardness-of-heart reading, an “indulgence” that never overturned the creation ordinance (Deuteronomy 24:1–4); and in Geneva the Ordonnances ecclésiastiques grant divorce with remarriage for adultery and malicious desertion to innocent husbands and wives alike — “qu’on luy ottroye: et par ce moyen qu’il ait puissance de se marier où bon lui semblera,” the wife suing on the same ground since “l’obligation est mutuelle et reciproque.”102

The Council of Trent (1563)

The Catholic council’s decree and canons on matrimony, issued against the Reformers’ positions. Session 24’s doctrine of matrimony states that the “first parent… pronounced the bond of matrimony perpetual and indissoluble, when he said; This now is bone of my bones” — and teaches marriage as a sacrament conferring grace to “confirm that indissoluble union,” the point Luther had denied.103 Canon 7 anathematizes anyone who says the Church errs in teaching “that the bond of matrimony cannot be dissolved on account of the adultery of one of the married parties; and that both, or even the innocent one who gave not occasion to the adultery, cannot contract another marriage, during the life-time of the other.”104 Canon 5 anathematizes the claim that heresy, irksome cohabitation or desertion dissolve the bond, grounds Luther and Calvin had allowed in part. Canon 7 is worded to anathematize not “anyone who dissolves a marriage for adultery” but anyone who says the Church errs in forbidding it; Giancarlo Pani (2014) reports that the Council adopted this construction after the Venetian delegates protested, so as not to condemn the Greek-rite Christians who permit remarriage,105 and Lehmkuhl (1909), citing the Venetian ambassadors’ testimony, writes that “the milder indirect form in which the Council of Trent pronounced its anathema was chosen expressly out of regard for the Greeks of that period.”106

John Milton (1643)

The poet argued for divorce on grounds beyond adultery and desertion. In The Doctrine and Discipline of Divorce (1643, expanded 1644) Milton argued that a settled incompatibility of mind is itself a just ground for divorce: “that indisposition, unfitnes, or contrariety of mind, arising from a cause in nature unchangeable… is a greater reason of divorce then naturall frigidity.”107 His case rests on his account of marriage’s purpose: reading Genesis 2:18 (“It is not good that the man should be alone”), Milton makes companionship, not procreation or the remedy of lust, the chief end — “a meet and happy conversation is the chiefest and the noblest end of mariage” — so a union that fails at that fails at its highest end.108 He reads Christ’s prohibition as aimed at the Pharisees’ hard-hearted, capricious “unbounded licence,” not at “a good mans requisit liberty” to divorce for blameless incompatibility, and takes Moses’ “some uncleanness” (Deuteronomy 24:1) broadly, of any “displeasing natural quality or unfitnes.”109 Matthew’s exception (Matthew 19:9) he reads as illustrative, not exhaustive, widening “fornication” itself to “such a continual headstrong behaviour, as tends to plain contempt of the husband.”110 Where Erasmus, Luther, and Calvin named adultery and desertion, Milton adds incompatibility. A divine preaching before Parliament in 1644 called the tract a “wicked booke” deserving to be burnt.

The Westminster Confession of Faith (1646)

The Westminster Assembly’s confession puts the two grounds of adultery and desertion into a subscribed standard of faith. Chapter 24, “Of Marriage and Divorce” — completed in 1646, its Scripture proofs added the next year — lays down in §5 that adultery after marriage gives the innocent party leave “to sue out a divorce: and, after the divorce, to marry another, as if the offending party were dead,” citing the exception clause of Matthew 5:31–32 and 19:9 (with Romans 7:2–3).111 §6 limits the grounds to two: “nothing but adultery, or such willful desertion as can no way be remedied by the church, or civil magistrate, is cause sufficient of dissolving the bond of marriage” — desertion resting on Paul’s “not under bondage” (1 Corinthians 7:15) — and requires the cause be adjudged by “a public and orderly course of proceeding,” the parties “not left to their own wills, and discretion.”112 The Confession thus grants dissolution and remarriage to the innocent party, against Trent’s canons, and limits the grounds to adultery and desertion, narrower than Milton’s incompatibility. Adopted whole, these divorce sections included, by the Church of Scotland (1647), it became the doctrinal standard of Presbyterianism; the English Parliament’s approval of 1648 omitted the two divorce paragraphs, and the near-identical Savoy Declaration (1658) and Second London Baptist Confession (1689) retitled the chapter “Of Marriage” and dropped them.113

David Instone-Brewer (c. 2002)

A Cambridge rabbinics scholar (Tyndale House) who reads Jesus’ divorce sayings against their first-century Jewish legal setting. Instone-Brewer argues that the Pharisees’ “is it lawful… for any cause?” (Matthew 19:3) is not a general question but a technical reference to the Hillelite “Any Cause” divorce, which, he argues, the school of Hillel had lately read out of Deuteronomy 24:1 by splitting Moses’ “some uncleanness” into two grounds — “indecency” and “a matter” — so that “anything, including a burnt meal… could be a cause.”114 On his reading Jesus’ “resounding no” rejects the Hillelite ground and agrees with the school of Shammai, who restricted the Deuteronomy 24 ground to adultery (“except immorality”) — which, he argues, is why Matthew’s exception clause (Matthew 19:9) states the Shammaite position, and why Mark and Luke, writing for readers unfamiliar with the rabbinic shorthand, could omit it.115 He further argues that Exodus 21:10–11 guaranteed every wife, “even a slave wife,” the rights to food, clothing, and marital love, whose neglect freed the wronged spouse — a ground no school disputed, and so assumed rather than discussed in the Gospel debates. He concludes that Jesus and Paul permit divorce, with remarriage for the innocent party, on the Old Testament grounds of adultery, neglect, and abuse (abandonment being the ultimate neglect, 1 Corinthians 7:15).116 He grounds the case in the rabbinic setting and the Exodus grounds rather than in the exception clause alone.

The Catholic magisterium on divorce: Familiaris Consortio to Amoris Laetitia (1981–2016)

Catholic teaching after Trent. The 1983 Code of Canon Law states that a ratified and consummated marriage “can be dissolved by no human power and by no cause, except death” (can. 1141), and provides for the dissolution of non-sacramental unions through the Pauline and Petrine privileges (cann. 1143–1150).117 The modern discussion concerns pastoral discipline: how the Church treats the civilly divorced and remarried, and whether they may receive communion. John Paul II’s Familiaris Consortio (1981) §84 keeps them within the Church — it “cannot abandon to their own devices” the baptized — yet “reaffirms her practice… of not admitting to Eucharistic Communion divorced persons who have remarried,” since their state “objectively contradict[s]” the union of Christ and the Church, opening the sacraments to those who undertake “to live in complete continence.”118 Francis’s Amoris Laetitia (2016, ch. 8) declines “a new set of general rules” for “responsible personal and pastoral discernment of particular cases,” holds that “it can no longer simply be said that all those in any ‘irregular’ situation are living in a state of mortal sin,” and in footnote 351 allows that the “help of the sacraments” may “in certain cases” be given — a reading Francis endorsed as having “no other interpretations” when the bishops of Buenos Aires drew from it “the possibility of access to the sacraments of Reconciliation and the Eucharist.”119 Four cardinals’ dubia (2016) asked whether this admission breaks with Familiaris Consortio §84, their explanatory note stating that such admission “would seem” to “mean for the Church to teach by her practice” one of three affirmations, among them that “A divorce dissolves the marriage bond”; Rocco Buttiglione (2017) holds that it develops that teaching without contradicting it, leaving the bond’s indissolubility untouched.120

Disputed readings

How three biblical texts on divorce have been read.

  • Why does Matthew alone carry the exception clause? — on whether “except for fornication” was added by Matthew, abbreviated by Mark and Luke, or read otherwise.121
  • Does Mark 10:12 (“if a woman shall put away her husband”) address Jesus’ own Jewish setting, or extend his saying to readers under Roman and Greek law? — on the clause in which the wife divorces her husband, and the divorce law of Jesus’ and Mark’s settings.122
  • Does Malachi 2:16 say that God hates divorce, that a man who hates his wife may send her away, or that the man who hates and divorces covers his garment with violence? — on the Hebrew text and the ancient versions of the verse.123
