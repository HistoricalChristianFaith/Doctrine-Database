Divorce and remarriage — what did Jesus permit?

May a Christian divorce, and if so, may either party marry again? No question in the Gospels has been read more variously than Jesus’ teaching on this, because he speaks to it in four places that do not obviously agree. In the Sermon on the Mount he says, “whosoever shall put away his wife, saving for the cause of fornication, causeth her to commit adultery: and whosoever shall marry her that is divorced committeth adultery” (Matthew 5:31–32). Questioned by the Pharisees, he grounds marriage in creation — “what therefore God hath joined together, let not man put asunder” — says Moses permitted the bill of divorcement only “because of the hardness of your hearts,” and repeats the exception: “whosoever shall put away his wife, except it be for fornication, and shall marry another, committeth adultery” (Matthew 19:3–9). Yet Mark and Luke record the same saying with no exception at all (Mark 10:11–12; Luke 16:18), and Paul adds a case of his own — that a believer deserted by an unbelieving spouse “is not under bondage” (1 Corinthians 7:10–16).

From these texts the church built centuries of dispute, along a few recurring fault lines: what “fornication” (porneia) means in Matthew’s exception, and why Matthew alone has it; whether the exception licenses remarriage or only separation from bed and board; whether the marriage bond is indissoluble while both live (the Latin and, later, Roman Catholic settlement), dissoluble for adultery with liberty to remarry (the Erasmian reading taken up by most Protestants), or dissoluble by pastoral “economy” (the Eastern Orthodox practice); how Jesus’ words engage the first-century rabbinic quarrel between the schools of Shammai and Hillel over Moses’ “some uncleanness” (Deuteronomy 24:1–4); and how all of this squares with the prophet’s “I hate putting away” (Malachi 2:16) and the creation ordinance of “one flesh” (Genesis 2:24). The “belief that developed” traced here is the church’s whole working answer to what Jesus permitted — not a single settled dogma.

Timeline

The Shepherd of Hermas (c. 140)

The earliest post-apostolic treatment. Put to the angelic Shepherd, the case of a man who catches his believing wife in adultery draws a two-part ruling: if she persists unrepentant, the husband must put her away — to go on living with her is to become “a sharer in her adultery” — yet “if he put his wife away and marry another, he also commits adultery.”1 The reason he must “remain by himself” is that the door to reconciliation must stay open: should the divorced wife repent, he is bound to take her back, so “the husband ought not to marry another, when his wife has been put away” — and “man and woman are to be treated exactly in the same way.”2 So the very earliest reader makes divorce for adultery all but obligatory for the innocent party while denying that it dissolves the bond — the “separation, not remarriage” reading of Matthew’s exception is the oldest attested one, not a late Latin invention.3

Justin Martyr (c. 155)

Writing to Antoninus Pius from Rome, Justin lists the divorce saying among the precepts of Christian chastity, in its bare, exceptionless form: “Whosoever shall marry her that is divorced from another husband, committeth adultery.”4 He glosses it as a verdict on remarriage — those who “by human law, are twice married” (the ANF editor notes he means a second spouse taken after divorce) are “in the eye of our Master” adulterers.5 So the second post-apostolic witness, like the first, gives no hint that a divorce frees either party to marry again. In the Second Apology Justin then records the case behind the teaching: a Christian wife at Rome who — lest she “become a partaker” in her debauched husband’s vices “by sharing his table and his bed” — gave him “what you call a bill of divorce, and was separated from him,” the earliest datable notice of a Christian acting on the divorce teaching, and the spark of a martyrdom.6

Athenagoras of Athens (c. 177)

Pleading the Christians’ innocence before Marcus Aurelius, Athenagoras cites the divorce saying itself to prove his people’s chastity, and glosses it into a flat bar on both divorce and any remarriage after it: “Whosoever puts away his wife,” says the Lord, “and marries another, commits adultery” — God “not permitting a man to send her away… nor to marry again.”7 This is the earliest surviving patristic quotation of the divorce logion (Matthew 19:9) as such — Hermas ruled the case without citing it and Justin quoted only its second half — and Athenagoras is the first to supply the first half, the man who initiates the divorce. He too attaches no “except for fornication,” and goes further than either predecessor: God does not permit the man “to send her away” at all, treating the bond as surviving the divorce — indissolubility in its purest early form, stated to a pagan court as evidence, not enacted as law.8

Clement of Alexandria (c. 200)

The first witness on this timeline to quote Matthew’s exception clause — and he reads it as licensing separation, not remarriage. In the chapter “On Marriage” that closes the second book of the Stromata, Clement lays down the rule that “Scripture… allows no release from the union,” adducing “Thou shalt not put away thy wife, except for the cause of fornication” — yet at once he “regards as fornication, the marriage of those separated while the other is alive.”9 So the one saying that seems to open a door, Clement reads as bolting another: the exception permits sending an adulterous wife away, but a new marriage while the first spouse lives is itself the adultery the Lord condemns — “He that taketh a woman that has been put away… committeth adultery; and if one puts away his wife, he makes her an adulteress.” His reason is the open door to reconciliation, the same logic Hermas gave: the second husband is guilty too, “for did he not take her, she would return to her husband.”10 With the exception clause now on the table and still barred from licensing remarriage, the Alexandrian master joins Hermas, Justin, and Athenagoras in the separation-not-remarriage reading.

Tertullian (c. 207)

The fullest early treatment of the divorce question, and the first to defend divorce-for-adultery outright. Answering Marcion’s claim that Christ’s ban on divorce contradicts the Creator’s Moses, Tertullian argues in Against Marcion that Christ did not abolish the Mosaic bill of divorcement (Deuteronomy 24:1) but limited it: the prohibition is conditional, aimed at the man who dismisses his wife “for the express purpose of marrying another,” so that “divorce, therefore, when justly deserved, has even in Christ a defender” — “He permits divorce when the marriage is spotted with unfaithfulness.”11 Christ, on this reading, “exculpated rather than abolished the enactment of Moses,” restoring the creation ordinance the concession had bent “because of the hardness of your hearts”; yet the bond is not dissolved, for “to marry… whilst matrimony is undissolved, is to commit adultery.”12 In the later, rigorist On Monogamy he presses the same doctrine to its hardest edge, permitting divorce “to no cause but one” and denying the justly-divorced any remarriage: “to us, even if we do divorce them, even marriage will not be lawful.”13 So Tertullian states, earlier and more sharply than anyone before him, both halves of what became the Latin settlement — separation for adultery is licit, remarriage while the spouse lives is not.

Origen of Alexandria (c. 246)

The fullest early Greek exegesis of the divorce sayings, and the first witness here to report a gap between the church’s written rule and its actual practice. Expounding “is it lawful to put away a wife for every cause?” (Matthew 19:3–12) in the fourteenth book of his Commentary on Matthew, Origen classes Moses’ bill of divorcement among “some laws… written not as excellent, but as by way of accommodation to the weakness” — the hardness-of-heart concession — and holds that Christ permits dissolution “for… fornication alone.”14 But he then records, with disapproval yet notable understanding, that “some even of the rulers of the church have permitted a woman to marry, even when her husband was living… not indeed altogether without reason, for it is probable this concession was permitted in comparison with worse things.”15 It is the earliest surviving notice of church leaders tolerating remarriage after divorce — the seed of the later Eastern practice of pastoral “economy” (oikonomia) — set down two centuries before the canons that would formalize it. Origen calls the permission “contrary to what was written,” yet concedes its motive; and, uniquely among the early witnesses, he airs the strict reading’s cost, asking whether a wife guilty of “poisoning, or… any form of murder” may not equally be put away — a dilemma in which “there appears to be something monstrous” either way, which he leaves unresolved.16

The Council of Elvira (c. 306)

The earliest surviving Western conciliar canon law, and the first point on this timeline where a church council — not a single teacher — legislates on divorce. Meeting at Elvira in southern Spain, nineteen bishops issued three canons (8, 9, 10) against the woman who leaves a husband and marries again. The mildest bars her from communion until the deserted spouse dies; the gravest — the wife who leaves “without acceptable cause” and remarries — withholds communion “even when death approaches.”17 The decisive canon is the ninth: even a baptized wife who leaves an adulterous husband — the one party the earlier writers granted a right to separate — “may not marry” another while he lives; if she does, she is barred “until her former husband dies.”18 So the “separation, not remarriage” reading taught by Hermas, Clement, and Tertullian now becomes enforceable law: the bond survives the divorce, and the penalty falls on the second marriage, lifting only at the first spouse’s death. Eight years later the Gallic Council of Arles (314), under Constantine, met the mirror case — a young husband whose wife commits adultery — and reached the same verdict by a softer instrument: not a penalty but a counsel, “as much as is able,” that he “not take other wives while their own wives are still living, even if the latter are adulterous.”19 Between them, Elvira and Arles are the earliest conciliar canon law on divorce in the Latin church.

Basil of Caesarea (c. 375)

The fullest procedural settlement of the fourth-century East, and the first witness here to legislate a frank double standard — and to admit it rests on custom, not Scripture. In three canonical letters to Amphilochius of Iconium (Letters 188, 199, 217), later given conciliar force in the Byzantine church, Basil grants that “the sentence of the Lord that it is unlawful to withdraw from wedlock, save on account of fornication… applies… to men and women alike” — then at once concedes, “Custom, however, does not so obtain.”20 The custom he codifies is markedly harsher on the wife: a married man who falls into fornication is punished as a fornicator but not charged with adultery, and “the wife will receive the husband on his return from fornication,” whereas “the husband will expel the polluted woman from his house” — “the argument here is not easy, but the custom has so obtained.”21 A wife may not leave even an adulterous husband; the man deserted by his wife is “deserving of pardon,” but the woman abandoned by her husband “ought… to remain as she is,” barred from remarriage by the Lord’s own word (Matthew 5:32) — “by calling her adulteress, He excludes her from intercourse with another man.”22 So Basil holds the strict, separation-not-remarriage reading of the exception clause taught by Hermas, Clement, and Tertullian, and fixes a seven-year penance for deserting a lawful spouse — but applies it unequally, and, uniquely candid, twice flags that the inequality is what “custom” ordains rather than what the Gospel requires. That self-aware gap between the Lord’s rule and the church’s practice — first reported by Origen — is the seed of the later Eastern settlement of pastoral “economy” (oikonomia).

Ambrosiaster (c. 375)

The most permissive early Latin voice on this timeline — and a frankly unequal one. Expounding Paul’s divorce charge (1 Corinthians 7:10–11), the anonymous Roman commentator known as Ambrosiaster fastens on the fact that Paul tells the separated woman to “remain single” but adds no such words to the man, and reads the silence as a licence: “it is lawful for a man to marry another woman, if he divorces a wife who sins. For a man is not bound by the law in the same way as a woman is, for the man is the head of the woman.”23 Alone among the early Latin fathers he thus reads Matthew’s exception clause as licensing not merely separation but a fresh marriage — for the innocent husband, treating an adulterous wife’s sin as a definitive breaking of the bond, exactly as Roman law did. The wife has no such right: even one who leaves an adulterous or apostate husband “does not enjoy the same rights as the man, who is the superior,” and “can neither marry another man nor return to her husband.”24 On the deserting unbeliever (1 Corinthians 7:15) he holds the abandoned union no longer binding — “it is not a sin if it is abandoned because of God” — the earliest clear Latin statement of the later “Pauline privilege.”25 So against the developing indissolubility consensus — Clement, Tertullian, Elvira, Basil, all barring remarriage while the first spouse lives — Ambrosiaster stands as the strongest patristic Latin support for divorce with remarriage, a Western outlier and a named forerunner of Erasmus. His double standard rests, as David Hunter shows, less on the Gospel than on Roman law and his own theology of male headship.26

Epiphanius of Salamis (c. 375)

The most openly lenient voice on this timeline — and, surprisingly, a fierce heresy-hunter’s. In section 59 of the Panarion Epiphanius, bishop of Salamis in Cyprus, refutes the rigorist Novatianist “Purists” (Cathari), who barred the “twice-married” from communion — “for if someone marries a second wife after baptism, they never admit him again.”27 Against them he lays down the Eastern principle of concession to weakness, and — uniquely explicit among the early witnesses — folds remarriage after divorce into it alongside remarriage after bereavement: “if there has been a divorce for some reason — fornication, adultery or something else — and the man marries a second wife or the woman a second husband, God’s word does not censure them or bar them from the church and life, but tolerates them because of their weakness.”28 The concession is carefully bounded: it is no licence for bigamy — “not by letting him have two wives at once while the one is still alive” — and no commendation, since the once-married is “more highly respected”; the second marriage is borne with as a mercy to human frailty, “the first wife a divine ordinance, the second a concession to human weakness.”29 So where Origen only reported the church tolerating remarriage-while-the-spouse-lives (and disapproved), and Basil legislated a double standard he traced to bare “custom,” Epiphanius positively defends the concession as the mind of “God’s holy church” — the clearest patristic seed of the later Eastern discipline of pastoral “economy” (oikonomia).

John Chrysostom (c. 390)

The great Greek preacher of strict indissolubility, and the fullest Eastern statement that the bond simply survives the divorce. Expounding the Sermon on the Mount’s divorce saying (Matthew 5:31–32) in his Homilies on Matthew at Antioch, Chrysostom presses the guilt onto both men in the case: the husband who dismisses his wife “though he take not another wife, by that act alone hath made himself liable to blame, having made the first an adulteress; the latter again is become an adulterer by taking her who is another’s.”30 The reason the second marriage is adultery is the single clause on which his whole doctrine rests — divorce does not end the marriage: “tell me not this, ‘the other hath cast her out;’ nay, for when cast out she continues to be the wife of him that expelled her,” so Christ “hath shut against her also the doors of him who was afterwards receiving her.” The one lawful ground for sending a wife away is fornication, and even that licenses only dismissal, not remarriage.31 On Paul’s own charge (1 Corinthians 7:10–11) he reaches the same verdict: the separated wife must “remain unmarried, or be reconciled unto her husband” — never “introduce any other to be her husband.”32 So against the permissive Latin reading of his near-contemporary Ambrosiaster and the concession-to-weakness defended by Epiphanius, Chrysostom joins Hermas, Clement, Tertullian, and Basil in the separation-not-remarriage reading — and, as the most influential Greek voice for it, becomes the standard against which the later Byzantine relaxation into pastoral “economy” (oikonomia) is measured.

Jerome (c. 394)

The strictest Latin voice on this timeline, and the scholar who fixes indissolubility as the Western rule. Across three works of the 390s — a letter of casuistry to the presbyter Amandus (c. 394), the Commentary on Matthew (398), and the eulogy of the Roman noblewoman Fabiola (399) — Jerome, the Vulgate translator, reads Matthew’s exception as licensing only the dismissal of an adulterous spouse, never a new marriage while the first partner lives. To Amandus, who had asked whether a woman who left an adulterous, sodomite husband and remarried under compulsion might commune without penance, he answers with a flat negative: “A husband may be an adulterer or a sodomite… yet he is still her husband and, so long as he lives, she may not marry another,” and the “second husband… ought to be called not a husband but an adulterer.”33 The Commentary on Matthew reads the exception clause (Matthew 19:9) as narrowly as anyone here — fornication alone severs the “one flesh” and frees the husband to send the wife away, but for that very reason “the husband is commanded to divorce the first wife so that he does not have a second wife while the first one is still alive.”34 Unlike his fellow Latin Ambrosiaster, who rested a permissive double standard on male headship, Jerome argues the bar from the equality of the sexes before God: “a commandment which is given to men logically applies to women also… what is unlawful for women is equally unlawful for men.” The Fabiola eulogy shows the rule tested on a real, pitiable life — Jerome admired her, calls her remarriage “a fault… at the same time… a case of necessity,” yet still refuses to call it lawful, and paints the vivid scene of her public penance “in the presence of all Rome” before her restoration to communion after the second husband’s death.35 So Jerome joins Hermas, Clement, Tertullian, Basil, and Chrysostom in the separation-not-remarriage reading, and gives it its most authoritative Latin statement on the eve of Augustine.

Augustine of Hippo (c. 419)

The theologian who gives the Latin West its decisive doctrine of indissolubility — not by changing the strict verdict of Tertullian, Elvira, Chrysostom, and Jerome, but by grounding it. In four works across a quarter-century — the exposition of Matthew 5:31–32 in On the Sermon on the Mount (c. 393–396), On the Good of Marriage (c. 401), On Marriage and Concupiscence (c. 419–421), and the two books On Adulterous Marriages to Pollentius (c. 419) — Augustine, bishop of Hippo, makes the marriage bond a sacramentum, a sacred and permanent sign of the union of Christ and the Church, which therefore “can no way be dissolved but by the death of one of them.”36 From that sacramental character the whole rule follows: the exception clause licenses only the dismissal of an adulterous spouse, never a new marriage while the first lives, and the bar binds husband and wife alike — “it is unlawful for one who leaves her husband, even when she has been put away, to be married to another, so long as her husband lives.”37 The sacramental bond, like the seal of baptism, “is lost neither by divorce nor by adultery”; even Moses’ bill was a hardness-of-heart concession the gospel withdraws, so that under Christ remarriage after divorce “is held to be guilty of adultery.”38 Uniquely among the witnesses, Augustine reaches this by open exegetical labour rather than assertion: answering Pollentius — who read Matthew’s exception as freeing an innocent husband to remarry — he governs Matthew by the unqualified Mark 10:11–12 and Luke 16:18, concedes that the gospel words are obscure and the question not fully solved, yet concludes that “any union with other persons while their first husband or wife is alive is not marriage but adultery.”39 This sacramentum synthesis, not merely the verdict it defends, is what becomes the settled Latin and later Roman Catholic doctrine, the framework the medieval canonists and the Council of Trent inherit.

The Council in Trullo (692)

The Eastern counterpart to the Western crystallization — the council that gives the Greek church its settled conciliar law on divorce, and the point where the tolerant strand Origen first noticed reaches conciliar form. Meeting “in Trullo” at Constantinople under Justinian II to supply the disciplinary canons the fifth and sixth ecumenical councils had omitted, the Quinisext Council legislates the divorce question in three canons. Canon 87, invoking “the holy and divine Basil” (whose canons canon 2 had just ratified as binding law), confirms the strict verdict of the whole timeline — “he who leaves the wife lawfully given him, and shall take another is guilty of adultery by the sentence of the Lord” — but heals it with the East’s graded penance (“weepers for a year, hearers for two years, prostrators for three years”) rather than Elvira’s permanent exclusion.40 Canon 93 then weighs the hard cases of the “grass widow” — the wife of a vanished or soldier husband — and, while still calling a premature remarriage adultery, grants that the case “has some excuse,” sorts culpable from ignorant second unions (the woman deceived “is not prevented from marrying”), and extends “pardon”; the returning soldier may yet “receive his own wife,” the first bond never having dissolved.41 Canon 98 brings the betrothed woman under the same rule.42 So Trullo confirms indissolubility for the East with the same verdict as the Latins — the second marriage is adultery, the bond survives the divorce — but by a gentler instrument, blending (canon 3) Rome’s “exact perfection” with Constantinople’s “kindness and consideration” so “that neither gentleness fall into licence, nor severity into harshness.” It does not yet license divorce-and-remarriage — the full Byzantine practice of dissolution by pastoral “economy” (oikonomia) develops later, in imperial civil law and the commentators — but its machinery of graded penance, weighed culpability, and pardon is the canonical seedbed that relaxation grows from. (Trullo’s separate handling of remarriage after bereavement is traced on the remarriage timeline.)

Erasmus (c. 1519)

The scholar who reopened divorce-with-remarriage in the Latin West, and the hinge between the patristic–medieval settlement traced above and the Protestant divergence. Eight centuries separate the Dutch humanist from Trullo, and in that interval the Western church had hardened Augustine’s sacramentum into settled canon law; Erasmus, editor of the first printed critical Greek New Testament (1516), broke with it. In his Greek text he set Matthew’s exception (Matthew 19:9) to read as an unambiguous exceptive clause — “except for fornication” — and glossed it so that the adulteress, by her sin, “has stopped being his wife,” freeing the innocent husband to marry again.43 The fuller case came in a treatise-length note attached from the 1519 edition of his Annotations to Paul’s word on the widow “free to be married to whom she will” (1 Corinthians 7:39): there Erasmus argued two propositions his contemporaries judged heretical — that the church could dissolve certain marriages for grave cause, through its own authorities or judges, and that the innocent party could then remarry — urging, as much pastorally as exegetically, that to bind spouses who have come to detest one another is not mercy but a danger — breeding “mutual quarrels, irremediable hatred, dread of poisoning and murder.”44 He read Paul’s desertion concession (1 Corinthians 7:15) as freeing the abandoned believer, pressing adultery and desertion together — the two grounds the Reformers would fix on. Against the near-unanimous fathers he had one ancient ally, the lone permissive Latin voice of Ambrosiaster (whom he knew, like everyone before the nineteenth century, under the name of Ambrose), whose reading he revived.45 Taken up by Luther, Zwingli, Bucer, and Calvin — “Erasmus laid the eggs, and Luther hatched them” — and passed through the Reformers into the Westminster Confession (1648), this became the “Erasmian view,” the majority Protestant position; the Council of Trent (Session 24, 1563) framed its carefully worded canons on marriage with exactly this claim in view.46

Martin Luther (c. 1522)

The first magisterial Reformer to turn the reopened Erasmian reading into the working divorce discipline of a church. Luther struck at the root of the Latin doctrine by denying that marriage is a sacrament at all — “we read nowhere that the man who marries a wife receives any grace of God” — dissolving the sacramentum on which Augustine had grounded indissolubility.47 On the question itself he taught, in The Estate of Marriage, that adultery so breaks the bond that “the innocent person may remarry”: the adulterer “has in fact himself already departed and is considered as one dead,” so the other “may remarry just as though his spouse had died.”48 He reached this reluctantly — “I so greatly detest divorce that I should prefer bigamy to it” — yet held that Christ “permits divorce, but for the cause of fornication only,” and “seems to permit a man to marry another woman in the stead of the one who has been put away.”49 Beyond adultery he added desertion, reading Paul’s “not under bondage” (1 Corinthians 7:15) to “set the believing spouse free to marry again,” while requiring that any remarriage-granting divorce be adjudicated by the civil authority and allowing mere incompatibility only a separation without remarriage.50 So against the strict line from Hermas through Jerome, Luther — building on Erasmus and the lone Latin dissent of Ambrosiaster before him — makes adultery and desertion dissolve the bond and free the innocent to marry again, the twin grounds the later Protestant confessions fix on.

John Calvin (c. 1555)

The reformer who gave the Erasmian–Lutheran reading its most careful exegesis and turned it into enforceable law. In the Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists Calvin reads Matthew’s exception as a real dissolution worked by the adultery itself — the unfaithful spouse “by fornication, cuts herself off, as a rotten member, from her husband, and sets him at liberty” — extends the liberty to both sexes on “an equal right” (“when, by committing adultery, he has dissolved the marriage, the wife is set at liberty”), and attacks by name the strict reading that would grant only separation and enjoin celibacy on the innocent, “very ill explained by many commentators… As if this liberty of divorce meant only not to lie with his wife.”51 A second ground he takes from Paul: the believer maliciously deserted by an unbeliever is freed, “the first and chief bond… not merely loosed, but even utterly broken through” (1 Corinthians 7:15); but mere quarrels dissolve nothing — spouses separated short of adultery or desertion “continue to be bound, so that they cannot take other husbands.”52 On Moses’ bill of divorce he follows Christ’s hardness-of-heart reading, an “indulgence” that never overturned the creation ordinance (Deuteronomy 24:1–4); and in Geneva his principles passed into the Marriage Ordinance, reported — though not yet sourced here from the ordinance text — to have granted divorce with remarriage for adultery and malicious desertion to innocent husbands and wives alike.53 With Calvin the exception-permits-remarriage reading, revived by Erasmus and hatched by Luther, becomes the settled Reformed position that flows into the Westminster Confession.

The Council of Trent (1563)

The definitive Roman Catholic dogmatic close of the question against the Reformers, and the Tridentine crystallization the whole strict line had been moving toward. Session 24’s doctrine of matrimony restates the creation ordinance the timeline began from — the “first parent… pronounced the bond of matrimony perpetual and indissoluble, when he said; This now is bone of my bones” — and reaffirms marriage as a sacrament conferring grace to “confirm that indissoluble union,” the very premise Luther had denied.54 The decisive canon 7 then fixes as dogma the separation-not-remarriage reading of Chrysostom, Jerome, and Augustine, anathematizing the Erasmian reading point by point: it is error to say the church errs in teaching “that the bond of matrimony cannot be dissolved on account of the adultery of one of the married parties; and that both, or even the innocent one who gave not occasion to the adultery, cannot contract another marriage, during the life-time of the other.”55 A neighbouring canon (5) closes the desertion-and-incompatibility grounds Luther and Calvin had allowed. Yet the drafting is famously careful: canon 7 anathematizes not “anyone who dissolves a marriage for adultery” but anyone who says the church errs in forbidding it — a construction the Council adopted, after the Venetian delegates protested, precisely so as not to condemn the Eastern-rite Christians who permit remarriage by pastoral “economy” (oikonomia).56 So the West’s dogmatic close reaffirms the strict verdict of Hermas through Augustine against the Reformers — while leaving, in its very wording, a studied silence toward the tolerant Eastern strand that Origen first noticed and the Council in Trullo shaped into graded penance.

David Instone-Brewer (c. 2002)

The modern anchor of this timeline — a Cambridge rabbinics scholar who reads Jesus’ divorce sayings against their first-century Jewish legal setting and reorganizes the whole debate around it. Instone-Brewer argues that the Pharisees’ “is it lawful… for any cause?” (Matthew 19:3) is not a general question but a technical reference to the Hillelite “Any Cause” divorce, an innovation the school of Hillel had lately read out of Deuteronomy 24:1 by splitting Moses’ “some uncleanness” into two grounds — “indecency” and “a matter” — so that “anything, including a burnt meal… could be a cause.”57 On this reading Jesus’ “resounding no” rejects the Hillelite loophole and sides with the rival school of Shammai, who restricted the Deut-24 ground to adultery alone (“except immorality”) — which is why Matthew’s exception clause (Matthew 19:9) states the Shammaite position in its own words, and why Mark and Luke, writing for readers ignorant of the rabbinic shorthand, could drop it.58 His more distinctive move is that Deuteronomy 24 was never the whole law: Exodus 21:10–11 guaranteed every wife, “even a slave wife,” the rights to food, clothing, and marital love, whose neglect freed the wronged spouse — an uncontested ground no school disputed, so absent from the Gospel debates but assumed by them. The upshot is that Jesus and Paul permit divorce, with remarriage for the innocent party, on the Old-Testament grounds of adultery, neglect, and abuse (abandonment being the ultimate neglect, 1 Corinthians 7:15).59 So Instone-Brewer stands in the divorce-with-remarriage line reopened by Erasmus and fixed by Luther and Calvin, but reaches it by a different route and widens it — grounding the case in the rabbinic frame and the Exodus grounds rather than the exception clause alone; on the disputed Deut-24 point his verdict (adultery only) coincides with the strict Shammaite–patristic reading of Chrysostom and Jerome. Whether the “Any Cause” reconstruction and the Exodus-21 grounds bear the weight he puts on them is weighed under Arguments.

Later witnesses are being added, earliest first — from the New Testament texts themselves, through the medieval and Tridentine crystallization (the Council of Trent’s carefully worded Session 24), to the modern rabbinic reframing of Instone-Brewer. Contested sub-questions — the meaning of the exception clause, whether it licenses remarriage, the Pauline privilege, and the Shammai/Hillel background — are weighed under Arguments below.

Arguments

The off-timeline sub-claims about Jesus’ divorce teaching — why Matthew alone carries the exception, whether the exception permits remarriage or only separation, the scope of the Pauline privilege, and how the sayings engage the Shammai–Hillel dispute — are being worked as argument pages and will be surfaced here, each with its assessment. The cluster on the meaning of the exception itself comes first, followed by the standalone questions of why Matthew alone carries the clause and whether it licenses remarriage or only separation.

Related arguments — the exception clause

What is the “fornication” (porneia) that Matthew’s Jesus makes the one exception (Matthew 5:32; 19:9)? Four readings have been independently advanced, weighed here as a set: the majority reading that it is a wife’s sexual unfaithfulness within marriage; and three rivals that each argue the plain sense cannot stand and must be displaced — an incestuous or otherwise unlawful marriage within the forbidden degrees, a betrothed girl’s premarital unchastity, and the broad “spiritual fornication” of idolatry. Only the first holds without strain; of the rivals, the incest reading is genuinely contested on first-century evidence, while the betrothal and spiritual readings do not hold on their best case.

Does porneia mean a wife’s marital unfaithfulness? (assessment: sound)

Does porneia in the exception clause mean a wife’s marital unfaithfulness? — the plain and majority reading, glossed as adultery by nearly every early witness (Tertullian renders the clause “except on the ground of adultery”; Chrysostom, Jerome, Theophylact, and Apollinaris the same) and held by modern critical commentary. The one puzzle it must answer — why Matthew wrote the wider porneia rather than the exact word for adultery (moicheia), which he lists as a distinct sin at Matthew 15:19 — is met by taking porneia as the broader genus of sexual immorality of which the wife’s adultery is the paradigm, not a bare synonym for it. Word and context (a man and his wife, in the Deuteronomy-24 frame) point one way. Sound.60

Does porneia mean an unlawful (incestuous) marriage? (assessment: contested)

Does porneia mean an unlawful (incestuous) marriage within the forbidden degrees? — the reading of Joseph Fitzmyer and much Catholic exegesis, on which the exception recognizes a Leviticus-18 kinship union that was void from the start (annulment, not divorce), leaving Jesus’ prohibition intact. Its lexical foundation is real: the Damascus Document at Qumran uses the Hebrew zĕnût (which the Greek Old Testament renders porneia) precisely of forbidden-degree marriage, and the Acts 15 decree plausibly does the same. But nothing in Matthew’s context signals the incest-sense, it is borrowed from a differently-aimed decree, and the plain reading explains the passage with fewer moving parts — strong by the Palestinian-Jewish evidence, weak on the fit to this text. Contested.61

Does porneia mean betrothal unchastity? (assessment: weak)

Does porneia mean betrothal unchastity (the betrothal view)? — Abel Isaksson’s and John Piper’s reading, on which Jesus permits only the breaking of a legally-binding betrothal on discovery of unchastity (as Joseph contemplated with the betrothed Mary, Matthew 1:19, using the same verb “divorce”), forbidding all divorce of a consummated marriage and so agreeing exactly with the absolute Mark and Luke. The linguistic hook is genuine, but Matthew 19 is framed from start to finish around a man putting away his wife, and the reading survives only by giving porneia a narrow betrothal-sense it is nowhere else shown to bear. Weak.62

Does porneia extend to spiritual fornication? (assessment: weak)

Does porneia extend to spiritual fornication (idolatry, apostasy)? — Augustine’s “latitude of meaning” reading, taken up by Aquinas, on which the “fornication” of the exception is the broad biblical harlotry of idolatry and unbelief, so a spouse’s apostasy could ground dismissal. The metaphor is scripturally real (the prophets’ “playing the harlot”), but the chain proves too much — if every sin is “fornication” the exception swallows the rule it exists to narrow — and Augustine himself retracted it, conceding it is “no question at all” that the ground is carnal unfaithfulness. Weak.63

Why does Matthew alone carry the exception clause? (assessment: contested)

Why does Matthew alone carry the exception clause? — Mark, Luke, and Paul all give the divorce saying with no exception; only Matthew adds “except for fornication,” and does it twice. Three explanations compete. That Matthew added the clause to soften an originally absolute prohibition is the majority verdict of critical commentary, on the transcriptional argument that one evangelist softening a saying is easier to explain than three independent witnesses each hardening a qualified one — hardest of all against Paul, who reports the Lord’s charge directly and still adds no exception. That Mark and Luke abbreviated a qualification everyone in the Jewish debate assumed — where adultery was uncontested as grounds and Mark demonstrably re-tooled the saying for Gentiles (Mark 10:12) — is well-grounded on the rabbinic and historical-Jesus evidence but does not overturn the redactional majority. That Matthew’s clause is “preteritive” and himself absolute is Vawter’s largely-abandoned harmonization. The field is genuinely divided, and the source question underdetermines the doctrine: whether the phrase is Matthew’s and whether Jesus permitted divorce for unfaithfulness come apart. Contested.64

Does the exception permit remarriage, or only separation? (assessment: contested)

Does the exception permit remarriage, or only separation? — the central disputed question of the whole doctrine. Grant that Jesus permits putting away a wife for porneia: does the exception dissolve the bond so the innocent party may marry again (the reading of Ambrosiaster, Erasmus, and the Protestant mainstream), or license only separation with the bond intact and no remarriage (the patristic consensus from Hermas to Augustine, fixed as dogma by Trent)? The crux is the Greek syntax of Matthew 19:9: does “except for fornication,” sitting between “puts away” and “marries another,” qualify the divorce alone (so remarriage stays adultery — Heth and Wenham’s adverbial-ellipsis argument) or the whole divorce-and-remarry conditional (so the innocent may remarry — Carson’s “govern the entire protasis”)? The grammar is genuinely ambiguous and cannot settle it, so the verdict turns on whether one weights Matthew’s single sentence or the three exceptionless parallels (Mark, Luke, Paul) and the church’s reception — a deadlock marked by Heth himself later changing sides. Kept distinct from dissolution by pastoral “economy” (a mechanism of practice, not a claim about the text). Contested.65

The Pauline privilege — does desertion free the believer to remarry? (assessment: contested)

The Pauline privilege — does desertion (1 Corinthians 7:15) free the believer to remarry? — Paul’s second ground, added on his own authority beyond Matthew’s porneia: when an unbelieving spouse departs, the believer “is not under bondage” (ou dedoulōtai, 1 Corinthians 7:15). Does this dissolve the marriage and free the believer to marry again (the “Pauline privilege” of Ambrosiaster, the Reformers, the Westminster Confession, and Roman canon law — canon 1143), or only release the believer from the obligation to preserve the marriage, with no right to remarry (the Greek fathers — Chrysostom, Theophylact, Theodoret — and modern critical exegesis in Fee and Robertson–Plummer)? The load-bearing word “not enslaved” underdetermines it, and the reception splits along the same fault line as Matthew’s exception. It is the same dissolution question reached by a different proof-text (see the remarriage-or-separation argument), and it raises whether Jesus’ grounds were meant to be exhaustive. Contested.66

Is Jesus siding with Shammai against Hillel’s “any cause” divorce? (assessment: contested)

Is Jesus siding with Shammai against Hillel’s “any cause” divorce? — David Instone-Brewer’s reframing, on which the Pharisees’ “for every cause?” (Matthew 19:3) quotes the technical name of the Hillelite “Any Cause” divorce — a procedure the school of Hillel read out of Moses’ “some uncleanness” (‘erwat dabar, Deuteronomy 24:1) by splitting the phrase into two grounds — so that Jesus’ “no” rejects the loophole and sides with Shammai (divorce for sexual sin only). The frame is real and, at the level that matters, first-century: the quarrel is preserved verbatim (m. Gittin 9:10 — Shammai “a matter of forbidden intercourse,” Hillel “even… because she burned his dish”), and the permissive practice is attested in Jesus’ own century by Philo and Josephus (“for any cause whatsoever”), not merely retrojected from the later rabbis. But the strong form over-reaches: that “Any Cause” was a fixed technical name by AD 30 is inferred from sources redacted c. 200; the claim that “all divorces then” were of that one type strains Mark, Luke, and Paul; and Jesus’ appeal behind Moses to creation arguably transcends the school-dispute rather than joining it. Strong by rabbinic and Second-Temple lights, weaker on the technical-term and “took-a-side” claims. Contested.67
