Divorce and remarriage — what did Jesus permit?

May a Christian divorce, and if so, may either party marry again? No question in the Gospels has been read more variously than Jesus’ teaching on this, because he speaks to it in four places that do not obviously agree. In the Sermon on the Mount he says, “whosoever shall put away his wife, saving for the cause of fornication, causeth her to commit adultery: and whosoever shall marry her that is divorced committeth adultery” (Matthew 5:31–32). Questioned by the Pharisees, he grounds marriage in creation — “what therefore God hath joined together, let not man put asunder” — says Moses permitted the bill of divorcement only “because of the hardness of your hearts,” and repeats the exception: “whosoever shall put away his wife, except it be for fornication, and shall marry another, committeth adultery” (Matthew 19:3–9). Yet Mark and Luke record the same saying with no exception at all (Mark 10:11–12; Luke 16:18), and Paul adds a case of his own — that a believer deserted by an unbelieving spouse “is not under bondage” (1 Corinthians 7:10–16).

From these texts the church built centuries of dispute, along a few recurring fault lines: what “fornication” (porneia) means in Matthew’s exception, and why Matthew alone has it; whether the exception licenses remarriage or only separation from bed and board; whether the marriage bond is indissoluble while both live (the Latin and, later, Roman Catholic settlement), dissoluble for adultery with liberty to remarry (the Erasmian reading taken up by most Protestants), or dissoluble by pastoral “economy” (the Eastern Orthodox practice); how Jesus’ words engage the first-century rabbinic quarrel between the schools of Shammai and Hillel over Moses’ “some uncleanness” (Deuteronomy 24:1–4); and how all of this squares with the prophet’s “I hate putting away” (Malachi 2:16) and the creation ordinance of “one flesh” (Genesis 2:24). The “belief that developed” traced here is the church’s whole working answer to what Jesus permitted — not a single settled dogma.

Timeline

The Shepherd of Hermas (c. 140)

The earliest post-apostolic treatment. Put to the angelic Shepherd, the case of a man who catches his believing wife in adultery draws a two-part ruling: if she persists unrepentant, the husband must put her away — to go on living with her is to become “a sharer in her adultery” — yet “if he put his wife away and marry another, he also commits adultery.”1 The reason he must “remain by himself” is that the door to reconciliation must stay open: should the divorced wife repent, he is bound to take her back, so “the husband ought not to marry another, when his wife has been put away” — and “man and woman are to be treated exactly in the same way.”2 So the very earliest reader makes divorce for adultery all but obligatory for the innocent party while denying that it dissolves the bond — the “separation, not remarriage” reading of Matthew’s exception is the oldest attested one, not a late Latin invention.3

Justin Martyr (c. 155)

Writing to Antoninus Pius from Rome, Justin lists the divorce saying among the precepts of Christian chastity, in its bare, exceptionless form: “Whosoever shall marry her that is divorced from another husband, committeth adultery.”4 He glosses it as a verdict on remarriage — those who “by human law, are twice married” (the ANF editor notes he means a second spouse taken after divorce) are “in the eye of our Master” adulterers.5 So the second post-apostolic witness, like the first, gives no hint that a divorce frees either party to marry again. In the Second Apology Justin then records the case behind the teaching: a Christian wife at Rome who — lest she “become a partaker” in her debauched husband’s vices “by sharing his table and his bed” — gave him “what you call a bill of divorce, and was separated from him,” the earliest datable notice of a Christian acting on the divorce teaching, and the spark of a martyrdom.6

Athenagoras of Athens (c. 177)

Pleading the Christians’ innocence before Marcus Aurelius, Athenagoras cites the divorce saying itself to prove his people’s chastity, and glosses it into a flat bar on both divorce and any remarriage after it: “Whosoever puts away his wife,” says the Lord, “and marries another, commits adultery” — God “not permitting a man to send her away… nor to marry again.”7 This is the earliest surviving patristic quotation of the divorce logion (Matthew 19:9) as such — Hermas ruled the case without citing it and Justin quoted only its second half — and Athenagoras is the first to supply the first half, the man who initiates the divorce. He too attaches no “except for fornication,” and goes further than either predecessor: God does not permit the man “to send her away” at all, treating the bond as surviving the divorce — indissolubility in its purest early form, stated to a pagan court as evidence, not enacted as law.8

Clement of Alexandria (c. 200)

The first witness on this timeline to quote Matthew’s exception clause — and he reads it as licensing separation, not remarriage. In the chapter “On Marriage” that closes the second book of the Stromata, Clement lays down the rule that “Scripture… allows no release from the union,” adducing “Thou shalt not put away thy wife, except for the cause of fornication” — yet at once he “regards as fornication, the marriage of those separated while the other is alive.”9 So the one saying that seems to open a door, Clement reads as bolting another: the exception permits sending an adulterous wife away, but a new marriage while the first spouse lives is itself the adultery the Lord condemns — “He that taketh a woman that has been put away… committeth adultery; and if one puts away his wife, he makes her an adulteress.” His reason is the open door to reconciliation, the same logic Hermas gave: the second husband is guilty too, “for did he not take her, she would return to her husband.”10 With the exception clause now on the table and still barred from licensing remarriage, the Alexandrian master joins Hermas, Justin, and Athenagoras in the separation-not-remarriage reading.

Tertullian (c. 207)

The fullest early treatment of the divorce question, and the first to defend divorce-for-adultery outright. Answering Marcion’s claim that Christ’s ban on divorce contradicts the Creator’s Moses, Tertullian argues in Against Marcion that Christ did not abolish the Mosaic bill of divorcement (Deuteronomy 24:1) but limited it: the prohibition is conditional, aimed at the man who dismisses his wife “for the express purpose of marrying another,” so that “divorce, therefore, when justly deserved, has even in Christ a defender” — “He permits divorce when the marriage is spotted with unfaithfulness.”11 Christ, on this reading, “exculpated rather than abolished the enactment of Moses,” restoring the creation ordinance the concession had bent “because of the hardness of your hearts”; yet the bond is not dissolved, for “to marry… whilst matrimony is undissolved, is to commit adultery.”12 In the later, rigorist On Monogamy he presses the same doctrine to its hardest edge, permitting divorce “to no cause but one” and denying the justly-divorced any remarriage: “to us, even if we do divorce them, even marriage will not be lawful.”13 So Tertullian states, earlier and more sharply than anyone before him, both halves of what became the Latin settlement — separation for adultery is licit, remarriage while the spouse lives is not.

Origen of Alexandria (c. 246)

The fullest early Greek exegesis of the divorce sayings, and the first witness here to report a gap between the church’s written rule and its actual practice. Expounding “is it lawful to put away a wife for every cause?” (Matthew 19:3–12) in the fourteenth book of his Commentary on Matthew, Origen classes Moses’ bill of divorcement among “some laws… written not as excellent, but as by way of accommodation to the weakness” — the hardness-of-heart concession — and holds that Christ permits dissolution “for… fornication alone.”14 But he then records, with disapproval yet notable understanding, that “some even of the rulers of the church have permitted a woman to marry, even when her husband was living… not indeed altogether without reason, for it is probable this concession was permitted in comparison with worse things.”15 It is the earliest surviving notice of church leaders tolerating remarriage after divorce — the seed of the later Eastern practice of pastoral “economy” (oikonomia) — set down two centuries before the canons that would formalize it. Origen calls the permission “contrary to what was written,” yet concedes its motive; and, uniquely among the early witnesses, he airs the strict reading’s cost, asking whether a wife guilty of “poisoning, or… any form of murder” may not equally be put away — a dilemma in which “there appears to be something monstrous” either way, which he leaves unresolved.16

The Council of Elvira (c. 306)

The earliest surviving Western conciliar canon law, and the first point on this timeline where a church council — not a single teacher — legislates on divorce. Meeting at Elvira in southern Spain, nineteen bishops issued three canons (8, 9, 10) against the woman who leaves a husband and marries again. The mildest bars her from communion until the deserted spouse dies; the gravest — the wife who leaves “without acceptable cause” and remarries — withholds communion “even when death approaches.”17 The decisive canon is the ninth: even a baptized wife who leaves an adulterous husband — the one party the earlier writers granted a right to separate — “may not marry” another while he lives; if she does, she is barred “until her former husband dies.”18 So the “separation, not remarriage” reading taught by Hermas, Clement, and Tertullian now becomes enforceable law: the bond survives the divorce, and the penalty falls on the second marriage, lifting only at the first spouse’s death. Eight years later the Gallic Council of Arles (314), under Constantine, met the mirror case — a young husband whose wife commits adultery — and reached the same verdict by a softer instrument: not a penalty but a counsel, “as much as is able,” that he “not take other wives while their own wives are still living, even if the latter are adulterous.”19 Between them, Elvira and Arles are the earliest conciliar canon law on divorce in the Latin church.

Basil of Caesarea (c. 375)

The fullest procedural settlement of the fourth-century East, and the first witness here to legislate a frank double standard — and to admit it rests on custom, not Scripture. In three canonical letters to Amphilochius of Iconium (Letters 188, 199, 217), later given conciliar force in the Byzantine church, Basil grants that “the sentence of the Lord that it is unlawful to withdraw from wedlock, save on account of fornication… applies… to men and women alike” — then at once concedes, “Custom, however, does not so obtain.”20 The custom he codifies is markedly harsher on the wife: a married man who falls into fornication is punished as a fornicator but not charged with adultery, and “the wife will receive the husband on his return from fornication,” whereas “the husband will expel the polluted woman from his house” — “the argument here is not easy, but the custom has so obtained.”21 A wife may not leave even an adulterous husband; the man deserted by his wife is “deserving of pardon,” but the woman abandoned by her husband “ought… to remain as she is,” barred from remarriage by the Lord’s own word (Matthew 5:32) — “by calling her adulteress, He excludes her from intercourse with another man.”22 So Basil holds the strict, separation-not-remarriage reading of the exception clause taught by Hermas, Clement, and Tertullian, and fixes a seven-year penance for deserting a lawful spouse — but applies it unequally, and, uniquely candid, twice flags that the inequality is what “custom” ordains rather than what the Gospel requires. That self-aware gap between the Lord’s rule and the church’s practice — first reported by Origen — is the seed of the later Eastern settlement of pastoral “economy” (oikonomia).

Ambrosiaster (c. 375)

The most permissive early Latin voice on this timeline — and a frankly unequal one. Expounding Paul’s divorce charge (1 Corinthians 7:10–11), the anonymous Roman commentator known as Ambrosiaster fastens on the fact that Paul tells the separated woman to “remain single” but adds no such words to the man, and reads the silence as a licence: “it is lawful for a man to marry another woman, if he divorces a wife who sins. For a man is not bound by the law in the same way as a woman is, for the man is the head of the woman.”23 Alone among the early Latin fathers he thus reads Matthew’s exception clause as licensing not merely separation but a fresh marriage — for the innocent husband, treating an adulterous wife’s sin as a definitive breaking of the bond, exactly as Roman law did. The wife has no such right: even one who leaves an adulterous or apostate husband “does not enjoy the same rights as the man, who is the superior,” and “can neither marry another man nor return to her husband.”24 On the deserting unbeliever (1 Corinthians 7:15) he holds the abandoned union no longer binding — “it is not a sin if it is abandoned because of God” — the earliest clear Latin statement of the later “Pauline privilege.”25 So against the developing indissolubility consensus — Clement, Tertullian, Elvira, Basil, all barring remarriage while the first spouse lives — Ambrosiaster stands as the strongest patristic Latin support for divorce with remarriage, a Western outlier and a named forerunner of Erasmus. His double standard rests, as David Hunter shows, less on the Gospel than on Roman law and his own theology of male headship.26

Epiphanius of Salamis (c. 375)

The most openly lenient voice on this timeline — and, surprisingly, a fierce heresy-hunter’s. In section 59 of the Panarion Epiphanius, bishop of Salamis in Cyprus, refutes the rigorist Novatianist “Purists” (Cathari), who barred the “twice-married” from communion — “for if someone marries a second wife after baptism, they never admit him again.”27 Against them he lays down the Eastern principle of concession to weakness, and — uniquely explicit among the early witnesses — folds remarriage after divorce into it alongside remarriage after bereavement: “if there has been a divorce for some reason — fornication, adultery or something else — and the man marries a second wife or the woman a second husband, God’s word does not censure them or bar them from the church and life, but tolerates them because of their weakness.”28 The concession is carefully bounded: it is no licence for bigamy — “not by letting him have two wives at once while the one is still alive” — and no commendation, since the once-married is “more highly respected”; the second marriage is borne with as a mercy to human frailty, “the first wife a divine ordinance, the second a concession to human weakness.”29 So where Origen only reported the church tolerating remarriage-while-the-spouse-lives (and disapproved), and Basil legislated a double standard he traced to bare “custom,” Epiphanius positively defends the concession as the mind of “God’s holy church” — the clearest patristic seed of the later Eastern discipline of pastoral “economy” (oikonomia).

John Chrysostom (c. 390)

The great Greek preacher of strict indissolubility, and the fullest Eastern statement that the bond simply survives the divorce. Expounding the Sermon on the Mount’s divorce saying (Matthew 5:31–32) in his Homilies on Matthew at Antioch, Chrysostom presses the guilt onto both men in the case: the husband who dismisses his wife “though he take not another wife, by that act alone hath made himself liable to blame, having made the first an adulteress; the latter again is become an adulterer by taking her who is another’s.”30 The reason the second marriage is adultery is the single clause on which his whole doctrine rests — divorce does not end the marriage: “tell me not this, ‘the other hath cast her out;’ nay, for when cast out she continues to be the wife of him that expelled her,” so Christ “hath shut against her also the doors of him who was afterwards receiving her.” The one lawful ground for sending a wife away is fornication, and even that licenses only dismissal, not remarriage.31 On Paul’s own charge (1 Corinthians 7:10–11) he reaches the same verdict: the separated wife must “remain unmarried, or be reconciled unto her husband” — never “introduce any other to be her husband.”32 So against the permissive Latin reading of his near-contemporary Ambrosiaster and the concession-to-weakness defended by Epiphanius, Chrysostom joins Hermas, Clement, Tertullian, and Basil in the separation-not-remarriage reading — and, as the most influential Greek voice for it, becomes the standard against which the later Byzantine relaxation into pastoral “economy” (oikonomia) is measured.

Jerome (c. 394)

The strictest Latin voice on this timeline, and the scholar who fixes indissolubility as the Western rule. Across three works of the 390s — a letter of casuistry to the presbyter Amandus (c. 394), the Commentary on Matthew (398), and the eulogy of the Roman noblewoman Fabiola (399) — Jerome, the Vulgate translator, reads Matthew’s exception as licensing only the dismissal of an adulterous spouse, never a new marriage while the first partner lives. To Amandus, who had asked whether a woman who left an adulterous, sodomite husband and remarried under compulsion might commune without penance, he answers with a flat negative: “A husband may be an adulterer or a sodomite… yet he is still her husband and, so long as he lives, she may not marry another,” and the “second husband… ought to be called not a husband but an adulterer.”33 The Commentary on Matthew reads the exception clause (Matthew 19:9) as narrowly as anyone here — fornication alone severs the “one flesh” and frees the husband to send the wife away, but for that very reason “the husband is commanded to divorce the first wife so that he does not have a second wife while the first one is still alive.”34 Unlike his fellow Latin Ambrosiaster, who rested a permissive double standard on male headship, Jerome argues the bar from the equality of the sexes before God: “a commandment which is given to men logically applies to women also… what is unlawful for women is equally unlawful for men.” The Fabiola eulogy shows the rule tested on a real, pitiable life — Jerome admired her, calls her remarriage “a fault… at the same time… a case of necessity,” yet still refuses to call it lawful, and paints the vivid scene of her public penance “in the presence of all Rome” before her restoration to communion after the second husband’s death.35 So Jerome joins Hermas, Clement, Tertullian, Basil, and Chrysostom in the separation-not-remarriage reading, and gives it its most authoritative Latin statement on the eve of Augustine.

Later witnesses are being added, earliest first — from the New Testament texts themselves, through the rest of the fourth- and fifth-century settlement in East and West (Augustine’s indissolubility case), the later conciliar canon law (the Council in Trullo), the medieval and Tridentine crystallization (the Council of Trent’s carefully worded Session 24), and the divergent verdicts of the Reformers (Erasmus, Luther, Calvin) and their modern heirs. Contested sub-questions — the meaning of the exception clause, whether it licenses remarriage, the Pauline privilege, and the Shammai/Hillel background — are weighed under Arguments below.

Arguments

The off-timeline sub-claims about Jesus’ divorce teaching — the sense of porneia, why Matthew alone carries the exception, whether the exception permits remarriage or only separation, the scope of the Pauline privilege, and how the sayings engage the Shammai–Hillel dispute — are being worked as argument pages and will be surfaced here, each with its assessment.
