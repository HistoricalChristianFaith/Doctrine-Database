The Old Greek and Theodotion (Daniel 4) — the confession expanded

Dates: the Old Greek c. 100 BC; Theodotion’s revision later (2nd century AD or a proto-Theodotion earlier) · Wikipedia
Summary: Daniel survives in two ancient Greek versions; in chapters 4–6 they diverge sharply, the Old Greek preserving a substantially different edition that relocates and expands Nebuchadnezzar’s confession and heightens his hubris — the earliest evidence of scribes reshaping the proud king’s repentance.

Claims & evidence

Chapters 4–6 are a different edition in the Old Greek

The content of the Old Greek translation of Daniel iv–vi is significantly different compared to the so-called Theodotion version and the Masoretic Text. In addition, the best witness to the Old Greek version (papyrus 967) has an alternative order for the chapters: chapters vii and viii intervene between iv and v.

— R. Timothy McLay, “The Old Greek Translation of Daniel Chapters 4–6 and the Formation of the Book of Daniel,” Vetus Testamentum 55 (2005) 304–323, source. For most of Daniel the Old Greek and Theodotion translate a Semitic text close to the Hebrew-Aramaic; chapters 4–6 are the exception, where the Old Greek and the Masoretic/Theodotion text “share little relationship with regard to style, grammar, and… vocabulary.”1

The king’s confession is moved to the end and expanded; his hubris is heightened

In the Old Greek, McLay notes, Nebuchadnezzar’s “confession and the publication of his decree occurs in a much expanded form at the end of the chapter… rather than the beginning,” and the two versions carry different pluses and minuses throughout the chapter. John J. Collins adds that the Old Greek “expands on the hubris of the king” and introduces notes of temple-desecration absent from Theodotion and the Masoretic text. Long before the Fathers debated whether the doxology was sincere, the oldest Greek edition had already reworked and heightened it — a reminder that Daniel 4 circulated in more than one shape in antiquity.2

Notes

  • Scholars dispute which edition is earlier: Collins reads the Old Greek expansions in 4–6 as secondary (Hellenistic-period rewriting); Ulrich takes both the Masoretic and Old Greek as revised editions of a lost common text; Lust and Munnich argue papyrus 967’s order is more original.
  • The Old Greek’s fluidity in Daniel 4 is often cited alongside the Prayer of Nabonidus as evidence that the madness-and-confession story was still being shaped in the Second Temple period.
