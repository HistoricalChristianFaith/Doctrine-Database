The almsgiving reading — Chrysostom, Cyprian, and Augustine on Nebuchadnezzar

Dates: c. AD 253 (Cyprian) · c. 390–407 (Chrysostom) · c. 400–420 (Augustine) · Wikipedia (Chrysostom)
Summary: A whole stream of the reception fastened not on the madness but on Daniel’s counsel before it — “break off thy sins by almsdeeds” (Daniel 4:27) — and made Nebuchadnezzar the standing proof that mercy to the poor cancels sin.

Claims & evidence

Chrysostom: reconciliation after countless sins

For Daniel saith to Nebuchadnezzar when he had come to all kinds of evil… ‘O King let my counsel be acceptable unto thee, redeem thy sins by almsgiving and thine iniquities by compassion on the poor.’ What could be compared with this lovingkindness? After countless sins, after so many transgressions, he is promised that he will be reconciled…

— John Chrysostom, Concerning the Power of Demons, Homily 2.6, source. Chrysostom returns to the theme in his Homilies on Titus 6: “nothing is so strong and powerful to extinguish the fire of our sins as almsgiving.”1

Cyprian: the misfortunes he might have escaped

Mindful of these teachings, Daniel, when king Nebuchadnezzar… was worried, gave a remedy for averting evils by obtaining divine help… When the king did not obey him, he suffered the misfortunes and trouble that he had seen, which he might have escaped and avoided, if he had redeemed his sins by almsgiving.

— Cyprian of Carthage, On Works and Alms 5, source. For Cyprian the madness is the penalty of a counsel refused; almsgiving would have averted it.2

Augustine: “you redeemed your sins with alms deeds”

Augustine preaches the same text — “there are many other teachings in the divine writings that show how much almsgiving and deeds of charity avail for extinguishing and wiping out sins… you redeemed your sins with alms deeds” — making Daniel’s word to the king a pattern for every hearer.3

Notes

  • This stream reads Nebuchadnezzar moralistically — a case study in the power of penance — rather than adjudicating whether his final confession was a saving conversion (that question is weighed on the true-conversion argument page).
  • The counsel they build on, Daniel 4:27, sits between the dream (4:19–26) and its fulfilment (4:28–33): a twelve-month reprieve that the king’s renewed pride threw away.
