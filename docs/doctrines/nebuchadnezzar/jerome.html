Jerome on Nebuchadnezzar — the doxology of a worldling, and the servant of God

Dates: c. AD 407 (the Commentary on Daniel; Jerome c. 347–420) · Wikipedia
Summary: Jerome defends the historicity of the madness and restoration against Origenist readers, yet is the tradition’s sharpest doubter of the king’s piety — the doxology spoken “like a worldling” — and presses the scandal of Jeremiah’s calling the pagan king God’s “servant.”

Claims & evidence

The doxology “like a worldling”

This too Nebuchadnezzar expresses like a worldling. For God does not simply do what He wishes, but rather God wishes only that which is good. Nebuchadnezzar, however, expressed himself in this way, in order that even while he declared God’s power, he might appear to impugn God’s justice, on the ground that he had suffered unmerited punishment.

— Jerome, Commentary on Daniel, on 4:35, trans. Gleason Archer, source. Where Hippolytus, Ephrem, and Theodoret hear a genuine confession, Jerome hears a proud man declaring God’s power while quietly resenting the justice of his own humbling — the tradition’s strongest voice against reading the doxology as conversion.1

The historicity of the repentance — against the Origenist devil-restoration reading

Those who contest the historicity of this incident and would have it that the devil’s original position of honor will be restored to him, make capital of this passage, on the ground that after Nebuchadnezzar has during the seven-year cycle endured torments and bestialization… he makes a confession of the Lord and becomes the person he was before. But they are bound to answer the question how it can be consistent for the angels who have never fallen to have someone rule over them once more who has only through repentance been restored to favor.

— Jerome, Commentary on Daniel, on 4:26, trans. Archer, source. Jerome defends the plain historical sense of the madness-and-restoration against readers who allegorized it into a proof of the eventual restoration of the devil (the Origenist apokatastasis); his objection is not that the king failed to repent, but that the analogy to the fallen angels breaks down.2

“Nebuchadnezzar, the servant of God”

Miserable must those Israelites have been compared with whom Nebuchadnezzar was called God’s servant… Therefore, if we wish to be lifted up, we must first prostrate ourselves.

— Jerome, Letter 60.17 (to Heliodorus), source. On Jeremiah’s startling title Jerome presses the humiliation home — that Israel had sunk so low the LORD called a pagan destroyer his “servant” — and in his Commentary on Jeremiah adds that Nebuchadnezzar “preceded” in the likeness of the devil, to whom “all these things have been handed over.”3

Notes

  • Jerome is the load-bearing patristic witness on the sceptical side of the true-conversion question, later joined by Calvin.
  • His defence of the historicity of Daniel 4 against the Origenist allegory is a small early node in the apokatastasis controversy — the passage was a proof-text for the restoration of all things, the devil included.
