Was Nebuchadnezzar’s final confession a true conversion?

Type: interpretive-crux argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — the doxology of Daniel 4:34–37 is beyond doubt a genuine acknowledgment of God’s sovereignty, but whether it marks a saving conversion (as Naaman’s confession does) is genuinely disputed: the reading is read as real repentance by Hippolytus, Ephrem, and Theodoret and heightened still further by the rabbis, yet the king’s prior relapses, the absence of an exclusivity vow, and Jerome’s and Calvin’s scepticism keep it short of the settled “true convert” verdict; the text gives no decisive tell · the genuine-conversion reading advanced by the Greek and Syriac Fathers; the counter by Jerome and Calvin

Related arguments: the God-fearing Gentile — the Hebrew Bible’s recurring outsider who confesses the God of Israel, the faith the New Testament reads as a foretype of the Gentile church (Matthew 8:11; Luke 4:27; 11:31); the theme runs across several timelines — see also Melchizedek, priest of El Elyon · Jethro’s conversion · Rahab in the line of Christ · the queen of the south as the Gentile church · Naaman’s confession · the type as a canonical whole.

The claim

And at the end of the days I Nebuchadnezzar lifted up mine eyes unto heaven, and mine understanding returned unto me, and I blessed the most High… Now I Nebuchadnezzar praise and extol and honour the King of heaven, all whose works are truth, and his ways judgment: and those that walk in pride he is able to abase.

— Daniel 4:34–37 (KJV). The question adjudicated here is what the doxology amounts to: a genuine conversion — the proud king truly turning to the God of Israel — or an acknowledgment wrung from a broken man, real as far as it goes but short of saving faith.1

Who advances it

The genuine-conversion reading is the mainstream of the Greek and Syriac reception. Hippolytus has the king, “having humbled himself… after begging the Lord, obtained from him a good heart”; Ephrem frames the whole ordeal as God “endeavoring to lead him to repentance,” the doxology its fruit; Theodoret credits his recovery to “divine grace” and puts real praise in his mouth. The rabbis push the estimate of his praise higher than any Father dared: had an angel not silenced him, his song “would have sought to overshadow all the songs and praises that David recited” (Sanhedrin 92b). The counter comes chiefly from Jerome and, later, Calvin, who hear in the confession not faith but the cry of a humbled worldling.2

The evidence for

Read as a real turn, the passage is strong. (1) The confession is public and self-abasing: the king publishes his own humiliation to “all people, nations, and languages,” giving God the glory at his own expense — the opposite of self-justification. (2) It is doxological, not merely fearful: he blesses, praises, extols, and honours “the King of heaven,” naming God’s dominion everlasting and his ways just — language as high as the Psalter’s. (3) It completes an arc: after the God-of-gods confession of Daniel 2 and the blessing of the three young men’s God in Daniel 3, the king at last acknowledges that God’s rule reaches over himself. (4) The whole reception, Christian and Jewish, read it as sincere — the Fathers as repentance, the rabbis as praise so genuine it had to be restrained. On this reading Nebuchadnezzar is the darkest instance of the Gentile who meets Israel’s God and turns: the world-emperor unmade and brought to bless.3

Adversarial assessment

The counter — and how far it reaches. Three features hold the confession short of a settled “conversion.” First, the pattern of relapse: Nebuchadnezzar had already confessed twice — “your God is a God of gods” (Daniel 2), “Blessed be the God of Shadrach” (Daniel 3) — and hardened again each time, so a fresh doxology does not by itself prove a settled heart. Second, and sharpest, the absence of exclusivity: where Naaman vows to “offer neither burnt offering nor sacrifice unto other gods, but unto the LORD,” Nebuchadnezzar makes no such renunciation — he exalts the Most High as supreme King of heaven without ever forswearing Bel and Nabu, so the confession is compatible with a henotheism that merely enthrones Israel’s God above the rest. Third, Jerome’s suspicion of the wording: the doxology is expressed “like a worldling,” even seeming to “impugn God’s justice, on the ground that he had suffered unmerited punishment” — a proud man declaring God’s power while resenting his own humbling. Calvin sharpens it: such confessions are the cry of men who “when… reduced to extremity… would rather lick the dust than not obtain the favour which they need.”4

Why it does not settle the matter either way. But the counter, real as it is, does not close the question against conversion. The relapse pattern cuts both ways: the doxology of chapter 4 is precisely the last and fullest confession, given after the humbling the earlier ones lacked — growth, not just another cycle. The missing exclusivity vow is an argument from silence: the account is a doxology, not a treaty, and its sweeping “all whose works are truth” is not the language of a man hedging his bets. And Jerome’s “impugns God’s justice” is a reading into the text that Hippolytus, Ephrem, and Theodoret, reading the same Greek, flatly reject. What the text withholds is any decisive tell — there is no vow to clinch conversion and no relapse to disprove it — and that is exactly why the question stays genuinely open.

Assessment

Contested. That the doxology is a real acknowledgment of God’s sovereignty is not in doubt; that it is a saving conversion is genuinely disputed, and the wiki declines to force it either way. The genuine-conversion reading rests on the plainest features of the scene — a public, self-abasing, doxological confession that crowns an arc of dawning acknowledgment — and it commands the whole reception, from Hippolytus, Ephrem, and Theodoret to the rabbis who thought his praise nearly outsang David’s. But it is held short of the “sound” verdict that Naaman’s confession earns, and for good reason: Naaman renounced all other sacrifice, and Nebuchadnezzar does not; Naaman confessed once and firmly, while Nebuchadnezzar had twice confessed and relapsed; and Jerome and Calvin heard in the words a broken man’s awe, not a convert’s faith. The honest verdict is the one the text invites and refuses to resolve: an acknowledgment of the God of heaven beyond dispute, a conversion of the heart beyond proof — the darkest and least certain of the Gentile confessions, neither to be celebrated as settled faith nor dismissed as mere servility.
