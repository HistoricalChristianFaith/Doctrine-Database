Does Daniel 4 rework a tradition about Nabonidus? (The Prayer of Nabonidus, 4Q242)

Disputed reading · on Who Was Nebuchadnezzar? · witness: the Prayer of Nabonidus (4Q242)

The passage

The words of the p[ra]yer which Nabonidus, king of [Ba]bylon, the great king, pray[ed when he was stricken] with an evil disease by the decree of G[o]d in Teiman. [I Nabonidus] was stricken with [an evil disease] for seven years… A diviner — who was a Jew o[f the Exiles — came to me and said:] ‘Recount and record (these things) in order to give honour and great[ness] to the name of the G[od Most High.’]

he was driven from men, and did eat grass as oxen, and his body was wet with the dew of heaven, till his hairs were grown like eagles’ feathers, and his nails like birds’ claws.

— The Prayer of Nabonidus (4Q242), trans. F. M. Cross; and Daniel 4:33 (KJV).1

The question

A short Aramaic text from Qumran tells of Nabonidus, the last king of Babylon, a story of a seven-year affliction, a Jewish adviser and a turn to the God Most High; Daniel 4 tells of Nebuchadnezzar a seven-year humbling ended by his acknowledgment of the Most High. What is disputed is how the two are related: whether Daniel 4 retells a tradition first told of Nabonidus, whether it records an episode of Nebuchadnezzar’s own reign, or whether the Qumran text depends on Daniel. The answer bears on where the account of Nebuchadnezzar’s madness came from.

A tradition about Nabonidus, retold of Nebuchadnezzar

On this reading the story of the humbled king first belonged to Nabonidus and was transferred in Jewish tradition to the better-known Nebuchadnezzar.

Held by: W. von Soden (1935) ⚠ unverified; D. N. Freedman (on the 1956 edition) ⚠ unverified; John J. Collins (Daniel, Hermeneia, 1993); Carol A. Newsom (Daniel, Old Testament Library, 2014).2

Von Soden argued in 1935, before the scroll was found, that a Nabonidus tradition underlies Daniel’s narrative. J. T. Milik’s publication of 4Q242 in 1956 supplied a text, and Freedman read the scroll as a more original form of the tradition. Collins and Newsom describe Daniel 4 as “ultimately based on traditions about Nabonidus.” The case rests on a cluster of shared features. Both texts have a seven-year term (“seven years” in the scroll, “seven times” in Daniel); a Babylonian king humbled by the Most High and brought to acknowledge him; a Judean figure who directs the king to God (the anonymous Jewish diviner, gōzer, answering to Daniel); and a turn from idols, the king’s prayer to “gods of silver and gold, bronze, iron, wood, stone and clay” abandoned for the God Most High. On this reading the setting anchors the identification: the scroll places the affliction at Teima (Tayma), and the Harran stelae, the Nabonidus Chronicle and the Verse Account record that Nabonidus spent about a decade at that Arabian oasis while his son Belshazzar acted as regent in Babylon. On this reading the same Nabonidus background shapes Daniel 5, where Belshazzar is king, and the transfer of the story from the last king of Babylon to Nebuchadnezzar, the Babylonian king of Jewish memory, is the kind of move oral tradition makes.3

Holders of this reading differ on the mechanism. Collins takes the scroll and Daniel 4 as “different developments of a common tradition” rather than a source and its reworking, noting that the scroll’s affliction is a skin inflammation, its adviser anonymous, and that the words “[like a beast]” in Cross’s edition are a restoration in a lacuna.4 For Collins and Newsom, on either mechanism, the shared features and the Tayma setting place the story’s origin in a tradition about Nabonidus.

An episode of Nebuchadnezzar’s own reign

On this reading Daniel 4 is what it presents itself as: Nebuchadnezzar’s own account of a madness that befell him.

Held by: R. K. Harrison (Introduction to the Old Testament, 1969) ⚠ unverified; John F. Walvoord (Daniel: The Key to Prophetic Revelation, 1971), as quoted by Guzik; David Guzik (Enduring Word Bible Commentary, on Daniel 4).5

The chapter is framed as a royal proclamation to “all people, nations, and languages” (Daniel 4:1), and Guzik reads it as “the testimony of a Gentile king and how God changed his heart.” Guzik identifies the malady as boanthropy, “the delusion that one is an ox,” a form of the condition in which a person believes himself an animal, and reports that Walvoord cites “a Dr. Raymond Harrison of Britain, who in 1946 had a patient suffering from boanthropy.” The absence of the episode from Babylonian records is, for Guzik, “exactly as we would expect, considering the customs of that time,” since Near Eastern kings proclaimed their achievements and hid their embarrassments; he points to the lack of any record of the king’s governmental activity between 582 and 575 BC. A pagan notice of a strange end also survives: Abydenus, citing Megasthenes in Eusebius’s Praeparatio Evangelica, reports that Nebuchadnezzar went up to his palace “being possessed by some god or other,” prophesied the coming of “a Persian mule,” and “had immediately disappeared,” a notice Guzik adduces alongside Daniel 4. For Walvoord the narrative “actually reads very sensibly,” as an authentic and reasonably accurate account of the king’s reign.6

The Prayer of Nabonidus depends on Daniel

On this reading the line of dependence runs the other way: the Qumran text is the later of the two and draws on the Daniel tradition.

Held by: Andrew E. Steinmann (Revue de Qumrân, 2002).7

Steinmann’s article, “The Chicken and the Egg,” presents “a new proposal for the relationship between the ‘Prayer of Nabonidus’ and the ‘Book of Daniel’”: that 4Q242 is later than Daniel and dependent on it. On this proposal the features the two texts share (a Babylonian king humbled for seven years, a Jewish adviser, the king’s turn to the Most High) come into the Qumran text from the Daniel story, and the Prayer is a later retelling that sets the story under Nabonidus at Teima, rather than the source from which Daniel 4 grew.
