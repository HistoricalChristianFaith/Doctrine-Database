Hippolytus of Rome on Nebuchadnezzar — the beast-king who confessed and was restored

Dates: c. AD 204 (the Commentary on Daniel; Hippolytus c. 170–235) · Wikipedia
Summary: In the earliest surviving Christian commentary on Daniel, Hippolytus reads the madness and the recovery as a single education in humility, and takes the king’s final confession as genuine repentance rewarded with restoration.

Claims & evidence

The king unmade into a beast

For no longer did the great king and ruler live as a man administering the kingdom, but being alone in the desert he ate grass and herbs until his heart was changed and became as a beast who lived in forests and caverns, until his hair grew as a lion’s and his nails grew large as a bird’s and seven seasons passed over him.

— Hippolytus, Commentary on Daniel 3.9, trans. T. C. Schmidt, shown at historicalchristian.faith, on Daniel 4:30. Hippolytus draws the bestialization in full — the loss of reason, the wilderness, the animal hair and claws — the fall that the boast “Is this Babylon not great, which I myself built?” brought down.1

A genuine repentance, and a kingdom restored

it is clear that Nebuchadnezzar, having humbled himself and recognized that he is a man lying under the authority of God, he, after begging the Lord, obtained from him a good heart, and again his kingdom and glory were reestablished.

— Hippolytus, Commentary on Daniel 4.2, trans. Schmidt, shown at historicalchristian.faith, on Daniel 4:34–35. Hippolytus reads the outcome as a real turn: the king “confessed to God, recognizing the heavenly authority,” and only then was restored. The whole ordeal was “an education for the king, so that he would not be high-minded,” teaching him to say “all who walk in arrogance are able to be humbled.”2

Notes

  • Hippolytus stands on the “genuine conversion” side of the question later contested by Jerome — weighed on the true-conversion argument page.
  • He also supplies the vivid boanthropy imagery (grass, animal hair, claws) that the boanthropy question takes up.
