Nebuchadnezzar in Jewish tradition — the blasphemer whose song outsang David

Dates: traditions from c. AD 200, redacted in the Babylonian Talmud by c. 500 · Sefaria (topic)
Summary: The rabbis made Nebuchadnezzar the arch-blasphemer — the “king of Babylon” of Isaiah 14 and heir of Nimrod — yet preserved the most extravagant estimate of his praise anywhere: had an angel not struck him silent, his doxology would have eclipsed the Psalter.

Claims & evidence

The song that nearly surpassed David’s

Rabbi Yitzḥak says: Molten gold should be poured into the mouth of that wicked person… if an angel had not come and struck him on his mouth to prevent him from continuing his praise, he would have sought to overshadow all the songs and praises that David recited in the book of Psalms.

— Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 92b (William Davidson translation), source. The tradition holds the blasphemer and the singer together: his praise (Daniel 3–4) was so genuine and so powerful that it had to be silenced by heaven lest it outshine David’s.1

The heir of Nimrod who said “I will be like the Most High”

The Talmud makes Nebuchadnezzar the boaster of Isaiah 14 — “I will ascend above the heights of the clouds; I will be like the Most High” — and “a disciple in corruption of Nimrod the wicked, who caused the entire world to rebel.” The Divine Voice answers his ambition with the next verse: “Yet you shall be brought down to the netherworld, to the uttermost parts of the pit.” This is the locus of the rabbinic conflation of Nebuchadnezzar with the fallen “king of Babylon” of Isaiah 14.2

Notes

  • The near-doxology of Sanhedrin 92b is the Jewish tradition’s witness on the highest end of the true-conversion question: it grants the praise real power while denying the king any innocence.
  • The Nimrod link ties this page to the Nimrod timeline, where Babel’s first tyrant is likewise read as the type of the world in rebellion.
