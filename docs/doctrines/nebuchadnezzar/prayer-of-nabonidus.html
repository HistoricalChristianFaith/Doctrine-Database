The Prayer of Nabonidus (4Q242) — the same story told of a different king

Dates: c. 2nd–1st century BC (the surviving Aramaic copy c. 75–50 BC) · Wikipedia
Summary: A short Aramaic text from Qumran that tells, of the last Babylonian king Nabonidus, a story strikingly like Daniel 4 — a seven-year affliction, a Jewish adviser, a turn from idols to the Most High — and the chief reason many scholars read Daniel 4 as the reworking of a tradition that first belonged to Nabonidus.

Claims & evidence

A Babylonian king, a seven-year affliction, and a Jewish diviner who names the Most High

The words of the p[ra]yer which Nabonidus, king of [Ba]bylon, the great king, pray[ed when he was stricken] with an evil disease by the decree of G[o]d in Teiman. [I Nabonidus] was stricken with [an evil disease] for seven years… and, as for my sin, he forgave it. A diviner — who was a Jew o[f the Exiles — came to me and said:] ‘Recount and record (these things) in order to give honour and great[ness] to the name of the G[od Most High.’…] seven years I was praying [to] gods of silver and gold, [bronze, iron,] wood, stone (and) clay…

— The Prayer of Nabonidus (4Q242), trans. Frank Moore Cross, “Fragments of the Prayer of Nabonidus,” Israel Exploration Journal 34 (1984) 260–264; text also at source. The parallels to Daniel 4 are dense: a Babylonian king humbled by God, a seven-year term, a Jewish adviser, prayer to idols of silver and gold renounced for the Most High. The setting is historical — Nabonidus really did spend about a decade at the Arabian oasis of Teima (Tayma) — which points to Nabonidus, not Nebuchadnezzar, as the king to whom the tale first belonged.1

One conjectural word: “like a beast”

Cross’s edition restores, in a broken line, that the king was “[like unto a beast]” — the very phrase that would bridge Nabonidus’s skin disease to Nebuchadnezzar’s ox-madness. But the words sit in a lacuna: they are the editor’s reconstruction, not preserved text, and other editors do not restore an animal there. The single most important link in the case that Daniel 4 reworks this scroll is thus a scholarly conjecture, not a reading anyone can point to on the leather — a caution weighed on the argument page.2

Notes

  • The scroll (siglum 4Q242 = 4QPrNab) was first published by J. T. Milik in 1956; the standard edition is DJD XXII (Oxford, 1996). W. von Soden had already argued in 1935, before its discovery, that a Nabonidus tradition underlies Daniel 4.
  • Nabonidus’s Teima sojourn and his son Belshazzar’s regency in Babylon are independently documented (the Harran stelae, the Nabonidus Chronicle, the Verse Account) — the same Nabonidus/Belshazzar background that stands behind Daniel 5.
  • Whether Daniel 4 directly reworks this fragment, or the two are independent developments of one older tradition, is the crux of the Nabonidus-tradition argument.
