Josephus on Nebuchadnezzar — the great builder, and a silence

Dates: c. AD 93 (the Antiquities of the Jews 10; Josephus b. c. AD 37, d. c. 100) · Wikipedia
Summary: Josephus retells Daniel at length but passes over the seven-year madness in silence, supplying instead the external record — the Babylonian historian Berossus on Nebuchadnezzar’s building works, and Megasthenes on his greatness.

Claims & evidence

The reign and death of “an active man”

Now when king Nebuchadnezzar had reigned forty-three years, he ended his life. He was an active man, and more fortunate than the kings that were before him.

— Josephus, Antiquities of the Jews 10.219, trans. William Whiston, source. Josephus’s paraphrase of Daniel runs through the dreams, the great image, and the fiery furnace, but the beast-madness of Daniel 4 is simply absent — a Jewish historian writing for a Greco-Roman readership passing over the king’s humiliation.1

Berossus and Megasthenes: the walls, the hanging garden, and a king greater than Hercules

He also erected what was called a pensile paradise, because his wife was desirous to have things like her own country, she having been bred up in the palaces of Media… Megasthenes also, in his fourth book of his Accounts of India, makes mention of these things, and thereby endeavors to show that this king exceeded Hercules in fortitude, and in the greatness of his actions.

— Josephus, Antiquities of the Jews 10.225–227, trans. Whiston, source. Josephus quotes Berossus’s “third book of his Chaldaic History” on Nebuchadnezzar’s triple walls and the “pensile paradise” (the “hanging gardens”), and repeats the same material in Against Apion 1.19–20. For Josephus the pagan king is remembered by his conquests and monuments, a builder to rival Hercules — not the grass-eating penitent of Daniel 4.2

Notes

  • The omission of the madness is itself a datum for the boanthropy question: the fullest early Jewish historian, drawing on Babylonian sources, records no seven-year insanity.
  • A garbled pagan memory of an odd end does survive elsewhere: Abydenus (via Eusebius, Praeparatio Evangelica 9.41), quoting Megasthenes, has Nebuchadnezzar “possessed by some god” on his palace roof, prophesying Babylon’s fall to a “Persian mule” before he “immediately disappeared” — which some read as a distorted echo of the Daniel 4 episode.3
