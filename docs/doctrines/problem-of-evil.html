The problem of evil (theodicy)

It is the oldest objection to belief in God: if God is perfectly good and all-powerful, why is there evil? The pagan schools sharpened it into an inconsistent triad — God is good, God is omnipotent, evil exists — and pressed that no two of the three seem to leave room for the last. This page traces how the church answered across time, earliest witness first. The answers fall into a few enduring lines, and it helps to keep two distinctions in view throughout. The first is between moral evil (the wrong that wills do) and natural evil (the suffering that merely befalls — disease, disaster, death). The second is between a defense — showing only that the triad is not self-contradictory, that a good omnipotent God could have a sufficient reason — and a theodicy — offering the positive reason itself.

Four replies recur and are already present in the Fathers below: that moral evil is the unavoidable price of a genuinely free will (a will that could not refuse God could not love Him); that a world containing evil can be schooled toward a greater good than a world without it (Irenaeus's growth-through-experience, Lactantius's “no wisdom without evils to overcome,” Augustine's better “to bring good out of evil”); that evil is no substance God made but the privation or corruption of a good (Basil, Gregory of Nyssa, Augustine); and — running deeper than human choice — that the disorder began in a rebellion in the unseen realm, the free fall of angels. This wiki does not treat “that answer requires the supernatural” as a refutation: the cosmic-fall and free-will-of-spirits readings are weighed on their evidence, not dismissed in advance.

Closely bound to this question are the timelines on original sin and the Fall (how evil entered and spreads), predestination, grace and free will (how God's sovereignty and the free will meet), universal restoration (the maximal answer — that evil is finally undone in all), and the divine council reading of a rebel cosmos. The theodicy device also surfaces on the Flood and other pages; here the problem itself is the subject.

Timeline

Irenaeus of Lyons (c. 180)

Irenaeus gives the earliest sustained Christian answer, and it is a “soul-making” answer before the name. Against the Gnostics, who blamed a defective creator, he lodges moral evil in the free creature: God “made man a free [agent] from the beginning… to obey the behests of God voluntarily, and not by compulsion… for there is no coercion with God.”1 And he answers the objection “why not make man unfallable?” developmentally: God “could have made man perfect from the first, but man could not receive this, being as yet an infant,”2 and one cannot knowingly choose the good without knowing its contrary — experience yields “a surer and an undoubted comprehension” than mere opinion.3 Evil is thus the cost of freedom and the schooling of a race made to grow.

Lactantius (c. 313)

Lactantius, the “Christian Cicero,” preserves the sharpest ancient statement of the problem — the fourfold dilemma he ascribes to Epicurus: God “either wishes to take away evils, and is unable; or He is able, and is unwilling; or He is neither willing nor able, or He is both willing and able… from what source then are evils?”4 He answers by breaking the last horn: God is able to remove evils but does not, “because He at the same time gives wisdom… unless we first know evil, we shall be unable to know good… no traces of virtue remain in man, the nature of which consists in enduring and overcoming the bitterness of evils.”5 A painless world would be a virtueless one; the permission of evil is the price of wisdom and courage.

Basil of Caesarea (c. 378)

Basil the Great supplies the two moves that organize the classical answer. First, evil is no thing: “it is equally impious to say that evil has its origin from God… Evil is not a living animated essence; it is the condition of the soul opposed to virtue, developed… on account of their falling away from good.”6 Second, moral evil is self-authored: “do not… imagine that there is an original nature of wickedness. Each of us… is the first author of his own vice… evil… has no other origin than our voluntary falls.”7 With no evil substance to blame and a free will to bear it, God is in no sense evil's author — the Greek form of the privation-plus-freedom answer.

Gregory of Nyssa (c. 380)

Basil's younger brother carries the same two moves and adds a third. Evil is no substance but a defect: “there is no evil other than wickedness… all wickedness has its form and character in the deprivation of the good; it exists not by itself… it is the non-existence of the good that is so denominated.”8 It arises not in God but in the free creature: “No growth of evil had its beginning in the Divine will… it is not God Who is the author of the present evils, seeing that He has ordered your nature so as to be its own master and free.”9 Gregory's own contribution answers the Manichee's harder question — why make man at all, foreseeing the fall? — from providence: God “knew what was going to be… As, then, He had in sight the perversion, so He devised man's recall to good,”10 a remedial ordering that runs on into his teaching on the final restoration of all.

Augustine of Hippo (c. 420)

Augustine, a former Manichee who had once made evil a rival dark substance, gives the Latin West its enduring synthesis. Evil is no nature but the corruption of one: it is “nothing else than corruption, either of the measure, or the form, or the order, that belong to nature… when incorrupt it is good; but even when corrupt, so far as it is nature it is good.”11 That answers what evil is; the why is providential greater good: God “judged it better to bring good out of evil, than not to permit any evil to exist.”12 Privation joined to greater good — evil real but parasitic, permitted only because omnipotent goodness can redeem it — became the standard Western frame, the seed of the later felix culpa and the ground Aquinas built on.
