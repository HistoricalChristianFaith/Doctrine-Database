Do the Iron I highland villages bear a material "signature" of early Israel?

Presupposition: inherited from The Conquest of Canaan in the Material Record — that Israel took Canaan as Joshua and Judges tell it, its date left open. This page works inside that presupposition: its assessment weighs a dating model of the conquest or a trace of it, not whether it happened.

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: sound — for the claim that the recurring Iron I trait-cluster (near-absence of pig bones, the four-room house, collared-rim jars, no temples or idols, an egalitarian village plan) characterizes the highland population that became Israel: it marks a distinct highland group by c. 1200 BC, continuous into the Israel and Judah of the monarchy, which scholars of every camp identify as early Israel and which the Merneptah Stele names. What fails is the fingerprint form: no single trait labels a given site Israelite. How Israel came into the land is a separate question — the pottery grows out of Late Bronze Canaanite forms, an open puzzle weighed below — and does not bear on this rating · advanced in its popular form by early-date writers (Athens Bible Church, the Armstrong Institute), and grounded scholarly in Avraham Faust

Related arguments: open puzzles for the presupposition — see also Was Jericho's "City IV" destroyed c. 1400 BC (Wood) or c. 1550 BC (Kenyon)? · Is Joshua's Ai to be identified with Khirbet el-Maqatir rather than et-Tell?

The claim

In the Iron I period, many highland sites show a cluster of traits — simple four-room houses, collar-rim jars, and dispersed village layouts — that differ from the urban centers of the lowland Canaanite cities.

— Athens Bible Church, "Material Culture of the Early Israelites." The argument, in the form weighed here, presses this cluster into service as the archaeological signature of early Israel — and, in its stronger apologetic form, as the material footprint of the tribes settling the land after Joshua's campaigns. Its constituents are the four traits set out on the highland-settlement detail page: the near-absence of pig bones (sharpest beside the pork-eating Philistine cities), the four-room "Israelite" house, collared-rim storage jars, and a striking lack of temples and idols, across hundreds of small new hill-country villages.1

Who advances it

The reading has a respectable scholarly form and a looser popular one. The scholarly form is Avraham Faust's (Israel's Ethnogenesis, 2006): the plain, undecorated, un-imported pottery, the uniform houses, and the pork-avoidance are not mere rural poverty but a deliberate "egalitarian ethos" and a boundary marker — a village society signalling we are not them against the neighbouring Philistines, hardening over time into a religious taboo. On Faust's account the trait-cluster genuinely tracks the ethnogenesis of Israel. The popular form — early-date apologetic writers such as Athens Bible Church and the Armstrong Institute — takes the same cluster as external corroboration of the biblical settlement, noting for instance that at Iron I Shiloh "less than 1 percent" of the excavated animal bones were pig. Against both, a cautionary line — Elizabeth Bloch-Smith (JBL 2003) and, on the underlying continuity, Israel Finkelstein — holds that no single trait is exclusively Israelite and that the assemblage develops out of local Canaanite tradition.2

The evidence for

It is a combination, not a coincidence. The force of the argument lies in the recurrence of the cluster together across hundreds of new villages, not in any one trait. Pig-avoidance, the four-room house, collared-rim jars, and the empty-of-shrines settlement pattern turn up as a package, and that package is distinct from both the lowland Canaanite towns and the coastal Philistine cities. A recurring combination of household, economic, and cultic practices does mark a distinct social formation, whatever one calls it; that much is common ground even to the skeptics.

It runs on into the Israel of the kingdoms. The strongest single argument is continuity forward, not backward. The villages are the first phase of a settled highland society that carries on without a break into the towns and villages of the Iron II kingdoms of Israel and Judah, in pottery, settlement, architecture and burial. Amihai Mazar puts the inference plainly: "The continuity between many of these sites and later Israelite towns and villages of the monarchic era legitimizes the definition of these settlers as Israelites," which makes Dever's "Proto-Israelites" in his view "superfluous" ⚠ unverified.3 The four-room house, the most distinctive trait, shows the same pattern: it appears in the Iron I hills, remains the standard house of both kingdoms, and disappears when they fall in the sixth century, which Avraham Faust and Shlomo Bunimovitz argue no functional explanation accounts for ⚠ unverified.4

Scholars of every camp identify the villagers as early Israel. The identification is not an apologetic one. Israel Finkelstein and Neil Silberman, after describing the some two hundred and fifty hilltop communities that "suddenly sprang up" around 1200 BC, conclude: "Here were the first Israelites."5 Mazar, Lawrence Stager ("That many of these villages belonged to premonarchic Israel … is beyond doubt"), Faust and William Dever make the same identification, whatever they hold about how Israel came to be there ⚠ unverified.6 The page's main critic concedes the point. Elizabeth Bloch-Smith writes of the "highland settlers, including the Israelites," and concludes that "Faint traces of the Iron I Israelite ethnos are discernible in material remains interpreted in conjunction with biblical testimony": in "the short beard, refraining from eating pork, circumcision, and military inferiority." Her objection is to reading any single trait as a fingerprint, not to identifying the population as Israel.7

It correlates in time and place with the Merneptah "Israel." The horizon (c. 1200 BC) sits right beside the Merneptah Stele's notice of a people called Israel already in Canaan by c. 1208 BC, and in the very hill country the biblical text assigns to the tribes. A distinct highland people, named "Israel" by an external Egyptian source, occupying exactly the zone where a distinct highland material culture appears at the same moment, is a real and non-trivial convergence. The pig-bone contrast is the sharpest single strand: on the order of 10–20% of animal bones at the Iron I Philistine centres versus near-absence in the highlands, a boundary that reads naturally as an ethnic one.8

Adversarial assessment

What holds. That the Iron I highland cluster belongs to the population that became Israel is well-grounded on three independent lines: its continuity into the Israel and Judah of the monarchy, its convergence with the Merneptah Stele's "Israel" in the same hills at the same moment, and the agreement of scholars across every camp, the cluster's chief critic included. Faust's "egalitarian ethos" reading is a serious, published account of what the cluster meant to the people who kept it. On its best case the claim "this cluster characterizes early Israel" is sound.

What is overstated. Two further claims are often run together with it, and neither follows from the cluster:

(1) No single trait is an exclusive ethnic marker. Bloch-Smith presses this fairly: "Earliest Israel remains terra incognita, literally and from the ground down," and "not a single feature of those settlements may be conclusively identified as exclusively 'Israelite.'" Four-room houses and collared-rim jars turn up outside the highlands (including at 'Umayri in Jordan); pig-avoidance may be partly ecological — pigs fare poorly on dry hilltops — and a pig skeleton later turns up in a First Temple building in the City of David, so even the sharpest strand is not absolute. The cluster characterizes a population; it is not a fingerprint that labels a single site.

(2) The pottery shows continuity with Late Bronze Canaan. The highland pottery "develops typologically out of Canaanite pottery that came before"; the architecture and burial customs largely continue Late Bronze Canaanite tradition rather than importing a foreign repertoire — the very opposite of the sharply alien Philistine assemblage arriving on the coast at the same time. So the cluster does not itself show a people arriving from outside; for a people who did, as Joshua tells it, that continuity is a puzzle, taken up below. A settlement process spread across generations is also not a battle report, and its timing sits after the early-date (~1400 BC) conquest scheme.9

The open puzzle for the presupposition

Holding that Israel entered Canaan from outside, as Joshua tells it, the villages' pottery is an open puzzle on either dating of the conquest. It "develops typologically out of Canaanite pottery that came before," with no foreign repertoire arriving alongside it, and no single trait of the highland cluster is exclusively Israelite; on the late date these villages are the settlement itself, and on the early date they are Israel's villages two centuries after the entry, so neither dating escapes the question.10 Six resolutions proposed by those who hold the presupposition are weighed below. The puzzle is stated here as a puzzle, not as a verdict on the conquest.

Resolutions proposed inside the presupposition

Each resolution is weighed here on its evidence, with two questions put to it. Does it keep the whole presupposition — a people who came out of Egypt, spent forty years in the wilderness, held the land east of the Jordan, then crossed it and took Canaan under Joshua — or does it keep it only by giving part of it up? And does it reach the puzzle where it actually lies? That is not in the towns Israel is said to have taken over, but in the hundreds of new villages founded on empty hilltops, whose pots somebody made new. Two of the six hold that newcomers would bring no pottery and would take over what they found. One makes many of Israel's potters Canaanites. One gives Israel two centuries in the land before the villages were built. One holds that the same pottery was in use east of the Jordan, where Israel came from. And one moves Israel's mark from the shapes of its pots to what it refused.

A people of tents and skins: nothing to bring

The simplest resolution denies that newcomers should be expected to bring pottery at all. A people that had spent a generation on the move, Kenneth Kitchen argues, “had no need to lug tons of heavy pottery around with them (just to oblige F. & S. with a few sherds!) if leatherwork or skins would do.” Once settled, they took up what they found and then made it their own: “Up in the hills the innumerable small settlements at first showed Canaanite-style pottery, but quickly went their own way producing their own typically dull, utilitarian wares … and almost no fancy novelties.” Henry B. Smith Jr. of Associates for Biblical Research puts the same point from the early-date side: “They had been living in tents as sojourners in the desert for 40 years. They would have had little or no pottery making expertise and none of them had ever built a city.” Richard Hess, citing Anson Rainey, adds that “a nomadic people would naturally adapt to the settled culture as they spent time in the same region,” so that the lack of “a clear ceramic (pottery) distinction between Canaanites and Israelites is not surprising.”11 It keeps the whole presupposition and suits either dating.

How it fares. On its central point it holds, and scholars who do not hold the conquest grant the premise. Pottery is made for use, by whoever makes it in a region, and Bloch-Smith herself judges that the restricted distribution of the collared-rim jar and the pillared house “favors an economic or functional rather than an ethnic impetus.” Finkelstein, who in 1988 had set the settlers' assemblage “in sharp contrast to the repertoire of the Canaanite centers,” wrote in 1997 that the pottery forms on both sides of the Jordan “tell us nothing about ethnicity.”12 If that is so, the continuity of the forms cannot count heavily against an entry from outside either. What the resolution does not explain is the lack of any trace of the pastoral life it describes. William Dever's reply is that newcomers from the steppe would be expected to leave at least some new forms of their own, such as the handmade “Negbite” ware made by non-sedentary groups in the Negev; instead, in his own words, “Early Israelites look ceramically just like Canaanites.”13 Pekka Pitkänen answers that Dever himself notes that the settlers brought no artistic traditions to the highlands either, which sits awkwardly with the argument from pottery. This resolution removes the expectation of a foreign repertoire. It does not show where the potters learned their craft, and that is where the next two take up.

Heirs of the land: “cities which ye built not”

The second resolution starts from the text. Moses promises “great and goodly cities, which thou buildedst not, and houses full of all good things, which thou filledst not, and wells digged, which thou diggedst not” (Deuteronomy 6:10–11), and Joshua's closing speech recalls “a land for which ye did not labour, and cities which ye built not, and ye dwell in them” (Joshua 24:13). James Hoffmeier draws the archaeological consequence: “This suggests that the arrival of the Israelites did not significantly affect the cultural continuity of the Late Bronze Age and may explain why there is no evidence of an intrusion into the land from outsiders, for they became heirs of the material culture of the Canaanites.” “A scorched-earth policy,” he adds, “is only logical for a conqueror who has no thought of occupying the devastated land,” and Joshua reports only three cities burned: Jericho, Ai and Hazor. Kitchen likewise: “The principal Hebrew policy under Joshua was to kill leaders and inhabitants, not to destroy the cities, but eventually to occupy them (cf. Deut. 6:10-11).” Pitkänen applies it to the pottery directly: “it would be conceivable that any ‘intruders’ could adapt to the local pottery traditions, especially as the biblical tradition stresses that the Israelites would inherit the material culture of the land (Deut. 6:10–11).”14 It keeps the whole presupposition; indeed it reads the continuity as something the text itself leads one to expect.

How it fares. As a reading of the text it is sound. Joshua does limit the burning to three cities (6:24; 8:28; 11:11–13), and a conquest that takes over towns and their goods would leave their material culture in place. But it covers less ground than the puzzle. Most of the Iron I highland villages were founded on hilltops where no Late Bronze town had stood (the hill country goes from about 25 villages to over 300), so there were no inherited houses or cisterns there, and every pot in them was made new by somebody. Inherited vessels would last a generation, not found a ceramic tradition; for the tradition to carry on, the settlers had to learn the local craft, or the potters had to be local. This resolution explains the towns and supports the first. For the new villages it needs the first or the third.15

A mixed multitude: Canaanites taken into Israel

The third resolution also starts from the text, which never presents Israel as a closed people. A “mixed multitude” went up with Israel out of Egypt (Exodus 12:38). Rahab's household “dwelleth in Israel even unto this day” (Joshua 6:25). The Gibeonite towns entered by treaty (Joshua 9). Shechem is never said to be conquered, yet Israel holds covenant there (8:30–35; 24). Judges lists the Canaanites the tribes did not drive out (1:27–33) and says that Israel took their daughters to wife (3:5–6). Richard Hess notes the areas “such as the region around Shechem, which Israel is portrayed as occupying … but for which there is no account of conquest,” and concludes that “other elements, both from outside and from within Canaan, became attached to any people who might be designated as Israel. In Joshua this is reflected in accounts concerning Rahab and concerning the Gibeonites.” Kitchen speaks of “an Israelite entry into Canaan, and settlement”: “neither only a conquest … nor simply an infiltration …, nor just re-formation of local Canaanites into a new society ‘Israel’ (although others, as at Shechem, may have joined the Hebrew nucleus; cf. Gibeon).” Pitkänen sums it up: “Israel originates in Egypt but grows in and from Canaan.”16 If many of the people who made Israel's pots were Canaanites, Canaanite pots follow. It keeps the presupposition and suits either dating.

How it fares. It is the best grounded of the resolutions in the text, and it fits the continuity well. Its cost lies in its own logic, and Pitkänen states it: “how many Canaanites would have been assimilated? Based on recent studies which emphasise the indigenous origins of Israel, there pretty much seems to be no upper limit.” Pressed far enough, it becomes Ann Killebrew's “mixed multitude” model, which borrows the same phrase from Exodus 12:38 for an Israel “whose origins are largely indigenous,” made of “largely indigenous, tribal, and kin-based groups whose porous borders permitted penetration by smaller numbers from external groups.”17 There the people from outside are a minority who joined, not the nation that entered the land, and that gives part of the presupposition up. In the form held by Hess, Kitchen and Pitkänen the nucleus is the people who came out of Egypt and the Canaanites join it, and in that form it holds. But the pottery cannot show which way round it was: it can only show that many hands were local. So this resolution is coherent and explains the pots; it keeps the presupposition so long as the nucleus stays the people from outside, which the pottery can neither show nor refute.

Two centuries among the Canaanites: the early date

On the early date the villages are not the entry at all. If Israel crossed the Jordan c. 1406 BC, the highland villages of c. 1200 were built by the great-grandchildren of the conquest generation, after two centuries of living beside, trading with and marrying into the Canaanites the tribes had not driven out (Judges 1:27–33; 3:5–6). Bryant Wood states it plainly: “When the Israelites arrived in Canaan in 1406 BC they had no material culture of their own since they had been living in the Sinai as nomads for the previous 40 years. What is more, those with craft skills learned in Egypt had died in the wilderness. As a result, the Israelites purchased their pottery, tools and weapons from the Canaanites making it difficult to distinguish Israelites from Canaanites until the Israelites began making their own material items ca. 1200 BC.” John Bimson, whose conquest falls at the end of the Middle Bronze Age, had argued the same: “It is not difficult, therefore, to imagine many of the Israelites mingling with the Canaanite population and adopting both their material culture and their religious practices only a short time after the Conquest,” quoting Bruce Waltke that such a people's “containers may well have been made mainly of skin.” Where were they in the meantime? “Most of them lived in tents,” David Livingston answers, citing the tents of Israel still in Samuel's and David's day, so “we need not expect evidence of them to show up in excavations of towns and cities.”18 It keeps the whole presupposition, and it serves the early date only.

How it fares. For the villages it works: on this model the continuity is what Judges would lead one to expect, and the pottery puzzle there dissolves. But it moves the puzzle rather than removing it. The entry itself now falls c. 1406 BC, and the same question can be put to that horizon, where the answer is the same: Bimson asks whether there are “any cultural changes at the start of LB I which could attest the arrival of Israelite groups,” and replies, “Unfortunately the answer to this question is, no.” The model answers with the first resolution, a people of tents and skins, and so it leaves Israel almost invisible for two centuries. Its one positive sign is Wood's: “A number of cemeteries from this time period have been found in the central hill country with no town site nearby. This suggests a pastoral-nomadic population, possibly Israelite.” “Possibly” is the right word.19 The Bible's-own-dates page counts the two silent centuries as a real cost of the model, and the early date itself is rated contested, leaning against, on the Jericho-dating page. This resolution is coherent and unrefuted, and it removes the puzzle from the villages; its strength is its fit with Judges, not evidence of Israel in the land before 1200.

From across the Jordan: Rainey's Transjordan and Zertal's cooking pots

Two archaeologists have argued that the pottery does not, after all, point to a local origin, because a people coming from the east would bring the same pottery. Rainey answered Dever that “the same ceramic traditions have recently been found in Transjordan”: “The new hill-country settlers acquired their pottery traditions from their life on the Transjordanian plateau and the Jordan Valley.” Joshua puts Israel there first, holding the land of Sihon and Og before the crossing (Numbers 21:21–35; 32; Joshua 12:1–6). Adam Zertal, from his survey of the hill country of Manasseh, went further and claimed to see the movement in the cooking pots. “Type A, which is a direct continuation of the Late Bronze cooking pot, comes in masses in the east, near the Jordan Valley,” and “the further the sites are located westwards, the later is the type of cooking pot. The logic explanation to this phenomenon is the slow, gradual movement of the settling elements from the east (Jordan Valley) to the west.”20 Neither held the conquest as Joshua tells it: Rainey's early Israelites were “pastoral nomads who came from east of the Jordan,” and Zertal pictured a gradual infiltration, though he took the Mount Ebal structure for Joshua's altar. But the argument is open to those who do, and it suits an entry in the thirteenth century, on the late date.

How it fares. Rainey's point is sound as far as it goes: if the Late Bronze ceramic tradition ran on both sides of the Jordan, continuity with it cannot show which side the settlers came from. It does not show that they came from the east either, and it does not reach Egypt. Zertal's trail is weaker. Its own key form is, in his words, “a direct continuation of the Late Bronze cooking pot,” and Killebrew notes that the form “is common throughout Canaan during the Late Bronze II,” so the trail is a matter of proportions, not of a foreign type. Those proportions come from surface sherds. Dever replied that the early pots occur at nearly all of Zertal's sites, in different percentages, and that “there was no general movement of peoples from east to west.” Finkelstein accepts an east-to-west drift in the settlement but reads it as a shift in economy, and finds “no archaeological evidence whatsoever supporting his view that the new settlers penetrated the country from the outside steppe.”21 This resolution takes away one premise of the puzzle, that continuity must mean a local origin, but its positive claim, a trail of newcomers from the east, is contested and rests on survey statistics.

Israel's mark in what it refused: Faust

The last resolution moves the question. On Avraham Faust's account, Israel marked itself off from its neighbours less by the shapes of its vessels than by what it would not do. The highland villages' pottery is plain and almost never imported: his reviewer William Dever notes, “as several of us have, the absence of imported or decorated pottery in the hundreds of Iron I highland settlements.” The villages also ate almost no pork and built no temples or rich tombs, and Faust reads these absences not as poverty but, in Dever's summary, as owing to “‘ideological, cognitive, and symbolic’ factors; that is, to choice.”22 If Israel's boundary lay there, a people from outside could take up local forms and still be visibly itself; the continuity of forms is then no evidence either way. Faust himself does not hold the conquest as Joshua tells it. On his reconstruction “the first group was composed mainly of Shasu pastoralists. Other groups, probably including a small ‘Exodus’ group which left Egypt, joined the process,” and “many of those who eventually became Israelites were of Canaanite origins.”23 But the argument about pottery stands apart from his history of origins and is open to those who hold the conquest.

How it fares. It reframes the puzzle rather than solving it. It shows that pottery forms are the wrong place to look for an incoming people's identity, and it gives the highland cluster a coherent meaning. But it says nothing of where that people came from. A boundary drawn by refusals fits a people from outside and a people forming inside the land equally well. Its strands are also uneven. The pork contrast is weaker than it looks: in the highlands “there is indeed clear avoidance of pig husbandry, but this is similar (or almost similar) to the general pattern documented at lowlands sites,” which “makes it difficult to use the presence/absence of pigs as an indicator of ethnic identity,” and Hess notes that the villages' egalitarian look may be “due as much to the scarcity of food and natural resources as to any ideology.”24 As a resolution it takes the sting out of the continuity of forms, but in Faust's own form it gives part of the presupposition up.

Where the puzzle stands

None of the six is refuted, and none is proved; together they soften the puzzle more than they solve it. Two weaken its premise. Pottery is a poor marker of who made it, as scholars on every side now grant, and the Late Bronze tradition ran east of the Jordan too, so continuity with it does not show that the villagers were local. Two explain where the continuity came from: a people of tents and skins who took over the land's goods and learned its potters' craft, and Canaanites taken into Israel, as the text itself says they were. The early date removes the puzzle from the villages altogether, but only by leaving Israel almost without a trace in the land for two centuries. Faust's account shows where an incoming people's mark could lie instead, in what it refused. What none of them supplies is a positive trace of a people arriving from outside: Zertal's trail from the east is the one attempt, and it rests on contested survey figures. Two give part of the presupposition up when pressed: the mixed multitude, if Israel's people from outside shrink to a minority who joined a largely local people, and Faust's own history of Israel's origins, though not his argument about pottery. In the forms held by those who hold the conquest, the first three together account for the pottery, and nothing in the pottery tells against them; what the pottery cannot do is show that Israel came from outside. The rating below concerns the “signature” argument, not this puzzle.

Assessment

Sound, for the claim as scoped: the recurring Iron I trait-cluster characterizes the highland population that became Israel. Three lines converge on it: the cluster's continuity into the Israel and Judah of the monarchy, its fit with the Merneptah Stele's "Israel" in the same hills at the same moment, and the agreement of scholars from every camp, including the cluster's chief critic, that the villagers were early Israel.7 Two things the rating does not cover. The fingerprint form fails: no single trait is exclusively Israelite, so none can label a given site. And the cluster does not show how Israel came to be in the land: the pottery's local continuity is the open puzzle stated above, which the resolutions weighed there soften but do not settle. Proponents who present the "signature" as archaeological proof of the conquest claim more than the dirt delivers; what it does deliver is a recognizable early Israel in the hill country.
