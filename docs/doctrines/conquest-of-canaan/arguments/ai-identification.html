Is Joshua's Ai to be identified with Khirbet el-Maqatir rather than et-Tell?

Presupposition: inherited from The Conquest of Canaan in the Material Record — that Israel took Canaan as Joshua and Judges tell it, its date left open. This page works inside that presupposition: its assessment weighs a dating model of the conquest or a trace of it, not whether it happened.

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: weak — the identification does not hold on its best available case. Unlike the other conquest data on this page, the starting datum here is an open puzzle, not evidence: the site long identified as Ai, et-Tell, had no city in the Late Bronze Age for Joshua 7–8 to destroy — a fact its own excavators established, and one that holds at either proposed date of the conquest. The answer most pressed by those who hold the conquest is to relocate Ai to nearby Khirbet el-Maqatir, a small Late Bronze I fortress that was destroyed by fire. The relocation rests on real textual hooks (a small site, smaller than Gibeon, a north gate, an LB I burn in the right region), but it holds together only by importing the contested early (~1400 BC) date and an ad hoc migrating-toponym harmonization for Genesis, it is a minority position, and even sympathetic supporters have defected from it · advanced for Khirbet el-Maqatir by David Livingston, Bryant Wood, Scott Stripling and Associates for Biblical Research; the et-Tell identification is the mainstream, from William Albright and Joseph Callaway onward

Related arguments: open puzzles for the presupposition — see also Was Jericho's "City IV" destroyed c. 1400 BC (Wood) or c. 1550 BC (Kenyon)? · Do the Iron I highland villages bear a material "signature" of early Israel?

What this page does and does not decide. This is a site-identification question — which ruin in the hills north of Jerusalem is the Ai of Joshua 7–8 — and it is bound tightly to the wider early-date vs. late-date chronology dispute, because the whole case for Khirbet el-Maqatir turns on reading its fortress as Late Bronze I (c. 1500–1400 BC), the horizon an early-date Joshua needs. It is a companion to the dating of Jericho's City IV: both ask whether the archaeology of a named conquest city can be squared with Joshua only on the early date, and both are proposed “site correlations” that stand or fall with it, and both feed the general destruction-layers-cluster test of whether the burnt Late Bronze cities Joshua names synchronize into one campaign at all. This page weighs the identification on the archaeology and the text; the wiki's rule against ruling out the supernatural bears on whether a city's walls can fall by God's hand (a question taken up on the Jericho detail page), not on which mound was standing in the fifteenth century BC.

The claim

“We are confident that the ‘problem of Ai’ has finally been solved!”

— Associates for Biblical Research, summing up its Khirbet el-Maqatir excavations. The “problem of Ai,” on this telling, is not a problem with the biblical text but with the location: archaeologists dug the wrong mound. Ai was placed at et-Tell by William F. Albright (1924) and excavated there by Judith Marquet-Krause (1930s) and Joseph Callaway (1964–76), who found a fortified Early Bronze city destroyed around 2400 BC and then abandoned — no Middle or Late Bronze occupation, and so no city standing for Joshua to take. ABR's answer is to identify Ai instead with Khirbet el-Maqatir, a small fortified site about 0.6 mi west of et-Tell that shows a Late Bronze I destruction by fire — thereby, it argues, dissolving the classic objection that Ai was uninhabited when Joshua came.1

The problem it addresses: an empty mound at et-Tell

The datum that opens this argument is a difficulty, and it must be stated at full strength. Albright fixed Ai at et-Tell in 1924; Marquet-Krause's excavations in the 1930s, and then Joseph Callaway's nine seasons of the Joint Expedition to Ai (1964–76), established that et-Tell was a substantial Early Bronze walled city that was destroyed and left in ruins around the middle of the third millennium BC, with no Middle or Late Bronze Age settlement whatever; only around the early twelfth century BC did a small, unwalled Iron I village rise on the forsaken mound, with no destruction layer beneath it. So if et-Tell is Ai, there was no city there for Joshua to take, whether the conquest is dated c. 1406 or c. 1250 BC.2

Who advances the relocation

The move to detach Ai from et-Tell began with David Livingston, who in the 1970s argued that Bethel and Ai had both been misplaced and that the true Ai lay elsewhere in the vicinity. Building on that, Bryant Wood and Associates for Biblical Research excavated Khirbet el-Maqatir across 1995–2000 and 2009–2016 (Wood directing, later joined by Scott Stripling), and concluded that its Late Bronze I fortress is Joshua's Ai. The identification is chronology-driven in the precise sense that it presupposes the early (~1400 BC) conquest date: only on that date does a Late Bronze I burn line up with Joshua at all. It is a minority position; most archaeologists continue to identify Ai with et-Tell.3

The case for Khirbet el-Maqatir (steelmanned)

Set at its strongest, the Maqatir case is not a bare assertion; it rests on a set of correspondences with the text that are real textual hooks, not inventions. ABR advances some fourteen, of which four carry the weight. (1) A small site. The spies report of Ai that “they are but few” (Joshua 7:3), and Joshua 10:2 says Gibeon “was greater than Ai” — so Ai must be smaller than Gibeon. Khirbet el-Maqatir's Late Bronze I fortress covers only about 2.5–3 acres, comfortably under Gibeon's ~7–11 acres, whereas et-Tell's ~20-acre Early Bronze city is far larger than Gibeon — the wrong way round for Ai. (2) A Late Bronze I destruction by fire. Maqatir has exactly what et-Tell lacks — an occupation of c. 1500–1400 BC that ended in a burn, at the horizon an early-date Joshua requires. (3) A north gate. Joshua camps “on the north side of Ai” (Joshua 8:11), the side a gate would face; excavators found the west chamber of a two-chambered gate on the fortress's north side, with socket and pivot stones in the passage. (4) Fitting geography. Maqatir sits east of Bethel near a ravine that suits the ambush of Joshua 8, on a plausible border between the highland city-states. To reconcile this with Genesis — where Abraham pitches his tent “having Bethel on the west, and Ai on the east” (Genesis 12:8), which fits et-Tell — ABR argues the toponym migrated: Abraham's Ai was et-Tell, and Joshua's Ai (after et-Tell's ruin) was the neighbouring Maqatir.4

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The correspondences are genuine, and the size point in particular has real force: the biblical Ai is emphatically a small place, and et-Tell is conspicuously the wrong scale — a large Early Bronze town, not the “few men” of Joshua 7:3. A Late Bronze I fortress with a north gate and a burn layer in the right neighbourhood is not nothing, and Wood is a trained field archaeologist advancing a coherent reconstruction, not a crank. If one already holds the early date on other grounds, Maqatir is a materially better fit for Joshua's Ai than the empty mound at et-Tell.

Where the case does not carry. That last clause is the problem: the whole identification is conditional on the early (~1400 BC) date, and that date is exactly what is in dispute — weighed, and found to lean the other way, on the wiki's Jericho-dating and chronology arguments. Strip out the early date and the Late Bronze I burn at Maqatir no longer answers to Joshua at all. Three further weaknesses compound this. First, the migrating-toponym move is an ad hoc harmonization: it is posited precisely to save the identification from Genesis 12:8, and there is no independent evidence that the name Ai ever shifted from one mound to its neighbour. Second, critics urge that Maqatir is a small border fortress, not the “city” (Hebrew ʿir) with a king that Joshua storms — a genuine tension, even granting ABR's reply that the text itself makes Ai small; a fort is not obviously a royal town. Third, the position has suffered notable defections: Joel Kramer of Expedition Bible, once a Maqatir supporter, now argues that Maqatir does not solve the Ai problem and that et-Tell (on unpublished Late Bronze finds, weighed below) is the better candidate — a reminder that the identification is contested even inside the maximalist camp that produced it.5

The shape of the disagreement. Two facts are not really in dispute: et-Tell had no city for Joshua to destroy, and Khirbet el-Maqatir did have a Late Bronze I town that burned. The whole question is whether the second fact answers the first — whether relocating Ai to Maqatir is a discovery or a device built to fit a prior chronology. On the evidence as it stands it leans toward the latter: a minority, chronology-driven relocation, patched to Genesis by a conjectural name-migration.6

The open puzzle for the presupposition

Holding that Israel took Ai as Joshua 7–8 tells it, the empty mound at et-Tell is an open puzzle, and it is not confined to one dating of the conquest. Et-Tell held no city in the Late Bronze Age at all: it was as empty c. 1250 BC, on the late date, as c. 1406 BC, on the early one, and its only later settlement is a small Iron I village with no burn beneath it.7 The resolution weighed in the sections above is the relocation of Ai to Khirbet el-Maqatir, which works only on the early date and is rated weak. Seven other resolutions proposed by those who hold the conquest are weighed below. The puzzle is stated as a puzzle, not as a verdict on the conquest.8

Resolutions proposed inside the presupposition

Each resolution is weighed here on its evidence, with two questions put to it. Does it keep the whole presupposition — a city with a king and a gate (Joshua 7:5; 8:23, 29), smaller than Gibeon (10:2), on the east side of Bethel (Genesis 12:8; Joshua 7:2), which Israel took by ambush, burned, and made “an heap for ever” (8:19, 28) — or does it keep it only by giving part of it up? And which dating of the conquest does it serve? Two of the seven look for Ai at another site. One looks again at et-Tell for a Late Bronze city the excavators missed. Two find something less than a Late Bronze city at et-Tell: a camp among the ruins, or the Iron Age village. One moves the conquest back to et-Tell's Early Bronze city. And one holds that the story of Ai is really the story of Bethel.

The older candidates: Khirbet Haiyan and its neighbours

Before Albright fixed Ai at et-Tell in 1924, nineteenth-century explorers proposed several other ruins east of Beitin. Edward Robinson in 1838 preferred Khirbet Haiyan, at the southern edge of Deir Dibwan, “because he did not see any evidence of an ancient settlement at et-Tell,” as Bryant Wood puts it, and Claude Conder and others followed him. Horatio Kitchener championed Khirbet el-Hai, T. H. Guest the village of Rammun, and Victor Guérin a ruin later taken to be Khirbet el-Khudriya. Each would simply be the Ai that et-Tell is not, so each keeps the presupposition and suits either dating.9

How they fare. Each has since been dug or surveyed, and none has a Late Bronze town. Joseph Callaway's 1964 sounding at Khirbet Haiyan “revealed that the earliest structures there were Byzantine and the earliest sherds on the site Roman”; later surveys found a few earlier sherds, but no earlier buildings. Khirbet el-Hai has “only medieval and Ottoman occupation,” Rammun six Iron Age I sherds and the rest Hellenistic or later, and Khudriya, which Callaway excavated in 1966 and 1968, “turned out to be a Byzantine settlement, possibly a monastery.” A related suggestion, that the Late Bronze town lies under the modern village of Deir Dibwan below et-Tell, meets the same difficulty: as John Monson notes, “one would expect to find traces of Late Bronze Age pottery near the modern village, but none has been found.”10 John Bimson, who doubts that et-Tell is Ai, draws the consequence: if Bethel is Beitin, “then et-Tell is the most plausible site for Ai, since the other nearby sites are even less suitable in view of their archaeological remains.”11 On the evidence, none of these holds. A different site for Ai has to begin by moving Bethel, which is where the next resolution begins.

Move Bethel, and Ai with it: Livingston's Khirbet Nisya

David Livingston argued that Bethel is not Beitin but el-Bireh, some two miles to the south, and that Ai should then be sought east of el-Bireh rather than at et-Tell. “A new location for Bethel also presents a new possibility for the location of Ai,” he wrote in 1970, proposing “the small unnamed ruins 1 ½ miles southeast of Bireh,” later excavated as Khirbet Nisya, and adding: “Of course, this can be nothing more than a suggested location. The identification of Ai is primarily linked with correct identification of Bethel.” He dug the site for more than a dozen seasons from 1979. In 1994 he reported that ten seasons “have clearly shown that the site was occupied during the biblical periods when Ai was in existence,” listing Late Bronze I among them, and that “the topography around the site matches every detail given in the account of the destruction of Ai in Joshua 7-8.”12 It serves the early date, and it keeps the presupposition at the price of relocating Bethel as well as Ai.

How it fares. Khirbet Nisya is the right size, about 4–6 acres and so smaller than Gibeon, and the terrain suits the story. The excavation did not produce the city. Livingston's own dissertation on the first six seasons concluded that “no claim can be made that it is Ai,” though “it does not seem necessary yet to rule it out, either.” His final report puts Late Bronze II among the periods marked with a question mark “because, although some evidence for them was found in surface surveys, we have found only traces of them while excavating,” and states that “little or no architecture remains from the early periods (MB, LB, and IA).”13 Bryant Wood, who supervised two squares at Nisya in 1985, counted 39 sherds that might date to Late Bronze I, 31 of them from the fill of a Byzantine building, and concluded that they “indicate a limited presence at that time, perhaps a small farmstead.” For the wall, the gate and the burn, his verdict is the same each time: “No architecture from the Late Bronze Age I was found.” Associates for Biblical Research, which sponsored the dig, now holds that “Khirbet Nisya did not satisfy all of the biblical criteria for being Joshua's Ai since it was unfortified in the Late Bronze Age.”14 The relocation of Bethel has fared no better. Anson Rainey answered Livingston in 1971 that Beitin's identification rests on “toponymy, archaeology, topography and the ancient texts,” and it remains the consensus.15 On the evidence, this resolution does not hold, and its own sponsors have set it aside for Khirbet el-Maqatir.

A Late Bronze city at et-Tell after all: Kramer and Kennedy

The newest resolution goes back to et-Tell. In a June 2025 video, “‘The Problem’ of Joshua's Ai…SOLVED!”, Joel Kramer of Expedition Bible, formerly a supporter of Maqatir, argues that “the details in the Bible easily line up with the archaeology of the site.” As Christopher Eames of the Armstrong Institute reports it, Kramer points to “a brief written statement from John Garstang that he had found Late Bronze Age pottery at et-Tell,” and to “an imposing mound of stones that had been piled over et-Tell's gate,” a fit with the “great heap of stones” raised over the king of Ai at the gate (Joshua 8:29). His new evidence is unpublished fieldwork: “in 2019, a small team led by Kennedy had excavated a series of squares at et-Tell, in which a multitude of Late Bronze Age pottery was found—as well as a burned destruction layer.” Kramer lays the long consensus at the door of Callaway, a student of Kathleen Kenyon.16 If it held, this resolution would keep the whole presupposition at the traditional site, with Genesis 12:8 untouched and no relocated Bethel, and it would serve the early date.

How it fares. Its decisive evidence is not yet published. Eames, who calls it “welcome news if et-Tell does indeed prove to be a good fit,” concludes: “For now, we can only await detailed reports concerning this Late Bronze Age material and destruction level at the site.” He raises three cautions. Kramer's own description of the 2019 squares, “layers that were literally filled with Late Bronze i pottery, and then, into a destruction by fire,” “would seem to imply that the destruction was underneath and predated this Late Bronze i material.” The “gate” under the stone heap is an Early Bronze passage “1-meter wide,” which “had apparently already been canceled out by a later period wall” before the stones were piled. And the size objection, that et-Tell is far larger than the small Ai of Joshua 7:3 and 10:2, is not addressed.17 The older evidence also needs care. Sherds from et-Tell that Albright and Garstang had once taken for Middle and Late Bronze were reassigned by Judith Marquet-Krause's excavation in the 1930s, the Late Bronze ones to the Iron Age village of the twelfth century, as Albright acknowledged in 1934. And the gap does not rest on Callaway alone. Marquet-Krause found it a generation before him, and L.-H. Vincent, who advised her excavation, confirmed it in 1937.18 So this resolution is untested rather than refuted. Like the Late Bronze town at Jericho, it is a claim that excavation and publication can settle.

An outpost among the ruins: Vincent and Monson

Vincent stated the dilemma in 1937. In the Armstrong Institute's translation: “Either et-Tell and its impressive ruins cannot be identified with Ai, or this identification goes directly against the Bible. It matters little at this point what date is considered for the Exodus and the conquest of Canaan, in the 15th century as well as in the 13th.” His answer, as Bimson summarises it, was that “though Ai was not a city at the time of the Conquest, the people of Bethel erected there a bulwark against the encroaching Israelites, and that nothing remains of this outpost because it was hastily established and its buildings only flimsy structures”; R. K. Harrison took a similar view in 1970.19 John Monson's 2012 essay gives the fullest modern form. The Hebrew name ha-Ai means “the ruin,” and Monson suggests that “the place was nothing more than a small squatter's settlement in the vicinity of the imposing ruins,” that while Bethel flourished “The Ruin was maintained as a meager outpost or an encampment on the road to Jericho,” and that its king “may well have been a local chieftain (or ‘sheikh’).” Its “gate,” he proposes, may be the ridge it guarded on the road to Bethel. “Such a seemingly simplistic proposal would account for the ‘king’ …, ‘gate’ …, and burning in the biblical account, as well as the words of the Israelite spies concerning the site and its environs: ‘they are few’ (Josh. 7:3).”20 It suits either dating.

How it fares. It is coherent and hard to test. A short-lived camp of flimsy buildings could leave little trace, and the “few” of Joshua 7:3 sit well with it. But it has no evidence on the ground, and it keeps the text by reading its terms down: the king becomes a chieftain, the gate a ridge, the city a camp. Bimson's objection is that such theories “really fail to account for certain of the narrative's features, such as the appearance of a king of Ai … and the apparent existence of a city gate (7:5), which implies the existence of fortified walls,” and that they “seem unsatisfactory in the absence of supporting archaeological evidence and of any hints to this effect in the narratives.” Monson grants that so far “none fully accounts for the specific description of a king and a gate at Ai in Joshua's day.”21 The twelve thousand “men of Ai” who fell that day (Joshua 8:25) press hardest on a camp. And in Vincent's own form, as the Armstrong Institute reports it, the biblical account embellishes the defeat somewhat, a step that gives part of the presupposition up; Monson's form does not take that step. This resolution is live, but it rests on no evidence, and it keeps the presupposition only by reading the text's king, gate and city in their smallest sense.

The Iron Age village: Callaway

Callaway, et-Tell's excavator, wrote in 1965 that both phases of the Iron Age I village “seem to have been terminated by abandonment of the village. There is no extensive evidence of burning or violent destruction of either phase,” and that “nothing in the present evidence warrants an identification of the [Iron Age] village with the city of Ai captured by Joshua.” By 1968–69 he had reversed this: “the biblical conquest of Ai was a conquest of the small, unfortified Iron Age I village on the acropolis at Et-Tell,” an attack that “probably occurred in the twelfth century B.C.” He argued that “there is actually no demand for fortifications in the conquest accounts,” and that the evidence supports “the essential historicity” of the story.22 It keeps et-Tell, Genesis 12:8 and the “few” of Joshua 7:3, but it moves the conquest into the twelfth century, later than both the early and the late date.

How it fares. The date is the first difficulty: the Merneptah Stele already names Israel in Canaan c. 1208 BC. The second is the village. It was unwalled, and its evidence of burning, Bimson notes, “appears to be quite unextensive, pertaining to a single building,” while “Jos 8:28 records that Ai was completely destroyed by burning.” “It is a clear implication of the account,” he concludes, “that the Ai attacked by Joshua was a walled stronghold rather than an open village.”23 Callaway held the reading inside a model of Israel's origins as a largely peaceful settlement, and he later concluded that “a military conquest as described in Joshua 1–11 is without archaeological support.”24 It gives up the dates of both models and the walled city of the text, and on the evidence it does not hold.

Lower the Early Bronze Age: Courville and Habermehl

Et-Tell does have a walled city that was violently destroyed: the Early Bronze city. A few writers keep et-Tell as Ai by moving the archaeological periods so that this destruction falls at the conquest. In Donovan Courville's The Exodus Problem and Its Ramifications (1971), as Bimson summarises it, “dates for all archaeological periods in Palestine are lowered by over six centuries.” A sympathetic 1973 review sums up the result: “Israel entered the Promised Land at the close of Early Bronze III or the transitional period between Early and Middle Bronze,” when “widespread destruction of Canaanite population centers, especially Jericho and Ai, occurred.” Anne Habermehl restated it in 2024. Ai “was most likely the ruin called et-Tell today,” and “the EB IIIB destruction of Jericho backs et-Tell as Ai, because et-Tell was violently destroyed at the same time.” She grants that on the conventional dates “the divergence of the biblical and secular timelines is nearly a thousand years at the time of Jericho's destruction.”25 It is the only resolution that gives Joshua a walled city, burned, at the traditional site, and it keeps the presupposition and the Bible's own dates.

How it fares. It is a dating claim, and it needs the whole Bronze Age sequence of Canaan, with the Egyptian chronology tied to it, compressed by close to a thousand years. Radiocarbon dates on the Early Bronze cities place the end of Early Bronze III around 2500 BC, earlier than the old estimate, so the gap widens rather than closes, and the Middle and Late Bronze strata that overlie the Early Bronze destructions across the region would all have to fit into the time that remains. Bimson, who himself redated the conquest, judged that Courville's revision of Egyptian chronology “does not seem workable,” that “even within its own framework, Courville's theory concerning the Conquest contains serious inadequacies,” and that “the destruction which ended the EBA must therefore be ruled out.” Edwin Yamauchi's review of Courville called its methodology “deplorable.”26 The fit also cuts against the text on size: the Early Bronze city is the one that is larger than Gibeon, the wrong way round for Joshua 10:2, as set out above. On the evidence, it does not hold.

Ai was Bethel: Albright's transfer

W. F. Albright, who had fixed Ai at et-Tell and held a thirteenth-century conquest, answered Marquet-Krause's results in 1934. “The vicissitudes of Ai and Bethel cannot be separated; the two towns are so close together that only one could have any importance … in a given period.” Bethel, at Beitin, “fell into the hands of the Israelites, who burned it to the ground, somewhere in the thirteenth century. In tradition, since Ai was the precursor of Bethel, and was also destroyed by a foe who burned it to the ground, some eight centuries before, the former replaced the latter.” In 1939 he called it “a shifting of scene from Bethel, which was actually destroyed by the Israelites in the thirteenth century B. C., to the great neighboring ruin,” and granted that “the narrative in Joshua applies to the site of et-Tell, not to that of Beitin, so that the story cannot be based on first-hand tradition throughout, but must have aetiological elements.” He set his view against that of Martin Noth and René Dussaud, for whom the story “is entirely legendary, having a purely aetiological origin.”27 Albright's reading keeps a conquest and a burned city, and it serves the late date.

How it fares. It gives part of the presupposition up, and Albright said so: on his reading the city Joshua 7–8 names is not the one Israel took, and the story's setting at et-Tell is the work of later tradition. The text also resists the merger. Bethel's men leave their own city to pursue Israel from Ai (Joshua 8:17). The kings of Ai and of Bethel are listed separately among the defeated (12:9, 16). And Bethel's capture is told separately, by another method, in Judges 1:22–26, where a man of the city shows the spies the way in. Bimson notes that “the events surrounding the conquest of Ai in Jos 7-8 bear little relation to the fall of Bethel in Jdg 1,” and that “Bethel is mentioned as well as Ai in Jos 7:2; 8:12 and 17.”28 As an account of how the text came to be, it belongs with the critical readings. It does not resolve the puzzle inside the presupposition, and Noth's purely aetiological reading, which Albright opposed, denies the presupposition outright.

Where the puzzle stands

Of the seven, the alternative sites fail on the ground. The nineteenth-century candidates have no Late Bronze occupation, and Khirbet Nisya, the most thoroughly dug, yielded at most a farmstead's sherds and has been set aside by its own sponsors. Moving the conquest to fit a city at et-Tell fails on the dates in either direction: the Iron Age village is too late and too slight, and the Early Bronze city is too early by close to a thousand years. Albright's transfer resolves the puzzle only by giving part of the presupposition up. Two remain open. An outpost or squatter settlement among the ruins is coherent and hard to rule out, but it rests on no evidence and keeps the text by reading its king, its gate and its twelve thousand dead in their smallest sense. Kramer and Kennedy's Late Bronze finds at et-Tell, if published and confirmed, would change the puzzle more than any other resolution, since they would put a Late Bronze presence and a burn at the traditional site. As reported so far, though, the burn may lie beneath the Late Bronze material rather than above it. With Khirbet el-Maqatir, rated weak above, these two are where the puzzle now stands. The rating below concerns the Maqatir identification alone and is unchanged.

Assessment

Weak — the identification does not hold on its best available case. This is not because the Maqatir correspondences are frivolous; the small size, the north gate, and the Late Bronze I burn are real hooks, and if the early date were secure Maqatir would be a strong candidate. But the identification cannot stand on its own feet: it is wholly conditional on the contested early (~1400 BC) chronology (rated contested, leaning against at the Jericho-dating and chronology arguments), it requires an ad hoc migrating-toponym harmonization to survive Genesis 12:8, it identifies a small border fortress rather than an obvious royal city, and it has lost supporters (Kramer) even among sympathetic excavators. So the relocation is best rated a less-probable reconstruction, not a demonstrated one: it does not dissolve the “problem of Ai” so much as trade it for a chain of contested premises. What it does not warrant is the further charge that it is impossible or false — the correspondences are honest, the excavation is real, and were the early date to prevail the case would rise with it. It simply does not carry on the evidence as it stands.
