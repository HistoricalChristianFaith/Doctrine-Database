Was Jericho's "City IV" destroyed c. 1400 BC (Wood) or c. 1550 BC (Kenyon)?

Presupposition: inherited from The Conquest of Canaan in the Material Record — that Israel took Canaan as Joshua and Judges tell it, its date left open. This page works inside that presupposition: its assessment weighs a dating model of the conquest or a trace of it, not whether it happened.

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — a genuine chronological dispute over a single burn layer, but one in which the strongest independent datum cuts against the early date: everyone agrees the fortified Bronze Age town at Tell es-Sultan ("City IV") was violently burned, and the fight is purely when. Bryant Wood's revival of Garstang's c. 1400 BC date rests on a real but minority reading of the pottery; against it, high-precision radiocarbon of short-lived charred grain from the destruction layer itself returned c. 1562 ± 38 BC, and the one radiocarbon figure Wood cited for 1400 collapsed on a known calibration error. So weigh this as contested-leaning-against the c. 1400 reading — a less-probable dating on current evidence, not a clean draw · advanced for c. 1400 by John Garstang, Bryant Wood and Associates for Biblical Research, and Titus Kennedy; for c. 1550 by Kathleen Kenyon, Piotr Bienkowski, and Bruins & van der Plicht

Related arguments: open puzzles for the presupposition — see also Is Joshua's Ai to be identified with Khirbet el-Maqatir rather than et-Tell? · Do the Iron I highland villages bear a material "signature" of early Israel?

What this page does and does not decide. This is a flagship instance of the larger early-date vs. late-date chronology dispute, narrowed to one site. If City IV fell c. 1400 BC, there was a walled Jericho for an early-date Joshua to take; if it fell c. 1550 BC (end of the Middle Bronze Age), then on every proposed conquest date — early (~1400) or late (~1250) — the burned City IV is too early to be Joshua's Jericho — an open puzzle for the presupposition, stated below with the resolutions proposed for it. The destruction facts — the fierce fire, the mudbrick wall fallen outward, the unplundered grain — are common ground and are not re-argued here; this page weighs only the date, because on the date the whole correlation with Joshua 6 stands or falls. This is a radiometric and ceramic dating question, not a question about the miraculous: the wiki's rule against ruling out the supernatural bears on whether walls can fall by God's hand, not on when a carbon sample died.

The claim

"The pottery, stratigraphic considerations, scarab data, and a Carbon-14 date all point to a destruction of the city around the end of Late Bronze I, ca. 1400 B.C.E. … Kenyon's analysis was based on an argument from silence."

— Bryant G. Wood, restating the case of "Did the Israelites Conquer Jericho?" (1990) against Kathleen Kenyon. Wood charged that Kenyon dated City IV to c. 1550 BC largely from the absence of imported Cypriot Bichrome ware in a small, poor sounding — and argued that the local pottery, correctly read, is Late Bronze I. Against him, Kenyon's redating and the subsequent radiocarbon work place the burn at the end of the Middle Bronze Age, c. 1550–1562 BC. The question here is not who is more faithful to the Bible but which date the dirt and the pottery, taken whole, best support.1

Who advances it

The c. 1400 BC (Late Bronze I) date was first proposed by John Garstang, who excavated Jericho in 1930–36, tied his burnt "City IV" to Joshua, and dated it to about 1400 BC. It was buried for a generation by Kathleen Kenyon's more rigorous 1952–58 excavation, then revived in 1990 by Bryant G. Wood of Associates for Biblical Research, whose Biblical Archaeology Review article is the load-bearing modern statement; Titus Kennedy's 2023 synthesis in Religions restates and extends it. The c. 1550 BC (end of Middle Bronze) date is Kenyon's, and is the mainstream position: it was defended in detail against Wood by Piotr Bienkowski and given an independent, sample-based anchor by Hendrik Bruins and Johannes van der Plicht's 1995 radiocarbon study; the current Italian–Palestinian excavations under Lorenzo Nigro continue to date City IV's fall to the Middle Bronze Age. Because the early date is the archaeological prop of the wider early-date conquest scheme, this is one of the most keenly contested single dates in biblical archaeology.2

The evidence for

For c. 1400 BC (Wood, steelmanned). Wood's case runs on four lines, the first his primary one. (1) Pottery. He argued that diagnostic forms in the destruction debris fit Late Bronze I rather than Middle Bronze — conical bowls with concentric circles on the interior (which he called "a strong diagnostic indicator for the latter half of Late Bronze I," with parallels at Hazor, Lachish, Ashdod, and elsewhere), carinated bowls, everted-rim cooking pots, and red-and-black decorated ware — and charged that Kenyon's Middle Bronze verdict rested on the absence of imported Cypriot Bichrome ware from one small, poor trench, an argument from silence. (2) Stratigraphy. He correlated the destroyed city (his Phase 52) with Late Bronze IB strata at Shechem, Lachish (Fosse Temple I), Hazor, and Megiddo. (3) Scarabs. A cemetery northwest of the tell yielded scarabs running through the Eighteenth Dynasty — Hatshepsut, Thutmose III, Amenhotep III — which he read as evidence the site was in use across the fifteenth century. (4) Radiocarbon. He cited a British-Museum radiocarbon date of 1410 ± 40 BC from charcoal in the destruction debris, apparently converging on his ceramic date.3

For c. 1550 BC (the mainstream). Piotr Bienkowski answered Wood point by point in Biblical Archaeology Review: the destruction pottery lacks the diagnostic Late Bronze I characteristics Wood claims, the absent Cypriot imports really do argue against a Late Bronze date, and the Bichrome ware Wood invoked belongs to a later "Middle Building," not to the City IV destruction. The scarabs, on a cemetery not stratified to the burn, attest continued visits to the site, not a standing fortified city in 1400. And the decisive datum is radiometric. Wood's 1410 date came from apparatus later found to carry a systematic calibration error (the British-Museum lab's 1980–84 dates, including the Jericho sample BM-1790, were subsequently revised — the Jericho figure to roughly 3300 ± 110 BP, i.e. the mid-second millennium, not 1410 BC), so the early radiocarbon prop collapsed. Then Bruins and van der Plicht (1995) high-precision-dated eighteen samples from the destruction horizon, including six short-lived charred cereal grains from the burn layer — grain from the last harvest before the fire, the ideal sample because it fixes the year of destruction rather than the age of long-lived timber. The weighted average was 3306 ± 7 BP, calibrating to a destruction c. 1562 ± 38 BC. They concluded that the city was not destroyed by c. 1400 BC as Wood had suggested. Nigro's ongoing excavations uphold a Middle Bronze fall for City IV.4

Adversarial assessment

What holds on each side. Wood's pottery argument is not frivolous: reading Palestinian ceramic sequences is a craft with real disagreement at the Middle–Late Bronze boundary, and his charge that a Middle Bronze verdict drawn from the absence of imported ware in a small trench is methodologically fragile has force in the abstract. The mainstream case is stronger on two independent grounds that do not depend on adjudicating the pottery at all: the imported-ware chronology (whose absence Wood must explain away) and, above all, the radiocarbon — and the radiocarbon here is unusually clean, because the short-lived grain removes the "old-wood" problem that plagues charcoal dates and pins the sample to the destruction year itself.

Where the early date is overstated. Three of Wood's four props are weaker than presented. The radiocarbon prop is not merely weak but withdrawn: the 1410 date rested on a lab error since corrected, so it can no longer be cited for 1400 as though it still stood. The scarabs date a cemetery, not the burnt city, and so speak to site-visitation, not to a fortified town standing in 1400. The stratigraphic correlations inherit exactly the boundary ambiguity they are meant to resolve — they are only as firm as the Late Bronze I ceramic call they presuppose. That leaves the pottery as the real load-bearer, and it is a minority reading contested by ceramic specialists. So the early date reduces, on inspection, to one disputed ceramic argument against a convergent case of imported-ware chronology plus sample-anchored radiocarbon.

The live wrinkles, stated fairly. The radiocarbon record for Jericho is not uniformly tidy. A large 2000 study of the site's samples produced a wide scatter, yielding dates as far apart as 1347 ± 85 and 1597 ± 91 BC depending on the material and the layer — so a single crisp number oversells the precision of the whole corpus. And Nigro's excavations posit a later occupation phase ("City V") with Late Bronze remains at the tell, to which some early-date defenders now point for a fourteenth-century Jericho. Neither wrinkle, though, rehabilitates a c. 1400 destruction of City IV: the scatter still centers on the second half of the second millennium and its best-controlled subset (the short-lived grain) lands at c. 1562 BC, and a Late Bronze town built over the ruins, whatever its walls, is a different city, not a later date for City IV's burn (the proposal that Joshua's Jericho is that later city is weighed below as a resolution in its own right). The honest reading is that the burn that fits Joshua 6 so strikingly is the one dated, on the best evidence, to c. 1550 BC.5

The open puzzle for the presupposition

Holding that Israel took Jericho as Joshua 6 tells it, the radiocarbon date is an open puzzle, and it is not confined to one dating of the conquest. Short-lived charred grain from the City IV burn layer dates its destruction to c. 1562 ± 38 BC; if that holds, the burn that matches Joshua 6 so closely is too early for an entry c. 1406 BC on the early date, and far too early for c. 1250 BC on the late one.6 The wider radiocarbon scatter and the Late Bronze town over the ruins, noted above as live wrinkles, do not move City IV's date.5 Five resolutions proposed by those who hold the conquest are weighed below. The puzzle is stated here as a puzzle, not as a verdict on the conquest.

Resolutions proposed inside the presupposition

Each resolution is weighed here on its evidence, with two questions put to it. Does it keep the whole presupposition — a walled Jericho that Israel took, whose wall fell and which Israel burned (Joshua 6:20, 24) — or does it keep it only by giving part of it up? And which dating of the conquest does it serve? Three of the five keep City IV as Joshua's Jericho and move its date; one explains why the later town cannot be found; one looks for Joshua's Jericho in the later town itself.

Redate the burn: Wood and Kennedy

Bryant Wood's c. 1400 BC date for City IV, weighed above, keeps the whole presupposition and the early date, and with them every point of the match with Joshua 6. Titus Kennedy's 2023 restatement is its fullest current defence, and the way it handles the radiocarbon matters, because the date is where this resolution stands or falls. Kennedy does not dispute the 1995 measurements. Recalibrating them, he grants that they are "significantly higher than the historical dates." He gives three answers. First, the wider set of dates: the samples published in 2000 "could accommodate either proposed approximate historical destruction dates of around 1550 BC or 1400 BC if the calibration curves were accurate." Second, a general caution: "it is a known issue that the radiocarbon dates for Bronze Age sites in the Levant often conflict with the chronological information derived from ceramics and inscriptions and that some samples can be from burned wooden beams cut from trees that were harvested over 100 years before their destruction." Third, method: "dating strata by means of pottery typology continues to be the primary and most reliable method." He concludes that the radiocarbon dates "at present appear to only rule out a ca. 1230 BC destruction hypothesis" and have not settled the choice between a Middle Bronze III and a Late Bronze I destruction.7

How it fares. The old-wood caution is sound for charcoal, and it is the reason the grain was dated: a cereal grain grows in a single season, so it carries no inherited age, and old timber cannot account for the six grain dates. The two dates published in 2000 that make up the wide scatter are both charcoal from a single locus in Area F, and they are about 250 years apart (medians c. 1615 and c. 1364 BC on the current calibration curve). Old wood could make the older one too old, but it cannot make the younger one too young, so the young sample is a real datum in Kennedy's favour. It is still one charcoal date from a context whose two samples disagree, set against six grain dates from the burn itself. The current excavators' own radiocarbon now places the final destruction of the Middle Bronze city "in the third quarter of the 16th century BC (1550-1525 BC)," in line with the 1995 result.8 And the general conflict between radiocarbon and archaeological dates at the end of the Middle Bronze Age runs the opposite way from the one this resolution needs. Recent radiocarbon work places the end of the period earlier, "probably even before the start of the New Kingdom," not later.9 The resolution stays where the assessment below leaves it: contested, leaning against.

Lower the end of the Middle Bronze Age: Bimson

John Bimson's Redating the Exodus and Conquest (1978; 2nd ed. 1981) keeps City IV as Joshua's Jericho by moving the archaeological period, not the burn. Working "within the framework of the conventional Egyptian chronology," he argued that "the date for the end of the MBA should be lowered from the 16th century to the second half of the following century," and concluded: "I submit, therefore, that MBA Jericho actually came to an end in the second half of the 15th century BC, and that its attackers were the Israelites as recorded in Jos 6," "somewhere within a decade of 1430 BC." His grounds were that the conventional date rested on an assumed link with the expulsion of the Hyksos from Egypt (he found "no reason to link the fall of MBA Jericho with the political situation in Egypt and Palestine in the 16th century BC"), and on a dating of imported Cypriot bichrome ware that he judged circular.10 It keeps the whole presupposition and the early date, and, unlike the Late Bronze resolution below, it keeps City IV's fallen wall and full grain jars as Joshua's.

How it fares. Bimson's negative point has worn well. The link between the Middle Bronze destructions and the Hyksos expulsion has since been given up, by the same radiocarbon research that dates the end of the period earlier.9 But his positive proposal is the one the radiocarbon tests most directly, because the grain fixes the burn in calendar years, independently of any period label or Egyptian synchronism. Bruins and van der Plicht drew the conclusion by name: "The 14C dates do not support a low 15th century BC chronology for the end of the MBA, as Bimson (1978) proposed." Manfred Bietak, the excavator of Tell el-Dabʿa, whose lowered chronology Bimson and David Livingston leaned on in 1987, answered that he had proposed lowering an earlier transition "by about 50 years (not 100!)," and that the Late Bronze Age cannot begin as late as 1400 BC.11 On the evidence, this resolution does not hold.

Lower Egypt's chronology: Rohl

David Rohl's New Chronology (A Test of Time, 1995) goes further. It lowers Egypt's own dates by some three centuries, so that the Middle Bronze destructions fall in the biblical period of the conquest. On his reading, in the Middle Bronze IIB "Jericho at this time was a heavily fortified city – its walls fell down and it was destroyed by fire, then to be abandoned for several centuries."12 It keeps the whole presupposition and the Bible's own dates.

How it fares. The sharpest reply comes from the early date's own leading archaeologist. Bryant Wood notes that Rohl's conquest date of 1410 BC "would fall in the middle of the MB IIB period according to Rohl's scheme," and that Rohl makes Jericho's final Bronze Age city fall partway through that period, which "cannot be, however, because there are late MB IIB, MB IIC … and LB I … phases of the Bronze Age city as attested by the pottery." Wood concludes that "from a Palestinian perspective, Rohl's hypothesis is quite unworkable."13 (The Late Bronze I phase is Wood's own redating; the late Middle Bronze phases are not in dispute.) The radiocarbon, again, does not move with Egypt. Lowering the king-list does not change the calendar age of grain at Jericho, which stays in the seventeenth or sixteenth century BC however the pharaohs are dated. And radiocarbon dating of Egypt itself places the start of the New Kingdom "between 1570 and 1544 B.C.E.," close to the conventional chronology.14 So the resolution needs both Egypt's radiocarbon and Jericho's to be wrong. On the evidence, it does not hold.

The Late Bronze town eroded away: Kenyon and Kitchen

The erosion resolution gives up City IV and explains why Joshua's Jericho cannot be found. Kathleen Kenyon wrote that "of the town of the Late Bronze Age, within which period the attack of the Israelites must fall by any dating, not a trace remains," and put it down to the erosion that had destroyed much of the defences.15 Kenneth Kitchen, who holds the late date, made it a resolution. After the Middle Bronze city fell its ruins lay empty for some two centuries, in which "a great deal of the former Middle Bronze township was entirely removed by erosion"; about four centuries more of erosion followed before Jericho was rebuilt under Ahab (1 Kings 16:34). "It is for this reason, and not mere harmonization, that this factor must be given its due weight," he writes: "We will never find 'Joshua's Jericho' for that very simple reason."16 It keeps the whole presupposition and suits either dating, though Kitchen argues it for the late one.

How it fares. The erosion is real: Kenyon documented it, and it is why so little of the Middle Bronze town survives on the summit. But as a resolution it explains an absence and cannot supply positive evidence, and critics on both sides say so. Israel Finkelstein and Neil Asher Silberman count it among the explanations offered once the excavations came up empty, and report a fourteenth-century settlement "small and poor, almost insignificant, and unfortified," with "no sign of a destruction." Kennedy, defending the early date, rejects erosion, dumping and levelling alike as explanations for a missing Late Bronze II town: "the evidence for entire strata does not simply disappear from archaeological sites and is not a phenomenon observed elsewhere in the region."17 The newest excavations change the picture in two ways. Lorenzo Nigro reports that the thirteenth-century (Late Bronze IIB) layers were "heavily cut by levelling operations carried out in the Iron Age," a second way, besides erosion, for the late town to have vanished. And his team has found more of the Late Bronze town than Kenyon did (see the next resolution), so "not a trace remains" no longer stands, but what survives belongs mostly to the fifteenth and fourteenth centuries, not the thirteenth.18 For the late date, the resolution is coherent and not ruled out, but nothing corroborates it.

Joshua's Jericho is the Late Bronze town: "City V"

The newest resolution gives up City IV as Joshua's Jericho and looks for it in the Late Bronze town above, the phase the current Italian–Palestinian expedition calls Sultan V. Its basis is the excavators' own reports. Lorenzo Nigro and Jehad Yasine's report on the 2019–2023 seasons describes "scanty Sultan V (Late Bronze) remains" along the head of the Middle Bronze stone rampart (the "Cyclopean Wall"), "in a layer of in situ and fallen down orange mudbricks … which apparently belonged to a wall erected on top of Wall 4 in Sultan Va (LB I, 1550-1400 BC) after the final destruction of Sultan IVc," and a mudbrick structure behind the stone wall "apparently forming the Sultan V city-wall." They note that the German expedition, Garstang and Kenyon had each identified stretches of a Late Bronze mudbrick city wall. On the Spring Hill, the burned Middle Bronze palace was "partially reused in the Late Bronze Age," in rooms probably belonging to Garstang's "Middle Building," with pottery that points "to a date in the 15th century BC."19 A Late Bronze Jericho with a wall, built on the old rampart, is therefore now the excavators' own reading, though Nigro does not connect it with Joshua. Bryan Windle, in a 2026 series drawn from his master's thesis, builds the resolution on it: "City V, not City IV, was the Jericho that Joshua and the Israelites conquered," within a window of 1426–1346 BC. For its fall he points to the fallen red bricks that Kenyon drew at the foot of the rampart in her Trench I, which Nigro now assigns to the Late Bronze wall; to a Late Bronze house on the Spring Hill whose destruction Kenyon dated to the third quarter of the fourteenth century; to burnt debris Garstang found at the foot of the stone wall; and to Nigro's remark in a 2021 lecture that "the Late Bronze ended in some kind of conflagration," a destruction "which is, however, not clearly documented."20 It serves the early date and keeps the presupposition: a walled city, a wall that fell, a city burned.

How it fares. On current evidence this is the most promising of the five, because it is the only one that does not need the grain dates to be wrong. It accepts that City IV fell in the sixteenth century and asks what stood afterwards. Its costs are of another kind. It gives up the identification on which the striking match with Joshua 6 rests: the full grain jars and collapsed wall described on the destruction page belong, on this reading, to a city Joshua did not take, although the presupposition itself does not depend on that match. Its own evidence is thin and preliminary. The excavators call the Late Bronze remains "scanty" and "apparently" a city wall; the destruction that would be Joshua's is undated and, by Nigro's account, "not clearly documented"; and Kenyon's house destruction, set in the third quarter of the fourteenth century, falls at or just after the end of Windle's window. Windle also reads "they burnt the city with fire, and all that was therein" (Joshua 6:24) as possibly the rhetoric of ancient war reports, so that only parts of the city need have burned. That reading follows Kitchen's work on such reports, but it also lowers what the ground is asked to show. And the Late Bronze use of the Spring Hill after the city's fall has to be squared with Joshua's curse on rebuilding it (Joshua 6:26; 1 Kings 16:34). Kennedy, for his own reading, identifies the Middle Building as the residence of Eglon of Moab at "the city of palm trees" (Judges 3:13), a building rather than a refounded city.21 It does little for the late date, since the thirteenth-century town is the part that is still missing. So this is a live resolution, coherent and not ruled out. It rests on the excavators' provisional stratigraphy rather than on a dated destruction, and the final publication of the Sultan V levels will decide whether it holds.

Where the puzzle stands

Of the five, the two that keep City IV by moving a framework, Bimson's and Rohl's, do not hold, because the grain date does not depend on the period labels or the Egyptian dates they move. Wood's and Kennedy's redating of the burn itself stays contested, leaning against. Erosion explains an absence but cannot fill it. The Late Bronze resolution changes the shape of the puzzle. If the current excavators' Late Bronze wall is confirmed, the puzzle for the early date is no longer that no walled Jericho stood after c. 1550 BC, but that the walled Late Bronze town has not yet yielded a dated destruction. That is a smaller puzzle than the one this page began with, and one further excavation and publication can answer. For the late date the puzzle is much as it was: the thirteenth-century town is still largely missing, whether through erosion or Iron Age levelling. The rating below concerns the date of City IV alone and is unchanged.

Assessment

Contested, leaning against the c. 1400 BC date. This is a real dispute — Wood's ceramic argument is a genuine minority position held by a trained archaeologist, not a crank thesis, and the Middle–Late Bronze pottery boundary is a place where specialists honestly differ. But it is not a balanced tie. Three of the four lines Wood advanced for 1400 are weak or withdrawn (the radiocarbon prop collapsed on a calibration error, the scarabs date a cemetery not the city, the stratigraphy only restates the pottery call), leaving one contested ceramic reading to carry the early date against a convergent mainstream case: the imported-ware chronology and, decisively, high-precision radiocarbon of short-lived grain from the burn layer itself at c. 1562 ± 38 BC — the single strongest and most independent datum on the site, and it points to the Middle Bronze Age. So the c. 1400 reading is a less-probable dating on current evidence, not a false one and not a coin-flip. The stakes are the same as for the wider chronology dispute, of which this is the flagship case: if City IV fell c. 1550 BC, the City IV burn is too early for any proposed conquest — early or late — and the otherwise-striking correlation of the burn layer with Joshua 6 loses the one thing that would make it Joshua's Jericho: a date. Whether Joshua's Jericho can be found instead in the Late Bronze town above it is a separate question, weighed among the resolutions above; this rating concerns the date of City IV alone. This is one input to the general destruction-layers-cluster test of whether the burnt Late Bronze cities Joshua names synchronize into a single campaign at all.
