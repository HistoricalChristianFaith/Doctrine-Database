Do the patristic defenses answer the moral problem of the ḥerem?

Presupposition: inherited from The Conquest of Canaan in the Material Record — that Israel took Canaan as Joshua and Judges tell it, its date left open. This page works inside that presupposition: its assessment weighs how the Church answered the moral problem the conquest raises, not whether it happened.

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — a serious, historically dominant Christian answer, more unified and more searching than a "two roads" summary suggests: nearly every witness keeps the history, defends the deed as God's just sentence on guilt long borne, faces the death of children by name, and forbids anyone to copy it; but the hardest case finally rests on God's sovereignty over life and His hidden judgment, which is exactly where ancient and modern critics press · advanced by Origen of Alexandria and Augustine of Hippo, with Eusebius, Jerome and Theodoret; carried on by Thomas Aquinas and John Calvin

A different axis from the rest of this page. Every other argument here asks whether the conquest happened — whether a synchronism or a date holds. This one asks a moral and theological question that arises whether or not the events are historical: the book of Joshua has God command the ḥerem, the "ban" or devotion-to-destruction of the Canaanite cities — "thou shalt save alive nothing that breatheth" (Deuteronomy 20:16–17; cf. Joshua 6:21; 10:40; 11:11–14). Can that command be reconciled with a good God? The Church's answers were given early and repeated, with variations, for fourteen centuries. The witnesses who gave them are set out in date order on The Conquest of Canaan in Christian Tradition; this page weighs the answers themselves. It is kept off the evidence timeline because it does not bear on what the ground shows of the campaigns, only on what to make of them morally.

The objection

The objection is as old as the Christian use of the Old Testament. Marcion (c. 144) set the Creator God of the Hebrew scriptures against the Father of Jesus, as a lower, harsher deity, and cut the Old Testament loose accordingly. His own Antitheses are lost. Tertullian's long reply reports the charge of cruelty in general terms, with the Fall, the Flood, Sodom, the plagues of Egypt and Elisha's bears as its cases; the only Marcionite complaint he reports about Jericho is that God ordered work on a Sabbath.1 That the conquest itself was in the Marcionite dossier is attested a century later. Origen, preaching on Joshua 10 (c. 250), answers "the heretics" who suppose that Joshua's slaughter teaches cruelty, and names Marcion among those who put forward "another God as creator and judge of the law"; the Dialogue of Adamantius (c. 300) preserves a Marcionite antithesis setting Joshua's sun, held still "that he might kill more," against Paul's "let not the sun go down upon your wrath"; and Jerome answers a heretic who called Joshua "a bloodthirsty man."2 The Manichaean bishop Faustus pressed the same kind of charge against the catholic church that kept both Testaments. The count he lays against the lawgiver is "how Moses committed murder, and plundered Egypt, and waged wars, and commanded, or himself perpetrated, many cruelties"; he names Moses, not Joshua. Augustine answers it in Book 22 of the Reply to Faustus the Manichaean (Contra Faustum), and twenty years later applies the same answer to Joshua by name.3 The modern form of the objection is the same complaint stripped of Marcion's two-gods metaphysics: that a command to exterminate a population — children included — is monstrous, and "God ordered it" does not soften but sharpens the difficulty. The interpretation assessed here is the historic Christian reply that it can be answered.

Origen: the history kept, the pattern refused

Origen's Homilies on Joshua (delivered at Caesarea, c. 249–251) gave the Church its first full reading of the book. His hinge is a fact of language: in Greek the name of Joshua son of Nun and the name of Jesus are one and the same word, Iēsous. So the whole book, Origen says, is less about its ostensible subject than about Christ:

the book does not so much indicate to us the deeds of the son of Nun, as it represents for us the mysteries of Jesus my Lord.

— Homilies on Joshua 1.3 (trans. Barbara J. Bruce).4 The sentence is comparative ("not so much … as"): the deeds are ranked below what they depict, not denied. Origen states the history before he builds on it. The Amorite war is "the history of the deeds done," which "has plainly been fulfilled"; the sun's standing still was "wrought indeed according to the history"; and Christians "shall not fight as the ancients fought, nor are battles to be waged by us on earth against men" — a contrast that assumes the ancients did fight on earth against men.5 His answer to the moral charge is aimed at what the slaughter teaches:

But meanwhile Jesus slew the enemies, not teaching cruelty thereby, as the heretics suppose, but designating in the things that are done the mysteries that are to come, so that, when he has slain those kings who hold the kingdom of sin in us, we may be able to fulfill that which the Apostle said …

— Homilies on Joshua 11.6. The killing is a thing "done"; what Origen denies is that it is a lesson in killing. Its lesson is the interior war: Christ "casts out of their cities, that is, out of our souls, those who used to occupy them. And he destroys the kings who were rulers in our souls 'that sin may no longer reign in us.'"6 That figure, Origen says, is the reason the church reads these books at all:

Unless those physical wars bore the figure of spiritual wars, I do not think the books of Jewish history would ever have been handed down by the apostles to the disciples of Christ, who came to teach peace, so that they could be read in the churches.

— Homilies on Joshua 15.1.7 "Those physical wars" are the subject of the sentence. The question it settles is why a church that preaches peace reads a book of wars aloud, not whether the wars took place. Against Celsus supplies the other half: Israel's wars belonged to a real and now superseded dispensation, for "in the case of the ancient Jews, who had a land and a form of government of their own, to take from them the right of making war upon their enemies … would be to subject them to sudden and utter destruction whenever the enemy fell upon them."8

Origen does reject the letter in one class of statement: the book's formulas of totality and perpetuity. Of "Jesus took all the land" he says, "I do not see that he 'took all the land.' For how much 'land did he take,' who held Judaea alone?" On the ban at Ai he faults Jewish readers who "become cruel and thirst for human blood, thinking that the saints too struck those who inhabited Ai" so that none escaped; and against Celsus he puts Deuteronomy's "We left not anything alive" among expressions that "any one who examines carefully … will find himself unable to interpret … literally." These two passages come closest to setting aside a literal extermination, and readers of Origen divide over how far they go; a recent study observes that he "seems to say different things in different places about whether the violence depicted in that text actually happened or not."9 Taken with the homilies as a whole, the likelier reading is that Origen keeps the campaign and denies three things: that the letter is sufficient for a Christian, that it gives the Church any warrant for war, and that the sweeping formulas are literally true. His answer to Marcion is therefore mainly an answer to what the text licenses; on whether the deed itself was just he says less, beyond granting ancient Israel a real right of war.

Augustine: the just, commanded war

Augustine defends the literal command as righteous, and in doing so lays the cornerstone of Western just-war theory. His governing principle is authorization. Just as the binding of Isaac is "shocking madness" if Abraham acts on his own but "faithful and submissive" obedience when God commands it, so the "act, the agent, and the authority for the action are all of great importance."10 On that principle the wars of Moses are not cruelty but obedience:

in wars carried on by divine command, he showed not ferocity but obedience; and God in giving the command, acted not in cruelty, but in righteous retribution, giving to all what they deserved, and warning those who needed warning.

Augustine then relocates the real moral weight of war away from killing as such and onto the interior dispositions of the heart:

What is the evil in war? Is it the death of some who will soon die in any case, that others may live in peaceful subjection? This is mere cowardly dislike, not any religious feeling. The real evils in war are love of violence, revengeful cruelty, fierce and implacable enmity, wild resistance, and the lust of power, and such like.

— Reply to Faustus the Manichaean 22.74. Legitimacy, he adds, turns on proper authority: "the natural order which seeks the peace of mankind, ordains that the monarch should have the power of undertaking war"; and "when war is undertaken in obedience to God… it must be allowed to be a righteous war." He meets "resist not evil" with a distinction of times: the old ministers served God by "putting sinners to death," the new by "being put to death by sinners."11 The two moves — that the deepest evil of war is a disposition of the heart, not the death of the body, and that a war is just when it is rightly authorized and rightly intended — became foundational for the whole later tradition of just-war reasoning, from Aquinas to modern international law.12

In his Questions on the Heptateuch (c. 419) Augustine applies the principle to Joshua by name. On the ambush at Ai he gives the definition of a just war that Gratian and Aquinas would copy, and adds that "without doubt that type of war is just which God commands, since there is no iniquity in him and he knows what ought to be done to each person." On the cities where nothing that breathed was left alive he is blunt:

One should not at all think it a horrible cruelty that Joshua did not leave anyone alive in those cities that fell to him, for God himself had ordered this.

— Questions on Joshua 16, under the heading "the Lord's command, even if it seems cruel, is carried out without cruelty."13

The common answer: a sentence on guilt long borne

Augustine's "righteous retribution" names the answer the tradition gave most often, in Greek and Latin alike: the ban was God's judicial sentence on peoples whose guilt He had long endured. Its proof-text is Genesis 15:16, "the iniquity of the Amorites is not yet full," with Deuteronomy 9:4–5. Eusebius takes it as a settled precedent:

… just as in the case of the ancient foreign inhabitants of the land of promise it was said to Abraham, "The sins of the Amorites are not yet fulfilled," and if they were not yet fulfilled they could not yet be driven from their native land, but when they were fulfilled, they were then destroyed by Joshua, the successor of Moses …

— Proof of the Gospel VIII.2.14 Augustine gives the victories to "the sins of the nations they subdued" rather than to Israel's merit (City of God 16.43). Theodoret answers the charge of cruelty in a sentence: "He who accuses the prophet accuses the one who commanded these things," since the Canaanites "had done deeds deserving total destruction and had gone to the extreme of lawlessness," like the generation of the Flood and Sodom. Aquinas states it as law: in the cities of the promised land "all were ordered to be slain, on account of their former crimes, to punish which God sent the Israelites as executor of Divine justice." And Calvin adds the patience the delay displays: the nations "had been borne with for four hundred years, until their iniquity was complete. Who will now presume to complain of excessive rigor, after God had so long delayed to execute judgment?"15 Jerome adds a note of reluctance the others leave implicit. Reading Isaiah's "as in the valley of Gibeon," he calls such destruction God's "strange work": "For it is not the Lord's work to destroy those whom he created, but to do a strange work, which pertains to cruelty rather than to mercy," done only when He is driven to it.16

The children and the innocent

Retribution answers for the guilty. The tradition did not leave the innocent unaddressed: its interpreters raise the case of the children themselves, most often through Achan, stoned "with all his" for taking from the devoted spoil of Jericho (Joshua 7:24–25). Augustine asks how punishment can justly fall on some for another's sin when the Law forbids punishing sons for fathers, and answers that the prohibition binds human judges, not God, "since by his deep and invisible counsel he knows how far to extend even the temporal punishment of men." He then denies that the children were damned:

Nor, surely, is it to be believed that for the sin of their father, of which they were innocent, they were also burned after death in the punishment of the fire of hell. … By what judgment of God, then, or by what mercy, it was inflicted, whether on this man's sons or on those thirty-six men, since all of them were strangers to his sin, lies hidden with him with whom there is no iniquity.

— Questions on Joshua 9.4 (translated on the witness page). He calls the children innocent, makes their death a temporal penalty only, and leaves its reason to God's hidden judgment; had Achan come before a human judge, "it is by no means to be thought" that anyone would have been punished with him.17 Jerome puts the question as sharply as any critic — "Granted, that he himself committed a sin. What sin did his children commit, his oxen, his asses, his sheep?" — and answers with Romans 9: "Why then does he find fault? For who can resist his will?"18 Aquinas formalizes both halves. God, "Lord of life and death," inflicts death on all for the sin of the first parent, so "by the command of God, death can be inflicted on any man, guilty or innocent, without any injustice whatever"; but "human judgment cannot be conformed to God's hidden judgments," and "according to human judgment a man should never be condemned without fault of his own to an inflictive punishment, such as death."19 Calvin gives the starkest version. On Joshua 6:21: "If any one object that children, at least, were still free from fault, it is easy to answer, that they perished justly, as the race was accursed and reprobated." On 10:40 he concedes more and claims more: "Though, in our judgment at least, the children and many of the women also were without blame, let us remember that the judgment-seat of heaven is not subject to our laws."20

One further point is held in common across all these answers: the ban is not repeatable. Origen will not let the slaughter stand as a lesson in killing; Tertullian refuses Joshua's "line of march" as a warrant for Christian soldiers; Augustine and Aquinas bar human courts from punishing anyone for another's fault. Whatever God may righteously command, no Christian may cite Joshua to do likewise.21

A modern church statement

The Pontifical Biblical Commission's Inspiration and Truth of Sacred Scripture (2014), §127, is the one modern church document that takes up the ban by chapter. It calls the complex "highly problematic" and rejects one defense outright: the appeal to "God's right to distribute the land, favouring his elect, is certainly not convincing, because it disregards the legitimate claim of the native populations." It keeps the retributive answer, reading the ban as "a duty of justice, analogous to the prosecution, condemnation and execution of one guilty of a capital crime, whether an individual or a collectivity." And it reads the whole non-literally: "As the best interpreters of the patristic tradition had already suggested, the story of the epic of the conquest is to be seen as a kind of parable …; the law of extermination, for its part, requires a non-literal interpretation." By its own preface the document is not an official act of the Magisterium.22

Adversarial assessment

What holds. These are not evasions; they are serious, coherent answers that shaped Christian thought for centuries, and they are more unified than a choice between "Origen's allegory" and "Augustine's divine command" suggests. Almost every witness keeps the history. Almost every witness who addresses the deed's justice gives the same answer: a judicial sentence on entrenched guilt, delayed in patience (Genesis 15:16), carried out at God's command and by His authority alone. Origen's distinctive contribution is not a rival account of the deed but a limit on its use, and that limit is shared across the tradition: the ban is God's act once, never a pattern for the Church. Augustine's core distinctions — that killing is not by itself the worst evil of war, and that the moral quality of a lethal act depends on its authority and intention — are genuine philosophical contributions that survive well outside the biblical case. The tradition also did not look away from the children. Augustine, Jerome, Aquinas and Calvin each raise the case by name, and the answer they give is coherent on its own premises: death is a temporal penalty on mortals who will die in any case, not damnation; God, as Lord of life, wrongs no one in fixing its hour; and no human court may imitate Him. This wiki does not treat a command as unjust merely because it issues in death, nor rule out a priori that God may righteously give and take life that is His to give and take; on that premise "no human ruler may licitly order this" is not itself a refutation of "God may."

What the answers do not settle. Three things keep the interpretation short of secure. First, the answer to the hardest case is, in the end, an appeal to sovereignty and hidden judgment — Augustine's "lies hidden with him," Jerome's "who can resist his will?", Calvin's "the judgment-seat of heaven is not subject to our laws." That is a consistent position, but it is exactly where critics press: it concedes the deed and relocates the justification to sheer authority (the Euthyphro-shaped worry that whatever God commands is thereby good), and Calvin's "the race was accursed and reprobated" assigns the children a collective guilt that Augustine, calling them innocent, declined to assign. The tradition is agreed on the conclusion and not wholly agreed on why the innocent die justly. Second, the retributive answer itself has a modern Catholic critic inside the tradition: the Biblical Commission keeps "a duty of justice" but rejects the land-right defense and reads the ban non-literally — and it does so in the name of "the best interpreters of the patristic tradition," an appeal the witnesses support only in part. Origen's reading of the totality formulas and of "We left not anything alive" gives it real patristic footing; but Origen keeps the campaign, and Eusebius, Augustine, Theodoret, Aquinas and Calvin take the ban literally. Third, the strongest contemporary defenses develop a line the Fathers only began: the "hyperbole" reading (the ban-language as Ancient Near Eastern battlefield convention, not a literal body-count) and the argument that the targets were combatant strongholds rather than civilian towns. Origen's "who held Judaea alone?" and Augustine's narrowing of "all the cities" to those Joshua actually attacked (Questions on Joshua 17) are early precedents for reading the totality formulas as less than literal; but neither applies that reading to reduce the dead, and the felt need for the modern defenses is itself a sign that the classic answers, taken alone, were not found sufficient.23 That God could justly command such a judgment does not by itself establish that the command, as the text frames it, raises no moral difficulty; possibility is not the same as vindication.

Assessment

Contested. The historic Christian reply to the moral problem of the ḥerem is a genuine, weighty, and still-live position, not a weak one. It is broadly unified — the history kept, the deed defended as God's just and patient sentence on guilt, its use as a pattern for Christians refused — and it faces the death of the innocent directly rather than ignoring it. Augustine's authorization-and-intention framework is a permanent contribution to ethics, and the wiki does not count the divine-command defense out on the naturalistic premise that God cannot righteously command death. But it is genuinely disputed and falls short of settled: its answer to the hardest case rests on God's sovereignty and hidden judgment, which is exactly what its critics, ancient and modern, contest; its own exponents differ on whether the children shared the guilt; and a modern church commission has stepped back from the literal ban while claiming the Fathers for it. A serious defense that holds by one discipline's lights and is contested by another's; strong enough to take seriously, not strong enough to call the matter closed. (Distinct from the material-record question: the moral verdict does not turn on the archaeology, and vice versa. The witnesses are set out in full on The Conquest of Canaan in Christian Tradition.)
