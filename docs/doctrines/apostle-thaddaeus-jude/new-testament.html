The New Testament on Thaddaeus / Jude

Dates: c. 30–90 (the events, and the writing of the Gospels and Acts) · Wikipedia
Summary: This apostle stands tenth in every list of the Twelve, under more than one name. Matthew and Mark name a “Thaddaeus” (some manuscripts “Lebbaeus”) in the place where Luke and Acts put “Judas of James.” In John’s Gospel a “Judas, not Iscariot” asks one question at the Last Supper. The New Testament also names a Jude who wrote the Epistle of Jude and calls himself “brother of James,” and a Judas among the brethren of the Lord. Jerome took the list-names as three names of one apostle; whether that apostle is also the epistle’s Jude is a disputed reading.

Claims & evidence

“Thaddaeus” (or “Lebbaeus”) in Matthew and Mark

In the first two Gospels the tenth apostle — standing in the third group of four, after James the son of Alphaeus — is named Thaddaeus. The Received Text of Matthew doubles the name:

… James the son of Alphaeus, and Lebbaeus, whose surname was Thaddaeus; Simon the Canaanite, and Judas Iscariot, who also betrayed him.

— Matthew 10:3–4. Mark has simply “Thaddaeus” (Mark 3:18). The double name in Matthew is a textual matter: the oldest Greek witnesses read only “Thaddaeus,” a “Western” strand (Codex Bezae) reads only “Lebbaeus,” and the Received Text (and the KJV) conflates the two as “Lebbaeus whose surname was Thaddaeus,” as documented in Bruce M. Metzger’s Textual Commentary at Matthew 10:3. Catrin H. Williams judges “Thaddaeus” “more likely to be the original reading not only in Mark but also in Matthew,” and reports the proposal (citing B. Lindars, 1957/58) that “Lebbaeus” was an attempt to include among the Twelve the Levi of the call narrative.

“Judas of James” in Luke and Acts — the same slot, a different name

Luke’s two lists put no Thaddaeus in that place at all. Instead they name a second Judas — carefully distinguished from the traitor:

Matthew and Thomas, James the son of Alphaeus, and Simon called Zelotes, and Judas the brother of James, and Judas Iscariot, which also was the traitor.

— Luke 6:15–16 (cf. Acts 1:13). The Greek is Ioudas Iakōbou — literally “Judas of James,” a bare genitive. The KJV supplies “brother of James,” while the RV and RSV render it “son of James,” and Williams reports that both references in Luke–Acts “are widely regarded as denoting father and son.” The rendering bears on whether this Judas is the Jude who wrote an epistle as “brother of James” (see the epistle-authorship page). Luke’s “Judas of James” occupies the place Matthew and Mark give to Thaddaeus.

“Judas, not Iscariot” at the Last Supper

John’s Gospel never lists the Twelve, but it names a second Judas among the disciples at the supper and gives him one saying:

Judas saith unto him, not Iscariot, Lord, how is it that thou wilt manifest thyself unto us, and not unto the world?

— John 14:22. John adds “not Iscariot” to distinguish him from the betrayer. Williams writes that if, “as is sometimes suggested” (citing R. E. Brown, 1966), the obscurity of this Judas points to authentic tradition, “his naming by John may provide additional support for Luke’s contention that there was another Judas among the 12.”

Jude the Lord’s brother, and the Epistle of Jude

Among the “brethren of the Lord” named at Nazareth is a Judas.

Is not this the carpenter’s son? is not his mother called Mary? and his brethren, James, and Joses, and Simon, and Judas?

— Matthew 13:55 (cf. Mark 6:3). The short Epistle of Jude opens by naming its author:

Jude, the servant of Jesus Christ, and brother of James…

— Jude 1. Richard Bauckham (Jude and the Relatives of Jesus, 1990) reads “brother of James” as the brother of James the Just, the Lord’s brother who led the Jerusalem church, and so identifies the epistle’s Jude with Judas the brother of the Lord. John reports that the brethren did not believe in Jesus (John 7:5), and Acts lists them beside the apostles after the Ascension (Acts 1:14). Whether that Jude is also the apostle “Judas of James” is a disputed reading; see the epistle-authorship page.

Reception and debate

  • One apostle or more than one? Jerome took Thaddaeus (Matthew, Mark), Lebbaeus (a variant in the same place) and Judas of James (Luke, Acts) as three names of one man. Williams reports that J. Jeremias (1971) and R. Bauckham (2006) treat Thaddaeus and Judas of James as alternate names for the same person, while J. A. Fitzmyer (1981) suggests that the names of all twelve may not have been recalled with complete accuracy and J. P. Meier (2001) that two individuals, or a replacement of one by the other, may lie behind the two names. The New Testament records no mission or death for him.
  • Two questions of identity. One question is internal to the apostle-lists: are Thaddaeus, Lebbaeus, and Judas of James one apostle? The other asks whether that apostle is also Jude the author of the epistle, and so a brother of the Lord. On the wider debate over whether the Lord’s “brethren” belonged to the Twelve, see the perpetual-virginity timeline.
  • “Judas of James”: son or brother? The bare genitive Iakōbou is rendered “brother of James” in the KJV and “son of James” in the RV and RSV. The two renderings are set out with their proponents on the epistle-authorship page.
