Eusebius and the letters of Abgar

Dates: c. 325 (Eusebius, Church History; the Abgar correspondence it quotes purports to date c. 29) · Wikipedia (Abgar legend)
Summary: The earliest extant connected story attached to any “Thaddaeus” is the legend of King Abgar of Edessa, whom a Thaddaeus is said to have healed and converted. Eusebius gives it in full, claiming to translate documents from the royal archives of Edessa. Eusebius three times places him among the seventy (1.12.3; 1.13.4; 1.13.11), sent to Edessa by the apostle Thomas; and the Syriac telling, the Doctrine of Addai (present form dated early 5th c. by Howard, as reported by Williams), names the missionary Addai and likewise counts him among the seventy-two. The sixth-century Decretum Gelasianum lists the letters among the apocrypha. Whether this missionary is the apostle of the Twelve is a disputed reading.

Claims & evidence

Thaddaeus “one of the seventy,” sent by Thomas

Eusebius introduces Thaddaeus not among the Twelve but among the wider band of disciples, and promises the Edessa story:

They say that Thaddeus also was one of them [the seventy], concerning whom I shall presently relate an account which has come down to us.

— Eusebius, Church History 1.12.3. He then tells it: after the resurrection,

Thomas, one of the twelve apostles, under divine impulse sent Thaddeus, who was also numbered among the seventy disciples of Christ, to Edessa, as a preacher and evangelist of the teaching of Christ.

— Church History 1.13.4.

The correspondence of Abgar and Jesus — “from the archives of Edessa”

The centre of the tale is an exchange of letters. King Abgar, mortally ill, writes to Jesus begging to be healed and offering him refuge; Jesus writes back blessing him and promising to send a disciple after his ascension. Eusebius says he found the letters in the public registers of Edessa and translated them from Syriac:

You have written evidence of these things taken from the archives of Edessa, which was at that time a royal city. For in the public registers there, which contain accounts of ancient times and the acts of Abgarus, these things have been found preserved down to the present time.

— Church History 1.13.5. The appended narrative recounts how Thaddaeus, lodging with Tobias, laid his hand on Abgar “in his name” and cured him, then preached the coming, death, descent, and resurrection of Christ to the assembled city. The account closes: “These things were done in the three hundred and fortieth year” — the year 340 of the Seleucid era, about A.D. 29.

Judas Thomas, and the Syriac Doctrine of Addai

The Syriac form of the story adds a further tangle of names. It opens: “After the ascension of Jesus, Judas, who was also called Thomas, sent to him Thaddeus, an apostle, one of the Seventy” (Church History 1.13.11). Here the sender Thomas himself bears the name Judas — “Judas Thomas,” the “twin,” as the Acts of Thomas also call him — so that two apostolic Judases hover over the mission. The Syriac telling, the Doctrine of Addai (present form dated early 5th c. by Howard, as reported by Williams), names the healer of Abgar “Addai” and, like Eusebius, expressly reckons him one of the seventy-two, not of the Twelve:

After that Christ had ascended to heaven, Judas Thomas sent to Abgar, Addai the Apostle, who was one of the seventy-two Apostles.

— The Doctrine of Addai, the Apostle, tr. George Phillips (London, 1876). Whether the apostle Thaddaeus and this Addai of the seventy are the same man is set out, with its readings, on the Abgar-legend page.

Reception and debate

  • The letters among the apocrypha. The correspondence of Abgar and Jesus is listed among the writings to be rejected as apocryphal in the sixth-century Decretum Gelasianum (“Epistle of Jesus to Abgar — apocryphal; Epistle of Abgar to Jesus — apocryphal”); no modern defender of its authenticity is named in the sources surveyed here ⚠ unverified. On the apocryphal-Acts layer generally, see the apostles hub.
  • Eusebius’s placing of Thaddaeus among the Seventy. The narrative Eusebius quotes calls the man “an apostle, one of the Seventy” (Church History 1.13.11). Catrin H. Williams (Brill Encyclopedia of Early Christianity) calls Thaddaeus’s “precise identity” in Eusebius “unclear,” reports the proposal of H. J. W. Drijvers (1991) and Richard Bauckham (2006) that Eusebius may have changed “Addai” to “Thaddaeus,” and notes that Ephrem the Syrian distinguishes the apostle from “the other Thaddaeus […] of the Seventy,” who was with Abgar, while the later Greek Acts of Thaddaeus make him one of the Twelve. These readings are set out on the Abgar-legend page.
  • A later royal Abgar, and the early Edessene church. The NPNF note to Church History 1.13, citing Gutschmid and Cureton, is reported to locate the seed of the legend in a later Christian king, Abgar the Great (late second century), whose faith the story projected back onto his namesake contemporary with Jesus ⚠ unverified. Edessa was an early centre of Syriac Christianity (survey).
