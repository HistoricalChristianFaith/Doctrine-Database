Was the Thaddaeus of the Abgar legend, the missionary to Edessa, the apostle of the Twelve?

Disputed reading · on Thaddaeus / Jude · witness: Eusebius and the letters of Abgar

Related readings: Is the author of the Epistle of Jude the apostle “Judas of James”?

The passage

Thomas, one of the twelve apostles, under divine impulse sent Thaddeus, who was also numbered among the seventy disciples of Christ, to Edessa, as a preacher and evangelist of the teaching of Christ.

— Eusebius, Church History 1.13.4. The Syriac telling names the missionary Addai:

After that Christ had ascended to heaven, Judas Thomas sent to Abgar, Addai the Apostle, who was one of the seventy-two Apostles.

— The Doctrine of Addai, the Apostle, tr. George Phillips (London, 1876).1

The question

The Abgar legend tells how a Thaddaeus, or Addai, healed King Abgar of Edessa and preached there after the Ascension. Its earliest tellers place him among the Seventy; other writers make the missionary the apostle Thaddaeus of the Twelve, or set both men at Edessa. The question is which figure the Edessene tradition remembers, and so whether the tenth apostle of the Gospel lists is the founder the church of Edessa claimed.2

The missionary was Thaddaeus, or Addai, of the Seventy, sent by the apostle Thomas

In this reading Edessa was evangelized by a disciple of the wider band of the Seventy, not by one of the Twelve.

Held by: Eusebius (Church History 1.12–13, c. 325); the Syriac Doctrine of Addai (early 5th c. in its present form); Ephrem the Syrian (Commentary on Acts 1.13, 4th c.).3

Eusebius gives the story as documented history, saying he translated the correspondence of Abgar and Jesus, and the narrative of the healing, from the public archives of Edessa. He three times places Thaddaeus among the Seventy (Church History 1.12.3; 1.13.4; 1.13.11), and the narrative makes him the envoy of the apostle Thomas, who sends him “under divine impulse.” The Syriac Doctrine of Addai tells the story at length in Edessa’s own tongue and likewise reckons Addai “one of the seventy-two Apostles,” sent by Judas Thomas; it styles him “Addai the Apostle” in the sense in which the seventy-two are also apostles. Ephrem the Syrian, writing in the Syriac church, distinguishes the apostle Thaddaeus of the Twelve from “the other Thaddaeus […] of the Seventy,” who was with Abgar. The fifth-century Armenian historian Moses of Chorene is described as an independent witness to the same Edessene records ⚠ unverified. On this reading two Syriac-speaking witnesses and the Greek historian agree on the missionary’s rank, Edessa’s place as an early centre of Syriac Christianity fits a mission there in the first generation, and the tenth apostle and the Edessene evangelist are two men who shared a name.4

The missionary to Abgar was the apostle Thaddaeus of the Twelve

In this reading the tenth apostle of the Gospel lists is the missionary sent to Abgar who founded the church of Edessa.

Held by: Jerome (Commentary on Matthew 10.4, 398); the Greek Acts of Thaddaeus (possibly 7th c.).5

Jerome, commenting on the list of the Twelve in Matthew, joins the Edessa mission to the apostle of the list:

The history of the Church relates that the apostle Thaddaeus was sent to Edessa, to Abgar king of Osrhoene; he is called Judas of James by the evangelist Luke, and elsewhere he is named Lebbaeus, which is interpreted 'little heart'; and we must believe that he had three names…

On this reading the healer’s name is the apostle’s: Greek “Thaddaeus,” Syriac “Addai.” The Syriac source repeatedly calls the missionary “Addai the Apostle” and tells the whole enterprise as an apostolic founding. On this reading Eusebius’s placing among the Seventy is a secondary gloss. The Acts of Thaddaeus, a Greek expansion of the legend, makes Thaddaeus a Hebrew named Lebbaeus who came to Jerusalem from Edessa, received the name Thaddaeus at his baptism, and was chosen by Jesus as “the tenth apostle according to the evangelists Matthew and Mark”; he preaches before Abgar, baptizes him and his household and the citizens of Edessa, and dies at Berytus. On this reading the many-named apostle of the lists and the founder of Edessa are one man, and the Edessene church’s memory is of an apostle of the Twelve.6

The story took shape after the apostolic age, and its missionary is Addai under a borrowed name

In this reading the Abgar legend reflects the Christian Edessa of a later period, and the name Thaddaeus was attached to an Edessene Addai.

Held by: Walter Bauer (Rechtgläubigkeit und Ketzerei im ältesten Christentum, 1934); H. J. W. Drijvers (“The Abgar Legend,” 1991); Richard Bauckham (Jesus and the Eyewitnesses, 2006), on the change of name.7

On this reading the legend’s seed is a Christian Edessa of a later reign, that of Abgar VIII “the Great” (late second century), whose faith the story projected back onto his namesake contemporary with Jesus ⚠ unverified. Bauer held that catholic Christianity reached Edessa after Marcionite and Bardaisanite forms of Christianity were established there, and that the succession the legend builds, from Addai to Palut to Serapion of Antioch to Rome, was constructed to give that community an apostolic pedigree. Drijvers and Bauckham, as reported by Catrin H. Williams, propose that if the underlying Syriac tradition is earlier than Eusebius, Eusebius may have changed “Addai” to “Thaddaeus,” forging a secondary identification that tied Thaddaeus to Edessa in Syriac Christianity. Drijvers is also reported to have dated the legend’s formation to the late third century, with an anti-Manichaean purpose ⚠ unverified. The Doctrine of Addai is dated in its present form to the early fifth century (Howard, as reported by Williams), and the correspondence of Abgar and Jesus was listed as apocryphal in the sixth-century Decretum Gelasianum. On this reading “Addai” is the proper Syriac name of Edessa’s remembered founder, and the Greek “Thaddaeus” is a later overlay.8

The apostle of the Twelve and a Thaddaeus of the Seventy-two both preached in Abgar’s domains

In this reading the apostle Thaddaeus came to Edessa, worked there beside a disciple of the same name, and went on to found the church of Armenia.

Held by: the Armenian Apostolic Church (liturgical calendar, “Holy Apostles Thaddeus and Bartholomew”).9

The Armenian Apostolic Church venerates Thaddaeus, one of the Twelve, with Bartholomew, as an apostle who carried the Gospel to Armenia. In its account Thaddaeus preached in Palestine, Syria and Caesarea of Cappadocia, then went to Edessa and, “together with Thaddeus, one of the Seventy-two Disciples of Christ,” preached in northern Mesopotamia within the domains of King Abgar. By Abgar’s letter the apostle set out for Abgar’s nephew Sanatruk in Greater Armenia. There Princess Sandukht became his disciple and was baptized by him, and she and other Christians were put to death by order of Sanatruk, who had proclaimed himself king after Abgar’s death. The apostle’s burial place is honoured at the monastery of St Thaddaeus in Artaz, whose pilgrimage UNESCO inscribed in 2020. On this reading the Seventy-two disciple of Eusebius’s account and the apostle of the Twelve are both remembered at Edessa, and the apostle’s own mission runs from Edessa into Armenia.10
