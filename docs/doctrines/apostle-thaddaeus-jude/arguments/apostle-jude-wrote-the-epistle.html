Is the author of the Epistle of Jude the apostle “Judas of James”?

Disputed reading · on Thaddaeus / Jude · witness: the New Testament

Related readings: Was the Thaddaeus of the Abgar legend the apostle of the Twelve?

The passage

Jude, the servant of Jesus Christ, and brother of James, to them that are sanctified by God the Father…

— Jude 1. Beside it stand the lists of the Twelve in Luke and Acts, which name an apostle Ioudas Iakōbou, “Judas of James” (Luke 6:16; Acts 1:13), and the Nazareth passage naming the Lord’s brethren “James, and Joses, and Simon, and Judas” (Matthew 13:55).1

The question

The epistle’s author calls himself “brother of James”; one apostle of the Twelve is “Judas of James.” The question is whether the two are one man, so that an apostle of the Twelve wrote the letter, or whether the author is Jude the brother of the Lord, a man distinct from the apostle. A further view, that the letter was written in Jude’s name by a later writer, is mentioned in the reference survey cited here; no proponent of it is named in the sources surveyed for this page.2

The apostle “Judas of James” is Jude the brother of James, and wrote the letter

In this reading one Judas carried the apostolate, the kinship to Jesus, and the canonical letter.

Held by: Ephrem the Syrian (Commentary on Acts 1.13, 4th c.) and the Ethiopic Contendings of the Apostles (on the kinship only); Bede (8th c., on the authorship) ⚠ unverified.3

The case rests on the pairing of two names. The apostle is “Judas of James” (Luke 6:16; Acts 1:13), an apostle named Judas defined by a James; the epistle’s author is “Jude… brother of James,” again a Jude defined by a James. A bare genitive can denote a brother as well as a father, and the King James Version renders Luke 6:16 “Judas the brother of James”; read so, Luke’s apostle and the epistle’s author bear the same description. Ephrem the Syrian makes this apostle the brother of Simon the Zealot and a son of Joseph, and so a brother of Jesus, placing a brother of the Lord within the Twelve; the Ethiopic Contendings speak of “the blessed Judas, the brother of our Lord, [who was surnamed] Thaddaeus.” Once the apostle is a brother of the Lord, the Judas of the Nazareth list (Matthew 13:55) and the Jude of the letter are the same family member, and the letter’s “brother of James” names the apostle’s own brother. On this reading, attested in Bede, one honoured name carries both an apostolic office and a canonical letter, and the letter speaks with the authority of one of the Twelve.4

The author is Jude the brother of the Lord, distinct from the apostle Judas son of James

In this reading there were two men named Judas: an apostle who was the son of a James, and a brother of the Lord, brother of James the Just, who wrote the letter.

Held by: Richard Bauckham (Jude, 2 Peter, 1983; Jude and the Relatives of Jesus in the Early Church, 1990).5

Bauckham takes the bare genitive Iakōbou in Luke 6:16 as paternity, “Judas son of James,” as the Revised Version and the Revised Standard Version render it; Catrin H. Williams reports that both references in Luke–Acts are widely regarded as denoting father and son. The epistle’s author, by contrast, is explicitly a brother of James (Jude 1), and a James who needs no further identification is, on this reading, James the Just, the Lord’s brother who led the Jerusalem church. The author is then the Judas named among the brethren at Nazareth (Matthew 13:55). The Gospels and Acts place those brethren in a group of their own: John reports that they “did not believe in him” (John 7:5); Acts lists them after the Ascension beside the apostles (Acts 1:14); and Paul names “the brethren of the Lord” alongside “the other apostles” (1 Corinthians 9:5). The letter’s author bids his readers “remember… the words which were spoken before of the apostles of our Lord Jesus Christ” (Jude 17), a phrase this reading takes as speaking of the apostles as a company to which the writer refers from outside. On this reading the letter belongs to a brother of the Lord, and the apostle Judas son of James left no writing.6
