Denis Pétau (Petavius) on Reckoning the Age of the World

Dates: 1583–1652 (Opus de doctrina temporum 1627; Rationarium temporum 1633) · Wikipedia
Summary: Denis Pétau — Dionysius Petavius in Latin — the French Jesuit who carried on and surpassed Scaliger's technical chronology. His Opus de doctrina temporum (1627) systematised the reckoning of years and, above all, popularised counting years ante Christum ("before Christ") backward from the Incarnation — the proleptic BC/AD framework that every later chronologer, Ussher included, then used to state a date. Where Scaliger built the neutral astronomical spine, Petavius fixed the era-count in the form the modern world still keeps: the reason "4004 BC" or "the Flood in 2348 BC" can be written as a plain number at all.

Claims & evidence

Carrying on Scaliger — Opus de doctrina temporum (1627)

Petavius took up chronology where Scaliger left it and, by common judgement, carried it further. His Opus de doctrina temporum (Paris, 1627) compiled a universal history from the earliest times down to his own day, digested in strict chronological order and documented throughout by reference to the ancient authorities; it "surpassed Scaliger's De emendatione temporum… and prepared the ground for the works of the Benedictines" who perfected the diplomatic dating of documents.1 His scholarly stature in the field was such that he claimed to have caught some eight thousand errors in Baronius' great ecclesiastical Annals. The Opus was reprinted through the century, and its abridgement, the Rationarium temporum (1633), was translated into French and English and kept up to date well into the nineteenth century.

Reckoning years "before Christ" — the proleptic BC/AD frame

Petavius' most consequential and most durable contribution was to systematise and popularise counting years backward from the Incarnation. The Anno Domini reckoning of Dionysius Exiguus (6th c.) numbered years forward from the birth of Christ but had no settled way of numbering the years before it; chronologers stated pre-Christian dates by Anno Mundi, by Olympiads, by the regnal years of kings, or by the founding of Rome. In the De doctrina temporum Petavius reckoned pre-Christian years proleptically as ante Christum ("before Christ"), counting backward from the era so that any point in ancient history could be given a single BC number continuous with the AD count.2 The convention spread through the reprintings and translations of the Rationarium — in English the years were marked "A.C." or "Ant. Chri." — and became the ordinary BC/AD framework of Western historiography. It is why the biblical anchors this timeline tracks can be, and now always are, quoted as plain BC numbers.

Petavius did not, strictly, invent the idea: isolated "before the incarnation" datings appear as early as Bede's Ecclesiastical History (Caesar crossed to Britain "in the sixtieth year before the incarnation of the Lord"), grow commoner from the thirteenth century, and appear in the graphic time-lines of the fifteenth (Werner Rolevinck's Fasciculus temporum, 1474). Scaliger too had discussed the shortcomings of ante Christum dating against his own Julian Period. What Petavius did was to standardise the proleptic count and make it the working convention of chronology — the sense in which the BC era is rightly traced to him.3

Notes

  • Why he sits on this timeline — the frame, not the figures. Like Scaliger, Petavius' bearing here is on the form in which a biblical date is stated rather than on which text-tradition's numbers are right. He supplied the BC/AD count that Ussher would use to write "4004 BC"; the choice between the Masoretic short and Septuagint long primeval intervals he inherited unresolved and passed on.
  • The pair that ends the patristic-to-modern bridge. Scaliger (the astronomical spine and the recovery of Eusebius) and Petavius (the BC/AD reckoning) together are the early-modern hinge between the ancient Anno Mundi chronographers and Ussher's canonical Annals; after them, biblical and secular chronology are stated on one common, convertible frame.
  • A theologian as well as a chronologer. Petavius is remembered in doctrine equally for his unfinished Dogmata theologica, a pioneering historical treatment of the development of dogma; the chronology, though, is what his own age prized him for and what bears on this timeline.
