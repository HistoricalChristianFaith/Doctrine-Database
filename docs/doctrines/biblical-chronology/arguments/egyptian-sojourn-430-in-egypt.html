Did Israel dwell in Egypt a full 430 years?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — read at face value the Masoretic text of Exodus 12:40 says the sojourning of Israel "who dwelt in Egypt" was 430 years, so the descent under Joseph and the Exodus stand 430 years apart. This is the harder reading of a plainly-worded verse, it is the text the Dead Sea Exodus scroll carries, and it fits Genesis 15:13's "four hundred years" of Egyptian affliction and the explosive growth of seventy persons into a nation. But it is genuinely disputed: the Levi–Kohath–Amram–Moses genealogy of Exodus 6 cannot span 430 years unless read as telescoped, and Paul's "four hundred and thirty years" in Galatians 3:17 — counted from the promise to Abraham, not from the descent — is most naturally read against it. Strong on the Hebrew text's own wording and manuscript pedigree, weaker on the internal genealogy and the Pauline datum — a contested question with a live rival.

Related arguments: the length of the Egyptian sojourn — see also the ~215-year reading (the rival, which reads the 430 as running from Abraham through Canaan and Egypt, halving the time in Egypt). The date of the Exodus itself is weighed under the date of the Exodus.

The claim

Now the sojourning of the children of Israel, who dwelt in Egypt, was four hundred and thirty years. And it came to pass at the end of the four hundred and thirty years, even the selfsame day it came to pass, that all the hosts of the LORD went out from the land of Egypt.

— Exodus 12:40–41 (KJV, following the Masoretic Hebrew), Exodus 12:40. Read at face value the verse fixes the interval in Egypt — from Jacob's household coming down to Joseph until the Exodus — at 430 years. The interpretation assessed here is that this is the original text and its plain sense: a long Egyptian sojourn of some four and a third centuries, against the rival reading that folds the patriarchs' 215 years in Canaan into the 430 and leaves only about 215 years actually in Egypt.1

Who advances it

The long sojourn is the plain reading of the received Hebrew and the majority reading of modern text critics, who hold the Masoretic "in Egypt, 430 years" to be the earlier text and the Greek and Samaritan "in Egypt and in Canaan" a later clarifying expansion. It is defended in the standard critical commentaries (William H. C. Propp's Anchor Bible Exodus) and by conservatives who take the Hebrew at face value and resolve the genealogy by telescoping; it is the reckoning most Egyptological Exodus reconstructions assume when they place the descent four centuries before the departure.2 It is set here against its advocated rival, the ~215-year reading of the Septuagint, the Samaritan Pentateuch, Josephus, and the rabbis.

The evidence for

The plain Hebrew, and the harder reading. The Masoretic verse is unambiguous: Israel "dwelt in Egypt" 430 years. The rival versions read "in the land of Egypt and in the land of Canaan" — and that longer reading is exactly the kind a scribe adds to smooth a difficulty, not one he deletes. By the ordinary canon that the shorter, more difficult reading is likelier original (lectio brevior, lectio difficilior), the Hebrew "in Egypt" is the earlier text and the "and in Canaan" a harmonizing gloss inserted to reconcile the 430 with the short genealogy and with Paul. The Samaritan copy compounds the expansion further, adding "and of their fathers" on top of "and in Canaan" — a patch upon a patch, betraying the direction of growth.3

The Dead Sea witness sides with the Hebrew. The point is not merely a rule of thumb. A fragment of Exodus from Qumran, 4Q14 (4QExodc), preserves verse 40 in the short form — the sojourn "in the land of Egypt," without the Canaan clause — so the Masoretic reading is attested in a manuscript centuries older than the great Greek codices. The oldest Hebrew evidence and the standard Hebrew text agree against the versional expansion.4

The affliction figure fits a long Egypt. Genesis 15:13 tells Abram his seed will be strangers in a land not theirs and be afflicted "four hundred years"; Stephen repeats it in Acts 7:6. On the long reading this 400 is simply the round number for the 430 years actually spent in Egypt — a nation held in a land not its own and, for most of that span, enslaved. The shorter reading must instead detach the 400 from Egypt altogether and start its clock generations earlier, at Isaac; the long reading takes the affliction where the text puts it.5

Seventy souls into a nation. The household that went down to Egypt numbered seventy; the host that came out is reckoned at six hundred thousand men on foot besides women and children — a population of the order of two million. That increase is demanding in four and a third centuries and severe in half that time. The long sojourn gives the growth the room it needs; a 215-year Egypt compresses roughly a thousand-fold multiplication into about six or seven generations, which strains harder still.6

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The textual case is the strong one and it should not be waved away. The Masoretic "in Egypt" is the harder reading, it is the shorter reading, and it is the reading of the oldest Hebrew manuscript of the verse; the versional "and in Canaan" bears every mark of a secondary harmonization, the Samaritan's extra clause proving that the tradition was still growing. Grant the Hebrew its plain sense and the affliction figure and the population increase fall into place. The burden on the rival is real: it must carry a reading that the best Hebrew evidence does not support.

Where it is contested — the Levite genealogy. The sharpest objection is internal. Kohath was already born when Jacob's family came down to Egypt (Genesis 46:11); his grandson through Amram is Moses (Exodus 6:16–20). The lifespans given — Kohath 133, Amram 137, Moses 80 at the Exodus — do not reach 430 even added end to end (133 + 137 + 80 = 350), and since a father's years overlap his son's the real span is far shorter. Genesis 15:16 likewise says the return would come "in the fourth generation." The long reading must therefore treat the Exodus 6 list as a telescoped genealogy — a selective line of clan-heads that skips generations, "Amram" naming an ancestral house rather than Moses' literal father — which biblical genealogies demonstrably do elsewhere. That answer is coherent and has parallels, but it is a concession the text does not force, and it is the rival's best foothold.7

Paul's 430 runs the other way. In Galatians 3:17 Paul says the Law came "four hundred and thirty years after" the covenant confirmed to Abraham — measuring the 430 from the promise to Sinai, which brackets the patriarchs' Canaan years inside the total and leaves the Egyptian stay far shorter. That is precisely the arithmetic of the ~215 reading, and it is an apostolic datum, not a scribal variant. The long reading answers that Paul follows the Greek text current in his churches, or cites the 430 as a familiar round span without adjudicating where it starts — a defensible reply, but one that concedes the most authoritative first-century voice reckoned the interval the other way.8

Assessment

Contested. The long sojourn is the stronger reading of the text: the Masoretic "in Egypt, 430 years" is the harder and shorter reading, it is what the Qumran Exodus scroll carries, and the versional "and in Canaan" looks like a harmonizing expansion — with Genesis 15:13's affliction and the population increase both fitting the long span. On the manuscript evidence it has the better of the argument. But it is genuinely disputed, and its weaknesses are internal: the Exodus 6 genealogy reaches Moses in too few generations to fill 430 years unless read as telescoped, and Paul in Galatians 3:17 counts his 430 from the promise, not the descent. The verdict tracks the standoff rather than a proponent's confidence: strong by the text-critical discipline's lights, weaker by the internal-chronology and New-Testament ones, with no clear winner. The opposing steelman, which takes the same figures the other way, is weighed on the ~215-year page.
