Did Israel dwell in Egypt only about 215 years?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — on this reading the 430 years of Exodus 12:40 cover the whole sojourning of Abraham's seed, in Canaan and Egypt, so the time actually in Egypt is only about half — some 215 years (the rabbis reckon ~210). It has a formidable pedigree: the Septuagint and Samaritan Pentateuch both read "in Egypt and in Canaan," Paul in Galatians 3:17 counts the 430 from the promise to Abraham, Josephus states 215 years in Egypt outright, and the short Levi–Moses genealogy fits it far better than a 430-year Egypt. Its weakness is textual: the oldest Hebrew evidence — the Masoretic text and a Qumran Exodus scroll — reads "in Egypt" only, and the "and in Canaan" of the versions looks like a secondary harmonization. Strong on the New Testament, the genealogy, and the tradition; weaker on the manuscript evidence — a contested question with a live rival.

Related arguments: the length of the Egyptian sojourn — see also the 430-in-Egypt reading (the rival, which takes the Masoretic "who dwelt in Egypt, 430 years" at face value). The date of the Exodus itself is weighed under the date of the Exodus.

The claim

And the sojourning of the children of Israel, while they sojourned in the land of Egypt and the land of Chanaan, was four hundred and thirty years.

— Exodus 12:40 in the Septuagint (Brenton), the Samaritan Pentateuch reading likewise "in the land of Canaan and in the land of Egypt" (and adding "and of their fathers").1 On this reading the 430 is the whole span from Abraham's entry into Canaan to the Exodus. Since Scripture puts 215 years between Abraham's call and Jacob's descent into Egypt (Abraham 75 at the call and 100 at Isaac's birth, Isaac 60 at Jacob's, Jacob 130 at the descent: 25 + 60 + 130 = 215), the time actually in Egypt is the remaining ~215 years. The interpretation assessed here is that this shorter Egyptian stay, not a full 430 years in Egypt, is the true reckoning.

Who advances it

The short sojourn is the older harmonizing tradition and it commands an impressive roster of witnesses: the Septuagint and the Samaritan Pentateuch in their text of Exodus 12:40; the Apostle Paul, read as dating the Law 430 years from the promise (Galatians 3:17); Josephus, who states the 215 explicitly; and the rabbinic chronology of Seder Olam Rabbah and Rashi, which reckons the descent-to-Exodus at about 210 years by counting the 430 from the "covenant between the pieces." James Ussher and Isaac Newton both adopted it — Ussher dating the descent to 1706 BC and the Exodus to 1491 BC, a 215-year Egypt, even while he reckoned the primeval chronology from the Hebrew.2 It is set here against its advocated rival, the 430-in-Egypt reading of the Masoretic text.

The evidence for

Paul counts from the promise. The strongest point is apostolic. In Galatians 3:17 Paul says the Law came "four hundred and thirty years after" the covenant God confirmed to Abraham. He measures the 430 not from the descent into Egypt but from the promise — which necessarily folds the patriarchs' Canaan years into the total and leaves the Egyptian stay far shorter. This is not a scribal variant but a New-Testament writer, reading the very 430 of Exodus 12:40 as the span from Abraham to Sinai. On the long reading Paul's arithmetic is simply wrong; on the short reading it is exact.3

Two versions and Josephus. Paul does not stand alone. The Septuagint — the Bible of the apostolic church — and the independent Samaritan Pentateuch both spell out that the 430 covers Canaan as well as Egypt, so two separate textual traditions carry the reading Paul assumes. And Josephus states the figure without ambiguity: Israel left Egypt "four hundred and thirty years after our forefather Abraham came into Canaan, but two hundred and fifteen years only after Jacob removed into Egypt." A first-century Jewish historian, two ancient versions, and an apostle converge on the same reckoning.4

The genealogy fits. The short Egypt resolves a strain the long reading must explain away. Kohath was born before the descent into Egypt (Genesis 46:11); his grandson Moses was 80 at the Exodus (Exodus 6:16–20; 7:7). The lifespans given — Kohath 133, Amram 137, Moses 80 — cannot fill 430 years even added end to end (they total 350, and overlap), and Genesis 15:16 says the return would come "in the fourth generation." Four generations and these lifespans sit naturally across ~215 years and awkwardly across 430. The plainest reading of the genealogy is the short one.5

The rabbinic reckoning agrees. The oldest post-biblical Jewish chronology reaches the same result from its own side. Seder Olam Rabbah and Rashi count the 430 from the covenant of Genesis 15 and the 400 of affliction from Isaac's birth, leaving about 210 years in Egypt — and Rashi notes expressly that Kohath's and Amram's years "fall far short of 430." The Jewish tradition that transmitted the Hebrew text nonetheless reckoned the Egyptian stay short.6

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The internal and traditional case is genuinely strong. Paul's Galatians 3:17 is the decisive datum: an apostle reading the 430 as the span from the promise, which no long-sojourn reckoning accommodates without treating Paul as loose or mistaken. The genealogy and the "fourth generation" of Genesis 15:16 point the same way, and two ancient versions, Josephus, and the rabbis all carry the short reckoning. Ussher's adoption of it — against his own preference for the Hebrew everywhere else — shows how heavily the New-Testament and genealogical evidence weighs even on a Masoretic-minded chronologer.

Where it is contested — the text is probably secondary. The weakness is at the root: the reading's headline proof-text is likely not original. The Masoretic Hebrew reads "who dwelt in Egypt, 430 years," and a Qumran Exodus scroll (4Q14) preserves that same short form — so the oldest Hebrew evidence lacks the "and in Canaan" clause. By the ordinary text-critical canons the Hebrew "in Egypt" is the harder and shorter reading and the versions' longer reading looks like a harmonizing expansion — added precisely to fix the genealogy and Paul — with the Samaritan's further "and of their fathers" betraying a still-growing gloss. If so, the short sojourn cannot rest its weight on Exodus 12:40; it must lean on Paul and the genealogy while conceding the verse itself, in its earliest form, tells the other way.7

The affliction figure needs a later start. Genesis 15:13 and Acts 7:6 give 400 years in which the seed is "a stranger in a land that is not theirs" and afflicted. On the long reading this is simply the Egyptian sojourn rounded; on the short reading the 400 cannot be the Egyptian stay (only ~215) and must be started earlier — commonly at Isaac's weaning and Ishmael's mocking (Genesis 21:9; the "persecution" Paul names in Galatians 4:29), so that the 400 spans the whole affliction of the seed from Isaac to the Exodus. That is coherent and has old support, but it is a harmonization: it detaches the "land not theirs" from Egypt, where Genesis 15:13 seems to locate the servitude.8

A tighter population squeeze, and a divided figure. The seventy who went down became a host of six hundred thousand men; compressing that increase into ~215 years is a harder demographic ask than into 430 (rapid growth is stated in Exodus 1:7, so it is not impossible, but the shorter span strains more). And the tradition is not even united on the number: Josephus and the Greek give 215, the rabbis about 210 — a small discrepancy, but a reminder that the short reckoning is a computed harmonization, not a figure the text states.9

Assessment

Contested. The short sojourn is the stronger reading of the internal chronology and its witnesses: Paul in Galatians 3:17 counts the 430 from the promise, the Levi–Moses genealogy and the "fourth generation" fit ~215 years far better than 430, and the Septuagint, the Samaritan Pentateuch, Josephus, the rabbis, and Ussher all carry the short reckoning. Anyone who dismisses it has underweighted the apostolic datum and the genealogy. But it is genuinely disputed, and its weakness is textual: the Masoretic Hebrew and a Qumran Exodus scroll read "in Egypt" only, so the "and in Canaan" that anchors the reading is most likely a secondary harmonizing expansion, and the 400-year affliction must be re-started earlier to fit. The verdict tracks the standoff rather than a proponent's confidence: strong by the New-Testament and internal-genealogy disciplines' lights, weaker by the text-critical one, with no clear winner.
