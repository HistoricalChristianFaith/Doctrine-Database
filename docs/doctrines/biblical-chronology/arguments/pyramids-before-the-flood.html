Were the pyramids built before the Flood?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline; a secular-anchor question feeding the scenario ledger) · Assessment: weak as a claim about the pyramids, and the finding is that it is the wrong question. The Giza pyramids are dated c. 2600–2500 BC by two independent methods that agree, and that date does not move with anyone's Bible. Whether it lands "before the Flood" is therefore a verdict about the biblical yardstick chosen, not about the monuments: on the Masoretic short reckoning (Ussher's Flood at 2348 BC) the pyramids predate the deluge by a century and a half, an embarrassment the short chronology can escape only by compressing Egyptian history — a repair that runs against the very radiocarbon it would need and against the linear Old Kingdom sequence it would have to fold up. On the Septuagint long reckoning (Flood c. 3300–3000 BC) the whole problem dissolves and the pyramids fall comfortably in a repopulated post-Flood Egypt. The compression repair is weak; the "predate the Flood" charge is date-dependent — true against one biblical number, false against another, and in neither case a fact about the stones.

This is the marquee discriminator among the reckoning systems weighed on the scenario ledger: the same anchor reads ✓ under one presupposition-bundle and ✗/△ under another. It is the pyramid-specific sharpening of the broader collision weighed at the uninterrupted-civilizations argument (the whole third-millennium record runs unbroken across a young Flood date), and it turns on how securely Egyptian chronology is fixed — the question of how load-bearing the Sothic cycle is. The who-built-them question (native labour, no lost super-technology) is a separate matter not at issue here; this page weighs only the dating sub-claim.

The two numbers

Conventional Egyptology dates the three Giza pyramids to the Fourth Dynasty, in a span of about eighty-five years under Khufu, Khafre, and Menkaure — "between 2589 and 2504 BC," in Mark Lehner's summary of the historical reckoning.1 Set that beside the biblical Flood dates this topic already weighs on the dating of the Flood:

  • Masoretic short / Ussher: the Flood at 2348 BC on the received Hebrew figures. The pyramids then predate the Flood by roughly 150–240 years — an unbroken, still-occupied Egypt is quarrying millions of tonnes of limestone and building the largest stone monuments on earth before a global deluge that, Scripture says, left eight survivors and covered the mountains. This is the sharpest single embarrassment the short chronology faces, and its own defenders name it as a problem to be solved.2
  • Septuagint long: the Flood c. 3300–3000 BC on the Greek figures. The pyramids fall five to seven centuries after it, in a repopulated post-Babel Egypt — no collision at all. The anchor fits the long bundle and breaks the short one, which is exactly why it discriminates.3

How securely the pyramids are dated — and which way the physics pushes

The pyramids' own date is one of the better-anchored in the ancient world, and it rests on two methods that do not share an assumption. The first is dead-reckoning from the king-lists: the Fourth Dynasty's place is fixed by summing reign-lengths backward from later, astronomically anchored points, using the Turin Royal Canon, the Palermo Stone annals, and Manetho's dynasty framework — the same skeleton weighed at the Sothic-cycle page, where the Egyptian spine is shown to be multiply anchored (lunar dates, the 763 BC-fixed Near-Eastern synchronisms, and radiocarbon) to within decades, not centuries.4

The second is independent radiocarbon, and here is the decisive turn for anyone hoping to move the date: it runs the wrong way for a compressor. The Ancient Egypt Research Associates / ETH-Zürich pyramid dating programme sampled charcoal and organic matter baked into the pyramids' own construction mortar. Far from making the monuments younger, the radiocarbon came out consistently older than the historical dates: the 1984 campaign averaged "374 years earlier than expected," the 1995 campaign "100 to 200 years older than the Cambridge Ancient History dates," and the full Bonani & Wenke programme (269 measurements, published in Radiocarbon 2001) found its largest offsets in the Fourth Dynasty, most dates 100–300 years older than the king-list figures.5 A short-chronology reader needs the pyramids younger — pushed down below 2348 BC. The one physical dating method that bypasses the king-lists entirely says the opposite: if anything the pyramids are older than 2589 BC, not younger. There is no thumb on this scale to press in the needed direction.

The short-chronology repair, steelmanned

The young-earth writers who face this squarely do not claim the pyramids stood before the Flood — they insist the opposite, and on good grounds of their own. The Giza plateau's core blocks are cut from nummulitic limestone full of marine fossils, which (on their reading) is Flood-deposited rock; the pyramids sit on those strata and show none of the scouring or burial a subsequent global cataclysm would leave. So the pyramids must be post-Flood, and "pyramids before the Flood" is a critics' framing the creationists reject.6 Their repair is therefore not to move the Flood but to compress Egyptian chronology so the Fourth Dynasty slides below 2348 BC. The mechanism, drawn from David Down's revised chronology, is that Manetho's thirty dynasties were not all sequential: several ran concurrently in different regions of a divided land (Upper, Middle, and Lower), and father–son coregencies further inflate the total, so the conventional dates are "far too long because dynasties have been placed sequentially, whereas they were… contemporary." Elizabeth Mitchell states the governing rule plainly: "Their dates would place the first dynasties before the biblical Flood, but we know that this cannot be true."7

Two things in this repair are true, and a fair hearing concedes them. Egyptian dynasties really were sometimes concurrent — the Intermediate Periods are precisely stretches when rival lines ruled different regions at once, and Manetho's own totals, listing them in series, do over-count those spans if read naively. And Manetho is a late (third-century BC), fragmentary, and interpretively slippery source. If Egyptian chronology were nothing but a stack of Manetho's dynasties summed in a line, the compression would be a live move.

Why the repair fails at the pyramids

The concurrency device works on the Intermediate Periods, not on the Old Kingdom. Overlapping dynasties are a real feature of the disunited phases, where the record itself attests rival houses. But the pyramid-building Fourth Dynasty sits inside the Old Kingdom — a single, centralized state ruled from Memphis, with one royal line running Dynasties 3–6 in an unbroken succession the monuments, tombs, and annals record as linear. There is no contemporaneous rival dynasty to fold Dynasty 4 into; folding it into Dynasty 1 or 6 would collapse a sequence of distinct, individually attested kings with their own pyramids and mastabas into simultaneity, which the archaeology flatly excludes. The one place the compressor most needs concurrency is the one place the record leaves him none.8

The compression is not one adjustment but several independent ones, and radiocarbon blocks it. To bring the Fourth Dynasty from c. 2550 BC down past a 2348 BC Flood — and to fit the three prior dynasties, the unification, and a post-Babel migration into Egypt after it — is not a tweak of decades but a compression of several centuries. That move must simultaneously overturn the king-list dead-reckoning, the lunar and Sothic anchors, the 763 BC-fixed Near-Eastern synchronisms, and the independent radiocarbon — four currencies that do not share a mechanism, and the radiocarbon actively pushes the date the wrong way (older, not younger). This is the mark of an ad-hoc compression paying many unrelated bills, not one correlated correction, exactly as the Sothic-spine page finds for any large shift of the Egyptian backbone.9

Tellingly, the young-earth camp is divided on the exit. Some concede that the cleaner solution is not to compress Egypt at all but to adopt the Septuagint's longer Genesis figures, which push the Flood back to c. 3300 BC and drop the collision without touching a single Egyptian date — the very move Scenario B makes. That an internal debate exists, with some defenders reaching for the LXX rather than the compression, is itself a signal that the compression is felt to be the weaker leg.10

The yardstick, and what it does and does not prove

Per this wiki's rule on the biblical Flood horizon as a yardstick, the honest verdict is stated as a verdict about the chosen number, not about the artifact. "The pyramids predate the Flood" is true against Ussher's 2348 BC and false against the Septuagint's c. 3300 BC — a claim that flips with the reckoning, and therefore a fact about which biblical figures one adopts, not about the Giza plateau. The pyramids' own date is date-independent of the biblical dispute: it is fixed by Egyptian king-lists and physical radiocarbon that owe Genesis nothing, and it does not move when a reader switches text-traditions.

Two temptations must be resisted on the other side. The Septuagint fit is real and worth scoring — it is a clean ✓ for the long bundle where the short bundle takes a ✗/△ — but it does not confirm the Septuagint chronology, and no fit should be laundered upward on the long reckoning's back. The pyramids being post-Flood on the LXX is consistent with that reckoning; it does not establish it, because a merely-later Flood is exactly what a longer set of Genesis numbers buys, and the deeper collision remains: even at c. 3300 BC the continuous Egyptian and Mesopotamian sequences run across the Flood horizon with no rupture, as the uninterrupted-civilizations page shows. The Septuagint moves the pyramids out of the line of fire; it does not by itself answer the continuity problem behind them.

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The pyramids really do predate a 2348 BC Flood, and the short chronology's own defenders know it and reject a pre-Flood pyramid. Their concurrency point is genuinely true of the Intermediate Periods, and Manetho is a late and imperfect source — a chronology built on him alone would be soft. And the Septuagint reckoning really does dissolve the specific collision, cleanly and without touching an Egyptian date.

Where it fails. The compression repair the short reckoning actually needs does not survive contact with the evidence. Concurrency is a feature of the disunited phases, not of the linear Old Kingdom the Fourth Dynasty sits in; the several-century move required must overturn four independent anchors at once; and the one physical method that bypasses the king-lists — radiocarbon — runs the pyramids older, not younger, denying the compressor the very direction he needs. The repair is weak on its own best case.

Symmetry, and no shortcut by the supernatural. The date is not defended by consensus alone: the residual openness in Old-Kingdom chronology is real and named at the Sothic page, worth tens of years, not the two-plus centuries the collision requires. Nor is a granted miracle able to relocate the date — that God could act does not move a regnal sum, a lunar date, or a charcoal sample; dating is weighed on dating evidence. And the LXX fit, though real, is not laundered into proof of the LXX.

Assessment

Weak — and the finding is that it is the wrong question. As a claim advanced by critics, "the pyramids prove the Flood was local or unhistorical" over-reaches: it presumes the Masoretic 2348 BC is the only biblical date, when the Septuagint reckoning this wiki rates arguably the stronger drops the collision entirely. As the short chronology's own repair — compress Egyptian history so the Fourth Dynasty postdates 2348 BC — the claim is weak: the concurrency device does not reach the linear Old Kingdom, the move must pay four independent bills, and radiocarbon pushes the date the wrong way. What is secure in every reckoning is the pyramids' own date, c. 2600–2500 BC; whether that counts as "before the Flood" is a verdict about the yardstick chosen, not about the stones. For the scenario ledger: this anchor scores ✓ for the Septuagint-long column and ✗/△ for the Masoretic-short/Ussher column (and for the rabbinic column, which inherits the same short primeval interval) — a textbook case of an anchor whose reading flips with the presupposition bundle.
