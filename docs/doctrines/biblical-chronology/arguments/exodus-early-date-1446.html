Does 1 Kings 6:1 fix the Exodus at c. 1446 BC?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — read at face value the Bible's own internal numbers place the Exodus in the mid-fifteenth century: 1 Kings 6:1 sets Solomon's fourth year (c. 966 BC) 480 years after the Exodus, giving c. 1446 BC, and Jephthah's "three hundred years" (Judges 11:26) is a second, independent internal datum pointing the same way. The reckoning is textually straightforward and has serious defenders, and Bryant Wood's redating of Jericho's fall to c. 1400 BC gives it a live archaeological toehold. But it is genuinely disputed, not established: it must read the Ramesside store-city name of Exodus 1:11 as a later editorial update, it sits awkwardly against an eighteenth-dynasty Egypt then at the height of its empire in Canaan, and the Jericho redating on which its conquest case leans is a minority reading most excavators reject. Strong on the biblical text's own chronology, weak on the material record — the paradigm of a contested question.

Related arguments: the date of the Exodus — see also the thirteenth-century (Ramesside) reading (the rival dating, which takes the same store-city name and the Merneptah terminus as decisive for a c. 1250 BC Exodus). The length of the Egyptian sojourn (430 years or 215) and the conquest/Jericho horizon are related sub-anchors developed on their own pages.

The claim

And it came to pass in the four hundred and eightieth year after the children of Israel were come out of the land of Egypt, in the fourth year of Solomon's reign over Israel… that he began to build the house of the LORD.

— 1 Kings 6:1 (KJV), 1 Kings 6:1. Solomon's fourth year is fixed by the synchronisms of the divided monarchy to c. 966 BC; 966 + 480 = c. 1446 BC for the Exodus, in the reign of Amenhotep II (and an oppression under Thutmose III). This is the "early" or "high" date. The interpretation assessed here is that the Bible's own datum should be taken at face value as a real chronological figure, not dissolved into a symbol, and that it fixes the Exodus two centuries before the mainstream archaeological reckoning.1

Who advances it

The early date is the older reading — the plain sense the pre-critical tradition assumed and the number Ussher's chronology carries — and it remains the majority position among conservative evangelical scholars. Its modern defenders include Eugene Merrill (Kingdom of Priests), whose whole reconstruction of Israel's history runs on the 1446 datum; John Bimson, whose Redating the Exodus and Conquest (1981) argued the archaeology could be re-anchored to the fifteenth century by lowering the end of the Middle Bronze Age; and, most influentially in recent work, Bryant G. Wood, whose 2005 JETS essay reopened the case by redating the fall of Jericho.2 It is set here against its advocated rival, the thirteenth-century Ramesside reading of Kitchen and Hoffmeier.

The evidence for

The plain number, twice over. The strongest point is the simplest: Scripture states the interval, and it does so twice from independent angles. 1 Kings 6:1 gives 480 years from the Exodus to Solomon's fourth year. And centuries earlier, Jephthah — arguing Israel's title to the Transjordan against Ammon — says Israel had by then dwelt in Heshbon, Aroer, and the Arnon towns "three hundred years" (Judges 11:26). Jephthah's judgeship falls c. 1100 BC; 300 years back puts the conquest of the Transjordan c. 1400, and the Exodus a generation earlier, c. 1446 — squarely confirming the 1 Kings figure from a wholly different book and setting.3 Two internal data converging on the same date is harder to dismiss as accident than one.

Jericho, redated. The early date's most consequential modern support is Bryant Wood's re-examination of Jericho. Kathleen Kenyon's mid-century excavation dated the destruction of City IV to c. 1550 BC and found the site unoccupied thereafter — a verdict that told against both biblical dates, since there was then no walled city for Joshua to take at either 1400 or 1250. Wood argued that Kenyon dated the fall too early: the local pottery of the final city, the scarab series running down to Amenhotep III, and a single radiocarbon determination all point, he contends, to a destruction c. 1400 BC — precisely the early-date conquest horizon, with the collapsed walls, the burnt grain stores, and the autumn-harvest timing matching Joshua 2–6.4 On this reading the archaeology is not an obstacle to the early date but its confirmation.

Egypt at the right distance. The Merneptah Stele (c. 1208 BC) names "Israel" as a people already in Canaan — the earliest extrabiblical mention. On the early date this is comfortable: Israel entered the land c. 1400 and had been settled for nearly two centuries by Merneptah's day, long enough to be a recognisable people. And the Amarna letters (c. 1350 BC), in which Canaanite city-kings beg Pharaoh for help against marauding ʿapiru, are read by early-date defenders as the disturbed Canaan of the Judges period, with the ʿapiru at least overlapping the incoming Hebrews.5

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The internal-chronology case is real and should not be waved away. 1 Kings 6:1 is a specific number in a book that elsewhere counts regnal years with care, and Judges 11:26 is a genuinely independent witness that lands in the same neighbourhood — the convergence is the argument's backbone and it is not nothing. Wood's Jericho work, whatever its final verdict, is a serious piece of scholarship that forced the field to re-argue a question many had treated as closed. Taken on the Bible's own terms, the early date is the natural reading, and the burden its critics carry — to explain away two plain numbers — is heavier than they often admit.

Where it is contested — the store-city name. The sharpest objection is Exodus 1:11: the Hebrews built the store-city Raamses, which most naturally names Pi-Ramesse, the Delta residence built by Rameses II (1279–1213) — a thirteenth-century, not fifteenth-century, setting for the oppression. Early-date defenders answer that the name is a later editorial updating of an older toponym, exactly as "Dan" appears anachronistically in Genesis 14:14 for a place not so named until the Judges. That answer is coherent and has real parallels, but it is a concession, not a datum: it makes the one place-name that could anchor the oppression to Egyptian chronology inadmissible, and the more economical reading of the verse is that the tradition was framed in the Ramesside age. The point stands as a live counter, not a defeated one.6

An awkward Egypt. A c. 1446 Exodus falls under the eighteenth dynasty at the zenith of Egyptian imperial power — Thutmose III and Amenhotep II campaigning through and garrisoning Canaan. It is strained to have Israel walk out of Egypt, wander, and conquer a Canaan that Egypt then firmly controlled, with the Amarna archive showing Pharaoh's grip on the same hill-country a half-century later — and no Egyptian record hints at the loss of a slave population or the destruction of an army. (The argument from silence cuts against the late date too, but the fifteenth century is the period Egypt documents its Levantine dominance most fully.)

The Jericho redating is a minority reading. Wood's c. 1400 date is disputed by most who have engaged it. Piotr Bienkowski and others answer that the pottery Wood leans on is not diagnostic of the fifteenth century, that Kenyon's stratigraphy stands, and that the lone radiocarbon result has been read against him. And the wider conquest picture is a problem for the early date independently of Jericho: Ai (et-Tell) was unoccupied in both the fifteenth and thirteenth centuries, so the site of Joshua 7–8 yields no destruction to date at all. The archaeological support the early date claims is therefore contested at its strongest point and simply absent at others.7

Is the 480 a schematic figure? The late date's central move is to read the 480 not as a literal sum but as a schematic 12 × 40 — twelve symbolic generations of forty years. If the "generations" in fact averaged the more realistic 25 years, twelve of them total about 300, telescoping the interval into the thirteenth century. Early-date defenders reply that this reads a plain number as a cipher on no textual warrant, and that the individually itemised judge- and oppression-figures of Judges, summed, actually exceed 480 (implying overlapping, not compressed, judgeships). The schematic reading is possible and internally motivated by the archaeology, but it is not demanded by the text; the honest situation is that the number can be read either way, which is exactly why the question is contested rather than settled.8

Assessment

Contested. The early date is the stronger reading of the Bible's own chronology: 1 Kings 6:1 and Judges 11:26 give two independent internal figures converging on c. 1446 BC, and Bryant Wood's Jericho redating gives that figure a genuine, if minority, archaeological anchor. Anyone who treats the early date as naïve has underweighted the convergence of the two texts. But it is genuinely disputed, and its weaknesses are on the material side: the store-city name of Exodus 1:11 points to the Ramesside age unless read as a later update, the eighteenth-dynasty Egypt of 1446 held the very Canaan Israel is said to have conquered, and the Jericho redating that carries its conquest case is rejected by most excavators. The verdict tracks the standoff, not a proponent's confidence: strong by the internal-biblical discipline's lights, weak by the Egyptian-archaeological discipline's, with no clear winner — the textbook shape of a contested anchor. The opposing steelman, which takes the same data the other way, is weighed on the thirteenth-century page.
