Julius Africanus on Reckoning the Age of the World

Dates: c. 160–c. 240 (the Chronographiai composed c. 221) · Wikipedia
Summary: Sextus Julius Africanus wrote the Chronographiai (five books, c. 221) — the first Christian universal chronology, a reckoning of the whole span from Creation to his own day that synchronised the sacred history with the Greek, Persian, and Egyptian records on a single Anno Mundi frame. He fixed the Flood at Anno Mundi 2262 and the Incarnation at Anno Mundi 5500, laying down the "5,500 years to Christ" architecture — read by later writers as the midpoint of a six-thousand-year world-week — that the Alexandrian monks and the whole Byzantine tradition inherited. He is the hinge between Demetrius' bare Septuagint reckoning and Eusebius' comparative tables, and the Christian fountainhead of the long chronology the Flood-dating witnesses presuppose.

Claims & evidence

The first Christian universal chronology — Creation to c. AD 221 on one Anno Mundi frame

Africanus' Chronographiai was a history of the world in five books running from the Creation down to the Roman consulship of his own day (c. AD 221), and its ambition was new among Christians: not a bare list of biblical intervals but a synchronism — the sacred chronology set alongside the Olympiads, the Persian and Egyptian king-lists, and Greek myth, so that the anchors of Scripture could be located against the fixed points of secular history.1 This is the enterprise Eusebius' Chronicon would carry to its classic tabular form a century later, and which Africanus is the first Christian to attempt whole. Against the Egyptian, Phoenician, and Chaldean boasts of tens of thousands of years, he set the modest sacred span: the Jews, he wrote, "have handed down to us, by their extant Hebrew histories, the number of 5500 years as the period up to the advent of the Word of salvation, that was announced to the world in the time of the sway of the Caesars."2

The Flood at Anno Mundi 2262 — the Septuagint's long reckoning

From Adam, therefore, to Noe and the flood, are 2262 years.

— Chronographiai, Fragment 5, Ante-Nicene Fathers translation, source. The figure is the Septuagint's long chronology, all but identical to Demetrius' 2,264 and Josephus' summed interval, and nowhere near the Masoretic 1,656 — Africanus reckons the Greek begetting-ages of Genesis 5, not the Hebrew's shorter ones. It is the number the later Alexandrian and Byzantine chronographers measured their own against: George Syncellus, preserving him, judged Annianus' Anno Mundi 2242 the more accurate "on Eusebius' authority," expressly against "Africanus' 2262."3 So the Christian long chronology of the Flood begins here, a millennium before Ussher fixed the rival Hebrew figure at 2348 BC.

The Incarnation at Anno Mundi 5500 — and the six-thousand-year world-week

The organising number of the whole system is not the Flood but the Incarnation. Africanus seated the coming of Christ at Anno Mundi 5500 (Creation therefore c. 5500 BC), and reckoned onward to the Resurrection at Anno Mundi 5531: "The period, then, to the advent of the Lord from Adam and the creation is 5531 years."4 Later writers read the 5,500 as no accident but a design: on the ancient typology of the six days of creation as six millennia (a day is "as a thousand years," Psalm 90:4 / 2 Peter 3:8), Christ enters at the midpoint of the sixth "day," so that the Sabbath-millennium — the world's completion at Anno Mundi 6000 — would fall some five centuries after him.5 Whether Africanus himself drew the eschatological capstone or merely supplied the reckoning others read that way is debated; what is certain is that the 5500/6000 frame he first fixed became the spine of Christian world-chronology for a thousand years.

The seventy weeks of Daniel computed to Christ — 475 solar years reckoned as 490

Africanus is also the earliest surviving writer to bring the reckoning to bear on prophecy, computing the "seventy weeks" of Daniel 9:24–27 as a chronological span terminating in Christ. From the twentieth year of Artaxerxes to the sixteenth year of Tiberius he counted 475 years, and resolved the shortfall against Daniel's 490 by reckoning the prophet's years as lunar: "from the 20th year of the reign of Artaxerxes… up to the 16th year of the reign of Tiberius Caesar… there are reckoned 475 years, which make 490 according to the Hebrew numeration."6 The device — converting solar years to shorter prophetic ones to make the count close on Christ — is the ancestor of every later "seventy weeks" chronology, and shows the chronographer's method turned to apologetic ends.

Notes

  • Survives only in fragments. The Chronographiai is lost; it is known from copious extracts in Eusebius' Chronicon and from the ninth-century Chronography of George Syncellus, with further scraps in Cedrenus and the Chronicon Paschale. Every figure quoted here is a fragment reported at second hand, and the numbers vary a little in transmission; the modern collection and study is William Adler's.7
  • The head of the Christian long chronology. His Flood at Anno Mundi 2262 and Incarnation at Anno Mundi 5500 are the Christian source of the scheme the Alexandrian world era (Panodorus and Annianus, who trimmed the Flood to 2242 but kept the 5,500 to Christ) and the Byzantine era carried forward unbroken. Where Africanus preferred the Septuagint's longer figures over the Hebrew, he stands with the case that the shorter Hebrew was a later recension — though, unlike some, he did not accuse the Jews of tampering, but simply reckoned the Greek text the churches read.
  • He appears on this wiki elsewhere too. The same chronographer is the earliest Christian witness to the Sethite reading of Genesis 6 — treated on his Nephilim detail page — and it is his Letter to Aristides on the two genealogies, and Origen's reply to his Letter to Origen on Susanna, that place him in the canon and text-criticism disputes. Here his bearing is the whole-span reckoning: the first Christian to fix the age of the world.
