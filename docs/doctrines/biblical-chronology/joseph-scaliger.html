Joseph Scaliger on Reckoning the Age of the World

Dates: 1540–1609 (De emendatione temporum 1583; Thesaurus temporum 1606) · Wikipedia
Summary: Joseph Justus Scaliger's De emendatione temporum (1583) carried the reckoning of the ancient world beyond the Greeks and Romans to the Persian, Babylonian, Egyptian, and Jewish records and put it on an astronomical and calendrical footing; in it he devised the Julian Period, a continuous 7,980-year count, still used by astronomers, onto which the sacred and secular chronologies alike can be mapped. His Thesaurus temporum (1606) reconstructed the lost Chronicle of Eusebius and gathered every surviving chronological relic in Greek or Latin. On the timeline he stands between the patristic and rabbinic Anno Mundi reckoners and Ussher's Annals.

Claims & evidence

Comparative chronology — De emendatione temporum (1583)

His Opus novum de emendatione temporum ("A New Work on the Correction of Chronology," Paris, 1583) insisted that ancient history "was not confined to that of the Greeks and Romans" but had to be reconstructed from the Persian, Babylonian, Egyptian, and Jewish records read together and subjected to systematic rules of criticism.1 The nineteenth-century scholar Mark Pattison judged that Scaliger had raised chronology into "a rational procedure subject to fixed laws." Scaliger's apparatus allowed ancient calendars to be converted into one another and compared on one frame.

The Julian Period — a continuous 7,980-year astronomical spine

The instrument for the comparison was Scaliger's Julian Period: a cycle of 7,980 years formed by multiplying the 28-year solar cycle, the 19-year lunar (Metonic) cycle, and the 15-year Roman indiction (28 × 19 × 15 = 7,980), so that each year of the period carries a unique combination of the three cycle-positions and no year repeats until all 7,980 have run.2 He fixed its year 1 at what became 4713 BC — chosen because it lay before every historical record, the last point at which all three cycles stood together at their own beginnings — giving historians a single continuous count running forward through and past all recorded history with no gap, no negative numbers, and no dependence on any disputed era. The astronomers' Julian Day count, still in use, derives from it, and it lets the biblical anchors (Creation, the Flood, the Exodus) and the secular king-lists be plotted on one scale.

On the name Scaliger himself was explicit. A story often repeated is that he named the period after his father, the scholar Julius Caesar Scaliger; in De emendatione temporum he wrote "Iulianam vocavimus: quia ad annum Iulianum accommodata" — "we have called it Julian because it is fitted to the Julian year," i.e. to the Julian calendar, not to his father.3

The Thesaurus temporum (1606) — Eusebius reconstructed, every chronological relic gathered

Scaliger's second major chronological work, the Thesaurus temporum (1606), reassembled the lost Chronicle of Eusebius of Caesarea, whose Greek original had perished, out of Jerome's Latin version, the Armenian tradition, and the fragments preserved by the Byzantine chronographers, and set beside it "every chronological relic extant in Greek or Latin."4 The reconstruction made Eusebius' parallel columns — the sacred reckoning run alongside the Chaldean, Egyptian, Greek, and Roman king-lists — available again to scholars.

Placement on the timeline

  • The frame, not the figures. Scaliger's work on this timeline concerns the machinery by which dates are stated and compared with the secular record, not a new date for Creation or the Flood. The Julian Period does not depend on which biblical numbers one reckons from. The choice between the Masoretic "short" and Septuagint "long" primeval interval is recorded at the later entries for Petavius, Ussher, and the modern revival.
  • Source-criticism. In the same De emendatione temporum Scaliger attacked as forgeries the "Berosus" and other pseudo-antiquities then current in Renaissance chronology ⚠ unverified.
