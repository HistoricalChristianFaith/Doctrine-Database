Joseph Scaliger on Reckoning the Age of the World

Dates: 1540–1609 (De emendatione temporum 1583; Thesaurus temporum 1606) · Wikipedia
Summary: Joseph Justus Scaliger turned chronology from a harmonising exercise into a technical discipline. His De emendatione temporum (1583) carried the reckoning of the ancient world beyond the Greeks and Romans to the Persian, Babylonian, Egyptian, and Jewish records and put it on an astronomical and calendrical footing; in it he devised the Julian Period, a continuous 7,980-year count still used by astronomers, giving every historian a single neutral time-line onto which the sacred and secular chronologies alike could be mapped. His Thesaurus temporum (1606) reconstructed the lost Chronicle of Eusebius and gathered every surviving chronological relic in Greek or Latin. He is the scientific hinge between the patristic and rabbinic Anno Mundi reckoners and Ussher's Annals: the moment biblical and secular chronology begin to be locked to one common spine.

Claims & evidence

Chronology made a science — De emendatione temporum (1583)

Before Scaliger, dating the ancient past was largely a matter of harmonising the biblical genealogies with a few classical king-lists. His Opus novum de emendatione temporum ("A New Work on the Correction of Chronology," Paris, 1583) changed the enterprise into a rigorous comparative science, insisting that ancient history "was not confined to that of the Greeks and Romans" but had to be reconstructed from the Persian, Babylonian, Egyptian, and Jewish records read together and subjected to systematic rules of criticism.1 The nineteenth-century scholar Mark Pattison judged that Scaliger had raised chronology into "a rational procedure subject to fixed laws" — the founding of the discipline as such. Where the earlier chronographers on this timeline reckoned the sacred span and reached out to the secular anchors piecemeal, Scaliger built the apparatus by which every ancient calendar could be converted to every other and the whole compared on one frame.

The Julian Period — a continuous 7,980-year astronomical spine

The instrument that made the comparison possible was Scaliger's Julian Period: a cycle of 7,980 years formed by multiplying the 28-year solar cycle, the 19-year lunar (Metonic) cycle, and the 15-year Roman indiction (28 × 19 × 15 = 7,980), so that each year of the period carries a unique combination of the three cycle-positions and no year repeats until all 7,980 have run.2 He fixed its year 1 at what became 4713 BC — chosen because it lay before every historical record, the last point at which all three cycles stood together at their own beginnings — giving historians a single continuous count running forward through and past all recorded history with no gap, no negative numbers, and no dependence on any disputed era. It is the direct ancestor of the astronomers' Julian Day still in use, and it did for the dating of the ancient world what a common zero does for a measuring scale: it let the biblical anchors (Creation, the Flood, the Exodus) and the secular king-lists be plotted against one another exactly, rather than merely harmonised.

On the name Scaliger himself was explicit — and it is a standing point of confusion. The popular story is that he named the period after his father, the scholar Julius Caesar Scaliger; but in De emendatione temporum he wrote "Iulianam vocavimus: quia ad annum Iulianum accommodata" — "we have called it Julian because it is fitted to the Julian year," i.e. to the Julian calendar, not to his father.3

The Thesaurus temporum (1606) — Eusebius reconstructed, every chronological relic gathered

Scaliger's second great work, the Thesaurus temporum (1606), reassembled the lost Chronicle of Eusebius of Caesarea — the fountainhead of comparative Christian chronology, whose Greek original had perished — out of Jerome's Latin version, the Armenian tradition, and the fragments preserved by the Byzantine chronographers, and set beside it "every chronological relic extant in Greek or Latin."4 The recovery of Eusebius' parallel columns — the sacred reckoning run alongside the Chaldean, Egyptian, Greek, and Roman king-lists — put the whole ancient comparative apparatus back into scholars' hands and is the tabular ancestor of every later universal chronology, including Ussher's.

Notes

  • Why he sits on this timeline. Scaliger did not fix a new date for Creation or the Flood so much as build the machinery by which any such date could be stated and checked against the secular record. His bearing here is as the hinge between the patristic/rabbinic Anno Mundi reckoners above and the early-modern chronologists below: after Scaliger, "the date of X" meant a point convertible to a common spine and testable against the astronomy, not merely a sum of begetting-ages.
  • He did not resolve the text-tradition dispute. The Julian Period is neutral as to which biblical numbers one reckons from; it fixes the frame, not the figures. The quarrel this timeline turns on — the Masoretic "short" against the Septuagint "long" primeval interval — Scaliger's apparatus sharpened but did not settle, and it passed on to Petavius and Ussher and, in the end, to the modern revival.
  • A critic as well as a chronologist. The same De emendatione temporum is where Scaliger exposed the forged "Berosus" and other pseudo-antiquities that had padded Renaissance chronology — part of the same turn from credulous harmonising to source-criticism that makes him the founder of technical chronology.
