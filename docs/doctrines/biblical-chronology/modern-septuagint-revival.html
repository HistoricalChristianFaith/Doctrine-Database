The modern Septuagint-chronology revival and the Masoretic-priority reply

Dates: the modern revival, c. 2015–present (Jeremy Sexton's Westminster Theological Journal article 2015; Sexton & Smith, "Primeval Chronology Restored," 2016; Henry B. Smith Jr.'s ICC paper 2018) · ABR Genesis 5 & 11 Project
Summary: The latest entry on the timeline: a twenty-first-century exchange over the long and short reckonings that Eusebius, Augustine, and Ussher took different sides of, now argued with the tools of manuscript criticism. Two positions: the Septuagint-priority revival of Henry B. Smith Jr. and the Associates for Biblical Research (the Genesis 5 & 11 Research Project), holding that the longer Greek figures are original and the Hebrew was deliberately shortened after AD 70; and the Masoretic-priority defence of Creation Ministries International (Lita Cosner/Sanders and Robert Carter), holding the received Hebrew figures the better text, with Andrew Steinmann arguing the separate case for chronological gaps. The Flood-date consequences are recorded on the dating of the Flood.

Claims & evidence

The Septuagint revival — the Genesis 5 & 11 Research Project (Smith / ABR)

The twenty-first-century revival of the long chronology is the work of Henry B. Smith Jr. and the Associates for Biblical Research, whose Genesis 5 & 11 Research Project reopened the case that the Septuagint, not the Masoretic Text, preserves the original primeval numbers. Reckoning the Greek begetting-ages Smith fixes the interval from Adam to Abraham at 3,394 years against the Masoretic 2,008, and places Creation at c. 5554 BC and the Flood at c. 3298 BC — roughly a millennium earlier than Ussher. His thesis is that the Hebrew was deliberately altered:

This paper proposes that the LXX preserves (most of) the original numbers in Genesis 5 and 11. Most of the MT's chronology in Genesis 5 and 11 does not represent the original text, and is the result of a deliberate and systematic post–AD 70 corruption.

The charge that the Hebrew figures were curtailed was made in the fourth century by Eusebius; Smith argues it with modern textual criticism, gathering the pre-Christian and patristic witnesses to a long chronology (Demetrius, Eupolemus, Pseudo-Philo, Josephus, Africanus) as evidence that the divergence predates any Christian–Jewish polemic.1 The project's findings are to be gathered in a forthcoming book, From Adam to Abraham: The Case for the Septuagint's Chronology in Genesis 5 and 11.

Sexton on the "chronological gaps"

The revival draws on a prior, semantic argument by Jeremy Sexton, whose 2015 Westminster Theological Journal article "Who Was Born When Enosh Was 90?" reevaluated William Henry Green's classic "chronological gaps" thesis — the reading (widely held among conservatives since 1890 ⚠ unverified) that the Genesis 5 and 11 formula "when X had lived n years he begat Y" need not name an immediate son, so the ages cannot be summed into a continuous chronology. Sexton granted that the Hebrew hiphil of yālad could occasionally mean "became the ancestor of" (allowing a genealogical gap), but argued that even so the descendant is still born at the stated age — so the reading yields genealogical gaps but not chronological ones, and the numbers remain a running clock.2 On Sexton's and Smith's account, if the ages are a continuous chronology with no gaps, the question becomes which set of ages is original; the two published a joint article, "Primeval Chronology Restored" (2016). The Septuagint also carries an extra Cainan in Genesis 11, which Luke 3:36 repeats.

The Masoretic-priority counter — Cosner/Sanders & Carter; Steinmann

A Masoretic-priority position is argued by Lita Cosner (Sanders) and Robert Carter of Creation Ministries International; on the separate question of gaps, Andrew Steinmann continued to argue for chronological gaps against Sexton. Sanders' 2025 Answers Research Journal paper "The Textual Superiority of the Masoretic Text of Genesis 5 and 11" puts the case that the shared Septuagint–Samaritan variants point to error in a common Vorlage rather than to an older Greek reading, and that the received Hebrew is the better-preserved text. Its conclusion reads:

This textual analysis provided by this paper shows that the MT is the better-preserved text and that the MT chronology was changed to create the chronologies in the LXX and SP, rather than the LXX or the SP being original.

Smith replied directly ("Setting the Record Straight on the Primeval Chronology of the Septuagint: A Response to Cosner and Carter," 2018), and Steinmann and Sexton exchanged rejoinders in the Journal of the Evangelical Theological Society.3 The exchange concerns the same two sets of genealogical figures the earlier chronographers chose between, now argued on manuscript grounds.

Placement on the timeline

  • The earlier reckoners. Earlier witnesses on the timeline reckoned from one set of figures or the other — Demetrius, Africanus, Eusebius, and the Alexandrian monks from the Septuagint's longer figures; Seder Olam, Augustine, and Ussher from the shorter Hebrew ones. The modern exchange argues the same textual question with manuscript-critical and semantic apparatus, and bears on the whole-span figures as well as the Flood: the total Adam-to-Abraham interval, the length of the Egyptian sojourn, and the Creation date.
