The modern Septuagint-chronology revival — the living terminus

Dates: the modern revival, c. 2015–present (Jeremy Sexton's Westminster Theological Journal article 2015; Sexton & Smith, "Primeval Chronology Restored," 2016; Henry B. Smith Jr.'s ICC paper 2018) · ABR Genesis 5 & 11 Project
Summary: The concluding witness on the timeline — the point where the long-vs-short reckoning that Eusebius, Augustine, and Ussher took different sides of is a live scholarly dispute again, now argued with the tools of manuscript criticism. Two camps: the Septuagint-priority revival of Henry B. Smith Jr. and the Associates for Biblical Research (the Genesis 5 & 11 Research Project), holding that the longer Greek figures are original and the Hebrew was deliberately shortened after AD 70; and the Masoretic-priority defence of Creation Ministries International (Lita Cosner/Sanders and Robert Carter) and Andrew Steinmann, holding the received Hebrew figures the better text. The same textual question the ancient chronographers faced, still unresolved — and this timeline's modern terminus. The Flood-date consequences are weighed elsewhere and cross-linked below, not re-argued here.

Claims & evidence

The Septuagint revival — the Genesis 5 & 11 Research Project (Smith / ABR)

The twenty-first-century revival of the long chronology is the work of Henry B. Smith Jr. and the Associates for Biblical Research, whose Genesis 5 & 11 Research Project reopened the case that the Septuagint, not the Masoretic Text, preserves the original primeval numbers. Reckoning the Greek begetting-ages Smith fixes the interval from Adam to Abraham at 3,394 years against the Masoretic 2,008, and places Creation at c. 5554 BC and the Flood at c. 3298 BC — roughly a millennium earlier than Ussher. His thesis is not that the Greek merely reads well but that the Hebrew was tampered with:

This paper proposes that the LXX preserves (most of) the original numbers in Genesis 5 and 11. Most of the MT's chronology in Genesis 5 and 11 does not represent the original text, and is the result of a deliberate and systematic post–AD 70 corruption.

The revival thus reaches back and picks up exactly the charge Eusebius first made in the fourth century — that the rabbis curtailed the Hebrew figures — and presses it with the full apparatus of modern textual criticism, gathering the pre-Christian and patristic witnesses to a long chronology (Demetrius, Eupolemus, Pseudo-Philo, Josephus, Africanus) as evidence that the divergence predates any Christian–Jewish polemic.1 The project's findings are to be gathered in a forthcoming book, From Adam to Abraham: The Case for the Septuagint's Chronology in Genesis 5 and 11.

The exegetical hinge — Sexton and the collapse of the "chronological gaps"

The revival turned on a prior, semantic argument by Jeremy Sexton, whose 2015 Westminster Theological Journal article "Who Was Born When Enosh Was 90?" reevaluated William Henry Green's classic "chronological gaps" thesis — the reading (assumed by most conservatives since 1890) that the Genesis 5 and 11 formula "when X had lived n years he begat Y" need not name an immediate son, so the ages cannot be summed into a continuous chronology. Sexton granted that the Hebrew hiphil of yālad could occasionally mean "became the ancestor of" (allowing a genealogical gap), but showed that even so the descendant is still born at the stated age — so the reading yields genealogical gaps but not chronological ones, and the numbers remain a running clock.2 That result is load-bearing for the whole revival: if the ages are a genuine chronology with no gaps to hide in, then which set of ages is original becomes the decisive question — which is why Smith's textual case and Sexton's exegetical one converged into a single program (their joint "Primeval Chronology Restored," 2016). It also cuts squarely across one of the counter-arguments to a literal-Flood date weighed on this wiki — the point that the genealogies are open lists, not calendars, shown by the extra Cainan the Greek carries and Luke repeats — a tension noted below and left to be weighed on the argument pages, not adjudicated here.

The Masoretic-priority counter — Cosner/Sanders & Carter; Steinmann

The revival did not go unanswered. Against it stands a Masoretic-priority defence, most fully from Lita Cosner (Sanders) and Robert Carter of Creation Ministries International and, in a mediating key, from Andrew Steinmann, who continued to argue for chronological gaps against Sexton. Sanders' 2025 Answers Research Journal paper "The Textual Superiority of the Masoretic Text of Genesis 5 and 11" puts the case that the shared Septuagint–Samaritan variants point to error in a common Vorlage rather than to an older Greek reading, and that the received Hebrew is the better-preserved text. Its conclusion states the counter-thesis flatly:

This textual analysis provided by this paper shows that the MT is the better-preserved text and that the MT chronology was changed to create the chronologies in the LXX and SP, rather than the LXX or the SP being original.

Smith replied directly ("Setting the Record Straight on the Primeval Chronology of the Septuagint: A Response to Cosner and Carter," 2018), and Steinmann and Sexton exchanged rejoinders in the Journal of the Evangelical Theological Society — so the modern dispute is a live, published back-and-forth, not a settled position.3 The exchange is, precisely, the continuation of the ancient quarrel this timeline traces: the same two readings of the same genealogies, now contested on manuscript grounds.

Notes

  • Why it sits on this timeline — the living terminus. Every earlier witness on this shelf took a side in the long-vs-short dispute — Demetrius, Africanus, Eusebius, and the Alexandrian monks on the Septuagint's longer figures; Seder Olam, Augustine, and Ussher on the shorter Hebrew ones. The modern revival closes the arc by reopening exactly that dispute with new tools: it is the same textual question, still unresolved, now argued with manuscript-critical and semantic apparatus rather than by chronographic authority. It also carries the whole-span consequences the Flood-dating pages never reach — the total Adam-to-Abraham interval, the length of the Egyptian sojourn, and the knock-on Creation date.
  • What this page does not settle. Whether the reckoning yields a defensible date for a literal global Flood is a separate question, weighed adversarially and rated on the Septuagint long chronology (assessment: weak as a Flood date, though "arguably the stronger of the two reckonings"), the Masoretic short chronology, and the Samaritan Pentateuch chronology pages. Which text-form is prior — the properly text-critical question the modern principals are fighting over — is worked on the transmission of the Hebrew text and its Genesis-chronology argument, where the revival's case is set against the text-critical mainstream (Hendel, Tov, Wevers). This page records the debate as the timeline's terminus; those pages weigh it.
  • A tension worth flagging, not resolving here. Sexton's "no chronological gaps" result and this wiki's own caution that "a genealogy is not a calendar" (the open-list point, shown by the extra Cainan) pull in opposite directions. Both survive scrutiny in part: Sexton's semantic case against a chronological gap in the begetting-age formula is strong, while the open-list evidence bears on whether the list of names is complete. The two are not simply contradictory — they concern different things — and the reconciliation is argued on the long-chronology page, not asserted here.
