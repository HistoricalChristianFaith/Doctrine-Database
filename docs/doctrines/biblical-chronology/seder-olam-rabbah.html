Seder Olam Rabbah on Reckoning the Age of the World

Dates: c. AD 160 (attributed to R. Yose ben Halafta, a second-century tanna; the received text edited later) · Wikipedia
Summary: Seder Olam Rabbah ("The Great Order of the World") is the foundational rabbinic chronology of the Bible — the earliest post-biblical Hebrew reckoning of the whole span from Creation, and the source of the count still carried by the Hebrew calendar's Anno Mundi era (Creation c. 3761 BC, so that the Jewish year running through most of AD 2026 is AM 5786). It reckons the primeval and patriarchal intervals entirely on the Masoretic figures — the Jewish counterpart to the Septuagint-based Christian world-eras this timeline traces — and closes with its signature feature, a drastically compressed Persian period: only about 52 years and a handful of kings for the whole Persian empire, roughly 165 years shorter than the conventional reckoning. That compression is the seed of the "missing years" dispute weighed in the Arguments section; here the system is presented as it states itself.

Claims & evidence

The foundational rabbinic chronology — attributed to R. Yose ben Halafta

Seder Olam ("Order of the World") is the oldest surviving systematic chronology in rabbinic literature: a running reckoning of biblical time from Adam down through the Persian period and, more briefly, on to the Bar Kokhba revolt. The Talmud twice names its author — "Rabbi Yoḥanan said: Who is the tanna who taught Seder Olam? It is Rabbi Yosei" — meaning Yose ben Halafta, a fourth-generation tanna of the mid-second century remembered for his attention to chronology.1 Modern scholarship treats Yose as the principal authority behind a work that was supplemented and edited afterward — it refers to him in the third person and cites teachers who lived after him — and the qualifier Rabbah ("greater") is a twelfth-century addition, distinguishing it from the later epitome Seder Olam Zuta. Whatever the exact history of the received text, it is the fountainhead of the Jewish reckoning of sacred time, the tradition against which the Christian Anno Mundi systems on this timeline are the counterpart.

The Anno Mundi count — Creation c. 3761 BC, on the Masoretic figures

The reckoning of Seder Olam is what seats the creation of Adam at c. 3761 BC, and so grounds the Anno Mundi ("in the year of the world") era the Hebrew calendar still counts by: adding roughly 3,760 to an AD year gives its Jewish-calendar year, so the year running from autumn 2025 through most of AD 2026 is AM 5786.2 Crucially, the reckoning is built on the Masoretic begetting-ages of Genesis 5 and 11 — the "short" figures — not the longer Septuagint numbers the Alexandrian and Byzantine Christian eras used. This is the same text-critical fork that runs through the dating of the Flood: which numbers one reckons from is the first question, and where the churches of the East reckoned the Greek, the synagogue reckoned the Hebrew.

Creation to the Flood — 1,656 years, the Masoretic sum

From Adam to the Flood was 1656 years, and this is their enumeration: Adam 130, Seth 105, Enosh 90, Kenan 70, Mahalalel 65, Jared 162, Enoch 65, Methuselah 187, Lamech 182, and Noah was six hundred years old, etc.

— Seder Olam Rabbah, ch. 1.3 The interval is the plain sum of the Masoretic begetting-ages — 1,656 years to the Flood, against the Septuagint's 2,262 that Demetrius, Africanus, and the Alexandrians reckoned. It is the "short" chronology's primeval anchor, the same 1,656 that Ussher would carry to Creation 4004 BC fifteen centuries later, here already fixed in the second-century rabbinic reckoning.

The intervals to the fall of the Second Temple — AM 3828

From the Flood the reckoning runs age by age through the patriarchs and the Exodus to the building of Solomon's Temple, which it dates — with 1 Kings 6:1 — to 480 years after the Exodus, in the fourth year of Solomon's reign. The First Temple, on its count, stood 410 years and was destroyed in AM 3338; the Babylonian exile lasted the prophesied 70 years ("for at the completion of seventy years of Babylon," Jeremiah 29:10; "since the destruction of Jerusalem seventy years," Daniel 9:2); and the Second Temple stood 420 years, so that its destruction — the effective terminus of the detailed reckoning — falls in AM 3828 (roughly AD 68 on this system).4 The whole arithmetic is internally self-consistent (3338 + 70 + 420 = 3828), and it is the spine on which the received Jewish dating of the two Temples still rests.

The compressed Persian period — ~52 years and four kings

The system's most consequential and most disputed feature is its reckoning of the Persian empire. Where secular chronology counts some fourteen Persian kings across roughly 207 years from Cyrus to Alexander, Seder Olam allots the whole span only about 52 years and collapses the biblical Persian kings into a short line — Cyrus, Ahasuerus, and Darius. In its own words:

Israel spent fifty-two years after the destruction of the Temple under the rule of the Chaldeans, and then they went up. Three years of Cyrus, fourteen of Ahasuerus, two of Darius. In the second year of Darius, the Temple was rebuilt.

— Seder Olam Rabbah, ch. 29.5 And in its closing tally of the 420 years the Second Temple stood, the Persian domination during the Temple's standing is given just 34 years: "the kingdom of Persia during the Temple, 34 years; the kingdom of Greece, 180; the kingdom of the Hasmonean house, 103; the kingdom of Herod, 103" (34 + 180 + 103 + 103 = 420), with Alexander of Macedon allotted a 12-year reign.6 The result is a Persian era roughly 165 years shorter than the conventional one — the celebrated "missing years" of the Jewish calendar, which ripple down through every date and are weighed on their own in the Arguments section. Here the point is only to record the system as it states itself: the compression is not an accident of transmission but the reckoning Seder Olam plainly makes.

Notes

  • The six-thousand-year frame. The rabbinic chronology sits inside a schematic view of history the Talmud states as a baraita: "The world is destined to exist for six thousand years. For two thousand years the world was waste [tohu]… the next two thousand years are the time period of the Torah… the last two thousand years are the days of the Messiah" (Avodah Zarah 9a) — the Jewish parallel to the six-millennia "world-week" Julius Africanus' 5,500-years-to-Christ scheme carried on the Christian side.7
  • "During the Temple," not "before" it. The closing tally's phrase bifnei ha-bayit (מלכות פרס בפני הבית) means Persia's 34 years while the Second Temple stood, not before it — a common gloss to guard against. The pre-rebuilding reigns (Cyrus 3 + Ahasuerus 14 + Darius to year 2) are counted separately in ch. 29, and together with the 34 years "during the Temple" make up the ~52-year total the secondary literature cites.
  • The counterpart, not a witness on the Flood page. Unlike most figures on this timeline, Seder Olam does not appear among the Flood-dating witnesses, which are predominantly the Christian long-chronology reckoners; it stands here as the Jewish short-chronology counterpart — the reckoning that fixed the Masoretic 1,656 to the Flood and the Anno Mundi count the calendar still keeps, against which the Septuagint eras on the shelf above defined a rival age of the world.
