Demetrius the Chronographer on Reckoning the Age of the World

Dates: fl. c. 221–204 BC (writing at Alexandria under Ptolemy IV Philopator) · Wikipedia
Summary: Demetrius — the earliest known Jewish author to write in Greek — is the fountainhead of biblical chronography: the first writer we can name who reckoned sacred history by the numbers of the genealogies, computing generation by generation (and even month by month) the intervals from Adam, from the Flood, and from Abraham down to Israel's descent into Egypt. He worked from the Septuagint, and his totals coincide with the Greek long chronology against the shorter Hebrew — making him, three centuries before Julius Africanus and Eusebius, both the head of the whole enterprise this timeline traces and the earliest external witness that the longer Genesis 5–11 figures were already in circulation.

Claims & evidence

The first reckoning of the biblical span by the numbers — 3,624 years from Adam to the descent into Egypt

And from Adam until Joseph's brethren came into Egypt there were three thousand six hundred and twenty-four years; and from the Deluge until Jacob's coming into Egypt one thousand three hundred and sixty years; and from the choice of Abraham from among the Gentiles and his coming from Charran into Canaan until Jacob and his family came into Egypt two hundred and fifteen years.

— Demetrius, quoted by Alexander Polyhistor in Eusebius, Praeparatio Evangelica IX.21 (Fr. 2), E. H. Gifford translation, source. This is the earliest surviving attempt to fix the age of the world from the figures of Scripture. Subtract the two totals — 3,624 less 1,360 — and Demetrius' interval from Adam to the Flood comes to 2,264 years, all but identical to the Septuagint's 2,262 and nowhere near the Masoretic 1,656. The concluding 215 years from Abraham's entry into Canaan to Jacob's descent into Egypt is the "short" reading of the Egyptian sojourn (215 years in Canaan + 215 in Egypt to make the 430 of Exodus 12:40) — the reckoning the Septuagint and Samaritan texts carry, and one of the secular-anchor questions weighed on this topic's summary.

He reckoned from the Septuagint — and coincides with its long chronology, not the Hebrew

That Demetrius used the Greek text is plain from the Grecianised spelling of the biblical names and from the totals themselves: an Adam-to-Flood span of 2,264 years can only be summed from the Septuagint's inflated begetting-ages, not the Hebrew's. Modern study makes the coincidence exact — his Flood falls at Anno Mundi 2264 against the Septuagint's 2262 (the Hebrew's 1656), and his later anchors track the Greek reckoning down the line, not the received Hebrew.1 More finely still, for his antediluvian numbers to sum as they do, his manuscript must have read Methuselah begetting Lamech at 187 in Genesis 5:25 — making Demetrius, writing barely two generations after the Pentateuch was first put into Greek, the earliest datable witness to that reading.2 (Where later Greek copies instead read 167 there, Methuselah is left alive fourteen years past a Flood he never boarded — the very crux that drove Augustine to prefer the Hebrew; Demetrius' text does not yet carry it.) His bearing on the long-versus-short dispute is therefore weighty: he is a third-century-BC datum that the longer figures are early, grist for the case that the Hebrew was later shortened and for the Septuagint long chronology this wiki rates the stronger reckoning.

A genuine chronographer, not a quoter of totals — the reckoning is built generation by generation

But Jacob came from Charran to Laban, when he was eighty years old, and begat Levi, and Levi was afterwards seventeen years in Egypt from the time of his coming from Canaan into Egypt, so that he was sixty years old when he begat Clath; and in the same year in which Clath was born Jacob died in Egypt, after he had blessed the sons of Joseph, being himself one hundred and forty-seven years old and leaving Joseph fifty-six years old…

— Demetrius, in Eusebius, Praeparatio Evangelica IX.21, Gifford translation, source. The summary totals are not asserted; they are computed. Demetrius works the genealogy age by age — and often month by month, dating the birth of each of Jacob's children — harmonising the patriarchs' begetting-ages into a continuous arithmetic. This is chronography in the technical sense the later tradition would inherit: the method Julius Africanus, Eusebius, and Ussher all practised, here attested at its source.

The kings of Judah reckoned to Ptolemy IV — the fragment that dates him

Demetrius, in his book, On the Kings in Judæa, says that the tribes of Juda, Benjamin, and Levi were not taken captive by Sennacherim; but that there were from this captivity to the last, which Nabuchodonosor made out of Jerusalem, a hundred and twenty-eight years and six months; and from the time that the ten tribes were carried captive from Samaria till Ptolemy the Fourth, were five hundred and seventy-three years, nine months; and from the time that the captivity from Jerusalem took place, three hundred and thirty-eight years and three months.

— Clement of Alexandria, Stromata I.21 (Demetrius Fr. 6), Ante-Nicene Fathers translation, source. Because Demetrius reckons the exile-intervals down to the reign of Ptolemy IV Philopator (221–204 BC) and stops there, this fragment is what fixes his own date and places him in Ptolemaic Alexandria. It also shows the enterprise did not stop at the primeval genealogies: he carried the reckoning through the monarchy and the captivities, the whole span from Adam to his own day — exactly the scope this timeline traces.

Notes

  • His figures are early, not flawless. Demetrius' arithmetic has small internal slips (his placement of Abraham's birth is off by some decades, and the transmitted "3,624 from Adam" sits a little high against his own sub-totals), and Clement notes that Philo's regnal figures diverged from his. But the slips are of the ordinary kind any long computation accrues; what is unmistakable is the text he reckoned from — the Septuagint's long chronology, not the Hebrew's short one. His value here mirrors Josephus' a few centuries later: a datable witness that the longer numbers were early, not a source precise enough to fix a calendar.
  • The head of a line that runs to the modern LXX revival. Josephus took over Demetrius' chronological frame for his own Antiquities, and the Alexandrian world-era and every later Septuagint reckoning stand in the same tradition. When present-day advocates of the Greek chronology (Henry B. Smith Jr. and Associates for Biblical Research) argue that the original Genesis figures were the longer ones, Demetrius is their earliest external exhibit — the third-century-BC witness that the long numbers predate the second-century-AD rabbinic recension they charge with shortening the text (see note 2 above).
  • Demetrius stands on this summary as its earliest biblical reckoner, following only the pagan benchmark of Berossus and preceding Josephus, Eusebius, and the world-eras that carried the long chronology forward.
