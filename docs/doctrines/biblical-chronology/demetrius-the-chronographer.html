Demetrius the Chronographer on Reckoning the Age of the World

Dates: fl. c. 221–204 BC (writing at Alexandria under Ptolemy IV Philopator) · Wikipedia
Summary: Demetrius — the earliest known Jewish author to write in Greek — is the earliest writer we can name who reckoned sacred history by the numbers of the genealogies, computing generation by generation (and even month by month) the intervals from Adam, from the Flood, and from Abraham down to Israel's descent into Egypt. He worked from the Septuagint, and his totals coincide with the Greek long chronology rather than the shorter Hebrew. He wrote some four centuries before Julius Africanus, and Henry B. Smith Jr. treats him as the earliest known external witness to the longer Genesis 5–11 figures.

Claims & evidence

The earliest extant reckoning of the biblical span by the numbers — 3,624 years from Adam to the descent into Egypt

And from Adam until Joseph's brethren came into Egypt there were three thousand six hundred and twenty-four years; and from the Deluge until Jacob's coming into Egypt one thousand three hundred and sixty years; and from the choice of Abraham from among the Gentiles and his coming from Charran into Canaan until Jacob and his family came into Egypt two hundred and fifteen years.

— Demetrius, quoted by Alexander Polyhistor in Eusebius, Praeparatio Evangelica IX.21 (Fr. 2), E. H. Gifford translation, source. This is the earliest surviving attempt to fix the age of the world from the figures of Scripture. Subtract the two totals — 3,624 less 1,360 — and Demetrius' interval from Adam to the Flood comes to 2,264 years, all but identical to the Septuagint's 2,262 and nowhere near the Masoretic 1,656. The concluding 215 years from Abraham's entry into Canaan to Jacob's descent into Egypt is the Canaan interval that the "short" reading of the Egyptian sojourn counts inside the 430 of Exodus 12:40 (215 in Canaan + 215 in Egypt), the reading the Septuagint and Samaritan texts carry; the readings of that verse are set out on the Egyptian-sojourn page.

He reckoned from the Septuagint — and coincides with its long chronology, not the Hebrew

Wacholder and the Encyclopaedia Judaica summary set out his use of the Greek text, seen in the Grecianised spelling of the biblical names and in the totals themselves: an Adam-to-Flood span of 2,264 years sums from the Septuagint's longer begetting-ages, not the Hebrew's. His Flood falls at Anno Mundi 2264 against the Septuagint's 2262 (the Hebrew's 1656), and his later anchors follow the Greek reckoning rather than the received Hebrew.1 An argument attributed to Henry B. Smith Jr. holds that, for his antediluvian numbers to sum as they do, his manuscript read Methuselah begetting Lamech at 187 in Genesis 5:25, which would make Demetrius, writing barely two generations after the Pentateuch was first put into Greek, the earliest datable witness to that reading ⚠ unverified.2 (Later Greek copies read 167 there, which leaves Methuselah alive fourteen years past the Flood — the crux Augustine discusses when he prefers the Hebrew.) Smith cites Demetrius as a third-century-BC witness that the longer figures are early, in the case that the Hebrew was later shortened.

The reckoning built generation by generation

But Jacob came from Charran to Laban, when he was eighty years old, and begat Levi, and Levi was afterwards seventeen years in Egypt from the time of his coming from Canaan into Egypt, so that he was sixty years old when he begat Clath; and in the same year in which Clath was born Jacob died in Egypt, after he had blessed the sons of Joseph, being himself one hundred and forty-seven years old and leaving Joseph fifty-six years old…

— Demetrius, in Eusebius, Praeparatio Evangelica IX.21, Gifford translation, source. In this fragment the totals are computed: Demetrius works the genealogy age by age — and often month by month, dating the birth of each of Jacob's children — harmonising the patriarchs' begetting-ages into a continuous arithmetic.

The kings of Judah reckoned to Ptolemy IV — the fragment that dates him

Demetrius, in his book, On the Kings in Judæa, says that the tribes of Juda, Benjamin, and Levi were not taken captive by Sennacherim; but that there were from this captivity to the last, which Nabuchodonosor made out of Jerusalem, a hundred and twenty-eight years and six months; and from the time that the ten tribes were carried captive from Samaria till Ptolemy the Fourth, were five hundred and seventy-three years, nine months; and from the time that the captivity from Jerusalem took place, three hundred and thirty-eight years and three months.

— Clement of Alexandria, Stromata I.21 (Demetrius Fr. 6), Ante-Nicene Fathers translation, source. Because Demetrius reckons the exile-intervals down to the reign of Ptolemy IV Philopator (221–204 BC) and stops there, this fragment is what fixes his own date and places him in Ptolemaic Alexandria. It also shows that he carried the reckoning beyond the primeval genealogies, through the monarchy and the captivities, from Adam to his own day.

Reception and debate

  • Internal discrepancies. His placement of Abraham's birth differs by some decades, and the transmitted "3,624 from Adam" sits a little high against his own sub-totals ⚠ unverified; Clement notes that Philo's regnal figures diverged from his.
  • Later use. That Josephus took over Demetrius' chronological frame for his own Antiquities is ⚠ unverified. Henry B. Smith Jr. and the Associates for Biblical Research, who hold that the original Genesis figures were the longer ones, treat Demetrius as the earliest known external witness, dating the long numbers before the second-century-AD rabbinic recension they hold shortened the text (see note 2 above).
  • Placement on the timeline. Demetrius stands on the timeline after the pagan Babylonian reckoning of Berossus and before Josephus, Eusebius, and the Septuagint world-eras.
