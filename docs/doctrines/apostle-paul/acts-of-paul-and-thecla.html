The Acts of Paul and Acts of Paul and Thecla on Paul

Dates: c. 160 (the Acts of Paul, a mid-2nd-century apocryphon; its Thecla episode circulated separately) · Acts of Paul and Thecla (ANF vol. 8) · Acts of Paul · back to the Paul timeline
Summary: A second-century narrative romance built around Paul. It contains the earliest surviving description of what Paul looked like and, in its closing Martyrdom, an account of his beheading at Rome under Nero with accompanying wonders. Tertullian records that its author, a presbyter in Asia, was removed from office for writing it.

Claims & evidence

The earliest portrait of Paul

As Onesiphorus waits on the road from Iconium to meet a Paul he has never seen, the text gives this description:

And he saw Paul coming, a man small in size, bald-headed, bandy-legged, well-built, with eyebrows meeting, rather long-nosed, full of grace. For sometimes he seemed like a man, and sometimes he had the countenance of an angel.

— Acts of Paul and Thecla 3 (tr. Alexander Walker). It is the oldest surviving description of Paul’s appearance. Christian art from the catacombs onward draws him small, balding, and long-bearded, and this portrait has been given as its source (⚠ unverified).

The Thecla romance

The tale itself follows Thecla, a young noblewoman of Iconium who hears Paul preach virginity, breaks her engagement to follow him, and is condemned to the fire and to the beasts for it — surviving both by miracle (a cloud quenches the flames; a lioness defends her in the arena), then baptizing herself in a tank of water and going out to teach. The story commends continence and the apostolic life; its Paul preaches a rigorous asceticism, and its central figure is Thecla, who became a widely venerated saint in her own right.

The Martyrdom: beheading under Nero

The Acts of Paul closes with a Martyrdom that has Paul beheaded at Rome under Nero, as Tertullian (c. 200) and later writers also report, and tells of wonders: Paul promises to appear again after death; when he is beheaded, milk rather than blood spurts from the severed neck onto the soldiers; and he returns to Nero in a vision. Clement of Rome’s notice of Paul’s martyrdom (c. 96) is older than this text; how far the sword and the reign of Nero go back is discussed on Was Paul beheaded at Rome under Nero?

Tertullian’s report on its author

Tertullian reports what became of the work’s author:

…in Asia, the presbyter who composed that writing, as if he were augmenting Paul’s fame from his own store, after being convicted, and confessing that he had done it from love of Paul, was removed from his office.

— Tertullian, On Baptism 17, objecting to those who cited Thecla’s self-baptism as warrant for women baptizing.

Reception and placement

  • Placement on the timeline. The work is set out here as a second-century narrative about Paul, with the earliest surviving physical portrait, and as a witness to how second-century Christians venerated him (see the apostles hub on the apocryphal Acts).
  • Also on the purgatory timeline. The Acts of Paul and Thecla is also a witness on the purgatory timeline, for its prayer that the dead Falconilla be “transferred to the place of the just”, a prayer for the dead.
  • Removal from office. Tertullian reports that the presbyter was removed from office after being convicted and confessing that he had written “from love of Paul”; the charge he reports is the composition of the work.
