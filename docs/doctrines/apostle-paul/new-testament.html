The New Testament on Paul

Dates: c. 34 to the 60s (Paul’s conversion to his death; the letters from c. 48, their end date depending on the dating and authorship of the Pastorals; Acts c. 62–90) · back to the Paul timeline
Summary: Paul is the best-documented figure in the New Testament after Jesus himself. Acts follows him from the stoning of Stephen to a Roman prison, and thirteen letters bear his name. The New Testament does not narrate his death: Acts stops with him under house-arrest at Rome, still preaching, and the later tradition concerns what happened after Acts 28.

Claims & evidence

Origin: a Pharisee of Tarsus, a Roman citizen

Paul gives his own pedigree more than once. He was “a Jew of Tarsus, a city in Cilicia,” brought up in Jerusalem “at the feet of Gamaliel,” taught “according to the perfect manner of the law of the fathers”; of “the stock of Israel, of the tribe of Benjamin, an Hebrew of the Hebrews; as touching the law, a Pharisee.” He was a Roman citizen — and, unusually, by birth:

And the chief captain answered, With a great sum obtained I this freedom. And Paul said, But I was free born.

— Acts 22:28. He worked a trade, tent-making, alongside Aquila and Priscilla (Acts 18:3). His citizenship is the ground of his appeal to Caesar, and in the later tradition it is linked to the manner of his death, the sword rather than the cross.

The persecutor

Paul enters the narrative not as a convert but as an enemy. He is first named as the young man at whose feet the witnesses laid their coats while they stoned Stephen — “And Saul was consenting unto his death” (Acts 8:1) — and then as the church’s chief hunter:

As for Saul, he made havock of the church, entering into every house, and haling men and women committed them to prison.

— Acts 8:3. He says so himself: “I persecuted this way unto the death” (Acts 22:4); “beyond measure I persecuted the church of God, and wasted it” (Galatians 1:13).

The Damascus road

On the way to Damascus with letters from the high priest to arrest believers there, Paul was struck down by a light from heaven and a voice — “Saul, Saul, why persecutest thou me?” — blinded, and sent on to be baptized by Ananias, who was told:

Go thy way: for he is a chosen vessel unto me, to bear my name before the Gentiles, and kings, and the children of Israel.

— Acts 9:15. Acts tells the conversion three times (chs. 9, 22, 26); Paul tells it himself as the moment God “called me by his grace… to reveal his Son in me, that I might preach him among the heathen” (Galatians 1:15–16).

The missionary journeys and the Jerusalem council

From Antioch, set apart with Barnabas by the Holy Spirit (Acts 13:2), Paul made three long journeys across Cyprus, Asia Minor, and Greece, planting churches at Galatia, Philippi, Thessalonica, Corinth, and Ephesus. The question whether his Gentile converts must be circumcised and keep the law of Moses was settled at the Jerusalem council (Acts 15; Paul’s own account, Galatians 2:1–10) in their favour. He describes the labour: “I laboured more abundantly than they all: yet not I, but the grace of God which was with me” (1 Corinthians 15:10).

Arrest, appeal to Caesar, and Rome

A riot at the Temple over a rumoured defilement led to Paul’s arrest (Acts 21:27–36). Held two years at Caesarea, he used his citizen’s right to appeal past the provincial governor to the emperor — “I appeal unto Caesar” (Acts 25:11) — and was shipped to Rome, surviving a storm and shipwreck off Malta. Acts ends not with a trial or a verdict but with the apostle in his own hired house under guard (Acts 28:16, 30):

Preaching the kingdom of God, and teaching those things which concern the Lord Jesus Christ, with all confidence, no man forbidding him.

— Acts 28:30–31. The later traditions of Paul’s release, a journey west, and his death concern the time after this ending.

Not one of the Twelve — and the plan for Spain

Paul calls himself an apostle by a distinct and later call: the risen Christ appeared to him “last of all… as of one born out of due time” (1 Corinthians 15:8), and his commission is specifically to the nations — “I am the apostle of the Gentiles” (Romans 11:13). Writing to Rome before his arrest, he lays out a plan:

Whensoever I take my journey into Spain, I will come to you… When therefore I have performed this, and have sealed to them this fruit, I will come by you into Spain.

— Romans 15:24, 28. Whether he carried it out is discussed on Did Paul reach Spain?

Placement on the timeline

  • Where Acts ends. Paul is documented at length up to his arrival at Rome, and then Acts stops. The later sources set out on this timeline concern what became of him after the two years of Acts 28.
  • Paul’s own voice. Much of what we know comes not from Acts but from Paul’s letters, where he reports his origin, conversion, journeys, and sufferings first-hand (2 Corinthians 11 catalogues the beatings, stonings, and shipwrecks; Clement of Rome’s list of Paul’s sufferings follows it).
  • Citizenship and the sword. Acts records that Paul was a Roman citizen “free born.” The tradition that Paul was beheaded while Peter, no citizen, was crucified is discussed with Roman law on the crucifixion of citizens on Was Paul beheaded at Rome under Nero?
