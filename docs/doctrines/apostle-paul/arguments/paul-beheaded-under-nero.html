Was Paul beheaded at Rome under Nero?

Disputed reading · on Paul · witness: Tertullian

Related readings: Did Paul reach Spain?

The passage

The earliest notice, from Rome about thirty years after the event, says only that Paul “suffered martyrdom under the prefects”; the fullest early notice, three centuries later, gives the year, the manner and the grave:

He then, in the fourteenth year of Nero on the same day with Peter, was beheaded at Rome for Christ’s sake and was buried in the Ostian way, the twenty-seventh year after our Lord’s passion.

— Clement of Rome, First Epistle to the Corinthians 5 (c. 96); Jerome, Lives of Illustrious Men 5 (c. 393).1

The question

Every early source surveyed here places Paul’s death at Rome, and none reports another end. What is discussed is how far back the particular details go — death by the sword rather than the cross, the reign of Nero, the year, the same day as Peter, and the tomb on the Ostian way — given that Clement names neither emperor nor manner, and the sword appears in the later second century, in the Acts of Paul and in Tertullian.2

Beheaded at Rome under Nero, as a Roman citizen

In this reading Paul was executed at Rome in Nero’s persecution of the mid-60s by the sword, the citizen’s death, and buried on the Ostian way, as the tradition from the Acts of Paul and Tertullian onward reports.

Held by: Tertullian (Scorpiace 15, c. 200); Eusebius (Ecclesiastical History 2.25, c. 325); Jerome (Lives of Illustrious Men 5, c. 393); Sean McDowell (grants the martyrdom).3

Clement, a Roman bishop within a generation, names Paul among the “pillars” martyred “in our own generation.” Ignatius of Antioch (c. 107), writing to Rome, appeals to Peter and Paul as those who had commanded there before him; Dionysius of Corinth (c. 170) says the two “suffered martyrdom at the same time”; Tertullian has Paul win his crown “in a death like John’s” and writes “that Paul is beheaded… Then does Paul obtain a birth suited to Roman citizenship”; Lactantius (c. 315) writes that Nero “crucified Peter, and slew Paul”; Eusebius, that “Paul was beheaded in Rome itself.” The witnesses span Rome, Antioch, Corinth, Carthage, Bithynia and Caesarea within about two and a half centuries.4 The manner is the one Roman law required. Cicero, a century before Paul, called it a crime to bind a Roman citizen, “to put him to death almost parricide,” and to crucify him beyond any fitting word, invoking the Porcian and Sempronian laws and the citizen’s right of appeal. Paul, “free born,” appealed “unto Caesar” as a citizen; the sword for Paul and the cross for the non-citizen Peter is the distinction that law draws.5 The place is marked. Gaius, a Roman presbyter of c. 200, could take a visitor to the “trophy” (tropaion) on the Ostian way. Constantine raised the first basilica of St Paul over that spot about 324; a fourth-century slab set into the tomb beneath the altar reads PAVLO APOSTOLO MART, “to Paul, Apostle, Martyr”; the Theodosian rebuilding from 386 left the tomb in place. In 2006 excavators reached the marble sarcophagus, and bone fragments probed from it were radiocarbon-dated, by testers told nothing of their provenance, to the first or second century. Benedict XVI announced the result in 2009, and the Vatican’s Ulderico Santamaria said it “doesn’t make certain, but also doesn’t exclude” that the remains are Paul’s. In this reading one venerated location, held from Gaius’s monument through Constantine’s basilica to the sarcophagus under the high altar, carries the first-century memory of Paul’s death and burial.6

An early memory of martyrdom at Rome, with the manner and the year specified later

In this reading the first-century memory is of a Roman martyrdom, and the sword, the year and the joint day with Peter were added to it in the following centuries.

Held by: ⚠ unverified — no named proponent located yet.

Clement, the earliest witness, says that Paul “suffered martyrdom under the prefects” and names neither the emperor nor the manner. The sword appears in the later second century, in the Martyrdom that closes the Acts of Paul and in Tertullian, who joins it to Paul’s Roman citizenship (“a birth suited to Roman citizenship”). The manner can thus be drawn from what Acts records of Paul — “free born,” appealing to Caesar — and from the law that spared a citizen the cross, by a writer who knew both.7 The year varies among the sources. Tacitus places Nero’s persecution of the Christians in the aftermath of the fire of Rome in 64; Jerome puts Paul’s death in Nero’s fourteenth year, 67/68; “under Nero,” who died in June 68, brackets them. Dionysius of Corinth has Peter and Paul suffer “at the same time,” Jerome “on the same day,” and Prudentius (c. 400) explains the joint feast by the same day of the year “recurring after a full year”; the feast on 29 June, kept at Rome since 258, fixes the day. (The same Neronian persecution is the setting for the readings of Revelation’s 666 as “Nero Caesar” and of the returning-Nero legend.)8 The details went on gathering: the Martyrdom of Paul that closes the Acts of Paul has milk spurt from the severed neck, and the medieval tradition of the Tre Fontane has the head bounce three times, a spring rising at each.9 Read so, the tradition is a first-century memory of Paul’s martyrdom at Rome around which each century set its own detail: Tertullian’s sword, Jerome’s year and day, and the wonders of the Acts of Paul and the Tre Fontane.
