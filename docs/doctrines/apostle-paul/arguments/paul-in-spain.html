Did Paul reach Spain?

Disputed reading · on Paul · witness: Clement of Rome

Related readings: Was Paul beheaded at Rome under Nero?

The passage

Paul told the Roman church, before his arrest, that Rome was to be a stop on the way west:

Whensoever I take my journey into Spain, I will come to you: for I trust to see you in my journey… When therefore I have performed this, and have sealed to them this fruit, I will come by you into Spain.

— Romans 15:24, 28. About thirty years later Clement of Rome wrote of him:1

After preaching both in the east and west, he gained the illustrious reputation due to his faith, having taught righteousness to the whole world, and come to the extreme limit of the west, and suffered martyrdom under the prefects.

— Clement of Rome, First Epistle to the Corinthians 5 (c. 96).2

The question

Acts ends with Paul under house-arrest at Rome, preaching “no man forbidding him,” and records no verdict, release or later journey. The question is whether Paul was released and carried out the Spanish mission announced in Romans 15, with a return east to the churches the Pastoral epistles name (Crete, Nicopolis, Miletus, Troas) and a second, fatal imprisonment at Rome, or whether the later notices of a western journey restate that announced plan. (A separate, later tradition, with its own page, sends James son of Zebedee to Spain.)3

Paul was released and carried the gospel to Spain

In this reading Paul was acquitted at the trial with which Acts leaves him, travelled west to Spain and then east again, and was arrested a second time and executed at Rome.

Held by: the Muratorian Fragment (c. 170–200); the Acts of Peter (c. 180–190); Jerome (Lives of Illustrious Men 5, c. 393); John Rutherfurd (grants the release and second imprisonment).4

The case starts from Clement, a bishop writing at Rome within a generation of Paul’s death. For the ancient geographical imagination the western terminus of the world was fixed: the Pillars of Hercules and Gades (Cadiz), on the Spanish coast beyond the Strait. Written from Rome, “the extreme limit of the west” points past the capital to Spain, and Clement sets the western reach before the martyrdom, as an earlier stage of Paul’s career.5 The Muratorian Fragment, a list of the New Testament books, names the journey outright among the things Luke left out of Acts: “the journey of Paul, when he went from the city — Rome — to Spain” (profectionem Pauli ab urbe… ad Spaniam). It pairs it with “the passion of Peter,” which stands in no text of Paul’s, so the writer treats both as received knowledge rather than as something read off Romans 15. The Vercelli Acts of Peter open, before Peter enters, with Paul at Rome directed by a vision — “Arise, Paul, and become a physician… to them that are in Spain” — and sailing west amid the weeping of the brethren: the journey circulating as narrated tradition within a century and a half.6 The Pastoral epistles presuppose journeys to Crete, Nicopolis, Miletus and Troas that have no place in Acts; a release, further travel and a second imprisonment give them a setting, with 2 Timothy written from the final captivity. Eusebius transmits the release (Paul “sent again upon the ministry of preaching… a second time”) and Jerome its purpose (“dismissed by Nero, that the gospel of Christ might be preached also in the West”). Rutherfurd lists Cyril of Jerusalem, Ephrem, Chrysostom and Theodoret among the writers who speak of Paul’s going to Spain, so that by the fourth century the journey was reported in East and West alike.7

The western journey is known from Paul’s plan, not from a record of its fulfilment

In this reading Paul’s recorded life ends at Rome, and the later notices of a Spanish mission restate the intention of Romans 15 as accomplished.

Held by: ⚠ unverified — no named proponent located yet.

Luke’s narrative, the fullest account of Paul’s travels, closes with him in Roman custody and contains no release, no Spain and no second trial. In Spain itself no church claims Paul as founder or keeps a local tradition of his preaching, beside the local memories that attach to Peter and Paul at Rome, Thomas in India and John at Ephesus; the later Spanish claims, at Tarragona and elsewhere, follow the literary tradition.8 The witnesses to the journey can be traced to a single root. Clement knew Paul’s letters (his catalogue of sufferings follows 2 Corinthians 11), and his line sits in a passage praising Paul’s endurance, where “the extreme limit of the west” expresses the reach of his preaching, or renders the plan of Romans 15 as done. The Muratorian Fragment can draw the journey from the same chapter of Romans. Whether the opening Spain chapters of the Acts of Peter are original to the work is disputed in studies by Vouaux, Poupon, Thomas and Lapham, listed in the e-Clavis bibliography; the chapters may depend on the Acts of Paul.9 Many critical scholars hold 1–2 Timothy and Titus to be post-Pauline, and on that view their itineraries belong to the later portrait of Paul, not to a record of his last years.10 Read so, the Spanish mission is the history of a hope: announced by Paul in Romans 15, spoken of as fulfilled from Clement on, and told as a story in the Acts of Peter, while Paul’s own course ran from Jerusalem to his death at Rome.
