Did the early church permit remarriage? (the deprecation of digamy)

May a Christian whose husband or wife has died marry again? This page records how Christian writers and councils answered, earliest extant witness first: Paul’s permission and his counsel of widowhood; the Shepherd of Hermas, Athenagoras, Clement of Alexandria and Origen; Tertullian’s treatises on marriage; the canons of Neocaesarea and Laodicea and the canonical letters of Basil, which set a term of penance for a second marriage; Ambrose, Jerome and Augustine; and the Council in Trullo, with the Byzantine regulation of third and fourth marriages in 920. Remarriage after a spouse’s death is “digamy” in the sources; remarriage after divorce is treated on a separate page.

Related doctrines: Marriage, celibacy, and the single life — see also divorce and remarriage — what did Jesus permit?

See also the perpetual virginity of Mary, and, on the “Was Tertullian a Heretic?” timeline, the disputed reading Was Tertullian’s ban on second marriage a matter of discipline or of doctrine?

Timeline

Paul the Apostle (c. 55)

Paul permits the widowed to remarry: a widow “is at liberty to be married to whom she will; only in the Lord,” and is “no adulteress” if she does; and he wills that “the younger women marry, bear children, guide the house.”1 He also writes that it is “good for them if they abide even as I,” that the widow “is happier if she so abide,” and that those who “cannot contain” should marry; and he requires that a bishop be “the husband of one wife” and that an enrolled widow have been “the wife of one man.”2

The Shepherd of Hermas (c. 140)

Asked whether a widow or widower who remarries sins, the angelic Shepherd replies: “There is no sin in marrying again… but if they remain unmarried, they gain greater honour and glory with the Lord.”3

Athenagoras of Athens (c. 177)

In his Plea to the emperors, Athenagoras writes that a man should “be content with one marriage; for a second marriage is only a specious adultery,” and that “he who deprives himself of his first wife, even though she be dead, is a cloaked adulterer.”4 Whether the phrase represents common Christian opinion independently of the New Prophecy, a trace of Montanist influence, or a strong expression within a church that never condemned second marriage is disputed; see the Disputed readings below.

Clement of Alexandria (c. 200)

In Stromateis III, written against those who held conduct indifferent and those who “impiously affect continence,” Clement writes of the case “if from sympathy the apostle allows a man a second marriage because he cannot control himself and burns with passion,” and quotes Paul that the widow is “happier… if she remains as she is.”5

Tertullian (c. 210)

Tertullian’s early To His Wife grants that the apostle “permits marrying indeed, but prefers abstinence,” and urges his wife to remain a widow; in the later On Exhortation to Chastity and On Monogamy he forbids a second marriage, which “will have to be termed no other than a species of fornication.”6 In On Monogamy he asks why the Paraclete may not have “abrogated an indulgence which Paul granted,” and holds that in the Greek original 1 Cor. 7:39 concerns a woman widowed before her conversion.7 How far that verse reaches is disputed (see the Disputed readings below), as is whether his ban was a matter of discipline or of doctrine, on the Tertullian timeline.

Origen of Alexandria (c. 245)

Origen reads Romans 7 as allowing that “there is no reproach for a woman whose first husband has died if she enters a second marriage.” On the bar against the twice-married in church office he writes that Paul “wishes no one of those of the church, who has attained to any eminence beyond the many… to make trial of a second marriage,” and that he was perplexed by it, since “some who have been married twice may be much better than those who have been married once.”8

The Council of Neocaesarea (c. 315)

Canon 7 forbids a presbyter to attend the wedding-feast of a second marriage, “for, since the digamist is worthy of penance, what kind of a presbyter shall he be, who… sanctioned the marriage?”; canon 3 refers to an appointed time of penance for those “who fall into many marriages.”9

The Council of Laodicea (c. 364)

Canon 1 restores communion “to those who have freely and lawfully joined in second marriages… after a short space… in prayer and fasting.”10

Basil of Caesarea (c. 375)

Basil’s first canonical letter records a year’s penance for a second marriage (“some authorities say two years”) and three or four for a third, of which he writes: “this is no longer described as marriage at all, but as polygamy; nay rather as limited fornication.”11

Ambrose of Milan (c. 377)

In Concerning Widows Ambrose urges widows to remain unmarried and writes: “I do not forbid second marriages, only I do not advise them”; the second marriage is one “which the precepts of the Apostle certainly do not condemn.”12

Jerome (c. 393)

In Against Jovinianus Jerome names Tertullian among those who wrote on the subject and argues at length for virginity over marriage; after objections were raised, Pammachius withdrew copies of the book from circulation. In the same work Jerome writes: “I do not condemn second, nor third, nor, pardon the expression, eighth marriages.”13

Augustine of Hippo (c. 414)

In On the Good of Widowhood Augustine writes that second marriages “are not condemned, but are set in lower honor,” that he dares “neither to condemn any marriage,” and that Tertullian “with railing tooth… attacks second marriages, as though unlawful, which the Apostle with sober mind allows to be altogether lawful.”14

The Council in Trullo (692)

Canon 3 describes its rule as a blend of the Roman “rule of exact perfection” and the Constantinopolitan one “of kindness and consideration,” “that neither gentleness fall into licence, nor severity into harshness,” and renews the canon barring from the clergy a man “joined in two marriages after his baptism.”15 After the fourth marriage of the emperor Leo VI (c. 906), the Tomos of Union (920) forbade fourth marriages and regulated third ones.16

Disputed readings

How Tertullian’s ban, his reading of 1 Cor. 7:39 and Athenagoras’s phrase have been read.

  • Was Tertullian’s ban on second marriage a matter of discipline or of doctrine? — on the ground Augustine gave for listing him among the heresies.17
  • Does 1 Corinthians 7:39 free every Christian widow to remarry, or only one widowed before her conversion (Tertullian, On Monogamy 11)? — on Tertullian’s appeal to the “authentic Greek” of the verse.18
  • Does Athenagoras’s “a second marriage is only a specious adultery” (Plea 33, c. 177) represent common Christian opinion independently of the New Prophecy? — on where the phrase stands among second-century witnesses.19
