Bethany & Bethphage: the tomb of Lazarus and the Palm Sunday sending-place

Two villages on the eastern flank of the Mount of Olives frame the approach to the Passion. At Bethany, John sets the raising of Lazarus and the anointing that opens Holy Week; at Bethphage, the Synoptics have Jesus send two disciples for the colt he rides into Jerusalem on Palm Sunday. They are a natural pair — both on the Bethany side of the mount, both stations on the same festal road — and pilgrims, builders and liturgies have marked them together since the fourth century. This page traces, source by source, how each Gospel location became a shown site, a church, a liturgy, and a relic.

It is a reception-history timeline: not a survey of who believed a doctrine, but a chronicle of how two identifications grew. Its “witnesses” are datable sources and events — a topographer's gazetteer, the oldest Christian travel-diary, a pilgrim's Holy-Week road-log, a translator's biography of a pilgrim, a Crusader queen's convent, a painted mounting-stone, an Ottoman mosque cut through with a new Franciscan door, and a nineteenth-century Franciscan church and a Barluzzi church — ordered earliest-source-first and each footnoted. A tomb at Bethany is reported as shown from the early fourth century (Eusebius, the Bordeaux Pilgrim); the painted Bethphage stone dates from the twelfth.

Related doctrines: Sites of the Passion — see also the tomb of Jesus (Golgotha & the Holy Sepulchre) · the Garden of Gethsemane · the site of the Ascension

Timeline

The Gospels place the two villages (1st century)

John fixes Bethany with a distance: it “was nigh unto Jerusalem, about fifteen furlongs off,”1 and there sets the raising of Lazarus — “Lazarus, come forth. And he that was dead came forth”2 — and, “six days before the passover,” the supper at which Mary anoints his feet.3 The Synoptics name the other village on the same ridge: “when they drew nigh unto Jerusalem, and were come to Bethphage, unto the mount of Olives, then sent Jesus two disciples” for the colt of the triumphal entry.4 So the two sites are given as neighbours a short walk east of the city, on the far slope of Olivet, tied to two moments that open Holy Week — a raising and a royal entry.

Eusebius: both villages in the gazetteer — the tomb “pointed out even now” (c. 325)

The earliest extant topographical notice of either place is in Eusebius's Onomasticon, his gazetteer of biblical sites, which carries consecutive entries for both. Bethphage is “a village on the Mount of Olives where the Lord Jesus came”; Bethany is a “village two miles from Jerusalem on the slope of the Mount of Olives where the Christ raised Lazarus,” and “the place (tomb) of Lazarus is pointed out even now.”5 As with Eusebius's Gethsemane entry, the wording preserves two stages: the “pointed out even now” is his own early-fourth-century Greek, while Jerome's Latin recension of the work adds that a church “has been constructed” on the spot — the shown grave already sanctuaried by Jerome's day. Eusebius's entry locates the village and reports a tomb being shown there; it mentions no church.

The Bordeaux Pilgrim marks the crypt of Lazarus (333)

The oldest surviving Christian travel diary, which names no Gethsemane station, does name one at Bethany. The anonymous pilgrim from Bordeaux, writing the very year he travelled, records: “to the east about a mile and a half is a villa which is called Bethany; there is the crypt where Lazarus was laid, whom the Lord raised.”6 It is a second notice, by a different writer in the same decade as Eusebius, of a specific tomb shown at Bethany by 333 — a crypt; the pilgrim names no church there. The pilgrim's terse list of what a visitor was taken to see puts the Lazarus-tomb among the stations of the Holy Land.

Egeria: Lazarus-Saturday at the Lazarium and the church where Mary met the Lord (c. 384)

By the 380s Egeria describes a church and a stational liturgy at Bethany. She describes the Saturday that opens Holy Week: at the morning dismissal the archdeacon announces, “At one o'clock today let us all be ready at the Lazarium,” and the whole city goes out to Bethany. Half a mile short of it they stop at “a church by the road… the spot where Lazarus' sister Mary met the Lord,” hear the Gospel of that meeting, then process to the Lazarium itself, where “so many people have collected that they fill not only the Lazarium itself, but all the fields around,” and a priest “mounts a platform, and reads the Gospel passage which begins ‘When Jesus came to Bethany six days before the Passover.’”7 Two things appear here, in the earliest extant description of them on this timeline: a church — the Lazarium — on the site, and a stational liturgy that reads each Gospel scene on its own ground. (Egeria's separate Palm Sunday procession begins at the summit of Olivet, not at Bethphage; the Custody of the Holy Land dates the extension of the palm-procession down to the Bethphage church to the ninth century.8)

Jerome names the Lazarium and leads Paula to both villages (c. 390–404)

Jerome, who translated Eusebius's gazetteer and settled at Bethlehem, supplies both the church's name and a firsthand pilgrimage. His Latin recension of the Onomasticon is where the Onomasticon's note that a church “has been constructed” over the tomb first appears, and he calls the church the Lazarium; the Wikipedia article “Tomb of Lazarus” credits Jerome (390) with the first mention of the name, which Egeria's diary (c. 381–384) also uses.9 In his memorial letter for the noblewoman Paula (Letter 108, c. 404) he retraces her route through both villages: “Paula visited the tomb of Lazarus and beheld the hospitable roof of Mary and Martha, as well as Bethphage,” where “a restive foal… received the bridle of God.”10 Letter 108 records Paula visiting the tomb, the sisters' house and Bethphage in one tour.

A Crusader queen rebuilds Bethany; the Bethphage stone is painted (12th century)

After the fourth-century Lazarium fell (destroyed by an earthquake in the sixth century, according to the “Tomb of Lazarus” article) and was replaced, the Crusader kingdom gave both sites their medieval fabric. In 1138 Queen Melisende obtained the village of Bethany and founded a large Benedictine convent of Mary and Martha beside the tomb, building a new church directly over the grave of Lazarus.11 At Bethphage the Crusaders dressed a cube of native rock into a square block and covered it with paintings — on one face the raising of Lazarus (Martha, Mary, and the risen Lazarus), on another the colt and its foal and a crowd with palms, with the Latin name Bethfage inscribed — venerating it as the stone from which Jesus mounted the colt. Buried when the site was lost, it was rediscovered in the nineteenth century during Franciscan building and still stands in the church.12

The tomb becomes a mosque; the Franciscans cut a new door (14th–16th centuries)

With the fall of the Crusader kingdom Bethany declined, and by the fourteenth century the churches were in ruins and the original entrance to the tomb had passed into Muslim hands; from the sixteenth century the spot has been occupied by the al-Uzair Mosque — the site is holy to Muslims too, al-‘Azar being the Arabic Lazarus, from which the modern village-name al-Eizariya derives. As Christian access to the grave grew harder, the Franciscans were permitted between roughly 1566 and 1575 to cut a new entrance into the tomb from the north, the flight of worn steps by which pilgrims still descend to the burial chamber.13

The modern churches and the revived Palm Sunday procession (1883–1955)

Both sites' present churches are Franciscan (Bethphage 1883; Bethany 1952–1955). The Bethphage church was rebuilt in 1883 on its twelfth-century Crusader ruins, and the Palm Sunday procession — lapsed after the Crusader period, revived by the Franciscans in the sixteenth–seventeenth centuries and re-solemnized in 1933 under the Latin Patriarch — once again sets out from it down the mount into Jerusalem.14 At Bethany the architect Antonio Barluzzi — the same hand as the Church of All Nations at Gethsemane — built the windowless, crypt-like Church of St. Lazarus in 1952–1955 over the remains of the Byzantine and Crusader east churches, its courtyard preserving stretches of the fourth-century mosaic floor and the west facade of the sixth-century basilica.15 The remains on the one spot thus include a fourth-century mosaic, a sixth-century basilica, the Crusader church and the modern church.
