Bethany & Bethphage: the tomb of Lazarus and the Palm Sunday sending-place

Two villages on the eastern flank of the Mount of Olives frame the approach to the Passion. At Bethany, John sets the raising of Lazarus and the anointing that opens Holy Week; at Bethphage, the Synoptics have Jesus send two disciples for the colt he rides into Jerusalem on Palm Sunday. They are a natural pair — both on the Bethany side of the mount, both stations on the same festal road — and the church has kept and marked them together for sixteen centuries. This page traces how each Gospel location became a shown site, a church, a liturgy, and a relic, and where the tradition is secure versus where it is only a devotional fix on a credible spot.

It is a reception-history timeline: not a survey of who believed a doctrine, but a chronicle of how two identifications grew and were tested. Its “witnesses” are datable sources and events — a topographer's gazetteer, the oldest Christian travel-diary, a nun's Holy-Week road-log, a translator's biography of a pilgrim, a Crusader queen's convent, a painted mounting-stone, an Ottoman mosque cut through with a new Franciscan door, and a pair of Barluzzi churches — ordered earliest-source-first and each footnoted. The shape to watch for is the one that recurs across the Passion sites: the general location holds firm from the fourth century, while the exact relic is a later and softer growth. Two questions — whether the tomb shown at al-Eizariya is really Lazarus's, and whether the Bethphage stone is the block Jesus mounted — are weighed on their own argument pages.

Timeline

The Gospels place the two villages (1st century)

John fixes Bethany with a distance: it “was nigh unto Jerusalem, about fifteen furlongs off,”1 and there sets the raising of Lazarus — “Lazarus, come forth. And he that was dead came forth”2 — and, “six days before the passover,” the supper at which Mary anoints his feet.3 The Synoptics name the other village on the same ridge: “when they drew nigh unto Jerusalem, and were come to Bethphage, unto the mount of Olives, then sent Jesus two disciples” for the colt of the triumphal entry.4 So the two sites are given as neighbours a short walk east of the city, on the far slope of Olivet, tied to two moments that open Holy Week — a raising and a royal entry. Everything later builds on that Gospel geography.

Eusebius: both villages in the gazetteer — the tomb “pointed out even now” (c. 325)

The earliest topographical notice of either place is in Eusebius's Onomasticon, his gazetteer of biblical sites, which carries consecutive entries for both. Bethphage is “a village on the Mount of Olives where the Lord Jesus came”; Bethany is a “village two miles from Jerusalem on the slope of the Mount of Olives where the Christ raised Lazarus,” and — the crucial clause — “the place (tomb) of Lazarus is pointed out even now.”5 As with Eusebius's Gethsemane entry, the wording preserves two stages: the “pointed out even now” is his own early-fourth-century Greek, while Jerome's Latin recension of the work adds that a church “has been constructed” on the spot — the shown grave already sanctuaried by Jerome's day. What Eusebius fixes is the general ground and the existence of a venerated tomb a generation before any church is attested on it.

The Bordeaux Pilgrim marks the crypt of Lazarus (333)

Where the oldest surviving Christian travel diary is conspicuously silent about Gethsemane, at Bethany it speaks. The anonymous pilgrim from Bordeaux, writing the very year he travelled, records: “to the east about a mile and a half is a villa which is called Bethany; there is the crypt where Lazarus was laid, whom the Lord raised.”6 This is independent confirmation, from a different pen the same decade as Eusebius, that a specific tomb was being shown at Bethany by 333 — the crypt, not yet a church. The pilgrim's terse gazetteer of what a visitor was taken to see puts the Lazarus-tomb among the fixed stations of the Holy Land while the site was still, physically, only a grave in a hillside.

Egeria: Lazarus-Saturday at the Lazarium and the church where Mary met the Lord (c. 384)

By the 380s Bethany is fully built and fully liturgical. The pilgrim Egeria describes the Saturday that opens Holy Week: at the morning dismissal the archdeacon announces, “At one o'clock today let us all be ready at the Lazarium,” and the whole city goes out to Bethany. Half a mile short of it they stop at “a church by the road… the spot where Lazarus' sister Mary met the Lord,” hear the Gospel of that meeting, then process to the Lazarium itself, where “so many people have collected that they fill not only the Lazarium itself, but all the fields around,” and a priest “mounts a platform, and reads the Gospel passage which begins ‘When Jesus came to Bethany six days before the Passover.’”7 Two things enter the record here: the first firm evidence of an actual church — the Lazarium — on the site, and a developed stational liturgy that reads each Gospel scene on its own ground. (Egeria's separate Palm Sunday procession still begins at the summit of Olivet, not yet at Bethphage; the extension of the palm-procession down to the Bethphage church is a later, ninth-century growth.8)

Jerome names the Lazarium and leads Paula to both villages (c. 390–404)

Jerome, who translated Eusebius's gazetteer and settled at Bethlehem, supplies both the church's name and a firsthand pilgrimage. His Latin recension of the Onomasticon is where the note that a church “has been built” over the tomb enters the record, and he calls the church the Lazarium — the first author to name it.9 In his memorial letter for the noblewoman Paula (Letter 108, c. 404) he retraces her route through both villages: “Paula visited the tomb of Lazarus and beheld the hospitable roof of Mary and Martha, as well as Bethphage,” where “a restive foal… received the bridle of God.”10 Between the two notices the whole pair is in view: a named church over Lazarus's tomb, the remembered house of the sisters, and Bethphage as the colt's sending-place — the eastern Holy-Week road walked as a single circuit by the late fourth century.

A Crusader queen rebuilds Bethany; the Bethphage stone is painted (12th century)

After the fourth-century Lazarium fell (an earthquake is blamed) and was replaced, the Crusader kingdom gave both sites their medieval fabric. In 1138 Queen Melisende obtained the village of Bethany and founded a large Benedictine convent of Mary and Martha beside the tomb, building a new church directly over the grave of Lazarus.11 At Bethphage the Crusaders dressed a cube of native rock into a square block and covered it with paintings — on one face the raising of Lazarus (Martha, Mary, and the risen Lazarus), on another the colt and its foal and a crowd with palms, with the Latin name Bethfage inscribed — venerating it as the very stone from which Jesus mounted the colt. Buried when the site was lost, it was rediscovered in the nineteenth century during Franciscan building and still stands in the church.12

The tomb becomes a mosque; the Franciscans cut a new door (14th–16th centuries)

With the fall of the Crusader kingdom Bethany declined, and by the fourteenth century the churches were in ruins and the original entrance to the tomb had passed into Muslim hands; from the sixteenth century the spot has been occupied by the al-Uzair Mosque — the site is holy to Muslims too, al-‘Azar being the Arabic Lazarus, from which the modern village-name al-Eizariya derives. As Christian access to the grave grew harder, the Franciscans were permitted between roughly 1566 and 1575 to cut a new entrance into the tomb from the north, the flight of worn steps by which pilgrims still descend to the burial chamber.13 The tomb's continuity is unbroken through this whole stretch — the same shown grave, only its doorway and its custodians changing.

Barluzzi's two churches and the revived Palm Sunday procession (1883–1955)

The modern shape of both sites is Franciscan and twentieth-century. The Bethphage church was rebuilt in 1883 on its twelfth-century Crusader ruins, and the Palm Sunday procession — lapsed after the Crusader period, revived by the Franciscans in the sixteenth–seventeenth centuries and re-solemnized in 1933 under the Latin Patriarch — once again sets out from it down the mount into Jerusalem.14 At Bethany the architect Antonio Barluzzi — the same hand as the Church of All Nations at Gethsemane — built the windowless, crypt-like Church of St. Lazarus in 1952–1955 over the remains of the Byzantine and Crusader east churches, its courtyard preserving stretches of the fourth-century mosaic floor and the west facade of the sixth-century basilica.15 The dig-confirmed layering — fourth-century mosaic, sixth-century basilica, Crusader church, modern basilica, all on one spot — is the physical counterpart of the written chain from Eusebius forward.

Arguments

Is the tomb shown at al-Eizariya the tomb of Lazarus? — plausible

The grave shown at Bethany has the strongest pedigree of any Passion-adjacent tomb after the Holy Sepulchre: it is attested as “pointed out even now” by Eusebius (c. 325) and as “the crypt where Lazarus was laid” by the Bordeaux Pilgrim (333), a full generation earlier than the earliest notices at Gethsemane, and the identification has run unbroken — through church, convent, mosque, and modern basilica — ever since. A two-level verdict, kept distinct: the village of Bethany is securely located (John's fifteen-furlong distance, Eusebius's two miles, and the continuous settlement at al-Eizariya agree), so this is sound; but that this rock-cut tomb is the actual grave of Lazarus is plausible, no more — the fourth-century sources fix a shown tomb, not a verified one, and no first-century marker or decisive excavation raises it to probable. Early, unbroken, and geographically right; short of proven.

Is the Bethphage stone the block from which Jesus mounted the colt? — weak

The painted cube venerated in the Bethphage church is a genuine and vivid twelfth-century relic — a Crusader-dressed stone whose images of the raising of Lazarus and the triumphal entry make it a real witness to medieval Holy-Week devotion. But the claim that it is the very block Jesus stepped on to mount the colt is weak: it is a Crusader identification with no ancient attestation, resting on a Gospel scene (disciples set Jesus on the colt, Luke 19:35) that never mentions a mounting-stone, and the notion of a dressed mounting-block fits a Frankish knight's war-charger, as Jerome Murphy-O'Connor observed, far better than a Palestinian donkey. Authentic as a medieval relic and a marker of the right village; without basis as a first-century object.
