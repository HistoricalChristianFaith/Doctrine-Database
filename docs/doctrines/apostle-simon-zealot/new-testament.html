The New Testament on Simon the Zealot

Dates: c. 30–90 (the events, and the writing of the Gospels and Acts) · Wikipedia
Summary: Simon appears only in the four lists of the Twelve, always eleventh — next to last, just before Judas Iscariot — and is given nothing but a surname to tell him apart from Simon Peter. Matthew and Mark call him “the Cananaean”; Luke and Acts, in the same slot, “the Zealot.” Klemens Löffler (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1912) reads the two as one epithet in two languages, meaning “the Zealous”; Jerome and the Greek tradition connected him with Cana. What the “zeal” signified — the revolutionary party, or devotion to the Law — is disputed.

Claims & evidence

“Simon the Cananaean” in Matthew and Mark

In the first two Gospels the eleventh apostle — standing in the third group of four, after James the son of Alphaeus and Thaddaeus — is Simon the Cananaean (the KJV, following the later spelling, has “Canaanite”):

… Simon the Canaanite, and Judas Iscariot, who also betrayed him.

— Matthew 10:4 (cf. Mark 3:18, “and Simon the Canaanite”). The Greek is ho Kananaios, which Löffler and C. M. Kerr (International Standard Bible Encyclopedia) take as a zeal-word: Löffler’s “Hebrew qana (the Zealous),” Kerr’s kanna’i, “the Jealous (or Zealous) One.” Löffler writes that “Jerome and others wrongly assumed that Kana was his native place; were this so, he should have been called Kanaios”: in his reading the word is not a place-name, and it is the word Luke translates (Catholic Encyclopedia; ISBE).

“Simon called Zelotes” in Luke and Acts

Luke gives the epithet in Greek in both of his lists:

Matthew and Thomas, James the son of Alphaeus, and Simon called Zelotes.

— Luke 6:15 (cf. Acts 1:13, “and Simon Zelotes”). Zēlōtēs is the Greek for “zealous one.” Löffler writes that “Both surnames have the same signification and are a translation of the Hebrew qana (the Zealous),” given “To distinguish him from St. Peter”: the Twelve already held one prominent Simon, Simon Peter (Catholic Encyclopedia).

The sense of the epithet

The lists agree on the word and do not state its force. In first-century usage “zealous” (zēlōtēs) could name a temper — a man ardent for God and the Law, as Paul calls himself “zealous toward God” and “exceedingly zealous of the traditions” — or a party: the armed nationalist faction Josephus calls “the Zealots,” which drove the revolt against Rome. The New Testament does not say which is meant here. Kerr (ISBE) reads the surname politically: on that reading the circle of Jesus held a man from the insurgent movement alongside Matthew the tax-gatherer, who had served Rome. Löffler and John P. Meier read it as zeal for the Law, Meier on the ground that Josephus dates the party to the war of the 60s, a generation after the call of the Twelve. The readings are set out on their own page.

Reception and debate

  • One epithet, two languages. Löffler and Kerr take “Cananaean” (Matthew, Mark) and “Zelotes” (Luke, Acts) as the Semitic and the Greek forms of the same word, “zealous”; the two names never appear together for one man. The New Testament records nothing of Simon’s life, mission or death.
  • “Of Cana” and “Canaanite.” The KJV prints “Canaanite” for Kananaios. Löffler reports that “The Greeks, Copts, and Ethiopians identify him with Nathanael of Cana; the first-mentioned also identify him with the bridegroom of the marriage of Cana,” and that Jerome took Cana as his native place; Löffler himself reads Kananaios as the zeal-word (Catholic Encyclopedia).
  • Three Simons in the record. Simon Peter, Simon the Zealot, and Simeon son of Clopas, the Lord’s kinsman who became second bishop of Jerusalem. Some later apostle-lists identify the last two; see the apostle-lists page and the interpretation page.
