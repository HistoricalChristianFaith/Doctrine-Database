The Golden Legend on Simon the Zealot

Dates: c. 1260 (compiled by Jacobus de Voragine; drawing on the Latin Passio Simonis et Iudae) · Golden Legend (Caxton)
Summary: Jacobus de Voragine’s life of Simon in the Golden Legend retells the Latin passion of Simon and Jude. It sends the two apostles together into Persia, pits them against a pair of sorcerers, and martyrs them side by side — the source of their joint feast on 28 October in the West and of Western art’s pairing of Simon’s saw with Jude’s club.

Claims & evidence

Into Persia together

The Golden Legend makes Simon and Jude a missionary pair, each with his own first field, meeting in Persia:

Judas preached first in Mesopotamia and in Pontus, and Simon preached in Egypt, and from thence came they into Persia…

— where they found “two enchanters, Zaroes and Arphaxat, whom S. Matthew had driven out of Ethiopia.” The account links Simon’s story to two others in the Golden Legend: to Jude, whose partner he becomes, and to Matthew, whose adversaries the two apostles meet. The Western calendar keeps Simon and Jude on one day; the Catholic Encyclopedia reports that “In the Western Church he is venerated together with Jude (Thaddaeus); in the East separately.” The parallel Jude/Thaddaeus timeline tells the same scene from the other apostle’s side.1

The martyrdom at Suanir

After many wonders and disputations, the priests of the sun-temple turn on the apostles:

… the bishops and the people took them and brought them to the temple of the sun… [and] they ran upon the apostles and hewed them to death anon.

This joint martyrdom in a pagan temple in Persia is the death commemorated in the Western feast of “SS. Simon and Jude” on 28 October. The instrument Western art gives Simon, a saw, comes from the parallel tradition that Simon was sawn asunder (“his body was said to have been sawed to pieces”).2

The crucifixion tradition and the other Simon

Jacobus also reports a rival crucifixion tradition, and a view that the crucified Simon was another man:

… it is founden in divers places, of S. Simon, that he was nailed to the cross… And some say verily that it was not this Simon that suffered the martyrdom of the cross, but it was another.

For the crucifixion Jacobus names Isidore’s Book of the Death of the Apostles, Eusebius’s History, Bede on Acts and John Beleth’s Summa, and relates that Simon “was made bishop in Jerusalem after the death of James the Less” and was crucified there at “six score years old.” The “another” is, in his next words, “the son of Cleophas, brother of Joseph,” for which he cites Eusebius’s chronicle, Bede’s retractions and Usuard’s Martyrology. Both views bear on whether Simon was Simeon son of Clopas; see the interpretation page.3

Reception and debate

  • Date and sources. The Golden Legend (c. 1260) is a thirteenth-century collection of saints’ lives drawing on the Latin Passio Simonis et Iudae; it records how Simon was commemorated in the medieval West. On the sources for the apostles generally, see the apostles hub.
  • Simon and Jude as a pair. The pairing gave the two apostles their common feast and their paired emblems (Simon’s saw, Jude’s club). The same joint martyrdom is treated, from Jude’s side, on the Thaddaeus/Jude Golden Legend page.
  • Other accounts of his death. Persia (Suanir) here; Britain, Egypt, and the Caucasus elsewhere; and the manner hewn, sawn, or crucified. The accounts are set out as readings on the mission-and-death interpretation page.
