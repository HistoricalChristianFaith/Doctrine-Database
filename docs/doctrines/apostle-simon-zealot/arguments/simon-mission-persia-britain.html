Where did Simon the Zealot preach and die — Persia, Britain, or elsewhere?

Disputed reading · on Simon the Zealot · witness: the apostle-lists and the Golden Legend

Related readings: Was Simon the Zealot the same man as Simeon son of Clopas? · Does Simon’s surname “the Zealot” mark him a member of the revolutionary party?

The passage

… according to the Greeks, he preached on the Black Sea, in Egypt, Northern Africa, and Britain, while, according to the Latin Passio Simonis et Judae… Simon laboured in Persia, and was there martyred at Suanir.

— Catholic Encyclopedia, “St. Simon the Apostle,” summarizing the sources.1

The question

The New Testament records no mission or death for Simon. Later traditions place his preaching and death in several regions: the Black Sea, Egypt, North Africa and Britain; Persia, with Jude; the Caucasus; Edessa; and Jerusalem, where some sources make him its bishop (treated under the Simeon question). The question is which, if any, of these preserves where Simon went and how he died. The sources used here do not date the Greek lists, the Latin Passio or the Caucasian traditions, so the readings below follow the order in which this timeline presents their sources.

The north and west: the Black Sea, Egypt, North Africa and Britain

In this reading Simon’s field lay along the Black Sea and across Egypt and North Africa, and ended in Britain.

Held by: the Greek tradition, as summarized by Löffler (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1912); the Greek menologies and the tradition behind Pseudo-Dorotheus; the British tradition of his crucifixion at Caistor ⚠ unverified.2

The Greek catalogues give Simon a northern and western mission: the coasts of the Black Sea, then Egypt and North Africa (Mauretania and “Africa the less”), and at last Britain. One strand of the British account, reported by Wikipedia, has him arrive a second time in the year 60, “the first of Boadicea’s rebellion,” and be crucified on 10 May 61 at Caistor in Lincolnshire; Caesar Baronius and Hippolytus are cited there for a first arrival in 44. On this reading the apostles were sent to the ends of the earth, and Simon’s share was the far west of the Roman world.3

Persia with Jude, ending in martyrdom at Suanir

In this reading Simon preached in Egypt, joined Jude in Persia, and died with him there.

Held by: the Latin Passio Simonis et Iudae; Jacobus de Voragine (Golden Legend, c. 1260).4

The Golden Legend, retelling the Passio, relates that “Judas preached first in Mesopotamia and in Pontus, and Simon preached in Egypt, and from thence came they into Persia.” There the two apostles confront the enchanters Zaroes and Arphaxat and are killed in a temple, where the priests “ran upon the apostles and hewed them to death anon.” Löffler reports that the author of the Passio was, as Lipsius maintains, “sufficiently familiar with the history of the Parthian Empire in the first century.” The Latin West carries this account in its calendar: it keeps one feast for Simon and Jude on 28 October, and the Breviarium Apostolorum and the liturgy send the two together to Persia. On this reading the joint mission and martyrdom is the account the Western stream of tradition holds in common.5

The Caucasus: Colchis, Iberia and Abkhazia

In this reading Simon worked and died in the lands east of the Black Sea, where his tomb is honoured.

Held by: Moses of Chorene ⚠ unverified; the Georgian tradition.6

Löffler reports that “According to Moses of Chorene, Simon met his death in Weriosphora in Iberia; according to the Georgians, he preached in Colchis.” He adds that Suanir, the place of the Latin martyrdom, “is probably to be sought in Colchis,” so that the Persian story and the Caucasian one may point to the same region. An Eastern tradition reported by Wikipedia has Simon travel to Georgia, die in Abkhazia and be buried “in Nicopsia, a not yet identified site on the Black Sea coast,” his remains later transferred to Anakopia in Abkhazia. On this reading the churches of the Caucasus kept the memory of the apostle who evangelized them, and the tomb they honour marks where he died.7

His field and death are not recoverable from the sources

In this reading Simon likely preached beyond Judaea, but no surviving tradition fixes where, or how he died.

Held by: Klemens Löffler (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1912); Sean McDowell (The Fate of the Apostles, 2015).8

Löffler writes: “Where he actually preached the Gospel is uncertain. Almost all the lands of the then known world, even as far as Britain, have been mentioned,” and “His place of burial is unknown.” McDowell, grading the martyrdom accounts of the Twelve, rates the evidence for any particular death of Simon among the weakest of all. On this reading the traditions are rival answers rather than stages of one itinerary, and their manner of death varies as much as their place: sawn asunder, crucified, or hewn down by temple priests. The Golden Legend reports that “some say” the crucified Simon “was another.” The apostles were sent out, and Simon may well have carried the gospel beyond Judaea; the sources that name a field are later catalogues and passions, and the destination is left open.9
