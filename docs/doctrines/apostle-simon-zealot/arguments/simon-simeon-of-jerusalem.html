Was Simon the Zealot the same man as Simeon son of Clopas, the second bishop of Jerusalem?

Disputed reading · on Simon the Zealot · witness: the apostle-lists (Pseudo-Hippolytus)

Related readings: Does Simon’s surname “the Zealot” mark him a member of the revolutionary party? · Where did Simon the Zealot preach and die?

The passage

Simon the Zealot, the son of Clopas, who is also called Jude, became bishop of Jerusalem after James the Just, and fell asleep and was buried there at the age of 120 years.

— Pseudo-Hippolytus, On the Twelve Apostles 11. Hegesippus, preserved in Eusebius, reports of Symeon son of Clopas that “All proposed him as second bishop because he was a cousin of the Lord,” and that he suffered martyrdom “at the age of one hundred and twenty years, while Trajan was emperor.”1

The question

Pseudo-Hippolytus gives the apostle Simon the Zealot the parentage, office and age at death that Hegesippus gives Symeon son of Clopas, the Lord’s cousin who led the Jerusalem church after James the Just. The question is whether the eleventh apostle and the second bishop of Jerusalem were one man, or two men with one name. It bears on the place of the Lord’s kindred among the Twelve and on the early leadership of the Jerusalem church.

One man: the apostle became bishop of Jerusalem

In this reading the silent eleventh apostle is the Lord’s kinsman who took the Jerusalem chair after James.

Held by: Pseudo-Hippolytus (On the Twelve Apostles, date uncertain); the Chronicon Paschale; the apocryphal Preaching of Simon, son of Cleopas; C. M. Kerr (International Standard Bible Encyclopedia, 1939 ed.).2

The identification recurs across the tradition. Pseudo-Hippolytus states it outright, and Löffler reports that “in the ‘Chronicon paschale’ and elsewhere he is identified with Simon Clopas.” The Preaching heads its account “The preaching of the blessed Simon, the son of Cleopas, who was surnamed Judas, which is interpreted Nathanael, who became bishop of Jerusalem after James the brother of our Lord.” The Golden Legend records the tradition that Simon, after preaching in Egypt, “was made bishop in Jerusalem after the death of James the Less,” governed that church until Trajan, and was crucified there at “six score years old.” Kerr gives the reading its family argument. Alphaeus, the father of James, “is generally regarded as the same as Clopas or Cleopas”; if so, Simon son of Cleopas and James son of Alphaeus were brothers, which “would shed light on the reason of the juxtaposition of James son of Alpheus and Simon Zelotes in the apostolic lists of Luke and Acts, i.e. they were brothers.” Kerr adds that Philip’s invitation in John 1:45 “would naturally be addressed to a follower of the Zealots.” The Lord’s brothers and cousins were prominent in the first church; on this reading one of them sat among the Twelve and later held Jerusalem, joining the apostolic and the kinship leadership in one man.3

Two men: the apostle and the Lord’s cousin who succeeded James

In this reading Symeon son of Clopas was a kinsman chosen to lead Jerusalem, not one of the Twelve.

Held by: those whom Jacobus de Voragine (Golden Legend, c. 1260) reports as saying the crucified Simon “was another”; Richard Bauckham (Jude and the Relatives of Jesus, 1990) ⚠ unverified.4

Jacobus reports that “some say verily that it was not this Simon that suffered the martyrdom of the cross, but it was another, the son of Cleophas, brother of Joseph.” He cites Eusebius’s chronicle for it, and adds that Isidore and Eusebius “corrected their chronicles,” that Bede “revoked it in his retractions,” and that Usuard’s Martyrology bears the same witness. The reading rests on Hegesippus, the second-century Palestinian writer whom Eusebius quotes on the Lord’s kindred. Hegesippus presents Symeon as the son of “the Lord’s uncle, Clopas,” put forward as second bishop “because he was a cousin of the Lord.” Lightfoot notes that “in Hegesippus Symeon is never once called the Lord’s brother, while James is always so designated,” and sees “no reason indeed for doubting the testimony of Hegesippus, who was perhaps born during the lifetime of this Symeon.” On this reading the succession followed kinship: after James was martyred, the church chose a cousin of the Lord. Eusebius, who transmits Hegesippus, does not call Symeon an apostle or link him with Simon the Zealot. Lightfoot also stresses how common the name was: “In the New Testament no less than twelve persons bear the name of Symeon or Simon.”5
