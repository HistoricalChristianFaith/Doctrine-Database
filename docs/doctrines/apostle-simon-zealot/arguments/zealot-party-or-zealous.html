Does Simon’s surname “the Zealot” mark him a member of the revolutionary party?

Disputed reading · on Simon the Zealot · witness: the New Testament

Related readings: Was Simon the Zealot the same man as Simeon son of Clopas? · Where did Simon the Zealot preach and die?

The passage

… Simon called Zelotes…

— Luke 6:15 (cf. Acts 1:13; and “Simon the Canaanite,” Matthew 10:4, Mark 3:18). Matthew and Mark’s Greek is ho Kananaios; Luke and Acts give zēlōtēs in the same slot of the list.1

The question

Jerome and the Orthodox Lives of the Saints connect Kananaios with Cana in Galilee and read Simon’s zeal as zeal for Christ. The writers of the other two readings take Kananaios and zēlōtēs as one zeal-word, a Semitic surname and its Greek rendering, and dispute its force: whether the surname marks Simon as one of the armed movement against Rome that Josephus names “the Zealots,” or as a man zealous for the Law. The answer bears on the make-up of the Twelve and on the date of the Zealot movement itself. A modern academic treatment holding the Cana reading was not located.

Simon of Cana, zealous for Christ

In this reading Simon is named from Cana in Galilee, and his zeal is the zeal of a follower of Christ.

Held by: Jerome (Latin, Commentary on Matthew, 398); the Orthodox Church in America (Lives of the Saints, 10 May).2

Jerome explains why Peter is named first in Matthew’s list: “to distinguish him from the other Simon, who is called the Cananaean, from Cana, the village of Galilee where the Lord turned water into wine.” In the same comment he gives the surname’s sense as well: Simon “is the one who in another evangelist is written as zelotes; for Cana is interpreted ‘zeal’.” On this reading the place and the zeal are not rival accounts of the name; they meet in one man. The Lives of the Saints of the Orthodox Church in America, for his feast on 10 May, give the tradition its narrative form: “Saint Simon was from Cana in Galilee, and was known to the Lord and His Mother. Tradition says that he was the bridegroom at the wedding where the Savior performed His first miracle. After witnessing the miracle of the water which had been turned into wine, he became a zealous follower of Christ. For this reason, he is known as Saint Simon the Zealot.” Löffler reports that “The Greeks, Copts, and Ethiopians identify him with Nathanael of Cana; the first-mentioned also identify him with the bridegroom of the marriage of Cana.” Read so, the surname records both where Simon came from and the zeal with which he followed Christ from the first sign at Cana.3

A man zealous for the Law

In this reading the surname names Simon’s religious ardour, not membership of a party.

Held by: Klemens Löffler (Catholic Encyclopedia, “St. Simon the Apostle,” 1912); John P. Meier (A Marginal Jew, vol. 3, 2001) ⚠ unverified.4

Löffler writes that the name “does not signify that he belonged to the party of Zealots, but that he had zeal for the Jewish law, which he practised before his call.” Meier grounds the reading in chronology. On Josephus’s account the faction that called itself “the Zealots” took shape during the revolt of 66–70, some thirty to forty years after Jesus called the Twelve, so that in the setting of the Gospels the word means “zealous.” Paul uses the word of himself with no militia in view, calling himself “zealous toward God” and “exceedingly zealous of the traditions of my fathers.” The Gospels attach no story of arms or rebellion to Simon. The surname distinguishes him from Simon Peter, and on this reading it does so by naming his temper: a Galilean devout in the Law, as “the Cananaean” would have sounded to an Aramaic ear.5

A man of the Zealot freedom movement

In this reading the surname carries the political charge of the movement that sought to drive out Rome.

Held by: C. M. Kerr (International Standard Bible Encyclopedia, “Simon the Cananaean; Simon the Zealot,” 1939 ed.); S. G. F. Brandon (Jesus and the Zealots, 1967) ⚠ unverified; Martin Hengel (The Zealots, Eng. tr. 1989).6

Kerr writes that the designation “Cananaean” “is regarded as of political rather than of geographical significance (compare Luke’s rendering)”. He identifies the Zealots as the faction, “headed by Judas of Galilee,” that opposed the census of Quirinius and “would have hastened by the sword the fulfillment of Messianic prophecy.” Josephus uses zēlōtai as the proper name of a revolutionary faction, and armed zeal for the Law and the land was a live current in first-century Judaea. Hengel traces a continuous Jewish “freedom movement” from Judas the Galilean’s revolt at the census (A.D. 6) to the war. On that chronology a man could be “a zealot” in a politically loaded sense before the faction took its formal name, and zeal for the Law was itself the movement’s animating principle. Read so, the Twelve held a man from that world beside Matthew, who had collected Rome’s taxes: followers drawn from opposite poles of Judaean politics.7
