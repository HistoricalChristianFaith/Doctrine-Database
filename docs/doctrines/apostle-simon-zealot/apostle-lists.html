The apostle-lists on Simon the Zealot

Dates: uncertain (3rd–7th c. and later) · Catholic Encyclopedia
Summary: Where the Gospels give Simon only a surname, the late catalogues of the apostles supply a mission and a death, with differing accounts of both, and one of them identifies him with Simeon of Jerusalem. This page gathers what the apostle-list literature (Pseudo-Hippolytus, Pseudo-Dorotheus, and their Byzantine and Latin kin) says of him. For this layer of sources as a whole, see the apostles hub.

Claims & evidence

Mission-fields in the lists

The lists send Simon to many regions. The Greek menologies and the tradition behind Pseudo-Dorotheus give him a northern and western field — the coasts of the Black Sea, then Egypt and North Africa (Mauretania and “Africa the less”), and at last Britain, where one strand has him crucified. The Latin tradition, by contrast, keeps him in the East, pairing him with Jude/Thaddaeus and sending both to Persia. An Eastern tradition places his death in Abkhazia and his burial at Nicopsia, “a not yet identified site on the Black Sea coast,” with his remains later moved to Anakopia. The Catholic Encyclopedia summarizes: “According to the Greeks, he preached on the Black Sea, in Egypt, Northern Africa, and Britain, while, according to the Latin Passio Simonis et Judae… Simon laboured in Persia, and was there martyred at Suanir.” Its author, Klemens Löffler (1912), concludes that “Where he actually preached the Gospel is uncertain.”1

Simon the Zealot and Simeon of Jerusalem

Pseudo-Hippolytus’s On the Twelve Apostles gives, at the apostle’s slot:

Simon the Zealot, the son of Clopas, who is also called Jude, became bishop of Jerusalem after James the Just, and fell asleep and was buried there at the age of 120 years.

The parentage, office and age are those Hegesippus (preserved in Eusebius) gives Simeon son of Clopas, a kinsman of the Lord remembered as the second bishop of Jerusalem, chosen to succeed James the Just, and martyred under Trajan at a hundred and twenty. The Chronicon Paschale likewise identifies the apostle with “Simon Clopas,” as the Catholic Encyclopedia reports without adopting the identification. Whether the list fuses two men who shared a name, or records that the apostle and the bishop were one man, is disputed; the readings are set out on the interpretation page.2

Death: sawn asunder, crucified, or hewn down

The accounts of his death differ as their fields do. Some have Simon sawn asunder, the tradition behind Western art’s emblem for him: the Catholic Encyclopedia gives the saw as his attribute “since his body was said to have been sawed to pieces.” Others report him crucified: at Suanir in Persia, or (in the British strand) among the Britons. The Golden Legend has the temple priests hew Simon and Jude to death together, and then reports the crucifixion tradition and the view that the crucified Simon “was another.”3

Reception and debate

  • Use of the lists. The apostle-lists differ among themselves; they record the feast-days, burial-claims and emblems by which Simon came to be commemorated. The layer as a whole, and how the lists depend on one another, is described on the apostles hub.
  • The Simeon identification. Pseudo-Hippolytus gives the apostle Simeon of Jerusalem’s episcopate and death at 120; some later lists make Simon the Zealot bishop of Jerusalem and a centenarian. Whether the apostle and the bishop were two men or one is set out on “Was Simon the Zealot the same man as Simeon son of Clopas?”
  • The missions. The Persian, British, Egyptian and Caucasian accounts are set out as readings on their own page.
