Hippolytus of Rome on the Montanists: the Phrygians catalogued without Tertullian’s name

Dates: c. 225 (the Refutation of All Heresies, c. 222–235); Hippolytus c. 170–235 · Wikipedia
Summary: Writing at Rome in Tertullian’s own generation, Hippolytus compiled a heresy-catalogue, the Refutation of All Heresies, in which he handles the Phrygian New Prophecy twice — naming Montanus, Priscilla and Maximilla and setting out what he counts their errors. Neither notice mentions Tertullian, the best-known Latin writer associated with the movement. This page records those notices for the timeline of Tertullian’s reception.

The notices

The Phrygians catalogued: Montanus, Priscilla, Maximilla — and no Tertullian (Refutation VIII.12)

But there are others who themselves are even more heretical in nature (than the foregoing), and are Phrygians by birth. These have been rendered victims of error from being previously captivated by (two) wretched women, called a certain Priscilla and Maximilla, whom they supposed (to be) prophetesses. And they assert that into these the Paraclete Spirit had departed; and antecedently to them, they in like manner consider Montanus as a prophet… they magnify these wretched women above the Apostles and every gift of Grace, so that some of them presume to assert that there is in them a something superior to Christ.

— Hippolytus, The Refutation of All Heresies, Book VIII, ch. 12, source. Hippolytus grants that the Phrygians “acknowledge God to be the Father of the universe, and Creator of all things, similarly with the Church, and (receive) as many things as the Gospel testifies concerning Christ” — the error, on his telling, lies in exalting the prophets and their new fasts above Scripture, and in a modalist wing that “assent to the heresy of the Noetians.” Throughout the notice he names the founders and the errors and never mentions Tertullian.1

The epitome repeats the roster, still without him (Refutation X.21)

The Phrygians, however, derive the principles of their heresy from a certain Montanus, and Priscilla, and Maximilla, and regard these wretched women as prophetesses, and Montanus as a prophet.

— Hippolytus, The Refutation of All Heresies, Book X, ch. 21, source. Book X is Hippolytus’s own recapitulation, a compact roll-call of every sect he has treated. When he comes to the Phrygians he lists the same three names and no fourth; here too Tertullian is not mentioned.2

Reception and placement on the timeline

  • What this page records is an absence: Hippolytus’s notices name the movement’s founders and do not name Tertullian. The silence does not by itself show whether Tertullian was then in good standing or not. Hippolytus wrote at Rome, where Tertullian was a Carthaginian; he describes the movement in detail. No surviving text of the generation after Tertullian names him as a heretic — see the summary timeline, third-century block.
  • The earliest Latin heresy-list to cover the group — the anonymous Against All Heresies that comes down appended to Tertullian’s own works (and long circulated under his name) — divides the “Cataphrygians” into the followers of Proclus and of Aeschines and likewise has no “Tertullianist” branch and does not mention Tertullian. Its ascription to Hippolytus’s lost Syntagma is ⚠ unverified here; on that ascription it would be a second text from the same author.3 It is quoted on the summary timeline.
  • Hippolytus is also a witness on the wiki’s timelines of astrology (Book IV of the same Refutation), the intermediate state, infant baptism, the ministerial priesthood, the Old Testament canon, and prayer to the saints. His own later standing is reported in the Catholic Encyclopedia (1910), which calls him “Martyr, presbyter and antipope,” holds that he “became reconciled with the legitimate bishop and the Church of Rome” before his death in exile on Sardinia, and notes that “the feast of St. Hippolytus is kept on 13 August.” Tertullian has no such feast.4
