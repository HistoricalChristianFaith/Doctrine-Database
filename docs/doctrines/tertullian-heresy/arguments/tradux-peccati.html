How is the fault Tertullian says descends from Adam with the soul related to the doctrine of original sin?

Disputed reading · on Was Tertullian a Heretic? · witness: Tertullian (on the origin of the soul)

Related readings: Was Tertullian’s subordination of the Son one of order and origin, or of nature? · How is Tertullian’s teaching that the soul is a body to be read?

The passage

Every soul, then, by reason of its birth, has its nature in Adam until it is born again in Christ; moreover, it is unclean all the while that it remains without this regeneration; and because unclean, it is actively sinful, and suffuses even the flesh (by reason of their conjunction) with its own shame.

There is, then, besides the evil which supervenes on the soul from the intervention of the evil spirit, an antecedent, and in a certain sense natural, evil which arises from its corrupt origin. For, as we have said before, the corruption of our nature is another nature having a god and father of its own, namely the author of (that) corruption.

— Tertullian, A Treatise on the Soul (De anima) 40–41, ANF vol. 3.1

The question

In On the Soul Tertullian teaches that the soul is propagated from Adam with the body, and that it carries with it a fault “antecedent” to any act, remitted in the “second birth by water.” What is disputed is how this inherited fault relates to the doctrine of original sin as later taught in the West: as the same doctrine in an early statement, or as a fault conceived in the terms of his corporeal soul, as “another nature.” The propagation of the soul itself is set out on Tertullian’s page on the origin of the soul, and his corporealism on its own page.

An inherited fault in the line of the Western doctrine of original sin

On this reading On the Soul 40–41 states an inherited corruption, prior to personal sin and removed by baptism, which Western theologians after Tertullian followed.

Held by: Philip Schaff (Reformed, History of the Christian Church II, §143); the editors of the ANF translation, in their heading to On the Soul 41.2

Schaff holds that Tertullian, “by his view of hereditary sin and its propagation by generation, looked towards the Augustinian system,” and that “Most Western divines followed Tertullian in this theory because it most easily explains the propagation of original sin by generation.” The ANF editors head chapter 41 “Notwithstanding the Depravity of Man’s Soul by Original Sin…”3 The text distinguishes two evils. One “supervenes… from the intervention of the evil spirit,” who, in chapter 39, besets souls “from the very portal of their birth”; beneath it lies “an antecedent, and in a certain sense natural, evil which arises from its corrupt origin.” The second is prior to any act and to the tempter: it is the condition in which the soul is born.4 The fault is tied to regeneration. The soul is unclean “until it is born again in Christ,” and when it is “renewed in its second birth by water and the power from above, then the veil of its former corruption being taken away, it beholds the light in all its brightness.” On this reading a fault that baptism removes, and that the soul did not commit, is an inherited one.5 Augustine, in 415, inclined toward a propagated soul because it accounted for the transmission of Adam’s guilt, and set out his difficulty in seeing how a soul newly created by God contracts it.6 The later censures of traducianism concern the soul’s generation. Aquinas holds it “heretical to say that the intellectual soul is transmitted with the semen,” because that would make the soul non-subsistent, and he teaches original sin transmitted with human nature within a frame in which each soul is created; Pope Anastasius II is reported to have condemned the propagation of souls. On this reading the fault Tertullian describes is the inherited sin the West continued to teach, carried in his work by a theory of the soul’s origin that others replaced.7

A fault materialized as “another nature” in the propagated soul

On this reading Tertullian conceives the inherited corruption as a second nature, borne physically in a corporeal soul, an account of sin distinct from the later Western account of evil as the privation of a good nature.

Held by: Philip Schaff (History of the Christian Church II, §143), on the materializing of sin; he also traces the line of inheritance set out above.8

Schaff, after noting that Western divines followed Tertullian’s traducianism, adds that “it materializes sin which originates in the mind,” and that in Tertullian “this theory was connected with a somewhat materialistic or strongly realistic tendency of thought.”9 The reading starts from Tertullian’s own definition: “the corruption of our nature is another nature having a god and father of its own, namely the author of (that) corruption.” The fault is a natura, with the devil as its author. It travels with a soul “derived from Adam as its root, and… propagated among his posterity,” and that soul, for Tertullian, is a body. On this reading the inheritance is physical: the corruption is carried in the soul-substance passed from parent to child.10 Augustine, writing against the Manichaeans, held that “no nature… as far as it is nature, is evil; but to each nature there is no evil except to be diminished in respect of good,” and that “what is called evil… is but the absence of good”; Aquinas describes original sin as a “privation of original justice.” On this reading Tertullian’s “another nature” is a different account of what the inherited fault is.11 Tertullian sets this second nature over a good one that remains. The God-given good “is rather obscured than extinguished… It can be obscured, indeed, because it is not God; extinguished, however, it cannot be, because it comes from God,” and “even in the worst there is something good.” On this reading his scheme is a good created nature overlaid by a corrupt one of the devil’s making, transmitted with the material soul: an inherited fault framed in the terms of his Stoic physics of the soul.12
