Did resentment of the Roman clergy lead Tertullian to the New Prophecy?

Disputed reading · on Was Tertullian a Heretic? · witness: Jerome

Related readings: Was Tertullian a presbyter, a jurist, and a convert in middle life?

The passage

He was presbyter of the church until middle life, afterwards driven by the envy and abuse of the clergy of the Roman church, he lapsed to the doctrine of Montanus.

— Jerome, On Illustrious Men 53 (c. 392), NPNF2 vol. 3. In the Latin: “Hic cum usque ad mediam aetatem presbyter ecclesiae permansisset, invidia postea et contumeliis clericorum Romanae ecclesiae, ad Montani dogma delapsus…”1

The question

Jerome’s notice gives not only that Tertullian joined the New Prophecy but why: invidia (envy) and contumeliae (insults) from the Roman clergy. What is disputed is whether that motive reports Tertullian’s history or is Jerome’s own construction. Its neighbour, the presbyterate named in the same sentence, is the subject of a companion page.

A turn provoked by friction with the Roman clergy

On this reading Tertullian’s adherence to the New Prophecy followed on hostile treatment at the hands of the Roman clergy, as Jerome reports.

Held by: Jerome (On Illustrious Men 53, c. 392).2

Jerome, who had read Tertullian closely and reports Cyprian’s daily reading of him, sets the turn in a story of conflict with Rome: Tertullian stayed in the church “until middle life,” then, “driven by the envy and abuse of the clergy of the Roman church,” went over to Montanus. Tertullian’s own writings record a Roman quarrel touching the New Prophecy. Opening Against Praxeas, he reports that “after the Bishop of Rome had acknowledged the prophetic gifts of Montanus, Prisca, and Maximilla, and, in consequence of the acknowledgment, had bestowed his peace on the churches of Asia and Phrygia,” Praxeas “compelled him to recall the pacific letter which he had issued,” so that Praxeas “did a twofold service for the devil at Rome: he drove away prophecy, and he brought in heresy; he put to flight the Paraclete, and he crucified the Father.”3 His late works carry the same heat against episcopal claims. On Modesty opens with the edict of “the Pontifex Maximus — that is, the bishop of bishops,” and ends by reserving the power to forgive to “the Church of the Spirit, by means of a spiritual man; not the Church which consists of a number of bishops.”4 On this reading the polemical temper of the New-Prophecy treatises, and the Roman reversal on the prophets that Tertullian himself recounts, are the documented setting of the motive Jerome names: a writer at odds with the Roman clergy over the prophets and over discipline, whose break with them took the form of adherence to the Paraclete.

Jerome’s construction, reflecting his own quarrel with Rome

On this reading the motive is Jerome’s embellishment of the life, coloured by his own recent conflict with the Roman clergy, and Tertullian’s reasons for the turn are those he gives himself.

Held by: Christine Mohrmann (“Saint Jerome and Saint Augustine on Tertullian,” 1951); T. D. Barnes (Tertullian, 1971).5

Mohrmann holds that the notice “is not confirmed by any other data,” and that what is reflected in it is “the resentment of saint Jerome himself with regard to the clergy of Rome.” Jerome had left Rome in 385 after a conflict with the Roman clergy following the death of his patron, Pope Damasus; he wrote the notice at Bethlehem about 392, more than a century and a half after Tertullian’s death (c. 220). Barnes places the motive, with the presbyterate, among Jerome’s inferences and embellishments.6 The motive appears in no earlier source. Eusebius, a century before Jerome, knew Tertullian as a man “well versed in the laws of the Romans” and records no quarrel.7 Tertullian’s own account of his adherence is theological. The rule of faith “is altogether one, alone immoveable and irreformable,” while discipline is “directed, and ordained, and carried on to perfection, by that Vicar of the Lord, the Holy Spirit”; the Prophecy is rejected “not that Montanus and Priscilla and Maximilla preach another God… but that they plainly teach more frequent fasting than marrying”; and On Monogamy divides Christians by their reception of the Spirit. Across the New-Prophecy treatises the reason he gives is the Comforter perfecting discipline.8 The Roman episode of Against Praxeas 1, on this reading, concerns a bishop’s decision on the Phrygian prophecies and the movement’s standing, a quarrel over doctrine and church policy, and the friction of On Modesty concerns penitential discipline and the keys. The reading locates Tertullian’s turn in the reasons his own treatises state, and the motive of envy and insult in Jerome’s biography.9
