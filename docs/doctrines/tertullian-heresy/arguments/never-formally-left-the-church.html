Did Tertullian ever formally leave the catholic church of Carthage?

Disputed reading · on Was Tertullian a Heretic? · witness: Tertullian; Augustine of Hippo

Related readings: Was the New Prophecy faulted for its doctrine, or for its prophecy and discipline? · Was Tertullian’s denial of reconciliation for adultery and apostasy continuous with earlier penitential discipline, or a departure from it? · Was Tertullian’s ban on second marriage a matter of discipline or of doctrine?

The passage

We indeed, on our part, subsequently withdrew from the carnally-minded on our acknowledgment and maintenance of the Paraclete.

— Tertullian, Against Praxeas 1, ANF vol. 3. Two centuries later Augustine, writing of Carthage, records a body of Tertullianistae whose last members “went over into the catholic church and handed their basilica… over to the catholic church.”1

The question

Tertullian’s late works announce his acceptance of the New Prophecy and a withdrawal from the “carnally-minded”; they record no synod, excommunication or founding of a congregation. What is disputed is whether he separated from the communion of the catholic church of Carthage, or remained within it at the head of a rigorist circle. The answer bears on how the later reports of a Tertullianist sect, and the verdicts built on them, are to be read.

A lapse into the New Prophecy and a separate assembly

On this reading Tertullian left the catholic communion, first for the Cataphrygians and then for assemblies of his own, which survived at Carthage as a distinct body.

Held by: Jerome (On Illustrious Men 53, c. 392); Augustine of Hippo (De haeresibus 86, c. 428).2

Jerome, the earliest connected report, says that Tertullian “lapsed to the doctrine of Montanus” (ad Montani dogma delapsus) and wrote “directly against the church” (proprie adversus ecclesiam) the treatises on modesty, fasting, monogamy and ecstasy.3 Augustine, bishop in Africa, gives the sequence: Tertullian went over to the Cataphrygians, and “afterwards, divided even from them, he propagated his own little assemblies” (sua conventicula propagavit). The Tertullianistae dwindled “little by little down to our own time,” and in Augustine’s day the last few handed their basilica in Carthage to the catholic church. Augustine names Tertullian as the group’s founder; on this reading a sect with a name, a building and a recorded reconciliation is the outcome of a separation in Tertullian’s lifetime.4 Tertullian’s own late writings supply the ground. Answering a bishop’s edict remitting adultery, On Modesty mocks “the Pontifex Maximus — that is, the bishop of bishops” and places the power to forgive in “the Church of the Spirit, by means of a spiritual man; not the Church which consists of a number of bishops.” On this reading that sentence locates the true church apart from the episcopal one, the ecclesiology of a separated body.5 The late works divide Christians into the “Spiritual,” who receive the Paraclete, and the Psychici, to whom “the things which are the Spirit’s are not pleasing,” and the passage above speaks of a withdrawal from the “carnally-minded.” On this reading these are the words of two peoples, and the withdrawal they name is the departure that Jerome and Augustine report.6

A rigorist circle within the church, an ecclesiola in ecclesia

On this reading Tertullian remained in the catholic congregation of Carthage as the voice of a rigorist party, neither excommunicated nor excommunicating.

Held by: Douglas Powell (“Tertullianists and Cataphrygians,” 1975); David F. Wright (“Why Were the Montanists Condemned?,” 1976); David Rankin (Tertullian and the Church, 1995); David E. Wilhite (Tertullian the African, 2007).7

Powell holds that “the African adherents of the New Prophecy formed, not a schismatic body, but an ecclesiola in ecclesia,” and Wright that they “remained a Holy Club within the catholic church.” The case begins from what Tertullian’s writings record: no synod that tried him, no excommunication suffered or pronounced, no congregation left or founded. The withdrawal of Against Praxeas 1 is, on this reading, a party’s separation in spirit from the “carnally-minded” within one church.8 In On the Soul 9 the New Prophecy’s visionary receives her revelations “amidst the sacred rites of the Lord’s day in the church,” and reports them “after the people are dismissed”: the setting is the ordinary Sunday assembly.9 Tertullian places the quarrel in discipline: the New Prophecies are rejected “not that Montanus and Priscilla and Maximilla preach another God… but that they plainly teach more frequent fasting than marrying.” Against Praxeas, his treatise on the Trinity against Praxeas, is itself a New-Prophecy work.10 On Monogamy sets “Heretics” who “do away with marriages” against “Psychics” who “accumulate them,” with the Spiritual as the mean: the heretics are outsiders, the Psychics fellow Christians to be corrected, the posture of an insider.11 No third-century source surveyed on the timeline calls Tertullian a heretic or schismatic; the catalogues of the Montanists by Hippolytus and the Latin Against All Heresies do not name him; and Cyprian, bishop of Carthage a generation later, read him daily, on Jerome’s report, as “the master.”12 Augustine’s sect is, on this reading, a later Carthaginian group; Powell distinguishes the African “Tertullianists” from the separatist “Cataphrygians” of Asia and Rome, and Tabbernee reads the African evidence as a movement that did not break communion.13
