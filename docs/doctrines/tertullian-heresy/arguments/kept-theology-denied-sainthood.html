How has the reception of Tertullian’s theology been held together with the withholding of sainthood?

Disputed reading · on Was Tertullian a Heretic? · witness: Jerome

Related readings: Did Tertullian ever formally leave the catholic church of Carthage? · Was the New Prophecy faulted for its doctrine, or for its prophecy and discipline?

The passage

I have praised the commentator but not the theologian, the man of intellect but not the believer, the philosopher but not the apostle… The blessed Cyprian takes Tertullian for his master, as his writings prove; yet, delighted as he is with the ability of this learned and zealous writer, he does not join him in following Montanus and Maximilla.

— Jerome, Letter 84.2, to Pammachius and Oceanus (c. 400), NPNF2 vol. 6.1

The question

Tertullian’s theological Latin and arguments continued in use in the Latin West, while he was not venerated as a saint, Augustine listed him among the heresies, and the Decretum Gelasianum placed his works among the apocrypha; no council is recorded as condemning him by name. What is disputed is how these belong together: as a principled distinction between a teaching and its teacher, as a line drawn at communion rather than doctrine, or as a standing reached without any deliberate judgement. The parallel case in the Greek East is Origen’s.

A distinction between the teaching and the teacher

On this reading a writer’s arguments can be received on their merits while his authority is withheld, and this is how Tertullian was read.

Held by: Jerome (Letter 84, c. 400); Vincent of Lérins (Commonitorium 18, 434).2

The rule is Tertullian’s own. Answering the objection that trusted teachers sometimes fall away, he asked in The Prescription Against Heretics: “Do we prove the faith by the persons, or the persons by the faith?” Jerome applied it to him. Defending his own reading of Origen, he praises “the commentator but not the theologian,” and gives Cyprian as the model: the martyr-bishop called Tertullian “the master” (da magistrum) and read him daily, yet did not follow him to Montanus and Maximilla.3 Vincent of Lérins sets Tertullian beside Origen, “For as Origen holds by far the first place among the Greeks, so does Tertullian among the Latins,” praises him (“every word almost was a sentence; every sentence a victory”), and then laments that he, “too little tenacious of Catholic doctrine… changed his belief”; the chapter cites no synod or anathema.4 What continued in use was central. René Braun’s study of Tertullian’s doctrinal vocabulary traces trinitas, persona, substantia, satisfactio and sacramentum in his works, and his rule of faith, his praescriptio argument, and his defences of the resurrection of the flesh and of Christ’s real humanity continued to be read. On this reading the arguments were taken up openly by writers who knew whose they were, and the honour of the man was withheld because he left the faith he had argued.5

A line drawn at communion, not at doctrine

On this reading what set Tertullian apart was his break of communion: doctrinal error held within unity is covered, and error joined to separation is not.

Held by: Augustine of Hippo (On Baptism, Against the Donatists I.18.28; De haeresibus 86, c. 428).6

In his catalogue of heresies Augustine states Tertullian’s corporealism of soul and God and then sets it aside as a ground: “yet he is not on this account said to have been made a heretic.” The grounds he gives are the turn to the Cataphrygians, the condemnation of second marriages “against apostolic teaching… as though they were fornication,” and the propagation of “his own little assemblies” (sua conventicula propagavit).7 The contrast case is Cyprian, who pressed against Pope Stephen a doctrine of rebaptizing heretics that the Council of Arles (314) settled against. Defending his memory against the Donatists, Augustine writes that Cyprian’s error “was compensated by his remaining in catholic unity, and by the abundance of his charity; and finally it was cleared away by the pruning-hook of martyrdom.”8 On this reading the two refusals in Tertullian’s case follow the shape of his faults. His doctrine was not made the ground of a heresy, so no anathema was pronounced on it and his arguments remained in use; his separation from the catholic communion forfeited the honour that unity and martyrdom secured for Cyprian, a fellow African who held his own error inside the church.

A standing reached without a formal judgement

On this reading no body ever weighed Tertullian, and his place between saint and heresiarch accumulated from scattered documents and was shaped by circumstance.

Held by: ⚠ unverified — no named proponent located yet.

The reading starts from the record. The heresy-catalogue entry is Augustine’s, written c. 428, two centuries after Tertullian; Vincent’s chapter is a teaching example; and the book-list placing his works among the apocrypha is, on von Dobschütz’s and Burkitt’s account, “the production of an anonymous scholar of the sixth century,” not a papal ordinance. No council or pope is recorded as trying him.9 The reading then sets his case beside others. Hippolytus of Rome, described in the Catholic Encyclopedia as the leader of a Roman schism against Callistus, was reconciled, died a martyr in the Sardinian mines, and is venerated as a saint. Cyprian, whose rebaptism doctrine the wider church rejected, is honoured as a martyr-saint. Origen was condemned by name three centuries after his death. Lactantius, whose works share Tertullian’s place on the Decretum’s list, whose Divine Institutes have God make, after a Spirit like himself, “another being” who “passed from good to evil,” and whom Jerome faulted for denying “the subsistence of the Holy Spirit,” drew no anathema and is remembered as the “Christian Cicero.” On this reading the outcomes follow whether a man joined a condemned movement, whether he died reconciled and a martyr, and how later centuries used his name; Tertullian, neither reconciled in memory nor martyred, and with works that came near to being lost, was left between the two honours by the course of events.10
