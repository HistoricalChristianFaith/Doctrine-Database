Was Tertullian’s subordination of the Son one of order and origin, or of nature?

Disputed reading · on Was Tertullian a Heretic? · witness: Tertullian

Related readings: How is Tertullian’s teaching that the soul is a body to be read? · How is the fault Tertullian says descends from Adam with the soul related to the doctrine of original sin?

The passage

the Father is the entire substance, but the Son is a derivation and portion of the whole, as He Himself acknowledges: “My Father is greater than I.”

— Tertullian, Against Praxeas 9, ANF vol. 3. In Against Hermogenes 3, arguing that “Lord” and “Father” are titles that need an object, he writes: “There was, however, a time when neither sin existed with Him, nor the Son; the former of which was to constitute the Lord a Judge, and the latter a Father.”1

The question

In the same treatise against Praxeas, Tertullian calls Father, Son and Spirit “of one substance” and ranks the Son “second” and “a portion of the whole.” What is disputed is whether that ranking is an order of derivation within one undivided substance, or a gradation of the divine substance itself with a Son whose sonship has a beginning. No council or synod is recorded as charging Tertullian with an error on the Trinity; the question bears on how his Trinitarian language is placed among the writers before Nicaea. A parallel question is set out for Origen.

An order of derivation within one undivided substance

On this reading the Son is “second” as a ray is second to the sun, by origin and order, while sharing the one substance of the Father.

Held by: John Kaye (Anglican, The Ecclesiastical History… Illustrated from the Writings of Tertullian, 3rd ed. 1845); René Braun (Deus Christianorum, 2nd ed. 1977); J. N. D. Kelly (Early Christian Doctrines, 5th ed. 1977); Eric Osborn (Tertullian, First Theologian of the West, 1997).2

Kaye, quoted by the ANF editor at this passage, grants that Tertullian “uses expressions which in aftertimes, when controversy had introduced greater precision of language, were studiously avoided by the orthodox,” and concludes: “there is no doubt of the orthodoxy of Tertullian’s doctrine.” Braun, Kelly and Osborn read the subordination as one of derivation within one substance.3 Tertullian’s controlling images are of undivided derivation: “the fountain and the river are also two forms, but indivisible; so likewise the sun and the ray are two forms, but coherent ones. Everything which proceeds from something else must needs be second to that from which it proceeds, without being on that account separated.”4 The Apology says the Son is “God of God”: “there is no division of substance, but merely an extension… He is made a second in manner of existence — in position, not in nature; and He did not withdraw from the original source, but went forth.”5 Against Praxeas 2 states the rank and denies it of substance in one sentence: the dispensation places “in their order the three Persons … three, however, not in condition, but in degree; not in substance, but in form; not in power, but in aspect; yet of one substance, and of one condition, and of one power.”6 On this reading the portio is drawn from the Father’s own substance, not made beside it; the ray is “still part of the parent mass.” The treatise’s purpose, against a modalist who had merged the Persons, is to show the Son really distinct from the Father and really one God with Him, and its gradations are the language of that distinction. Kaye’s point is that the expressions belong to a period before controversy had fixed the vocabulary; read in their period, they describe an order of origin within the one Godhead.

A gradation of the divine substance, with a Son whose sonship begins

On this reading Tertullian’s “portion” measures the divine substance itself, the Father having the whole and the Son a part, and his Son is Son from the moment of going forth for creation.

Held by: ⚠ unverified — no named proponent located yet.7

The reading takes Against Praxeas 9 at its word. To call the Father “the entire substance” and the Son “a derivation and portion of the whole” is, on this reading, a statement about the Godhead rather than about the order of the Persons’ work: the substance is treated as a whole of which the Son has a share. The same chapter cites “My Father is greater than I” as a statement of the Son’s relation to the Father as God, and ranks the Son “the second degree” and the Spirit “the third.” Kaye, who defends the doctrine, grants that “he calls the Father the whole substance, the Son a derivation from or portion of the whole.”8 Against Hermogenes 3 dates the Son: “He could not have been the Father previous to the Son… There was, however, a time when neither sin existed with Him, nor the Son.” Against Praxeas gives the sequence. God “had not Word from the beginning, but He had Reason even before the beginning”; “This is the perfect nativity of the Word, when He proceeds forth from God”; and the Word “became also the Son of God, and was begotten when He proceeded forth from Him.” On this reading Reason is eternally in God, while the Son’s being as Son begins with his going forth to create, so that his generation is tied to the making of the world.9 Taken together, a whole-and-part substance and a sonship with a beginning are, on this reading, a subordination that reaches the Son’s being and not only his order, held alongside the “one substance” of chapter 2 in the same work.
