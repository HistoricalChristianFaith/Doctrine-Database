Was Tertullian’s counsel to delay the baptism of little children (On Baptism 18) a Montanist position?

Disputed reading · on Was Tertullian a Heretic? · witness: Tertullian (on infant baptism)

Related readings: Was Tertullian’s denial of reconciliation for adultery and apostasy continuous with earlier penitential discipline, or a departure from it? · Was the New Prophecy faulted for its doctrine, or for its prophecy and discipline?

The passage

Near the close of On Baptism, having defended the sacrament against a woman of the Cainite sect who denied it, Tertullian turns to the question of when baptism should be given:

And so, according to the circumstances and disposition, and even age, of each individual, the delay of baptism is preferable; principally, however, in the case of little children. For why is it necessary—if (baptism itself) is not so necessary—that the sponsors likewise should be thrust into danger? Who both themselves, by reason of mortality, may fail to fulfil their promises, and may be disappointed by the development of an evil disposition, in those for whom they stood? The Lord does indeed say, “Forbid them not to come unto me.” Let them “come,” then, while they are growing up; let them “come” while they are learning, while they are learning whither to come; let them become Christians when they have become able to know Christ. Why does the innocent period of life hasten to the “remission of sins?”… For no less cause must the unwedded also be deferred—in whom the ground of temptation is prepared…—until they either marry, or else be more fully strengthened for continence.

— Tertullian, On Baptism (De Baptismo) 18, trans. S. Thelwall (ANF vol. 3).1

The question

Tertullian later joined the New Prophecy (Montanism), and this passage is the earliest extant counsel to defer the baptism of little children. What is disputed is whether the counsel belongs to the rigorism of his Montanist years, and whether the New Prophecy as a movement can be counted among early opponents of infant baptism. The wider patristic practice of deferring baptism is taken up on the infant-baptism timeline.

An expression of the rigorism that carried him to the New Prophecy

On this reading the counsel to defer the baptism of children springs from the same austere root as Tertullian’s later Montanist discipline, and the movement belongs among early dissenters from infant baptism.

Held by: ⚠ unverified — no named proponent located yet.

The reading starts from the motive Tertullian gives. The “innocent period of life” need not “hasten to the remission of sins,” and, in the sentence that follows, “if any understand the weighty import of baptism, they will fear its reception more than its delay.” Baptism washes away what is past; grave sin committed after it is the danger, so the washing is kept in reserve until the storms of youth are over. The same fear of post-baptismal sin drives his later refusal, in On Modesty, to let the church reconcile adulterers and apostates, and the extension of the counsel to the unwedded, “until they either marry, or else be more fully strengthened for continence,” shares the concern with continence that marks his later works on marriage. On this reading the counsel is an early expression of one settled cast of mind, the one that took him into the New Prophecy.2

Tertullian is also the New Prophecy’s best-known Latin adherent, and the counsel of delay is his. Andrew Koperski reports the view that the position was “the product of, say, his later fling with Montanism,” without naming a holder. Successionist church-histories place the Montanists in a line of dissenting churches running to the Baptists: J. M. Carroll’s The Trail of Blood (1931) lists “Montanists, Novationists, Donatists, Paulicians, Albigenses, Waldenses, etc., and Ana-Baptists.” On this reading the most famous Montanist writer’s counsel against baptizing little children joins the movement to that line of dissent.3

A prudential counsel from his earlier period

On this reading On Baptism belongs to Tertullian’s years before the New Prophecy, and its reasons are pastoral ones that do not depend on the movement.

Held by: Everett Ferguson (Baptism in the Early Church, 2009); Andrew Koperski (“Baptismal Trajectories in Early Christianity, Part II,” Ad Fontes).4

The reading starts from the date. On Baptism is placed in Tertullian’s earlier period, c. 198–203, and the works with New-Prophecy sympathies from c. 204–207 onward, so the counsel was written before he took up the movement.5 The reasons Tertullian gives are pastoral: the sponsors who make the promises are “thrust into danger” if they die first or the child turns out badly; children should come “while they are learning” and “become Christians when they have become able to know Christ.” He calls the practice inadvisable, not invalid, and assumes it is already in use; the treatise as a whole is a defence of baptism against the Cainite denial with which it opens. Ferguson, as Koperski reports him, finds Tertullian “sticking to the Christian mainstream” throughout the treatise, and Koperski judges the Montanist explanation unlikely.6 The one detail adduced to make the treatise anti-Montanist or Montanist in setting, a prophetess “Quintilla” in ch. 1, is treated by the tertullian.org edition notes as a reading introduced by the Gelenius edition of 1550, without manuscript support.7 For the movement, the reading points to the ancient catalogues of the New Prophecy: the Anonymous and Apollonius in Eusebius, Hippolytus, the Latin Against All Heresies and Epiphanius charge ecstatic prophecy, new fasts, broken marriages and, in one wing, modalism, and none of them mentions the baptism of infants. On this reading the counsel of delay is Tertullian’s own pastoral judgement, written as a churchman of Carthage before his turn to the Paraclete.8
