Does “certum est, quia impossibile” (On the Flesh of Christ 5) express fideism?

Disputed reading · on Was Tertullian a Heretic? · witness: Tertullian

Related readings: Was Tertullian’s ban on second marriage a matter of discipline or of doctrine? · Was the New Prophecy faulted for its doctrine, or for its prophecy and discipline?

The passage

The Son of God was crucified; I am not ashamed because men must needs be ashamed of it. And the Son of God died; it is by all means to be believed, because it is absurd. And He was buried, and rose again; the fact is certain, because it is impossible.

— Tertullian, On the Flesh of Christ (De carne Christi) 5, ANF vol. 3. The Latin runs: Crucifixus est Dei Filius; non pudet, quia pudendum est. Et mortuus est Dei Filius; prorsus credibile est, quia ineptum est. Et sepultus resurrexit; certum est, quia impossibile.1

The question

The maxim most often attached to Tertullian, credo quia absurdum (“I believe because it is absurd”), does not occur in his works; the nearest text is the passage above, written against Marcion’s teaching that Christ’s flesh was a phantom. What is disputed is whether its paradox sets faith against reason, or is a step in an argument for the reality of Christ’s birth, death and resurrection. The question bears on Tertullian’s reputation as a fideist, a charge brought against him by no ancient writer surveyed here.

A resolution of faith set against reason

On this reading the paradox commits faith to what reason finds impossible, and expresses a stance toward reason found elsewhere in Tertullian.

Held by: Thomas Browne (Religio Medici, 1643); Voltaire (eighteenth century, as reported by Peter Harrison, 2017).2

Browne quoted the words in their accurate form and took them as a rule for faith: “methinks there be not impossibilities enough in religion for an active faith… I can answer all the objections of Satan and my rebellious reason with that odd resolution I learned of Tertullian, Certum est quia impossibile est.” A credo form, “I believe because it is impossible,” became common in seventeenth-century England, and Harrison traces to Voltaire the addition of the “absurdity condition” that produced “I believe because it is absurd,” used to present religious faith as irrational.3 The reading can draw on Tertullian’s own words about philosophy. In The Prescription Against Heretics 7 he calls it “the material of the world’s wisdom, the rash interpreter of the nature and dispensation of God,” holds that “heresies are themselves instigated by philosophy,” and asks: “What indeed has Athens to do with Jerusalem? What concord is there between the Academy and the Church?… Away with all attempts to produce a mottled Christianity of Stoic, Platonic, and dialectic composition!”4 In his later works the decisive word in a dispute is often an oracle of the Paraclete: he forbids second marriage because “the Paraclete… has abolished” the apostle’s concession, forbids flight in persecution on the strength of a New-Prophecy utterance, and grounds the tightening of discipline on the Spirit leading into “all truth.” On this reading the paradox of On the Flesh of Christ 5 belongs with these: faith embraces the incarnation and resurrection precisely where the wisdom of the world finds them shameful and impossible.5

An argument from improbability against Marcion

On this reading the paradox is the turn of a reasoned argument: the shamefulness of Christ’s death is a mark of its reality, since no one would invent it.

Held by: James Moffatt (1916); Robert D. Sider (1980); Eric Osborn (Tertullian, First Theologian of the West, 1997); Peter Harrison (2017).6

The reading starts from the words. Tertullian wrote credibile est, quia ineptum est (“it is to be believed, because it is unfitting”) and certum est, quia impossibile, not “I believe” and not “absurd.” Sider holds that “the difference between the imputed and actual words is striking and important.” Ineptum carries the “foolishness” of 1 Corinthians 1 that Tertullian has been expounding for two chapters: “Whatsoever is unworthy of God, is of gain to me.”7 The chapter answers Marcion step by step: was God really crucified? then he really died, and really rose; if the sufferings were a phantom’s, “false, therefore, is our faith also.” Moffatt observed that here Tertullian “follows in the footsteps of that cool philosopher Aristotle,” whose Rhetoric 2.23.22 sets out the topos that a story too improbable to be invented is credible for that reason; the humiliation authenticates the history. Sider accordingly reads the passage as “a manifesto on behalf of reason in religious faith.”8 Osborn and Barnes describe a writer formed by rhetoric, Roman law and Stoic logic: The Prescription is built on a procedural plea, A Treatise on the Soul marshals the Stoics syllogism by syllogism, and Against Hermogenes and Against Praxeas are dialectical treatises.9 Harrison dates the fideist maxim to seventeenth-century England and Voltaire, and notes that patristic scholars have long held that Tertullian “never said this or meant anything like it.” On this reading the sentence is a compressed proof that Christ’s flesh was what the senses reported.10
