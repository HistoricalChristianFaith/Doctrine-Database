Was Tertullian’s denial of reconciliation for adultery and apostasy continuous with earlier penitential discipline, or a departure from it?

Disputed reading · on Was Tertullian a Heretic? · witness: Tertullian (on the gravity of sins)

Related readings: Did Tertullian ever formally leave the catholic church of Carthage? · Was the New Prophecy faulted for its doctrine, or for its prophecy and discipline? · Was Tertullian’s ban on second marriage a matter of discipline or of doctrine?

The passage

I hear that there has even been an edict set forth, and a peremptory one too. The Pontifex Maximus — that is, the bishop of bishops — issues an edict: “I remit, to such as have discharged (the requirements of) repentance, the sins both of adultery and of fornication.” O edict, on which cannot be inscribed, “Good deed!”

— Tertullian, On Modesty 1, ANF vol. 4.1

The question

Against this edict, attributed on the traditional view to Callistus of Rome (217–222) and by Monceaux to a bishop of Carthage, Tertullian sorts sins into “some remissible, some irremissible,” and places adultery, fornication, idolatry, murder and apostasy in the second class: for these the church grants no peace, and the sinner is left to the judgement of God. What is disputed is whether the earlier church had already withheld reconciliation for these sins, so that Tertullian was defending an older discipline, or had always claimed the power to reconcile them, so that On Modesty departs from received practice. The two-class division of sins also anchors his block on the gravity-of-sins timeline.

The older severity, which the edict relaxed

On this reading the early church reserved the capital sins to God, the edict was the innovation, and Tertullian defended the earlier rule.

Held by: Hippolytus of Rome (Refutation of All Heresies IX.12, c. 225); Adolf von Harnack (Protestant historian, History of Dogma).2

The reading starts from the once-only character of post-baptismal penance. Tertullian’s earlier On Repentance (c. 203) says God stationed “the second repentance” in the vestibule, “but now once for all, because now for the second time; but never more.”3 His list of irremissible sins is drawn from Scripture: the Decalogue sets idolatry, adultery and murder “among the primary counts of the celestial edict,” and Revelation gives “the cowardly, and unbelieving, and infamous, and fornicators, and murderers, and sorcerers, and idolaters” their share in the lake of fire.4 The fear that such sins lay beyond the church’s pardon was widely held. The Shepherd of Hermas, a Roman book of the mid-second century, addresses a circle that held post-baptismal sinners “could not again obtain forgiveness,” and allows only a single further repentance. A generation after Tertullian, Novatian, a Roman presbyter, and a large following refused to readmit those who had sacrificed under Decius, and carried a rival episcopate on that principle.5 Hippolytus, writing at Rome, charges that Callistus “first invented the device of conniving with men in regard to their pleasures, saying that all had their sins forgiven by himself.” The readmission of the lapsed that Cyprian and the African councils worked out after 250 was itself reached against strenuous resistance. On this reading the discipline was still forming in Tertullian’s day, the relaxation was the new thing, and the repeatable penance of later centuries is a development by which he should not be measured.6

A departure from a reconciling practice

On this reading the church always claimed and used the power to reconcile every sin, however sternly, and On Modesty departs both from that practice and from Tertullian’s own earlier teaching.

Held by: Adhémar d’Alès (Catholic, L’Édit de Calliste, 1914); Bernhard Poschmann (Catholic, Penance and the Anointing of the Sick); Karl Rahner (Catholic, Theological Investigations 15).7

The reading starts from Tertullian’s two treatises. On Repentance offers the second penance to grave sinners without excepting the capital sins: its discipline of exomologesis, sackcloth, ashes and prostration before the presbyters, exists to reconcile them. On Modesty opens by disowning “the fellowship of sentiment… which I myself formerly maintained,” and adds, “I blush not at an error which I have ceased to hold.”8 The Shepherd of Hermas, read in the churches, granted its one repentance to apostates and fornicators, and Tertullian now rejects it as “that apocryphal ‘Shepherd’ of adulterers.”9 The ground he gives is also new. The power of the keys belongs to “the Church of the Spirit, by means of a spiritual man; not the Church which consists of a number of bishops,” and the Paraclete speaking in the new prophets says, “The Church has the power to forgive sins; but I will not do it, lest they commit others withal.” In On Repentance the limit on pardon rested on the economy of God’s mercy, “the plank after shipwreck”; in On Modesty it rests on the New Prophecy and the division of Christians into Spiritales and Psychici.10 When the question returned after the Decian persecution, Cornelius at Rome and Cyprian with the African councils reconciled the lapsed on completed penance, and Novatian’s party was put out of communion as a schism. On this reading the reconciling practice was the church’s standing discipline, which Hermas attests, the catholic Tertullian taught, and the mid-third-century bishops affirmed.11
