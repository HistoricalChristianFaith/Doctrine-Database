Was Tertullian a presbyter, a jurist, and a convert in middle life?

Disputed reading · on Was Tertullian a Heretic? · witness: Jerome

Related readings: Did resentment of the Roman clergy lead Tertullian to the New Prophecy? · Did Tertullian ever formally leave the catholic church of Carthage?

The passage

Tertullian the presbyter, now regarded as chief of the Latin writers after Victor and Apollonius, was from the city of Carthage in the province of Africa, and was the son of a proconsul or Centurion… He was presbyter of the church until middle life, afterwards driven by the envy and abuse of the clergy of the Roman church, he lapsed to the doctrine of Montanus.

— Jerome, On Illustrious Men 53 (c. 392), NPNF2 vol. 3.1

The question

Most details of the received life of Tertullian — the presbyterate, the soldier-father, a tenure in the church “until middle life” — come from this notice; later readers added his identification with the jurist Tertullianus cited in Justinian’s Digest. What is disputed is how far these details report his life, and in particular whether he was an ordained presbyter or a layman. The answer bears on the description of him as a lapsed presbyter. The motive named in the same sentence is the subject of a companion page.

The life as Jerome reports it: a presbyter of legal learning

On this reading Jerome’s notice preserves a usable biography: Tertullian served as a presbyter of Carthage, and his learning in Roman law was known before Jerome wrote.

Held by: Jerome (On Illustrious Men 53, c. 392); Eusebius (Church History II.2.4, c. 313–325), on his legal learning only.2

Jerome gives the connected life: a Carthaginian, son of “a proconsul or Centurion,” a presbyter “until middle life,” then an adherent of Montanus. Eusebius, writing about two generations earlier and independently of Jerome, describes Tertullian as “a man well versed in the laws of the Romans, and in other respects of high repute, and one of those especially distinguished in Rome.”3 Tertullian’s own works bear out the legal training. The Prescription Against Heretics is built on the forensic praescriptio, and To Scapula is addressed to a sitting proconsul of Africa. The equation of the writer with the Digest’s Tertullianus, author of libri quaestionum, was widely made from the sixteenth century onward on the strength of the shared name and this legal fluency.4 That he was an adult convert is his own testimony: “Christians are made, not born” (fiunt, non nascuntur Christiani).5 The passages read as lay self-description belong to arguments about lay standing. “Are not even we laics priests?” is part of a case that a layman who remarries forfeits a priestly prerogative, and on this reading its “we” is the orator’s solidarity with his lay readers rather than a statement of his own rank. Rankin holds that Tertullian conceived himself in strongly clerical terms. Tertullian volunteers little about his own life of any kind; on this reading his silence about orders leaves Jerome’s report of the presbyterate as the one direct statement on the point.6

A layman of rhetorical training; the details as Jerome’s inferences

On this reading Tertullian was an educated lay convert, not the Digest’s jurist, and the presbyterate, the father and the mid-life lapse are inferences Jerome supplied.

Held by: T. D. Barnes (Tertullian, 1971); Geoffrey D. Dunn (Tertullian, 2004); David E. Wilhite (Tertullian the African, 2007).7

Tertullian writes freely in the first person and nowhere claims holy orders, and in several places he reasons from inside the lay estate. In On Exhortation to Chastity: “Vain shall we be if we think that what is not lawful for priests is lawful for laics. Are not even we laics priests?… where there is no joint session of the ecclesiastical Order, you offer, and baptize, and are priest, alone for yourself. But where three are, a church is, albeit they be laics.” On Monogamy grounds clerical monogamy on the discipline of “laics, from whose ranks the Ecclesiastical Order proceeds,” and On Baptism sets out the layman’s right to baptize in necessity.8 The presbyterate, the “proconsul or centurion” father and the Roman-clergy grievance appear in no source before Jerome, who wrote at Bethlehem more than a century and a half after Tertullian’s death (c. 220); Barnes reads them as inferences without independent support.9 Barnes rejects the identification with the jurist. The Digest’s Tertullianus is nowhere given a Christian character, and Tertullian’s legal knowledge, on Barnes’s reading, “does not demonstrably exceed what could be expected from a sufficient Roman education”: the praescriptio and the address to Scapula are the work of a trained rhetor.10 The adult conversion stands on Tertullian’s own word; the “middle life” hinge does not. On Barnes’s chronology the datable treatises fall c. 197–212, with the New-Prophecy works clustered late but interleaved with the others, so that the corpus does not divide at a single break. Wilhite, building on this, reads Tertullian as a layman who never left the Carthaginian church.11
