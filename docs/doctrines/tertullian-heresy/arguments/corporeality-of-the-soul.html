How is Tertullian’s teaching that the soul is a body to be read?

Disputed reading · on Was Tertullian a Heretic? · witness: Tertullian (on the origin of the soul)

Related readings: Was Tertullian’s subordination of the Son one of order and origin, or of nature? · How is the fault Tertullian says descends from Adam with the soul related to the doctrine of original sin?

The passage

When we aver that the soul has a body of a quality and kind peculiar to itself, in this special condition of it we shall be already supplied with a decision respecting all the other accidents of its corporeity… we shall not be at all inconsistent if we declare that the more usual characteristics of a body, such as invariably accrue to the corporeal condition, belong also to the soul—such as form and limitation; and that triad of dimensions—I mean length, and breadth and height—by which philosophers gauge all bodies.

— Tertullian, A Treatise on the Soul (De anima) 9, ANF vol. 3. The same principle is carried up to God in Against Praxeas 7: “For who will deny that God is a body, although ‘God is a Spirit’? For Spirit has a bodily substance of its own kind, in its own form.”1

The question

Tertullian teaches that the soul, and God, are bodies. What is disputed is what the word corpus carries in his usage: a claim that the soul is a real, created substance, in a Stoic sense of “body” as whatever exists and acts, or a corporealism of soul and God in the ordinary sense. The question bears on Augustine’s listing of the teaching among Tertullian’s views in his catalogue of heresies. The doctrine’s setting in Tertullian’s traducianism is on his page on the origin of the soul.

“Body” as the real subsistence of a created soul

On this reading corpus names the soul’s reality and creatureliness, against a Platonic soul that is unborn and quasi-divine, rather than a material stuff.

Held by: Tertullian himself (De anima, c. 210); J. H. Waszink (Tertullianus: De anima, 1947).2

The reading starts from Tertullian’s qualifier: the soul’s body is proprii generis, “of a quality and kind peculiar to itself.” He separates his view from those who “mould the soul out of manifest bodily substances,” Heraclitus out of fire, Thales out of water, Epicurus out of atoms, and defines the soul as “sprung from the breath of God, immortal, possessing body, having form, simple in its substance.”3 The target is Plato’s soul, “unborn and unmade”: against it Tertullian holds that “from the very fact of its having had a beginning… it had both birth and creation,” and reckons it “very far below God… as the breath (of God), though not His Spirit.” In On the Flesh of Christ he presses the soul’s corporeity to secure that Christ took a real human soul, not a phantasm.4 The word itself comes from the Stoics, whom he “call[s] on… to help me,” with Zeno’s syllogism that what departs at death is a body. In that physics “body” is the criterion of the real, what can act and be acted upon; Waszink’s commentary sets Tertullian’s corporealism in that frame. Writing early technical Latin theology, with substantia among the terms he was still shaping, Tertullian used corpus for real subsistence. On this reading the corporeal God of Against Praxeas 7 is a way of saying that God truly is, with substance and form, in a treatise whose burden is the reality and distinction of the divine Persons against Praxeas.5

A corporealism of soul and God, named an error

On this reading Tertullian means “body” in its ordinary sense, giving the soul extension, shape and colour and calling God a body, and the teaching is an error.

Held by: Augustine of Hippo (On the Soul and its Origin, c. 419; De haeresibus 86, c. 428).6

The reading takes the detail of De anima 9 at its word. Tertullian gives the soul “form and limitation,” the “triad of dimensions,” a “figure” that is the human form, and colour, and he rests the shape and colour on a Montanist sister’s vision of “a soul in bodily shape… soft and transparent and of an etherial colour, and in form resembling that of a human being in every respect.”7 His proof in ch. 7 is the Stoic criterion applied to the Gospel: the rich man’s soul suffers in Hades and Lazarus’s is refreshed, “For an incorporeal thing suffers nothing,” therefore souls are bodies. On this reading he takes over a physics in which only bodies act and are acted upon, and carries it up to God: “who will deny that God is a body?”8 Augustine, who left open whether the soul is propagated, argued at length that the soul is not a body against Vincentius Victor, who held the soul to be corporeal and to be breathed by God afresh into each man rather than derived by propagation. In De haeresibus 86 he records that Tertullian held the soul to be a body, “and not this only, but God Himself,” adding that he was not on this account made a heretic. On this reading the doctrine is a positive picture of the soul as an extended, shaped, coloured thing, and of God as a body of his own kind, which Augustine recorded as Tertullian’s view while teaching the contrary.9
