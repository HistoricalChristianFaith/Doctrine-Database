Was the New Prophecy faulted for its doctrine, or for its prophecy and discipline?

Disputed reading · on Was Tertullian a Heretic? · witness: Tertullian

Related readings: Did Tertullian ever formally leave the catholic church of Carthage? · Was Tertullian’s denial of reconciliation for adultery and apostasy continuous with earlier penitential discipline, or a departure from it? · Was Tertullian’s ban on second marriage a matter of discipline or of doctrine?

The passage

…the New Prophecies are rejected, not that Montanus and Priscilla and Maximilla preach another God, nor that they disjoin Jesus Christ (from God), nor that they overturn any particular rule of faith or hope, but that they plainly teach more frequent fasting than marrying.

— Tertullian, On Fasting, Against the Psychics 1, ANF vol. 4.1

The question

The movement of Montanus, Priscilla and Maximilla, which Tertullian joined, was catalogued among the heresies as the “Cataphrygian” sect. What is disputed is what it was faulted for: a claim to prophecy and revelation that touched the faith itself, or only the stricter discipline the prophecy imposed and the church order it implied. The answer bears on how the label “heretic” attached to Tertullian is to be understood.

A false prophecy claiming revelation beyond the Gospel

On this reading the movement’s offence lay in its prophecy itself, judged false in manner and in its claim to add to what Christ delivered, a matter of the faith and not only of discipline.

Held by: the Anonymous anti-Montanist writer (c. 192, in Eusebius); the Latin Against All Heresies appended to Tertullian’s works (third century); Jerome (Letter 41, c. 385); J. Chapman (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1911).2

The earliest charge concerns how the prophets spoke. The Anonymous writer says Montanus “became beside himself, and being suddenly in a sort of frenzy and ecstasy, he raved, and began to babble,” prophesying “in a manner contrary to the constant custom of the Church”; the false prophet “falls into an ecstasy, in which he is without shame or fear.” Chapman summarizes that “the manner of prophesying… was denounced as contrary to custom and to tradition.” On this reading the discernment of true from false prophecy is a judgement about how the Spirit speaks.3 The Latin Against All Heresies names the sect’s common blasphemy: “that the Paraclete has spoken in Montanus more things than Christ brought forward into (the compass of) the Gospel.” A claim that the Spirit still delivers revelation binding on the whole church bears on the sufficiency of the apostolic deposit, which Tertullian himself, in The Prescription Against Heretics, had described as delivered whole by the apostles to the churches.4 The same catalogue reports that the followers of Aeschines “affirm Christ to be Himself Son and Father,” and Jerome charges the Montanists of his day with “following the doctrine of Sabellius,” forcing “the Trinity into the narrow limits of a single personality,” and claiming for Montanus a fullness of knowledge “such as was never claimed by Paul.” On this reading the New Prophecy’s claim to new revelation was itself a matter of doctrine, and in one wing it carried a doctrine of God the catholic church rejected.5

A quarrel over prophecy and discipline, not the rule of faith

On this reading the movement confessed the same God and Christ as the catholic church, and was faulted for its prophecy, its stricter fasts and its ban on remarriage, and the church order these implied.

Held by: Tertullian (On Fasting 1, c. 210); David F. Wright (“Why Were the Montanists Condemned?,” 1976); Christine Trevett (Montanism, 1996); William Tabbernee (Montanist Inscriptions and Testimonia, 1997).6

Tertullian states the quarrel’s terms himself: the rule of faith is “altogether one, alone immoveable and irreformable,” while “the other succeeding points of discipline and conversation admit the novelty of correction.”7 The early bill of particulars is about conduct. Apollonius charges Montanus as “he who taught the dissolution of marriage; who made laws for fasting,” and asks of the prophets, “Does a prophet dye his hair? Does a prophet stain his eyelids?”8 Epiphanius, cataloguing the Phrygians, records that concerning the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit they hold the same as the catholic church; the Latin catalogue confines the modalist confession to the followers of Aeschines; and Tertullian’s own New-Prophecy treatise Against Praxeas is written against the confusion of the divine Persons.9 Augustine, giving Tertullian an entry among the heresies, names his corporealism of soul and God and adds that he was “not on this account” made a heretic; the grounds he gives are the turn to the Cataphrygians, the condemnation of second marriage, and Tertullian’s “own little assemblies.”10 Tertullian also reports that a bishop of Rome had “acknowledged the prophetic gifts of Montanus, Prisca, and Maximilla,” and recalled his letters of peace only when Praxeas pressed him; Wright notes that the Roman action “apparently did not amount to formal censure or excommunication.” On this reading Wright, Trevett and Tabbernee find a movement disputed over the authority of its prophets and the rigour of its rule, sharing the church’s confession of God and Christ.11
