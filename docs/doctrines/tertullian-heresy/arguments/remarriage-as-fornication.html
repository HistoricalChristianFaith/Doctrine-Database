Was Tertullian’s ban on second marriage a matter of discipline or of doctrine?

Disputed reading · on Was Tertullian a Heretic? · witness: Tertullian; Augustine of Hippo

Related readings: Did Tertullian ever formally leave the catholic church of Carthage? · Was the New Prophecy faulted for its doctrine, or for its prophecy and discipline? · Was Tertullian’s denial of reconciliation for adultery and apostasy continuous with earlier penitential discipline, or a departure from it?

The passage

If we look deeply into his meanings, and interpret them, second marriage will have to be termed no other than a species of fornication.

— Tertullian, On Exhortation to Chastity 9, ANF vol. 4.1

The question

In To His Wife (c. 203) Tertullian urges widowhood but grants that the apostle permits a second marriage; in On Exhortation to Chastity and On Monogamy (c. 208–212) he forbids it and classes it as stuprum. Augustine, cataloguing Tertullian among the heresies, set aside his corporealism as the ground and named instead that he “began also to condemn second marriages, against apostolic teaching, as though they were fornication.” What is disputed is whether the ban is a severe discipline within an early Christian deprecation of remarriage, or a teaching contrary to the apostle’s.2

A discipline perfected by the Paraclete, at the strict end of an early deprecation

On this reading the ban is a rule of conduct, the strictest form of a preference for the single marriage found in Scripture and the early church, and touches no article of the rule of faith.

Held by: Tertullian (On the Veiling of Virgins 1; On Fasting 1, c. 208–212); T. D. Barnes (Tertullian, 1971) and Douglas Powell (1975), on his rigorism as a severity within the church.3

Tertullian frames the whole quarrel as one of discipline: the rule of faith is fixed, while the Paraclete carries discipline “to perfection,” and the New Prophecy overturns no “rule of faith or hope” but teaches “more frequent fasting than marrying.” He casts the Paraclete as the restitutor rather than the institutor of monogamy, since second marriage “was not from the beginning.”4 The preference he presses is scriptural. Paul tells the widows “it is good for them if they abide even as I,” permits marriage as better than burning, and says the widow “is happier if she so abide”; the Pastorals require a bishop to be “the husband of one wife” and enrol only the widow who was “the wife of one man.” Tertullian marshals these same texts.5 The language of adultery predates the New Prophecy. Athenagoras of Athens, about 177, taught that a man should “either remain as he was born, or be content with one marriage; for a second marriage is only a specious adultery,” and that “he who deprives himself of his first wife, even though she be dead, is a cloaked adulterer.”6 The deprecation passed into canon law. The Council of Neocaesarea (c. 315) forbade a presbyter to attend the wedding feast of a second marriage, “since the digamist is worthy of penance,” and set a penance for those who married repeatedly; Basil’s canons gave a year’s penance to the digamist and more to the trigamist; and the twice-married were barred from ordination.7 On this reading Tertullian stands at the far end of a real spectrum, between Athenagoras’s “specious adultery” and the penances of the fourth-century canons, and his ban is a matter of how strictly a shared preference is to be kept, a question of conduct and not of the confession of God and Christ.

A prohibition against apostolic teaching, resting on new revelation

On this reading the ban forbids what the apostle expressly permits and names as fornication a union he calls lawful, on the authority of the Paraclete, and so reaches into teaching.

Held by: Augustine of Hippo (De haeresibus 86, c. 428).8

Augustine names the ban among the grounds of Tertullian’s heresy: he condemned second marriages “against apostolic teaching, as though they were fornication.” The apostle’s teaching, on this reading, is a permission. Of the widow Paul writes that “if her husband be dead, she is at liberty to be married to whom she will; only in the Lord”; of the woman whose husband has died, that “she is free from that law; so that she is no adulteress, though she be married to another man”; and he wills that “the younger women marry, bear children, guide the house.”9 Deprecating remarriage as the lesser state and prohibiting it as sin are, on this reading, different in kind: Paul says the remarried widow “is no adulteress,” and Tertullian calls her union “a species of fornication.”10 Tertullian grounds the prohibition on the Paraclete: “why may not the Paraclete, too, have abrogated an indulgence which Paul granted?” He also holds that the Greek of 1 Cor. 7:39 has been altered “through the either crafty or simple alteration of two syllables,” so that the permission covers only women widowed before their conversion. On this reading a rule that sets aside an apostolic concession by a later revelation is a claim about the authority of new prophecy over the apostolic deposit, the point at which discipline becomes teaching.11 Tertullian’s own To His Wife had granted that marriage is “permitted, yet singly” and that the apostle “permits marrying indeed, but prefers abstinence.” The canons that penanced digamists did not forbid lay remarriage; Percival’s note on Neocaesarea records that the church “never disapproved of, nor reputed second… marriages illicit.”12 Jerome, who drew on Tertullian’s monogamist treatises (“Tertullian, while still a young man, gave himself full play with this subject”), wrote in the same work: “I do not condemn second, nor third, nor, pardon the expression, eighth marriages.” On this reading the line between deprecation and prohibition is the line Augustine names.13
