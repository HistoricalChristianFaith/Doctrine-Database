Jerome on Tertullian: the biographical notice of De viris illustribus 53

Dates: c. 347–420 · Wikipedia
Summary: The earliest extant connected account of Tertullian’s life is chapter 53 of Jerome’s De viris illustribus (c. 392), written more than a century and a half after Tertullian died. It states that he was a presbyter; that he was the son of a proconsular centurion; that he stayed in the church until middle life; that the envy and abuse of the Roman clergy drove him to Montanus; that he wrote books On Ecstasy against the church, now lost; and that he lived to extreme old age. Several of these details appear in no earlier extant source, and T. D. Barnes (1971) holds that they lack independent support; two of them — that Tertullian was ordained a presbyter, and that resentment of the Roman clergy was his motive — are set out as disputed readings. Jerome also drew on Tertullian’s writings in his own works. This page records Jerome’s part in the timeline of Tertullian’s reception.

The notice: De viris illustribus 53 (c. 392)

Jerome compiled his catalogue of Christian writers at Bethlehem about 392, modelling it on Suetonius’s lives of the pagan authors. Tertullian is number 53, placed among the writers of the age of Severus, and the whole chapter runs to a paragraph:

Tertullian the presbyter, now regarded as chief of the Latin writers after Victor and Apollonius, was from the city of Carthage in the province of Africa, and was the son of a proconsul or Centurion, a man of keen and vigorous character, he flourished chiefly in the reign of the emperor Severus and Antoninus Caracalla and wrote many volumes which we pass by because they are well known to most. I myself have seen a certain Paul an old man of Concordia, a town of Italy, who, while he himself was a very young man had been secretary to the blessed Cyprian who was already advanced in age. He said that he himself had seen how Cyprian was accustomed never to pass a day without reading Tertullian, and that he frequently said to him, “Give me the master,” meaning by this, Tertullian. He was presbyter of the church until middle life, afterwards driven by the envy and abuse of the clergy of the Roman church, he lapsed to the doctrine of Montanus, and mentions the new prophecy in many of his books. He composed, moreover, directly against the church, volumes: On modesty, On persecution, On fasts, On monogamy, six books On ecstasy, and a seventh which he wrote Against Apollonius. He is said to have lived to a decrepit old age, and to have composed many small works, which are not extant.1

The notice gives Tertullian a rank (“chief of the Latin writers”), a city, a father, a career, a motive, a list of works written “directly against the church,” and an old age. It describes the turn with the verb rendered “he lapsed” (the Latin printed in note 1 reads ad Montani dogma delapsus).

Details attested only in this chapter

T. D. Barnes (1971) holds that, apart from Jerome, several details of the standard life lack independent support: on his reading Tertullian’s own surviving works do not call him a presbyter, do not mention his father, and do not mention an ordination, a quarrel with the Roman clergy, or a “lapse.”2 The works themselves place him in Africa. Four details of the notice rest on Jerome:

  • “The presbyter.” Jerome twice calls him presbyter. Tertullian’s surviving works do not describe an ordination; Barnes reads On Exhortation to Chastity 7 (“Are not even we laics priests?”) and On Monogamy 12 as placing him among the laity. Whether Jerome reports a fact or infers a rank is set out as a disputed reading.
  • “Son of a proconsul or Centurion.” The phrase (proconsulis centurionis filius) is attested nowhere else; Barnes counts the father among the details that lack independent support.
  • The Roman-clergy motive. That it was the “envy and abuse of the clergy of the Roman church” that drove Tertullian to Montanus is Jerome’s explanation; no earlier extant source gives it. Tertullian’s own writings on the New Prophecy frame his adherence in terms of discipline and of the Paraclete. The question is set out as a disputed reading.
  • The lost On Ecstasy. The “six books On ecstasy, and a seventh… Against Apollonius” are known only from Jerome’s lists. Jerome had read the work: he quotes it elsewhere in the same catalogue, reporting that Tertullian there mocked Melito as a “prophet.”3 The books themselves are lost, and Jerome’s notices are the extant record of Tertullian’s treatise on prophetic ecstasy.

Jerome’s date, sources and own writings

Jerome wrote more than a century and a half after Tertullian’s death (c. 220). Barnes holds that Jerome’s notices of earlier writers draw on earlier catalogues, Eusebius above all, and fill gaps by inference; he reads the presbyterate, the “proconsul or centurion” father and the “envy and abuse” motive as Jerome’s inference or embellishment, and rejects the once-standard identification of this Tertullian with the Roman jurist of the same name.2 Jerome’s polemical works include treatises against Helvidius on Mary, against Jovinian on marriage, and against Vigilantius on relics, and in Against Jovinianus he drew on Tertullian’s works on marriage (below); the notice both ranks Tertullian “chief of the Latin writers” and records that he “lapsed.”

The notice is the earliest connected account of the life, and for the Cyprian anecdote it names its chain of transmission (the aged Paul of Concordia). Eusebius, writing c. 313–325, independently of Jerome, describes Tertullian as “a man well versed in the laws of the Romans, and in other respects of high repute, and one of those especially distinguished in Rome.”4 Those who read the life as Jerome reports it, and those who read its details as his inferences, are set out on the disputed reading on the biography.

On the motive, Christine Mohrmann (1951) writes of Jerome’s report as information “which is not confirmed by any other data,” and that in it “one sees reflected the resentment of saint Jerome himself with regard to the clergy of Rome.”5 Jerome had left Rome in 385, after his conflict with the Roman clergy. Jerome’s own account and Mohrmann’s are set out on the disputed reading on the motive.

Jerome as a reader of Tertullian: Letter 84 and Against Jovinianus

Elsewhere Jerome draws on Tertullian’s writings. In Letter 84 (c. 400), defending his own use of Origen against the charge that to translate a man is to endorse him, Jerome adduces Tertullian and Cyprian as precedent: on his argument one may admire a writer’s ability without following his errors.

The blessed Cyprian takes Tertullian for his master, as his writings prove; yet, delighted as he is with the ability of this learned and zealous writer he does not join him in following Montanus and Maximilla.6

Jerome presents the distinction as Cyprian’s established practice. He followed it himself: in Against Jovinianus (393), which discourages remarriage without condemning it, he draws on Tertullian’s writing on the married state — “Tertullian, while still a young man, gave himself full play with this subject” — a year after recording in the catalogue that Tertullian had lapsed to Montanus.7 Jerome thus both wrote the notice of the lapse and drew on Tertullian’s works in his own.

Reception and placement on the timeline

  • Jerome is the only extant source for the Cyprian “da magistrum” anecdote and for the Roman-clergy motive; both reach us only through De viris illustribus 53. The unacknowledged dependence of Cyprian’s treatises on Tertullian’s is set out on Cyprian’s page and on the summary timeline under the third-century block.
  • Jerome is also a witness on the wiki’s timelines of the Origenist controversy and the Old Testament canon, among others; his notices and letters are there also often the earliest, and sometimes the only, extant witness to a fact.
  • The absolute date of De viris illustribus is fixed by Jerome’s own note in the closing chapter that he wrote in the fourteenth year of the emperor Theodosius — i.e. 392/393.
