Augustine on Tertullian: the heresy-catalogue entry (De haeresibus 86)

Dates: c. 354–430 · Wikipedia
Summary: Near the end of his life, cataloguing the heresies for the deacon Quodvultdeus of Carthage, Augustine gave Tertullian his own numbered entry, no. 86 of the De haeresibus (c. 428) — the earliest extant heresy-catalogue entry under Tertullian’s name, and the earliest extant notice of a distinct Tertullianist group, whose last members, Augustine says, handed their Carthaginian basilica over to the catholic church in his own day. Augustine reports Tertullian’s teaching that the soul, and God himself, is a body, and states that this is not the ground of the heresy. The grounds he gives are that Tertullian went over to the Cataphrygians he had once refuted, condemned second marriage as fornication, and afterwards separated even from them to found assemblies of his own. Elsewhere Augustine argues against Tertullian’s corporealism on the origin of the soul, and, against the Manichees, names him with Cyprian as an early marker of date. This page records Augustine’s part in the timeline of Tertullian’s reception.

The entry: De haeresibus 86 (c. 428)

About 428, at the request of Quodvultdeus — then a deacon, later bishop of Carthage — Augustine compiled a handbook of the heresies that had arisen since Christ, eighty-odd of them, each in a short notice. The Cataphrygians, that is the Montanists of Montanus, Prisca, and Maximilla, are no. 26. Tertullian gets a separate entry of his own, no. 86: Augustine does not fold him into the Montanists but treats the Tertullianistae as a distinct group.1 The notice opens not with doctrine but with the group’s end, which Augustine reports from his own time at Carthage:

Tertullianistae a Tertulliano, cuius multa leguntur opuscula eloquentissime scripta, usque ad nostrum tempus paulatim deficientes, in extremis reliquiis durare potuerunt in urbe Carthaginensi: me autem ibi posito ante aliquot annos, quod etiam te meminisse arbitror, omni ex parte consumpti sunt. Paucissimi enim qui remanserant, in Catholicam transierunt, suamque basilicam, quae nunc etiam notissima est, Catholicae tradiderunt.

— Augustine, De haeresibus 86, Latin text: “The Tertullianistae take their name from Tertullian, many of whose works — most eloquently written — are read; dwindling little by little down to our own time, they managed to last on in their final remnants in the city of Carthage. But when I was there some years ago — as I think you too remember — they were consumed utterly. For the very few who remained went over into the catholic church and handed their basilica, which is even now very well known, over to the catholic church.” (The English renderings from the Latin are made for this page; a full scholarly translation is L. G. Muller’s of 1956.)2 The notice describes a single congregation with one building in one city, which on Augustine’s report went over to the catholic church when he was at Carthage some years before he wrote.

The charge of corporealism and the grounds Augustine gives

Augustine next states a doctrinal charge and then says that it is not the ground of the heresy. Tertullian, he reports, held that the soul is immortal but a body — and not the soul only, but God himself:

Tertullianus ergo, sicut scripta eius indicant, animam dicit immortalem quidem, sed eam corpus esse contendit: neque hanc tantum, sed ipsum etiam Deum. Nec tamen hinc haereticus dicitur factus.

— De haeresibus 86: “Tertullian, then, as his writings show, says the soul is indeed immortal, but contends that it is a body — and not this only, but God himself as well. Yet he is not on this account said to have been made a heretic.” Augustine goes on to set out a sense in which calling God a “body” need not be impious — not a body with larger and smaller parts, but a nature that is “nowhere partitioned through the spaces of place… yet in its own nature and substance remains unchangeably” — and grants that Tertullian thought “something of this kind” even about the soul. Augustine argues against the same corporealism in his writing on the origin of the soul (below); here he states that it is not what made Tertullian a heretic, and he repeats the point.3

The grounds Augustine does give concern discipline and separation, and they also give his account of the group’s history:

Non ergo ideo est Tertullianus factus haereticus; sed quia transiens ad Cataphrygas, quos ante destruxerat, coepit etiam secundas nuptias contra apostolicam doctrinam tanquam stupra damnare, et postmodum etiam ab ipsis divisus, sua conventicula propagavit. Dicit sane etiam ipse animas hominum pessimas post mortem in daemones verti.

— De haeresibus 86: “It is not, therefore, for this that Tertullian was made a heretic; but because, going over to the Cataphrygians — whom he had earlier destroyed — he began also to condemn second marriages, against apostolic teaching, as though they were fornication; and afterwards, divided even from them, he propagated his own little assemblies. He does indeed also say that the very worst souls of men are turned into demons after death.” Augustine names three grounds: the turn to the Cataphrygians he had once refuted, the condemnation of second marriage, which he calls contrary to apostolic teaching, and the separation — ab ipsis divisus, sua conventicula propagavit, the earliest extant statement that Tertullian founded a group of his own. The claim about the souls of the worst men follows, introduced with sane etiam (“indeed also”).4

The notice as evidence for a Tertullianist group

Augustine’s notice is the earliest extant testimony to a distinct Tertullianist group; the later ancient report, in the anonymous Praedestinatus (c. 435), follows Augustine’s and expands it. Augustine’s account is that Tertullian founded the Tertullianistae by separating from the Cataphrygians. In the notice, sicut scripta eius indicant (“as his writings show”) introduces the teaching on the soul; no source is named for the founding of the group. Douglas Powell (1975), for whom a formal schism “should have left unambiguous traces in polemical writings which deal with the very matters at issue,” and William Tabbernee (1997) read the African circle as a rigorist party that never left the church, an ecclesiola in ecclesia. The question is set out as a disputed reading.5

Augustine elsewhere: the origin of the soul and the reply to Faustus

Outside the catalogue, Augustine argues against Tertullian’s corporealism. Disputing the young layman Vincentius Victor, who held the soul to be corporeal and breathed forth by God from his own substance, Augustine writes:

In that he believes God to be truly incorporeal, I congratulate him that herein, at all events, he has kept himself uninfluenced by the ravings of Tertullian. For he insisted, that as the soul is corporeal, so likewise is God.

— On the Soul and Its Origin II.9 [V.], source. In the same chapter he mocks the appeal of Victor’s own teaching, a “wonderful learning… which contrives to gain for its disciples men of advanced years, and even presbyters.” Augustine did not reject traducianism outright. John Huber’s Philosophie der Kirchenväter, as quoted by Philip Schaff, holds that Augustine perceived that generationism “explains the transmission of original sin,” and that “as to generationism, he remembers Tertullian, and fears being compelled, like him, to affirm the corporeality of the soul.”6

Against the Manichees, Augustine cites Tertullian as a marker of date. Answering Faustus, who dressed Mani in the title “apostle of Jesus Christ,” Augustine dates the Manichaean sect by the church’s own calendar: “it is well known that this heresy began not only after Tertullian, but after Cyprian.” Augustine here names Tertullian and Cyprian together as earlier writers by whose dates he measures the beginning of the Manichaean sect.7

Reception and placement on the timeline

  • In the De haeresibus Augustine gives Tertullian a numbered entry and states that his corporealism is not the ground of the heresy, resting it instead on the turn to the Cataphrygians, the condemnation of second marriage, and the separation. In his other works he argues against that corporealism and cites Tertullian, with Cyprian, as an early marker of date.
  • Whether a group founded by Tertullian existed, or whether the Tertullianistae were the African New Prophecy under another name: the ancient witnesses to a distinct group are this notice and the Praedestinatus that follows it; the reading of an ecclesiola in ecclesia that never broke communion is set out on its own page and on the summary timeline.
  • Augustine is also a witness on the wiki’s timelines of the origin of the soul (where the corporealism quarrel belongs), infant baptism, and the Nephilim; his correspondence with Jerome is on its own page. Tertullian’s own corporeal-soul doctrine is set out on his origin-of-the-soul page.
