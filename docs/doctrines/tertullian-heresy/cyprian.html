Cyprian of Carthage and Tertullian: the reported “master” and the unnamed source

Dates: c. 253 · Wikipedia — Cyprian, c. 200–258; bishop of Carthage from c. 248, martyred under Valerian
Summary: In the generation after Tertullian’s turn to the New Prophecy, Cyprian, bishop of the same city, drew on Tertullian’s writings in his own treatises and never named him in any surviving work. Two things reach us: a later anecdote, reported by Jerome, that Cyprian read Tertullian every day and called for him as “the master”; and the unacknowledged dependence of Cyprian’s treatises on Tertullian’s. The silence has been read in more than one way. This page records Cyprian’s part in the timeline of Tertullian’s reception.

The evidence

“Give me the master”: Jerome’s report of Cyprian’s daily reading

I myself have seen a certain Paul an old man of Concordia, a town of Italy, who, while he himself was a very young man had been secretary to the blessed Cyprian who was already advanced in age. He said that he himself had seen how Cyprian was accustomed never to pass a day without reading Tertullian, and that he frequently said to him, “Give me the master,” meaning by this, Tertullian.

— Jerome, Lives of Illustrious Men (De viris illustribus) 53, source. The anecdote is not Cyprian’s own word but Jerome’s, written about 393 — roughly a century and a half after Cyprian — and reported at second hand through a chain Jerome names: the aged Paul of Concordia, who as a youth had been secretary to the aged Cyprian. Jerome is the only extant source for it. The dependence of Cyprian’s treatises on Tertullian’s, set out below, is documented from the texts themselves, apart from the anecdote.1

The unacknowledged dependence: Cyprian’s treatises and Tertullian’s

Cyprian never names Tertullian in his surviving works, and several of his treatises draw on Tertullian’s. The framework of his treatise on the Lord’s Prayer (De dominica oratione) follows Tertullian’s On Prayer clause by clause; his book on the good of patience (De bono patientiae) reworks Tertullian’s On Patience; his tract on the dress of virgins (De habitu virginum) draws on the On the Apparel of Women — each recast in Cyprian’s own Latin, and in each the source goes unnamed. Jerome states the dependence in Letter 84 (c. 400), where he adduces Cyprian’s use of Tertullian as precedent for reading a writer’s learning without following him into Montanism:

The blessed Cyprian takes Tertullian for his master, as his writings prove; yet, delighted as he is with the ability of this learned and zealous writer he does not join him in following Montanus and Maximilla.

— Jerome, Letter 84 (To Pammachius and Oceanus), §2, source. “As his writings prove” (ut eius scripta declarant): here Jerome grounds the point on Cyprian’s writings rather than on the anecdote. The parallels are traced in the prefatory notes of the Ante-Nicene Fathers translation of Cyprian and by T. D. Barnes (1971).2

Readings of the silence

Cyprian himself gives no reason for leaving his source unnamed. Ernest Evans (1953) connects the matter to Tertullian’s turn to the New Prophecy, which on his account “caused later generations to regard all his works with suspicion,” so that “it was through the disciple that his thoughts on the Lord’s Prayer became the common property of Christendom.”3 Douglas Powell (1975) reads the Carthaginian New Prophecy of Tertullian’s day as a rigorist party that remained within the church rather than a separated sect.4 No surviving text of Cyprian’s generation names Tertullian as a heretic; the heresy-catalogue entries under his name begin with Augustine, c. 428 (see the summary timeline).

Reception and placement on the timeline

  • Cyprian says nothing about Tertullian in his own hand. This page records his unacknowledged use of Tertullian’s treatises and the later anecdote that names it — see the summary timeline, third-century block.
  • The da magistrum anecdote and the Roman-clergy motive both reach us only through Jerome’s De viris illustribus 53, which is set out, with the scholarly discussion of it, on Jerome’s page. In Letter 84 Jerome adduces Cyprian’s practice as the precedent for his own reading of Origen.
  • The anonymous book-list known as the Decretum Gelasianum, which lists the works of Tertullian among those not received, also places there the works of “Thascius Cyprianus” (further down the timeline).
  • Cyprian is also a witness on the wiki’s timelines of purgatory, the ministerial priesthood, infant baptism, the Old Testament canon, the Eucharist, prayer to the saints, and the gravity of sins — several of them on disciplinary questions (post-baptismal penance, the unity of the church) that Tertullian also addressed.
