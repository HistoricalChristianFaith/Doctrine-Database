Papias of Hierapolis on John, son of Zebedee

Dates: c. 60–130 (writing c. 110–130; surviving only in fragments) · Wikipedia
Summary: Papias, bishop of Hierapolis in Asia and a collector of the elders' living memory, is the earliest post-apostolic writer on this timeline to name John, and two passages connected with him have been read in different ways. In the one surviving sentence of his preface he names a John twice, an apostle and a “presbyter,” the text on which the “two Johns” question turns; and a lost passage of his second book was later reported (by writers of the fifth and ninth centuries) to have said that John, like his brother, “was killed by the Jews,” the report on which the reading that John was killed rests.

Claims & evidence

The preface: the name John in two places

Papias explains that he prized the “living and abiding voice” of those who had known the apostles. In doing so he lists two ranks of informant — and the name John falls in both.

…what Andrew or what Peter said, or what was said by Philip, or by Thomas, or by James, or by John, or by Matthew, or by any other of the disciples of the Lord, and what things Aristion and the presbyter John, the disciples of the Lord, say. For I did not think that what was to be gotten from the books would profit me as much as what came from the living and abiding voice.

— Papias, preface to the Expositions of the Oracles of the Lord, quoted in Eusebius, Ecclesiastical History 3.39.4. The first “John” stands among the apostles (past tense, “said”); the second, “the presbyter John,” stands after Aristion among those still speaking in Papias's own day. Whether these are two men or one, named twice in different capacities, is set out on the disputed reading on the two Johns.

The report that John “was killed by the Jews”

A passage in the Chronicon ascribed to George Hamartolus (“the Monk”, 9th c.) reports — citing the lost second book of Papias — that John was martyred.

After Domitian, Nerva reigned one year, who recalled John from the island (i.e. Patmos), and allowed him to dwell in Ephesus. He was at that time the sole survivor of the twelve Apostles, and after writing his Gospel received the honour of martyrdom. For Papias, bishop of Hierapolis, who was an eye-witness of him, in the second book of the Oracles of the Lord says that he was killed by the Jews, and thereby evidently fulfilled, together with his brother, Christ's prophecy concerning them.

— Chronicon ascribed to George Hamartolus (9th c.), a fragment of Papias, in J. B. Lightfoot, The Apostolic Fathers (1891), Fragments of Papias. A similar note is ascribed to the fifth-century epitomator Philip of Side: “Papias in his second book says that John the Divine and James his brother were killed by the Jews” (the fragments of Papias). T. C. Schmidt, in his notes to the same collection, writes that the George Hamartolus passage “appears to be a later interpolation and was not written by George Hamartolus.” Both reports are set out on the disputed reading on John's death.

Reception and debate

  • Only fragments survive. Papias's five-book work is lost; every notice comes to us secondhand, chiefly through Eusebius, who thought Papias “of very limited understanding” and who suggested that the second John may have been the one who saw the Revelation (Eusebius, Ecclesiastical History 3.39.6, 13).
  • Two disputed readings. The preface is the text of the question of Papias's “two Johns”, which bears on who wrote the Gospel, the epistles, and Revelation; the martyrdom fragment is the text of the question whether John died of old age or was killed. See the disputed readings on the summary timeline.
  • The sequence in George's notice. George's sentence has John recalled from Patmos, dwelling at Ephesus as sole survivor of the Twelve, and writing his Gospel, and then adds that he “received the honour of martyrdom.” The same passage goes on to quote Eusebius that John “made his residence and died at Ephesus” (the fragments of Papias).
