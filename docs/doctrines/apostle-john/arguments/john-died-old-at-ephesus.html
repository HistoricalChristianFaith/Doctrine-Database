Did John die of old age at Ephesus, or was he killed, as a report ascribed to Papias says?

Disputed reading · on John, son of Zebedee · witnesses: Irenaeus · Papias (as later reported)

Related readings: Does Papias's preface name one John twice, or two different Johns?

The passage

those who were conversant in Asia with John, the disciple of the Lord, [affirming] that John conveyed to them that information. And he remained among them up to the times of Trajan.

— Irenaeus, Against Heresies 2.22.5.1

Papias in his second book says that John the Divine and James his brother were killed by the Jews.

— an epitome ascribed to Philip of Side, History of Christianity (5th c.).2

After Domitian, Nerva reigned one year, who recalled John from the island (i.e. Patmos), and allowed him to dwell in Ephesus. He was at that time the sole survivor of the twelve Apostles, and after writing his Gospel received the honour of martyrdom. For Papias, bishop of Hierapolis, who was an eye-witness of him, in the second book of the Oracles of the Lord says that he was killed by the Jews, and thereby evidently fulfilled, together with his brother, Christ's prophecy concerning them.

— a passage in the Chronicon ascribed to George Hamartolus (9th c.).3

The question

The New Testament records the death of John's brother James (Acts 12:2) but not John's own. The question is whether John lived to old age and died a natural death at Ephesus, as the second-century Asian witnesses report, or was killed, as two later writers say Papias's lost second book stated.4 It bears on whether the Ephesian traditions about John belong to the son of Zebedee; on that, see the two-Johns question.

A long life and a natural death at Ephesus

In this reading John lived on in Roman Asia into the reign of Trajan (98–117), overseeing the churches from Ephesus, and died there of old age.

Held by: Irenaeus (Against Heresies, c. 180); Polycrates of Ephesus (letter preserved by Eusebius, c. 190); Leopold Fonck (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1910); Sean McDowell (The Fate of the Apostles, 2015).5

The reading rests on several second-century witnesses from different places. Irenaeus, who as a boy had heard Polycarp of Smyrna, a hearer of John, writes that John “did himself publish a Gospel during his residence at Ephesus in Asia” and “remained among them up to the times of Trajan”: a chain of John, Polycarp and Irenaeus. Clement of Alexandria tells of John returning “after the tyrant's death” from Patmos to Ephesus and travelling the neighbouring country to appoint bishops, and riding, an old man, after a convert who had become a robber. Polycrates, bishop of Ephesus, numbers John among the great dead of Asia: “He fell asleep at Ephesus.” Eusebius and Jerome follow: Jerome has John die “worn out by old age” and buried near the city. The apocryphal Acts of John narrates a quiet death in a grave John has dug himself. Fonck sets out the same chronology: banishment to Patmos under Domitian, return to Ephesus, and death there “about A.D. 100 at a great age.” On this reading the joint commemoration of John and James in early calendars reflects the brothers' shared feast: Fonck records that the feast of 27 December was originally John's and James's together, and that both names stand in the Carthage Calendar. The “cup” and “baptism” of Mark 10:39 are taken in this reading as a share in Christ's sufferings, which the seer of Patmos describes as being a “companion in tribulation” (Revelation 1:9) (⚠ unverified: no named holder of this point located). On this reading, the memory of a natural death is carried by witnesses from Asia, Gaul and Alexandria, including Polycrates, bishop of Ephesus.6

A killing of John, reported from Papias

In this reading John, like his brother James, was killed, as a report from Papias's second book states, so that Jesus's saying about the cup was fulfilled in both brothers.

Held by: the epitome ascribed to Philip of Side (5th c.), reporting Papias (c. 110–130); the passage ascribed to George Hamartolus (9th c.); E. Schwartz (Über den Tod der Söhne Zebedäi, 1904); J. Wellhausen (⚠ unverified: work not named in the sources consulted).7

The reading starts from a saying of Jesus. When the sons of Zebedee ask for the seats at his right and left, he tells both of them: “Ye shall indeed drink of the cup that I drink of; and with the baptism that I am baptized withal shall ye be baptized.” Read as a prophecy of martyrdom, the saying applies to John as much as to James, who was killed by Herod Agrippa about 44; and on this reading a community would not have kept a saying foretelling a martyrdom that never came. The second support is a report from Papias, bishop of Hierapolis and a hearer of the elders of Asia, whose testimony would be the earliest of all. Two later writers ascribe the same statement to the same place, Papias's second book: the epitome ascribed to Philip of Side, that “John the Divine and James his brother were killed by the Jews,” and the passage ascribed to George Hamartolus, which says the killing fulfilled “together with his brother, Christ's prophecy concerning them.” The same passage adds that Origen, in his commentary on Matthew, affirmed John's martyrdom, “declaring that he had learnt the fact from the successors of the Apostles.” Third, the Syriac Martyrology (411) and the Carthage Calendar commemorate John and James together, which this reading takes as the memory of a shared martyrdom. Schwartz argued for a martyrdom of John, possibly an early one alongside his brother. On this reading the saying of Jesus, the report from Papias and the joint commemorations preserve one memory: that John, like James, died for his witness.8
