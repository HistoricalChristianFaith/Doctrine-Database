Does Papias's preface name one John twice, or two different Johns?

Disputed reading · on John, son of Zebedee · witness: Papias of Hierapolis

Related readings: Did John die of old age at Ephesus, or was he killed, as a report ascribed to Papias says?

The passage

…what Andrew or what Peter said, or what was said by Philip, or by Thomas, or by James, or by John, or by Matthew, or by any other of the disciples of the Lord, and what things Aristion and the presbyter John, the disciples of the Lord, say. For I did not think that what was to be gotten from the books would profit me as much as what came from the living and abiding voice.

— Papias, preface to the Expositions of the Oracles of the Lord, quoted in Eusebius, Ecclesiastical History 3.39.4.1

The question

Papias names a John among the disciples of the Lord whose words the elders reported, and again, after Aristion, “the presbyter John” among those still speaking. Whether these are one man or two bears on how the Gospel, the three epistles and the Revelation ascribed to John have been assigned to authors, and on the date of Revelation (see the reading of Irenaeus's “was seen”).2

One John, the apostle, named in two capacities

In this reading Papias names the apostle John twice: once among the apostles whose words he learned through others, and again as a living informant he heard himself.

Held by: Irenaeus (Against Heresies 5.33.4, c. 180), who calls Papias “a hearer of John” without discussing the preface; Leopold Fonck (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1910).3

Irenaeus, who had heard Polycarp, calls Papias “an ancient man who was a hearer of John and a companion of Polycarp,” and knows one John in Asia, the disciple who leaned on the Lord's breast and published a Gospel at Ephesus. Fonck reads the preface in that light. He holds that “in this passage by the word presbyters only Apostles can be understood,” and explains the double naming by Papias's relation to his chief teacher: “By inquiring of others he had learned some things indirectly from John, just as he had from the other Apostles referred to. In addition he had received information concerning the teachings and acts of Jesus directly, without the intervention of others, from the still living ‘Presbyter John’, as he also had from Aristion.” Both figures are called “disciples of the Lord,” and the past tense (“said”) and present tense (“say”) mark, on this reading, two channels of report about one man, the long-lived apostle belonging to both. Fonck cites Eusebius's Chronicle and Jerome's Letter 75 as calling the apostle the teacher of Papias, and that Irenaeus “positively designates the Apostle and Evangelist John as the teacher of Papias.” For the two Ephesian tombs, Jerome (393) reports that “some think that there are two memorials of this same John the evangelist.” On this reading the preface records how Papias came by the apostle's teaching, and the line from Polycarp to Irenaeus knows the apostle as the one John of Asia.4

Two Johns in Asia: the apostle and the elder

In this reading Papias names two men, the apostle John among the apostles and a distinct “presbyter John,” a disciple of the Lord still living in Papias's day, and some of the Johannine writings belong to the second.

Held by: Dionysius of Alexandria (c. 250), on the author of Revelation only; Eusebius (Ecclesiastical History 3.39.5–6, c. 325); the epitome ascribed to Philip of Side (5th c.); Richard Bauckham (Jesus and the Eyewitnesses, 2006).5

Eusebius, the earliest extant interpreter of the sentence, observes that “the name John is twice enumerated”: the first John stands with Peter, James, Matthew “and the rest of the apostles,” while the other is named “after an interval,” placed “among others outside of the number of the apostles, putting Aristion before him,” and called “a presbyter.” The change of tense sets the first among past authorities and the second among those still speaking, and Eusebius adds that Papias “says that he was himself a hearer of Aristion and the presbyter John.” He joins the report of “two tombs in Ephesus, each of which, even to the present day, is called John's.” Dionysius of Alexandria had earlier reported “two monuments in Ephesus, each bearing the name of John,” and judged from style that the Revelation is not by the writer of the Gospel and the First Epistle: those were written “without error as regards the Greek language,” while the seer's “dialect and language are not accurate Greek.” The author of 2 and 3 John calls himself “the elder,” the title Papias gives the second John, and the epitome ascribed to Philip of Side reads Papias as listing “Aristion and a second John, whom also he called ‘The Elder’,” reporting that some think him the author of the two short epistles. Bauckham identifies the evangelist with Papias's John the elder, an eyewitness disciple of Jesus who was not one of the Twelve. On this reading the preface preserves the memory of a second John of Asia, distinct from the son of Zebedee.6
