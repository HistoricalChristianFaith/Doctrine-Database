The Acts of John on John, son of Zebedee

Dates: c. 150–200 (the “Leucian” apocryphal Acts) · Wikipedia
Summary: The apocryphal Acts of John is a second-century romance with encratite and docetic features, condemned at the Second Council of Nicaea (787). It is the earliest extant source for several of John's best-known scenes — the destruction of the temple of Artemis at Ephesus, the raisings of the dead, and his own peaceful “Metastasis,” a death in a grave he digs himself.

Claims & evidence

The collapse of the temple of Artemis at Ephesus

The book's set-piece has John stand in the temple of Artemis (Diana) at Ephesus on the goddess's feast day and pray that God will overthrow her cult. As he prays, the great temple gives way — the altar shatters, the idols fall, and half the building comes down, killing the priest — and the crowd turns to John's God on the spot.

And as John spake these things, immediately the altar of Artemis was parted into many pieces, and all the things that were dedicated in the temple fell, and [MS. that which seemed good to him] was rent asunder, and likewise of the images of the gods more than seven. And the half of the temple fell down, so that the priest was slain at one blow by the falling of the (?roof, ? beam).

— M. R. James, The Apocryphal New Testament (Acts of John), §42 (within §§37–45). The crowd's response follows at once: “One is the God of John, one is the God that hath pity on us, for thou only art God: now are we turned to thee, beholding thy marvellous works!”

Raisings of the dead (Drusiana and others)

Across the book John raises several people to life; the most developed episode is that of Drusiana, a chaste married woman who dies of grief. John comes to her tomb, takes her hand, prays, and calls her out — a scene that also serves the book's insistent theme of continence, since her would-be violator is struck dead and then himself restored.

And after these words John said unto Drusiana: Drusiana, arise. And she arose and came out of the tomb; and when she saw herself in her shift only, she was perplexed at the thing…

— ibid., §80 (within §§63–86). Later accounts, down to the Golden Legend, also tell of John raising the dead at Ephesus.

The “Metastasis”: John's peaceful death in a self-dug grave

The book closes with John's death — called the Metastasis (“passing over”). At the last, an aged John goes out from the gathering, has the brethren dig a trench beside a brother's tomb (bidding them make it deeper), lays aside his garments, prays a long farewell prayer, and then lies down in the grave and quietly gives up his spirit — a serene old-age death, with no violence and no executioner. Some copies add that when the brethren came the next day his body was not found, only his sandals or a fine dust (“manna”) welling up from the grave. Later tradition also told that John had not died but had been translated alive (⚠ unverified).

— ibid., §§106–115 (the account of John's death; wording varies across the Greek recensions). The book narrates John's end as a peaceful death, not a martyrdom.

Reception and debate

  • Genre and reception. The Acts of John belongs to the second-century “Leucian” corpus of apostolic romances (with the Acts of Peter, Andrew, and Thomas). Its Christology is docetic in places — notably in the “Hymn of Jesus” and the vision on the mount, where the crucified Lord's body seems unreal (§§87–105) — and it has encratite features; it was condemned at the Second Council of Nicaea (787). Leopold Fonck (“St. John the Evangelist,” Catholic Encyclopedia, 1910) calls its stories “unhistorical invention.” On the apocryphal Acts as a class of source, see the apostles hub.
  • The poisoned cup. The ordeal in which John drinks poison unharmed — linked with the chalice-and-serpent in his iconography — is not in the surviving Acts of John. It appears in the Byzantine and Latin apostle-lists and the medieval Golden Legend. See the summary timeline.
  • The Metastasis and the question of John's death. The book narrates a quiet death in old age at Ephesus, not a killing, and is cited in the reading that John died of old age there; see the disputed reading on John's death.
