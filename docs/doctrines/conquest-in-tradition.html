The Conquest of Canaan in Christian Tradition

The book of Joshua tells how Israel, under Moses' successor, crossed the Jordan, took Jericho and Ai, defeated the kings of the south and the north, put the cities under the ban (ḥerem), and divided the land by lot. Judges tells of the unfinished settlement that followed. This timeline records how the Church has read that conquest across time, earliest witness first. It is a belief-history. It asks what each witness held, not what the ground shows; the material record is weighed on its own page. From the New Testament on, the reading that runs through the witnesses is that the conquest was real history. It is dated, located, and its miracles are real. The figure of Joshua as Jesus (the two share the Greek name Iēsous) is laid on top of the history and does not replace it. Three questions divide the witnesses. The first is how far the spiritual sense displaces the letter: Origen's homilies, the most allegorical, still call the wars "deeds done." The second is how to read the book's sweeping formulas, such as "Joshua took all the land," which Origen, Jerome and Calvin all read as less than literal totality. The third is how to answer the moral problem of the ban, raised by Marcion in the 140s. The witnesses answer it with retribution for the Canaanites' sins, with God's command, with God's inscrutable will, and with a refusal to let the wars serve as a pattern for Christians. In the modern period the statements of the churches diverge. The one Canaanite the conquest spared has a timeline of her own, Rahab of Jericho.

Timeline

The New Testament (c. AD 50–90)

The New Testament never narrates the conquest, but it refers to it often enough to show how it was held. It is a past event in Israel's history, recited in sermons as one link in the chain from Egypt to David. Stephen says the fathers brought the tabernacle into the land "with Jesus [Joshua] into the possession of the Gentiles, whom God drave out" (Acts 7:45). Paul at Pisidian Antioch says God "destroyed seven nations in the land of Chanaan" and "divided their land to them by lot" (Acts 13:19). Hebrews puts the fall of Jericho and the rescue of Rahab in its roll of historical exemplars of faith, between Moses and the judges (11:30–32). James makes Rahab's deed a case of justification by works (2:25), and Matthew names her in the Messiah's genealogy (1:5). The one typology of Joshua rests on the shared Greek name Iēsous. The rest Joshua gave in the land was real but not final (Hebrews 4:8–9). The figure is built on the history. None of these texts raises the moral question of the ban; the destruction is credited to God, and the dead of Jericho are "them that believed not."1

Josephus (c. AD 93–94)

Flavius Josephus, a Jewish historian writing at Rome, gives the earliest full retelling of Joshua outside the Bible (Antiquities V). He is not a Christian witness, but his history of the conquest was the one Christian readers inherited alongside Joshua itself. He tells it as a dated military campaign. There are spies who note "which parts of the walls were strong," a war over by "the fifth year," "thirty one" defeated kings, surveyors who measure the land, and a general who dies at 110 after twenty-five years in command. He keeps the ban unsoftened: at Jericho "they all perished, even to the women and the children," and the law of war spares every enemy "excepting the nation of the Canaanites." He keeps the miracles but tells them with restraint. The walls fall with no force applied, and at Gibeon "the day was lengthened," vouched for by "the Books laid up in the temple." His Rahab is an innkeeper. He uses no typology at all.2

Clement of Rome (c. 96)

Clement of Rome is the first Christian writer after the New Testament to take up the conquest, and he does so through the spies at Jericho (1 Clement 12). He retells Joshua 2 closely, with the king's officers, the flax on the roof, and Rahab's confession that "the Lord your God hath given you this city." He places the episode in a chain of historical exempla after Enoch, Noah, Abraham and Lot. The spies' oath holds the logic of the ban in a line ("all that are found outside of thy dwelling shall perish"), and Clement adds no comment on it. Only at the end does he draw a figure, and he draws it from a detail of the history: the scarlet thread showed "that redemption should flow through the blood of the Lord," so that "there was not only faith, but prophecy, in this woman." The "not only … but" states the layered reading the Fathers would make standard.3

Justin Martyr (c. 155)

Justin Martyr, in his Dialogue with Trypho, is the first Christian writer to make Joshua himself a full figure of Christ. His argument rests on a fact of the record: Moses renamed Hoshea "Jesus," and it was this Jesus, not Moses, who "led the surviving people into the Holy Land" and divided it by lot. Justin argues with a Jew from texts both accept, so the events must be real for the argument to work. Trypho's fathers "witnessed the sun stand still … for thirty-six hours." The second circumcision with stone knives, the Captain of the Lord's host before Jericho (whom Justin takes for the pre-incarnate Son), the battle with Amalek, and Rahab's scarlet thread are deeds from which figures are drawn. Justin also marks where the type falls short. Joshua gave "a temporary inheritance," and "the memorial of Amalek remained after the son of Nave." Israel "invaded the territory of the sons of Canaan by the will of God," fulfilling Noah's curse. Justin names Marcion as a living teacher but never raises the moral problem of the ban.4

Irenaeus (c. 180)

Irenaeus tells the conquest as a plain step in the history of salvation. In his Demonstration of the Apostolic Preaching, Joshua "divided the Jordan," "overthrew and destroyed the seven races," and settled the people in the land, in a sequence that runs on to David and Solomon. The peoples of Canaan are the posterity of Ham, "cut off by God, being delivered up to judgment." In Against Heresies Jericho "fell to ruins at the sounding of the seven trumpets," while Rahab, who received spies he reads as a type of the Trinity, was saved. Against the Marcionites, who set the punishing God of the Old Testament against the Father of Jesus, he does not single out the Canaanite wars. He answers all the old judgments at once: one God takes vengeance in both Testaments, "in the one case indeed typically, temporarily, and more moderately; but in the other, really, enduringly, and more rigidly." He passes on a presbyter's rule that deeds Scripture records without censure are to be searched for a type, not accused. Moses led the people out, and "Jesus" leads them into the inheritance.5

Tertullian (c. 207)

Tertullian wrote the longest early reply to Marcion and treats the conquest throughout as fact. Jericho's walls fell on the seventh day "just as was predicted," and the city was "made a prey by the edge of the sword." The Jordan "wholly ceased to run at the bidding of Joshua." The sun stood still at Gibeon, and Joshua's "mighty deeds … you yourselves read." Over this he lays Justin's typology: Hoshea renamed Jesus, the land of promise as eternal life, the knife of stone as Christ's circumcision. The Marcionite charge he reports about Jericho concerns the Sabbath, broken by God's own command, not bloodshed. Against the wider charge of a cruel Creator he argues that God's severity is "good, because just," though he never applies this to the Canaanites by name. He refuses to let Joshua's march warrant Christian soldiering ("if it pleases you to sport with the subject"). The one surviving Marcionite antithesis on the conquest comes from a later report, the Dialogue of Adamantius (c. 300). It sets Joshua holding the sun "that he might kill more" against Paul's "let not the sun go down upon your wrath."6

Origen (c. 249–251)

Origen gave the Church its first full reading of Joshua, twenty-six Homilies on Joshua preached at Caesarea, which survive in Rufinus's Latin. In them Joshua, who bears Jesus' name, is a figure of Christ, and the conquest is the believer's war against sin and the demons. The book "does not so much set forth for us the deeds of the son of Nave as it depicts for us the mysteries of my Lord Jesus" (1.3). Yet he sets out the history first and calls it history. The Amorite war is "the history of the deeds done," which "has plainly been fulfilled" (9.10). The sun at Gibeon is among the "miracles of divine power, wrought indeed according to the history" (11.1). Christians "shall not fight as the ancients fought" (12.1). What he refuses is to let the letter suffice, or to let it serve the Church as a pattern for killing ("not teaching cruelty thereby, as the heretics suppose," 11.6). He denies the literal truth only of particular formulas and details: "Jesus took all the land," when "he held Judaea alone" (15.7), and Ai left uninhabited "forever" (8.5). On First Principles lists the events Scripture includes that "did not take place," and none of them is from the conquest. Two passages come closest to setting the extermination itself aside (Hom. 8.7; Against Celsus 7.19), and modern readers differ on how far he went.7

Eusebius of Caesarea (c. 325)

Eusebius, bishop of Caesarea in Palestine, treats the conquest as history in three ways. His Onomasticon, a gazetteer of biblical place-names finished before c. 330, records the city "Josue razed" and where it is "pointed out" in his own day. Of Jericho it says that the remains of the earlier cities are still shown. It places the stones "brought up out of the Jordan" two miles from Jericho at Gilgal, the ruins of Ai near Bethel, the cave at Makkedah where Joshua shut up five kings, and Joshua's own tomb. His Chronicle gives Joshua twenty-seven years "according to the tradition of the Hebrews." It sets Joshua's allotment of the land beside the kings of Argos and Athens, and it argues for 480 years from the Exodus to Solomon. His apologetic works read the shared name Iēsous as a sign of Christ, given by Moses to "the successor of his rule." They take the commander at Jericho to be the divine Word, "the fellow-combatant of His people." The Amorites, he says, were "destroyed by Joshua" once "their sins were fulfilled." The typology rests on the history and never replaces it.8

Jerome (c. 394)

Jerome, who lived at Bethlehem from 386, sums up Joshua as history read as a type. Joshua, "a type of the Lord in name as well as in deed," "crossed over Jordan, subdued hostile kingdoms, divided the land among the conquering people," and so "marked out the spiritual realms of the heavenly Jerusalem" (Letter 53, 394). He put Eusebius's gazetteer of the sites into Latin. His life of Paula (Letter 108, 404) takes her past Gibeon, where Joshua "gave commandments to the sun and moon," to Gilgal's twelve stones, the Jordan, and Joshua's tomb. On Isaiah he counts the sun's standing still among the signs from heaven, and he calls the slaughter at Gibeon God's "strange work." Against a heretic who called Joshua "a bloodthirsty man," he appeals to Joshua's holiness. Against the Pelagians he cites Achan's household and the hardened Canaanite cities as cases where no one may call God's will to account. He qualifies the letter twice. The "second circumcision," taken literally, "cannot possibly stand." The land itself was small, never fully won, and "promised, not given" (Letter 129).9

Augustine of Hippo (c. 400)

Augustine keeps the figure of Joshua as Jesus. Jericho's walls and Rahab's scarlet line are signs of Christ and the Church, and Joshua's changed name is "a prediction." He sets the figure on a history he states, dates and defends. In the City of God Joshua leads Israel into the land, divides it and rules twenty-seven years. His death is synchronized with the kings of Assyria, Argos and Athens, and the years from the entry into Canaan to the founding of Rome are counted. The Jordan crossing, Jericho's fall and the sun at Gibeon are real portents, cited against pagan sceptics. Against the Manichaean Faustus he defends war at God's command as "not ferocity but obedience" and "righteous retribution." His Questions on Joshua (c. 419) take the hard cases one by one. The ambush at Ai gives the classic definition of a just war as one that "avenges injuries." The cities left with none alive he calls "no cruelty," because God commanded it. Achan's innocent children he leaves to God's hidden judgment, while denying that they were damned. He also treats the Gibeonite oath. Where Origen read the book's "all the land" formulas spiritually, Augustine reconciles them with the later history down to Solomon.10

Theodoret of Cyrus (c. 453)

Theodoret of Cyrus, the last great exegete of the Antiochene school, answered twenty questions on Joshua late in life in his Questions on the Octateuch. He reads the book as history first and then sets a figure of Christ beside it, as he says Paul did, "not casting out the history." The Jordan was held back by the Creator "more than any wall of adamant." Jericho fell "without weapons or siege-engines." From Caleb's age he works out that Israel "divided the land after fighting for seven years." To the charge of cruelty he answers that "he who accuses the prophet accuses the one who commanded these things." The Canaanites "had done deeds deserving total destruction," as the generations of the Flood and of Sodom had. He explains the land left unconquered historically: the promise was conditional, and Israel broke the law. The sun at Gibeon stopped at Joshua's word alone. From the citation of an older book he concludes that "someone else, one of those who came later, composed this book," drawing on an earlier source. He sets typology on top of each event and never puts it in the event's place.11

Thomas Aquinas (c. 1270)

Thomas Aquinas wrote no commentary on Joshua, but he uses the book throughout his theology, and always as history. His rule is that every spiritual sense "is based on the literal, and presupposes it." So his lectures on Hebrews first tell how Jericho's walls fell "at God's command," and only then add a moral reading. He counts the sun's standing still among miracles of the highest rank, done by God "obeying the voice of a man." On the ban he is direct. In the cities of the promised land "all were ordered to be slain, on account of their former crimes," and God sent Israel "as executor of Divine justice." Behind this stands a principle: God is "Lord of life and death," and whoever carries out his sentence "will be no murderer." Through Augustine's questions on Joshua, the conquest gives his theory of war its definition of a just cause and its proof that the ambush at Ai was lawful. On Achan he sets a limit: what God does by hidden judgments, a human court may never copy.12

John Calvin (c. 1564)

John Calvin's Commentary on Joshua, dictated in the months before his death in 1564, reads the book as plain history: the record of how God kept his promise of the land, which was "truly and distinctly divided by His orders." The miracles are real events. The Jordan stood "gathered up into a heap," "neither a natural nor fortuitous event"; Jericho's walls "fell of their own accord"; at Gibeon "the day was continued through the whole night," and the lost book of Jasher is cited to back it. He leaves the authorship open and reads the sweeping formulas with care: "it was far from being true that Joshua had actually acquired the whole land." He meets the ban head-on, without allegory. The slaughter "might seem an inhuman massacre, had it not been executed by the command of God." The Canaanites had been "borne with for four hundred years, until their iniquity was complete," God hardened their hearts, and "the judgment-seat of heaven is not subject to our laws." Rahab's lie was a fault, "not imputed." He draws no typology from the shared name of Joshua and Jesus. His Christ in the book is the "captain of the Lord's host" (the pre-incarnate Son), together with a promise left half-kept that pointed believers forward.13

Modern church statements (1965–2014)

Modern church statements divide over whether Joshua is to be read as factual history. Vatican II's Dei Verbum (1965) names no passage of Joshua. It teaches that Scripture teaches "without error that truth which God wanted put into sacred writings for the sake of salvation," directs interpreters to "literary forms," and says the Old Testament contains "some things which are incomplete and temporary" yet shows "true divine pedagogy." The evangelical Chicago Statements also name no passage of Joshua. The 1978 statement denies that inerrancy is limited to "spiritual, religious, or redemptive themes, exclusive of assertions in the fields of history and science." The 1982 statement affirms that "the biblical record of events … corresponds to historical fact." The Pontifical Biblical Commission (2014) does take up Joshua. It says the conquest narratives "do not present the characteristics of a historical account," since "the walls of a city do not collapse at the sound of trumpets." Citing "the best interpreters of the patristic tradition," it reads the conquest as "a kind of parable" and the ban by a "non-literal interpretation." It also holds that salvation history "does not exist without a historical nucleus." By its own preface it is not an official act of the Magisterium.14

Arguments

Sub-claims marshaled for (or against) a reading, which cut across the timeline rather than sitting at one date. Each is weighed adversarially on its own page, with an assessment of the interpretation it serves.

Do the patristic defenses answer the moral problem of the ḥerem? (assessment: contested)

The moral problem of the ḥerem and the patristic answers is homed on the material-record page but belongs to this tradition. It weighs the two classic answers to the objection that runs from Marcion to modern critics: Origen's spiritual warfare and Augustine's divine command and just war. It rates the question contested. Both answers are serious and coherent, and the wiki does not count the divine-command defense out a priori. But the two pull against each other, and the death of non-combatants remains where critics still press. The witnesses above add answers that page does not yet weigh: retribution for sins "fulfilled" (Eusebius, Theodoret, Calvin), God's inscrutable will (Jerome), and Augustine's own treatment of Achan's children.15
