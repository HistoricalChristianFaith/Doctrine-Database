The Resurrection of the Body

Both ancient creeds confess a resurrection of the dead as an article of faith — the Apostles' Creed "the resurrection of the flesh" (carnis resurrectionem), the Nicene "the resurrection of the dead" — and every witness on this timeline holds that at the last day the dead are raised bodily, never to die again. Much of what these witnesses write concerns the nature and identity of the risen body: in what sense is it the same body that died?

Two readings of that question appear in the record, and both cite the same text — Paul's seed-and-body analogy in 1 Corinthians 15, "It is sown a natural body; it is raised a spiritual body" (sōma psychikon → sōma pneumatikon), with the further verse "flesh and blood cannot inherit the kingdom of God" (15:50). The identity-of-substance reading, held by the second-century anti-Gnostic writers and most later witnesses below, Greek and Latin, holds the risen body is numerically the very same flesh that died — changed in glory and quality, but not in substance (Tertullian: "the very same flesh which was once sown in death will bear fruit in resurrection-life"). The transformed-body reading, set out above all by Origen, keeps a real and continuous resurrection body but lodges its identity in a persisting form or pattern rather than in the return of the same material particles, reading the "spiritual body" as a genuinely changed quality of existence. Later disputes, including whether a resurrection of the form is a resurrection of the flesh, run along this axis. Two of them have their own pages: did Origen deny the resurrection of the flesh?, and was the Seven Sleepers legend composed for the fifth-century resurrection controversy?

This timeline traces the nature and identity of the risen body, not the two neighbouring questions it is easily confused with: the intermediate state is the interval — what becomes of the soul between death and resurrection — while eternal conscious torment, universal restoration, and conditional immortality concern the final fate of the risen (who is saved, annihilated, or restored). The resurrection of the body sits between the two questions.

Related doctrines: The living and the dead (the communion of the saints) — see also the intermediate state · purgatory and prayer for the dead · prayer to the saints · eternal conscious torment · universal restoration (apokatastasis) · conditional immortality (annihilationism) · indulgences · the treasury of merit · dream-apparitions of the dead (the interval before the resurrection, the raising of the body itself, and the fate of the risen).

Timeline

The Apostle Paul — 1 Corinthians 15 (c. 55)

Paul writes the chapter every later witness on this timeline reads. Against Corinthians who asked "how are the dead raised up? and with what body do they come?" he answers with the analogy of the seed: "that which thou sowest is not quickened, except it die," and God gives it a body of his choosing — "It is sown a natural body; it is raised a spiritual body." The risen body is continuous with the one sown ("this corruptible must put on incorruption") yet transfigured ("sown in dishonour…raised in glory"); and he adds the verse both later readings interpret: "flesh and blood cannot inherit the kingdom of God; neither doth corruption inherit incorruption" (15:50).1

Justin Martyr (c. 165)

Justin — in the fragments On the Resurrection — states the same-flesh reading against those who "say that there is no resurrection of the flesh": "the resurrection is a resurrection of the flesh which died," and it rises "entire," God being able to "collect again the decomposed members of the flesh, and make the same body as was formerly produced by Him." The flesh is God's own handiwork, made "in the image of God," and therefore saved with the soul; Christ's own bodily rising — he "let them handle Him," and ate before them — is for Justin the proof that the body, not the soul only, rises.2

Athenagoras of Athens (c. 190)

Athenagoras, in an extant Christian treatise devoted wholly to the subject, argues the resurrection from God's power and will: reuniting "bodies that are dead, or even entirely dissolved into their elements, so as to constitute the same persons" is a work the Creator "can perform, and can will, and such as is worthy of Him," for the God who made the body from nothing "knows…whither each of the dissolved particles" has gone. He meets the era's stock objection — that bodies eaten by beasts or (in famine) by other men cannot be reassembled — by arguing that alien matter never truly becomes part of a body, so the same person's own parts are reconstituted; and he grounds the whole hope in the nature of man as a lasting composite of soul and body, who must rise as the same composite to be justly judged.3

Irenaeus (c. 202)

Irenaeus, in Against Heresies Book 5, defends the flesh's resurrection against the Gnostic denial and gives a reading of the crux verse. If the flesh is not saved, he argues, "then neither did the Lord redeem us with His blood, nor is the cup of the Eucharist the communion of His blood" — the very bodies "nourished" by Christ's body and blood must rise. And "flesh and blood cannot inherit the kingdom of God" does not exclude the substance of the flesh: "flesh and blood" names those who lack the Spirit, and the flesh is not what inherits but what is inherited — taken up by the life-giving Spirit "into the kingdom of God," "not repudiating…the substance of flesh, but showing that into it the Spirit must be infused."4

Tertullian (c. 220)

Tertullian, in On the Resurrection of the Flesh, sets out the identity-of-substance reading at treatise length: "the very same flesh which was once sown in death will bear fruit in resurrection-life — the same in essence, only more full and perfect; not another, although reappearing in another form." Paul's "spiritual body" marks "a difference of honour, not of nature"; change is not destruction ("a thing may undergo a complete change, and yet remain still the same thing"), so "the substance of the flesh will still be preserved safe." For Tertullian the identity is required by justice — it would be "unjust…for one substance to do the work, and another to reap the reward" — and so "the flesh shall rise again, wholly in every man, in its own identity, in its absolute integrity."5

Origen (c. 230)

Origen, in On First Principles and Against Celsus, sets out a third-century alternative to the same-flesh reading. What rises is genuinely a body — “What is that which died? Was it not a body? It is of the body, then, that there will be a resurrection” — but a spiritual body “educed” out of the animal one, not the same corpse resuscitated. He lodges its identity in a persisting bodily principle, a “germ…which is always safe in the substance of the body” that raises the scattered matter “as the power which is in the grain of wheat…restores the grain into a body having stalk and ear.” He rejects both the pagan charge that Christians expect “the same bodies, without a change to a higher condition” and what he calls the “low and abject” view of simpler believers — the dead rise “after casting away corruption.” His fullest name for the persisting principle, the eidos or form, survives chiefly through his critics; whether this amounts to denying “the resurrection of the flesh” is a disputed reading, set out on its own page.6

Methodius of Olympus (c. 311)

Methodius, in his dialogue On the Resurrection (the Aglaophon), writes the earliest extant sustained rebuttal of Origen's resurrection teaching and restates the identity of the same flesh. He “cannot endure the trifling of some who…do violence to Scripture” to make “the resurrection… without flesh…by allegorizing”; the “coats of skins” signify mortality, not the body; and man, “composed of soul and body,” must rise as the composite. Like Irenaeus he reads “flesh and blood cannot inherit” as the carnal impulse, not the substance of the flesh, citing Justin for the same. Against Origen's form he presses that “there is no resurrection of the form without the flesh” — the form is a quality of the flesh and cannot outlast it, “for the shape which disappears around the brass is separated from the melted statue” — and that if a formed soul could appear before Christ (Moses and Elijah did), He could not be “the first begotten of the dead.”7

Cyril of Jerusalem (c. 350)

Cyril of Jerusalem, catechizing baptismal candidates on the Jerusalem creed's article “the resurrection of the flesh,” gives the same-flesh reading its mid-fourth-century catechetical form. “The root of all good works is the hope of the Resurrection,” and “faith…in the Resurrection of the dead, is a great…doctrine of the Holy Catholic Church,” though “Greeks contradict it, Samaritans disbelieve it, heretics mutilate it.” Reading Paul's demonstratives (“this corruptible must put on incorruption”), he states the identity: “this body shall be raised…the very same body, though by putting on incorruption it shall be fashioned anew,—as iron blending with fire becomes fire” — the same flesh remade, not replaced (“thou wilt be raised…to be judged with this body… Heed not those who say that this body is not raised”). He answers the scattered-body objection from God's power — the God “who holds the whole earth in the hollow of His hands” can gather a body scattered to India, Persia, and the Goths as a man separates mixed seeds in his hand — and grounds the hope in a train of nature-analogies (the dying and rising grain of 1 Cor. 15, the winter trees, the waning and filling moon, the Phoenix).8

Ambrose of Milan (c. 378)

Ambrose, in the funeral oration for his brother Satyrus (De excessu fratris II, circulated as On the Belief in the Resurrection), gives the same-flesh hope a fourth-century Latin statement. He rests it on “three grounds… reason, analogy from universal example, and the evidence of what has happened, since many have risen”: reason, because the body acted with the soul and so must rise to be judged; analogy, from a nature that everywhere dies and rises; and evidence, from the Gospel raisings. The risen flesh is the very same substance — “we shall be the same persons as we were,” “flesh…restored from flesh, bone from bone, blood from blood” — and he reads the resurrection out of a long train of nature-analogies (the turning seasons, the sown seed and vine of 1 Cor. 15, the Phoenix of Arabia). He meets the scattered-body objection — bodies rotted, dispersed, swallowed by the sea or torn by beasts — from God's creating power, “as though it were not a greater wonder to give life to clay than to join it together,” and grounds the whole in Christ, “the firstfruits of them that rest,” whose rising is “a pledge of that future resurrection… rather itself truth than a pattern.”9

Gregory of Nyssa (c. 380)

Gregory of Nyssa, in the deathbed dialogue with his sister Macrina (On the Soul and the Resurrection), defines the resurrection as "the reconstitution of our nature in its original form" — the Edenic pattern, not the body as it lay dying. On the matter he speaks of "the identical atoms (belonging to each soul) reassembling together in the same order as before," the simple soul never wholly losing its bond with its own scattered dust. He presses the flux objection himself — the living body is a flame "always passing itself along," so a resurrection of "the same" body would raise "a crowd" (infant, youth, and old man at once) — and answers it with Paul's seed: the grain must die to rise as the ear, "neither entirely the same thing as that seed nor something altogether different." What is stripped away is the "coats of skins," the whole apparatus of mortal animal life added after the fall.10

Jerome (c. 399)

Jerome, in To Pammachius Against John of Jerusalem (c. 397) and Letter 84 (c. 400) — both weapons of the Origenist controversy — argues at length, in Latin, for the resurrection of this same flesh. His wedge is the word itself: the Origenists will confess “the resurrection of the body” but never “of the flesh,” and “all flesh is body, but not every body is flesh” — so that, he charges, the vaguer word admits an ethereal body under an orthodox-sounding confession. Against it he presses the return of the very members: “after the resurrection we shall have the same members which we now use, the same flesh and blood and bones,” for Scripture condemns not their substance but their works. Because the members return, so does sex — “John is John, Mary is Mary” — and even the organs of generation, though “they are to have no office” (the reward of chastity presupposes a body that could have sinned). His proof-text is Job's “in my flesh shall see God… and not another,” capped by the reductio that a resurrection keeping only the name is “as if you were to order a ship to be restored after shipwreck, and then were to refuse each particular thing of which a ship is made”; and he reads “flesh and blood cannot inherit” (1 Cor 15:50) as the carnal condition that is changed, “its change, not its destruction.”11

The Apostles' Creed — "the resurrection of this flesh" (Rufinus, c. 404)

The article of the creed. The older Western baptismal creed confesses not "the resurrection of the dead" (the Nicene ἀνάστασιν νεκρῶν) but "the resurrection of the flesh" (carnis resurrectionem), and Rufinus of Aquileia's Commentary on the Apostles' Creed is an early full exposition of why. Rufinus sets the word against those who deny it: "Valentinus altogether denies the resurrection of the flesh, so do the Manicheans." Rufinus's own church sharpened it further, to "the resurrection of this flesh": the confessor signs his own forehead as he says the word, confessing the rising of the very body he stands in, "not a confused or foreign body but its own which it had when alive." The dispersed particles, held by "an immortal principle" because it is "the flesh of an immortal soul," are gathered and "restored to that form which death had formerly dissolved."12

Augustine of Hippo (c. 426)

Augustine, in the last book of The City of God (Book 22), restates the identity-of-substance reading and presses it into a series of concrete questions on the character of the risen body. What rises is the very same flesh, "transformed from the oldness of the animal body into the newness of the spiritual body … but still flesh, not spirit"; God can "recall all the portions which have been consumed by beasts or fire, or … evaporated into the air," so even eaten flesh reverts to its first owner "like a pecuniary loan." All rise at the prime of life — the age of Christ, "about … thirty"; both sexes are kept, for "the sex of woman is not a vice, but nature," and "He…who created both sexes will restore both"; every defect and monstrous deformity is healed while no substance is lost (hair and nails redistributed, not restored to ugliness); and the martyrs' scars alone remain, "not … a deformity, but a mark of honor," on the pattern of Christ's wounds. Throughout he flags where he speaks by conjecture — of the aborted, "I make bold neither to affirm nor to deny."13

John of Damascus (c. 743)

John of Damascus, in An Exact Exposition of the Orthodox Faith IV.27 ("Concerning the Resurrection"), summarizes the earlier Greek patristic teaching. Resurrection is by definition "the re-union of soul and body," and it is "this very body, which is corruptible and liable to dissolution, that will rise again incorruptible" — the same flesh remade, proved by Lazarus ("not another body but the very one that was corrupt") and by the palpable risen Christ ("a spirit hath not flesh or bones, as ye see Me have"). He answers the dispersal objection as Athenagoras and Cyril did — from the Creator's power and Paul's buried seed — and rejects a change “into another form”: the change of Philippians 3:21 is "not…change into another form (God forbid!), but…from corruption into incorruption."14

The medieval councils define the resurrection “with their own bodies” (Fourth Lateran, 1215; Second Lyons, 1274; Benedictus Deus, 1336)

The high-medieval councils and a papal constitution confess the bodily resurrection for the Latin Church; Lateran IV defines the identity of the risen body with the present one, while Lyons II and Benedictus Deus confess that all appear “with their bodies” at the judgment. The Fourth Lateran Council (1215), in the confession Firmiter credimus that opened it against the Cathar dualists, defined that Christ will come to judge the living and the dead and render to each according to his works, “All of them will rise with their own bodies, which they now wear” (qui omnes cum suis propriis resurgent corporibus quae nunc gestant) — defining as an article of faith the identity of the risen body with this present flesh. The Second Council of Lyons (1274), in the profession of faith the emperor Michael VIII Palaeologus signed for the union of Greeks and Latins proclaimed at the council (undone at Constantinople by his son Andronicus II after Michael's death in 1282), confessed that “on the day of judgment all men will be brought together with their bodies before the tribunal of Christ to render an account of their own deeds.” And Benedict XII's Benedictus Deus (1336) fixed the sequence — the souls of the blessed “see the divine essence” immediately after death, before they take up their bodies again, yet “on the day of judgment all men will appear with their bodies before the judgment seat of Christ” — so the beatific vision of the interval precedes, but does not replace, the bodily resurrection.15

Thomas Aquinas (c. 1272)

Aquinas, in the Summa contra Gentiles (IV.79–89) and the Summa Theologiae Supplement (qq. 75–86), affirms in scholastic form that the risen body is numerically identical — "we cannot call it resurrection unless the soul return to the same body… if it be not the same body which the soul resumes, it will not be a resurrection, but rather the assuming of a new body" — but its identity is secured by the returning soul, not by retained matter. On the flux problem raised by earlier writers he argues that a living body's matter is already in constant flux ("An example of this can be taken from fire… it is called numerically one because its species persists, yet wood is consumed and new wood is applied"), yet the man stays one; so the rational soul, the body's substantial form, is what makes it the same, and God need gather only matter enough "to complete the quantity due." To this he adds the four dotes of the glorified body — impassibility, subtlety, agility, and clarity — insisting all the while that "subtle" and "spiritual" never mean the flesh is thinned into spirit (the risen Christ "was palpable," Luke 24:39), and he takes over from Augustine the resurrection age (about thirty) and the removal of every defect.16

John Calvin (c. 1559)

John Calvin, in the final (1559) Latin Institutes III.25 ("Of the Last Resurrection"), states the same-flesh hope for the Reformed churches. Reading Paul's demonstratives, he lays a finger on the present body: "this corruptible must put on incorruption," so a change of quality in this flesh, not a substitute, is what is promised — "he very plainly affirms that no new bodies are to be fabricated." He rejects as "equally monstrous" the error "of those who imagine that the soul, instead of resuming the body with which it is now clothed, will obtain a new and different body" (against the ancient Manichees and the spiritualist deniers of his own day). He argues from Christ's own body: our rising mirrors his, and "the mortal body which he had formerly carried he again received; for it would not have availed us much if a new body had been substituted, and that which had been offered in expiatory sacrifice been destroyed."17

The Reformed confessions on “this my flesh” and “the selfsame bodies” (Heidelberg Catechism, 1563; Westminster Confession, 1646)

The Reformed confessions state the same-flesh reading, which Calvin had set out, for catechesis and church order. The Heidelberg Catechism (1563), expounding the creed's "resurrection of the body," confesses that "not only shall my soul after this life immediately be taken up to Christ, my Head, but also this my flesh, raised by the power of Christ, shall be reunited with my soul and made like Christ's glorious body" (Q&A 57). The Westminster Confession (1646) states numerical identity: "all the dead shall be raised up, with the selfsame bodies, and none other (although with different qualities), which shall be united again to their souls forever" (ch. 32.2), its parenthesis distinguishing unchanged substance from glorified quality.18

Oscar Cullmann (c. 1955)

Oscar Cullmann, in the Ingersoll Lecture Immortality of the Soul or Resurrection of the Dead? The Witness of the New Testament (Harvard, 1955; published 1958), argues that the New Testament hope of resurrection is something wholly different from, and incompatible with, the Greek immortality of the soul. His opening image fixes the contrast — "Like Jesus, Socrates has his disciples about him on the day of his death; but he discourses serenely with them on immortality. Jesus, a few hours before His death, trembles and begs His disciples not to leave Him alone." On Cullmann's reading, Socrates can be calm because for the Greek "death is the soul's great friend," the release of the immortal soul from the body's prison; Jesus recoils because for the New Testament death is "the last enemy" (1 Cor 15:26), a real horror abolished only by God's act. So "belief in the immortality of the soul is not belief in a revolutionary event," whereas "resurrection is a positive assertion: the whole man, who has really died, is recalled to life by a new act of creation by God" — the risen body a "spiritual body" animated by the Holy Spirit, not an immortal soul the man always possessed.19

N. T. Wright (2003, 2008)

N. T. Wright, in The Resurrection of the Son of God (2003) and Surprised by Hope (2008), argues for the bodily hope against what he describes as a modern disembodied piety of "going to heaven when you die." "Resurrection" in the ancient world, he argues, always meant new bodily life — "something that everybody knows doesn't happen" — which the whole pagan world denied; the Christian claim was its reversal, not the survival of the soul. Heaven, for Wright, is a "temporary resting place," not the final destiny, which is "to be bodily raised into the transformed, glorious likeness of Jesus Christ" in a renewed creation. And Paul's "spiritual body" (sōma pneumatikon) he reads as a body animated by the Spirit, not made of spirit: the –ikos adjective is "not about the substance of which the body is composed, but about the driving force that animates it" — "the difference between… a ship made of steel or timber, and a ship powered by sail or steam."20
