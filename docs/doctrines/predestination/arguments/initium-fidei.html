The initium fidei — can the first movement toward faith be the will’s own?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: weak — the reading is coherent and genuinely not Pelagian, but on its best case the strong-form claim (a first assent that arises from the unaided will) founders on 1 Corinthians 4:7 and Philippians 2:13 and stands condemned by the one council that ruled on it (Orange, 529); the church’s Augustinian answer — that even the beginning of faith is grace — holds. · advanced by John Cassian

The claim

…does God have compassion upon us because we have shown the beginning of a good will, or does the beginning of a good will follow because God has had compassion upon us?… if we say that the beginning of free will is in our own power, what about Paul the persecutor, what about Matthew the publican…? But if we say that the beginning of our free will is always due to the inspiration of the grace of God, what about the faith of Zaccheus, or… the goodness of the thief on the cross, who by their own desires brought violence to bear on the kingdom of heaven…?

— John Cassian, Conferences XIII.11, c. 426, link.1 The advocated interpretation: grace is necessary to complete and sustain salvation, but the initium fidei — the first assent, the initial turning to God — can sometimes be the will’s own act, which God’s grace then meets and rewards. It is the “both-and” the sixteenth century would label semi-Pelagian; Cassian’s own word is that grace and free will, though they “seem opposed,… really are in harmony.”

Who advances it

The classic statement is John Cassian’s thirteenth Conference (put in the mouth of Abbot Chaeremon), the manifesto of the monks of Marseilles (the Massilienses) whom Prosper of Aquitaine answered. It is not the Pelagian thesis: Cassian affirms the Fall, insists nothing good is done without God’s help, and holds that the very “seeds of goodness” in nature are God’s own implanting. His quarrel with Augustine is narrow and single: whether the first movement of a good will is always God’s prior gift. On that one hinge he answers “not always.” The same instinct — that saving faith must be, at its root, a free human response God foresees rather than a gift He first works — recurs downstream in Molina’s scientia media and in the Remonstrant conditional election weighed on the main timeline, though those later forms take care to keep grace prevenient in a way the bare Massilian claim did not.

The evidence for

The Scriptural data really does cut both ways. This is the argument’s genuine strength, and it is descriptive, not speculative. Cassian’s paired examples are exact: Paul is thrown down and drawn while he is “yet breathing out threatenings” (grace plainly precedes the will), and Matthew is called from the tax-booth; yet Zacchaeus climbs the sycamore and the thief on the cross turns to Christ by what reads, on the surface of the text, as their own longing (the will seems to reach first).1 Scripture narrates both patterns; a theology that flattens every conversion into the “grace-precedes” mould has to explain away the second set. To this Cassian adds two supports that keep the position off the Pelagian rocks: God “increas[es] that which He Himself implanted or which He sees to have arisen from our own efforts,” and there are “by nature some seeds of goodness in every soul implanted by the kindness of the Creator” which nonetheless cannot ripen “unless… quickened by the assistance of God.”2 The reading is therefore internally coherent: it preserves grace as necessary throughout while declining to make the very first stirring a divine work in every case. Nothing in it requires denying original sin, and its pastoral motive — that exhortation, prayer, and effort are not idle theatre — is serious.

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The descriptive point stands: Scripture does present conversions of both shapes, and the distinction between “the will contributes something” (a real synergist position, held in various forms East and West) and “Pelagius was right about nature” (a distinct, condemned claim) is a distinction the argument correctly insists on. Cassian is not a Pelagian, and reading him as one — as some of his opponents did — is a caricature. So the weak verdict below is a verdict on the strong-form thesis, not a charge of heresy against the whole synergist family.

What is overstated or imprecise. The argument’s decisive weakness is exegetical, and it is one the Massilians could soften but never answer. Augustine says he was “convinced” out of the very position Cassian defends by 1 Corinthians 4:7 — “What hast thou that thou didst not receive?”: if faith is itself among the gifts of God, then it cannot be the one thing a man brings to grace before grace, for a first assent produced by the unaided will would be precisely a thing not received. Paired with Philippians 2:13 — “it is God which worketh in you both to will and to do” — the texts reach behind the act to the willing itself, which is exactly where the Massilian claim locates the human contribution.3 Cassian’s counter-examples do not rescue the thesis, because they prove too little: that Zacchaeus and the thief appear to move first is a fact about the narrative surface, not about the hidden order of grace — the Augustinian can grant every scene and still hold that the longing on display was itself already grace-given (Christ, on the Augustinian reading, sees Zacchaeus before Zacchaeus sees Him). The examples establish that grace need not always be felt as prior; they do not establish that it is ever ontologically posterior, which is the actual claim. Prosper pressed exactly this: faith is God’s gift “without whose grace no one runs to grace,” so that “from the beginning right to the end… the inception and the perfection of the good work is attributed to the Lord.”4

Does Orange settle it, or merely rule ecclesially? Both, and the distinction matters. The Second Council of Orange (529), confirmed by Pope Boniface II, made the rejection of the initium fidei-from-the-will the settled teaching of the Latin West: Canon 5 anathematizes anyone who holds that “the increase of faith… and the beginning of belief and the very desire of credulity… belong to us naturally and not by a gift of grace,” and Canon 4 that God does not wait upon a will we cleanse first.5 As an ecclesial act this is decisive only for those who grant Orange’s authority — and the Christian East, which never received it, holds a synergism of its own. But Orange is not merely a fiat: it is the West codifying the exegetical result that the strong-form claim could not carry the proof-texts, and the two later traditions that reopened everything else — the Reformed at Dort and Tridentine Catholicism — both received precisely this point, that the beginning of faith is grace. That convergence, across parties that agreed on little else, is why the verdict is weak rather than merely contested. Note too that even Eastern synergism characteristically keeps grace prevenient and co-present rather than asserting an unaided first movement; the bare Massilian form Orange condemned is stronger than what most of its later sympathizers actually want to defend.

Assessment

Weak. The interpretation — that the first movement toward faith can be the will’s own, with grace meeting it — is coherent, sincerely non-Pelagian, and anchored in a real observation that Scripture narrates conversions of two shapes. But on its best available case it does not hold: its decisive proof-texts run the other way (1 Cor 4:7 makes faith a thing received; Phil 2:13 puts God behind the willing), its counter-examples show only that grace need not be felt as prior, not that it is ever posterior, and the one council that adjudicated it condemned it — a judgement the otherwise-divided Reformed and Catholic traditions both kept. Keep two things distinct on the proponent’s page: Cassian’s narrow “not always” on the initium is what fails, and it fails as a less-probable reading of the order of grace, not as the Pelagian error he is often carelessly lumped with; his affirmation that grace is necessary throughout, and that nature’s “seeds” are God’s own, is sound and survives the verdict.
