Erasmus on predestination, grace & free will

Dates: c. 1524 (De libero arbitrio διατριβή sive collatio — On the Freedom of the Will: A Diatribe or Discourse, September 1524; Desiderius Erasmus of Rotterdam, c. 1466–1536, Dutch Catholic humanist and biblical scholar) · Wikipedia
Summary: The prince of the northern humanists, provoked at last into print against Luther. Pressed by Rome and by his own friends to break with the Wittenberg reformer, Erasmus chose the one doctrine he judged both central and defensible — the freedom of the will. His Diatribe (September 1524) is not a systematic treatise but, by design, a discourse: a survey of the Scriptures that “seem to set forth free choice” and of those that “seem to take it wholly away,” weighing them with deliberate moderation and a preference for the settled mind of the Church over any single teacher’s novelty. Against Luther he defends a real cooperating freedom — a will, damaged by the Fall but “not extinguished,” that grace heals and to which grace is joined, so that God and man work together toward salvation, grace as the leader. He is no Pelagian: he grants that fallen man can do nothing saving without grace, and attributes most to grace and little to the will. His quarrel is narrower, and older — the same hinge John Cassian would not close: whether the will contributes anything of its own. Luther answered within the year with De servo arbitrio, judging this the one adversary who had gone for the throat of the whole dispute.

Claims & evidence

The definition: free choice is a power to apply oneself toward salvation or turn away

By free choice in this place we mean a power of the human will by which a man can apply himself to the things which lead to eternal salvation, or turn away from them.

— On the Freedom of the Will (1524), trans. E. Gordon Rupp & Philip Watson, source.1 Everything turns on this definition. Erasmus does not claim the will is autonomous or unaided; he claims it is a genuine power, a real second cause that can “apply itself” to grace or refuse it. That the will can turn either way — toward salvation or from it — is precisely what Luther’s bondage-of-the-will denies: for Luther the unregenerate will can only turn away, and grace does not meet a choice but creates one.

The will is damaged by the Fall but not destroyed

For although free choice is damaged by sin, it is nevertheless not extinguished by it. And although it has become so lame in the process that before we receive grace we are more readily inclined toward evil than good, yet it is not altogether cut out.

— On the Freedom of the Will (1524), trans. Rupp & Watson, source.2 This is the exact middle ground the West had staked out at Orange (529) — and the edge Luther means to erase. Erasmus concedes total inability for the saving good apart from grace (so he is no Pelagian), but insists the Fall left the faculty of choice bent, not amputated. On his account grace restores the will’s freedom rather than replacing the will; against Augustine’s sharpest heirs he will not say the first movement toward God is always grace working the will from outside.

His real target: the denial that the will contributes anything, and the doctrine of “sheer necessity”

Hardest of all seems the view of all those who say that free choice is a mere empty name, nor does it avail either in the case of the angels or in Adam or in us, either before or after grace, but it is God who works evil as well as good in us, and all things that happen come about by sheer necessity. My dispute will be most concerned with the two last positions.

— On the Freedom of the Will (1524), trans. Rupp & Watson, source.3 Erasmus names the position he is writing against: that “free choice is a mere empty name” and that everything happens “by sheer necessity” — the reading he takes Luther’s Assertio to have advanced. His whole method follows from this. Because he thinks the Scriptures cut both ways and the question is genuinely obscure, he prefers to “not contend too superstitiously about things of this kind, particularly before the multitude,” fearing the loophole a bare doctrine of necessity “would open to godlessness among innumerable people.” It is this professed willingness to leave the matter undecided — to “take refuge in the opinion of the Skeptics” where Scripture and the Church permit — that Luther seized on and made the opening move of his reply.

Notes

  • Why this is the hinge, not a footnote. Erasmus deliberately narrowed the whole Reformation quarrel to a single doctrine. Luther returned the compliment (see his detail page): unlike the critics who wearied him with “the papacy, purgatory, indulgences, and such like trifles,” Erasmus “alone” had attacked “the real issue, the essence of the matter in dispute.” Both men agreed that grace-and-free-will, not the visible abuses, was the true fault line — which is why the exchange belongs on this timeline between the medieval controversies and the early-modern settlements at Trent and Dort.
  • Not Pelagius, and not quite Cassian. Erasmus attributes most to grace and grants that without grace the will is “not effectively free to do good”; he is closer to the cautious synergism the East never abandoned than to the British monk’s possibilitas. His position is the humanist restatement of the “both-and” that John Cassian voiced and Orange trimmed on one side only — whether the will’s first turn toward God can be its own. Whether that initium fidei reading holds is weighed on the argument page The initium fidei — can the first movement toward faith be the will’s own? (assessed weak).
  • The sequel. Luther’s De servo arbitrio (1525) answered the Diatribe phrase by phrase; Erasmus replied with the two-part Hyperaspistes (1526–27), to which Luther did not respond. The “Erasmian” defense of cooperating freedom fed the later Arminian and Tridentine reaction against an unqualified bondage of the will.
  • Erasmus appears on this wiki also as a witness on the divorce timeline, where his Annotationes reopened divorce-with-remarriage in the Latin West.
