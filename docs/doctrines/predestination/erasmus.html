Erasmus on predestination, grace & free will

Dates: c. 1524 (De libero arbitrio διατριβή sive collatio — On the Freedom of the Will: A Diatribe or Discourse, September 1524; Desiderius Erasmus of Rotterdam, c. 1466–1536, Dutch Catholic humanist and biblical scholar) · Wikipedia
Summary: Erasmus, the Dutch humanist scholar and editor of the Greek New Testament, wrote against Luther on the freedom of the will. His Diatribe (September 1524) is, by its own title, a discourse: a survey of the Scriptures that “seem to set forth free choice” and of those that “seem to take it wholly away.” He defends a will damaged by the Fall but “not extinguished,” which grace heals and with which grace works toward salvation, grace leading. Of those who “attribute most of all to grace and practically nothing to free choice, yet do not entirely remove it,” he writes that their view “seems probably enough.” Luther answered the next year with De servo arbitrio, in which he thanks Erasmus for attacking “the real issue.”

Claims & evidence

Erasmus’s definition of free choice

By free choice in this place we mean a power of the human will by which a man can apply himself to the things which lead to eternal salvation, or turn away from them.

— On the Freedom of the Will (1524), trans. E. Gordon Rupp & Philip Watson, source.1 Erasmus defines free choice as a power of the will by which a man can apply himself to what leads to salvation or turn away from it. Luther’s reply denies the unregenerate will such a power toward God (see Luther).

The will after the Fall: “damaged” but “not extinguished”

For although free choice is damaged by sin, it is nevertheless not extinguished by it. And although it has become so lame in the process that before we receive grace we are more readily inclined toward evil than good, yet it is not altogether cut out.

— On the Freedom of the Will (1524), trans. Rupp & Watson, source.2 On Erasmus’s account the faculty of choice remains after the Fall, though before grace it is “more readily inclined toward evil than good,” and grace restores its freedom.

The positions Erasmus names as his target

Hardest of all seems the view of all those who say that free choice is a mere empty name, nor does it avail either in the case of the angels or in Adam or in us, either before or after grace, but it is God who works evil as well as good in us, and all things that happen come about by sheer necessity. My dispute will be most concerned with the two last positions.

— On the Freedom of the Will (1524), trans. Rupp & Watson, source.3 Erasmus names the position he is writing against: that “free choice is a mere empty name” and that everything happens “by sheer necessity” — the reading he takes Luther’s Assertio to have advanced. Holding that the Scriptures on the question are obscure, he prefers to “not contend too superstitiously about things of this kind, particularly before the multitude,” asking, of an opinion that God works good and evil in us, “what a window to impiety would the public avowal of such an opinion open to countless mortals!”4 Luther opened his reply by answering Erasmus’s readiness to “take refuge in the opinion of the Skeptics” where Scripture and the Church permit.

Reception and debate

  • Luther on Erasmus’s choice of subject. In closing De servo arbitrio Luther wrote that, unlike the critics who wearied him with “the papacy, purgatory, indulgences, and such like trifles,” Erasmus “alone” had attacked “the real issue, the essence of the matter in dispute” (see his detail page).
  • Grace and the will in the Diatribe. Of the view of those who “attribute most of all to grace and practically nothing to free choice, yet do not entirely remove it,” Erasmus writes that it “seems probably enough in that it leaves man to study and strive, but it does not leave aught for him to ascribe to his own powers.” In the same survey he reports Pelagius’s teaching that “man owed his salvation to God, without whose grace the will of man was not effectively free to do good.”5 The question of whether the will’s first turn toward God can be its own was argued earlier between John Cassian and Prosper of Aquitaine, and addressed in the canons of Orange (529).
  • The sequel. Luther’s De servo arbitrio (1525) answered the Diatribe phrase by phrase; Erasmus replied with the two-part Hyperaspistes (1526–27), to which Luther did not respond.
  • Erasmus appears on this wiki also as a witness on the divorce timeline, where his Annotationes discussed divorce with remarriage.
