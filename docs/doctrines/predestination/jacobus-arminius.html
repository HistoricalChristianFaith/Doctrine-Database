Jacobus Arminius, the Remonstrants & the Synod of Dort

Dates: 1608–1610 (Arminius’s Declaration and the Remonstrance) / 1618–1619 (the Synod of Dort) · Jacobus Arminius 1560–1609 · Wikipedia
Summary: In the Dutch Reformed church a dispute over election arose between Jacobus Arminius — pastor at Amsterdam, then professor at Leiden from 1603 — and his Leiden colleague Franciscus Gomarus. In his Declaration of Sentiments (1608) Arminius set out four divine decrees, of which the decree to save particular persons rests on God’s foreknowledge of who would believe and persevere. He died in 1609; his followers set out their position in the Remonstrance of 1610, whose five articles taught election of those who believe and persevere, Christ’s death for all, man’s inability to do good apart from grace, and grace that is “not irresistible,” and left the perseverance of the regenerate for further study of Scripture. The Synod of Dort (1618–19), with delegates from Reformed churches abroad, answered in its Canons, teaching among other things that election “was not founded upon foreseen faith.” This page records Arminius, the Remonstrance, and Dort as witnesses on the timeline.

Claims & evidence

Arminius’s fourth decree: the election of particular persons and foreknowledge

God decreed to save and damn certain particular persons. This decree has its foundation in the foreknowledge of God, by which he knew from all eternity those individuals who would, through his preventing grace, believe, and, through his subsequent grace would persevere, according to the before described administration of those means which are suitable and proper for conversion and faith…

— Jacobus Arminius, Declaration of Sentiments (1608), the fourth of his four decrees of predestination, trans. James Nichols (Works, vol. 1), source.1 Arminius’s first decree is to appoint Christ as Mediator; his second, to receive into favour “those who repent and believe… and persevere”; his third, to supply sufficient and efficacious means of grace; and only his fourth touches individuals — and it is “founded… in the foreknowledge of God.” In this order the election of persons is the last decree and follows God’s foresight of faith. Arminius also held grace to be resistible: he describes the scriptures’ representations of grace as “such as describe it capable of ‘being resisted, (Acts, 7:51,) and received in vain;’ (2 Corinthians 6:1,) and that it is possible for man to avoid yielding his assent to it.”2

The Remonstrance of 1610

That God, by an eternal and unchangeable purpose in Jesus Christ his Son before the foundation of the world, has determined… to save in Christ… those who through the grace of the Holy Spirit shall believe on this his son Jesus, and shall persevere in this faith and obedience of faith, through this grace, even to the end; and, on the other hand, to leave the incorrigible and unbelieving in sin and under wrath…

— The Remonstrance of 1610, Article 1 (conditional election), trans. Philip Schaff, source.3 The five articles were drawn up by Arminius’s followers the year after his death. Article 3 states man’s inability apart from grace — fallen man “can of and by himself neither think, will, nor do any thing that is truly good” — and Article 4 makes grace “the beginning, continuance, and accomplishment of all good.” The same Article 4 adds: “as respects the mode of the operation of this grace, it is not irresistible, since it is written concerning many, that they have resisted the Holy Spirit.” Article 5 leaves the perseverance question open — whether the truly regenerate can “forsake again… their life in Christ… must be more particularly determined out of the Holy Scripture, before we ourselves can teach it with the full confidence of our mind.”4

The Synod of Dort on election (First Head of Doctrine)

Election is the unchangeable purpose of God, whereby, before the foundation of the world, He has out of mere grace, according to the sovereign good pleasure of His own will, chosen from the whole human race… a certain number of persons to redemption in Christ…

This election was not founded upon foreseen faith and the obedience of faith, holiness, or any other good quality or disposition in man, as the prerequisite, cause, or condition on which it depended; but men are chosen to faith and to the obedience of faith, holiness, etc.

— Canons of the Synod of Dort (1618–19), First Head of Doctrine, Articles 7 and 9, source.5 Article 9 states that election is not “founded upon foreseen faith… as the… condition on which it depended,” but that men are “chosen to faith,” so that faith is the fruit of election and not its ground. The synod set this within its account of reprobation (Article 15: some are “passed by in the eternal election of God” and left “in the common misery,” God remaining thereby “an… irreprehensible, and righteous Judge,” not “the Author of sin”), and treated the remaining Remonstrant articles under its further heads of doctrine.

Notes

  • Election and foreseen faith. The question argued between the Remonstrants and Dort — whether God elects those He foresees will believe, or elects them in order that they may believe — is the one Augustine addressed in On the Predestination of the Saints (c. 429), where he takes the second view.
  • The “TULIP” acronym. The Canons are arranged in five “Heads of Doctrine” (the third and fourth combined), answering the Remonstrants’ five articles one by one. The Wikipedia article on the Five Points of Calvinism reports that the acrostic TULIP (Total depravity · Unconditional election · Limited atonement · Irresistible grace · Perseverance of the saints) “was used by Cleland Boyd McAfee as early as circa 1905,” that an early printed appearance is in Loraine Boettner’s 1932 The Reformed Doctrine of Predestination, and that “phrases like ‘total depravity’ cannot be found in the Canons of Dort.”6
  • The political aftermath. The quarrel was entangled with Dutch politics: the Arminians sided with the statesman Johan van Oldenbarnevelt, the Gomarists with the stadtholder Maurice of Nassau. When Dort condemned the Remonstrants it also deposed their ministers; Oldenbarnevelt was tried and executed on 13 May 1619, days after the synod closed. Arminius had died in 1609.7
  • Reception and debate. Whether Arminius’s conditional predestination is “semi-Pelagian” is disputed. Two readings are attributed below, in date order.
    • A theology of prevenient grace. Roger E. Olson (Arminian, “Prevenient Grace: Why It Matters,” 2012) writes that “Arminius and his faithful followers (Episcopius, Wesley, Fletcher, Watson, Summers, Pope, Miley, Wiley, et al.) adamantly rejected semi-Pelagianism and all, to a person, affirmed the necessity of supernatural grace for the first exercise of a good will toward God,” a case he says he documents in Arminian Theology: Myths and Realities.8 Keith D. Stanglin and Thomas H. McCall (Jacob Arminius: Theologian of Grace, 2012), as reported by their reviewer W. Robert Godfrey, call Arminius a “theologian of grace” and a “modified Thomist,” and show his use of Molina’s middle knowledge in his account of predestination (⚠ unverified beyond the review).9
    • Semi-Pelagian at the Augustinian end. W. Robert Godfrey (Reformed, reviewing Stanglin and McCall in reformation21, 2014) grants that the book shows Arminius taught that “man is completely lost in sin and that grace is absolutely necessary as the efficient cause of regeneration,” and calls the result “a very Augustinian Arminianism”; because Arminius holds that grace “can be resisted and lost by the regenerate,” he concludes: “Arminius is semi-Pelagian, but towards the Augustinian end of that spectrum.”10
