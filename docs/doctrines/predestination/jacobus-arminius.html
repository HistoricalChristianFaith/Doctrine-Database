Jacobus Arminius, the Remonstrants & the Synod of Dort

Dates: 1608–1610 (Arminius’s Declaration and the Remonstrance) / 1618–1619 (the Synod of Dort) · Jacobus Arminius 1560–1609 · Wikipedia
Summary: Within the Reformed world the same fault line that ran between Molinist and Thomist in the Catholic schools reopened between Jacobus Arminius — pastor at Amsterdam, then professor at Leiden from 1603 — and the strict Calvinists led by Franciscus Gomarus. Arminius rejected the supralapsarian election he found taught around him and reordered the divine decrees so that God’s decree to save particular persons rests on His foreknowledge of who would believe and persevere. He died in 1609, before the quarrel came to synod; his followers pressed his case in the Remonstrance of 1610, whose five articles taught conditional election, universal atonement, total inability apart from grace, and — the decisive break — resistible grace, leaving the perseverance of the saints an open question. The Synod of Dort (1618–19), with delegates from across the Reformed churches, answered with the “five points”: unconditional election “not on the basis of foreseen faith,” limited atonement, total depravity, irresistible grace, and the perseverance of the saints — Augustine’s election-before-merit carried, on the Protestant side, to its systematic end. Whether election rests on foreseen faith is weighed on its own argument page; this page records Arminius, the Remonstrance, and Dort as a witness on the timeline.

Claims & evidence

Arminius: the decree to save particular persons rests on foreknowledge of their faith

God decreed to save and damn certain particular persons. This decree has its foundation in the foreknowledge of God, by which he knew from all eternity those individuals who would, through his preventing grace, believe, and, through his subsequent grace would persevere, according to the before described administration of those means which are suitable and proper for conversion and faith…

— Jacobus Arminius, Declaration of Sentiments (1608), the fourth of his four decrees of predestination, trans. James Nichols (Works, vol. 1), source.1 The reordering is the whole point. Arminius’s first decree is to appoint Christ as Mediator; his second, to receive into favour “those who repent and believe… and persevere”; his third, to supply sufficient and efficacious means of grace; and only his fourth touches individuals — and it is “founded… in the foreknowledge of God.” Election of persons is thus the last decree, not the first, and it follows God’s foresight of faith rather than grounding it. Against the Calvinist scheme Arminius held grace to be resistible — men are able “of avoid yielding” their assent, for the Spirit is “resisted, (Acts, 7:51,) and received in vain.”2

The Remonstrance of 1610: conditional election and resistible grace

That God, by an eternal and unchangeable purpose in Jesus Christ his Son before the foundation of the world, has determined… to save in Christ… those who through the grace of the Holy Spirit shall believe on this his son Jesus, and shall persevere in this faith and obedience of faith, through this grace, even to the end; and, on the other hand, to leave the incorrigible and unbelieving in sin and under wrath…

— The Remonstrance of 1610, Article 1 (conditional election), trans. Philip Schaff, source.3 The five articles, drawn up by Arminius’s heirs the year after his death, are no Pelagian revival: Article 3 concedes total inability — fallen man “can of and by himself neither think, will, nor do any thing that is truly good” — and Article 4 makes grace “the beginning, continuance, and accomplishment of all good.” The break comes in the same Article 4: “as respects the mode of the operation of this grace, it is not irresistible, since it is written concerning many, that they have resisted the Holy Spirit.” And Article 5 left the perseverance question deliberately open — whether the truly regenerate can “forsake again… their life in Christ… must be more particularly determined out of the Holy Scripture, before we ourselves can teach it with the full confidence of our mind.”4

The Synod of Dort’s answer: unconditional election, not on foreseen faith

Election is the unchangeable purpose of God, whereby, before the foundation of the world, He has out of mere grace, according to the sovereign good pleasure of His own will, chosen from the whole human race… a certain number of persons to redemption in Christ…

This election was not founded upon foreseen faith and the obedience of faith, holiness, or any other good quality or disposition in man, as the prerequisite, cause, or condition on which it depended; but men are chosen to faith and to the obedience of faith, holiness, etc.

— Canons of the Synod of Dort (1618–19), First Head of Doctrine, Articles 7 and 9, source.5 Article 9 answers Arminius point for point: election is not “founded upon foreseen faith… as the… condition on which it depended,” but men are “chosen to faith.” Faith is election’s fruit, not its ground — the exact reversal of Arminius’s fourth decree. The synod set this within its account of reprobation (Article 15: some are “passed by in the eternal election of God” and left “in the common misery,” God remaining thereby “an… irreprehensible, and righteous Judge,” not “the Author of sin”), and rounded it out with the four further heads that the later English mnemonic “TULIP” would gather up.

Notes

  • Whether the argument holds is weighed elsewhere. The claim that God elects those He foresees will believe (the Remonstrant post praevisa merita) is adjudicated on the argument page Election on foreseen faith — does God choose post praevisa merita? (assessed contested: strong by Eastern and Arminian lights, denied by the Latin convergence of Augustine, Aquinas, and Dort). This detail page records the historical witnesses; the argument page rates the interpretation. The universal-atonement side of the Remonstrance connects to God “wills all to be saved.”
  • “TULIP” is a later label, not Dort’s. The Canons are arranged in five “Heads of Doctrine” (the third and fourth combined), answering the Remonstrants’ five articles one by one; the acronym Total depravity · Unconditional election · Limited atonement · Irresistible grace · Perseverance of the saints is a twentieth-century English mnemonic laid over them, and Dort never uses it.
  • Augustine, carried through. Dort is the Protestant terminus of the line this timeline traces from Augustine through Gottschalk: election-before-merit, efficacious grace, and a particular redemption, now stated as a confession. Its Catholic counterpart, condemning the opposite excess, is Cum occasione’s censure of Jansen a generation later — the two great seventeenth-century church-courts of grace, ruling from opposite sides of the same road Orange had first paved.
  • The political aftermath. The quarrel was entangled with Dutch politics: the Arminians sided with the statesman Johan van Oldenbarnevelt, the Gomarists with the stadtholder Maurice of Nassau. When Dort condemned the Remonstrants it also deposed their ministers; Oldenbarnevelt was tried and executed on 13 May 1619, days after the synod closed. Arminius himself, dead since 1609, saw none of it.6
