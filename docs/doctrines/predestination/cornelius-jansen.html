Cornelius Jansen on predestination, grace & free will

Dates: 1640 (his Augustinus, published posthumously) / 1653 (its condemnation) · Cornelius Jansen (Jansenius), bishop of Ypres, 1585–1638 · Wikipedia
Summary: Cornelius Jansen, a Dutch-born theologian of Louvain, died bishop of Ypres in 1638; his Latin folio on Augustine’s doctrine of grace, the Augustinus, was published posthumously in 1640. The Catholic Encyclopedia summarizes its theses as follows: in fallen nature the will is moved by whichever “delectation” — heavenly grace or earthly concupiscence — is the stronger; grace, when given, is efficacious; and there is “no place for purely sufficient grace.” In 1653 Innocent X’s bull Cum occasione condemned five propositions as drawn from the book. Jansen’s defenders, led by Antoine Arnauld and Port-Royal, accepted the condemnation of the propositions but denied that they were taught in the Augustinus, and the resulting controversy continued until the bull Unigenitus (1713).

Claims & evidence

The Augustinus on the two “delectations,” as summarized in the Catholic Encyclopedia

At once inevitable and irresistible, this delectation, if it come from heaven or from grace, leads man to virtue; if it come from nature or concupiscence, it determines him to sin. In the one case as in the other, the will is fatally swept on by the preponderant impulse. The two delectations, says Jansenius, are like the two arms of a balance, of which the one cannot rise unless the other be lowered and vice versa.

— Cornelius Jansen, Augustinus (1640), as characterized (with the “two arms of a balance” image attributed to Jansen) in the Catholic Encyclopedia, “Jansenius and Jansenism,” source.1 The same article summarizes Jansen’s position in the words “the will is powerless to resist; it has become purely passive,” and “man irresistibly, although voluntarily, does either good or evil, according as he is dominated by grace or by concupiscence,” so that there is “no place for purely sufficient grace.” (These theses are reported from the Catholic Encyclopedia’s article; no English translation of the Latin Augustinus itself is quoted on this page.)

The Five Propositions condemned by Cum occasione (1653)

1. Some of God’s precepts are impossible to the just, who wish and strive to keep them, according to the present powers which they have; the grace, by which they are made possible, is also wanting.
2. In the state of fallen nature one never resists interior grace.
3. In order to merit or demerit in the state of fallen nature, freedom from necessity is not required in man, but freedom from external compulsion is sufficient.
4. The Semipelagians admitted the necessity of a prevenient interior grace for each act, even for the beginning of faith; and in this they were heretics, because they wished this grace to be such that the human will could either resist or obey.
5. It is Semipelagian to say that Christ died or shed His blood for all men without exception.

— Innocent X, bull Cum occasione (31 May 1653), the five propositions of Jansen’s Augustinus, Denzinger §§2001–2007, source.2 The first four were condemned outright (the first as “rash, impious, blasphemous… and heretical,” the next three as “heretical”); the fifth was condemned as “false… and, understood in this sense, that Christ died for the salvation of the predestined [only],… heretical.” The propositions concern interior grace that is not resisted (props. 2, 4), commands impossible to the just for want of grace (prop. 1), a liberty that requires freedom from external compulsion but not from necessity (prop. 3), and the scope of Christ’s death (prop. 5).

Reception and debate

  • Related condemnations on this timeline. The Second Council of Orange (529) taught that the beginning of faith is a gift of grace. In 1607 Paul V permitted the Thomist and Molinist schools each to continue teaching its own view (see Molina and the De Auxiliis controversy); in 1653 Cum occasione condemned the five propositions above.
  • Christ died for all. Proposition 5 condemns calling it “Semipelagian” to say that Christ died for all men. In the ninth century Gottschalk of Orbais had taught that “God did not suffer for the baptized reprobate,” and Hincmar’s chapters at Quiersy (853) affirmed that God wills all to be saved, citing 1 Timothy 2:4 (see Gottschalk of Orbais).
  • Fact versus right. According to the Catholic Encyclopedia, the Jansenists (led after 1643 by Antoine Arnauld, with Port-Royal as their centre, and defended in Pascal’s Provincial Letters of 1656) accepted that the Church could condemn the five propositions as doctrine (a question “of right”) but denied that it could bind consciences to the “fact” that the propositions were taught in Jansen’s book; the article’s author characterizes this distinction as “the denial of the doctrinal power of the Church.” The “formulary” controversy that followed, over signing the condemnation, continued down to the bull Unigenitus (1713).3
  • Pascal’s account of Jansen’s sense. Blaise Pascal, writing for Port-Royal in the Provincial Letters, described “the great dispute” as being “about the sense of Jansenius, the one party saying that they could see nothing in it inconsistent with the sense of St. Augustine and St. Thomas, and the other party asserting that they saw in it an heretical sense which they would not express.” He wrote of “doctors, who are persuaded that Jansenius can bear no other sense than that of efficacious grace,” and set out that sense as “the divine principles of St. Augustine and St. Thomas, according to which it is equally true that we have the power of resisting grace… and that, nevertheless,… ‘God forms within us the motion of our will.’” On the question of fact he wrote: “though it be matter of faith to believe these propositions to be heretical, it will never be matter of faith to hold that they are to be found in the pages of Jansenius.”4
