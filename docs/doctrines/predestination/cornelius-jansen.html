Cornelius Jansen on predestination, grace & free will

Dates: 1640 (his Augustinus, published posthumously) / 1653 (its condemnation) · Cornelius Jansen (Jansenius), bishop of Ypres, 1585–1638 · Wikipedia
Summary: A Dutch-born theologian who spent more than twenty years mining Augustine, Cornelius Jansen died bishop of Ypres in 1638 leaving behind a vast Latin folio, the Augustinus, published by his friends in 1640. In it he revived Augustine’s hardest teaching in its most rigorist form: fallen human nature is so mastered by concupiscence that the will is “purely passive,” swept along by whichever “delectation” — heavenly grace or earthly desire — happens to be the stronger; grace, when God gives it, is intrinsically efficacious and never resisted, so that a merely “sufficient” grace that does not in fact suffice is a fiction; and Christ died for the elect alone. Where Fulgentius and Gottschalk had pressed Augustine hard from within the medieval church, Jansen pressed him past the line Rome would allow: in 1653 Innocent X’s bull Cum occasione condemned five propositions drawn from the book. If Orange (529) fixed the Catholic boundary against the semi-Pelagian softening of grace, Cum occasione fixed the opposite boundary against an over-Augustinian denial of the will — and touched off a century of controversy (Port-Royal, Arnauld, Pascal, the “formulary”) over whether the condemned propositions were really in Jansen’s book at all.

Claims & evidence

The Augustinus: the will is passive, swept by the stronger “delectation”

At once inevitable and irresistible, this delectation, if it come from heaven or from grace, leads man to virtue; if it come from nature or concupiscence, it determines him to sin. In the one case as in the other, the will is fatally swept on by the preponderant impulse. The two delectations, says Jansenius, are like the two arms of a balance, of which the one cannot rise unless the other be lowered and vice versa.

— Cornelius Jansen, Augustinus (1640), as characterized (with the “two arms of a balance” image attributed to Jansen) in the Catholic Encyclopedia, “Jansenius and Jansenism,” source.1 On Jansen’s reading of Augustine, the fallen will is not a scale that weighs and chooses but one that is simply tipped: “the will is powerless to resist; it has become purely passive.” Grace and concupiscence are two competing delights, and the stronger always wins — “man irresistibly, although voluntarily, does either good or evil, according as he is dominated by grace or by concupiscence.” From this it follows, Jansen argued, that there is “no place for purely sufficient grace”: grace either conquers or is not given. (These theses are reported here from the standard reference literature; no English translation of the massive Latin Augustinus itself is quoted directly on this page.)

The Five Propositions condemned by Cum occasione (1653)

1. Some of God’s precepts are impossible to the just, who wish and strive to keep them, according to the present powers which they have; the grace, by which they are made possible, is also wanting.
2. In the state of fallen nature one never resists interior grace.
3. In order to merit or demerit in the state of fallen nature, freedom from necessity is not required in man, but freedom from external compulsion is sufficient.
4. The Semipelagians admitted the necessity of a prevenient interior grace for each act, even for the beginning of faith; and in this they were heretics, because they wished this grace to be such that the human will could either resist or obey.
5. It is Semipelagian to say that Christ died or shed His blood for all men without exception.

— Innocent X, bull Cum occasione (31 May 1653), the five propositions of Jansen’s Augustinus, Denzinger §§2001–2007, source.2 The first four were condemned outright (the first as “rash, impious, blasphemous… and heretical,” the next three as “heretical”); the fifth was condemned as “false… and, understood in this sense, that Christ died for the salvation of the predestined [only],… heretical.” Read against the timeline, the condemned propositions are Jansen’s (and Gottschalk’s) hard Augustinianism stated as bluntly as possible: grace so efficacious it cannot be resisted (props. 2, 4), commands that bind even where the grace to keep them is withheld (prop. 1), a liberty that needs only freedom from external compulsion, not from interior necessity (prop. 3), and a redemption limited to the elect (prop. 5). In condemning them Rome drew, from the Augustinian side, the same line Orange had drawn from the Pelagian side.

Notes

  • The boundary, from the other side. Orange (529) had ruled out the semi-Pelagian claim that the beginning of faith can be the unaided will’s (see the initium fidei argument, assessed weak). Cum occasione ruled out the opposite excess: that grace is so irresistible and redemption so restricted that the will is left no real freedom and Christ no universal saving will. The two condemnations together mark the width of the licit Catholic road between Pelagius and a predestinarianism as strict as Gottschalk’s — the road the Molinists and Thomists were left free to dispute within.
  • Christ died for all. Proposition 5 is the mirror image of Gottschalk’s teaching that “God did not suffer for the baptized reprobate”: Rome declared it heretical to call the universal-atonement reading “semi-Pelagian.” This is the same universal saving will that Quiersy (853) had confessed against Gottschalk and that turns on 1 Timothy 2:4 — weighed on this timeline at God “wills all to be saved.”
  • Fact versus right. The Jansenists (led after 1643 by Antoine Arnauld, with Port-Royal as their centre, and given deadly satirical voice by Pascal’s Provincial Letters of 1656) accepted that the Church could condemn the five propositions as doctrine (a question “of right”) but denied that it could bind consciences to the “fact” that the propositions were actually taught in Jansen’s book — a distinction Rome and its defenders read as “the denial of the doctrinal power of the Church.” The “formulary” controversy that followed — over whether one could honestly sign a condemnation of a meaning one claimed not to find in the text — kept Jansenism alive for another century, down to the bull Unigenitus (1713).3
  • A gap worth noting. Jansen’s theses are given here from the standard reference works, which characterize (and in one case quote) the Augustinus; a directly-translated passage from the folio itself would strengthen the page and is not yet in hand.
