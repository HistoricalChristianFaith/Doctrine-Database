John Cassian on predestination, grace & free will

Dates: c. 426 (Conferences, Part II, Conf. 13) · Wikipedia
Summary: John Cassian (c. 360–435), the monk who carried Egyptian ascetic wisdom to southern Gaul, is the great voice of the “both-and” that a later age would label semi-Pelagian. In the thirteenth Conference (put in the mouth of Abbot Chaeremon), he refuses both extremes: against Pelagius he insists that nothing good is accomplished without God’s help, and that grace often precedes and draws the unwilling (Paul, Matthew); but against the sharpest edge of Augustine he holds that God sometimes waits for, and rewards, the first stirrings of a good will that “arise from our own efforts” (Zacchaeus, the good thief). Grace and free will, he says, are not really opposed but “in harmony”; to deny either is to break the rule of faith. It is precisely his allowance that the initium fidei may sometimes be man’s that Prosper attacked and the Council of Orange later excluded.

Claims & evidence

God fosters even the “smallest spark of good will” — whether He implanted it or it arose from our own effort

And when He sees in us some beginnings of a good will, He at once enlightens it and strengthens it and urges it on towards salvation, increasing that which He Himself implanted or which He sees to have arisen from our own efforts.

— Conferences XIII (Third Conf. of Abbot Chaeremon), ch. 8, c. 426, source.1 This single clause — “which He Himself implanted or which He sees to have arisen from our own efforts” — is the whole of what the later West found unacceptable. Cassian will not say that the good will is always God’s prior gift; sometimes God responds to a beginning that is genuinely ours. Augustine had insisted the willing itself is God’s gift; Cassian leaves the door open for a good will that God does not implant but finds and fosters.

The open question: does grace precede the good will, or the good will grace?

…does God have compassion upon us because we have shown the beginning of a good will, or does the beginning of a good will follow because God has had compassion upon us? For… if we say that the beginning of free will is in our own power, what about Paul the persecutor, what about Matthew the publican…? But if we say that the beginning of our free will is always due to the inspiration of the grace of God, what about the faith of Zaccheus, or what are we to say of the goodness of the thief on the cross, who by their own desires brought violence to bear on the kingdom of heaven…?

— Conferences XIII, ch. 11, c. 426, source.2 Cassian deliberately leaves the question unresolved by rule. His examples cut both ways on purpose: Paul the persecutor and Matthew the publican are drawn by grace while they are set on evil (grace precedes); but Zacchaeus climbing the tree and the thief on the cross seem to reach for heaven by their own longing (the will precedes). The conclusion he draws is not a system but a refusal to systematize: both patterns are real.

Grace and free will are not opposed but “in harmony”

These two then; viz., the grace of God and free will seem opposed to each other, but really are in harmony, and we gather from the system of goodness that we ought to have both alike, lest if we withdraw one of them from man, we may seem to have broken the rule of the Church’s faith. For when God sees us inclined to will what is good, He meets, guides, and strengthens us… And again, if He finds that we are unwilling or have grown cold, He stirs our hearts with salutary exhortations, by which a good will is either renewed or formed in us.

— Conferences XIII, ch. 11, c. 426, source.3 This is Cassian’s positive thesis: the “rule of the Church’s faith” requires both, and any account that dissolves one into the other has erred. Note the careful symmetry — sometimes God meets a will already “inclined to will what is good”; sometimes He must first form the good will in the “unwilling or… cold.” Where Augustine subordinated the will wholly to prevenient grace, Cassian keeps the two as genuine co-workers.

Nature holds “seeds of goodness” that grace must quicken

It cannot then be doubted that there are by nature some seeds of goodness in every soul implanted by the kindness of the Creator: but unless these are quickened by the assistance of God, they will not be able to attain to an increase of perfection.

— Conferences XIII, ch. 12, c. 426, source.4 The “seeds of goodness” (semina bonitatis) surviving in fallen nature are the crux. Cassian is no Pelagian — the seeds are God’s implanting and cannot ripen “unless… quickened by the assistance of God.” But by granting that nature retains a real, if impotent, orientation to the good, he denies the total incapacity Augustine drew from the Fall, and so leaves room for a first movement toward God that grace then completes rather than creates.

Notes

  • Why “semi-Pelagian” is a later, contested label. Cassian never called himself anything but catholic, and the term semi-Pelagian is a sixteenth-century coinage. He explicitly rejects the Pelagian denial of the Fall and the sufficiency of nature; his quarrel with Augustine is narrower — over whether the very first movement of a good will is always God’s prior gift. On that one hinge he answers “not always,” and that answer is what Prosper and Orange targeted.
  • The line of reception. Prosper of Aquitaine attacked Conference 13 by name in his Contra Collatorem (“against the Conferencer”), charging Cassian with making the beginning of faith man’s own; the Council of Orange (529) then defined the initium fidei as wholly of grace — excluding Cassian’s “or… arisen from our own efforts” — while stopping short of Augustine’s predestination to death. Cassian thus marks the middle term the settled Western answer would trim on one side only. Whether that middle term holds as a reading of the order of grace is weighed on the argument page The initium fidei — can the first movement toward faith be the will’s own? (assessed weak: coherent and not Pelagian, but its decisive proof-texts — 1 Cor 4:7, Phil 2:13 — run the other way, and Orange condemned it).
  • Cassian appears on this wiki also as a witness on the friendship timeline. His Conferences and Institutes remained monastic classics throughout the tradition, “semi-Pelagian” controversy notwithstanding.
