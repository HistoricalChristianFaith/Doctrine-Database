John Cassian on predestination, grace & free will

Dates: c. 426 (Conferences, Part II, Conf. 13) · Wikipedia
Summary: John Cassian (c. 360–435), a monk who brought the teaching of the Egyptian desert to southern Gaul, treats grace and free will in the thirteenth Conference (put in the mouth of Abbot Chaeremon). There he holds that nothing good is accomplished without God’s help and that grace often precedes and draws the unwilling (Paul, Matthew), and also that God sometimes fosters a good will which “He sees to have arisen from our own efforts” (Zacchaeus, the good thief). Grace and free will, he says, “seem opposed… but really are in harmony,” and to withdraw either is to break “the rule of the Church’s faith.” Prosper of Aquitaine attacked Conference 13 by name. Joseph Pohle (Catholic, 1912) places Cassian among the Semipelagians, a term the same encyclopedia dates to 1590–1600; Orthodox writers, among them Seraphim Rose (1983), read the Conference as the Orthodox teaching of synergy; and A. M. C. Casiday (2006) argues that Cassian was not a Pelagian of any sort (see Notes).

Claims & evidence

God fosters the beginnings of a good will, “which He Himself implanted or which He sees to have arisen from our own efforts”

And when He sees in us some beginnings of a good will, He at once enlightens it and strengthens it and urges it on towards salvation, increasing that which He Himself implanted or which He sees to have arisen from our own efforts.

— Conferences XIII (Third Conf. of Abbot Chaeremon), ch. 8, c. 426, source.1 In this clause Cassian speaks of two cases: a good will that God “Himself implanted,” and one that “He sees to have arisen from our own efforts,” which God then enlightens, strengthens and urges on toward salvation. Prosper of Aquitaine’s Contra Collatorem directs its attack at this teaching (see Prosper).

Whether grace precedes the good will, or the good will grace

…does God have compassion upon us because we have shown the beginning of a good will, or does the beginning of a good will follow because God has had compassion upon us? For… if we say that the beginning of free will is in our own power, what about Paul the persecutor, what about Matthew the publican…? But if we say that the beginning of our free will is always due to the inspiration of the grace of God, what about the faith of Zaccheus, or what are we to say of the goodness of the thief on the cross, who by their own desires brought violence to bear on the kingdom of heaven…?

— Conferences XIII, ch. 11, c. 426, source.2 Cassian answers with examples of both patterns: Paul the persecutor and Matthew the publican are drawn by grace while set on evil, while Zacchaeus and the thief on the cross “by their own desires brought violence to bear on the kingdom of heaven.” He does not reduce the two patterns to a single rule.

Grace and free will “in harmony”

These two then; viz., the grace of God and free will seem opposed to each other, but really are in harmony, and we gather from the system of goodness that we ought to have both alike, lest if we withdraw one of them from man, we may seem to have broken the rule of the Church’s faith. For when God sees us inclined to will what is good, He meets, guides, and strengthens us… And again, if He finds that we are unwilling or have grown cold, He stirs our hearts with salutary exhortations, by which a good will is either renewed or formed in us.

— Conferences XIII, ch. 11, c. 426, source.3 Cassian holds that the “rule of the Church’s faith” requires both grace and free will. Sometimes God meets a will already “inclined to will what is good”; sometimes He first forms the good will in the “unwilling or… cold.”

“Seeds of goodness” in nature, quickened by God

It cannot then be doubted that there are by nature some seeds of goodness in every soul implanted by the kindness of the Creator: but unless these are quickened by the assistance of God, they will not be able to attain to an increase of perfection.

— Conferences XIII, ch. 12, c. 426, source.4 The “seeds of goodness” (semina bonitatis) are, for Cassian, implanted “by the kindness of the Creator” in every soul, and cannot attain their increase “unless… quickened by the assistance of God.”

Notes

  • The label “semi-Pelagian.” The Catholic Encyclopedia states that “the name Semipelagianism was unknown both in Christian antiquity and throughout the Middle Ages” and that “the word was coined between 1590 and 1600 in connexion with Molina’s doctrine of grace.”5
  • How Conference 13 has been read. Three readings are attributed below, in date order.
    • A departure from Augustine. Prosper of Aquitaine attacked Conference 13 by name in his Contra Collatorem (“against the Conferencer,” c. 433/434), charging Cassian with making the beginning of faith man’s own. Joseph Pohle (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, “Semipelagianism,” 1912) reports that the monks of Marseilles held that Augustine “far overshot the mark” on prevenient grace, calls Cassian “the soul and head of the whole movement,” and says of his teaching that “the will frequently preceded the grace”: “This view was no longer Augustinian.” He names Faustus of Riez “the most important representative of Semipelagianism after Cassian,” and notes that Cassian, Hilary of Arles and Faustus are honoured as saints in France. He reports that the Second Synod of Orange (529), which taught in Canon 5 that “not only the increase of faith but also its beginning and the very desire for faith” is a gift of grace (see Caesarius of Arles), “condemned Semipelagianism as heresy.”6
    • A statement of synergy. Orthodox writers read the Conference as the teaching of their own Church. An academic Orthodox study of Conference 13 was not located for this page; it is given here through Fr. Seraphim Rose (Orthodox, The Place of Blessed Augustine in the Orthodox Church, 1983), who writes, as quoted on the Orthodox site orthodoxinfo.com: “The true teaching of St. Cassian [see Conferences, XIII], on the other hand, which is the teaching of the Orthodox Church, was something of a mystification to the Latin mind.” Rose adds that “the Orthodox doctrine of synergy does not at all deny ‘prevenient grace,’ but only teaches its cooperation with free will.” The site itself calls Conference XIII “a superb statement of the Orthodox doctrine of synergy (wrongly dubbed ‘semi-Pelagianism’ by modern Western writers): God working with man to effect his salvation.”7 The Orthodox historian John Meyendorff (Byzantine Theology, 1974), without discussing Cassian, describes the Byzantine frame in which grace secures “that cooperation, or synergy, between the divine will and human choice.”8
    • Not a battle with Augustine. A. M. C. Casiday’s Tradition and Theology in St John Cassian (Oxford, 2006) argues, in the words of M. A. Claussen’s review, that “not only was Cassian not a Pelagian of any sort”; that “it was Prosper who tarred Cassian with the brush of semi-Pelagianism” and “Prosper’s understanding of Cassian is defective”; and that in Conference 13 “Cassian was not involved in a battle against Augustine and his teachings.”9
  • Cassian appears on this wiki also as a witness on the friendship timeline.
