Martin Luther on predestination, grace & free will

Dates: c. 1525 (De servo arbitrio — The Bondage of the Will, December 1525; Martin Luther, 1483–1546, Augustinian friar and Wittenberg professor) · Wikipedia
Summary: The Bondage of the Will is Luther’s reply to Erasmus’s Diatribe. In a letter to Wolfgang Capito of 9 July 1537 Luther wrote that he acknowledged none of his books as truly his own “except perhaps the one On the Bound Will and the Catechism.”1 Where Erasmus defended a will that can “apply itself” to grace or refuse it, Luther denies the unregenerate will any such power in things pertaining to salvation: on his account fallen man does not choose his master but is ridden by one, and grace works the willing itself. Against Erasmus’s preference for restraint he holds that the question must be asserted, and he treats it as vital to Christian godliness; it is bound up, for him, with the doctrine of justification by faith.

Claims & evidence

“Take away assertions and you take away Christianity”

Nothing is better known or more common among Christians than assertion. Take away assertions and you take away Christianity.

— The Bondage of the Will (1525), Part I (Review of Erasmus’ Preface), trans. Philip Watson, source.2 Luther opens with Erasmus’s method. Erasmus had said he would gladly “take refuge in the opinion of the Skeptics” where Scripture and the Church allow, and that the free-will question was obscure enough to leave undecided. Luther answers that Scripture is clear on this and that a Christian must be a maker of assertions: “the Holy Spirit is no Skeptic.” For Luther the certainty of the doctrine belongs to the doctrine itself.

“I consider it vital”

If you consider this subject unnecessary for Christians, then please quit the field; you and I have nothing in common, for I consider it vital.

— The Bondage of the Will (1525), Part I, trans. Watson, source.3 Luther charged that Erasmus counted free choice “among the things that are useless and unnecessary.” Luther answers that to know “whether our will accomplishes anything in things pertaining to eternal salvation, or simply suffers the action of grace” is, for him, to know whether salvation is God’s work or partly man’s — a matter he counts central to Christian godliness. The related question is traced on the justification timeline.

The image of the will as a beast ridden by God or by Satan

Thus the human will is, as it were, a beast between the two. If God sit thereon, it wills and goes where God will: as the Psalm saith, “I am become as it were a beast before thee, and I am continually with thee.” If Satan sit thereon, it wills and goes as Satan will. Nor is it in the power of its own will to choose, to which rider it will run, nor which it will seek; but the riders themselves contend, which shall have and hold it.

— The Bondage of the Will (1525), trans. Henry Cole (1823), source.4 In this image the will truly wills, but does not stand neutral between God and Satan; it goes where its rider drives, and “nor is it in the power of its own will to choose, to which rider it will run.” Luther sets this against Erasmus’s definition of free choice as a power to apply oneself to salvation or turn away. On Luther’s account the will is not coerced against itself — it wills what it wills — but it has no power of its own to change riders.

Luther’s closing words to Erasmus: “you alone have attacked the real issue”

I praise and commend you highly…, that unlike all the rest you alone have attacked the real issue, the essence of the matter in dispute, and have not wearied me with irrelevancies about the papacy, purgatory, indulgences, and such like trifles (for trifles they are rather than basic issues)…

— The Bondage of the Will (1525), Conclusion, in Luther’s Works (American Edition) vol. 33, p. 294, trans. Philip S. Watson, source.5 Luther ends his reply by thanking Erasmus for his choice of subject. The papacy, purgatory and indulgences are, he says, “trifles” beside the question whether the will is free toward God; in the Packer–Johnston rendering, Erasmus “alone… [has] seen the hinge on which all turns.”

Reception and debate

  • Luther and Augustine. Luther appeals to Augustine, whose teaching that God works the willing to believe is set out on Augustine’s detail page.
  • Necessity and God’s working in evil men. In The Bondage of the Will Luther writes of “necessity”: “I could wish indeed that another and a better word had been introduced into our discussion than this usual one, ‘necessity,’ which is not rightly applied either to the divine or the human will… For neither the divine nor the human will does what it does, whether good or evil, under any compulsion, but from sheer pleasure or desire, as with true freedom; and yet the will of God is immutable and infallible, and it governs our mutable will.”6 Later in the treatise he writes that “when God works in, and by, evil men, the evils themselves are inwrought, but yet, God cannot do evil, although He thus works the evils by evil men; because, being good Himself He cannot do evil.”7 The Lutheran Formula of Concord (1577) appeals to De servo arbitrio on the bondage of the will, and states that Luther’s “meaning and understanding of some other peculiar disputations introduced incidentally by Erasmus, as of absolute necessity, etc., have been secured by him in the best and most careful way against all misunderstanding and perversion” in his exposition of Genesis; its article on election rejects the teaching that some “are ordained to condemnation so that they cannot be saved.”8
  • The sequel. Erasmus answered with the two-part Hyperaspistes (1526–27); Luther did not reply. Later disputes over election and the will are recorded on this timeline under Arminius and the Synod of Dort.
  • Luther appears on this wiki on other timelines — among them the indulgences controversy, the New Testament canon, and purgatory.
