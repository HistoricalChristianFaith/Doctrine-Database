Martin Luther on predestination, grace & free will

Dates: c. 1525 (De servo arbitrio — The Bondage of the Will, December 1525; Martin Luther, 1483–1546, Augustinian friar and Wittenberg professor) · Wikipedia
Summary: Luther’s reply to Erasmus’s Diatribe, and by his own reckoning the truest thing he ever wrote — late in life he judged that of all his books only the Catechism and The Bondage of the Will deserved to survive. Where Erasmus defended a will that can “apply itself” to grace or refuse it, Luther denies the unregenerate will any such power over salvation at all: fallen man does not choose his master but is ridden by one, and grace does not wait on a decision it finds but works the very willing it requires. He recasts the whole Augustinian settlement in its hardest form — certainty against Erasmus’s skeptical restraint, necessity against cooperating freedom, God’s sovereign working against any human contribution — and he does so because he sees the doctrine as the hinge of the gospel itself: that a sinner contributes nothing to his acceptance is, for Luther, simply the doctrine of grace, the root of justification by faith laid bare. It is the sharpest Protestant statement of the sovereignty of grace on the timeline, and the direct provocation of the Arminian and Tridentine replies that follow.

Claims & evidence

Against Erasmus’s skepticism: “Take away assertions and you take away Christianity”

Nothing is better known or more common among Christians than assertion. Take away assertions and you take away Christianity.

— The Bondage of the Will (1525), Part I (Review of Erasmus’ Preface), trans. Philip Watson, source.1 Luther opens by refusing Erasmus’s premise. Erasmus had said he would gladly “take refuge in the opinion of the Skeptics” where Scripture and the Church allow, and that the free-will question was obscure enough to leave undecided. Luther answers that Scripture is clear on this and that a Christian must be a maker of assertions: “the Holy Spirit is no Skeptic.” The methodological quarrel is not a preliminary to the doctrine — it is the doctrine’s foundation, for if the will’s bondage cannot be asserted with certainty, the sole-glory-of-grace it secures collapses.

The doctrine is not superfluous but vital — “you and I have nothing in common” without it

If you consider this subject unnecessary for Christians, then please quit the field; you and I have nothing in common, for I consider it vital.

— The Bondage of the Will (1525), Part I, trans. Watson, source.2 Erasmus had counted free choice among things “useless and unnecessary” to know. Luther will not let the stakes be lowered: to know “whether our will accomplishes anything in things pertaining to eternal salvation, or simply suffers the action of grace” is, for him, to know whether salvation is God’s work or partly man’s — the whole of Christian godliness. This is why the exchange sits at the doctrinal root of the wiki’s justification timeline.

The will as a beast of burden: ridden by God or by Satan, but never choosing its rider

Thus the human will is, as it were, a beast between the two. If God sit thereon, it wills and goes where God will: as the Psalm saith, “I am become as it were a beast before thee, and I am continually with thee.” If Satan sit thereon, it wills and goes as Satan will. Nor is it in the power of its own will to choose, to which rider it will run, nor which it will seek; but the riders themselves contend, which shall have and hold it.

— The Bondage of the Will (1525), trans. Henry Cole (1823), source.3 This is the most famous image in the book, and the sharpest denial of Erasmus’s definition. The will is real and it truly wills — but it does not stand neutral between God and Satan, weighing which to serve; it is already mounted, and the utmost it can do is go where its rider drives. Erasmus’s “power… to apply oneself… or turn away” is, on this picture, an illusion: the unregenerate will can only go Satan’s way until God unseats him. Luther is careful that this is not fatalistic indifference — the will is not coerced against itself; it wills what it wills — but it has no self-determining power to change riders. That is the bondage.

The verdict on Erasmus: “you alone have attacked the real issue”

I praise and commend you highly…, that unlike all the rest you alone have attacked the real issue, the essence of the matter in dispute, and have not wearied me with irrelevancies about the papacy, purgatory, indulgences, and such like trifles (for trifles they are rather than basic issues).

— The Bondage of the Will (1525), Conclusion, in Luther’s Works (American Edition) vol. 33, p. 294, trans. Philip S. Watson, source.4 For all his contempt of the Diatribe, Luther ends by thanking Erasmus for choosing the right battlefield. The Reformation’s public quarrels — the papacy, purgatory, the sale of indulgences — are, he says, “trifles” beside this: whether the will is free toward God. Erasmus “alone… saw the hinge on which all turns.” The judgement is the reason both men, and this timeline, treat grace-and-free-will as the true fault line of the Reformation, deeper than any of its more famous flashpoints.

Notes

  • Augustine pressed to his hardest edge. Luther claims Augustine outright, and on the decisive point — that God works even the willing to believe — the claim is fair (see Augustine’s detail page). But Luther goes past Augustine’s reticence about reprobation, teaching that everything comes to pass by necessity and that God works in the ungodly as well as the godly. On the symmetry of the decree — whether there is a positive predestination of the lost — see the argument page Double predestination (assessed contested).
  • The reply and the tradition. Erasmus answered with the two-part Hyperaspistes (1526–27); Luther did not reply. The Reformed side carried Luther’s bondage-of-the-will through to the systematic unconditional election defined at the Synod of Dort, while the Arminian Remonstrants and the Catholic Council of Trent both reacted against it — Trent’s canons on justification aimed squarely at the denial of cooperating freedom.
  • Luther appears across this wiki on many timelines — among them the indulgences controversy his Ninety-Five Theses opened, the New Testament canon, and purgatory. He himself ranked The Bondage of the Will above nearly all of it.
