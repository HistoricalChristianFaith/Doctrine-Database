Caesarius of Arles and the Council of Orange on predestination, grace & free will

Dates: 529 (the Second Council of Orange) · Caesarius c. 470–542 · Wikipedia
Summary: Caesarius, bishop of Arles, presided over the Second Council of Orange in 529 and drove through it the settled Western answer to the century-old dispute. Orange received Augustine on the decisive point — that even the beginning of faith and the very desire to believe are the gift of grace, not a movement of unaided nature — and so closed the door on both Pelagius and the “semi-Pelagian” middle of Cassian. But it deliberately stopped short of Augustine’s hardest edge: it affirmed that after grace the baptized truly cooperate, and it anathematized with “utter abhorrence” the idea that anyone is predestined by God to evil. Confirmed by Pope Boniface II in 531, its canons became the West’s authoritative statement on grace — grace-alone to begin, without double predestination.

Claims & evidence

The beginning of faith itself is grace, not nature

If anyone says that not only the increase of faith but also its beginning and the very desire for faith, by which we believe in Him who justifies the ungodly and comes to the regeneration of holy baptism—if anyone says that this belongs to us by nature and not by a gift of grace… it is proof that he is opposed to the teaching of the Apostles.

— Second Council of Orange (529), Canon 5, source.1 This is the canon that decides the whole controversy. The Massilians had conceded that the increase of faith is God’s gift while making its beginning man’s own; Orange condemns exactly that division, folding “its beginning and the very desire for faith” into grace. Cassian’s good will that “arises from our own efforts” is thereby excluded. The neighbouring canon (4) adds that God does not merely await our will to be cleansed but Himself works that will in us “through the infusion… of the Holy Spirit.”

Not even a right choice toward salvation without the Spirit’s illumination

If anyone affirms that we can form any right opinion or make any right choice which relates to the salvation of eternal life… or that we can be saved, that is, assent to the preaching of the gospel through our natural powers without the illumination and inspiration of the Holy Spirit… he is led astray by a heretical spirit.

— Second Council of Orange (529), Canon 7, source.2 Orange presses the point down to its root: not even the assent that receives the gospel lies within “our natural powers.” The council grounds this in John 15:5 (“apart from me you can do nothing”) and 2 Corinthians 3:5 (“our competence is from God”) — the very texts Augustine had used. This is the full Augustinian doctrine of prevenient grace, made conciliar.

The rejection of predestination to evil

We not only do not believe that any are foreordained to evil by the power of God, but even state with utter abhorrence that if there are those who want to believe so evil a thing, they are anathema.

— Second Council of Orange (529), Conclusion, source.3 Here is the exact boundary the West drew. Orange takes Augustine’s grace but refuses to follow the logic some drew from it into a symmetrical predestination of the lost. There is a predestination of the elect to life; there is no foreordination of anyone to evil or to death. Three centuries later Gottschalk would press just such a “twofold predestination” and be condemned at Quiersy (853) on the strength of this very line.

After grace, the baptized truly cooperate; but God takes the initiative in every good work

According to the catholic faith we also believe that after grace has been received through baptism, all baptized persons have the ability and responsibility, if they desire to labor faithfully, to perform with the aid and cooperation of Christ what is of essential importance in regard to the salvation of their soul. We also believe and confess… that in every good work it is not we who take the initiative and are then assisted through the mercy of God, but God himself first inspires in us both faith in him and love for him without any previous good works of our own that deserve reward.

— Second Council of Orange (529), Conclusion, source.4 The balance Orange strikes is precise. Before grace, man contributes nothing — not even the initiative, not any prior merit. After baptismal grace, the will really cooperates and labours “with the aid and cooperation of Christ.” This is grace-first without determinism: it secures Augustine’s sola gratia at the outset while preserving the genuine agency Cassian feared to lose. The council states the merit-order flatly elsewhere: “grace, to which we have no claim, precedes” good works “to enable them to be done.”5

Notes

  • What Orange settled, and what it left open. It settled that the initium fidei is grace (closing the Pelagian and semi-Pelagian question) and that there is no predestination to evil (closing the door Gottschalk would try to reopen). It left open the finer question of how prevenient grace and cooperating grace relate — the space in which the medieval schools, and then Molina and Báñez in the De Auxiliis controversy, would still contend.
  • Caesarius’s hand. The canons largely compile sententiae from Augustine and Prosper (some drawn from a Roman collection sent by Pope Felix IV); Caesarius’s role was to convene the council, secure the bishops’ subscription, and obtain papal confirmation, giving the Augustinian settlement conciliar and papal authority it had not previously carried in the West.
  • Transmission. Orange’s canons were largely forgotten by the tenth century and recovered only in the sixteenth — which is why the Reformers, rediscovering them, pointed to Orange as evidence that the medieval church had drifted from its own conciliar teaching on grace, while Trent (1547) framed its decree on justification so as to remain consistent with it. The council thus sits behind both later camps on the justification timeline.
