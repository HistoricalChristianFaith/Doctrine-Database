Caesarius of Arles and the Council of Orange on predestination, grace & free will

Dates: 529 (the Second Council of Orange) · Caesarius c. 470–542 · Wikipedia
Summary: Caesarius, bishop of Arles, presided over the Second Council of Orange in 529. Its canons teach that the beginning of faith and the very desire to believe are a gift of grace and not of nature; that after baptismal grace the baptized are able, with Christ’s aid and cooperation, to do what pertains to salvation; and they anathematize with “utter abhorrence” the idea that anyone is foreordained to evil by the power of God. Pope Boniface II confirmed the council’s acts in 531.

Claims & evidence

The beginning of faith (Canon 5)

If anyone says that not only the increase of faith but also its beginning and the very desire for faith, by which we believe in Him who justifies the ungodly and comes to the regeneration of holy baptism—if anyone says that this belongs to us by nature and not by a gift of grace… it is proof that he is opposed to the teaching of the Apostles.

— Second Council of Orange (529), Canon 5, source.1 The canon addresses the distinction reported of the monks of Marseilles by Prosper of Aquitaine, who granted that the increase of faith is God’s gift while making its beginning man’s own (see Prosper): the canon places “its beginning and the very desire for faith” under grace. The neighbouring canon (4) adds that God does not merely await our will to be cleansed but Himself works that will in us “through the infusion… of the Holy Spirit.”

Assent to the gospel and the Spirit’s illumination (Canon 7)

If anyone affirms that we can form any right opinion or make any right choice which relates to the salvation of eternal life… or that we can be saved, that is, assent to the preaching of the gospel through our natural powers without the illumination and inspiration of the Holy Spirit… he is led astray by a heretical spirit.

— Second Council of Orange (529), Canon 7, source.2 The canon extends the point to the assent that receives the gospel, which it denies lies within “our natural powers” without the Spirit’s illumination. The council grounds this in John 15:5 (“apart from me you can do nothing”) and 2 Corinthians 3:5 (“our competence is from God”).

The anathema on predestination to evil (Conclusion)

We not only do not believe that any are foreordained to evil by the power of God, but even state with utter abhorrence that if there are those who want to believe so evil a thing, they are anathema.

— Second Council of Orange (529), Conclusion, source.3 The conclusion anathematizes belief that any are foreordained to evil by the power of God. In the ninth century both Gottschalk of Orbais, who taught a twofold predestination of the elect to life and the reprobate to death, and the synods that ruled on his teaching at Quiersy (853) and Valence (855) denied that God foreordains anyone to evil.

Cooperation after baptismal grace, and God’s initiative in every good work

According to the catholic faith we also believe that after grace has been received through baptism, all baptized persons have the ability and responsibility, if they desire to labor faithfully, to perform with the aid and cooperation of Christ what is of essential importance in regard to the salvation of their soul. We also believe and confess… that in every good work it is not we who take the initiative and are then assisted through the mercy of God, but God himself first inspires in us both faith in him and love for him without any previous good works of our own that deserve reward.

— Second Council of Orange (529), Conclusion, source.4 The conclusion assigns the initiative in every good work to God, “without any previous good works of our own,” and states that after baptismal grace the baptized labour “with the aid and cooperation of Christ.” Canon 18 states the order of grace and merit: “grace, to which we have no claim, precedes” good works “to enable them to be done.”5

Reception and debate

  • Composition. According to the Catholic Encyclopedia, Caesarius had written to Pope Felix IV about the teaching current in Gaul, and the pope sent a series of capitula “drawn almost in their entirety from the works of St. Augustine” and the Sententiae of Prosper of Aquitaine; the council, “attended by fourteen bishops with St. Cæsarius of Arles as president,” adopted them, and its acts were “approved by Boniface II on 25 January, 531.”6
  • Transmission. The Wikipedia article on the council states that its canons “seem to have been lost in the tenth century” and were “recovered and consequently published in 1543”; the Catholic Encyclopedia states that the capitula of Felix IV “were freely used by the Council of Trent in its condemnation of Luther.”7 The Reformation-era debate is traced on the justification timeline.
