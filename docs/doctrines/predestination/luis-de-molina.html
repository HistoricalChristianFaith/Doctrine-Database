Luis de Molina, Domingo Báñez & the De Auxiliis controversy

Dates: 1588–1607 (the controversy) · Luis de Molina 1535–1600 (Jesuit) · Domingo Báñez 1528–1604 (Dominican) · Wikipedia
Summary: In the late sixteenth century Jesuit and Dominican theologians disputed how efficacious grace and the freedom of the will are related. The Jesuit Luis de Molina, in his Concordia (1588), answered with scientia media (“middle knowledge”): God knows what any free creature would freely do in every possible circumstance, and elects by choosing which set of circumstances to bring about. The Dominican Domingo Báñez answered that grace is intrinsically efficacious — its power lies in the grace itself, moving the will by a praemotio physica (physical premotion), not in any foreseen consent. Clement VIII convened a special commission, the Congregatio de Auxiliis (“on the helps” of grace), in 1598; in 1607 Paul V allowed each party to defend its own doctrine and forbade each to condemn the other. This page records the two men and the controversy as witnesses on the timeline.

Claims & evidence

Molina on scientia media: God’s knowledge of what each free will would do

…middle knowledge, by which, in virtue of the most profound and inscrutable comprehension of each faculty of free choice, He saw in His own essence what each such faculty would do with its innate freedom were it to be placed in this or in that or, indeed, in infinitely many orders of things — even though it would really be able, if it so willed, to do the opposite.

— Luis de Molina, Concordia IV, disp. 52, §9, trans. Alfred J. Freddoso, source.1 In Molina’s scheme middle knowledge stands between God’s knowledge of what is merely possible (“natural knowledge”) and His knowledge of what He wills actually to be (“free knowledge”). Its objects are the counterfactuals of freedom — truths of the form “if this creature were in these circumstances, it would freely do X.” Because God possesses this knowledge before His decree, Molina argued, He can elect infallibly — by choosing which world of freely-responding creatures to actualize — without coercing a single will, since each still “would really be able, if it so willed, to do the opposite.” On Molina’s account the decree is prior and the creature’s freedom remains.

Báñez on intrinsically efficacious grace and physical premotion

The Dominican Domingo Báñez taught, in the Thomist school, that what makes efficacious grace efficacious is not God’s foresight of how the will would respond but a power intrinsic to the grace itself. God moves the created will really and antecedently — a praemotio physica (physical premotion) — so that the will infallibly acts, yet acts freely, because the divine motion reaches and activates the will’s own liberty rather than overriding it. On this view predestination is “irrespective of foreseen merit.” J. Volz (Catholic Encyclopedia, “Domingo Báñez,” 1907) reports that the “predetermination” of Báñez and his contemporaries stressed God’s priority to future free acts, which “in the Catharino-Molinistic theories, seemed to them less clearly to fall under God’s causal action.” Báñez examined the Concordia and reported that several of its propositions were suspect; he became the controversy’s leading Thomist voice.2 Joseph Pohle (Catholic Encyclopedia, “Molinism,” 1911) reports the Thomist argument against Molina: “if the decision ultimately depends on the free will, whether a given grace shall be efficacious or not, the result of the salutary act must be attributed to man and not to God.”3

The Congregatio de Auxiliis and Paul V’s 1607 settlement

In 1588 Molina “published at Lisbon his ‘Concordia liberi arbitrii cum gratiæ donis’… in which he explained efficacious grace on the basis of scientia media”; “the Dominicans attacked it on the grounds that Molina and all the Jesuits denied efficacious grace.” Clement VIII appointed a commission — the Congregatio de Auxiliis — which “began its labours 2 Jan., 1598.” It sat until 1607, when Paul V issued the decree described below:

The pope’s decree communicated (5 September, 1607) to both Dominicans and Jesuits, allowed each party to defend its own doctrine, enjoined each from censoring or condemning the opposite opinion, and commanded them to await, as loyal sons of the Church, the final decision of the Apostolic See.

— Catholic Encyclopedia, “Congregatio de Auxiliis,” source.4 No further decision on the question is recorded on this timeline.
