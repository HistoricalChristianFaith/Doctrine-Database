Luis de Molina, Domingo Báñez & the De Auxiliis controversy

Dates: 1588–1607 (the controversy) · Luis de Molina 1535–1600 (Jesuit) · Domingo Báñez 1528–1604 (Dominican) · Wikipedia
Summary: A century after the West settled the Pelagian question at Orange, the Catholic schools reopened the finer question Orange had left untouched — how grace can be at once wholly God’s and infallibly effective, yet leave the will genuinely free — without any of them reviving Pelagius. The Jesuit Luis de Molina, in his Concordia (1588), answered with scientia media (“middle knowledge”): God knows what any free creature would freely do in every possible circumstance, and elects by choosing which set of circumstances to bring about. The Dominican Domingo Báñez answered that grace is intrinsically efficacious — its power lies in the grace itself, moving the will by a praemotio physica (physical premotion), not in any foreseen consent. Clement VIII convened a special commission, the Congregatio de Auxiliis (“on the helps” of grace), in 1598; after nine years it condemned neither side, and in 1607 Paul V left Thomists and Molinists each free to teach their own view. The mechanism Molina proposed is weighed on its own argument page; this page records the two men and the controversy as a witness on the timeline.

Claims & evidence

Molina: scientia media — God knows what each free will would do

…middle knowledge, by which, in virtue of the most profound and inscrutable comprehension of each faculty of free choice, He saw in His own essence what each such faculty would do with its innate freedom were it to be placed in this or in that or, indeed, in infinitely many orders of things — even though it would really be able, if it so willed, to do the opposite.

— Luis de Molina, Concordia IV, disp. 52, §9, trans. Alfred J. Freddoso, source.1 The name marks its place: middle knowledge stands between God’s knowledge of what is merely possible (“natural knowledge”) and His knowledge of what He wills actually to be (“free knowledge”). Its objects are the counterfactuals of freedom — truths of the form “if this creature were in these circumstances, it would freely do X.” Because God possesses this knowledge before His decree, Molina argued, He can elect infallibly — by choosing which world of freely-responding creatures to actualize — without coercing a single will, since each still “would really be able, if it so willed, to do the opposite.” That is the whole reconciliation: the decree is prior, yet freedom is untouched.

Báñez: grace is intrinsically efficacious, moving the will by physical premotion

Against Molina the Dominican Domingo Báñez defended the Thomist tradition: what makes efficacious grace efficacious is not God’s foresight of how the will would respond but a power intrinsic to the grace itself. God moves the created will really and antecedently — a praemotio physica (physical premotion) — so that the will infallibly acts, yet acts freely, because the divine motion reaches and activates the will’s own liberty rather than overriding it. On this view election rests on God’s sovereign choice, “irrespective of foreseen merit,” and Molina’s middle knowledge threatens to make God’s decree depend on the creature. Báñez examined the Concordia and reported that several of its propositions were suspect; he became the controversy’s leading Thomist voice.2 (Báñez’s doctrine is here reported from the standard reference literature; his own words lie in his Latin commentaries on the Summa and are not quoted directly on this page.)

The Congregatio de Auxiliis and Paul V’s 1607 settlement

The dispute grew so bitter between the two orders that Rome intervened. In 1588 Molina “published at Lisbon his ‘Concordia liberi arbitrii cum gratiæ donis’… in which he explained efficacious grace on the basis of scientia media”; “the Dominicans attacked it on the grounds that Molina and all the Jesuits denied efficacious grace.” Clement VIII appointed a commission — the Congregatio de Auxiliis — which “began its labours 2 Jan., 1598.” It sat for nine years without resolving the question. The settlement, when it came under Paul V, was to impose silence rather than a verdict:

The pope’s decree communicated (5 September, 1607) to both Dominicans and Jesuits, allowed each party to defend its own doctrine, enjoined each from censoring or condemning the opposite opinion, and commanded them to await, as loyal sons of the Church, the final decision of the Apostolic See.

— Catholic Encyclopedia, “Congregatio de Auxiliis,” source.3 That “final decision” never came. Both schools were left standing, and both remain licit Catholic positions — the one point on which the De Auxiliis controversy is genuinely settled is that the Church declined to settle it.

Notes

  • The mechanism is weighed elsewhere. Whether scientia media actually does the reconciling work it claims — and whether the grounding objection (what makes counterfactuals of freedom true before the creature exists and before God wills anything?) defeats it — is adjudicated on the argument page Middle knowledge (scientia media) — does it reconcile efficacious grace and free will? (assessed contested: strong by the lights of analytic theology, weak by those of Thomist metaphysics). This detail page records the historical witness; the argument page rates the idea.
  • Not a Pelagian revival. Both Molina and Báñez affirm that saving grace is necessary, prevenient, and God’s free gift — the ground Orange secured. Their quarrel is entirely downstream of Pelagius: it concerns the internal structure of efficacious grace, not whether the first movement toward faith is grace (both say it is). This is why the controversy sits on the timeline as a Catholic in-house dispute, distinct from the semi-Pelagian question weighed at the initium fidei argument.
  • Its cousins on this timeline. The Thomist/Molinist divide over the ground of election is the same fault line the Reformed world crossed a decade later at Dort (unconditional election vs. election on foreseen faith) — see the argument on election post praevisa merita — and the same one Rome policed from the other side when it condemned Jansen’s over-Augustinian reading in 1653. Orange had set the floor; these controversies contest the ceiling.
