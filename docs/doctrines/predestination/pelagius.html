Pelagius on predestination, grace & free will

Dates: c. 354–420 (teaching at Rome to 410, then Africa and Palestine) · Wikipedia
Summary: Pelagius, a British ascetic teacher at Rome, taught that God had implanted in human nature a possibilitas — a power to choose either good or evil — so that a man “is able to be without sin if he wishes.” Grace, on his account, is chiefly the good of that created nature, together with the law and the example and teaching of Christ, which instruct and encourage the will; in his scheme the willing and the doing of the good belong to man. Augustine and the African bishops held that this made the beginning of salvation man’s own act and denied grace, and Pelagius’s teaching was condemned at Carthage and by Pope Zosimus in 418.

Claims & evidence

The capacity to choose good or evil as God’s gift in nature

Our most excellent creator wished us to be able to do either but actually to do only one, that is, good, which he also commanded, giving us the capacity to do evil only so that we might do his will by exercising our own. That being so, this very capacity to do evil is also good — good, I say, because it makes the good part better by making it voluntary and independent, not bound by necessity but free to decide for itself.

— Letter to Demetrias §3.2 (c. 413), trans. B. R. Rees, source.1 For Pelagius the freedom to do either good or evil is itself God’s gift, and so good; the power is an endowment of the created nature, by which the will is “free to decide for itself.”

“A man is able to be without sin if he wishes”

We asserted that a man could be without sin, and could keep God’s commandments if he wished; for this capacity has been given to him by God.

— Pelagius, quoted by Augustine, On the Proceedings of Pelagius (c. 417), source.2 This is the thesis (in Latin the tag posse hominem sine peccato esse) examined at the Synod of Diospolis in 415, where Pelagius was acquitted after affirming that the capacity is “given… by God.” Pelagius held that this qualification affirmed grace; Augustine answered that a grace which supplies only the ability, leaving the actual willing to man, is not the grace Scripture means.

Ability, volition and actuality: Pelagius’s threefold distinction

We distinguish, says he, three things, arranging them in a certain graduated order. We put in the first place ‘ability;’ in the second, ‘volition;’ and in the third, ‘actuality.’ The ‘ability’ we place in our nature, the ‘volition’ in our will, and the ‘actuality’ in the effect. The first, that is, the ‘ability,’ properly belongs to God, who has bestowed it on His creature; the other two… must be referred to man, because they flow forth from the fountain of the will.

— Pelagius, quoted by Augustine, On the Grace of Christ I.4–5 (c. 418), source.3 In this scheme God bestows the ability (the power in nature); the volition and the actuality — the willing and the doing of the good — are referred to man. Augustine replied that if the willing to believe is man’s own contribution, grace no longer “goes before” the will, contrary to 1 Corinthians 4:7 (“What hast thou that thou didst not receive?”). Pelagius further identified grace with the instruction and example of Christ, writing that Christians, “encouraged by his example to pursue perfect righteousness,” ought now to surpass those who lived before the law.4

Reception and debate

  • What Pelagius affirmed and denied. He affirmed that the capacity for good is a divine gift and that Christ’s grace assists; he denied that grace must interiorly move the will to each good act, and (with his follower Caelestius) that Adam’s sin is transmitted as an inherited guilt corrupting the will — the linked point on which the African councils also ruled (see the original sin timeline).
  • The condemnation. After acquittal at Diospolis (415), Pelagius was condemned by the African councils and by Pope Zosimus’s Epistola Tractoria in 418; the canons of the Council of Orange (529) addressed the initium fidei. His own Commentary on Romans (in which Benjamin M. Guyer, writing in The Living Church in 2025, finds that Pelagius takes Paul’s justification by faith “to mean faith alone”)5 is not reproduced here from a primary text; the verified quotations above rest on the Letter to Demetrias and on his words as preserved verbatim inside Augustine’s replies.
  • Pelagius’s teaching was answered by Augustine; the later entries on this timeline under John Cassian and Orange concern the beginning of faith. The “faith alone” language Guyer finds in his Romans commentary is a matter for the justification timeline.
