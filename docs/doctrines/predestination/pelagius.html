Pelagius on predestination, grace & free will

Dates: c. 354–420 (teaching at Rome to 410, then Africa and Palestine) · Wikipedia
Summary: Pelagius, a British ascetic teacher, is the provocation that forced the whole doctrine into the open. Reacting against moral laxity, he insisted that God had implanted in human nature a genuine possibilitas — a real power to choose either good or evil — so that a man “is able to be without sin if he wishes.” Grace, on his account, is chiefly the good of that created nature, together with the law and the example and teaching of Christ, which instruct and encourage the will; it is not an interior enabling that God must work in a man before he can will the good. Because this made the beginning of salvation man’s own act, Augustine and the African councils judged it a denial of grace, and Pelagius’s teaching was condemned at Carthage and by Rome in 418.

Claims & evidence

God implanted in nature a real power to choose either good or evil

Our most excellent creator wished us to be able to do either but actually to do only one, that is, good, which he also commanded, giving us the capacity to do evil only so that we might do his will by exercising our own. That being so, this very capacity to do evil is also good — good, I say, because it makes the good part better by making it voluntary and independent, not bound by necessity but free to decide for itself.

— Letter to Demetrias §3.2 (c. 413), trans. B. R. Rees, source.1 The whole system stands on this created capacity. For Pelagius the freedom to do either is itself God’s gift, and so good; but the crucial point is that the power is a standing endowment of nature, not something God must freshly supply at each act. The will, so equipped, is genuinely poised between the alternatives on its own.

“A man is able to be without sin if he wishes”

We asserted that a man could be without sin, and could keep God’s commandments if he wished; for this capacity has been given to him by God.

— Pelagius, quoted by Augustine, On the Proceedings of Pelagius (c. 417), source.2 This is the thesis (in Latin the tag posse hominem sine peccato esse) examined at the Synod of Diospolis in 415, where Pelagius was acquitted after affirming that the capacity is “given… by God.” The qualification is what let him claim to teach grace; his opponents answered that a grace which supplies only the ability, leaving the actual willing entirely to man, is not the grace Scripture means — and that in practice it makes a sinless life achievable by natural effort.

Grace helps only the “capacity”: nature, law, and the example of Christ

We distinguish, says he, three things, arranging them in a certain graduated order. We put in the first place ‘ability;’ in the second, ‘volition;’ and in the third, ‘actuality.’ The ‘ability’ we place in our nature, the ‘volition’ in our will, and the ‘actuality’ in the effect. The first, that is, the ‘ability,’ properly belongs to God, who has bestowed it on His creature; the other two… must be referred to man, because they flow forth from the fountain of the will.

— Pelagius, quoted by Augustine, On the Grace of Christ I.4–5 (c. 418), source.3 Here in Pelagius’s own scheme is the exact point of rupture. God’s grace secures the ability (the standing power in nature); the volition and the actuality — the willing and the doing of the good — are man’s. Augustine seized on this: if the willing to believe is man’s own contribution, grace no longer “goes before” the will, and 1 Corinthians 4:7 (“What hast thou that thou didst not receive?”) is contradicted. Pelagius further identified grace with the instruction and example of Christ, writing that Christians, “encouraged by his example to pursue perfect righteousness,” ought now to surpass those who lived before the law.4

Notes

  • What Pelagius did and did not deny. He affirmed that the capacity for good is a divine gift and that Christ’s grace assists; he denied that grace must interiorly move the will to each good act, and (with his follower Caelestius) that Adam’s sin is transmitted as an inherited guilt corrupting the will — the linked premise the African councils fixed on. The two denials travel together: if nature is not fallen, it needs no interior healing, and the standing possibilitas suffices.
  • The condemnation. After acquittal at Diospolis (415), Pelagius was condemned by the African councils and by Pope Zosimus’s Epistola Tractoria in 418; the Council of Orange in 529 would seal the Western verdict on the initium fidei against him. His own Commentary on Romans (in which some later readers find a “justified by faith alone” strain) is not reproduced here from a primary text; the verified quotations above rest on the Letter to Demetrias and on his words as preserved verbatim inside Augustine’s replies.
  • Pelagius’s reading is answered point-for-point by Augustine, softened into a “both-and” by John Cassian, and formally excluded — on the beginning of faith — at Orange. The distinct question of whether “faith alone” language in his Romans commentary anticipates the Reformation is a matter for the justification timeline, not this one.
