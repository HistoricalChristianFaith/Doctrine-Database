Gottschalk of Orbais on predestination, grace & free will

Dates: c. 848–855 (his predestination controversy) · Gottschalk of Orbais c. 808–868 · Wikipedia
Summary: A Saxon nobleman’s son given as a child oblate to the monastery of Fulda under Rabanus Maurus, Gottschalk sought and won release from vows he had never chosen — only to be forced back into the cloister by Rabanus’s appeal to the emperor. Settled at Orbais, he steeped himself in Augustine and Fulgentius and pressed their teaching to a symmetrical conclusion the West had never formally embraced: a gemina praedestinatio, a “twofold predestination” in which God predestines the elect to life and the reprobate to death. Because God is immutable and His foreknowledge is His will, Gottschalk reasoned, the destiny of the lost can be no less eternally fixed than that of the saved — though he was careful to ground their death on foreseen demerit, never making God the author of sin. Condemned at Mainz (848) and flogged, deposed, and imprisoned for life at Quiersy (849), he refused to recant and died about twenty years later at Hautvillers. The controversy he provoked split the Frankish church: Hincmar of Reims’s four chapters at Quiersy (853) confessed one predestination, but a rival synod at Valence (855) confessed his “twofold” shape almost word for word. He is the medieval hinge of the double-predestination question.

Claims & evidence

The twofold predestination — elect to life, reprobate to death

I believe and confess that the omnipotent and immutable God has gratuitously foreknown and predestined the holy angels and elect human beings to eternal life, and that he equally predestined the devil himself, the head of all demons, with all of his apostate angels and also with all reprobate human beings, namely, his members, to rightly eternal death, on account of their own future, most certainly foreknown evil merits, through his most righteous judgment.

— Confessio brevior (his shorter confession), trans. Victor Genke & Francis X. Gumerlock, source.1 This is the whole controversy in one sentence. Note the two guardrails Gottschalk keeps even at his most symmetrical: the reprobate are predestined “to… death” but “on account of their own… foreknown evil merits” — the death is decreed, the sin that earns it is not. His symmetry is therefore not the crude parity his enemies charged (God foreordaining men to sin); it is Fulgentius’s predestination-to-punishment made explicitly parallel to the predestination of the elect.

“Twofold” does not mean two predestinations — it is one decree with two outcomes

For he does not say that there are two predestinations, because there are not. But he says “a twofold,” that is, a bipartite one, because you, Lord, spoke once of how by one, but still by a twofold predestination, you both gratuitously justify and eternally save the elect and also rightly reject and justly condemn the reprobate…

— Confessio prolixior (his longer confession) §10, quoting Isidore of Seville’s Sententiae 2.6 (“Predestination is twofold, either of the elect to rest or of the reprobate to death”), trans. Genke & Gumerlock, source.2 Gottschalk anticipated the charge that he taught two divine plans, one to save and one to damn. He insists there is one predestination that is “bipartite” — a single eternal act with two effects. His warrant is Isidore’s formula gemina est praedestinatio, from which he took the very word; the innovation was pressing it as a confession of faith. His deeper argument is metaphysical: since God is immutable and “to foreknow is the same as to will,” whatever will befall the reprobate must already have been fixed in God from eternity, or God would change in time — “they would not go to it unless they were destined, and they would not… be destined unless they had been predestined.”3

Grace is God, and nature without grace is powerless

…no one who thinks healthy thoughts can ever say that nature is greater than grace, since grace is of course God and therefore always omnipotent, but nature without grace is altogether powerless. Is not grace God and omnipotent, which gratuitously sets free and saves whomever it wants?

— On Predestination (De praedestinatione) VII, trans. Genke & Gumerlock, source.4 For Gottschalk grace is not a created assistance but God Himself acting — the Son or the Spirit “given to us by God the Father gratuitously.” That identification is why he can allow the fallen will no independent purchase on salvation: to add anything of nature to grace would be to make the creature “greater than the creator.” This is the same anti-Pelagian nerve that runs through the whole timeline, from Augustine to Luther; Gottschalk holds it in its hardest form.

Christ died only for the elect (a particular redemption)

That redemption that has been produced by the blood of Christ’s cross is proper to and special for the elect only. It is not temporal like the other, but undoubtedly everlasting… Only the elect were redeemed by this redemption, that is, only those foreknown and predestined to eternal life, those called, justified, and glorified.

— On Predestination XII, trans. Genke & Gumerlock, source.5 Gottschalk drew the further inference that would resurface, systematized, at Dort eight centuries later: if the reprobate are eternally predestined to death, the cross cannot have been intended to redeem them. “God did not suffer for the baptized reprobate,” he wrote. This particular-redemption corollary was one of the points his opponents found most alarming, since it seemed to deny the universal saving will of 1 Timothy 2:4 — the very verse Hincmar’s rebuttal would put front and centre.

The condemnation: Mainz (848), Quiersy (849), and the war of the synods

Gottschalk carried his doctrine on his wanderings through Italy and the Balkans until, at the Synod of Mainz (1 October 848), Rabanus Maurus — his old abbot, now archbishop — had him condemned and handed over to his metropolitan, Hincmar of Reims. At the Synod of Quiersy (spring 849) Gottschalk was made to burn his writings, deposed from the priesthood, flogged, and imprisoned for life at the monastery of Hautvillers, where he remained about twenty years, refusing every demand to recant until his death.6

The punishment did not settle the doctrine. A second Synod of Quiersy (853) adopted four chapters drafted by Hincmar affirming one predestination only — “we speak of only one predestination of God, which pertains either to the gift of grace or to the retribution of justice” — with the reprobate merely foreknown: God “knew [they] would perish, but He did not predestine that they would perish, because He is just.” Quiersy also confessed that God “wishes all men without exception to be saved” and that Christ suffered for all.7 But the southern bishops rejected Hincmar’s chapters. At the Council of Valence (855), Remigius of Lyon and Florus led a synod that confessed “the predestination of the elect to life, and the predestination of the impious to death” — Gottschalk’s own twofold shape — while still, exactly as he had, denying that God foreordains anyone to evil: “we do not… believe… that some have been predestined to evil by divine power… as the Synod of Orange, we say anathema to them.” Valence expressly repudiated the four Quiersy chapters as “absurdly concluded.”8 The two synods drew the same line Orange had — no predestination to sin — but divided on whether there is a real decree of the reprobate’s death. That is the unresolved seam the double-predestination argument still runs along.

Notes

  • Where he stands on the timeline. Gottschalk is the ninth-century flare-up of the Augustinian inheritance: three centuries after Fulgentius carried the hard line across, he pressed it into a symmetrical confession and forced the Carolingian church to rule on it. His condemnation at Quiersy (849/853) and the counter-confession at Valence (855) sit between Orange (529) and the early-modern grace controversies on this timeline.
  • Not a predestination to sin. Gottschalk’s critics, then and since, have often read him as teaching that God foreordains the lost to their sins — making God the author of evil. His own confessions deny exactly this: the reprobate are predestined to death “on account of their own… foreknown evil merits.” His symmetry is between two decrees (life and death), not between grace and sin; the sin remains the creature’s. This is why the double-predestination argument rates the reading contested rather than weak: even his sternest form keeps the asymmetry that grounds damnation on foreseen demerit.
  • Supporters and opponents. Gottschalk was defended by a formidable rank of scholars — Lupus of Ferrières, Ratramnus of Corbie, Prudentius of Troyes, and Florus of Lyon — against Hincmar of Reims, Rabanus Maurus, Pardulus of Laon, and John Scotus Eriugena, whose treatise On Predestination against Gottschalk was itself judged heterodox (its “nineteen syllogisms” were condemned at Valence). The affair was as much a clash of the Carolingian schools as a heresy trial.
  • A note on his dates. The year of birth is uncertain — sources range c. 800–808 — and his death is usually placed on 30 October 868 (occasionally 869), after roughly two decades’ imprisonment. The c. 808–868 given here follows the commonest modern reckoning.
