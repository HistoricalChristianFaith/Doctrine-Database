Gottschalk of Orbais on predestination, grace & free will

Dates: c. 848–855 (his predestination controversy) · Gottschalk of Orbais c. 808–868 · Wikipedia
Summary: A Saxon nobleman’s son given as a child oblate to the monastery of Fulda under Rabanus Maurus, Gottschalk sought and won release from vows he had not chosen, and was returned to the cloister after Rabanus appealed to the emperor. At Orbais he studied Augustine and Fulgentius and confessed a gemina praedestinatio, a “twofold predestination” in which God predestines the elect to life and the reprobate to death, the latter “on account of their own… foreknown evil merits.” He argued that because God is immutable and His foreknowing is His willing, the destiny of the lost is fixed from eternity as that of the saved is. Condemned at Mainz (848) and flogged, deposed, and imprisoned at Quiersy (849), he did not recant and died about twenty years later at Hautvillers. Hincmar of Reims’s four chapters at Quiersy (853) confessed one predestination, of the elect; a synod at Valence (855) confessed “the predestination of the elect to life, and the predestination of the impious to death.”

Claims & evidence

The twofold predestination in the Confessio brevior

I believe and confess that the omnipotent and immutable God has gratuitously foreknown and predestined the holy angels and elect human beings to eternal life, and that he equally predestined the devil himself, the head of all demons, with all of his apostate angels and also with all reprobate human beings, namely, his members, to rightly eternal death, on account of their own future, most certainly foreknown evil merits, through his most righteous judgment.

— Confessio brevior (his shorter confession), trans. Victor Genke & Francis X. Gumerlock, source.1 In the confession the reprobate are predestined “to… death” “on account of their own… foreknown evil merits,” and the predestination of the reprobate is set in parallel with that of the elect. The formula of predestination to punishment for foreseen sin is also found in Fulgentius of Ruspe, whom Gottschalk read.

Gottschalk’s account of “twofold” as one bipartite predestination

For he does not say that there are two predestinations, because there are not. But he says “a twofold,” that is, a bipartite one, because you, Lord, spoke once of how by one, but still by a twofold predestination, you both gratuitously justify and eternally save the elect and also rightly reject and justly condemn the reprobate…

— Confessio prolixior (his longer confession) §10, quoting Isidore of Seville’s Sententiae 2.6 (“Predestination is twofold, either of the elect to rest or of the reprobate to death”), trans. Genke & Gumerlock, source.2 Gottschalk denies that he teaches two predestinations and describes one predestination that is “bipartite,” a single eternal act with two effects. He takes the word from Isidore of Seville’s formula gemina est praedestinatio. He also argues from God’s immutability: since “to foreknow is the same as to will,” what befalls the reprobate must be fixed in God from eternity — “they would not go to it unless they were destined, and they would not… be destined unless they had been predestined.”3

Grace identified with God; nature without grace

…no one who thinks healthy thoughts can ever say that nature is greater than grace, since grace is of course God and therefore always omnipotent, but nature without grace is altogether powerless. Is not grace God and omnipotent, which gratuitously sets free and saves whomever it wants?

— On Predestination (De praedestinatione) VII, trans. Genke & Gumerlock, source.4 Gottschalk identifies grace with God Himself — the Son or the Spirit “given to us by God the Father gratuitously” — and on that ground denies that nature contributes to salvation apart from grace, since that would make the creature “greater than the creator.”

Redemption “proper to and special for the elect”

That redemption that has been produced by the blood of Christ’s cross is proper to and special for the elect only. It is not temporal like the other, but undoubtedly everlasting… Only the elect were redeemed by this redemption, that is, only those foreknown and predestined to eternal life, those called, justified, and glorified.

— On Predestination XII, trans. Genke & Gumerlock, source.5 Gottschalk held that the redemption of the cross was intended for the elect: “God did not suffer for the baptized reprobate,” he wrote. Hincmar’s chapters at Quiersy (853) affirmed, against this, that God wills all men to be saved and that Christ suffered for all (below). Hincmar also argued from the Church’s prayer for all, quoting the old rule “let the law of supplicating establish the law of believing” (see Hincmar on lex orandi).

The condemnations at Mainz (848) and Quiersy (849), and the synods of 853 and 855

Gottschalk carried his doctrine on his wanderings through Italy and the Balkans until, at the Synod of Mainz (1 October 848), Rabanus Maurus — his old abbot, now archbishop — had him condemned and handed over to his metropolitan, Hincmar of Reims. At the Synod of Quiersy (spring 849) Gottschalk was made to burn his writings, deposed from the priesthood, flogged, and imprisoned for life at the monastery of Hautvillers, where he remained about twenty years, refusing every demand to recant until his death.6

A second Synod of Quiersy (853) adopted four chapters drafted by Hincmar affirming one predestination only — “we speak of only one predestination of God, which pertains either to the gift of grace or to the retribution of justice” — with the reprobate merely foreknown: God “knew [they] would perish, but He did not predestine that they would perish, because He is just.” Quiersy also confessed that God “wishes all men without exception to be saved” and that Christ suffered for all.7 At the Council of Valence (855), bishops of the southern provinces (with Remigius of Lyon presiding, according to Wikipedia’s article on him) confessed “the predestination of the elect to life, and the predestination of the impious to death,” while denying that God foreordains anyone to evil: “we do not… believe… that some have been predestined to evil by divine power… as the Synod of Orange, we say anathema to them.” Valence removed from “the pious hearing of the faithful” the four chapters “which by the council of our brothers have been unwisely accepted” (Hincmar’s Quiersy chapters), together with the chapters “absurdly concluded with nineteen syllogisms” (the Latin: capitula… XIX syllogismis ineptissime conclusa).8 Both synods denied a predestination to sin, as the Second Council of Orange had; they differed on whether there is a predestination of the reprobate to death.

Reception and debate

  • Placement on the timeline. Gottschalk’s condemnations at Mainz (848) and Quiersy (849), the Quiersy chapters (853) and the Valence canons (855) sit between Orange (529) and the sixteenth-century entries on this timeline.
  • How his twofold predestination has been read. The Catholic Encyclopedia reports that his opponents charged him with “a false doctrine of double complete predestination,” and its author judges that “it is doubtful whether Gottschalk’s doctrine on predestination was heretical” and that “there is nothing in his extant writings that cannot be interpreted in a Catholic sense.”9 Peter Sammons (2020), as reported by Wikipedia, argues that “Gottschalk did not believe in an arbitrary damnation for the reprobate; it was always based on the justice of God” (⚠ unverified: Sammons’s own work not opened).10
  • Supporters and opponents. Writers who took Gottschalk’s side on predestination include Lupus of Ferrières, Ratramnus of Corbie, Prudentius of Troyes, and Florus of Lyon (⚠ unverified, except Ratramnus, whom Wikipedia describes as one “who composed ideas on predestination similar to those of Gottschalk”11); among his opponents were Hincmar of Reims, Rabanus Maurus, Pardulus of Laon, and John Scotus Eriugena, whose “nineteen syllogisms” were condemned at Valence (855).
  • A note on his dates. The year of birth is uncertain: the Catholic Encyclopedia gives “about 800,” Wikipedia c. 808. His death is placed on 30 October 868 (the Catholic Encyclopedia: “probable 30 October, 868 (or 869)”), after roughly two decades’ imprisonment.12
