Augustine of Hippo on predestination, grace & free will

Dates: c. 412–429 (the anti-Pelagian works) · Wikipedia
Summary: Augustine (354–430), bishop of Hippo, is the hinge of the whole doctrine. Forced by the Pelagian crisis to define grace, he taught that grace is necessary from the very first stirring of faith — that even the will to believe (the initium fidei) is God’s gift, not man’s own contribution; that predestination is simply God’s foreknowledge and preparation of the gifts by which the elect are certainly saved, prior to and not on the ground of any foreseen merit or faith; and that grace, so far from destroying free will, heals and establishes it. He reached this position against his own earlier view, which he openly retracted. Both later camps — those who softened him (the “semi-Pelagians”) and those who hardened him into double predestination — are measured against him.

Claims & evidence

Grace is necessary from the very beginning — even the will to believe is God’s gift

Let the objector, however, attentively observe that this will is to be ascribed to the divine gift, not merely because it arises from our free will, which was created naturally with us… it surely follows that it is God who both works in man the willing to believe, and in all things prevents us with His mercy.

— On the Spirit and the Letter LX [XXXIV], c. 412, source.1 This is the whole Augustinian axis in one sentence, and the point on which the later “semi-Pelagian” compromise would break: the very act of assenting to God’s call is not a work man brings to grace but a gift God works in him. Against Pelagius’s teaching that God’s help extends only to the capacity, leaving the willing and the doing to man, Augustine insists that God “prevents” (goes before) even the willing.

He reached this against his own earlier view, which he retracted

And it was chiefly by this testimony that I myself also was convinced when I was in a similar error, thinking that faith whereby we believe on God is not God’s gift, but that it is in us from ourselves… And this my error is sufficiently indicated in some small works of mine written before my episcopate.

— On the Predestination of the Saints I.7–8 [IV], c. 429, source.2 The “small works” he names are his early Exposition of Certain Propositions from the Epistle to the Romans and the first book To Simplician (c. 394–396) — where the young Augustine had still made the initial act of faith man’s own, to which God then responds. The mature Augustine says he was “convinced” otherwise by 1 Corinthians 4:7 (“What hast thou that thou didst not receive?”): if faith itself is among the gifts, it cannot be the one thing a man contributes before grace. His development, not just his conclusion, is doctrinally load-bearing — the semi-Pelagians would later try to hold precisely the position he had abandoned.

What predestination is: God’s foreknowledge and preparation of His own gifts

This is the predestination of the saints,—nothing else; to wit, the foreknowledge and the preparation of God’s kindnesses, whereby they are most certainly delivered, whoever they are that are delivered.

— On the Gift of Perseverance 35 [XIV], c. 429, source.3 Augustine keeps predestination and grace carefully distinct: “predestination is the preparation for grace, while grace is the donation itself… predestination of good is… the preparation of grace; which grace is the effect of that predestination.” Predestination is thus not a bare decree over faceless names but God’s foreknowing arrangement of the very gifts — faith, love, perseverance — by which the saved are in fact saved.4

Election is not on the ground of foreseen faith

Let us, then, understand the calling whereby they become elected,—not those who are elected because they have believed, but who are elected that they may believe… He chose them that they might be so, not because they were already so.

— On the Predestination of the Saints I.34 [XVII], c. 429, source.5 This is the exact point later disputed by the Massilians, by Molina, and by the Remonstrants at Dort: does God elect those He foresees will believe, or does He elect them in order that they may believe? Augustine takes the second, quoting Christ’s “Ye have not chosen me, but I have chosen you.” Election is the source of the faith, not its reward.

Grace does not destroy free will but establishes it

Do we then by grace make void free will? God forbid! Nay, rather we establish free will. For even as the law by faith, so free will by grace, is not made void, but established… since grace cures the will whereby righteousness is freely loved.

— On the Spirit and the Letter LII [XXX], c. 412, source.6 Augustine is not a determinist who abolishes the will; his whole treatise On Grace and Free Will is a sustained both-and, holding that Scripture commends the two together — “work out your own salvation with fear and trembling, for it is God which worketh in you both to will and to do.” The will is real, but a healed will: liberated by grace to love the good it could not, wounded, freely choose.

God crowns His own gifts, not our merits

It is His own gifts that God crowns, not your merits,—if, at least, your merits are of your own self, not of Him… If, then, your good merits are God’s gifts, God does not crown your merits as your merits, but as His own gifts.

— On Grace and Free Will 15 [VI], c. 426/427, source.7 This is the maxim that both later traditions quote back at each other — the Reformers to exclude merit from the ground of salvation, Trent to insist that the reward of grace-enabled works is still God’s own gift being crowned. The same line is weighed, as read on both sides, on the justification timeline and the treasury-of-merit congruent-and-condign-merit page.

Final perseverance to the end is itself a gift of God

I assert, therefore, that the perseverance by which we persevere in Christ even to the end is the gift of God; and I call that the end by which is finished that life wherein alone there is peril of falling.

— On the Gift of Perseverance 1 [I], c. 429, source.8 The gift of perseverance is the sharpest edge of the doctrine and the one the Massilian monks found pastorally intolerable: if even staying faithful to the end is God’s gift given to some and not others, then the difference between the saved and the lost lies finally in God’s hidden choice, not in human effort. This is the claim John Cassian would soften and the Council of Orange would receive without following Augustine all the way into predestination to death.

Notes

  • Augustine is claimed by every later party. The Massilians (“semi-Pelagians”) revered him yet recoiled from unconditional election and the gift of perseverance; Prosper of Aquitaine defended him against them; the Council of Orange (529) canonized his grace-alone teaching on the initium fidei while pointedly refusing predestination to evil; Gottschalk pressed him into a symmetrical double predestination and was condemned; and the sixteenth-century Reformers and the Council of Trent alike appealed to him. The wiki does not resolve the doctrine by counting his votes on each side — it reads him as the source from which the later divisions branch.
  • The unresolved edge: single or double? Augustine plainly teaches predestination of the elect to life and speaks of the “reprobate” whom God passes by; whether his logic requires a symmetrical predestination of the lost to death (Gottschalk’s reading) or only a just preterition that leaves them in the fault they chose is a genuine dispute queued as an argument page for this timeline.
  • Augustine’s doctrine of original sin — that the whole race fell in Adam into a “mass of perdition” from which grace rescues some — is the root of the whole quarrel with Pelagius, but it is a distinct doctrine; this page stays on grace, election, and the will. He appears across this wiki also as a witness on justification, infant baptism, and many other timelines.
