Augustine of Hippo on predestination, grace & free will

Dates: c. 412–429 (the anti-Pelagian works) · Wikipedia
Summary: Augustine (354–430), bishop of Hippo, wrote against Pelagius and his followers from c. 412 to his death. In these works he taught that grace is necessary from the very first stirring of faith — that even the will to believe (the initium fidei) is God’s gift; that predestination is God’s foreknowledge and preparation of the gifts by which the elect are delivered, and that the elect are chosen not because they already believed but in order that they may believe; that grace heals and establishes free will rather than destroying it; and that final perseverance is God’s gift. He describes this as a change from his own earlier view, which he retracted.

Claims & evidence

Grace and the will to believe

Let the objector, however, attentively observe that this will is to be ascribed to the divine gift, not merely because it arises from our free will, which was created naturally with us… it surely follows that it is God who both works in man the willing to believe, and in all things prevents us with His mercy.

— On the Spirit and the Letter LX [XXXIV], c. 412, source.1 Here Augustine treats the act of assenting to God’s call as a gift God works in the believer. Against Pelagius’s teaching that God’s help extends to the capacity, with the willing and the doing left to man, Augustine holds that God “prevents” (goes before) the willing as well.

Augustine’s account of his own earlier view, which he retracted

And it was chiefly by this testimony that I myself also was convinced when I was in a similar error, thinking that faith whereby we believe on God is not God’s gift, but that it is in us from ourselves… And this my error is sufficiently indicated in some small works of mine written before my episcopate.

— On the Predestination of the Saints I.7–8 [IV], c. 429, source.2 Among the “small works” he names his early Exposition of Certain Propositions from the Epistle to the Romans, in which, he writes, consent to the gospel was “our own doing.” He dates the change to the first book of the two he “wrote in the very beginning of my episcopate to Simplicianus,” where he says the question was “solved,” and he says he was “convinced” chiefly by 1 Corinthians 4:7 (“What hast thou that thou hast not received?”). In the same treatise he writes that the monks of southern Gaul who disputed his teaching held the opinion he had held “at that time.”3

Augustine’s definition of predestination

This is the predestination of the saints,—nothing else; to wit, the foreknowledge and the preparation of God’s kindnesses, whereby they are most certainly delivered, whoever they are that are delivered.

— On the Gift of Perseverance 35 [XIV], c. 429, source.4 Augustine distinguishes predestination from grace: “predestination is the preparation for grace, while grace is the donation itself… predestination of good is… the preparation of grace; which grace is the effect of that predestination.” On this account predestination is God’s foreknowing arrangement of the gifts — faith, love, perseverance — by which the saved are saved.5

Election and foreseen faith: “elected that they may believe”

Let us, then, understand the calling whereby they become elected,—not those who are elected because they have believed, but who are elected that they may believe… He chose them that they might be so, not because they were already so.

— On the Predestination of the Saints I.34 [XVII], c. 429, source.6 Augustine frames the alternative as whether God elects those He foresees will believe or elects them in order that they may believe, and takes the second, quoting Christ’s “Ye have not chosen me, but I have chosen you.” The same question was argued by the monks of southern Gaul in his lifetime and, later, in the disputes recorded on this timeline under Molina and under Arminius and the Synod of Dort.

Grace and free will: “we establish free will”

Do we then by grace make void free will? God forbid! Nay, rather we establish free will. For even as the law by faith, so free will by grace, is not made void, but established… since grace cures the will whereby righteousness is freely loved.

— On the Spirit and the Letter LII [XXX], c. 412, source.7 In On Grace and Free Will Augustine argues that Scripture commends grace and free will together — “work out your own salvation with fear and trembling, for it is God which worketh in you both to will and to do.” On his account the will is real and is healed by grace, so that it freely loves the good.

“It is His own gifts that God crowns”

It is His own gifts that God crowns, not your merits,—if, at least, your merits are of your own self, not of Him… If, then, your good merits are God’s gifts, God does not crown your merits as your merits, but as His own gifts.

— On Grace and Free Will 15 [VI], c. 426/427, source.8 The same line is cited in the Reformation-era debates over merit and is traced on the justification timeline.

Perseverance to the end as a gift of God

I assert, therefore, that the perseverance by which we persevere in Christ even to the end is the gift of God; and I call that the end by which is finished that life wherein alone there is peril of falling.

— On the Gift of Perseverance 1 [I], c. 429, source.9 On this teaching, perseverance to the end is God’s gift, given to some and not to others. Prosper of Aquitaine’s letter to Augustine (c. 428/429) reported the objections of monks at Marseilles to this teaching; the treatise On the Gift of Perseverance is Augustine’s reply (see Prosper of Aquitaine).

Reception and debate

  • Later appeals to Augustine. Later parties on this timeline cite him: Prosper of Aquitaine wrote in his defence against the monks of Marseilles; the Council of Orange (529) drew many of its canons from his teaching on the initium fidei and anathematized the claim that any are foreordained to evil; and Gottschalk of Orbais and his ninth-century opponents, and the sixteenth- and seventeenth-century parties recorded under Erasmus, Luther, Jansen and Arminius, each appealed to him. The Orthodox historian John Meyendorff (Byzantine Theology, 1974) writes of “the extraordinary impact upon Western thought of Augustine’s polemics against Pelagius and Julian of Eclanum,” and that “in the Byzantine world where Augustinian thought exercised practically no influence, the significance of the sin of Adam and of its consequences for mankind was understood along quite different lines.”10
  • Single or twofold predestination. In the Enchiridion Augustine writes that God turns the creature’s evil will to good account “to the condemnation of those whom in His justice He has predestined to punishment, and to the salvation of those whom in His mercy He has predestined to grace.”11 Later readers differ on whether he teaches a predestination of the reprobate to punishment set beside that of the elect, or one predestination, of the elect, with the rest foreknown. Gottschalk of Orbais read him as teaching a twofold predestination, of the elect to life and of the reprobate to death; Hincmar of Reims and the Second Synod of Quiersy (853) affirmed one predestination, of the elect, with the reprobate foreknown; the Council of Valence (855), confessing “the predestination of the impious to death,” cited “Saint Augustine” for “so fixed a decree” in God concerning the punishment their evil deserved (see Gottschalk of Orbais).12 John Calvin (Reformed, Institutes III.xxiii.5, 1559) writes: “I say with Augustine, that the Lord has created those who, as he certainly foreknew, were to go to destruction, and he did so because he so willed.”13 Eugène Portalié (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1907) holds that Augustine “formulated (not invented)” a doctrine in which the eternal choice of the elect “does not destroy the Divine will to save all men,” and that his teaching was not grasped by “the Reformers of the sixteenth century” or by later Protestant critics “who father that doctrine on St. Augustine.”14 Peter Sammons (Reformed, Reprobation: from Augustine to the Synod of Dort, 2020) writes: “Regarding the doctrine of reprobation, Augustine was the first to explain it in detail.” He presents his survey as establishing “the positive affirmation of reprobation by every generation from Augustine to the Synod of Dort.”15
  • Augustine’s doctrine of original sin — that the whole race fell in Adam into a “mass of perdition” from which grace rescues some — is traced on the original sin timeline; this page stays on grace, election, and the will. He appears on this wiki also as a witness on justification, infant baptism, and other timelines.
