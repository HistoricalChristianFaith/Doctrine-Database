Fulgentius of Ruspe on predestination, grace & free will

Dates: c. 520 (his predestination treatises, written in Sardinian exile) · Fulgentius c. 468–533 · Wikipedia
Summary: Fulgentius of Ruspe, bishop of a small see in Byzacena (Roman North Africa) and twice exiled to Sardinia under the Arian Vandal kings, wrote in the century between Augustine (d. 430) and the Second Council of Orange (529). The Catholic Encyclopedia describes him as “saturated with” Augustine’s writings. He taught an election resting on God’s will and not on foreseen merit, a fixed number of the elect, and faith itself as a divine gift; and, answering his correspondent Monimus, he taught that God predestines the reprobate to punishment, justly, for their own foreseen sins, but never to sin itself.

Claims & evidence

Predestination as God’s gracious empowering of the elect

The reason for saying “he destined us in love” is that he empowered us with his gracious love in order to predestine us.

— On the Truth of Predestination 3.5 (De veritate praedestinationis et gratiae Dei), on Ephesians 1:5, source.1 Fulgentius reads Paul’s “destined us in love” as God’s gracious love empowering the elect, and identifies that empowering with the predestining.

The fixed number of the elect

The eternal firmness and firm eternity of God’s predestinating will consist not only in the ordaining of works. God also knows in advance the number of the elect. No one of that full number may lose his eternal grace, nor may any outside that total attain the gift of eternal salvation. For God, who knows all things before they come to pass, is not confused about the number of the predestined, any more than he doubts the effectiveness of the works he has ordained.

— On the Truth of Predestination 3.6, on Ephesians 1:5, source.2 Fulgentius here teaches a fixed number of the predestined, none of whom can be lost and to which none outside can be added; Augustine speaks of the same fixed number (certus numerus electorum) in On Rebuke and Grace.

Faith as a gift, and the distribution of grace

The blessed Paul argues that we are saved by faith, which he declares to be not from us but a gift from God. Thus there cannot possibly be true salvation where there is no true faith, and, since this faith is divinely enabled, it is without doubt bestowed by his free generosity. Where there is true belief through true faith, true salvation certainly accompanies it. Anyone who departs from true faith will not possess the grace of true salvation.

— On the Incarnation 1 (De incarnatione et gratia), on Ephesians 2:8, source.3 Fulgentius calls faith God’s gift, “not from us,” “divinely enabled.” The Catholic Encyclopedia reports his teaching that grace is not given to all alike: “It is to think unworthily of grace, to suppose that it is given to all men.”4

Predestination to punishment, not to sin (To Monimus)

The evil are not predestined to do evil, drawn away and enticed by their own concupiscence; rather, they are predestined to this: that, against their will, they suffer justly. For the term predestination does not express some compulsory necessity of the human will, but it foretells the eternal disposition, merciful and just, of a future divine operation. The Church, however, sings to God of mercy and judgment, to God, whose predestination is operative in man in such a way that by a hidden though not unjust resolution of His will, He may either award a gratuitous mercy to the wretched or weigh out due justice to the unrighteous.

— To Monimus 1.7.1 (Ad Monimum).5 Fulgentius’s three books Ad Monimum (c. 518–523) were written, according to the Catholic Encyclopedia, for a friend who thought Augustine taught that God predestines evil. Fulgentius answers that God predestines the reprobate to the punishment their sins deserve, not to the sins themselves. God “did not promise… but only foretold a torment for the punishment of the unrighteous”; He “was never the perpetrator of depravity, because no one ever was depraved except insofar as he withdrew from God,” and “nor did God predestine any to withdraw from Him, although the divine knowledge foresaw who would so withdraw.”6 Paul, he noted, calls them “vessels of wrath” (Rom 9:22), not vessels of sin. On this account there is a praedestinatio ad poenam (predestination to punishment), as merited, alongside the unmerited predestination of the elect to grace and glory, and no predestination to evil: the one who deserts God is deserted by God.

In the ninth-century controversy Gottschalk of Orbais, who confessed a gemina praedestinatio, drew on Augustinian and Fulgentian texts, and Prudentius of Troyes and the Synod of Valence (855) confessed a predestination of the elect to life and of the impious to death (⚠ unverified as to the use of Fulgentius’s texts by name).

Reception and debate

  • Placement on the timeline. Fulgentius sits between Augustine (d. 430) / Prosper (c. 429–434) and Orange (529). Like the conclusion of Orange, which anathematizes belief that any are foreordained “to evil,” he denies a predestination to sin.
  • Beyond scope here. Fulgentius also led the African exiles’ reply to the Scythian monks in the theopaschite controversy (“one of the Trinity suffered in the flesh”); that debate concerns Christology rather than the grace-and-will question traced on this timeline.
