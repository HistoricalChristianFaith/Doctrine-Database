Fulgentius of Ruspe on predestination, grace & free will

Dates: c. 520 (his predestination treatises, written in Sardinian exile) · Fulgentius c. 468–533 · Wikipedia
Summary: Fulgentius of Ruspe, bishop of a small see in Byzacena (Roman North Africa) and twice exiled to Sardinia under the Arian Vandals, was the most rigorous Augustinian of the generation between Augustine (d. 430) and the Second Council of Orange (529) — so faithful a summarizer of his master that later ages called him the “abbreviated Augustine” (Augustinus abbreviatus). He taught an unconditional election resting on God’s secret will (not on foreseen merit), a fixed and numbered company of the elect, and faith itself as a divine gift — and, pressed by his correspondent Monimus, he worked out the careful twofold formula that the ninth-century controversy would turn on: God predestines the reprobate to punishment (justly, for their own foreseen sins) but never to sin itself, since God cannot be the author of evil. He is the chief bridge carrying Augustine’s hardest teaching intact across the century to Gottschalk.

Claims & evidence

Predestination is God’s gracious empowering of the elect

The reason for saying “he destined us in love” is that he empowered us with his gracious love in order to predestine us.

— On the Truth of Predestination 3.5 (De veritate praedestinationis et gratiae Dei), on Ephesians 1:5, source.1 Fulgentius reads Paul’s “predestined… in love” as making the whole movement God’s: it is God’s gracious love that empowers, and that empowering is the predestining. Grace is not a response to something already in the elect but the very thing that constitutes them elect.

The number of the elect is fixed and cannot be added to or lost from

The eternal firmness and firm eternity of God’s predestinating will consist not only in the ordaining of works. God also knows in advance the number of the elect. No one of that full number may lose his eternal grace, nor may any outside that total attain the gift of eternal salvation. For God, who knows all things before they come to pass, is not confused about the number of the predestined, any more than he doubts the effectiveness of the works he has ordained.

— On the Truth of Predestination 3.6, on Ephesians 1:5, source.2 This is Augustine’s certus numerus electorum at its most explicit: the predestined are a fixed company, none of whom can be lost and to which none outside can be added. Election is therefore effectual, not merely offered — the strong Augustinian edge that Cassian’s both-and and the Massilians had recoiled from, and that Orange would receive only in part.

Faith itself is a gift, not from us — and grace is not given to all

The blessed Paul argues that we are saved by faith, which he declares to be not from us but a gift from God. Thus there cannot possibly be true salvation where there is no true faith, and, since this faith is divinely enabled, it is without doubt bestowed by his free generosity. Where there is true belief through true faith, true salvation certainly accompanies it. Anyone who departs from true faith will not possess the grace of true salvation.

— On the Incarnation 1 (De incarnatione et gratia), on Ephesians 2:8, source.3 For Fulgentius even the initium fidei — the first assent of faith — is God’s gift, “not from us,” “divinely enabled.” And because saving faith is a gift God bestows where He wills, grace is not distributed to all alike: it was “to think unworthily of grace, to suppose that it is given to all men.” This particularism is the counterpart to the fixed number of the elect — and the point at which his rigorism runs hardest against the later reading that God wills every individual’s salvation.4

God predestines the reprobate to punishment, but never to sin

The evil are not predestined to do evil, drawn away and enticed by their own concupiscence; rather, they are predestined to this: that, against their will, they suffer justly. For the term predestination does not express some compulsory necessity of the human will, but it foretells the eternal disposition, merciful and just, of a future divine operation. The Church, however, sings to God of mercy and judgment, to God, whose predestination is operative in man in such a way that by a hidden though not unjust resolution of His will, He may either award a gratuitous mercy to the wretched or weigh out due justice to the unrighteous.

— To Monimus 1.7.1 (Ad Monimum).5 Fulgentius’s three books Ad Monimum (c. 518–523) were written to answer a friend, Monimus, who had read Augustine as teaching that God predestines evil — and, on the rebound, urged Fulgentius to embrace a full double predestination and stop merely saying God foresaw the wickedness of the lost. Fulgentius’s answer became the standard Augustinian solution: God does predestine the reprobate — but to the punishment their sins deserve, not to the sins themselves. God “did not promise… but only foretold a torment for the punishment of the unrighteous”; He “was never the perpetrator of depravity, because no one ever was depraved except insofar as he withdrew from God,” and “nor did God predestine any to withdraw from Him, although the divine knowledge foresaw who would so withdraw.”6 Paul, he noted, calls them “vessels of wrath” (Rom 9:22), not vessels of sin. So there is a genuine praedestinatio ad poenam (predestination to punishment), just because it is merited, alongside the unmerited predestination of the elect to grace and glory — but no predestination to evil, for the one who deserts God is deserted by God.

This is the formula the ninth century inherited. When Gottschalk of Orbais pressed a gemina praedestinatio, he marshalled a florilegium of Augustinian and Fulgentian texts; and Hincmar’s opponents at Valence, and Prudentius of Troyes, confessed exactly Fulgentius’s shape — predestination of the elect to life and of the punishment (not the sin) of the reprobate. Fulgentius is thus the pivot: he keeps Augustine’s reprobation a real act of the divine will while resting the death on foreseen demerit, the very asymmetry that the double-predestination argument turns on.

Notes

  • Where he stands on the timeline. Fulgentius fills the long gap between Augustine (d. 430) / Prosper (c. 429–434) and Orange (529). He holds Augustine’s hardest line — unconditional election, a fixed number of the elect, grace not given to all — where Orange, a few years later, would receive grace-alone-to-begin but stop short of double predestination and anathematize any foreordination “to evil.” Fulgentius and Orange do not conflict on that last point: Fulgentius, too, denies predestination to sin. They differ in emphasis — his particularism (grace not for all, a numbered elect) is sterner than Orange’s pastoral settlement.
  • Key witness for the double-predestination argument. He is the chief patristic steelman for a real decree of reprobation-to-punishment that still refuses to make God the author of sin — the position that keeps that argument contested rather than weak. See Double predestination — is there a positive decree of reprobation?
  • Beyond scope here. Fulgentius also led the African exiles’ reply to the Scythian monks in the theopaschite controversy (“one of the Trinity suffered in the flesh”); that debate touches Christology, not the grace-and-will question traced on this timeline, and is noted only in passing.
