Prosper of Aquitaine on predestination, grace & free will

Dates: c. 429–434 (the Massilian controversy) · Prosper c. 390–455 · Wikipedia
Summary: Prosper of Aquitaine, a lay theologian of southern Gaul, was Augustine’s foremost defender in the generation after his death. When the monks of Marseilles (the “Massilians,” later called semi-Pelagians) accepted grace yet insisted that the beginning of faith is man’s own first step, Prosper answered that faith itself, and perseverance to the end, are wholly God’s gift — “without whose grace no one runs to grace.” With his friend Hilary he wrote to the aged Augustine (prompting the reply-treatises On the Predestination of the Saints and On the Gift of Perseverance), appealed to Rome, and attacked Cassian’s thirteenth Conference by name in his Against the Collator. He is the bridge from Augustine to the settled answer at Orange.

Claims & evidence

The Massilian error: making obedience prior to grace, so the beginning of salvation is man’s

And so, since in those who have reached the age of free will there are two things that effect man’s salvation — namely the grace of God and the obedience of man — they want obedience to be prior to grace, so that the beginning of salvation is to be believed to stand from him who is saved, not from him who saves; and that the will of man procures for itself the help of divine grace, rather than grace subjecting the human will to itself.

— Letter to Augustine §6 (c. 428/429), the wiki’s translation from the Latin, source.1 Prosper states the Massilian position with precision, and puts his finger on the exact hinge: do we say “the beginning of salvation… stands from him who is saved” or from “him who saves”? The monks of Marseilles revered Augustine but recoiled from unconditional election and the gift of perseverance as pastorally ruinous — they granted that grace increases faith while holding that man begins it. It was this letter (with Hilary’s) that moved Augustine to write his last two anti-Pelagian works in reply.

Faith is God’s gift — “without whose grace no one runs to grace”

But let us ascribe the unbelief of men to men themselves; the faith of men, however, let us confess to be the gift of God, without whose grace no one runs to grace.

— Answers to the Objections of the Gauls (Pro Augustino Responsiones), Response 8 (c. 431/432), the wiki’s translation from the Latin, source.2 Prosper’s formula — sine cujus gratia nemo currit ad gratiam — is the whole Augustinian answer in seven words: a man cannot even “run to” grace except by grace. He is careful to keep the asymmetry: unbelief is man’s own, but faith is God’s gift. In the same set he grants that free will then increases “what has been begun in him by the grace of Christ,” the help of God “never removed” — anticipating the cooperation-after-grace that Orange would define.

Against Cassian: from first to last, the good work is God’s

What of virtue or of piety is excepted, that has not flowed from the fount of grace, where from the beginning right to the end both the inception and the perfection of the good work is attributed to the Lord?

— Against the Collator (Contra Collatorem), ch. 16 (c. 433/434), the wiki’s translation from the Latin, source.3 The “Collator” is Cassian, author of the Collationes (Conferences); Prosper’s title is a pointed jab. He works through Conference 13 step by step, turning Cassian’s own concessions against him: Cassian had admitted grace gives “both the beginning of a good work and perseverance,” yet elsewhere let the first movement be man’s — and Prosper presses that a good work cannot be God’s at the end if it is man’s at the start. This is the sharpest single-author attack on the semi-Pelagian middle, and it frames the question Orange would answer a century later.

Notes

  • Prosper softens over time. The early Prosper defends even the starkest Augustinian language on predestination; the later Prosper (esp. in The Call of All Nations) emphasizes God’s universal salvific will and is more reticent about reprobation — a trajectory that itself foreshadows Orange’s balance (grace-alone to begin, but no predestination to evil). The verified quotations here are from his combative middle period, 429–434.
  • A gap in the sourcing. The standard English translation of these works (P. De Letter, Prosper of Aquitaine: Defense of St. Augustine, ACW 32, 1963) is in copyright and not freely linkable; the blockquotes above are the wiki’s own renderings from the verbatim Latin of Migne’s Patrologia Latina (served via The Latin Library and Latin Wikisource), given verbatim in the footnotes so a reader can check them. A reader with the De Letter volume can compare its published English.
  • Prosper’s appeal to Rome drew from Pope Celestine the letter Apostolici verba (431), praising Augustine and rebuking the Gallic bishops’ laxity toward “the enemies of grace” — a step on the road that ends at Orange (529) and its papal confirmation.
