Does the absence of Hanukkah date the Bene Israel’s departure before the Maccabees?

Type: historical argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: weak — the argument from the missing feast cannot distinguish an observance never received from one lost in a community that demonstrably lost far more (Hebrew, the whole rabbinic tradition), and it is contradicted by the genetic dating of the Jewish–Indian admixture to the last millennium; it is a real and interesting datum, but it does not carry the two-thousand-year weight placed on it · a specific dating prop for the ancient-descent claim on the Bene Israel timeline.

The claim

The Bene Israel keep the Sabbath, circumcision, the dietary laws, and the older festivals — but not Hanukkah. They must therefore have left the land of Israel before Hanukkah arose, that is, before the Maccabean victory of c. 165 BCE.

This is the community’s best-known internal proof of great antiquity, and it is genuinely ingenious. Hanukkah commemorates the rededication of the Temple under the Maccabees in 164 BCE; a Jewish community that never knew it, the argument runs, must have been cut off from the main body of Jewry before the feast was instituted — which would push the Bene Israel departure back to the persecution of Antiochus Epiphanes (c. 175–164 BCE) or earlier, and make them, in effect, a pre-Maccabean remnant. The same reasoning is sometimes extended to the absence of Purim. The interpretation this page weighs is that specific inference: that the missing feast dates the departure to before c. 165 BCE.1

The evidence for

The datum is real, and that is the argument’s strength. The Bene Israel did preserve an unusually old-looking core — the Shema, the seventh-day Sabbath, eighth-day circumcision, the dietary laws, and some festivals — while lacking the post-biblical feast par excellence. If a community’s observances were a clean stratigraphy, freezing at the moment of separation, the absence of a feast known to be instituted in 164 BCE would indeed set an upper bound on the date of separation, and the inference would be sound. The argument also has the merit of pointing to something the community genuinely did not practise, rather than resting on a resemblance that could be read either way.2

Adversarial assessment

It is an argument from silence, and the silence is over-determined. The inference works only if a missing observance can mean just one thing — that it was never received. But it can equally mean it was received and then lost, and the Bene Israel are the textbook case of a community that lost things: over their long isolation they lost Hebrew, lost literacy in their own scriptures, lost the entire rabbinic tradition, and kept only a handful of the most deeply-embedded practices. A community that could lose Hebrew could certainly lose Hanukkah — a relatively minor, home-centred, rabbinically-elaborated feast — without that loss telling us anything about the date of departure. The very isolation the tradition invokes to explain the community’s survival also dissolves the argument: in deep isolation, feasts erode, and the deepest-rooted observances (Sabbath, circumcision, diet) are exactly the ones most likely to survive when the rest falls away.3

The genetics contradicts the conclusion directly. Even granting the inference every benefit, it reaches a conclusion — departure before c. 165 BCE — that the population-genetic evidence flatly denies. The fullest study (Waldman, Biddanda, et al., 2016) dates the Jewish–Indian admixture to the last millennium, roughly nineteen to thirty-three generations ago, not to the second century BCE. Whatever the Jewish input to the Bene Israel was, it entered the Konkan population well over a thousand years after the missing feast supposedly fixes the departure. A dating argument whose conclusion is off by more than a millennium against the hard evidence cannot be sustained.4

What survives. The observation itself is not worthless: the absence of Hanukkah is a genuine and telling feature of Bene Israel practice, consistent with an old and biblically-rooted (rather than rabbinically-shaped) Judaism, and it belongs in any honest description of the community. What fails is the dating load placed on it — the leap from “they did not keep Hanukkah” to “they left before 165 BCE.” The feature tells us the community’s Judaism was archaic in cast; it does not tell us when the community began.5

Assessment

Weak. The missing-Hanukkah argument does not hold on its best case. As an argument from silence it cannot distinguish a feast never received from one lost in isolation, and it is advanced by the one community whose history — the loss of Hebrew, of literacy, of the whole rabbinic tradition — makes the “lost, not never-had” reading the more natural one. And its conclusion is contradicted head-on by the genetic dating of the admixture to the last millennium. It is rated weak rather than unsupported because the underlying observation is real and points to something true about the archaic character of Bene Israel Judaism — but the specific inference, that the feast’s absence dates the departure to before the Maccabees, does not survive scrutiny.
