The First Vision — the multiple accounts

Joseph Smith's foundational theophany — the First Vision, dated to the spring of about 1820, in which he says he prayed in a grove near Palmyra, New York, and saw the divine — is the origin story of the whole Latter-day Saint Restoration. It is also preserved in several accounts he wrote or authorized between 1832 and 1844, together with reports set down by others who heard him tell it. Those accounts do not agree on load-bearing details. They differ on how many divine personages appeared (the earliest, holograph account of 1832 describes the Lord alone and centers on the forgiveness of Joseph's sins; the canonical 1838 account describes two Personages, the Father and the Son, and centers on which church to join), and on his age, his motive, and the revival that is said to have prompted the prayer.1 This page sets those tellings side by side as a source-critical timeline: the “witnesses” below are the datable accounts of the vision, ordered earliest-source-first by the date each was written or published — not by the 1820 event they all report, which is fixed, but by when each record of it was made, because the growth across the records is exactly what the page examines.

One discipline governs the page. A real vision is not excluded because it is miraculous: this wiki does not count a theophany weak merely for requiring the supernatural, and “visions cannot happen” is no part of the argument here. The critical leverage is entirely textual and source-critical — the divergences among Joseph's own successive tellings — and that is a different question from whether the event occurred. Keep the two sharp: that God could appear to Joseph does not settle what Joseph saw or said on any one occasion, and the record we can weigh is the record of the tellings. This is adjudication, not anti-Mormon polemic. The faithful reading — that one man retold a genuine experience to different hearers over a dozen years, each time emphasizing what fit the moment — is a serious case; so is the developmental reading that the story grew in the telling. Both are steelmanned and rated honestly under Arguments below.

Related doctrines: The apostasy-and-restoration thesis — see also the Great Apostasy (the vision's verdict that the churches “were all wrong” is the seed of the total-apostasy premise) and the Book of Mormon and the material record (the positive scripture the same Restoration produced).

Timeline

The chronological witnesses are the datable accounts of the vision, earliest source first. A note on the ordering, as on the other source-critical pages here: the sequence is by the age of the record — when each account was written or printed — not by the age of the event, which every account places in about 1820. It is the change from record to record, 1832 to 1844, that the shelf is arranged to show.

The 1832 history — the earliest account, in Joseph's own hand (1832)

The first and only account written in Joseph Smith's own handwriting is a short, unpublished autobiography of about 1832, some twelve years after the event. Its vision is a conversion experience centered on the forgiveness of sins, and it describes one divine personage, “the Lord,” who identifies himself as the crucified Christ. Convicted of his sins, Joseph writes that “there was no society or denomination that built upon the gospel of Jesus Christ as recorded in the new testament,” and then, praying in his sixteenth year:

a piller of fire light above the brightness of the sun at noon day come down from above and rested upon me and I was filled with the spirit of god and the <Lord> opened the heavens upon me and I saw the Lord and he spake unto me saying Joseph <my son> thy sins are forgiven thee. go thy <way> walk in my statutes and keep my commandments behold I am the Lord of glory I was crucifyed for the world

The which-church question is present only as background (Joseph has already concluded that the churches have “apostatised”), and there is no Father-and-Son, no command to join none, and no man-of-sin creeds “abomination” language. The single figure both forgives and is identified as the crucified Lord.1

The 1835 journal accounts — two personages, and “many angels” (1835)

Two entries in Joseph's 1835 journal record him telling the story aloud. On 9 November 1835, recounting it to a visitor (the imposter “Joshua the Jewish minister,” Robert Matthews), the scribe Warren Parrish set down a version with two personages and, uniquely, “many angels”:

a personage appeard in the midst of this pillar of flame which was spread all around, and yet nothing consumed, another personage soon appeard like unto the first, he said unto me thy sins are forgiven thee, he testifyed unto me that Jesus Christ is the Son of God; <and I saw many angels in this vision> I was about 14 years old when I received this first communication

Here the second figure — not the first — forgives and bears witness that Christ is the Son of God, and the “many angels” appear in no other telling. A brief entry five days later, to Erastus Holmes (14 November 1835), calls the same experience simply “the first visitation of Angels which was when I was about 14. years old.” The age has shifted from “sixteenth year” (1832) to “about 14,” and the second personage has appeared, three years before the canonical account fixes the two.2

The 1838–39 history — the canonical account (1838)

The version now scripture — Joseph Smith—History in the Pearl of Great Price, drawn from the history begun in 1838 — is the fullest and the most institutional. It is set against a named revival (“there was in the place where we lived an unusual excitement on the subject of religion”), dates the event “early in the spring of eighteen hundred and twenty,” puts Joseph “at this time in my fifteenth year,” and makes the animating question ecclesiastical: “which of all the sects was right … which I should join.” The theophany is now unambiguously of two Personages, one presenting the other:

I saw two Personages, whose brightness and glory defy all description, standing above me in the air. One of them spake unto me, calling me by name and said, pointing to the other—This is My Beloved Son. Hear Him!

And the answer is the total-apostasy verdict that grounds the Restoration:

I was answered that I must join none of them, for they were all wrong; and the Personage who addressed me said that all their creeds were an abomination in his sight; that those professors were all corrupt

Every distinctive of the mature story — the Father and the Son as two beings, the revival, the demand to know which church to join, the sweeping condemnation of the creeds — is here, and several are here for the first time.3

Orson Pratt's Remarkable Visions — the first published account (1840)

The vision first reached print not from Joseph but from the apostle Orson Pratt, whose 1840 Edinburgh pamphlet An Interesting Account of Several Remarkable Visions gave the two-personage story to the public for the first time. Pratt's account, contemporary with the 1838 history and independent of it in wording, has the same two figures:

he saw two glorious personages, who exactly resembled each other in their features or likeness

and the same ecclesiastical verdict — that “all the religious denominations were believing in incorrect doctrines; and, consequently, that none of them was acknowledged of God, as his church and kingdom.” The “exactly resembled each other” formula — the two beings as indistinguishable in likeness — recurs in the 1842 accounts and is the period's characteristic phrasing.4

The Wentworth Letter — Joseph's public compression (1842)

Written for the newspaper editor John Wentworth and printed in Times and Seasons on 1 March 1842, this is Joseph's own compressed public telling. It keeps the two personages and the “exactly resembled” formula, and gives the churches' error as doctrinal:

I saw two glorious personages who exactly resembled each other in features, and likeness, surrounded with a brilliant light which eclipsed the sun at noon-day. They told me that all religious denominations were believing in incorrect doctrines, and that none of them was acknowledged of God as his church and kingdom.

The wording is milder than the 1838 “abomination… all corrupt” — a public letter to a non-Mormon editor — but the two-personage structure and the which-church answer are settled.5

Later reports by others — Hyde, Richards, and Neibaur (1842–1844)

Three further contemporary records, set down by men who heard Joseph, bracket the end of his life. Orson Hyde's German missionary tract Ein Ruf aus der Wüste (“A Cry out of the Wilderness,” 1842) — the first foreign-language account — has “two glorious heavenly personages … resembling each other exactly in features and stature,” commanding Joseph to “not join any of the religious sects.” But Levi Richards's journal for 11 June 1843 records only that Joseph “enquired of the Lord which of all the sects were right— re received for answer that none of them were right,” naming no personages at all. And the latest firsthand report, Alexander Neibaur's journal for 24 May 1844, gives a vivid, idiosyncratic retelling — “saw a personage in the fire … after a wile a other person came to the side of the first” — in which Joseph asks “must I join the Methodist Church” and is told “No= they are not my People.” The two-personage shape now dominates, but the tellings still vary in what each figure does and even in how many appear.6

The “First Vision Accounts” essay — the church reckons with the multiplicity (2013)

The most recent witness is the church's own. Having for generations taught the 1838 account alone, the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints published in its Gospel Topics essays a candid catalogue of all the accounts and a harmonizing frame for their differences:

The various accounts of the First Vision tell a consistent story, though naturally they differ in emphasis and detail. Historians expect that when an individual retells an experience in multiple settings to different audiences over many years, each account will emphasize various aspects of the experience and contain unique details.

The essay likens the variation to the differing accounts of Paul's Damascus vision and the Transfiguration, and reads the 1832 “the Lord” as not necessarily excluding a second personage. It is the institutional steelman the first argument below is built to weigh.7

Arguments

The contested questions the accounts raise, each weighed adversarially on its own page with an assessment of the interpretation it serves. The two rival readings of the divergences — that they are one memory told for different audiences, or that the story developed — are genuinely advocated positions and are given separate, grouped pages; the revival-dating question stands on its own.

Related arguments — the divergent accounts

The same body of evidence — the 1832–1844 tellings and their differences over the number of personages, Joseph's age, and the animating question — is read two opposite ways by two camps that both actually hold their reading. Each gets its own steelmanned page; both come out contested, from opposite directions.

Are the accounts harmonizable as one event told for different purposes? (assessment: contested)

One memory, many tellings — the faithful reading that a single genuine experience, retold to different hearers across twelve years, will naturally vary in emphasis and detail without contradiction, so that the 1832 “the Lord” and the 1838 “two Personages” are the same event differently framed. The core does stay stable across every account — a theophany, a verdict against the churches, a promised restoration — and ordinary memory does behave this way. What the reading strains to absorb is the direction of the changes: the earliest, most personal, own-hand account is the one missing the second personage and the ecclesiastical commission, and harmonizing has to treat exactly that account as merely abbreviated.8

Do the accounts show a later, theologically developed reconstruction? (assessment: contested)

The story that grew — the critical reading that the accounts trace a one-directional development (1832: one being, personal forgiveness → 1835: two personages and “many angels” → 1838: a fixed Father-and-Son answering which church to join), tracking Joseph's own maturing theology of a corporeal, plural Godhead. The trajectory is real and hard to explain away. But the reading overreaches if it treats variation itself as proof of invention — a genuine experience retold does vary — and the two-personage form already appears in 1835, three years before the canonical account, complicating a neat line from one being to two.9

Was there an 1820 revival near Palmyra to occasion the vision? (assessment: contested)

The revival that dates the vision — the 1838 account grounds the prayer in “an unusual excitement on the subject of religion” in about 1820, and the question is whether the record bears that out. Wesley Walters showed that the documented Palmyra church-membership revival falls in 1824–25, not 1820, and that the ministers Joseph's account seems to invoke were not in the district until then; Milton Backman answered that region-wide awakening across the Burned-over District in 1819–20 amply fits a claim about “the place where we lived” read broadly. The dispute turns on the scope of the phrase, and neither side lands a knockout.10
