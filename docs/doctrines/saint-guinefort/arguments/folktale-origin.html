Does the Guinefort story descend from the Indian “faithful animal wrongly slain” fable by the literary road west?

Disputed reading · on Saint Guinefort, the holy greyhound · witness: Stephen of Bourbon

Related readings: Where did the greyhound’s name and child-patronage come from? · What was the faun-invocation rite at Guinefort’s grove? · Do the later traces record one continuous cult?

The passage

“…the lord of which and his wife had a baby boy. One day… the baby [was left] alone in the cradle, [when] a huge serpent entered the house and approached the baby’s cradle. Seeing this, the greyhound… chased the serpent and, attacking it beneath the cradle, upset the cradle and bit the serpent all over… Finally, the dog killed it and threw it well away from the cradle… the knight… thought [the dog had devoured the child] and drew his sword and killed the dog. Then, when they went closer to the baby they found it safe and sound… they threw [the dog] into a well in front of the manor door, threw a great pile of stones on top of it, and planted trees beside it… the peasants… honoured the dog as a martyr, prayed to it when they were sick.”

— Stephen of Bourbon, De supersticione (c. 1250–1261), English in the Fordham Medieval Sourcebook.1

The question

Stephen’s story has the same plot as the tale of the faithful animal rashly killed (Aarne–Thompson–Uther type 178A), whose Indian versions include the Panchatantra fable of the Brahmin’s mongoose. What is disputed is how the Dombes peasants came by it: from the literary chain that carried the fable west, or as an oral folk tale independent of those texts.2

See also (on another doctrine): how the Buddha-legend reached the Christian West, on the Barlaam and Josaphat timeline.

An oral folk tale, full of thirteenth-century detail

On this reading the peasants had the story by word of mouth, told it as a local event, and joined it to a healing rite and a saint’s name.

Held by: Rebecca Rist (Reinardus, 2019); Jean-Claude Schmitt (Le Saint Lévrier, 1979; English 1983), as one of two routes.3

Lester K. Little, reviewing Schmitt, writes that “it is no easy matter to get from these precisely dated texts to the illiterate peasants who prayed to St. Guinefort,” and that “the oral traditions of the peasantry, which may be rooted in ancient Indo-European culture, are, according to Schmitt, as plausible a route as any direct descent from one of those twelfth-century texts.” Schmitt finds in the story details “not all found in earlier versions” that match thirteenth-century society: the chateau, the lord and lady, the male heir, a nurse, a greyhound held in esteem by a class of hunters, and a settlement called Neuville of recent foundation. In Little’s words, “the story is therefore much more than an old tale repeated in the thirteenth century; it is full of contemporary details.” Rist, citing Schmitt, notes that the first Latin translation of the Arabic line “does not appear in the West until several years after Stephen of Bourbon’s death, which suggests that the story he heard was not related to any of these.” The peasants “regarded the greyhound’s death as a matter of fact, not fiction,” and for Rist “the tale is certainly the product of folk culture.” On Schmitt’s reconstruction the tale is one strand of three, with a healing rite for sickly children and the name of a human Saint Guinefort: in Little’s summary, a saint’s name and cult “joined to a nexus of traditional folklore about languishing children and a very ancient legend about a canine martyr to babysitting.” The plot could settle in a place of its own, as the Welsh Gelert story settled at Beddgelert, and in the Dombes it became the story of a local saint.4

A descendant of the Indian fable by the literary road west

On this reading the Dombes story is the western end of a chain of written versions running from the Panchatantra to the medieval European collections.

Held by: ⚠ unverified — no named proponent located yet.5

The structural match is point for point. In the Panchatantra a Brahmin’s loyal mongoose, left to guard the infant, kills a snake at the cradle; the mother returns, sees the blood-smeared mouth, strikes the animal dead, and finds the baby safe beside the dead serpent. Stephen’s Guinefort has the guarding animal, the serpent at the cradle, the master’s killing on the evidence of blood, and the child found alive. Little reports the tale’s earliest known version “in sixth-century BC Sanskrit” (following Stith Thompson’s Motif-Index; the Panchatantra itself is dated between the third century BC and the fifth century AD, see the timeline) and versions in other languages “including one in Old French of 1155 and one in Latin of 1184,” decades before Stephen wrote. The fable passed from Sanskrit into the Arabic Kalīla wa-Dimna (eighth century) and on into Latin and vernacular collections: John of Capua’s Directorium vitae humanae (c. 1263–1278), the “Canis” story of the Seven Sages of Rome, and the Gesta Romanorum. Along that road the rescuer became a nobleman’s greyhound, the form the Dombes story has. D. L. Ashliman’s type-178A dossier sets the Indian and European texts side by side and traces the Panchatantra’s fables to Europe “through oral folklore channels and by way of Persian and Arabic translations.” The same eastern current carried the Buddha-legend into Christian hagiography as Barlaam and Josaphat. On this reading the twelfth-century French and Latin versions put the story within reach of the Dombes before Stephen heard it.6
