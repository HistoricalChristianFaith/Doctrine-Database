Is the faun-invocation changeling rite a genuine pre-Christian survival?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: plausible (leaning probable) — the exposure-and-exchange rite Stephen of Bourbon describes is a textbook changeling ritual, and the belief it enacts — that woodland spirits (fauni) steal a healthy infant and leave a sickly substitute that must be given back to reclaim the true child — belongs to a nature-spirit complex attested in Latin sources from antiquity (Augustine, Isidore) through the eleventh century (Papias) into the high Middle Ages; so a genuine folk survival, not a scribal invention, is the natural reading. What holds it short of sound is that the very word “fauni” is the learned inquisitor’s classicizing Latin, so direct pre-Christian continuity (Roman/Celtic religion → thirteenth-century Dombes) is inferred rather than documented, and an independent medieval crystallization of the same widespread changeling motif cannot be excluded · advanced by Jean-Claude Schmitt and the changeling-folklore literature.

The claim

This page weighs the “practice” leg of Schmitt’s three-strand reconstruction (the other two — the faithful-hound fable and the human saint’s name — are weighed on their own pages). The interpretation: the healing ritual conducted at the greyhound’s grove is not an invention of the cult but a pre-existing magical rite for a “changeling” child — a genuine survival of pre-Christian nature-spirit and child-exchange belief, absorbed into the folk cult of the dog-saint. The rite is preserved in unusual detail on Stephen of Bourbon’s page: offerings of salt, swaddling-clothes hung on the bushes, nails driven into the trees, the naked infant thrown nine times between two tree-trunks from mother to old woman, and an explicit invocation of the fauns of the forest of Rimite to take back “their” sick changeling and return the true child “fat and well.”

“…they would pass the naked babies between the trunks of two trees… Invoking the demons, they called upon the fauns in the forest of Rimite to take the sick, feeble child which, they said, was theirs, and to return their child that the fauns had taken away, fat and well, safe and sound…”

— Stephen of Bourbon, De supersticione, English in the Fordham Medieval Sourcebook (from Lecoy de la Marche, Anecdotes historiques, 1877, tr. Paul Hyams).1

Who advances it

Jean-Claude Schmitt (Le Saint Lévrier, 1979; English The Holy Greyhound, 1983) makes the changeling healing-rite one of the three folk currents whose confluence produced the dog-saint — the strand independent of both the fable and the human martyr’s name. He reads the exposure rite as a way for adults to confront the reality of infant mortality: the sickly child “given back” to the fauns was often one already dying, so the cult had less to do with infanticide than with a ritual of reassurance around a death the community could not prevent. The wider changeling-folklore literature (D. L. Ashliman’s dossier) places the Guinefort rite squarely within the pan-European belief that supernatural beings steal a healthy infant and leave a substitute, recovered through rites of testing or exposure.2

The evidence for

The rite’s every feature is a known idiom of European changeling practice, not a local peculiarity. The core premise — that the fauni have exchanged the true child for a sickly one of their own, and can be induced to swap them back — is the classic changeling schema; the demand that the spirits take “their” child and return the parents’ own is its verbal signature. Passing or throwing the infant through a cleft between two trees, immersion (the child was, on some accounts, then plunged nine times in the Chalaronne), and exposure between lights are all recognized changeling-recovery and ordeal motifs; the sacred number nine and the offerings tie it to a wider folk-magical grammar.3

Crucially, the fauni themselves are not an isolated thirteenth-century word. The Latin fauni — the lesser woodland spirits, the rustic genii of Roman religion — run as a continuous demonic category through the Christian centuries. Augustine reports (and does not flatly deny) the “very general rumour” that “Silvans and Fauns, commonly called incubi” assault women, and that the Gauls call such spirits Dusii; Isidore of Seville repeats the identification; the eleventh- century lexicographer Papias glosses the Gaulish dusii as “those whom the Romans call Fauni ficarii”; and Gervase of Tilbury still treats the dusii under the same head c. 1210. A folk rite that names the fauni of a particular wood as the spirit-parents of changelings is therefore drawing on a live, long-lived category, exactly the kind of stratum a survival argument predicts — the peasants and the inquisitor alike took the fauns to be real powers, not a figure of speech.4

Does the rite touch the wiki’s unseen-realm material?

The lead that opened this page asked whether the fauni-invocation connects to this wiki’s divine-council / serpent-of-Eden / UFO-demonic treatment of the gods of the nations as real created spirits rather than mere superstition. Steelmanned honestly, the tie is real, and it runs through one text in particular. Augustine discusses the fauni/dusii in City of God 15.23 in the very act of weighing the Genesis 6 “sons of God” — whether spirits in aerial bodies can lust after and mingle with women — which is the same Watchers/Nephilim question the wiki treats on nephilim and divine-council. On that reading the fauns of Rimite are not a category apart from the “gods of the nations” but their humblest local instance: the same real, lesser daemones of place that the patristic tradition (Justin’s angels set over the nations, Tertullian’s “deserters … lovers of women,” the Septuagint’s “all the gods of the nations are demons,” Psalm 96:5) read as genuine spirits behind pagan cult. The wiki does not discount the peasants’ belief merely because it invokes unseen powers; on this frame the changeling belief is a folk apprehension of a real spiritual order.

But the tie should be scoped, not oversold. It is a tie to the general patristic demonization of pagan nature-spirits, not to the specific Deuteronomy 32 apportionment of the nations that anchors the divine-council reading proper: the fauni are rustic genii of a single wood, not the presiding territorial powers of the bene elohim schema. And the metaphysics cuts both ways: that the fauns were taken to be real establishes what the people believed, not that the beings exist — possibility ≠ proof. The connection belongs on the page as a genuine thematic cross-link (the Christian tradition really did file the fauni under the same daemones-of-the-nations as the gods of the pagans), weighed and bounded — not as a load-bearing plank of the survival argument, which stands on the folklore evidence with or without it.

Adversarial assessment

What holds. That the rite is a changeling ritual is not in serious doubt: the exchange-premise, the demand that the spirits take back their own, the passage through the cleft trees, the ordeal-immersion and exposure, and the naming of the fauni as the abductors are all standard changeling idiom, and the belief in child-stealing nature-spirits is attested across Europe. The fauni/dusii as a living demonic category from Augustine to Gervase is documented, so the rite plainly draws on old and durable materials.

What is overstated or imprecise. Two limits keep it short of certainty. (i) The Latin is the friar’s. Schmitt’s own methodological point — that the peasants’ belief reaches us only “refracted through the cultivated mind and Latin vocabulary of Friar Stephen” — cuts against reading the word fauni as the villagers’ own term; a learned Dominican would naturally reach for the classical name (and for “invoking the demons”) to label whatever spirits the mothers actually addressed. The belief is the people’s; the vocabulary may be the inquisitor’s. (ii) “Survival” is inferred, not traced. A continuous descent from pre-Christian (Roman or Celtic) religion into the medieval Dombes is a reasonable inference from the antiquity of the fauni category, but it is not documented step by step; a widespread changeling belief can also crystallize independently wherever infant mortality and wild woods meet, so “genuine pre-Christian survival” is the likeliest reading rather than a proven pedigree.

Assessment

Plausible, at the probable end of the band. The rite is unmistakably a changeling ritual, and the child-stealing fauni it names sit in a demonic category that Latin sources carry from Augustine to the thirteenth century — so a genuine folk survival, absorbed into the greyhound’s cult, is the natural and standard reading, and this “practice” strand deserves a page of its own alongside the fable and the name. What keeps it from sound is that the diagnostic word fauni is the learned inquisitor’s Latin, so unbroken pre-Christian continuity is inferred from the category’s antiquity rather than traced, and an independent medieval crystallization of the same motif cannot be ruled out. The cross-link to the wiki’s unseen-realm material is real but bounded: the patristic tradition genuinely filed the fauni among the daemones behind pagan cult — Augustine does so in the same breath as the Genesis 6 “sons of God” — yet that is the broad demonization of nature-spirits, not the Deuteronomy 32 apportionment proper, and belief in the fauns proves belief, not the beings.
