What was the faun-invocation rite at Guinefort’s grove?

Disputed reading · on Saint Guinefort, the holy greyhound · witness: Stephen of Bourbon

Related readings: Does the Guinefort story descend from the Indian fable by the literary road west? · Where did the greyhound’s name and child-patronage come from? · Do the later traces record one continuous cult?

The passage

“…they would pass the naked babies between the trunks of two trees… Invoking the demons, they called upon the fauns in the forest of Rimite to take the sick, feeble child which, they said, was theirs, and to return their child that the fauns had taken away, fat and well, safe and sound…”

— Stephen of Bourbon, De supersticione (c. 1250–1261), English in the Fordham Medieval Sourcebook (from Lecoy de la Marche, Anecdotes historiques, 1877).1

The question

Stephen describes a rite taught by an old woman: offerings of salt, swaddling-clothes hung on the bushes, nails driven into the trees, the naked child thrown nine times between two trunks, the fauns of the forest of Rimite called on to exchange a sick child for a healthy one, the child left between two lit candles, then plunged nine times in the river Chalaronne. What is disputed is what the rite was: an invocation of demons, as Stephen judged it, or an older folk rite for children believed to be changelings, which fed into the cult of the dog.2

See also (on other doctrines): the patristic reading of the gods of the nations as daemones, on the divine council; the Genesis 6 question of spirits and women that Augustine raises beside the fauns, on the Nephilim.

An invocation of demons

On this reading the fauns the mothers called on were demons, and the rite was an offering to them.

Held by: Stephen of Bourbon (De supersticione, c. 1250–1261).3

Stephen files the cult under superstitions “which honour demons or other creatures as if they were divine.” The old woman, he writes, taught the mothers “the rituals they should enact in order to make offerings to demons, and in order to invoke them”; the peasants at the grove “fell victim to the enticements and illusions of the devil.” One woman told him that after invoking the fauns she saw a wolf come at the baby, “or as she put it, the devil in the shape of a wolf.” Rist comments: “Just as the ‘fauns’ appear as demons, so for the mother the wolf appears as the devil.” Stephen’s word fauni belongs to a Latin category that Christian writers placed among the demons. Augustine, discussing the Genesis 6 “sons of God,” reports “that sylvans and fauns, who are commonly called incubi, had often made wicked assaults upon women,” and that the Gauls called such spirits Dusii. Isidore of Seville repeats the identification; the eleventh-century lexicographer Papias glosses the dusii as those the Romans call Fauni ficarii; and Gervase of Tilbury treats the dusii among the incubi c. 1210. In naming the fauns, Stephen names powers that the Latin tradition before him had already counted among the demons.4

An older folk rite for changeling children

On this reading the rite was a pre-existing folk practice for sick infants believed to be changelings, one of the strands that met in the cult of the dog.

Held by: Jean-Claude Schmitt (Le Saint Lévrier, 1979; English 1983); Lester K. Little (New York Review of Books, 1981); Rebecca Rist (Reinardus, 2019).5

Schmitt makes the healing rite one of the three folk currents whose meeting produced the dog-saint, independent of both the fable and the human martyr’s name. Little, reviewing him, writes that “all the parts of the rite had clearly established folkloric meanings at the time as well.” The rite’s premise is the changeling schema of European folklore: spirits have exchanged a healthy child for a sickly one of their own and can be made to swap them back, which is why the mothers ask the fauns to take the child “which, they said, was theirs.” D. L. Ashliman’s essay on changelings records suspected changelings “thrown into water” or “left unfed and crying in an open field” so that the spirits would return the stolen child, and Wikipedia’s “Changeling” article a Cornish rite in which “a changeling baby was passed through the stone for the mother to have her real child returned to her.” Rist reads the rite as playing “on fears that a child might be taken immediately following birth and a changeling substituted,” and the mothers as believing “that what they were engaged in was a life-saving rite for their children which in fact ended by killing the weakest of them.” Schmitt, as Little reports, reads the rite as having “less to do with infanticide than with the need for adults to find reassuring ways to confront infant mortality.” On the vocabulary, Little observes that the peasants’ belief “has reached us only as refracted through the cultivated mind and Latin vocabulary of Friar Stephen,” so the word fauni may be the friar’s name for the spirits the mothers addressed. Little reports Schmitt’s view that “the oral traditions of the peasantry” may be “rooted in ancient Indo-European culture”, and Rist, citing Dubois and Hamilton, that the cult “may represent a Christian form of an originally pagan cult.” On this reading the mothers were practising an inherited rite for the recovery of a taken child.6
