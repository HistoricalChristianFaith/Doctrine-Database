Where did the greyhound’s name and child-patronage come from?

Disputed reading · on Saint Guinefort, the holy greyhound · witness: Stephen of Bourbon

Related readings: Does the Guinefort story descend from the Indian fable by the literary road west? · What was the faun-invocation rite at Guinefort’s grove? · Do the later traces record one continuous cult?

The passage

“…numerous women confessed that they had taken their children to Saint Guinefort. As I thought that this was some holy person, I continued with my enquiry and finally learned that this was actually a greyhound, which had been killed in the following manner.”

— Stephen of Bourbon, De supersticione (c. 1250–1261), English in the Fordham Medieval Sourcebook.1

The question

Stephen records the dog under the name “Saint Guinefort” and as a healer of sick children, but says nothing of where the name came from. What is disputed is whether the name and the office came from the martyr Guinefortus of Pavia or belong with a wider regional family of saints with similar names.

Taken from the martyr of Pavia

On this reading the Dombes cult took the name and the care of children from a human martyr whose cult monks had carried north from Pavia.

Held by: Jean-Claude Schmitt (Le Saint Lévrier, 1979; English 1983); Lester K. Little (New York Review of Books, 1981).2

The case rests on three markers together, with a mechanism. A martyr Guinefortus (Gunifortus) was venerated at Pavia, said to have died under Diocletian; his Acts tell that the lictors were ordered “to discharge arrows against his body,” and the Roman Martyrology commemorates him on 22 August. Little reports Schmitt’s finding that “since the eleventh century there existed across the Alps at Pavia a cult dedicated to Sanctus Guinifortus (man and martyr).” Pursuing the name, the feast and the emphasis on sick children, Schmitt mapped fifty-nine places where a variant of the cult was observed, eight in Italy and the rest in France. For the passage north he points to the circumvectio, “a fund-raising campaign by monks who paraded relics from one village to another”; Little judged that Schmitt “offers a convincing explanation of how Guinefort could have arrived in the Dombes.” The result, in Little’s summary, was “the unusual mixture” of “the name of a saint and martyr along with the principal components of a cult brought by monks from Pavia, joined to a nexus of traditional folklore.” On Schmitt’s reconstruction, name and child-patronage reached the Dombes by the circumvectio and attached to the dog of the local tale.3

One of a regional family of child-healer saints

On this reading the dog belongs among several French saints of similar names, all of them healers of children, with Pavia granted as a possible source of the name.

Held by: Rebecca Rist (Reinardus, 2019).4

Rist grants that “possibly this veneration of St Guinefort is the source for the name,” and that Cluniac monks “probably spread his cult to the west of the Alps from the end of the eleventh century.” She then sets the dog in a wider group: “the region also boasted other cults such as that of Saint Genefort, Saint Guinefort of Bourges, and Saint Guinefort of Sens,” and other healers of children, Saint Millefort, Saint Dignefort, bishop of Meaux, and Sainte Wilgeforte. “All these saints, with the exception of our Saint Guinefort, were in human, not animal form. Yet, what united all these saints and their cults which spread all over France was the idea that they possessed the faculty to cure children of different illnesses and afflictions.” Their invocations, she adds, show all of them associated “not just with the curing of children, but with the power to determine whether a child would live or die.” Rist, granting Pavia as a possible source, places the dog among a regional family of child-healer saints of similar names.5

Proposed etymologies of the name

Two sources propose etymologies of the name “Guinefort”; neither addresses where the dog’s name came from. The note introducing the Fordham translation (Paul Halsall, 2000) reads the name as “probably derived from ‘Cynephoros’ in Greek, sometimes a name for St. Christopher,” linking it to Saint Christopher. The Behind the Name entry “Guinefort” (2024) takes the first element either from Old German wini, “friend”, since names with that element often become Guin- in French, or from Old French guignier, “to signal”, and the second from Old French fort, “strong”; on the wini derivation the name means “strong friend”.6
