Do the later traces of Saint Guinefort’s cult record one continuous cult from the thirteenth century to the twentieth?

Disputed reading · on Saint Guinefort, the holy greyhound · witness: Stephen of Bourbon

Related readings: Does the Guinefort story descend from the Indian fable by the literary road west? · Where did the greyhound’s name and child-patronage come from? · What was the faun-invocation rite at Guinefort’s grove?

The passage

“We went to this place, we called together all the people on the estate, and we preached against everything that had been said. We had the dead dog disinterred, and the sacred wood cut down and burnt, along with the remains of the dog. And I had an edict passed by the lords of the estate, warning that anyone going thenceforth to that place for any such reason would be liable to have his possessions seized and then sold.”

— Stephen of Bourbon, De supersticione (c. 1250–1261), English in the Fordham Medieval Sourcebook.1

The question

After Stephen’s report, a cult of Saint Guinefort at or near the same wood is next recorded in 1632, then in a letter of 1826, in a folklorist’s inquiry after 1877, and in oral testimony about the 1930s and 1940s. What is disputed is whether these traces record one cult carried on without a break from the thirteenth century, or devotion that recurred at the site across a largely unrecorded span.2

One cult, carried on for six centuries

On this reading the mothers who brought sick children to the wood in the twentieth century kept up the same cult Stephen tried to end.

Held by: Jean-Claude Schmitt (Le Saint Lévrier, 1979; English 1983); Lester K. Little (New York Review of Books, 1981); Rebecca Rist (Reinardus, 2019).3

Schmitt’s subtitle names the thesis: Healer of Children since the Thirteenth Century. In 1632 a document refers to “a chapel of St. Guy le Fort located right where he would place the original cult.” In 1826 a survey by the local bishop produced a letter about “the spurious cult of St. Guinefort to whom mothers brought sickly children.” After Stephen’s exemplum was published in 1877, the folklorist A. Vayssière found that everyone he spoke with “avowed that Guinefort was a dog.” Schmitt’s own oral inquiry in the mid-1970s gathered a dozen statements about an old woman still active in the 1930s, “thought to be the last guardian of the cult.” The last known visit to cure a sick child fell around the 1940s. On Schmitt’s reconstruction the 1632 chapel stands where he places the original cult, the 1826 letter records mothers bringing sickly children to St. Guinefort, and Vayssière’s informants identify Guinefort as a dog. Schmitt, as Little reports, “explains the continuation of this cult through six centuries by the stable economic and social conditions that prevailed throughout the same period”: marginal lands flooded as fish ponds stayed in place for six centuries, and in the 1840s local life expectancy was twenty-three years against a national thirty-five. The ponds were drained from 1836, and “the fish ponds dried up, and so did the cult of St. Guinefort.” Rist writes that “although Stephen had the site destroyed, we know that it remained a site of pilgrimage for mothers with sick children for many centuries to come.” Schmitt ties the cult’s decline to the draining of the ponds from 1836 onward; the oral testimony places the last visits in the 1930s and 1940s.4

Devotion that recurred at the site

On this reading the traces show a place and a name to which devotion returned, with the cult’s continuity across the late Middle Ages left unrecorded.

The record is dense at the two ends and thin in the middle. Before 1826 Schmitt found, in Little’s words, “only one intervening trace,” and that trace gives a chapel and a form of the name, “St. Guy le Fort,” rather than a dog or a rite. The nineteenth-century witnesses tell the story in their own way: Vayssière’s informants placed the dog’s death in “a woodsman’s cabin” rather than a knight’s castle. A healing place in a wood, under a saint’s name and with a story attached, could draw mothers of sick children afresh in each generation. On this reading the later traces record what each age found at the site, a wood, a name and a story of a faithful dog, with the late-medieval links between them unattested.5
