Modern scholarship: Schmitt’s Holy Greyhound

Dates: 1979 (French) / 1983 (English) → today · Wikipedia
Summary: The greyhound-saint was an obscure footnote until Jean-Claude Schmitt built a whole book around Stephen of Bourbon’s single exemplum. Schmitt’s Le Saint Lévrier (1979; English The Holy Greyhound, 1983) reconstructs the cult from that hostile source and reads it as a case-study in how the historian recovers medieval popular culture; later work (Rebecca Rist) has sharpened the account of why the church moved against it.

Claims & evidence

The dog-saint as a confluence of three folk strands

Schmitt’s central thesis is that the holy greyhound was not invented from nothing but formed where three existing currents met, all of them concerned with the protection of children:

  • the international faithful-hound fable (Aarne–Thompson–Uther 178A) — the animal that saves the child, kills the beast, and is rashly slain — supplying the story;
  • a pre-existing magical healing rite for sickly, “changeling”-type children (the faun-invocation and exposure between the trees) — supplying the practice;
  • the ready name and child-patronage of the human Saint Guinefort of Pavia, whose cult had spread into the region — supplying the name.

On this reading the greyhound is a folk crystallization at the meeting-point of tale, rite, and name, rather than the simple end-product of a migrating text.

— Jean-Claude Schmitt, The Holy Greyhound (Cambridge UP, 1983), Google Books; summarized in Lester K. Little, “The Greyhound Saint,” New York Review of Books (30 April 1981).

Fifty-nine cult centres and the human saint’s road into France

Schmitt documented some fifty-nine Guinefort cult-centres — eight in Italy, the rest in France — and reconstructed how the human martyr of Pavia’s cult crossed the Alps, plausibly carried by Cluniac monks and their relic-tours (circumvectio), so that the name and its association with children were already established in the Dombes when the dog-cult attached to it.

— Schmitt, The Holy Greyhound; Little, “The Greyhound Saint” (on the circumvectio and the fifty-nine centres).

A method: reading popular culture through a hostile source

Because the only medieval record is Stephen of Bourbon’s condemnation, the book is as much about method as about Guinefort: how to reconstruct the beliefs of the unlettered from a document written to suppress them, reading against the grain of the churchman’s hostility. This is what made the study a landmark of the history of mentalités.

— reviews in the American Historical Review 89.4 (1984): 1063, Oxford Academic, and at Project MUSE.

Why the church moved: a dangerous cult, not only a canine one

Rebecca Rist has argued that Stephen’s response is best understood as aimed at a healing cult that had grown dangerous — the rite of leaving a naked infant exposed between burning candles could and did put children at risk — as much as at the theological scandal of venerating a dog. That the penalty was confiscation (imposed through the secular lords) rather than a heresy trial fits a pastoral crackdown on a perilous superstition rather than a formal inquisition into heretics.

— Rebecca Rist, “The Papacy, Inquisition and Saint Guinefort the Holy Greyhound” (University of Reading CentAUR).
