Modern scholarship: Schmitt’s Holy Greyhound

Dates: 1979 (French) / 1983 (English) → today · Wikipedia
Summary: Jean-Claude Schmitt built a book-length study around Stephen of Bourbon’s single exemplum. His Le Saint Lévrier (1979; English The Holy Greyhound, 1983) reconstructs the cult from that hostile source and reads it as a case-study in how the historian recovers medieval popular culture; Rebecca Rist (2019) has studied why Stephen moved against it.

Claims & evidence

The dog-saint as a confluence of three folk strands

Schmitt’s central thesis is that the holy greyhound formed where three existing currents met, all of them concerned with the protection of children:

  • the international faithful-hound fable (Aarne–Thompson–Uther 178A) — the animal that saves the child, kills the beast, and is rashly slain — supplying the story;
  • a pre-existing magical healing rite for sickly, “changeling”-type children (the faun-invocation and exposure between the trees) — supplying the practice;
  • the name and child-patronage of the human Saint Guinefort of Pavia, whose cult had spread into the region — supplying the name.

On this reading the greyhound is a folk crystallization at the meeting-point of tale, rite, and name. Lester K. Little reports Schmitt’s view that the peasants’ oral traditions are “as plausible a route as any direct descent from one of those twelfth-century texts” of the tale.

— Jean-Claude Schmitt, The Holy Greyhound (Cambridge UP, 1983), Google Books; summarized in Lester K. Little, “The Greyhound Saint,” New York Review of Books (30 April 1981).

Fifty-nine cult centres and the human saint’s road into France

Schmitt documented some fifty-nine Guinefort cult-centres — eight in Italy, the rest in France — and reconstructed how the human martyr of Pavia’s cult crossed the Alps through the circumvectio, “a fund-raising campaign by monks who paraded relics from one village to another.” On this reconstruction the name and its association with children were established in the Dombes when the dog-cult attached to it. Rebecca Rist (2019) writes that Cluniac monks “probably spread his cult to the west of the Alps from the end of the eleventh century.”

— Schmitt, The Holy Greyhound; Little, “The Greyhound Saint” (on the circumvectio and the fifty-nine centres); Rebecca Rist, “The Papacy, Inquisition and Saint Guinefort the Holy Greyhound” (CentAUR accepted version, p. 11).

A method: reading popular culture through a hostile source

Because the only medieval record is Stephen of Bourbon’s condemnation, the book is as much about method as about Guinefort: how to reconstruct the beliefs of the unlettered from a document written to suppress them, reading against the grain of the churchman’s hostility. Little writes that the peasants’ story “has reached us only as refracted through the cultivated mind and Latin vocabulary of Friar Stephen.”

— Little, “The Greyhound Saint”; reviews in the American Historical Review 89.4 (1984): 1063, Oxford Academic, and at Project MUSE.

Why Stephen moved against the cult: Rist’s reading

Rebecca Rist (2019) writes that Stephen’s censure “was not just a ban on a non-orthodox cult, nor merely a statement that humans, not animals, could be saints, nor only a condemnation of magical and heretical practices – although it was all these things. It was also a condemnation by the papal inquisition of a healing cult which had got out of hand and had led to the death of infants.” She reads the penalty, the seizure of goods decreed through the lords of the estate, as a sign of restraint: “That Stephen chose to shut the rite down, rather than imposing more severe penalties, suggests that he almost certainly did not regard the cult as ritual infanticide.”

— Rebecca Rist, “The Papacy, Inquisition and Saint Guinefort the Holy Greyhound”, Reinardus 30 (2019) (University of Reading CentAUR accepted version, pp. 24–25).
