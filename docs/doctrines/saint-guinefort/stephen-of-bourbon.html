Stephen of Bourbon: the inquisitor who wrote down the holy greyhound

Dates: c. 1180–c. 1261 (the Guinefort exemplum composed c. 1250–1261) · Wikipedia
Summary: A Dominican preacher and one of the first inquisitors of southern France, Stephen of Bourbon recorded the cult of the greyhound-saint Guinefort in the De supersticione section of his sermon-manual, having uncovered it while hearing confessions in the diocese of Lyon. He is the sole medieval witness to the dog-saint, and he reports the legend, the healing rite, and his own suppression of both.

Claims & evidence

He mistook a dog for a saint

Stephen found the cult by accident, in the confessional, and only gradually grasped what he was hearing:

“…numerous women confessed that they had taken their children to Saint Guinefort. As I thought that this was some holy person, I continued with my enquiry and finally learned that this was actually a greyhound, which had been killed in the following manner.”

— Stephen of Bourbon, De supersticione, English in the Fordham Medieval Sourcebook (from Lecoy de la Marche, Anecdotes historiques, 1877, tr. Paul Hyams). This one sentence is the hinge of the whole story: the veneration was so ordinary a part of local devotion that a trained inquisitor first took “Saint Guinefort” for a human holy person.

The legend: the guarding dog and the serpent

“…the lord of which and his wife had a baby boy. One day… the baby [was left] alone in the cradle, [when] a huge serpent entered the house and approached the baby’s cradle. Seeing this, the greyhound… chased the serpent and, attacking it beneath the cradle, upset the cradle and bit the serpent all over… Finally, the dog killed it and threw it well away from the cradle… the knight… thought [the dog had devoured the child] and drew his sword and killed the dog. Then, when they went closer to the baby they found it safe and sound… they threw [the dog] into a well in front of the manor door, threw a great pile of stones on top of it, and planted trees beside it… the peasants… honoured the dog as a martyr, prayed to it when they were sick.”

— De supersticione, Fordham Sourcebook. The beast is a serpent (not the wolf of the Welsh Gelert line), and the dog’s burial in a well ringed with planted trees is what turned a knight’s estate into the sacred grove of a saint. The setting is placed “in the diocese of Lyons, near the enclosed nuns’ village called Neuville, on the estate of the Lord of Villars” — the Dombes north of Lyon.

The healing ritual at the grove

Stephen’s account is unusually precise about what the mothers actually did — a rite for a sickly, “changeling”-like child, officiated by an old woman:

“…they would make offerings of salt and other things; they would hang their babies’ swaddling-clothes on the bushes roundabout; they would drive nails into the trees which had grown in this place; they would pass the naked babies between the trunks of two trees — the mother, on one side, held the baby and threw it nine times to the old woman, who was on the other side. Invoking the demons, they called upon the fauns in the forest of Rimite to take the sick, feeble child which, they said, was theirs, and to return their child that the fauns had taken away, fat and well, safe and sound… then… they lit two candles… one on each side of the child’s head… Then they withdrew until the candles had burnt out, so as not to see the child or hear him crying.”

— De supersticione, Fordham Sourcebook. The invocation of the fauni to exchange a “taken” child for the true one is a classic changeling belief; the exposure of the naked infant between burning candles is the element that later scholars read as physically dangerous — the practical, and not merely doctrinal, reason for the crackdown.

The suppression

“We went to this place, we called together all the people on the estate, and we preached against everything that had been said. We had the dead dog disinterred, and the sacred wood cut down and burnt, along with the remains of the dog. And I had an edict passed by the lords of the estate, warning that anyone going thenceforth to that place for any such reason would be liable to have his possessions seized and then sold.”

— De supersticione, Fordham Sourcebook. Note the penalty: confiscation, imposed through the secular lords, not a heresy prosecution — the response of a preacher stamping out a dangerous superstition rather than an inquisitor trying heretics. The edict conspicuously failed: the cult outlived it by six centuries.

Notes

  • Dating. The Tractatus de diversis materiis praedicabilibus was left unfinished at Stephen’s death (c. 1261); the Guinefort exemplum belongs to work done c. 1250–1261, with the observed cult usually dated c. 1260. The loose “described in 1250” sometimes seen is best replaced by “before 1261.”
  • A hostile source that preserves what it attacks. Everything later scholarship knows about the dog-saint descends from this single passage — a parallel to Saint George, whose wild Passion survives partly through the church’s condemnation of it. The methodological problem this poses — recovering a folk cult only through the words of the man who despised it — is the subject of Schmitt’s study.
