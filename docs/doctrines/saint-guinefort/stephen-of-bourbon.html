Stephen of Bourbon: the inquisitor who wrote down the holy greyhound

Dates: c. 1180–c. 1261 (the Guinefort exemplum composed c. 1250–1261) · Wikipedia
Summary: A Dominican preacher and inquisitor, Stephen of Bourbon recorded the cult of the greyhound-saint Guinefort in the De supersticione section of his sermon-manual, having learned of it while hearing confessions in the diocese of Lyon. His is the only known medieval source for the dog-saint, and he reports the legend, the healing rite, and his own suppression of both.

Claims & evidence

How he learned of the cult

Stephen learned of the cult in the confessional, and reports that he first took Guinefort for a holy person:

“…numerous women confessed that they had taken their children to Saint Guinefort. As I thought that this was some holy person, I continued with my enquiry and finally learned that this was actually a greyhound, which had been killed in the following manner.”

— Stephen of Bourbon, De supersticione, English in the Fordham Medieval Sourcebook (from Lecoy de la Marche, Anecdotes historiques, 1877; the Sourcebook credits the translation, with a query, to Paul Hyams).

The legend: the guarding dog and the serpent

“…the lord of which and his wife had a baby boy. One day… the baby [was left] alone in the cradle, [when] a huge serpent entered the house and approached the baby’s cradle. Seeing this, the greyhound… chased the serpent and, attacking it beneath the cradle, upset the cradle and bit the serpent all over… Finally, the dog killed it and threw it well away from the cradle… the knight… thought [the dog had devoured the child] and drew his sword and killed the dog. Then, when they went closer to the baby they found it safe and sound… they threw [the dog] into a well in front of the manor door, threw a great pile of stones on top of it, and planted trees beside it… the peasants… honoured the dog as a martyr, prayed to it when they were sick.”

— De supersticione, Fordham Sourcebook. In Stephen’s telling the beast is a serpent (in the Welsh Gelert story it is a wolf), and the dog is buried in a well with trees planted beside it, the place the peasants later visited. The setting is placed “in the diocese of Lyons, near the enclosed nuns’ village called Neuville, on the estate of the Lord of Villars” — the Dombes north of Lyon.

The healing ritual at the grove

Stephen describes in detail the rite the mothers performed for a sick or weak child, led by an old woman:

“…they would make offerings of salt and other things; they would hang their babies’ swaddling-clothes on the bushes roundabout; they would drive nails into the trees which had grown in this place; they would pass the naked babies between the trunks of two trees — the mother, on one side, held the baby and threw it nine times to the old woman, who was on the other side. Invoking the demons, they called upon the fauns in the forest of Rimite to take the sick, feeble child which, they said, was theirs, and to return their child that the fauns had taken away, fat and well, safe and sound… then… they lit two candles… one on each side of the child’s head… Then they withdrew until the candles had burnt out, so as not to see the child or hear him crying.”

— De supersticione, Fordham Sourcebook. Stephen calls the women “infanticidal mothers” and reports that “several people have told us that while the candies were burning like this they burnt and killed several babies” (“candies” so in the Sourcebook text). Schmitt and Rebecca Rist read the invocation of the fauni to exchange a “taken” child for the true one as changeling belief; Rist reads Stephen’s censure as also “a condemnation by the papal inquisition of a healing cult which had got out of hand and had led to the death of infants” (“The Papacy, Inquisition and Saint Guinefort the Holy Greyhound”, Reinardus 30, 2019, University of Reading CentAUR accepted version, p. 25). The readings of the rite are set out on their own page.

The suppression

“We went to this place, we called together all the people on the estate, and we preached against everything that had been said. We had the dead dog disinterred, and the sacred wood cut down and burnt, along with the remains of the dog. And I had an edict passed by the lords of the estate, warning that anyone going thenceforth to that place for any such reason would be liable to have his possessions seized and then sold.”

— De supersticione, Fordham Sourcebook. The penalty Stephen names is the seizure and sale of possessions, decreed through the lords of the estate. Rist writes that his choice “to shut the rite down, rather than imposing more severe penalties, suggests that he almost certainly did not regard the cult as ritual infanticide” (“The Papacy, Inquisition and Saint Guinefort the Holy Greyhound”, Reinardus 30, 2019, University of Reading CentAUR accepted version, p. 24). Later sources record a cult of Saint Guinefort at the site from 1632 into the twentieth century; see the timeline.

Dating and reception

  • Dating. The Tractatus de diversis materiis praedicabilibus was left unfinished at Stephen’s death (c. 1261); the Guinefort exemplum belongs to work done c. 1250–1261, before Stephen’s death. Accounts of the cult give dates within that span, both c. 1250 and c. 1260.
  • A hostile source as the only medieval record. Later scholarship on the dog-saint’s medieval cult rests on this single passage, as the condemned Passion of Saint George is known partly through the condemnation of it. Recovering a folk cult through the words of the man who condemned it is the subject of Schmitt’s study.
