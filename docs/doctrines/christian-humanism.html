Christian Humanism (ad fontes / bonae litterae)

In the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries a movement of scholars set out to reform Christian faith by going back to its sources. Their two watchwords name the program. Ad fontes — “to the fountains” — meant returning past the accumulated glosses and Latin paraphrases of the medieval schools to the original texts in their original tongues: the Greek New Testament, the Hebrew Old Testament, the fathers read whole rather than in scholastic snippets, the classical authors read for the elegance and wisdom of their own words. Bonae litterae — “good letters” — named the disciplined study of grammar, rhetoric, poetry, history, and moral philosophy (the studia humanitatis, from which “humanist” and “humanities” descend) that the movement believed would purify the mind, the church, and the text of Scripture itself. These were Christian humanists: not the modern secular sense of “humanist,” but scholars who held that a return to the sources and to good letters was the road to a reformed and heartfelt Christianity.

The belief that develops on this page is the question that program forces: does a return to the original sources — pagan as well as patristic, Greek and Hebrew as well as Latin — purify Christian faith, or corrode it? The humanists answered that it purifies: that Jerome’s Vulgate and the schoolmen’s glosses had let errors creep in, that the philosophy of Christ (philosophia Christi) lay open in the plain sense of the Gospels once the text was cleared, and that piety was starved by a religion of ceremonies and syllogisms rather than of Scripture read at the fountainhead. Their critics answered that it corrodes: that turning the Vulgate over to grammarians unsettled the church’s authorized text, that a taste for Cicero and Plato pulled Christians back toward paganism, and — once the Reformation broke — that “Erasmus laid the egg that Luther hatched.” The movement’s own internal fault line runs right through that charge: Erasmus, who did more than anyone to open the Greek New Testament, broke publicly with Luther over the freedom of the will and refused to follow the reform he had helped set in motion. Whether Christian humanism was the mother of the Reformation, its road not taken, or a third way between Rome and Wittenberg is the argument this timeline exists to weigh.

The witnesses run from the Italian philologists who first turned textual criticism on sacred and ecclesiastical documents, through the northern circle who carried ad fontes into biblical scholarship and church reform, to the collision of humanism and the Reformation that split the movement. Several of its figures also appear on the timeline of the liberal arts, where the question is what education is for; here the question is narrower and sharper — what happens to Christian faith when it is taken back to its sources.

Lorenzo Valla (c. 1407–1457)

The Italian philologist who first proved what the new learning could do to a sacred text. In a Declamation of 1440 Valla demonstrated that the Donation of Constantine — the charter on which the medieval papacy’s temporal claims most leaned — was an anachronistic forgery, convicted out of its own Latin and chronology: a fourth-century emperor could not have written of “satraps” in Roman decrees, nor named Constantinople a patriarchal see “when it was not yet… a Christian city, nor named Constantinople, nor founded, nor planned.”1 His Elegantiae linguae Latinae made the recovery of classical Latinity a program, and his Collatio collated the Vulgate against the Greek New Testament — insisting he corrected “not Sacred Scripture, but rather its translation.”2 That notebook is the load-bearing link from Italian philology to northern biblical humanism: the young Erasmus found it, printed it in 1505 as the Annotationes, and took from it the pattern for his own Greek New Testament of 1516.3 So the man who unmasked a forged charter also set in motion the re-editing of the New Testament.
