Christian Humanism (ad fontes / bonae litterae)

In the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries a movement of scholars set out to reform Christian faith by going back to its sources. Their two watchwords name the program. Ad fontes — “to the fountains” — meant returning past the accumulated glosses and Latin paraphrases of the medieval schools to the original texts in their original tongues: the Greek New Testament, the Hebrew Old Testament, the fathers read whole rather than in scholastic snippets, the classical authors read for the elegance and wisdom of their own words. Bonae litterae — “good letters” — named the disciplined study of grammar, rhetoric, poetry, history, and moral philosophy (the studia humanitatis, from which “humanist” and “humanities” descend) that the movement believed would purify the mind, the church, and the text of Scripture itself. These were Christian humanists: not the modern secular sense of “humanist,” but scholars who held that a return to the sources and to good letters was the road to a reformed and heartfelt Christianity.

The belief that develops on this page is the question that program forces: does a return to the original sources — pagan as well as patristic, Greek and Hebrew as well as Latin — purify Christian faith, or corrode it? The humanists answered that it purifies: that Jerome’s Vulgate and the schoolmen’s glosses had let errors creep in, that the philosophy of Christ (philosophia Christi) lay open in the plain sense of the Gospels once the text was cleared, and that piety was starved by a religion of ceremonies and syllogisms rather than of Scripture read at the fountainhead. Their critics answered that it corrodes: that turning the Vulgate over to grammarians unsettled the church’s authorized text, that a taste for Cicero and Plato pulled Christians back toward paganism, and — once the Reformation broke — that “Erasmus laid the egg that Luther hatched.” Erasmus, who published the Greek New Testament of 1516, declined to join Luther’s reform and broke publicly with him over the freedom of the will in 1524–25. How the movement stood to the Reformation is read differently by the historians and theologians named under Disputed readings.

The witnesses run from the Italian philologists who turned textual criticism on sacred and ecclesiastical documents, through the northern circle who carried ad fontes into biblical scholarship and church reform, to the collision of humanism and the Reformation that split the movement. Several of its figures also appear on the timeline of the liberal arts, where the question is what education is for; here the question is narrower and sharper — what happens to Christian faith when it is taken back to its sources.

Timeline

The witnesses are listed earliest first — the Italian humanists who applied philology to Scripture and to the church’s own documents (Lorenzo Valla), the northern circle who made ad fontes a program of biblical scholarship and reform (John Colet, Johann Reuchlin, the Complutensian Polyglot, Erasmus, Thomas More), and the generation in which humanism and the Reformation met and divided (Philip Melanchthon; Erasmus against Luther on the freedom of the will). Individual witness blocks — each fully sourced — are slotted in chronological order as they are researched; witnesses shared with the liberal-arts timeline (Erasmus, Melanchthon) are cross-linked there rather than restated.

Lorenzo Valla (c. 1407–1457)

The Italian philologist who turned grammar and history on documents of the church. In a Declamation of 1440 Valla argued that the Donation of Constantine, a charter cited for the medieval papacy’s temporal claims, was a later forgery, pointing to its Latin and chronology: Roman decrees, he held, did not speak of “satraps,” and the charter names Constantinople a patriarchal see “when it was not yet… a Christian city, nor named Constantinople, nor founded, nor planned.”1 His Elegantiae linguae Latinae made the recovery of classical Latinity a program, and his Collatio collated the Vulgate against the Greek New Testament — insisting he corrected “not Sacred Scripture, but rather its translation.”2 The young Erasmus found that notebook, printed it in 1505 as the Annotationes, and on Jerry Bentley’s account took from it the pattern for his own Greek New Testament of 1516.3 Whether the Donation is Constantine’s, and if not when it was composed, is a disputed reading. Giovanni Pico della Mirandola (1463–1494)

The Platonist wing of the movement. Where Valla and the northern circle took ad fontes back to the Greek and Hebrew of Scripture, Pico — a prodigy of the Florentine Academy round Marsilio Ficino — carried it back to Plato, Plotinus, the Hermetica, Zoroaster, Orpheus, Pythagoras, and the Hebrew Kabbalah, in pursuit of a prisca theologia, an “ancient theology” he held to converge on the truth of Christ. His Oration on the Dignity of Man (c. 1486), long called the manifesto of the Renaissance, opens on Hermes Trismegistus and stages God setting man at the world’s centre with no fixed nature: “as though the maker and molder of thyself, thou mayest fashion thyself in whatever shape thou shalt prefer.”4 Prefaced to 900 theses he offered to defend at Rome, drawn from “all the schools” on the premise that truth is one and scattered through them all, it argued that pagan and Jewish wisdom confirms the faith: that “no science assures us more of the divinity of Christ than magic and Kabbalah,” and that in the Hebrew esoterica he had read “not so much the Mosaic as the Christian religion… the mystery of the Trinity, the Incarnation of the Word, the divinity of the Messiah.”5 Innocent VIII’s commission condemned thirteen theses in 1487, and after Pico’s Apologia the pope condemned the whole nine hundred as “in part heretical, in part the flower of heresy… reproducing the errors of the pagan philosophers”; Pico fled, was briefly arrested in France, and was absolved under Alexander VI in 1493.6 It is the earliest condemnation recorded on this timeline. John Colet (c. 1467–1519)

The Englishman who carried ad fontes from the Italian study into the pulpit and the lecture-hall. Returning from Italy, Colet lectured freely at Oxford on St Paul’s epistles — setting aside the scholastic four-fold sense to read the letters continuously, as one man’s words to a real church at a datable moment, dating Romans to “the reign of Claudius… about the twentieth year of St. Paul’s ministry” and reconstructing its occasion out of Suetonius.7 He became the hub of the English humanist circle: Erasmus heard him lecture in 1499 and credited him with the push toward theology — Colet “almost reproachfully” pressing him to expound Moses and Isaiah as Colet was expounding Paul.8 As Dean of St Paul’s he founded St Paul’s School on humanist lines under lay governors, and in 1512 preached a reform sermon to Convocation — calling the “evil and wicked life of priests… a certain kind of heresy” and demanding not new laws but the keeping of the old.9 Full detail →

Johann Reuchlin (c. 1455–1522)

The scholar who carried ad fontes to the Hebrew Old Testament. His De rudimentis hebraicis (1506), described by Britannica as the first substantial Christian Hebrew grammar, opened the language of Moses and the prophets to Latin-reading Christians; Reuchlin closed the volume with Horace’s boast — “I have raised a monument more lasting than bronze” — claiming for a Hebrew grammar the immortality the poet had claimed for his odes.10 When the convert Johannes Pfefferkorn and the Cologne Dominicans won an imperial mandate to confiscate and burn Jewish books, Reuchlin, alone of the experts consulted, argued for their preservation: one may not condemn “the Talmud, a work which they themselves do not even understand,” and the rabbinic commentaries “keep alive the original Hebrew” that Christian study of the Old Testament cannot do without. As a jurist he added that Jews were “fellow citizens of one and the same Roman Empire,” their books protected property.11 For this he was tried for heresy (acquitted at Speyer in 1514, but the Augenspiegel condemned by Leo X in 1520); his younger backers answered with the Letters of Obscure Men, a satire in mock letters from Cologne theologians.12 Full detail →

The Complutensian Polyglot (Cardinal Cisneros’s Alcalá, Greek New Testament printed 1514)

The institutional face of ad fontes — the sources-program carried out not by one scholar and a printer but by a cardinal’s university. Where Reuchlin opened the Hebrew fountain almost single-handed, Cardinal Francisco Jiménez de Cisneros founded the University of Alcalá and its trilingual College of San Ildefonso, gathered a standing team of Greek, Hebrew, and Aramaic scholars, and bought and borrowed manuscripts to produce the first printed Greek New Testament and the editio princeps of the Septuagint.13 When Antonio de Nebrija pressed to correct the Vulgate against the Hebrew and Greek — urging Cisneros to “light up once more the two unlit torches of our religion” — the cardinal refused and Nebrija withdrew; and the preface set the recovered originals below the Latin, the Hebrew and Greek flanking Jerome’s Vulgate “just as the thieves were hung, one on each side of Jesus, who represents the Roman, or Latin, Church.” The Polyglot printed no vernacular column.14 Printed first (1514) but held back and released c. 1522, it reached readers after Erasmus’s edition of 1516; why Erasmus’s text became the base of the received text is a disputed reading. Erasmus of Rotterdam (c. 1466–1536)

The scholar who applied ad fontes to the New Testament itself. In 1516 Erasmus edited and printed the first published Greek New Testament (the Novum Instrumentum), with a fresh Latin translation and critical Annotationes going behind the Vulgate to the Greek. At Matthew 3:2 he judged that the Baptist’s metanoeite, for generations rendered poenitentiam agite, “do penance,” “could suitably have been translated Resipiscite” (“come to your senses”). Roland Bainton holds that the rendering removed any philological connection with the sacrament of penance and reports that Luther made use of it; the Council of Trent (1551) cites “do penance” for a penitence it holds necessary at all times and places the institution of the sacrament at John 20:22–23.15 He framed the whole as a philosophia Christi — a plain philosophy of Christ open to all — and prefixed to it the Paraclesis, wishing the Scriptures translated into every tongue, “that the plowman would sing a text of the scripture at his plowbeam, and that the weaver at his loom.”16 Already in the Enchiridion (1503) he had made the case that religion is inward, not outward ceremony — asking why a Christian would “worship the bones of Paul preserved in a relic casket, but not… the mind of Paul hidden away in his writings.”17 He declined to join Luther’s reform and in 1524 wrote against him on the freedom of the will. Whether his work prepared the Reformation, and how his philosophia Christi stands to the doctrine of grace, are disputed readings. Thomas More (c. 1478–1535)

The lay, civic face of the movement — the London lawyer and courtier who turned the new learning on his own society. Utopia (1516), written in polished humanist Latin and seen through the press with Erasmus’s help, is the studia humanitatis aimed at a Christian commonwealth’s ills: in Book 1 More indicts the enclosures that were depopulating England — sheep “become so great devourers… that they eat up, and swallow down the very men themselves” — and in Book 2 sets a rational, propertyless island against it, the “only commonwealth that truly deserves that name,” blocked from imitation only by pride, “that plague of human nature… that infernal serpent.”18 Its title is Greek ou-topos, “no-place.” More was the closest English friend of Erasmus, who wrote The Praise of Folly in More’s house and punned its Greek title (Moriae) on More’s name, “which comes so near the word… folly… as you are far from the thing,”19 and he defended Greek studies at Oxford against the scholars who mocked them.20 He served as Lord Chancellor, wrote against Luther and Tyndale, and was beheaded on 6 July 1535 after refusing the oath acknowledging the king as supreme head of the church.21 Full detail →

Philip Melanchthon (c. 1497–1560)

A humanist who joined the Wittenberg Reformation, where Erasmus declined to and More wrote against it. A great-nephew and protégé of Reuchlin — who Graecized the boy’s German name “Schwartzerdt” (“black earth”) into its Greek form and won him the Wittenberg chair of Greek at twenty-one, judging him second among the Germans only to “Erasmus…a Dutchman” — Melanchthon arrived a finished humanist and at once joined Luther’s cause. His 1518 inaugural De corrigendis adolescentiae studiis made the recovery of the sources a necessity of theology itself: since theology is “partly Hebrew and partly Greek,… the eternal languages are to be learned, and not be just mute actors when we deal with theology.”22 His Loci Communes (1521), “the first system of Protestant theology” in Philip Schaff’s phrase, drawn from his lectures on Romans, opens by setting the reformer’s salvation-centred theology against scholastic speculation: “to know Christ means to know his benefits and not… to reflect upon his natures and the modes of his incarnation.”23 Yet he kept the pagan classics in the Christian school while denying them any part in salvation — in his 1534 preface to Cicero’s De Officiis, “Philosophy does not provide confirmation of the will of God…; that pertains properly to the Gospel,” but its “precepts for civic life” a Christian commonwealth still needs.24 He was called the Praeceptor Germaniae, the “teacher of Germany.” (His education program is on the liberal-arts timeline.)
