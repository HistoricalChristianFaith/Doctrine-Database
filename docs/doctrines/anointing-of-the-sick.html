Anointing of the Sick (James 5:14 and the rite of anointing across the traditions)

"Is any sick among you? let him call for the elders of the church; and let them pray over him, anointing him with oil in the name of the Lord: and the prayer of faith shall save the sick, and the Lord shall raise him up; and if he have committed sins, they shall be forgiven him" (James 5:14–15), with the Gospel report that the apostles "anointed with oil many that were sick, and healed them" (Mark 6:13). This timeline records, earliest extant attestation first, how these verses were read and how the rite built on them was practised: the blessing of oil for health in the early church orders (Hippolytus, Serapion); citations of James 5 in discussions of the forgiveness of sins (Origen, Chrysostom); Innocent I's ruling that the bishop-blessed oil may be used by "all Christians" and that the anointing is "a kind of sacrament" (416), repeated by Bede; the Frankish texts that join unction to penance and viaticum and assign it to priests (the ordo printed under Theodulf of Orléans's name, whose date is disputed; the Council of Chalon, 813); the Byzantine euchelaion and the Greek commentators (the Barberini Euchologion; Oecumenius; Theophylact); the scholastic treatments of extreme unction (Bonaventure, Aquinas); the Council of Florence's rule that it be given "only to the sick of whose death there is fear" (1439); the Council of Trent's definition (1551); and the Second Vatican Council's renaming of the rite as "anointing of the sick" (1963). How the changes in the rite's recipients and ministers, and its relation to penance, are to be read is disputed between the traditions; see the Disputed readings.

Timeline

Hippolytus of Rome (c. 215)

The church order known as the Apostolic Tradition, ascribed to Hippolytus, preserves the earliest surviving prayer for the blessing of oil: offered like the bread and wine, the oil is consecrated that God "wouldest grant health to them who use it and partake of it, so that it may bestow comfort on all who taste it and health on all who use it." The oil is to be tasted as well as applied.1

Origen (c. 245)

Origen, in his second homily on Leviticus, gives the earliest extant citation of James 5:14 in a discussion of the forgiveness of sins: the last and "hard and laborious" of the seven ways sins are remitted is "the remission of sins through penance… when [the sinner] is not ashamed to make known his sin to the priest of the Lord and to seek the remedy," in which "is fulfilled also that which the Apostle James says: 'let him call the elders of the church… anointing him with oil… and if he be in sins, they will be remitted to him.'" Whether he ascribes that forgiveness to penance or to the anointing is disputed; see the forgiveness of James 5:15.2

Serapion of Thmuis (c. 350)

Serapion's Euchologion preserves the fullest early prayer over the oil of the sick: that God "send healing power of the only-begotten from heaven upon this oil," so that those anointed with it or partaking of it may find "a throwing off of every sickness… good grace and remission of sins… health and soundness of soul, body, spirit." The one prayer asks for bodily healing and for the remission of sins.3

John Chrysostom (c. 390)

Chrysostom, in On the Priesthood, cites James 5:14–15 for his statement that priests, "not only at the time of regeneration, but afterwards also… have authority to forgive sins," quoting with it the recovery of the body ("the prayer of faith shall save the sick, and the Lord will raise him up"). How the passage relates the anointing to that forgiveness is disputed; see the forgiveness of James 5:15.4

Innocent I (416)

Innocent I, answering Decentius of Gubbio, gives the earliest extant papal ruling on James 5:14: the oil is chrism "prepared by a bishop," and its use in anointing is permitted "not only to priests but also to all Christians… in their own necessity or in that of their [people]," the "elders" being named because bishops cannot visit every sick person. It "cannot be administered to penitents, because it is a kind of sacrament" (genus sacramenti), the other sacraments being denied them. Whether the lay use he permits was the rite of James itself is disputed; see the lay anointing permitted by Innocent I.5

Hilary of Arles (c. 430)

Hilary's brief tractate on James reads the "sickness" of v. 14 as the sickness of sin and the oil as a symbol of the "grace of mercy," and notes that "the custom mentioned here is followed even today."6

Caesarius of Arles (c. 520)

Caesarius, warning a laity tempted to run to charmers and amulets, sets the church's remedy against the sorcerer's: the sick "should hurry back to the church," receive the Eucharist, "be anointed by the presbyters with consecrated oil" and prayed over, and so "receive not only bodily health but also the forgiveness of his sins."7

Bede (c. 710)

Bede's commentary on James describes the practice of his day: "the custom of the Church holds that the sick are anointed with consecrated oil by the presbyters, and are healed with accompanying prayer"; and, citing Innocent, "not only the presbyters, but… also all Christians are permitted to use the same oil to anoint in their own or their relatives' necessity," provided a bishop has blessed it. On v. 15 he makes forgiveness turn on confession to the elders and amendment.8

The Barberini Euchologion (c. 790)

The oldest surviving Byzantine service-book, Codex Barberini gr. 336 (Greek south Italy, later 8th c.), gives the earliest datable form of the Eastern euchelaion, the “Office of the Holy Oil”: in Paul Meyendorff's reconstruction, three prayers over the sick and two for the blessing of the oil. The received Byzantine prayers of the service address God as the “Physician of souls and bodies” and ask Him to heal the sick. Meyendorff dates the seven-priest service to later manuscripts (Paris Coislin 213, 11th c.; Sinai gr. 973, 12th c.), and a survey citing Cyprian Hutcheon (Worship, 2002; ⚠ unverified) reports that healthy persons were also anointed in the eleventh century.9

Theodulf of Orléans (c. 810)

The second diocesan statute printed under the name of Theodulf gives a Frankish parish ordo for the unction of the sick that names unction, penance, and viaticum together, "that no one die without the viaticum," and orders that "first let penance be given to the sick person." The words spoken at the anointing echo James ("that the prayer of faith may save you, and the Lord raise you up, and if you are in sins, they may be forgiven you").10 F. W. Puller (Anglican, 1904) reads the ordo as Theodulf's instruction to his clergy; J. M. Donohue (Catholic, New Catholic Encyclopedia) holds that the rite printed under Theodulf's name "is probably descriptive of late tenth or early eleventh-century procedures."11

The Council of Chalon-sur-Saône (813)

One of Charlemagne's five reform councils of 813, Chalon makes the anointing a standing ecclesiastical duty: on the authority of James 5:14–15, "with which the decrees of the fathers also agree, the sick ought to be anointed by priests with oil that is blessed by bishops… a medicine… which heals the ailments of both soul and body." The canon assigns the anointing to presbyteri.12 Whether this narrowed the rite Innocent I and Bede describe is disputed; see Was the lay anointing permitted by Innocent I (416) the same rite as the presbyters' anointing of James 5:14?

Oecumenius (c. 990)

Oecumenius, the Greek catenist, glosses "anointing him with oil" by the Gospel: "This is what the apostles did while the Lord was still among men, anointing the sick with oil and healing them (Mark 6:13)." Theophylact repeats the gloss almost verbatim a century later.13

Theophylact of Ohrid (c. 1107)

Theophylact, the Byzantine archbishop, roots the anointing in the Gospel practice: "The apostles were already doing this even when the Lord still lived among men: they anointed the sick with oil (Mark 6:13)."14

Bonaventure (c. 1257)

Bonaventure, in the Breviloquium, defines the rite: "Extreme unction is the Sacrament of those departing from this life, preparing and disposing them for perfect health; it avails also for… the recovery of present health, if it be expedient."15

Thomas Aquinas (c. 1274)

Aquinas, in the treatise on Extreme Unction (Summa Theologiae Supplement, qq. 29–33), sets out its matter, olive oil blessed by a bishop; its form, a prayer of petition; its principal effect, the remission of the "remnants of sin," with bodily health following "only when it is requisite for the spiritual healing"; its minister, a priest, "not a layman, not a deacon"; and its recipients: it "ought to be given to those only, who are so sick as to be in a state of departure from this life."16

The Council of Florence (1439)

The Council of Florence's Decree for the Armenians defines extreme unction as the fifth of seven sacraments: "the fifth sacrament is extreme unction, whose matter is the olive oil blessed by the bishop. This sacrament should be given only to the sick of whose death there is fear… The minister of this sacrament is the priest."17

The Council of Trent (1551)

The Council of Trent (Session 14) defined extreme unction as "truly and properly a sacrament of the new law, insinuated indeed in Mark, but… promulgated… by James the Apostle," whose ministers are ordained priests (the "elders"), "to be applied to the sick, but to those especially who lie in such danger as to seem to be about to depart this life," and anathematized the denial that it is a sacrament.18

The Second Vatican Council (1963)

In Sacrosanctum Concilium, the Second Vatican Council declared that "'Extreme unction,' which may also and more fittingly be called 'anointing of the sick,' is not a sacrament for those only who are at the point of death," the fitting time being whenever a member of the faithful "begins to be in danger of death from sickness or old age." Paul VI's 1972 apostolic constitution Sacram unctionem infirmorum gave the rite its revised form.19

Disputed readings
