Anointing of the Sick (from James 5:14 to extreme unction and back)

"Is any sick among you? let him call for the elders of the church; and let them pray over him, anointing him with oil in the name of the Lord: and the prayer of faith shall save the sick, and the Lord shall raise him up; and if he have committed sins, they shall be forgiven him" (James 5:14–15) — with the Gospel precedent that the apostles "anointed with oil many that were sick, and healed them" (Mark 6:13). Around these verses the church built a rite whose horizon swung across the centuries: from a healing anointing of the sick, through a narrowing into extreme unction — a last rite for the dying — and, in the twentieth century, a deliberate re-broadening back to "anointing of the sick." This timeline traces that arc earliest-first: the blessing of oil "for health" in the early church orders (Hippolytus, Serapion) and its early reading as a channel of forgiveness (Origen, Chrysostom); the authoritative Latin gloss that it is "a kind of sacrament" open even to lay anointing (Innocent I), echoed by Bede; the Carolingian reform that welds unction to penance and the viaticum and makes its observance a mandated priestly duty (Theodulf of Orléans; the Council of Chalon, 813) — the mechanism of the Latin narrowing; the persistence of the healing rite in Gaul (Caesarius) and in the Greek East, whose euchelaion never narrowed (the Barberini Euchologion; Oecumenius; Theophylact); the medieval turn to a deathbed sacrament (Bonaventure), fixed "only" for those in danger of death at Florence (1439) and dogmatized at Trent (1551); and its reversal at the Second Vatican Council (1963).

Timeline

Hippolytus of Rome (c. 215)

The church order known as the Apostolic Tradition, ascribed to Hippolytus, preserves the earliest surviving prayer for the blessing of oil: offered like the bread and wine, the oil is consecrated that God "wouldest grant health to them who use it and partake of it, so that it may bestow comfort on all who taste it and health on all who use it" — a blessing for general therapeutic use, oil to be tasted as well as applied.1

Origen (c. 245)

Origen, in his second homily on Leviticus, gives the earliest surviving reading of James 5:14 as a channel of the forgiveness of sins: the last and "hard and laborious" of the seven ways sins are remitted is "the remission of sins through penance… when [the sinner] is not ashamed to make known his sin to the priest of the Lord and to seek the remedy," in which "is fulfilled also that which the Apostle James says: 'let him call the elders of the church… anointing him with oil… and if he be in sins, they will be remitted to him.'"2

Serapion of Thmuis (c. 350)

Serapion's Euchologion preserves the fullest early prayer over the oil of the sick: that God "send healing power of the only-begotten from heaven upon this oil," so that those anointed with it or partaking of it may find "a throwing off of every sickness… good grace and remission of sins… health and soundness of soul, body, spirit" — body and soul, healing and forgiveness, in one prayer.3

John Chrysostom (c. 390)

Chrysostom, in On the Priesthood, cites James 5:14–15 to prove that priests, "not only at the time of regeneration, but afterwards also… have authority to forgive sins" — the elders' anointing read as an exercise of the priestly power over sin, and paired with the recovery of the body ("the prayer of faith shall save the sick, and the Lord will raise him up").4

Innocent I (416)

Innocent I, answering Decentius of Gubbio, gives the earliest authoritative gloss on James 5:14: the oil is chrism "prepared by a bishop," and its use in anointing is permitted "not only to priests but also to all Christians… in their own necessity or in that of their [people]" — the "elders" named only because bishops cannot visit every sick person. Yet it "cannot be administered to penitents, because it is a kind of sacrament" (genus sacramenti), the other sacraments being denied them.5

Hilary of Arles (c. 430)

Hilary's brief tractate on James reads the "sickness" of v. 14 as the sickness of sin and the oil as a symbol of the "grace of mercy" — an allegorizing strand pulling the passage toward spiritual healing and forgiveness — while still noting that "the custom mentioned here is followed even today."6

Caesarius of Arles (c. 520)

Caesarius, warning a laity tempted to run to charmers and amulets, sets the church's remedy against the sorcerer's: the sick "should hurry back to the church," receive the Eucharist, "be anointed by the presbyters with consecrated oil" and prayed over — and so "receive not only bodily health but also the forgiveness of his sins." Still a rite of recovery, not a preparation for death.7

Bede (c. 710)

Bede is the fullest early Latin witness to the living rite: "the custom of the Church holds that the sick are anointed with consecrated oil by the presbyters, and are healed with accompanying prayer" — and, following Innocent, "not only the presbyters, but… also all Christians are permitted to use the same oil to anoint in their own or their relatives' necessity," provided a bishop has blessed it. On v. 15 he makes forgiveness turn on confession to the elders and real amendment.8

The Barberini Euchologion (c. 790)

The oldest surviving Byzantine service-book, Codex Barberini gr. 336 (Greek south Italy, later 8th c.), gives the earliest datable form of the Eastern euchelaion — the “Office of the Holy Oil.” Its prayers over the oil and over the sick ask God, the “Physician of souls and bodies,” to “heal… every ailment,” and the rite grew toward the classic seven-priest service in which even the well were anointed — a healing anointing of the sick that, in the East, never contracted to the deathbed. The “East never narrowed” control case in the rite's own liturgical text, from the very century the Latin West was welding unction to death.9

Theodulf of Orléans (c. 810)

Theodulf's second diocesan statute gives the earliest Frankish parish ordo for the unction of the sick — and shows the Latin narrowing beginning: he binds unction, penance, and viaticum together as one deathbed duty, "that no one die without the viaticum," and orders that "first let penance be given to the sick person," the anointing set inside a penitential rite. The words spoken still echo James ("that the prayer of faith may save you, and the Lord raise you up, and if you are in sins, they may be forgiven you"), but the setting has become the deathbed.10

The Council of Chalon-sur-Saône (813)

One of Charlemagne's five reform councils of 813, Chalon makes the anointing a standing ecclesiastical duty: on the authority of James 5:14–15, "with which the decrees of the fathers also agree, the sick ought to be anointed by priests with oil that is blessed by bishops… a medicine… which heals the ailments of both soul and body." Still a healing rite — but now legislated, and assigned to presbyteri, where Innocent and Bede had still allowed lay anointing.11

Oecumenius (c. 990)

Oecumenius, the Greek catenist, gives the earlier Greek witness behind Theophylact: glossing "anointing him with oil," he ties the elders' rite straight back to the Gospel — "This is what the apostles did while the Lord was still among men, anointing the sick with oil and healing them (Mark 6:13)." The same healing-oriented reading Theophylact repeats almost verbatim a century later, carrying the Eastern reading back into the tenth century.12

Theophylact of Ohrid (c. 1107)

Theophylact, the Byzantine archbishop, roots the anointing directly in the Gospel practice: "The apostles were already doing this even when the Lord still lived among men: they anointed the sick with oil (Mark 6:13)." His Greek witness keeps the rite tied to healing — the Eastern euchelaion that never narrowed to the dying.13

Bonaventure (c. 1257)

Bonaventure, in the Breviloquium, gives the classic scholastic definition of the narrowed rite: "Extreme unction is the Sacrament of those departing from this life, preparing and disposing them for perfect health; it avails also for… the recovery of present health, if it be expedient." The healing anointing has become, first of all, a rite for the deathbed — bodily cure demoted to a conditional side-effect.14

Thomas Aquinas (c. 1274)

Aquinas, in the treatise on Extreme Unction (Summa Theologiae Supplement, qq. 29–33), gives the fullest scholastic systematization of the deathbed sacrament: its matter is olive oil blessed by a bishop; its form a prayer of petition; its principal effect the remission of the "remnants of sin" (with bodily health following "only when it is requisite for the spiritual healing"); its minister a priest, "not a layman, not a deacon"; and it "ought to be given to those only, who are so sick as to be in a state of departure from this life." The definitive statement of the narrowed rite that Florence would then legislate.15

The Council of Florence (1439)

The Council of Florence's Decree for the Armenians gives the sacrament its first ecumenical definition and sharpest narrowing: "the fifth sacrament is extreme unction, whose matter is the olive oil blessed by the bishop. This sacrament should be given only to the sick of whose death there is fear… The minister of this sacrament is the priest" — the healing rite of James now formally reserved to the dying.16

The Council of Trent (1551)

Against the Reformers, the Council of Trent (Session 14) defined extreme unction as "truly and properly a sacrament of the new law, insinuated indeed in Mark, but… promulgated… by James the Apostle," whose ministers are ordained priests (the "elders"), "to be applied to the sick, but to those especially who lie in such danger as to seem to be about to depart this life" — and anathematized the denial that it is a sacrament. (Trent's "especially" stops just short of Florence's flat "only.")17

The Second Vatican Council (1963)

In Sacrosanctum Concilium, the Second Vatican Council reversed the narrowing: "'Extreme unction,' which may also and more fittingly be called 'anointing of the sick,' is not a sacrament for those only who are at the point of death," the fitting time being whenever a member of the faithful "begins to be in danger of death from sickness or old age." Paul VI's 1972 revision gave the rite its new form — Rome's own return toward the healing horizon of the earliest witnesses.18

Arguments

Related arguments — the anointing of James 5:14

The whole doctrine rests on a single proof-text — "let him call for the elders of the church… anointing him with oil… and the prayer of faith shall save the sick, and the Lord shall raise him up; and if he have committed sins, they shall be forgiven him" (James 5:14–15). But what does it institute? Two readings, each independently advocated and best read together, divide the confessions: does James promulgate a grace-conferring sacrament, or describe a prayer for the bodily healing of the sick? Weighed adversarially on their own pages.

Does James 5:14 institute a grace-conferring sacrament? (the sacramental reading) — contested

The Catholic reading, defined at Trent: the anointing-with-prayer by the elders is a true sacrament, "insinuated" in Mark 6:13 and promulgated by James, conferring grace and the forgiveness of sins. Strong on the antiquity and universality of the rite (Origen already ties James 5 to remission; Innocent calls it "a kind of sacrament"; the Eastern euchelaion and the Latin rite both descend from it) and on the forgiveness clause the text itself supplies. But weaker where the full apparatus depends on later development: "instituted by Christ" leans on the thin warrant of Mark 6:13 (the disciples' pre-Passion mission), and the grace-conferring parity and closure among exactly seven sacraments are a twelfth-century overlay, so that whether James intends a permanent sacrament or the charismatic healing of the apostolic age is genuinely disputed. Strong by one discipline's lights, weaker by another's (contested).

Is James 5:14 a prayer for bodily healing rather than a deathbed sacrament? (the healing reading) — plausible

The Reformers' reading (Luther, Calvin), with a notable Catholic ally (Cajetan): James's promise — "the prayer of faith shall save the sick, and the Lord shall raise him up" — is about the recovery of the sick, not a rite for the dying, so the medieval extreme unction inverts what the apostle wrote. Coherent and strongly grounded — "raise up" reads as restoration to health, and the earliest practice (blessed oil for "health," lay anointing "in their own… necessity") aims at cure, not death. But short of the whole truth: the text itself adds "if he have committed sins, they shall be forgiven," and the ritual character (elders, blessed oil, prayer in the Lord's name) is more than a private prayer for recovery; and Calvin's further claim that the healing charism has ceased, voiding the rite, overreaches beyond what James says. Coherent, not ruled out, but overstated in its strong form (plausible).

Does Mark 6:13 institute the sacrament, or record a healing mission?

Trent grounds the sacrament's dominical institution on the Gospel notice that the Twelve "anointed with oil many that were sick, and healed them" (Mark 6:13) — the sacrament "instituted by Christ… insinuated indeed in Mark." Whether that verse bears the weight of "instituted by Christ" is weighed adversarially on its own page.

  • Does Mark 6:13 institute the sacrament, or record a healing mission? (the dominical-institution argument) — weak (as the grounding for "instituted by Christ" it does not hold — Mark 6:13 records a pre-Passion charismatic healing mission, with no institution, promise of grace, or note of permanence, and even Trent hedges to "insinuated"; what it does establish is the antiquity and continuity of the rite, not the institution of a sacrament).

Was extreme unction a medieval narrowing of an apostolic healing rite?

One historical thesis runs the length of the timeline rather than sitting at any point on it: that the rite drifted from a healing anointing of the sick into a last rite for the dying — a narrowing later reversed. It is weighed adversarially on its own page, with an assessment of the reading it serves.

  • Was extreme unction a medieval narrowing of an apostolic healing rite? (the narrowing thesis) — sound (the healing→dying→healing arc is documented from the sources themselves — oil "for health" and lay anointing early, "only… in danger of death" at Florence, and Rome's own reversal at Vatican II).
