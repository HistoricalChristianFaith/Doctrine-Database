The historical core: a martyr venerated at Lydda

Dates: earliest datable evidence 4th c. — the Shaqqa (Eaccaea) building-inscription of the 360s, the martyrium at Lydda/Diospolis
Summary: Before the legend there is a cult. A martyr named George was venerated at Lydda (Greek Diospolis, modern Lod) in Palestine from the fourth century, with a shrine reputedly over his grave, and a Hauran building-inscription of the 360s already honours “the saints and triumphant martyrs George and companions.” That is the whole of the reliable residue: a Christian soldier martyred at Diospolis around 303, whose cult ran centuries ahead of any trustworthy account of him.

Claims & evidence

The shrine at Diospolis (Lydda)

The firmest anchor of the cult is a place, not a text. From the fourth century a martyrium of George stood at Lydda—Diospolis in Greek, modern Lod in Israel—reputedly raised over his grave, and it remained the devotional centre of his cult through late antiquity: pilgrims came, and George’s name spread outward from this fixed point rather than from any circulating life. That a venerated tomb-shrine existed within two or three generations of the persecution in which George is said to have died is the strongest single argument that a real martyr lies at the bottom of the tradition.

The Shaqqa (Eaccaea) inscription of the 360s

Older still than any surviving narrative is a Greek building-inscription from Shaqqa (ancient Eaccaea) in the Hauran, in the basalt country south of Damascus, dated to the 360s, which dedicates a structure to “the saints and triumphant martyrs George and companions.” It is among the oldest datable evidence of the cult anywhere, and it does two things at once: it fixes a terminus by which George was already an established object of veneration, and it shows the cult radiating well beyond Lydda—into inland Syria—within a couple of generations of the martyrdom. Hippolyte Delehaye placed this inscription at the head of the George dossier for exactly that reason.

— Hippolyte Delehaye, Les légendes grecques des saints militaires (Paris: Picard, 1909), ch. II, “Saint Georges,” at archive.org; Christopher Walter, “The Origins of the Cult of Saint George,” Revue des études byzantines 53 (1995): 295–326, at Persée.

The modest historical residue

Strip away the Passion, the al-Khiḍr fusion, and the medieval dragon, and what remains is small and coherent: a Christian soldier martyred at Diospolis around 303, most likely in the Great Persecution under Diocletian. Nothing trustworthy survives of his trial, his rank, or the manner of his death; the Catholic Encyclopedia summed up the honest position by ranking George among those saints “whose names are justly reverenced among men, but whose acts are known only to God.” His cult, in other words, is far better attested than his life.

— Catholic Encyclopedia, “St. George”; cf. “Saint George,” Wikipedia.
