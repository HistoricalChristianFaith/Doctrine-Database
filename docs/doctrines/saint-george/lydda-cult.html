The cult of a martyr at Lydda

Dates: earliest datable evidence 4th c. — the Shaqqa (Eaccaea) building-inscription (AD 368 on the era of Bostra) and the martyrium at Lydda/Diospolis
Summary: The earliest extant evidence for George is a cult. A martyr named George was venerated at Lydda (Greek Diospolis, modern Lod) in Palestine, with a shrine reputedly over his grave that pilgrims of the sixth to eighth centuries describe, and a Hauran building-inscription honours “the saints and triumphant martyrs George and companions.” No account of the man survives older than the apocryphal Passion; Delehaye and Thurston (1909) both ground the martyr on this cult. Wikipedia’s summary places his death around 303; Thurston writes that he suffered at or near Lydda “probably before the time of Constantine.”

Claims & evidence

The shrine at Diospolis (Lydda)

The cult’s anchor is a place, not a text. A martyrium of George stood at Lydda—Diospolis in Greek, modern Lod in Israel—reputedly raised over his grave, and it remained the devotional centre of his cult through late antiquity. The pilgrim Theodosius (6th c.) counts “de Emmau usque in Diospolim milia XII, ubi S. Georgius martyrizatus est; ibi et corpus eius et multa mirabilia fiunt” (from Emmaus to Diospolis twelve miles, where St George was martyred; there too is his body, and many wonders take place); Antoninus and Arculf likewise place his rest at Diospolis. Herbert Thurston (1909) writes that “an ancient cultus, going back to a very early epoch and connected with a definite locality, in itself constitutes a strong historical argument,” and Hippolyte Delehaye (1909) reads the sanctuary’s swift fame as reason to believe it rose over the tomb of a local martyr.

— Hippolyte Delehaye, Les légendes grecques des saints militaires (Paris: Picard, 1909), ch. II, pp. 45–48, 70, at archive.org; Herbert Thurston, “St. George,” Catholic Encyclopedia, vol. 6 (1909).

The Shaqqa (Eaccaea) inscription

A Greek building-inscription from Shaqqa (ancient Eaccaea) in the Hauran, in the basalt country south of Damascus, dedicates a structure to “the saints and triumphant martyrs George and companions.” Its year is 263: reckoned on the era of Bostra that is AD 368, but 368 is not a fifteenth indiction, and Waddington therefore preferred a local era whose starting point has not been fixed. Delehaye counts it among the oldest churches raised in honour of a saint. Christopher Walter treats it among the earliest evidence for the cult. It attests a cult of George in inland Syria, well beyond Lydda.

— Hippolyte Delehaye, Les légendes grecques des saints militaires (Paris: Picard, 1909), ch. II, “Saint Georges,” at archive.org; Christopher Walter, “The Origins of the Cult of Saint George,” Revue des études byzantines 53 (1995): 295–326, at Persée.

What the sources report of the man

Apart from the Passion, the al-Khiḍr identification, and the medieval dragon, the sources report a martyr venerated at Diospolis. Wikipedia’s summary places his death there around 303, in the Great Persecution under Diocletian; Thurston writes that he “suffered at or near Lydda, also known as Diospolis, in Palestine, probably before the time of Constantine,” and that the version of the legend naming Diocletian “is not primitive.” No account of his trial, his rank, or the manner of his death survives outside the Passion. Thurston reads the Decretum Gelasianum as including George among those saints “whose names are justly reverenced among men, but whose actions are only known to God.”

— Herbert Thurston, “St. George,” Catholic Encyclopedia; cf. “Saint George,” Wikipedia.
