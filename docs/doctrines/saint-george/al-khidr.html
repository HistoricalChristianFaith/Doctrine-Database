The Islamic reception: George and al-Khiḍr

Dates: medieval → modern Near East; documented in the shared shrines of Palestine, Jordan, and the Antakya region, and in the Ottoman spring festival of Hıdırellez
Summary: In the Levant George’s cult crossed a religious frontier. There he came to be identified, in living popular devotion, with al-Khiḍr (“the Green One”)— the deathless sage of Islamic lore—so that a single holy man is honoured at the same shrines as George by Christians and Khiḍr by Muslims. This page reports the evidence for the shared veneration; how the identification arose is set out on the interpretation page “How did St George come to be identified with al-Khiḍr?”

Claims & evidence

Who al-Khiḍr is

Al-Khiḍr (Arabic al-Khaḍir, “the green one”; Turkish Hızır) is, in Islamic tradition, the mysterious immortal servant of God—most famously the unnamed sage who accompanies and instructs Moses in Sūrat al-Kahf (Qur’an 18:60–82). Popular piety made him a deathless wanderer who appears to the pious, rescues travellers, and brings fertility—greenness springing up where he treads. It is this figure, not the Qur’anic text as such, with whom the Christian George was merged in the devotional life of the Near East.

— Wikipedia, “Khidr”; Josef W. Meri, The Cult of Saints among Muslims and Jews in Medieval Syria (Oxford University Press, 2002).

The shared shrines

Muslims, Christians, and sometimes Jews venerated a single figure at sites where the same holy man is George to one worshipper and Khiḍr to the next: the monastery of al-Khader (“[St George] of al-Khiḍr”) near Bethlehem, whose very name fuses the two; the church of St George at al-Salt in Jordan; and sites clustered around Mount Carmel and Antioch. At al-Khader the same shrine drew Christian and Muslim petitioners—especially for healing, and for the cure of madness—without either party surrendering its own name for the saint.

— Meri, The Cult of Saints among Muslims and Jews in Medieval Syria (2002); Wikipedia, “St. George’s Monastery, al-Khader.”

Hıdırellez: the fused festival

In the Ottoman calendar the day of St George fused with the spring festival of Hıdırellez—a name compounding Hızır (Khiḍr) and İlyās (Elijah), celebrated in early May at the turn of spring and kept close to St George’s Day (23 April, Julian). The festival marks the meeting of Khiḍr and Elijah and the renewal of the earth, and the two observances ran together in the calendar. Modern fieldwork—Jens Kreinath’s among the mixed communities of the Antakya region— documents living shared veneration of Hızır / St George / Elijah at the same shrines into the present day.

— Jens Kreinath, fieldwork on shared veneration of Hızır / St George / Elijah in the Antakya region; overview at Wikipedia, “Hıdırellez.”

Reception and debate

  • A second shared figure. After the Seven Sleepers, venerated by both Christians and Muslims, George/al-Khiḍr is the second figure on these pages held across the two faiths.
  • How the identification arose. Josef Meri and Sarah Islam treat it as a convergence of two figures of distinct origin in a shared cult of saints; Hassan Haddad and Robert D. Miller II trace it to an older figure of the Levant, the Canaanite storm-god Baal. See “How did St George come to be identified with al-Khiḍr?” and “Does the George/al-Khiḍr figure continue the cult of the Canaanite storm-god Baal?”
