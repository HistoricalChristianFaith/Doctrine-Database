The Gelasian Decree: the Passion listed among the apocrypha

Dates: attributed to Pope Gelasius (d. 496) in 495, as Herbert Thurston (1909) reports; a “6th century” document according to David Bryson and Roger Pearse (2020); a southern-Gaulish origin is not yet sourced ⚠ unverified
Summary: The Decretum Gelasianum de libris recipiendis et non recipiendis names the Passio of George among the apocryphal writings which “the Catholic and Apostolic Roman Church does not in any way receive,” and among the martyr-accounts which “appear to have been composed by heretics.” In the same passage it declares that it venerates “all the martyrs and their glorious sufferings, which are better known to God than to men.”

Claims & evidence

George named among passions “composed by heretics”

The Decretum names the George Passion in its section on martyr-acta, among passions that are not read in the Roman church:

[the accounts] are thought less attached to the order of events than they should have been; for instance the [accounts of] Cyricus and Julitta, like Georgius and the sufferings of others like these which appear to have been composed by heretics.

The modern translators of the Passion, David Bryson and Roger Pearse, connect this ruling with their Arian reading of the text—its persecutor-king and its pagan-magician “Athanasius”—and describe the text as “condemned as foolish and heretical by the church in the 6th century Decretum Gelasianum.”

— Decretum Gelasianum, sect. IV.4, English at tertullian.org; text ed. Ernst von Dobschütz, Texte und Untersuchungen 38.4 (1912).

“Does not in any way receive”

In the same document’s roll of rejected writings, under the heading that “the remaining writings which have been compiled or been recognised by heretics or schismatics the Catholic and Apostolic Roman Church does not in any way receive,” “the Passion of Georgius” stands listed as apocrypha. George’s feast and cult continued alongside this ruling. Herbert Thurston (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1909) reads the decree as including George among those saints “whose names are justly reverenced among men, but whose actions are only known to God.”

— Decretum Gelasianum, sect. V, tertullian.org; Catholic Encyclopedia, “St. George.”

The later revisions

The later Greek revisions of the Passion are soberer than the form the Decretum lists. Herbert Thurston holds that “this mitigated version has been derived from the more extravagant by a process of elimination and rationalization, not vice versa”; John Matzke, as Roger Pearse summarises him, distinguished the earlier “apocryphal version” from a “canonical version” produced by an educated reviser who made the emperor Diocletian. The dragon episode, absent from both, is first attested in the eleventh century, and was told first of Theodore Tiro (Walter).

— Herbert Thurston, “St. George,” Catholic Encyclopedia; Christopher Walter, The Warrior Saints in Byzantine Art and Tradition (2003), as cited on “St George and the dragon”; summary of Matzke at Roger Pearse, “Something at last on the Martyrdom of St George.”

Reception and debate

  • The book and the cult. The Roman church that excluded the Passio from public reading continued to venerate the martyr of Lydda.
  • The ruling in the Golden Legend. Seven centuries on, the Golden Legend records that George’s legend was “numbered among other scriptures apocryphal in the council of Nicene, because his martyrdom hath no certain relation,” ascribing the ruling to Nicaea, while also retelling the dragon episode.
