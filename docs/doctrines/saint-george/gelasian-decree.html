The Gelasian Decree: the Passion condemned as apocryphal

Dates: early 6th c. (probably southern Gaul; traditionally ascribed to Pope Gelasius, d. 496)
Summary: The Roman church’s verdict on the George legend came fast. The Decretum Gelasianum de libris recipiendis et non recipiendis names the Passio Georgii among the apocryphal writings the church “does not in any way receive,” and singles it out as the sort of martyr-account “composed by heretics.” It is a rejection of the fabulous Acts, never of the saint—and it fixes the honest position that George’s deeds “are known only to God.”

Claims & evidence

George named among the “composed by heretics”

The Decretum does not merely omit the George Passion; it flags it by name as a specimen of a whole disreputable class. In the section on martyr-acta, it warns that some passions have strayed from history and bear the marks of heretical authorship:

[the accounts] are thought less attached to the order of events than they should have been; for instance the [accounts of] Cyricus and Julitta, like Georgius and the sufferings of others like these which appear to have been composed by heretics.

The verdict “composed by heretics” dovetails exactly with the Arian reading of the Passion—its fictional king and its pagan-magician “Athanasius”—and it is why the modern translators say the text was “condemned as foolish and heretical by the church in the 6th century Decretum Gelasianum.”

— Decretum Gelasianum, sect. IV.4, English at tertullian.org; text ed. Ernst von Dobschütz, Texte und Untersuchungen 38.4 (1912).

“Does not in any way receive”

In the same document’s roll of rejected writings, under the heading that “the remaining writings which have been compiled or been recognised by heretics or schismatics the Catholic and Apostolic Roman Church does not in any way receive,” the Passion of George stands listed as apocrypha. What is disowned is the book, not the man: the church continued to keep George’s feast and honour his cult while binning the account of his deeds. The complementary tradition, preserved in the Roman martyrology’s reticence, ranks him among those martyrs “whose names are justly reverenced among men, but whose acts are known only to God.”

— Decretum Gelasianum, sect. V, tertullian.org; Catholic Encyclopedia, “St. George.”

Omission, not accretion

George’s legend inverts the usual life-cycle of a saint’s story. For most saints the earliest life is the soberest and the marvels pile up over centuries; George’s original Passion was already so outlandish that Rome condemned it, and the later Greek revisions toned it down rather than embellishing it—an “omission, not accretion” trajectory. The lone great addition to the story, the dragon, arrived seven hundred years later and from another saint’s legend entirely. So the sixth-century condemnation marks the high-water mark of the excess, not its beginning.

— discussion at Roger Pearse, “Something at last on the Martyrdom of St George.”
