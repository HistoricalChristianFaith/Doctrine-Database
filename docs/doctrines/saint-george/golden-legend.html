The Golden Legend: the dragon-slayer in the Latin West

Dates: Jacobus de Voragine, Legenda aurea, c. 1260; Englished by William Caxton, 1483
Summary: Jacobus de Voragine gathered the dragon-tale into his Golden Legend, and through Caxton’s printing it became the standard English story: the pond-dragon of Silene, the king’s daughter led out by lot, the saint who wounds the beast and leads it tamed on the maiden’s girdle, and the mass baptism of a whole city. In the prologue to the life Voragine records that George’s legend was reckoned apocryphal, ascribing the ruling to “the council of Nicene”; the list of apocrypha that names the George Passion is the Decretum Gelasianum.

Claims & evidence

The dragon of Silene

Voragine sets the story at “Silene, in the province of Libya,” by a poisonous lake:

And by this city was a stagne or a pond like a sea, wherein was a dragon which envenomed all the country.

The townsfolk feed the beast two sheep a day, then a sheep and a human chosen by lot, until the lot falls on the king’s own daughter:

So it happed that many of them of the town were then delivered, insomuch that the lot fell upon the king’s daughter, whereof the king was sorry, and said unto the people: For the love of the gods take gold and silver and all that I have, and let me have my daughter.

— Jacobus de Voragine, The Golden Legend, “The Life of S. George,” tr. William Caxton (1483), at CatholicSaints.info; also in the Fordham Internet Medieval Sourcebook (Golden Legend, vol. 3).

The girdle and the tamed beast

George rides up, wounds the dragon with his spear, and then bids the princess bind it with her girdle, whereupon it follows her into the city like a pet:

Deliver to me your girdle, and bind it about the neck of the dragon and be not afeard. When she had done so the dragon followed her as it had been a meek beast and debonair.

The mass baptism

George promises to slay the beast if the city will believe and be baptized; the king and people consent, and George kills it:

Then were there well fifteen thousand men baptized, without women and children.

Herbert Thurston (1909) holds that the dragon episode “probably owes its wide diffusion” to the Golden Legend. George is patron of England, where the national synod of Oxford ordered his day kept in 1222, and principal patron of the Order of the Garter, founded by Edward III c. 1347; Thurston notes that the girdle incident “may possibly have something to do with St. George’s selection as patron of the Order of the Garter.” His patronages of Georgia, Aragon and Genoa are not yet sourced ⚠ unverified.

— Herbert Thurston, “St. George,” Catholic Encyclopedia, vol. 6 (1909).

Voragine’s note on the apocryphal legend

In the prologue that precedes the narrative, Voragine records the standing of George’s legend:

And his legend is numbered among other scriptures apocryphal in the council of Nicene, because his martyrdom hath no certain relation.

The list of apocrypha that names the George Passion is the Decretum Gelasianum, attributed to Pope Gelasius in 495 (Thurston) and called a sixth-century document by David Bryson and Roger Pearse (2020).

— Herbert Thurston, “St. George,” Catholic Encyclopedia; David Bryson and Roger Pearse, “The Passio of St George (BHL 3363)” (2020).

Reception and debate

  • The dragon episode. The episode Voragine retells is absent from the early sources. Christopher Walter dates its earliest narrative and images to the eleventh century, the story having been told first of St Theodore; Charles Clermont-Ganneau (1876) linked it to the Perseus-and-Andromeda story localized at Joppa near Lydda; Herbert Thurston (1909) rejected derivation from pagan sources. Whether the dragon-fight is a historical event is set out on “St George and the dragon”.
  • Ruling and legend together. The Golden Legend life carries both the note that the legend was classed as apocryphal and the dragon-story, which the earlier form of the legend did not contain.
