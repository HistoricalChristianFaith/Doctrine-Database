The Golden Legend: the dragon-slayer fixed for the West

Dates: Jacobus de Voragine, Legenda aurea, c. 1260; Englished by William Caxton, 1483
Summary: Jacobus de Voragine gathered the newly-attached dragon-tale into his Golden Legend, and through Caxton’s printing it became the standard English story: the pond-dragon of Silene, the king’s daughter led out by lot, the saint who wounds the beast and leads it tamed on the maiden’s girdle, and the mass baptism of a whole city. Voragine is even candid that George’s Acts were reckoned apocryphal—a garbled echo of the very condemnation the church had pronounced seven centuries before.

Claims & evidence

The dragon of Silene

Voragine sets the story at “Silene, in the province of Libya,” by a poisonous lake:

And by this city was a stagne or a pond like a sea, wherein was a dragon which envenomed all the country.

The townsfolk feed the beast two sheep a day, then a sheep and a human chosen by lot, until the lot falls on the king’s own daughter:

So it happed that many of them of the town were then delivered, insomuch that the lot fell upon the king’s daughter, whereof the king was sorry, and said unto the people: For the love of the gods take gold and silver and all that I have, and let me have my daughter.

— Jacobus de Voragine, The Golden Legend, “The Life of S. George,” tr. William Caxton (1483), at CatholicSaints.info; also in the Fordham Internet Medieval Sourcebook (Golden Legend, vol. 3).

The girdle and the tamed beast

George rides up, wounds the dragon with his spear, and then bids the princess bind it with her girdle, whereupon it follows her into the city like a pet:

Deliver to me your girdle, and bind it about the neck of the dragon and be not afeard. When she had done so the dragon followed her as it had been a meek beast and debonair.

The mass baptism

George promises to slay the beast if the city will believe and be baptized; the king and people consent, and George kills it:

Then were there well fifteen thousand men baptized, without women and children.

From this scene the dragon-slayer passed into heraldry, patronage, and pageant—George made patron of England (and of Georgia, Aragon, Genoa, and more) and titular of the Order of the Garter.

Voragine’s own apocryphal caveat

Strikingly, Voragine closes by conceding that the material he has just retold is not to be trusted as history:

And his legend is numbered among other scriptures apocryphal in the council of Nicene, because his martyrdom hath no certain relation.

The attribution to “the council of Nicene” is a muddle—the actual ruling was the sixth-century Gelasian Decree, not Nicaea—but the memory that George’s Acts had been branded apocryphal survived even in the book that did most to popularize them.

Notes

  • The dragon is the late graft. The episode Voragine fixes for the West is absent from every early source; it surfaces only in the eleventh century, first attached to St Theodore and only then transferred to George, and is structurally the Perseus-and-Andromeda myth localized near Lydda. Whether the dragon-fight is a historical event is rated weak on its own page (the martyr beneath it remaining plausible).
  • Condemnation remembered, marvel retold. The page preserves both halves of the George paradox at once—the church’s verdict of “apocryphal” and the very legend it condemned, now crowned with a brand-new dragon.
