The extravagant Passio Georgii (Pseudo-Pasicrates)

Dates: original apocryphal form most likely 5th c.; Greek (BHG 670) mostly lost, a 5th-c. Vienna palimpsest and early Latin translations surviving (BHL 3363)
Summary: Into the documentary vacuum left by the martyr poured one of the most lurid legends of late antiquity. The Passio Georgii—ascribed to a servant, “Pasicrates,” who claims to have witnessed it—has George tried by a fictional king Datianus/Dacianus, tortured for seven years by seventy-two assembled kings, dying three times and being raised three times by the archangel Michael before a final beheading. Its excesses are so plain, and its villain-names so pointed, that scholars read an Arian hand behind it—and the church would soon disown it outright.

Claims & evidence

The text and its witnesses

This is the oldest recoverable form of the George legend, which John Matzke labelled “the apocryphal text” to distinguish it from a later, cleaned-up “canonical” revision. The translators of the modern English version describe it plainly:

This is a translation into English of the oldest form of the St George legend, most likely 5th century in date, and condemned as foolish and heretical by the church in the 6th century Decretum Gelasianum… The Greek text of this original form is mostly lost, but a palimpsest in Vienna contains some 5th century leaves… Two Latin translations were made of this text at an early date.

— David Bryson & Roger Pearse, “Pseudo-Passecrates, The Passio of St George (BHL 3363)” (2020), Introduction; cf. the Oxford Cult of Saints record E05759.

The seventy-two kings and the catalogue of torments

The tale opens not with a Roman governor but with a mythical world-emperor. “The devil took hold of Datianus, king of the Persians and king over the four corners of the world,” who summons the kings of the earth— “to the number of seventy-two”—and displays an arsenal of tortures to cow the Christians:

Among these were brass boxes, in which were twice-sharpened swords, frying-pans, cooking pots, very sharp saws, bronze bulls, fiery hooks fixed into boots, iron wheels and many other types of torments, without number.

George—here “a native of Cappadocia, and an officer over many soldiers”—gives his gold to the poor and denounces the idols, and the torments begin: the rack and iron claws “so that his intestines came out,” salt in the wounds, spiked boots, a pierced helmet, a column laid on his stomach that “eighteen men could with difficulty carry.” None of it harms him.

— Bryson & Pearse, Passio of St George, chs. 1–4.

Three deaths, three resurrections by Michael

The structural heart of the legend is announced by Christ himself in the prison, and it is what most sharply marks the text as fable rather than martyr-record:

Because in your suffering, which you are about to suffer from these seventy-two kings for seven years, no tortures will be injurious to you. Three times you will die and three times I will resurrect you. The fourth time, indeed, I will come to you.

And so it runs. Bound to the wheel, George is “chopped into ten pieces,” and “he gave up his spirit”; the king throws his bones into a pit, but the Lord descends on a cloud, “the angel over the cherubim” Michael sounds a trumpet, and George is raised: “St George, having been raised from death, immediately began to walk about.” Twice more he is killed and restored, and at the last, the seven years and the resurrections spent, “the servant of God was peacefully beheaded, and water and milk came forth from his body.”

— Bryson & Pearse, Passio of St George, chs. 5, 9, 20.

The sorcerer Athanasius

When ordinary torture fails, Dacianus advertises for a magician—“If any magician can dispel the magic of the Christians, let him come to me”—and a sorcerer named Athanasius answers, splitting a bull in two by an incantation and rejoining it. Ordered to poison George, he watches the cup do nothing, and is himself converted on the spot: “George, friend of Christ… set free my soul and give me the seal of Christ.” For this he is beheaded. The choice of name is not innocent, as the translators note: to give a pagan magician the name of Athanasius, the great anti-Arian champion of Nicaea, “suggests a deliberate slur, and that the author of the work was an Arian.”

— Bryson & Pearse, Passio of St George, chs. 6–7 and n. 32.

The Arian coloring and the George-of-Cappadocia theory

The fictional persecutor, the villain-name Athanasius, and the hero’s stated Cappadocian origin have led a line of scholars from Matzke onward to suspect that the martyr-George of Lydda was here entangled with a very different historical George—George of Cappadocia, the Arian intruder-bishop of Alexandria lynched under Julian in 361, an enemy of the Athanasian party. On this reading the wildest layer of the legend is not naïve piety but Arian polemic, later “quietly changed” when the offending king Datianus was replaced by the historically real Diocletian in the revised recension. The theory is a plausible account of the text’s strange features rather than a settled fact, and it is weighed no further here; the point for the timeline is that the earliest recoverable George-story is already a fully-fledged and tendentious fiction.

— John E. Matzke, “Contributions to the History of the Legend of Saint George,” PMLA 17 (1902): 464–535; discussion at Roger Pearse, “Something at last on the Martyrdom of St George”; Delehaye, Les légendes grecques des saints militaires (1909), archive.org.
