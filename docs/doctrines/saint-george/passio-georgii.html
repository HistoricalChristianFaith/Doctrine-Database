The Passio Georgii (Pseudo-Pasicrates)

Dates: original apocryphal form most likely 5th c.; Greek (BHG 670) mostly lost, a 5th-c. Vienna palimpsest and early Latin translations surviving (BHL 3363)
Summary: The Passio Georgii is the oldest recoverable account of George’s martyrdom. Ascribed to a servant, “Pasicrates,” who claims to have witnessed it, it has George tried by a king Datianus/Dacianus, whom its translators call fictional, tortured for seven years by seventy-two assembled kings, dying three times and being raised three times by the archangel Michael before a final beheading. Matzke, Delehaye, and the translators Bryson and Pearse read in it traces of George of Cappadocia or of an Arian author, and the Decretum Gelasianum lists it among the apocrypha.

Claims & evidence

The text and its witnesses

This is the oldest recoverable form of the George legend, which John Matzke labelled “the apocryphal text” to distinguish it from a later, cleaned-up “canonical” revision. The translators of the modern English version describe it:

This is a translation into English of the oldest form of the St George legend, most likely 5th century in date, and condemned as foolish and heretical by the church in the 6th century Decretum Gelasianum… The Greek text of this original form is mostly lost, but a palimpsest in Vienna contains some 5th century leaves… Two Latin translations were made of this text at an early date.

— David Bryson & Roger Pearse, “Pseudo-Passecrates, The Passio of St George (BHL 3363)” (2020), Introduction; cf. the Oxford Cult of Saints record E05759.

The seventy-two kings and the catalogue of torments

The tale opens not with a Roman governor but with a mythical world-emperor. “The devil took hold of Datianus, king of the Persians and king over the four corners of the world,” who summons the kings of the earth— “to the number of seventy-two”—and displays an arsenal of tortures to cow the Christians:

Among these were brass boxes, in which were twice-sharpened swords, frying-pans, cooking pots, very sharp saws, bronze bulls, fiery hooks fixed into boots, iron wheels and many other types of torments, without number.

George—here “a native of Cappadocia, and an officer over many soldiers”—gives his gold to the poor and denounces the idols, and the torments begin: the rack and iron claws “so that his intestines came out,” salt in the wounds, spiked boots, a pierced helmet, a column laid on his stomach that “eighteen men could with difficulty carry.” None of it harms him.

— Bryson & Pearse, Passio of St George, chs. 1–4.

Three deaths, three resurrections by Michael

The pattern of the legend is announced by Christ in the prison:

Because in your suffering, which you are about to suffer from these seventy-two kings for seven years, no tortures will be injurious to you. Three times you will die and three times I will resurrect you. The fourth time, indeed, I will come to you.

And so it runs. Bound to the wheel, George is “chopped into ten pieces,” and “he gave up his spirit”; the king throws his bones into a pit, but the Lord descends on a cloud, “the angel over the cherubim” Michael sounds a trumpet, and George is raised: “St George, having been raised from death, immediately began to walk about.” Twice more he is killed and restored, and at the last, the seven years and the resurrections spent, “the servant of God was peacefully beheaded, and water and milk came forth from his body.”

— Bryson & Pearse, Passio of St George, chs. 5, 9, 20.

The sorcerer Athanasius

When ordinary torture fails, Dacianus advertises for a magician—“If any magician can dispel the magic of the Christians, let him come to me”—and a sorcerer named Athanasius answers, splitting a bull in two by an incantation and rejoining it. Ordered to poison George, he watches the cup do nothing, and is himself converted on the spot: “George, friend of Christ… set free my soul and give me the seal of Christ.” For this he is beheaded. The translators comment on the name: Athanasius is “the famous and revered leader of the anti-Arian party after the First Council of Nicaea,” and to give it to a pagan magician “suggests a deliberate slur, and that the author of the work was an Arian.”

— Bryson & Pearse, Passio of St George, chs. 6–7 and n. 32.

The Arian reading and George of Cappadocia

The persecutor-king, the villain-name Athanasius, and the hero’s stated Cappadocian origin have led scholars from Matzke (1902) onward to connect the text with a different historical George—George of Cappadocia, the Arian bishop of Alexandria lynched under Julian in 361, an opponent of Athanasius. Delehaye (1909) judges the reminiscences of the bishop’s story in the Pseudo-Pasicrates to be “incontestables” while denying that the two Georges are one person; Bryson and Pearse read the text as the work of an Arian author, which they suggest “may explain why the Decretum Gelasianum condemns a Passio S. Georgis as the work of heretics.” They note that “there was no Roman emperor named ‘Datianus’, and later revisions of the legend quietly change it to Diocletian,” and Matzke, as Roger Pearse summarises him, held that a “canonical version” was produced by an educated reviser who made the emperor Diocletian. Edward Gibbon’s thesis, that the saint is the Arian bishop himself, is set out with the local-martyr reading of Bury, Delehaye and Thurston on the interpretation page “Was the George venerated at Lydda a local martyr, or the Arian bishop George of Cappadocia?”

— John E. Matzke, “Contributions to the History of the Legend of Saint George,” PMLA 17 (1902): 464–535; Bryson & Pearse, Passio of St George, nn. 8, 32; Herbert Thurston, “St. George,” Catholic Encyclopedia; discussion at Roger Pearse, “Something at last on the Martyrdom of St George”; Delehaye, Les légendes grecques des saints militaires (1909), pp. 71–72, archive.org.

Reception and debate

  • Diffusion and rejection. This Passion is the basis of the Syriac, Coptic, and other oriental versions, and is the George Passio listed among the apocrypha in the Decretum Gelasianum: see the Gelasian Decree.
  • The later revisions. Later Greek revisers (Andrew of Crete and others) produced a soberer form; Thurston (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1909) holds that “this mitigated version has been derived from the more extravagant by a process of elimination and rationalization, not vice versa.”
  • No dragon here. This fifth-century legend contains no princess, no lake, and no dragon; the dragon episode is first attested in the eleventh century (Walter).
