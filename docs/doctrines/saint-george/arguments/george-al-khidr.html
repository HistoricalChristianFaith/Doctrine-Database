St George and al-Khiḍr: a genuine shared Christian–Muslim cult?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: sound — that St George and the Islamic al-Khiḍr (“the Green One”) were identified and venerated as one figure across the medieval and modern Near East, at shrines and festivals frequented by Christians and Muslims alike, is thoroughly documented in the medieval sources and in modern ethnography. The fusion is a real, living phenomenon — not an apologetic construct — and makes George, after the Seven Sleepers, the wiki’s second and most vivid case of a holy figure shared by both faiths. What is weaker, and kept strictly distinct, is the further claim about the fusion’s origin and direction: that George is merely a late Christian overlay on a far older “Green One” (Elijah, or the Canaanite storm-god Baal) reaching back to pre-Christian antiquity — a suggestive but speculative thesis that outruns the evidence · advanced by the shared devotional tradition itself and documented by Meri, Kreinath, and Miller.

The claim

Two claims must be told apart, and only the first is what this page rates sound.

  1. The shared cult (rated here). That in the Levant a single holy man is honoured under two names — George by Christians, al-Khiḍr (Arabic al-Khaḍir, Turkish Hızır) by Muslims — at the same shrines, on overlapping feast-days, with the same protective and healing powers ascribed to him.
  2. The deep-origins thesis (kept distinct). That this identification is the visible tip of an unbroken continuity running back through the prophet Elijah to the Canaanite storm-god Baal, another dragon-slaying “Green One” of the same Levantine ground — so that the historical martyr of Lydda is almost incidental to a figure “thousands of years old.”

Who al-Khiḍr is

Al-Khiḍr (“the green” or “the verdant one”) is not named in the Qur’an but is traditionally identified with the mysterious “servant of God” whom Moses seeks out and follows in Sūrat al-Kahf (18:60–82) — the same chapter that carries the Companions of the Cave and Dhū’l-Qarnayn. In later Islamic (especially Sufi) lore al-Khiḍr acquires immortality, hidden knowledge, and the power to appear to the pious in need — a deathless wanderer who guides and rescues. It is this figure, not the Qur’anic verses as such, that fused with the Christian George.

The evidence: the shared cult is real and well documented

Shrines held in common. The identification is anchored in places, not merely in books. The monastery and village of al-Khader (“[St George] the Green”) near Bethlehem — the very name is Khiḍr — is an Orthodox site of St George long frequented by Muslims (and formerly Jews) seeking his cure, especially of madness; the church of St George at al-Salt in Jordan functions to this day as a “co-produced” Christian–Muslim shrine, restored in 2004 by a joint municipal vote; comparable George/Khiḍr sites cluster around Mount Carmel, Lydda itself, and the Antioch region. Muslims venerating a saint at a Christian church, and identifying him with their own Khiḍr, is not an anomaly at these sites but their normal life.1

Medieval attestation. The equation is not a modern folkloric curiosity read back into the past. Medieval Muslim writers on the shrines of Syria — the material gathered by Josef Meri — already report the identification of al-Khiḍr with George (and with Elijah) at named holy places, and the Christian and Muslim veneration converging on them. The cult of saints in medieval Syria was, in Meri’s account, routinely shared across the confessional line, and George/Khiḍr is its clearest instance.2

A festival that fused. In the Ottoman world St George’s Day (23 April) coincided with the spring festival of Hıdırellez — a compound of Hızır (al-Khiḍr) and Ilyās (Elijah), the two immortals believed to meet on that day — and the two observances ran together. Modern anthropological fieldwork (Jens Kreinath, in the Antakya/Hatay region) has documented the still-living result: a single “shared infrastructure” of pilgrimage in which Muslim devotion to Hızır, Jewish memory of Elijah, and Christian cult of St George are woven together at the same sites and seasons.3

Adversarial assessment

The shared cult holds; the deep-origins thesis overreaches. On the first claim there is little to argue: the shrines, the medieval reports, the festival, and the fieldwork converge, and the phenomenon is precisely the kind of cross-confessional veneration that the Seven Sleepers case also displays. Granting it costs nothing and explains much. The caution attaches only to the second claim, and it is real:

  • Why George and Khiḍr merged is genuinely uncertain. The shared “green”/fertility associations, the dragon- or serpent-slaying motif, the role as protector and healer, and the common Levantine ground all plausibly drew the two figures together — but which way the influence ran, and how early, is not fixed by the evidence. That the identification is attested is one thing; that it is explained is another.
  • The Baal-continuity thesis is a hypothesis, not a datum. Robert Miller’s argument that St George/Khiḍr shrines sit atop, and inherit from, Bronze-Age cult of the dragon-slaying storm-god Baal — and that the modern saint is a “conglomeration of mentifacts thousands of years old” — is a serious, multidisciplinary case, but it depends on inferring continuity across long documentary gaps from site-geography and motif-parallels. Parallels of function (a green, life-giving, monster-slaying protector) recur independently and do not by themselves establish a single unbroken line; the thesis is suggestive and worth stating, but it is not on the same footing as the well-attested medieval fusion, and it should not be smuggled in on the strength of it.
  • Keep the historical martyr distinct. None of this dissolves the real, if shadowy, martyr of Lydda (see the timeline). That a historical George existed and that his cult later fused with Khiḍr are compatible; the fusion is a fact about the reception of the saint, not a verdict that there was no saint to receive.

Assessment

Sound — as the claim that St George and al-Khiḍr were identified and venerated together in a genuine shared Christian–Muslim cult. The shrines held in common (al-Khader, al-Salt, the Carmel and Antioch sites), the medieval attestation gathered by Meri, and the living festival of Hıdırellez documented into the present make that as secure as such things get; it is one of the wiki’s two clearest cases — with the Seven Sleepers — of a single holy figure honoured across both faiths. The rating stops there. The further, more romantic claim — that George is a thin recent veneer on an immemorial “Green One” descending from Elijah and ultimately from Canaanite Baal — is a stimulating hypothesis that reaches past what the evidence secures, and is left as speculative, not folded into the sound verdict on the shared cult itself.
