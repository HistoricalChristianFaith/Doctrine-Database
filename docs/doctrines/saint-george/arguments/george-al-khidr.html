How did St George come to be identified with al-Khiḍr?

Disputed reading · on Saint George · witness: the Islamic reception (George and al-Khiḍr)

Related readings: Does the George/al-Khiḍr figure continue the cult of the Canaanite storm-god Baal? · Was the George venerated at Lydda a local martyr, or the Arian bishop George of Cappadocia?

The passage

A nineteenth-century report of the shared veneration at George’s shrine at Lydda (it does not name al-Khiḍr):

Les musulmans comme les chrétiens vénèrent cet endroit, car ils croient que sous l’autel repose encore, dans une crypte, une partie des restes du saint martyr.

— Victor Guérin, Description géographique de la Palestine: Judée, vol. I (1868), on the ruined church of St George at Lydda (“Muslims as well as Christians venerate this place, for they believe that part of the remains of the holy martyr still rests in a crypt beneath the altar”), as quoted by Hippolyte Delehaye, Les légendes grecques des saints militaires (1909), p. 47, archive.org.1

Al-Khiḍr (“the green” or “the verdant one”; Turkish Hızır) is not named in the Qur’an but is traditionally identified with the “servant of God” whom Moses follows in Sūrat al-Kahf (18:60–82), the chapter that also carries the Companions of the Cave and Dhū’l-Qarnayn; in later Islamic, especially Sufi, lore he is a deathless wanderer with hidden knowledge who appears to the pious in need. At shrines such as the monastery of al-Khader near Bethlehem, the church of St George at al-Salt in Jordan, and sites around Mount Carmel, Lydda and Antioch, the same holy man is honoured as George by Christians and as al-Khiḍr by Muslims, and in the Ottoman world St George’s Day (23 April) ran together with the spring festival of Hıdırellez (Hızır + Ilyās, Elijah).2

The question

The shared veneration of George and al-Khiḍr is documented from medieval sources to modern fieldwork. What is disputed is how the identification arose: whether it carries forward an older figure of the Levant’s sacred landscape, or whether two figures of different origin converged within the cult of saints shared by Christians and Muslims.

An older Levantine figure carried into the cult of George

On this reading George and al-Khiḍr meet because both inherited the shrines and functions of an older storm-and-fertility figure of the same ground.

Held by: Hassan S. Haddad (“‘Georgic’ Cults and Saints of the Levant,” 1969); Nicolas Wyatt; Ali Qleibo; Robert D. Miller II (Baal, St. George, and Khiḍr, 2019).3

Haddad proposed that many Levantine George-shrines lie atop earlier shrines of Baal, because both figures slay dragons and bring fertility; Wyatt speaks of a “narrative continuity” from Baal to George. On this reading the features that join George and al-Khiḍr belong to that older layer: the “green,” life-giving character built into al-Khiḍr’s name, the dragon- or serpent-slaying deed, the protective and healing role, and the concentration of George/Khiḍr sites on the heights of the old storm-god, Mount Carmel, Bashan and Hermon. Al-Khiḍr’s popular merger with Elijah, the prophet of the Carmel contest with the prophets of Baal, gives the chain a further link. Miller tests this continuity site by site in a book-length study; his case is set out on its own page. On this reading the shared cult of George and al-Khiḍr is the latest form of a figure the communities of the Levant had long honoured on the same ground.

A convergence of two holy figures in a shared cult of saints

On this reading a Christian martyr and an Islamic sage of distinct origins were drawn together in a cult of saints that Christians and Muslims shared, and the composite figure took shape in that common practice.

Held by: Josef W. Meri (“Re-Appropriating Sacred Space,” 1999; The Cult of Saints among Muslims and Jews in Medieval Syria, 2002); Jens Kreinath (fieldwork in the Antakya region); Sarah Islam (“The Church of Saint George in al-Salt as a Co-Produced Religious Site,” 2025).4

Meri gathers medieval Muslim writers on the holy places of Syria who already report the identification of al-Khiḍr with George, and with Elijah, at named sites, and describes a cult of saints routinely shared across the confessional line, of which George/Khiḍr is a leading instance. Sarah Islam starts from the distinct origins of the two figures: “Saint George and al-Khidr emerge in different eras and civilizations.” Medieval Muslim exegetes identified the servant of Sūrat al-Kahf with al-Khiḍr; Muslim and Druze communities then joined him with the Christian saint, so that sites venerate “both individuals as a composite and co-produced figure.” At al-Salt the church is known to Christians as al-Khader Church and to Muslims as Maqam al-Khidr, and in 2004 a local council of Muslim and Christian members voted communal funds for its restoration. Kreinath’s fieldwork in Antakya documents the living form of this practice: a shared infrastructure of pilgrimage in which devotion to Hızır, the memory of Elijah and the cult of St George meet at the same sites and seasons, as Hıdırellez meets St George’s Day in the calendar. On this reading the George/Khiḍr figure was formed by the communities that honour him together, in the lived religion of the medieval and modern Levant.5
