Does the George/al-Khiḍr figure continue the cult of the Canaanite storm-god Baal?

Disputed reading · on Saint George · witness: modern scholarship

Related readings: How did St George come to be identified with al-Khiḍr? · Was the George venerated at Lydda a local martyr, or the Arian bishop George of Cappadocia?

The passage

In this book, Robert D. Miller II untangles these complicated connections and reveals how, especially in his Middle Eastern guise, St. George is a reincarnation of the Canaanite storm god Baal, another “Green One” who in Ugaritic texts slays dragons.

— Publisher’s description of Robert D. Miller II, Baal, St. George, and Khiḍr: A Study of the Historical Geography of the Levant (Penn State University Press, 2019), at Project MUSE; the Eisenbrauns description adds that the book argues “direct continuity exists” from Baal to St George/Khiḍr.1

The question

George and al-Khiḍr are honoured together at shrines across the Levant, some of them on heights sacred to the storm-god in the Bronze Age. What is disputed is whether the figure they share began as the cult of a fourth-century martyr later joined to al-Khiḍr, or continues a figure of the region’s religion reaching back to the Canaanite Baal.

A fourth-century martyr’s cult, later shared with al-Khiḍr

On this reading the figure begins at a martyr’s tomb at Lydda, and its identification with al-Khiḍr and its dragon came to it in later centuries.

Held by: Hippolyte Delehaye (Les légendes grecques des saints militaires, 1909); Herbert Thurston (“St. George,” Catholic Encyclopedia, 1909); Christopher Walter (“The Origins of the Cult of Saint George,” 1995); Josef W. Meri (The Cult of Saints among Muslims and Jews in Medieval Syria, 2002).2

Delehaye begins the dossier with places and dates: the Eaccaea inscription in the Hauran, and the sanctuary at Lydda where, from the sixth century, pilgrims found the martyr’s tomb. He reads the sanctuary’s swift fame as reason to believe it rose over the tomb of a local martyr whose acts were never written or were soon forgotten (see the identity question). Walter traces the cult outward from Diospolis. The identification with al-Khiḍr is reported by medieval Muslim writers on the holy places of Syria, which Meri gathers as part of a cult of saints Christians and Muslims shared in the medieval period. The dragon belongs to a later layer still: Thurston calls it “a very late development,” on the ground of its late occurrence rejects the attempts made to derive it from pagan sources, and suggests it may have grown from an allegory of the tyrant Dadianus, whom the older text sometimes calls a dragon; Walter dates its earliest narrative and images to the eleventh century, the story having been told first of Theodore Tiro (see the dragon question). On this reading the George/Khiḍr figure was built in dated stages on a martyr-cult of late antiquity.3

A continuation of the storm-god Baal

On this reading the George/Khiḍr figure, especially in the Levant, continues the Canaanite storm-god Baal, another dragon-slaying “Green One” honoured on the same heights.

Held by: Hassan S. Haddad (“‘Georgic’ Cults and Saints of the Levant,” 1969); Nicolas Wyatt; Ali Qleibo; Robert D. Miller II (Catholic University of America, Baal, St. George, and Khiḍr, 2019).4

Haddad proposed in 1969 that many Levantine George-shrines lie atop earlier Baal-shrines, both figures slaying dragons and bringing fertility; Miller calls this “a path of research … that has been followed by no one” save the Palestinian anthropologist Ali Qleibo and the Ugaritologist Nicolas Wyatt, who speaks of a “narrative continuity” from Baal to George. Miller tests that continuity on its literary and archaeological sides across a chain of sites: Mount Carmel, Western Galilee, Bashan and the Mount Hermon range, Taybeh, Lod (Lydda) and Jerusalem. Four strands carry the case. Al-Khiḍr is “the Green One,” and Baal is the storm-and-fertility god of the greening land. Baal’s signature deed in the Ugaritic Baal Cycle is the defeat of Yamm and the twisting seven-headed serpent Lôtān (biblical Leviathan), the dragon-slaying that later attaches to George. The George/Khiḍr sites cluster on the ground of the ancient weather-god: Carmel, the mountain of the Elijah–Baal contest of 1 Kings 18, Bashan, and Hermon, a storm-god high place since the Bronze Age. And al-Khiḍr is popularly merged with Elijah, who in the Hıdırellez festival meets him each spring, so that the prophet of the Carmel contest forms a bridge by which the storm-god’s mountain and functions pass to a saint. On this reading the figure is “thousands of years old,” and the green, life-giving, dragon-slaying protector honoured on the heights where the storm-god was worshipped is one figure across the ages.5
