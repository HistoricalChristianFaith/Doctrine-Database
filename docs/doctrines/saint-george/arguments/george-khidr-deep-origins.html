Is George/al-Khiḍr the Canaanite storm-god Baal in a new guise?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: weak — that the George/al-Khiḍr figure descends by a direct, unbroken line from the Bronze-Age Canaanite storm-god Baal — another dragon-slaying “Green One” of the same Levantine ground, with George-shrines sitting atop former Baal cult-places — is a serious, genuinely-advocated thesis, but on its best case it does not hold: it must bridge a roughly thousand-year documentary gap on the strength of shrine-superposition and motif-parallels, and the dragon-slaying storm-god is exactly the kind of type that recurs independently, so parallel does not establish descent. A soft version — that the later cult reused older sacred landscape and absorbed storm/fertility associations — is more defensible, but that is not the distinctive claim advanced · advanced by Robert D. Miller II (2019/2022), building on Haddad, Wyatt, and Qleibo.

Related arguments: the Green One — see also Are St George and al-Khiḍr venerated as a single figure in a genuine shared Christian–Muslim cult?

The claim

The companion argument rates sound the fact that George and al-Khiḍr are venerated together as one figure across the medieval and modern Near East. This page rates the further, separable claim about the fusion’s origin and depth: that the identification is the visible tip of an unbroken continuity running back through the prophet Elijah to the Canaanite storm-god Baal, so that — especially in his Middle-Eastern guise — St George is, in effect, “a reincarnation of the Canaanite storm god Baal, another ‘Green One’ who in Ugaritic texts slays dragons,” and the historical martyr of Lydda is almost incidental to a figure “thousands of years old.”1

Who advances it

The thesis has a named proponent and a monograph. Robert D. Miller II — Ordinary Professor of Old Testament at the Catholic University of America — argued it at book length in Baal, St. George, and Khiḍr: A Study of the Historical Geography of the Levant (Eisenbrauns / Penn State University Press, 2019; the study edition 2022). Miller himself notes the case rests on very thin prior scholarship: Hassan S. Haddad, in a 1969 essay, first proposed that many Levantine George-shrines lie atop earlier Baal-shrines because both figures slay dragons and bring fertility — “a path of research,” Miller writes, “that has been followed by no one,” save musings by the Palestinian anthropologist Ali Qleibo and the Ugaritologist Nicolas Wyatt, who speaks of a “narrative continuity” from Baal to George. Miller sets out to test both the literary and the archaeological sides of that continuity across a chain of sites — Mount Carmel, Western Galilee, Bashan and the Mount Hermon range, Taybeh, Lod (Lydda), and Jerusalem.2

The evidence for

Steelmanned, the case is genuinely suggestive and has several real strands:

  • A shared epithet. Al-Khiḍr is “the Green One,” and Baal is a storm-and-fertility god of the greening land; the “green,” life-giving character of the figure is not a modern gloss but built into the name.
  • A shared combat-myth. The dragon- or serpent-slaying that later attaches to George is precisely Baal’s signature deed in the Ugaritic Baal Cycle, where he defeats Yamm and the twisting seven-headed serpent Lôtān (biblical Leviathan) — the same Near-Eastern combat-myth (Chaoskampf) the wiki weighs on its chaos-monster argument.
  • A concentration on the old storm-god ground. The George/Khiḍr sites Miller maps cluster on exactly the terrain of the ancient weather-god: Mount Carmel (the mountain of the Elijah–Baal contest of 1 Kings 18), Bashan, and the Mount Hermon massif, a storm-god high place since the Bronze Age.
  • The Elijah hinge. Al-Khiḍr is popularly merged with Elijah (in the Ottoman Hıdırellez the two immortals meet), and Elijah is the prophet who confronts and defeats the prophets of Baal on Carmel — a plausible bridge by which a storm-god’s mountain and functions could pass to a saint.

Taken together these make it more than idle: a green, life-giving, dragon-slaying protector honoured on the very heights where the storm-god was once worshipped is a pattern worth explaining.3

Adversarial assessment

What holds. That the George/Khiḍr cult occupies a landscape earlier sacred to the storm-god, and that it carries green/fertility and dragon-slaying associations continuous in type with Baal’s, is fair and even likely: sacred places are notoriously reused, and the functional profile does overlap. A modest reading — the later cult absorbed the associations and the topography of an older one — is defensible.

Where it overreaches. The distinctive, advocated claim is stronger than that, and it is on the strong claim that the argument fails:

  • The documentary gap is about a millennium. Attested Baal cult in most of the Levant fades through the second half of the first millennium BC; a venerated George appears only in the fourth century AD. Nothing documents a continuous cult of the storm-god surviving down to, and handing off to, the Christian saint. Continuity has to be inferred across the gap, not traced through it — and site-reuse a thousand years apart is as easily explained by the enduring desirability of springs, heights, and old holy ground as by an unbroken religious line.
  • Shrine-superposition proves reuse, not lineage. That a George-church stands on or near a former high place shows the ground stayed sacred; it does not show that the saint is the god, any more than a church on a temple platform makes Christ a version of the temple’s deity. The inference from stratigraphy to genealogy is the argument’s load-bearing move, and it is the weakest.
  • The combat-myth is a widespread type, not a fingerprint. The dragon- or chaos-serpent-slaying storm/hero recurs independently across the ancient world (Marduk–Tiamat, Zeus–Typhon, Indra–Vṛtra, Perseus–the sea-monster). A shared motif of function is exactly what one expects to arise more than once; it cannot by itself establish a single genetic descent from Baal in particular. Indeed George’s own dragon is independently traceable to the Perseus–Andromeda myth localized at nearby Joppa — a rival, better-documented channel for the same motif that does not need Baal at all.
  • It is a minority thesis, not a live dispute. By Miller’s own account the line of research has been “followed by no one”; the mainstream treats the George/Khiḍr fusion as a real late-antique and medieval phenomenon (so Meri’s shared cult), without positing Bronze-Age descent. That is not a genuine two-camp contest with no clear winner; it is a single advocated reading that outruns what the evidence secures.
  • It need not touch the martyr. Even granting every parallel, none of it dissolves the shadowy but real martyr of Lydda (rated plausible): a fusion in the reception of a saint is compatible with there having been a saint to receive.

Assessment

Weak. The deep-origins thesis — a direct, unbroken descent from the Canaanite storm-god Baal to St George/al-Khiḍr, such that the historical martyr is “almost incidental” — is a serious and honestly-argued minority case, but it does not hold on its best available evidence. Its two pillars, shrine-superposition and motif-parallel, each prove something weaker than descent (reuse of sacred ground; a recurrent combat-myth type), and neither can bridge the roughly thousand-year gap with no attested continuous transmission. A soft, functional version — that the later cult inherited the topography and green/dragon associations of an older Levantine layer — is defensible, but it is not the distinctive claim, and it does not carry the “George is Baal” conclusion. The rating is confined to the origins claim: the shared Christian–Muslim cult itself remains sound, and the martyr beneath it plausible.
