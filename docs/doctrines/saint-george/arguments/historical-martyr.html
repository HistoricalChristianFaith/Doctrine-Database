Was the George venerated at Lydda a local martyr, or the Arian bishop George of Cappadocia?

Disputed reading · on Saint George · witness: the cult at Lydda

Related readings: How did St George come to be identified with al-Khiḍr? · Does the George/al-Khiḍr figure continue the cult of the Canaanite storm-god Baal?

The passage

de Emmau usque in Diospolim milia XII, ubi S. Georgius martyrizatus est; ibi et corpus eius et multa mirabilia fiunt.

— Theodosius, De situ terrae sanctae (6th c.): “from Emmaus to Diospolis twelve miles, where St George was martyred; there too is his body, and many wonders take place,” as quoted by Hippolyte Delehaye, Les légendes grecques des saints militaires (1909), archive.org. Older still is a Greek building-inscription from Eaccaea (Shaqqa) in the Hauran, dedicated to “the saints and triumphant martyrs George and companions”; Delehaye reports its year 263 as AD 368 on the era of Bostra, and that Waddington preferred a local era because 368 is not a fifteenth indiction.1

The question

A martyr George was venerated at Lydda (Diospolis) in Palestine and honoured in the Hauran in the fourth century, but no account of his life survives older than the apocryphal Passio. What is disputed is whether the cult honoured a local martyr whose acts were lost, or whether the figure of St George derives from George of Cappadocia, the Arian bishop of Alexandria killed by an Alexandrian crowd in 361.

St George as George of Cappadocia transformed

On this reading the saint of the legend is a transformed memory of George of Cappadocia, bishop of Alexandria, lynched under Julian.

Held by: Edward Gibbon (The Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire, ch. 23); Vetter (Der heilige Georg des Reinbot von Durne) ⚠ unverified.2

Gibbon put the thesis in a passage the Catholic Encyclopedia calls famous: “This odious stranger, disguising every circumstance of time and place, assumed the mask of a martyr, a saint, and a Christian hero, and the infamous George of Cappadocia has been transformed into the renowned St. George of England, the patron of arms, of chivalry, and of the Garter.” The bishop was a Cappadocian, a former army contractor, installed at Alexandria in 356 as an opponent of Athanasius and killed in 361. According to Delehaye, the critics who made the identification were led to it by the primitive elements of the George legend itself, and he lists the features that point to the bishop: the name George; the hero’s homeland, Cappadocia; his military rank; an adversary named Athanasius; an empress named Alexandra, recalling Alexandria; the name Magnentius; and episodes such as the destruction of idols and the order to make the martyr’s remains disappear. David Bryson and Roger Pearse, translating the earliest Latin form, note that giving a pagan magician the name of Athanasius “suggests a deliberate slur, and that the author of the work was an Arian.” On this reading the features Delehaye lists are traces of the bishop in the figure of the saint, and the St George of later devotion is the Cappadocian bishop of Alexandria under another guise.3

A local martyr of Lydda whose acts were not preserved

On this reading the shrine at Lydda grew up at the tomb of a local martyr, and the Passio borrowed from the bishop’s story without making the two men one.

Held by: J. B. Bury (editor of Gibbon); Hippolyte Delehaye (Les légendes grecques des saints militaires, 1909); Herbert Thurston (Catholic, “St. George,” Catholic Encyclopedia, 1909).4

Bury, Gibbon’s editor, judged that the identification “has nothing to be said for it.” Thurston grounds the martyr on the cult: “An ancient cultus, going back to a very early epoch and connected with a definite locality, in itself constitutes a strong historical argument.” The pilgrims Theodosius, Antoninus and Arculf, writing from the sixth to the eighth century, name Lydda or Diospolis as the seat of the veneration and the resting-place of his remains; inscriptions of ruined churches in Syria, Mesopotamia and Egypt attest early dedications; and the Decretum Gelasianum rejects the Passio while, in Thurston’s words, it “includes him among those saints ‘whose names are justly reverenced among men, but whose actions are only known to God’.” For Thurston “the cultus of St. George is too ancient to allow of such an identification, though it is not improbable that the apocryphal Acts have borrowed some incidents from the story of the Arian bishop.” Delehaye grants that the reminiscences of the bishop in the Pseudo-Pasicrates “paraissent incontestables,” but holds that most legendary narratives borrow something from reality: the martyr’s name probably led the hagiographer to draw on the deeds of George of Alexandria to write the story of a namesake, and a Palestinian cult of the Alexandrian bishop would itself need explaining. Delehaye places the Eaccaea inscription among the oldest churches raised in a saint’s honour, and reads the swift fame of the Lydda sanctuary as the mark of a real tomb: “l’on peut croire qu’il s’éleva sur la tombe d’un martyr local dont les Actes n’ont jamais été écrits ou sont rapidement tombés dans l’oubli.” On this reading the cult preserves a martyr venerated at his grave, and his trial, rank and manner of death are unknown.5
