Modern scholarship on the cult and the legend of St George

Dates: 20th c. → today (Delehaye 1909; Walter 1995–2003; Riches 2000; Meri 2002; Miller 2019/2022)
Summary: Delehaye, Walter and Riches studied the cult and the development of the legend: the martyr of Diospolis, the seven-year Passion, the dragon, and the al-Khiḍr identification. Delehaye, Walter and Riches date the cult and its layers; Meri, Kreinath and Sarah Islam document the figure shared by Christians and Muslims; and Miller argues that the shared figure continues the Canaanite storm-god Baal.

Claims & evidence

Delehaye and the Greek Passions

Hippolyte Delehaye’s Les légendes grecques des saints militaires (1909) devoted a chapter to Saint George that anatomized the Passions and set George within the pattern of the Byzantine warrior-saints. Delehaye began the dossier with the early cult-evidence—the Lydda shrine and the Hauran inscription—and held that the sanctuary rose over the tomb of a local martyr “dont les Actes n’ont jamais été écrits ou sont rapidement tombés dans l’oubli” (whose acts were never written or soon fell into oblivion).

— Hippolyte Delehaye, Les légendes grecques des saints militaires (Paris: Picard, 1909), ch. II, archive.org.

Walter on the origins of the cult

Christopher Walter’s “The Origins of the Cult of Saint George” (1995) traced the cult from Diospolis outward and, in his Warrior Saints in Byzantine Art and Tradition (2003), set George beside Theodore and Demetrius and documented the late arrival of the dragon in the visual record—absent from the early icons of the standing or mounted soldier, appearing only from around the eleventh century.

— Christopher Walter, “The Origins of the Cult of Saint George,” Revue des études byzantines 53 (1995): 295–326, Persée; and The Warrior Saints in Byzantine Art and Tradition (Aldershot: Ashgate, 2003).

Riches on the martyr and the myth

Samantha Riches’ St George: Hero, Martyr and Myth (2000) treats the martyr of the early cult, the medieval dragon-hero, and the saint’s long afterlife in English national and civic identity.

— Samantha Riches, St George: Hero, Martyr and Myth (Stroud: Sutton, 2000).

Meri and Miller on the Islamic side

On the crossing into Islam, Josef Meri’s The Cult of Saints among Muslims and Jews in Medieval Syria (2002) documented the George–al-Khiḍr shrines from the medieval sources, and Robert D. Miller’s Baal, St. George, and Khiḍr (2019) asked how far the identification reaches back, arguing that “direct continuity exists” from the Canaanite storm-god Baal of the same “green” ground to St George/Khiḍr, with George-shrines reused atop Baal-shrines; Miller describes Hassan Haddad’s 1969 proposal of this line as one “followed by no one” save Ali Qleibo and Nicolas Wyatt. Sarah Islam’s case study of al-Salt (2025) treats the figure as composite: “Saint George and al-Khidr emerge in different eras and civilizations.” The readings are set out on “How did St George come to be identified with al-Khiḍr?” and “Does the George/al-Khiḍr figure continue the cult of the Canaanite storm-god Baal?”

— Josef W. Meri, The Cult of Saints among Muslims and Jews in Medieval Syria (Oxford, 2002); Robert D. Miller II, Baal, St. George, and Khiḍr: A Study of the Historical Geography of the Levant (University Park: Penn State / Eisenbrauns, 2019; study ed. 2022), at JSTOR, publisher’s description at Eisenbrauns; Sarah Islam, “The Church of Saint George in al-Salt as a Co-Produced Religious Site” (2025).

Reception and debate
