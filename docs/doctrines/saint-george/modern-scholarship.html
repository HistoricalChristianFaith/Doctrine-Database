Modern scholarship: the historical core sifted from the accretion

Dates: 20th c. → today (Delehaye 1909; Walter 1995–2003; Riches 2000; Meri 2002; Miller 2022)
Summary: Critical study has worked to separate the datable cult from the legend piled on it—the martyr of Diospolis from the seven-year Passion, the borrowed dragon, and the al-Khiḍr fusion. Its settled findings: the martyr is real but almost unrecoverable, the marvels are late and largely borrowed, and one saint came, in the Levant, to be shared across two religions.

Claims & evidence

Delehaye and the Greek Passions

The foundational work is Hippolyte Delehaye’s Les légendes grecques des saints militaires (1909), whose chapter on Saint George anatomized the Passions and set George within the pattern of the Byzantine warrior-saints. Delehaye placed the early cult-evidence—the Lydda shrine and the Hauran inscription—at the head of the dossier and treated the extravagant Acts as a later, worthless overlay for the historian of the man, however valuable for the historian of devotion.

— Hippolyte Delehaye, Les légendes grecques des saints militaires (Paris: Picard, 1909), ch. II, archive.org.

Walter on the origins of the cult

Christopher Walter’s “The Origins of the Cult of Saint George” (1995) traced the cult from Diospolis outward and, in his Warrior Saints in Byzantine Art and Tradition (2003), set George beside Theodore and Demetrius and documented the late arrival of the dragon in the visual record—absent from the early icons of the standing or mounted soldier, appearing only from around the eleventh century.

— Christopher Walter, “The Origins of the Cult of Saint George,” Revue des études byzantines 53 (1995): 295–326, Persée; and The Warrior Saints in Byzantine Art and Tradition (Aldershot: Ashgate, 2003).

Riches on the martyr and the myth

Samantha Riches’ St George: Hero, Martyr and Myth (2000) mapped the whole trajectory for a general scholarly readership—the shadowy martyr beneath, the medieval dragon-hero above, and the saint’s long afterlife in English national and civic identity—keeping the historical residue and the accreted legend firmly apart.

— Samantha Riches, St George: Hero, Martyr and Myth (Stroud: Sutton, 2000).

Meri and Miller on the Islamic side

On the crossing into Islam, Josef Meri’s The Cult of Saints among Muslims and Jews in Medieval Syria (2002) documented the George–al-Khiḍr shrines from the medieval sources, and Robert D. Miller’s Baal, St. George, and Khiḍr (2022) pressed the harder question of how far the identification reaches back—arguing a direct line to the Canaanite storm-god Baal of the same “green” ground, with George-shrines reused atop Baal-shrines. That deep-origins thesis is rated weak on this wiki—it must bridge a roughly thousand-year gap on shrine-superposition and motif-parallel—and it is a minority case Miller himself says has been “followed by no one”; the shared cult itself, by contrast, is not in doubt.

— Josef W. Meri, The Cult of Saints among Muslims and Jews in Medieval Syria (Oxford, 2002); Robert D. Miller II, Baal, St. George, and Khiḍr: A Study of the Historical Geography of the Levant (University Park: Penn State / Eisenbrauns, 2022), at JSTOR.
