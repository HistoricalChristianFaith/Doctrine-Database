The Babylonian Talmud (Bava Batra 14b, Bava Kamma 92b & Eruvin 65a) on the Old Testament Canon

Dates: c. 200 AD (the baraita of Bava Batra 14b is tannaitic material conventionally placed at the Mishnaic horizon; embedded in the Babylonian Talmud, whose redaction is c. 500–600) · Wikipedia
Witness: The baraita at Bava Batra 14b enumerates the Prophets and the Writings, ending the whole with Chronicles, and lists none of the Greek extras (Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch, 1–2 Maccabees). The same compilation, at Bava Kamma 92b, quotes Ben Sira (Sirach) as a verse of the Writings, and at Eruvin 65a cites a Ben Sira saying with the formula "as it is stated," a line Rashi suggests is "perhaps" in "the book of Ben Sira."

Claims & evidence

Bava Batra 14b: an enumerated Hebrew canon ending in Chronicles

"The order of the books of the Prophets when they are attached together is as follows: Joshua and Judges, Samuel and Kings, Jeremiah and Ezekiel, and Isaiah and the Twelve Prophets… The order of the Writings is: Ruth and the book of Psalms, and Job and Proverbs; Ecclesiastes, Song of Songs, and Lamentations; Daniel and the Scroll of Esther; and Ezra and Chronicles."

— a baraita preserved in the Babylonian Talmud, Bava Batra 14b (William Davidson translation, Sefaria).1 Two features bear on the canon question. First, the list is Hebrew-sized: it runs through exactly the books of the Law, Prophets, and Writings that the synagogue kept, and it names none of the deuterocanonical books the Greek Septuagint carried. Second, it closes the entire Scripture with Chronicles — the Genesis-to-Chronicles arrangement cited in the question whether "from Abel to Zechariah" marks the first and last books. A baraita is tannaitic material (the stratum of the Mishnah, roughly the first two centuries AD). The Tiberian Masoretic codices (Aleppo, Leningrad) open the Writings with Chronicles, and the Chronicles-last order became common after 1207 and standard with print; how early and how widely it was current is set out on the interpretation page on whether the Hebrew Bible of Jesus' day ended with Chronicles, where Roger Beckwith reads it as the first-century order.

Bava Kamma 92b: Ben Sira (Sirach) cited as a verse of the Writings

"… And it is triplicated in the Writings, as it is written: All fowl will live with its kind, and men with those like him."

— the Babylonian Talmud, Bava Kamma 92b (William Davidson translation, Sefaria); the quoted line is Ben Sira (Sirach) 13:15–17.2 Here Rabba bar Mari supports a popular saying ("bad people seek out other bad people") from all three divisions of Scripture — "written in the Torah, repeated in the Prophets, and triplicated in the Writings" — and for the Writings he adduces a verse of Ben Sira, a deuterocanonical book, with the same scriptural formula ("as it is written") he uses for the Torah and Prophets quotations beside it. It is a single citation, not a canon list, in the same compilation that hands down the Chronicles-last order.

Eruvin 65a: a saying cited "as it is stated" that Rashi traces to Ben Sira

"Anyone whose mind is unsettled should not pray, as it is stated: When distressed, one should not issue decisions."

— the Babylonian Talmud, Eruvin 65a (William Davidson translation, Sefaria), Rav Ḥiyya bar Ashi in the name of Rav.3 The proof-line, be-tzar al yoreh ("בְּצָר אַל יוֹרֶה"), is introduced with she-ne'emar ("as it is stated") — a formula the Talmud uses for quoting Scripture. Rashi, searching for the line, writes "I looked for this verse and it is not in all the Writings, and perhaps it is in the book of Ben Sira"; Tosafot, on the same passage, observe "that we find in several places that the Talmud brings verses written in the book of Ben Sira."4 If the line is from Ben Sira, this is a second place, beside Bava Kamma 92b, where the Talmud cites Ben Sira with a scriptural formula; Rashi's gloss, in the eleventh century, places Ben Sira outside "all the Writings." Both passages bear on the interpretation page on whether first-century Judaism held a closed canon that excluded the deuterocanon.

Reception and debate

  • Placement on the timeline. The deuterocanon question concerns two forms of the Old Testament — the broader Greek Septuagint collection in which the early churches received the Scriptures and the narrower Hebrew collection kept in the synagogue. The Bava Batra baraita gives the rabbinic table of the books as an explicit enumeration rather than a bare count; it does not address the Christian controversy.
  • Distinct from the other Jewish markers. The "Council of Jamnia" marker concerns whether a datable council closed the Hebrew canon (see its interpretation page); the Masoretic Text is the medieval crystallization of the Hebrew letter-text. This page adds what neither does: the named, ordered list of the books (Bava Batra) and a rabbinic source treating a deuterocanonical book as Scripture — twice, at Bava Kamma 92b and Eruvin 65a.
  • Disputed readings. The Bava Batra enumeration, and its dating, are discussed on whether the Bava Batra 14b list attests a Hebrew canon closed before the Christian era; its Chronicles-last order on whether the Hebrew Bible of Jesus' day ended with Chronicles (and on Abel to Zechariah); the Bava Kamma and Eruvin Sirach citations bear on whether first-century Judaism held a closed canon that excluded the deuterocanon.
  • Dating. The Bava Batra list is a baraita — tannaitic material conventionally placed c. 200 (the Mishnaic horizon) — embedded in the Babylonian Talmud, whose redaction runs c. 500–600. It is slotted on the timeline by the earlier date, as a witness to the tradition rather than to the date of the Talmud's final redaction, following the same convention used for the b. Megillah 9a baraita elsewhere on this site.
