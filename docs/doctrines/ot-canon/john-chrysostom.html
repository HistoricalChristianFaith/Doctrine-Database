John Chrysostom on the Old Testament Canon

Dates: c. 347–407 AD (John "Chrysostom" — "the golden-mouthed" — presbyter and preacher at Antioch from 386, archbishop of Constantinople from 398; trained under Diodore of Tarsus in the Antiochene school of exegesis. The citations below come from his Antiochene preaching of the 390s — the Four Discourses on Lazarus, the Homilies on 1 Corinthians and on Philippians, and the sermon Against Marcionists and Manichaeans) · Wikipedia
Witness: Chrysostom leaves no catalogue of the Old Testament books. In his homilies he adduces Sirach "from the Divine Scriptures," Wisdom under "it is written," the Greek Susanna beside the Psalms as a scriptural instance, and Baruch, "in the book of Jeremiah," as a prophecy of the Incarnation, and sets Tobit beside Paul and the Psalter on almsgiving. The Cappadocians Basil, Gregory of Nyssa, and Gregory of Nazianzus also cite these books.

Claims & evidence

Sirach adduced "from the Divine Scriptures": "Do not deprive the poor of life"

In the second of his Four Discourses on Lazarus (II.3), preaching that withholding our goods from the poor is itself a kind of robbery, Chrysostom announces that he will prove the point from Scripture; the chain of testimony he then gives runs from Malachi to the deuterocanonical Sirach (4:1), both under the heading "the Divine Scriptures":1

I will, from the Divine Scriptures, bring testimony showing that not only robbery of other men's goods, but also the not imparting our own good things to others,——that this also is robbery, and covetousness, and fraud… And, again, in another place, it is said, "Do not deprive the poor of life," (Ecclus. iv. 1.)

The label "the Divine Scriptures" is announced over the whole proof, and Sirach stands beside Malachi as one of its witnesses on a moral question.

Wisdom of Solomon under "it is written": "Thou hast mercy upon all"

In the fourth Homily on Philippians, expounding why the priests, kings, and prophets of Israel were anointed with oil — a type, he says, of God's mercy to man — Chrysostom grounds the point in a verse of the deuterocanonical Wisdom of Solomon (11:23), introduced with "it is written":2

For, "Thou hast mercy upon all," it is written, "for Thou canst do all things." (Wisd. xi. 23.)

Wisdom has no Hebrew text. Chrysostom's contemporary Jerome set it outside the Hebrew canon, "among the apocrypha" (see his page).

The Greek Susanna beside two Psalms: "O thou that art become old in evil days"

In the fifteenth Homily on 1 Corinthians, illustrating how one and the same word ("old") carries now a good and now an evil sense in Scripture, Chrysostom gathers his examples, and sets the Greek addition to Daniel (Susanna, Daniel 13:52) beside two Psalms:3

"They waxed old, and they halted from their paths." And again, "I have become old in the midst of all mine enemies." And again, (Daniel chapter 13, verse 52. Hist. Susan.) "O thou that art become old in evil days."

The Susanna verse is adduced as a scriptural instance of the word's usage, chained with Psalm 17 and Psalm 6 under the same "and again." Jerome, in his preface to Daniel, notes that Daniel "among the Hebrews has neither the history of Susanna, nor the hymn of the three young men, nor the fables of Bel and the dragon.4

Baruch cited as prophecy of the Incarnation "in the book of Jeremiah": "this is our God"

In the sermon Against Marcionists and Manichaeans (§3), marshalling the prophets who foretold that God would take flesh and walk among men — against the Marcionite denial of a real Incarnation — Chrysostom strings Isaiah, the rod of Jesse, and David together, and into that chain of prophecy he sets Baruch (3:35–37), reckoning the book part of Jeremiah's prophecy:5

And Baruch in the book of Jeremiah says "this is our God: no other shall be reckoned by the side of Him: He found out every path of knowledge and gave it to Jacob His servant, and Israel his beloved. After these things also He appeared upon the earth, and held converse with men."

The passage is adduced in a controversy over the reality of Christ's coming in the flesh, set among Isaiah and David, and ascribed, as in the Greek and Latin tradition, to "the book of Jeremiah." Hippolytus, Gregory of Nazianzus, and Epiphanius cite the same passage of Baruch; Irenaeus and Ambrose cite other passages of the book (see their pages).

Tobit set beside Paul and the Psalter on almsgiving: "Alms do deliver from death"

In the same fourth Homily on Philippians, urging the people to works of mercy, Chrysostom gathers testimonies — from Titus, from the Psalms — and folds in Tobit (12:9) among them as "a certain other one who saith":6

Listen to a certain other one who saith, "Alms do deliver from death" (Tob. xii. 9.); If Thou takest away pity, "Lord, Lord, who shall stand" (Ps. cxxx. 3.)

Tobit stands in a chain of witnesses on almsgiving, beside Paul to Titus and Psalm 130.

Reception and debate

  • An Antiochene preacher. Chrysostom trained under Diodore of Tarsus in the Antiochene school; his homilies cite the disputed books, as the Cappadocians and the Alexandrians on this timeline also do.
  • Citation formulas. Sirach is cited "from the Divine Scriptures," Wisdom under "it is written," Susanna under "and again" beside two Psalms, and Baruch "in the book of Jeremiah" against the Marcionites; Tobit is introduced as "a certain other one who saith," in a chain of moral testimony.
  • No list. Chrysostom drew up no catalogue of the Old Testament books. Fathers on this timeline who did draw up Hebrew-sized lists, among them Athanasius and Cyril, also cite the disputed books (see their pages). How such citations bear on the early church's canon is set out on “How did the early church receive the deuterocanonical books?”
