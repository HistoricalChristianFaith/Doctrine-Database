What does the rabbinic ban on Ben Sira and the “outside books” (Tosefta Yadayim 2:13) show about their earlier use?

Disputed reading · on the Old Testament canon · witnesses: Akiva ben Joseph, the Babylonian Talmud

Related readings: Was the rabbinic narrowing directed against Christians? · The Jewish canon and the deuterocanon · Jamnia

The passage

Tosefta Yadayim 2:13 bars, together, “the scrolls of the heretics” (the gilyonim, read by many as the Gospels) and “the scrolls of Ben Sira and all scrolls that are written from that point forward,” which “do not defile the hands”; the Mishnah denies a share in the world to come to one who reads “the outside books” (m. Sanhedrin 10:1).1,2

The question

Everyone grants that a ban implies books in use. What is disputed is whether the ban implies that some Jews, as well as Christians, held these books as Scripture before the rabbis excluded them, or only that they were read and their standing contested. The reading that the ban shows scriptural use among Christians and some Jews is stated here from a Catholic apologist; academic literature holding it was not located.

The ban attests use and dispute, not prior canonical standing

A decree against a book shows it was read, and that its standing was being denied; it does not show the community had held it canonical.

Held by: the Jewish Encyclopedia (“Akiba ben Joseph,” 1906);3 James White (Reformed Baptist apologist, debate with Gary Michuta, 2004).4

The Jewish Encyclopedia holds that Akiva “has… no objection to the private reading of the Apocrypha, as is evident from the fact that he himself makes frequent use of Ecclesiasticus [Ben Sira],” his ban falling on reading non-canonical books “as if they were canonical.”3 White: the books “were listed because there were those who believed or made the claim that they were canonical — there’s no question about that. But it doesn’t follow… that even this decree had any kind of binding force,” nor that it means “anything relevant to the Apocryphal books being considered canonical by any Jewish individuals whatsoever.”4 A Hebrew-sized list without the deuterocanon is visible before the ban, in Josephus’s “twenty-two” (c. AD 95) and the twenty-four public books of 4 Ezra 14 (c. AD 100).5 The Babylonian Talmud glosses Akiva’s “outside books” as the “books of the heretics,” Rav Yosef adding that Ben Sira too is problematic.2 The same decree names the gilyonim, and a ban on them does not show the Gospels had been Jewish Scripture.6 On this reading the decree draws a boundary across a live edge: it shows the books in circulation and their status disputed.

The ban shows the books were in scriptural use among Christians and some Jews

Rabbis do not legislate against books no one treats as authoritative; the decree shows the deuterocanon was used as Scripture by Christians and by some Jews, which the rabbinic canon then excluded.

Held by: Gary Michuta (Catholic apologist, Why Catholic Bibles Are Bigger, 2007, and debate with White, 2004).1,7

“…it must have been common knowledge even at this early date that the Christians accepted the Deuterocanon and used it as Scripture (along with the Gospels), otherwise, there would have been no need to rule against them; secondly that at least some Jews must also have shared that acceptance, otherwise Akiba's decree would have been superfluous.”

1 In debate Michuta asked: “If there was unanimous Jewish acceptance that the Deuterocanonical books were never Scripture and that Christians did not accept these books as Scripture — remember, this is around 132 AD — why in the world would Rabbi Akiba make a declaration to forbid these books or exclude them from inspiration?”7 The cutoff, “Ben Sira and all scrolls written from that point forward,” falls at the date beyond which the Greek books and the Christian writings both lie, and the decree names them together.1 The Talmud continues to cite Ben Sira as Scripture after the ruling: among the Writings with “as it is written” at Bava Kamma 92b, and at Eruvin 65a, where Rashi traces an unlocated verse to “the book of Ben Sira.”8 On this reading the shorter Jewish canon followed a ruling against books some Jews had read as Scripture.
