Did the Council of Trent receive the deuterocanon in order to supply proof-texts for purgatory and prayer for the dead?

Disputed reading · on the Old Testament canon · witness: the Council of Trent

Related readings: Did Trent add the books? · Luther and 2 Maccabees · Was the deuterocanon held below Scripture until Trent?

The passage

There was no support for Purgatory. So what did the Roman Catholic Church do? Find some books that do talk about it and they added those to the canon in order to support their doctrines that had actually no biblical support… Protestants did not take out books, it was the Roman Catholic Church in the middle of the sixteenth century who added them.

— Todd Friel, Wretched.1

These books helped teach the doctrines that the church was fighting [for] and the Protestants were fighting against. "Show us where it is in the Bible!" "OK — pop — now it's in the Bible."

— Mike Winger.1

The question

Trent placed the canon at the head of its doctrinal work because the Reformers had reopened it, and later defined purgatory (Session 25, 1563), with 2 Maccabees 12 and Tobit 12 live proof-texts in the controversy.2 What is disputed is whether the books were received in 1546 in order to supply support for those doctrines, or whether a council confirmed books long in the church’s Bible, from which the practice of prayer for the dead had already drawn. The motive reading is stated here from popular apologetic sources; academic literature holding it was not located.

Trent confirmed books long read, from which the practice of prayer for the dead had drawn

The books stood in the church’s Bible for a millennium before the Reformation, and prayer for the dead, read in 2 Maccabees 12 by the Fathers, preceded the controversy.

Held by: George Reid (Catholic, “Canon of the Old Testament,” Catholic Encyclopedia, 1908);3 Trent Horn (Catholic apologist, “Did the Catholic Church Add Books to the Bible?”).4

The deuterocanon stood in the Septuagint and the great uncials, in the Latin Vulgate from c. 405, in the lists of Hippo (393) and Carthage (397, 419), of Innocent I (405) and Augustine, and in the Council of Florence’s bull Cantate Domino (4 February 1442), 104 years before Trent.5 Prayer and offering for the dead is attested by Tertullian, Cyprian, Cyril of Jerusalem and Augustine, and kept by the Greek East to the present; 2 Maccabees 12 was read as its scriptural warrant in the patristic and medieval Church.6 On this reading the doctrine grew with a book the church already had. Horn makes the point against the broader “added at Trent” charge: the books were not new in 1546.4 Allen Parr, on the Protestant side, grants that “before then, these books were in the Bible.”7 Reid writes that Trent acted “to meet this radical departure of the Protestants, and as well define clearly the inspired sources from which the Catholic Faith draws its defence,” and that it “based its decision on immemorial tradition as manifested in the decrees of previous councils and popes, and liturgical reading.”3 Trent’s timing and emphasis answered the Reformation, which had reopened the canon; the books it listed were those it had received. On this reading Trent confirmed, under controversy, a canon the Latin church had read for a thousand years.

Trent added the books to support doctrines the Reformers had challenged

When the Reformers demanded scriptural proof for purgatory, the council answered by placing in the canon the books that taught it.

Held by: Todd Friel (Wretched); Mike Winger (evangelical teacher); Bible.org.1

Friel: “There was no support for Purgatory. So what did the Roman Catholic Church do? Find some books that do talk about it and they added those to the canon in order to support their doctrines that had actually no biblical support.” Winger dramatizes the sequence: “‘Show us where it is in the Bible!’ ‘OK — [popping noise] now it’s in the Bible.’”1 Bible.org supplies the chronology: “the Catholic Church officially recognized these books as scripture in A.D. 1546, only 29 years after Martin Luther criticized these doctrines as unbiblical,” purgatory and prayer for the dead from 2 Maccabees 12:39–46 and works and almsgiving from Tobit 12:9.1 The decree came first among Trent’s doctrinal acts, and the council went on to define the very doctrines the Reformers had attacked, affirming that the souls in purgatory “are helped by the suffrages of the faithful.”2 Luther had gathered the disputed books into a separate “Apocrypha” in 1534.7 On this reading the binding canonical status of the books, with the anathema attached, dates from a council that needed them for its doctrinal answer to the Reformation.
