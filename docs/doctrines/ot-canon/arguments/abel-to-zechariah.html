Does “from the blood of Abel to the blood of Zechariah” mark the first and last books of the Old Testament canon?

Disputed reading · on the Old Testament canon · witness: the saying of Jesus at Matthew 23:35 and Luke 11:51

Related readings: Which Zechariah does Jesus name? · Did the Hebrew canon end with Chronicles in the first century?

The passage

That upon you may come all the righteous blood shed upon the earth, from the blood of righteous Abel unto the blood of Zacharias son of Barachias, whom ye slew between the temple and the altar.

— Matthew 23:35 (KJV); Luke 11:51 has the saying without the patronym. Abel’s murder stands in Genesis 4; the murder of Zechariah son of Jehoiada in the temple court stands in 2 Chronicles 24:20–22.1

The question

Jesus names Abel and a Zechariah as the first and last of the righteous slain. What is disputed is whether the pair also names the first and last books of the canon he and his hearers knew (Genesis to Chronicles), so that the saying attests a Hebrew-shaped canon without the deuterocanonical books, or whether it spans the history of martyrdom without reference to the order of books.

A span of martyrdom from the beginning to the last recorded prophet-murder

The pair sums up all the righteous blood shed from the beginning, to indict “this generation,” and says nothing about the order or extent of the canon.

Held by: Tertullian (Scorpiace, c. 220), John Chrysostom (Homilies on Matthew, c. 390) and the reception before 1780;2 Edmon Gallagher (Protestant, New Testament Studies, 2014); H. G. L. Peels (Protestant, ZAW, 2001); Gary Michuta (Catholic apologist).3

On this reading the saying belongs to the woe against those who kill the prophets, and Abel and Zechariah are named because Scripture records a cry for vengeance over each. Tertullian lists the righteous slain “from the beginning” down to Zechariah “butchered between the altar and the temple,” and on to John the Baptist; Chrysostom reads the point as “a holy man slain in a holy place,” meant “to alarm” the Pharisees and “comfort His disciples”; Remigius has the pair “denote all holy martyrs, both of lay and priestly order”; Calvin (1555) reads it of the murder of the prophets; John Lightfoot (1675) and J. D. Michaelis (1750) explain the pairing by the cry for vengeance over these two alone. Jerome, who discusses the verse at length, treats the question of which Zechariah is meant.2 Gallagher’s survey of the saying’s interpretation finds no canon-boundary reading on record before Johann Gottfried Eichhorn in 1780.2 Gallagher also surveys the first-millennium book-lists and finds no dominant Chronicles-last order: Melito, Origen, Cyril of Jerusalem, Athanasius, Epiphanius, Jerome, Augustine and the great codices close on other books, and the Chronicles-last order becomes common after the Adath Deborim (1207).4 The patronym “son of Barachias” in Matthew, and the range of patristic identifications of this Zechariah, are treated on the companion page.5 On this reading the saying spans the whole history of martyrdom to charge one generation with it.

The first and last martyrs of the canon, Genesis to Chronicles

Jesus names the first martyr in Genesis and the last in Chronicles, the opening and closing books of the Hebrew Bible he knew, so the saying presupposes that canon.

Held by: Johann Gottfried Eichhorn (Protestant, Introduction to the Old Testament, 1780);2 F. F. Bruce (Brethren, “The Canon of Scripture,” 1954); Roger Beckwith (Anglican, The Old Testament Canon of the New Testament Church, 1985); Doug Wilson (Reformed pastor, 2020).1

Eichhorn reads “the example of … Abel from Genesis as the first book of the Old Testament, and that of Zachariah from the book of Chronicles as the last book of all.”2 Bruce writes: “when our Lord sums up all the martyrs of Old Testament times He does so by mentioning the first martyr in Genesis (Abel) and the last martyr in Chronicles (Zechariah) … The books of the Apocrypha were not included in the Hebrew Bible.” Beckwith gives the scholarly form: the saying “in all probability implies that for Jesus and his hearers the canon began with Genesis and ended with Chronicles,” reflecting “the traditional Jewish arrangement of the books (recorded in the Talmud).”1 The Talmudic witness is the baraita at Bava Batra 14b, which lists the Writings as ending “and Ezra and Chronicles.”6 The murder of Zechariah son of Jehoiada in the temple court (2 Chronicles 24:20–22) is the last such murder in that order of books, and Jesus places his Zechariah, as Chronicles does, between the temple and the altar. A first-to-last figure is a natural way to span a whole body of writing, and a span from Genesis to Chronicles leaves outside it the later martyrs of the Greek collection, such as the Maccabean martyrs. The reading is repeated widely in Protestant teaching, among others by Steve Christie (2019), who has Jesus “limiting the Old Testament canon to the boundaries of the Hebrew Bible of the Pharisees.”7 On this reading the saying shows the Hebrew Bible, Genesis to Chronicles, as the Scripture of Jesus and his hearers.
