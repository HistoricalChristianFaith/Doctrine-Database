Was the Protestant setting-aside of the deuterocanon driven by its doctrinal content, and what would that show?

Disputed reading · on the Old Testament canon · witnesses: Martin Luther, the Edinburgh and Glasgow Bible Societies

Related readings: Luther and 2 Maccabees · Trent and purgatory · Luther and the later removal

The passage

The Deuterocanonical books teach doctrines such as purgatory, free will, and the intercession of the saints. This has been recognized from the very beginning in Protestantism. In fact, as Bruce Metzger notes, the Reformers wished to correct the Church from doctrinal abuses, so first they had to determine which books are indeed Scripture. Then, they began to disparage the Deuterocanonical books specifically because they teach things like purgatory, intercession, and so on.

those two Scottish Bible societies pointed out that these books do claim inspiration and they do teach Catholic doctrine… the reason the Reformers opted to first put them on a lower level than Scripture, then eventually move them to the back of the Bible, and then eventually not only get rid of them but also remove all cross-references to these books, indicates that there is something very important about them — something that just doesn't fit into Protestant theology.

— Gary Michuta, in debate with James White (2004).1

The question

The deuterocanon carries proof-texts for distinctively Catholic doctrines: 2 Maccabees 12:43–45 (prayer and offering for the dead), 2 Maccabees 15:12–16 (the departed interceding), Tobit 12:9 (“alms… shall purge away all sin”), Sirach 15:14–17 (free will); Metzger sets out how the Reformers’ canon question and the purgatory dispute were entangled.2 What is disputed is the ground of the setting-aside, from the Reformers to the Bible societies of the 1820s, and whether a doctrinal motive tells for the books’ weight. The reading that the books were set aside because of their doctrine is stated here from a Catholic apologist; academic literature holding it was not located.

The books were set aside as uninspired, on the Hebrew canon

The stated ground, from Luther to the Scottish Bible societies, was that the books lack inspired, canonical authority; their doctrines were judged by that.

Held by: the Edinburgh and Glasgow Bible Societies (Statement of the Bible Society concerning the Apocrypha, 1825);3 Bruce M. Metzger (Protestant, An Introduction to the Apocrypha, 1957).4

The two Scottish societies seceded from the British and Foreign Bible Society in the 1820s on the ground that the Apocrypha is not divinely inspired and ought not be bound with and circulated as the Word of God, adding the economy of not printing uninspired matter and the parent society’s departure from its mandate.3 Luther answered the 2 Maccabees proof-text on several grounds, arguing that purgatory fails even granting the book’s authority.3 Metzger grants that canon and doctrine were entangled for the Reformers, and holds the standard Protestant view: “a continuous succession of the more learned fathers and theologians in the West maintained the distinctive and unique authority of the books of the Hebrew canon.”4 On this reading the doctrines the books carry are what one would expect of writings outside the Hebrew canon, and the Reformers’ and the societies’ judgement rested on the books’ standing, the doctrinal dispute shaping its timing and emphasis.

The books were set aside because their doctrine did not fit Protestant theology

The Reformers disparaged the books because they teach purgatory, intercession and free will; the staged removal shows the books carry a weight Protestantism could not absorb.

Held by: Gary Michuta (Catholic apologist, debate with James White, 2004, and Why Catholic Bibles Are Bigger, 2017).1

Michuta draws on Metzger for the entanglement: needing to determine which books could establish doctrine, the Reformers reopened the canon, and the purgatory dispute turned on the authority of 2 Maccabees 12:43–45; Michuta’s wording paraphrases Metzger.2 The books concentrate the proof-texts for the doctrines the Reformation denied.2 He reads the sequence as evidence: the Reformers chose “to first put them on a lower level than Scripture, then eventually move them to the back of the Bible, and then eventually not only get rid of them but also remove all cross-references to these books,” which “indicates that there is something very important about them — something that just doesn’t fit into Protestant theology.”1 He states that the Scottish societies “pointed out that these books do claim inspiration and they do teach Catholic doctrine.”1 On this reading the doctrinal content of the deuterocanon explains why Protestantism moved it out of the Bible, and the persistence of that effort testifies to the books’ theological weight.
