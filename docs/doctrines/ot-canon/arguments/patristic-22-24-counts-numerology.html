Do the Fathers’ counts of “twenty-two” or “twenty-four” books mark canon boundaries, or alphabetic correspondences?

Disputed reading · on the Old Testament canon · witnesses: Hilary of Poitiers, Origen, Epiphanius of Salamis

Related readings: Josephus’s twenty-two books · How the early church received the deuterocanon

The passage

Hilary of Poitiers, Instructio Psalmorum §15: “The reason for reckoning twenty-two books of the Old Testament is that this corresponds with the number of the [Hebrew] letters … To this some add Tobit and Judith to make twenty-four books, according to the number of the Greek letters.”1 Epiphanius, On Weights and Measures §§23–24: “This number twenty-two, found in all these places but counted in different ways, in the twenty-two works that God did in the six days of the making of the world, in the twenty-two generations from Adam to Israel, in the twenty-two signs of the letters from alef to taw, and in the twenty-two books from Genesis to Esther.”2

The question

The Fathers announce their totals as correspondences with alphabets, the days of creation and the generations, and fit the books to them, folding Ruth into Judges and Lamentations into Jeremiah. What is disputed is whether such counts are symbolic frames that leave the canon question aside, or memory-frames for a real list that carry a judgement on which books belong. The reading of the counts as a symbolic correspondence is stated here from a Catholic apologist; academic literature holding it was not located.

A symbolic correspondence, not a canon boundary

The Fathers who number the Old Testament at twenty-two or twenty-four are concerned with the mystical correspondence of books to letters, and the elastic total shows they were not drawing a line against the deuterocanon.

Held by: Gary Michuta (Catholic apologist, debate with James White, 2004, and Why Catholic Bibles Are Bigger, 2007).3

Hilary of Poitiers … cites that there are 22 books, corresponding to the 22 letters of the Hebrew alphabet. And they say, "Look, there—he affirms that the canon had been closed to exclude the Deuterocanonical books." Well, if you continue reading in that same passage, he says, "But if you want to add Tobit and Judith so as to make 24 and correspond to the Greek alphabet, then you could do that." … The fact of the matter is, Hilary of Poitiers wasn't concerned about which books were in the canon. What he was concerned with was the mystical correspondence of numbers, letters of the alphabet, and books. The same is true of Origen … he accidentally omits the Twelve Minor Prophets, which count as one book. And then, when he comes up with only 21 names at the end, he has to come up with a 22nd, so he adds Maccabees and gives it a Hebrew name, even though he says it was excluded from the canon. … The same is true with Epiphanius.

3 Hilary’s total slides from twenty-two to twenty-four to match the Greek alphabet when “some add Tobit and Judith.”1 Epiphanius sets the twenty-two books beside the twenty-two works of creation and the twenty-two generations from Adam to Israel.2 Origen’s count, as Eusebius preserves it, names twenty-one books and the Maccabees, under a Hebrew title, Sarbeth Sabanaiel; and the books are fitted to the number, Ruth folded into Judges, “Jeremiah, with Lamentations and the epistle, in one.”4 On this reading the number came first and the books were arranged to suit it, so that the totals record a symbolic correspondence, not a decision that the books outside the count were not Scripture.

A memory-frame for a real list, carrying a judgement on named books

The alphabet count frames the Hebrew canon the Fathers inherited; the books beyond it are named and set outside.

Held by: James White (Reformed Baptist apologist, debate with Michuta, 2004).5

White answers that the alphabet-tied count is a mnemonic for a real list: Josephus’s twenty-two and the Fathers’ twenty-two converge on the same books because that is what the Jews handed down, and Hilary says the Law “was divided into 22 books, so that it might agree with the number of letters.”5,6 Folded to match the letters, the Hebrew canon the Fathers inherited does come to twenty-two.6 The enumerations under the numbers render judgements on named books. Hilary recites twenty-two and ascribes the twenty-four to “some”; at twenty-four the additions stop at Tobit and Judith.1 Epiphanius names Wisdom and Sirach and rules them “helpful and useful, but… not included in the number of the recognized; and therefore they were not kept in the chest, that is, in the ark of the covenant,” and the Panarion calls them “of disputed canonicity.”7 Origen files the Maccabees outside the count: “And besides these there are the Maccabees” (exô de toutôn); the missing Twelve Prophets are read as a scribal omission in Eusebius’s copy.8 On this reading the count is the form in which the Hebrew canon was remembered, and the books placed outside it were placed there by name.
