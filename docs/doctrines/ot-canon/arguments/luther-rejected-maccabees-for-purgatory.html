Why did Luther deny the canonical authority of 2 Maccabees at Leipzig in 1519?

Disputed reading · on the Old Testament canon · witness: Martin Luther

Related readings: Did Luther remove the deuterocanon? · Did Trent affirm the books for the sake of purgatory? · Luther and the later removal

The passage

At the Leipzig Disputation (8 July 1519), with Johann Eck pressing 2 Maccabees 12:43–46 as a proof of purgatory, Luther answered: “nam et liber Machabeorum, cum non sit in canone, pro fidelibus potens est, contra pertinaces nihil facit” (“for the book of Maccabees, since it is not in the canon, has force for the faithful but avails nothing against the obstinate”).1

The question

In 1518 Luther cited Sirach and Tobit among his proof-texts; his first recorded denial of a deuterocanonical book’s canonicity came at Leipzig, over a proof-text for purgatory.1 What is disputed is the cause: whether he rejected the book in order to defeat the doctrine, or whether a standing Hebrew-canon judgement surfaced in that controversy. The reading that Luther rejected the book because it supported prayer for the dead is stated here from Catholic apologists; academic literature holding it was not located.

A Hebrew-canon judgement, applied to all the disputed books

Luther judged 2 Maccabees by Jerome’s Hebrew canon and by its contents, and argued that the doctrine would fail even granting the book’s authority.

Held by: Martin Luther (Defense and Explanation of All the Articles, 1521), stating his reasons;2 James Swan (Reformed apologist, Beggars All Reformation, 2010–11); James White (Reformed Baptist apologist).3,4

Luther’s 1521 answer gives several grounds: the passage proves nothing, for that book is not among the books of Holy Scripture, and, as St. Jerome says, it is not found in a Hebrew version… In other respects, too, this book deserves little authority, for it contradicts the first Book of Maccabees in its description of King Antiochus… But even were the book authoritative, it would still be necessary… that at least one passage out of the chief books should support it.2 Swan documents that Luther still held to purgatory in 1519 and for some years afterward, abandoning it gradually, so that his rejection of purgatory did not precede his judgement on the book.3 Swan and White point out that Luther cited Sirach and Tobit by the same method before and after 1519, continuing to the Lectures on Genesis at the end of his life, and that in 1534 he gathered the whole deuterocanon, not 2 Maccabees alone, into a section commended “to read”; a 2025 study finds Leipzig a real but selective and gradual first explicit denial.4 On this reading the purgatory dispute was the occasion on which Luther first stated a Hieronymian canon judgement that he applied to all the disputed books.

Luther rejected the book because it supported prayer for the dead

Luther quoted the deuterocanon freely in 1518; pressed with 2 Maccabees 12 for purgatory in 1519, he turned against the book, his theology deciding his canon.

Held by: Jimmy Akin (Catholic apologist, “Defending the Deuterocanonicals”); Gary Michuta (Catholic apologist, Why Catholic Bibles Are Bigger, 2017).5,6

Akin:

Why would Martin Luther cut out this book when it is so clearly held up as an example to us by the New Testament? Simple: A few chapters later it endorses the practice of praying for the dead… Since Luther chose to reject the historic Christian teaching of purgatory… he had to remove that book from the Bible and appendicize it.

5 Michuta gives the historical form: “In 1518, Luther freely quoted Sirach and Tobit against his Catholic detractors; but by the following year, Luther’s view of the Deuterocanon had taken a decidedly negative turn.”6 The record supports the sequence: Luther’s 1518 Resolutiones, his response to Prierias and the Heidelberg Disputation cite Sirach and Tobit with nothing marking them sub-canonical, and the first denial comes at Leipzig precisely when Eck presses 2 Maccabees 12:43–45, the offerings for the dead; rather than reinterpret the passage, Luther denied the book’s canonical standing.1 The deuterocanon’s proof-texts, 2 Maccabees 12 for the dead and Tobit 12 for almsgiving, were live stakes in the Reformation controversy over justification. On this reading the canon judgement followed the theological controversy, and the book Luther set below Scripture was the one that stood against his developing doctrine.
