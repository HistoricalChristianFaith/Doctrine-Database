Was there a period without prophets between Malachi and Christ, and does it bear on the extent of the canon?

Disputed reading · on the Old Testament canon · witness: Josephus

Related readings: Does the cessation of prophecy exclude the deuterocanon? · Josephus’s twenty-two books · The Jewish canon and the deuterocanon

The passage

It is true, our history hath been written since Artaxerxes very particularly, but hath not been esteemed of the like authority with the former by our forefathers, because there hath not been an exact succession of prophets since that time;

— Josephus, Against Apion I.8 (§41), c. 95, Whiston translation.1 1 Maccabees 9:27: “So was there a great affliction in Israel, the like whereof was not since the time that a prophet was not seen among them.”2

The question

A Jewish tradition held that the line of prophets ended with Haggai, Zechariah and Malachi, and the deuterocanonical books were written after them. What is disputed is whether the sources attest a time with no prophets at all between Malachi and Christ, and whether such a time would bound the canon. Allen Parr and CARM state the “silent years” reading in popular sources; Beckwith gives the cutoff in a scholarly form.

Prophecy ceased after the last prophets, and the later books are not prophetic Scripture

With Haggai, Zechariah and Malachi the prophetic Spirit departed from Israel; books written afterward lack prophetic authority and stand outside the Scriptures.

Held by: Josephus (Against Apion, c. 95); the rabbinic tradition (Tosefta Sotah 13:2–3; b. Yoma 9b);1,2 Roger Beckwith (Anglican, 1985);2 Allen Parr and CARM.1,2

Josephus bounds the books of full authority at Artaxerxes “because there hath not been an exact succession of prophets since that time.”1 1 Maccabees, written after Malachi, three times registers that no prophet was present: the defiled altar-stones are stored “until there should come a prophet” (4:46); the affliction is “such as was not since the time that a prophet was not seen among them” (9:27); Simon is made leader “until there should arise a faithful prophet” (14:41). The rabbis say that with Haggai, Zechariah and Malachi “the Divine Spirit of prophetic revelation departed from the Jewish people,” only the lesser bat kol remaining.2,3 On this reading the books of the interval belong to a lower, non-prophetic order. CARM notes that the deuterocanonical books “are not prophetic… and they even acknowledge that there were no prophets in Israel at their time (cf. 1 Macc. 9:27; 14:41).”2 Parr states the popular form: “Josephus is recognizing that Malachi was the last prophet inspired by God, and there were no other prophetic voices between that 400 years.”1 On this reading the end of the prophetic succession marks the end of the writing of Israel’s Scripture, and the books composed after it, including the deuterocanon, fall outside the canon.

Prophecy continued in the interval, and prophetic authorship was not the canon’s measure

Josephus’s “exact succession” concerns the continuous record, not prophecy as such; Josephus and the Gospels report prophets in the interval, and Justin places the end of Jewish prophecy at Christ.

Held by: Justin Martyr (Dialogue with Trypho, c. 160);4 Rebecca Gray (Prophetic Figures in Late Second Temple Jewish Palestine, 1993);5 Gary Michuta and Joe Heschmeyer (Catholic apologists).6,4

Gray reads Josephus’s “exact succession” as marking the continuous historical narrative from Genesis to Esther, in the service of Against Apion’s case for Jewish antiquity, and catalogues the prophetic figures Josephus reports after Artaxerxes: John Hyrcanus, who had “the government of his nation, and the high-priesthood, and the gift of prophecy”; Menahem the Essene, who “saluted” the boy Herod “as king of the Jews”; Jesus son of Ananus, crying his oracle over Jerusalem; and Josephus himself at Jotapata. Michuta adds that in retelling the rededication of the altar Josephus omits 1 Maccabees’ “until there should come a prophet.”5 Michuta reads 4:46 and 14:41 as near expectation, “like ‘leave these until a policeman comes’,” and 9:27 as a past moment.7 The Gospels report Anna, “a prophetess,” at Jesus’ presentation, and Jesus says that “all the prophets and the law prophesied until John.” Justin tells Trypho that “there never failed to be a prophet among you… But after the manifestation and death of our Jesus Christ in your nation, there was and is nowhere any prophet.”4 Proverbs ascribes “the prophecy” to Agur son of Jakeh and to king Lemuel’s mother, neither counted among the prophets, and the rabbinic corpus that records the cessation still cites Ben Sira as a verse of the Writings.8 On this reading the prophetic voice continued until John, and the extent of the canon was not fixed by the succession of named prophets.
