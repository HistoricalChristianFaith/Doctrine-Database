Does the Fathers’ word “canon” (kanôn) carry the modern sense of a closed list of inspired books?

Disputed reading · on the Old Testament canon · witnesses: Athanasius, Laodicea, Amphilochius of Iconium

Related readings: The label “apocrypha” · The “ecclesiastical” category · Citation “as Scripture” and canonical standing

The passage

Athanasius, Festal Letter 39 (367), speaks of the books “being canonized” (kanonizomena) and of others “not… included in the Canon, but appointed… to be read”; Amphilochius of Iconium closes his verse catalogue (c. 380s) by calling it “the most reliable canon (kanôn) of the divinely inspired Scriptures.”1

The question

The history of the word is shared by Bruce, Metzger, and Gallagher and Meade: kanôn moves from “rod” or “standard,” to the “rule of faith” or “rule of truth” of the second and third centuries, to conciliar decrees and Eusebius’s Gospel tables, and in the fourth century to a list of sacred books.2,3,4 What is disputed is what that history implies for reading the earlier witnesses: whether a determinate body of Scripture was already present before the word named a list, or whether modern questions about “the canon” import a framework the early witnesses did not use.

The books functioned as the rule before they were listed

Before kanôn named a list, the Fathers already set the prophetic and apostolic writings apart as Scripture; the list recorded an authority already recognized.

Held by: F. F. Bruce (Brethren, The Canon of Scripture, 1988).5

Bruce dates the two secondary senses of the word to different Fathers: “Origen, early in the third century A.D., applied the former… to the Bible, to indicate that it is the ‘standard’ or ‘rule’ of faith,” while “Athanasius, a century later, used the latter… in the sense in which these books constitute the ‘list’ of writings which the Church reckons as the authoritative documents of divine revelation.”3 The concept, on his reading, came before the word: “The writings are not authoritative because they are included in the list; they are in the list because their authority has been recognized… Divine authority comes first: canonicity follows authority and is dependent upon it.”5 Long before kanôn meant a table, the Fathers wrote “it is written” and “the Scriptures,” marking off a collection from all other writing, and a Father who never says “canon” can have a definite sense of which writings are Scripture. When the word does attach to a list, in Athanasius, Laodicea (whose canons 59–60 restrict public reading to the “canonical” books) and Amphilochius, it names a delimited set of “divinely inspired Scriptures.”1 On this reading the fourth-century usage gave a name to a recognition the churches had already made.

The modern sense should not be read back onto the earlier witnesses

The modern “canon” bundles a list, closure and exclusivity to inspired books; the early witnesses worked with other words, and even the first list-sense of “canon” sat inside a graded view.

Held by: John Meade (Protestant, canon-list scholar); Edmon L. Gallagher and John D. Meade (The Biblical Canon Lists from Early Christianity, 2017).2

On this reading the question “which books were in the canon?” is framed in a fourth-century-and-later vocabulary and applied to centuries that used other words: “scripture,” “covenant,” “the rule of truth,” “read in church.” The four New Testament uses of kanôn mean “rule,” “measure” or “province” (2 Corinthians 10:13–16; Galatians 6:16; Philippians 3:16), and the pre-Nicene Christian kanôn is the “rule of faith”: Clement of Rome’s “rule of our tradition,” the “rule of truth” of Irenaeus and Clement of Alexandria.3,4 Gallagher and Meade summarize the fourth-century development as a fusion of two strands, canon as the rule of faith and canon as the list of sacred books.1 Even then the list-sense is graded: Athanasius’s letter names a middle tier “appointed… to be read” that is neither canon nor apocrypha, and he cites Wisdom and Sirach as Scripture elsewhere.6 The caution cuts in both directions: early witnesses were not drawing modern canon lists, and early citation “as Scripture” is not yet a verdict of canon-membership in the modern sense.2 On this reading each witness is described in its own categories rather than placed in or out of a later two-way scheme.
