Did the Wisdom of Solomon supply doctrine-confirming language for Nicaea’s “Light from Light”?

Disputed reading · on the Old Testament canon · witnesses: the Council of Nicaea, Athanasius, Jerome

Related readings: Was the deuterocanon held below Scripture until Trent? · Was Trent the first universal, binding definition? · Doctrine and the undisputed books

The passage

Wisdom 7:25–26 (KJV): divine Wisdom is “the breath of the power of God, and a pure influence flowing from the glory of the Almighty… the brightness of the everlasting light, the unspotted mirror of the power of God, and the image of his goodness.”1 Athanasius, De Decretis §25: “This then is the sense in which they who met at Nicaea made use of these expressions. But next that they did not invent them for themselves… but spoke what they had received from their predecessors.”2

The question

The Nicene Creed calls the Son “Light from Light”; Hebrews 1:3 calls him “the brightness (apaugasma) of his glory,” using the rare word of Wisdom 7:26.3 Jerome held that the disputed books may be read “for the edification of the people, [but] not for confirming the authority of ecclesiastical dogmas.”4 What is disputed is whether Wisdom confirmed doctrine in the Nicene argument, showing the books’ dogmatic use long before Trent, or whether Nicaea’s language rests on Hebrews and the Logos tradition, with Wisdom in its background. The reading that Wisdom confirmed doctrine in the Nicene argument is stated here from Catholic apologists; academic literature holding it was not located.

Wisdom confirmed doctrine in the Nicene argument

The radiance-theology of the Son, which Nicaea condensed into “Light from Light,” comes from Wisdom 7:25–26, and the council’s defender grounds the Nicene terms in it.

Held by: Gary Michuta (Catholic apologist, The Case for the Deuterocanon, 2015); Jimmy Akin and Catholic Answers;5,6 with the NPNF editor’s note that Athanasius’s “effluence” “is derived from Wisd. vii. 25.”2

Michuta presses the instance as part of the continuity of the books’ doctrinal use across the councils.5 Athanasius explains that the bishops described the Son “as the radiance of light,” and that “one in essence” was meant “to signify the relation of the radiance to the light”; to show the terms were not invented, he quotes Theognostus: the Son’s essence “sprang from the Father’s essence, as the radiance of light, as the vapour of water… it is an effluence of the Father’s essence.” The NPNF editor traces “effluence” to Wisdom 7:25, with the same usage in Origen.2 Athanasius in his own voice cites Wisdom 9:2 with “it is written,” Sirach 15:9 as words “spoken by the Spirit,” Tobit 4:18 under “it is written,” and vouches that Dionysius drew the Son’s titles, including “breath” and “brightness of the Father,” “from the Scriptures.”7 Jerome himself reports that “the Nicene Council is read [legitur] to have counted this book [Judith] in the number of the sacred Scriptures.”8 On this reading Wisdom was doing doctrinal work in 325, and Jerome’s restriction describes his own rule rather than the church’s practice.

The books are read, not used to confirm dogma; Nicaea’s language rests on Hebrews

The disputed books serve edification, not the proof of doctrine; the Creed quotes no book, and the “brightness” the Fathers cite verbatim is Hebrews 1:3.

Held by: Jerome (Prologus Galeatus and Prologue to the Books of Solomon), on the general principle.4

Jerome’s rule is that the church reads the disputed books “for the edification of the people, [but] not for confirming the authority of ecclesiastical dogmas.”4 On this reading the Nicene formula phôs ek phôtos is a theological formulation, not a quotation, and the text the Fathers adduce verbatim for the Son’s radiance is Hebrews 1:3, “Who is the brightness of His glory, and the Expression of His Person,” which Athanasius sets out in De Decretis §12 beside Psalm 36:9 and Baruch 3:12.9 Wisdom’s language reaches the Nicene terms through Hebrews and through pre-Nicene writers such as Theognostus, and the council issued a creed and anathemas, not an exegesis: “they… made use of these expressions” is Athanasius’s report of how the Nicene terms were understood.10 Jerome’s report about Judith at Nicaea is second-hand (legitur), the acts of Nicaea are lost, and none of the surviving Nicene canons treats the biblical canon; Catholic Answers acknowledges the difficulty.8 On this reading Nicaea’s confession of the Son rests on the apostolic text of Hebrews, and the place of Wisdom in its background leaves the book’s rank where Jerome put it.
