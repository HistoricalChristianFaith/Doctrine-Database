Was the Council of Trent the first universal, binding affirmation of the deuterocanon?

Disputed reading · on the Old Testament canon · witnesses: the Council of Trent, the Council of Florence, the Synod of Jerusalem

Related readings: Did Trent add the books? · Do the councils’ lists contradict one another? · Was the deuterocanon held below Scripture until Trent?

The passage

less than 60 men gathered in a place called Trent on April 8, 1546 decreed that it should be so. This was the first ecumenical dogmatic binding definition for Roman Catholics of the actual content of the canon.

— James White, in debate with Gary Michuta (2004).1

Council of Trent, Session 4 (8 April 1546): “But if any one receive not, as sacred and canonical, the said books entire with all their parts… let him be anathema.”2

The question

Trent was the first council to attach an anathema to the list; earlier receptions include the regional African councils, the Roman list and Innocent I, and the Council of Florence’s bull Cantate Domino (1442), which “accepts and venerates” the same books.2,3 What is disputed is the scope of Trent’s priority: whether it was the first universal, binding affirmation of the deuterocanon, or the first under anathema after an earlier ecumenical profession. Eastern Orthodox writers place the question in their own conciliar history.

Trent’s decree was the first universal, binding definition

Earlier councils were regional, or their acts were not binding on the church at large; at Trent a small assembly first defined the content of the canon as binding dogma.

Held by: B. F. Westcott (Anglican, A General Survey of the History of the Canon of the New Testament, 1875);4 James White (Reformed Baptist apologist, 2004); Matt Whitman (evangelical teacher); CARM.1

Westcott, whom White cites, writes that by this “fatal decree” the Council “gave a new aspect to the whole question of the Canon,” and that it “was ratified by fifty-three prelates, among whom there was not one German, not one scholar distinguished for historical learning.”4 White: “This was the first ecumenical dogmatic binding definition for Roman Catholics of the actual content of the canon.”1 He grants the books’ earlier use and rests his claim on binding definition; Whitman grants “little regional councils that affirmed it.”5 Whitman: “at no point do you have all of Christendom getting together to sign off on that set of books.” CARM: the books were “not officially accepted by the Catholic Church at a universal council until 1546… it is doubtful that these local church councils’ decisions were binding on the Church at large.”1 The African councils were regional; the Roman list and Innocent I’s letter were receptions rather than definitions.2 On this reading the deuterocanon’s binding conciliar standing begins in 1546.

The first definition under anathema, after an earlier ecumenical profession

Trent first closed the question under anathema; an ecumenical council had already professed the same list in 1442, and the books rest on continuous reception.

Held by: George Reid (Catholic, “Canon of the Old Testament,” Catholic Encyclopedia, 1908), who grants Trent’s priority as the first infallible definition and holds that Florence taught the books’ inspiration without defining their canonicity;6 Catholic Answers (Catholic apologetics apostolate); Dave Armstrong (Catholic apologist, 2020).7,8

Reid calls the Tridentine decree “the first infallible and effectually promulgated pronouncement on the Canon, addressed to the Church Universal,” and holds that, “being dogmatic in its purport, it implies that the Apostles bequeathed the same Canon to the Church, as a part of the depositum fidei”: the larger canon “passed through the Apostles’ hands to the church tacitly, by way of their usage.” Of Florence he writes that it “taught the inspiration of all the Scriptures, but did not formally pass on their canonicity.”6 On this reading the force of Trent’s decree was new: no earlier act attached an anathema to denial. Catholic Answers calls Trent “the first infallible conciliar listing of each individual book,” by which the council “infallibly reiterated what the Church had long taught.”7 Armstrong writes that “the canonical lists were not infallible in the highest sense until Trent.”8 The scope of “first” is another matter. The Council of Florence, reckoned in Catholic usage the seventeenth ecumenical council, in Cantate Domino (4 February 1442) “accepts and venerates” and lists Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Ecclesiasticus, Baruch and “two books of the Maccabees,” with the receptive verb (suscipit ac veneratur) Trent repeats.3 No council since 1054 has bound Greek and Latin Christendom together; the Protestant canon was fixed by confessions binding their own communions, the Thirty-Nine Articles and the Westminster Confession, and the Orthodox Synod of Jerusalem (1672) received Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach and the Maccabees.9 The books’ standing rests on continuous reception: the Septuagint, the great codices, the Vulgate, the African and Roman lists, Florence.10 On this reading Trent was the first definition under anathema of a canon an ecumenical council had already professed.

For the Orthodox, the conciliar ratification is Trullo’s, and the books’ rank is not closed by definition

The councils the Orthodox reckon ecumenical ratified the earlier canon-lists in Trullo’s second canon, confirmed at Nicaea II; later local synods received the books, and no ecumenical definition has fixed their rank.

Held by: R. Stergiou (Orthodox priest, “The Old Testament in the Orthodox Church,” 1997); Kallistos Ware (Orthodox bishop, The Orthodox Church); Eugen J. Pentiuc (Orthodox, The Old Testament in Eastern Orthodox Tradition, 2014).11

George Nedungatt (Catholic, 2010) reports that “in the East the status of the Council in Trullo as an ecumenical council was never questioned”; its second canon “received and confirmed” the canons of the earlier councils, Carthage among them, and of the Fathers, and Nicaea II listed it with Nicaea I.12 Stergiou reads that ratification as covering differing lists: there have been “inconsistencies not only by the Church Fathers, but also by many local and even Ecumenical Synods as to which Canon is to be used”; the second canon of Trullo “sanctioned the use of the Hebrew Canon,” while the Church “accepted the Alexandrian Canon (Septuagint LXX) as divinely inspired, appropriate for reading in Church.” He reports P. Bratsiotes’s observation, as quoted by S. Agourides, that “the fixing of the Canon of the Old Testament is proposed as one of the subjects of a future Great Synod.”11 Ware sets the later local synods below the ecumenical definitions: Jassy (1642) and Jerusalem (1672) declared the books “genuine parts of Scripture,” but “the decrees of Jassy or Jerusalem do not stand on the same level as the Nicene Creed,” and “most Orthodox scholars at the present day” hold the books “part of the Bible” on “a lower footing than the rest of the Old Testament.”11 Pentiuc describes the current Orthodox position as strict about the thirty-nine books of the Jewish Bible and flexible about the anaginōskomena.11 On this reading Trent’s decree defined the canon for the Catholic Church, while the ecumenical councils of the Orthodox ratified the lists together and left the rank of the read books to the Church’s continuing practice.
