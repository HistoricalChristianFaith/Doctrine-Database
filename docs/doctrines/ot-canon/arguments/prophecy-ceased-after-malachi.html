Does the tradition that prophecy ceased after Malachi exclude the deuterocanon from Scripture?

Disputed reading · on the Old Testament canon · witnesses: the Babylonian Talmud, Josephus

Related readings: A period without prophets? · Josephus’s twenty-two books · The rabbinic ban on Ben Sira

The passage

"After the last prophets Haggai, Zechariah, and Malachi died, the Divine Spirit of prophetic revelation departed from the Jewish people…"

— b. Yoma 9b; the same tradition at b. Sotah 48b and, earliest, Tosefta Sotah 13:2–3 (“with the death of Haggai, Zechariah, and Malachi, the latter prophets, the Holy Spirit ceased out of Israel”).1

The question

The deuterocanonical books were composed after Haggai, Zechariah and Malachi, and 1 Maccabees itself registers that no prophet was then present (4:46; 9:27; 14:41).2 What is disputed is whether the cessation of prophecy closed the canon against books written afterward, or whether cessation and canon were distinct questions, the same rabbinic corpus still treating a later book as Scripture.

No prophet, no Scripture: the later books fall outside the canon

Inspiration ceased with the last prophets; books written afterward lack prophetic authority, and the canon was closed before the deuterocanon was written.

Held by: Roger Beckwith (Anglican, 1985); James White (Reformed Baptist apologist, 2004); CARM;3 Benjamin D. Sommer (JBL, 1996) defends a real Second-Temple sense that prophecy had ceased, without addressing the canon.4

Beckwith gathers the rabbinic cessation statements, Tosefta Sotah 13:2 among them, to argue that the canon was effectively closed before the deuterocanon was written.3 The Talmud counts the Holy Spirit among the things the Second Temple lacked that the First possessed: “the Ark of the Covenant… fire; and the Divine Presence; and the Divine Spirit; and the Urim VeTummim.”5 White reads the sources aloud in debate: “since the death of the last prophets Haggai, Zechariah, and Malachi the Holy Spirit of prophetic inspiration departed from Israel.”3 The idea is older than the rabbis: 1 Maccabees speaks of “a great affliction in Israel, the like whereof was not since the time that a prophet was not seen among them” (9:27) and stores the defiled altar-stones “until there should come a prophet” (4:46); Josephus bounds the books of full authority at Artaxerxes “because there hath not been an exact succession of prophets since that time.”2 Sommer defends a real Second-Temple sense that prophecy had ceased.4 On this reading the deuterocanonical books, all composed after the prophetic age had closed, were not received as inspired by the Jews, and the church should not receive them.

Cessation of prophecy is not closure of the canon

“No prophet now” and “no further book may be received” are different claims; the rabbinic corpus still quoted Ben Sira as Scripture, and the cessation sayings may project a later canon back into the past.

Held by: Frederick E. Greenspahn (“Why Prophecy Ceased,” JBL, 1989);4 Gary Michuta, Trent Horn and Joe Heschmeyer (Catholic apologists).6,7

At Bava Kamma 92b the Talmud cites Ben Sira (Sirach 13:15–17) as a verse of the Writings, “it is triplicated in the Writings, as it is written,” and the Mishnah records the second-century dispute over whether Ecclesiastes and the Song of Songs “render the hands impure”; the same rabbis elsewhere barred Ben Sira among the “outside books.”8 Greenspahn makes the case that the Tannaitic sayings retroject a later settlement; Michuta that they “do not predate the second Christian century” and that “the idea of the cessation of prophecy was, in a sense, a rabbinical tool to retroject or push the post-Bar Kokhba canon back into history.”4 1 Maccabees, which records the absence of prophets, is itself one of the books said to be disqualified by it. Horn and Heschmeyer argue that the rule reaches too far: if the Holy Spirit fell silent c. 450 BC, it “wouldn’t just be a reason to reject the Deuterocanon… It would be a reason to reject the entire New Testament”; Heschmeyer adds Anna the prophetess and “the prophets… prophesied until John.”7 On this reading the extent of Scripture was settled by reception, not by the date at which prophecy was thought to have lapsed.
