Did first-century Judaism hold a closed canon that excluded the deuterocanon?

Disputed reading · on the Old Testament canon · witnesses: Josephus, the Babylonian Talmud, Jerome, Augustine

Related readings: A Pharisaic canon accepted by Jesus and Paul? · Josephus’s twenty-two books · Jamnia · Philo’s silence · The apostles’ Greek Bible

The passage

The books of the Apocrypha were not included in the Hebrew Bible, nor were they regarded as canonical by the leaders of official Judaism anywhere. Even Jews who wrote in Greek at the beginning of our era, like Philo and Josephus, recognized only the Canon of the Hebrew Bible, although they used the "Septuagint" translation. The books of the Apocrypha … first received canonical recognition from Greek-speaking Christians.

— F. F. Bruce, “The Canon of Scripture” (1954).1

The question

The deuterocanonical books are absent from the Hebrew Bible that the rabbinic tradition transmitted. What is disputed is whether, in the apostolic age, Judaism already held a closed, Hebrew-only canon that had excluded them, so that the church received a settled Jewish verdict, or whether Jewish communities then held overlapping collections and the rabbinic canon closed later.

The Jews held the Hebrew books only, and the church should follow them

The Jewish people, entrusted with the oracles of God, recognized only the Hebrew books; the deuterocanon first received canonical recognition from Greek-speaking Christians.

Held by: Jerome (Prologue to Tobit), for whom Tobit is the book “which the Hebrews exclude from the catalogue of Divine Scriptures”;2 F. F. Bruce (Brethren, 1954);1 James White (Reformed Baptist apologist, 2004); CARM and other popular writers.3

Bruce: “The books of the Apocrypha were not included in the Hebrew Bible, nor were they regarded as canonical by the leaders of official Judaism anywhere. Even Jews who wrote in Greek at the beginning of our era, like Philo and Josephus, recognized only the Canon of the Hebrew Bible.”1 Josephus, writing to a Roman audience at the close of the first century, says the Jews hold “only twenty-two books,” divinely given, and treats the writings after Artaxerxes as a lower order; Philo quotes the Pentateuch constantly and never cites a deuterocanonical book as Scripture; the rabbis hold that prophecy ceased after Haggai, Zechariah and Malachi; and the Hebrew Bible of the Pharisaic-rabbinic tradition excludes the books.4 Romans 3:2 supplies the premise that God committed his oracles to the Jews; White states the conclusion: these books “were not accepted as canonical scripture by the Jews, to whom the oracles of God had been committed.” Winger puts it: “if the Jews say that wasn’t one of the oracles, then I should take that word.”3 On this reading the Old Testament is the Scripture of Israel as Israel received it, and the church owes its Old Testament to that reception.

No closed Jewish canon yet existed; the Church received books the synagogue did not

In the first century Jewish communities held overlapping collections, some using the disputed books; the rabbinic table closed later, and the Church’s reception is its own.

Held by: Augustine (City of God XVIII.36, c. 425), for the Church’s own reception;5 Albert C. Sundberg Jr. (1964); Lee Martin McDonald (2007); Timothy H. Lim (2013).6,7

Augustine grants that the Maccabees stand outside the Hebrew table and holds them “canonical, not by the Jews, but by the Church.”5 Sundberg argued that no Jewish canon, Palestinian or Alexandrian, was closed in the first century.6 Lim: “throughout the post-exilic period up to around 100 CE there was not one official ‘canon’ accepted by all Jews,” but collections held by Pharisees, Sadducees, Essenes, the Qumran community, Samaritans and the Diaspora. McDonald: “the evidence simply is not there to make the jump backward to the first century to argue for a fully closed canon at that early date.”7 The Mishnah preserves dispute over whether Ecclesiastes and the Song of Songs “defile the hands”; the Tosefta rules that “the books of Ben Sira and all books written from then on do not defile the hands,” while the Talmud quotes Ben Sira with “as it is written”; Epiphanius reports Jews of his day disputing Wisdom and Sirach.8 Qumran kept Tobit in Aramaic and Hebrew and Ben Sira in Hebrew, and the Greek Epistle of Jeremiah (7Q2) is a Jewish copy of the first century BC.9 The canon-closing “Council of Jamnia” is a discarded hypothesis, and the definitive Hebrew table is the medieval Masoretic Text.5 Bruce himself judged it “probably unwise to talk as if there were a Council or Synod of Jamnia.”10 On this reading the rabbinic canon was settled after the apostolic age, and the Church’s Old Testament rests on the Church’s reception.
