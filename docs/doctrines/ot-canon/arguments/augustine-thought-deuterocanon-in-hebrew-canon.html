Did Augustine receive the deuterocanon in the belief that it stood in the Hebrew canon?

Disputed reading · on the Old Testament canon · witness: Augustine of Hippo

Related readings: Augustine’s lack of Hebrew and his canon witness · Did Augustine retract or rank the deuterocanon lower?

The passage

From this time, when the temple was rebuilt, down to the time of Aristobulus, the Jews had not kings but princes; and the reckoning of their dates is found, not in the Holy Scriptures which are called canonical, but in others, among which are also the books of the Maccabees. These are held as canonical, not by the Jews, but by the Church, on account of the extreme and wonderful sufferings of certain martyrs, who, before Christ had come in the flesh, contended for the law of God even unto death, and endured most grievous and horrible evils.

— Augustine, The City of God XVIII.36 (c. 425).1

Augustine, however, disagreed. Augustine could not read Hebrew, and the manuscripts that he had contained these books but interestingly enough he is actually a witness for my side this evening. Why? Because though he accepted the Apocryphal books, he did so because he thought they were part of the Hebrew canon. If he had known what the Hebrew canon was, he would have taken a different position than that which he took, but he was unaware of those particular facts.

— James White, in debate with Gary Michuta (2004).2

The question

Augustine read little or no Hebrew and held the Septuagint inspired; his list of forty-four books includes the deuterocanon.3,4 What is disputed is whether he received those books in the belief that they belonged to the Jewish canon, so that his acceptance rested on a mistake of fact, or knowing that they did not. The reading that he was mistaken is stated here from a popular apologetic source; academic literature holding it was not located.

Augustine knew the books stood outside the Jewish canon and received them on the Church’s reception

Augustine says in his own words that Wisdom, Sirach and the Maccabees are not in the canon of the Jews, and rests their standing on the churches.

Held by: Augustine (The City of God XVII.20, XVIII.36, c. 425);5,1 William Whitaker (Church of England, 1588), who grants the point and reads Augustine’s “canonical” in a wider sense;6 Archibald Alexander (Presbyterian, 1826), who grants that Augustine knew the Jewish books and rejects the conclusion that he held the deuterocanon equal to them.7

After quoting Wisdom 2 and Ecclesiasticus as prophecy of Christ, Augustine adds: “the things which are not written in the canon of the Jews cannot be quoted against their contradictions with so great validity,” and contrasts “those three books which it is evident are Solomon’s and held canonical by the Jews.”5 Of the dates of the Maccabean period he writes that they “are found, not in the Holy Scriptures which are called canonical, but in others, among which are also the books of the Maccabees,” held canonical “not by the Jews, but by the Church.”1 He calls the Jews “our librarians” who keep the writings in which Christ was prophesied.7 He corresponded with Jerome for years and urged him not to translate the Old Testament afresh “out of the Hebrew,” citing the uproar at Oea over Jerome’s rendering of the “gourd” in Jonah.3 His criterion was the reception of the churches: “prefer those that are received by all the catholic churches to those which some do not receive.”4 Late works still cite the books as Scripture: On Rebuke and Grace (c. 426) quotes the mother of the Maccabees as authoritative testimony.6 On this reading Augustine received the deuterocanon knowing its absence from the Jewish canon, on the authority of the churches that read it.

Augustine received the books because he took the Greek Bible to represent the Hebrew canon

Augustine, unable to read Hebrew, received what his Greek and Latin manuscripts contained, and would have judged otherwise had he known the Hebrew canon.

Held by: James White (Reformed Baptist apologist, debate with Michuta, 2004, and “The Canon According to Augustine”).2

White reads Augustine as “a witness for my side”: “though he accepted the Apocryphal books, he did so because he thought they were part of the Hebrew canon. If he had known what the Hebrew canon was, he would have taken a different position.” In writing he states the mechanism: Augustine “defended the Deutero-canonicals … because they were in the Greek Septuagint and he thought that represented the Hebrew canon.”2 On this reading Augustine’s canon follows from his view of the Greek Bible. He held that “the same Spirit who was in the prophets when they spoke these things was also in the seventy men when they translated them,” so that the Greek carried prophetic authority of its own;3 he worked from the Old Latin and the Greek and could not check them against a Hebrew text, and the manuscripts he had “contained these books.”2 A reader who takes the Septuagint as the Spirit-given form of Israel’s Scripture receives as Israel’s Scripture what the Septuagint contains. Augustine’s own remark that books outside the Jewish canon cannot be pressed against the Jews “with so great validity” shows, on this reading, the weight he gave to the Jewish canon where he knew it.5 On White’s reading Augustine’s longer canon rested on the Greek Bible he received, and a full knowledge of the Hebrew canon would have led him to Jerome’s position.
