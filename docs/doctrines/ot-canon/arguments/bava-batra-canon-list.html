Does the book-list of Bava Batra 14b attest a Hebrew canon closed before the Christian era?

Disputed reading · on the Old Testament canon · witness: the Babylonian Talmud

Related readings: Josephus’s twenty-two books · Did the Hebrew canon end with Chronicles? · The Jewish canon and the deuterocanon

The passage

"The order of the books of the Prophets when they are attached together is as follows: Joshua and Judges, Samuel and Kings, Jeremiah and Ezekiel, and Isaiah and the Twelve Prophets… The order of the Writings is: Ruth and the book of Psalms, and Job and Proverbs; Ecclesiastes, Song of Songs, and Lamentations; Daniel and the Scroll of Esther; and Ezra and Chronicles."

— a baraita preserved in the Babylonian Talmud, Bava Batra 14b.1 The nineteen books of the Prophets and Writings it orders, with the five of the Law, make the rabbinic “twenty-four”; Josephus and the Greek Fathers count the same books as twenty-two by joining Ruth to Judges and Lamentations to Jeremiah.2 None of Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch or the Maccabees appears.

The question

The list is a Hebrew-sized enumeration of the received books. What is disputed is its date: whether the arrangement it preserves goes back before the Christian era, and so witnesses to a closed canon in the apostolic age, or belongs to the rabbinic settlement of the second and third centuries AD or later.

An arrangement fixed before the Christian era

The list is cast as a baraita, tannaitic tradition older than the Talmud that carries it, and its arrangement reaches back into the pre-Christian centuries.

Held by: Roger Beckwith (Anglican, The Old Testament Canon of the New Testament Church, 1985); James White (Reformed Baptist apologist, debate with Gary Michuta, 2004).3,2

Beckwith argues that the Bava Batra arrangement reflects a canon fixed long before its Talmudic setting, in the Maccabean period, and dates it to the second century BC, resting the date on the baraita’s tannaitic form and internal features.3,4 Where Josephus gives only a count (“twenty-two books”) and names no titles, the baraita names every book in order, the Prophets and then the Writings, closing with Chronicles, and every title is one the synagogue transmitted.4 White introduced the passage in debate as a positive Jewish canon-list: “in Baba Batra — an ancient Jewish work — we have what’s called a baraita. A baraita is an ancient tradition. Many of these traditions go well beyond the time of the New Testament, even into the intertestamental period… 19 plus 5 is 24… Both numbers represent the same Canon found in Protestant Bibles today, and this is found in a baraita, an ancient tradition going back even before the time of the New Testament.”2 The twenty-four of the rabbis and the twenty-two of Josephus are, on this reading, the same collection counted two ways, so the baraita names the books that Josephus counts at the end of the first century.5 On this reading Bava Batra 14b preserves the Hebrew canon as Jesus’ hearers knew it, a list without the deuterocanon.

A rabbinic list of the second or third century AD, or later

A baraita is older than the Talmud but not indefinitely old; this list belongs to the rabbinic period after the New Testament.

Held by: Jack N. Lightstone (“The Rabbis’ Bible,” in The Canon Debate, 2002); Gary Michuta (Catholic apologist, debate with White, 2004).6,7

A baraita is tannaitic material preserved outside the Mishnah and embedded in the Amoraic Talmud; the form dates a tradition earlier than the Talmud’s sixth-century redaction, “possibly… back to the second century CE,” but does not by itself date it before Christ. Dates proposed for this list run from Beckwith’s second century BC through “roughly 200 CE,” contemporary with the Mishnah, to the third century or later; John Barton asks why, if the list were so early, it was not included in the Mishnah. Lightstone doubts the list is tannaitic at all and places it later.6 Michuta pressed the dating in debate, drawing from White that the Mishnah was “codified around the year 250” and the Talmud roughly “600 years after the time of Christ,” with Akiba (early second century) named as the rabbi who selected the Mishnah’s traditions.7 The same compilation quotes Ben Sira as Scripture: Bava Kamma 92b cites Sirach 13:15–17 for the Writings with “as it is written,” and at Eruvin 65a Rashi traces an unlocated line to “the book of Ben Sira,” while marking Ben Sira as outside “all the Writings.”8 Its Chronicles-last order is rare in the first millennium, most lists closing with Daniel, Ezra, Esther or Maccabees, and becomes common after the Adath Deborim (1207).5 On this reading Bava Batra 14b records the rabbinic list of about AD 200 or later.
