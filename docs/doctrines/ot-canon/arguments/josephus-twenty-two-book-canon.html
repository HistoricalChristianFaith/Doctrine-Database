Does Josephus’s count of twenty-two books (Against Apion I.8) attest a closed Hebrew canon excluding the deuterocanon?

Disputed reading · on the Old Testament canon · witness: Josephus

Related readings: The Jewish canon and the deuterocanon · Philo’s silence · A period without prophets? · The Bava Batra 14b book-list

The passage

"…only twenty-two books, which contain the records of all the past times; which are justly believed to be divine … the time from the death of Moses till the reign of Artaxerxes … the prophets … wrote down what was done in their times in thirteen books. The remaining four books contain hymns to God, and precepts for the conduct of human life. It is true, our history hath been written since Artaxerxes very particularly, but hath not been esteemed of the like authority with the former by our forefathers, because there hath not been an exact succession of prophets since that time."

— Josephus, Against Apion I.8 (§§37–41), c. AD 93, Whiston translation.1

The question

Josephus gives a Hebrew-sized count and places the books written after Artaxerxes, the period of the deuterocanonical histories and wisdom books, in a lower order. What is disputed is what the count attests: a closed canon held by the Jews as a whole that excluded these books, or one reckoning, without a list of titles, within a Judaism whose collections still varied.

The clearest Jewish witness to a closed, Hebrew-sized canon

Within living memory of the Temple, Josephus states that the Jews revere a fixed set of twenty-two books, to which nothing is added, and that the later books are of lesser authority.

Held by: F. F. Bruce (Brethren, 1954); Roger Beckwith (Anglican, 1985); John Piper (Baptist pastor); James White (Reformed Baptist apologist, 2004).2,3

Josephus writes c. AD 93 that the reverenced books are a bounded set: “no one has been so bold as either to add any thing to them, to take any thing from them,” and Jews would “willingly … die for” them.4 His twenty-two is the count Origen and Jerome also report, Ruth joined to Judges and Lamentations to Jeremiah to match the Hebrew alphabet, and the rabbinic twenty-four is the same collection counted another way.4 The post-Artaxerxes writings “hath not been esteemed of the like authority with the former by our forefathers.”1 Bruce: “Even Jews who wrote in Greek at the beginning of our era, like Philo and Josephus, recognized only the Canon of the Hebrew Bible, although they used the ‘Septuagint’”; Piper, that they “used the LXX but did not accord the Apocrypha authority.” White holds that Josephus “specifically rejects those books written after Malachi” and that his canon had been “in place for 300 years.”2 When Gary Michuta noted that Josephus names only the Pentateuch and Joshua as “laid up in the temple,” White answered with Beckwith that the temple’s contents are not limited to the books those asides mention, the fuller content being given by the twenty-two count.3 Others repeat the conclusion in popular form, among them Steve Christie (“the same thirty-nine … in a condensed twenty-two-book format”) and CARM.2 On this reading Josephus records the Hebrew canon, closed and without the deuterocanon, as his nation held it.

One reckoning, without titles, in a Judaism whose collections still varied

Josephus names no books, writes apologetic for a Roman readership, and himself used the later Greek histories; his count reflects one stream, not a canon closed for all Jews.

Held by: John Barton (on the count’s contents);5 Rebecca Gray (Prophetic Figures, 1993, on the “exact succession”);6 Gary Michuta and Trent Horn (Catholic apologists).7

Barton: “it is impossible to know exactly which books he included, or how he counted them.” Josephus followed 1 Esdras for the post-exilic narrative, including the tale of the three bodyguards found only there, and used 1 Maccabees as his principal source for the Hasmonean history.5 Gray reads “exact succession of prophets” as marking the continuous record from Genesis to Esther that his apologetic for Jewish antiquity turns on; Josephus claims a prophetic gift for himself and reports later prophetic figures.6 Sundberg, Lim and McDonald hold that no single Jewish canon was fixed in the first century; Qumran kept Tobit, Ben Sira and the Epistle of Jeremiah, and the Diaspora read the Greek Bible.8 Horn reads Josephus’s absolutes as apologetic, “exaggerating, not lying,” and points to his reading of a scriptural oracle as denoting “the government of Vespasian.”9 Michuta notes, with Frank Moore Cross, that several Hebrew text-types coexisted until the consonantal text was standardized between the revolts, so the claim of no “change” describes reverence rather than transmission.10 On this reading Josephus attests the reckoning of the priestly and proto-rabbinic stream to which he belonged, a count whose contents must be inferred.
