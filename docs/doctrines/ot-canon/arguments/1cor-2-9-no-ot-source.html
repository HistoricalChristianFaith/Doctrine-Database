Does 1 Corinthians 2:9 (“Eye hath not seen”) quote an apocryphon or Isaiah?

Disputed reading · on the Old Testament canon · witness: Paul (1 Corinthians 2:9), as read by Origen and Jerome

Related readings: Ephesians 5:14 · John 7:38 · Luke 11:49

The passage

"But as it is written, Eye hath not seen, nor ear heard, neither have entered into the heart of man, the things which God hath prepared for them that love him."

— 1 Corinthians 2:9 (KJV).1 The formula kathôs gégraptai (“as it is written”) is the usual New Testament header for an Old Testament citation; no Old Testament passage reads exactly this way.

The question

From what written source does Paul draw the words he introduces with “as it is written”: a paraphrase of Isaiah 64:4, or an apocryphal writing ascribed to Elijah? The answer has been used in the canon debate, since a Scripture formula attached to an apocryphon would bear on how far the apostolic category of “Scripture” reached beyond the Hebrew books.

The words come from an Elijah apocryphon

Paul’s words stand in a writing ascribed to Elijah, the “Secrets” or “Apocalypse” of Elijah, rather than in any canonical book.

Held by: Origen (Alexandrian, Commentary on Matthew, c. 246–248); Ambrosiaster (Latin, Commentary on 1 Corinthians, c. 370s), who also finds the words “somewhat differently” in Isaiah.2

Origen, who knew both the Hebrew and the Greek Old Testament through his Hexapla, writes that the words “are found in no canonical book, except in the Secrets of Elijah the prophet” (in nullo regulari libro hoc positum invenitur, nisi in Secretis Eliae prophetae). He identifies a specific book and reports that the wording is there, not in the canonical text. Ambrosiaster, commenting on the same verse, records both sources: “These words were expressed somewhat differently by Isaiah, and they are also found in the apocryphal Apocalypse of Elijah.”2 On this reading the citation formula introduces words whose closest written form stood in an Elijah apocryphon, and the verbal distance from Isaiah 64:4 is accounted for by a different source rather than by paraphrase. Those who use the verse in the canon debate draw from it that the apostolic practice of citing “Scripture” was not confined to the books of a closed Hebrew list.1

The words are a free rendering of Isaiah 64:4

Paul gives the sense of Isaiah 64:4 in his own words, as the apostles often cite by meaning rather than wording.

Held by: Jerome (Latin, Letter 57 to Pammachius, c. 395, and Preface to the Pentateuch, c. 403).3

Jerome takes up this verse as a model case of apostolic paraphrase:

"Let us pass on now to the apostle Paul who writes thus to the Corinthians… 'as it is written, Eye hath not seen nor ear heard, neither have entered into the heart of man, the things which God hath prepared for them that love Him.' Some writers on this passage betake themselves to the ravings of the apocryphal books and assert that the quotation comes from the Revelation of Elijah; whereas the truth is that it is found in Isaiah according to the Hebrew text… We see then from what place the quotation is taken and yet the apostle has not rendered his original word for word, but, using a paraphrase, he has given the sense in different terms."

He sets the Hebrew of Isaiah 64:4 beside it: “Since the beginning of the world men have not heard nor perceived by the ear, neither hath the eye seen, O God, beside thee what thou hast prepared for them that wait for thee.” The vocabulary and the structure (unseen, unheard, prepared by God for those who wait on him) correspond, and Jerome places the verse in his list of apostolic citations that give “the meaning rather than the words.” In his Preface to the Pentateuch he files the same words among the Evangelists’ and Apostles’ citations “from the Old Testament” that are to be sought in the Hebrew text.3 Earlier writers quote the saying as a known scriptural promise without naming an apocryphal source: Tertullian sets it in a chain of Isaiah promises about the renewed flesh, and the Apostolic Constitutions cite it as the inheritance of the righteous.4 On this reading the formula introduces Isaiah, rendered freely.
