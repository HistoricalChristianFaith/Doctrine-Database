Does the absence of any recorded canon dispute between Jesus and his Jewish opponents show a settled, shared canon?

Disputed reading · on the Old Testament canon · witness: the Gospel controversies

Related readings: The New Testament’s silence and allusions · The Jewish canon and the deuterocanon · A Pharisaic canon accepted by Jesus and Paul?

The passage

James White: “Nor do we find any evidence of disagreement on the extent of the canon between the Lord… or the Apostles and their Jewish opponents… in the gospel narrative.” Mike Winger: the Jews Jesus argued with “never say ‘Well, but that’s not even in the real Hebrew Bible, what are you talking about, Jesus?’ Nobody ever says this, even though all their debates are about this issue.”1

The question

Jesus and the Pharisees argue from a common Scripture (“have ye not read,” “it is written,” “the scripture cannot be broken”), and no recorded controversy turns on the standing of Tobit, Judith or Wisdom.2 What is disputed is what that silence shows: a closed, Hebrew-sized canon shared by both sides, or a time when the boundary had not yet been drawn.

The silence shows a shared, settled canon

Jesus rebuked his opponents’ errors freely; had they wrongly excluded Scripture he would have corrected them, and the absence of such a dispute shows that both sides held one canon.

Held by: Roger Beckwith (Anglican, 1985), as reported by Lee Martin McDonald;3 James White (Reformed Baptist apologist, 2004); Mike Winger (evangelical teacher).1

On Beckwith’s account, as McDonald reports it, the closure of the canon was so self-evident in the first century that it needed no statement.3 The Gospels record friction close to the question of Scripture: the Sadducees deny the resurrection and angels, and the Pharisees press “the tradition of the elders,” which Jesus answers by setting “the commandment of God” against “the tradition of men,” charging them with “making the word of God of none effect through your tradition.”2 In that setting no exchange runs “that book is not Scripture.” White asks: if the Jews had wrongly cast out these books, “would we not have Jesus correcting them?… Would we not have Jesus and the Apostles citing from these books as authoritative Scripture?”1 Jesus cites a common Scripture against his opponents with confidence that it binds them as it binds him. On this reading the Scripture over which Jesus and his opponents argued was a settled body that both recognized, the Hebrew Scriptures, and the apostolic church received that canon.

The silence fits an undrawn boundary as well as a drawn one

No closed Jewish canon existed for Jesus and the Pharisees to share; the Gospel debates concern other subjects, so their silence on the canon’s extent does not settle it.

Held by: Albert C. Sundberg Jr. (1964); Lee Martin McDonald (Bulletin for Biblical Research, 1996; The Biblical Canon, 2007); Timothy H. Lim (2013).4,3

Sundberg argued that in the first century only the Law and the Prophets were fixed and the Writings still fluid; Lim that “up to around 100 CE there was not one official ‘canon’ accepted by all Jews.”4 McDonald turns the argument: if the early church received a complete Old Testament canon, “it nowhere identified it,” and Beckwith’s supposition is “itself a huge argument from silence”; “the evidence simply is not there to make the jump backward to the first century to argue for a fully closed canon at that early date.”3 The recorded controversies concern the sabbath, divorce, hand-washing and corban, tribute to Caesar, the greatest commandment, the resurrection and Jesus’ own authority, not the extent of the Scriptures.2 Where a narrower scriptural authority does appear, the Sadducees’, Jesus answers from the Pentateuch, “in the book of Moses… in the bush,” rather than from Daniel 12:2; Origen and Jerome read this as reasoning from the ground the Sadducees granted, a reading most modern scholars (Schürer, Bruce, N. T. Wright) doubt.5 On this reading the Gospels’ silence leaves the canon question to the history of reception.

Lee Martin McDonald, The Biblical Canon: Its Origin, Transmission, and Authority (Peabody, MA: Hendrickson, 2007), reaches the same conclusion from the range of scriptural books at Qumran. The point: "no recorded canon dispute" cannot show a settled shared canon when the period's leading historians hold that no such settled canon yet existed for Jesus and the Pharisees to share. ↩ ↩
