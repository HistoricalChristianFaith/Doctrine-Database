When the Fathers called a book “not canonical, not for confirming doctrine,” did they deny it the standing of Scripture?

Disputed reading · on the Old Testament canon · witnesses: Rufinus, Jerome, Athanasius

Related readings: The “ecclesiastical” category · Doctrine and the undisputed books · What the Fathers meant by “canon”

The passage

Rufinus, Commentary on the Apostles’ Creed §38: books “our fathers call not ‘Canonical’ but ‘Ecclesiastical’… all of which they would have read in the Churches, but not appealed to for the confirmation of doctrine.” Jerome: the church reads Judith, Tobit and the Maccabees “for the strengthening of the people, (but) not for confirming the authority of ecclesiastical dogmas.”1

The question

The middle tier is named by a denial of doctrine-proving authority, and the medieval Gloss and commentators kept that axis.1 What is disputed is what the denial implies: that a book barred from establishing doctrine stands short of Scripture, or that “canonical” and “inspired” came apart for these Fathers, so that a book could be ranked below the doctrine-proving canon and still be Scripture. The reading that “not canonical” ranked the books in probative weight without denying them inspiration is stated here from a Catholic apologist; academic literature holding it was not located. The Orthodox name the middle class of books as their own category, the anaginōskomena, and give a reading of it.

A book barred from establishing doctrine stands short of Scripture

To call a book “not canonical” was a substantive judgement that it lacks the authority to establish doctrine; since that authority belongs to Scripture’s office, such a book is not Scripture’s peer.

Held by: John Calvin (Reformed, Acts of the Council of Trent, with the Antidote, 1547); James White (Reformed Baptist apologist, debate with Gary Michuta, 2004).2

Calvin calls the disputed books the “ecclesiastical books, which might indeed be read to the people, but were not entitled to establish doctrine.”2 On this reading the label is not a filing convenience: the middle rank is marked “not for confirming doctrine” by the very Fathers who coined it, Rufinus and Jerome, and Athanasius sets the read books against the “canonized” books that are the rule.1 Scripture, by Paul’s account, is God-breathed and therefore “profitable for doctrine, for reproof, for correction, for instruction in righteousness” (2 Timothy 3:16); the power to settle what the church must believe belongs near the centre of what makes a writing Scripture.3 A book solemnly withheld from that office has been ranked short of full Scriptural standing. On this reading the Fathers’ “not canonical” was a judgement on the books’ authority, and a book without authority to establish doctrine cannot function as Scripture’s equal.

The read books are part of the Bible on a lower footing: read in the Church, not counted in the canon

The Fathers’ “not canonical” left these books outside the counted canon but inside the Church’s Scripture, as books appointed to be read; they stand in the Bible at a lower rank.

Held by: the Longer Catechism of Philaret of Moscow (Orthodox, 1830); R. Stergiou (Orthodox priest, 1997); Kallistos Ware (Orthodox bishop, The Orthodox Church); Eugen J. Pentiuc (Orthodox, The Old Testament in Eastern Orthodox Tradition, 2014).4

Philaret’s catechism counts the Old Testament at twenty-two books with Cyril of Jerusalem, Athanasius and John of Damascus, “agreeing therein with the Jews”; Sirach “and certain others” are not in the count “Because they do not exist in the Hebrew,” and, in Athanasius’s words, “have been appointed of the Fathers to be read by proselytes who are preparing for admission into the Church.”4 Ware gives the standing of the books: Jassy (1642) and Jerusalem (1672) declared them “genuine parts of Scripture,” and “most Orthodox scholars at the present day, however, following the opinion of Athanasius and Jerome, consider that the Deutero-Canonical Books, although part of the Bible, stand on a lower footing than the rest of the Old Testament.”5 Stergiou describes the two ranks by their use: the Church “accepted the Alexandrian Canon (Septuagint LXX) as divinely inspired, appropriate for reading in Church, and on a personal reading level,” while “the shorter or Hebrew Canon remained as the Canon par excellence, and was most valuable for giving validity to basic Christian doctrines.”5 Pentiuc describes the current Orthodox position as strict about the thirty-nine books of the Jewish Bible and flexible about the anaginōskomena found in the Septuagint.5 On this reading Athanasius’s books “appointed… to be read” and Rufinus’s “ecclesiastical” books name a rank within the Bible: outside the count of the canon, below it in authority, and read in the Church as Scripture.

“Not canonical” ranked the books in probative weight; it did not deny them inspiration

“Canon” is a technical term for a measured list; the decisive question is whether a book is inspired, and the Fathers who ranked these books below the canon cited them as the Spirit’s word.

Held by: Gary Michuta (Catholic apologist, debate with White, 2004, and “Did Athanasius Reject the Deuterocanon?”).2

Michuta: “the canon is technical terminology… go by the scriptural definition: is it inspired?”2 The Fathers who file the books below the canon read them in worship and cite them as Scripture: Athanasius grounds his account of idolatry on Wisdom (“Scripture taught us beforehand”), calls Sirach 15:9 “the words spoken by the Spirit,” and sets Tobit 4:18 under “it is written”; Rufinus, who coined ecclesiastici, introduces Sirach with “ita enim Scriptura dicit.”6 They use the books for doctrine: Hippolytus reads Wisdom as “the prophecy of Solomon which speaketh of Christ,” Cyprian cites Tobit and Sirach as “Holy Scripture,” Basil argues from Judith 9:5–6 and Baruch 3:3–4 in On the Holy Spirit, and Jerome quotes Sirach as “Holy scripture says.”7 On this reading the Fathers ran a three-tier scheme in which the middle rank is read liturgically, cited as the Spirit’s words and drawn on for doctrine, but not made the first proof in a contested case, because it was later, disputed among the churches, or absent from the Jews’ reckoning. On this reading “not canonical” is a judgement of rank in argument, held together with the conviction that the books are inspired.
