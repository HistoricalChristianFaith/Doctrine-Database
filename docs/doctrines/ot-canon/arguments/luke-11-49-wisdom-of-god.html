Whom or what does “the wisdom of God” name in Luke 11:49?

Disputed reading · on the Old Testament canon · witnesses: Ambrose of Milan, Bede

Related readings: Ephesians 5:14 · 1 Corinthians 2:9 · John 7:38

The passage

"Therefore also said the wisdom of God, I will send them prophets and apostles, and some of them they shall slay and persecute."

— Luke 11:49 (KJV). The parallel at Matthew 23:34 has Jesus say in the first person, “Behold, I send unto you prophets, and wise men, and scribes.”1

The question

The words introduced by “said the wisdom of God” stand in no surviving Old Testament book. The verse is listed among New Testament citations without an exact Old Testament source, a group sometimes adduced for a wider apostolic “Scripture”; no named proponent of reading it as a quotation from a lost book was located.1 What is disputed is whom the formula names: Christ speaking as the Wisdom of God, or personified Wisdom speaking in a saying the Gospels preserve.

Christ speaks in his own person as the Wisdom of God

“The wisdom of God” is a title of the speaker; Jesus utters the saying himself, as Matthew’s “I send” shows.

Held by: Ambrose of Milan (Exposition of the Gospel of Luke 7.107); Bede (on Luke 11:49, in the Catena Aurea).2

Ambrose: “Therefore Wisdom sends apostles and prophets to them. Who is Wisdom if not Christ? Finally, in Matthew you have: Behold, I send you prophets and wise men.” Bede follows him: “He calls himself the wisdom of God, for he is indeed the power of God and the wisdom of God” (1 Corinthians 1:24), and adds that “in Matthew, you have it thus: Therefore behold, I send unto you prophets, and wise men, and scribes.”2 Bede draws a further consequence against the Marcionites: since the same Wisdom of God sent both the prophets and the apostles, there are not two Gods, one of the Law and another of the Gospel.2 On this reading the formula identifies the speaker rather than citing a written authority, and the Matthean parallel, which puts the same words in Jesus’ mouth as “I send,” shows how the evangelists understood the saying. Paul’s title for Christ, “the power of God, and the wisdom of God,” supplies the sense of the phrase within the apostolic writings themselves. On this reading Luke 11:49 is Jesus speaking as the divine Wisdom who sends prophets and apostles alike.

Personified Wisdom speaks in a saying of the sayings tradition

The saying stands in Matthew and Luke but not Mark; Luke preserves its attribution to Wisdom personified, as in Proverbs 8, and Matthew places it on Jesus’ lips.

Held by: Joseph A. Fitzmyer (Catholic, The Gospel According to Luke X–XXIV, Anchor Bible, 1985), ⚠ unverified, as cited on the upstream page; John S. Kloppenborg (The Formation of Q, 1987), ⚠ unverified, likewise.2

On this reading the saying belongs to the material Matthew and Luke share beyond Mark, and the wording “the Wisdom of God said” gives voice to personified Wisdom (Sophia), the figure who speaks in Proverbs 8 and in the Jewish wisdom tradition as the one who sends God’s messengers and is rejected by them.2 Luke’s form keeps the attribution; Matthew, by writing “I send,” identifies Wisdom with Jesus. Whether the words come from a lost wisdom writing or are a composition placed in Wisdom’s mouth is, on the summary cited, uncertain.2 The saying’s setting, the woes against those who build the tombs of the prophets, joins the sending of “prophets and apostles” to the blood of all the prophets “from the blood of Abel unto the blood of Zacharias” (Luke 11:50–51), the history of Wisdom’s envoys and their rejection. On this reading Luke 11:49 preserves an early Wisdom saying, which Matthew’s first person applies to Jesus.
