Was the shorter, Hieronymian canon the mainstream of the medieval Latin West before Trent?

Disputed reading · on the Old Testament canon · witnesses: Gregory the Great, the Glossa Ordinaria, Cajetan, the Council of Florence

Related readings: Was the deuterocanon held below Scripture until Trent? · How the early church received the deuterocanon · What the Fathers meant by “canon”

The passage

… surely they could not have been unaware of the long list of scholars down through the years who had rejected those books — some count as many as 52 major writers during that time period. Surely they knew that the Glossa Ordinaria introduced each book of the Apocrypha with the assertion that it was not a part of the canon.

— James White, in debate with Gary Michuta (2004).1

Bruce Metzger: “Subsequent to Jerome’s time and down to the period of the reformation a continuous succession of the more learned Fathers and theologians in the West maintained the distinctive and unique authority of the books of the Hebrew canon.”2

The question

A line of medieval Latin teachers repeated Jerome’s distinction, and the Glossa Ordinaria carried his prefaces; the same medieval church read the disputed books in worship, copied them in every Vulgate, and at Florence (1442) listed them.3,4 What is disputed is whether the Hieronymian current was the settled learning of the medieval West, making Trent’s longer canon the departure, or one of two currents in an unsettled church.

The shorter canon was the learned consensus of the medieval West

From Jerome to the eve of Trent the West’s most learned teachers maintained the Hebrew canon and set the disputed books outside it; Trent’s close in 1546 departed from that consensus.

Held by: John Cosin (Anglican, A Scholastical History of the Canon of the Holy Scripture, 1657); William Webster (Reformed, with David T. King, 2001); James White (Reformed Baptist apologist, 2004).2,1

Cosin tabulates ecclesiastical writers century by century as they distinguish the canonical from the apocryphal; Webster’s essay “From Jerome to the Reformation” compiles the medieval roll.2 It runs from Jerome, Rufinus and Gregory the Great, who calls 1 Maccabees a book “not… Canonical,” through Bede, Alcuin, Haymo of Halberstadt, Hugh and Richard of St. Victor, Peter Comestor, Hugh of St. Cher, Nicholas of Lyra, William of Ockham, Antoninus of Florence and Dionysius the Carthusian, to Cardinal Cajetan (1532) and Cardinal Ximénes’ Complutensian Polyglot (1514–17).3 The Glossa Ordinaria, the standard glossed Bible, introduces the books with “not in the canon” rubrics; White calls it “the standard commentary used in every single center of theological learning for hundreds of years.”1,5 Catholic reference works record the hesitation: the Catholic Encyclopedia (1913) finds “a current… distinctly unfavourable to their authority and sacredness,” names Thomas Aquinas among the hesitant, and says “Few are found to unequivocally acknowledge their canonicity”; the New Catholic Encyclopedia (1967) that the binding “decision was not given until rather late… (at the Council of Trent).”6 Webster’s roll names about two dozen writers; White, in the 2004 debate, puts the number higher: “some count as many as 52.”6 On this reading the Hebrew canon was the learned Western position until 1546, and Trent’s longer canon set that consensus aside.

Two currents in one church; the Hieronymian roll repeats Jerome’s preface

The medieval West held a friendly and an unfavourable current at once; the writers on the roll largely repeat Jerome’s preface while citing the books as Scripture, and the church read the books throughout.

Held by: the Catholic Encyclopedia (1913, “Canon of the Old Testament”);6 Gary Michuta (Catholic apologist, debate with White, 2004).7

The Catholic Encyclopedia: “In the Latin Church, all through the Middle Ages we find evidence of hesitation about the character of the deuterocanonicals. There is a current friendly to them, another one distinctly unfavourable,” and “there is no lack of evidence that during this long period the deuteros were read in the churches of Western Christendom.”6,4 Michuta answered White in the debate: “you have 50 major theological writers who denied the Deuterocanonical books. But what you find — and I’m the type who actually looks up all these 50 people — is that the vast majority of them will parrot Jerome’s words, but in their own writings, they affirm the inspiration of the books.”7 Jerome prefaces Sirach 22:6 with “Holy scripture says”; Gregory calls Wisdom what “the Truth says through His Scripture.”4 The Glossa is a twelfth-century compilation of the school of Anselm of Laon whose prologues reproduce Jerome’s prefaces, and the Catholic Encyclopedia describes it as having “embodied the prefaces” of Jerome and so “perpetuated and diffused his unfavourable view”; the long canon-prologue often quoted as the Glossa’s comes from a printed edition of c. 1498, as Webster notes.5 The Council of Florence enumerated the full canon in 1442.4 On this reading the medieval West held an unsettled division, transmitted largely through Jerome’s own words, which Trent closed.
