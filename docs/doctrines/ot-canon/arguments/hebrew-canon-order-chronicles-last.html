Did the Hebrew Bible of Jesus’ day end with Chronicles?

Disputed reading · on the Old Testament canon · witnesses: the Babylonian Talmud, the Masoretic Text, Innocent I

Related readings: Does “from Abel to Zechariah” mark the first and last books? · The Bava Batra 14b book-list · Which Zechariah does Jesus name?

The passage

The baraita at Bava Batra 14b gives the order of the Writings ending “… Daniel and the Scroll of Esther; and Ezra and Chronicles.”1 The Adath Deborim (1207): “the Hagiographa are divided into eleven sections: Chronicles, Psalms, Job … But the Babylonians have altered this order: some place Chronicles at the end of the volume, others place the Scroll of Esther at the end … we intend to begin with the first of these books, Chronicles, according to the order of the land of Israel; for this order is the right and true one.”2

The question

The “Abel to Zechariah” argument for a Hebrew canon assumes that the Bible Jesus’ hearers knew ran from Genesis to Chronicles. What is disputed is whether a Chronicles-last order was the established Jewish arrangement in the first century, or one arrangement among several that became standard much later.

Chronicles stood first in the Writings in the tradition of the land of Israel; Chronicles-last is a later Babylonian order

The order of the land of Israel put Chronicles at the head of the Writings; the Chronicles-last order is a Babylonian arrangement, and most ancient lists close with other books.

Held by: the Adath Deborim (1207);2 Edmon Gallagher (Protestant, Tyndale Bulletin, 2014).3

The Adath Deborim calls Chronicles-first “the right and true” order, “the order of the land of Israel,” and records that “the Babylonians have altered this order.” The Tiberian Masoretic codices agree: the Aleppo Codex (c. 930) and the Leningrad Codex (1008) open the Writings with Chronicles. The Chronicles-last order became common after 1207 and standard with print.2 Gallagher surveys where Chronicles stood and concludes that Chronicles-last is not securely the ancient order.3 The first-millennium book-lists close variously on Daniel, Ezra, Esther or the Maccabees: Melito, Origen, Cyril, Hilary, Athanasius, Amphilochius, Epiphanius, Gregory of Nazianzus, Jerome, Augustine, Carthage, the codices Claromontanus and Amiatinus, John of Damascus and the Stichometry of Nicephorus; Jerome and Isidore place Chronicles in the Hagiographa but not last.4 The two clear early Chronicles-last witnesses are Bava Batra 14b and Innocent I’s letter of 405, which ends “… two of Ezra, two of Chronicles” but lists Tobit, Judith and two of Maccabees earlier; the Talmud cites Sirach for the Writings at Bava Kamma 92b, and Epiphanius reports the Jews of his day holding Sirach and Wisdom as “of disputed canonicity.”5 On this reading no Genesis-to-Chronicles order is attested as dominant before the Middle Ages.6

Genesis to Chronicles was the traditional Jewish order known to Jesus’ hearers

The Talmud records a traditional Jewish arrangement ending with Chronicles, a tannaitic tradition that can preserve the order of the first century.

Held by: Roger Beckwith (Anglican, The Old Testament Canon of the New Testament Church, 1985); Doug Wilson (Reformed pastor, 2020).7

Beckwith rests his reading on “the traditional Jewish arrangement of the books (recorded in the Talmud)”: the saying of Jesus “in all probability implies that for Jesus and his hearers the canon began with Genesis and ended with Chronicles.”7 The baraita of Bava Batra 14b is tannaitic material, of the stratum of the Mishnah and capable of preserving older tradition, and it is the clearest ancient attestation of a Hebrew Bible ending in Chronicles; Beckwith frames the inference from it as a probability.1 The Bible it orders is the Hebrew collection, without the Greek books. The Babylonian order it records is the order that later became standard in printed Hebrew Bibles, and the medieval witness that calls Chronicles-first the order of the land of Israel is itself a millennium later than the baraita.2 The murder of Zechariah son of Jehoiada in the temple court stands at 2 Chronicles 24:20–22, near the close of that order.7 Wilson states the conclusion: the saying gives “the A to Z, the table of contents — Genesis to 2 Chronicles,” “the order … Jesus would have been familiar with.”7 On this reading the Talmud’s order is the traditional Jewish arrangement, and a span from Genesis to Chronicles ran from the first book of Jesus’ Bible to the last.
