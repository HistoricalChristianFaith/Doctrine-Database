Does Jude’s citation of 1 Enoch as prophecy (Jude 14–15) show that the Old Testament canon was open in the apostolic age?

Disputed reading · on the Old Testament canon · witness: Jude 14–15, with 4 Ezra

Related readings: Ephesians 5:14 · 1 Corinthians 2:9 · The threefold division

The passage

"And Enoch also, the seventh from Adam, prophesied of these, saying, Behold, the Lord cometh with ten thousands of his saints, To execute judgment upon all, and to convince all that are ungodly among them of all their ungodly deeds which they have ungodly committed, and of all their hard speeches which ungodly sinners have spoken against him."

— Jude 14–15 (KJV), quoting almost verbatim 1 Enoch 1:9.1

The question

Jude introduces words from 1 Enoch with “Enoch… prophesied… saying,” and draws at verse 9 on what is probably the lost ending of the Testament of Moses; Bauckham grants that “the introductory formula… indicates that Jude regarded this text as inspired prophecy.”2 What is disputed is what this shows about the canon: that the Old Testament’s edge was still open, or that a defined canon was flanked by other works some regarded as inspired.

An open boundary: Enoch stood among the inspired writings

An apostolic writer cites a widely valued book as prophecy; in the first century the Writings were not yet a closed category, and Enoch stood within the undefined body of Scripture.

Held by: Tertullian (On the Apparel of Women 1.3, c. 200), who holds Enoch should be received because Jude bears witness to it;2 Albert C. Sundberg Jr. (1964); John Barton (Oracles of God, 1986); Lee Martin McDonald and Timothy H. Lim.3

Tertullian argued that 1 Enoch ought to be received because the apostle Jude bears witness to it.2 Sundberg held that the early church inherited from Judaism not a closed Old Testament but a wider, undefined body of religious literature; Barton that the Torah was fixed early while “the Prophets” and the other writings remained an open category through the first century; McDonald and Lim that a bounded tripartite canon cannot be securely documented before the end of the first century.3 The source is a real, extant book, not a lost phrase, and Jude knows it closely: his verses 6 and 12–13 draw on it, and the citation stands at the centre of his letter. The formula is prophetic, unlike Paul’s illustrative quotations of Aratus, Epimenides and Menander.1 1 Enoch was among the most widely valued texts of Second-Temple Judaism: the Aramaic Book of the Watchers survives in several copies at Qumran, and the Ethiopian Orthodox Church receives 1 Enoch in its Old Testament.3 Jerome records that Jude was “rejected by many” for quoting Enoch, yet “by age and use it has gained authority and is reckoned among the Holy Scriptures.”2 On this reading Jude witnesses a first-century Scripture whose outer edge was still undrawn.

A closed canon with an inspired penumbra beside it

Citing a work as inspired is not reckoning it canonical; a defined canon coexisted with other writings valued as inspired, and Jude pairs Enoch with the prophecy of the apostles.

Held by: Roger Beckwith (Anglican, 1985); Richard Bauckham (Anglican, Jude and the Relatives of Jesus in the Early Church, 1990).4,5

Bauckham looks at how often such works are quoted as Scripture: at Qumran the pesharim comment only on canonical books, with 4QTestimonia and 4Q180 “the exceptions which prove the rule”; “of the hundreds of formal quotations from Scripture to be found in early Christian literature, the overwhelming majority are from books which belong to the Hebrew canon.” “If the canon really were still open… we should expect much more quotation from such works than we find.”5 4 Ezra 14 (c. AD 100) has Ezra write twenty-four books “made public… that the worthy and the unworthy may read them” and seventy kept “for the wise among your people”: a closed collection recognized by all Jews, and other works valued as inspired set beside it.4 Jude pairs the Enoch prophecy with the prophecy “of the apostles” (Jude 17–18): “That his quotation from Enoch is paired with this apostolic prophecy itself shows that there is no need to suppose that Jude included Enoch in the canon of Jewish Scriptures… Precisely what kind of authority it had by comparison with the canon we cannot tell; nor need he have done.”6 Hebrews 11:5 treats the figure of Enoch from Genesis alone.6 On this reading Jude witnesses an inspiration wider than the canon within a canon already defined.
