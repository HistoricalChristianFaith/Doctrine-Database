Was the rabbinic exclusion of the deuterocanon and the turn from the Septuagint a response to Christian use of them?

Disputed reading · on the Old Testament canon · witness: Akiva ben Joseph

Related readings: The rabbinic ban on Ben Sira · Jamnia · The Jewish canon and the deuterocanon

The passage

"To the same motive underlying his antagonism to the Apocrypha, namely, the desire to disarm Christians—especially Jewish Christians—who drew their 'proofs' from the Apocrypha, must also be attributed his wish to emancipate the Jews of the Dispersion from the domination of the Septuagint, the errors and inaccuracies in which frequently distorted the true meaning of Scripture, and were even used as arguments against the Jews by the Christians."

— Jewish Encyclopedia (1906), “Akiba ben Joseph.”1

The question

The Mishnah reports Akiva’s ruling that one who reads the “external books” has no share in the World-to-Come; the Tosefta rules that “the scrolls of Ben Sira and all scrolls that are written from that point forward” do not defile the hands; and Aquila, reported as Akiva’s disciple, produced a new Greek version for Greek-speaking Jews.2 What is disputed is the cause of the shorter Jewish canon and of the turn from the Septuagint: a reaction to Christian use of the Greek books, or the Pharisaic-rabbinic stream’s own criteria, with Christian controversy at most sharpening an existing boundary. No Orthodox holder of either reading was located in an openly accessible source.

The narrowing answered Christian use of the Greek books

Akiva’s opposition to the Apocrypha and to the Septuagint shared one motive: to disarm Christians, especially Jewish Christians, who drew their proofs from them.

Held by: Heinrich Graetz (Jewish historian, History of the Jews, and Kohelet, 1871); the Jewish Encyclopedia (1906);1 nineteenth-century Catholic exegetes (Nickes, Movers, Danko, Mullen), as reported by George Reid (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1908) — ⚠ unverified;3 Gary Michuta (Catholic apologist, 2004, 2007).4

The Jewish Encyclopedia, following Graetz, attributes Akiva’s “antagonism to the Apocrypha” and his “wish to emancipate the Jews of the Dispersion from the domination of the Septuagint” to “the desire to disarm Christians.”1 The timing places the boundary beside the break with Christianity: Akiva led the Yavneh generation and hailed Bar Kokhba as messiah, sharpening the rupture with Jewish Christians who confessed Jesus; he ruled that one who reads the “external books” “has no share in the World-to-Come”; the Tosefta rules that the gilyonim, read on this reading as a pun on euangelion, “and the scrolls of the heretics” do not defile the hands, and likewise “the scrolls of Ben Sira and all scrolls that are written from that point forward.”2 The synagogue dropped the Septuagint, which had become the church’s Bible, and adopted the Akiva-linked revision of Aquila.5 Michuta calls Akiva “the man who actually closed the Old Testament canon for rabbinical Judaism,” dates the exclusion of the deuterocanon and the New Testament to the Bar Kokhba years, and notes that “it is after Rabbi Akiva that we find a consistent argument made by Christians that the Jews had deleted texts from the books.”4 On this reading the shorter Jewish canon is a product of controversy with the church, and so not a neutral witness to an original table.

The rabbis applied their own criteria to a stream that never held the books

The Pharisaic-rabbinic stream measured Scripture by a Hebrew table that never included the deuterocanon; prophecy’s cessation and the Hebrew measure explain the outcome, and the rabbis were not closing a canon in the later sense.

Held by: Jack P. Lewis (Protestant, 1964), Albert C. Sundberg Jr. (1997) and Sid Z. Leiman (1976), on the Jamnia council;6,7 Jacob Neusner (Jewish scholar, 1987–88, ⚠ unverified), on canon in second-century rabbinic Judaism.6

Lewis, Sundberg and Leiman set aside the canon-closing “Council of Jamnia” (Graetz’s “Synod of Jamnia,” 1871); Sundberg writes that “Lewis’ attack brought to an end the appeal to Jamnia about 90 C.E. as the place and time when the Hebrew canon was closed”: the Yavneh debates concerned whether the Song of Songs and Ecclesiastes “defile the hands,” and the deuterocanon never came up.7 Neusner is reported as arguing that the notion of a biblical canon was not prominent in second-century rabbinic Judaism.6 A Hebrew-sized short list appears before the rupture: Josephus’s twenty-two “justly accredited” books (c. AD 95) and the twenty-four public books of 4 Ezra 14 (c. AD 100).8 The rabbis’ stated reasons are internal: prophecy ceased after Ezra and Malachi, and Scripture was measured in Hebrew.9 The same Jewish Encyclopedia article records that Akiva “has… no objection to the private reading of the Apocrypha, as is evident from the fact that he himself makes frequent use of Ecclesiasticus,” his ban falling on reading non-canonical books “as if they were canonical.”10 On this reading the rabbinic boundary confirmed the existing practice of the Pharisaic stream, and Christian controversy coloured its use, not its shape.
