Did the early church’s reception of the deuterocanon arise from unfamiliarity with Hebrew and the Hebrew canon?

Disputed reading · on the Old Testament canon · witnesses: Origen, Jerome, Rufinus, Augustine

Related readings: Augustine’s competence as a canon witness · Jerome’s grounds for a Hebrew canon · How the early church received the deuterocanon · The apostles’ Greek Bible

The passage

Evidently some in the early church, ignorant of the Old Testament canon and the backgrounds of the people of Israel assumed that since some or all of the Apocryphal books appeared in their Greek translations then they must be canonical. Those early writers who knew the most about the Old Testament did not make this mistake. Remember, very few of the early church writers knew the Hebrew language. There is a division/schism between Judaism and Christianity very early on … without knowing the Hebrew language, and without knowing the backgrounds that are so important to proper exegesis of biblical texts, it is easy to see how that kind of ignorance could take place.

— James White, in debate with Gary Michuta (2004).1

There has been from the first in the churches of God, and especially in that of Jerusalem, a plentiful supply of men who being born Jews have become Christians; and their perfect acquaintance with both languages and their sufficient knowledge of the law is shewn by their administration of the pontifical office. In all this abundance of learned men, has there been one who has dared to make havoc of the divine record handed down to the Churches by the Apostles and the deposit of the Holy Spirit? … the whole of the history of Susanna, which gave a lesson of chastity to the churches of God, has by him been cut out, thrown aside and dismissed. The hymn of the three children, which is regularly sung on festivals in the Church of God, he has wholly erased from the place where it stood.

— Rufinus of Aquileia, Apology against Jerome II.33 (c. 401).2

The question

The Greek-speaking church read the Septuagint, its codices carried the disputed books among the others, and few Fathers read Hebrew.3 What is disputed is whether these facts explain the wider canon as a consequence of not knowing the Hebrew text and the Jewish list, or whether the Fathers who received the books did so by a considered judgment while knowing the narrower list. The reading that ties reception to unfamiliarity with Hebrew is set out here from popular apologetic sources; academic literature holding it was not located.

The books were received by a considered judgment of churches that knew the Hebrew question

The Fathers who kept the disputed books knew the Hebrew list or had bilingual members to consult, and received the books on the ground of the churches’ use.

Held by: Rufinus of Aquileia (Latin, Apology against Jerome, c. 401);2 Gary Michuta (Catholic apologist, in debate with White, 2004).4

Rufinus answered Jerome’s Hebrew-based revision by pointing to the bilingual church: there had been “from the first in the churches of God, and especially in that of Jerusalem, a plentiful supply of men who being born Jews have become Christians,” with “perfect acquaintance with both languages,” and none of them had removed Susanna or the hymn of the three children from the text the churches read.2 The record of the most Hebrew-competent Fathers is read the same way. Origen, who built the Hexapla to map the Greek against the Hebrew and counted the Hebrew books as twenty-two, defended the Greek Susanna against Julius Africanus and cited Tobit, Sirach, Baruch, Wisdom, Judith and the Maccabees as Scripture.5 Jerome, who ruled the books “not in the canon,” translated Tobit and Judith and quoted Sirach as “Holy scripture says.”6 Greek-speaking Fathers without Hebrew, such as Melito and Gregory of Nazianzus, reached a Hebrew-sized list by inquiry into Jewish usage,7 and Athanasius, Cyril of Jerusalem, Epiphanius and Rufinus recite the narrower list and still keep some of the books, in a tier read in the churches.8 Augustine fixed his list by an explicit criterion, the books “received by all the catholic churches,” while corresponding with Jerome on the Hebrew and the Greek.9 Hippolytus, Lactantius and Irenaeus use the books as prophecy of Christ.10 Michuta pressed the evidence for a bilingual Palestine (Goodenough); Hengel puts the Greek-mother-tongue share of Jerusalem’s population “at around 10-20% as a minimum.”4 On this reading the wider canon is the churches’ considered reception of books they read in worship.

The books were taken as Scripture by Greek readers who did not know the Hebrew canon

Christians who read only the Greek Bible met the disputed books in it and took them for Scripture, while those who knew the Hebrew and the Jewish list did not.

Held by: James White (Reformed Baptist apologist, debate with Michuta, 2004);1 Mike Winger (evangelical teacher, How we got the OT Canon).11

White traces the wider canon to the separation of church and synagogue: “some in the early church, ignorant of the Old Testament canon and the backgrounds of the people of Israel assumed that since some or all of the Apocryphal books appeared in their Greek translations then they must be canonical. Those early writers who knew the most about the Old Testament did not make this mistake.” Few early writers read Hebrew, and “without knowing the Hebrew language, and without knowing the backgrounds that are so important to proper exegesis of biblical texts, it is easy to see how that kind of ignorance could take place.”1 Winger states the correlation: “the least educated about Hebrew and about the original languages, the least educated of the Fathers were the ones who endorsed the Apocrypha, because they just assumed that the Jews did … And the ones that were educated and knew the languages, they were the ones that rejected it.”11 On this reading the Septuagint codices, which carried the disputed books with no mark of distinction, were the working Bible of a church that read Greek, and a reader without Hebrew had no means within the codex to tell the books of the Hebrew canon from the rest.3 The Fathers who knew the Hebrew list best drew the line: Jerome, the one Latin Hebraist, ruled the books “not in the canon,” and Origen counted “the canonical books, as the Hebrews have handed them down” as twenty-two.5 White grants that first-century Judaea was bilingual and locates the division elsewhere: “the division was not a linguistic division … It was, sadly, an interpersonal division.”4 On this reading the separation of church and synagogue left Greek-reading Christians to take the contents of their codices as Scripture.
