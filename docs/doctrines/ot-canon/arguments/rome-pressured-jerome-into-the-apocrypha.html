How did the deuterocanonical books come to stand in the Latin Vulgate?

Disputed reading · on the Old Testament canon · witness: Jerome

Related readings: How the early church received the deuterocanon · Did Trent add the books? · The medieval reception

The passage

However, under tremendous pressure from Rome, Jerome, the translator of the Latin Vulgate, included the Apocrypha, despite Jerome's insistence that the Apocrypha did not belong in the Bible. The Latin Vulgate became the dominant and officially sanctioned Catholic Bible, and remained that way for around 1200 years. Thus, the Apocrypha became a part of the Catholic Bible.

— GotQuestions, “What is the Catholic Bible?”1

Jerome, Prologue to Tobit: Tobit is one “which the Hebrews exclude from the catalogue of Divine Scriptures… to translate this for the ears of Latins contrary to their canon. But it is better to be judging the opinion of the Pharisees to displease and to be subject to the commands of bishops.”2

The question

Jerome translated Tobit and Judith against his own canon judgement at others’ urging, and the Vulgate that bears his name carried the deuterocanon as the Western Bible for some twelve centuries.2,3 What is disputed is how the books came to be there: through pressure on Jerome from Rome, so that the Vulgate brought them into the Catholic Bible, or through a request from other bishops for two books, with the rest inherited from the Old Latin Bible the church already read. The reading that Rome pressured Jerome is stated here from a popular apologetic source; academic literature holding it was not located.

Two books translated at bishops’ request; the rest inherited from the Old Latin

Jerome rendered Tobit and Judith for two north-Italian bishops; the other disputed books entered the Vulgate from the older Latin Bible, which already carried them.

Held by: Jerome (Prologues to Tobit and Judith, c. 405), on the request.2,4

Jerome’s prologues address Chromatius of Aquileia and Heliodorus of Altinum, regional bishops of northern Italy, and he wrote from Bethlehem, where he had lived since 386; he renders Judith “because… you request” it.2,4 The Roman commission he did receive, from Damasus I c. 382, was to revise the Old Latin Gospels and Psalter.4 Of the disputed books he rendered only Tobit and Judith, from the Aramaic, with the Greek additions to Esther and Daniel; Wisdom, Sirach, 1–2 Maccabees and Baruch “were not revised by Jerome, but taken from the Old Latin,” itself made from the Septuagint, and bound into the later Vulgate by other hands; of Baruch he wrote that, “neither read nor found among the Hebrews, we have omitted” it.5 The books stood in the Septuagint and the great uncials, and were named among the Scriptures by Hippo and Carthage (393–419), Innocent I (405), Augustine and Florence (1442).6 On this reading the Vulgate inherited a deuterocanon the Latin church already read, with Jerome’s own prologues ranking it below the canon travelling alongside.

Rome pressed Jerome to include the books, and the Vulgate made them Catholic Scripture

Jerome held that the Apocrypha did not belong in the Bible; under pressure he included it, and through the Vulgate the books became part of the Catholic Bible.

Held by: GotQuestions (evangelical question-and-answer ministry).1

GotQuestions: “under tremendous pressure from Rome, Jerome, the translator of the Latin Vulgate, included the Apocrypha, despite Jerome’s insistence that the Apocrypha did not belong in the Bible. The Latin Vulgate became the dominant and officially sanctioned Catholic Bible, and remained that way for around 1200 years. Thus, the Apocrypha became a part of the Catholic Bible.”1 Jerome’s own words record the strain under which he worked: Tobit is a book “which the Hebrews exclude from the catalogue of Divine Scriptures,” translated “contrary to their canon” in order “to be subject to the commands of bishops.”2 His prefaces set the disputed books “among the apocrypha.”7 The Vulgate became the Western church’s Bible for a millennium and carried the books.3 GotQuestions adds that the books were “not formally/officially made a part of the Catholic Bible… until the Council of Trent.”1,8 On this reading the books’ place in Catholic Bibles goes back to their inclusion in the Vulgate against the judgement of the translator, ratified at Trent.
