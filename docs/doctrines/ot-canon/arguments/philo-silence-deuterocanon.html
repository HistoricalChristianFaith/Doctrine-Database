Does Philo’s never quoting the deuterocanon show that Alexandrian Jews held a Hebrew-only canon?

Disputed reading · on the Old Testament canon · witness: Philo of Alexandria

Related readings: Josephus’s twenty-two books · The Jewish canon and the deuterocanon · The apostles’ Greek Bible

The passage

Philo of Alexandria … (20 B.C.–A.D. 40), quoted the Old Testament prolifically from virtually every canonical book. However, he never once quoted from the Apocrypha as inspired.

— Norman L. Geisler, “Apocrypha, Old and New Testaments,” Baker Encyclopedia of Christian Apologetics (1999).1

The question

Philo (c. 20 BC–c. AD 50), the leading Greek-writing Jewish exegete of Alexandria, never cites Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch or the Maccabees as Scripture. What is disputed is what the silence shows: that the Greek-speaking Jews of Alexandria measured Scripture by the Hebrew table, or nothing about the canon’s extent, given the shape of his writings.

The silence shows a Hebrew-measured Scripture among Greek-speaking Jews

The most learned Greek-writing Jew of the apostolic age, steeped in the Septuagint, never treats a deuterocanonical book as a divine oracle; Alexandrian Jewry held the Hebrew canon.

Held by: F. F. Bruce (Brethren, 1954); Norman L. Geisler (evangelical, 1999); John Piper (Baptist pastor); CARM.2,1

Bruce: “Even Jews who wrote in Greek at the beginning of our era, like Philo and Josephus, recognized only the Canon of the Hebrew Bible, although they used the ‘Septuagint’”; Piper, that they “used the LXX but did not accord the Apocrypha authority.”2 Philo describes the Therapeutae taking into their holy place “the laws and the sacred oracles of God enunciated by the holy prophets, and hymns, and psalms,” a description read with the prologue to Sirach, Luke 24:44 and Josephus as an early witness to the tripartite Hebrew Scriptures.3 Philo’s silence is also evidence against a wider “Alexandrian canon” including the deuterocanon, the hypothesis Sundberg set aside; its most prolific Alexandrian witness shows no such Bible.4 The Wisdom of Solomon was composed in Philo’s own city around his lifetime, yet he does not cite it as Scripture.5 On this reading Philo stands beside Josephus as a Greek-speaking Jew who knew the Septuagint and gave Scriptural authority only to the books of the Hebrew canon.

The silence reflects a Torah commentary and renders no verdict

Philo’s works are commentary on the Pentateuch; he quotes the rest of the Old Testament only a few dozen times, and leaves seven protocanonical books unquoted.

Held by: Herbert Edward Ryle (Anglican, Philo and Holy Scripture, 1895), on the quotation pattern;6 the Jewish Encyclopedia (“Bible Canon,” 1902–06);7 Gary Michuta (Catholic apologist, The Case for the Deuterocanon, 2015).4

Ryle prints Philo’s quotations and shows that “the great mass of Philo’s quotations … were drawn from the Pentateuch,” usually cited as a unit, “Holy Scripture,” rather than by book.6 The Jewish Encyclopedia: “Philo, in his extant works, makes no mention of Ezekiel, Daniel, or the Five Rolls … his failure to name the other books cannot be taken as a proof that they were not in his canon,” the silence being “undoubtedly accidental.”7 Michuta argues that the “2,050” quotations are overwhelmingly Pentateuchal, and that the non-Pentateuchal remainder does not cover the rest of the Hebrew canon either.4 The editors of the Philo of Alexandria Scripture Index (2019) retain possible allusions to Wisdom and Sirach while noting they “were not always convinced that Philo alluded to them intentionally.”5 Philo gives no list of books and no ruling on any book’s standing. On this reading Philo’s silence about the deuterocanon is of a piece with his silence about Ruth, Esther, Ecclesiastes, the Song of Songs, Lamentations, Ezekiel and Daniel, and leaves the extent of his Scripture unstated.
