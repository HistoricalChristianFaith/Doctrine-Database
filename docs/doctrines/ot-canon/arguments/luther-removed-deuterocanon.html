Did Martin Luther remove the deuterocanonical books from the Bible, or rank and relocate them?

Disputed reading · on the Old Testament canon · witness: Martin Luther

Related readings: Luther and 2 Maccabees · Luther and the later removal · Did Trent add the books?

The passage

The deuterocanonicals teach Catholic doctrine, and for this reason they were taken out of the Old Testament by Martin Luther and placed in an appendix without page numbers.

— Jimmy Akin, “Defending the Deuterocanonicals.”1

Catholic apologists sometimes claim that Martin Luther removed the deuterocanonical books from Scripture. This is not entirely true. Luther's German translation of the Scriptures included the deuterocanon.

— Gary Michuta, Why Catholic Bibles Are Bigger.2

The question

Luther denied the disputed books canonical, doctrine-establishing status, and in his 1534 Bible gathered them into a separate section between the Testaments.3,4 What is disputed is how to describe that act: as taking the books out of the Old Testament, or as ranking them below the canonical books while keeping them in his Bible. The reading that Luther took the books out of the Old Testament is stated here from a Catholic apologist; academic literature holding it was not located.

Luther ranked and relocated the books, and kept them in his Bible

The 1534 Luther Bible contains the books, printed, prefaced and commended “to read”; they were removed from most Protestant Bibles in the nineteenth century.

Held by: Bruce M. Metzger (Protestant, 1957);4 Gary Michuta (Catholic apologist, Why Catholic Bibles Are Bigger, 2017), who grants that Luther’s Bible included the books and holds that Luther began the process that ended in their removal (see the paved-the-way page).2

Metzger: Luther “gathered together all but two of the books of the Apocrypha… and printed them between the Old and New Testaments,” under the heading “Apocrypha — that is, books which are not held equal to the Holy Scriptures, and yet are profitable and good to read”; the layout became the model for the Swedish (1541) and Danish (1550) Bibles.4 Michuta writes that “Luther’s German translation of the Scriptures included the deuterocanon,” and reports that Protestant Bibles carried the Apocrypha as the rule from c. 1526 into the seventeenth century.2 Every King James Bible before 1666 carried the Apocrypha, and in 1615 Archbishop George Abbot forbade printing an English Bible without it.5 The books left most Protestant Bibles after the British and Foreign Bible Society’s resolution of 1826 to print “the Canonical Books of Scripture, to the exclusion of those Books and parts of Books usually termed Apocryphal,” the American Bible Society following in 1827.6 On this reading Luther changed the books’ rank and place in the printed Bible, and their removal belongs to 1826.

Luther took the books out of the Old Testament

Luther took the deuterocanonical books out of the Old Testament where they had stood, denied their authority, and placed them in an appendix because they taught doctrines he rejected.

Held by: Jimmy Akin (Catholic apologist, “Defending the Deuterocanonicals”).1

Akin: “The deuterocanonicals teach Catholic doctrine, and for this reason they were taken out of the Old Testament by Martin Luther and placed in an appendix without page numbers.”1 Before 1534 the disputed books stood interleaved among the Old Testament books of the Vulgate; Luther lifted Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch and 1–2 Maccabees out of those places, dropped 1–2 Esdras altogether, and prefaced Judith and Tobit as edifying sacred drama and comedy rather than history.4 He denied the books doctrinal authority: of 2 Maccabees 12, pressed by Eck at Leipzig in 1519, he wrote in 1521 that “that book is not among the books of Holy Scripture, and, as St. Jerome says, it is not found in a Hebrew version… this book deserves little authority.”3 Akin ties the act to doctrine:

Why would Martin Luther cut out this book when it is so clearly held up as an example to us by the New Testament? Simple: A few chapters later it endorses the practice of praying for the dead… Since Luther chose to reject the historic Christian teaching of purgatory… he had to remove that book from the Bible and appendicize it.

1 On this reading the books ceased to be part of the Old Testament in Luther’s Bible: set outside it, between the Testaments, under a heading that denied them equality with Scripture.

The "Apocrypha controversy" of the 1820s and the British and Foreign Bible Society's resolution of 1826 — "That the funds of the Society be applied to the printing and circulation of the Canonical Books of Scripture, to the exclusion of those Books and parts of Books usually termed Apocryphal" — are the actual occasion on which the deuterocanon left most Protestant Bibles, some three centuries after Luther. The campaign was led from Scotland by Robert Haldane and William Thorp (from 1821) and escalated by Andrew Mitchell Thomson of Edinburgh in 1825–26; the compromise broke down in the spring of 1826, the resolution carried, and the Edinburgh and Glasgow auxiliaries seceded. Stated motives ran from Reformed-Puritan conviction that the books were uninspired to the plain economy of not printing them. The American Bible Society followed in 1827, ruling that no Bible from its depository should contain the Apocrypha. Jimmy Akin's own account (above) gestures at this mechanism but garbles the particulars — he dates the drop to "1827 by the British and Foreign Bible Society," whereas the BFBS act was 1826 and 1827 belongs to the American society.
