Do the canon lists of Hippo and Carthage, Second Nicaea and Trent contradict one another?

Disputed reading · on the Old Testament canon · witnesses: the African councils, the Council in Trullo, the Council of Trent

Related readings: Does Trent’s list differ from Hippo and Carthage’s? · Was Trent the first universal, binding definition?

The passage

Given that Pope Innocent I and Pope Gelasius both affirmed the canon list of Hippo and Carthage, yet Trent and every pope since then has affirmed a different list that excludes the extra material in Esdras, which popes were right and which popes were wrong on this issue?

— James White, cross-examining Gary Michuta (2004).1

Trent, Session IV (1546): “But if any one receive not, as sacred and canonical, the said books entire with all their parts… let him be anathema.”2

The question

The African councils’ “two books of Esdras” are taken by Bogaert, and by Gallagher and Meade, to include the Greek 1 Esdras (Esdras A, Latin 3 Esdras), which Trent’s list does not name; the two lists are not identical.3 What is disputed is whether the difference amounts to a contradiction between councils, so that Trent’s anathema or a council received as ecumenical stands against another, or whether the lists can be read together. The contradiction reading is stated here from popular apologetic sources; academic literature holding it was not located. The reading that the councils can be read together is likewise stated from Catholic apologetic sources; academic literature holding it was not located. Orthodox writers give a reading of their own, from their reception of these councils.

The councils ratified differing lists, which the Orthodox hold together as a core and books read

The councils’ lists differ, and the Orthodox Church has held them together: a shorter canon used to ground doctrine, and the further Septuagint books received and read in the Church, with the exact boundary left open.

Held by: R. Stergiou (Orthodox priest, “The Old Testament in the Orthodox Church,” 1997); Kallistos Ware (Orthodox bishop, The Orthodox Church); Eugen J. Pentiuc (Orthodox, The Old Testament in Eastern Orthodox Tradition, 2014).4

Stergiou names the differences openly: there have been “inconsistencies not only by the Church Fathers, but also by many local and even Ecumenical Synods as to which Canon is to be used,” Cyril of Jerusalem and Athanasius supporting the Hebrew canon and John Chrysostom and Basil the Alexandrian. The Orthodox Church, he writes, “accepted the Alexandrian Canon (Septuagint LXX) as divinely inspired, appropriate for reading in Church, and on a personal reading level,” while “the shorter or Hebrew Canon remained as the Canon par excellence, and was most valuable for giving validity to basic Christian doctrines”; and the traditions still differ on the wider canon’s contents, the Slavonic Bible including a book the Greek does not.4 Ware’s list of the ten further Septuagint books “known in the Orthodox Church as the ‘Deutero-Canonical Books’” begins with “3 Esdras,” the Latin numbering of the Esdras book at issue here; Jassy (1642) and Jerusalem (1672) declared these books “genuine parts of Scripture,” and “most Orthodox scholars at the present day” hold them “part of the Bible” on “a lower footing than the rest of the Old Testament.”5 Pentiuc describes the current position as strict about the thirty-nine books of the Jewish Bible and flexible about the anaginōskomena.4 On this reading the councils’ differing lists, ratified together in Trullo’s second canon, describe a core and a wider body of books read in the Church, and the Esdras book belongs to that wider body as the Orthodox receive it.

The councils contradict each other

An earlier set of councils, ratified by popes and by Second Nicaea, received 1 Esdras; Trent’s list leaves it out under anathema; both cannot be right.

Held by: James White (Reformed Baptist apologist, 2004); Mike Winger (evangelical teacher); Steve Christie (Why Protestant Bibles Are Smaller, 2019), as reported by Dave Armstrong.1,6,7

On this reading the councils that Catholics cite for the antiquity of their canon did not all receive the same books. Innocent I and Gelasius affirmed the list of Hippo and Carthage, whose two books of Esdras included the Greek 1 Esdras, a book Augustine cites for its contest of the three guardsmen as possible prophecy of Christ; Trent and the popes since it affirm a list without it.1,3 White frames the dilemma: “which popes were right and which popes were wrong on this issue?”1 Winger draws the consequence from the decree: since Trent’s list “disagrees with Hippo and Carthage,” one “can’t pretend that one supports the other.”6 Christie presses it against conciliar infallibility: 1 Esdras was “recognized at 2 Nicaea in 787,” which ratified the African canons, but not at Florence or Trent, so the Catholic must say which of these “mutually contradicting infallible councils” to believe.7 On this reading the claim that the councils from Hippo to Trent attest one unchanging canon cannot be sustained, and the Tridentine list is one reckoning among several.

The councils can be read together

The earlier councils did not rule on the exact Esdras unit and Trent did not deny it; Trent’s anathema falls on removing listed books, and Nicaea II’s ratification was general.

Held by: Gary Michuta (Catholic apologist, debate with White, 2004);8 Dave Armstrong (Catholic apologist, 2020);7 Catholic Answers.9

Michuta replies that the African councils, like Trent, “passed over in silence; they didn’t pass judgment” on the precise Esdras enumeration: under-specification on both sides, not opposed verdicts.8 Trent had the wider Latin appendix (3 and 4 Esdras, the Prayer of Manasseh) before it and left it unaddressed, with only a small minority voting to reject those books explicitly; the Clementine Vulgate of 1592 printed them in an appendix “lest they utterly perish.”8 Trent’s anathema falls on whoever does “not receive” a listed book; Hippo and Carthage received every book on Trent’s list, so the anathema does not touch them.2 Second Nicaea received the Council in Trullo, whose second canon ratified at once the lists of the Apostolic Canons, Laodicea, Carthage, Athanasius and Amphilochius; Henry Percival (Anglican, NPNF 14, 1900) concluded that “the approval given was not specific but general,” a note William Webster quotes against the Catholic appeal to Nicaea II.10 Armstrong holds the canons harmonizable and writes that “the canonical lists were not infallible in the highest sense until Trent”; Catholic Answers holds that Trent “infallibly reiterated what the Church had long taught” and reads Rome, Hippo, Carthage and Florence as affirming the list Trent reaffirmed.7,9 On this reading a later ecumenical council that names a core and leaves a boundary book unaddressed narrows an earlier, looser reckoning, and the difference over Esdras A is a difference between communions, the Eastern churches receiving it.9
