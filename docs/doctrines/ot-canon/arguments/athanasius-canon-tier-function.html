How do the three tiers of Athanasius’s Festal Letter 39 (367) bear on the standing of the deuterocanon?

Disputed reading · on the Old Testament canon · witness: Athanasius of Alexandria

Related readings: The “ecclesiastical” category · How the early church received the deuterocanon · What the Fathers meant by “canon”

The passage

But for greater exactness I add this also, writing of necessity; that there are other books besides these not indeed included in the Canon, but appointed by the Fathers to be read by those who newly join us, and who wish for instruction in the word of godliness. The Wisdom of Solomon, and the Wisdom of Sirach, and Esther, and Judith, and Tobit, and that which is called the Teaching of the Apostles, and the Shepherd. But the former, my brethren, are included in the Canon, the latter being [merely] read; nor is there in any place a mention of apocryphal writings. But they are an invention of heretics, who write them when they choose, bestowing upon them their approbation, and assigning to them a date, that so, using them as ancient writings, they may find occasion to lead astray the simple.

— Athanasius, Festal Letter 39 §7 (367).1

The question

The letter counts twenty-two canonical books of the Old Testament, without Tobit, Judith, Sirach, Wisdom or the Maccabees, and keeps Baruch and the Epistle of Jeremiah within Jeremiah; it sets Wisdom, Sirach, Esther, Judith and Tobit in a second rank “read” for instruction; and it rejects a third class of “apocryphal” writings.1 Elsewhere Athanasius quotes Wisdom, Sirach and Tobit in his own voice under Scripture formulas.2 What is disputed is what the second rank signifies for the deuterocanon: a judgement that these books are outside Scripture, a narrower sense of “canon” that leaves them Scripture read for formation, or a class of books read in the Church below a fixed core.

An early witness for a Hebrew-sized Old Testament without the deuterocanon

Athanasius fixes the Old Testament at the Hebrew twenty-two and expressly sets the freestanding disputed books outside the canon.

Held by: Archibald Alexander (Presbyterian, The Canon of the Old and New Testaments Ascertained, 1851); Roger Beckwith (Anglican, 1985), for the wider thesis; James White (Reformed Baptist apologist, 2004); Gavin Ortlund (Baptist theologian, 2023).3

The letter’s catalogue begins from “twenty-two books… the number of the letters among the Hebrews,” the count given by Origen and Cyril of Jerusalem, and names a list with no Tobit, Judith, Sirach, Wisdom or Maccabees; the disputed books are then named and placed “not… in the Canon.”4 White: Athanasius “rejected all the freestanding apocryphal books, only accepting the Greek additions to the canonical books,” such as Baruch within Jeremiah and Susanna within Daniel. Ortlund places him in an Eastern “22-book Hebrew canon” tradition, with Cyril, Gregory of Nazianzus and John of Damascus, under which, with “little exceptions,” “the vast majority of the Deuterocanonical books are excluded.” Steve Christie: Athanasius “affirmed… all the books of the Hebrew Bible except for Esther, while rejecting all of the Deuterocanonical books except for Baruch.”3 Alexander concluded that “Athanasius takes no notice of these books,” quoting the Synopsis Scripturae Sacrae under Athanasius’s name; the Synopsis is placed on this timeline as a separate witness of c. 550.3,5 The letter’s own words close the canon: “Let no man add to these, neither let him take ought from these.”6 On this reading the most influential fourth-century Alexandrian list stands with the Hebrew-sized lists of Melito, Origen, Cyril, Epiphanius and Jerome.

Excluded from the “canon” list but read and cited as Scripture

Athanasius’s “canon” is a measured category narrower than “inspired Scripture”; the middle tier is books for catechesis, which he himself cites as Scripture.

Held by: George Reid (Catholic, “Canon of the Old Testament,” Catholic Encyclopedia, 1908); Gary Michuta (Catholic apologist, Why Catholic Bibles Are Bigger, 2007, and “Did Athanasius Reject the Deuterocanon?”).7

Reid holds that Athanasius “practically admitted the deutero books to a Scriptural dignity, as is evident from his general usage.”7

The letter has three tiers, not two, and keeps the “read” books apart from the apocrypha it calls “an invention of heretics.”8 Athanasius cites the read books in his own voice: Wisdom 14 as “Scripture taught us beforehand long ago,” Wisdom 9:2 with “it is written” among texts “divine Scripture recognises,” Sirach 15:9 as “the words spoken by the Spirit,” and Tobit 4:18 under “it is written,” in works addressed to bishops and an emperor.2 On this reading the line between the tiers is one of function. The read tier is defined in the letter by its use, for “those who newly join us,” and Origen had described the same practice: Esther, Judith, Tobit and the precepts of Wisdom are the “milk” beginners “gladly receive”:9

"…the food some have in the Word of God is milk, that is, the clearer and simpler doctrine… And so, when some such reading from the divine books is recited, in which there seems to be nothing obscure, they gladly receive it, for example, the brief books of Esther, Judith, or even Tobit, or the precepts of the book of Wisdom. But if the book of Leviticus is read to him his mind immediately stumbles…"

The canon tier is grounded in §6 on the books by which the Lord “put to shame the Sadducees” and “reproved the Jews,” and it tracks the books the Jews received, down to the demotion of Esther, whose standing was disputed among Jews (Melito omits it; b. Megillah 7a reports Samuel holding that Esther does not “defile the hands”).10 On Michuta’s reading the exclusion is from a list, not from Scripture: the “read” books are Scripture set apart for the instruction of those who newly join the Church.11

A fixed core with a further class of books read in the Church

The Orthodox position, traced from the early writers to John of Damascus, is strict about the books of the Jewish Bible and flexible about the anaginōskomena, the books “read.”

Held by: Eugen J. Pentiuc (Orthodox, The Old Testament in Eastern Orthodox Tradition, 2014); Kallistos Ware (Orthodox bishop, The Orthodox Church).12

Pentiuc’s chapter on the canon traces the Eastern Orthodox position from Ecclesiasticus through the early Christian writers to the synthesis of John of Damascus, and finds that “the current Orthodox position on canon still looks very similar to the Damascene’s concluding remarks”: strict regarding the thirty-nine books of the Jewish Bible, and flexible regarding the anaginōskomena found in the Septuagint.12 Kallistos Ware reports that Jassy (1642) and Jerusalem (1672) declared these books “genuine parts of Scripture,” and that “most Orthodox scholars at the present day, however, following the opinion of Athanasius and Jerome, consider that the Deutero-Canonical Books, although part of the Bible, stand on a lower footing than the rest of the Old Testament.”12 Athanasius’s middle tier, “appointed by the Fathers to be read,” is the class those later Orthodox terms name: neither added to the canon nor set among the apocrypha, but read in the Church.1 His canon keeps Baruch and the Epistle of Jeremiah within Jeremiah and places Esther among the read books, which fits neither a Hebrew list of thirty-nine nor a later Western list of forty-six.13 On this reading the letter records a fixed core and an open, received class of books that the Church reads.
