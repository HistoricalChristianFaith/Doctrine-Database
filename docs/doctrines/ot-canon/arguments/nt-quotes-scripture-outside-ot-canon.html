What is the source of the words Ephesians 5:14 introduces with “Wherefore he saith”?

Disputed reading · on the Old Testament canon · witnesses: Hippolytus, Epiphanius of Salamis, Jerome

Related readings: 1 Corinthians 2:9 · John 7:38 · Luke 11:49 · Jude and 1 Enoch

The passage

"Wherefore he saith, Awake thou that sleepest, and arise from the dead, and Christ shall give thee light."

— Ephesians 5:14 (KJV). The formula dió légei heads an Old Testament citation at Ephesians 4:8 (Psalm 68:18); the words that follow here match no Old Testament passage.1

"For my part, scanty as my knowledge is, I have nowhere found this written after diligently scouring all the editions of the ancient Scriptures and the texts of the Hebrews themselves."

— Jerome, Commentary on Ephesians, on 5:14.2

The question

Ephesians 5:14 heads a small group of New Testament citations with no exact Old Testament source (with 1 Corinthians 2:9, John 7:38 and Luke 11:49), which has been adduced for an apostolic “Scripture” reaching beyond the Old Testament.1 What is disputed is where these words come from: a free rendering of Isaiah, a writing outside the Old Testament, or an early Christian hymn.

A free rendering of Isaiah’s resurrection prophecies

Paul joins Isaiah’s “Awake, awake,” “Arise, shine; for thy light is come” and “Awake and sing, ye that dwell in dust.”

Held by: Hippolytus (Treatise on Christ and Antichrist 65, c. 200), who ascribes the words to “the prophet”; Karl Holl, who emended Epiphanius’s “Elijah” to “Isaiah.”3

Hippolytus strings together the resurrection prophecies, Daniel 12:2 and “Esaias says, ‘The dead men shall arise, and they that are in their tombs shall awake’” (Isaiah 26:19), then the Lord’s word (John 5:25), and adds: “And the prophet says, ‘Awake, thou that sleepest…’”, setting Ephesians 5:14 among Isaiah’s texts.3 On this reading the words fuse Isaiah 52:1, 60:1 and 26:19, and the formula introduces Scripture rendered freely, as Paul elsewhere renders it. Holl accordingly read Epiphanius’s “in Elijah” as a corruption of “in Isaiah,” backing the change with the Hippolytus parallel.3 On this reading Ephesians 5:14 cites the prophet Isaiah.

A writing outside the Old Testament, possibly an Elijah text

Paul drew the words from a prophet or writing not in the Hebrew Bible, as he quotes the poets elsewhere.

Held by: Epiphanius (Panarion 42.12.3, c. 375), who places the words “in Elijah”;4 Jerome, who reports this as the “simple answer” and leaves it open.2

Epiphanius says only that the quotation stands “in Elijah” (pará tôi Ēlíai); Gallagher notes that he does not name the Apocalypse of Elijah, though handbooks often say so.4 Jerome’s “simple answer” is that “Paul must have read this phrase in some arcane prophet or in the writings called apocryphal,” brought “not to substantiate the apocryphal texts but in the same way that he makes use of verses elsewhere from Aratus, Epimenides and Menander”; he also records the view that Paul speaks “as if he were assuming the voice of the Holy Spirit.”2 For the sister verse 1 Corinthians 2:9, Origen and Ambrosiaster name the Elijah apocryphon outright.5 On this reading the apostolic formula could introduce words from outside the Hebrew Scriptures, as Jude 14–15 cites 1 Enoch as prophecy.6

A fragment of an early Christian hymn

The three short rhythmic lines are a liturgical acclamation, probably baptismal, cited from the church’s worship.

Held by: Markus Barth (Protestant, Ephesians, Anchor Bible 34A, 1974); Andrew T. Lincoln (Protestant, Ephesians, Word Biblical Commentary 42, 1990).3

On this reading the lines “Awake… arise… Christ shall give thee light” move from sleep to rising to illumination, the imagery of baptism, and dió légei marks the citation of a known liturgical text.3 The words are no quotation of any book, canonical or apocryphal, but of the church’s own worship. The surviving Apocalypse of Elijah is a Coptic Christian recension dated c. 150–300 that does not contain the phrase, and Stone and Strugnell collect the Elijah fragments.7 The Naassenes whom Hippolytus reports quote “Awake thou that sleepest…” as words “the Scripture speaks.”3 On this reading Ephesians 5:14 preserves an early hymn, quoted by Paul to the church that sang it.

  3. The early-Christian-hymn reading of Ephesians 5:14 — the three short rhythmic lines, the baptismal imagery of waking, rising, and illumination, and the dió légei citation of a known liturgical fragment: so Andrew T. Lincoln, Ephesians (Word Biblical Commentary 42; Dallas: Word, 1990), 318–20, and Markus Barth, Ephesians (Anchor Bible 34A), both treating it as a hymn (likely baptismal) rather than an Old-Testament or apocryphal quotation. The rival free-adaptation-of-Isaiah reading fuses Isaiah 52:1 ("Awake, awake"), Isaiah 60:1 ("Arise, shine; for thy light is come"), and Isaiah 26:19 ("Awake and sing, ye that dwell in dust"); this is the source Karl Holl proposed by emending Epiphanius's "Elijah" to "Isaiah," backing the change "with a parallel in Hippolytus" (Gallagher, "Epiphanius on Ephesians 5:14" (2017)). That parallel can be read at first hand. In his Treatise on Christ and Antichrist 65 Hippolytus strings together the resurrection prophecies — Daniel 12:2, then "Esaias says, 'The dead men shall arise, and they that are in their tombs shall awake; for the dew from thee is healing to them'" (Isaiah 26:19), then the Lord's word (John 5:25) — and immediately adds "And the prophet says, 'Awake, thou that sleepest, and arise from the dead, and Christ shall give thee light'" (Ephesians 5:14), setting the citation among the Isaian resurrection texts and ascribing it to "the prophet" rather than to any apocryphon — exactly the association Holl's emendation supposes. (The same words circulated as "Scripture" elsewhere in Hippolytus's milieu: the Naassene Gnostics he reports quote "Awake thou that sleepest…" as words "the Scripture speaks," Refutation of All Heresies 5.8.) ↩ ↩ ↩ ↩ ↩ ↩
