Has later evidence undercut Jerome’s grounds for a Hebrew-sized Old Testament?

Disputed reading · on the Old Testament canon · witness: Jerome

Related readings: Augustine’s lack of Hebrew and his canon witness · Did a council at Jamnia close the canon? · The Jewish canon and the deuterocanon

The passage

Jerome, Prologus Galeatus (c. 391): “whatever is outside of these is set aside among the apocrypha. Therefore, Wisdom… and the book of Jesus son of Sirach, and Judith and Tobias, and The Shepherd are not in the canon.”1 Prologue to Tobit: Tobit is the book “which the Hebrews exclude from the catalogue of Divine Scriptures.”2

The question

Jerome measured the Old Testament by “the truth of the Hebrew,” the books the Jews kept, and recorded judgements on the language of the disputed books: Tobit “written in Chaldean words,” 1 Maccabees Hebrew and 2 Maccabees Greek, Wisdom “never among the Hebrews,” Sirach “found in Hebrew.”3 The Dead Sea Scrolls and modern canon history have since added evidence on both the languages and the dating of the Jewish canon. What is disputed is whether that evidence undercuts the grounds Jerome gave. The reading that the Scrolls’ Semitic copies overturn his exclusion is stated here from popular apologetic sources; academic literature holding that argument was not located.

The Hebrew canon the Jews keep is the measure of the Old Testament

In brief: The church should reckon the Old Testament by the Hebrew Scriptures the Jewish people preserved; books outside that catalogue are read, but are not in the canon.

Held by: Jerome (Prologus Galeatus, c. 391; Prologue to Tobit);1,2 Roger Beckwith (Anglican, 1985), who dates the Hebrew arrangement to the pre-Christian period.4

Jerome’s criterion is the catalogue the Jews keep, not the language a book was written in. He knew that Tobit came from “a book written in Chaldean words,” that Sirach existed “in Hebrew, titled not Ecclesiasticus as among the Latins, but Parables,” and that “the First Book of the Maccabees (is) Hebrew,” and he set them outside the canon because “the Hebrews exclude” them from the catalogue.3,2 His identifications of the books’ languages match the later evidence: the Qumran copies of Tobit are four in Aramaic and one in Hebrew; the Geniza, Masada and Qumran manuscripts of Ben Sira confirm its Hebrew original; 1 Maccabees is held to have been composed in Hebrew and 2 Maccabees in Greek; Wisdom is held to be a Greek composition of Alexandria.5,6,7,8 Jerome learned his Hebrew from Jewish teachers, among them Baraninas, who came to him by night, and a teacher from Lydda for Job and one from Tiberias for Chronicles, and he made the synagogue’s text his court of appeal.9 Beckwith holds that the Hebrew canon was fixed long before its Talmudic attestation, in the Maccabean period, so that the catalogue Jerome deferred to was the canon of the apostolic age.4 On this reading Jerome’s grounds rest on the Hebrew Scriptures as the Jews received them, and the books outside that catalogue belong to the church’s reading, not its canon.

The Septuagint the Church received is its Old Testament, and the Church delivered these books as Scripture

In brief: The Church’s Old Testament is the Septuagint it received, with the books Jerome set aside delivered within it as Scripture, so the Hebrew catalogue is not the Church’s measure.

Held by: the Synod of Jerusalem (Eastern Orthodox, Confession of Dositheus, 1672);10 Kallistos Ware (Orthodox bishop, The Orthodox Church, 1963).11

Asked “What Books do you call Sacred Scripture?”, the Confession of Dositheus adds to the list Cyril Lucaris had drawn “from the Synod of Laodicea” the books he had called Apocrypha: “‘The Wisdom of Solomon,’ ‘Judith,’ ‘Tobit,’ ‘The History of the Dragon,’ ‘The History of Susanna,’ ‘The Maccabees,’ and ‘The Wisdom of Sirach.’” Its ground is the Church’s delivering of them, not their language or a Jewish list: “the Catholic Church, which hath delivered to us as genuine the Sacred Gospels and the other Books of Scripture, hath undoubtedly delivered these also as parts of Scripture, and the denial of these is the rejection of those.” It allows for the earlier hesitation: “if, perhaps, it seemeth that not always have all been by all reckoned with the others, yet nevertheless these also have been counted and reckoned with the rest of Scripture, as well by Synods, as by how many of the most ancient and eminent Theologians of the Catholic Church.”10 Ware states that the Orthodox Church takes as “its authoritative text for the Old Testament” the “ancient Greek translation known as the Septuagint,” and that where it differs from the Hebrew, “Orthodox believe that the changes in the Septuagint were made under the inspiration of the Holy Spirit.” The ten further books “not present in the Hebrew” are “known in the Orthodox Church as the Deutero-Canonical Books”; Jassy (1642) and Jerusalem (1672) declared them “genuine parts of Scripture,” and “most Orthodox scholars at the present day, however, following the opinion of Athanasius and Jerome, consider that the Deutero-Canonical Books, although part of the Bible, stand on a lower footing than the rest of the Old Testament.”11 On this reading the books’ place rests on the Church’s reception of the Septuagint, and Jerome’s judgement lives on in Orthodox scholarship as a ranking within the Bible rather than an exclusion from it.

Jerome’s grounds rested on too rigid a conception of canonicity, which the church at large did not adopt

In brief: Jerome’s exclusion came from a test demanding “the sanction of Jewish antiquity”; the church’s tradition and liturgical use received the books, and Jerome’s own use of them attests their standing.

Held by: George Reid (Catholic, “Canon of the Old Testament,” Catholic Encyclopedia, 1908).12

Reid writes that Jerome “cast his weighty suffrage on the side unfavourable to the disputed books,” and accounts for it from his setting: Jerome “lived long in Palestine, in an environment where everything outside the Jewish Canon was suspect,” and “had an excessive veneration for the Hebrew text, the Hebraica veritas as he called it.” Reid’s analysis of the letters and prefaces finds that Jerome “strongly doubted their inspiration,” and that “the fact that he occasionally quotes them, and translated some of them as a concession to ecclesiastical tradition, is an involuntary testimony on his part to the high standing these writings enjoyed in the Church at large, and to the strength of the practical tradition which prescribed their readings in public worship.” The lower rank given the books by “authorities like Origen, Athanasius, and Jerome” Reid traces to “too rigid a conception of canonicity, one demanding that a book, to be entitled to this supreme dignity, must be received by all, must have the sanction of Jewish antiquity, and must moreover be adapted not only to edification, but also to the ‘confirmation of the doctrine of the Church’, to borrow Jerome’s phrase.” Against that test he sets the African councils, which “base their canon on tradition and liturgical usage,” and the medieval copies of the Vulgate, which “with a slight, probably accidental, exception, uniformly embrace the complete Old Testament.”12 On this reading Jerome’s exclusion rested on a criterion of canonicity, the sanction of Jewish antiquity, that the church’s tradition and liturgical use did not adopt.

Jerome deferred to a Jewish canon that closed after the apostolic age

In brief: No closed Jewish canon existed in the apostolic generation; the table Jerome took as the ancient standard was the rabbinic settlement of later centuries, received through rabbinic teachers.

Held by: Timothy Michael Law (When God Spoke Greek, 2013); Edmon L. Gallagher and John D. Meade (2017), who identify his canon with that of fourth-century rabbinic Palestine;9 drawing on Timothy H. Lim (2013) and Eugene Ulrich (2002) for the dating of the Jewish canon.13

Lim finds, down to about AD 100, “not one official ‘canon’ accepted by all Jews” but a plurality of authoritative collections, the rabbinic table closing “probably between 150 and 250 C.E.”; Ulrich defines a canon as “a closed list” reached only by later “reflective judgment.”13 The council once thought to have closed the canon at Jamnia is now treated as a discarded hypothesis.14 The Qumran library kept in Hebrew and Aramaic copies of Tobit, Ben Sira, Psalm 151, Jubilees and 1 Enoch, and in Greek a fragment of the Letter of Jeremiah (7Q2), so the Hebrew the Jews kept was wider than the rabbinic list of twenty-four (Jerome’s twenty-two).15 Law writes that “one of antiquity’s brightest minds rejected the Septuagint in favor of the Bible of the rabbis,” and Gallagher and Meade identify Jerome’s Old Testament canon with that of fourth-century rabbinic Palestine.9 Jerome suspected the synagogue’s text: he compared Aquila with the Hebrew “to see if from hatred to Christ the synagogue has changed the text,” and allowed that the Jews may “afterward” have “removed the testimonies used by the Apostles against them.”16 On this reading Jerome’s conclusion matches the rabbinic canon that emerged, while his premise, a fixed Jewish canon already standing for the apostolic church to follow, belongs to a later settlement.

The Scrolls’ Semitic copies remove the ground for excluding the books

In brief: The disputed books were set aside as Greek-only, absent from the Hebrew; Qumran’s Hebrew Sirach and Aramaic Tobit show they were not.

Held by: Restless Pilgrim (“Quick Apology: The Deuterocanon,” 2014); Catholic Answers.17

The objection answered is that these books “were never written in Hebrew.” Restless Pilgrim replies: “While at certain points in history it could be asserted that none of the deuterocanonical books were written in Hebrew, that is no longer possible… among these scrolls were found deuterocanonical books, which included a Hebrew version of Sirach, as well as Tobit which was also found in Aramaic.”17 Before these finds Tobit’s Semitic original was uncertain and the book was often treated as Greek; the Qumran copies date to about 100 BC–AD 50.5 The Masada scroll of Ben Sira dates to the first century BC.6 Catholic Answers presses the Qumran evidence further, from the number of copies and the scribal layout of Ben Sira, to the books’ Jewish use.17 On this reading the Semitic antiquity and Jewish use of Tobit and Sirach, now documented, remove one of the grounds on which the deuterocanon was set apart from the Hebrew Scriptures.
