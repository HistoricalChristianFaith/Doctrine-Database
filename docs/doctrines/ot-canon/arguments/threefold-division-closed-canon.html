Does the threefold division of the Hebrew Scriptures show a closed canon, without the deuterocanon, by Jesus’ day?

Disputed reading · on the Old Testament canon · witnesses: the prologue to Sirach, Luke 24:44, Josephus

Related readings: “The Law and the Prophets” · Josephus’s twenty-two books · Philo’s silence · The order of the books

The passage

The prologue to Sirach (c. 132 BC) refers three times to a Scripture in parts: “the law and the prophets, and the others that have followed in their steps”; “the law itself, and the prophets, and the rest of the books”; “the law, and the prophets, and other books of our fathers.”1 Luke 24:44: “all things must be fulfilled, which were written in the law of Moses, and in the prophets, and in the psalms, concerning me.”2

The question

A tripartite arrangement of the Scriptures, with the Law and the Prophets firmly bounded, is attested before Jesus and was known to him.2 What is disputed is whether the three-part structure shows a closed list of a fixed number of books, without the deuterocanon, or whether its third division was still open.

The three divisions show a closed, recognized canon

From the second century BC the Hebrew Scriptures stood in three recognized divisions, which Jesus names; the canon was in place in the first century.

Held by: F. F. Bruce (Brethren, 1954), who calls the closure-in-stages account “a reasonable hypothesis”;3 James White (Reformed Baptist apologist, 2004); Allen Parr and Matt Whitman (evangelical teachers).4

Bruce: “it appears from Lk. xxiv. 44 that our Lord knew the threefold division of the Hebrew Bible, for when He speaks there of ‘the law of Moses, and the prophets, and the psalms’, the last word may refer not merely to the Book of Psalms but to the whole division of the ‘Writings’ in which the Book of Psalms took first place,” and the division “was known in the second century B.C.” He adds: “It is widely supposed that the threefold division reflects three stages in which the Canon of the Old Testament received recognition. There is no direct evidence for this, however, but it is a reasonable hypothesis.”3 White points to the Sirach prologue’s “no less than three references to the divisions of the Jewish canon,” the books enumerable “as either 22 or 24.” Parr takes Luke 24:44 as Jesus “affirming… the Old Testament canon is closed,” the divisions being “the Law, the Prophets, and the Writings, 22 Old Testament Jewish books”; Whitman that Jesus’ Bible “was codified” and “already in place by the… first century A.D.,” with “external corroboration… from Josephus… [and] Philo of Alexandria.”4 On this reading the threefold Scriptures to which Jesus appealed were a recognized whole, the Hebrew canon, without the later books.

The third division was open, and the deuterocanon would fall within it

A structure is not a list: the early witnesses name the third division vaguely, its count varied, and its edges were disputed into the second century.

Held by: Albert C. Sundberg Jr. (1964), John Barton (1986), Timothy H. Lim (2013);5 Gary Michuta (Catholic apologist, Why Catholic Bibles Are Bigger, 2007);6 Otto Kaiser, ⚠ unverified, as summarized by Catholic Answers.7

Michuta: the Sirach prologue “never speaks of ‘the Law, the Prophets, and the Writings’; it speaks only of ‘the Law, the Prophets, and the other books,’” and “such a vague name as the ‘other books’… cannot be said to effectively exclude much of anything”; in three tries the translator never lands on a fixed term.6 Philo’s parallel ends “and all kinds of other things,” and Qumran’s 4QMMT names “the book of Moses and the books of the Prophets and David.”2 The count floats: twenty-two in Josephus, twenty-four in the Talmud and 4 Ezra; and the Mishnah records second-century dispute over whether Ecclesiastes and the Song of Songs “defile the hands.”8 The disputed books are wisdom, narrative and history, which in the Hebrew frame belong, if anywhere, among the Writings; Kaiser, as Catholic Answers reports him, holds that their authors “presuppose the validity of the Law and the Prophets and also utilize the… Writings collection, which was, at the time, still in the process of formation,” and the Talmud introduces a line of Ben Sira with the formula used for the Writings.7 On this reading the threefold structure fixes two divisions and leaves the third, where the disputed books would stand, undefined.
