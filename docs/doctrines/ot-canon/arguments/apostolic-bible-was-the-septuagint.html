Does the apostles’ use of a Greek Old Testament attest the deuterocanon as part of their Scripture?

Disputed reading · on the Old Testament canon · witness: the Septuagint

Related readings: The New Testament’s silence and allusions · The Apostolic Fathers’ use of the deuterocanon

The passage

That the residue of men might seek after the Lord, and all the Gentiles, upon whom my name is called, saith the Lord, who doeth all these things.

— Acts 15:17 (KJV), James quoting Amos 9:12 in the Septuagint’s form; the Hebrew, as the KJV renders it, reads “That they may possess the remnant of Edom, and of all the heathen.”1

The question

The New Testament quotes the Old chiefly in a Greek form, at times following the Greek where it differs from the Hebrew; Archer and Chirichigno count about 340 citations agreeing with the Septuagint against about 33 with the Masoretic Hebrew.2 What is disputed is what this shows about the extent of the apostles’ Scripture: whether the Greek Bible they used, which in the fourth-century Christian codices contains the deuterocanonical books, carried those books as Scripture for them, or whether their usage bears on the text-form of the shared books only.

The apostles’ Scripture was the Greek Bible that carried these books

The apostles read and quoted the Greek Old Testament, and that Greek Bible is the one that holds Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch and the Maccabees.

Held by: Irenaeus (Against Heresies, c. 180), on the apostles’ agreement with the Septuagint translation; George Reid (Catholic, “Canon of the Old Testament,” Catholic Encyclopedia, 1908), who grants that the Septuagint “was the vehicle which conveyed these additional Scriptures” and doubts “an Apostolic adoption of the Septuagint en bloc”; Kallistos Ware (Orthodox bishop, The Orthodox Church), on the Septuagint as the Orthodox Church’s authoritative Old Testament text; Catholic Answers.3

Reid writes that the Septuagint “was the vehicle which conveyed these additional Scriptures into the Catholic Church,” and that its oldest copies, though “made by Christian hands,” are such that “scholars generally admit that these faithfully represent the Old Testament as it was current among the Hellenist or Alexandrian Jews in the age immediately preceding Christ.”3

Catholic Answers states that “by the first century, the LXX was the Bible of Greek-speaking Jews and so was the most frequently used version of the Old Testament in the early Church,” and that the New Testament writers usually quote from it.3 Irenaeus wrote that “the apostles, since they are of more ancient date than all these [heretics], agree with this aforesaid translation [of the Septuagint]; and the translation harmonizes with the tradition of the apostles.”3 The apostles follow the Greek even where an argument rests on it: James’s case for the Gentile mission at the Council of Jerusalem turns on the Septuagint of Amos 9:11–12, which read ’adam (“men”) for ’edom and “seek” for “possess”;1 Hebrews 1:6 quotes “let all the angels of God worship him” from the Greek of Deuteronomy 32:43, a clause the Masoretic text lacks and a Qumran Hebrew copy (4QDeutq) supports;4 Hebrews 10:5 builds on the Greek “a body hast thou prepared me” of Psalm 40:6;5 Matthew 1:23 follows the Greek parthenos of Isaiah 7:14.6 The Greek Old Testament of the great Christian codices, Vaticanus, Sinaiticus and Alexandrinus, sets the disputed books among the others without separating them.7 The New Testament also echoes these books: Hebrews 11:35 recalls the martyrs of 2 Maccabees 6–7, and Romans 1 recalls Wisdom 13–14.8 Ware writes that the Orthodox Church, “as its authoritative text for the Old Testament, … uses the ancient Greek translation known as the Septuagint,” and that “the New Testament follows the Septuagint text (Matthew 1:23)”; its further books he counts “part of the Bible.”9 On this reading the Bible from which the apostles preached was the Greek one that carried the deuterocanonical books, and the church that received that Bible from them received the books with it.

The apostles followed the Hebrew where the Greek departs from it

Where the Greek and the Hebrew differ, the evangelists and apostles set down what they found in the Hebrew, so their citations point to the Hebrew Scriptures.

Held by: Jerome (Latin, Letter 57, 395; Commentary on Matthew, 398; Apology against Rufinus, 402).10

Jerome lists New Testament quotations that follow the Hebrew against the Septuagint: Matthew’s “Out of Egypt have I called my son” (Hosea 11:1), “He shall be called a Nazarene,” and John 19:37, “they shall look on him whom they pierced,” which “the evangelist John quotes from the Hebrew.” He concludes that “the apostles and evangelists … have sought to give the meaning rather than the words.” In 402 he states his rule: “Wherever the Seventy agree with the Hebrew, the apostles took their quotations from that translation; but, where they disagree, they set down in Greek what they had found in the Hebrew,” adding that “the Apostles of Christ have an authority superior to theirs.” Of “Out of Egypt I called my son” he challenges “those who deny the truth of the Hebrew scriptures” to “answer where this is read in the Septuagint translators.”10 On this reading the apostles’ use of Greek was a matter of language, and the authority behind their citations was the Hebrew text, which Jerome counted as the measure of the Old Testament canon. On Jerome’s reading, the evangelists’ Hebrew-based citations show the apostles’ Scripture to be the Hebrew Bible.

The apostles used a Greek text, but no Greek canon including these books yet existed

The apostles quoted the Greek, but in the first century there was no fixed Greek collection with a table of contents to inherit; the one-volume Greek Bible holding these books is a fourth-century Christian product.

Held by: George Reid (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1908), on the unfixed first-century Septuagint only; he holds that the further books reached the Church through the Apostles by “a selective process”;11 Albert C. Sundberg Jr. (Protestant, The Old Testament of the Early Church, 1964); Martin Hengel (Protestant, The Septuagint as Christian Scripture, 2002); Timothy H. Lim (historian of the Jewish canon, The Formation of the Jewish Canon, 2013).12

Reid, writing in 1908, doubts “an Apostolic adoption of the Septuagint en bloc”: “there are cogent reasons for believing that it was not a fixed quantity at the time,” its oldest manuscripts “are not entirely identical in the books they contain,” and the version “must have been current in separate books or groups of books, a condition favourable to a certain variability of compass.”11 Sundberg set aside the older hypothesis of a wider “Alexandrian canon” kept by Diaspora Jews: Alexandrian Jewish writers such as Philo show no canon wider than the Palestinian, the Jewish canon was not yet closed on either side in the first century, and the broader Christian Old Testament was a Christian formation after the parting from Judaism.12 Hengel carries the point further: “the Septuagint” as a single volume bearing these books is a Christian artifact.12 In the apostolic age the Greek Scriptures circulated as separate scrolls, one or a few works to a roll, and no scroll fixed a canon by its contents; the great uncials that interleave the disputed books belong to the fourth and fifth centuries.7 The divergent readings the New Testament follows (Amos, Deuteronomy, the Psalms, Isaiah) all belong to protocanonical books, so they show which text of the shared books the apostles read, and allusions to the deuterocanon show acquaintance; Lim judges the New Testament’s use of these books not to be use as Scripture.8,12 On this reading the apostles’ Greek Bible was a set of scrolls in a Greek-Jewish literary world, and its extent is a question for the later history of reception.
