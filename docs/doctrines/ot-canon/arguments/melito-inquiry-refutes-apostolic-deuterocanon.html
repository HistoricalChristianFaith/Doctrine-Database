What does Melito’s inquiry in the East (c. 170) show about the deuterocanon and apostolic tradition?

Disputed reading · on the Old Testament canon · witness: Melito of Sardis

Related readings: How the early church received the deuterocanon · Trent and the earlier reception

The passage

Melito, letter to Onesimus, preserved by Eusebius (Ecclesiastical History iv.26.13–14): “when I went East… I learned accurately the books of the Old Testament.” His list is Hebrew-sized, without Tobit, Judith, Sirach, Baruch or the Maccabees, and without Esther.1

"Melito of Sardis inquired of the church in Palestine concerning the Old Testament canon around AD 175. He discovered that the canon did not include the Apocryphal books. How can it be that he would inquire in Palestine if in fact the canon of the Old Testament including the Apocrypha was a matter of Apostolic tradition, as Trent had claimed?"

— James White, in debate with Gary Michuta (2004).2

The question

The Council of Trent received the books together with “the traditions… which, received by the Apostles… have come down even unto us.”3 What is disputed is what Melito’s having to inquire, and the answer he brought back, show: that the longer canon was no apostolic tradition in the second century, or that Melito recorded the Jewish enumeration while the Christian list was still being articulated.

The inquiry shows no settled apostolic tradition of the longer canon

A careful bishop had to travel to learn the books, and the answer was Hebrew-sized; the deuterocanon was not a received apostolic deposit.

Held by: James White (Reformed Baptist apologist, 2004); Steve Christie (Why Protestant Bibles Are Smaller, 2019).2

White asks: “How can it be that he would inquire in Palestine if in fact the canon of the Old Testament including the Apocrypha was a matter of Apostolic tradition, as Trent had claimed?”2 Melito, bishop of Sardis, went “to the East… to the place where these things were preached and done,” and “learned accurately” the books; he did not relay a received Christian convention.1 Christie states the content: “Melito did not include any of the Deuterocanonical books in his Old Testament list, but only the books from the Hebrew Bible (except for Esther).”2 On this reading the earliest surviving Christian list of the Old Testament, the work of an inquiring bishop c. 170, gives no place to Tobit, Judith, Sirach, Baruch or the Maccabees; and since the inquiry was needed at all, no apostolic enumeration including them was in the hands of the second-century churches. The answer stands at the head of the Hebrew-conscious stream from Melito to Jerome. On this reading Melito’s inquiry tells against Trent’s claim that the longer canon came down from the apostles.

Melito reported the Jewish enumeration while the Christian list was still being articulated

Melito inquired at the Hebrew source and reported how the Jews there counted their books; the gradual articulation of a list does not show the books were a late addition.

Held by: Gary Michuta (Catholic apologist, debate with White, 2004, and Why Catholic Bibles Are Bigger, 2017);4 Edmon L. Gallagher and John D. Meade (2017), on the list as a report of the Jewish reckoning.5

Gallagher and Meade read the two earliest Christian lists, Melito’s and Origen’s, as reports of the Hebrew reckoning rather than prescriptive Christian rulings.5 On this reading Trent’s “apostolic tradition” concerns the books’ reception and authority, not a circulated list every bishop held; the New Testament’s precise list was likewise not in any second-century bishop’s hands. Michuta presses the inquiry the other way: Sardis had a large Jewish community, civically recognized from the first century BC, yet Melito travelled to Palestine for the answer, so the Jewish canon too was not a closed, universally known list.6,4 Melito framed his Eclogae as extracts “concerning the Savior and the whole of our faith,” an apologetic purpose.5 Michuta adds that the list “omits the books of Lamentations, Nehemiah, and Esther,” so that “Melito’s list, therefore, is not identical to the Protestant canon”; he reads “Wisdom” as the Book of Wisdom, where Bruce and others read it as a second title for Proverbs.4,7 On this reading Melito records the Jewish count of his day, and the canon question for the church lay with the books its Greek Bible carried.
