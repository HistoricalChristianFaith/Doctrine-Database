What did the Fathers’ “ecclesiastical” or “read” category signify for the books placed in it?

Disputed reading · on the Old Testament canon · witnesses: Athanasius, Rufinus of Aquileia, Jerome

Related readings: The tiers of Athanasius’s Festal Letter · Citation “as Scripture” and canonical standing · What the Fathers meant by “canon”

The passage

Rufinus, Commentary on the Apostles’ Creed §38 (c. 404): books “our fathers call not ‘Canonical’ but ‘Ecclesiastical’” — Wisdom, Sirach, Tobit, Judith, the Maccabees, and in the New Testament the Shepherd and the Two Ways — “all of which they would have read in the Churches, but not appealed to for the confirmation of doctrine.” Athanasius (367) has books “not… included in the Canon, but appointed by the Fathers to be read by those who newly join us,” including the Didache and the Shepherd; Jerome has the church read Wisdom and Sirach “for the strengthening of the people, (but) not for confirming the authority of ecclesiastical dogmas.”1

The question

Several Fathers name a middle class of books between the canon and the apocrypha; the same Fathers cite some of these books in their own voice as Scripture.2 What is disputed is what the middle class is: a grouping within Scripture sorted by its readers, a class read in the Church beside a fixed core, or a rank below the canon. The reading of the middle class as a catechetical grouping within Scripture is stated here from a Catholic apologist; academic literature holding it was not located.

A catechetical grouping within Scripture

The “ecclesiastical” books were those used to teach the faith, especially to newcomers; the distinction is one of audience and use, and the books were held as Scripture.

Held by: Gary Michuta (Catholic apologist, Why Catholic Bibles Are Bigger, 2007; The Case for the Deuterocanon, 2017).3

Michuta holds that the Fathers had three categories where moderns see two, and that the “ecclesiastical” books were used to teach doctrine “especially to recent converts.”3 Athanasius defines the tier by its readers: books “read by those who newly join us, and who wish for instruction in the word of godliness.” Origen, a century and a half earlier, sorts church reading into “milk” for beginners, naming Esther, Judith, Tobit and Wisdom among the “clearer and simpler” books they “gladly receive,” and “solid food” for the mature.4 The same Fathers quote these books as Scripture: Athanasius introduces Wisdom with “Scripture taught us beforehand,” cites Sirach 15:9 as “the words spoken by the Spirit,” and sets Tobit 4:18 under “it is written”; Rufinus, who coined ecclesiastici, introduces Sirach with “ita enim Scriptura dicit” to settle a point of moral doctrine.2 On this reading the Fathers’ “canon” is a narrower, technical rule, and the books read for formation are a pedagogical group inside Scripture.

Books read in the Church beside a fixed core

Orthodox tradition keeps a strict core of the books of the Jewish Bible and a flexible class of anaginōskomena, “books read,” the class Athanasius named.

Held by: Eugen J. Pentiuc (Orthodox, The Old Testament in Eastern Orthodox Tradition, 2014); Kallistos Ware (Orthodox bishop, The Orthodox Church).5

Pentiuc traces the Orthodox position from Ecclesiasticus through the early Christian writers to John of Damascus and finds that “the current Orthodox position on canon still looks very similar to the Damascene’s concluding remarks”: strict regarding the thirty-nine books of the Jewish Bible, and flexible regarding the anaginōskomena found in the Septuagint.5 Kallistos Ware reports that Jassy (1642) and Jerusalem (1672) declared these books “genuine parts of Scripture,” and that “most Orthodox scholars at the present day, however, following the opinion of Athanasius and Jerome, consider that the Deutero-Canonical Books, although part of the Bible, stand on a lower footing than the rest of the Old Testament.”5 On this reading the patristic middle class is neither demoted nor absorbed: the books are read in the Church’s worship, quoted as Scripture, and kept distinct from the core whose extent is fixed. Athanasius’s wording, books “appointed by the Fathers to be read,” gives the later name, and his letter keeps them apart both from the canon and from the apocrypha he calls “an invention of heretics.”1 The class is open-ended rather than closed: the Fathers placed different books in it, Athanasius setting Esther among them and Rufinus the Maccabees.1 On this reading the “read” books are a received part of the Church’s Old Testament, below the core in strictness and above the apocrypha in use.

A rank below the canon

The Fathers named the middle tier by denying it the canon; it is edifying and read, but kept out of the rule by which doctrine is proved.

Held by: John Meade (Protestant, canon-list scholar); Edmon L. Gallagher and John D. Meade (The Biblical Canon Lists from Early Christianity, 2017).3

Meade frames the patristic data as three categories, Apocrypha, Ecclesiastical and Canon, with the middle a real sub-canonical rank.3 The label is built as a negation: Rufinus’s “not ‘Canonical’ but ‘Ecclesiastical’”; Athanasius’s “not… included in the Canon, but appointed… to be read”; Jerome’s “not for confirming the authority of ecclesiastical dogmas.” In each case the tier is defined by what it is denied, a place in the canon and the authority to prove doctrine. The company it keeps points the same way: Athanasius files the Shepherd and the Didache in the same tier, and Rufinus the Shepherd and the Two Ways.1 The Catholic Encyclopedia describes Rufinus’s rank as “ecclesiastical” books “in authority unequal to the other Scriptures.”3 Gallagher and Meade note the Fathers’ “dual standard,” listing the Hebrew canon while quoting the wider Greek collection in preaching and prayer.2 The catechetical readership Athanasius names describes the tier’s use, and he states it in the same sentence as its exclusion from the canon.4 On this reading the ecclesiastical books are a graded middle rank: read, edifying and quotable, but below the canon.
