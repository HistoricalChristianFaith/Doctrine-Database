How did Luther’s 1534 grouping of the disputed books bear on their later removal from Protestant Bibles?

Disputed reading · on the Old Testament canon · witnesses: Martin Luther, the Thirty-Nine Articles, the Westminster Confession

Related readings: Did Luther remove the deuterocanon? · Luther and 2 Maccabees · Was the removal doctrinally motivated?

The passage

Luther’s German Bible of 1534 gathers Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch and 1–2 Maccabees out of their Vulgate places into one section between the Testaments, headed “Apocrypha — that is, books which are not held equal to the Holy Scriptures, and yet are profitable and good to read.”1 The British and Foreign Bible Society resolved in 1826 “That the funds of the Society be applied to the printing and circulation of the Canonical Books of Scripture, to the exclusion of those Books and parts of Books usually termed Apocryphal.”2

The question

The facts of 1534 are agreed: Luther did not delete the books; he ranked them below Scripture and set them apart. What is disputed is how his act relates to their disappearance from most Protestant Bibles three centuries later: the first step of one process, or one moment in a history whose removal came from later and separate causes. The reading that Luther began the process that ended in removal is stated here from a Catholic apologist; academic literature holding it was not located.

Luther began the process that ended in removal

By ranking the books below Scripture and printing them as a separate section, Luther created the unit that later generations moved and then deleted.

Held by: Gary Michuta (Catholic apologist, Why Catholic Bibles Are Bigger, 2007/2017, and debate with James White, 2004).3,4

Michuta corrects the slogan that Luther removed the books (“This is not entirely true. Luther’s German translation of the Scriptures included the deuterocanon”) and credits Luther with beginning the process that produced the smaller Protestant Bible.3 Before 1534 there was no “Apocrypha” section to drop; the 1826 resolution removed “those Books and parts of Books usually termed Apocryphal” as a class.1 Luther’s layout became the model for the Swedish (1541) and Danish (1550) Bibles and for the English Bibles that carried the Apocrypha between the Testaments.5 His rank became a doctrinal precedent: Article VI of the Thirty-Nine Articles reads the books “for example of life and instruction of manners” but not to “establish any doctrine,” and the Westminster Confession declares them “no part of the canon of Scripture” and not to be used otherwise “than other human writings.”6 Michuta describes the sequence: the Reformers chose “to first put them on a lower level than Scripture, then eventually move them to the back of the Bible, and then eventually not only get rid of them but also remove all cross-references to these books.” The 1611 King James Bible keyed Hebrews 11:35 to 2 Maccabees 7 in its margin, and Blayney’s 1769 Oxford edition dropped such cross-references.4 On this reading Luther’s demotion and segregation is the originating step of the Apocrypha-free Protestant Bible.

The Reformation churches kept and used the books; the removal was a later act

For three centuries Protestant Bibles carried the books, at times by requirement, and Luther’s own church never removed them; the removal came from a nineteenth-century controversy.

Held by: W. Heaford Daubney (Anglican, The Use of the Apocrypha in the Christian Church, 1900).7

Daubney gathers evidence that the Reformation churches went on prizing the books: the S.P.C.K. printed no Bible without the Apocrypha until 1743, a Dutch Free State town was named “Bethulie” after Judith, and a French Protestant, Claude Baduel, wrote a commentary on them. He quotes the Cambridge divine John Hey (1796–98): “At the Reformation, when men had been brought up to revere them, it would have been both imprudent and cruel to set them aside”; Hey himself upheld the Article VI ranking.7 Every King James Bible before 1666 carried the Apocrypha, and in 1615 Archbishop George Abbot forbade, on pain of a year’s imprisonment, printing an English Bible without it.5 Luther kept, prefaced and commended the books “to read,” and German Lutheran Bibles retained the section for centuries, Lutheranism never adopting the excision as a confessional principle.8 The removal belongs to the “Apocrypha controversy” of the 1820s, a campaign led from Scotland by Robert Haldane and Andrew Thomson, with the economy of mass Bible printing among its stated motives; the American Bible Society followed in 1827.2 Blayney’s pruning of cross-references in 1769 came while his edition still printed the Apocrypha.4 On this reading the books’ removal was the act of a particular later tradition and moment, distinct from Luther’s ranking of them.
