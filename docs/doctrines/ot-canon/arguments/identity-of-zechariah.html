Which Zechariah does Jesus name in “from the blood of Abel to the blood of Zechariah”?

Disputed reading · on the Old Testament canon · witnesses: Origen, Jerome, John Chrysostom

Related readings: Does “from Abel to Zechariah” mark the first and last books? · Did the Hebrew Bible of Jesus’ day end with Chronicles?

The passage

That upon you may come all the righteous blood shed upon the earth, from the blood of righteous Abel unto the blood of Zacharias son of Barachias, whom ye slew between the temple and the altar.

— Matthew 23:35 (KJV); Luke 11:51 reads “From the blood of Abel unto the blood of Zacharias, which perished between the altar and the temple,” without a patronym.1

The question

The saying gives three marks: a killing in the temple court, Matthew’s patronym “son of Barachias,” and a second-person “whom ye slew.” The candidates are Zechariah son of Jehoiada, stoned in the temple court (2 Chronicles 24:20–22); the prophet Zechariah son of Berechiah (Zechariah 1:1), whose death no early source records; Zechariah the father of John the Baptist; and Zechariah son of Baruch, killed in the temple about AD 67–68 (Josephus, War IV.5.4).2,3,4 Gallagher observes that interpreters chose according to the mark they treated as decisive.5 The identification bears on the canon debate because the “bookends” argument requires the Chronicles figure.

The father of John the Baptist, martyred in the temple

“Whom ye slew” points to a man of Jesus’ own generation: the Baptist’s father, killed in the temple according to tradition.

Held by: Origen (Commentary on Matthew, c. 240); Basil of Caesarea (c. 379); Gregory of Nyssa (c. 390); Cyril of Alexandria (c. 430), as reported in catena.6,7

Origen takes the Zechariah to be the father of John the Baptist, killed for admitting the Virgin to the place of virgins after the Nativity, a story told in the Protoevangelium of James 23 (“Zacharias was murdered … at the vestibule of the temple of the Lord”). He states the ground of the identification: “Of [him] we have no scriptural evidence, neither that he was the son of Berechiah, nor that the scribes and Pharisees killed him between the temple and the altar … But it is from tradition,” and he ascribes the patronym to Josephus.6 On this reading the second person of “whom ye slew” is addressed to the men of Jesus’ own day, and the martyr is the priest of Luke’s opening chapter. The reading became standard among the Greek Fathers: Basil has Zechariah “slain between the temple and the altar” for placing Mary among the virgins after the Lord’s conception; Gregory of Nyssa writes that “they slaughtered the one who testified … sacrificing the priest at the altar itself”; Cyril of Alexandria’s agreement is reported from catena by his translator Robert Payne-Smith, who himself prefers Jerome’s identification.7 Tertullian (c. 220) names “Zacharias butchered between the altar and the temple” in a list of martyrs running on to John the Baptist without saying which Zechariah he means.8 On this reading the saying closes with the martyrdom of a priest of Jesus’ own generation.

Zechariah son of Jehoiada, stoned in the temple court

The one Zechariah whose killing in the temple court Scripture records is the priest of 2 Chronicles 24; the patronym is accounted for in several ways.

Held by: Jerome (Commentary on Matthew, c. 398); Bede (c. 730); John Calvin (Reformed, Harmony of the Evangelists, 1555); John Lightfoot (Anglican, Horae Hebraicae, 1675).9,10,11

Jerome canvasses the prophet, the Baptist’s father and the Chronicles priest and concludes “that Zechariah was not the son of Barachiah, but the son of Jehoiada the priest,” because his murder is the one Scripture records; he offers a reason for the name, “Barachiah means ‘blessed of the Lord’ in our language, and the justice of the priest Jehoiada is shown,” and notes that the Gospel the Nazarenes use reads “son of Jehoiada.” Of the Baptist’s-father story he writes that “since this view does not have the authority of the Scriptures, it is rejected with the same facility with which it is approved.”9 The Chronicles priest was stoned “in the court of the house of the Lord,” and as he died said “The Lord look upon it, and require it,” a cry for vengeance like the blood of Abel that “crieth” (Genesis 4:10).2 Calvin writes that “the sacred history relates what agrees perfectly with this passage”; Lightfoot that “the killing of our Zacharias in the Temple is related in express words”; J. D. Michaelis (1750) that the murder was “supposed to call aloud for vengeance,” and he treats the patronym as a scribal gloss.11,9 Other accounts of the name are a second name for Jehoiada, “son” as “descendant,” and a rabbinic conflation of two righteous men.9 On this reading Jesus pairs the two murders over which Scripture records a cry for vengeance.

The identification left open; a holy man slain in a holy place

The candidates are listed without a decision; what the saying stresses is the killing of a holy man in a holy place, and the pairing stands for all martyrs.

Held by: John Chrysostom (Homily 74 on Matthew, c. 390s); Remigius of Auxerre, as excerpted in the Catena Aurea; Thomas Aquinas (Catena Aurea and Lectura on Matthew, c. 1270).12

Chrysostom lists the field: “Some say, the father of John; some, the prophet; some, a priest with two different names, whom the Scripture also calls, the son of Jehoiada,” and takes the point to be “a holy man slain in a holy place.” Remigius reads Abel and Zechariah as standing for “all holy martyrs, both of lay and priestly order,” a layman and a priest. Aquinas gathers Origen, Jerome, Chrysostom and Remigius in the Catena Aurea without arbitrating, and in his lectures on Matthew sets out the three named Zechariahs and says of the son of Jehoiada that “the place agrees, but the name disagrees” (concordat locus, sed nomen dissonat).12 On this reading the marks of the saying do not converge on one man: the place fits the Chronicles priest, the patronym fits the prophet, and the second person fits a contemporary. The meaning of the saying does not wait on the identification, since its force is the guilt of all righteous blood from the first to the last. On this reading the saying’s force lies in the holiness of the victim and the place.

Targum on Lamentations 2:20. ↩

Zechariah son of Baruch (Josephus, The War of the Jews IV.5.4): "they intended to have Zacharias the son of Baruch, one of the most eminent of the citizens, slain … So two of the boldest of them fell upon Zacharias in the middle of the temple, and slew him" — about AD 67–68, during the Zealot terror. "Baruch" is close to "Berechiah," and the temple setting fits; but the death falls a generation after Jesus' saying, so the identification requires reading "whom ye slew" as future-tense prophecy. Josephus, War IV.5.4. ↩

  5. The three identifying marks and the candidate Zechariah of Isaiah. Both Gospels place the killing in the temple court; only Matthew gives the patronym "son of Barachias"; the second-person "whom ye slew" / "this generation" is taken by some (Origen) to require a contemporary. Isaiah 8:2 names "Zechariah the son of Jeberechiah" (a fuller form of Berechiah) as one of Isaiah's "faithful witnesses," but records nothing of his death, so he drops from contention. That the choice of candidate tracks which mark an interpreter treated as primary is the observation of Edmon Gallagher, "The Blood from Abel to Zechariah in the History of Interpretation," New Testament Studies 60 (2014). ↩

Origen, Commentary on Matthew (ser. 25 / new frgm.), identifies the Zechariah of Matt 23:35 as the Baptist's father, martyred for admitting the Virgin to the place of virgins after the Nativity, and candidly concedes: "Of [him] we have no scriptural evidence, neither that he was the son of Berechiah, nor that the scribes and Pharisees killed him between the temple and the altar … But it is from tradition." He further supposes the patronym derives from Josephus' Hellenizing of the name ( Josephus' War IV.5.4 names a Zechariah son of Baruch).
