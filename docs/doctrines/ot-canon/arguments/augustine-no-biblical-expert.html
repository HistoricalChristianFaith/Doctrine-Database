Does Augustine’s lack of Hebrew bear on the weight of his witness to the Old Testament canon?

Disputed reading · on the Old Testament canon · witness: Augustine of Hippo, with Jerome

Related readings: Did Augustine think the deuterocanon stood in the Hebrew canon? · Did Augustine retract or rank the deuterocanon lower? · Unfamiliarity with Hebrew and the wider canon

The passage

It is true that the Catholic Church accepted the Apocryphal books at earlier councils at Rome (A.D. 382), Hippo (A.D. 393), Carthage (A.D. 397), and Florence (A.D. 1442). However, these were not universal Church councils and the earlier councils were influenced heavily by Augustine, who was no Biblical expert, compared to the scholar Jerome, who rejected the Apocrypha as part of the Old Testament Canon.

— CARM, “Reasons why the Apocrypha does not belong in the Bible.”1

The question

Jerome read Hebrew and made the Hebrew the measure of his Old Testament; Augustine read little or no Hebrew, deferred Hebrew questions to Jerome, and addressed the plenary council of Hippo (393), whose list, repeated at Carthage (397), matches his own.2,3 What is disputed is whether that difference in competence bears on how much weight Augustine’s canon carries. The reading that discounts Augustine on this ground is stated here from popular apologetic sources; academic literature holding it was not located.

The canon is settled by the churches’ reception, on which Hebrew is not the qualification

Augustine fixed the canon by what the churches received, a question of testimony rather than philology, and he stood within a Western reception of the books older than himself.

Held by: Augustine (On Christian Doctrine II.8.12, c. 397), stating his own criterion;4 Gary Michuta (Catholic apologist, in debate with James White, 2004, answering the argument from Hebrew competence) ⚠ unverified.5

Augustine’s rule is explicit: “prefer those that are received by all the catholic churches to those which some do not receive,” weighing a book by how many churches, and which of greater authority, receive it; his list that follows names Tobit, Judith, the two Maccabees, Wisdom and Ecclesiasticus.4 He knew the limits of his Hebrew and said so: “should he speak in the Hebrew tongue, in vain would it beat on my senses”; he asked Jerome for a Latin version of “the Seventy translators” rather than of the Hebrew, objecting that if Jews consulted on the Hebrew differed from Jerome “it will seem as if your presence were indispensable, as being the only one who could refute their view”; and he held that “even if anything is found in the original Hebrew in a different form … I think we must give way to the dispensation of Providence” that guided the Seventy.2 On this reading the question Augustine answered was what the churches had received, and the Latin West had received the books before him: Tertullian (c. 210) cites the Letter of Jeremiah, Wisdom, Judith and Susanna; Cyprian (c. 253) cites Tobit, Wisdom, Sirach and the Maccabees as “Holy Scripture”; the African Cheltenham List (c. 360) carries them; Ambrose, who baptized Augustine in 387, cites them as “Scripture”; the Roman Damasine list (conventionally dated 382, a date that is disputed) and Innocent I (c. 405) name them.6 The Latin of the 397 list directs that “the church over the sea be consulted to confirm this canon,” and the council of 419 sent the list to “our brother and fellow bishop, Boniface” of Rome and the bishops of those parts for confirmation,7 and the Council of Florence, which CARM lists among the councils it calls “not universal,” is reckoned ecumenical in Catholic usage, with its bull Cantate Domino enumerating the full canon.7 On this reading Augustine’s canon carries the weight of the reception it reports.

The Hebraist’s judgement outweighs the non-Hebraist’s

On a question about the Old Testament’s extent, Jerome, who read Hebrew, is the better judge than Augustine, who did not, and the councils Augustine shaped were regional.

Held by: CARM (Christian Apologetics & Research Ministry);1 James White (Reformed Baptist apologist, debate with Gary Michuta, 2004).8

CARM grants that councils at Rome (382), Hippo (393), Carthage (397) and Florence (1442) received the disputed books, and holds that “these were not universal Church councils and the earlier councils were influenced heavily by Augustine, who was no Biblical expert, compared to the scholar Jerome, who rejected the Apocrypha as part of the Old Testament Canon.”1 White supplies the specific deficiency: “Augustine could not read Hebrew, and the manuscripts that he had contained these books.”8 On this reading the Old Testament is the Scripture of Israel, and the person equipped to say what it contains is the one who can read it in its own language and knows what the Jews received; Jerome translated iuxta Hebraeos and made the Hebrew the measure of his Old Testament.9 Augustine’s own letters show him leaving the Hebrew to Jerome and reading the Old Testament through the Greek and the Old Latin, whose manuscripts carried the disputed books.2 He was the leading voice at Hippo, where the plenary council charged him, still a presbyter, to address the bishops on the faith and the creed, and the conciliar list matches his own book for book.3 The African councils were regional synods that did not bind the whole church.7 On this reading the agreement of the Augustinian councils reflects Augustine’s Greek-and-Latin Bible, while the Hebraist’s judgement reflects the Hebrew Scriptures themselves.
