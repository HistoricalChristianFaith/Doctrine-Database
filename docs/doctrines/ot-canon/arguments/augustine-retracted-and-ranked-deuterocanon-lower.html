Did Augustine retract his placing of the deuterocanon in the canon, or rank it below the other books?

Disputed reading · on the Old Testament canon · witness: Augustine of Hippo

Related readings: Augustine’s lack of Hebrew and his canon witness · Did Augustine think the deuterocanon stood in the Hebrew canon? · The “ecclesiastical” category

The passage

In the second book I made a mistake as to the authorship of the book commonly called the Wisdom of Solomon.

— Augustine, Retractations II.4 (c. 426–427), on On Christian Doctrine.1

Augustine’s criterion in On Christian Doctrine II.8.12 (c. 397) is to “prefer those that are received by all the catholic churches to those which some do not receive,” and among the latter to prefer those of “the greater number and those of greater authority”; his list of forty-four books places Tobit, Judith and the two Maccabees among the histories and Wisdom and Ecclesiasticus among the prophetical books.2

The question

What is disputed is whether Augustine, the patristic witness most often cited for the longer canon, held the disputed books on a level with the others: whether his later correction withdrew their canonical place, and whether his graded criterion ranks them below the universally received books.

Augustine placed the books in the canon and kept them there

Augustine’s list puts the disputed books inside the canon with no separate tier; his later correction concerns who wrote Wisdom, and he went on citing these books as Scripture.

Held by: George Reid (Catholic, “Canon of the Old Testament,” Catholic Encyclopedia, 1908); Robert Bellarmine and Thomas Stapleton (Catholic controversialists, 1580s), ⚠ unverified, as reported by their opponent Whitaker.3

Reid writes that Augustine “seems to theoretically recognize degrees of inspiration; in practice he employs protos and deuteros without any discrimination whatsoever. Moreover in his ‘De Doctrinâ Christianâ’ he enumerates the components of the complete Old Testament.”3

On this reading Augustine’s forty-four-book list is the canon, and the disputed books stand inside it among the histories and the prophets.2 The correction in the Retractations withdraws an attribution, the view that the son of Sirach wrote Wisdom; in the same review Augustine qualifies his phrase “Old Testament” (“in accordance with ecclesiastical usage”) and leaves the contents of the list as they were.1 His use of the books matches the list across his career: Sirach 40:1 as “the words of Scripture” (City of God XXI.14, c. 426), Wisdom 6:24 with “Holy Scripture says” (On the Trinity XIV.1.1, c. 415), and 2 Maccabees 7:29 as authoritative testimony (On Rebuke and Grace 41, c. 426–427), written about the time of the Retractations.4 The two-class distinction of “canonical” and “ecclesiastical” books belongs to Rufinus, who lists Wisdom, Ecclesiasticus, Tobit, Judith and the Maccabees as “not ‘Canonical’ but ‘Ecclesiastical’,” read but “not appealed to for the confirmation of doctrine,” and to Jerome, who has them read “for the edification of the people”; Augustine’s list draws no such line.5 His graded rule, on this reading, orders books within the canon by breadth of reception and does not place any of them outside it. On this reading Augustine held the longer canon to the end of his life.

Augustine called the books canonical in a wider sense and ranked them below the rest

Augustine’s “canonical” is wider than Jerome’s, his rule ranks the books not universally received below the others, and he later qualified his view.

Held by: William Whitaker (Church of England, Disputation on Holy Scripture, 1588);3 Archibald Alexander (Presbyterian, The Canon of the Old and New Testaments Ascertained, 1826).6

Whitaker reconciles Augustine with Jerome by the sense of the word: “Jerome takes this word ‘canonical’ in one sense, while Augustine, Innocent, and the fathers of Carthage understand it in another … Augustine, therefore, takes this name in a larger sense than Jerome.” Augustine’s own rule shows the ranking: he sorts books into those received by all churches and those received by some, and tells the student to prefer the former, which would be “entirely superfluous” if all were equal; therefore “Augustine did not think that they were all of the same account, credit, and authority.”3 Alexander grants that Augustine “seems to favour the introduction of these six disputed books into the Canon,” and reclaims him on three counts:

These two last mentioned, he says, "are called Solomon's, on account of their resemblance to his writings; although it is known that one of them was composed by the son of Sirach: which deserves to be received among the prophetical books." But this opinion he retracted afterwards.

It may be admitted, however, that Augustine entertained too high an opinion of these apocryphal books, but it is certain that he did not put them on a level with the genuine canonical books. He mentions a custom which prevailed in his time, from which it appears that although the apocryphal books were read in some of the churches, they were not read as Holy Scripture, nor put on a level with the canonical books; for he informs us that they were not permitted to be read from the same desk as the Canonical Scriptures, but from a lower place in the church.

Alexander gives no citation for the custom of reading from “a lower place.”6 Augustine’s Retractations do revisit the passage of On Christian Doctrine on Wisdom and Ecclesiasticus, and his criterion is graded by degree of reception.1,2 On this reading Augustine received the disputed books in a wider sense of “canonical,” and ranked them below the books all the churches received.
