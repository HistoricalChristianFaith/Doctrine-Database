What does the New Testament’s use of the deuterocanon, by allusion but never by formal citation, show about its standing?

Disputed reading · on the Old Testament canon · witness: the New Testament’s use of the Old

Related readings: The apostles’ Greek Bible · The absence of a canon dispute · Jude and 1 Enoch

The passage

Women received their dead raised to life again: and others were tortured, not accepting deliverance; that they might obtain a better resurrection:

— Hebrews 11:35 (KJV), set beside the martyrs of 2 Maccabees 6–7, who refuse deliverance in hope that “the King of the world shall raise us up … unto everlasting life” (2 Maccabees 7:9).1

The question

The New Testament cites the protocanonical books constantly with formulas such as “it is written”; no deuterocanonical book is introduced that way, while several are echoed: Hebrews 11:35 and 2 Maccabees 7, Romans 1 and Wisdom 13–14, Romans 9:21 and Wisdom 15:7, Matthew 27:43 and Wisdom 2.2,1 What is disputed is what this pattern shows: that the apostles did not count the books Scripture, or that they used them as Scripture, citation by formula being no test of canonicity.

The apostles cite the Hebrew books as Scripture and never the others

The apostles adduce the Law, the Prophets and the Psalms as Scripture hundreds of times and never a deuterocanonical book; acquaintance with the books is not reception of them as Scripture.

Held by: Roger Beckwith (Anglican, The Old Testament Canon of the New Testament Church, 1985);2 Timothy H. Lim (The Formation of the Jewish Canon, 2013), who grants the allusions and denies any citation as Scripture.1

Beckwith rests the argument on the absence of any apostolic citation of the Apocrypha as Scripture, and grants that allusions and acquaintance are another matter.2 The New Testament cites the Old with the full apparatus of Scripture, “it is written,” “that it might be fulfilled,” “David saith,” “Isaiah prophesied,” and the books so cited are protocanonical.2 The Hebrew-minded Fathers read the same pattern: Melito returned from the East with a Hebrew-sized list, Origen counted “twenty-two, as the Hebrews have handed them down,” and Jerome set “whatever is outside of these” among the apocrypha.3 Allusion is not citation: Hebrews 11:35 recalls the Maccabean martyrs as examples of faith, as Hebrews 11 recalls others, without calling the book Scripture. The bible.ca form of the argument adds that by quoting one book of a division the apostles verify the whole division, and that “None of the apocryphal books were ever quoted in the New Testament. Not even once!”4 On this reading the apostles’ usage draws the line where the Hebrew canon draws it: the deuterocanonical books were known and echoed, but never adduced as Scripture.

The New Testament uses the books, and formal citation is no test of canonicity

Hebrews 11:35 has no protocanonical referent and draws on 2 Maccabees; Paul echoes Wisdom repeatedly; and the New Testament leaves protocanonical books unquoted while quoting books no one canonizes.

Held by: Albert C. Sundberg Jr. (1964) and Lee Martin McDonald (2017), who read the New Testament’s use of these books affirmatively;1 Jimmy Akin (Catholic apologist, “Defending the Deuterocanonicals,” 1996);5 Taylor Marshall (Catholic author, 2023), on Paul and Wisdom.6

Akin: Hebrews 11:35 has “no episode in the protocanonical books of the Old Testament to which this passage might refer, but it has a direct parallel with an account in 2 Maccabees.”5 Marshall: “Saint Paul the Apostle quotes the Book of Wisdom about seven times, so clearly Saint Paul the Apostle also held this belief and considered Wisdom as part of his Bible.”6 Sundberg lists some 152 New Testament passages that may depend on a deuterocanonical book, drawn from the margins of Nestle’s 22nd edition. Among the echoes: Romans 1:18–32 with Wisdom 13–14, Romans 9:21 with Wisdom 15:7, Matthew 27:43 with Wisdom 2:12–20, James 1:19 with Sirach 5:11.1 The New Testament never quotes Esther, Ecclesiastes, the Song of Songs, Ezra–Nehemiah, Obadiah or Nahum as Scripture, yet all are kept;7 it quotes 1 Enoch by name as prophecy (Jude 14–15) and the pagan poets Aratus, Epimenides and Menander, whom no one canonizes.8 The apostles’ Bible was the Greek one that carried these books, and Gallagher and Meade document the gap between the books the Fathers listed and the books they quoted.9 On this reading the apostles drew on the deuterocanon as part of their Scripture, and the absence of a citation formula leaves its standing undecided by that test.

  7. Protocanonical Old-Testament books not quoted (with a citation formula, as Scripture) anywhere in the New Testament include Esther, Ecclesiastes, the Song of Songs, Ezra–Nehemiah, Obadiah, and Nahum — a standard list noted in discussions of New Testament use of the Old (see, e.g., "New Testament use of the Old Testament," Wikipedia, and the data tabulated in Archer & Chirichigno, OT Quotations in the NT). These books are retained on all sides. Esther as the test case. Esther is never quoted as Scripture in the New Testament; it is the only book of the Hebrew Bible of which no copy or quotation has been identified among the Dead Sea Scrolls — true strictly only on the Jewish counting of Ezra–Nehemiah as one book, since only a fragment of Ezra (not Nehemiah) has been found (Sidnie White Crawford, "Has Every Book of the Bible Been Found Among the Dead Sea Scrolls?", Bible Review 12.5 [1996], digitized in the University of Nebraska faculty repository). Its ancient Jewish canonicity was the subject of a recorded rabbinic dispute over whether the scroll "renders the hands impure" — the idiom for inspired, sacred status — Samuel holding it did not, against R. Eliezer, R. Akiba, and others who adduced verses to prove it was composed "under the inspiration of the holy spirit" (b. Megillah 7a; on the wider uncertainty cf. "Bible Canon," Jewish Encyclopedia, citing also Sanhedrin 100a and Megillah 19b; and this timeline's Jamnia entry). And it is omitted or doubted across several of the earliest Christian canon-lists — absent from Melito (c. 170), demoted by Athanasius (Festal Letter 39, 367) to the books "to be read" rather than canonized, omitted entirely from Gregory of Nazianzus's canon poem, and admitted by Amphilochius of Iconium only as a book "some approve"; the lists are collated in Gallagher & Meade, The Biblical Canon Lists from Early Christianity (Oxford, 2017). Esther is retained on all sides. Joshua. The book of Joshua is likewise never cited with a Scripture-formula; Hebrews 4:8 engages it only by allusion, reasoning from Psalm 95 that the "rest" Joshua gave (Joshua 21:43–44) was not the final rest. The nearest thing to a formulaic citation, Hebrews 13:5 ("I will never leave thee, nor forsake thee"), follows the wording of Deuteronomy 31:6 as closely as Joshua 1:5 and is commonly assigned to Deuteronomy (the Joshua / Hebrews 4 point is made in this discussion of New-Testament use of Joshua). ↩

On the limits of arguing canon from citation, Edmon L. Gallagher & John D. Meade, The Biblical Canon Lists from Early Christianity: Texts and Analysis (Oxford, 2017), document the recurring gap between which books the Fathers listed as canonical and which they actually quoted as Scripture (the "list-versus-practice" tension — e.g. Origen, who lists a Hebrew twenty-two yet quotes Wisdom, Sirach, and Tobit as graphē); Gallagher likewise judges that "Jerome's scriptural citations are not a reliable guide for determining the books he would list as belonging to the canon" (Hebrew Scripture in Patristic Biblical Theory, Brill, 2012). ↩
