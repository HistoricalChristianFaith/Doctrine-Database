When Jesus and Paul spoke of “the Law and the Prophets,” did they mean a fixed Pharisaic canon matching the Protestant Old Testament?

Disputed reading · on the Old Testament canon · witness: the New Testament phrase “the Law and the Prophets”

Related readings: The Jewish canon and the deuterocanon · The absence of a canon dispute · The threefold division

The passage

As a Pharisee, the apostle Paul would have understood "the Law and the Prophets" to refer to only those writings found in Protestant Old Testaments today.

… What would the Pharisees have understood "the Law and the Prophets" to mean when they hear Jesus use this term? They would have understood it to be limited to only those writings found in the Protestant Old Testaments today.

— Steve Christie, Why Protestant Bibles Are Smaller (2019), on Romans 3:21 (“witnessed by the law and the prophets”) and Luke 16:14–16 (“The law and the prophets were until John,” with the Pharisees listening).1

The question

Paul was “a Pharisee, the son of a Pharisee,” and the Pharisaic-rabbinic stream became normative Judaism, transmitting a Hebrew table without the deuterocanon.2 What is disputed is whether that stream’s canon was already fixed, with the content of the Protestant thirty-nine, when Jesus and Paul used the two-part phrase “the Law and the Prophets,” or whether its third division was still open.

“The Law and the Prophets” named a closed Pharisaic canon

The Pharisees, and the Jewish parties generally, recognized a closed canon before the New Testament era; “the Law and the Prophets” named the whole of it.

Held by: R. Laird Harris (Presbyterian, JETS, 1966); Roger Beckwith (Anglican, 1985);3 Steve Christie (2019).1

Harris argues that the bipartite phrase is a complete designation of the whole Old Testament, not a two-part fragment that leaves the Writings outside.3 Beckwith argues that Pharisees, Sadducees and Essenes alike recognized a closed canon, that prophecy was held to have ceased, and that the non-Mosaic Scriptures had been divided into Prophets and Hagiographa, on his reconstruction by Judas Maccabeus c. 164 BC, against Ryle’s three-stage model.3 Christie relocates the argument from “all Jews” to the Pharisees: “What would the Pharisees have understood ‘the Law and the Prophets’ to mean when they hear Jesus use this term? They would have understood it to be limited to only those writings found in the Protestant Old Testaments today.”1 Jesus granted that the Pharisees “sit in Moses’ seat” and debated Scripture with them within a shared text; the rabbinic twenty-four books of Bava Batra 14b are the same content as the Protestant thirty-nine.2 The formula recurs at Matthew 5:17, 7:12, 11:13, 22:40 and Acts 24:14.1 On this reading the Scripture to which Jesus and Paul appealed in the hearing of Pharisees was the closed Hebrew canon, without the deuterocanon.

The two-part phrase left the third division open

No Jewish canon was closed in the apostolic age; “the Law and the Prophets” names two divisions, and the edge of the Writings was still disputed among the Pharisees’ successors.

Held by: Albert C. Sundberg Jr. (1964); John Barton (Oracles of God, 1986); Lee Martin McDonald (1996); Timothy H. Lim (2013).4

Sundberg held that no Jewish canon, Palestinian or Alexandrian, was closed in the first century; Lim that “throughout the post-exilic period up to around 100 CE there was not one official ‘canon’ accepted by all Jews,” the Pharisaic collection becoming the rabbinic canon after 70.4 Barton reads the dominance of “the Law and the Prophets” in the New Testament and the Mishnah as evidence that the Writings were a looser, open body rather than a defined section; the threefold form appears earlier, in the prologue to Ben Sira and at Luke 24:44, but Jesus and Paul use the two-part phrase in the texts Christie cites. Harris himself grants that the Dead Sea Scrolls show no threefold division.5 The rabbis were still disputing into the second century whether Ecclesiastes and the Song of Songs “defile the hands,” and the disputes reach Esther and Ezekiel; these concern books the stream already held, not additions.6 Augustine, who knew the Maccabees stood outside the Jewish table, held them “canonical, not by the Jews, but by the Church.”2 On this reading Jesus and Paul stood within a Hebrew-measured stream whose canon was still being settled, and their phrase does not by itself fix its extent.
