When a Father quotes a book “as Scripture,” does that show he held it canonical?

Disputed reading · on the Old Testament canon · witnesses: Athanasius, Epiphanius of Salamis

Related readings: The tiers of Athanasius’s Festal Letter · What the Fathers meant by “canon” · The “ecclesiastical” category · The Apostolic Fathers’ usage

The passage

Athanasius, Festal Letter 39 §7 (367), places Wisdom, Sirach, Judith and Tobit among books “not… included in the Canon, but appointed by the Fathers to be read”; in other works he introduces Wisdom with “Scripture taught us beforehand long ago,” quotes Wisdom 9:2 with “it is written,” cites Sirach 15:9 as “the words spoken by the Spirit,” and sets Tobit 4:18 under “it is written.”1

The question

A canon list is a deliberate enumeration; a citation formula marks a book as authoritative in passing. The two can diverge in a single Father, as in Athanasius and Epiphanius.1 What is disputed is which kind of evidence governs: whether a Father’s list is the controlling evidence for what he held canonical, or whether his use of a book to settle doctrine shows its standing, especially for the many Fathers who left no list. The reading that use to settle doctrine is use as Scripture is stated here from a Catholic apologist; academic literature holding it was not located.

Citation is not a canon verdict; the lists are the primary evidence

Quoting a book as Scripture marks it as authoritative or useful without ruling on its place in the canon; what a Father held canonical is shown by his list, and the early lists are Hebrew-sized.

Held by: Roger Beckwith (Anglican, The Old Testament Canon of the New Testament Church, 1985);2 John Meade (Protestant, canon-list scholar); Edmon L. Gallagher and John D. Meade (The Biblical Canon Lists from Early Christianity, 2017).3

Meade’s worked example is Athanasius: he quotes the deuterocanon as Scripture yet does not reckon it canon, so one must compare a Father’s usage against his list; whether readers “considered them as having authority or not is a separate question” from the canon.3 Epiphanius ranks Wisdom and Sirach “helpful and useful, but… not… of the recognized” and “of disputed canonicity,” and in the Panarion quotes Sirach with “Scripture says.”1 Gallagher and Meade assemble the deliberate catalogues, from Melito and Origen to Jerome, Rufinus and Augustine, as the primary evidence for the early canon’s shape, and note that the earliest authors “never cite the deuterocanonical books as scripture” by formula yet “do allude to them.”3 Beckwith audited the passages offered for the deuterocanon’s canonicity: “some of them are taken from the alternative Greek text of Ezra (1 Esdras) or from additions or appendices to Daniel, Jeremiah or some other canonical book… others of them are not quotations from the Apocrypha at all; and… of those which are, many do not give any indication that the book is regarded as Scripture.” Justin and Theophilus “never refer to any of the books of the Apocrypha at all,” and “until the final years of the second century, there is only one isolated example of any of the books being treated as Scripture.”2 The Fathers’ “Scripture” vocabulary is broad: Jude cites 1 Enoch as prophecy, and Paul quotes pagan poets. On this reading the canon is what a Father, asked to draw up the canon, drew up.

Use to settle doctrine is use as Scripture; lists record enumeration, not rejection

A Father who settles doctrine from a book under “it is written” treats it as Scripture; the patristic “canon” is a narrower, measured category; and most Fathers left no list.

Held by: Gary Michuta (Catholic apologist, Why Catholic Bibles Are Bigger, 2007, and debate with James White, 2004).4

On this reading the Athanasian datum separates list from usage in both directions: if quoting Wisdom as Scripture does not show he held it canonical, then his Hebrew-sized list does not show he rejected it as Scripture, and his own citations, addressed to bishops and an emperor, show he treated it as the word of God.5 The list records which books he enumerated in the “canon”; it does not record a judgement that the others were uninspired. Epiphanius lets the two Wisdoms ride at the end of a sweep he calls “all the sacred scriptures,” and presses Baruch as “the sacred scripture.”1 Most witnesses left no list: Clement of Rome, Irenaeus, Clement of Alexandria, Cyprian, Ambrose and Chrysostom drew up no catalogue, and for them usage is the only evidence there is.6 The Hebrew-sized lists reproduce an inherited count, “twenty-two… the number of the letters among the Hebrews,” passed from Melito and Origen to Cyril, Athanasius and Gregory of Nazianzus; Rufinus reproduces it and then files the deuterocanon as “Ecclesiastical,” read in the churches.7 Michuta holds that the “it is written” formula “is applied so often to Deuterocanonical books that it is impossible to list them all.”2 The Synod of Jerusalem (1672) records that “not always have all of these been considered on the same level as the rest.”8 On this reading a Father’s working use of a book to settle doctrine shows the standing it had for him, whatever its place in a learned enumeration.
