Did the Fathers rest doctrine only on the undisputed books, and what does their argument with the Jews from shared books show?

Disputed reading · on the Old Testament canon · witnesses: Origen, Jerome, the Thirty-Nine Articles, the Westminster Confession

Related readings: The “ecclesiastical” category · Citation “as Scripture” and canonical standing · How the early church received the deuterocanon

The passage

Jerome, Prologue to the Books of Solomon: the church “reads the books of Judith, Tobias, and the Maccabees, but does not receive them among the canonical Scriptures, so also one may read these two scrolls for the strengthening of the people, but not for confirming the authority of ecclesiastical dogmas.”1

Origen, Letter to Africanus §5 (c. 240): “I make it my endeavour not to be ignorant of their various readings, lest in my controversies with the Jews I should quote to them what is not found in their copies, and that I may make some use of what is found there, even although it should not be in our Scriptures.”2

The question

Some Fathers distinguish books read for edification from books that establish doctrine, and some, in argument with Jews, keep to texts the Jews receive. What is disputed is whether these practices show a settled judgement that doctrine rests only on the undisputed books, ranking the deuterocanon lower, or a restraint suited to argument with opponents who granted only the shared books.

Arguing from the opponent’s books, while keeping the disputed books as the church’s Scripture

When disputing with Jews a Christian argues from what is in their copies; that is a rule of debate, and the disputed books remain Scripture for the church.

Held by: Origen (Letter to Africanus §§4–5, c. 240), stating his own practice.2

Origen keeps to the Jews’ copies in controversy so that they “will not, as is their manner, scornfully laugh at Gentile believers for their ignorance of the true reading as they have them.” In the same letter he refuses Africanus’s proposal to drop Susanna and the Greek additions: are we “to reject as spurious the copies in use in our Churches… [to] put away the sacred books current among them”? He appeals to the “Providence which in the sacred Scriptures has ministered to the edification of all the Churches of Christ.”2 On this reading the rule belongs to the argument, not to the canon. Justin Martyr’s Dialogue with Trypho, the central surviving work of the genre, presses on Trypho readings the Jews reject, the Septuagint’s “virgin” of Isaiah 7:14 and the long form of Psalm 96:10, “the Lord hath reigned from the wood,” and charges that the Jews “have taken away” passages from Esdras and Jeremiah.3 Outside such disputes the Fathers use the disputed books for doctrine: Hippolytus reads Wisdom as “the prophecy of Solomon which speaketh of Christ”; Cyprian cites Tobit and Sirach as “Holy Scripture”; Basil draws on Judith 9:5–6 and Baruch 3:3–4 in On the Holy Spirit; Jerome himself quotes Sirach 22:6 as “Holy scripture says” and Wisdom 6:7 under scriptum est.4 On this reading the Fathers argued from shared premises when their opponents required it and used the disputed books as Scripture within the church.

Doctrine rests on the canonical books; the others are read for edification

The church reads the disputed books for the strengthening of the people but does not confirm dogma from them, a graded standing the Fathers drew and the Reformed confessions adopted.

Held by: Jerome (Prologue to the Books of Solomon, c. 398); the Thirty-Nine Articles (Article VI, 1571); the Westminster Confession (1.3, 1647);1 John Meade (Protestant, canon-list scholar), in a careful form.5

Jerome draws the line in words: the church reads Judith, Tobit and the Maccabees “but does not receive them among the canonical Scriptures,” and Wisdom and Sirach may be read “for the strengthening of the people, but not for confirming the authority of ecclesiastical dogmas.” The Thirty-Nine Articles cite him by name for this use, and the Westminster Confession places the books outside the canon of Scripture.1 Behind Jerome stands a graded tier that several Fathers run: Athanasius’s books “appointed to be read,” set apart from the “canonized”; Rufinus’s ecclesiastici, read in the churches but not appealed to for the confirmation of doctrine; the “useful” middle rank of Cyril of Jerusalem and Epiphanius. The tier is defined by its exclusion from the doctrine-proving canon.6 Meade traces this patristic distinction between a doctrine-proving canon and an edifying tier.5 In argument with the Jews the same instinct appears: Origen keeps his proofs to “what is found” in the Jews’ copies, the books both sides receive.2 On this reading the Fathers’ own distinction between reading and proving, stated most clearly by Jerome, assigns the disputed books to edification and reserves doctrine to the undisputed books.
