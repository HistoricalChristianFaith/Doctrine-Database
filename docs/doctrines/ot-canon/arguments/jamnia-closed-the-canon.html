Did a council at Jamnia (Yavneh), c. AD 90, close or affirm the Hebrew canon?

Disputed reading · on the Old Testament canon · witness: the “Council of Jamnia”

Related readings: Was the rabbinic narrowing directed against Christians? · Josephus’s twenty-two books · The Jewish canon and the deuterocanon

The passage

All the Holy Scriptures defile the hands. The Song of Songs and Ecclesiastes defile the hands… Rabbi Akiva said… the Song of Songs is the holiest of all.

— Mishnah Yadayim 3:5, as quoted on the timeline’s Jamnia page.1

“[A.D.] 90 and 118: Councils of Jamnia give final affirmation to the Old Testament canon (39 books).”

— Christianity Today, “How We Got Our Bible” timeline.2

The question

After AD 70 a rabbinic academy and court sat at Yavneh, and the Mishnah preserves debate there on whether the Song of Songs and Ecclesiastes “defile the hands.” What is disputed is whether this amounted to a council that closed or affirmed the Hebrew canon about AD 90, dating a fixed Jewish canon to the first century, or to discussion of particular books without a canon-deciding act.

A synod at Jamnia fixed the Hebrew canon

The rabbis at Yavneh, after the fall of the Temple, settled the Hebrew canon in council, at a time when the Christian movement was using a Greek Bible.

Held by: Heinrich Graetz (Jewish historian, appendix to Kohelet, 1871);3 Bible.org; Christianity Today’s “How We Got Our Bible” timeline.2

Graetz inferred from the rabbinic debates over particular books that a synod convened at Yavneh around AD 70–90 and there fixed the Hebrew canon, partly in reaction to the rising Christian movement and its Greek Bible.3 The rabbinic academy at Yavneh was the centre from which, under Yohanan ben Zakkai and his successors, Judaism was reconstituted after AD 70, and the Mishnah places there a decision on the holiness of disputed books: “All the Holy Scriptures defile the hands. The Song of Songs and Ecclesiastes defile the hands.”1 The canon that emerged from that stream is the Hebrew-sized one that never included Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch or the Maccabees.1 Bible.org states the result: “The Council of Jamnia (A.D. 90) officially recognized our 39 Old Testament books,” adding that Josephus (AD 95) “indicated that the 39 books were recognized as authoritative.” Christianity Today’s timeline records “[A.D.] 90 and 118: Councils of Jamnia give final affirmation to the Old Testament canon (39 books).”2 On this reading the end of the first century saw the Jewish canon closed at Jamnia, without the Greek books.

Discussion of particular books, without a canon-deciding council

No ancient source reports a council at Jamnia deciding the canon; the recorded debates concern books already received, and the deuterocanon is not mentioned.

Held by: W. M. Christie (Journal of Theological Studies, 1925); Jack P. Lewis (Protestant, “What Do We Mean by Jabneh?”, 1964); Sid Z. Leiman (The Canonization of Hebrew Scripture, 1976); Albert C. Sundberg Jr.4

Christie first disputed the synod; Lewis’s paper, read before the Society of Biblical Literature in 1962, examined the sources; Leiman and Sundberg reached kindred conclusions. Sundberg summarizes: “canonicity was not the issue and the debate was not connected with Jabneh… No book is ever mentioned in the sources as being excluded from the canon at Jabneh.” F. F. Bruce judged it “probably unwise to talk as if there were a Council or Synod of Jamnia.”4 On this reading the Mishnah, Tosefta and Talmuds report no canon-deciding council. The Yavneh discussions concern the Song of Songs and Ecclesiastes, books already received, and the degree of their holiness, settled by Akiva’s “the Song of Songs is the holiest of all”; the dispute continued into the second century, and the recorded discussions never name Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch or the Maccabees.5,6 The reading is shared across confessional lines: Answers in Genesis, arguing for the sixty-six-book canon, states that “it’s now generally accepted that Jamnia actually wasn’t a council, it certainly didn’t pronounce on the Jewish canon,” and Catholic Answers titles its treatment “The Council That Wasn’t.”7,3 On this reading Yavneh was a centre of rabbinic discussion about certain books, and the Hebrew canon’s closure is not dated by a council there.
