Do the Apostolic Fathers use the deuterocanonical books as Scripture?

Disputed reading · on the Old Testament canon · witnesses: Clement of Rome, Polycarp of Smyrna, the Didache

Related readings: How the early church received the deuterocanon · Citation “as Scripture” and canonical standing · The apostles’ Greek Bible

The passage

So, what do we find in the Apostolic Fathers? I'd like to refer you to a doctoral dissertation written by R. J. Brabban, who, after an exhaustive study of the Apostolic Fathers, concludes that the Deuterocanonical books: "Were indeed known and used in a manner identical to those books now recognized as canonical or Scripture." … Brabban further concludes that the Deuterocanonical books are never refuted by the Apostolic Fathers, are never reduced to inferior sources, are never dismissed as inaccurate, are never treated as less than Scripture.

— Gary Michuta, opening statement in debate with James White (2004).1

The question

The Apostolic Fathers (c. 96–150) wrote before any surviving Christian list of Old Testament books, and they draw on Wisdom, Judith, Tobit and Sirach. What is disputed is the standing their usage implies: whether they treat these books as they treat the books now received as Scripture, or whether the usage is occasional, partly historical, and rarely marked by a Scripture formula.

The books are known and used as the canonical books are

A book-by-book study of the Apostolic Fathers finds the disputed books used in the same way as the books now counted canonical, and nowhere set apart or demoted.

Held by: George Reid (Catholic, “Canon of the Old Testament,” Catholic Encyclopedia, 1908); Ralph J. Brabban II (Baylor University dissertation, 1984);2 Gary Michuta (Catholic apologist, Why Catholic Bibles Are Bigger, 2007, and debate, 2004).3,1

Reid finds that the sub-Apostolic writings “contain implicit quotations from or allusions to all the deuterocanonicals” with a few exceptions, and holds that “No unfavourable argument can be drawn from the loose, implicit character of these citations, since these Apostolic Fathers quote the protocanonical Scriptures in precisely the same manner.”3

Brabban concludes, as Michuta quotes him, that the deuterocanonical books “were indeed known and used in a manner identical to those books now recognized as canonical or Scripture,” and that the Apostolic Fathers’ working “‘canon’ must have included Wisdom, Sirach, Judith, Esther (expanded version), Tobit, 4 Esdras, (1) Enoch, an expanded Jeremiah, and perhaps others as well.”3 The texts bear it out. Clement of Rome (c. 96) joins two verses of Wisdom (12:12; 11:21) into his argument in the same breath as Matthew and the Psalms, and holds up “the blessed Judith” beside Esther as a pattern of faith. Polycarp (c. 125) urges almsgiving with Tobit’s maxim, “alms delivers from death” (Tobit 4:10; 12:9), within a chain of phrases from 1 Peter and Isaiah. The Didache places Sirach 4:31 in its “Two Ways.”4 Michuta adds Barnabas 6:7, read as a prophecy of Christ’s sufferings from Wisdom 2:12, noting in his own apparatus that the connection is “disputed”; the Shepherd of Hermas appears to use 2 Maccabees 7:28 on creation from nothing.3,4 Brabban finds the disputed books never refuted, never reduced to inferior sources, and never dismissed as inaccurate.1 On this reading, which Michuta takes as the earliest stratum of his thesis that before Jerome “every early Christian writer who used the Deuterocanonical books did so in a manner commensurate with sacred Scripture,”1 the first post-apostolic generation used these books as Scripture.

The early usage is occasional, often historical, and rarely marked as Scripture

An audit of the passages adduced finds many to be Greek additions to canonical books, echoes rather than quotations, or historical uses, with few treating a disputed book as Scripture.

Held by: Roger Beckwith (Anglican, The Old Testament Canon of the New Testament Church, 1985, pp. 388–389); James White (Reformed Baptist apologist, debate with Michuta, 2004).5

Beckwith examines the second-century passages offered for the deuterocanon’s canonicity and finds that many are drawn from the Greek additions to canonical books rather than the disputed books proper, or are not quotations, or do not “give any indication that the book is regarded as Scripture.” On his tally Justin Martyr and Theophilus of Antioch never cite the apocrypha; 3 and 4 Maccabees are never referred to; Judith and 1–2 Maccabees are “little used, and only as historical sources”; and “until the final years of the second century, there is only one isolated example of any of the books being treated as Scripture.” Beckwith and others take the source of Barnabas 6:7 to be Isaiah 3:9–10 rather than Wisdom.5,4 White draws the conclusion for the debate: those who applied “the proper use of the citation ‘it is written’” to these books “were actually in the minority at that point, not the majority,” while “there were certainly some who, using the Greek Septuagint, believed that it was canonical — no one’s ever denied that.”5 Beckwith’s survey ranges over second-century writers generally, not the Apostolic Fathers alone. The Apostolic Fathers drew up no lists and used no vocabulary of rank for any book, and the grading terms (“canonical,” “ecclesiastical,” “read but not for confirming doctrine”) appear in Origen, Athanasius and Rufinus. On this reading the earliest usage shows acquaintance with and use of these books, varying from book to book, without a settled judgement on their standing as Scripture.
