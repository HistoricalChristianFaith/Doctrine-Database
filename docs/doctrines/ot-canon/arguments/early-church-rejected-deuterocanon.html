How did the early church receive the deuterocanonical books: as Scripture, below the canon, or outside it?

Disputed reading · on the Old Testament canon · witnesses: Melito, Origen, Athanasius, Jerome, the African councils

Related readings: Citation “as Scripture” and canonical standing · The tiers of Athanasius’s Festal Letter · The Apostolic Fathers’ usage · The “ecclesiastical” category

The passage

The earliest Christian Old Testament lists are Hebrew-sized: Melito (c. 170), learning the books “accurately” in the East; Origen (c. 240), “twenty-two, as the Hebrews have handed them down”; Cyril of Jerusalem (c. 350), “have nothing to do with the apocryphal writings”; Athanasius (367), Epiphanius (c. 385), Jerome (c. 390) and Rufinus (c. 404).1 The same period’s Greek Bibles, Codices Vaticanus, Sinaiticus and Alexandrinus, carry Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach and the Maccabees among the other books,2 and Clement of Rome (c. 96), Polycarp (c. 125), Cyprian, Basil, Chrysostom, the African councils (393, 397, 419), Augustine and Innocent I (405) use or list them.3

The question

What is disputed is how these two bodies of evidence fit together: whether the primitive church held a Hebrew-sized canon, so that the later reception of the deuterocanon is a development; whether the church read the books throughout while ranking them unevenly; whether the books were part of the church’s Scripture from the start and the shorter lists a learned minority; or whether the church kept a fixed core with further books read beside it, their rank left open.

The early canon was Hebrew-sized, and the later reception is a development

The Fathers who investigated the Old Testament’s extent fixed it at the Hebrew books and set the others below or outside it; their later reception as canonical belongs to the councils and to Trent.

Held by: John Calvin (Reformed, Antidote to Trent, 1547); Archibald Alexander (Presbyterian, 1851); Roger Beckwith (Anglican, The Old Testament Canon of the New Testament Church, 1985); James White (Reformed Baptist apologist, 2004).4,5

Calvin faulted Trent for giving the books “an authority which they never before possessed,” while granting that “in the Council of Carthage” and in Augustine the contrary view was held, so that “the point was then undecided.”4 Beckwith reads the Hebrew-canon Fathers as preserving the apostolic canon.4 The earliest list, Melito’s, came from an inquiry made in the East, “where these things were preached and done,” and omits Tobit, Judith, Sirach, Baruch and the Maccabees; Origen counts twenty-two; Cyril charges his catechumens to “have nothing to do with the apocryphal writings”; Athanasius, Epiphanius and Rufinus set the disputed books in a tier below the canon; Jerome draws the hardest line and supplies the word apocrypha.1 On this reading the Fathers who investigated the Hebrew enumeration consistently ranked these books below it. White names Hilary, Cyril, Athanasius, Gregory of Nazianzus, Basil, Epiphanius, Theophilus and Rufinus as having “rejected the freestanding Apocrypha as fully canonical in their writings,” and adds a caution about the earliest evidence: “those early writings are so fragmentary that many books of the Apocrypha are never even made reference to… it is a very large step from a few references to saying that all held a particular perspective.”5 F. F. Bruce (1954) holds the theoretical distinction and grants that the Greek Fathers “in practice made little distinction”; Bruce Shelley grants that the West “under the influence of Augustine” usually received the books.6,4 On this reading the canon of the earliest learned witnesses is the Hebrew one, and the deuterocanon’s formal entry into the canon came later, in the West.

A divided reception: read throughout, ranked unevenly

The church read the books from the start; a learned stream ranked them below a Hebrew-measured canon while others used them as Scripture, and the unevenness was acknowledged before it was settled.

Held by: the Synod of Jerusalem (Orthodox, Confession of Dositheus, 1672), which grants the uneven ranking and holds the books genuine Scripture;7 Bruce M. Metzger (Protestant, An Introduction to the Apocrypha, 1957); Edmon L. Gallagher and John D. Meade (The Biblical Canon Lists from Early Christianity, 2017).8

The Synod of Jerusalem acknowledged that “not always have all of these been considered on the same level as the rest,” and received the books on the Church’s delivering tradition.7 Gallagher and Meade document both the Hebrew-sized lists and the recurring gap between the books the Fathers listed and the books they used as Scripture, and read Melito’s and Origen’s lists as reports of the Jewish reckoning rather than prescriptive Christian rulings.8 The same Fathers who list a Hebrew canon read the other books. Origen refuses to “reject as spurious the copies in use in our Churches”; Athanasius files the books “to be read” and cites Tobit 4:18 under “it is written”; Cyril cites Baruch as “the Prophet”; Hilary cites 2 Maccabees 7:28 “as the Prophet says”; Gregory of Nazianzus, with the strictest list, cites Judith 5:6 “in the Scripture”; Rufinus introduces Sirach with “ita enim Scriptura dicit”; Gregory the Great calls 1 Maccabees “though not Canonical, yet brought out for the edifying of the Church.”9 Bruce’s formula states the pattern: the Greek Fathers distinguished the classes “in theory” but “in practice made little distinction,” and the Latin Fathers, Jerome excepted, “made no distinction either in theory or in practice.”6 On this reading the early church held a graded inheritance: a Hebrew-measured core and books read beside it, whose rank was disputed for centuries.

The books belonged to the church’s Scripture from the start

From the first post-apostolic writer onward the books were read and used as Scripture in the church’s Greek Bible; the Hebrew-sized lists are a learned minority, and the late development is their removal.

Held by: George Reid (Catholic, “Canon of the Old Testament,” Catholic Encyclopedia, 1908); Gary Michuta (Catholic apologist, debate with James White, 2004).10

Reid holds that the sub-Apostolic writers quote the disputed books in the same “loose, implicit” manner as the protocanonical Scriptures, that Athanasius “practically admitted the deutero books to a Scriptural dignity, as is evident from his general usage,” and that “while eminent scholars and theorists were thus depreciating the additional writings, the official attitude of the Latin Church, always favourable to them, kept the majestic tenor of its way.”10

Michuta holds that until Jerome “every early Christian writer who used the Deuterocanonical books did so in a manner commensurate with sacred Scripture.”10 The church’s Old Testament was the Greek Septuagint, “the most frequently used version of the Old Testament in the early Church,” and the great codices interleave the disputed books with the rest.2 Use precedes any list: Clement of Rome (c. 96) sets Wisdom 12:12 beside Matthew and the Psalms and holds up “the blessed Judith”; Polycarp cites Tobit’s “alms delivers from death”; the Muratorian Fragment ranks Wisdom among the received books; Eusebius, who transmits Melito’s and Origen’s lists, calls Wisdom “the Scripture” and Baruch “inspired words”; Aphrahat reads the Maccabean martyrdoms as fulfilled prophecy; Basil quotes Sirach 27:11 as “Scripture”; Chrysostom adduces Sirach “from the Divine Scriptures.” The African councils list Tobit, Judith and the Maccabees among the Canonical Scriptures, Augustine receives them by the rule of churchly reception, and Innocent I (405) lists them with no second tier.3 Melito’s and Origen’s catalogues report how the Jews counted their books.8 On this reading the deuterocanon was the church’s inheritance in its Greek Bible from the first, and the Western councils recorded a reception already in place.

A fixed core, with further books read in the Church

The Church has kept the books of the Jewish Bible as a fixed core and read the further books of the Septuagint beside them, with their rank left open.

Held by: Eugen J. Pentiuc (Orthodox, The Old Testament in Eastern Orthodox Tradition, 2014).11

Pentiuc traces the Eastern Orthodox position on the canon from Ecclesiasticus through the early Christian writers to the synthesis of John of Damascus, and finds that “the current Orthodox position on canon still looks very similar to the Damascene’s concluding remarks”: strict regarding the thirty-nine books of the Jewish Bible, and flexible regarding the anaginōskomena found in the Septuagint.11 The early lists name the core: Melito’s, Origen’s, Cyril’s and Athanasius’s twenty-two books, with Baruch carried inside Jeremiah. Beside the core the same writers keep the further books: Athanasius’s books “appointed by the Fathers to be read,” Origen’s “copies in use in our Churches,” and the Wisdom books that John of Damascus calls “virtuous and noble” though “not counted,” while citing Wisdom as “the divine Scripture.”11 On this reading the early church’s reception is the inheritance the Orthodox Church still keeps: a fixed core, and books read in the Church beside it.
