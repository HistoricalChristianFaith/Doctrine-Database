What did the label “apocrypha” signify when applied to the deuterocanon between Jerome and the Council of Trent?

Disputed reading · on the Old Testament canon · witnesses: Athanasius, Jerome, Cajetan, the Council of Trent

Related readings: What the Fathers meant by “canon” · The “ecclesiastical” category · The medieval reception of Jerome’s prefaces

The passage

For example, the word Apocrypha by the time of the 15th century had become so twisted—this is usually due to medieval writers who tried to make Jerome fit into an orthodox stance—that they would twist his words on calling the deuterocanonical books Apocrypha to mean something like "inspired scripture that is somehow hidden." By the time of the Council of Trent, the word Apocrypha had been so twisted that you would actually find fathers in the Council of Trent who argued for the full divinity and canonicity of these books, calling them Apocrypha.

— Gary Michuta, in debate with James White (2004).1

Jerome’s Prologus Galeatus (c. 391): “what is outside of them must be placed aside among the Apocryphal writings” (quicquid extra hos est, inter apocrifa seponendum).2

The question

Greek apokryphos means “hidden,” and the word has carried several senses.3 What is disputed is what a late-medieval or sixteenth-century writer signified in calling a deuterocanonical book “apocrypha,” and so whether the label, where it occurs, can be read as a judgement that the book stood below the canon. The reading that the word had shifted toward a sense compatible with canonicity is stated here from a Catholic apologist; academic literature holding it was not located.

The label marks the books as below the canon, read but not for doctrine

From the fourth century to the Reformation, writers who define the word use “apocrypha” of the disputed books to place them below canonical Scripture.

Held by: Bruce Metzger (Protestant, An Introduction to the Apocrypha, 1957), on the Reformation-era usage;4 David Brakke (Harvard Theological Review, 2010), on the fourth-century sense.5

Athanasius’s Festal Letter 39 (367) sets three tiers: the canonical books; books “appointed by the Fathers to be read” (Wisdom, Sirach, Esther, Judith, Tobit, the Didache, the Shepherd), which he does not call apocrypha; and the apocrypha, “the invention of heretics, who indeed write them whenever they wish, bestowing upon them their approval, and assigning to them a date.”5 Jerome applies the term to the deuterocanon itself: Wisdom, Sirach, Judith, Tobit and the Shepherd “are not in the canon” (non sunt in canone).2 On the eve of Trent, Cajetan, following Jerome, calls these books “not canonical — that is, not in the nature of a rule for confirming matters of faith — yet they may be called canonical… for the edification of the faithful.” Luther’s Bible of 1534 heads the section “Apocrypha: that is, books which are not held equal to the Holy Scriptures, and yet are profitable and good to read,” and Coverdale (1535) calls them “not reckoned to be of like authority with the other books of the Bible.”4 At Trent, Girolamo Seripando, following Jerome, Rufinus and Cajetan, proposed classing the deuterocanon as canonici et ecclesiastici, “suited for the edification of the people, but… not sufficient to prove a dogma”; the decree of 8 April 1546 received all the listed books “as sacred and canonical” and used the word apocrypha of none of them. Jedin’s account of the acta records no Trent father calling the books “apocrypha” while affirming their canonicity.6 On this reading, where a writer of this period calls a deuterocanonical book “apocrypha,” he places it below the canon, as a book read for edification.

The word’s meaning shifted, so the label does not by itself signal rejection

“Apocrypha” began as an honorific “hidden” and by the fifteenth century could be used of books held to be inspired, so each use must be read in its writer’s own sense.

Held by: Gary Michuta (Catholic apologist; debate with James White, 2004, and The Case for the Deuterocanon, 2015).1,7

Michuta’s published statement is that “the meaning of term ‘apocrypha’ has changed over time. So varied are its meanings that even a brief historical sketch would go beyond the scope of this book. The word apocrypha means ‘hidden’ or perhaps ‘not recognized.’”7 The word’s earliest religious use is honorific: 4 Ezra 14 has Ezra publish the twenty-four public books and keep back “the seventy that were written last” for “the wise among your people,” since “in them is the spring of understanding,” and Metzger opens his introduction on the “hidden” or esoteric origin of the term.3 The Catholic Encyclopedia notes that the Fathers “have sometimes entirely lost sight of the etymology” and that the word runs through several senses.3 In the 2004 debate Michuta stated a stronger form: that medieval writers seeking to reconcile Jerome with the received canon read his “apocrypha” to mean “inspired scripture that is somehow hidden,” and that “you would actually find fathers in the Council of Trent who argued for the full divinity and canonicity of these books, calling them Apocrypha.”1 His published books state the shift in the general form and refer the history to Why Catholic Bibles Are Bigger.7 On this reading a medieval or Reformation-era writer’s use of “apocrypha” is not self-interpreting, and the shorter-canon appeals to the label depend on recovering each writer’s own sense of the word.
