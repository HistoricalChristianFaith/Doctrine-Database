Does the deuterocanon’s long-disputed standing before Trent, and Trent’s late definition, tell against the books?

Disputed reading · on the Old Testament canon · witnesses: Jerome, Gregory the Great, Cajetan, the Council of Trent

Related readings: Did Trent add the books? · Was Trent the first universal, binding definition? · The medieval reception

The passage

It wasn't until the Council of Trent in 1546 where the Catholic church canonized, or considered, these books as being on the same level of inspiration as all of the other books. Before then, these books were in the Bible, but it was generally considered that they were not on the same level… so if the majority of Roman Catholic scholars saw enough inconsistency and errors in these books for a thousand years that kept them from really viewing these books as being divinely inspired, then … that says something about whether we should accept these books as being inspired by God.

— Allen Parr.1

The question

A learned stream ranked the disputed books below the canon from Jerome to Cajetan, and Trent (1546) was the first to close the question under anathema.2,3 What is disputed is whether that long doubt and late decree count against the books’ standing as Scripture, or whether a late definition ratifies a long reception and the doubt was one current among others.

The long doubt and the late decree count against the books

For a millennium the learned tradition held the books below the inspired canon; their equal standing was decreed only at Trent, by a council that set aside that tradition.

Held by: B. F. Westcott (Anglican, 1875);4 Roger Beckwith (Anglican, 1985);5 Allen Parr (evangelical teacher).1

Westcott calls Session 4 a “fatal decree” that “gave a new aspect to the whole question of the Canon,” “ratified by fifty-three prelates, among whom there was not one German, not one scholar distinguished for historical learning, not one who was fitted by special study for the examination of a subject in which the truth could only be determined by the voice of antiquity.”4 The voice of antiquity, on this reading, is the Hieronymian current: Jerome set the books “among the apocrypha… not… for confirming the authority of ecclesiastical dogmas”; Rufinus made them “Ecclesiastical”; Gregory the Great called 1 Maccabees not canonical; Hugh of St. Victor and Nicholas of Lyra repeated the canon of the Hebrews; Cajetan followed Jerome on the eve of the council.2,6 Beckwith reads the Hebrew-canon Fathers as preserving the apostolic canon.5 Parr: “Before then, these books were in the Bible, but it was generally considered that they were not on the same level,” and he grounds the long doubt in “inconsistency and errors” that kept scholars from viewing the books as inspired.1 The Thirty-Nine Articles speak of “those canonical Books… of whose authority was never any doubt in the Church.”7 On this reading a standing doubted for a thousand years and decreed only in 1546 is not the standing of the undoubted Scriptures.

A late definition ratifies a long reception, and the doubt was a minority current

Councils define when a question is forced; Trent’s decree closed a dispute over books the wider church had long read and received, and its timing says nothing about inspiration.

Held by: George Reid (Catholic, “Canon of the Old Testament,” Catholic Encyclopedia, 1908); Gary Michuta (Catholic apologist, Why Catholics Have More, 2017); Trent Horn (Catholic apologist, “Did the Catholic Church Add Books to the Bible?”).8

Reid holds that “while eminent scholars and theorists were thus depreciating the additional writings, the official attitude of the Latin Church, always favourable to them, kept the majestic tenor of its way,” and that the Damasine list and the canon of Innocent I (405) “contain all the deuterocanonicals, without any distinction, and are identical with the catalogue of Trent.”8

Michuta and Horn hold that a conciliar definition ratifies a long reception rather than inventing it.8 On this reading the date of a definition measures when the church was forced to rule. The same Session 4 decree that lists the Old Testament also enumerates the New, “the four Gospels… the Acts of the Apostles… fourteen Epistles of Paul… the Apocalypse of John,” and binds the whole under one anathema; the lateness of that definition is not taken to tell against the New Testament books.9 The doubt ran alongside a wider reception: the Septuagint carried the books, the great codices interleave them with the protocanon, and the African councils, Augustine, Innocent I and Florence received them, while the Hebrew-minded Fathers cited them as Scripture in their own works.10 Jerome grounded his tiering on the Hebrew reckoning of the canon.6 Luther’s 1534 Bible first gathered the books into a separate “Apocrypha,” and the Reformers presented their removal as the recovery of an older canon.7 On this reading Trent closed, under controversy, a question of rank about books the church had received for a millennium.
