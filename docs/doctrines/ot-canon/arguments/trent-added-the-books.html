Did the Council of Trent add the deuterocanonical books to the Bible in 1546, or receive books already there?

Disputed reading · on the Old Testament canon · witnesses: the Council of Trent, the Council of Florence

Related readings: Was Trent the first universal, binding definition? · Trent and purgatory · Was the deuterocanon held below Scripture until Trent? · Did Luther remove the books?

The passage

Prior to the Council of Trent in 1546, the historical Christian church did not consider the Deuterocanonical books to be divinely inspired sacred Scripture… In the Council of Trent, a small group of men met and decreed that the Deuterocanonical books were inspired Scripture, on the same level as any other Scripture… The Catholic church added the Deuterocanonical books to the list of inspired scriptures in 1546.

— a popular Protestant summary of the claim.1

Council of Trent, Session 4 (8 April 1546): the Synod, “following the examples of the orthodox Fathers, receives and venerates with an equal affection of piety, and reverence, all the books both of the Old and of the New Testament,” and anathematizes whoever does not receive them “as sacred and canonical… as they have been used to be read in the Catholic Church.”2

The question

A learned stream from Jerome to Cajetan ranked the disputed books below the canon, and Trent’s decree was the first to attach an anathema to the list.3 What is disputed is how to describe Trent’s act: as adding books that had stood outside the Bible, or as binding a reception of books already in it. The reading that Trent added the books is stated here from popular apologetic sources; academic literature holding it was not located.

Trent received and bound books the church already read

The books stood in the church’s Bible from the Septuagint onward, a council professed them in 1442, and Trent describes its own act as reception.

Held by: George Reid (Catholic, “Canon of the Old Testament,” Catholic Encyclopedia, 1908); Catholic Answers (Catholic apologetics apostolate); the Council of Trent (Session 4, 1546), in its own account of its decree.2,4

The decree speaks of following “the examples of the orthodox Fathers” and of books received “as they have been used to be read in the Catholic Church, and as they are contained in the old Latin vulgate edition.”2,5 The books stood in the Septuagint and in Codices Vaticanus, Sinaiticus and Alexandrinus, interleaved among the protocanonical books.6 The Vulgate carried them in the West from c. 405; Hippo, Carthage, Innocent I and Augustine named them.5 The Council of Florence, in Cantate Domino (4 February 1442), “professes that one and the same God is the author of the Old and New Testament… since it accepts and venerates their books,” and lists Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Ecclesiasticus and two books of the Maccabees, with the same receptive verb Trent repeats.7 The Council of Ephesus (431) wrote, “Forasmuch as the divinely inspired Scripture says, ‘Do all things with advice’,” a line Schaff’s note traces to Ecclesiasticus.8 Reid holds that the larger canon “passed through the Apostles’ hands to the church tacitly, by way of their usage and whole attitude toward its components,” and Catholic Answers that Trent “certainly did not add those books to the canon” but “infallibly reiterated what the Church had long taught.”4 On this reading Trent closed, under anathema, a question of rank about books long in the church’s Bible.

Trent added the books to Scripture

Before 1546 the historical church did not treat the disputed books as inspired Scripture; a council in the sixteenth century voted them in.

Held by: Mark Driscoll (2015); Mike Winger; Todd Friel (Wretched); Bible.org.1

Driscoll: “the Catholic Church voted to insert new books into the Bible, more than a millennium after the Old Testament canon had been closed.” Friel: “Protestants did not take out books, it was the Roman Catholic Church in the middle of the sixteenth century who added them.”1 The popular summary puts it: “Prior to the Council of Trent in 1546, the historical Christian church did not consider the Deuterocanonical books to be divinely inspired sacred Scripture.”1 The dissent before Trent was real and learned: Jerome set the books “among the apocrypha… not… for confirming the authority of ecclesiastical dogmas”; Rufinus filed them “Ecclesiastical”; Gregory the Great called 1 Maccabees not canonical; Cajetan followed Jerome on the eve of the council.3 Luther’s 1534 Bible set them apart as “Apocrypha — that is, books which are not held equal to the Holy Scriptures.”9 On this reading the decree of 1546, binding the books under anathema as equal to the rest, made them Scripture in a way the church had not held before.
