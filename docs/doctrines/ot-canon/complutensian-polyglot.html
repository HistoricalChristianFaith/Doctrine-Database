The Complutensian Polyglot (Cardinal Ximénez) on the Old Testament Canon

Dates: Old Testament volumes printed at Alcalá de Henares (Complutum) 1514–17, sanctioned by Pope Leo X (privilege traditionally dated 22 March 1520), published c. 1522; edited under Cardinal Francisco Jiménez de Cisneros, called Ximénez (c. 1436–1517) · Wikipedia
Witness: The first printed polyglot of the whole Bible, a project of Cardinal Ximénez published with a privilege from Leo X, sets the books outside the Hebrew canon apart from the rest: its preface says the Church receives them "for the edification of the people" rather than "for confirming the authority of ecclesiastical dogmas," and it prints them without a Hebrew column. Leo X's grant was a privilege to print.

Claims & evidence

The preface Ad lectorem on the books "outside the canon"

At vero libri extra canonem, quos Ecclesia potius ad aedificationem populi quam ad autoritatem ecclesiasticorum dogmatum confirmandam recipit, Graecam tantum habent scripturam, sed cum duplici latina interpretatione, altera beati Hieronymi, altera interlineari de verbo ad verbum, eo modo quo in caeteris.

— Complutensian Polyglot Bible, second preface Ad lectorem ("To the Reader"), c. 1517; facsimile at the Internet Archive.1 In English: "But the books outside the canon, which the Church receives for the edification of the people rather than for confirming the authority of ecclesiastical dogmas, have only the Greek text, but with a double Latin translation — one of blessed Jerome's, the other interlinear, word for word, in the manner of the rest." The sentence does two things. It states a rank — these books are "outside the canon," received to edify rather than to establish doctrine — and it explains a layout: because they have no Hebrew original, they are printed in Greek with a double Latin column instead of the three-column Hebrew/Latin/Greek arrangement used for the canonical books. The typography marks the class as well as the words: a reader could see which books the preface placed "outside the canon" by the absence of the Hebrew.

The source of the formula

"Received… for the edification of the people, not for confirming the authority of ecclesiastical dogmas" follows almost verbatim Jerome's preface to the books of Solomon, where he says the Church reads Wisdom and Sirach "for the edifying of the people, not for confirming the authority of ecclesiastical dogmas" (ad aedificationem plebis, non ad auctoritatem ecclesiasticorum dogmatum confirmandam).2 Jerome's distinction also appears in the medieval Glossa Ordinaria and, in 1532, in Cardinal Cajetan's commentaries; comparable middle categories appear in Rufinus's "Ecclesiastical, not Canonical" and Athanasius's books "read," not "in the canon" (see their pages). What such a category signified is the subject of an interpretation page.

What the preface does not say: it names no books

Alexander McCaul's summary, reproduced in later Protestant surveys of the canon, reports that Ximénez "in the preface… admonishes the reader that Judith, Tobit, Wisdom, Ecclesiasticus, Maccabees, with the additions to Esther and Daniel… are not canonical Scriptures."3 The prologue itself does not enumerate the disputed books one by one, as the Sanctus Hieronymus transcription notes.3 It draws the line around a class — "the books outside the canon" — and the printing (Greek and Latin, no Hebrew) shows which titles fall within it. The preface thus states a principle for a class of books rather than a list of titles.

Leo X’s privilege to print

Leo X granted a printing privilege for the work (the date usually given is 22 March 1520; some scholars question it, since the presentation copy did not enter the Vatican Library until December 1521).4 The privilege preceded the Council of Trent's 1546 decree by about a generation, and the Catholic apologist Gary Michuta and the Protestant apologist James White, debating in 2004, drew different conclusions from it. Michuta distinguishes approving the work from judging the notes in it:

[He] approved the Complutensian Polyglot, which isn't necessarily a dogmatic declaration of whatever opinions were in that book… he approved the work… without passing judgment on whatever critical notes were attached to those books.

— Gary Michuta, cross-examined by James White, The Great Debate IX (20 May 2004).5 Michuta adds that Ximénez's position was licit before Trent only because the canon "had not yet been defined" — and that "anathemas don't work in reverse": Trent's 1546 penalty cannot retroactively condemn a view held while the question was open. James White reads the same facts as showing that the Polyglot, "approved for publication by Pope Leo X… clearly and without apology rejected the apocryphal books as fully canonical, following the lead of Jerome."6 Both accept the privilege as a fact and differ over what it shows.

Further books in the Polyglot’s Greek volumes

The Polyglot's Greek Old Testament printed books that Trent's 1546 list does not include — 3 Esdras (Greek Esdras A) and 3 Maccabees, both carried in the Septuagint tradition; in the same debate Michuta notes the work ran "beyond even the Council of Trent" (naming 3 and 4 Maccabees).7 The edition thus sets the deuterocanon apart from the Hebrew books in its preface and layout, and also prints Greek texts beyond those in Trent's list.

Reception and debate

  • Placement on the timeline. Between the list of Florence (1442) and Luther's Bible (1534). Florence had listed the deuterocanon among the canonical books; the Complutensian, printed in the same Latin Church under a later pope, voices Jerome's graded distinction, as Cardinal Cajetan did in 1532. How such pre-Reformation Catholic witnesses bear on the medieval canon is set out on the interpretation page.
  • Ximénez the man. Francisco Jiménez de Cisneros (c. 1436–1517), Archbishop of Toledo, Grand Inquisitor, and twice regent of Castile, founded the University of Alcalá and financed the Polyglot from his own revenues. He died in November 1517, months after the Old Testament volumes were printed and years before the work was published — so the "cardinal's Bible" was compiled under his direction but sanctioned and released after his death.
  • Two claims about the Polyglot. The preface's wording places the books "outside the canon" in Jerome's formula; what Leo X's printing privilege implies about that wording is read differently by Michuta and White (see above).
  • The Polyglot elsewhere on this wiki. Beyond the canon question, the Complutensian belongs to Renaissance ad fontes scholarship — the collaborative, church-sponsored recovery of the Greek and Hebrew sources, with the first printed Greek New Testament and the editio princeps of the Septuagint. That humanist dimension — Cisneros’s trilingual college, Nebrija’s withdrawal over correcting the Vulgate, the “two thieves” prologue, and the printing race with Erasmus — is treated on its witness page on the Christian-humanism timeline.
  • Translation and text provenance. The Latin of the preface is given from the facsimile of the first edition (Alcalá, 1514–17/1522) at the Internet Archive; the English renderings here are literal. The parallel to Jerome is to his Prologue to the Books of Solomon in the Vulgate prefaces.
