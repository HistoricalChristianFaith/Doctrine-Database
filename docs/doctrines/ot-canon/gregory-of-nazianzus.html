Gregory of Nazianzus on the Old Testament Canon

Dates: c. 389 AD (the canon list is the verse poem Concerning the Genuine Books of Divinely Inspired Scripture [Carmina dogmatica I.1.12], a didactic poem composed in his retirement years at Cappadocia, c. 383–389; Gregory c. 329–390, "the Theologian," one of the Cappadocian Fathers and briefly archbishop of Constantinople) · Wikipedia
Witness: Gregory versifies the Old Testament for a friend: Tobit, Judith, the Wisdom of Solomon, Sirach, and the Maccabees are not in the list, and it omits Esther, fixing the total at twenty-two "corresponding to the number of the Hebrew letters." He warns "let not extraneous books seduce your mind. For many malignant writings have been disseminated." In his orations he quotes Judith as "the Scripture," cites the Wisdom of Solomon with "as it is written," and adduces Baruch on the Incarnation against the Arians. His cousin Amphilochius of Iconium gives a similar list.

Claims & evidence

The twenty-two books — "corresponding to the number of the Hebrew letters"

Casting the canon as a teaching poem for a friend, Gregory opens by charging him to rehearse "the divine oracles" and to refuse the books that fall outside the list, then recites the Old Testament book by book and seals it with the Hebrew-letter count:1

The divine oracles should always on the tongue and in the mind be rehearsed… But let not extraneous books seduce your mind. For many malignant writings have been disseminated. Accept, o friend, this my approved number. These are all twelve of the historical books, of the most ancient Hebrew wisdom: First there is Genesis, then Exodus, Leviticus too. Then Numbers, and the Second Law. Then Joshua and Judges. Ruth is eighth. The ninth and tenth books [are] the acts of Kings, and [the eleventh is] Chronicles. Last you have Ezra. The poetic books are five: Job being first, then [the Psalms of] David; and three of Solomon, Ecclesiastes, Canticles and Proverbs. And similarly five of prophetic inspiration. There are the Twelve written in one book: Hosea and Amos, and Micah the third; then Joel, and Jonah, Obadiah, Nahum also, and Habakkuk, and Zephaniah, Haggai, then Zechariah, and Malachi. All these are one. The second is of Isaiah. Then the one called as an infant, Jeremiah, Then Ezekiel, and the gift of Daniel. I count therefore, twenty-two of the ancient books, corresponding to the number of the Hebrew letters.

The poem groups twelve historical books (with Ruth folded after Judges, the paired books of Kings counted with Chronicles, and "Ezra" standing for Ezra–Nehemiah), five poetic, and five prophetic (the Twelve Minor Prophets as a single book, then Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, Daniel). The books named are those of the Hebrew canon without Esther; compare the lists of Cyril and Athanasius on this timeline, and the verse list of his cousin Amphilochius.

The deuterocanon is absent

Tobit, Judith, the Wisdom of Solomon, Sirach, and the Maccabees do not appear in the poem.1 Unlike Cyril's Jeremiah, his carries no Baruch or Epistle of Jeremiah: Gregory names "Jeremiah" alone. The recitation is framed by the warning "let not extraneous books seduce your mind. For many malignant writings have been disseminated," and offered as "this my approved number."

Esther omitted

Gregory's list also omits Esther. Where Cyril counts Esther as his twelfth book and Amphilochius appends it as a book "some approve," Gregory does not name it at all — his enumeration runs from Genesis to Daniel without it, and lands on twenty-two precisely because Esther is left out.1 Athanasius's festal list likewise places Esther outside the canon proper.

The disputed books cited in his orations

In his orations Gregory quotes several books his twenty-two do not include: with scriptural formulas, as prophecy, and as proof of the Incarnation.

Baruch, a proof-text for the Incarnation against the Arians. In the Fourth Theological Oration, arguing that certain Old-Testament words name not the Father but the incarnate Son, Gregory adduces Baruch 3:36–38 and draws his conclusion from its closing clause:2

This is your God; no other shall be accounted of in comparison with Him… after this did He show Himself upon earth, and conversed with men. This addition proves clearly that the words are not used of the Father, but of the Son; for it was He Who in bodily form companied with us, and was in this lower world.

Baruch is not named in his verse catalogue; here the closing clause of v. 38, which he calls "this addition," carries his argument against the Arians that the words refer to the Son.

Judith, called "the Scripture." In the Second Oration on Easter, allegorizing the Exodus plagues, Gregory writes:3

and Egypt mourns the first-born of her own reasonings and actions which are also called in the Scripture the Seed of the Chaldeans removed, and the children of Babylon dashed against the rocks and destroyed.

The phrase "the Seed of the Chaldeans" is drawn from Judith 5:6 (Israel's forebears reckoned "of the seed of the Chaldeans"), and Gregory introduces it with "in the Scripture." Judith is not named in his poem.

The Wisdom of Solomon, cited "as it is written." In the Second Theological Oration, arguing that God pervades and fills all things, Gregory pairs Jeremiah and Wisdom under a single citation formula:4

And how shall we preserve the truth that God pervades all things and fills all, as it is written—Do not I fill heaven and earth? says the Lord—and, The Spirit of the Lord fills the world?

The clause "The Spirit of the Lord fills the world" is Wisdom 1:7. Gregory joins it to Jeremiah 23:24 under one "as it is written." Wisdom is not among his five "poetic" books.

Sirach, a charge to honour both parents. In the oration on the equality of the marriage law, criticizing laws that bore harder on women, Gregory supports the duty owed equally to mother and father with Sirach 3:9:5

The blessing of a father strengthens the houses of children, but the curse of a mother uproots the foundations.

He follows it at once with "See the equality of the legislation."

The Maccabees, among the martyr-exemplars. In the funeral oration for Basil, Gregory ranges the seven Maccabean martyrs alongside the canonical heroes of endurance — the three children in the fire, Jonah in the whale, Daniel in the lions' den:6

I pass by the rest, the three young men bedewed in the fire, the fugitive prophet praying in the whale's belly, and coming forth from the creature, as from a chamber; the just man in the den, restraining the lions' rage, and the struggle of the seven Maccabees, who were perfected with their father and mother in blood, and in all kinds of tortures.

Here the martyrs of 2 Maccabees 7 appear as an example of endurance, without a citation formula, beside Daniel and Jonah. Gregory also devoted a homily to them (Oration 15).

His poem fixes the list at twenty-two books. How such lists relate to citations like those above is one of the questions set out on "How did the early church receive the deuterocanonical books?"

Reception and debate

  • A canon in verse, paired with Amphilochius. The poem's form — the Scripture canon set out as didactic verse — is shared by the Iambics for Seleucus of Amphilochius of Iconium, Gregory's cousin and fellow associate of Basil, who gives the books "one by one, so that you may learn them clearly." Both are teaching poems, not conciliar decrees.
  • How the count reaches twenty-two. Gregory groups the books so that Ruth attaches to Judges, the two books of Kings (1–2 Samuel + 1–2 Kings) go with Chronicles, Ezra stands for Ezra–Nehemiah, and the Twelve Minor Prophets count as one. With Esther omitted, the historical, poetic, and prophetic groups total exactly twenty-two — the number of letters in the Hebrew alphabet, the frame Cyril, Athanasius, and Jerome also use. Cyril and Jerome keep Esther and reach twenty-two by a different grouping; Athanasius, like Gregory, leaves Esther out of the canon proper.
  • The New Testament is treated elsewhere. The same poem goes on to list the New Testament books, where Gregory's catalogue omits the Apocalypse — a matter belonging to the New-Testament-canon question, not treated here.
  • The list among the others. Of the Old-Testament lists on this timeline, Gregory's names no deuterocanonical book, no Baruch or Epistle of Jeremiah, and no Esther. Like the other Eastern writers here, he read from the Greek Bible, which also carried the books he leaves off the list.
  • List and usage. His poem omits the disputed books and Esther; his orations quote Baruch on the Incarnation, Judith as "the Scripture," the Wisdom of Solomon "as it is written," Sirach on the duty to both parents, and the Maccabees among the martyr-exemplars (see "The disputed books cited in his orations" above). The readings of how lists and usage relate are set out on "How did the early church receive the deuterocanonical books?"
