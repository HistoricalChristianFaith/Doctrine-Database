Vincent of Lérins on the Old Testament Canon

Dates: c. 400–c. 445 AD (Vincent of Lérins — a Gallic monk and priest of the island monastery of Lérins, off the southern coast of Gaul, who wrote under the pen-name Peregrinus. His Commonitory (Commonitorium, AD 434) states the rule for distinguishing catholic faith from heresy known as the “Vincentian canon,” that we hold “what has been believed everywhere, always, and by all”) · Wikipedia
Witness: Vincent leaves no catalogue of the Old Testament books. In the Commonitory, on his rule that nothing new may be added to the faith, he strings three Old-Testament wisdom sayings together under one formula, “the divine Oracles cry aloud,” and the middle of the three is the deuterocanonical Sirach (8:14), set between Proverbs 22:28 and Ecclesiastes 10:8. He wrote on the same Gallic coast as John Cassian.

The citation

Sirach quoted among “the divine Oracles,” between Proverbs and Ecclesiastes

The Commonitory argues that the catholic faith may be deepened but never altered — nothing added, changed, or taken away. Writing against those who would alter it, Vincent cites three wisdom-sayings, introduced together as “the divine Oracles”:1

… whereas the divine Oracles cry aloud, “Remove not the landmarks, which thy fathers have set,” and “Go not to law with a Judge,” and “Whoso breaketh through a fence a serpent shall bite him” …

The three quotations are Proverbs 22:28 (“Remove not the ancient landmark, which thy fathers have set”), the deuterocanonical Sirach 8:14 (“Go not to law with a Judge”), and Ecclesiastes 10:8 (“whoso breaketh an hedge, a serpent shall bite him”) — the identifications NewAdvent's edition gives at the line itself. Vincent introduces the three with one formula and does not mark the Sirach line off from the two protocanonical sayings on either side of it.

Reception and debate

  • The formula. “The divine Oracles cry aloud” renders the Latin cum divina clament oracula; Vincent applies it to all three sayings at once, in support of the central thesis of his book.1
  • No canon-list. Vincent compiles no catalogue of Old-Testament books. How the citations of writers who give no list relate to the lists of those who give a Hebrew-sized canon is among the questions set out in “How did the early church receive the deuterocanonical books: as Scripture, below the canon, or outside it?”
  • Placement on the timeline. Vincent wrote on the same Gallic coast and within a few years of John Cassian, and a generation after Ambrose, Augustine, and the African councils.
