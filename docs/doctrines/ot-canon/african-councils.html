The African councils of Hippo and Carthage on the Old Testament Canon

Dates: 393 (the Synod of Hippo, canon 36) · 397 (the Third Council of Carthage, re-enacting the Hippo list) · reaffirmed 419 (the Code of Canons of the African Church, canon 24) · Wikipedia · New Testament canon page
Witness: The North-African synods of Hippo (393) and Carthage (397), reaffirmed at Carthage in 419, list Tobit, Judith, the two books of Maccabees, and the deuterocanonical Wisdom books (within "the five books of Solomon") among the canonical Scriptures, with no separate rank. A generation earlier Athanasius's Festal Letter had placed Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, and Sirach among the books "to be read," outside the canon.

Hippo (393), canon 36

The acts of the Synod of Hippo of 393 are mostly lost, but its canons survive in the Breviarium Hipponense, the abstract read back into the record and adopted at Carthage in 397. Its thirty-sixth canon is the earliest extant list of the biblical books issued by a church council. After the governing rule, its Old-Testament catalogue gives the longer Septuagint reckoning, with the disputed books listed among the others and not in a separate rank:1

Item, that besides the Canonical Scriptures nothing be read in church under the name of divine Scripture.

But the Canonical Scriptures are as follows: Genesis. Exodus. Leviticus. Numbers. Deuteronomy. Joshua the Son of Nun. The Judges. Ruth. The Kings, iv. books. The Chronicles, ij. books. Job. The Psalter. The Five books of Solomon. The Twelve Books of the Prophets. Isaiah. Jeremiah. Ezechiel. Daniel. Tobit. Judith. Esther. Ezra, ij. books. Macchabees, ij. books.

So Tobit, Judith, and the two books of Maccabees are named outright in the canon, and the two deuterocanonical Wisdom books — the Wisdom of Solomon and Sirach (Ecclesiasticus) — are carried in the entry "the five books of Solomon" (Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, Song of Songs, Wisdom, and Sirach). In 367 Athanasius, in the East, had ranked these books "not… in the Canon, but appointed by the Fathers to be read"; the Hippo list names them among the canonical Scriptures.2

Carthage (397), and the reaffirmation of 419

Four years later the Third Council of Carthage re-enacted the Hippo canon, in a list that differs only in detail. Its Old Testament names the same books:3

The Canonical Scriptures are these: Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, Deuteronomy, Joshua the son of Nun, Judges, Ruth, four books of Kings, two books of Paraleipomena, Job, the Psalter, five books of Solomon, the books of the twelve prophets, Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezechiel, Daniel, Tobit, Judith, Esther, two books of Esdras, two books of the Maccabees.

The same canon was read into the record once more at the council of Carthage of 419 (the assembly whose collected canons became the Code of Canons of the African Church), where the list appears as canon 24 with a closing note: "Let this be sent to our brother and fellow bishop, Boniface, and to the other bishops of those parts, that they may confirm this canon, for these are the things which we have received from our fathers to be read in church." Percival's note identifies Boniface as the bishop of Rome (Boniface I, 418–422). The Latin text of the list as given for 397 also directs that "the church over the sea be consulted to confirm this canon," and in another translation asks that the list "be made known also to our brother and fellow-priest Boniface, or to other bishops of those parts, for the purpose of confirming that Canon.3

Augustine and the African lists

Augustine was a priest at Hippo in 393 and bishop by 397. His part at Hippo is recorded in his own Retractations: it was there — "in presence of the bishops, who gave me orders to that effect, and who were holding a plenary Council of the whole of Africa at Hippo-Regius" — that he, "as presbyter," delivered the address On Faith and the Creed before the assembled bishops.5 His own enumeration of the canon, set down in On Christian Doctrine (2.8.13, c. 397), matches the conciliar lists — "Tobias, and Esther, and Judith, and the two books of Maccabees" are all named among the Scriptures — and states a criterion for judging the canonical books: to "prefer those that are received by all the catholic churches to those which some do not receive.4

Reception and debate

  • Standing of the synods. Hippo and Carthage are regional African synods, not ecumenical councils, and their canon is introduced as a rule for what may be "read in church." The texts ask that the list be sent to Boniface and the bishops "of those parts," or to "the church over the sea," for confirmation, and give as its ground "the things which we have received from our fathers to be read in church." A list of the same books is ascribed to a Roman synod under Damasus in 382 (see the Rome witness page). Whether the councils' scope and later reception make them binding beyond Africa is discussed on the interpretation pages on whether the canon-councils contradict one another and Trent and the African councils on Esdras.
  • Beside Athanasius and Jerome. Athanasius (367) set Wisdom, Sirach, Judith, and Tobit in a second rank — "read" but "not in the canon" — and Jerome, in his Vulgate prefaces (c. 390), set them "among the apocrypha." The African lists place Tobit, Judith, the two books of Maccabees, and the Wisdom books among the canonical Scriptures.
  • The same synods on the New Testament. This page treats the African councils' list of the disputed Old-Testament books; the parallel New Testament canon page treats the same synods' ratification of the twenty-seven-book New Testament (with Carthage still setting Hebrews a half-step apart from Paul's thirteen). Both rest on the one canon-list of Hippo 393, re-enacted at Carthage.
  • A later manuscript heading naming Nicaea. It is this African list — Hippo's canon 36, reaffirmed at Carthage — that a lone eighth/ninth-century manuscript (Vallicelliana B.58) heads "Chapter XXIV of the Council of Nicaea," a heading of the same kind as the Nicaea-counted-Judith report, which ascribes a canon ruling to Nicaea; the council's own surviving acts contain none.
