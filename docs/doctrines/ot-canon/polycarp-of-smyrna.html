Polycarp of Smyrna on the Old Testament Canon

Dates: c. 125 AD (Epistle to the Philippians, c. 110–140; Polycarp lived c. 69–155, martyred at Smyrna) · Wikipedia
Summary: In his Epistle to the Philippians, written a generation after Clement, Polycarp — by tradition a disciple of the apostle John — urges his readers to almsgiving with the maxim of the deuterocanonical Tobit ("alms delivers from death"), set in a chain of phrases from 1 Peter and Isaiah. He gives no list of the books and uses no "it is written" formula. His letter is, with Clement's, among the earliest Christian datapoints for the use of a disputed book, before the earliest extant Christian Old-Testament list.

Claims & evidence

Tobit's "alms delivers from death," woven with 1 Peter and Isaiah

Stand fast, therefore, in these things, and follow the example of the Lord, being firm and unchangeable in the faith, loving the brotherhood, and being attached to one another, joined together in the truth, exhibiting the meekness of the Lord in your intercourse with one another, and despising no one. When you can do good, defer it not, because "alms delivers from death." Be all of you subject one to another, having your conduct blameless among the Gentiles, that ye may both receive praise for your good works, and the Lord may not be blasphemed through you.

— Epistle to the Philippians, ch. 10, source (Roberts–Donaldson translation; also at the Ante-Nicene Fathers). The framed sentence — "alms delivers from death" — is the maxim of the deuterocanonical Tobit, fusing Tobit 4:10 ("for alms doth deliver from death") and 12:9 ("alms doth deliver from death, and shall purge away all sin").1 Polycarp sets it in an unbroken chain of apostolic and prophetic phrases — "loving the brotherhood" (1 Peter 2:17), "be all of you subject one to another" (1 Peter 5:5), "having your conduct blameless among the Gentiles" (1 Peter 2:12), and the warning that "the Lord may not be blasphemed" (Isaiah 52:5) — without distinguishing the Tobit maxim from the phrases around it.2 He introduces the maxim with no citation formula such as "it is written"; it stands, in quotation, within his exhortation.

Reception and debate

  • No list of books. The Epistle to the Philippians contains no enumeration of the Old Testament books. What it records is use: a deuterocanonical maxim given as moral counsel in the early second century, set with phrases from 1 Peter and Isaiah. It predates the extant Christian lists and is among the witnesses discussed on the interpretation page "How did the early church receive the deuterocanonical books?"
  • Form of the citation and the text. The citation carries no "it is written" formula; it is a proverbial maxim within exhortation rather than a proof-text adduced for a doctrine. Whether such use shows that a writer held a book canonical is discussed on the interpretation page "When a Father quotes a book 'as Scripture,' does that show he held it canonical?" Chapters 10–14 of the letter survive only in a later Latin version, the Greek being lost; the Latin reads "quia eleemosyna de morte liberat."
  • Place on the timeline. At c. 125 AD Polycarp stands a generation after Clement of Rome (c. 96) and before Melito of Sardis (c. 170), whose list is the earliest extant Christian enumeration of the Old Testament books and does not name the deuterocanonical books. Clement and Polycarp are the earliest Christian writers on this timeline to use the disputed books, before any extant Christian Old-Testament catalogue.
