The Claromontanus Catalogue on the Old Testament Canon

Dates: c. 400 AD (the catalogue is dated c. 300–400 as an older Latin/Alexandrian list copied into the manuscript, see below; the manuscript that preserves it — Codex Claromontanus, the Greek-Latin bilingual of Paul's epistles known as Codex D — is itself 5th/6th century, the list inserted between Philemon and Hebrews at folio 468r) · Wikipedia
Witness: The stichometric catalogue (the Catalogus Claromontanus) lists Tobit, Judith, the Wisdom of Solomon (Sapientia), Sirach (the “Wisdom of Jesus,” Sapientia Iesu), and the first, second, and fourth books of Maccabees among the Old Testament Scriptures, each with its line-count. It is a Latin list of the period of the African councils, the Roman synod, and Innocent I, and resembles the stichometric Cheltenham List. (Its horizontal dashes fall on several New Testament titles and on Judith; see “Reception and debate.”)

Claims & evidence

The Old-Testament list and its line-counts

The catalogue is a stichometric list — a table of the books of Scripture with the number of stichoi (standard lines) in each, a scribe's measure for checking a copy's completeness and pricing the work. Its Old-Testament section runs from Genesis to Tobias, and the disputed books are interleaved with the protocanonical ones and given line-counts like the rest:1

(The figures are Roman numerals written with an overbar for thousands — Ā = 1,000 — so Sapientia vers. Ā credits the Wisdom of Solomon 1,000 lines, Sapientia ihu [Iesu, the Wisdom of Jesus son of Sirach] 2,500, the first and second Maccabees 2,300 apiece, the fourth 1,000, Judith 1,300, and Tobias 1,000.) Tobit, Judith, the Wisdom of Solomon, Sirach, and the Maccabees all appear, counted into the Old Testament with the rest. Of the Greek books of Maccabees the catalogue has 1, 2, and 4, but not 3.1

The catalogue is older than the manuscript that carries it

The list is bound into Codex Claromontanus, the bilingual Greek-Latin manuscript of the Pauline epistles (designated D) written in the fifth or sixth century, where it sits oddly between Philemon and Hebrews, on the leaf numbered folio 468.2 The Wikipedia article on the catalogue treats it as an older list copied into the codex: "the catalog of the 6th-century Codex Claromontanus was probably copying a 3rd- or 4th-century source."2 Its New-Testament half counts the Epistle of Barnabas, the Shepherd of Hermas, the Acts of Paul, and the Apocalypse of Peter.2 On that dating the timeline places it at c. 400, beside the African councils, rather than in the sixth century of its surviving copy.

A Latin list of the period of the African councils

The Claromontanus catalogue is a Latin list of the same period as the Western conciliar lists at the turn of the fifth century. Where Cyril and the Eastern fathers counted twenty-two Hebrew books and set the apocrypha aside, the Western lists of this era — the canon of Hippo and Carthage under Augustine's hand, the Roman synod's list, the letter of Innocent I to Exsuperius — receive Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, and the Maccabees as Scripture (see their pages). Claromontanus is a table of contents without argument, in which the deuterocanonical books are line-counted like Genesis and Isaiah. The Cheltenham (Mommsen) List of 359/365 is another Latin stichometric catalogue that carries the same books (see its page).

Reception and debate

  • Where the marks fall. Horizontal strokes (obeli) stand beside several titles. In the New Testament they stand beside Barnabas, the Shepherd, the Acts of Paul, the Apocalypse of Peter, and 1 Peter; on the Old-Testament side only beside Judith. Tobit, Wisdom, Sirach, and the Maccabees carry no mark.2
  • Readings of the obeli. The reading given from Tischendorf onward takes the dashes as marking four extracanonical New-Testament works as secondary; Metzger (1987) took the stroke beside 1 Peter instead for a paragraph-mark separating Peter's epistles from Paul's. Kelsie Rodenbiker (2021) argues that the obeli — including the one by Judith — were added by a later hand than the one that wrote the list, so that they may register a reader's later doubts rather than the original cataloguer's.2 These readings differ over what the dash beside Judith signals; the list itself names the book with its line-count.
  • Why a Pauline manuscript carries an Old-Testament list. The catalogue is a self-contained inventory of both Testaments that the Claromontanus scribe inserted into a copy of Paul; the leaf interrupts the Pauline corpus between Philemon and Hebrews. Its Old-Testament half is the part that bears on this timeline.
