The Council of Florence on the Old Testament Canon

Dates: 4 February 1442 (Session 11 of the Council of Basel–Ferrara–Florence, reckoned in Catholic usage the seventeenth ecumenical council; the bull Cantate Domino, the Bull of Union with the Copts, promulgated by Pope Eugene IV) · Wikipedia
Witness: In the bull Cantate Domino (1442), its profession of faith for reunion with the Copts, the council declares "one and the same God" the author of both Testaments and lists the books it "accepts and venerates": Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Ecclesiasticus, Baruch, and the two books of Maccabees stand in one list beside Genesis, Isaiah, and the Gospels. The list matches those of the African councils and Innocent I.

Claims & evidence

The canon enumerated in Cantate Domino

The Bull of Union with the Copts is a profession of the Catholic faith drawn up for reunion with the Copts. In the course of professing what the Roman church holds, it sets out the canon of Scripture under a single heading — the one God who authored both Testaments — and then lists the books:

It professes that one and the same God is the author of the old and the new Testament — that is, the law and the prophets, and the gospel — since the saints of both testaments spoke under the inspiration of the same Spirit. It accepts and venerates their books, whose titles are as follows. Five books of Moses, namely Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers and Deuteronomy; Joshua, Judges, Ruth, four books of Kings, two of Paralipomenon, Esdras, Nehemiah, Tobit, Judith, Esther, Job, psalms of David, Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, Song of Songs, Wisdom, Ecclesiasticus, Isaiah, Jeremiah, Baruch, Ezechiel, Daniel; the twelve minor prophets, namely Hosea, Joel, Amos, Obadiah, Jonah, Micah, Nahum, Habakkuk, Zephaniah, Haggai, Zechariah, Malachi; two books of the Maccabees; the four gospels, of Matthew, Mark, Luke and John…

— Council of Florence, Cantate Domino (1442), trans. Norman P. Tanner.1 Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Ecclesiasticus (Sirach), Baruch, and the two books of Maccabees are named in the one list of books the council "accepts and venerates," with no mark of a lesser rank, as in the lists of Hippo and Carthage, Augustine, and Innocent I; Athanasius, Rufinus, and Jerome had ranked these titles below the canon.2

The verb “accepts and venerates”

The council suscipit ac veneratur, "accepts and venerates." The Council of Trent uses the same verb in 1546, when it decrees that it "receives and venerates with an equal affection of piety and reverence" all the books of the same list.3 Florence's list is among the evidence adduced on whether Trent added the books or defined a canon already received, set out on its own interpretation page, and whether Florence or Trent gave the first universal, binding statement on another.

Reception and debate

  • The disputed books listed with no second tier. Florence does not reproduce the graded schemes of Athanasius ("read" but "not in the Canon"), Rufinus ("Ecclesiastical," not "Canonical"), or Jerome (read "for the strengthening of the people, but not for confirming… dogmas"). The disputed books are listed among the rest, as in Augustine’s list.
  • A list in a reunion bull. The canon-list appears not as a stand-alone canon-decree but inside a profession of faith handed to the Copts (and, in parallel bulls, the Armenians and others) as the terms of reunion, and it carries no anathema. The Wikipedia article on the bull reports that the attempted union with the Copts failed.2 Florence is reckoned in Catholic usage an ecumenical council. How the standing of the bull bears on whether Trent was the first universal, binding definition is set out on its own interpretation page.
  • After Florence. The Glossa Ordinaria continued to carry Jerome's prologues, and in 1532 Cardinal Cajetan ranked these books for edification rather than for establishing doctrine, as Jerome had. Trent's decree of 1546 lists the same books under anathema.4
  • Translation provenance. The English is Norman P. Tanner's, in his Decrees of the Ecumenical Councils (Sheed & Ward / Georgetown, 1990), a parallel-text edition of the conciliar acts; the full bull is reproduced online in that translation.
