Augustine of Hippo on the Old Testament Canon

Dates: c. 397 (On Christian Doctrine 2.8.13) · 354–430 · Wikipedia · the African councils
Witness: Augustine, bishop of Hippo, sets out his canon in On Christian Doctrine (c. 397), naming Tobias, Judith, the two books of Maccabees, and the two of Ezra among the histories and Wisdom and Ecclesiasticus among the prophetical books, with no separate rank. He weighs a book by the breadth of its reception across the catholic churches. In The City of God he writes that the Maccabees are held canonical "not by the Jews, but by the Church." His list matches that of the African synods of Hippo and Carthage.

Claims & evidence

The canon-list: a forty-four-book Old Testament (c. 397)

In Book II of On Christian Doctrine, instructing the reader how to handle Scripture, Augustine lists the whole Old Testament. He files Tobias, Judith, the two Maccabees, and the two of Ezra among the historical books, and sets Wisdom and Ecclesiasticus among the prophetical — the longer Septuagint canon, with no second rank:1

There are other books which seem to follow no regular order, and are connected neither with the order of the preceding books nor with one another, such as Job, and Tobias, and Esther, and Judith, and the two books of Maccabees, and the two of Ezra… Next are the Prophets, in which there is one book of the Psalms of David; and three books of Solomon, viz., Proverbs, Song of Songs, and Ecclesiastes. For two books, one called Wisdom and the other Ecclesiasticus, are ascribed to Solomon from a certain resemblance of style… Still they are to be reckoned among the prophetical books, since they have attained recognition as being authoritative… The authority of the Old Testament is contained within the limits of these forty-four books.

— On Christian Doctrine II.8.13, source. The list matches the canon of the African councils; Augustine, then a priest, addressed the plenary council at Hippo in 393 (see that page).

The criterion: prefer what is received by all the catholic churches

Augustine states a rule of reception immediately before the list: the more widely a book is received among the catholic churches, and by those of greater authority, the higher its standing:2

Now, in regard to the canonical Scriptures, he must follow the judgment of the greater number of catholic churches… he will judge according to the following standard: to prefer those that are received by all the catholic churches to those which some do not receive. Among those, again, which are not received by all, he will prefer such as have the sanction of the greater number and those of greater authority, to such as are held by the smaller number and those of less authority.

— On Christian Doctrine II.8.12, source. Jerome, under his "Hebraica veritas," measured the canon against the Hebrew the Jews kept; Augustine here measures it by the breadth of reception among the catholic churches.

The Maccabees: "held as canonical, not by the Jews, but by the Church" (c. 425)

Late in life, in The City of God, Augustine writes that the books of the Maccabees are held as canonical by the Church and not by the Jews:3

the reckoning of their dates is found, not in the Holy Scriptures which are called canonical, but in others, among which are also the books of the Maccabees. These are held as canonical, not by the Jews, but by the Church, on account of the extreme and wonderful sufferings of certain martyrs, who, before Christ had come in the flesh, contended for the law of God even unto death, and endured most grievous and horrible evils.

— The City of God XVIII.36, source. How this passage bears on his canon is read differently: see the interpretation pages on whether Augustine thought the books stood in the Hebrew canon and whether he ranked them lower.

Sirach, Wisdom, and 2 Maccabees cited as "Scripture"

Augustine also quotes the disputed books, in works spanning decades, with formulae such as "the words of Scripture" and "Holy Scripture says." Three citations come from three genres — apologetic, doctrinal, and pastoral.

In The City of God, weighing the punishments common to this life, he adduces Sirach as "the words of Scripture":4

the words of Scripture, "An heavy yoke is upon the sons of Adam, from the day that they go out of their mother's womb till the day that they return to the mother of all things,"…

— The City of God XXI.14 (quoting Sirach 40:1), source.

In On the Trinity, defining wisdom as the worship of God, he grounds the point in Wisdom with "Holy Scripture says":5

It is in respect to this wisdom, therefore, which is the worship of God, that Holy Scripture says, "The multitude of the wise is the welfare of the world."

— On the Trinity XIV.1.1 (quoting Wisdom 6:24), source.

And in On Rebuke and Grace, arguing that even the righteous will need mercy at the judgment, he quotes the mother of the Maccabees, from a book he elsewhere says is held as canonical "not by the Jews, but by the Church":6

…some will be judged with mercy, others without mercy. On which account also the mother of the Maccabees says to her son, "That in that mercy I may receive thee with thy brethren."

— On Rebuke and Grace 41 (quoting 2 Maccabees 7:29), source.

Reception and debate

  • Augustine and the African councils. Augustine was a priest at Hippo in 393, when he addressed the plenary council there, and bishop by 397; the canon-lists of the North-African synods match his own.
  • Augustine and Jerome. The two Latin Fathers drew up different lists. Jerome, translating iuxta Hebraeos, set Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, and the Maccabees "among the apocrypha"; Augustine, on his criterion of reception, listed them in the canon. They also exchanged letters over the translation of Scripture. In 1546 Trent listed the books of Augustine's list; in 1571 the Thirty-Nine Articles cited Jerome by name for reading them but not for establishing doctrine (see his page). James White holds that Augustine kept the disputed books because he thought they stood in the Hebrew table; Augustine’s statements on the Jewish canon (City of God XVII.20, XVIII.36) and the readings of them are set out on the interpretation page. CARM and White weigh Augustine's witness on competence: he "was no Biblical expert" and "could not read Hebrew," unlike the Hebraist Jerome, so on their reading Jerome's "No" should prevail where the two disagree and the African councils' "Yes" be set aside as his and regional. Augustine's criterion of churchly reception (On Christian Doctrine II.8.12), the Western witnesses to the longer canon before and beside him (Cyprian, the Cheltenham List, Ambrose, Rome, Innocent I) and the readings of his competence are set out on the interpretation page on his lack of Hebrew. Archibald Alexander (The Canon of the Old and New Testaments Ascertained, 1826) cites Augustine for the shorter canon: that he "retracted" his opinion of Ecclesiasticus and reported a custom of reading the apocrypha "from a lower place," "not as Holy Scripture" (⚠ unverified: the Augustine passage Alexander cites for the "lower place" custom). George Reid (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1908) reads Augustine as placing the books in the canon and keeping them there. The Retractations passage (II.4) and the readings of whether Augustine retracted or ranked the books lower are set out on their own interpretation page.
  • The same witness, several doctrines. Augustine also retells the origin of the Greek Bible that carried these books — see his page on the Origin of the Septuagint — and one of the books on his list, 2 Maccabees, is cited for prayer for the dead, a practice he defends (see his purgatory page).
