Melito of Sardis on the Old Testament Canon

Dates: c. 170 AD (bishop of Sardis; the canon list is preserved in a letter to Onesimus quoted by Eusebius) · Wikipedia
Witness: Melito's letter to Onesimus carries the earliest extant Christian list of the Old Testament books. Tobit, Judith, Sirach, Baruch, and 1–2 Maccabees are absent (so too Esther). Whether its "Wisdom" is a second name for Proverbs is discussed below. The list ends with "Esdras."

Claims & evidence

The earliest Christian Old Testament list

Melito, bishop of Sardis in Asia Minor, travelled "to the East," to "the place where these things were preached and done," and sent his correspondent Onesimus the books of the Old Testament and their order. His report, preserved by Eusebius in the Ecclesiastical History (iv.26.13–14, written c. 324 AD), is the oldest extant catalogue of the Christian Old Testament.1 Melito describes how he learned the list:

"Accordingly when I went East and came to the place where these things were preached and done, I learned accurately the books of the Old Testament, and send them to thee as written below. Their names are as follows: Of Moses, five books: Genesis, Exodus, Numbers, Leviticus, Deuteronomy; Jesus Nave, Judges, Ruth; of Kings, four books; of Chronicles, two; the Psalms of David, the Proverbs of Solomon, Wisdom also, Ecclesiastes, Song of Songs, Job; of Prophets, Isaiah, Jeremiah; of the twelve prophets, one book; Daniel, Ezekiel, Esdras. From which also I have made the extracts, dividing them into six books."

Books not in the list

The list does not name Tobit, Judith, Sirach (Ecclesiasticus), Baruch, or 1–2 Maccabees. Esther is not named either, and the catalogue closes at "Esdras." The bible-researcher.com catalogue describes it as giving "the Hebrew canon minus Esther."2 Arthur Cushman McGiffert, annotating Eusebius (1890), takes "Esdras" as the book of Ezra "which, among the Jews, commonly included our Ezra and Nehemiah"; Gary Michuta (Catholic apologist) reads the list as omitting "Lamentations, Nehemiah, and Esther."3 The list predates the canon decisions of the fourth-century synods on the timeline.

The meaning of “Wisdom” in the list

The list's "Wisdom also" stands immediately after "the Proverbs of Solomon." McGiffert glosses the Greek (ἣ καὶ Σοφία) "i.e. the Book of Proverbs," and F. F. Bruce and the bible-researcher.com catalogue likewise read it as a second title for Proverbs, not the deuterocanonical Wisdom of Solomon. In support, Proverbs was anciently called "the all-virtuous Wisdom" (ἡ πανάρετος Σοφία), and Eusebius elsewhere records that several early writers used "Wisdom" as a name for the same book. On this reading the list names no deuterocanonical book.3 Michuta reads "Wisdom" as the separate Book of Wisdom, so that the list "includes the Book of Wisdom"; on that reading it names one deuterocanonical book and not the others.3

Reception and debate

  • Placement on the timeline. Melito's is the earliest extant Christian list of the Old Testament books. Later lists on this timeline by Origen, Cyril of Jerusalem, Athanasius, and Jerome also count a Hebrew-sized canon, while other writers cite the deuterocanonical books from the Greek Septuagint (see their entries); how the early churches received these books is set out on the interpretation page "How did the early church receive the deuterocanonical books?"
  • Report or prescription? Melito says he travelled "East… to the place where these things were preached" and "learned accurately" the books. Edmon L. Gallagher and John D. Meade (2017) read the list as a report of the Hebrew enumeration rather than a prescriptive Christian ruling, a reading they apply also to Origen's list ("as the Hebrews have handed them down"); Roger T. Beckwith (1985) holds that the Hebrew-conscious Fathers reported the Jewish canon because they took it to be the right one for the church as well, so that describing and prescribing need not be opposed.4 The list's order is not the Jewish tripartite arrangement, and it lacks Esther. What Melito's inquiry shows about an apostolic tradition of the longer canon is the subject of the interpretation page "What does Melito's inquiry in the East (c. 170) show about the deuterocanon and apostolic tradition?", where James White and Gary Michuta's readings are set out.4
  • The Jewish community at Sardis. Sardis had a large Jewish community. The monumental synagogue excavated there — the largest yet found outside the land of Israel — belongs, as it survives, to a later phase: the Jewish community acquired and converted the hall around the late third / turn of the fourth century, after Melito's day (fl. c. 170). For earlier centuries, Josephus preserves decrees of c. 50–48 BC recognizing a long-established, civically-recognized Jewish assembly at Sardis "from the beginning."5 Robert L. Wilken (1976) writes that "in Sardis the Christians lived in the shadow of the large and influential Jewish community," and that "the situation in Sardis bred hostility on the part of the Christians to the Jews, as the writings of Melito testify," citing Melito's paschal homily On Pascha (Peri Pascha). How the local community bears on Melito's inquiry is discussed on the interpretation page.6
  • Why Eusebius is the source. Melito's own works survive only in fragments; the canon list reaches us because Eusebius quoted the letter to Onesimus verbatim. The quotation gives Melito's words as transmitted through Eusebius; the citation is to Eusebius's text.
