Gregory of Nyssa on the Old Testament Canon

Dates: c. 335–395 AD (Gregory, bishop of Nyssa in Cappadocia from 372, younger brother of Basil of Caesarea and the third of the three Cappadocian Fathers; the citations below come from On the Making of Man [De hominis opificio, 379], Against Eunomius [Contra Eunomium, c. 380–383], and On Virginity [De virginitate, c. 371], the works of his episcopate, which is why he is slotted on the timeline at c. 380 rather than at his death) · Wikipedia
Witness: Gregory leaves no catalogue of the Old Testament books. Across his doctrinal and ascetic works he quotes the Greek addition to Daniel (Susanna) as "the prophetical writing," names Baruch as a voice "delivered to us by the Holy Scriptures" in an argument on the Incarnation against Eunomius, and introduces the Wisdom of Solomon with "the Scripture tells us." His brother Basil cites the books likewise; Gregory of Nazianzus and Amphilochius of Iconium wrote Hebrew-sized lists in verse.

Claims & evidence

The addition to Daniel (Susanna) quoted as "the prophetical writing"

In On the Making of Man (16.14, written 379), arguing that God's foreknowledge governs the whole order of creation, Gregory grounds the divine prescience in a line from the Greek addition to Daniel — the prayer of Susanna, "O everlasting God… that knowest all things before they be" (Susanna 42 / Daniel 13:42) — introduced as "the prophetical writing":1

He Who, as the prophetical writing says, "knoweth all things before they be," following out, or rather perceiving beforehand by His power of foreknowledge what, in a state of independence and freedom, is the tendency of the motion of man's will… devised for His image the distinction of male and female.

The Susanna verse is introduced as the prophetical writing and carries the point about divine foreknowledge. Irenaeus had cited Susanna as "to be found in Daniel the prophet," and Eusebius, within an extract from Origen, had adduced "the description by Susanna of God as knowing all things before they come to pass" for the same point.

Baruch cited as Scripture on the Incarnation

The most explicit use is in Against Eunomius (c. 380–383), Gregory's long treatise against Eunomius. Defending the eternity and full deity of the Son who "afterward… was seen upon earth," Gregory twice quotes the deuterocanonical Baruch (3:37, LXX 3:38). In Book V he names the prophet and says the words were delivered by the Holy Scriptures:2

…delivered to us by the Holy Scriptures, that the God who was in the beginning, "afterwards," as Baruch says, "was seen upon the earth, and conversed with men."

And in Book VI (§4), answering Eunomius, he repeats the same verse as the word of "the prophet":2

For since He was the Word and was God, and "afterwards," as the prophet says, "was seen upon earth and conversed with men"…

In the Greek lists on this timeline Baruch is counted with Jeremiah, as by Origen, Cyril, and Athanasius. Athenagoras, Hippolytus, and Eusebius had also cited Baruch 3 as prophecy ("This is our God… afterward did He show Himself upon earth"). Gregory calls the words "delivered to us by the Holy Scriptures" and adduces them in his argument on the Incarnation.

The Wisdom of Solomon introduced as "the Scripture"

In his ascetic treatise On Virginity (ch. 15, c. 371), warning the soul betrothed to God against admitting defilement, Gregory closes the exhortation with a verse of the deuterocanonical Wisdom of Solomon (1:4), under the bare label "Scripture":3

…she would herself have broken the covenant of her spiritual marriage; and, as the Scripture tells us, "into the malicious soul Wisdom cannot come."

The Wisdom phrase is introduced with "the Scripture." His brother Basil introduces Sirach with "What Scripture says," and Clement of Alexandria and Methodius introduce the book of Wisdom with "the Scripture" formulas.

Reception and debate

  • With Basil. His brother Basil calls Sirach "Scripture" and cites Baruch, Judith, and Wisdom in doctrinal argument; Gregory cites Susanna as "the prophetical writing," Baruch as words "delivered to us by the Holy Scriptures," and Wisdom as "the Scripture." The Cappadocian verse lists are by their friends Gregory of Nazianzus and Amphilochius, not by either brother.
  • Lists and usage elsewhere. Athanasius, Cyril of Jerusalem, Hilary, and Epiphanius each recite a Hebrew-sized canon and quote disputed books in argument (see their pages). Gregory, like Basil, leaves no list of his own. The readings of how lists and usage relate are set out on "How did the early church receive the deuterocanonical books?"
  • The three citations. In Against Eunomius Baruch is named, its words are "delivered to us by the Holy Scriptures," and they are adduced on the Incarnation: "afterwards," … "was seen upon the earth, and conversed with men." The Susanna citation calls the addition to Daniel "the prophetical writing"; the Wisdom citation is introduced with "the Scripture."
