Isidore of Seville on the Old Testament Canon

Dates: c. 636 AD (d. 636; the canon-list is in the Etymologiae VI.i.3–9, the encyclopaedia compiled c. 615–630 and left unfinished at his death; Isidore c. 560–636, archbishop of Seville) · Wikipedia
Witness: Isidore reproduces, after Jerome, the twenty-two-book Old Testament in three Hebrew classes (Law, Prophets, and Sacred Writings), with the deuterocanon outside the twenty-two and Ruth and Lamentations counted only by "some" who "make twenty-four." In the next sentence he adds a fourth class: "those books of the Old Testament that are not in the Hebrew canon" (Wisdom, Ecclesiasticus, Tobit, Judith, and the two books of Maccabees), which "the Church of Christ honors and proclaims among the divine books."

Claims & evidence

The Hebrew canon: twenty-two books in three classes, the deuterocanon absent

Isidore opens his account of Scripture by giving the Hebrew reckoning, credited (as Jerome had) to Ezra the redactor, and counted to the twenty-two letters of the Hebrew alphabet. He runs out the three Hebrew classes — the five of the Law, the eight Prophets, the nine Sacred Writings — and notes only that "some" stretch the count to twenty-four:1

The Hebrews take the Old Testament, with Ezra as its redactor, as consisting of twenty-two books, corresponding to the number of letters in their alphabet. They divide these books into three classes: Law, Prophets, and Sacred Writings… These are the five books of Moses, which the Hebrews call Torah… The second class is of Prophets, in which are contained eight books… The third class is of Sacred Writings (Hagiographa), that is, of 'those writing about holy things', in which there are nine books: first Job; second the Psalter; third Masloth, which is the Proverbs of Solomon; fourth Coheleth, which is Ecclesiastes; fifth Sir hassirim, which is the Song of Songs; sixth Daniel; seventh Dibre haiamim, which means 'words of the days' (verba dierum), that is Paralipomenon (i.e. Chronicles); eighth Ezra; ninth Esther. All together these books – five, eight, and nine – make up the twenty-two as was reckoned above. Some add Ruth and Cinoth, which in Latin is the Lamentations of Jeremiah, to the Sacred Writings, and make twenty-four books of the Old Testament, corresponding to the twenty-four Elders who stand present before the face of God.

No Tobit, no Judith, no Wisdom, no Sirach, no Maccabees stand among the twenty-two (or the variant twenty-four). The list follows Jerome's Hebrew list: the three-fold Hebrew division, the Ezra-as-redactor note, the twenty-two-letter count, and Jerome's transliterated Hebrew titles (Masloth, Coheleth, Sir hassirim, Dibre haiamim).

The fourth class: "not in the Hebrew canon"; "the Church of Christ honors and proclaims them"

In the next section he adds a further class:2

We also have a fourth class: those books of the Old Testament that are not in the Hebrew canon. Of these the first is the Book of Wisdom, the second Ecclesiasticus; the third Tobit; the fourth Judith; the fifth and sixth, the books of Maccabees. The Jews hold these separate among the apocrypha (apocrypha), but the Church of Christ honors and proclaims them among the divine books.

The count follows Jerome: the books are "not in the Hebrew canon," held by the Jews "among the apocrypha." The same books are those Augustine and the African councils had listed among the canonical books, and Isidore says the Church "honors and proclaims [them]… among the divine books" (inter divinos libros et honorat et praedicat). Rufinus had described such books as "read in the Churches, but not appealed to for the confirmation of doctrine"; Isidore's notice carries no clause about doctrine.

Isidore's "apocrypha": "no canonical authority"

In the following chapter of Book VI Isidore defines the apocrypha as works whose origin is hidden and doubtful, and holds that, whatever truth they contain, their false statements leave them without canonical authority:3

These are called 'apocrypha' (apocryphus, lit. "hidden, secret") because they come into doubt… Although some truth is found in them, nevertheless on account of their many false statements there is no canonical authority in them.

He does not name the books of his "fourth class" in this passage; he places Wisdom and Maccabees among the divine books the Church proclaims. In his book-by-book notices earlier in the same chapter, he remarks of Ecclesiasticus that, found among the Hebrews, it "is regarded as belonging to the apocrypha" (Hic et apud Hebraeos reperitur, sed inter apocryphos habetur), the label Jerome had used.4

Reception and debate

  • Two ranks. Isidore places the deuterocanon outside the twenty-two-book Hebrew canon, following Jerome, and in a named "fourth class" that the Church "honors and proclaims among the divine books."
  • Where VI.9 sits. Both the twenty-two/twenty-four-book Hebrew list and the honored "fourth class" fall in the same chapter and run consecutively — Etymologiae VI.i ("On the Old and New Testament"), the Hebrew classes in sections 3–8 and the fourth class in section 9. The list often cited as "VI.9" is that section 9; the canon material is one continuous passage.
  • The same scheme in his liturgical handbook. Isidore gives the identical four-fold arrangement in De ecclesiasticis officiis I.12 (On the books of the Old and New Testament): the Hebrew twenty-two in three orders, then the books "which are not in the canon of the Hebrews" — Wisdom, Ecclesiasticus, Tobit, Judith, and the Maccabees — which, "though the Jews separate them among the apocrypha, the Church of Christ honors and proclaims among the divine books."2
  • Later use. ⚠ unverified — Rabanus Maurus in the ninth century is said to reproduce Isidore's four-fold scheme verbatim, and the school of St Victor and others to carry the same "fourth order" language into the later Middle Ages; no source for these reports has yet been located.
