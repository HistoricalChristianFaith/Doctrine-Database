The Didache on the Old Testament Canon

Dates: c. 100 AD (The Teaching of the Twelve Apostles; compiled c. AD 100, range c. 50–150; anonymous, no single author) · Wikipedia
Summary: The Didache — the early church-order manual whose moral half is built on the Jewish "Two Ways" tradition — embeds a line of the deuterocanonical Sirach (Ecclesiasticus) in its catalogue of duties: "Be not a stretcher forth of the hands to receive and a drawer of them back to give" (Sirach 4:31), set among the same string of commandments as a paraphrase of the apostolic charge to raise one's children "in the fear of God" (cf. Ephesians 6:4). It gives no canon-list and uses no citation formula, as in Clement and Polycarp. The verse stands inside the Didache's "Two Ways" material, which it shares with the Epistle of Barnabas.

Claims & evidence

It embeds Sirach 4:31 in its catalogue of moral duties, beside an apostolic child-rearing charge

You shall not be undecided whether it shall be or no. Be not a stretcher forth of the hands to receive and a drawer of them back to give. If you have anything, through your hands you shall give ransom for your sins. You shall not hesitate to give, nor murmur when you give; for you shall know who is the good repayer of the hire. You shall not turn away from him that is in want, but you shall share all things with your brother, and shall not say that they are your own… You shall not remove your hand from your son or from your daughter, but from their youth shall teach them the fear of God.

— Didache (The Teaching of the Twelve Apostles), ch. 4, Ante-Nicene Fathers (Roberts–Donaldson translation). The framed sentence — "Be not a stretcher forth of the hands to receive and a drawer of them back to give" — reproduces Sirach 4:31 ("Let not thine hand be stretched out to receive, and shut when thou shouldest repay").1 It sits in an unbroken chain of moral commands that also folds in the apostolic charge to bring up one's children "in the fear of God" (cf. Ephesians 6:4, "bring them up in the nurture and admonition of the Lord"), with no mark setting the Sirach material apart from the rest.2 As in Clement and Polycarp, the deuterocanonical line is woven in as counsel, introduced by no "it is written."

Reception and debate

  • No canon-list. The Didache enumerates no books of the Old Testament. Its use of a Sirach maxim among its commandments is among the evidence adduced in "How did the early church receive the deuterocanonical books: as Scripture, below the canon, or outside it?" and "Do the Apostolic Fathers use the deuterocanonical books as Scripture?"; the readings of it are set out there.
  • The "Two Ways" material. The moral section of the Didache (chs. 1–6) is a Christian recension of an older Jewish "Two Ways" catechism, material it shares closely with the Epistle of Barnabas (chs. 18–20). Sirach 4:31 stands inside this inherited block.2
  • Placement on the timeline. The Didache's date is debated (estimates run c. 50–150 AD); the timeline places it c. 100, after Clement of Rome (c. 96) and beside Polycarp (c. 125), and before Melito of Sardis (c. 170), whose list is the earliest extant Christian count of the Old Testament books (see his page).1
