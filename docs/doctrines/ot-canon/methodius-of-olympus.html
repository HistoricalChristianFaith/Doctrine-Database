Methodius of Olympus on the Old Testament Canon

Dates: c. 290 AD (Methodius of Olympus, also called Methodius of Patara or Eubulius; a Greek bishop and theologian of Asia Minor, an early critic of Origen whose chief surviving work, the Banquet of the Ten Virgins (Symposium), was composed c. 290; reckoned a martyr under the Diocletianic persecution, d. c. 311) · Wikipedia
Summary: A Greek bishop of the turn of the fourth century and a critic of Origen. In his Banquet of the Ten Virgins a speaker introduces Wisdom of Solomon 3:16 with the formula “the Scripture which says.” The Oration on Simeon and Anna, transmitted under his name but of disputed authenticity, sets Sirach 16:6 under “as it is written” beside a Psalm. He compiles no list of the Old Testament books. His citation is a Greek datapoint, between Dionysius and Lactantius and before Nicaea, in the record of how the disputed books were used.

Claims & evidence

Wisdom of Solomon 3:16 cited as “the Scripture”

And that you may not take refuge behind a safe wall, bringing forward the Scripture which says, “As for the children of the adulterers, they shall not come to their perfection,”…

— The Banquet of the Ten Virgins, Discourse II (Theophila), ch. 3, in the Ante-Nicene Fathers translation, source.1 The quoted words — “As for the children of the adulterers, they shall not come to their perfection” — are Wisdom of Solomon 3:16, a deuterocanonical text. Methodius (here in the mouth of his interlocutor Marcella) introduces it as “the Scripture which says,” as a text an opponent might bring forward in the debate over the divine ordering of every birth. The Banquet is Methodius’s chief surviving work.

Sirach 16:6 under “as it is written,” beside a Psalm (a work of disputed authenticity)

Now smoke is a sign and sure evidence of wrath; as it is written, There went up a smoke in His anger, and fire from His countenance devoured; and in another place, Amongst the disobedient people shall the fire burn…

— Oration on Simeon and Anna, §12, in the Ante-Nicene Fathers translation, source.2 The single formula “as it is written” governs two quotations set in parallel: “There went up a smoke in His anger, and fire from His countenance devoured” is Psalm 18:8 (17:9 LXX), and the clause joined to it “in another place,” “Amongst the disobedient people shall the fire burn,” is Sirach (Ecclesiasticus) 16:6. Both stand under the one formula “as it is written.”

Reception and debate

  • The authenticity of the Oration on Simeon and Anna. The Oration is transmitted under Methodius’s name. The editors of the Ante-Nicene Fathers (1886) note that it is “not mentioned by the ancients, nor even by Photius” (citing Dupin), that its style “resembles that of Methodius in many places,” and that “the whole Oration is probably after” the Council of Ephesus (431). Roger Pearse (2023) reports that it is classed as pseudo-Methodian (CPG 1827), and that Roberto Caro dates it to the fifth or sixth century.2 The Banquet, where the Wisdom citation stands, is not disputed.
  • A critic of Origen. Methodius wrote against Origen on the resurrection and the pre-existence of souls, and stood outside the Alexandrian catechetical tradition of Dionysius; his citation of Wisdom comes from a writer of that other Greek milieu.
  • Use, not a canon-list. Like Irenaeus, Clement of Alexandria, Hippolytus, Tertullian, Cyprian, Dionysius, and Lactantius, Methodius nowhere enumerates the Old-Testament books. His citation is a datum of late-third-century Greek usage, among the witnesses discussed on the interpretation page “How did the early church receive the deuterocanonical books?”
