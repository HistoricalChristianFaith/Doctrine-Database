Tertullian on the Old Testament Canon

Dates: c. 210 AD (Tertullian, c. 155 – c. 220; a Christian writer of Carthage, the earliest extant major Christian theologian writing in Latin; traditionally called a presbyter of Carthage, as Jerome calls him (On Illustrious Men 53); whether his own works place him among the clergy or the laity is set out on its own page; his treatises — Scorpiace, De Anima, De Monogamia, De Corona, the Prescription against Heretics — belong to the first two decades of the third century) · Wikipedia
Summary: Tertullian, writing in Latin at Carthage, draws on the disputed books in his argument and gives no canon-list. He quotes the deuterocanonical Letter of Jeremiah (Baruch 6) as the words of “Jeremias,” beside Daniel; he cites words identified by the Ante-Nicene Fathers editors as the Wisdom of Solomon twice, once among “our Christian authorities” (an identification Roger Beckwith disputes) and once as the teaching of Solomon; and he names Judith and Susanna as examples.

The citations

He quotes the Letter of Jeremiah (Baruch 6:3) as the words of “Jeremias,” beside Daniel 3

Wherefore the brotherhood of the three also, when the dedication of the royal image was the occasion of the citizens being pressed to offer worship, knew well what faith, which alone in them had not been taken captive, required,—namely, that they must resist idolatry to the death. For they remembered also the words of Jeremias writing to those over whom that captivity was impending: “And now ye shall see borne upon (men’s) shoulders the gods of the Babylonians, of gold and silver and wood, causing fear to the Gentiles. Beware, therefore, that ye also do not be altogether like the foreigners, and be seized with fear while ye behold crowds worshipping those gods before and behind, but say in your mind, Our duty is to worship Thee, O Lord.” Therefore, having got confidence from God, they said, when with strength of mind they set at defiance the king’s threats against the disobedient…

— Scorpiace (Antidote for the Scorpion’s Sting), ch. 8, source.1 The words Tertullian ascribes to “Jeremias” are not from the canonical Jeremiah but from the deuterocanonical Letter of Jeremiah (carried in the Greek and Latin Bibles as Baruch ch. 6, the “Epistle of Jeremy”; the warning against the Babylonian idols stands at v. 3). He cites it under Jeremiah’s name as words the three youths “remembered,” in the same exhortation as the protocanonical Daniel 3.

“The searcher and witness of the heart” among “our Christian authorities” (identified as Wisdom 1:6 or Psalm 139:23)

…and better than all others, there are our Christian authorities. We are taught by God concerning both these questions—viz. that there is a ruling power in the soul, and that it is enshrined in one particular recess of the body. For, when one reads of God as being “the searcher and witness of the heart;” when His prophet is reproved by His discovering to him the secrets of the heart; when God Himself anticipates in His people the thoughts of their heart, “Why think ye evil in your hearts?” when David prays “Create in me a clean heart, O God,” and Paul declares, “With the heart man believeth unto righteousness,” and John says, “By his own heart is each man condemned”…

— A Treatise on the Soul (De Anima), ch. 15, source.2 Tertullian opens the chain of proofs with “our Christian authorities” and reads of God as “the searcher and witness of the heart”; the Ante-Nicene Fathers editors identify the phrase as Wisdom of Solomon 1:6 (“Wisd. i. 6”), set beside Proverbs, Matthew, the Psalms, Paul, and 1 John with no mark of distinction. Roger Beckwith (1985) reads the words instead as an echo of Psalm 139:23 (“Search me, O God, and know my heart”).

The opening of Wisdom (1:1) as taught from “the porch of Solomon”

What indeed has Athens to do with Jerusalem? What concord is there between the Academy and the Church? what between heretics and Christians? Our instruction comes from “the porch of Solomon,” who had himself taught that “the Lord should be sought in simplicity of heart.”

— The Prescription against Heretics, ch. 7, source.3 The saying “the Lord should be sought in simplicity of heart” is the opening verse of the deuterocanonical Wisdom of Solomon (1:1, “seek him in simplicity of heart”), which the Ante-Nicene Fathers note at this passage marks as “Wisdom of Solomon, i. 1.” Tertullian ascribes it to Solomon (“the porch of Solomon, who had himself taught”) and sets it as the source of Christian instruction over against the Academy.

Judith, “daughter of Merari,” among “examples of saints”

But upon this (infirmity) will sit in judgment no longer an Isaac, our monogamist father; or a John, a noted voluntary celibate of Christ’s; or a Judith, daughter of Merari; or so many other examples of saints.

— On Monogamy (De Monogamia), ch. 17, source.4 Judith — the heroine of the deuterocanonical book, widowed and never remarried — is placed beside the patriarch Isaac and John (whether the Baptist or the apostle) as a pattern of the single-married life. She is named as an example; no words of the book are quoted. Clement of Rome had named “the blessed Judith” as an example about a century earlier.

Susanna’s unveiling beside Rebecca’s veil

If Susanna also, who was subjected to unveiling on her trial, furnishes an argument for the veiling of women, I can say here also, the veil was a voluntary thing. She had come accused, ashamed of the disgrace she had brought on herself, properly concealing her beauty, even because now she feared to please.

— The Chaplet (De Corona), ch. 4, source.5 Susanna — the deuterocanonical addition to Daniel (the Ante-Nicene Fathers note gives “Vulgate, Dan. xiii. 32”) — is set beside the protocanonical Rebecca (Genesis 24:65) in a discussion of veiling. As with Judith, the figure is named as an example; no words of the book are quoted.

Reception and debate

  • No canon-list. Tertullian nowhere enumerates the Old-Testament books; like Irenaeus, Clement of Alexandria, and Hippolytus, he draws on the disputed books in the course of his argument.
  • Forms of citation. The citations take different forms. The Letter of Jeremiah (Baruch 6) is quoted under a prophet’s name. Wisdom is cited once among “our Christian authorities” (De Anima 15, where the identification is disputed) and once as the teaching of Solomon (Prescription 7). Judith and Susanna are named as examples, without quotation.
  • The De Anima identification. The De Anima 15 phrase “the searcher and witness of the heart” is identified as Wisdom 1:6 by the Ante-Nicene Fathers editors and as Psalm 139:23 by Roger Beckwith.2 The Prescription against Heretics 7 saying is marked “Wisdom of Solomon, i. 1” by the same editors.3
  • Placement on the timeline. Tertullian wrote at Carthage, c. 210; the councils of Hippo and Carthage (393, 397) and Cyprian of Carthage, who cites several of these books with formulae such as “the sacred Scripture speaks,” have their own entries.
  • Disputed readings. Tertullian’s citations are among the evidence adduced in “How did the early church receive the deuterocanonical books: as Scripture, below the canon, or outside it?”; the readings of that evidence are set out there. His writings are a generation earlier than Origen’s list.
  • Later life and reputation. Tertullian’s association with the Montanist “New Prophecy” in his later years, and how later writers have judged his standing, are set out on the timeline “Was Tertullian a Heretic?” (the presbyter-of-Carthage question in the head-note is one strand of it).
