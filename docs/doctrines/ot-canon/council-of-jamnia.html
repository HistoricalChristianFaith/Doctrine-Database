The "Council of Jamnia" on the Old Testament Canon

Dates: c. 90 AD (the date Heinrich Graetz proposed for a rabbinic council at Yavneh / Jabneh, c. 70–90 AD, after the fall of the Temple) · Wikipedia
Witness: The later rabbinic canon does not include Tobit or the other disputed books. The account that a formal Council of Jamnia sat about 90 AD and closed the Jewish canon against the Greek books, proposed by Heinrich Graetz in 1871, was disputed in the twentieth century by Jack P. Lewis, Sid Z. Leiman, and others. How the Jamnia tradition bears on the canon is set out on its own interpretation page.

Claims & evidence

The origin of the Jamnia hypothesis

Heinrich Graetz proposed in 1871 that a council convened at Yavneh around 70–90 AD and there fixed the Hebrew canon, in part as a reaction to the emerging Christian movement and its Greek Bible.1 The Wikipedia article on the council reports that the Christian scholars Herbert Edward Ryle and Frants Buhl subscribed to Graetz's thesis at the end of the nineteenth century, after which it gained wider acceptance among scholars.1

Twentieth-century critiques

Jewish sources contain echoes of debate about biblical books but canonicity was not the issue and debate was not connected with Jabneh... Moreover, specific canonical discussion at Jabneh is attested only for Chronicles and Song of Songs. Both circulated prior to Jabneh. … No book is ever mentioned in the sources as being excluded from the canon at Jabneh.

So Albert C. Sundberg Jr. summarized the crux of Jack P. Lewis's critique, "What Do We Mean by Jabneh?" (read before the Society of Biblical Literature in 1962, published 1964).2 In the same summary, the one text that speaks of official action at Jabneh (Mishnah Yadayim 3:5) concerns whether the Song of Songs and Ecclesiastes "render the hands unclean."2 W. M. Christie had disputed the synod in 1925 (the first to do so, in the Wikipedia article's account); Sid Z. Leiman (1976) made an independent challenge; Raymond E. Brown largely supported Lewis; and F. F. Bruce judged it "probably unwise to talk as if there were a Council or Synod of Jamnia."3 How the Jamnia tradition bears on the closing of the Hebrew canon is set out on its interpretation page.

The date of the Hebrew canon’s closing

On the date of closing, a survey of the question reports: "There is no scholarly consensus as to when the canon of the Hebrew Bible (or Tanakh) was fixed": proposals run from the Hasmonean period (c. 140–40 BC) to the second century AD and later, the one firm boundary being that the canon must postdate the final chapters of Daniel (c. 168–164 BC).4 The rabbinic canon that emerged does not include Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch, or 1–2 Maccabees, books the Greek Septuagint carries.4

Reception and debate

  • Placement on a Christian-canon timeline. The deuterocanon dispute concerns books that the Greek Septuagint carries and the Hebrew collection of the rabbinic tradition does not. The Jamnia marker records when and how the rabbinic collection has been thought to have been fixed.
  • The Septuagint-versus-Hebrew question. Because the rabbinic canon both omits these books and diverges from the Greek elsewhere, this marker belongs to the larger dispute gathered on the Rabbinic Corruption of Scripture page; the readings of the MT-versus-LXX question are set out there and not repeated here.
