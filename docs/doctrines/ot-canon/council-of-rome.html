The Council of Rome on the Old Testament Canon

Dates: 382 AD (the Roman synod under Pope Damasus I; the canon-list ascribed to it — the "Damasine list" — survives only in the later Decretum Gelasianum) · Wikipedia · New Testament canon page
Witness: The canon-list ascribed to the Roman synod of 382 gives the longer Septuagint Old Testament, filing Tobit, Judith, and the two books of Maccabees among "the Histories" and Wisdom and Ecclesiasticus (Sirach) among the Solomonic books. The list survives only as a chapter of the Decretum Gelasianum; its attribution to the synod of 382 and the date of the compilation are disputed (see below).

The Damasine list

The canon-list ascribed to the Roman synod of 382 stands as the second chapter of the Decretum Gelasianum. Its Old-Testament catalogue is the longer Septuagint reckoning, with the disputed books set among the protocanonical ones rather than ranked below them:1

Three books of Solomon: proverbs one book, ecclesiastes one book, song of songs one book. The same [Solomon's]: of Wisdom one book, ecclesiasticus one book… Likewise the order of the histories: Job one book, Tobit one book, Esdras two books, Esther one book, Judith one book, Maccabees two books.

Wisdom and Sirach stand with the Solomonic writings, and Tobit, Judith, and 1–2 Maccabees among "the Histories," with no separate rank. The list matches the longer Old Testament of the African councils (Hippo 393, Carthage 397 and 419); Athanasius and Jerome give twenty-two-book lists.2

The dating of the Damasine list

A synod met at Rome in 382 under Damasus: convened in the aftermath of the Council of Constantinople (381) and occupied chiefly with the Antiochene/Meletian schism, it is the synod to which Jerome — newly arrived in Rome that year, soon Damasus's secretary — twice refers in passing. Whether that synod promulgated the canon-list now ascribed to it is disputed. The list appears in no independent fourth-century text; it survives only inside the Decretum Gelasianum, which Ernst von Dobschütz (1912) judged a single pseudonymous compilation of the first half of the sixth century, noting that its opening chapter quotes a work Augustine wrote in 416. The attribution of its canon-chapters to a Damasan council was proposed by Faustino Arévalo (1794), and C. H. Turner edited them as "The Roman Council under Damasus"; W. A. Jurgens and George Reid (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1908) hold the 382 attribution, Reid writing that the essential part "is now generally attributed to a synod convoked by Pope Damasus in the year 382." Geoffrey Hahneman notes that Jerome, at Rome in 382, nowhere mentions a Roman conciliar canon; in his own prefaces Jerome ranks Judith, Tobit, and the Maccabees below the canon.3

The readings of the attribution (Arévalo, Turner, Jurgens, and Reid for the authenticity of the canon-chapters; von Dobschütz, Burkitt, Hahneman, and Metzger against the 382 promulgation) are set out on the page shared with the New-Testament canon timeline: The Council of Rome (382) and the "Damasine list" — did Damasus promulgate the canon?.4

Reception and debate

  • Placement on the timeline. The list is placed at the year of its ascription, 382; its dating is disputed, as set out above.
  • The same witness, two doctrines. This page treats the list's Old-Testament books; the parallel New Testament canon page treats the same list's twenty-seven-book New Testament.
  • Other Western lists of the period. The same longer Old Testament is listed by the African councils (Hippo 393, Carthage 397/419) and by Innocent I of Rome in 405. A related question, whether Nicaea counted Judith among the Scriptures, is set out on its own page.
