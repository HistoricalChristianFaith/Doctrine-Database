Basil of Caesarea on the Old Testament Canon

Dates: c. 330–379 AD (Basil "the Great," bishop of Caesarea in Cappadocia from 370, the elder of the three Cappadocian Fathers; the citations below come from the Hexaemeron, the treatise On the Holy Spirit [De Spiritu Sancto], and his Letters, all from his episcopate of the 370s) · Wikipedia
Witness: Basil leaves no catalogue of the Old Testament books. Across his exegetical and doctrinal works he quotes Sirach under the formula "What Scripture says is very true," Wisdom on the Lord who "ordereth all things in measure and weight," and Baruch and Judith by name on points of doctrine, and holds up the Maccabean mother as a martyr-model. His fellow Cappadocians Gregory of Nazianzus and Amphilochius wrote Hebrew-sized lists in verse.

Claims & evidence

Sirach quoted as "Scripture": "As for a fool he changeth as the moon"

The first case is in the sixth homily of the Hexaemeron, Basil's sermons on the six days of creation. Reflecting on the waxing and waning of the moon as an image of the instability of the soul that "after having possessed virtue, loses its beauty by neglect," he closes the point with a verse of the deuterocanonical Sirach (Ecclesiasticus 27:11), introduced with "What Scripture says":1

If you cannot behold without sadness the moon losing its splendour by gradual and imperceptible decrease, how much more distressed should you be at the sight of a soul, who, after having possessed virtue, loses its beauty by neglect, and does not remain constant to its affections, but is agitated and constantly changes because its purposes are unstable. What Scripture says is very true, "As for a fool he changeth as the moon."

Here the Sirach verse is introduced as what "Scripture" (ἡ γραφή) says and called "very true." A few lines further on he writes, "Evidently the moon must be, as Scripture says, of enormous size and power.1

Wisdom: the Lord "ordereth all things in measure and weight"

Writing to console a persecuted congregation (Letter 219), Basil opens by grounding the providence that proportions every trial in a verse of the deuterocanonical Wisdom of Solomon (11:20), set in the same breath as Paul's promise that God will not suffer His own "to be tempted above that they are able to bear" (1 Corinthians 10:13):2

The Lord ordereth "all things in measure and weight," and brings on us the temptations which do not exceed our power to endure them, but tests all that fight in the cause of true religion by affliction, not suffering them to be tempted above that they are able to bear.

The Wisdom phrase stands beside the Pauline text without a citation formula.

Baruch cited by name on a point of doctrine

In On the Holy Spirit, defending the equal honour of the Son against those who would seat Him "below" the Father, Basil reads the language of God's "sitting" as an image of the divine immutability — and supports the point from the deuterocanonical Baruch (3:3), cited under the prophet's own name:3

Standing and sitting, I apprehend, indicate the fixity and entire stability of the nature, as Baruch, when he wishes to exhibit the immutability and immobility of the Divine mode of existence, says, "For thou sittest for ever and we perish utterly."

Baruch was reckoned in the Greek tradition an appendix to Jeremiah, and Basil cites him by name, as Irenaeus, Hippolytus, and Athenagoras had cited the book, in an argument on a point of doctrine.

Judith cited by name on God's purpose

In the same treatise (8.19), expounding how the Son "upholds all things by the word of His power" without bodily labour, Basil cites a line of the deuterocanonical Judith (9:5–6), again ascribed to the speaker by name:4

So as Judith says, "Thou hast thought, and what things thou didst determine were ready at hand."

Where Clement of Rome had adduced "the blessed Judith" as a historical example of faith, Basil cites the book for its words, a sentence of Judith's prayer adduced for the point that God's purpose and its accomplishment are one.

The Maccabean mother as a pattern of martyrdom

The fifth use is a letter of consolation to the bereaved wife of Nectarius (Letter 6), where Basil holds up the mother of the seven sons from 2 Maccabees (6:18–7:42) as the model of a faith that gives thanks even in the loss of children, set beside the Gospel's sparrow that does not fall "without the will of our Father" (Matthew 10:29):5

The mother of the Maccabees saw the death of seven sons without a sigh, without even shedding one unworthy tear. She gave thanks to God for seeing them freed from the fetters of the flesh by fire and steel and cruel blows, and she won praise from God, and fame among men.

This is a historical example, not a Scripture-formula citation; the Maccabean martyrs stand in the same exhortation as the words of Christ.

Reception and debate

  • Basil in the canon debate. James White (Reformed Baptist apologist, debate with Gary Michuta, 2004) names "Basil the Great" among Fathers who "rejected the freestanding Apocrypha as fully canonical in their writings" (as quoted on the interpretation page on how the early church received the deuterocanonical books). No canon-list by Basil survives; the Hebrew-sized catalogues of the Cappadocian circle are those of Gregory of Nazianzus and Amphilochius. Basil's surviving works cite Sirach as "Scripture," Baruch and Judith by name on points of doctrine, and Wisdom on providence. Whether such citation attests canonical standing is set out on its own interpretation page.
  • Lists and usage. Elsewhere on the timeline a single figure gives both a list and citations: Athanasius, Cyril of Jerusalem, and Hilary each recite a Hebrew-sized canon and quote the disputed books in argument. Basil leaves citations only.
  • The Sirach citation. Of the five, only the Hexaemeron's "What Scripture says is very true" attaches the explicit word "Scripture" to a deuterocanonical text; the Baruch and Judith citations name the human author and cite the book on points of doctrine; the Wisdom citation uses the verse without a formula; the Maccabean mother is a moral example.
