Amphilochius of Iconium on the Old Testament Canon

Dates: c. 380 AD (the canon catalogue is the verse epistle Iambics for Seleucus [Iambi ad Seleucus], composed for a young man named Seleucus; Amphilochius c. 340–403, bishop of Iconium in Lycaonia and friend of the Cappadocians) · Wikipedia
Witness: Amphilochius versifies the canon book by book for Seleucus: Tobit, Judith, the Wisdom of Solomon, Sirach, and the Maccabees are not in the list, and Esther is given with the note "with these, some approve the inclusion of Esther." He opens by warning that "not every book is safe which has acquired the venerable name of Scripture." His list is of the Hebrew size, like those of Gregory of Nazianzus, Cyril of Jerusalem, Athanasius, and Epiphanius.

Claims & evidence

"Not every book is safe which has acquired the venerable name of Scripture"

Writing to instruct Seleucus, Amphilochius opens with a warning about books that bear the name of Scripture and then undertakes to "state for you the divinely inspired books one by one." He reaches the Old Testament after this warning and recites it book by book:1

But this especially for you to learn is fitting: not every book is safe which has acquired the venerable name of Scripture. For there appear from time to time pseudonymous books, some of which are intermediate or neighbours, as one might say, to the words of Truth, while others are spurious and utterly unsafe, like counterfeit and spurious coins which bear the king's inscription, but as regards their material are base forgeries. For this reason I will state for you the divinely inspired books one by one, so that you may learn them clearly. I will first recite those of the Old Testament. The Pentateuch has Creation, then Exodus, and Leviticus, the middle book, after which is Numbers, then Deuteronomy. Add to these Joshua, and Judges, then Ruth, and of Kingdoms the four books, and the double team of Chronicles; after these, Esdras, one and then the second. Then I would review for you five in verse: Job, crowned in the contests of many sufferings, and the Book of Psalms, soothing remedy for the soul, three of Solomon the Wise: Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, Canticle of Canticles. Add to these the Prophets Twelve… After these prophets learn yet another four: The great and fearless Isaiah, the sympathetic Jeremiah, and mysterious Ezekiel, and finally Daniel, most wise in his deeds and words. With these, some approve the inclusion of Esther.

Books named and not named

The list names the Pentateuch; Joshua, Judges, Ruth; the four books of Kingdoms (1–2 Samuel, 1–2 Kings) and two of Chronicles; the two of Esdras (Ezra–Nehemiah); the poetic five (Job, Psalms, Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, Song of Songs); the Twelve Minor Prophets; and the four major prophets (Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, Daniel). Tobit, Judith, the Wisdom of Solomon, Sirach, and Maccabees are not named. The list is of the Hebrew size, like those of Cyril and Athanasius on this timeline.1

Esther: "some approve"

Amphilochius does not list Esther outright; he appends it as a book on which opinion differs — "with these, some approve the inclusion of Esther."1 Athanasius places Esther outside the canon, among the books to be read (see his page).

Reception and debate

  • A teaching poem. The Iambics for Seleucus is a catechetical verse letter addressed to one student, giving the books "one by one, so that you may learn them clearly." Like Cyril's baptismal catechesis and Gregory of Nazianzus's canon poem, it is a teaching text rather than a conciliar act.
  • The Cappadocian cluster. Amphilochius was a cousin of Gregory of Nazianzus and a close associate of Basil of Caesarea, who consecrated him bishop of Iconium and addressed several letters and a major treatise to him. His Hebrew-sized list stands beside Gregory of Nazianzus's canon poem and the lists of Cyril in Jerusalem and Athanasius in Alexandria.
  • Jeremiah without Baruch. Where Cyril reckons Jeremiah "including Baruch… and the Epistle," Amphilochius lists Jeremiah without any such appendix. His poem names no deuterocanonical work, and leaves Esther as a book "some approve."
  • The opening warning. His coin metaphor — books that "bear the king's inscription, but as regards their material are base forgeries" — stands before both Testaments' lists; in the New Testament section he goes on to name disputed and rejected books. The Old Testament section names the "divinely inspired books" and does not name the deuterocanonical books or place them in his categories of books "intermediate or neighbours… to the words of Truth" or "spurious."
