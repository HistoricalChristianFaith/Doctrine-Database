The Cheltenham List on the Old Testament Canon

Dates: c. 360 AD (the catalogue is dated c. 359–365; it survives appended to the Liber generationis in a tenth-century Latin manuscript Theodor Mommsen found at Cheltenham, England, with a fuller copy at St Gall — hence "Cheltenham List," "Mommsen Catalogue," or "Index Cheltonianus") · Wikipedia
Witness: This anonymous Latin canon-catalogue, probably North African, enumerates 1–2 Maccabees, Tobit, and Judith among the Old Testament Scriptures, each with its line-count (stichometry); its "books of Solomon" are reckoned at 6,500 lines (on whether they include Wisdom and Sirach, see below). The Old-Testament list is sealed with the "twenty-four elders" of Revelation. It predates Hippo (393) and Carthage (397) and Innocent I.

Claims & evidence

The Old-Testament list

The catalogue is essentially a stichometric document: it names each book and records its length in standard sixteen-syllable lines, the bookseller's unit of the day. Its Old-Testament roll lists the protocanonical books and, among them, the two books of Maccabees, Tobit, and Judith, each with its own line-count:1

Here begins a list of the canonical books of the Old Testament —

Genesis 3700 lines, Exodus 3000 lines, Numbers 3000 lines, Leviticus 2300 lines, Deuteronomy 2700 lines, Joshua son of Nave 1750 lines, Judges 1750 lines … Ruth 250 lines, Kings book I 2300 lines, Kings book II 2200 lines, Kings book III 2550 lines, Kings book IIII 2250 lines … Chronicles book I 2040 lines, book II 2100 lines, Maccabees book I 2300 lines, book II 1800 lines, Job 1700 lines, Tobit 900 lines, Esther 700 lines, Judith 1100 lines, The 151 Psalms of David 5000 lines, The books of Solomon 6500 lines, The Major Prophets 15,370 lines, 4 in number — Isaiah 3580 lines, Jeremiah 4450 lines, Daniel 1350 lines, Ezekiel 3340 lines, The Twelve Minor Prophets 3800 lines…

But as it was said in the Apocalypse of John, "I saw 24 elders casting their crowns before the throne," our predecessors prove these books to be canonical, and that the elders signify this.

Maccabees, Tobit, and Judith stand among Chronicles, Job, and Esther under the single heading "the canonical books of the Old Testament," with no separate rank.

The “books of Solomon” at 6,500 lines

The list does not itemize the Solomonic books; it lumps them as "The books of Solomon" and assigns the bundle 6,500 lines. The work cited at n. 2 (Gallagher and Meade) takes a total of that size, beyond the three Solomonic books of the Hebrew canon (Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, the Song of Songs), to include the Wisdom of Solomon and Sirach (Ecclesiasticus), the two further "Solomonic" books the Latin Bible carried under his name.2 On that reading the count includes two deuterocanonical works beyond the three named (Maccabees, Tobit, Judith); the African councils (393–397) list "five books of Solomon."

The canon sealed by the "twenty-four elders"

The Old-Testament portion closes with an appeal to the New Testament rather than to the number of the Hebrew letters. The compiler appeals to the Apocalypse — "I saw 24 elders casting their crowns before the throne" (Rev. 4:10) — and reads the elders as figures of the canonical books: "our predecessors prove these books to be canonical, and that the elders signify this."1 Victorinus of Pettau and others also read the elders as the books of Scripture ⚠ unverified. The catalogue's own enumeration does not total twenty-four by an evident count: it names no Ezra–Nehemiah or Lamentations and includes Maccabees, Tobit, and Judith.

Reception and debate

  • A Western list before the African councils. If it stands c. 359–365 and is North African, it is a Latin Old-Testament list including deuterocanonical books, drawn up a generation before Hippo (393) and Carthage (397) issued their lists. It records what a mid-fourth-century Latin compiler listed as "the canonical books of the Old Testament." Later Western lists of the longer canon on this timeline include Innocent I's letter to Exsuperius and Augustine's On Christian Doctrine.
  • Dating it. The list is bound to the Liber generationis ("Book of Generation"), whose king-and-consul tables run down to the consulship of Valentinian and Valens in 365 — so the compendium reached its present form around then, though the book-list itself may be a little earlier (it is commonly cited as "c. 359"). Mommsen first published it from the Cheltenham manuscript in Hermes 21 (1886); a second, fuller copy turned up at St Gall. More recent scholarship (Rouse and McNelis) reads the whole compendium as an intact North-African reference book, possibly Donatist in origin.
  • The "una sola" marginal note. Against the New-Testament items the Cheltenham copyist twice scribbles "una sola" ("one only") — after the three epistles ascribed to John and the two ascribed to Peter, apparently demurring that only 1 John and 1 Peter are canonical. The note concerns the New-Testament Catholic Epistles; no such note stands against the Old-Testament books. (The catalogue's New-Testament list has its own oddities — four gospels in the order Matthew, Mark, John, Luke; Hebrews, James, and Jude absent — but those bear on the New-Testament canon, not the question here.)
