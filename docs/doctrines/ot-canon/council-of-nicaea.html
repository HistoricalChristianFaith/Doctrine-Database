The Council of Nicaea on the Old Testament Canon

Dates: 325 AD (the first ecumenical council; its twenty surviving canons and synodal letter) · Wikipedia
Witness: Nicaea (325) left no extant canon-list and no extant decree on Scripture; its acts concern the Arian and Melitian schisms, the lapsed, the date of Easter, and clerical discipline. Eighty years later Jerome reports, in his Preface to Judith, that the council "is found… to have been counted" Judith among the Scriptures. How that report is read is set out on its own page.

Placement on the timeline

Nicaea published no Old Testament canon. It appears here as the earliest council on this timeline to which a judgement on a deuterocanonical book has been attributed. The attribution is a sentence of Jerome, written c. 405 — he explains that he had been reluctant to translate Judith, a book the Hebrews place "among the Hagiographa," but took it up because of what he understood the Nicene synod to have done with it:1

But since the Nicene Council is considered to have counted this book among the number of sacred Scriptures, I have acquiesced to your request.

The sentence concerns only Judith, not Tobit, Sirach, Wisdom, Baruch, or the Maccabees, and does not say in what form the council counted the book.

Readings of the report

The council's twenty surviving canons say nothing of Scripture.2 The Jesuit Rudolph Cornely (1830–1908), author of a nineteenth-century Catholic Introductio to Scripture, read Jerome's sentence as testimony that the synod enumerated the sacred books and counted Judith among them. The Catholic introduction-scholar A. E. Breen and the conciliar historian Karl Joseph von Hefele read it as a report of a use of Judith in the council's deliberations — a book quoted or appealed to as Scripture — rather than of a promulgated canon-list; Breen compares the Council of Ephesus (431), which rested Cyril's anathemas on Hebrews without decreeing a New Testament canon. Jerome's verb is legitur ("is found," "is read"), and in his Festal Letter of 367 Athanasius, who was present at Nicaea, placed Judith among the books "not indeed included in the Canon, but appointed by the Fathers to be read."3

These readings, with Cornely's two further props (Cassiodorus, and the Vallicellian "Nicaea" heading on Hippo's canon 36), are set out on the dedicated page: Jerome's Preface to Judith — did the Council of Nicaea count Judith among the Scriptures?.

Reception and debate

  • A council without a list. Nicaea gives no canon-list, as Melito, Origen, Cyril, Athanasius, and Jerome do; it appears on this timeline as the earliest council to which a judgement on Judith has been attributed. The councils that enumerate the broader Old Testament — Hippo (393), Carthage (397, 419), and the list attributed to Rome — come later on this timeline.
  • The page on the report lives under the New Testament canon timeline. It was first written for the companion Biblical Canon page (which gathers the Nicene-canon traditions) and is shared, not duplicated, here.
