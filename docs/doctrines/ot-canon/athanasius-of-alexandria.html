Athanasius of Alexandria on the Old Testament Canon

Dates: c. 367 AD (the thirty-ninth Festal [Easter] Letter, issued for the year 367; Athanasius c. 296/298–373) · Wikipedia · his New Testament canon page
Witness: Athanasius's thirty-ninth Festal Letter (367) sorts the books into three ranks. The Old Testament canon is the twenty-two-book Hebrew reckoning, with Baruch and the Epistle of Jeremiah folded into Jeremiah, as Origen and Cyril had done. Below it stand books "not… included in the Canon, but appointed by the Fathers to be read," among them Wisdom, Sirach, Judith, Tobit, and Esther; below those, the "apocryphal" writings he calls "an invention of heretics." How the middle rank is read is disputed (see Reception and debate).

Claims & evidence

Tier one: the canon — "twenty-two books… the number of the letters among the Hebrews"

Athanasius opens his catalogue with the Old Testament, fixing the count at the Hebrew twenty-two and naming the books in order:1

There are, then, of the Old Testament, twenty-two books in number; for, as I have heard, it is handed down that this is the number of the letters among the Hebrews… The first is Genesis, then Exodus, next Leviticus, after that Numbers, and then Deuteronomy. Following these there is Joshua, the son of Nun, then Judges, then Ruth. And again, after these four books of Kings, the first and second being reckoned as one book, and so likewise the third and fourth as one book. And again, the first and second of the Chronicles are reckoned as one book. Again Ezra, the first and second are similarly one book. After these there is the book of Psalms, then the Proverbs, next Ecclesiastes, and the Song of Songs. Job follows, then the Prophets, the twelve being reckoned as one book. Then Isaiah, one book, then Jeremiah with Baruch, Lamentations, and the epistle, one book; afterwards, Ezekiel and Daniel, each one book. Thus far constitutes the Old Testament.

Tobit, Judith, Sirach, the Wisdom of Solomon, and the Maccabees are not in the canon list. Origen and Cyril (c. 350) also give Hebrew-sized counts (see their pages).

Jeremiah "with Baruch… and the epistle, one book"

As in Cyril's list, Jeremiah carries additions. Athanasius's single book of Jeremiah is reckoned "Jeremiah with Baruch, Lamentations, and the epistle, one book" — folding both Baruch and the Epistle of Jeremiah (the "epistle" = Baruch ch. 6 in the Vulgate numbering) into the canonical book, not as separate titles but as appendices, as the Greek manuscripts carried them.1 Origen likewise bundled "the epistle" with Jeremiah, and Cyril read Jeremiah "including Baruch… and the Epistle."

Tier two: "not… included in the Canon, but appointed by the Fathers to be read"

Between the canon and the apocrypha Athanasius sets a middle class of books "not indeed included in the Canon, but appointed by the Fathers to be read," and here he names Wisdom, Sirach, Esther, Judith, and Tobit:2

But for greater exactness I add this also, writing of necessity; that there are other books besides these not indeed included in the Canon, but appointed by the Fathers to be read by those who newly join us, and who wish for instruction in the word of godliness. The Wisdom of Solomon, and the Wisdom of Sirach, and Esther, and Judith, and Tobit, and that which is called the Teaching of the Apostles, and the Shepherd. But the former, my brethren, are included in the Canon, the latter being [merely] read…

In 367, a generation before the African councils list these books among the Scriptures (further down the timeline), Athanasius places them in this second rank. A distinction between canonical books and books read in the church appears also in Origen, and later in Jerome and Rufinus (c. 404) (see their pages).

Esther in the second rank; Baruch in the canon

Two details of the list bear on its comparison with the Jewish reckoning. First, he places Esther — a protocanonical, Hebrew book — in the second rank alongside Tobit and Judith, so his twenty-two differ from the Jewish reckoning both in leaving out a Hebrew book (Esther) and in including two Greek ones (Baruch, the Epistle).2 Second, the middle tier is commended "for instruction in the word of godliness" and distinguished from the third tier — the "apocryphal writings… an invention of heretics," which he describes as given an ancient date "that… they may find occasion to lead astray the simple."2

How Athanasius cites the disputed books

Beside the Festal-Letter ranking stand Athanasius's citations across his apologetic, polemical, and pastoral works. They fall into three groups.

(a) Books his list places inside the canon. Twice Athanasius cites as divine speech material that his catalogue places within a canonical book. In De Decretis (the Defence of the Nicene Definition) he makes the eternal Word the speaker of Baruch: "when the Word chides Israel, He says, 'You have forsaken the Fountain of wisdom'" (Baruch 3:12), set in a chain with Hebrews, the Psalms, and Jeremiah.3 And in the first Oration against the Arians he sets the Greek Susanna (the addition to Daniel) beside Isaiah as parallel testimony to God's eternity: "if Isaiah says, 'The Everlasting God, the Creator of the ends of the earth;' and Susanna said, 'O Everlasting God'" (Daniel 13:42).4 His list reckons Baruch within Jeremiah, and names Daniel as one book; Susanna stands in the Greek text of Daniel.

(b) Second-rank books cited in his own voice with Scripture formulas. The Wisdom of Solomon, which his catalogue places in the second rank, he quotes repeatedly with Scripture formulas. In Against the Heathen (Contra Gentes) he grounds his account of the origin of idolatry on a long passage of Wisdom (14:12–21), introducing it — "Scripture taught us beforehand long ago, when it said, 'The devising of idols was the beginning of fornication…'" — and glossing the same passage as "the wisdom of God testifies beforehand when it says" and, further on, as "what Scripture calls the incommunicable name and honour of God."5 In the second Oration against the Arians he marshals Wisdom 9:2 under an express citation formula — "and in the book of Wisdom it is written, 'Having ordained man through Your wisdom, that he should have dominion over the creatures…'" — naming the book and quoting it with "it is written" in a chain of texts "divine Scripture recognises," beside the Psalms, Revelation, 1 Timothy, Colossians, and the words of the Lord.6 The same Oration draws on Wisdom 13:5 — "for by the greatness and beauty of the creatures proportionably the Maker of them is seen" — among the texts of which he says in the same breath "hearing the Scriptures we believe."7 And in On the Opinion of Dionysius he vouches that Dionysius drew the Son's titles "the word, wisdom, power, breath… and brightness of the Father" "from the Scriptures," the "breath" being Wisdom 7:25.8 Sirach appears likewise: in the encyclical To the Bishops of Egypt and Libya Athanasius answers his opponents "in the words spoken by the Spirit," chaining Psalm 50:16 with Sirach 15:9 — "Praise is not seemly in the mouth of a sinner."9 And Tobit: in the Defence before Constantius he sets "Ask counsel of all that are wise" (Tobit 4:18) under an "it is written" frame.10 Of the four deuterocanonical books his Festal Letter places in the second rank, he cites Wisdom, Sirach, and Tobit in his own voice under Scripture formulas. How this bears on the meaning of his tiers is discussed on the interpretation page on the three tiers. Gregory the Great and John of Damascus, later on this timeline, also both give a ranking or list and cite disputed books (see their pages).

(c) A moral example, and a phrase matching two books. Two further passages bear on the question. In Festal Letter 4 Athanasius holds up "blessed Judith, when having first exercised herself in fastings and prayers, she overcame the enemies, and killed Olophernes" — as an exemplum of fasting and prayer set beside "blessed Esther," without a Scripture formula.11 And the line "But God is not as man, as Scripture has said" (Oration II.35) matches both Judith 8:16 and Numbers 23:19 in the Septuagint; Athanasius elsewhere uses "God is not as man" as a maxim with no Scripture tag.12

Attribution of two citations. Two lines quoted in the debate as Athanasius himself treating the deuterocanon as "holy Scripture" — Wisdom 1:11 ("The mouth that belies slays the soul") and Sirach 30:4 ("though his father die, yet he is as though he were not dead") — are not his own words. Both sit inside documents he compiled into his Defence against the Arians (Apologia contra Arianos), a dossier largely of letters by others: Wisdom 1:11 appears in the Encyclical Letter of the Council of Egypt — the synodal letter the assembled Egyptian bishops wrote on Athanasius's behalf, which speaks of him throughout in the third person, and which in the same breath cites Tobit 12:7 ("It is good to keep close the secret of a king") as "what is written" — and Sirach 30:4 in a letter of Alexander, bishop of Thessalonica.13 Those three citations are the words of the Egyptian council and of Alexander of Thessalonica, not Athanasius's own. In his own voice Athanasius quotes Wisdom and Sirach as Scripture elsewhere (Wisdom 14, 9:2, 13:5; Sirach 15:9).

Reception and debate

  • Athanasius in the canon debate. He names Wisdom, Sirach, Judith, and Tobit in the tier "not… included in the Canon, but appointed by the Fathers to be read," and the same passage prescribes them for the catechumens; his canon keeps Baruch and the Epistle of Jeremiah folded into Jeremiah. Archibald Alexander, James White, Steve Christie, and Gavin Ortlund cite him as a witness for a Hebrew-sized canon without the deuterocanon; Gary Michuta reads his "canon" as a narrower category that leaves the read books Scripture; Eugen Pentiuc reads the read books as the Orthodox anaginōskomena, a class read in the Church beside a fixed core. The readings are set out on their own page under Disputed readings.
  • The three tiers. The letter sets out a threefold scheme: canonical, read, and apocryphal writings he calls "an invention of heretics." What divides the top two tiers, given that he cites middle-tier books as Scripture, is discussed on the same page.
  • The Nicaea question. Athanasius sat at Nicaea (325) and in 367 places Judith among the books "appointed by the Fathers to be read," not "included in the Canon." How this bears on Jerome's report that Nicaea counted Judith among the Scriptures is discussed on the Nicaea-and-Judith page and noted on the Nicaea witness.
  • The New-Testament list. The same Festal Letter gives the earliest extant catalogue identical to the present twenty-seven-book New Testament — the four Gospels, Acts, seven Catholic Epistles, fourteen Paulines, and Revelation, "let no man add to these." That list, and its bearing on the close of the New-Testament canon, is treated on his New Testament canon page.
