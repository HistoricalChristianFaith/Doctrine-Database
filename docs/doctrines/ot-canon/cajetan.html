Cardinal Cajetan (Thomas de Vio) on the Old Testament Canon

Dates: Thomas de Vio, called Cajetan (Gaetano), c. 1469–1534 — Dominican theologian, Master General of the order, cardinal (1517), and papal legate; the man who examined Luther at Augsburg in October 1518. The witness here is his In omnes authenticos Veteris Testamenti historiales libros commentarii ("Commentary on all the authentic historical books of the Old Testament"), dedicated to Pope Clement VII and published in 1532, slotted on the timeline at that year · Wikipedia
Witness: Closing his commentary at the end of Esther (1532), Cajetan follows Jerome expressly: Judith, Tobit, the Maccabees, Wisdom, and Sirach are "counted out of the canonical books" and stand among the apocrypha, and the words of any council or Father that calls them canonical are "to be reduced to the correction of Jerome." They are canonical in a second sense, a rule "for the edification of the faithful," not "for confirming matters of faith." He had examined Luther at Augsburg in 1518.

Claims & evidence

The comment at the end of Esther: two senses of "canonical," and Jerome as the measure

Et hoc in loco terminamus commentaria librorum historialium Veteris Testamenti. Nam reliqui (videlicet Judith, Tobiae, et Machabaeorum libri) a divo Hieronymo extra canonicos libros supputantur, et inter apocrypha locantur, cum Sapientia et Ecclesiastico, ut patet in Prologo Galeato. Nec turberis, novitie, si alicubi reperies libros istos inter canonicos supputari, vel in sacris conciliis vel in sacris doctoribus. Nam ad Hieronymi limam reducenda sunt tam verba conciliorum quam doctorum; et juxta illius sententiam, ad Chromatium et Heliodorum episcopos, libri isti (et si qui alii sunt in canone Bibliae similes) non sunt canonici, id est, non sunt regulares ad firmandum ea quae sunt fidei; possunt tamen dici canonici, id est, regulares ad aedificationem fidelium, utpote in canone Bibliae ad hoc recepti et auctorati. Cum hac distinctione discernere poteris dicta Augustini, et scripta in Provinciali Concilio Carthaginensi.

— Thomas de Vio (Cajetan), In omnes authenticos Veteris Testamenti historiales libros commentarii (1532), comment on the last chapter of Esther (In ult. cap. Esther, ad fin.).1 In William Whitaker's English: "Here we close our commentaries on the historical books of the old Testament. For the rest (that is, Judith, Tobit, and the books of Maccabees) are counted by St Jerome out of the canonical books, and are placed amongst the Apocrypha, along with Wisdom and Ecclesiasticus, as is plain from the Prologus Galeatus. Nor be thou disturbed, like a raw scholar, if thou shouldest find any where, either in the sacred councils or the sacred doctors, these books reckoned as canonical. For the words as well of councils as of doctors are to be reduced to the correction of Jerome. Now, according to his judgment, in the epistle to the bishops Chromatius and Heliodorus, these books (and any other like books in the canon of the bible) are not canonical, that is, not in the nature of a rule for confirming matters of faith. Yet, they may be called canonical, that is, in the nature of a rule for the edification of the faithful, as being received and authorised in the canon of the bible for that purpose. By the help of this distinction thou mayest see thy way clearly through that which Augustine says, and what is written in the provincial council of Carthage."2

The passage in the debate

Two features of the passage are cited in the debate. First, he makes Jerome the interpretive rule: wherever a council (the provincial council of Carthage) or a Father (Augustine) calls these books canonical, "the words as well of councils as of doctors are to be reduced to the correction of Jerome" — i.e. read through Jerome's distinction, not against it. The preface of the Complutensian Polyglot uses Jerome's wording ("for the edification of the people" rather than "for confirming the authority of ecclesiastical dogmas"), as does the medieval Glossa Ordinaria. Second, the books "may be called canonical" in a second sense — "in the nature of a rule for the edification of the faithful," received into the canon of the Bible for that purpose, though not "for confirming matters of faith." Two-rank distinctions appear also in Jerome, Rufinus ("Ecclesiastical, not Canonical"), and Athanasius ("read, but not in the canon").

Whitaker on proto- and deutero-canonical

Whitaker, immediately after quoting the passage, writes that Protestants "may use, if we please, the same distinction" that Catholic writers — Sixtus of Siena and Thomas Stapleton — used in calling some books proto-canonical and others deutero-canonical, the proto-canonical being "those which are counted in the legitimate and genuine canon, i.e. of the Hebrews."3 Whitaker thus sets Cajetan's two senses of "canonical" beside the proto-/deutero-canonical terms of these later writers.

Reception and debate

  • Placement on the timeline. Between the Complutensian Polyglot (printed 1514–17, published 1522) and Luther's complete German Bible (1534), which printed the disputed books in a separate section headed Apocrypha, and fourteen years before the decree of the Council of Trent (1546). Cajetan, the Complutensian, and the Glossa Ordinaria are cited together in the disputed reading on whether the shorter, Hieronymian canon was the mainstream of the medieval Latin West before Trent.
  • The author. Cajetan was Master General of the Dominicans and a cardinal; his commentary on Aquinas's Summa is printed with the text. He was the papal legate who interviewed Luther at Augsburg in 1518. The commentary was dedicated to Pope Clement VII in 1532; Metzger (1957) cites it as showing that "even Cardinal Cajetan, Luther's opponent at Augsburg in 1518, gave an unhesitating approval to the Hebrew canon," and Whitaker (1588) quotes it in his Disputation against Catholic controversialists.
  • The Trent page. The Council of Trent witness page already notes that "on the very eve of Trent, Cardinal Cajetan had restated the old Hieronymian view, ranking the deuterocanon for edification below the protocanon." This page supplies the primary text that footnote asserted — Cajetan's own words at the end of Esther — and shows the two-sense distinction verbatim.
  • Before the Tridentine decree. Cajetan wrote in 1532, before the Council of Trent's decree of 1546. The same two-sense distinction appears in Jerome, the Glossa, and the Complutensian editors; on a minority at Trent who wished to keep Jerome's two tiers, see n. 3.
  • Text and translation provenance. The Latin is given as transcribed in the footnotes of William Whitaker's A Disputation on Holy Scripture (Parker Society, 1849), which reproduces Cajetan's comment in ult. cap. Esther; the original stands in Cajetan's In omnes authenticos Veteris Testamenti historiales libros commentarii (1532). The English is Whitaker's own rendering.
