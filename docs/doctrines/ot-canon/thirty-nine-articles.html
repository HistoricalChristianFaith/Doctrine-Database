The Thirty-Nine Articles on the Old Testament Canon

Dates: c. 1571 (the Articles of Religion of the Church of England in their final form; revised from the Forty-Two Articles of 1553 and the 1563 Convocation text, ratified by Convocation and Parliament in 1571) · Wikipedia
Witness: Article VI of the Church of England's Articles of Religion defines canonical Scripture as the books "of whose authority was never any doubt in the Church," lists a Hebrew-sized Old Testament, and files Tobias, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch, and 1–2 Maccabees among "the other Books" that "the Church doth read for example of life and instruction of manners; but yet doth it not apply them to establish any doctrine," citing Jerome ("as Hierome saith") by name.

The text of Article VI

Article VI: canonical = books "of whose authority was never any doubt in the Church"

The sixth Article opens with the rule of sufficiency — "Holy Scripture containeth all things necessary to salvation" — and then defines what counts as Holy Scripture by a test of undisputed reception:

In the name of the Holy Scripture we do understand those canonical Books of the Old and New Testament, of whose authority was never any doubt in the Church.

— Articles of Religion, Article VI, "Of the Sufficiency of the holy Scriptures for Salvation."1 The Article then lists the canonical Old Testament book by book — Genesis through Malachi, the Hebrew-sized thirty-nine — and receives "all the Books of the New Testament, as they are commonly received." The test the Article states is that the books' authority "was never any doubt in the Church"; on Augustine's criteria, see his page.1

The "other Books": "as Hierome saith," read "for example of life," not "to establish any doctrine"

Article VI then ranks the other books, citing Jerome:

And the other Books (as Hierome saith) the Church doth read for example of life and instruction of manners; but yet doth it not apply them to establish any doctrine; such are these following:

— Articles of Religion, Article VI.1 There follows a list of the books so read: "The Third Book of Esdras, The Fourth Book of Esdras, The Book of Tobias, The Book of Judith, The rest of the Book of Esther, The Book of Wisdom, Jesus the Son of Sirach, Baruch the Prophet, The Song of the Three Children, The Story of Susanna, Of Bel and the Dragon, The Prayer of Manasses, The First Book of Maccabees, The Second Book of Maccabees."1 The list is wider than the seven Tridentine deuterocanonical books: it brackets together the books Trent had defined as canonical (Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch, 1–2 Maccabees, with the Greek additions to Esther and Daniel) and the three the medieval Vulgate had carried but Trent's list does not name (3–4 Esdras and the Prayer of Manasses) — all alike "read for example of life and instruction of manners," none of them admissible "to establish any doctrine."

Earlier statements of the formula: Jerome, Rufinus and Luther

"As Hierome saith" points to Jerome's statement in the Prologus Galeatus and the prefaces — that the disputed books may be read "for the strengthening of the people, but not for confirming the authority of ecclesiastical dogmas" — a formula that Rufinus had also stated ("read in the Churches, but not appealed to for the confirmation of doctrine") and that Luther had set in print as a Bible heading ("not held equal to the Holy Scriptures, and yet are profitable and good to read").2 The Article's two ranks resemble those of Athanasius, Jerome, and Rufinus; Trent (1546) had listed the same books among the canonical Scriptures.2

Reception and debate

  • The Articles and later confessions. Like Luther and Rufinus, the Articles keep the deuterocanon as reading while denying it doctrine-establishing authority. The Westminster Confession (1647), the next witness on this timeline, holds the Apocrypha "of no authority in the Church of God, nor to be any otherwise approved, or made use of, than other human writings."
  • The list is wider than Trent's. Article VI's fourteen "other Books" include the three texts (3–4 Esdras, the Prayer of Manasses) that Trent's canonical list does not name, so the Article's list covers both the seven books Trent defined and the further Vulgate texts. It also lists the Greek additions (the rest of Esther; the Song of the Three Children, Susanna, Bel and the Dragon, all additions to Daniel) as separate items, where Trent had covered them with the phrase "entire with all their parts."
  • Texts in the books. The books include texts adduced in the controversies of the time, 2 Maccabees 12 for prayer and offerings for the dead and Tobit 12 for almsgiving that "saves from death." Whether doctrinal content drove the setting-aside is disputed; see the interpretation page.
  • Provenance. Article VI is quoted from the text of the Articles of Religion as printed in the Book of Common Prayer (1662) and reproduced at Wikisource; the date 1571 follows the Articles' final ratification, the 1563 Convocation text having been revised and confirmed in that year.
