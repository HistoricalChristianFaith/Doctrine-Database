Junilius Africanus on the Old Testament Canon

Dates: c. 542–549 (Junilius was quaestor sacri palatii — chief legal minister — at Justinian's court in Constantinople for seven years from c. 542; his Instituta Regularia Divinae Legis was composed in these years, its publication placed by Kihn c. 551) · Wikipedia
Witness: A North-African layman serving as Justinian's quaestor, Junilius translated into Latin a Greek handbook of Scripture-teaching learned from Paul the Persian of the school of Nisibis. It grades the books by authority: a first rank of "perfect authority" and a second of "middle authority" that "many add." Into the second rank he files the deuterocanonical histories (Tobit, Judith, Maccabees) and the Wisdom of Solomon, and also Chronicles, Job, Ezra, Esther, and Song of Songs, because "among the Hebrews too" these books "were received with this same distinction, as Jerome and the rest testify." Sirach stands in the first rank.

Claims & evidence

Three grades of authority: "perfect," "middle," and "none"

Quomodo diuinorum librorum consideratur auctoritas? — Quia quaedam perfectae auctoritatis sunt, quaedam mediae, quaedam nullius.

— Instituta Regularia Divinae Legis I (on the authority of the divine books), Latin text ed. from H. Kihn, Georgetown Latin edition.1 "How is the authority of the divine books considered? — Because certain of them are of perfect authority, certain of middle authority, certain of none." The books "of perfect authority" are, he says, "those which we have enumerated as canonical in each class" (Quae canonica in singulis speciebus absolute numerauimus); those "of middle" authority are "those we said are added by many" (Quae adiungi a pluribus diximus). The books "added by many" are thus listed, in a second tier below the first.

The historical books "added by many," with an appeal to the Hebrews and Jerome

Adiungunt plures Paralipomenon II, Iob I, Tobiae I, Esdrae I, Iudith I, Esther I, Machabaeorum II… Quoniam apud Hebraeos quoque super hac differentia recipiebantur, sicut Hieronymus ceterique testantur.

— Instituta I.3 (the historical books), Georgetown Latin edition.2 After listing the canonical histories, Junilius adds: "Many add the two books of Chronicles, Job, Tobit, Ezra, Judith, Esther, and the two of Maccabees… because among the Hebrews too they were received with this same distinction, as Jerome and the rest testify." The deuterocanonical Tobit, Judith, and the Maccabees are put in the second, "middle" rank, and with them Chronicles, Job, Ezra, and Esther, which the Hebrew canon contains. His stated warrant is the Hebrews' practice (to which Cyril and Athanasius also appeal), cited on the authority of Jerome "and the rest." The Dictionary of Christian Biography article calls the appeal "clearly incorrect with regard to several of them," and suggests that Junilius may himself have added the reference to Jerome to his Greek original; it reports H. Kihn's finding that the handbook is founded on the teaching of Theodore of Mopsuestia.2

The proverbial books: Proverbs and Sirach in the first rank, Wisdom and Song of Songs "added"

In duobus: Salomonis prouerbiorum liber I et Iesu, filii Sirach, liber I. Adiungunt quidam librum qui uocatur Sapientiae et Canticum canticorum.

— Instituta I.5 (the proverbial books), Georgetown Latin edition.3 Among the "proverbial" writings his first, canonical class holds just two: "Solomon's book of Proverbs, and the book of Jesus son of Sirach" — so the deuterocanonical Sirach (Ecclesiasticus) stands in his first rank, while "certain add the book called Wisdom and the Song of Songs" in the second. Sirach is thus ranked above the Wisdom of Solomon and above the Song of Songs, which the Hebrew canon contains.

The Apocalypse "much doubted among the Easterns"

Ceterum de Ioannis Apocalypsi apud orientales admodum dubitatur.

— Instituta I (on the doctrinal books), Georgetown Latin edition.4 "For the rest, concerning John's Apocalypse there is much doubt among the Easterns." Writing at Constantinople, Junilius reports an Eastern hesitancy over Revelation; on the New-Testament side Revelation is also absent from the lists of Cyril and the Council of Laodicea. His grading applies to both Testaments.

Reception and debate

  • A layman, and a Nisibene source. Junilius was a layman, Justinian's quaestor; his handbook renders into Latin the teaching of Paul the Persian from the school of Nisibis, which Kihn traced to the teaching of Theodore of Mopsuestia,2 and Cassiodorus mentions it with praise in his Institutes (1.10).4 Like Athanasius ("canon" vs. "read") and Rufinus ("canonical" vs. "ecclesiastical"), he sorts the books into tiers; his second tier also takes in Chronicles, Job, Ezra, and Esther.
  • The appeal to Jerome. Junilius grounds the second tier on "the Hebrews… as Jerome and the rest testify." Jerome counted Chronicles, Job, Ezra, and Esther within the twenty-two-book Hebrew canon (see the Jerome page); the Dictionary of Christian Biography article judges the appeal "clearly incorrect with regard to several of them."2
  • Tobit and the list. The Dictionary of Christian Biography article reports Tobit absent from Junilius's list (the Latin printed above, from the Georgetown text of Kihn's edition, includes it) and quoted later in the treatise. Kihn regarded the omission as an early transcriber's error, and the article agrees: "for no writer of the time would have designedly refused to include Tobit even in his list of deuterocanonical books."2
  • Near the Synopsis. Junilius sits beside the Synopsis of Sacred Scripture (c. 550) on the timeline as a mid-sixth-century list from the East; both place the disputed books in a lower rank. Later in the century, in the West, Gregory the Great quotes 1 Maccabees while describing it as not canonical.
