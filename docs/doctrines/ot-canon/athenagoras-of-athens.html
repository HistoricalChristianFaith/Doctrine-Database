Athenagoras of Athens on the Old Testament Canon

Dates: c. 177 AD (A Plea for the Christians / Legatio pro Christianis, addressed to the emperors Marcus Aurelius and Commodus, c. 176–177; Athenagoras a Christian philosopher of Athens, active in the later second century) · Wikipedia
Summary: Defending Christians against the charge of atheism, Athenagoras calls the Hebrew prophets as his witnesses to the one God. He names "Moses or… Isaiah and Jeremiah, and the other prophets" who, "lifted in ecstasy… by the impulses of the Divine Spirit, uttered the things with which they were inspired," and then quotes a series of their utterances; the first, "The Lord is our God; no other can be compared with Him," matches the deuterocanonical Baruch (Baruch 3:35–36), set beside three sayings of Isaiah. He does not name Baruch — he attributes the whole series to "the prophets," and Baruch travelled in the Greek tradition bound with Jeremiah, whom he does name. He gives no canon-list; the passage records his use of the line as prophecy.

Claims & evidence

A line of Baruch among the prophets' utterances, beside Isaiah

But, since the voices of the prophets confirm our arguments—for I think that you also, with your great zeal for knowledge, and your great attainments in learning, cannot be ignorant of the writings either of Moses or of Isaiah and Jeremiah, and the other prophets, who, lifted in ecstasy above the natural operations of their minds by the impulses of the Divine Spirit, uttered the things with which they were inspired, the Spirit making use of them as a flute-player breathes into a flute;—what, then, do these men say? "The Lord is our God; no other can be compared with Him." And again: "I am God, the first and the last, and besides Me there is no God." In like manner: "Before Me there was no other God, and after Me there shall be none; I am God, and there is none besides Me." And as to His greatness: "Heaven is My throne, and the earth is the footstool of My feet: what house will ye build for Me, or what is the place of My rest?"

— A Plea for the Christians 9.1 The first of the four utterances — "The Lord is our God; no other can be compared with Him" — matches the deuterocanonical Baruch (Baruch 3:35–36, "This is our God… there shall none other be accounted of in comparison of him"); the three that follow are Isaiah (Isaiah 44:6; 43:10–11; 66:1).2 Athenagoras places the Baruch line among the others, as one of the things the prophets "uttered… by the impulses of the Divine Spirit," in his argument for Christian monotheism from the prophets.

Reception and debate

  • Quoted as prophecy, not named. Athenagoras does not attribute the line to "Baruch": he gathers all four utterances under the generic heading of "the prophets," naming only Moses, Isaiah, and Jeremiah as the writers he expects his learned readers to know. Baruch was reckoned in the Greek and Old-Latin tradition an appendix to Jeremiah and was routinely cited under the prophet's name; Irenaeus, a few years later, quotes Baruch as "Jeremiah the prophet," and Cyril, Athanasius, and Epiphanius in the fourth century list Baruch within Jeremiah.
  • Kind of use. Unlike the moral maxims that Clement, the Didache, and Polycarp use without a citation formula, the Baruch line is classed among the words the prophets spoke "by the impulses of the Divine Spirit." Athenagoras gives no Old-Testament list and does not name the source book; Irenaeus introduces the same book with "Jeremiah the prophet… saying."
  • Disputed readings. His use is cited in the disputed reading "How did the early church receive the deuterocanonical books…?", as a second-century apologist quoting a line of Baruch among the prophets in a defence addressed to the emperors.
  • Place on the timeline. At c. 177 Athenagoras is a contemporary of Melito of Sardis (c. 170), whose list is the earliest extant Christian list without the deuterocanon, and of the Muratorian Fragment (c. 170); he stands just before Irenaeus (c. 180) — Melito enumerating a list without the disputed books, Athenagoras and Irenaeus quoting Baruch from the Greek text as prophecy.
