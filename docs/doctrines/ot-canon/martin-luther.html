Martin Luther on the Old Testament Canon

Dates: c. 1534 (the complete Luther Bible, Wittenberg; the prefaces to Judith and Tobit date with the 1534 edition, the Table Talk remarks from notes taken 1531–1546); his writings on the disputed books run from 1518, and his earliest recorded denial of a deuterocanonical book's canonicity is at the Leipzig Disputation of 1519 · Wikipedia
Witness: In his 1534 German Bible Luther printed the disputed books in a separate section between the Testaments under the heading Apocrypha: "books which are not held equal to the Holy Scriptures, and yet are profitable and good to read." In 1518 he had cited deuterocanonical books among his proof-texts; his earliest recorded denial of a deuterocanonical book's canonicity came at the Leipzig debate of 1519, over a proof-text for purgatory (below).

Claims & evidence

From the 1518 writings to the Leipzig Disputation (1519) and the Defense (1521)

In his earliest Reformation writings Luther cites the disputed books without marking them as a lower tier. In the Explanations of the Ninety-Five Theses (the Resolutiones, 1518) he quotes Sirach in the ordinary run of his proof-texts — "Be not without fear concerning sin forgiven" (Sir. 5:5) and "Do not delay to turn to the Lord" (Sir. 5:8) — and in his 1518 Response to Silvester Prierias he appeals to Tobit; the Heidelberg Disputation of the same year likewise draws on Sirach.1 These citations carry no mark of a lower rank.

At the Leipzig Disputation (July 1519), debating purgatory against Johann Eck, Luther was pressed with 2 Maccabees 12:43–45 — the offerings for the dead — as a scriptural proof. Luther answered by denying the book's canonical standing, his earliest recorded denial of a deuterocanonical book's canonicity:

There is no proof of purgatory in any portion of Sacred Scripture, which can enter into the argument and serve as a proof; for the book of Maccabees, not being in the canon, is of weight with the faithful, but avails nothing with the obstinate.

— Luther at Leipzig, July 1519. The familiar English renders the printed Latin acta of the disputation, where Luther first grants the proof-text — 2 Maccabees 12:46, "Sancta est et salubris cogitatio pro defunctis exorare" ("It is a holy and wholesome thought to pray for the dead") — and denies the book probative force: "nam et liber Machabeorum, cum non sit in canone, pro fidelibus potens est, contra pertinaces nihil facit" ("for the book of Maccabees, since it is not in the canon, has force for the faithful but avails nothing against the obstinate").2 Eck rejoined that Maccabees had always belonged to the Christian canon — rejected by the Jews but received by the Church — and Luther appealed to Jerome and the Hebrew canon (the canon apud Hebreos against the wider canon apud ecclesiam). He repeated the point in his own printed treatise two years later: in the 1521 Defense and Explanation of All the Articles he writes of 2 Maccabees that "that book is not among the books of Holy Scripture, and, as St. Jerome says, it is not found in a Hebrew version… this book deserves little authority."2

How this development is read is disputed. The Catholic apologist Gary Michuta reads Leipzig as a sudden, motivated reversal: Luther treated the deuterocanon as Scripture in 1518, then, pressed on purgatory, set 2 Maccabees aside, his "theological convictions" deciding the canon. The Protestant writer James Swan holds that Luther cited Sirach and Tobit by the identical method before and after 1519 — and went on doing so to the end of his life, in the Lectures on Genesis — so that the Leipzig statement is his first explicit articulation of a Jeromist two-tier view rather than a reversal; he adds that Luther still believed in purgatory in 1519 and for some years after, which he sets against the "rejected 2 Maccabees in order to defeat purgatory" motive. A 2025 paper, "Did Luther Change His Mind on the Canon?", holds that from 1516 to 1519 Luther used deuterocanonical and protocanonical texts interchangeably, and Leipzig marks a first explicit denial, but a selective and gradual one (2 Maccabees named, other deuterocanonical texts still used) rather than a wholesale rejection.3 The readings are set out under Disputed readings on the summary timeline, on the 2 Maccabees proof-text and the "did Luther remove the deuterocanon?" pages; Leipzig 1519 also appears on the purgatory timeline.

The 1534 Bible: the Apocrypha section

Where the Latin Vulgate had interleaved Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch, and 1–2 Maccabees among the Old Testament books, Luther gathered them — all but 1–2 Esdras, which he omitted entirely — and printed them between the Old and New Testaments under a single heading:

Apocrypha — that is, books which are not held equal to the Holy Scriptures, and yet are profitable and good to read.

— Luther Bible (1534), heading to the Apocrypha section.4 In this arrangement the disputed books are printed in the Bible but not numbered among the canonical Old Testament. The arrangement became the model for the vernacular Protestant Bibles that followed — the Swedish (1541), Danish (1550), and others — all carrying the Apocrypha as a distinct section with Luther's heading and prefaces.4

The prefaces: "a fine, good, holy, useful book"

To each apocryphal book Luther wrote a preface pointing out the devotional good a Christian might draw from it. For Judith he weighs the historical difficulties, finds the book will not square with the chronology of Jeremiah and Ezra, and concludes that it may be read as a sacred poem or drama rather than a history; he commends it:

If one could prove from established and reliable histories that the events in Judith really happened, it would be a noble and fine book, and should properly be in the Bible.… Therefore this is a fine, good, holy, useful book, well worth reading by us Christians.

— Luther, Preface to the Book of Judith (1534).5 For Tobit he likewise reads the book as a lesson about married life, teaching patience under hardship:

Tobit shows how things may go badly with a pious peasant or townsman, and there may be suffering in married life, yet God always graciously helps and finally crowns the outcome with joy, in order that married folk should learn to have patience and, in genuine fear of God and firm faith, put up gladly with all sorts of hardships.

— Luther, Preface to the Book of Tobit (1534).5 He commends the books as "holy, useful" reading and does not number them among the canonical books.

The Table Talk: Judith a "tragedy," Tobit "a comedy concerning women"

In his recorded dinner-table conversation Luther classifies the two books by the literary genres of the ancient poets:

The book of Judith is no history, nor does it accord with geography… I believe it is a poem… 'Tis a figurative work, like that of Homer about Troy, and that of Virgil about Aeneas, wherein is shown how a great prince ought to be adorned with surpassing valor… It is a tragedy, setting forth what the end of tyrants is. I take the book of Tobit to be a comedy concerning women, an example for house-government.

— Luther, Table Talk XXIV.6 The same passage speaks of 2 Maccabees and of the protocanonical Esther: "I am so great an enemy to the second book of the Maccabees, and to Esther, that I wish they had not come to us at all, for they have too many heathen unnaturalities."6 He also expressed a low estimate of Esther and, in the New Testament, of James.

Reception and debate

  • Luther's heading and the patristic ranks. Luther's heading sets the books apart as "not held equal to the Holy Scriptures, and yet … profitable and good to read," and prints them as a separate section. Two-tier distinctions between the canonical books and books read for edification appear earlier in Jerome, Rufinus, and Athanasius (see their pages); Swan reads Luther's distinction as Jeromist, while Michuta holds that Luther began from the wider canon (n. 3).
  • The course of the change. By 1521 Luther was in print denying 2 Maccabees canonical authority on Jerome's Hebrew-canon ground, and by 1534 he had printed the deuterocanon as a separate "Apocrypha" section. How he came to it is read as a reversal at Leipzig under the pressure of the purgatory debate (Michuta) or as the first explicit statement of a Jeromist distinction (Swan); see Disputed readings.
  • Texts in the books set apart. The deuterocanon includes texts adduced in the controversies of the time, 2 Maccabees 12 for prayer and offerings for the dead and Tobit 12 for almsgiving that "saves from death." Whether doctrinal content drove the setting-aside is disputed; see the interpretation page.
  • Later Protestant usage. Article VI of the Thirty-Nine Articles (1571) has the Apocrypha read "for example of life and instruction of manners," but not to "establish any doctrine"; the Westminster Confession (1647, 1.3) gives them no authority beyond "other human writings." Luther's Bible printed the books, and Protestant Bibles — the 1611 King James included, which printed the Apocrypha in its own section between the Testaments — went on printing them. Amid the "Apocrypha controversy" of the 1820s the British and Foreign Bible Society resolved in 1826 to apply its funds "to the printing and circulation of the Canonical Books of Scripture, to the exclusion of those Books and parts of Books usually termed Apocryphal," and the American Bible Society followed in 1827; after these decisions most Protestant Bibles were printed without the books. Whether Luther removed the books or ranked and relocated them is set out on its own interpretation page.7
  • Provenance. The Apocrypha heading is given as standardly translated and documented by Bruce M. Metzger; the prefaces are quoted from Luther's Works, vol. 35 (the American Edition, trans. Charles M. Jacobs); the Table Talk from the William Hazlitt translation (paragraph XXIV).
