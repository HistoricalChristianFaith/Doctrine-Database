The Septuagint on the Old Testament Canon

Dates: c. 132 BC (the Greek Old Testament as a maturing collection — the date the grandson and translator of Ben Sira gives in his prologue for his arrival in Egypt, the earliest external marker for a Greek "Law, Prophets, and the rest of the books") · Wikipedia
Witness: The extant complete Christian copies of the Greek Old Testament, the fourth- and fifth-century codices, carry Tobit together with Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch, and 1–2 Maccabees. The New Testament's scriptural citations largely follow the Greek text. Whether the first-century Greek Scriptures already carried these books is disputed; see the interpretation page.

Claims & evidence

The disputed books in the Septuagint codices

The three Christian pandect Bibles of the fourth and fifth centuries — Codex Vaticanus, Codex Sinaiticus, and Codex Alexandrinus, the oldest near-complete copies of the Septuagint — all carry Tobit among the Old Testament books, interleaved with the protocanonical ones rather than set off in a separate section.1 (Vaticanus and Alexandrinus preserve one textual form of Tobit; Sinaiticus a distinct, longer recension.) What the Fathers and councils said about the rank of these books is recorded in their own entries.

Use in the early church

"By the first century, the LXX was the Bible of Greek-speaking Jews and so was the most frequently used version of the Old Testament in the early Church."

— attributed to Catholic Answers, "In Which Passages Does Jesus Quote the Septuagint…?" ⚠ unverified: the sentence is not in the current text of that article, which says that "of the places where the New Testament quotes the Old, the great majority is from the Septuagint version."2 The Jewish Encyclopedia writes that the Septuagint, made for the Greek-speaking synagogue, "became part of the Bible of the Christian Church."3 Whether the Greek Scriptures the first Christians read already included the deuterocanonical books is disputed; see the interpretation page "Does the apostles' use of a Greek Old Testament attest the deuterocanon as part of their Scripture?"

New Testament quotations

"Protestant authors Archer and Chirichigno list 340 places where the New Testament cites the Septuagint but only 33 places where it cites from the Masoretic Text rather than the Septuagint."

— Catholic Answers, summarizing G. L. Archer & G. C. Chirichigno, Old Testament Quotations in the New Testament: A Complete Survey (Chicago: Moody, 1983), pp. 25–32.4 On this summary, the New Testament cites the Septuagint in 340 places, and the Masoretic Text, where it differs from the Septuagint, in 33. What the New Testament's quotations and allusions show about the deuterocanon's standing is discussed on the interpretation pages "Does the apostles' use of a Greek Old Testament attest the deuterocanon?" and "What does the New Testament's use of the deuterocanon, by allusion but never by formal citation, show about its standing?"

Reception and debate

  • A collection, not a list. The Septuagint was a body of Greek scrolls, not a single fixed table of contents; the Christian lists and conciliar statements on the books' canonicity come further down this timeline — Melito, Origen, the African councils, and the rest. Whether the disputed books belonged to the Greek Scriptures the first churches received is set out on the interpretation page "Does the apostles' use of a Greek Old Testament attest the deuterocanon as part of their Scripture?"
  • How the Greek Bible got its authority. The standing of this translation is the subject of the companion Origin and Inspiration of the Septuagint timeline — the Aristeas legend, the seventy-two translators, and the Christian claim that the work was itself inspired (Augustine, City of God XVIII.42–43, treats the LXX as Spirit-given).
  • The Septuagint-versus-Hebrew quarrel. Because the synagogue's later Hebrew (the Masoretic Text) omits these books and diverges from the Greek elsewhere, the inclusion question is bound up with the charge of a Rabbinic Corruption of Scripture, set out on that page.
