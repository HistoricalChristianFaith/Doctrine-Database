The Council of Trent on the Old Testament Canon

Dates: 8 April 1546 (Session 4 of the Council of Trent, reckoned in Catholic usage the nineteenth ecumenical council, under Pope Paul III; the Decree Concerning the Canonical Scriptures) · Wikipedia
Witness: In its fourth session (1546) Trent "receives and venerates with an equal affection of piety and reverence" all the books of both Testaments and prints the list, Tobias, Judith, Wisdom, Ecclesiasticus (Sirach), Baruch, and the two books of Maccabees among them, then anathematizes anyone who does not receive "the said books entire with all their parts." Luther's Bible of 1534 had printed the same books in a separate section.

Claims & evidence

The decree and its list

In its fourth session Trent took up the canon of Scripture, ahead of its decrees on justification and the sacraments. The decree declares the church's reception of both Testaments and then, "lest a doubt may arise in any one's mind," prints the list:

(the Synod) following the examples of the orthodox Fathers, receives and venerates with an equal affection of piety, and reverence, all the books both of the Old and of the New Testament—seeing that one God is the author of both… They are as set down here below: of the Old Testament: the five books of Moses, to wit, Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, Deuteronomy; Josue, Judges, Ruth, four books of Kings, two of Paralipomenon, the first book of Esdras, and the second which is entitled Nehemias; Tobias, Judith, Esther, Job, the Davidical Psalter, consisting of a hundred and fifty psalms; the Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, the Canticle of Canticles, Wisdom, Ecclesiasticus, Isaias, Jeremias, with Baruch; Ezechiel, Daniel; the twelve minor prophets… two books of the Machabees, the first and the second.

— Council of Trent, Session 4, Decree Concerning the Canonical Scriptures (1546), trans. J. Waterworth.1 Tobias, Judith, Wisdom, Ecclesiasticus (Sirach), Baruch, and the two books of Maccabees stand in the one list of books the council "receives and venerates," among Genesis, the Psalter, and Isaiah, with no mark of a lesser rank. The verb (suscipit ac veneratur) is the one the Council of Florence used in 1442 for the same books.2

Florence and Trent

Florence's list stands in a reunion profession that carries no anathema. Trent's decree adds one:

But if any one receive not, as sacred and canonical, the said books entire with all their parts, as they have been used to be read in the Catholic Church, and as they are contained in the old Latin vulgate edition… let him be anathema.

— Council of Trent, Session 4 (1546).3 Two features of the anathema tie the canon to the Latin tradition. First, the books are received "entire with all their parts," a phrase taken (see the note) as covering the deuterocanonical additions as well as the disputed books: the Greek portions of Daniel (Susanna, Bel and the Dragon, the Song of the Three Children) and the additions to Esther, which Jerome had marked off with an obelus. Second, the standard of "all their parts" is the books "as they are contained in the old Latin vulgate edition," which the same session's companion decree declared "authentic" for public use.4 Whether Trent added these books or defined a canon already received is set out on one interpretation page, and whether its decree was the first universal, binding one on another.

Books of the Latin Vulgate not named in Trent’s list

Vulgate manuscripts and earlier Latin lists had carried, beyond the seven disputed books, three further texts that Trent's enumeration does not name: 3 and 4 Esdras (the books Trent calls simply "the first book of Esdras, and the second which is entitled Nehemias" being the protocanonical Ezra and Nehemiah, not these) and the Prayer of Manasses.5 The Clementine Vulgate of 1592 placed these three — the Prayer of Manasseh and 3 and 4 Esdras — in an appendix after the New Testament, "lest they utterly perish," outside the canon. The Eastern Orthodox canon keeps some of these texts (e.g. 1 Esdras, the Prayer of Manasseh).6

Reception and debate

  • The disputed books received "as sacred and canonical." Athanasius, Rufinus, and Jerome rank these books a tier below the canon; Augustine and the African councils list them among the canonical books; Florence "accepts and venerates" them in a reunion bull; Trent receives them "as sacred and canonical," with an anathema (see their pages).
  • Luther's Bible and Trent. Luther's 1534 Bible had gathered the same books into a separate section headed "Apocrypha… not held equal to the Holy Scriptures" (see his page); Trent's decree, twelve years later, lists them among the canonical books and binds the text to the Vulgate.
  • Cajetan and the minority at the council. In 1532 Cardinal Cajetan ranked the deuterocanon for edification below the protocanon, as Jerome had, and at the council itself a minority wished to keep Jerome's two-tier distinction; the decree as passed lists the books without distinction.7
  • Disputed proof-texts in the defined books. Two of the books carry texts disputed in the Reformation — 2 Maccabees 12:43–45 (offering and prayer for the dead) and Tobit 12:9 (almsgiving "delivers from death"). Whether Trent received the books for the sake of such texts is set out on an interpretation page.
  • Readings of the definition. How Trent’s definition stands to the earlier receptions of these books, and the readings of B. F. Westcott (Anglican, 1875), James White, George Reid (Catholic, 1908) and others, are set out on the first-universal-binding-definition and late-novelty interpretation pages.
  • Translation provenance. The English is J. Waterworth's, The Canons and Decrees of the Sacred and Oecumenical Council of Trent (London: Dolman, 1848), a nineteenth-century English edition of the conciliar acts, reproduced from pp. 17–21.
