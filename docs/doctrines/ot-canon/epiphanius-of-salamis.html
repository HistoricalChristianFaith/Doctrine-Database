Epiphanius of Salamis on the Old Testament Canon

Dates: c. 385 AD (his canon catalogues span the Panarion, c. 377, and On Weights and Measures, c. 392; Epiphanius c. 310/320–403) · Wikipedia
Witness: Epiphanius gives a Hebrew-sized canon: twenty-two books by the count of the Hebrew letters (twenty-seven when the doubled books are separated), with Tobit, Judith, and the Maccabees absent. Baruch and the Epistle of Jeremiah stand inside the canonical Jeremiah, as in Origen, Cyril, and Athanasius, and the two Wisdom books are named as "useful" and "of disputed canonicity." In his own writing he quotes Sirach as "Scripture," argues the Incarnation from Baruch as "the sacred scripture," and ends a summary of "all the sacred scriptures" with "the Wisdoms, I mean Solomon's and Sirach's."

Claims & evidence

The canon: "twenty-seven recognized canonical books, but twenty-two when counted according to the letters"

In On Weights and Measures Epiphanius embeds his Old-Testament catalogue inside the Septuagint translation-legend: what the Seventy-(two) rendered, he says, was the recognized canon — counted two ways.1

…and thus were translated the twenty-seven recognized canonical books, but twenty-two when counted according to the letters of the alphabet of the Hebrews. For the names of the letters are twenty-two. But there are five of them that have a double form… Therefore in this manner the books also are counted as twenty-two; but there are twenty-seven, because five of them are double.

He then runs the list out in four "pentateuchs" plus two leftover books: the Law (Genesis–Deuteronomy); five "in verse" (Job, Psalms, Proverbs, Koheleth/Ecclesiastes, Song of Songs); the Writings (Joshua, Judges with Ruth, 1–2 Paraleipomena/Chronicles, 1–4 Kingdoms); the Prophets (the Twelve as one, Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, Daniel); and the two remaining — Ezra (the two counted as one) and Esther.1 Of the disputed books, Tobit, Judith, and the Maccabees are absent from the list. The Hebrew-letter reckoning is the one Origen, Cyril, and Athanasius also give. (The parallel list in the Panarion agrees: "twenty-seven books of the Old Testament, which are (also) numbered as twenty-two."2)

The Wisdom books: "helpful and useful, but… not… of the recognized" — "of disputed canonicity"

Epiphanius names the two Wisdom books, commends them, and places them outside the number of the recognized books:3

For there are two (other) poetical books, that by Solomon called "Most Excellent," and that by Jesus the son of Sirach and grandson of Jesus… which also are helpful and useful, but are not included in the number of the recognized; and therefore they were not kept in the chest, that is, in the ark of the covenant.

The Panarion says the same thing in a word: the Jews "have two more books of disputed canonicity, the Wisdom of Sirach and the Wisdom of Solomon, apart from certain other apocrypha."2 A generation earlier Athanasius had placed the deuterocanon among books "appointed by the Fathers to be read," outside the canon yet read. Epiphanius's image for the rank is his own: helpful books, but not in "the chest… the ark of the covenant."

Jeremiah with "the epistles of Baruch and of Jeremiah"

As in several other Eastern lists on this timeline, the Hebrew count includes material carried only in the Greek. Closing the catalogue, Epiphanius notes that the twenty-two are reckoned "even with the book of Psalms and those by Jeremiah," and spells out what "those by Jeremiah" means:4

…those by Jeremiah—I mean Lamentations and the epistles of Baruch and of Jeremiah, although the epistles are not in use among the Hebrews, but only Lamentations, which is joined to Jeremiah.

Baruch and the Epistle of Jeremiah are thus counted within Jeremiah, as the Greek manuscripts carried them and as Origen, Cyril, and Athanasius list them. Epiphanius adds that "the epistles are not in use among the Hebrews": the pieces he counts inside Jeremiah are not in the Hebrew text. The Panarion list likewise gives "the Prophet Jeremiah, with the Lamentations and the Epistles of Jeremiah and Baruch."2

The disputed books cited in his own writing

In the Panarion Epiphanius also cites books his catalogue places outside "the recognized," as Cyril, Hilary, and Athanasius do on their pages. He introduces Sirach and Baruch with scriptural formulas, and once names the two Wisdom books at the end of a list of "all the sacred scriptures."

Sirach, introduced "Scripture says." Writing against the Valentinian Ptolemy, Epiphanius closes a passage on Ptolemy's followers with a proverb he introduces as Scripture — Sirach 13:16:5

Scripture says, "Every bird flocketh with its kind, and a man will cleave to his like."

The formula is "Scripture says"; the book is one his Old-Testament list places "not… of the recognized."

Baruch, cited as "the sacred scripture" on the Incarnation. Twice Epiphanius cites Baruch 3:36–38 in Christological argument. Against the Arians, expounding why the Son could say "my God and your God" only after the Word took flesh, he folds the Baruch line into a chain of proof-texts running through Genesis, the Psalms, and John — that it was "when he had taken our body,"6

…[when he] "appeared on earth and consorted with men," and become one of us, then he said "my God and your God…" to his disciples…

And against Photinus, whom he opposes for denying the Son's pre-existence, Epiphanius reads the same Baruch verse as proof of both the Word's eternity and his coming in the flesh, calling it "the sacred scripture": "He which hath found out every path of knowledge" implies "his preexistence," while the next clause, "He appeared on earth," he says, "indicates his coming incarnation."7 Baruch, which his list counts within Jeremiah, here serves as a proof-text in his writing against both the Arians and the Photinians.

The two Wisdom books named among "all the sacred scriptures." Confronting the Anomoean over a term "scripture never mentions," Epiphanius bids him search the whole of Scripture — and his sweep of "all the sacred scriptures" runs the Old Testament, the Gospels, the Epistles, Acts, the Revelation of John, and then:8

…the Revelation of John, and the Wisdoms, I mean Solomon's and Sirach's—and, in a word, in all the sacred scriptures…

Here the two Wisdoms are named, not only cited, at the end of a roll he calls "the sacred scriptures." In On Weights and Measures the same two books are "helpful and useful, but… not… of the recognized" and "not kept in the chest… the ark of the covenant."

Every list he gives counts the canon as the Hebrew twenty-two, and Tobit, Judith, and the Maccabees appear in none of them. How his lists relate to his citations of Sirach, Baruch and the Wisdom books is one of the questions set out on "How did the early church receive the deuterocanonical books?"

Reception and debate

  • List and usage. His catalogues leave out Tobit, Judith, and the Maccabees; they keep Baruch and the Epistle of Jeremiah inside Jeremiah, and name the two Wisdom books as "helpful and useful" and "of disputed canonicity." His Panarion quotes Sirach with "Scripture says," cites Baruch as "the sacred scripture" on the Incarnation, and ends a sweep of "all the sacred scriptures" with "the Wisdoms, I mean Solomon's and Sirach's." The readings of how lists and usage relate are set out on "How did the early church receive the deuterocanonical books?"
  • The "ark of the covenant" image. Where Athanasius spoke of books "appointed by the Fathers to be read," Epiphanius pictures a middle rank in concrete terms: useful books that "were not kept in the chest, that is, in the ark of the covenant."
  • Two works, one position. The fuller list (with the "ark" line) is in On Weights and Measures (c. 392); a parallel catalogue, with the "disputed canonicity" phrase, stands in the earlier Panarion (c. 377). They agree, which is why the witness is dated to the mid-380s between them.
  • The numerology. In On Weights and Measures Epiphanius ties his twenty-two to a full number-scheme — "twenty-two works that God did in the six days … twenty-two generations from Adam to Israel … twenty-two signs of the letters … twenty-two books from Genesis to Esther." The same treatise names the two Wisdom books and places them "not … of the recognized," "not … in the ark." Gary Michuta (Catholic apologist) cites the number-scheme to argue that the patristic 22/24 counts are mystical numerology, not canon boundaries; James White (Reformed Baptist apologist), in their 2004 debate, reads such counts as a mnemonic for a real list. The readings are set out on their own page.
  • The canon set inside the Septuagint legend. Epiphanius gives this catalogue while retelling the story of the Seventy-two translators — the same legend discussed on the Origin and Inspiration of the Septuagint page. In the same passage he presents the Greek translation as the work of the Seventy-two and counts its canon as the Hebrew twenty-two, with the Wisdom books marked off as not "recognized" and the epistles of Baruch and Jeremiah, kept inside Jeremiah, noted as "not in use among the Hebrews."
