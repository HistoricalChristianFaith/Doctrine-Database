The Glossa Ordinaria on the Old Testament Canon

Dates: The standard glossed Bible of the medieval Latin West; its core compiled at the cathedral school of Laon c. 1120–1135 (Anselm of Laon, d. 1117; his brother Ralph of Laon, d. c. 1133; Gilbert the Universal, d. 1134), reaching its settled "ordinary" Parisian form by the later twelfth century, then diffused in manuscript across the medieval schools and printed from the editio princeps of Adolf Rusch (Strasbourg, 1480/81) · Wikipedia
Witness: The glossed Bible carried Jerome's Vulgate prefaces as front matter to Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, and the Maccabees, ranking them outside the canon, read "for the edification of the people, not for confirming the authority of ecclesiastical doctrines." The fuller "not in the canon" rubrics quoted in the modern debate come from Bernardino Gadolo's treatise written for the 1495 Venice glossed Bible.

Claims & evidence

The glossed Bible: Scripture ringed by the Fathers

The Glossa Ordinaria ("the ordinary/standard gloss") was the medieval West's default study Bible: the Vulgate text laid out in a large central column, with brief word-level notes written between the lines (the interlinear gloss) and longer patristic excerpts — Jerome, Augustine, Gregory, Bede, Isidore, Rabanus Maurus — arrayed around the margins (the marginal gloss). Assembled at the cathedral school of Laon in the early twelfth century and standardized at Paris by the century's end, it became, in the words of James White (Reformed Baptist apologist, 2004), "the standard commentary used in every single center of theological learning for hundreds of years."1 Its authorship was for centuries misassigned to the ninth-century monk Walafrid Strabo — an attribution first floated by Johannes Trithemius around 1500 and printed in the gloss itself only from the 1590 Paris edition, then queried by Samuel Berger (1893) and dismantled by Ceslas Spicq (1944) and Beryl Smalley, who reassigned the work to the school of Anselm of Laon (d. 1117), his brother Ralph of Laon, and Gilbert the Universal (d. 1134, to whom the gloss on Lamentations is firmly credited). Under the "Strabo" attribution the Glossa was dated to the ninth century; under the Laon attribution it is a twelfth-century compilation.2

The canon-prologues are Jerome's prefaces, carried as front matter

The Glossa's Old-Testament book-prologues largely reproduce Jerome's Vulgate prefaces. The line quoted in the modern debate as the Glossa's — that the Church reads the disputed books "for the edification of the people, not for confirming the authority of ecclesiastical doctrines" — is verbatim Jerome, from his Prologue to the Books of Solomon:

Sicut ergo Iudith et Tobi et Macchabeorum libros legit quidem Ecclesia, sed inter canonicas scripturas non recipit, sic et haec duo volumina legat ad aedificationem plebis, non ad auctoritatem ecclesiasticorum dogmatum confirmandam.

— Jerome, Prologus in libris Salomonis (the Vulgate preface the Glossa carries).3 In English: "Therefore, just as the Church reads the books of Judith and Tobit and the Maccabees but does not receive them among the canonical Scriptures, so let it read these two volumes [Wisdom and Sirach] for the edification of the people, not for confirming the authority of ecclesiastical doctrines." The companion formula sits in Jerome's Prologus Galeatus, likewise transmitted in the front matter: "Igitur Sapientia, quae vulgo Salomonis inscribitur, et Iesu filii Sirach liber et Iudith et Tobias et Pastor non sunt in canone" — "Therefore Wisdom, which is commonly ascribed to Solomon, and the book of Jesus son of Sirach, and Judith and Tobias and the Shepherd are not in the canon."4 The Glossa's "not in the canon" is thus Jerome's preface, carried in the front matter of the standard gloss into the schools that used it. The Latin Bibles that carried these prologues also contained the disputed books; on the ranking of books read but placed outside the canon compare Athanasius and Rufinus.

The Glossa and Jerome’s prefaces

The 1913 Catholic Encyclopedia describes the Glossa as having "embodied the prefaces in which the Doctor of Bethlehem had written in terms derogatory to the deuteros, and thus perpetuated and diffused his unfavourable view," and the same article records that "during this long period the deuteros were read in the churches of Western Christendom."5 James White counts the Glossa among the medieval witnesses to the shorter canon (n. 1). Whether the Glossa, with the Complutensian Polyglot and Cardinal Cajetan, shows the shorter canon to have been the medieval mainstream, or the diffusion of Jerome's prefaces, is set out on Was the shorter, Hieronymian canon the mainstream of the medieval Latin West before Trent?

Gadolo’s rubrics in the 1495 Venice edition

William Webster's survey (n. 6) quotes the glossed Bible as prefacing each disputed book with a blunt rubric — "Here begins the book of Tobit, which is not in the canon; here begins the book of Judith, which is not in the canon," and likewise for Wisdom, Ecclesiasticus, and the Maccabees — together with a fuller framing prologue distinguishing "the canonical and the non-canonical books, which the Hebrews separate from the canon":

…inter libros canonicos, et non canonicos, quos Hebraei a canone separant, et Graeci inter apocrypha computant… Sunt igitur libri canonici Veteris Testamenti viginti duo, ad numerum viginti duarum litterarum Hebraeorum.

— "…between the canonical and the non-canonical books, which the Hebrews separate from the canon, and the Greeks reckon among the apocrypha… There are therefore twenty-two canonical books of the Old Testament, to the number of the twenty-two letters of the Hebrews."6 This framing prologue is a self-contained editorial treatise, De canonicis et non canonicis libris, written by the Camaldolese humanist Bernardino Gadolo (1463–1499), prior of San Michele in Isola, expressly for the four-volume glossed Bible printed by Paganino Paganini at Venice in 1495; it was carried into the Basel Bible of 1498 (Froben & Petri, with Nicholas of Lyra's Postilla) that William Webster quotes, and later reprinted in Migne's Patrologia Latina (113:19–24) under the same "Walafrid Strabo" attribution that once misdated the gloss itself.7 It is a Renaissance editor's apparatus, post-dating Rusch's 1480/81 editio princeps by some fifteen years and the manuscript gloss by about three and a half centuries; its twenty-two-book count and its "not in the canon" restate Jerome's prefaces.

(The exact Latin of the running per-book rubrics — "Incipit liber Tobiae, qui non est de canone," and the like — is reproduced only in English translation in the surveys quoting it; that precise wording is not yet checked against a digitized folio, though the underlying "not in the canon" is Jerome's verbatim, carried in the Vulgate front matter from the start – nn. 3–4.)

Reception and debate

  • Placement on the timeline. Between the Masoretic Text (c. 1000) and the list of Florence (1442). The Glossa carried Jerome's prologues in the front matter of the Bible used in the medieval schools.
  • Authorship. The traditional "Walafrid Strabo, ninth century" attribution was questioned by Samuel Berger (1893) and set aside by Ceslas Spicq (1944) and Beryl Smalley, who assign the Glossa to the twelfth-century school of Laon (n. 2).
  • Diffusion and count. White and Webster count the Glossa among the medieval witnesses to the shorter canon; the 1913 Catholic Encyclopedia describes it as having "perpetuated and diffused" Jerome's view. The readings are set out on the medieval-mainstream page.
  • Text provenance. Jerome's prologues are quoted from the Vulgate prefaces (bible-researcher.com; The Latin Library). The systematized "De canonicis et non canonicis libris" front matter is Bernardino Gadolo's treatise for the 1495 Venice (Paganini) glossed Bible, edited in Martin Morard's Gloss-e / Sacra Pagina (IRHT-CNRS) and printed in Migne, PL 113:19–24 (Gallica scan of PL 113/114, bpt6k5505319w). A digitized glossed Bible carrying it is the Basel 1498 edition at e-rara.ch; the editio princeps (which lacks Gadolo's tract) is reproduced in Karlfried Froehlich & Margaret T. Gibson, eds., Biblia Latina cum Glossa Ordinaria (facsimile of Rusch, Strasbourg 1480/81; Turnhout: Brepols, 1992).
