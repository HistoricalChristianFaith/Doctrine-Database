Jerome on the Old Testament Canon

Dates: c. 390 AD (the Vulgate prefaces and the Hebraica veritas program, c. 391–405; Jerome c. 347–420) · Wikipedia
Witness: In the Prologus Galeatus Jerome counts the Old Testament by the Hebrew reckoning and sets "whatever is outside of these… among the apocrypha": Wisdom, Sirach, Judith, Tobias, and the Shepherd are "not in the canon," and in his preface to the books of Solomon the Maccabees are named with Judith and Tobias as books the Church "reads" but "does not receive… among the canonical Scriptures." Where Cyril, Athanasius, and Epiphanius folded Baruch into Jeremiah, Jerome omits it as "neither read nor found among the Hebrews." At the request of the bishops Chromatius and Heliodorus he translated Tobit and Judith, and he ranked Wisdom and Sirach as books to "read… for the strengthening of the people, (but) not for confirming the authority of ecclesiastical dogmas." In letters and commentaries he introduces Sirach with "Holy scripture says" and Wisdom with "it is written," ranges Baruch among "the prophets," and names Judith among "sacred volumes."

Claims & evidence

The canon: "whatever is outside of these is set aside among the apocrypha"

The Prologus Galeatus — the "helmeted" preface Jerome set at the head of the books he turned "from Hebrew into Latin" — fixes the Old Testament at the Hebrew count and names the books that fall outside it:1

This prologue to the Scriptures may be appropriate as a helmeted introduction to all the books which we turn from Hebrew into Latin, so we may be able to know whatever is outside of these is set aside among the apocrypha. Therefore, Wisdom, which is commonly ascribed to Solomon, and the book of Jesus son of Sirach, and Judith and Tobias, and The Shepherd are not in the canon. I have found the First Book of the Maccabees (is) Hebrew, the Second is Greek, which may also be proven by their styles.

Just before, he runs the list out in the Hebrew order — "twenty-two scrolls… five of Moses, eight of the Prophets, nine of the Hagiographa" — counted by the twenty-two letters of the Hebrew alphabet, the same reckoning Origen, Cyril, Athanasius, and Epiphanius gave before him.1 Where the Eastern Fathers ranked the disputed books below the canon as "read in the churches" or "useful," Jerome applies to them the word apocrypha.

Baruch outside the canon

Cyril, Athanasius, and Epiphanius fold Baruch and the Epistle of Jeremiah into the canonical book of Jeremiah, as the Greek manuscripts carried them. In his preface to Jeremiah Jerome states that he has left Baruch out:2

And the Book of Baruch, his scribe, which is neither read nor found among the Hebrews, we have omitted, standing ready, because of these things, for all the curses from the jealous…

Cyril, Athanasius, and Epiphanius counted "twenty-two" and carried Baruch inside Jeremiah; Jerome follows the Hebrew text, expecting the omission to draw "all the curses from the jealous." His rule is the Hebraica veritas, the "truth of the Hebrew," the program traced on his Rabbinic Corruption of Scripture page.

The translation of Tobit and Judith for Chromatius and Heliodorus

Jerome rendered Tobit and Judith into Latin at the request of the bishops Chromatius and Heliodorus. His prologue to Tobit gives his reasons:3

For you demand that I bring a book written in Chaldean words into Latin writing, indeed the book of Tobias, which the Hebrews exclude from the catalogue of Divine Scriptures, being mindful of those things which they have titled Hagiographa… For the studies of the Hebrews rebuke us and find fault with us, to translate this for the ears of Latins contrary to their canon. But it is better to be judging the opinion of the Pharisees to displease and to be subject to the commands of bishops.

He describes the translation as "contrary to their canon" and states that he prefers to displease "the opinion of the Pharisees" rather than the "commands of bishops." His Judith prologue likewise notes that "among the Hebrews the Book of Judith is found among the Hagiographa, the authority of which toward confirming those which have come into contention is judged less appropriate," and says that he has "acquiesced to your request."4

The formula: “read… for the strengthening of the people, not for… ecclesiastical dogmas”

In his preface to the books of Solomon Jerome deals with the two Wisdom books. He calls the Wisdom of Solomon a "falsely ascribed work" that "was never among the Hebrews," says that he has found Sirach "in Hebrew," and then sets a rule for both:5

Therefore, just as the Church also reads the books of Judith, Tobias, and the Maccabees, but does not receive them among the canonical Scriptures, so also one may read these two scrolls for the strengthening of the people, (but) not for confirming the authority of ecclesiastical dogmas.

The rule has two clauses: read them, but do not prove doctrine by them. The Church of England's Article VI (1571) has the other books read "for example of life and instruction of manners," but not "to establish any doctrine," citing Jerome by name ("as Hierome saith").

The Nicaea report — discussed on its own page

The Judith prologue carries a further claim. Jerome gives, as his reason for translating a book he otherwise ranks below the canon, a conciliar precedent:4

But because this book is found by the Nicene Council to have been counted among the number of the Sacred Scriptures, I have acquiesced to your request…

No canon-list from the council is extant. How Jerome's report has been read, as a canon decision or as the council's citation of Judith in argument, is set out on its own page.6

Jerome’s citations of the books as “Scripture”

In his letters and commentaries Jerome at times cites books his Prologus Galeatus files "among the apocrypha" with the formulae of Scripture. Writing to Julian (c. 406) he introduces a maxim from Sirach thus:7

Holy scripture says: "a tale out of season is as musick in mourning."

Expounding Isaiah 1:24, he glosses the "mighty" with a line from the Wisdom of Solomon under the formula scriptum est — "it is written":8

… de quibus scriptum est: "Potentes potenter tormenta patientur."

— "… of whom it is written: 'The mighty shall be mightily tormented'" (Wisdom of Solomon 6:7). Writing to Oceanus (Letter 77 §4) he ranges a phrase of Baruch among the prophets' own proclamations:9

… and those of Baruch, "Arise, arise, O Jerusalem," and many other proclamations made by the trumpets of the prophets.

And in Letter 65 (on Psalm 44, to the virgin Principia) he sets Judith beside the canonical Ruth and Esther as women who gave their names to "sacred volumes":10

Ruth et Esther et Judith tantae gloriae sunt, ut sacris voluminibus nomina indiderint.

— "Ruth and Esther and Judith are of such glory that they gave their names to sacred volumes."

These citations are in letters and commentaries; the canon-statements are in the prologues. How such citations bear on a Father's canon, in Jerome as in Cyril, Hilary, Epiphanius, and Gregory of Nazianzus, is set out on the interpretation pages on citation and canonical standing and on the early church's reception.

Letter 53 to Paulinus: a book-by-book survey of the Bible (394)

Writing to Paulinus of Nola in 394 — a letter (Letter 53) urging Paulinus to a diligent study of Scripture — Jerome takes him on a tour of the whole Bible, book by book, pausing on each to draw out its spiritual sense. The Old Testament half of that survey closes with Esther, Chronicles, and Ezra–Nehemiah:11

Solomon, a lover of peace and of the Lord, corrects morals, teaches nature, unites Christ and the church, and sings a sweet marriage song to celebrate that holy bridal. Esther, a type of the church, frees her people from danger… The book of things omitted… is of such importance and value that without it any one who should claim to himself a knowledge of the scriptures would make himself a laughing stock in his own eyes… Ezra and Nehemiah, that is the Lord's helper and His consoler, are united in a single book.

The survey runs through the five books of Moses, Job, "Jesus the son of Nave" (Joshua), Judges with Ruth, Samuel, the third and fourth books of Kings ("called in Hebrew Malachim"), the twelve prophets "compressed within the narrow limits of a single volume," then Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, and Daniel, the Psalms of David, the three books of Solomon, Esther, Chronicles, and Ezra–Nehemiah, and from there he turns to the New Testament. Wisdom, Sirach, Tobit, Judith, the Maccabees, and Baruch do not appear in the survey; the twelve minor prophets form a single volume, and Ezra and Nehemiah are "united in a single book."11

Letter 53 is an exhortation to the study of Scripture, not a formal canon-list: Jerome does not use the words "canon" or "apocrypha" here and draws no boundary line.

The Presbyterian scholar Archibald Alexander (1826) read Letter 53 as giving "a catalogue of the books of the Old Testament, exactly corresponding with that which Protestants receive." He also quotes Jerome as adding that these books were, "agreeably to the tradition of our ancestors, … inspired by the Holy Spirit." That clause does not appear in Letter 53; similar wording introduces the canon-list of Rufinus's Commentary on the Apostles' Creed ("which, according to the tradition of our forefathers, are believed to have been inspired by the Holy Ghost, and have been handed down to the Churches of Christ").12

Reception and debate

  • Jerome's two categories. Jerome calls the books "apocrypha" and does not keep Baruch inside Jeremiah; he translates Tobit and Judith, commends "the widow Judith, an example of chastity,"4 and grants Wisdom and Sirach a place in church reading. In his Solomon preface a book may be read in the Church without being received "among the canonical Scriptures."
  • Translation and canon. Kevin P. Edgecomb, the translator of the prologues quoted here, reads the Solomon and Judith prologues together: "we know that he recognized the authority of the Council of Nicea in advancing Judith as canonical. So, St Jerome's equivocation on the matter reveals a more subtle understanding, in which he considers the apocrypha good 'for the strengthening of the people.'"13 The wider readings of the record are set out on the interpretation pages on the early church's reception and on how the books came to stand in the Vulgate.
  • The word "apocrypha." Cyril's charge to "have nothing to do with the apocryphal writings" names no books; the Prologus Galeatus names Wisdom, Sirach, Judith, Tobias, and the Shepherd as set "among the apocrypha."1 What the label signified in later centuries is set out on its own interpretation page.
  • Jerome's numbering of the "books of Ezra." On Pierre-Maurice Bogaert's reconstruction, Jerome's first and second "books of Ezra" were Ezra–Nehemiah (a single book) and the Greek/Old-Latin Esdras A (the "3 Esdras" of the Vulgate appendix); his third book was the Apocalypse of Ezra (4 Ezra proper, minus the later additions); and Bogaert leaves the fourth to conjecture, perhaps 5 Ezra or 6 Ezra, or the two combined. Ambrose cites one and the same verse, 4 Ezra 6:41, as the "third book" of Esdras in his Latin, where his modern translator renders "fourth"; the numbering of the "books of Ezra" thus differs between the two Latin writers.14
  • His reasons and later evidence. Jerome's language judgments (Tobit a "Chaldean" book, 1 Maccabees Hebrew and 2 Maccabees Greek, the Wisdom of Solomon a Greek composition, Sirach "found in Hebrew") are set beside the twentieth-century manuscript finds (the Aramaic and Hebrew Tobit of Qumran, the Hebrew Ben Sira of the Cairo Geniza and Masada). Whether later evidence on the languages and on the dating of the Jewish canon bears on his grounds is set out on its own interpretation page.
  • Jerome in popular apologetics. Popular Protestant teachers cite the prefaces set out above: that "even Jerome… said the Apocrypha is not Bible," translating it only because "he thought it had some value" while going "out of his way to say this thing isn't scripture" (Matt Whitman); that he rendered the books "with a note saying… these are not inspired writ" (Todd Friel); that he "wrote in there 'These aren't scripture'" though he held them "highly esteemed" (Mike Winger); and that the Vulgate carried them "despite Jerome's insistence that the Apocrypha did not belong in the Bible" (GotQuestions).15
  • Jerome and the word apocrypha. The Greek word apocrypha ("hidden things") is attested before Jerome: Irenaeus (c. 180) charges the Marcosians with adducing "an unspeakable number of apocryphal and spurious writings," and the word appears after him in Clement of Alexandria, Tertullian, and Origen. In the Prologus Galeatus Jerome applies it to a class of Old-Testament books outside the Hebrew canon. Mike Winger says that Jerome "invented the term apocrypha," taking it from a Jewish practice of "hiding"/setting aside non-scriptural books rather than destroying them.16
  • "Apocrypha" or "Hagiographa"? The Prologus Galeatus sets Wisdom, Sirach, Judith, Tobias, and the Shepherd "among the apocrypha" and "not in the canon"; the Tobit and Judith prologues, in Edgecomb's renderings quoted above, say the Hebrews place those books "among the Hagiographa," the name of the third division of the Hebrew canon (the nine "Hagiographa" Jerome counts inside the twenty-two). Many printed editions, including Migne's Patrologia Latina, read apocrypha at this point in the prologues. Edgecomb (2006) takes "Hagiographa" as a term which in the Tobit prologue Jerome "certainly seems to have equated with the Scriptures," so that "at one point in history, in the late 4th century, Judith was considered by at least some Jews in Palestine to have been Scripture."13 Edmon Gallagher (2012) reports that the critical Vulgate (the Benedictine edition) reads Hagiographa in both prologues, with only a few manuscripts giving apocrypha for Judith and none for Tobit, and concludes that "it would seem that Hagiographa is the correct reading"; he asks whether Jerome uses "Hagiographa" in two senses, noting older commentators, among them Dionysius the Carthusian, who glossed it here as "apocrypha," with the word taken broadly. In his Journal of Early Christian Studies article of the same year Gallagher holds that Jerome's "apocrypha" was not a neutral "non-canonical" tag that hardened late in his life but a consistent and pejorative judgment, the Prologus Galeatus propagating "an especially harsh judgment against these books."17
  • Cross-links. Jerome's preference for the Hebrew over the Septuagint, and his description of the Greek translators as interpreters rather than prophets, are treated on his Rabbinic Corruption of Scripture page; the inspiration of the Greek Bible is the subject of the Origin and Inspiration of the Septuagint timeline. (Jerome also appears, on a different question, on the Nephilim page.)
  • Texts used. The quotations are from Jerome's prefaces to the Vulgate — the "Helmeted" Prologue (Prologus Galeatus), and the prologues to Jeremiah, Tobit, Judith, and the books of Solomon — composed c. 391–405, in Kevin P. Edgecomb's public-domain English; the book-by-book survey of Letter 53 to Paulinus (394) is in W. H. Fremantle's English (NPNF 2.6). The individual prefaces vary in date; the timeline places Jerome's canon-statement at c. 390, with the Prologus Galeatus.
