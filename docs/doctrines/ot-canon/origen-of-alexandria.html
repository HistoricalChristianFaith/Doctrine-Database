Origen of Alexandria on the Old Testament Canon

Dates: c. 240 AD (the Old Testament list is from his Commentary on Psalm 1, quoted by Eusebius; the Letter to Africanus defending the churches' Greek text of Daniel is c. 240; d. c. 253) · Wikipedia · his New Testament canon page
Witness: Origen names the Old Testament books "as the Hebrews have handed them down" as twenty-two, the Hebrew-letter count; Tobit and Judith are absent, and the Maccabees he sets "besides these." Writing to Julius Africanus, he defends the additions to Daniel (Susanna) that the Hebrew lacks, refusing to "reject as spurious the copies in use in our Churches." In his sermons and treatises he cites Tobit, Sirach, Baruch, the Maccabees, Wisdom, Judith, and Susanna as "Scripture."

Claims & evidence

The twenty-two books "as the Hebrews have handed them down"

In his Commentary on Psalm 1 Origen gave a catalogue of the Old Testament, which Eusebius preserved in the Ecclesiastical History (vi.25.1–2). He frames it explicitly as the Hebrew reckoning — twenty-two books, matching the twenty-two letters of the Hebrew alphabet — and lists each with its transliterated Hebrew title:1

It should be stated that the canonical books, as the Hebrews have handed them down, are twenty-two; corresponding with the number of their letters… The twenty-two books of the Hebrews are the following: That which is called by us Genesis… Exodus… Leviticus… Numbers… Deuteronomy… Jesus, the son of Nave… Judges and Ruth, among them in one book… the First and Second of Kings, among them one… the Third and Fourth of Kings in one… of the Chronicles, the First and Second in one… Esdras, First and Second in one… the book of Psalms… the Proverbs of Solomon… Ecclesiastes… the Song of Songs… Isaiah… Jeremiah, with Lamentations and the epistle in one… Daniel… Ezekiel… Job… Esther. And besides these there are the Maccabees, which are entitled Sarbeth Sabanaiel.

Books outside the twenty-two: the Maccabees set "besides these"

The list does not name Tobit, Judith, Sirach, the Wisdom of Solomon, or Baruch as a separate book. The Maccabees Origen does name, after the twenty-two: "besides these there are the Maccabees."2 Melito's earlier list, and the later lists of Cyril of Jerusalem, Athanasius, and Jerome on this timeline, also count a Hebrew-sized canon.

"Jeremiah, with Lamentations and the epistle"

"Jeremiah, with Lamentations and the epistle" includes the Epistle of Jeremiah — a short deuterocanonical work (Baruch ch. 6 in the Vulgate numbering) — counted as part of Jeremiah, as the Greek manuscripts transmitted it, within a list Origen introduces "as the Hebrews have handed them down."2 The received text of the list also omits the Twelve Minor Prophets, which leaves the named books short of twenty-two; the bible-researcher.com collation treats the omission as a scribal lapse.2

The Letter to Africanus on the Greek text of Daniel

When Julius Africanus wrote to challenge the story of Susanna — one of the Greek additions to Daniel absent from the Hebrew — as no genuine part of Scripture, Origen defended the churches' copies:

… are we immediately to reject as spurious the copies in use in our Churches, and enjoin the brotherhood to put away the sacred books current among them, and to coax the Jews, and persuade them to give us copies which shall be untampered with, and free from forgery! Are we to suppose that that Providence which in the sacred Scriptures has ministered to the edification of all the Churches of Christ, had no thought for those bought with a price, for whom Christ died…?

— Letter to Africanus §§4–5 (defending Susanna and the wider Greek text of Daniel against the shorter Hebrew).3 Origen, who compiled the Hexapla setting the Hebrew and the Greek versions side by side, argues here that the churches should keep what Providence had given them in the Greek Scriptures. The same letter engages objections to Susanna's authenticity and answers them.

In the same letter he explains that he studies the Jews' own readings so that, in debate with them, he will not argue from what their copies lack:

And I make it my endeavour not to be ignorant of their various readings, lest in my controversies with the Jews I should quote to them what is not found in their copies, and that I may make some use of what is found there, even although it should not be in our Scriptures… For if we are so prepared for them in our discussions, they will not, as is their manner, scornfully laugh at Gentile believers for their ignorance of the true reading as they have them.

— Letter to Africanus §5.3 In controversy with Jews, then, Origen reasons from what stands in the Hebrew, while in the same letter he declines to "reject as spurious the copies in use in our Churches." What this rule of debate shows about the standing of the disputed books is discussed on the interpretation page "Did the Fathers rest doctrine only on the undisputed books, and what does their argument with the Jews from shared books show?"

Citations of the disputed books with Scripture formulae

Across his sermons and treatises Origen introduces the disputed books with the formulae "the scripture says," "it is written," "holy Scripture," "as Scripture declares." Six disputed books and the additions to Daniel appear this way:

  • Tobit. Arguing that angels and the saints at rest pray alongside us, he cites the answered prayer of Tobit and Sarah as Scripture — "After both had prayed, the scripture says, 'The prayer of both was heard before the presence of the great Raphael and he was sent to heal them both'" (Tobit 3:16–17).4
  • Sirach. Reproaching those who reproach the penitent, he corrects them by Scripture — "though the Scripture says: 'Do not reproach a man who turns away from sin'" (Sirach 8:5).5
  • Baruch. He frames Baruch with the written-word formula — "the land about which it is written: Hear Israel. 'Why is it that you are in the land of enemies… You have forsaken the fountain of life, the Lord…'" (Baruch 3:9–13).6
  • 2 Maccabees. For creation from nothing he appeals to the book of Maccabees — "But that we may believe in the authority of holy Scripture that such is the case, hear how in the book of Maccabees… she says, 'I ask of you, my son, to look at the heaven and the earth… to know that God made all these things when they did not exist'" (2 Maccabees 7:28).7
  • Wisdom of Solomon. Closing a discussion of Pharaoh, he quotes Wisdom as Scripture — "'for in the hand of God are both we and our words; all wisdom, also, and knowledge of workmanship,' as Scripture declares" (Wisdom 7:16).8
  • Judith. Illustrating a righteous breaking of faith, he draws "an example from Scripture" — "Judith made an agreement with Holophernes that… she also would present herself… at the marriage bed of Holophernes."9
  • Susanna (additions to Daniel). He turns Susanna against corrupt clergy and speaks of its place among the divine books — "it is time for us to use the words of holy Susanna against these wicked presbyters, which indeed those who deny the story of Susanna excise from the list of divine books. But we both receive it and aptly use it against them."10 This is the Susanna he defends to Africanus.

Origen thus gives a twenty-two-book list "as the Hebrews have handed them down" and, in his homilies and treatises, cites the wider Greek books under Scripture formulae. How the list and the citations fit together is read differently by the writers noted below; whether citation "as Scripture" shows that a Father held a book canonical is discussed on the interpretation page "When a Father quotes a book 'as Scripture,' does that show he held it canonical?"

Reading practice: the easy books as "milk" for beginners

In his Homily on Numbers 27 Origen sorts the divine reading into "milk" for beginners and solid food for the mature, and names, among the "clearer and simpler" readings that those "starting out in divine studies" gladly receive, Esther, Judith, Tobit, and the precepts of Wisdom:11

That is why, as in the physical illustration, the food some have in the Word of God is milk, that is, the clearer and simpler doctrine. This normally consists in moral instruction, which is customarily given to those who are starting out in divine studies and who are receiving the first elements of a rational education. And so, when some such reading from the divine books is recited, in which there seems to be nothing obscure, they gladly receive it, for example, the brief books of Esther, Judith or even Tobit, or the precepts of the book of Wisdom. But if the book of Leviticus is read to him, his mind immediately stumbles and he flees from it as from something that is not his own food.

The passage describes church reading practice, the diet suited to a beginner; it is not a list of the canonical books, and Origen cites Tobit, Judith, and Wisdom under Scripture formulae elsewhere (above). In 367 Athanasius lists Wisdom, Sirach, Judith, Tobit, and Esther among books "appointed by the Fathers to be read by those who newly join us… for instruction." How Athanasius's tiers bear on the standing of these books is discussed on the interpretation page "How do the three tiers of Athanasius's Festal Letter 39 (367) bear on the standing of the deuterocanon?"

Reception and debate

  • Origen the Hebraist. Origen compiled the Hexapla, setting the Hebrew and the Greek versions in parallel columns. In his list he gives the Hebrew canon (the twenty-two); in the Letter to Africanus and his citations he keeps the churches' wider Greek Scriptures. Later writers on this timeline — Rufinus, Jerome, and Athanasius — distinguish formally between canonical books and books read in the churches.
  • Report or prescription? Origen frames the list as the books "as the Hebrews have handed them down" (ὡ Ἑβραῖοι παραδιδόασιν). Edmon L. Gallagher and John D. Meade (2017) read it as a report of the Jewish reckoning rather than a prescriptive Christian ruling, and on that reading the list sits beside his defense of the Greek Susanna and his citations of the wider books; Roger T. Beckwith (1985) holds that the Hebrew-conscious Fathers reported the Jewish canon because they judged it the right one for Christians too, so that describing and prescribing need not be opposed.12 Further down the timeline Cyril of Jerusalem charges the catechumen to "have nothing to do with the apocryphal writings," and Jerome calls the books outside the Hebrew canon apocrypha (see their pages). The Epistle of Jeremiah, bundled into Origen's Jeremiah, is not part of the Hebrew Bible.
  • Gary Michuta's "adds Maccabees to reach 22" reading. Because the received text of the catalogue omits the Twelve Minor Prophets, the named books run to twenty-one, and Origen then names the Maccabees. Gary Michuta reads this as numerology: Origen, "one name short," supplies the Maccabees (with a Hebrew title, Sarbeth Sabanaiel) "to reach 22." The bible-researcher.com collation reads the absence of the Twelve as a scribal lapse; with the Twelve restored the list gives twenty-two books, with the Maccabees named "besides these" (ἔξω δὲ τούτων).2 Whether the patristic 22/24 counts mark canon boundaries or alphabetic correspondences is discussed on its own interpretation page, with Origen's list among its exhibits.1
  • Modern citation of the list. Origen's twenty-two-book catalogue is cited in the modern case for the shorter Old Testament. The nineteenth-century Princeton theologian Archibald Alexander quotes it — "the canonical books are the same which the Hebrews delivered unto us, and are twenty-two in number, according to the number of letters of the Hebrew alphabet" — and adds that "Origen barely mentions the Maccabees."13 Origen's list, as Eusebius gives it, names "the epistle" — the Epistle of Jeremiah (Baruch ch. 6 in the Vulgate numbering), folded into "Jeremiah, with Lamentations and the epistle" — and does not name Baruch proper (chs. 1–5). A surmise (⚠ unverified; no named holder located) is that "the epistle" might stand for Baruch and its epistle together, since the Epistle traveled as Baruch's sixth chapter. Steve Christie writes that Origen "rejected all of the Deuterocanonical books, except for Baruch and the epistle of Jeremiah," while accepting "all" the Hebrew books.14
  • Eduard Reuss on Origen's citations. Eduard Reuss — the nineteenth-century Strasbourg Protestant professor whose History of the Canon of the Holy Scriptures held that the canon was still open in Origen's day — writes that Origen quotes "the Wisdom of Solomon, Ecclesiasticus, and the books of the Maccabees, as the Scriptures, the Word of God" (Origen's Greek formula θεῖος λόγος, "the divine word"), "and making frequent reference to them," and notes that Origen reckoned Wisdom "among the volumes of Solomon" (citing the Homily on Numbers).15 Reuss also writes that for Origen the church's "traditional custom… could not but be an authoritative rule in conflict with historical science": Origen knew the shorter Hebrew reckoning, and on Reuss's reading it was church usage that carried the wider Greek books into his citations. (A sentence sometimes quoted from Reuss — that a "divine word" formula "not only indicates the intrinsic value of the passage quoted, but ought certainly to remind us of its supernatural origin" — is his gloss on Theophilus of Antioch citing 1 Timothy, on a different page of the same book, not a remark about Origen and Wisdom.)
  • Why Eusebius is the source for the list. Origen's Commentary on Psalm 1 survives only in fragments; the catalogue reaches us because Eusebius quoted it. The words are Origen's, transmitted through Eusebius — the citation is to Eusebius's text (with the parallel collation at bible-researcher.com).
  • The New Testament counterpart. Origen's New Testament list is on his canon page — the "priestly trumpets" enumeration of the twenty-seven and the exclusion of the apocryphal gospels.
