Gregory the Great on the Old Testament Canon

Dates: c. 540–604 AD (Pope Gregory I — Roman aristocrat, monk, and from 590 bishop of Rome; one of the four traditional Latin Doctors of the Church, author of the Pastoral Rule and the Morals on the Book of Job, both widely read in the Middle Ages. His pontificate (590–604) saw liturgical reform and the mission to Anglo-Saxon England) · Wikipedia
Witness: In his letters and his Pastoral Rule Gregory quotes the Wisdom of Solomon as what "the Truth says through His Scripture," and Tobit and Sirach together under "what is written." In the Morals on the Book of Job, about to adduce a story from 1 Maccabees, he says the books he is quoting, though "not Canonical," are "brought out for the edifying of the Church."

Claims & evidence

Wisdom of Solomon cited as "His Scripture"

Writing to John, bishop of Constantinople, Gregory anchors a point about the tongue's power to destroy the soul in a sentence of the deuterocanonical Wisdom of Solomon (1:11), introduced as what "the Truth says through His Scripture":1

For the Truth says through His Scripture, The mouth that lies slays the soul.

The line is Wisdom 1:11 (“the mouth that belieth slayeth the soul”). Gregory introduces it without qualification, as spoken by “the Truth” in “His Scripture.”

Tobit and Sirach ranged together under "what is written"

In the Book of Pastoral Rule — his manual on the cure of souls — Gregory instructs the almsgiver whom to help and whom to withhold from, and gives four scriptural proofs under one formula, “what is written.” Two of the four are the deuterocanonical Sirach and Tobit, beside Luke and Matthew:2

Lest they should give something, however little, to those on whom they ought to bestow nothing at all, let them hear what is written, Give to the good man, and receive not a sinner: do well to him that is lowly, and give not to the ungodly. And again, Set out your bread and wine on the burial of the just, but eat and drink not thereof with sinners.

The first sentence is Sirach (Ecclesiasticus) 12:4–5; the second is Tobit 4:17 (Vulgate), which reads “bread and wine” where the Greek has only bread. The formula — “let them hear what is written” — introduces a chain whose other links are Luke 6:30 and Matthew 6:25.

Sirach and Tobit under "it is written" in his letters

In his letters too Gregory cites the disputed books under scriptural formulas. Writing to Theodore, physician at Constantinople, to thank him for alms sent for the redemption of captives and the poor, Gregory gathers Sirach and Tobit into a chain of proofs headed “it is written,” together with Luke and Paul:3

For this same deed of yours has a voice of its own, which calls to the secret ears of God, as it is written, Hide thy alms in the bosom of the poor, and it shall entreat for thee… you carefully attend to and observe what the Truth says; Give alms, and behold, all things are clean unto you; and this which is written, Even as water quencheth fire, so alms quench sin… Tobias admonishes his son, saying, If thou hast much, give abundantly; but if thou hast little, of that little impart willingly.

The three deuterocanonical links in the chain are Sirach 29:15 (“Hide thy alms…”), Sirach 3:33 (“as water quencheth fire, so alms quench sin”), and Tobit 4:9 (“If thou hast much…”), beside Luke 11:41 and 2 Corinthians 8:14, under “it is written” and “what the Truth says.”

Of 1 Maccabees: "though not Canonical … for the edifying of the Church"

In the Morals on the Book of Job, about to illustrate a point with the death of the Maccabean hero Eleazar, Gregory pauses to describe the source as among books “though not Canonical, yet brought out for the edifying of the Church”:4

With reference to which particular we are not acting irregularly, if from the books, though not Canonical, yet brought out for the edifying of the Church, we bring forward testimony. Thus Eleazar in the battle smote and brought down an elephant, but fell under the very beast that he killed.

The Latin is de qua re non inordinate agimus, si ex libris licet non canonicis, tamen ad aedificationem ecclesiae editis, testimonium proferamus, and the story that follows is 1 Maccabees 6:42–47. Gregory, bishop of Rome from 590, here calls the book “not Canonical” and quotes it “for the edifying of the Church.” How the sentence is read is set out under Reception and debate.

Reception and debate

  • Citation and ranking. Gregory cites Wisdom, Tobit, and Sirach with scriptural formulas in the letters and the Pastoral Rule, and calls 1 Maccabees “not Canonical” in the Morals. Origen, Cyril, Epiphanius, Rufinus, and Jerome on this timeline likewise both rank books by the Hebrew count and quote the disputed books.
  • Its place in the debate. Gregory wrote c. 600, after the African councils, the list ascribed to the Roman synod of 382, and Innocent I had listed the longer canon. James White (Reformed Baptist apologist, 2004) counts him among the Fathers who did not hold the books fully canonical, and William Webster places him on his medieval roll. Other readings of the early and medieval reception as a whole, as a divided or graded standing or as the church's use of the books as Scripture from the start, are set out with their holders on “How did the early church receive the deuterocanonical books?” and “Was the shorter, Hieronymian canon the mainstream of the medieval Latin West before Trent?”
