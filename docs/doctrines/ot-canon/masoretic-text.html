The Masoretic Text on the Old Testament Canon

Dates: c. 1000 AD (the Masoretic scribal work spans the 7th–10th centuries; the standard codices are the Aleppo Codex, c. 920, and the Leningrad Codex, 1008/1009) · Wikipedia
Witness: The Masoretic Text, the Hebrew (and Aramaic) text that Rabbinic Judaism fixed letter by letter and that most Protestant Old Testaments translate, carries the twenty-four books of the Hebrew Bible. Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch, and 1–2 Maccabees are not in it (see also the "Council of Jamnia" entry).

Claims & evidence

What the Masoretic Text is

The Masoretic Text is "the authoritative Hebrew and Aramaic text of the 24 books of the Hebrew Bible (Tanakh) in Rabbinic Judaism."1 It is the work of the Masoretes — scribal scholars working "between the 7th and 10th centuries" of the Common Era, chiefly at Tiberias and Jerusalem — who not only transmitted the consonantal Hebrew but added the vowel-points, accents, and marginal apparatus (the masora) that fixed its reading down to the letter. The most prominent was Aaron ben Moses ben Asher of the Ben Asher family, whose codex came to be reckoned the standard; the two principal surviving witnesses are the tenth-century Aleppo Codex and the early-eleventh-century Leningrad Codex, "the oldest-known complete copy," dated 1008/1009.2

Its contents: the twenty-four books of the Hebrew Bible

The twenty-four books the Masoretes counted are the books of the Hebrew Bible: the Law, the Prophets, and the Writings, closing (in the Jewish order) with Chronicles. The deuterocanonical books carried in the Greek Septuagint — Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch, and the Maccabees — are not among them.1 The count of twenty-four is older than the Masoretic codices: it appears in 4 Ezra and in the Babylonian Talmud (Bava Batra 14b). When and by whom the boundary of the Jewish canon was settled is discussed under the "Council of Jamnia" entry. The Masoretic codices give the twenty-four books a standardized written form, with vowel-points, accents and masora.

Its use in the synagogue and in Protestant translation

The Masoretic Text "defines both the Jewish scriptural canon and its precise letter-text," and it is the textual basis from which "most Protestant translations of the Old Testament" are made.1 The Reformation-era appeal to the Hebrew — to Jerome's Hebraica veritas — is recorded on the Luther page; the Protestant confessions (the Thirty-Nine Articles, the Westminster Confession) list as canonical the books of the Hebrew Bible, counted as thirty-nine. Catholic and Orthodox Old Testaments include books carried in the Greek Septuagint.

The Qumran and En-Gedi evidence on its transmission

The twentieth-century manuscript finds bear on how the Masoretic consonantal text was transmitted. "Most of the Qumran fragments can be classified as being closer to the Masoretic Text than to any other text group," and the Dead Sea Scrolls also show that "multiple versions of the Hebrew scriptures already existed" in antiquity.3 The En-Gedi scroll, a charred Leviticus scroll of roughly the third or fourth century AD that was read by computed-tomography imaging ("virtual unwrapping"), was found "completely identical with the consonantal framework of the Masoretic Text," some seven or eight centuries before the Masoretes' own codices.4

Reception and debate

  • Placement on the timeline. The Masoretic Text is the medieval form of the Hebrew Bible kept in the synagogue, and the textual basis of most Protestant Old Testaments; the Septuagint, the Greek Old Testament with the longer table of contents, has its own entry. The Masoretic Text stands here as the medieval Jewish witness on the timeline.
  • The Septuagint-versus-Hebrew question. The Masoretic Text both lacks these books and differs from the Greek in places; that larger dispute is set out on the Rabbinic Corruption of Scripture and Origin and Inspiration of the Septuagint pages. The Qumran and En-Gedi data above concern the transmission of the Hebrew text; which text preserves the older reading where the Masoretic Text and the Septuagint diverge is treated on those pages.
  • Transmission and canon. How accurately the Hebrew text was copied and which books belong to the Old Testament are distinct questions. The second — whether the Old Testament is measured by the Hebrew table of contents or by the Greek Bible read in the early churches — is the subject of the exchange between Jerome and Augustine, recorded on their pages.
