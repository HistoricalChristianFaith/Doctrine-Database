Aphrahat the Persian Sage on the Old Testament Canon

Dates: c. 337 AD (Aphrahat, also spelled Aphraates, “the Persian Sage” — a Syriac-speaking ascetic and bishop in the Sasanian Persian empire, east of the Roman frontier, c. 270–345; his twenty-three Demonstrations are the earliest extant substantial body of Syriac Christian theology, the first group, including Demonstration 5, Of Wars, dated by their author to AD 337) · Wikipedia
Summary: A Syriac writer in the Sasanian Persian empire, outside the Roman world, reads the Maccabean history as the fulfilment of Psalm and prophecy. In Demonstration 5 (Of Wars) he reads the persecution under Antiochus Epiphanes — the martyrdom of Eleazar and “the sons of the blessed Samuna, seven in number,” and the worm-eaten death of the tyrant — as the events foretold by Daniel and lamented in the Psalms, and he reads Antiochus’s death (narrated in 2 Maccabees 9) as “a judgment from heaven,” the judgment of Daniel 7, applying Isaiah’s “worm Jacob” to the worms that devoured him. He draws up no canon-list and prefaces the Maccabean material with no “it is written” formula; his use is historical and typological. He is a Syriac witness, writing outside the Greek and Latin worlds, some years before Cyril’s Hebrew-sized list (c. 350).

Claims & evidence

He reads the Maccabean martyrdoms as the fulfillment of Psalm and prophecy

And he slew righteous men and gave them to the birds of heaven and to the beasts of the earth. For in his days was fulfilled the word that David spoke: O God, the Gentiles have come into your inheritance, and have defiled Your holy temple. They have made Jerusalem desolate. They have given the dead bodies of Your servants as food to the birds of heaven, and the flesh of Your righteous ones to the beasts of the earth. They have poured out their blood like water round about Jerusalem, and there is none to bury them. For this was accomplished at that time, when the venerable and aged Eleazar was slain, and the sons of the blessed Samuna, seven in number, and when Judas (Maccabeus) and his brethren were struggling on behalf of their people, when they were dwelling in hiding-places.

— Demonstrations 5 (Of Wars), §20, in the Gwynn translation, Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, 2nd series, vol. 13, source.1 Aphrahat takes Psalm 79 (“O God, the Gentiles have come into your inheritance…”) and declares it “fulfilled” “when the venerable and aged Eleazar was slain, and the sons of the blessed Samuna, seven in number” — the aged scribe Eleazar of 2 Maccabees 6:18–31 and the mother and her seven sons of 2 Maccabees 7 (named in the Syriac tradition for the mother, “Shmuni”) — alongside “Judas (Maccabeus) and his brethren.”

He reads Antiochus’s worm-eaten death (2 Maccabees 9) as the judgment Daniel foretold

Again he said: The judgment was set and they took away his authority from him, to injure and destroy him until the end of the kingdom. For the judgment came upon Antiochus, a judgment from heaven; and he became sick with a grievous and evil sickness, and on account of the smell of him as he rotted, no man could approach him, for worms were crawling and falling from him and eating his flesh because he oppressed the worm Jacob. And his flesh rotted in his lifetime, because he caused the dead bodies of the sons of Jerusalem to rot and they were not buried.

— Demonstrations 5 (Of Wars), §20, source.2 The whole section is built on Daniel 7: Aphrahat identifies Antiochus with the “little horn” that “humbled three kings” and “exalted himself against the saints of the Most High” (Daniel 7:8, 21, 24–25), and he reads “The judgment was set and they took away his authority from him… until the end of the kingdom” (Daniel 7:26) as the sentence executed in the tyrant’s death. That death — sickness, an unbearable stench, worms breeding in and falling from the living body — is the end narrated in 2 Maccabees 9:5–12, here told as history within the argument from Daniel. Isaiah’s “worm Jacob” (Isaiah 41:14) is applied to it: the worms devour him “because he oppressed the worm Jacob.”

Reception and debate

  • Kind of use. Like Clement of Rome with “the blessed Judith,” Aphrahat adduces the Maccabean material without an explicit “it is written” or “the Scripture says,” and he enumerates no Old-Testament books. Unlike the explicit-formula citations of Clement of Alexandria or Cyprian, his use consists in reading the Maccabean martyrdoms and Antiochus’s death as events in which Psalm and prophecy are “fulfilled,” told together with Daniel and the Psalms.
  • A Syriac witness. Aphrahat wrote in Syriac, inside the Sasanian Persian empire, outside the Roman world of the Greek and Latin witnesses gathered here. Use of the Maccabean books appears on this timeline also in the Greek Dionysius and Methodius and the Latin Cyprian and Lactantius. The Syriac Peshitta Old Testament carries several deuterocanonical books ⚠ unverified.
  • Disputed readings. He gives no list; his use is cited in the disputed reading “How did the early church receive the deuterocanonical books…?”, as a fourth-century Syriac witness near in date to Cyril’s Hebrew-sized list.
