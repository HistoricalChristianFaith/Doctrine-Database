Clement of Alexandria on the Old Testament Canon

Dates: c. 200 AD (Titus Flavius Clemens, c. 150–215; head of the catechetical school of Alexandria, teacher of Origen; his Paedagogus and Stromateis are dated c. 198–203) · Wikipedia
Summary: Clement quotes four of the disputed deuterocanonical books — Sirach, Tobit, Wisdom, and Baruch — with the same explicit citation formula he uses for the protocanonical books: “the Scripture which says,” “Scripture,” “the Divine Scripture.” A fifth disputed book, Judith, he strings into a chain of scriptural maxims without the formula. Where Clement of Rome, the Didache, and Polycarp wove the disputed books in as authoritative counsel, and Irenaeus ascribed them to named prophets, Clement introduces four of them with the word “Scripture.” He gives no Old-Testament list. Whether citation as Scripture shows that a book was held canonical is itself disputed (see the interpretation page).

Claims & evidence

He cites Sirach as “the Scripture which says” (Sirach 21:6)

At this stage some rise up, saying that the Lord, by reason of the rod, and threatening, and fear, is not good; misapprehending, as appears, the Scripture which says, “And he that feareth the Lord will turn to his heart;” and most of all, oblivious of His love, in that for us He became man.

— The Instructor (Paedagogus) I.8.1 The quoted line is Sirach (Ecclesiasticus) 21:6, one of the deuterocanonical books carried in the Greek Septuagint but not the Hebrew canon. Clement introduces it with the same formula — “the Scripture which says” — that he uses for the protocanonical books.

He cites Tobit as “the Scripture which says” (Tobit 12:8)

…having heard the Scripture which says, “Fasting with prayer is a good thing.”

— Stromata VI.12.2 The saying is Tobit 12:8 (the angel Raphael's counsel to Tobit), again introduced with the formula “the Scripture which says.” Polycarp, about a century earlier, had woven in Tobit's maxim “alms delivers from death” without a formula (see his page).

He cites Wisdom as “Scripture” (Wisdom 2:12)

The Elegies of Solon, too, intensely deify the seventh day. And how? Is it not similar to Scripture when it says, “Let us remove the righteous man from us, because he is troublesome to us?” when Plato, all but predicting the economy of salvation, says in the second book of the Republic as follows…

— Stromata V.14.3 The words are Wisdom of Solomon 2:12, on the plot against the righteous man. Clement sets the line, introduced as “Scripture,” beside sayings of Solon and Plato.

He cites Baruch as “the Divine Scripture” (Baruch 3:16–19)

Excellently, therefore, the Divine Scripture, addressing boasters and lovers of their own selves, says, “Where are the rulers of the nations, and the lords of the wild beasts of the earth, who sport among the birds of heaven, who treasured up silver and gold, in whom men trusted, and there was no end of their substance, who fashioned silver and gold, and were full of care? There is no finding of their works. They have vanished, and gone down to Hades.” Such is the reward of display.

— The Instructor II.3.4 The passage is the deuterocanonical Baruch 3:16–19, here introduced as “the Divine Scripture.” The same book Athenagoras and Irenaeus cited as prophecy (see their pages); in the Greek and Latin transmission Baruch travelled with Jeremiah.

He adduces Judith alongside Galatians, Isaiah, and Proverbs (Judith 8:27)

And how can the law be not good, which trains, which is given as the instructor (παιδαγωγος) to Christ, that being corrected by fear, in the way of discipline, in order to the attainment of the perfection which is by Christ?… And he that is near the Lord is full of stripes. Plainly, he, that draws near to knowledge, has the benefit of perils, fears, troubles, afflictions, by reason of his desire for the truth… Whence “the fear of God” is divinely said to be the beginning of wisdom.

— Stromata II.7.5 The clause “he that is near the Lord is full of stripes” is Judith 8:27, woven into a chain with Galatians 3:24, Isaiah 5:21, and Proverbs 1:7. Here Clement uses no “Scripture” formula: the Judith line is woven in as a maxim, as disputed books are in Clement of Rome's letter (see his page).

Reception and debate

  • Citation formulas. Four of the five citations — Sirach, Tobit, Wisdom, Baruch — are introduced with “the Scripture which says,” “Scripture,” or “the Divine Scripture,” the wording Clement uses for the protocanonical books; the fifth, Judith, carries no formula. The earlier witnesses on this timeline — Clement of Rome, the Didache, and Polycarp — use the disputed books without a formula, and Irenaeus ascribes passages from them to “Daniel the prophet” and “Jeremiah the prophet” (see their pages).
  • Use, not a list. Clement gives no Old-Testament catalogue; these are citations made in the course of argument. His pupil Origen (c. 240) gives a twenty-two-book enumeration of the Hebrew books (see his page).
  • In the disputed readings. Clement's citations are among the evidence adduced in “How did the early church receive the deuterocanonical books: as Scripture, below the canon, or outside it?” and “When a Father quotes a book ‘as Scripture,’ does that show he held it canonical?”; the readings of them are set out there.
  • Placement on the timeline. At c. 200 AD Clement stands a generation after Irenaeus (c. 180) and a generation before Origen (c. 240), who counts the disputed books outside the Hebrew twenty-two while the Greek text remained in church use (see his page).
