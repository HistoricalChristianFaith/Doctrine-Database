The Codex Amiatinus on the Old Testament Canon

Dates: c. 700 AD (written c. 692–716 at the twin monastery of Wearmouth–Jarrow in Northumbria under Abbot Ceolfrith, and carried toward Rome in 716 as a gift for the pope) · Wikipedia
Witness: The Amiatinus is the oldest surviving complete single-volume Latin Vulgate Bible, a "pandect." Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach (Ecclesiasticus), and 1–2 Maccabees stand among its Old Testament books (the separate book of Baruch is absent; on the Epistle of Jeremiah the sources cited below differ). Its opening leaves reproduce diagrams of three different divisions of Scripture: Jerome's Hebrew-sized scheme, the larger Septuagint scheme (labelled for "Hilary"), and Augustine's.

Claims & evidence

The oldest complete Vulgate pandect and its Old Testament books

The Amiatinus is "the earliest surviving manuscript of the complete Christian Bible in the Latin Vulgate version" and was "produced around 700 AD… in the northeast of England, at the Benedictine Monkwearmouth–Jarrow Abbey in the Kingdom of Northumbria."1 A pandect is the entire Bible in one volume; Ceolfrith commissioned three, of which this is the one survivor. The deuterocanonical books are present and distributed through the codex's five-part Old Testament: by the Augustinian grouping the Amiatinus follows, Tobit, Judith, and 1–2 Maccabees sit among the "Narratives," while Wisdom and Sirach (Ecclesiasticus) stand with the books of David and Solomon.2 The same books appear in the lists attributed to the Council of Rome (382), in those of the African councils in Augustine's time, and in the letter of Innocent I (405) (see their pages).

Baruch and the Epistle of Jeremiah

The codex "is usually said to contain the whole Bible; but it should be noted that the Book of Baruch is missing, though the Epistle of Jeremias, usually incorporated with it, is here appended to the Book of Jeremias."3 The Wikipedia article on Baruch, citing the Benedictine scholar Pierre-Maurice Bogaert (2005), gives a different account of the Epistle: it states that neither the Amiatinus nor the Leon palimpsest, the two surviving early Latin pandects (both seventh century), "includes either the Book of Baruch or the Letter of Jeremiah," and names the ninth-century Codex Cavensis and the Theodulfian Bibles as the earliest Latin witnesses to the text of Baruch.4 In the East, Cyril of Jerusalem and Origen reckon Baruch and the Epistle with Jeremiah (see their pages). The other six deuterocanonical books — Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, and 1–2 Maccabees — stand in the codex as full books.

The front matter: three divisions of Scripture

Before Genesis, the Amiatinus sets out, among its opening leaves, "a list of the books contained in the codex, a picture of the Tabernacle…, a division of the Biblical books according to Jerome, another according to Hilary and Epiphanius, and a third according to Augustine."3 The three divisions stand side by side at the front of a Bible whose own order follows the Augustinian grouping.2 These diagrams correspond to chapters 12–14 of Book I of Cassiodorus's Institutiones — whose chapter-titles read "Divisio Scripturae divinae secundum sanctum Hieronymum," "… secundum sanctum Augustinum," and "… secundum LXX" — and Cassiodorus tied each to a book-count he found significant, reckoning Jerome's division at forty-nine books (with the mystical fifty in view) and the Septuagint division at seventy-one (with seventy-two in view).5 Jerome's division is the shorter, Hebrew-sized count (see the pages on Jerome and the Masoretic Text).

The scheme labelled “Hilary”

The middle diagram, captioned in the Amiatinus for Hilary (and Epiphanius), is in substance the Septuagint division — Cassiodorus's own term for it was "secundum LXX," and Paul Meyvaert, who traces the page back to him, prefers Cassiodorus's "Septuaginta" to the prevalent "Hilary" label.5 Of the three, the Septuagint division gives the largest count (seventy-one, in Cassiodorus's reckoning) and Jerome's the smallest (forty-nine); the third is Augustine's.

Descended from Cassiodorus's Codex Grandior

The Amiatinus's prefatory apparatus is so close to what is reported of Cassiodorus's sixth-century pandect, the Codex Grandior — which the Wearmouth–Jarrow library possessed — that early scholars "supposed that the leaves were transferred from it bodily," though it is more likely the Grandior "served as the model."3 In Meyvaert's account, Cassiodorus had placed these three divisions of Scripture in the Grandior itself, the vehicle that carried them north to Northumbria, where the monks reworked the diagrams onto the Amiatinus's leaves.5 The three divisions thus reached the Amiatinus from Cassiodorus's sixth-century Italian pandect.

The dedication and its later alteration

Ceolfrith set out for Rome with the pandect in 716, intending it as a gift to St Peter's (he died en route, at Langres, on 29 September 716).1 Its dedication page once read, in part, "CORPUS AD EXIMII MERITO VENERABILE PETRI… CEOLFRIDUS, ANGLORUM EXTIMIS DE FINIBUS ABBAS" — the body of Scripture dedicated to Peter by Ceolfrith, abbot from the utmost bounds of the English. Centuries later, at the monastery of San Salvatore on Monte Amiata (whence "Amiatinus"), the names were erased and rewritten so that "PETRUS LANGOBARDORUM," Peter of the Lombards, appeared as donor and the abbey itself as recipient; F. J. A. Hort matched the surviving verses to the original preserved in the anonymous Life of Ceolfrith, restoring the Northumbrian origin.6

Reception and debate

  • A witness by contents. The Amiatinus gives no argument about the canon; its witness is its contents. Its Old Testament binds Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, and the Maccabees with the other books. The great Greek codices (Vaticanus, Sinaiticus, Alexandrinus) are witnesses of the same kind for the Greek church.
  • The three diagrams. The codex sets three divisions of Scripture side by side — Jerome's shorter count, the Septuagint's larger one, and Augustine's — while its own order follows the Augustinian grouping.
  • Baruch. The separate book of Baruch is absent. The Catholic Encyclopedia reports the Epistle of Jeremiah appended to Jeremiah; the Wikipedia article on Baruch, citing Bogaert, reports neither text in the codex and dates the earliest Latin witnesses to Baruch to the ninth century (see above).
  • The Cassiodorus thread. Because the prefatory divisions descend from Cassiodorus's Codex Grandior and his Institutiones, the Amiatinus also preserves a sixth-century Italian way of schematizing the canon, carried to eighth-century England.
