John of Damascus on the Old Testament Canon

Dates: c. 730 AD (the Old-Testament catalogue is in An Exposition of the Orthodox Faith, Book IV, ch. 17, "Concerning Scripture" — the third part of the Fount of Knowledge, composed in the 730s; John of Damascus c. 675/676–749) · Wikipedia
Witness: In the Exposition of the Orthodox Faith (IV.17) John gives the Old Testament as twenty-two books (twenty-seven when the five doubled books are separated), in four "pentateuchs" plus two, as Athanasius and Epiphanius had done. Tobit, Judith, and the Maccabees are absent; the Wisdom of Solomon and the Wisdom of Sirach he calls "virtuous and noble," but "they are not counted nor were they placed in the ark." Two chapters earlier (IV.15) the same work cites the Wisdom of Solomon as "the divine Scripture."

Claims & evidence

The canon: "two and twenty books… one for each letter of the Hebrew tongue"

In the chapter "Concerning Scripture" John fixes the count by the Hebrew alphabet, then runs the books out in the four-"pentateuch" arrangement Epiphanius had used — the Law, the historical "Hagiographa," the poetical books, and the Prophets, with Ezra and Esther left over:1

Observe, further, that there are two and twenty books of the Old Testament, one for each letter of the Hebrew tongue. For there are twenty-two letters of which five are double, and so they come to be twenty-seven… And thus the number of the books in this way is twenty-two… Five of them are of the Law, viz. Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, Deuteronomy. This which is the code of the Law, constitutes the first Pentateuch. Then comes another Pentateuch, the so-called Grapheia, or as they are called by some, the Hagiographa, which are the following: Jesus the Son of Nave, Judges along with Ruth, first and second Kings, which are one book, third and fourth Kings, which are one book, and the two books of the Paraleipomena which are one book. This is the second Pentateuch. The third Pentateuch is the books in verse, viz. Job, Psalms, Proverbs of Solomon, Ecclesiastes of Solomon and the Song of Songs of Solomon. The fourth Pentateuch is the Prophetical books, viz the twelve prophets constituting one book, Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, Daniel. Then come the two books of Esdra made into one, and Esther.

No Tobit, no Judith, no Maccabees stand among the twenty-two; the two Wisdom books are treated separately (below). These twenty-two are the protocanonical Hebrew books, with Ruth folded into Judges, the paired books of Kings and Chronicles each counted as one, and Ezra ("the two books of Esdra") counted as one — the same Hebrew-letter reckoning given by Athanasius, Cyril, the pseudo-Athanasian Synopsis, and Epiphanius before him. John's arrangement matches Epiphanius's in the "four pentateuchs plus two" frame, the twenty-two/twenty-seven double count, and the five "double" letters.

The Wisdom books: "virtuous and noble, but are not counted nor were they placed in the ark"

As Epiphanius had, John names the two Wisdom books, commends them, and sets them outside the count:1

There are also the Panaretus, that is the Wisdom of Solomon, and the Wisdom of Jesus, which was published in Hebrew by the father of Sirach, and afterwards translated into Greek by his grandson, Jesus, the Son of Sirach. These are virtuous and noble, but are not counted nor were they placed in the ark.

The image of the ark appears earlier in Epiphanius, who said the Wisdom books "were not kept in the chest, that is, in the ark of the covenant"; Athanasius had described the books outside his canon as "appointed by the Fathers to be read". "Panaretus" (Panaretos, "all-virtuous") is the old Greek title for the Wisdom of Solomon, and John's note on the book's history — a Hebrew original by the elder Sirach, rendered into Greek by his grandson — agrees with the prologue Ben Sira's grandson attached to the Greek translation.

Wisdom cited as "the divine Scripture": the souls of the just (IV.15)

Two chapters before the canon-catalogue, defending the honour paid to the saints and their relics — IV.15, "Concerning the honour due to the Saints and their remains" — John argues that the holy dead are alive in God and so may rightly be venerated, and he grounds the point on a deuterocanonical text introduced as "the divine Scripture":2

The divine Scripture likewise says that the souls of the just are in God's hand and death cannot lay hold of them.

The words are the Wisdom of Solomon 3:1 — "But the souls of the righteous are in the hand of God, and there shall no torment touch them" — and John adduces them in a doctrinal argument on the honour of the saints, under the label "the divine Scripture." The same work's canon-list, two chapters later, sets Wisdom outside the count and "not… placed in the ark." A Hebrew-sized list and the citation of a disputed book as Scripture stand in one work, as they do in Origen and Eusebius (see their pages).

Reception and debate

  • Not counted, but commended. John's twenty-two omit Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, and the Maccabees; he names the two Wisdom books "virtuous and noble" while setting them outside the count, in the arrangement of Athanasius and Epiphanius.
  • Parallels with Epiphanius. The four-"pentateuch"-plus-two frame, the twenty-two-by-the-Hebrew-letters count with twenty-seven when the five doubled books are split, and the "ark" image for the Wisdom books all appear earlier in Epiphanius's On Weights and Measures.
  • Date relative to the Masoretes. John writes c. 730, before the Tiberian Masoretes fixed the pointed Hebrew text (9th–10th c.). He counts the books by the Hebrew letters, writing in a Greek-speaking church that read the Old Testament in the Septuagint.
  • List and citation. IV.17 sets the Wisdom of Solomon outside the twenty-two; IV.15 quotes it as "the divine Scripture." John does not comment on the relation between the two passages. How such lists and citations together bear on the canon is set out on “How did the early church receive the deuterocanonical books?”
  • Jeremiah without Baruch. Unlike Cyril and Epiphanius, John's chapter as printed does not spell out the usual folding of Baruch and the Epistle of Jeremiah into the book of Jeremiah; he lists "Jeremiah" bare among the four prophets. The deuterocanonical books he addresses by name are the two Wisdom books, which he sets outside the count.
