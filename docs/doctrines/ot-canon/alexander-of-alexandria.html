Alexander of Alexandria on the Old Testament Canon

Dates: c. 321 AD (Alexander of Alexandria, also Alexander I; bishop — Pope and Patriarch — of Alexandria from 313 until his death on 17 April 326/328; the bishop who condemned Arius and presided over the Alexandrian synod that deposed him (c. 318–321), and whose deacon and chosen successor was Athanasius. The encyclical letter to Alexander of Byzantium in which the citations below occur is dated c. 319–324; distinct from Clement and Athanasius of Alexandria) · Wikipedia
Summary: The bishop of Alexandria at the outbreak of the Arian controversy cites the deuterocanonical Sirach (Ecclesiasticus) in his christological argument. Arguing that the eternal generation of the Word lies beyond inquiry, he sets in one series — introduced by “not listening to this saying” and “that passage” — Sirach 3:21, the Apostle Paul (1 Corinthians 2:9), Genesis 15:5, Sirach 1:2, and Isaiah 53:8. He does not introduce the Sirach lines with the formula “Scripture says” (as Methodius does for Wisdom); he cites them among texts from Paul, Genesis, and the prophet, without a label distinguishing them. He compiles no canon-list; the page records his early-fourth-century use of Sirach, between Eusebius and the Council of Nicaea.

Claims & evidence

Sirach 3:21 and 1:2 cited with Paul, Genesis, and Isaiah

In his encyclical against Arius — the letter to Alexander, bishop of Byzantium (Constantinople) — Alexander argues that the manner of the Son’s eternal generation lies beyond human inquiry. Against such speculation he cites a series of texts:

Wherefore I do not think that he is to be reckoned among the pious who presumes to inquire into anything beyond these things, not listening to this saying: Seek not out the things that are too hard for you, neither search the things that are above your strength. For if the knowledge of many other things that are incomparably inferior to this, are hidden from human comprehension, such as in the apostle Paul, Eye has not seen, nor ear heard, neither have entered into the heart of man, the things which God has prepared for them that love Him. As also God said to Abraham, that he could not number the stars; and that passage, Who can number the sand of the sea, and the drops of rain… How shall any one be able to investigate too curiously the subsistence of the divine Word, unless he be smitten with frenzy? Concerning which the Spirit of prophecy says, Who shall declare his generation?

— Epistles on the Arian Heresy and the Deposition of Arius, Epistle I (to Alexander of Constantinople), §5, in the Ante-Nicene Fathers translation, source.1 Two of the chained texts are deuterocanonical. “Seek not out the things that are too hard for you, neither search the things that are above your strength” is Sirach (Ecclesiasticus) 3:21, introduced as “this saying” which one who presumes to inquire is “not listening to.” “Who can number the sand of the sea, and the drops of rain” is Sirach 1:2, set as “that passage” in series with Paul’s “Eye has not seen” (1 Corinthians 2:9) and God’s word to Abraham that he could not number the stars (Genesis 15:5) as things hidden from human comprehension, and followed by the prophet’s “Who shall declare his generation?” (Isaiah 53:8). The Sirach lines carry no label distinguishing them from the other texts in the series.

Reception and debate

  • Citation without a Scripture formula. Alexander does not preface the Sirach quotations with “the Scripture says” or “it is written” — the formulas that Cyprian, Dionysius, and Methodius attach to disputed books. His use of Sirach appears in its placement beside Paul, Genesis, and Isaiah within one doctrinal argument, and in the phrase he uses of Sirach 3:21 (“this saying” which the over-curious are “not listening to”). The letter gives no list and does not say how he would rank the book.
  • An Alexandrian witness before Nicaea. Alexander was bishop at the outbreak of the Arian controversy and the predecessor of his deacon Athanasius, whose Festal Letter 39 (367) places Sirach among the books “not… included in the Canon, but appointed by the Fathers to be read.” His contemporary Eusebius transmits Hebrew-sized lists and also cites disputed books as Scripture (see his page).
  • Use, not a canon-list. Like Irenaeus, Clement of Alexandria, Hippolytus, Tertullian, Cyprian, Dionysius, Methodius, and Lactantius, Alexander nowhere enumerates the Old-Testament books. He bears on the canon question as a witness to early-fourth-century Alexandrian practice, cited in the disputed reading “How did the early church receive the deuterocanonical books…?”; his use is by placement in a series rather than by an explicit formula.
