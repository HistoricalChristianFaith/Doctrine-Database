Rabbi Akiva ben Joseph on the Old Testament Canon

Dates: c. 50–135 AD (also spelled Akiba) · Wikipedia
Summary: A Tanna of the Yavneh generation, named in the Mishnah for a ruling against the “external books.” The Mishnah records his ruling that one who reads the sefarim ha-chitzonim (the books outside the Hebrew canon) has no share in the world to come; he also defended the disputed Hebrew books (Song of Songs, Esther) as holy Scripture. He is cited as a Jewish witness to a rabbinic canon without the Greek deuterocanonical books. Two points bear on that use: the referent of his “external books” (the Apocrypha broadly, or sectarian/Christian writings specifically) is discussed already within the Talmud, and the Jewish Encyclopedia (1906) reads his ban as falling on reading those books “as if they were canonical,” not on private reading.

Claims & evidence

The named ruling against the “external books”

Rabbi Akiva says: Also one who reads external literature… has no share in the World-to-Come.

— Mishnah Sanhedrin 10:1 (Sefaria translation), source; the same ruling is carried in the Babylonian Talmud at b. Sanhedrin 90a. The Hebrew phrase rendered “external literature” is ha-sefarim ha-chitzonim — the “outside books,” i.e. those beyond the received Hebrew Scriptures; the word chitzonim (“outer”) itself is connected by the Jewish Encyclopedia with the Greek apokrypha. In the Mishnah the ruling follows the list of those who have no share in the World-to-Come, among them one who says the Torah is not from Heaven.1

The referent of the “external books” in the Talmud

The Babylonian Talmud, expounding Akiva’s dictum, glosses the “external books” as sifrei minim — the books of the heretics — and Rav Yosef then adds that even reading Ben Sira is problematic on account of certain of its verses.

The Sages taught… this [refers to the] books of the heretics [minim].

— Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 100b (Sefaria), source. The Jewish Encyclopedia (“Apocrypha,” 1906) takes minim in this period to mean Jewish-Christians or Gnostics; on this gloss the ruling is read as aimed at Christian and sectarian writings. Rav Yosef’s discussion of Ben Sira in the same passage bears on a broader referent, and the Jewish Encyclopedia (“Akiba ben Joseph,” 1906) reads the dictum as a protest against “the canonicity of certain of the Apocrypha, Ecclesiasticus, for instance.”2

Tosefta Yadayim 2:13: the “margins,” the heretics’ scrolls and Ben Sira “do not defile the hands”

The margins [gilyonim] and the scrolls of the heretics do not defile the hands. The scrolls of Ben Sira and all scrolls that are written from that point forward — they do not defile the hands.

— Tosefta Yadayim 2:13 (Sefaria translation), source. “Defiles the hands” is the rabbinic idiom for canonical holiness — to say a book does not defile the hands is to deny it Scriptural status. This ruling is collective and anonymous (not Akiva by name), a tannaitic text set beside his ruling here. The word gilyonim literally means the blank “margins” of a scroll; it is also read as a pun on euangelion (“gospel”), hence the translation “the Gospels” ⚠ unverified. The ruling names Ben Sira (early second century BC) and “all scrolls that are written from that point forward.”3

The Song of Songs, and the question of private reading

…for all the writings are holy but the Song of Songs is the holy of holies.

— Mishnah Yadayim 3:5 (Sefaria translation), source. Akiva defended the disputed Hebrew books: against those who doubted whether the Song of Songs (and Ecclesiastes, and elsewhere Esther) “defile the hands,” he insisted the Song was the “holy of holies.” On the outside books, the Jewish Encyclopedia observes that Akiva “has… no objection to the private reading of the Apocrypha, as is evident from the fact that he himself makes frequent use of Ecclesiasticus [Ben Sira],” his objection being to reading non-canonical books “as if they were canonical.”4

Reception and debate

  • Placement on the timeline. Akiva is placed among the Jewish witnesses on the canon timeline, near the “Council of Jamnia” (the Yavneh gathering; whether it fixed the Hebrew canon by decree is discussed on its interpretation page), Josephus’s twenty-two-book count (c. AD 93), and the later Babylonian Talmud. The Jewish Encyclopedia (1906) calls Akiva “the one who definitely fixed the canon of the Old Testament books.”
  • The anti-Christian-motive question. Heinrich Graetz and the Jewish Encyclopedia (1906) held that Akiva’s hostility to the outside books and to the Septuagint was partly strategic — a desire “to disarm Christians… who drew their proofs from the Apocrypha” and to free diaspora Jews “from the domination of the Septuagint” that had become the church’s Bible; the same article records that “under Akiba’s guidance” his pupil Aquila “gave the Greek-speaking Jews a rabbinical Bible,” a Greek version whose readings at passages cited by Christians (e.g. Isaiah 7:14) are discussed on the Aquila-revision page. Jack P. Lewis, Albert C. Sundberg Jr. and Sid Z. Leiman read the rabbis instead as applying their own criteria to a Hebrew collection that had not held the Greek books. The question is set out on its interpretation page.
  • The scope of the ruling. Two points bear on how Akiva is cited in the canon debate: the referent of his “external books” is discussed within the Talmud itself (Apocrypha broadly vs. sectarian/Christian books), and the Jewish Encyclopedia reads his ban as falling on authoritative use, not on private reading, noting that “he himself makes frequent use of Ecclesiasticus.”
