4 Ezra (2 Esdras 14:44–48) on the Old Testament Canon

Dates: c. AD 100 (the Jewish apocalypse forming chapters 3–14 of the composite book called 2 Esdras; written after the fall of Jerusalem in AD 70, its "eagle vision" read as the Flavian dynasty) · Wikipedia
Witness: 4 Ezra gives a first-century Jewish count beside Josephus: in its closing vision God commands Ezra to publish twenty-four books openly, the standard rabbinic reckoning of the Hebrew Bible, from which the Greek-only books are absent, while keeping "seventy" further books for the wise. The "seventy" are prized as "the spring of understanding," and 4 Ezra is itself one of the books outside the Hebrew twenty-four.

Claims & evidence

2 Esdras 14:44–48: a public canon of twenty-four, seventy kept back

The book closes with a scene of Scripture-making. The Law had been burned; at God's command in the burning bush, Ezra takes five scribes and, inspired by "a full cup… full as it were with water, but the colour of it was like fire," dictates for forty days. The tally and its division follow:

"So in forty days were written ninety-four books. And it came to pass when the forty days were fulfilled, that the Most High spake unto me saying: The twenty-four books that thou hast written publish, that the worthy and unworthy may read (therein): but the seventy last thou shalt keep, to deliver them to the wise among thy people. For in them is the spring of understanding, the fountain of wisdom, and the stream of knowledge. And I did so."

— 4 Ezra 14:44–48, in G. H. Box's translation (The Ezra-Apocalypse, 1912).1 The passage gives two numbers. The twenty-four to be "read openly" — published for "the worthy and unworthy," i.e. the whole people — is the ordinary rabbinic count of the Hebrew Bible: "In the Talmud and Midrash," Box notes, "the O.T. is regularly termed 'the twenty-four holy Scriptures'… The number 24 is the ordinary reckoning of the O.T. books (5 + 8 + 11)." It is the same collection Josephus, a few years earlier, tallies as twenty-two — a total Box explains as obtained by combining Ruth with Judges and Lamentations with Jeremiah, in accordance with the twenty-two letters of the Hebrew alphabet.2 None of the Greek-only books (Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch, 1–2 Maccabees) falls inside the published twenty-four. The seventy "last" are the esoteric writings — apocalypses of the kind 4 Ezra itself is — reserved for "the wise." Box identifies the twenty-four as "the books of the O.T., which were read openly in the synagogue," and the seventy as "the apocalypses, which were secret books." The passage thus distinguishes a public, numbered collection from a body of books kept back, in a Jewish text of c. AD 100, alongside the twenty-two-book count of Josephus.

A textual note: "ninety-four" vs. the Latin's larger total

The versions disagree on the arithmetic. The Syriac, Ethiopic, Arabic, and Armenian read "ninety-four" (94 = 24 published + 70 kept back) and name "twenty-four"; the Latin manuscripts on which the King James Apocrypha depends, which Box treats as corrupt here, give a larger total ("two hundred and four books" in the KJV, other Latin witnesses 904/970/973) and, at v. 45, merely "the first that thou hast written" with no number.3 Box judges the "94" reading "undoubtedly right," and reports Hilgenfeld's view that "twenty-four" was omitted in the versions lacking it "because the Christians reckoned 22, not 24, Books of the O.T."4

Reception and debate

  • Placement on the timeline. The deuterocanon question concerns two forms of the Old Testament — the broader Greek Septuagint collection in which the early churches received the Scriptures and the narrower Hebrew collection kept in the synagogue. 4 Ezra is a Jewish text of c. AD 100 which, like Josephus, gives a number for the publicly read books. It is placed here as a witness to the synagogue's public collection; it predates the later Christian controversy and does not address it.
  • Scope of the passage. The passage names no individual book, inside or outside the twenty-four, and does not mention Tobit, Judith, or the Maccabees. Its "seventy" books kept back are held in high honour ("the spring of understanding… the fountain of wisdom"); the distinction it draws is between books to be published and books reserved for "the wise."
  • The book's own standing. 4 Ezra is itself one of the books outside the Hebrew twenty-four — it travels in the Latin and Slavonic Bibles as part of 2 Esdras (2 Esdras chs. 3–14), is not in the Hebrew Bible, and is not named in the 1546 list of the Council of Trent. Box classes it among the secret apocalypses that the passage itself sets apart from the twenty-four.
  • Disputed readings. The numbered-collection datum is cited, beside Josephus, in the disputed reading on Josephus's twenty-two books; the 22/24 reckoning also comes up on the Babylonian Talmud and Masoretic Text pages.
