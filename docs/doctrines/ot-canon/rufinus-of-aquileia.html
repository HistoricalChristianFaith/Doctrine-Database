Rufinus of Aquileia on the Old Testament Canon

Dates: c. 404 AD (the Commentary on the Apostles' Creed, written at Aquileia on his return from the East; Rufinus c. 345–411) · Wikipedia
Witness: Rufinus sorts the books into three named ranks: the Canonical Scriptures (a Hebrew-sized Old Testament); the Ecclesiastical books (Wisdom, Sirach, Tobit, Judith, and the Maccabees), "read in the Churches, but not appealed to for the confirmation of doctrine"; and the Apocrypha, which "they would not have read in the Churches" at all. Article VI of the Thirty-Nine Articles (1571) uses a similar distinction.

Claims & evidence

The canonical Old Testament list

Rufinus introduces his catalogue as a list "of the New and of the Old Testament, which, according to the tradition of our forefathers, are believed to have been inspired by the Holy Ghost, and have been handed down to the Churches of Christ."1 The Old Testament he then lists is a Hebrew-sized canon:

Of the Old Testament, therefore, first of all there have been handed down five books of Moses, Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, Deuteronomy; Then Jesus Nave, (Joshua the son of Nun), The Book of Judges together with Ruth; then four books of Kings (Reigns), which the Hebrews reckon two; the Book of Omissions, which is entitled the Book of Days (Chronicles), and two books of Ezra (Ezra and Nehemiah), which the Hebrews reckon one, and Esther; of the Prophets, Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, and Daniel; moreover of the twelve (minor) Prophets, one book; Job also and the Psalms of David, each one book. Solomon gave three books to the Churches, Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, Canticles. These comprise the books of the Old Testament.

The canonical list does not include Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, or the Maccabees.1 Origen, Cyril, Athanasius, and Jerome also give Hebrew-sized lists. Where the Eastern lists of Cyril and Athanasius include Baruch and the Epistle of Jeremiah with Jeremiah, Rufinus names "Jeremiah" alone, as Jerome also leaves Baruch out of the canon.

The "Ecclesiastical" books: Wisdom, Sirach, Tobit, Judith, Maccabees

Rufinus names a second rank for the disputed books, distinct from the Apocrypha:2

But it should be known that there are also other books which our fathers call not "Canonical" but "Ecclesiastical:" that is to say, Wisdom, called the Wisdom of Solomon, and another Wisdom, called the Wisdom of the Son of Syrach, which last-mentioned the Latins called by the general title Ecclesiasticus, designating not the author of the book, but the character of the writing. To the same class belong the Book of Tobit, and the Book of Judith, and the Books of the Maccabees. In the New Testament the little book which is called the Book of the Pastor of Hermas, [and that] which is called The Two Ways, or the Judgment of Peter; all of which they would have read in the Churches, but not appealed to for the confirmation of doctrine. The other writings they have named "Apocrypha." These they would not have read in the Churches.

Rufinus thus names three ranks: Canonical (believed "to have been inspired by the Holy Ghost"), Ecclesiastical ("read in the Churches, but not appealed to for the confirmation of doctrine"), and Apocrypha (not "read in the Churches"). The deuterocanonical books are in the second rank. His formula is close to Jerome's "read… for the strengthening of the people, but not for confirming the authority of ecclesiastical dogmas"; Athanasius had described a similar group as "appointed by the Fathers to be read."

The New Testament canon in the same scheme

Rufinus's list also gives the New Testament — the four Gospels, Acts, fourteen Pauline Epistles, the Catholic Epistles, and Revelation, "the books which the Fathers have comprised within the Canon, and from which they would have us deduce the proofs of our faith"3 — and then files New-Testament books in the same Ecclesiastical tier (the Shepherd of Hermas and the Two Ways / Judgment of Peter). The same three ranks apply to both Testaments. Among the Eastern lists on this timeline, those of Origen, Cyril, Athanasius, and Epiphanius also distinguish the canonical books from others (see their pages).

Sirach cited as “Scriptura” in the Blessings of the Patriarchs

A few years before the Creed commentary, expounding Jacob's blessings in his Commentary on the Blessings of the Patriarchs (c. 398–400), he comes to the blessing of Gad and argues that no one reaches perfection without first being proved in temptation, quoting Sirach with the formula "Scriptura dicit":4

Nec ullo tempore omnino quis perveniet ad perfectionem, nisi prius in tentationibus suis documenta praestiterit: ita enim Scriptura dicit, Qui non est tentatus, non est probabilis.

— "No one will ever attain perfection at all, unless he has first given proof of himself in his temptations; for thus the Scripture says, 'He that has not been tempted is not approved'" (Sirach 34, the chapter Rufinus cites as "Ecclesiasticus 34"). A book he lists among the "Ecclesiastical" books, "not appealed to for the confirmation of doctrine," he here introduces as "Scriptura" on a point of moral teaching. Cyril, Athanasius, and Epiphanius likewise both list a Hebrew-sized canon and quote books outside it (see their pages). How such citations bear on a writer's canon is discussed on the interpretation pages "When a Father quotes a book 'as Scripture,' does that show he held it canonical?" and "How did the early church receive the deuterocanonical books?"

The Apology against Jerome on the Greek additions to Daniel

In his Apology against Jerome (Book II, c. 401), written during their quarrel, Rufinus defends the old Greek Old Testament against Jerome's new Hebrew-based version. He treats the Septuagint, including the Greek-Daniel additions (Susanna and the Song, or Hymn, of the Three Children), as the deposit handed down by the Apostles, and charges Jerome with doing "havoc" to it. He points to the Jewish-born Christians of the early churches, "especially" at Jerusalem, who knew both languages:5

There has been from the first in the churches of God, and especially in that of Jerusalem, a plentiful supply of men who being born Jews have become Christians; and their perfect acquaintance with both languages and their sufficient knowledge of the law is shewn by their administration of the pontifical office. In all this abundance of learned men, has there been one who has dared to make havoc of the divine record handed down to the Churches by the Apostles and the deposit of the Holy Spirit? … the whole of the history of Susanna, which gave a lesson of chastity to the churches of God, has by him been cut out, thrown aside and dismissed. The hymn of the three children, which is regularly sung on festivals in the Church of God, he has wholly erased from the place where it stood.

In the Creed commentary Rufinus names "Daniel" without mention of the additions; here he counts the Greek Daniel, Susanna and the Hymn of the Three Children included, as "the divine record handed down to the Churches by the Apostles." The same section of the Apology repeats the account of the seventy translators working in separate cells, and says that Peter delivered the Septuagint books to the Roman church.5 Whether the early church's reception of the deuterocanon arose from unfamiliarity with Hebrew is discussed on the interpretation page "Did the early church's reception of the deuterocanon arise from unfamiliarity with Hebrew and the Hebrew canon?"

Reception and debate

  • Three ranks. Rufinus's canonical Old Testament is the Hebrew list; Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, and the Maccabees he places in the "Ecclesiastical" rank, read in church though not cited to prove doctrine. Jerome and Athanasius also describe a rank of books read but not canonical (see their pages).
  • The name "Ecclesiastical." Rufinus ascribes the name "Ecclesiastical" for the read-but-not-doctrinal books to "our fathers." Article VI of the Thirty-Nine Articles (1571) quotes Jerome for reading such books "for example of life and instruction of manners" but not to "establish any doctrine"; the Westminster Confession (1647) gives them no authority beyond "other human writings." What the middle category means — a rank below the canon (John Meade) or a catechetical subset within it (Gary Michuta) — is discussed on the interpretation page "what did the 'ecclesiastical' category mean?"
  • Rufinus and Jerome. Rufinus and Jerome were antagonists in the Origenist controversy of these years; their canon lists are close: a Hebrew-sized canon, the deuterocanonical books read but not used for doctrine, and Baruch given no separate canonical place. Augustine and the African councils of the same years list the disputed books among the canonical Scriptures (see their pages).
  • Translation provenance. The English is the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers rendering by William Henry Fremantle (NPNF 2.3); the sections are §37 (the canonical list) and §38 (the Ecclesiastical books and the Apocrypha).
