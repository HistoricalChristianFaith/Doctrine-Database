Lactantius on the Old Testament Canon

Dates: c. 310 AD (Lucius Caecilius Firmianus Lactantius, c. 250 – c. 325; a Latin rhetorician of North African origin, pupil of Arnobius, summoned by Diocletian to teach rhetoric at Nicomedia, later tutor to Constantine’s son Crispus, called the “Christian Cicero” by the early humanists; his Divine Institutes were composed c. 303–311 during and after the Great Persecution) · Wikipedia
Summary: In the Divine Institutes, an early systematic presentation of Christian thought in Latin, Lactantius cites a deuterocanonical text as the testimony of “Solomon” to the divine Word. Arguing that the Word by whom the world was made became flesh, he sets Sirach 24:3–4 — Wisdom’s self-declaration, “I came forth out of the mouth of the Most High before all creatures” — immediately beside John 1:1. He draws up no list of the Old Testament books. His citation is a Western datapoint of the early fourth century, between Dionysius and Nicaea, in the record of how the disputed books were used.

Claims & evidence

Sirach 24:3–4, cited as “Solomon,” on the Word of God, beside John 1

Solomon also shows that it is the Word of God, and no other, by whose hands these works of the world were made. “I,” He says, “came forth out of the mouth of the Most High before all creatures: I caused the light that fails not to arise in the heavens, and covered the whole earth with a cloud.” … John also thus taught: “In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God.”

— The Divine Institutes, Book IV (“Of True Wisdom and Religion”), ch. 8, in the Ante-Nicene Fathers translation, source.1 The quoted words — “I came forth out of the mouth of the Most High… and covered the whole earth with a cloud” — are Sirach (Ecclesiasticus) 24:3–4 (the self-speech of personified Wisdom), not the book of Proverbs: the distinctive phrase “out of the mouth of the Most High” and the covering of the earth “as a cloud” belong to Sirach 24 alone. Arguing that the Word by whom the world was made became incarnate, Lactantius adduces the deuterocanonical text as testimony that “it is the Word of God, and no other,” by whom the world was made, and follows it with John 1:1: what “Solomon also shows,” “John also thus taught.” The two texts are set side by side as testimonies to the Word.

Reception and debate

  • The “Solomon” ascription. Lactantius credits the words to “Solomon,” but they are Sirach, written by Jesus ben Sira (c. 180 BC), not Solomon. Sirach (Ecclesiasticus) circulated among the “books of Solomon” — grouped with Proverbs, Wisdom, and Ecclesiastes — and was cited under the Solomonic name. Lactantius sets the text beside John’s Gospel as Solomonic testimony to the Word.
  • A christological use. Where the earlier citations of the book by Clement of Rome and Polycarp on the timeline are moral maxims, Lactantius adduces Sirach on the eternity and creative agency of the Word, and pairs it with John 1, as Hippolytus’s reads Wisdom as “the prophecy of Solomon which speaketh of Christ.”
  • Use, not a canon-list. Like Irenaeus, Clement of Alexandria, Hippolytus, Tertullian, Cyprian, and Dionysius, Lactantius nowhere enumerates the Old-Testament books. His citation is a datum of early-fourth-century Western usage, before Nicaea, and is among the witnesses discussed on the interpretation page “How did the early church receive the deuterocanonical books?”
