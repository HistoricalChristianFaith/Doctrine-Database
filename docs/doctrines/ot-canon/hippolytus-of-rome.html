Hippolytus of Rome on the Old Testament Canon

Dates: c. 205 AD (Hippolytus of Rome, c. 170–235; presbyter and theologian of the early-third-century Roman church, later reckoned a martyr; his Against Noetus and Expository Treatise Against the Jews belong to the first decades of the third century) · Wikipedia
Summary: Hippolytus cites two of the disputed books as prophecy. He introduces a long passage of the Wisdom of Solomon as “the prophecy of Solomon, which speaketh of Christ,” reading Wisdom 2 as a foretelling of the plot against the righteous one; and he expounds Baruch 3:35–37 (“This is our God… afterward did He show Himself upon earth”), adduced by his Noetian opponents under “Thus it is written,” as spoken of Christ’s appearing on earth. Neither is a canon-list; both are citations in argument by a Roman teacher writing a few years after Clement of Alexandria.

Claims & evidence

He calls the Wisdom of Solomon “the prophecy of Solomon, which speaketh of Christ” (Wisdom 2:1, 12–20)

I produce now the prophecy of Solomon, which speaketh of Christ, and announces clearly and perspicuously things concerning the Jews; and those which not only are befalling them at the present time, but those, too, which shall befall them in the future age, on account of the contumacy and audacity which they exhibited toward the Prince of Life; for the prophet says, “The ungodly said, reasoning with themselves, but not aright,” that is, about Christ, “Let us lie in wait for the righteous, because he is not for our turn, and he is clean contrary to our doings and words, and upbraideth us with our offending the law, and professeth to have knowledge of God; and he calleth himself the Child of God.”

— The Expository Treatise Against the Jews, §9.1 The quoted lines are the deuterocanonical Wisdom of Solomon 2:1 and 2:12–20 — the passage on the plot against “the righteous” who “calleth himself the Child of God.” Hippolytus frames the whole as “the prophecy of Solomon, which speaketh of Christ,” calls its author “the prophet,” and reads it of the Passion, in a treatise addressed against a Jewish interlocutor.

Baruch 3:35–37 in Against Noetus (“Thus it is written”)

But they make use also of other testimonies, and say, Thus it is written: “This is our God, and there shall none other be accounted of in comparison of Him. He hath found out all the way of knowledge, and hath given it unto Jacob His servant (son), and to Israel His beloved. Afterward did He show Himself upon earth, and conversed with men.”

— Against the Heresy of One Noetus, §2.2 The cited words are the deuterocanonical Baruch 3:35–37 (carried in the Greek and Latin tradition as an appendix to Jeremiah). The “Thus it is written” formula is here on the lips of the Noetian opponents who adduce the passage; Hippolytus answers it in §5, expounding it of Christ:

But what is meant, says he, in the other passage: “This is God, and there shall none other be accounted of in comparison of Him?” That said he rightly. For in comparison of the Father who shall be accounted of? But he says: “This is our God; there shall none other be accounted of in comparison of Him. He hath found out all the way of knowledge, and hath given it unto Jacob His servant, and to Israel His beloved.” He saith well… Having received, then, all knowledge from the Father, the perfect Israel, the true Jacob, afterward did show Himself upon earth, and conversed with men.

— Against the Heresy of One Noetus, §5.2 His reply (“That said he rightly… He saith well”) turns on whom the passage speaks of; he does not question its source, and reads its appearing “upon earth” of the incarnation.

Reception and debate

  • Prophecy, not a canon-list. Like the witnesses before him, Hippolytus renders no Old-Testament catalogue; he cites the disputed books in the course of argument — Wisdom as the “prophecy of Solomon” in a treatise against the Jews, Baruch of the incarnation against Noetus. Clement of Alexandria, a few years earlier, had cited such books with the “Scripture” formula.
  • The form of the Wisdom citation. Hippolytus ascribes the Book of Wisdom to Solomon and calls the passage “the prophecy of Solomon, which speaketh of Christ.” Irenaeus, a generation earlier, had cited Susanna and Baruch under the names of “Daniel the prophet” and “Jeremiah the prophet.”
  • Who speaks the formula. The “Thus it is written” that introduces Baruch in Against Noetus §2 is spoken by the Noetians, not by Hippolytus in his own voice; his own words on the passage are the reply in §5 quoted above.
  • Bearing on the disputed readings. How citations of this kind bear on the early church’s canon is set out on “How did the early church receive the deuterocanonical books?” Hippolytus’s citations date from c. 205, before the Western conciliar lists of the late fourth century.
  • Place on the timeline. At c. 205 Hippolytus falls between Clement of Alexandria (c. 200) and Origen (c. 240) — a Roman witness from the same decades as the Alexandrian Clement.
