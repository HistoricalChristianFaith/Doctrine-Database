The Westminster Confession of Faith on the Old Testament Canon

Dates: c. 1647 (the Confession of Faith drawn up by the Westminster Assembly of Divines — The Humble Advice of the Assembly of Divines, completed 1646 and presented to the English Parliament in 1647, adopted by the Church of Scotland in 1647) · Wikipedia
Witness: Chapter 1.3 of the Westminster Confession rules that the Apocrypha, "not being of divine inspiration, are no part of the canon of the Scripture; and therefore are of no authority in the Church of God, nor to be any otherwise approved, or made use of, than other human writings." The Thirty-Nine Articles (1571) had kept these books as reading "for example of life."

The text of Chapter 1

WCF 1.2: the canon is the books "given by inspiration of God"

Chapter 1, "Of the Holy Scripture," names the canon by enumeration. Section 2 opens:

Under the name of holy Scripture, or the Word of God written, are now contained all the books of the Old and New Testaments, which are these:

— Westminster Confession of Faith, 1.2.1 There follow the thirty-nine books of the Old Testament — Genesis through Malachi, the Hebrew-sized canon — and the twenty-seven of the New, after which the section closes: "All which are given by inspiration of God, to be the rule of faith and life."1 The criterion the section states is inspiration: the books are "given by inspiration of God, to be the rule of faith and life." The Old Testament list has the contents of the Masoretic Text. On Augustine's criteria, see his page.

WCF 1.3: the Apocrypha "no part of the canon" and ranked with "other human writings"

The next section turns to the disputed books:

The books commonly called Apocrypha, not being of divine inspiration, are no part of the canon of the Scripture; and therefore are of no authority in the Church of God, nor to be any otherwise approved, or made use of, than other human writings.

— Westminster Confession of Faith, 1.3.2 The first clause denies canonicity on the ground of inspiration, the test stated in 1.2. The second sets their standing: "of no authority in the Church of God, nor to be any otherwise approved, or made use of, than other human writings." Jerome, Rufinus, Luther, and the Thirty-Nine Articles had all kept the deuterocanon as edifying reading — read in church "for example of life," "profitable and good to read," though not to prove doctrine. Westminster keeps no such middle rank.

Westminster and the Thirty-Nine Articles

Article VI of the Thirty-Nine Articles had drawn the same Hebrew-sized canon and assigned the deuterocanon a named use: the Church "doth read" these books "for example of life and instruction of manners," withholding only their power to "establish any doctrine."3 The Westminster Confession, drawn up in the 1640s, names no such use; it allows the books to be "approved, or made use of" only as "other human writings." In the early nineteenth century the British and Foreign Bible Society resolved to issue Bibles without the Apocrypha.3

Reception and debate

  • Westminster beside earlier lists. Athanasius, Jerome, Rufinus, Luther, and the Thirty-Nine Articles had kept the deuterocanon in a "read" or "ecclesiastical" rank. The Westminster Confession ranks the Apocrypha with "other human writings"; Trent, a century before, had listed the same books among the canonical Scriptures.
  • The stated criterion. Westminster states its ground as divine inspiration, and the Apocrypha's exclusion as following from their "not being of divine inspiration." The criteria stated by Augustine, the African councils and Jerome are set out on their pages.
  • Texts in the books. The books include texts adduced in the controversies of the time, 2 Maccabees 12 for prayer and offerings for the dead and Tobit 12 for almsgiving that "saves from death." Whether doctrinal content drove the setting-aside is disputed; see the interpretation page.
  • Provenance. Chapter 1 is quoted from the Westminster Confession of Faith (1646/1647) in its received text. The date 1647 follows the Confession's presentation to the English Parliament and its adoption by the Church of Scotland; the Assembly completed the document in 1646.
