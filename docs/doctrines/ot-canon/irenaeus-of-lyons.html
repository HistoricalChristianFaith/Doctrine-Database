Irenaeus of Lyons on the Old Testament Canon

Dates: c. 180 AD (Against Heresies, c. 175–189; Irenaeus lived c. 130–202, bishop of Lugdunum/Lyons, who reports that in his youth he heard Polycarp, and that Polycarp had known the apostle John) · Wikipedia
Summary: In Against Heresies Irenaeus quotes two of the disputed deuterocanonical texts as the words of named prophets. He cites the Greek addition to Daniel — the story of Susanna — as words "to be found in Daniel the prophet," and he quotes a long passage of Baruch as the words "Jeremiah the prophet has pointed out" (Baruch being reckoned in the Greek tradition an appendix to Jeremiah). Clement, the Didache, and Polycarp echo the disputed books without a citation formula; Irenaeus introduces them with "the prophet." He gives no Old-Testament list.

Claims & evidence

He cites the addition to Daniel (Susanna) as the words of "Daniel the prophet"

Those, however, who are believed to be presbyters by many, but serve their own lusts, and, do not place the fear of God supreme in their hearts, but conduct themselves with contempt towards others, and are puffed up with the pride of holding the chief seat, and work evil deeds in secret, saying, "No man sees us," shall be convicted by the Word, who does not judge after outward appearance (secundum gloriam), nor looks upon the countenance, but the heart; and they shall hear those words, to be found in Daniel the prophet: "O thou seed of Canaan, and not of Judah, beauty hath deceived thee, and lust perverted thy heart. Thou that art waxen old in wicked days, now thy sins which thou hast committed aforetime are come to light; for thou hast pronounced false judgments, and hast been accustomed to condemn the innocent, and to let the guilty go free, albeit the Lord saith, The innocent and the righteous shalt thou not slay."

— Against Heresies IV.26.3.1 The quoted words are Daniel's rebuke of the elders in the story of Susanna — Susanna verses 56 ("O thou seed of Canaan") and 52 — one of the Greek additions to Daniel that stand in the Septuagint but not in the Hebrew text, and which Protestants reckon apocryphal.2 Irenaeus introduces them with the formula "to be found in Daniel the prophet," citing the Susanna material as part of the book of Daniel.

He quotes Baruch as the words of "Jeremiah the prophet"

And Jeremiah the prophet has pointed out, that as many believers as God has prepared for this purpose, to multiply those left upon earth, should both be under the rule of the saints to minister to this Jerusalem, and that [His] kingdom shall be in it, saying, "Look around Jerusalem towards the east, and behold the joy which comes to thee from God Himself. Behold, thy sons shall come whom thou hast sent forth: they shall come in a band from the east even unto the west, by the word of that Holy One, rejoicing in that splendour which is from thy God. O Jerusalem, put off thy robe of mourning and of affliction, and put on that beauty of eternal splendour from thy God. Gird thyself with the double garment of that righteousness proceeding from thy God; place the mitre of eternal glory upon thine head."

— Against Heresies V.35.1.3 The long quotation is not from Jeremiah as the Hebrew text has it but from the deuterocanonical Baruch — Baruch 4:36 through chapter 5 — which the Greek and Latin tradition treated as an appendix to Jeremiah and routinely cited under the prophet's name.4 Irenaeus introduces it as what "Jeremiah the prophet has pointed out… saying," and adduces it for the resurrection and the earthly kingdom, alongside Isaiah in the same chapter.

Reception and debate

  • The Baruch-as-Jeremiah convention. Irenaeus names "Jeremiah" while quoting Baruch. In the Septuagint and the Old Latin, Baruch and the Epistle of Jeremiah travelled bound with Jeremiah and were commonly cited as his,4 the same fold-in by which Cyril, Athanasius, and Epiphanius later kept Baruch inside a "canonical" Jeremiah while counting a Hebrew-sized list. The Ante-Nicene Fathers editors identify the quotation as Baruch in their apparatus.3
  • Citation formula, no list. Clement of Rome, the Didache, and Polycarp echo the disputed books in exhortation with no citation formula; Irenaeus attributes both Susanna and Baruch to "the prophet," under the names of Daniel and Jeremiah. He gives no Old-Testament list; his is a witness to use.
  • Bearing on the disputed readings. How second-century citations of this kind bear on the early church’s canon is set out on "How did the early church receive the deuterocanonical books?"
  • Place on the timeline. At c. 180 AD Irenaeus stands a decade after Melito of Sardis (c. 170), the earliest extant Christian Old-Testament list on this timeline, which does not include the deuterocanon, and before Origen (c. 240). In the same generation Melito enumerates a list without the disputed books, and Irenaeus quotes Greek additions under the prophets’ names.
