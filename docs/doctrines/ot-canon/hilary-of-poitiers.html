Hilary of Poitiers on the Old Testament Canon

Dates: c. 360 AD (the Old Testament list is in the Instructio Psalmorum §15, the prologue to his Tractatus super Psalmos / Expositions of the Psalms, written c. 360; Hilary c. 310–367) · Wikipedia
Witness: Opening his commentary on the Psalter, Hilary fixes the Old Testament at twenty-two books, the Hebrew-letter count, reciting them book by book: Tobit, Judith, the Wisdom of Solomon, Sirach, and 1–2 Maccabees are absent from the enumeration. He records that "some add Tobit and Judith to make twenty-four books, according to the number of the Greek letters." His count resembles those of Melito, Cyril, and Athanasius, and precedes Jerome's by a generation.

Claims & evidence

The twenty-two books — "that this corresponds with the number of the [Hebrew] letters"

In the prologue to his exposition of the Psalms, Hilary gives the Old Testament book by book and fixes the count at twenty-two, tying the number to the Hebrew alphabet "according to old tradition":1

The reason for reckoning twenty-two books of the Old Testament is that this corresponds with the number of the [Hebrew] letters. They are counted thus according to old tradition: the books of Moses are five, Joshua son of Nun the sixth, Judges and Ruth the seventh, first and second Kings the eighth, third and fourth [Kings] the ninth, the two of Chronicles make ten, the words of the days of Ezra the eleventh, the book of Psalms twelfth, of Solomon the Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, and Song of Songs are thirteenth, fourteenth, and fifteenth, the Twelve Prophets sixteenth, then Isaiah and Jeremiah (with Lamentations and the Epistle) and Daniel and Ezekiel and Job and Esther complete the number of the books at twenty-two. To this some add Tobit and Judith to make twenty-four books, according to the number of the Greek letters, which is the language used among Hebrews and Greeks gathered in Rome.

The deuterocanon is absent from the count

Measured against the disputed books the result is: no Tobit, no Judith, no Wisdom of Solomon, no Sirach, no Maccabees among Hilary's twenty-two. His list is the protocanonical Hebrew set (Ruth folded into Judges, the paired books of Kings and Chronicles each counted once, Ezra standing for Ezra–Nehemiah), and he frames it as "old tradition."1 As with Cyril and Origen, his single Jeremiah is reckoned "with Lamentations and the Epistle" — the Epistle of Jeremiah carried inside Jeremiah rather than listed on its own; the other deuterocanonical books do not appear.

“To this some add Tobit and Judith”

He records that "some add Tobit and Judith to make twenty-four books, according to the number of the Greek letters."1 The enumeration he recites runs to twenty-two; the twenty-four-book count he ascribes to others ("some add"). The expanded total he reports adds Tobit and Judith only, to match the Greek alphabet, and does not include Wisdom, Sirach, or Maccabees. What the elastic count signifies is disputed. James White (Reformed Baptist apologist, 2004) reads the alphabet-tied number as a mnemonic for a real list handed down from the Jews; Gary Michuta (Catholic apologist, 2004) reads the twenty-two / twenty-four count as a "mystical correspondence of numbers, letters of the alphabet, and books," not a canon boundary.2 Both readings are set out on their own page.

A twenty-two-book count in the West

Hilary spent his exile (c. 356–360) in Phrygia among the Greek-speaking churches of Asia Minor before returning to Gaul. The twenty-two-book, Hebrew-letter count he sets out matches the counts of the Greek writers Melito, Origen, Cyril, and Athanasius; here it appears in Latin, in the West, c. 360.1 It is among the earliest extant Latin canon-lists; the anonymous Latin Cheltenham List, which includes the deuterocanon, is of about the same date. Hilary wrote about a generation before Jerome's prefaces (the Prologus Galeatus and the Vulgate prologues of the 390s).

The usage beside the list — citing the disputed books as "Prophet," "Scripture," and "law"

James White names Hilary first among the Fathers who, he says, "all rejected the freestanding Apocrypha as fully canonical in their writings," on the strength of the enumeration above.2 Arguing against the Arians in the De Trinitate, and in his Psalm commentary, Hilary also quotes books his list does not enumerate, under the formulas "the Prophet says," "the prophet's voice," the name of Jeremiah, "as the blessed Susanna says," and "from the law." Five cases:

2 Maccabees 7:28 — "as the Prophet says." Proving creation out of nothing against the Arian charge that the Son was made from pre-existing matter, Hilary appeals to the mother of the Maccabean martyrs as the Prophet and binds the citation to "the clear sense of Scripture":3

Such suggestions are inconsistent with the clear sense of Scripture. For all things, as the Prophet says, were made out of nothing; it was no transformation of existing things, but the creation into a perfect form of the non-existent.

Wisdom 13:5 — "from the prophet's voice." Tracing the soul's ascent to God through creation, Hilary credits the insight to the prophet and quotes the Wisdom of Solomon as that prophet's word:4

Then, while the devout soul was baffled and astray through its own feebleness, it caught from the prophet's voice this scale of comparison for God, admirably expressed, "By the greatness of His works and the beauty of the things that He has made the Creator of worlds is rightly discerned."

Baruch 3:35–37 — quoted as Jeremiah, alongside Moses and Isaiah. Hilary chains the prophets to prove God seen on earth, and cites Baruch under the prophet Jeremiah's own name, ranking it with the Law and Isaiah and setting it against John 1:18:5

As you have listened already to Moses and Isaiah, so listen now to Jeremiah inculcating the same truth as they:— "This is our God, and there shall be none other likened unto Him, Who has found out all the way of knowledge, and has given it unto Jacob His servant and to Israel His beloved. Afterward did He show Himself upon earth and dwelt among men."

Susanna (Daniel 13:42) — "as the blessed Susanna says." Listing the divine attributes from Scripture, Hilary strings Susanna's prayer between Matthew and Isaiah as one scriptural testimony among the rest:6

They say that the Father has prescience of all things, as the blessed Susanna says, "O eternal God, that know secrets, and know all things before they be"; that He is incomprehensible, as it is written, "The heaven is My throne…"

Judith 16:3 — sung "from the law." In his exposition of Psalm 125, Hilary catalogues what the converted soul sings and confesses from the four sources of revelation — law, prophets, Gospel, apostle — and slots Judith's hymn under the law (ex lege), beside Isaiah, John, and Colossians. The passage survives only in Latin:7

…et cantantes ex lege: Dominus conterens bella, Dominus nomen est illi (Judith. XVI, 3); cognoscentes a propheta: Ego sum qui feci coelum et terram ex nihilo…

— "…and singing from the law: 'The Lord that crushes wars, the Lord is His name' (Judith 16:3); learning from the prophet: 'I am he that made heaven and earth out of nothing'…" Judith's song is filed under lex, in the same series as Isaiah, the Gospel of John, and Paul.

White adduces the list; how the list and the citations together bear on the early church's reception of these books is set out on “How did the early church receive the deuterocanonical books?”

Reception and debate

  • The Wisdom tradition in De Trinitate XII. In Book XII of the De Trinitate, on the begetting of Wisdom, Hilary draws on the Wisdom tradition, including the Wisdom of Solomon, in his argument against the Arians.8 The individual citations — 2 Maccabees and Wisdom as "the Prophet," Baruch as Jeremiah, Susanna, and Judith "from the law" — are gathered above under the usage beside the list.
  • §15 of the prologue, not "book 22." The list sits in the Instructio Psalmorum, the twenty-four-chapter introduction to the Tractatus super Psalmos, at section 15. (The "vol. 22" attached to the standard Vienna critical edition — CSEL 22 — is the series volume number, not a chapter; the canon passage is §15 of the prologue.)
