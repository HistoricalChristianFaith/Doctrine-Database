The Synod of Jerusalem (1672) on the Old Testament Canon

Dates: c. 1672 (the Synod of Jerusalem — also called the Synod of Bethlehem, convened at the Church of the Nativity by Dositheus II, Patriarch of Jerusalem, in March 1672; Philip Schaff gives the date as 16 March, Wikipedia dates the acts 20 March; its decrees are the Confession of Dositheus, with sixty-eight signatures) · Wikipedia
Witness: The Confession of Dositheus names the Wisdom of Solomon, Judith, Tobit, Bel and the Dragon, Susanna, the Maccabees, and Sirach as "genuine parts of Scripture," answering the confession issued under Cyril Lucaris's name, which had called them Apocrypha, and grounds their place in what it calls the delivery of "the Catholic Church": "the denial of these is the rejection of those." The synod answered a confession of Reformed teaching circulated under the name of a former Patriarch of Constantinople.

Background: the confession of Cyril Lucaris

The Synod was convened to repudiate the Eastern Confession of the Christian Faith (Geneva, 1629/1633) circulated under the name of Cyril Lucaris, Patriarch of Constantinople, a confession that adopted the Reformed canon and called the deuterocanon "Apocrypha" on the authority of the Synod of Laodicea's list.1 Dositheus's council disowned that confession and answered its eighteen chapters point by point; it also set out to show that Lucaris had not written the confession, and Wikipedia reports that his Eastern Orthodox defenders hold the book a forgery.1 The third of its decrees (numbered as the answer to "Question 3" in the appended catechism) takes up the canon and names the disputed books.

The decree on the canon

The disputed books named "genuine parts of Scripture"

Decree 3 of the Confession lists by name the books it says Cyril had "called Apocrypha" and rejects that label for them:

Following the rule of the Catholic Church, we call Sacred Scripture all those which Cyril collected from the Synod of Laodicea, and enumerated, adding thereto those which he foolishly, and ignorantly, or rather maliciously called Apocrypha; to wit, "The Wisdom of Solomon," "Judith," "Tobit," "The History of the Dragon" [Bel and the Dragon], "The History of Susanna," "The Maccabees," and "The Wisdom of Sirach." For we judge these also to be with the other genuine Books of Divine Scripture genuine parts of Scripture.

— Confession of Dositheus, Decree 3 (Synod of Jerusalem, 1672).2 The catalogue (Wisdom, Judith, Tobit, the Greek Daniel-additions (Bel and the Dragon, Susanna), 1–2 Maccabees, and Sirach) is declared "genuine parts of Scripture," "with the other genuine Books of Divine Scripture."

The ground given: delivery by “the Catholic Church”

The Confession grounds the books in what it calls the delivery of “ancient custom, or rather the Catholic Church”:

For ancient custom, or rather the Catholic Church, which hath delivered to us as genuine the Sacred Gospels and the other Books of Scripture, hath undoubtedly delivered these also as parts of Scripture, and the denial of these is the rejection of those. And if, perhaps, it seemeth that not always have all of these been considered on the same level as the rest, yet nevertheless these also have been counted and reckoned with the rest of Scripture, both by Synods and by many of the most ancient and eminent Theologians of the Catholic Church. All of these we also judge to be Canonical Books, and confess them to be Sacred Scripture.

— Confession of Dositheus, Decree 3.2 The Confession holds that the books stand with the Gospels, because the same Church, in its terms, handed both down: "the denial of these is the rejection of those." It grants that "not always have all of these been considered on the same level as the rest," and holds that they "have been counted and reckoned with the rest of Scripture, both by Synods and by many of the most ancient and eminent Theologians of the Catholic Church."

Reception and debate

  • Trent and Jerusalem. Trent (1546) and the Synod of Jerusalem (1672) both list the disputed books among the Scriptures, Trent under anathema; Jerusalem answers a confession circulated under an Orthodox patriarch's name.
  • Westminster and Jerusalem. Westminster (1647) ranks the Apocrypha with "other human writings," on the ground that they are "not… of divine inspiration"; Jerusalem (1672) ranks the same books with the Gospels, on the ground of their delivery by "the Catholic Church," in the Confession's words.
  • Later Orthodox teaching. The Longer Catechism of Metropolitan Philaret of Moscow (approved by the Holy Synod, Moscow, 1830) counts the Old Testament at twenty-two books with Cyril of Jerusalem, Athanasius and John of Damascus, “agreeing therein with the Jews”; asked why Sirach “and certain others” are not in the count, it answers “Because they do not exist in the Hebrew,” and, citing Athanasius, that they “have been appointed of the Fathers to be read by proselytes who are preparing for admission into the Church.”3 Eugen Pentiuc (Orthodox, Holy Cross Greek Orthodox School of Theology, 2014) traces the Orthodox position from the early writers to John of Damascus and describes the current position as strict about the thirty-nine books of the Jewish Bible and flexible about the anaginōskomena found in the Septuagint.4 Kallistos Ware (Orthodox bishop, The Orthodox Church) sets the two side by side: Jassy (1642) and Jerusalem (1672) declared the books “genuine parts of Scripture,” while “most Orthodox scholars at the present day, however, following the opinion of Athanasius and Jerome, consider that the Deutero-Canonical Books, although part of the Bible, stand on a lower footing than the rest of the Old Testament.”5
  • Texts in the books received. The books received include 2 Maccabees 12:43–45, adduced for prayer and offerings for the dead, and Tobit 12:9 on almsgiving; how such texts figure in the canon debate is set out under Disputed readings on the summary timeline.
  • Provenance. The decrees are quoted from the Confession of Dositheus in the English translation (J. N. W. B. Robertson, 1899), reproduced in Philip Schaff's Creeds of Christendom and elsewhere; the synod sat at Bethlehem in March 1672 under Patriarch Dositheus II of Jerusalem. Schaff gives the Confession's date as "Jerusalem, March 16, 1672"; Wikipedia gives 20 March 1672 for the acts.2,1
