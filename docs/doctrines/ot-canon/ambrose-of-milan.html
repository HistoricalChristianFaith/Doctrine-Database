Ambrose of Milan on the Old Testament Canon

Dates: c. 339–397 AD (Ambrose, bishop of Milan from 374, and the bishop who catechized and baptized Augustine at Easter 387; the citations below come from works spanning his episcopate — On the Death of Satyrus [c. 378], the Exposition of the Christian Faith [De Fide, c. 378–380], On the Holy Spirit [381], Concerning Repentance, Concerning Virginity, and On the Duties of the Clergy [c. 388]) · Wikipedia
Witness: Ambrose leaves no catalogue of the Old Testament books. Across his works he quotes the Wisdom of Solomon as what "Scripture says," Baruch as the word of "the Lord," the Greek Susanna under "it is written," and Sirach against Arius, and adduces Tobit, Judith, and the Maccabean martyrs as patterns of holiness, as do his fellow Westerners Tertullian, Cyprian, and Lactantius. He baptized Augustine in 387.

Claims & evidence

Wisdom of Solomon quoted as "Scripture": "He was taken away"

Preaching at the funeral of his brother in On the Death of Satyrus (I.30), Ambrose consoles himself that Satyrus died at an opportune hour, spared the barbarian invasions to come — just as Enoch was "translated" from a corrupt world. He grounds the consolation in a verse of the deuterocanonical Wisdom of Solomon (4:11), introduced with "Scripture says":1

For if no one mourns for Enoch, who was translated when the world was at peace and wars were not raging, but the people rather congratulated him, as Scripture says concerning him: "He was taken away, lest that wickedness should alter his understanding."

Here the Wisdom verse is introduced as what "Scripture" says and applied to the death of Satyrus.

Baruch read as the voice of "the Lord": "Put off, O Jerusalem, the garment of your mourning"

In the first book of Concerning Repentance (I.9.43), expounding God's readiness to forgive the penitent, Ambrose puts a passage of the deuterocanonical Baruch (5:1) directly into the mouth of "the Lord," as His own answer to repentant Jerusalem:2

And the Lord bids them lay aside the garments of mourning, and to cease the groanings of repentance, saying: "Put off, O Jerusalem, the garment of your mourning and affliction, and clothe yourself in beauty, the glory which God has given you forever."

Baruch was reckoned in the Greek and Latin tradition an appendix to Jeremiah, and Ambrose cites it as the direct speech of God. Irenaeus, Hippolytus, and Basil also cite Baruch (see their pages).

The Greek Susanna under "it is written": "The Lord heard her voice"

In On the Holy Spirit (III.6.39), arguing that God hears the prayer of the chaste and the wronged, Ambrose tells the story of Susanna — the Greek addition to Daniel (ch. 13) — and introduces the verse with "it is written":3

For when Susanna, assailed by the conspiracy of the elders, saw that the mind of the people was moved by consideration for the old men, and destitute of all help, alone among men, conscious of her chastity she prayed God to judge; it is written: "The Lord heard her voice, when she was being led to be put to death."

The "it is written" introduces a verse of the Greek-only Susanna narrative. Jerome ranks Susanna among the parts of Daniel "not in the Hebrew" ⚠ unverified.

Sirach cited against Arius: "Seek not that which is too deep for you"

In the fifth book of the Exposition of the Christian Faith (De Fide V.19.236), pressing the Arians on the unsearchable generation of the Son, Ambrose applies a verse of the deuterocanonical Sirach (3:22) to Arius by name:4

But Arius is not the Holy Spirit. Nay, it was said even to Arius and to all men: "Seek not that which is too deep for you."

The verse is cited in a controversy over the deity of Christ. A generation earlier Alexander of Alexandria had cited the neighbouring Sirach 3:21 in his own letter against Arius.

Tobit, Judith, and the Maccabean mother as patterns of holiness

Three further uses adduce deuterocanonical figures as moral examples, set beside protocanonical ones. In the Exposition of the Christian Faith (V.7.94), arguing that to be sent implies no inferiority in the one sent — his point about the Holy Spirit — Ambrose cites Tobit (9:3) beside Numbers 22:5

For Tobias sent Raphael the archangel, and an angel was sent to Balaam.

In Concerning Virginity (II.4.24), arguing that an act done for religion's sake is not defiled by its appearance, he sets Judith beside Rahab:6

And Judith adorned herself that she might please an adulterer, but because she did this for religion and not for love, no one considered her an adulteress.

And in On the Duties of the Clergy (I.41.212), cataloguing the church's martyrs, he holds up the mother of the seven sons from 2 Maccabees (7:20) — set, in the next breath, beside "those two-year-old children of Bethlehem" (Matthew 2:16):7

What shall I say of the mother who with joy looked on the corpses of her children as so many trophies, and found delight in the voices of her dying sons, as though in the songs of singers, noting in her children the tones of the glorious harp of her own heart, and a sweeter harmony of love than any strain of the lute could give?

These are uses of the books as historical example rather than citations with a Scripture formula, like Clement of Rome's "blessed Judith"; Ambrose sets the figures beside Balaam (Numbers), Rahab (Joshua), and the children of Bethlehem (Matthew).

Esdras adduced as a teaching authority

One citation concerns a book outside both the Tridentine and the Protestant lists. In On the Holy Spirit (II.6.49), answering Arians who pressed a verse of Amos to make the Holy Spirit a creature, Ambrose grants that a spirit — the wind, the spirit of the firmament — is indeed created, and grounds the point on the authority of "Esdras," citing a numbered book of Esdras as instruction:8

Esdras taught us that spirit is created, saying in the fourth book: "And upon the second day Thou madest the spirit of the firmament."

The verse is 4 Ezra 6:41 (the Latin 4 Esdras, the Apocalypse of Ezra; = 2 Esdras 6:41 in the King James Apocrypha) — introduced with "Esdras taught us," a named book cited in a doctrinal dispute. García Martínez notes Ambrose as the most explicit patristic user of 4 Ezra, and he draws on the same book in On the Death of Satyrus. Two further points bear on the citation. First, the book's number differs between text and translation: the de Romestin English here reads "the fourth book," but the critical Latin (CSEL) reads in tertio libro — "in the third book" — the translation following the later Vulgate numbering "4 Esdras." Bogaert, who cites this passage, discusses the varying Latin numbering of the "books of Ezra," including how Jerome counted them. Second, 4 Ezra is not named in Trent's 1546 list, stands in an appendix to the Clementine Vulgate, and is not in the Protestant canons.

Reception and debate

  • A Western witness before Augustine. Ambrose, bishop of Milan 374–397, cites the disputed books in works of c. 378–388, before Augustine — whom he baptized in 387 — set out his list in On Christian Doctrine (c. 397), and before the African councils (393–419).
  • Kinds of use. Three citations carry an explicit formula — Wisdom "as Scripture says," Baruch as the word of "the Lord," Susanna under "it is written"; the Sirach verse is applied to Arius in a doctrinal argument; 4 Ezra is introduced with "Esdras taught us"; and the Tobit, Judith, and Maccabees citations are moral examples.
  • Lists and usage. Ambrose draws up no list. Athanasius and Cyril drew up Hebrew-sized lists and also cite disputed books (see their pages); Basil, like Ambrose, leaves no list. His Latin contemporary Jerome drew up a Hebrew-sized list, setting Wisdom, Sirach, Judith, and Tobit outside the canon and omitting Baruch; the African councils (393–419) list the longer canon. Ambrose's use is cited in the disputed reading on how the early church received the deuterocanonical books.
  • 4 Ezra. Ambrose cites 4 Ezra 6:41 (above) as a teaching authority; the book is in neither the Protestant canon nor Trent's list (1546). The footnote records the numbering difference: Ambrose's own Latin says the "third" book, his modern translator the "fourth," for one and the same verse.
