Josephus on the Old Testament Canon

Dates: c. AD 93 · Wikipedia
Summary: The Jewish historian gives, in Against Apion 1.8, the earliest extant statement that fixes the Jewish Scriptures at a definite number: twenty-two books "justly believed to be divine" — five of Moses, thirteen of the prophets, four of hymns and precepts — with the prophets' record running "from the death of Moses till the reign of Artaxerxes." What was written after Artaxerxes, he says, "hath not been esteemed of the like authority with the former by our forefathers, because there hath not been an exact succession of prophets since that time." He names no titles. Protestant writers (F. F. Bruce, Roger Beckwith, James White) cite the passage as a first-century Jewish witness to a closed Hebrew canon without the deuterocanonical books; others (John Barton, Rebecca Gray, and the Catholic apologists Gary Michuta and Trent Horn) read it as one reckoning, without a list of titles, by a Greek-writing Jew who also used the Septuagint — see the interpretation page, "Does Josephus's count of twenty-two books attest a closed Hebrew canon excluding the deuterocanon?"

Claims & evidence

Twenty-two books, “justly believed to be divine”

"For we have not an innumerable multitude of books among us, disagreeing from and contradicting one another, [as the Greeks have,] but only twenty-two books, which contain the records of all the past times; which are justly believed to be divine; and of them five belong to Moses, which contain his laws and the traditions of the origin of mankind till his death. This interval of time was little short of three thousand years; but as to the time from the death of Moses till the reign of Artaxerxes king of Persia, who reigned after Xerxes, the prophets, who were after Moses, wrote down what was done in their times in thirteen books. The remaining four books contain hymns to God, and precepts for the conduct of human life."

— Against Apion, I.8 (§§37–40), Whiston translation, source. This is the earliest extant statement that fixes the Jewish Scriptures at a definite, bounded number. The three-part division — five (the Law), thirteen ("the prophets"), four (hymns and precepts) — is Josephus's own grouping, broader than the later rabbinic Law–Prophets–Writings, and he gives no list of titles, so exactly which books fill the thirteen and the four must be inferred.

The cutoff at Artaxerxes and the "exact succession" of prophets

"It is true, our history hath been written since Artaxerxes very particularly, but hath not been esteemed of the like authority with the former by our forefathers, because there hath not been an exact succession of prophets since that time;"

— Against Apion, I.8 (§41), Whiston translation, source. The reign of Artaxerxes I (mid-fifth century BC, the age of Ezra and Nehemiah) is the boundary: books written after it are said to exist and to have been written "very particularly," but not to have been "esteemed of the like authority" by the forefathers, because there had not been an "exact succession of prophets." The words he uses are the lapse of an exact succession of prophets. On Rebecca Gray's reading (Prophetic Figures in Late Second Temple Jewish Palestine, Oxford, 1993, pp. 12–13) the phrase marks the continuous historical narrative from Genesis to Esther — the very antiquity the apologetic is defending — not a claim that inspiration stopped. In the Antiquities Josephus retells the Maccabean rededication without 1 Maccabees' “until there should come a prophet” (4:46; Antiquities XII.7.6). How the cutoff bears on the canon question is discussed on the cessation-of-prophecy argument page.

The books described as inviolable, and worth dying for

"…no one has been so bold as either to add any thing to them, to take any thing from them, or to make any change in them; but it is become natural to all Jews immediately, and from their very birth, to esteem these books to contain Divine doctrines, and to persist in them, and, if occasion be willingly to die for them."

— Against Apion, I.8 (§42), Whiston translation, source. Josephus describes the books as a settled, inherited and inviolable body of writings for which Jews would die, a fixed inheritance rather than an open question of his own day.

Reception and debate

  • "Twenty-two" vs. the rabbinic "twenty-four." The Babylonian Talmud (Bava Batra 14b) and 4 Ezra count twenty-four books. S. Z. Leiman ("Josephus and the Canon of the Bible," 1989) argues that the two enumerate the same collection counted differently: Josephus coupled Ruth with Judges and Lamentations with Jeremiah (couplings that recur in Origen, Epiphanius, Jerome and Cyril), so that the twenty-four books of the later rabbinic canon fall into as many numbers as the Hebrew alphabet has letters. Theodor Dunkelgrün, reporting Leiman's reconstruction, adds that its probability "is not certainty," and quotes John Barton: "it is impossible to know exactly which books [Josephus] included, or how he counted them" (Theodor Dunkelgrün, "The Testimonium Flavianum Canonicum: Josephus as a Witness to the Biblical Canon, 1566–1823," International Journal of the Classical Tradition 23 (2016): 252–268, citing Leiman, pp. 53–54, and Barton, "The Old Testament Canons," New Cambridge History of the Bible I, 2013, p. 159). Juan Carlos Ossandón Widow (Catholic, The Origins of the Canon of the Hebrew Bible: An Analysis of Josephus and 4 Ezra, Brill, 2018, pp. 42–46) is reported to hold that when Josephus wrote, books such as Song of Songs and Ecclesiastes were not yet considered canonical ⚠ unverified (reported in "Development of the Hebrew Bible canon," Wikipedia).
  • Josephus and the Vespasian oracle. Josephus was a Jewish general who surrendered at Jotapata, hailed Vespasian as the coming world-ruler, and composed his histories under Flavian patronage. He refers the "ambiguous oracle… in their sacred [writings]" that "one from their country should become governor of the habitable earth" not to a Jewish Messiah but to Vespasian, "appointed emperor in Judea" (Jewish War VI.5.4, source). On that ground the Catholic apologist Trent Horn reads the sweeping statements in Against Apion — no disagreement among the books, nothing ever added or removed, every Jew ready to die for them — as apologetic rhetoric, Josephus "exaggerating, not lying" (source); James White (Reformed Baptist) takes the count as Josephus's report of what his nation held. How the count is read is set out on the interpretation page, "Does Josephus's count of twenty-two books attest a closed Hebrew canon excluding the deuterocanon?"
  • A Greek-writing Jew who used the Septuagint. Josephus wrote in Greek for a Greco-Roman readership and drew on the Septuagint for the Greek form of the biblical history: in retelling the return from exile he follows 1 Esdras, including the contest of the three bodyguards, and in the Esther story he gives the text of a royal letter found in the Greek additions (Antiquities XI.3.2–6, XI.6.12). F. F. Bruce ("The Canon of Scripture," 1954) holds that Philo and Josephus "recognized only the Canon of the Hebrew Bible, although they used the ‘Septuagint’"; Barton and Gray read his count as one reckoning whose contents must be inferred. Both readings are set out on the interpretation page.
