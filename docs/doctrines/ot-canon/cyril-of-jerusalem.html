Cyril of Jerusalem on the Old Testament Canon

Dates: c. 350 AD (the Old Testament list is in Catechetical Lecture 4.33–37, the baptismal catechesis delivered at Jerusalem c. 348–350; Cyril c. 313–386) · Wikipedia · his New Testament canon page
Witness: Catechizing the candidates for baptism, Cyril gives an Old-Testament list of twenty-two books, the Hebrew-letter count. Tobit, Judith, Sirach, the Wisdom of Solomon, and 1–2 Maccabees are absent, and he charges the catechumen to "have nothing to do with the apocryphal writings." His Jeremiah carries Baruch and the Epistle of Jeremiah, and elsewhere in the same lectures he quotes Baruch as "the Prophet," the Wisdom of Solomon "according to Solomon," Sirach, and the Greek Daniel's Susanna. He credits the whole list to the Septuagint's seventy-two translators.

Claims & evidence

The twenty-two books — "study earnestly these only which we read openly in the Church"

In the fourth Catechetical Lecture, instructing the baptismal candidates "Of the Divine Scriptures," Cyril recites the Old Testament book by book and fixes the count at twenty-two, the number of the Hebrew letters:1

Of these read the two and twenty books, but have nothing to do with the apocryphal writings. Study earnestly these only which we read openly in the Church… And of the Old Testament, as we have said, study the two and twenty books… For of the Law the books of Moses are the first five, Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, Deuteronomy. And next, Joshua the son of Nave, and the book of Judges, including Ruth, counted as seventh. And of the other historical books, the first and second books of the Kings are among the Hebrews one book; also the third and fourth one book. And in like manner, the first and second of Chronicles are with them one book; and the first and second of Esdras are counted one. Esther is the twelfth book; and these are the Historical writings. But those which are written in verses are five, Job, and the book of Psalms, and Proverbs, and Ecclesiastes, and the Song of Songs, which is the seventeenth book. And after these come the five Prophetic books: of the Twelve Prophets one book, of Isaiah one, of Jeremiah one, including Baruch and Lamentations and the Epistle; then Ezekiel, and the Book of Daniel, the twenty-second of the Old Testament.

Books not in the list

Tobit, Judith, Sirach, the Wisdom of Solomon, and the Maccabees are not in the list. Cyril's twenty-two are the protocanonical Hebrew books (Ruth folded into Judges, the paired books of Kings, Chronicles, and Esdras each counted as one), and he adds — "have nothing to do with the apocryphal writings," "study earnestly these only which we read openly in the Church."1 Melito and Origen before him, and Athanasius and Jerome after him, also give Hebrew-sized counts (see their pages and the timeline).

Jeremiah "including Baruch and… the Epistle"

His single book of Jeremiah is reckoned "including Baruch and Lamentations and the Epistle" — folding in both Baruch and the Epistle of Jeremiah (Baruch ch. 6 in the Vulgate numbering), two short deuterocanonical works, not as separate books but as appendices to Jeremiah, as the Greek manuscripts transmitted them.1 Origen likewise bundles "the epistle" with Jeremiah, and Athanasius and Epiphanius also keep Baruch within Jeremiah (see their pages).

The disputed books cited in his catechesis

Elsewhere in the Catechetical Lectures Cyril quotes Baruch, which his list reckons within Jeremiah, and books and passages his twenty-two do not name: the Wisdom of Solomon, Sirach, and the Greek Daniel's Susanna.

Baruch, called "the Prophet." Proving that the One begotten of the Father became man and is God, Cyril says — "Hear the Prophet saying" — and quotes Baruch 3:36–37:2

This is our God, none other shall be accounted of in comparison with Him. He hath found out every way of knowledge, and given it to Jacob His servant, and to Israel His beloved. Afterwards He was seen on earth, and conversed among men.

His list reckons Baruch within Jeremiah; here he introduces the passage with "Hear the Prophet saying," as a proof of the Incarnation.

The Wisdom of Solomon, cited "according to Solomon." Arguing upward from the creation to the Creator, Cyril writes that this is "according to Solomon, who says," and quotes Wisdom 13:5:3

For by the greatness and beauty of the creatures proportionably the Maker of them is seen.

The Wisdom of Solomon is nowhere in his list — his five books "written in verses" are Job, Psalms, Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, and the Song — — and here he ascribes it to Solomon by name in his argument from the creation to the Creator.

Sirach, as a charge to humility. Warning the catechumens off speculation about the divine nature — "first gaze steadfastly upon the sun, and then curiously scan the Lord of the sun" — he clinches it with Sirach 3:21–22:4

Seek not the things that are too deep for thee, neither search out the things that are above thy strength: what is commanded thee, think thereupon.

Susanna, as a work of the Spirit. Cataloguing the operations of the Holy Spirit, Cyril sets the Greek Daniel's Susanna among them: the Spirit "endued with wisdom the soul of Daniel, that young as he was he should become a judge of Elders," for5

The chaste Susanna was condemned as a wanton; there was none to plead her cause; for who was to deliver her from the rulers?

Susanna belongs to the Greek additions to Daniel; the Hebrew Daniel does not contain it. Cyril adduces her vindication among the works of the Spirit, brought forward "as a testimony."

How a Father's citations relate to his list is set out on the interpretation pages “When a Father quotes a book ‘as Scripture,’ does that show he held it canonical?” and “How did the early church receive the deuterocanonical books: as Scripture, below the canon, or outside it?”

A Hebrew count credited to the Septuagint

Cyril ascribes his Hebrew-sized list to the Greek translation: just before reciting the list he retells the Letter of Aristeas legend — Ptolemy's seventy-two interpreters, each in a separate chamber on Pharos, producing translations that "agreed not only in the sense but even in words," so that "the translation of the Divine Scriptures, spoken by the Holy Ghost, was of the Holy Ghost accomplished" — and bids the catechumen "read the Divine Scriptures, the twenty-two books of the Old Testament, these that have been translated by the Seventy-two Interpreters."6 (The account of the Septuagint's origin and inspiration is gathered on the Origin and Inspiration of the Septuagint page and not repeated here.)

Reception and debate

  • Cyril's stated criterion. Cyril draws the boundary by what is read — what "we read openly in the Church," because "the Apostles, and the bishops of old time… handed down these books"; the apocrypha are to be "put aside" and "read not even by thyself." His fuller charge on the point, with the New-Testament list that omits the Apocalypse, is treated on his New Testament canon page.
  • List and usage. Cyril's list does not include Tobit, Judith, or the Maccabees. His Jeremiah includes Baruch and the Epistle of Jeremiah, and elsewhere in the lectures he quotes Baruch as "the Prophet," the Wisdom of Solomon "according to Solomon," Sirach as a charge to humility, and the Greek Daniel's Susanna among the Spirit's works (see "The disputed books cited in his catechesis" above).
  • The Hebrew count and the Greek translators. Cyril's twenty-two match the Hebrew reckoning (as do the counts of Origen, Athanasius, and Jerome), and he credits them to the seventy-two translators of the Septuagint, whose Greek Bible also carried Tobit, Judith, and the other books his list does not include.
