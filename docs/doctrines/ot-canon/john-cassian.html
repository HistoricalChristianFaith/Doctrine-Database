John Cassian on the Old Testament Canon

Dates: c. 360–435 AD (John Cassian — a monk, probably born in the Roman province of Scythia Minor, who trained in the monasteries of Bethlehem and the Egyptian desert, was ordained at Constantinople and Rome, and about AD 415 founded two monasteries near Marseilles in Gaul. His Institutes and Conferences (Collationes), written c. 420–429, transmit the teaching of the desert abbots to the West; the Rule of Benedict (c. xlii) orders the Conferences read to the brethren after supper1) · Wikipedia
Witness: Cassian leaves no catalogue of the Old Testament books. In the Conferences he quotes the Wisdom of Solomon under the formula "as Scripture itself testifies," Baruch as the word of "the prophet," and Sirach beside the apostles among the remedies for sin. He wrote in Gaul in the 420s, in the years of the African councils and Augustine.

Claims & evidence

Wisdom of Solomon cited as "Scripture itself testifies"

In Conference 13 (the second of Abbot Chaeremon's conferences, "On the Protection of God"), arguing that God sometimes works our good against our own will, Cassian quotes the Wisdom of Solomon under the "Scripture" formula:2

… and, while we are rushing headlong towards death, draws us back to salvation, and rescues us without our knowing it from the jaws of hell. For God made not death, neither rejoices in the destruction of the living.

The clause "For God made not death, neither rejoices in the destruction of the living" is Wisdom 1:13, introduced — in the sentence Cassian builds toward it — as that to which "Scripture itself testifies," in an argument about God's providential protection.

Baruch quoted as the word of "the prophet"

In Conference 7 (the first of Abbot Serenus's conferences, "On the Changeableness of the Soul and Evil Spirits"), describing the soul that, armed with spiritual weapons, will not be carried off captive into the enemy's country, Cassian quotes the deuterocanonical Baruch as prophecy:3

… nor hear the words of the prophet: Why are you grown old in a strange country?

"Why are you grown old in a strange country?" is Baruch 3:11, and Cassian attributes it to "the prophet." The Greek and Latin tradition carried Baruch as an appendix to Jeremiah and cited it under the prophet's name; Athenagoras and Irenaeus had likewise cited Baruch under "the prophets."

Sirach set beside the apostles among the remedies for sin

In Conference 20 (Abbot Pinufius "On the End of Penitence and the Marks of Satisfaction"), enumerating the many ways by which forgiveness of sins may be obtained, Cassian sets a line of Sirach in a chain of texts alongside the apostles and the Gospel:4

As water extinguishes fire, so does almsgiving extinguish sin.

The maxim is Sirach 3:33 (3:30 in some editions), and it stands in the same series as 1 Peter 4:8 ("charity covers a multitude of sins"), Matthew 6:14 (forgiving others to be forgiven), and James 5:20 (converting a sinner to cover a multitude of sins). The Sirach line carries no separate citation formula such as "it is written"; it stands in the series with the texts from Peter, Matthew, and James.

Reception and debate

  • Citation formulas. The Wisdom citation ("Scripture itself testifies") and the Baruch citation ("the words of the prophet") carry explicit attributions; the Sirach line carries none.
  • No list. Cassian compiles no catalogue of Old-Testament books; his witness is to citation, not to an enumeration.
  • A Western, monastic witness of the 420s. Cassian wrote for the monks of Marseilles a generation after Ambrose and in Augustine's lifetime. How citations of this kind bear on the early church's canon is set out on “How did the early church receive the deuterocanonical books?”
