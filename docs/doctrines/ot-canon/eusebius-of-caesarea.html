Eusebius of Caesarea on the Old Testament Canon

Dates: c. 320 (the Praeparatio Evangelica and Demonstratio Evangelica, c. 313–323; his Ecclesiastical History, which preserves Melito's and Origen's Old-Testament lists, reached its final edition c. 324; Eusebius was present at Nicaea in 325; c. 260 – c. 339) · Wikipedia
Summary: Eusebius, bishop of Caesarea and author of the Ecclesiastical History, is the channel through which two Hebrew-sized Old-Testament lists survive: he transcribes Melito of Sardis's catalogue and Origen's "twenty-two, as the Hebrews have handed them down." In his own apologetic works he quotes disputed books: he follows a quotation of the Wisdom of Solomon with "Thus the Scripture speaks," adduces the Greek Susanna as proof "from Scripture" (within an extract from Origen), and calls Baruch "inspired words." He draws up no Old-Testament canon-list of his own.

Claims & evidence

The transmitter of the Hebrew-sized lists

Eusebius is the sole source for the two earliest extant Christian lists of a Hebrew-sized Old Testament. In the Ecclesiastical History he quotes Melito of Sardis's catalogue verbatim (iv.26.13–14) — the earliest Christian Old-Testament list, in which Tobit, Judith, Sirach, Baruch, and the Maccabees are all absent — and Origen's enumeration of the books "as the Hebrews have handed them down… twenty-two, the number of the Hebrew letters" (vi.25.1–2).1 Neither list survives except in his transcription, and later Protestant appeals to the Fathers' Hebrew-sized count draw on it (⚠ unverified). He sets no list of his own beside them; his own citation practice is set out below.

The Wisdom of Solomon, labelled "the Scripture"

In the Praeparatio Evangelica, arguing that the Hebrew sages had glimpsed the doctrine of a divine Wisdom or second cause, Eusebius strings together passages of "Solomon" and then a long extract of the deuterocanonical Wisdom of Solomon (7:22–8:1), closing on Wisdom 8:1:

For she is a breath of the power of God, and a clear effluence of the glory of the Almighty… For she is an effulgence from everlasting light, and an unspotted mirror of the operation of God, and an image of His goodness… And she reacheth from end to end with full strength, and ordereth all things graciously.

Thus the Scripture speaks: but Philo the Hebrew, explaining the meaning of the doctrine more clearly, represents it in the manner following…

— Praeparatio Evangelica xi.14, trans. E. H. Gifford.2 The connective "Thus the Scripture speaks" (ἡ μὲν γραφή) applies the label the Scripture to the passage from Wisdom, and the next sentence turns to Philo, "explaining the meaning of the doctrine more clearly." The comment is Eusebius's own, not part of a transcribed extract.

Susanna adduced "from Scripture" for God's foreknowledge

Earlier in the same work, in the long discussion of fate, free will, and foreknowledge, the Greek addition to Daniel — the story of Susanna — is brought forward as scriptural proof that God knows all things before they happen:

But if it is necessary to prove this from Scripture also, the prophecies are full of examples of this kind, and so also is the description by Susanna of God as knowing all things before they come to pass, where she speaks as follows: "O God, the Eternal, the discerner of secrets, that knowest all things before they be, Thou understandest that these have borne false witness against me."

— Praeparatio Evangelica vi.11 (Susanna 42–43).3 The passage falls within a long extract from Origen (the chapter is headed "From Origen") that Eusebius incorporates into the Praeparatio: the use of Susanna here is Origen's, transmitted by Eusebius within his own argument, whereas the Wisdom and Baruch comments are in his own voice.

Baruch as "inspired words" prophesying the incarnation

In the Demonstratio Evangelica, gathering Old-Testament prophecies that God would one day appear and dwell among men, Eusebius devotes a chapter to the deuterocanonical Baruch (3:29–37), whose climax is the verse "Afterward did he show himself upon earth, and conversed with men" (Baruch 3:38). The chapter heading and his comment on it read:

From Baruch. It is prophesied that the God of the Prophets, having laid down the Complete Way of Knowledge by the Mosaic Law to the Jews, will some day afterwards be seen on earth, and mingle among men. [Passage quoted, Baruch iii. 29–37.] I need add nothing to these inspired words, which so clearly support my argument.

— Demonstratio Evangelica vi.19, trans. W. J. Ferrar (the translation abbreviates the quoted Baruch passage with the bracketed note).4 He calls Baruch's words inspired and reads the passage as a prophecy of the incarnation; Hippolytus had read Baruch 3 the same way a century earlier.

Reception and debate

  • Transmitter and user. Eusebius both transmits the Hebrew-sized lists and cites disputed books with scriptural formulas. Origen, whose list he preserves, distinguished between the books the Hebrews enumerate and the books the churches read (see the Origen witness page). He stands on the timeline beside Lactantius, c. 320, before Nicaea. How such lists and such usage relate is set out on How did the early church receive the deuterocanonical books: as Scripture, below the canon, or outside it?
  • No Old-Testament canon-list of his own. Unlike Athanasius or Cyril, Eusebius left no canon-catalogue of his own for the Old Testament — only the lists he transcribed from others. His witness on this timeline is to transcription and to citation in argument.
  • His New-Testament sorting. Eusebius's threefold ranking of the New Testament books — accepted, disputed, rejected (Ecclesiastical History 3.25) — is treated on the companion Biblical Canon timeline; it is not re-argued here.
