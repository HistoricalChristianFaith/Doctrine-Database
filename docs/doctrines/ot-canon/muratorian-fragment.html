The Muratorian Fragment on the Old Testament Canon

Dates: c. 170 AD (an eighty-five-line Latin canon list, mutilated at both ends, surviving in an eighth-century copy; traditionally placed at Rome in the later second century) · Wikipedia
Witness: The Muratorian Fragment is a list of New Testament books — on its usual later-second-century dating the oldest surviving one; A. C. Sundberg and Geoffrey Hahneman date it to the fourth century (see the New Testament canon page). In the middle of its New-Testament reckoning it counts the Wisdom of Solomon, a deuterocanonical Old-Testament book, among the received writings. It names no other disputed book.

Claims & evidence

The Wisdom of Solomon in the list of received books

The fragment runs through the books it receives — the four Gospels, Acts, the Pauline letters, Jude, two epistles of John — and in the same sentence names an Old-Testament wisdom book:

Moreover, the epistle of Jude and two of the above-mentioned (or, bearing the name of) John are counted (or, used) in the catholic [Church]; and [the book of] Wisdom, written by the friends of Solomon in his honour.

— lines 68–70, in Bruce Metzger's translation.1 The Wisdom of Solomon is one of the deuterocanonical books — part of the Greek Septuagint, and not in the later Hebrew canon. Here it is listed with the apostolic writings as "counted (or, used) in the catholic Church," without a qualifying remark of the kind the fragment attaches elsewhere to the Apocalypse of Peter and the Shepherd (see the New Testament canon page).

Wisdom alone, within the New Testament list

The fragment names Wisdom alone among the disputed books; it does not mention Tobit, Judith, Sirach, Baruch, or 1–2 Maccabees, and as a New-Testament list it does not set out to catalogue the Old. Wisdom appears among the New Testament books, not in any Old-Testament section.2 The clause "written by the friends of Solomon" is, on Bruce Metzger's note, a corrupt Latin, commonly emended to a Greek original reading the book "written by Philo" or "for the honour of Solomon."3

Reception and debate

  • Placement on the timeline. The fragment's main business is the New Testament — the four Gospels, the thirteen Pauline letters, the edges where the Apocalypse of Peter and the Shepherd of Hermas sit. Its full treatment, including the dating debate (second-century Rome, or the fourth-century redating argued by Sundberg and Hahneman), is on the companion New Testament canon page. It is repeated here only for the single line that bears on the Old-Testament question: the reception of the Wisdom of Solomon.
  • Set beside Melito. The other c. 170 witness on this timeline, Melito of Sardis, also names "Wisdom." Melito's "Wisdom" is read as a second title for Proverbs by McGiffert and F. F. Bruce, and as the Book of Wisdom by Gary Michuta (see the Melito page). The Muratorian's "Wisdom, written by the friends of Solomon in his honour" names the Wisdom of Solomon.
