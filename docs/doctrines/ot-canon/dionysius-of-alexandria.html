Dionysius of Alexandria on the Old Testament Canon

Dates: c. 264 AD (Dionysius, c. 190 – c. 264/265; pupil of Origen, head of the Alexandrian catechetical school, and bishop of Alexandria from c. 248, “Dionysius the Great” of the mid-third-century Eastern church — his treatises and letters survive only as fragments quoted by Eusebius and others) · Wikipedia
Summary: Origen’s own pupil and successor at Alexandria, writing a few years after his master’s death, cites two of the disputed books. In his treatise On Nature he introduces Sirach with the formula “listen to the divine oracles”; and in his doctrinal Epistle to Dionysius, Bishop of Rome he glosses Christ’s deity by reading the Wisdom of Solomon’s “breath of God’s power” alongside John 4:24. He draws up no canon-list. His teacher Origen gives a Hebrew-sized enumeration and also cites books read in the churches (see his page).

Claims & evidence

He cites Sirach (16:26–27) as “the divine oracles”

But listen to the divine oracles: “The works of the Lord are in judgment; from the beginning, and from His making of them, He disposed the parts thereof. He garnished His works for ever, and their principles unto their generations.”

— From the Books on Nature, §3 (against the Epicureans), in the extant fragments of Dionysius preserved by Eusebius, source.1 The quoted words are Sirach (Ecclesiasticus) 16:26–27. Arguing against the Epicurean doctrine that the ordered heavens arose from random atoms, Dionysius introduces Sirach’s account of the Lord ordering His works with “listen to the divine oracles” (ta theia logia). In the same treatise he weaves in the protocanonical Psalms (Ps 19:1; 33:5; 24:1) and the like; the Sirach verse stands among them.

He cites the Wisdom of Solomon (7:25) on the deity of Christ

Since, then, God is the Light, Christ is the Brightness. And since He is a Spirit — for says He, “God is a Spirit” — fittingly again is Christ called Breath; for “He,” saith He, “is the breath of God’s power.”

— Epistle to Dionysius, Bishop of Rome, From the First Book (§4), in the extant fragments preserved by Athanasius and Eusebius, source.2 The words “He is the breath of God’s power” are Wisdom of Solomon 7:25 (“she is the breath of the power of God”). Defending the eternity and consubstantiality of the Son against the charge of Sabellianism, Dionysius builds a chain of scriptural titles — Light of Light, Brightness, Breath — glossing John 4:24 (“God is a Spirit”) with the Wisdom text to ground the title “Breath” for Christ. “Saith He” introduces both the Johannine verse and the Wisdom text; no “it is written” formula is used.

Reception and debate

  • Origen’s pupil. Dionysius was taught by Origen and succeeded to his Alexandrian chair. Origen gives a Hebrew-sized enumeration and also cites the books read in the churches (see his page); Dionysius gives no enumeration.
  • Citation formulae. The Sirach citation is introduced with “listen to the divine oracles” (ta theia logia); Clement of Alexandria and Cyprian use “Scripture” formulae for these books (see their pages). The Wisdom citation is introduced with “saith He,” within a christological argument, beside John’s Gospel.
  • Survives only as fragments. Neither work is extant whole; both passages reach us as fragments quoted by later writers (Eusebius for On Nature, Athanasius and Eusebius for the Epistle to Dionysius of Rome), so the citations are given here by their section within the collected fragments rather than by a continuous text.
  • Use, not a canon-list. Like Irenaeus, Clement of Alexandria, Hippolytus, Tertullian, and Cyprian, Dionysius nowhere enumerates the Old-Testament books. His citations are among the evidence adduced in “How did the early church receive the deuterocanonical books: as Scripture, below the canon, or outside it?”; the readings of them are set out there.
