The New Testament on Simon Peter

Dates: c. 30–90 (the span of the New Testament writings) · Wikipedia
Summary: The earliest extant layer. It gives Peter's origin, family, name and place among the Twelve in detail; of his death it records Jesus' prophecy of it, and names neither the place nor the manner, which appear in later sources.

Claims & evidence

Origin: a fisherman of Bethsaida, brother of Andrew

Now Philip was of Bethsaida, the city of Andrew and Peter.

— Gospel of John, John 1:44. Simon and his brother Andrew are fishermen called from their nets by the Sea of Galilee, partners of James and John; the family works out of Capernaum, where Jesus stays in Peter's house.

The name: Simon renamed Cephas / Peter

And he brought him to Jesus. And when Jesus beheld him, he said, Thou art Simon the son of Jona: thou shalt be called Cephas, which is by interpretation, A stone.

— Gospel of John, John 1:42. “Cephas” is Aramaic kepha, “rock”; “Peter” renders it into Greek (Petros). On the confession at Caesarea Philippi Jesus builds the name into a saying:

And I say also unto thee, That thou art Peter, and upon this rock I will build my church; and the gates of hell shall not prevail against it.

— Gospel of Matthew, Matthew 16:18. (What the “rock” is, and what it implies for later claims of Roman primacy, is a separate question, treated under those doctrines, not here.)

Family: a married apostle

But Simon's wife's mother lay sick of a fever, and anon they tell him of her.

— Gospel of Mark, Mark 1:30. Paul names Cephas in a question about the apostles’ rights:

Have we not power to lead about a sister, a wife, as well as other apostles, and as the brethren of the Lord, and Cephas?

— 1 Corinthians, 1 Corinthians 9:5. The verse has been read of the apostles’ wives and of believing women who served them. Tertullian writes that the apostles “had a ‘licence’ to marry, and lead wives about” (On Exhortation to Chastity 8), and John Chrysostom’s text reads “a wife that is a believer” (Homily 21 on 1 Corinthians). Clement of Alexandria has the apostles take their wives with them “not as women with whom they had marriage relations, but as sisters, that they might be their fellow-ministers in dealing with housewives” (Stromata 3.6). Jerome (Commentary on Matthew, on 27:55), Ambrosiaster (Commentary on 1 Corinthians), Augustine (Of the Work of Monks 5) and Theophylact of Ohrid (Commentary on 1 Corinthians) read it of women who supplied the apostles from their means, as women had ministered to Jesus: in Ambrosiaster’s words, women “followed the apostles, supplying them both provisions and services, just as they also followed the Savior, ministering to him from their means.”

Peter in the Gospels and Acts, and the clash at Antioch

Peter is named first in every Gospel list of the Twelve, confesses Jesus as the Christ, preaches the first Christian sermon at Pentecost, and is the chief speaker in Acts 1–12. When he withdrew from eating with Gentile believers at Antioch, Paul reports opposing him publicly.

But when Peter was come to Antioch, I withstood him to the face, because he was to be blamed.

— Galatians, Galatians 2:11. (How to read this rebuke — a real fault, or a staged pretense — is itself an old dispute, recorded under Jerome and Augustine.)

His death: foretold, not narrated

Verily, verily, I say unto thee, When thou wast young, thou girdedst thyself, and walkedst whither thou wouldest: but when thou shalt be old, thou shalt stretch forth thy hands, and another shall gird thee, and carry thee whither thou wouldest not.

— Gospel of John, John 21:18–19, which the Evangelist glosses: “This spake he, signifying by what death he should glorify God.” Tertullian reads it of crucifixion: “Then is Peter girt by another, when he is made fast to the cross” (Scorpiace 15). The New Testament names neither the place nor the manner of Peter's death.

“Babylon”

The church that is at Babylon, elected together with you, saluteth you; and so doth Marcus my son.

— 1 Peter, 1 Peter 5:13. Eusebius reports that “they say” Peter wrote the letter at Rome, “when he calls the city, by a figure, Babylon,” and links the “Marcus” of the greeting with the tradition that Mark set down Peter's preaching at Rome (Ecclesiastical History 2.15); J. P. Kirsch (1911) adds that Revelation also calls Rome Babylon (“St. Peter, Prince of the Apostles”). Otto Zwierlein reads “Babylon” as a metaphor for exile and places the letter's greeting, in its fiction, in Jerusalem (“Has St. Peter ever been in Rome?”, p. 2). See Did Peter lead and die at Rome?
