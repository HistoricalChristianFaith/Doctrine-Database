The Acts of Peter on Simon Peter

Dates: c. 190 (the martyrdom material; the fuller Greek Acts of Peter and Paul is a later recension) · Wikipedia
Summary: A second-century apocryphal narrative of Peter. It is the earliest extant source for two scenes of his death — the Quo vadis and the head-downward crucifixion — which the later Greek Acts of Peter and Paul also tells.

Claims & evidence

“Lord, whither art Thou going?” (the Quo vadis)

A few days ago, being exhorted by the brethren, I was going away; and my Lord Jesus Christ met me, and having adored Him, I said, Lord, whither art Thou going? And He said to me, I am going to Rome to be crucified. And I said to Him, Lord, wast Thou not crucified once for all? And the Lord answering, said, I saw thee fleeing from death, and I wish to be crucified instead of thee.

— Acts of Peter and Paul 42, source. Fleeing Rome to escape martyrdom, Peter meets Christ on the road; shamed by the vision, he turns back to die. The scene gives the Domine, quo vadis? tradition its name; the church of Santa Maria in Palmis, or Domine Quo Vadis, on the Appian Way about 800 m from Porta San Sebastiano, stands where the tradition places the meeting (Santa Maria in Palmis).

Crucified head-downward, “not worthy… like my Lord”

Since my Lord Jesus Christ, who came down from the heaven upon the earth, was raised upon the cross upright, and He has deigned to call to heaven me, who am of the earth, my cross ought to be fixed head down most, so as to direct my feet towards heaven; for I am not worthy to be crucified like my Lord. Then, having reversed the cross, they nailed his feet up.

— Acts of Peter and Paul 41, source. This later Greek recension gives the reason that Peter asked to be inverted so as not to die as his Lord died. In the second-century Acts of Peter (the “Vercelli Acts,” in M. R. James's translation) Peter asks the executioners “to crucify me head-downwards in this way and no other”; Sean McDowell notes that the speech there makes the reversed cross a cosmic symbol — Adam fell head-downwards and inverted the world — and that “the text does not say this outright” that the request shows humility. How the scene has been read as history is set out at Was Peter crucified head-downward? (McDowell, “Was Peter Crucified Upside Down?”.)

Reception and debate

  • Standing of the Acts. Jerome lists the Acts of Peter among the books “rejected as apocryphal” (On Illustrious Men 1). On the apocryphal Acts as a class of source, see the apostles hub.
  • The head-downward posture. A. C. McGiffert (1890) judges the tradition, which he traces in writing to Origen, “exceedingly doubtful” (note on Church History 3.1); McDowell leaves open whether Origen drew on the Acts or on an independent tradition. See Was Peter crucified head-downward?
