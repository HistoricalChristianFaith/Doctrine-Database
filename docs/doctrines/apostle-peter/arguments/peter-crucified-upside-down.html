Was Peter crucified head-downward?

Disputed reading · on Simon Peter · witness: Origen

Related readings: Did Peter lead and die at Rome?

The passage

… my cross ought to be fixed head down most, so as to direct my feet towards heaven; for I am not worthy to be crucified like my Lord. Then, having reversed the cross, they nailed his feet up.

— Acts of Peter and Paul 41, source. The earliest named writer to report the posture is Origen (c. 230), quoted by Eusebius: Peter, “at last, having come to Rome, … was crucified head-downwards; for he had requested that he might suffer in this way.”1

The question

Tertullian (c. 200) is the earliest extant writer to say that Peter died by crucifixion, “a passion like his Lord’s,” without naming a posture. The head-downward posture appears in the second-century Acts of Peter and in Origen; the reason that Peter judged himself unworthy to die as his Lord died appears in the later Greek Acts of Peter and Paul and in Jerome (393). What is disputed is whether the posture, and Peter’s request for it, preserve a memory of his death. For the first reading below, a modern academic statement could not be located; it is given from the ancient witnesses and from the lives of the saints read in the Orthodox and Coptic Orthodox churches.2

Crucified head-downward at his own request, as unworthy to die as his Lord died

In this reading Peter was crucified at Rome with his head toward the ground because he asked for it, counting himself unworthy of the same death as Christ.

Held by: Origen (Commentary on Genesis 3, c. 230); Jerome (On Illustrious Men 1, 393); the Orthodox Church in America (lives of the saints); the Coptic Synaxarium, as read by Peter Farrington (Coptic Orthodox priest, of the Oriental Orthodox communion, 2018).3

Jesus’ prophecy that in old age Peter would “stretch forth thy hands, and another shall gird thee,” which the Evangelist glosses as “signifying by what death he should glorify God,” was read of crucifixion by Tertullian: “Then is Peter girt by another, when he is made fast to the cross.” Origen, the earliest named writer to describe the manner, gives both the posture and the request in one sentence, at the close of his account of Peter’s mission to the dispersion.4 Jerome (393) states the reason: Peter received martyrdom “being nailed to the cross with his head towards the ground and his feet raised on high, asserting that he was unworthy to be crucified in the same manner as his Lord.” The Greek Acts of Peter and Paul gives the same reason in Peter’s own words, after the Quo vadis meeting in which Peter, leaving Rome, meets Christ “going to Rome to be crucified” and turns back.5 The account stands in the lives of the saints of the Orthodox Church in America: “Saint Peter was put to death in Rome during the reign of Nero. According to Tradition, he asked to be crucified upside down, since he did not feel worthy to die in the same manner as his Lord.” It stands also in the Coptic Synaxarium, which Farrington says is read “on the occasion of their joint feast”: “Peter asked them to crucify him head downwards, and he delivered up his soul into the hand of the Lord.”6 In this reading the posture and the request travel together from Origen’s first notice through Jerome to the lives of the saints read in the Orthodox churches, as one remembered account of how the apostle chose to die.

A legendary detail first carried by the apocryphal Acts

In McGiffert’s view Peter’s crucifixion is well attested, but the head-downward posture is not attested before the Acts of Peter and Origen, and belongs to the tradition that grew up around the apocryphal Acts.

Held by: Arthur Cushman McGiffert (translator and annotator of Eusebius, NPNF2 vol. 1, 1890).7

McGiffert separates the manner of death from its posture. On the crucifixion he follows Tertullian, who “mentions it as a fact already known, and tradition since his time is so unanimous in regard to it that we may consider it in the highest degree probable.” On the posture he writes: “Origen is the first to record that Peter was crucified with his head downward, but the tradition afterward became quite common. It is of course not impossible, but the absence of any reference to it by earlier Fathers (even by Tertullian, who mentions the crucifixion), and its decidedly legendary character, render it exceedingly doubtful.”8 The scene’s earliest surviving setting is the Acts of Peter, a second-century narrative of Peter’s contest with Simon Magus at Rome, his Quo vadis encounter and his martyrdom. Tertullian, who records the cross and reads John 21 of it, names no inversion. In this reading the earliest stratum is Peter’s death on a cross at Rome, and the inverted cross with its request belongs to the narrative tradition that the Acts began and Origen, Jerome and the later lives carried on.9

A possible memory of the posture, with its meaning shaped in the telling

In this reading the inverted cross may reflect how Peter was in fact executed, while the request and the meaning given to it come from the narratives that tell it.

Held by: Sean McDowell (Biola University, The Fate of the Apostles, 2015) (grants the posture is possible; holds the evidence inconclusive).10

Roman executioners varied the posture of the crucified. Seneca describes some who “turn their victims head towards the ground,” and Josephus reports that during the siege of Jerusalem the soldiers nailed their captives “in various postures … by way of jest.” McDowell concludes that an inverted crucifixion is “not intrinsically implausible.”11 In the Acts of Peter, he notes, Peter asks the executioners “to crucify me head-downwards in this way and no other,” and the speech that follows makes the posture a sign of creation restored: Adam, “the first man,” fell head-downwards and turned the world upside down. “Many often assume that Peter’s request shows humility,” McDowell writes, “But the text does not say this outright”; Jonathan Z. Smith read the inversion as “an act of cosmic audacity.” Origen, writing about 230, gives none of the speech, and McDowell leaves open “whether Origen derived this from an independent tradition or from the Acts of Peter.” He doubts that executioners took a condemned man’s request (“Probably not”) and holds that the tradition may preserve “an actual memory of his fate” while the evidence remains inconclusive.12 Read so, an execution in one of the postures Roman soldiers used could have been remembered at Rome, then given its theological meaning in the Acts and its humble motive in the later Greek recension and in Jerome.
