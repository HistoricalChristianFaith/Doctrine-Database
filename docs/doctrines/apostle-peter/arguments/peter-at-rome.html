Did Peter lead and die at Rome?

Disputed reading · on Simon Peter · witness: Eusebius of Caesarea

Related readings: Was Peter crucified head-downward?

The passage

It is, therefore, recorded that Paul was beheaded in Rome itself, and that Peter likewise was crucified under Nero. This account of Peter and Paul is substantiated by the fact that their names are preserved in the cemeteries of that place even to the present day.

— Eusebius, Ecclesiastical History 2.25 (c. 325).1

The question

The New Testament does not narrate Peter at Rome. Clement of Rome, writing from Rome (c. 96), records Peter’s martyrdom without naming the place; A. C. McGiffert (1890) and J. P. Kirsch (1911) read him as assuming Rome, and Otto Zwierlein (2009) does not. Dionysius of Corinth (c. 170) has Peter and Paul teach and die together in Italy, and Irenaeus (c. 180) names the two as founders of its church. What is disputed is whether Peter was ever at Rome, and, if he was, whether he founded the church there and whether he held its episcopate. The historical question is distinct from the bearing the communions give it on the primacy of the Roman see, which is recorded on that page.2 Peter’s presence and martyrdom at Rome, which every reading below except the third affirms, are held without taking up his office there by Markus Bockmuehl (2012), for whom they follow “from the cumulative weight of a complex of literary, archaeological, liturgical and traditional sources,” and by Sean McDowell, who thinks Peter “probably made it to Rome.”3 For the last reading below, no twentieth- or twenty-first-century academic statement could be opened.

Peter and Paul founded the Roman church as apostles and committed its episcopate to Linus

In this reading Peter and Paul preached, founded the church and died at Rome as apostles, and the first bishop of Rome was Linus.

Held by: Irenaeus (Against Heresies 3.3.3, c. 180); Nicholas Afanassieff (Orthodox theologian, “The Church Which Presides in Love”, English text 1992); Thomas Hopko (Orthodox priest, “Roman Presidency and Christian Unity in Our Time”, 2005, read in 2014); Peter Farrington (Coptic Orthodox priest, of the Oriental Orthodox communion, 2018).4

Irenaeus calls Rome the church “founded and organized at Rome by the two most glorious apostles, Peter and Paul,” and goes on: “The blessed apostles, then, having founded and built up the Church, committed into the hands of Linus the office of the episcopate”; after Anacletus, “in the third place from the apostles, Clement was allotted the bishopric.” The earlier witnesses join the two apostles at Rome. Ignatius (c. 107) writes to the Romans, “I do not, as Peter and Paul, issue commandments unto you. They were apostles; I am but a condemned man”; Dionysius of Corinth says the two “taught together in like manner in Italy, and suffered martyrdom at the same time”; and Afanassieff notes that 1 Clement “mentions the Apostles Peter and Paul and their death under Nero’s persecution.”5 The apostles’ office, in this reading, was of another kind than a local bishop’s. Afanassieff writes of Paul that “He was like the other apostles in never being bishop of a church founded by himself.” Hopko calls Linus “technically Rome’s first bishop, since the apostles were not overseers, that is, bishops, of local churches”: their “unique and universal apostolic ministry” was “to be foundation-stones of God’s household.” The Roman church held its “place of honor” and its “presidency in love” because “It was founded on the teaching and blood of the foremost Christian apostles, Peter and Paul, and it was the church of the capital city of the Roman Empire”; and “Linus and the bishops of Rome who followed him … were successors of the apostles together with all orthodox bishops in catholic churches.” Farrington writes that the early church “had no doubt that St Peter had preached in Rome, established the Church there on an Apostolic basis with St Paul, and was martyred there.”6 Read so, the Roman church holds its place as the church of the two apostles’ preaching and martyrdom, and its bishops, from Linus on, succeed the apostles together with the bishops of every church.

Peter founded the Roman see and held its chair, which passes to his successors

In this reading Peter founded the Roman church and held its chair until his martyrdom (Jerome and Kirsch add that he laboured there long), and the bishops of Rome succeed to that chair.

Held by: Jerome (On Illustrious Men 1, 393); the First Vatican Council (Pastor aeternus ch. 2, 1870); Johann Peter Kirsch (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1911); Benedict XVI (General Audience, 22 February 2006).7

Jerome has Peter push on “to Rome in the second year of Claudius to overthrow Simon Magus,” and hold “the sacerdotal chair there for twenty-five years until the last, that is the fourteenth, year of Nero.” The First Vatican Council teaches of Peter that “to this day and for ever he lives and presides and exercises judgment in his successors the bishops of the holy Roman see, which he founded and consecrated with his blood.”8 For Kirsch, “The essential fact is that Peter died at Rome: this constitutes the historical foundation of the claim of the Bishops of Rome to the Apostolic Primacy of Peter.” He holds Peter’s “residence and death in Rome” to be “established beyond contention as historical facts by a series of distinct testimonies extending from the end of the first to the end of the second centuries, and issuing from several lands”: Clement, whose “among us” he reads as “among the Romans”; Ignatius, whose words in his reading mean “that the two Apostles laboured personally in Rome”; Dionysius; Irenaeus; and Gaius’s “trophies” on the Vatican and the Ostian way.9 He reads the “Babylon” of 1 Peter 5:13 as Rome, “the only city which is called Babylon elsewhere in ancient Christian literature,” and the gloss on John 21:19 as presupposing that readers knew how Peter died.10 From the Roman origin of Mark’s Gospel he concludes “that Peter laboured for a long period in Rome,” a conclusion “confirmed by the unanimous voice of tradition which, as early as the second half of the second century, designates the Prince of the Apostles the founder of the Roman Church.” The excavations of 1939–49 beneath St Peter’s found a shrine of about 160, known as the “Trophy of Gaius”; Margherita Guarducci read a graffito there as naming Peter, and Paul VI announced in 1968 that the relics had been identified.11 Benedict XVI, on the feast of the Chair of Peter in 2006, taught that “The See of Rome, after St Peter’s travels, thus came to be recognized as the See of the Successor of Peter.”12 Read so, Peter’s founding of the Roman church and his death there made it his see, and those who follow him as its bishops hold his chair.

Peter never came to Rome

In this reading Peter’s work lay in Palestine and the East, and the tradition of his Roman stay and death took shape in the second half of the second century.

Held by: Ulrich Velenus (1520, ⚠ unverified); Otto Zwierlein (classicist, Petrus in Rom, 2009, and Petrus und Paulus in Jerusalem und Rom, 2013); Pieter W. van der Horst (review, 2010).13

Zwierlein starts from the New Testament: “Rome is not mentioned in connection with Peter in the New Testament anywhere.” Paul’s letter to the Romans and the end of Acts know nothing of Peter in the city, and in his reading Romans 15:20 and 2 Corinthians 10:16 exclude such a stay. Under the division of Galatians 2:7 Peter took the mission to the circumcised; his last appearance is a visit to Antioch, and “around the year 50 or 54 at the latest (cf. 1Cor 9,5) we lose track of the apostle Peter in the Bible.” He holds that John 21 is attested only from Irenaeus and does not name Rome, and that the “Babylon” of 1 Peter 5:13 is “not a geographic, but an ontological metaphor” for Christian exile, the greeting being sent in the letter’s fiction from Jerusalem.14 He dates 1 Clement to about 120–125 and finds that none of the passages datable to the mid-second century “presupposes any knowledge of Peter’s ever having been in Rome”; in van der Horst’s summary, Clement knows of Peter only what he could take from Acts. Justin Martyr, writing at Rome about 150–154, tells of Simon Magus in the city but not of Peter; the statue-base Justin read as honouring Simon, Zwierlein notes, reads SEMONI SANCO DEO FIDIO, a dedication to the god Semo Sancus. Zwierlein places the rise of the Roman tradition between about 155 and 174, from Justin to Dionysius of Corinth.15 For the archaeology he cites Harald Mielsch: “The archaeological evidence for St. Peter’s grave and its veneration begins at the earliest around 160–180 AD, starting with the erection of the memorial.” Van der Horst writes that “Zwierlein presents a strong case and his conclusions have a great historical plausibility.”16 In this reading the Roman Peter took shape in the later second century, in the story of his contest with Simon Magus told in the apocryphal Acts and at the Vatican memorial of about 160–180.

Peter died at Rome after at most a short stay, without founding or governing its church

In this reading Peter was martyred at Rome, but he arrived late, after the Roman church already existed, and he was not its bishop.

Held by: John Calvin (Reformed, Institutes IV.6.14–15, 1559 ed.); Philip Schaff (Protestant church historian, 1819–1893, History of the Christian Church, vol. I, §25); A. C. McGiffert (Protestant church historian, notes to Eusebius, 1890).17

Calvin works from the New Testament chronology. Galatians places Peter “at Jerusalem about twenty years after the death of Christ, and afterwards” at Antioch, which leaves at most seventeen years before the end of Nero, to be divided between Antioch and Rome. Romans greets many believers at Rome and does not name Peter, and Paul’s letters from Rome are silent about him; of Paul’s first defence, when “all men forsook” him, Calvin asks, “Where then was Peter?” His conclusion: “from this agreement of authors, I do not dispute that he died there, but that he was bishop, particularly for a long period, I cannot believe.”18 Schaff holds that Peter’s presence at Rome before 63 is “extremely doubtful, if not impossible, by the silence of Luke and Paul,” that his presence after 63 “can neither be proved nor disproved from the New Testament,” and that “It is the uniform tradition of the eastern and western churches that Peter preached the gospel in Rome, and suffered martyrdom there in the Neronian persecution.” The testimonies “can only be accounted for on the supposition of some fact at the bottom”; the silence of Acts and Paul’s letters “allows him only a short period of labor there, after 63,” and Schaff calls the twenty- or twenty-five-year episcopate “a colossal chronological mistake.” The founding he assigns to others: “strangers from Rome” who heard Peter at Pentecost, and disciples scattered after Stephen’s death, carried the gospel to Rome, and “these converts of Peter became the real founders of the Jewish-Christian congregation in the metropolis.”19 McGiffert accepts “as certain that he did visit Rome, and that he met his death there,” and holds it “no less certain that he did not reach there until late in the reign of Nero”; he traces the twenty-five-year tradition to Justin’s report that Simon Magus came to Rome under Claudius, with whose visit later tradition joined Peter’s.20 Read so, Peter came late in life to a church already founded at Rome, and died there.
