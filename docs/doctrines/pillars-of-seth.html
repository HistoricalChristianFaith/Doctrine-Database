The Pillars of Seth

In its earliest extant form, written down by the Jewish historian Josephus (c. AD 93–94), the tradition holds that the righteous descendants of Seth were the inventors of astronomy — "that peculiar sort of wisdom which is concerned with the heavenly bodies, and their order" — and, warned by Adam's prophecy that the world would be destroyed once by fire and once by water, they inscribed their discoveries on two pillars, one of brick and one of stone, so that whichever catastrophe came, the knowledge would survive. The stone pillar, Josephus adds, still stood "in the land of Siriad" in his own day. This page follows that legend of antediluvian wisdom preserved through the Flood — its earliest attestations, and what later readers made of it.

The story credits the origin of celestial science to human discovery by the line of Seth. Another ancient stream — the Watcher tradition of 1 Enoch 8, taken up by Tatian and Tertullian — ascribed the same science to the rebel angels who "taught" it to enslave mankind; this tradition credits it instead to the holy line of Seth, discovering it by their own virtue.1 The same knowledge thus has two origins in the ancient sources, impious revelation and righteous discovery, and this legend carries the second. It also touches two further topics: it is a knowledge-survives-the-deluge story, tied to Noah's Flood; and it belongs to the wider lore of the antediluvian sages — with Enoch the seventh from Adam — that runs through the sons of God and the Nephilim.2 The dignifying of Seth's posterity as the righteous race is attested before the legend: a generation before Josephus, Philo of Alexandria (early 1st c.) made Seth the "author" of the race of "those who love virtue and piety" — the virtuous line "enlarged" down through Noah, Abraham, and Moses. Philo's Seth is moral allegory, with no two pillars, no astronomy, and no antediluvian discoveries carried through the Flood; the earliest extant form of the knowledge-preserving legend is Josephus's.3

Timeline

Listed earliest first. Josephus is the earliest datable witness to the legend in the form the later tradition received. From him it passed not through the doctrinal Fathers — who preserve no pious Sethite-pillars form of it, though the underlying motif of antediluvian knowledge surviving the Flood on inscribed stone does surface among them in its impious form (John Cassian's Ham carrying the forbidden arts past the Flood on plates of metal and hard rock, c. 435) and in the Watchers-as-originators strand of Christian chronography (Panodorus via Syncellus; see note 1) — but through the chronicles and Bible-histories, along two branches. On the Greek/Byzantine side the pious Sethite form was kept: George Syncellus's Chronography (c. 810), which preserves the Egyptian Seiriadic columns of Hermes and, with other Christian chronographers, the Babylonian Sippar tablets, and George Kedrenos's Synopsis Historion (c. 1050), which credits Seth himself with the planets, the two pillars, and the invention of writing. In the Latin West — Isidore of Seville's Chronica (c. 620), Peter Comestor's Historia Scholastica (c. 1170), and Ranulf Higden's Polychronicon (c. 1350) — the legend changed hands: Josephus's righteous line of Seth became, with Comestor, the Cainite musician Jubal, so that what the medieval West often received under the story's name were the pillars of Cain's line. Kedrenos's Sethite pillars and Comestor's Cainite ones are a little over a century apart.

Josephus (c. 100)

Josephus gives the earliest extant form of the legend in the Antiquities of the Jews (I.2.3). Seth's posterity, virtuous men, "were the inventors of that peculiar sort of wisdom which is concerned with the heavenly bodies, and their order"; and foreseeing on "Adam's prediction that the world was to be destroyed at one time by the force of fire, and at another time by the violence and quantity of water, they made two pillars, the one of brick, the other of stone," inscribing their discoveries on both so that one would outlast whichever destruction came — the stone pillar, he reports, still "remains in the land of Siriad to this day."4

Josephus also records, later in the same book, the other account of the antediluvian age — the "many angels of God" who consorted with women and begat unjust giants (I.3.1) — so the Sethite pillars and the angels of the Watcher tradition stand side by side in his own text (his angelic passage is treated on the Nephilim timeline).5 Where the pillar "in the land of Siriad" was, and what it was, is disputed; see What is the pillar "in the land of Siriad" that Josephus says still stood in his day?

Isidore of Seville (c. 620)

Isidore carries the legend into the Latin chronicle tradition. His universal chronicle, the Chronica, reports "as Josephus reports, some men, who knew that they were about to die by either fire or water, inscribed their discoveries on two columns made of brick and stone," the stone columns said to "remain in Syria to this very day." Isidore tells it generically, of "some men," and lodges it one entry below his notice of the Cainite Jubal, "from the line of Cain," inventor of music before the flood. (The legend is not in his better-known Etymologies, which carries only the Cainite-music note.)6

George Syncellus (c. 810)

George Syncellus, a monk of Constantinople, carries the legend's comparative material into the Greek/Byzantine chronicle tradition. His Chronography alone preserves the Egyptian fragment of the (pseudo-Manethonian) Book of Sothis, whose sacred antediluvian knowledge was inscribed "upon the columns set up in the Seriadic land, by Thoth, the first Hermes," and re-transcribed after the deluge — the same region-name as Josephus's "land of Siriad," and the same before-and-after-the-Flood logic; and with the other Christian chronographers he transmits Berossus's Babylonian account of the antediluvian writings buried at Sippar before the flood and dug up after. M. R. James notes that the passage on Seth he quotes from George Kedrenos — Seth naming the planets and raising the two pillars — is found "both in Syncellus and Cedrenus." On the Nephilim timeline Syncellus also transcribes the Watchers who "taught" astronomy, so his chronicle holds both ancient accounts of the origin of celestial knowledge.7

George Kedrenos (c. 1050)

George Kedrenos (Latin Cedrenus) gives the legend its fullest surviving Greek form, and gives it to Seth himself. In the primeval-history opening of his Synopsis Historion, "Seth gave names to the seven planets, and comprehended the lore of the movements of the heavens. He also prepared two pillars, one of stone and one of brick, and wrote these things upon them… He also devised the Hebrew letters." Where Josephus credits Seth's posterity collectively and Isidore tells it of "some men," Kedrenos names Seth as discoverer, planet-namer, pillar-builder, and inventor of the alphabet. He writes about a century before the Latin Comestor, whose Historia Scholastica assigns the same pillars to the Cainite Jubal.8

Peter Comestor (c. 1170)

Peter Comestor's Historia Scholastica — the "school Bible" of the medieval West, surviving in some 800 manuscripts and a principal channel for Josephus's material — retells the pillars and cites Josephus by name, but ascribes them not to Seth's line but to the Cainite musician Jubal. "He wrote it on two columns, the whole of it on each, as Josephus says — one of marble, the other of brick — of which the one would not be washed away by the flood, the other not dissolved by fire." With Comestor the pillars of Seth become, in the West, the pillars of Cain's line.9

Ranulf Higden (c. 1350)

Ranulf Higden's Polychronicon — Englished by John Trevisa in 1387 — carries both forms. Its main text keeps the plain Josephan version, "men" who foresaw fire and flood and "closede hem in tweie greet pileres i-made of marbyl and of brend tyle"; but in the chapter on Jubal several manuscripts splice in Comestor's Cainite retelling verbatim. The one chronicle thus documents the legend's two streams — the older generic/Sethite and the medieval Latin Cainite. The Cooke Manuscript of the Masonic "Old Charges" (c. 1410–1450) names the Polychronicon as a source for its own account of the two pillars.10

  • Where does the fire-and-water double destruction in the pillars story come from? — cosmic cycle, prophecy to the first family, judgment on wickedness, or inherited tradition.
  • Is the pillars-of-Seth legend a recasting of an Enoch tradition? — on the Enochic roles Josephus gives the Sethites.
