The Old Testament Canon (Is the Deuterocanon Scripture?)

Whether Tobit — with Judith, Wisdom, Sirach (Ecclesiasticus), Baruch, and 1–2 Maccabees, the books Catholics call the deuterocanon, Orthodox writers the anagignoskomena (“books read”), and Protestants the Apocrypha — belongs to the Christian Old Testament has been answered differently across the centuries. The Greek Septuagint that the early church read included these books; several Fathers (Melito, Origen, Cyril, Jerome) gave Hebrew-sized lists, some ranking the other books below them; how those lists relate to the books the same writers cited is disputed; the African councils, Innocent I, Florence, Trent, and the Orthodox Synod of Jerusalem listed them among the Scriptures; the Reformed confessions placed them outside the canon. This page records those witnesses, earliest extant attestation first, each footnoted to its source; how particular texts and witnesses have been read is set out under Disputed readings.

The question touches neighbouring topics. Because the disputed books reached the church through the Greek Bible, it bears on the standing of that translation — the Origin and Inspiration of the Septuagint — and on the Septuagint-versus-Hebrew question gathered on the Rabbinic Corruption of Scripture page. (The companion Biblical Canon timeline treats the parallel question for the New Testament.) Two of the disputed books carry texts adduced in Reformation-era doctrinal controversy: Tobit's “almsgiving saves from death” (Tobit 12:9) and 2 Maccabees' prayer for the dead (2 Macc 12:43–45). A third, Sirach 46:20, which reports that after his death Samuel “prophesied, and shewed the king his end,” is adduced on a neighbouring timeline in the discussion of the apparition at Endor; readers who receive Sirach as Scripture and readers who do not give that text different weight.

Timeline

The Septuagint-to-Reformation timeline is in progress; it runs from the LXX (c. 132 BC) toward the councils and confessions and the Synod of Jerusalem (1672). It opens with a framing note on the test case — Tobit — before the witnesses proper.

The test case: dating Tobit (composed c. 200 BC)

The dispute turns on a handful of books, and Tobit is the convenient bellwether, named on nearly every list below. Its own story is set in the eighth century BC: Tobit is a pious Israelite carried off to Nineveh in the Assyrian deportation that followed the fall of the northern kingdom (c. 722 BC).1 But scholars date the book itself far later — to roughly 200 BC (a range of about 225–175 BC), on the absence of any trace of the Maccabean persecution and on geographical slips that betray an author writing centuries after the events he narrates.2 Critical scholarship posits a comparable gap between narrative setting and date of writing for Daniel, whose sixth-century-BC court tales it dates in their final form to the Hellenistic age (c. 165 BC).3

The Septuagint (c. 132 BC onward)

The timeline opens with the Greek Old Testament, the Septuagint. Its extant complete Christian copies, the three great fourth- and fifth-century codices (Vaticanus, Sinaiticus, and Alexandrinus), carry Tobit, with others of the disputed books, among the Old Testament books, interleaved with the protocanonical ones rather than set apart.4 This Greek Bible was "the most frequently used version of the Old Testament in the early Church,"5 and the New Testament's own scriptural citations follow it overwhelmingly — Archer and Chirichigno count 340 quotations agreeing with the Septuagint against only 33 with the (later-named) Masoretic Hebrew.6 Whether the Greek Scriptures of the first century already carried these books is disputed; see the interpretation page. (How that Greek Bible came to be reckoned authoritative is the subject of the companion Origin and Inspiration of the Septuagint timeline; it is not re-argued here.)

The "Council of Jamnia" (c. 90 AD, Judaism): a reported rabbinic council

An older account dated the closing of the Jewish canon to a Council of Jamnia: Heinrich Graetz proposed in 1871 that a rabbinic council sat at Yavneh about 70–90 AD and there closed the Hebrew canon, shutting out the Greek extras in reaction to the rise of Christianity.7 Jack P. Lewis, in "What Do We Mean by Jabneh?" (1964), concluded that "canonicity was not the issue and the debate was not connected with Jabneh… No book is ever mentioned in the sources as being excluded from the canon at Jabneh," and F. F. Bruce judged it "probably unwise to talk as if there were a Council or Synod of Jamnia."8 On the date at which the Hebrew canon was fixed, a survey of the question reports that "there is no scholarly consensus as to when the canon of the Hebrew Bible… was fixed," with proposals ranging from the Hasmonean period (c. 140–40 BC) to the second century AD and later.9 The rabbinic canon does not include Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch, or 1–2 Maccabees.10 (The broader Septuagint-versus-Hebrew quarrel is gathered on the Rabbinic Corruption of Scripture page and not re-argued here.)

Josephus (c. AD 93, Judaism): a twenty-two-book count of the Jewish Scriptures

The single most-cited Jewish witness for a settled Hebrew canon writes within a few years of the supposed “Council of Jamnia.” Defending the antiquity of his nation against the grammarian Apion, the historian Josephus (c. AD 37 – c. 100) states that the Jews hold not “an innumerable multitude of books… disagreeing from and contradicting one another,” but “only twenty-two books, which contain the records of all the past times” and “are justly believed to be divine” — five of Moses, thirteen of “the prophets, who were after Moses,” and four of “hymns to God, and precepts for the conduct of human life.”11 The reign of Artaxerxes (mid-fifth century BC) is the cutoff: the prophetic record runs “from the death of Moses till the reign of Artaxerxes,” and everything written “since Artaxerxes” he reckons has “not been esteemed of the like authority with the former… because there hath not been an exact succession of prophets since that time” — the very window in which the Greek-only books fall.12 So fixed is this body of writings, he adds, that “no one has been so bold as either to add any thing to them, to take any thing from them, or to make any change in them,” and Jews would “willingly… die for them.”13 That a Greek-writing Jew who himself drew on the Septuagint should bound the inspired books at Artaxerxes is adduced for a closed Hebrew canon already fixed in the first century; how the count is read is set out on its own page under Disputed readings. Two features of the passage bear on that reading: his “twenty-two” is the Greek-and-patristic count, not the rabbinic “twenty-four” of the Talmud (usually explained as the same books folded differently — Ruth into Judges, Lamentations into Jeremiah — to match the twenty-two letters of the Hebrew alphabet), and he names no titles, so which books fill his thirteen and four is inferred, not stated — “it is impossible to know exactly which books he included, or how he counted them.”14

Clement of Rome (c. 96 AD): cites Wisdom and Judith; no list

Before any Christian canon-list exists, the disputed books are already at work in the church's own argument. Writing from Rome to quell a schism at Corinth, Clement — whose letter, 1 Clement, is among the earliest post-apostolic writings, usually dated to about 95–96 AD15 — weaves a line of the deuterocanonical Wisdom of Solomon into his appeal in the same breath as the Gospel and the Psalms: "Who shall say unto Him, What hast thou done? or, Who shall resist the power of His strength?" — the words of Wisdom 12:12 and 11:21, set beside Matthew 24:35 and Psalm 19 with no mark of distinction.16 Later he holds up "the blessed Judith," who, "exposing herself to danger," went out to the enemy camp for her besieged people, paired with Esther as a pattern of faith.17 The two citations differ in force — Wisdom is woven in as authority (though with no "it is written" formula), Judith adduced as a historical example — and together they are the earliest extant Christian use of the disputed books, generations before Melito’s list, the earliest extant Christian Old-Testament list. The letter enumerates no books; it uses them, and so bears on the question, discussed under Disputed readings below, of whether the primitive church reckoned these books Scripture.

The Didache (c. 100 AD): a line of Sirach in its moral teaching; no list

Between Clement and the canon-lists stands an anonymous document witness: the Didache, or Teaching of the Twelve Apostles, the primitive church-order manual usually dated near AD 100 (estimates run c. 50–150).18 In its catalogue of moral duties it embeds a line of the deuterocanonical Sirach (Ecclesiasticus): "Be not a stretcher forth of the hands to receive and a drawer of them back to give" — the words of Sirach 4:31 — set in the same chain of commandments as a paraphrase of the apostolic charge to raise one's children "in the fear of God" (cf. Ephesians 6:4), with no mark of distinction.19 Like Clement and Polycarp, it renders no canon-list and prefaces the maxim with no "it is written"; the Sirach line is woven in as authoritative counsel, a third early datapoint of practical use before Melito’s list. The moral section is a Christian recension of the Jewish "Two Ways" tradition (shared with the Epistle of Barnabas), and the Sirach verse stands inside that inherited block — so the echo may travel as common ethical tradition rather than as a deliberate citation; how such uses are read is set out under Disputed readings below.

4 Ezra (2 Esdras 3–14) (c. 100 AD, Judaism): twenty-four books made public, seventy kept for the wise

An independent Jewish near-contemporary of Josephus gives the other first-century count. 4 Ezra — the Jewish apocalypse that forms chapters 3–14 of the composite book called 2 Esdras, written in the aftermath of the fall of Jerusalem and usually dated c. AD 10020 — closes with a scene of canon-making: at God's command Ezra dictates for forty days until "ninety-four books" are written, whereupon the Most High divides them, "The twenty-four books that thou hast written publish, that the worthy and unworthy may read (therein): but the seventy last thou shalt keep, to deliver them to the wise among thy people."21 The twenty-four made public is the standard rabbinic reckoning of the Hebrew Bible — the Talmud's "twenty-four holy Scriptures," the same collection Josephus tallies as twenty-two by folding Ruth into Judges and Lamentations into Jeremiah — and none of the Greek-only books falls inside it.22 So beside Josephus's twenty-two stands a second, independent Jewish witness of c. AD 100 to a closed, numbered public Scripture, here explicitly bounded at the Hebrew count. Two features of the passage bear on its reading. First, the passage does not discard the extra books: the "seventy" held back are esoteric writings — apocalypses like 4 Ezra itself — and they are prized, reserved for the wise as "the spring of understanding, the fountain of wisdom, and the stream of knowledge," not condemned; the text draws a line between a public canon and a higher secret wisdom, not between Scripture and worthless books. Second, 4 Ezra is one of the books outside the Hebrew twenty-four — it travels as part of 2 Esdras, beyond the very list it here numbers — so a book outside the Hebrew canon numbers that canon's books. It names no deuterocanonical book; it is discussed with Josephus under Disputed readings below.

Polycarp of Smyrna (c. 125 AD): cites Tobit’s “alms delivers from death”; no list

A generation after Clement, and still before any Christian canon-list, the disputed books surface again as edifying maxims in the church's own exhortation. Writing from Smyrna to the church at Philippi — a letter usually dated c. 110–140 AD23 — Polycarp urges, "When you can do good, defer it not, because alms delivers from death" — the saying of the deuterocanonical Tobit (Tob 4:10; 12:9), set in a chain of apostolic and prophetic phrases (1 Peter 2:12, 17 and 5:5; Isaiah 52:5) with no mark of distinction.24 Polycarp renders no canon-list and prefaces the maxim with no "it is written"; the Tobit line is woven in as authoritative counsel, not adduced as a proof-text, and the chapter survives only in a later Latin version. Polycarp is a second early witness, beside Clement, to the use of the disputed books before Melito’s list; such use is discussed under Disputed readings below.

Rabbi Akiva ben Joseph (c. 130 AD, Judaism): the “external books” barred; the Song of Songs defended

A named early-rabbinic voice on the canon is Rabbi Akiva (c. 50–135), the leading Tanna of the Yavneh generation. The Mishnah sets his name against the outside books: among those with "no share in the World-to-Come," he adds "one who reads external literature" — ha-sefarim ha-chitzonim, the books beyond the Hebrew canon (the word chitzonim, "outer," itself echoing Greek apokrypha) — the earliest rabbinic ruling to name a whole category of writings and shut it out.25 What that category covers is disputed already within the tradition: the Babylonian Talmud glosses the "external books" as sifrei minim, the books of heretics/sectarians (in the second century often Jewish-Christian or Gnostic writings), which would point the ruling chiefly at the Gospels rather than the Apocrypha broadly — though the plain sense of "outside books," and Rav Yosef's inclusion of Ben Sira, keep the wider referent in view.26 His Yavneh circle’s Tosefta rules the point collectively: the gilyonim (the blank "margins," widely read as a pun on euangelion, the Gospels) "and the scrolls of the heretics do not defile the hands. The scrolls of Ben Sira and all scrolls… written from that point forward… do not defile the hands" — one of the earliest rabbinic rejections of the Christian writings and the late Jewish books in a single breath ("defiles the hands" being the idiom for canonical holiness).27 Akiva also defended the borderline Hebrew books inside the canon ("all the writings are holy, but the Song of Songs is the holy of holies," securing the Song and, with it, Esther), and he had "no objection to the private reading of the Apocrypha," quoting Ben Sira himself; on that account his ban fell on treating the outside books as authoritative Scripture, not on reading them.28

Melito of Sardis (c. 170 AD): a Hebrew-sized list

The earliest Christian list of the Old Testament books comes from Melito, bishop of Sardis, who travelled East "to the place where these things were preached" to learn "accurately the books of the Old Testament" and sent the catalogue to his friend Onesimus; Eusebius preserved it verbatim. It is a Hebrew-sized canon: Tobit, Judith, Sirach, Baruch, and 1–2 Maccabees are all absent (so too Esther), and it closes at "Esdras."29 The one word that might name a deuterocanonical book — "Wisdom," set right after "the Proverbs of Solomon" — is identified as a second title for Proverbs (the "all-virtuous Wisdom") by McGiffert and Bruce, and as the Book of Wisdom by Michuta; see the Melito page.30 Origen, Cyril, Athanasius and Jerome later give lists of a similar size.

The Muratorian Fragment (c. 170 AD): Wisdom among the received books

The same years yield a Western witness pointing the other way. The Muratorian Fragment — the oldest surviving list of New Testament books — counts, in the middle of its New-Testament reckoning, "the [book of] Wisdom, written by the friends of Solomon," ranking the deuterocanonical Wisdom of Solomon among the received writings.31 It is the earliest extant Western list to name a disputed Old-Testament book among the received writings. It is a New-Testament list, not an Old-Testament one: it names only Wisdom (not Tobit, Judith, Sirach, Baruch, or Maccabees), and it files the book among the New Testament writings.32 Set beside Melito's contemporary list — whose "Wisdom" is read by some as Proverbs — the two lists use the same word.

Athenagoras of Athens (c. 177 AD): Baruch quoted among the prophets; no list

A few years before Irenaeus, an Athenian philosopher-convert presses a disputed book into service as prophecy. Defending Christians against the charge of atheism in his Plea for the Christians (c. 177), addressed to the emperors Marcus Aurelius and Commodus, Athenagoras calls the Hebrew prophets — "Moses or… Isaiah and Jeremiah, and the other prophets," who "lifted in ecstasy… by the impulses of the Divine Spirit, uttered the things with which they were inspired" — and strings together their utterances. The first he quotes, "The Lord is our God; no other can be compared with Him," is not protocanonical but the deuterocanonical Baruch (Baruch 3:35–36), set among the inspired words of the prophets beside three sayings of Isaiah (44:6; 43:10–11; 66:1).33 He does not name Baruch — he gathers the chain under "the prophets," and Baruch was reckoned in the Greek tradition an appendix to Jeremiah, whom he does name — the line is classed among words spoken "by the… Divine Spirit." Irenaeus cites the same book under the formula "Jeremiah the prophet… saying."34 A contemporary of Melito's list, he gives no canon-list; he uses a disputed book among the words of the prophets — discussed under Disputed readings below.

Irenaeus of Lyons (c. 180 AD): Susanna and Baruch cited under the prophets’ names; no list

A decade after Melito's narrower list, and from the other direction, the bishop of Lyons quotes the disputed books not as edifying maxims but as the words of named prophets. In Irenaeus's Against Heresies (c. 175–189) the Greek addition to Daniel — the story of Susanna — is cited as words "to be found in Daniel the prophet": "O thou seed of Canaan, and not of Judah, beauty hath deceived thee…" (Susanna 56, 52).35 And a long passage of the deuterocanonical Baruch (Baruch 4:36–5:9) is adduced as genuine prophecy — "Jeremiah the prophet has pointed out… saying, 'Look around Jerusalem towards the east…'" — Baruch being reckoned in the Greek and Latin tradition an appendix to Jeremiah and routinely cited under his name.36 Where Clement, the Didache, and Polycarp braid the disputed books in without a citation formula, Irenaeus — a hearer of Polycarp, himself a disciple of the apostle John — gives them the explicit "the prophet" attribution. He renders no canon-verdict (his work enumerates no books); he simply uses them as prophecy — discussed under Disputed readings below, on whether the primitive church reckoned these books Scripture.

Clement of Alexandria (c. 200 AD): the disputed books cited as “Scripture”; no list

The head of the Alexandrian catechetical school cites the disputed books with an explicit citation formula. A generation after Irenaeus, Clement (c. 150–215; his Paedagogus and Stromateis written c. 198–203) quotes four of the disputed books the way he quotes Moses or the Gospel — introduced as “Scripture.” He cites Sirach 21:6 as “the Scripture which says, ‘And he that feareth the Lord will turn to his heart’”; Tobit 12:8 as “the Scripture which says, ‘Fasting with prayer is a good thing’”; Wisdom 2:12 as “Scripture… when it says, ‘Let us remove the righteous man from us’”; and a long passage of Baruch (3:16–19) as the words of “the Divine Scripture.”37 He also strings Judith 8:27 (“he that is near the Lord is full of stripes”) into a chain with Galatians 3:24, Isaiah 5:21, and Proverbs 1:7, a use closer to Clement of Rome's woven maxims than to the explicit-formula citations.38 Where Irenaeus ascribed Susanna and Baruch to named prophets, Clement uses the bare word “Scripture” / “the Divine Scripture,” the label he also uses for the protocanonical books; such citation is discussed under Disputed readings below. He renders no canon-list; he cites these books as Scripture, in the same decades as the Hebrew-sized lists of Melito and (a generation on) Origen, his own successor at Alexandria.

The Babylonian Talmud (c. 200 AD, Judaism): a Hebrew-sized order ending in Chronicles; Sirach cited as Writings

The fullest early Jewish articulation of the canon — and a witness that cuts both ways. A baraita preserved in the Babylonian Talmud, Bava Batra 14b, enumerates the Prophets and then the Writings — "… Daniel and the Scroll of Esther; and Ezra and Chronicles" — closing the whole of Scripture with Chronicles and listing none of the Greek extras. It is the single clearest ancient Jewish attestation of a Hebrew-sized, Genesis-to-Chronicles arrangement (the order on which the "from Abel to Zechariah" argument leans), and because a baraita is tannaitic material it is comparatively early, though it stands almost alone for a millennium.39 Yet the same compilation, at Bava Kamma 92b, quotes Ben Sira (Sirach 13) as a verse of the Writings — "triplicated in the Writings, as it is written…" — with the same "as it is written" formula it gives Torah and Prophets, so the rabbinic stream that supplies the Chronicles-last order did not draw an airtight line around the deuterocanon.40 The baraita is conventionally placed c. 200 (the Mishnaic horizon) and slotted here by that earlier date, though the Talmud's redaction runs c. 500–600; both passages are discussed under Disputed readings below.41

Hippolytus of Rome (c. 205 AD): Wisdom and Baruch read as prophecy of Christ; no list

In the same decades, and from the West, a teacher of the Roman church cites two of the disputed books as prophecy. Hippolytus (c. 170–235) opens his Expository Treatise Against the Jews by adducing — against his Jewish interlocutor — “the prophecy of Solomon, which speaketh of Christ”: the deuterocanonical Wisdom of Solomon (2:1, 12–20), the foretelling of the plot against the righteous one who “calleth himself the Child of God,” introduced with “the prophet says” and read as a Solomonic prophecy of the Passion.42 And in Against Noetus he expounds the deuterocanonical Baruch (3:35–37) — “This is our God… afterward did He show Himself upon earth, and conversed with men” — as a genuine prophecy of the incarnation; the “Thus it is written” formula is on the lips of his Noetian opponents, but Hippolytus does not contest the citation’s scriptural standing, granting it (“That said he rightly”) and disputing only whom it speaks of.43 Where Irenaeus ascribed Susanna and Baruch to named prophets and Clement labelled the books “Scripture,” Hippolytus reads Wisdom as the very “prophecy of Solomon” concerning Christ — a Western witness, between Clement and Origen, on the “using” side of the divide. He renders no canon-verdict; he simply uses the disputed books as prophecy — discussed under Disputed readings below.

Tertullian (c. 210 AD): the disputed books used as prophecy and as example; no list

Writing in Latin from Carthage, Tertullian cites the disputed books. Tertullian (c. 155 – c. 220) quotes the deuterocanonical Letter of Jeremiah (Baruch 6:3) as the very “words of Jeremias,” set beside Daniel 3 in his exhortation to martyrdom: the three youths “remembered also the words of Jeremias writing to those over whom that captivity was impending… Beware, therefore, that ye also do not be altogether like the foreigners… but say in your mind, Our duty is to worship Thee, O Lord.”44 He ranks the Wisdom of Solomon among “our Christian authorities,” reading of God as “the searcher and witness of the heart” (Wisdom 1:6) in a chain with Proverbs, Matthew, the Psalms, Paul, and 1 John — though that particular citation is disputed: Roger Beckwith reads the phrase as Psalm 139:23 rather than Wisdom, and Tertullian’s firmer, undisputed use of the book comes in the Prescription against Heretics, where “the porch of Solomon” is said to have “taught that ‘the Lord should be sought in simplicity of heart’” — the opening words of Wisdom 1:1.45 And he twice adduces deuterocanonical figures as patterns of holiness: “a Judith, daughter of Merari” stands among “so many other examples of saints” beside Isaac and John,46 and Susanna’s modest unveiling at her trial is set beside Rebecca’s veil — though these are historical examples, not cited authorities.47 The force is graded: Baruch is quoted under the prophet’s name as the others quote “Jeremiah,” Wisdom adduced (with one disputed text) as a “Christian authority,” Judith and Susanna only as saintly examples. Like Irenaeus and Hippolytus he renders no canon-list; he simply uses the disputed books — the earliest extant Latin witness to that practice, a generation before Origen’s list; the datapoint is discussed under Disputed readings below.

Origen of Alexandria (c. 240 AD): twenty-two books “as the Hebrews have handed them down”; the Greek copies kept

Origen gives a Hebrew-sized list. In his Commentary on Psalm 1, quoted by Eusebius, Origen lists the Old Testament books "as the Hebrews have handed them down" as twenty-two, the count of the Hebrew letters — Tobit, Judith, Sirach, and the Wisdom of Solomon all absent, and the Maccabees named only to be set "besides these."48 Yet the same Origen will not surrender the longer Greek text the churches actually read: answering Julius Africanus, who challenged the story of Susanna as no genuine part of Daniel, he refuses to "reject as spurious the copies in use in our Churches" and appeals to the Providence that gave those Scriptures to "all the Churches of Christ."49 Across his homilies and treatises Origen quotes Tobit, Sirach, Baruch, the Maccabees, Wisdom, Judith, and Susanna outright as "Scripture," "holy Scripture," or what "is written." Athanasius, Rufinus, and Jerome later draw a distinction between canonical books and books read in the churches.50

Cyprian of Carthage (c. 253 AD): the disputed books cited as “Holy Scripture”; no list

From the Latin West, and with the fullest formulae yet, the bishop and martyr of Carthage cites the disputed books exactly as he cites the Law and the Gospel. A generation after his fellow Carthaginian Tertullian, and a contemporary of Origen in the East, Cyprian (c. 200–258, bishop of Carthage from c. 248, martyred under Valerian) quotes Tobit 12:8 as what “Holy Scripture instructs us, saying” — “Prayer is good with fasting and almsgiving”51 Sirach 2:1, 4–5 as what “Holy Scripture teaches and forewarns, saying,”52 and the Wisdom of Solomon 3:4–8 — the martyrs “tried… as gold in the furnace” — as “the sacred Scripture.”53 He gathers 1 Maccabees 2:62–63 under “Holy Scripture meets and warns us, saying,”54 ascribes the deuterocanonical Letter of Jeremiah (Baruch 6:6) to “the Holy Spirit… by Jeremiah,”55 and quotes the Greek addition to Daniel (Bel and the Dragon 14:5) as the inspired word of Daniel “filled with the Holy Spirit.”56 He draws up no canon-list, so he renders no canon-verdict; but these are the same labels he uses for Moses, the Psalms, and the Gospel, a pre-Nicene Western witness to the disputed books cited as Scripture. The datapoint is discussed under Disputed readings below.

Dionysius of Alexandria (c. 264 AD): Sirach cited as “the divine oracles”; no list

The Greek East supplies a near-contemporary of Cyprian from the very school that produced the era's most careful canon-list. Dionysius (c. 190–264), pupil and successor of Origen as head of the Alexandrian catechetical school and bishop of Alexandria from c. 248, twice presses a disputed book into service as inspired Scripture. In his treatise On Nature, arguing against the Epicurean doctrine that the ordered cosmos arose by chance from random atoms, he answers the philosophers by appeal to Sirach: “listen to the divine oracles: ‘The works of the Lord are in judgment…’” (Sirach 16:26–27) — the deuterocanonical wisdom book introduced as ta theia logia, the very category of inspired utterance.57 And in his doctrinal Epistle to Dionysius, Bishop of Rome, defending the Son's eternity against Sabellianism, he glosses the deity of Christ by reading the Wisdom of Solomon's “He is the breath of God's power” (Wisdom 7:25) alongside John 4:24, “God is a Spirit,” to ground the title “Breath” for Christ — the Wisdom text handled exactly as the Johannine one beside it.58 He leaves no canon-list; the Greek-East datapoint, beside Cyprian's Latin one, discussed under Disputed readings below.

Methodius of Olympus (c. 290 AD): Wisdom and Sirach cited with Scripture formulas; no list

A second Greek witness, from outside the Alexandrian school, joins Dionysius on the “using” side. Methodius (c. 290; martyred c. 311), bishop of Olympus in Lycia and a pointed early critic of Origen, twice handles a disputed book as Scripture. In his major work, the Banquet of the Ten Virgins, debating the divine ordering of every birth, he brings forward Wisdom of Solomon 3:16 with the bare formula “the Scripture which says”: “As for the children of the adulterers, they shall not come to their perfection” — the deuterocanonical text introduced under the unqualified label he also uses for the protocanonical books.59 And the Oration on Simeon and Anna — a work transmitted under his name but of contested authenticity — ranges Sirach 16:6, “Amongst the disobedient people shall the fire burn,” under “as it is written,” in the same breath as Psalm 18:8.60 His anti-Origenist writing reaches for the disputed books, placing the “using” practice outside Alexandria as well, in one of Origen’s critics. He draws up no canon-list, so renders no canon-verdict; the datapoint is discussed under Disputed readings below.

Lactantius (c. 310 AD): Sirach cited as “Solomon’s” testimony to the Word of God; no list

On the eve of Nicaea, a Latin rhetorician cites a disputed book in an argument about Christ. Lactantius (c. 250 – c. 325), the rhetorician Constantine would set over his son’s education, builds in his Divine Institutes (c. 303–311) a case for the eternal Word made flesh and calls “Solomon” as its Old-Testament witness: “I came forth out of the mouth of the Most High before all creatures… and covered the whole earth with a cloud” — the words of the deuterocanonical Sirach (Ecclesiasticus 24:3–4), the self-speech of personified Wisdom — set immediately beside John 1:1, “In the beginning was the Word.”61 The ascription to “Solomon” reflects the circulation of Sirach, written by Jesus ben Sira, among the “books of Solomon,” under whose name it was cited; Lactantius sets the text beside the Gospel’s prologue as Solomonic testimony. He uses the verse in an argument for the deity and creative agency of the Word, as Hippolytus reading Wisdom as “the prophecy of Solomon which speaketh of Christ.” Like the witnesses before him he gives no canon-list; he uses a disputed book as prophecy of Christ — a Western, Constantinian-era datapoint, beside the Greek-East Dionysius, discussed under Disputed readings below.

Eusebius of Caesarea (c. 320 AD): transmits the Hebrew-sized lists; cites the disputed books as Scripture

Eusebius (c. 260–339), bishop of Caesarea and father of church history, is the very channel through which the two great Hebrew-sized lists reach us — he preserves Melito's catalogue (Ecclesiastical History iv.26) and Origen's "twenty-two, as the Hebrews have handed them down" (vi.25), the two earliest extant Christian Old-Testament lists.62 Yet in his own apologetic works he uses the disputed books as Scripture. In the Praeparatio Evangelica (c. 313–320), having quoted the deuterocanonical Wisdom of Solomon — "she reacheth from end to end with full strength, and ordereth all things graciously" (Wisdom 8:1) — among Solomon's words, he caps the citation "Thus the Scripture speaks," then sets Philo's lesser clarity beside it: the bare label "the Scripture" for a disputed book, the same he gives the protocanon.63 In the same work he adduces the Greek addition to Daniel as proof "from Scripture" of God's foreknowledge — "the description by Susanna of God as knowing all things before they come to pass" (Susanna 42) — though here he is transcribing, with approval, a long extract from Origen rather than writing in his own voice.64 And in the Demonstratio Evangelica (c. 318–323), expounding the incarnation, he quotes the deuterocanonical Baruch (3:29–37) — "Afterward did he show himself upon earth, and conversed with men" — and pronounces: "I need add nothing to these inspired words, which so clearly support my argument," reading Baruch exactly as Hippolytus had, as genuine prophecy of Christ.65 He compiles no Old-Testament canon-list of his own; he transmits the Hebrew-sized lists and cites the disputed books as Scripture, as Origen had. The pattern is discussed under Disputed readings below.

Alexander of Alexandria (c. 321 AD): Sirach cited with Paul, Genesis and Isaiah; no list

The bishop who opened the Arian controversy — Alexander of Alexandria (d. 326), Athanasius’s mentor and predecessor — weaves the deuterocanonical Sirach into the texture of his christology. In his encyclical against Arius (the letter to Alexander of Byzantium, c. 319–324), arguing that the eternal generation of the Word lies beyond inquiry, he chains in one breath Sirach 3:21 — “Seek not out the things that are too hard for you, neither search the things that are above your strength,” adduced as binding authority one is impious “not listening to” — with the Apostle Paul (“Eye has not seen…,” 1 Corinthians 2:9), God’s word to Abraham that he could not number the stars (Genesis 15:5), Sirach 1:2 (“Who can number the sand of the sea, and the drops of rain”), and the prophet’s “Who shall declare his generation?” (Isaiah 53:8).66 He does not label the Sirach lines “Scripture” or “it is written”; he simply uses them — undistinguished from the canonical texts they stand among — as testimony in a doctrinal argument. His witness is of a different kind from an explicit Scripture-formula: it rests on juxtaposition and on the authority-language he applies to Sirach 3:21. Like the witnesses before him he renders no canon-list; he attests early-fourth-century Alexandrian practice, a datapoint — with that caveat — discussed under Disputed readings below.

The Council of Nicaea (325 AD): no extant canon ruling; a later report on Judith

The first ecumenical council published no canon-list. Its twenty surviving canons and synodal letter concern the Arian and Melitian schisms, the lapsed, the date of Easter, and clerical discipline — not the books of Scripture.67 It belongs on this timeline for one reason: it is the earliest council to which a verdict on a deuterocanonical book has ever been attributed. Eighty years later Jerome reports, in his Preface to Judith, that the council "is found… to have been counted" the deuterocanonical book of Judith "among the number of the Sacred Scriptures," and gives that as his reason for translating a book he otherwise ranked below the canon.68 In his prefaces Jerome ranks Judith outside the canon, and Athanasius, who was present at Nicaea, placed Judith in 367 among the books "not… included in the Canon, but appointed by the Fathers to be read."69 How the report has been read is set out on its own page on the Biblical Canon timeline.70

Aphrahat the Persian Sage (c. 337 AD): Maccabean history read as fulfilled prophecy; no list

The timeline reaches the Syriac East, beyond the Roman frontier. Aphrahat (c. 270–345), “the Persian Sage,” a bishop in the Sasanian empire whose Demonstrations are the earliest substantial body of Syriac theology, treats the Maccabean history as authoritative, prophecy-fulfilling Scripture. In Demonstration 5 (Of Wars, dated by its author to AD 337) he declares Psalm 79 “fulfilled… when the venerable and aged Eleazar was slain, and the sons of the blessed Samuna, seven in number” — the martyrs of 2 Maccabees 6:18–7:42 — alongside “Judas (Maccabeus) and his brethren.”71 And, expounding Daniel 7, he reads the worm-eaten death of Antiochus — “he became sick with a grievous and evil sickness… for worms were crawling and falling from him and eating his flesh” — the death narrated in 2 Maccabees 9:5–12, as the heaven-sent judgment Daniel foretold (“the judgment was set… until the end of the kingdom,” Daniel 7:26), with Isaiah’s “worm Jacob” (Isaiah 41:14) supplying the retribution: the worms devour him “because he oppressed the worm Jacob.”72 He prefaces the Maccabean material with no “it is written” formula and draws up no canon-list, so the witness is of the historical-and-typological kind — like Clement of Rome with “the blessed Judith” — but it weaves the deuterocanonical narrative into Daniel and the Psalms as sacred history come true, and it adds the Syriac/Eastern tradition, geographically distinct from the Greek and Latin witnesses, to the “using” side of the divide discussed under Disputed readings below.

Cyril of Jerusalem (c. 350 AD): a Hebrew-sized list with Baruch in Jeremiah

Catechizing the candidates for baptism at Jerusalem, Cyril recites an Eastern Old-Testament list of twenty-two books, the Hebrew-letter count, and draws the line sharply: "have nothing to do with the apocryphal writings… study earnestly these only which we read openly in the Church." Tobit, Judith, Sirach, the Wisdom of Solomon, and 1–2 Maccabees are all absent.73 Yet the list is not the bare Hebrew thirty-nine: Cyril's single book of Jeremiah is reckoned "including Baruch and Lamentations and the Epistle" — two short deuterocanonical works folded in as appendices, exactly as the Greek manuscripts carried them — and he credits the whole Hebrew-sized canon to the Septuagint's seventy-two translators.74 His list keeps Baruch inside a canonical book, as Origen’s did and as Athanasius’s and Epiphanius’s also do.

Hilary of Poitiers (c. 360 AD): a twenty-two-book list

The earliest extant canon-list by a Latin Father gives the Eastern, Hebrew-sized reckoning in the West. In the prologue to his Tractatus super Psalmos (Expositions of the Psalms) Hilary reckons the Old Testament at twenty-two books, "according to the number of the [Hebrew] letters" — Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, and the Maccabees absent from the count — recording only that "to this some add Tobit and Judith to make twenty-four books." A Westerner who had spent his exile among the Greek bishops, he carries the Hebrew enumeration of Origen and Cyril into Latin a generation before Jerome. Hilary, like the others, quotes the disputed books in his own argument.75

The Cheltenham List (c. 360 AD): a Latin list including the deuterocanon

From the same years, and the other direction, comes an anonymous Latin catalogue — the Cheltenham (or Mommsen) List, a stichometric table of the canonical books with their line-counts, most likely North African. Its Old Testament runs out the longer canon: the two books of Maccabees, Tobit, and Judith are enumerated among the Scriptures with the rest, and the whole is sealed by the Apocalypse's "four and twenty elders."76 It is a Western witness to the deuterocanon a full generation before the African councils of Hippo and Carthage gave that same canon conciliar form.

The Council of Laodicea (c. 363 AD): a Hebrew-sized list with Baruch and the Epistle of Jeremiah

From the Eastern, list-making side of the same years comes a conciliar canon of the region. A regional synod of Phrygian bishops meeting at Laodicea rules that "only the Canonical Books… of the New and Old Testament" may be read in church (canon 59), and in the much-disputed canon 60 appends a book-list whose Old Testament is the Hebrew twenty-two: Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, and the two books of Maccabees are all absent, and only Baruch and the Epistle of Jeremiah are kept, folded into "Jeremiah" as Origen, Cyril, and Athanasius also count their Jeremiah.77 It is of the same generation as the West's Cheltenham List. Canon 60's genuineness is doubted, missing from several early collections of the synod's acts and possibly appended later to spell out the "canonical books" canon 59 left unnamed.78 Trullo (692) confirmed the Laodicean canons with others. (The same canon 60's New Testament, stopping short of the Apocalypse, is treated on the New Testament canon page.)

Athanasius of Alexandria (367 AD): a Hebrew-sized list with Baruch; other books “to be read”

Athanasius sorts the books into a three-tier scheme. In his thirty-ninth Festal Letter Athanasius first lists the Old-Testament canon as the Hebrew twenty-two — Tobit, Judith, Sirach, the Wisdom of Solomon, and the Maccabees all absent — though his Jeremiah, like Origen's and Cyril's, carries Baruch and the Epistle of Jeremiah folded in.79 Below the canon he sets a second rank — books "not indeed included in the Canon, but appointed by the Fathers to be read… for instruction in the word of godliness" — and there he files the deuterocanon proper: the Wisdom of Solomon, Sirach, Judith, Tobit, and the protocanonical Esther; below that again stand the "apocryphal writings… an invention of heretics."80 The letter thus has three ranks: the canon, the books "appointed by the Fathers to be read," and the apocrypha. How the middle rank is read is disputed; see the interpretation page. (The same letter gives the first New-Testament catalogue identical to the present twenty-seven, treated on his New Testament canon page.)

Basil of Caesarea (c. 379 AD): Sirach cited as “Scripture”; no list

The eldest of the three Cappadocian Fathers stands on the "using" side of the divide his own circle is famous for narrowing. Basil (c. 330–379), bishop of Caesarea, draws up no Old-Testament canon-list, but across his works he quotes the disputed books as authority. In the Hexaemeron he clinches a lesson on the soul's instability with the bare formula he also uses for the protocanonical books — "What Scripture says is very true, 'As for a fool he changeth as the moon'" — the words of the deuterocanonical Sirach (27:11); and a consoling letter grounds divine providence in the Wisdom of Solomon: the Lord "ordereth 'all things in measure and weight'" (Wisdom 11:20), set beside 1 Corinthians 10:13.81 In On the Holy Spirit he settles points of doctrine by naming the authors of two more disputed books — Baruch, who "exhibit[s] the immutability… of the Divine mode of existence… 'For thou sittest for ever and we perish utterly'" (Baruch 3:3), and Judith, "'Thou hast thought, and what things thou didst determine were ready at hand'" (Judith 9:5–6) — and holds up the Maccabean mother of the seven sons as a martyr-model.82 No canon-list by Basil survives; the Hebrew-sized lists of his circle are those of Gregory of Nazianzus and Amphilochius. James White lists "Basil the Great" among the Fathers who rejected the deuterocanon; whether such citations attest canonical standing is discussed on its interpretation page.

Gregory of Nyssa (c. 380 AD): the disputed books cited as "the prophetical writing," as Baruch "delivered… by the Holy Scriptures," and as "the Scripture"; no list

The third Cappadocian joins his brother Basil on the "using" side of the divide. Gregory of Nyssa (c. 335–395), bishop of Nyssa from 372, draws up no canon-list, but across his doctrinal and ascetic works he quotes three of the disputed books as inspired authority. In On the Making of Man (16.14, 379) he grounds the divine foreknowledge in the Greek addition to Daniel — the prayer of Susanna, "knoweth all things before they be" (Susanna 42 / Daniel 13:42) — introduced as the word of "the prophetical writing."83 In Against Eunomius (c. 380–383) he twice presses the deuterocanonical Baruch (3:37) into service as the prophetic proof of the Incarnation — "the God who was in the beginning, 'afterwards,' as Baruch says, 'was seen upon the earth, and conversed with men'" — the verse named both as Baruch's and as words "delivered to us by the Holy Scriptures" (and, in Book VI.4, "as the prophet says"), turning the heretic's own argument against him.84 And in On Virginity (15, c. 371) he clinches an exhortation with the deuterocanonical Wisdom of Solomon (1:4) under the bare label "the Scripture": "into the malicious soul Wisdom cannot come."85 Where his brother Basil called Sirach "Scripture" and cited Baruch, Judith, and Wisdom to settle doctrine, Gregory does the same — the two Cappadocian brothers standing together on the "using" side while the Hebrew-sized lists of the circle belong to their friends Gregory of Nazianzus and Amphilochius. He renders no canon-verdict; he simply uses the disputed books — discussed under Disputed readings below.

Amphilochius of Iconium (c. 380 AD): a Hebrew-sized list in verse

An Eastern bishop, friend of the Cappadocians, casts the canon into verse. In his Iambics for Seleucus Amphilochius warns that "not every book is safe which has acquired the venerable name of Scripture" — some are "spurious and utterly unsafe, like counterfeit… coins" — and then recites a Hebrew-sized Old Testament that omits Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, and the Maccabees, and gives even Esther only as a book "some approve."86 It is the Hebrew-canon posture of his fellow Cappadocian Gregory of Nazianzus, set to meter for memorizing.

The Apostolic Canons (c. 380 AD): a list with Judith and three Maccabees; Sirach commended

The Eastern church's own canon-law collection returns a "Yes" of singular shape. Canon 85, the closing canon of the Antiochene Apostolic Canons appended to the Apostolic Constitutions, bids "the following books be esteemed venerable and holy by all of you" and lists, among the Old Testament, "[one of Judith;] three of the Maccabees," and urges the young to learn "the Wisdom of the learned Sirach."87 Its canon runs wider than the later Catholic deuterocanon — it receives three books of Maccabees, not two — yet it names neither Tobit nor the Wisdom of Solomon: a reminder that the Eastern "longer" canon was never a single fixed list. (The same canon's New-Testament reckoning omits Revelation and adds 1–2 Clement.)

The Council of Rome (382 AD): a list including the deuterocanon; its dating disputed

The earliest extant Latin council-list running out the full deuterocanon is ascribed to the Roman synod that met under Pope Damasus I in 382. Its canon-catalogue — the so-called "Damasine list" — gives the longer Septuagint Old Testament, filing Tobit, Judith, and 1–2 Maccabees among "the Histories" and Wisdom and Ecclesiasticus (Sirach) among the books of Solomon: the whole disputed deuterocanon received as Scripture, 88 The list survives in no fourth-century text, only as a chapter of the Decretum Gelasianum. Its canon-chapters are attributed to the synod of 382 by Faustino Arévalo (1794), C. H. Turner, W. A. Jurgens (Catholic patrologist) and George Reid (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1908), for whom the essential part is "now generally attributed to a synod convoked by Pope Damasus in the year 382"; Ernst von Dobschütz (critical edition, 1912), followed by F. C. Burkitt, dates the Decretum to the first half of the sixth century, and Geoffrey Hahneman points to Jerome's silence, though Jerome was at Rome in 382. How the documentary basis is read is set out on its own page on the Biblical Canon timeline.89

Epiphanius of Salamis (c. 385 AD): a Hebrew-sized list with Baruch; Wisdom and Sirach "useful"

The bishop of Salamis gives a Hebrew-sized list. In both the Panarion and On Weights and Measures, Epiphanius gives the Hebrew-sized canon — twenty-seven "recognized canonical books, but twenty-two when counted according to the letters of the alphabet of the Hebrews" — running them out in four "pentateuchs" plus Ezra and Esther, with Tobit, Judith, and the Maccabees all absent.90 Like Athanasius, he ranks the other books: he names the two great Wisdom books — the Wisdom of Solomon and the Wisdom of Sirach — and rules them "of disputed canonicity," yet "helpful and useful," books that "were not kept in the chest, that is, in the ark of the covenant"; and his single Jeremiah carries Baruch and the Epistle of Jeremiah folded in, exactly as Origen, Cyril, and Athanasius had kept them — though he notes "the epistles are not in use among the Hebrews."91

Ambrose of Milan (c. 387 AD): the disputed books cited as "Scripture" and "it is written"; no list

The bishop of Milan who catechized and baptized Augustine (at Easter 387) cites the disputed books in his own works. Ambrose (c. 339–397, bishop of Milan from 374) draws up no canon-list, but across his works he quotes the disputed books with the plain formulae he also uses for the protocanonical books. He grounds his grief for his brother in the deuterocanonical Wisdom of Solomon — "as Scripture says concerning him: 'He was taken away, lest that wickedness should alter his understanding'" (Wisdom 4:11); reads the deuterocanonical Baruch as the very speech of "the Lord," who "saying: 'Put off, O Jerusalem, the garment of your mourning…'" bids penitent Jerusalem rise (Baruch 5:1); and tells the story of Susanna — the Greek addition to Daniel — under "it is written: 'The Lord heard her voice, when she was being led to be put to death'" (Daniel 13:44).92 He presses the books doctrinally too: against Arius he turns the deuterocanonical Sirach into a divine rebuke — "it was said even to Arius and to all men: 'Seek not that which is too deep for you'" (Sirach 3:22).93 And he adduces deuterocanonical figures as moral examples — Tobit sending "Raphael the archangel" (Tobit 9:3) beside the angel sent to Balaam, "Judith adorned herself that she might please an adulterer" for religion's sake (beside Rahab), and the Maccabean mother who "looked on the corpses of her children as so many trophies" (2 Maccabees 7:20) beside the mothers of Bethlehem.94 Like Cyprian and Tertullian before him he renders no canon-verdict; he uses the disputed books, a Latin witness between Cyprian and the African councils. The datapoint is discussed under Disputed readings below.

Gregory of Nazianzus (c. 389 AD): a twenty-two-book list without Esther

The Cappadocian theologian sets out a Hebrew-sized list in verse. His catalogue Concerning the Genuine Books of Divinely Inspired Scripture warns "let not extraneous books seduce your mind, for many malignant writings have been disseminated," and counts "twenty-two of the ancient books, corresponding to the number of the Hebrew letters."95 Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, and the Maccabees are absent, and the list omits Esther as well, the same omission Athanasius had hesitated over. The poem became a standard Eastern measure of the canon, carried into the medieval Greek canon-law collections. In his orations Gregory quotes the disputed books — Judith as "the Scripture," the Wisdom of Solomon "as it is written," and Baruch pressed as a proof-text for the Incarnation against the Arians — a case of ranking by the Hebrew list while reading from the wider Greek Bible.95

Jerome (c. 390 AD): the Hebrew twenty-two, without Baruch

The man who gave the Latin West its Bible gives a Hebrew-sized "No," without Baruch. In the Prologus Galeatus — the "helmeted" preface to his translation from the Hebrew — Jerome fixes the Old Testament at the Hebrew twenty-two and rules that "whatever is outside of these is set aside among the apocrypha. Therefore, Wisdom… and the book of Jesus son of Sirach, and Judith and Tobias, and The Shepherd are not in the canon."96 Where Cyril, Athanasius, and Epiphanius all folded Baruch into the canonical Jeremiah, Jerome omits it as "neither read nor found among the Hebrews," and he applies the word apocrypha to the disputed books.96 At his bishops' command he translated Tobit and Judith anyway — Tobit "contrary to their canon," he grants, choosing "to be subject to the commands of bishops" over "the opinion of the Pharisees" — and he ranked Wisdom and Sirach, with Judith, Tobit, and the Maccabees, as books the Church may "read… for the strengthening of the people, but not for confirming the authority of ecclesiastical dogmas." Away from the prefaces he quotes Sirach with "Holy scripture says," the Wisdom of Solomon under "it is written," and ranging Baruch among "the trumpets of the prophets".97 The Thirty-Nine Articles (1571) cite this formula by Jerome’s name. (His Judith preface also carries the report that the Council of Nicaea "counted" Judith among the Scriptures; see its own page.98)

The African councils — Hippo & Carthage (393–419 AD): a conciliar list including the deuterocanon

The North-African synods gathered around Augustine and Aurelius of Carthage — Hippo in 393 (canon 36), re-enacted at Carthage in 397 and reaffirmed in 419 — each enact the rule "that besides the Canonical Scriptures nothing be read in church under the name of divine Scripture," and then list the books. Their Old Testament is the longer Septuagint canon: Tobit, Judith, and the two books of Maccabees named outright among the Scriptures, and the deuterocanonical Wisdom of Solomon and Sirach carried in "the five books of Solomon."99 Where Athanasius a generation earlier had ranked exactly these books a tier below — "read" but "not in the canon" — the African lists place them among the canonical books. Augustine’s On Christian Doctrine (2.8.13, c. 397) matches the conciliar canon book for book and states his criterion — to "prefer those that are received by all the catholic churches to those which some do not receive."100 These are regional synods; their list stands beside Athanasius's Eastern list of a generation earlier. (The same councils' New-Testament list is treated on the companion Biblical Canon page.)

John Chrysostom (c. 395 AD): the disputed books cited as Scripture; no list

The preacher of Antioch cites the deuterocanon in his homilies. Chrysostom adduces Sirach (4:1) "from the Divine Scriptures," cites Wisdom (11:23) under "it is written," ranks the Greek Susanna (Daniel 13:52) beside two Psalms as a scriptural instance, and sets Tobit (12:9) beside Paul and the Psalter on almsgiving.101102103104 The use rises to doctrine: against the Marcionite denial of a real Incarnation he presses Baruch (3:35–37) — "in the book of Jeremiah" — as a prophecy that "this is our God… He appeared upon the earth, and held converse with men."105 He leaves no canon-list; like Basil and Gregory of Nyssa, he cites the books in his own argument.

Augustine of Hippo (c. 397): a list including the deuterocanon, by breadth of reception

Augustine sets out a list under his own name. In On Christian Doctrine Augustine runs out a forty-four-book Old Testament that files Tobias, Judith, the two books of Maccabees, and the two of Ezra among the histories, and reckons Wisdom and Ecclesiasticus among the prophetical books — the longer Septuagint canon, with no second tier.106 What carries the disputed books in is not a Hebrew enumeration but a rule of reception: "to prefer those that are received by all the catholic churches to those which some do not receive," weighing a book by the breadth and antiquity of its use.107 That test differs from Jerome's Hebraica veritas, and the two contemporaries disagreed on the canon. Late in life, in The City of God, Augustine takes up the objection that the Maccabees are not in the Hebrew Scriptures, grants it, and answers: those books "are held as canonical, not by the Jews, but by the Church, on account of the extreme and wonderful sufferings of certain martyrs."108 His canon rests on the church's reception rather than the Jewish reckoning.

The Catalogue of Codex Claromontanus (c. 400 AD): a list including the deuterocanon

A stichometric book-list, copied into the sixth-century Greek–Latin Codex Claromontanus but reflecting an older (c. 300–400) table, gives the longer canon with line-counts: Tobit, Judith, the Wisdom of Solomon, the Wisdom of Jesus (Sirach), and the books of Maccabees all stand among the Old Testament books.109 The list marks several disputed titles with a small dash — but those obeli fall on contested New-Testament books, not on the Old-Testament deuterocanon, which it carries without such a mark. Like the Damasine list, it is preserved in a later codex.

Sulpicius Severus (c. 403): Judith narrated within his epitome of "the sacred Scriptures"

A Gallic aristocrat-turned-ascetic, friend and biographer of Martin of Tours, supplies a quieter Western datapoint: not a canon-list and not a proof-text, but a history. In his Sacred History (Chronica, c. 403) Sulpicius Severus sets out to "give a condensed account of those things which are set forth in the sacred Scriptures from the beginning of the world… down to" his own day — an epitome of the biblical narrative whose contents are the canon he is summarizing.110 Into that survey, alongside Esther and the post-exilic books, he folds Judith: "To this series of events it will be right that I should append an account of the doings of Judith; for she is related to have lived after the captivity" — the deuterocanonical narrative narrated as a stretch of the same sacred history.111 The witness is graded: Severus also flags the book's historical difficulty — "the sacred history has not revealed who was king of the Persians in her day" — and works to reconcile its "Nebuchadnezzar" with the Persian chronology, so he handles Judith as genuine but chronologically puzzling sacred history rather than reciting it under an "it is written." Like Clement of Rome with "the blessed Judith" and Aphrahat with the Maccabean martyrs, he renders no canon-list; he simply uses the disputed book — weaving it, without seam, into his epitome of "the sacred Scriptures" — the Western, turn-of-the-fifth-century discussed under Disputed readings below.

Rufinus of Aquileia (c. 404): a Hebrew-sized list; other books "Ecclesiastical," read but not for doctrine

Rufinus names three ranks of books. In his Commentary on the Apostles' Creed he sorts the books into three named ranks. The Canonical Old Testament he lists is the Hebrew-sized canon — Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, and the Maccabees all absent, and Jeremiah named without Baruch.112 Below it he sets a second tier, the books "our fathers call not 'Canonical' but 'Ecclesiastical'": the Wisdom of Solomon, the Wisdom of the Son of Sirach (Ecclesiasticus), Tobit, Judith, and the Maccabees — "all of which they would have read in the Churches, but not appealed to for the confirmation of doctrine"; and below that the Apocrypha, which "they would not have read in the Churches" at all.113 His operative formula — read in church, but not to prove doctrine — is almost word-for-word Jerome's, and the rank Athanasius had called "appointed by the Fathers to be read." On the canon these two antagonists agree, and both differ from Augustine and the African councils, who list the disputed books in the canon. Rufinus's scheme has three ranks: Canonical, Ecclesiastical, Apocrypha. In his exegesis Rufinus also cites a book he files as Ecclesiastical: expounding the blessing of Gad in his Commentary on the Blessings of the Patriarchs, he introduces Sirach 34 with "ita enim Scriptura dicit" ("for thus the Scripture says") and uses it on a point of moral doctrine.113

Innocent I (c. 405): a list including the deuterocanon, sent from Rome

A sitting bishop of Rome restates the Western verdict. Answering a query from Exuperius, bishop of Toulouse, on which books "really are received in the canon," Innocent I appends a canon-list whose Old Testament is the longer Septuagint form: Tobit, Judith, and the two books of Maccabees named outright among "the historical books," and the deuterocanonical Wisdom and Ecclesiasticus (Sirach) carried in "the five books of Solomon."114 He files these books among the canon, with none of the "read but not canonical" qualification that Athanasius, Jerome, and Rufinus had attached to the very same titles .115 The letter came a decade after the African councils of Hippo and Carthage. (The same letter's New-Testament list, and its condemnation of the forged apostolic gospels, are treated on the companion Biblical Canon page.)

John Cassian (c. 426 AD): Wisdom cited as "Scripture itself testifies," Baruch as "the prophet"; no list

The father of Western monasticism, carrying the desert tradition of Egypt into Gaul, cites the disputed books as Scripture and prophecy in the very work that shaped Latin monastic life. John Cassian (c. 360 – c. 435), whose Conferences (Collationes, c. 426–429) report the teaching of the Egyptian abbots to the monks of Marseilles, introduces the deuterocanonical Wisdom of Solomon with the bare formula he reserves for Scripture: "as Scripture itself testifies: 'For God made not death, neither rejoices in the destruction of the living'" (Wisdom 1:13).116 He quotes the deuterocanonical Baruch as the word of "the prophet" — "nor hear the words of the prophet: Why are you grown old in a strange country?" (Baruch 3:11) — the same book Athenagoras and Irenaeus had cited under the prophets' name.117 And expounding the remedies for sin he sets Sirach beside the apostles — "As water extinguishes fire, so does almsgiving extinguish sin" (Sirach 3:33) — in one chain with 1 Peter 4:8, Matthew 6:14, and James 5:20.118 He draws up no canon-list, so renders no canon-verdict; he simply uses the disputed books as Scripture — a Western, monastic datapoint of the 420s, contemporary with the close of the African councils, on the "using" side discussed under Disputed readings below.

Vincent of Lérins (c. 434 AD): Sirach quoted among "the divine Oracles," with Proverbs and Ecclesiastes; no list

At the very hinge of the West's classic rule for telling catholic truth from heresy, a disputed book is named among "the divine Oracles." In his Commonitory (AD 434) Vincent of Lérins (c. 400 – c. 445), the Gallic monk whose "what has been believed everywhere, always, and by all" became the touchstone of orthodoxy, clinches his thesis that the faith may never be added to by stringing three wisdom-sayings together under one formula — "the divine Oracles cry aloud, 'Remove not the landmarks, which thy fathers have set,' and 'Go not to law with a Judge,' and 'Whoso breaketh through a fence a serpent shall bite him'" — and the middle of the three is the deuterocanonical Sirach (8:14), set without distinction between Proverbs 22:28 and Ecclesiastes 10:8.119 He draws up no canon-list, so renders no canon-verdict; he simply uses the disputed book as Scripture, on a par with the two undisputed wisdom books beside it — a Western, monastic datapoint of the 430s, a near-contemporary of John Cassian on the same Gallic coast, on the "using" side discussed under Disputed readings below.

Junilius Africanus (c. 542 AD): a first class of "perfect authority" and a second of "middle authority"

From Justinian's court comes a tiered canon. Junilius, a North-African layman serving as quaestor sacri palatii at Constantinople, turned into Latin a Greek handbook of Scripture-teaching learned from Paul the Persian of the school of Nisibis, and near its start graded the books by authority: some "of perfect authority" (perfectae auctoritatis), the canonical class; some "of middle" authority (mediae), "added by many"; and some of none.120 Into the second, "middle" rank he files the deuterocanonical Tobit, Judith, and Maccabees, and the Wisdom of Solomon — and, unusually, the protocanonical Chronicles, Job, Ezra, Esther, and Song of Songs with them — on the ground that "among the Hebrews too they were received with this same distinction, as Jerome and the rest testify"; yet he keeps the deuterocanonical Sirach inside his first, canonical class beside Proverbs.121 He is a near-contemporary of the Synopsis, which also ranks the books.

The Synopsis of Sacred Scripture (c. 550 AD): a Hebrew-sized list; other books "only read"

A handbook transmitted under Athanasius's name but later in date, the Synopsis Scripturae Sacrae repeats his ranking. Its canonical Old Testament is the Hebrew-sized list, the disputed books absent; but it then sets out a second rank — books "not… canonical, but only read" to the catechumens — and there it files the deuterocanon: the Wisdom of Solomon, the Wisdom of Sirach, Esther, Judith, and Tobit.122 Its second rank matches Athanasius's books "appointed by the Fathers to be read."

Gregory the Great (c. 590 AD): quotes Wisdom, Tobit and Sirach as Scripture; calls 1 Maccabees "not Canonical"

A bishop of Rome cites the deuterocanon as Scripture and, of one book, withholds the word canonical. In his Register III.53 Gregory the Great (c. 540–604) adduces the Wisdom of Solomon (1:11) as what "the Truth says through His Scripture" — "The mouth that lies slays the soul";123 and in the Book of Pastoral Rule (III.21) he ranges Tobit (4:17) and Sirach (12:4) together under "what is written," beside Luke and Matthew.124 Yet in his great Morals on the Book of Job (XIX, §34), retelling Eleazar's death from 1 Maccabees 6, he pauses to qualify the source: "we are not acting irregularly, if from the books, though not Canonical, yet brought out for the edifying of the Church, we bring forward testimony."125 How the two usages bear on the early church’s reception is discussed under Disputed readings below.

Isidore of Seville (c. 636 AD): Jerome's count, with a further class read

The great encyclopedist of the Latin West restates Jerome's Hebrew canon. In the Etymologiae (VI.9) Isidore divides the Old Testament into the three Hebrew classes — Law, Prophets, Sacred Writings — totalling twenty-two books (some, he notes, add Ruth and Lamentations to make twenty-four), with the deuterocanon outside the count.126 But he then names a further class the Church "honors and proclaims" though it stands outside the Hebrew canon — Wisdom, Ecclesiasticus (Sirach), Tobit, Judith, and the Maccabees — a ranking like those of Rufinus and Jerome.

The Council in Trullo (Quinisext) (692 AD): earlier canons with differing lists confirmed together

The Eastern church's great disciplinary council issues no book-list of its own, but in its second canon confirms — together, without reconciling them — the canons of the earlier councils and Fathers, whose Scripture-lists differ; whether they contradict one another is disputed (see the interpretation page). Trullo ratifies "the eighty-five canons… in the name of the… Apostles" and the canons "at Laodicea in Phrygia… and those of Carthage… of Athanasius… of Amphilochius of Iconium" — but the Apostolic Canons and Carthage run the longer, deuterocanon-bearing Old Testament, while Laodicea, Athanasius, and Amphilochius rank the disputed books out or below.127 Its editor H. R. Percival describes the earlier canons as "received in block." The canon names the earlier collections without reconciling their lists; the next Eastern conciliar statement on the books on this timeline is the Synod of Jerusalem (1672).

Codex Amiatinus (c. 700 AD): a pandect including the deuterocanon

The oldest surviving complete Latin Vulgate in a single volume — a pandect made c. 700 at the Wearmouth–Jarrow monastery in Northumbria — embodies the longer canon physically: Codex Amiatinus carries Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, and the two books of Maccabees among its Old Testament books.128 Yet the same manuscript prints the disagreement in its own front matter: its opening leaves diagram three rival canon-divisions — Jerome's Hebrew-sized twenty-two, the Septuagint order, and Augustine's longer list — so the codex carries the books and, on its first pages, Jerome’s Hebrew-sized scheme.

John of Damascus (c. 730 AD): a twenty-two-book list; cites Wisdom as "the divine Scripture"

The last of the Greek Fathers gives the Eastern Hebrew canon its closing classic statement. In An Exposition of the Orthodox Faith (IV.17) John of Damascus arranges the twenty-two books in four "pentateuchs" plus two — the deuterocanon outside the count — and names the Wisdom of Solomon and the Wisdom of Sirach only to rule them "virtuous and noble, but… not counted nor… placed in the ark."129 It is the verdict of Epiphanius three centuries on, repeated almost word for word — the disputed books named as honoured but not counted. In the same work, two chapters earlier, arguing in IV.15 that the holy dead live and may rightly be honoured, John quotes the book his list leaves uncounted — "the divine Scripture likewise says that the souls of the just are in God's hand and death cannot lay hold of them," the Wisdom of Solomon 3:1 adduced under the bare formula "the divine Scripture."130 The Hebrew-sized list and the use of a disputed book as Scripture stand in one volume, as in Origen and Eusebius; the pattern is discussed under Disputed readings below.

The Stichometry of Nicephorus (c. 810 AD): the deuterocanon listed among the antilegomena

A line-count catalogue attached to the Chronography of Nicephorus, Patriarch of Constantinople, gives the last great Eastern list before the medieval Hebrew codices. Its canonical Old Testament is the Hebrew-sized protocanon (Baruch folded into Jeremiah); the deuterocanon is not omitted in silence but sorted into a named lower rank of antilegomena — "those spoken against" — where the books of Maccabees, the Wisdom of Solomon, Sirach, Esther, Judith, Susanna, and Tobit are each listed with their line-counts.131 Like the Synopsis, it sorts the books into ranks, here three: canonical, disputed, apocryphal.

The Masoretic Text (c. 1000 AD, Judaism): the text of the twenty-four books

The medieval Jewish standard text carries the Hebrew books alone. The Masoretic Text — the work of the Masoretes, scribal scholars of the seventh through tenth centuries who fixed the Hebrew Bible letter by letter, surviving in the tenth-century Aleppo Codex and the 1008/1009 Leningrad Codex — is "the authoritative Hebrew and Aramaic text of the 24 books of the Hebrew Bible (Tanakh) in Rabbinic Judaism," carrying the protocanonical books alone: Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch, and the Maccabees have no place in it.132 It did not decide the canon — it preserved the boundary the rabbinic stream had long since drawn (the "Council of Jamnia" marker) — but it gave that Hebrew-sized Scripture its permanent written form, and it is the text "most Protestant translations of the Old Testament" render; the thirty-nine-book Protestant Old Testament has the contents of its twenty-four books.132 On its scribes' fidelity the manuscript finds are reported thus: "most of the Qumran fragments… closer to the Masoretic Text than to any other text group," and the En-Gedi Leviticus scroll (c. 3rd–4th century AD) found "completely identical with the consonantal framework of the Masoretic Text".133 (The Septuagint-versus-Hebrew quarrel itself is gathered on the Rabbinic Corruption of Scripture and Origin and Inspiration of the Septuagint pages and not re-argued here.)

The Glossa Ordinaria (c. 1130, the standard medieval glossed Bible): Jerome’s prologues carried as front matter to the disputed books

In the medieval West the Hieronymian distinction was carried by a textbook. The Glossa Ordinaria — the standard glossed Bible assembled at the school of Laon in the early twelfth century (long misattributed to the ninth-century Walafrid Strabo, now ascribed to Anselm of Laon and his successors) and read "in every single center of theological learning" thereafter — carried, as front matter to Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, and the Maccabees, prologues declaring them outside the canon, read "for the edification of the people, not for confirming the authority of ecclesiastical doctrines."134 But those prologues are Jerome's own Vulgate prefaces reproduced: the 1913 Catholic Encyclopedia writes that the Glossa "embodied the prefaces in which the Doctor of Bethlehem had written in terms derogatory to the deuteros, and thus perpetuated and diffused his unfavourable view," even as the same church read those books in worship and bound them in every Vulgate.135 The most systematized "not in the canon" rubrics quoted in the modern debate come from a Renaissance editor's treatise (Bernardino Gadolo's, written for the 1495 Venice glossed Bible), not the twelfth-century gloss at all. Whether the Glossa shows the shorter canon to have been the medieval mainstream is set out under Disputed readings: Was the shorter, Hieronymian canon the mainstream of the medieval Latin West?136

The Council of Florence (1442): a list including the deuterocanon, in the bull Cantate Domino

The Council of Florence, reckoned in Catholic usage the seventeenth ecumenical council, issued in 1442 the bull Cantate Domino, the Bull of Union with the Copts. It professes that "one and the same God is the author of the old and the new Testament" and lists the books it "accepts and venerates" — and there, threaded into one undifferentiated list beside Genesis, Isaiah, and the Gospels, stand Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Ecclesiasticus (Sirach), Baruch, and the two books of Maccabees.137 The list matches those of the African councils and Innocent I, with no "read but not canonical" tier such as Athanasius, Jerome, and Rufinus had drawn. The canon-list sits inside a reunion profession, not a stand-alone canon-decree, and carries no anathema; the union with the Copts was not lasting. Its verb is suscipit ac veneratur, "accepts and venerates." Whether Florence or Trent was the first universal, binding statement of the canon is disputed; see the interpretation page.138

The Complutensian Polyglot (Cardinal Ximénez, c. 1520): the books "outside the canon" printed apart, received "for the edification of the people"

A cardinal’s Bible, printed with a papal privilege, sets the books outside the Hebrew canon apart. The Complutensian Polyglot, the first printed polyglot of the whole Bible, planned and financed by Cardinal Francisco Jiménez de Cisneros (Ximénez), printed at Alcalá 1514–17 and published in 1522 with the sanction of Pope Leo X, sets the books outside the Hebrew canon apart from the rest. Its prologue Ad lectorem professes that these are "books outside the canon, which the Church receives for the edification of the people rather than for confirming the authority of ecclesiastical dogmas" (libri extra canonem, quos Ecclesia potius ad aedificationem populi quam ad autoritatem ecclesiasticorum dogmatum confirmandam recipit) — Jerome's very formula, lifted from his Preface to the Books of Solomon — and marks them typographically, printing them in Greek with a double Latin column and no Hebrew.139 The prologue does not name Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, and the Maccabees one by one; it draws the line around a whole class and lets the missing Hebrew column mark which books fall on the far side of it. Leo X granted a privilege to print the work (traditionally dated 22 March 1520), and Gary Michuta (Catholic apologist) writes that the pope "approved the work… without passing judgment on whatever critical notes were attached to those books," and holding Ximénez's view stayed licit only because the canon "had not yet been defined" before Trent.140 The Polyglot's own Greek volumes also printed 3 Esdras and 3 Maccabees, books not on Trent's 1546 list.141

Cardinal Cajetan (Thomas de Vio, 1532, Roman Catholic): two senses of "canonical"; Jerome as the measure

The distinction is stated again by the cardinal who had examined Luther at Augsburg in 1518. Closing his Commentary on all the authentic historical books of the Old Testament (dedicated to Pope Clement VII, 1532) at the end of Esther, Cajetan writes that Judith, Tobit, the Maccabees, Wisdom, and Ecclesiasticus "are counted by St Jerome out of the canonical books, and are placed amongst the Apocrypha… as is plain from the Prologus Galeatus," and warns the "raw scholar" not to be disturbed at finding them called canonical in a council or a Father: "the words as well of councils as of doctors are to be reduced to the correction of Jerome."142 He then draws the two-tier line exactly: these books "are not canonical, that is, not in the nature of a rule for confirming matters of faith. Yet they may be called canonical, that is, in the nature of a rule for the edification of the faithful" — the two ranks of Jerome, Rufinus, and Athanasius. Bruce Metzger writes that Cajetan, "Luther's opponent at Augsburg," gave "unhesitating approval to the Hebrew canon," maintaining that the disputed books "must not be relied upon to establish points of faith, but used only for the edification of the faithful."143

Martin Luther (1534, Protestant): "not equal to Holy Scripture, yet useful and good to read"

In his complete German Bible of 1534 Luther gathered Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch, and 1–2 Maccabees — all but 1–2 Esdras, which he dropped — and printed them between the Old and New Testaments under the heading "Apocrypha — that is, books which are not held equal to the Holy Scriptures, and yet are profitable and good to read."144 In his prefaces he commends Judith as "a fine, good, holy, useful book, well worth reading by us Christians" and reads Tobit as edifying fiction of married life, while denying both the canonical rank — and in the Table Talk he files them by genre, Judith "a tragedy, setting forth what the end of tyrants is… like that of Homer about Troy," Tobit "a comedy concerning women, an example for house-government."145 The 1534 Bible prints the books between the Testaments under the Apocrypha heading. James Swan reads Luther’s distinction as Jerome’s; Gary Michuta holds that Luther began from the wider canon and reversed after Leipzig; whether Luther removed the books or relocated them is disputed; see the interpretation page. Two of the books carried texts adduced in the controversies of the time, 2 Maccabees 12 for prayer for the dead and Tobit 12 for almsgiving; whether doctrinal content drove the setting-aside is disputed; see the interpretation page.146

The Council of Trent (1546, Roman Catholic): the deuterocanon defined as canonical, under anathema

In its fourth session, the first to take up doctrine, the Council of Trent declares that it "receives and venerates with an equal affection of piety, and reverence, all the books both of the Old and of the New Testament," and prints the list — Tobias, Judith, Wisdom, Ecclesiasticus (Sirach), Baruch, and the two books of Maccabees threaded among the rest with no second tier — the same receptive verb (suscipit ac veneratur) the Council of Florence had used a century before.147 The decree anathematizes anyone who "receive not, as sacred and canonical, the said books entire with all their parts, as… contained in the old Latin vulgate edition" — the earliest canon-list on this timeline issued under anathema; "entire with all their parts" covers the Greek additions to Daniel and Esther.148 Whether Trent added the books or defined a canon already received is disputed; see the interpretation page. Its list does not name three texts the medieval Vulgate had carried, 3 and 4 Esdras and the Prayer of Manasses, which the Clementine Vulgate of 1592 printed in an appendix after the New Testament.149

The Thirty-Nine Articles (1571, Church of England): the other books read "for example of life," not to establish doctrine

The Church of England cites Jerome's rule by name. Article VI of the Articles of Religion defines canonical Scripture as "those canonical Books of the Old and New Testament, of whose authority was never any doubt in the Church," lists the Hebrew-sized Old Testament book by book, and then files the deuterocanon in a separate rank: "the other Books (as Hierome saith) the Church doth read for example of life and instruction of manners; but yet doth it not apply them to establish any doctrine."150 The list of those "other Books" is wider than Trent's: it brackets Tobias, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch, and 1–2 Maccabees together with the Greek additions to Esther and Daniel and the three texts (3–4 Esdras and the Prayer of Manasses) that Trent's list does not name — all alike read for edification, none admissible to prove doctrine.151 The Article names its authority for the second rank as Jerome (“as Hierome saith”). Whether doctrinal content drove the setting-aside is disputed; see the interpretation page.

The Westminster Confession of Faith (1647, Reformed): the Apocrypha "of no authority… than other human writings"

Chapter 1 of the Westminster Confession names the canon by inspiration — "all the books of the Old and New Testaments… All which are given by inspiration of God, to be the rule of faith and life" — and lists the Hebrew-sized thirty-nine Old Testament books (Genesis through Malachi) with the twenty-seven of the New.152 The next section rules on the disputed books: "The books commonly called Apocrypha, not being of divine inspiration, are no part of the canon of the Scripture; and therefore are of no authority in the Church of God, nor to be any otherwise approved, or made use of, than other human writings."152 Where Jerome, Rufinus, Luther, and the Thirty-Nine Articles had all kept the deuterocanon as edifying reading — read in church "for example of life," profitable "but not for confirming… doctrine" — Westminster sets the Apocrypha with "other human writings" and keeps no middle rank.153 Whether doctrinal content drove the setting-aside is disputed; see the interpretation page.

The Synod of Jerusalem (1672, Eastern Orthodox): the disputed books received as Scripture

The timeline closes in the East. A generation after Westminster, the Synod of Jerusalem — convened at Bethlehem by Patriarch Dositheus II to repudiate a Calvinizing confession that had circulated under the name of Cyril Lucaris, Patriarch of Constantinople — issued a ruling on the canon. The Confession of Dositheus names the books Lucaris had set aside as "Apocrypha" — the Wisdom of Solomon, Judith, Tobit, Bel and the Dragon, Susanna, the Maccabees, and Sirach — and declares them "genuine parts of Scripture," on a level "with the other genuine Books of Divine Scripture."154 It grounds them in the Church's delivering tradition: the same church "which hath delivered to us as genuine the Sacred Gospels… hath undoubtedly delivered these also as parts of Scripture, and the denial of these is the rejection of those." The Confession grants that "not always have all of these been considered on the same level as the rest," and holds that "these also have been counted and reckoned with the rest of Scripture, both by Synods and by many of the most ancient and eminent Theologians."155 The Longer Catechism of Metropolitan Philaret of Moscow (Moscow, 1830) counts the Old Testament at twenty-two books with Cyril of Jerusalem, Athanasius and John of Damascus, says Sirach “and certain others” are not in the count “Because they do not exist in the Hebrew,” and, after Athanasius, that they “have been appointed of the Fathers to be read by proselytes.” Kallistos Ware (Orthodox bishop, The Orthodox Church) reports that Jassy (1642) and Jerusalem (1672) declared the books “genuine parts of Scripture,” and that “most Orthodox scholars at the present day” hold them “part of the Bible” on “a lower footing than the rest of the Old Testament.” Eugen Pentiuc (Orthodox, 2014) describes the Orthodox position, traced from the early writers to John of Damascus, as strict about the thirty-nine books of the Jewish Bible and flexible about the anaginōskomena found in the Septuagint.156
