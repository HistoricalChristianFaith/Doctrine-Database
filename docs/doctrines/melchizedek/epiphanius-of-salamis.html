Epiphanius of Salamis: against the Melchizedekians — the sect that made Melchizedek a power above Christ

Dates: c. AD 315 – 403; bishop of Salamis (Constantia) in Cyprus from 367. The Melchizedek material is Panarion (“medicine chest”) 55, “Against Melchizedekians,” and 67, “Against the Hieracites,” both in the heresy-catalogue issued c. 375–377 · Wikipedia
Summary: Epiphanius gives the fullest ancient account of the Melchizedekians — a small sect, an offshoot of the Theodotians, who honored Melchizedek as “a sort of great power… greater than Christ,” reading Psalm 110:4 (“a priest for ever after the order of Melchizedek”) to make Christ the later and lesser priest. He records their proof-texts, their veneration of the figure, and answers them: Melchizedek was a man — a just king and priest of Salem, with a human father and mother — “not of the heavenly beings,” who did not come down from heaven. A separate entry (67) refutes the Egyptian ascetic Hieracas, who instead identified Melchizedek with the Holy Spirit. This is the same Epiphanius whose Panarion 64 gave Origen his numbered heresy-entry, treated on the timeline of verdicts passed on Origen.

Where it appears

The Panarion is Epiphanius’s catalogue of eighty heresies from Adam to his own day, begun in 375 and issued about 377. Heresy 55 is “Against Melchizedekians”; heresy 67 is “Against the Hieracites.” The two are kept distinct on purpose: the Melchizedekians proper (heresy 55) are the Theodotian sect who made Melchizedek a heavenly Power ranked above Christ; Hieracas of Leontopolis (heresy 67, and cross-referenced at 55) is a separate figure who identified Melchizedek with the third person of the Trinity. Epiphanius’s table of contents sums up the sect in a line: “Melchizedekians, who honor Melchizedek and claim he is a power of some sort and not a mere man, and have dared to ascribe everything to his name.”1

Claims & evidence

“A sort of great power… greater than Christ”

They honor the Melchizedek who is mentioned in the scriptures and regard him as a sort of great power. He is on high in places which cannot be named, and in fact is not just a power; indeed, they claim in their error that he is greater than Christ.

— Epiphanius, Panarion 55,1, in the Frank Williams translation, The Panarion of Epiphanius of Salamis, Books II and III, reproduced at the Internet Archive. This is the same doctrine Hippolytus had reported a century and a half earlier — that Melchizedek “constitutes the greatest power, and that this one is greater than Christ” — now set out at length.2

The proof-text: “after the order of Melchizedek” makes Christ the younger priest

Based, if you please, on the literal wording of, “Thou art a priest forever after the order of Melchizedek,” they believe that Christ has merely come and been given the order of Melchizedek. Christ is thus younger than Melchizedek, they say. For if his place were not somehow second in line he would have no need of Melchizedek’s rank.

— Panarion 55 (Williams). The whole sect turns on a literal reading of Psalm 110:4 the reverse of the Epistle’s: where Hebrews 7:3 makes Melchizedek the shadow “made like unto the Son of God,” the Melchizedekians make the Son the copy — a priest “after the order of” a being who therefore outranks him. The mediatorial version of the same claim is preserved by Pseudo-Tertullian: Melchizedek “was a heavenly Virtue of pre-eminent grace; in that Christ acts for human beings, being made their Deprecator and Advocate, Melchizedek does so for heavenly angels and Virtues” — a second, higher mediator for the angels, of whom Christ’s mediation for men is the inferior copy.3

The refutation: a man, with a father and mother, “not of the heavenly beings”

Epiphanius answers the sect by insisting on Melchizedek’s humanity. Melchizedek was indeed “just, holy, God’s priest, and king of Salem” — but a man, born of human parents (Epiphanius even reports the names some gave for his father and mother), who “was not of the heavenly beings, nor did he come down from heaven.” The “without father, without mother, without genealogy” of Hebrews 7:3, on which the sect leaned, describes the silence of the record, not a being without parents — the same reading the Greek Fathers pressed (Chrysostom’s “from its not being expressed,” Theodoret’s “if he had really been without father… he would not have been an image, but a reality”). Against the separate identification by Hieracas — that Melchizedek was the Holy Spirit — Epiphanius drives an incarnation reductio: “The Spirit never assumed flesh. And not having assumed flesh, he could not be king of Salem and priest of anywhere” (Panarion 55,5,4).4

Notes

  • Where it sits on this timeline. The Melchizedekian heresy is a reception-stratum anchored early — the sect arose around AD 200 (Theodotus “the banker,” a Roman group split from the adoptionists) and was first reported by Hippolytus c. 235, before Cyprian — but its fullest account is this one, written c. 377. Epiphanius is the witness the timeline block hubs; the heavenly-Melchizedek reading it exalts is itself the “dark endpoint” of a trajectory that runs from 11QMelchizedek and 2 Enoch.
  • The heresy is adjudicated on its own page. Whether the Melchizedekians’ central claim holds — that Melchizedek was a heavenly power greater than Christ — is weighed on the argument Was Melchizedek a heavenly power greater than Christ? (rated weak). The distinct Holy-Spirit identification of Hieracas is weighed on Was Melchizedek the Holy Spirit?
  • Not to be confused with the Hieracites. Epiphanius himself separates the two: the Melchizedekians (heresy 55) make Melchizedek a Power above Christ; Hieracas (heresy 67) makes him the Holy Spirit. Later heresiologists — Theodoret (Compendium of Heretical Fables 2.6) and Augustine (De haeresibus 34) — catalogue the sect after him, and Mark the Hermit later wrote a whole treatise Against the Melchizedekians.
  • The same Epiphanius. This is the heresiarch-hunter whose Panarion 64 gave Origen his numbered entry — his bio and method are set out on his witness page for the condemnation of Origen.
