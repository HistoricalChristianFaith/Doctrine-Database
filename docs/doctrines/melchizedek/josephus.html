Josephus: Melchizedek, the Canaanite founder-priest of Salem (= Jerusalem)

Dates: c. AD 37 – c. 100 (the Jewish War completed c. AD 75; the Antiquities of the Jews c. AD 93–94) · Wikipedia
Summary: The historicizing pole of the tradition. Against the heavenly redeemer-judge of Qumran and the allegorized Logos-priest of Philo, the Jewish historian Josephus reads Melchizedek as a real man — a Canaanite chief (Χαναναιων δυναστησ) whose name means “Righteous King,” the first priest of God, who first built the temple and gave the city, formerly Salem, the name Jerusalem. He is the chief early source for the equation Salem = Jerusalem and for the reading of Melchizedek as an ordinary (if remarkable) priest-king — the exact opposite pole from the exalted heavenly Melchizedek of 11Q13.

Where it appears

Josephus treats Melchizedek twice, once in each of his great works. In the Antiquities of the Jews I.10.2 (§§180–181) he retells the Genesis 14 scene: Melchizedek receives Abram, feasts his army, blesses him, and takes the tithe. In the Jewish War VI.10.1 (§438), pausing over Jerusalem’s history at the moment of its fall to Rome, he names its founder as this same “Righteous King” who first built its temple. In both places the interpretive move is the same and deliberate: Melchizedek is no angel and no cipher for the soul but a historical Canaanite ruler, and his city Salem is identified outright with Jerusalem — the reading that would ground the whole later “Jebusite / Canaanite priest-king” understanding of the figure.1

Claims & evidence

Melchizedek is “the righteous king,” made priest of God, and Salem is Jerusalem

Melchisedec, king of the city Salem, received him. That name signifies, the righteous king: and such he was, without dispute, insomuch that, on this account he was made the priest of God: however, they afterward called Salem Jerusalem.

— Antiquities of the Jews I.10.2 (§180), in the William Whiston translation, reproduced online. Josephus glosses the name exactly as Hebrews 7:2 and Philo do — “the righteous king” — but draws the opposite conclusion from theirs: not an allegory and not a type, but a plain judgment that the man deserved his priesthood (“such he was, without dispute”). The clause “they afterward called Salem Jerusalem” is the earliest explicit equation of the two names in a narrative source.2

Melchizedek feasts Abram’s army and receives the tithe

Now this Melchisedec supplied Abram’s army in an hospitable manner, and gave them provisions in abundance; and as they were feasting, he began to praise him, and to bless God for subduing his enemies under him. And when Abram gave him the tenth part of his prey, he accepted of the gift.

— Antiquities of the Jews I.10.2 (§181), Whiston, online. Josephus expands the terse Genesis notice into an ordinary act of hospitality: the “bread and wine” of Genesis 14:18 become abundant provisions for a returning war-band, and the blessing is spoken over a feast. There is nothing sacramental or typological here — the Eucharistic reading of the bread and wine is a distinct, later Christian development (weighed on this wiki’s real presence and ministerial priesthood timelines). What Josephus keeps from the source is the tithe, the mark of Melchizedek’s dignity that Hebrews would make so much of.3

The founder of Jerusalem: “a potent man among the Canaanites” who first built its temple

…he who first built it was a potent man among the Canaanites, and is in our own tongue called [Melchisedec], the Righteous King; for such he really was; on which account he was [there] the first priest of God, and first built a temple [there], and called the city Jerusalem, which was formerly called Salem.

— Jewish War VI.10.1 (§438), Whiston, online. Here Josephus is not retelling Genesis but reflecting, at the climax of the war, on how often Jerusalem had been captured — and he reaches back to its first founder. The identification is explicit and threefold: Melchizedek was (1) a Canaanite ruler (Χαναναιων δυναστησ), (2) the first priest of God, and (3) the first builder of the city’s temple, who renamed Salem as Jerusalem. This is the “mere Canaanite priest-king” reading in its strongest early form: Melchizedek is folded into the political and cultic pre-history of the Jebusite city that David would later take.4

Notes

  • Where it sits on this timeline. Josephus (writing c. AD 75–94) is placed after Philo (c. AD 40) and represents the third distinct Second-Temple reading of the same figure. The contrast across the three is the whole point of the early timeline: Qumran makes Melchizedek a heavenly elohim built from other texts entirely; Philo makes him an allegory of the rational soul drawn from Genesis 14; Josephus makes him a historical Canaanite priest-king and founder of Jerusalem. He is the pole furthest from Qumran — the “historicizing” option against the exalted one.
  • Salem = Jerusalem. Josephus’s flat identification (“they afterward called Salem Jerusalem”; “called the city Jerusalem, which was formerly called Salem”) is one of the earliest and most influential statements of the equation. It is not the only ancient view — Jerome would later report a rival tradition placing Salem near Scythopolis (Beth-shan) in the Jordan valley — but the Jerusalem identification, which Josephus does so much to fix, became standard and underlies the psalm-title tradition and the later Christian reading of Melchizedek as king of the holy city. It is also encouraged by the poetic parallel of Psalm 76:2 (“In Salem also is his tabernacle, and his dwelling place in Zion”).
  • A shift between the two works. Readers have long noticed that the War stresses Melchizedek’s Canaanite ethnicity (“a potent man among the Canaanites”) while the Antiquities, written nearly two decades later, drops the ethnic label and dwells on his righteousness and priesthood — a softening some attribute to Josephus’s deepening biblical study between the two works, others to the different rhetorical setting (a Roman war history versus a retelling of Scripture). Either way, both works agree on the core historicizing claim: a real man, the first priest of God, founder and temple-builder of the city later called Jerusalem.
  • What Josephus does not do. He makes nothing of the silences that Hebrews exploits — no “without father, without mother, without descent,” no eternal priesthood, no typology of Christ. For Josephus Melchizedek is simply an early and admirable figure of sacred history, which is exactly why he anchors the reading that resists every exalted or christological interpretation of the man.
