Josephus: Melchizedek, the Canaanite founder-priest of Salem (= Jerusalem)

Dates: c. AD 37 – c. 100 (the Jewish War completed c. AD 75; the Antiquities of the Jews c. AD 93–94) · Wikipedia
Summary: In contrast to the heavenly redeemer-judge of Qumran and the allegorized Logos-priest of Philo, the Jewish historian Josephus reads Melchizedek as a real man — a Canaanite chief (Χαναναιων δυναστησ) whose name means “Righteous King,” whom he calls the first priest of God, who first built the temple and gave the city, formerly Salem, the name Jerusalem. William Horbury treats him as the principal early witness to the equation Salem = Jerusalem and to the reading of Melchizedek as a historical Canaanite priest-king, set beside the heavenly Melchizedek of 11Q13.

Where it appears

Josephus treats Melchizedek twice, once in each of his great works. In the Antiquities of the Jews I.10.2 (§§180–181) he retells the Genesis 14 scene: Melchizedek receives Abram, feasts his army, blesses him, and takes the tithe. In the Jewish War VI.10.1 (§438), pausing over Jerusalem’s history at the moment of its fall to Rome, he names its founder as this same “Righteous King” who first built its temple. In both places the reading is the same: Melchizedek is a historical Canaanite ruler, and his city Salem is identified outright with Jerusalem.1

Claims & evidence

Melchizedek is “the righteous king,” made priest of God, and Salem is Jerusalem

Melchisedec, king of the city Salem, received him. That name signifies, the righteous king: and such he was, without dispute, insomuch that, on this account he was made the priest of God: however, they afterward called Salem Jerusalem.

— Antiquities of the Jews I.10.2 (§180), in the William Whiston translation, reproduced online. Josephus glosses the name as Hebrews 7:2 and Philo do — “the righteous king” — and draws from it neither an allegory nor a type but a judgment of character: the man deserved his priesthood (“such he was, without dispute”). The clause “they afterward called Salem Jerusalem” equates the two names explicitly.2

Melchizedek feasts Abram’s army and receives the tithe

Now this Melchisedec supplied Abram’s army in an hospitable manner, and gave them provisions in abundance; and as they were feasting, he began to praise him, and to bless God for subduing his enemies under him. And when Abram gave him the tenth part of his prey, he accepted of the gift.

— Antiquities of the Jews I.10.2 (§181), Whiston, online. Josephus expands the terse Genesis notice into an ordinary act of hospitality: the “bread and wine” of Genesis 14:18 become abundant provisions for a returning war-band, and the blessing is spoken over a feast. Josephus gives the scene no sacramental or typological reading; the Christian Eucharistic reading of the bread and wine is traced on the real presence and ministerial priesthood timelines. What Josephus keeps from the source is the tithe, which also figures in Hebrews 7:4–10.3

The founder of Jerusalem: “a potent man among the Canaanites” who first built its temple

…he who first built it was a potent man among the Canaanites, and is in our own tongue called [Melchisedec], the Righteous King; for such he really was; on which account he was [there] the first priest of God, and first built a temple [there], and called the city Jerusalem, which was formerly called Salem.

— Jewish War VI.10.1 (§438), Whiston, online. Here Josephus is not retelling Genesis but reflecting, at the climax of the war, on how often Jerusalem had been captured — and he reaches back to its first founder. The identification is explicit and threefold: Melchizedek was (1) a Canaanite ruler (Χαναναιων δυναστησ), (2) the first priest of God, and (3) the first builder of the city’s temple, who renamed Salem as Jerusalem. Melchizedek is here placed in the political and cultic pre-history of the city that, in the biblical narrative, David took from the Jebusites.4

Notes

  • Placement on the timeline. Josephus (writing c. AD 75–94) is placed after Philo (c. AD 40) and gives the third distinct Second-Temple reading of the same figure on this timeline: Qumran makes Melchizedek a heavenly elohim built from other texts entirely; Philo makes him an allegory of the rational soul drawn from Genesis 14; Josephus makes him a historical Canaanite priest-king and founder of Jerusalem. Horbury studies Josephus and 11Q13 on Melchizedek side by side.
  • Salem = Jerusalem. Josephus identifies the two in both works (“they afterward called Salem Jerusalem”; “called the city Jerusalem, which was formerly called Salem”). Jerome, Letter 73, reports a rival tradition placing Salem near Scythopolis (Beth-shan) in the Jordan valley, and also reports the Greek writers who made Melchizedek “the king of the city of Jerusalem.” The Jerusalem identification has the poetic parallel of Psalm 76:2 (“In Salem also is his tabernacle, and his dwelling place in Zion”).
  • A difference between the two works. The War names Melchizedek’s Canaanite ethnicity (“a potent man among the Canaanites”), while the Antiquities, written nearly two decades later, has no ethnic label and dwells on his righteousness and priesthood; the two works are of different kinds (a war history and a retelling of Scripture). Both present Melchizedek as a real man, the first priest of God, and the founder and temple-builder of the city later called Jerusalem.
  • Themes of Hebrews absent from Josephus. Josephus makes nothing of the silences that Hebrews reads — no “without father, without mother, without descent,” no eternal priesthood, no typology of Christ. In his two passages Melchizedek is an early figure of sacred history, a “righteous king” who deserved his priesthood.
