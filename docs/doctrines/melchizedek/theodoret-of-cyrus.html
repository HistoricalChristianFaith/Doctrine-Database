Theodoret of Cyrus: a real man and an image of Christ — against those who made Melchizedek a divine power

Dates: c. AD 393 – 458/466 (bishop of Cyrrhus in Syria from 423). The Melchizedek material falls in the Commentary on Hebrews (part of his commentary on the Pauline epistles, c. 435–448), the Eranistes (Dialogue II, c. 447), and the Questions on Genesis (c. 453); the Melchizedekian heresy is catalogued in his Compendium of Heretical Fables · Wikipedia
Summary: Theodoret closes the patristic line on this timeline by stating the orthodox reading with dialectical precision. Melchizedek is a real man — a Canaanite priest-king — and an image or type of Christ; he is called “without father, without mother, without genealogy” only “according to the dispensation of the divine Scripture,” because the record omits his lineage, not because he truly lacked one. “The image has not all the properties of the archetype”: what is said of Melchizedek by Scripture’s silence belongs to Christ “by nature and in reality.” Against the Melchizedekians — who made him a divine Power, even the Holy Spirit — Theodoret insists the figure is a man and a type, and that it is “Melchizedek [who was likened] to Christ the Lord,” not the reverse.

Where it appears

Theodoret returns to Melchizedek across his exegetical and controversial works. In the Commentary on Hebrews (chapter 7) he works through the Epistle’s argument phrase by phrase. In the christological dialogue Eranistes (Dialogue II, “The Unconfounded”) he uses Melchizedek as a test case for the logic of image and archetype — pressing an interlocutor to admit that an image never carries all the properties of its original. In the Questions on Genesis he treats the Genesis 14 scene directly and holds, with the majority of the Fathers, that Melchizedek was a real man (a Canaanite priest-king), not a heavenly being. And in the Compendium of Heretical Fables he catalogues and reprobates the Melchizedekians, who had made the priest-king a divine power. Across all four the interpretive centre is constant: Melchizedek is a man, a type; Christ is the reality.1

Claims & evidence

“The image has not all the properties of the archetype”

If he had really been without father and without mother, he would not have been an image, but a reality. But since these are his qualities not by nature, but according to the dispensation of the divine Scripture, he exhibits the type of the reality.

— Eranistes, Dialogue II, in the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers translation, reproduced online (also on this wiki’s source edition). The dialogue’s orthodox speaker draws the point out step by step: Scripture “records neither the father nor the mother of Melchizedek… to the end that he may be a type of him who is in reality without father and without mother.” Because Genesis, which gives the pedigree of Abraham, Isaac, Jacob and their sons, is silent about Melchizedek’s, he can serve as the image of the one who genuinely has no beginning; but an image, Theodoret insists, never possesses every property of its archetype — else it would be the archetype and not its type.2

Christ has these qualities “by nature and in reality”; Melchizedek by Scripture’s dispensation

Christ the Lord, of course, has each of these by nature and in reality: while as God he is “without mother,” being begotten only of the Father, as man he is “without father,” being born only of a mother—the Virgin, I mean… This was the reason he likened not Christ the Lord to Melchizedek, but Melchizedek to Christ the Lord: one was type of the other, and the other the realization of the type.

— Commentary on Hebrews 7 (on Hebrews 7:3), Robert C. Hill trans., Theodoret of Cyrus: Commentary on the Letters of St. Paul, vol. 2, pp. 162–163 (Holy Cross Orthodox Press, 2001); the passage is reproduced online. Theodoret is careful about the direction of the likeness: the type images the reality, never the reverse. On “without genealogy” he is equally plain — Melchizedek “was not of their family tree. So it is clear that he was not really without a family tree but only to provide a type” — the same discipline Chrysostom applied a generation earlier, now stated as a rule of type and archetype.3

Against the Melchizedekians: he was a man, not a divine power

Theodoret’s type-and-image reading is aimed, in part, at a live heresy. In the Compendium of Heretical Fables he lists the Melchizedekians — followers of Theodotus the money-changer, and (per Epiphanius) of Hierakas — who had made Melchizedek a divine Power or Virtue, some even identifying him with the Holy Spirit and ranking him above Christ. Against them Theodoret’s whole exegesis is decisive: if Melchizedek is “without father, without mother” only by the silence of the record, and if an image cannot hold every property of its archetype, then he cannot be a divine being, still less a power above the Son. He is a man who, by God’s ordering of the Scripture, was made a type of Christ.4

Notes

  • Where it sits on this timeline. Theodoret (c. 447) is the last of the Fathers on this timeline, following Augustine (c. 419). Where Augustine put the weight on the offering and the order, Theodoret concentrates on the identity question — and answers it with the sharpest statement of the type/reality logic the ancient church produced.
  • The Melchizedekian heresy is weighed elsewhere. The sect that made Melchizedek a power above Christ — reported by Hippolytus (Refutation 7.35–36) and Epiphanius (Panarion 55) as well as Theodoret — is treated on this wiki as its own reception-history question rather than settled here; Theodoret’s block records only that he stood against it.
  • A Canaanite man, with the majority. In the Questions on Genesis Theodoret takes Melchizedek for a real Canaanite priest-king — the reading Jerome reports as that of “most of the early fathers,” and the same historicizing pole as Josephus — not an angel or a heavenly being.
