11QMelchizedek (11Q13): the heavenly redeemer-judge

Dates: composition c. 100 BC (Hasmonean); the surviving copy in a Herodian bookhand, so the manuscript itself is usually dated to the later first century BC or the turn of the era · Wikipedia
Summary: The earliest datable interpretation of Melchizedek outside the Bible, and a startling one. In this fragmentary eschatological midrash from Qumran Cave 11, Melchizedek is no longer a mortal priest-king but a heavenly, quasi-divine deliverer — an elohim who presides over the final Day of Atonement and the tenth Jubilee, proclaims “liberty” to the captives (Leviticus 25 / Isaiah 61), and executes God’s judgment on Belial and the spirits of his lot. The text reads Psalm 82:1 (“Elohim stands in the divine council”) with Melchizedek as the judging elohim. Chronologically this witness sits between the biblical portrait and Philo — the wellspring of the later “Melchizedek = angel / heavenly being” reading.

What it is and how it was found

Among the fragments recovered from Qumran Cave 11 (discovered by Bedouin in 1956) were thirteen pieces of a single Hebrew manuscript that A. S. van der Woude first published in 1965 under the title “Melchisedek als himmlische Erlösergestalt” — “Melchizedek as a heavenly redeemer-figure.” Only the second column can be reconstructed at any length; the work originally ran to at least three columns. It is not a running commentary on one book but a thematic midrash (or pesher) that strings together a chain of scriptural citations — Leviticus 25, Deuteronomy 15, Isaiah 61 and 52, Psalms 82 and 7, Daniel 9 — and reads them all as pointing to a single eschatological drama of release and judgment, with Melchizedek at its centre. The copy is written in a Herodian formal hand, so the surviving manuscript belongs to the later first century BC or the early first century AD, while the composition is commonly dated somewhat earlier, around 100 BC.1

Claims & evidence

Melchizedek proclaims the eschatological Jubilee and “liberty” to the captives

And liberty will be proclaimed for them, to free them from [the debt of] all their iniquities… in the first week of the jubilee which follows the ni[ne] jubilees. And the d[ay of aton]ement is the e[nd of] the tenth [ju]bilee in which atonement shall be made for all the sons of [light and] for the men [of] the lot of Mel[chi]zedek.

— 11Q13, column II, in the García Martínez & Tigchelaar translation (The Dead Sea Scrolls Study Edition), reproduced online. The midrash takes the “liberty” (Hebrew deror) proclaimed in the Jubilee year of Leviticus 25:10,13 and the remission of debts of Deuteronomy 15:2, fuses them with the “liberty to the captives” of Isaiah 61:1, and projects them onto the last days: a climactic tenth Jubilee, whose Day of Atonement Melchizedek presides over, when the captives of his “lot” are released and their iniquities covered.2

Psalm 82:1 is read with Melchizedek as the elohim in the divine council

Elohim will [st]and in the assem[bly of God,] in the midst of the gods he judges.

— 11Q13, column II, quoting Psalm 82:1 (García Martínez & Tigchelaar), online. This is the text’s most audacious move. The psalm’s presiding ’elohim — the God who takes his stand in the council of divine beings to judge — is applied not to YHWH but, on the majority reading of the translators, to Melchizedek, who is then made the subject of the judgment-language of Psalm 7:7–8 as well. A heavenly Melchizedek is thereby seated among the elohim as God’s eschatological judge. (That the pesher’s “elohim” is Melchizedek rather than YHWH is how Vermes, García Martínez, and most editors render it, but the line is genuinely contested — see the Notes.)3

He executes God’s vengeance on Belial and the spirits of his lot

Melchizedek will carry out the vengeance of Go[d’s] judgments, [and on that day he will fr]e[e them from the hand of] Belial and from the hand of all the sp[irits of his lot.]

— 11Q13, column II (García Martínez & Tigchelaar), online. Over against Melchizedek stands Belial and “the spirits of his lot” — the powers of darkness. Melchizedek is the heavenly champion who wages and wins the final judgment against them, delivering “the men of the lot of Melchizedek” from their grip. A companion Qumran text names Belial’s counterpart-prince Melchireša’ (“king of wickedness”), the dark mirror of Melchizedek (“king of righteousness”); Paul Kobelski’s study reads the pair as opposing angelic princes, with Melchizedek functioning much like the archangel Michael, prince of light.4

A herald announces the good news — but the herald is not Melchizedek

the messenger i[s] the anointed of the spir[it]… the messenger of good who announ[ces salvation].

— 11Q13, column II, glossing Isaiah 52:7 (García Martínez & Tigchelaar), online. The “beautiful upon the mountains” herald of Isaiah 52:7 is identified as “the anointed of the spirit” (mashiaḥ ha-ruaḥ), tied to the “anointed prince” of Daniel 9:25 — a distinct messianic or prophetic figure who proclaims Melchizedek’s triumph, not Melchizedek himself. So the text carries two figures: Melchizedek the heavenly elohim-judge, and a separate anointed herald who announces his day of salvation.5

Notes

  • Where it sits on this timeline. By the age of its composition (c. 100 BC) 11QMelchizedek is the earliest interpretation of Melchizedek known outside Scripture, and it is placed here as the first witness after the biblical baseline and before Philo — the point at which the obscure priest-king of Genesis first becomes a heavenly being in the sources.
  • It never cites Genesis 14 or Psalm 110. Strikingly, the two Old Testament Melchizedek passages — the Genesis 14 encounter and the priestly oath of Psalm 110:4 — are absent from 11Q13. Its Melchizedek is built entirely from the Jubilee texts, Isaiah, and the Psalms of judgment. The exalted, heavenly Melchizedek of Qumran is thus not derived from the priest-king scene at all; it is a parallel development.
  • Contested at the line level: is the “elohim” of the pesher Melchizedek or YHWH? Vermes, García Martínez, and most editors take the presiding elohim of Psalm 82:1 to be Melchizedek — the reading that makes the fragment so remarkable. But it has been challenged: Rick Van de Water argues the elohim is YHWH, with Melchizedek his agent, which would soften the near-deification. The mainstream rendering stands, but the identification is debated, not settled.
  • Bearing on Hebrews. 11Q13 shows that a pre-Christian Judaism could already imagine an exalted, heavenly Melchizedek — helpful background for the Epistle to the Hebrews, which makes Melchizedek “without father, without mother, without descent… made like unto the Son of God” (Hebrews 7:3). But the parallel is thematic, not genetic: Hebrews builds its case precisely on Genesis 14 and Psalm 110, the two texts 11Q13 ignores, and Hebrews likens Melchizedek to the Son of God without making him a divine being in his own right. The two are independent Second-Temple trajectories from the same enigmatic figure.
  • Bearing on the divine council. The fragment is a prime exhibit that Second-Temple Jews read Psalm 82:1’s “elohim… in the divine council” as a scene among real heavenly beings, and could assign the presiding judge-role to a figure other than YHWH — the reading developed on this wiki’s Divine Council timeline and drawn on by the unseen-realm framework.
  • Editions. The editio princeps is A. S. van der Woude, Oudtestamentische Studiën 14 (1965): 354–373; the critical edition is F. García Martínez, E. J. C. Tigchelaar & A. S. van der Woude, Qumran Cave 11.II (DJD XXIII; Oxford: Clarendon, 1998), 221–241. Joseph Fitzmyer’s “Further Light on Melchizedek from Qumran Cave 11” (JBL 86, 1967) remains the classic study; Paul Kobelski, Melchizedek and Melchireša’ (CBQMS 10, 1981), the standard monograph.
