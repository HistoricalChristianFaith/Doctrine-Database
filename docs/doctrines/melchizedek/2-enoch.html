2 Enoch 71–72: the marvelous birth of Melchizedek

Dates: the Slavonic apocalypse 2 Enoch (the Book of the Secrets of Enoch) is of contested date — its core is often placed in the first century AD (before the Temple’s fall in AD 70, on the strength of its sacrificial interest), but many scholars date the surviving Slavonic form, and the Melchizedek fragment in particular, considerably later; the birth-legend (chs. 71–72) may be a distinct unit appended to the book. It is placed on this timeline as a reception-stratum, not a firm first-century anchor · Wikipedia
Summary: The closing chapters of the longer recension of 2 Enoch tell a birth-legend: in the generation before the Flood, Melchizedek is born miraculously from the corpse of Sopanima, the barren wife of Noah’s brother Nir the priest — already a fully-formed child “like one of three years old,” speaking and blessing God, with “the seal of the priesthood… on his breast.” The archangel Michael carries him into the paradise of Eden so that he survives the deluge and the priesthood continues. It is a second route to the “more-than-human Melchizedek” — parallel to, but independent of, the heavenly elohim of 11Q13 — and it literalizes the Hebrews portrait of a priest “without father, without mother… having neither beginning of days, nor end of life.”

Where it appears

The legend stands at the very end of the longer recension of 2 Enoch, after Enoch’s ascent and his instruction of Methuselah. Enoch is gone from the scene; the actors are now Methuselah, Nir (Noah’s brother, whom the Lord had clothed in the robes of the priesthood), Noah, and the infant Melchizedek. In the Morfill–Charles edition of 1896 this material is printed as a “Melchizedekian Fragment” appended to the translation (pp. 88–93); in the chapter-numbering now standard it is chapters 71–72. Because Enoch has already been taken up, the fragment reads as a separate narrative unit — more prose story than the ethical exhortation that fills the rest of the book — and its date and origin are debated (below).1

Claims & evidence

A miraculous conception: Sopanima bears a child though Nir “did not sleep with her”

And the wife of Nir, named Sopanima, being barren, brought forth no child to Nir. And Sopanima was in the time of her old age, and on the day of her death she conceived in her womb, and Nir the priest did not sleep with her, nor knew her from the day that the Lord appointed him to serve before the face of the people.

— 2 Enoch 71:1–2, in the W. R. Morfill translation edited by R. H. Charles (1896), reproduced online. The old and barren wife of a priest conceives without her husband; ashamed, she “concealed herself all the days, till she brought forth, and no one of the people knew.” When Nir at last sees her with child he rebukes her and orders her away — and at his word “Sopanima fell at the feet of Nir, and died.” The conception is thus both virginal-in-effect and posthumous in its issue: the child comes from a dead mother.2

The child comes forth from the corpse, fully formed and speaking

And then came an infant from the dead Sopanima, and sat on the bed at her right hand. And Noe, and Nir entered, and saw the infant sitting by the dead Sopanima and wiping its clothes. And Noe, and Nir were tempted with a great fear, for the child was complete in its body, like one of three years old; and spake with its lips, and blessed the Lord.

— 2 Enoch 71:17–18 (Morfill–Charles), online. The birth is doubly marvelous: the mother is already dead, and the newborn is no infant but a walking, speaking three-year-old who blesses God with his first breath. The motif deliberately outdoes an ordinary nativity — this priest is born already a fully constituted worshipper.3

“The seal of the priesthood was on his breast” — and they name him Melchizedek

And Noe, and Nir gazed upon it; and lo! the seal of the priesthood was on its breast, and it was glorious in countenance. And Noe, and Nir said ‘See the Lord renews the consecration according to our blood, as he desires’… And Noe and Nir hastened, and washed the child, and clothed it in priestly raiment, and gave it the blessed bread. And it ate. And they called its name Melchizedek.

— 2 Enoch 71:19–21 (Morfill–Charles), online. The infant is marked from the womb with a bodily “seal” or badge of the priesthood on his chest — the priesthood is inborn, not conferred by descent or ordination. Noah and Nir read the wonder as God “renewing the consecration” in their bloodline against the coming judgment, clothe him in priestly vestments, feed him “the blessed bread,” and give him his name. The scene fuses the two things the Genesis Melchizedek is known for — the priesthood and the bread — into the very moment of his birth.4

Michael carries him into the paradise of Eden to survive the Flood

But do not trouble thyself about the child, Nir, for in a short time I will send my chief captain Michael, and he shall take the child and place him in the paradise of Eden, in the garden where Adam was formerly… And this child shall not perish with those who perish in this generation… but shall be a holy priest in all things, Melchizedek.

And Michael took the child on that night on which he came, and took him on his wings, and placed him in the paradise of Eden.

— 2 Enoch 71:28 and 72:9 (Morfill–Charles), online. Warned in a night-vision that the Flood is coming, Nir is told the child will be spared: forty days after his birth the archangel Michael bears Melchizedek off “on his wings” into Eden, “and there shall he be for ever.” He thus outlives the deluge without entering the ark, and the priesthood passes down “the twelve priests” to a later, earthly Melchizedek — “the first king in the town of Salem” — the priest-king of Genesis 14. (Some accounts of the longer recension name the archangel Gabriel rather than Michael; the Morfill–Charles text reads Michael.)5

Notes

  • Where it sits on this timeline — and the date problem. The core of 2 Enoch is often dated to the first century AD (its concern with a functioning sacrificial cult suggesting a date before AD 70), which would make it roughly contemporary with Philo and Josephus. But the book survives only in later Slavonic manuscripts, its date and even its Jewish-versus-Christian origin are genuinely disputed, and the Melchizedek fragment (chs. 71–72) may be a separate composition attached to the work. It is therefore placed here as a reception-stratum block after Josephus, with the date-uncertainty flagged, rather than pinned to a firm first-century year.
  • A second road to the exalted Melchizedek. Like 11Q13, this legend makes Melchizedek far more than a Canaanite priest-king — but by a wholly different route. The Qumran scroll never mentions Genesis 14 and builds its heavenly elohim from Psalm 82, Leviticus 25, and Isaiah 61; 2 Enoch, by contrast, is a birth-and-priesthood story set in the antediluvian world. The two are independent developments of the same instinct — that so strange a priest cannot be merely a man — and together they feed the later “Melchizedek = heavenly / more-than-human being” reading weighed on this wiki’s argument pages.
  • It literalizes the silences of Hebrews. Hebrews 7:3 reads Melchizedek as “without father, without mother, without descent, having neither beginning of days, nor end of life” — an argument from the gaps in the Genesis record. 2 Enoch turns the figure of speech into narrative: a child with no human father, whose mother is dead before he draws breath, born already sealed a priest and swept out of ordinary history into Eden. Whether that convergence reflects Christian shaping of the fragment or an independent Jewish legend is part of what makes its origin contested.
  • “The great high priest, the Word of God.” One striking line has Nir foretell that Melchizedek “shall be the head over all, (being) the great high priest, the Word of God, and the power to work great and glorious marvels above all that have been.” The phrase “the Word of God” (Logos) reads to many as a Christian touch — and is one reason the fragment’s date and religious provenance are disputed — but it also rhymes with the Logos-priest that Philo drew from the same Melchizedek.
