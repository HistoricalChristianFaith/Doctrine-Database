Ambrose of Milan: a “holy man and priest of God,” not an angel, made on the model of Christ

Dates: c. AD 339 – 397 (bishop of Milan from 374). The Melchizedek passages fall across his episcopate: On Abraham and the Exposition of the Christian Faith (De fide) in the later 370s and 380s, On the Sacraments (baptismal catechesis) c. 390 · Wikipedia
Summary: Ambrose addresses two questions. On the identity question he states an identity where Cyprian’s reading was typological: in his words the Church holds Melchizedek to be not an angel “(as some Jewish triflers do), but a holy man and priest of God” who, by the silences of Hebrews 7:3 (“without father or mother”), prefigures the eternal Son. On the offering he reads the bread and wine as a Eucharistic figure — Melchizedek is “the author of the sacraments.”

Where it appears

Ambrose returns to Melchizedek in at least three works. In On Abraham (De Abraham) I.3 he expounds the Genesis 14 scene morally and christologically. In the Exposition of the Christian Faith (De fide) III.88–89, arguing against the Arians that Scripture’s language of Christ being “made” refers to the Incarnation, he uses Melchizedek as a test case for reading a figure rightly — and pauses to state what, in his words, the Church holds about what kind of being he was. And in the baptismal catechesis On the Sacraments (De sacramentis) IV–V he develops the bread and wine as, for him, the earliest figure of the Eucharist. Across all three the interpretive centre is the same: Melchizedek is a human priest whose office and offering are “made upon the model of Christ.”1

Claims & evidence

Not an angel but “a holy man and priest of God,” prefiguring the eternal Son

Let no man, therefore, when he beholds an order of human establishment, contend that in it resides the claim of Divinity; for even that Melchizedek, by whose office Abraham offered sacrifice, the Church doth certainly not hold to be an angel (as some Jewish triflers do), but a holy man and priest of God, who, prefiguring our Lord, is described as “without father or mother, without history of his descent, without beginning and without end,” in order to show… the coming into this world of the eternal Son of God.

— Exposition of the Christian Faith (De fide) III.88, in the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers translation, reproduced online. Here Ambrose states the identity directly. He names the reading that Melchizedek was an angel — a reading this timeline also records in 11Q13, and which Jerome reports from Origen — and rejects it. For Ambrose Melchizedek is a man, but a man in whom the recorded silences of Genesis were made to prefigure the fatherless, motherless, beginningless generation of the incarnate Word.2

“Received as a priest of God made upon the model of Christ” — the type and the original

This Melchizedek, then, have we received as a priest of God made upon the model of Christ, but the one we regard as the type, the other as the original. Now a type is a shadow of the truth, and we have accepted the royalty of the one in the name of a single city, but that of the other as shown in the reconciliation of the whole world.

— De fide III.89, NPNF, online. The framework (type/original, shadow/truth) is also Cyprian’s; Ambrose states it so: Melchizedek is the type, Christ the original; the king of one city (Salem/Jerusalem) is the shadow of the king who reconciles the whole world. In On Abraham I.3 the same christology is put as a question: “Who is the king of justice and the priest of God, except the one to whom it is said: You are a priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek… that is, the Son of God, the priest of the Father, who by the sacrifice of his own body reconciled the Father to our sins?”3

“He is therefore the author of the sacraments” — the bread and wine as the figure of the Mass

Melchizedek the priest met him, and offered him bread and wine. Who had bread and wine? Abraham did not have. But who had? Melchizedek. He is therefore the author of the sacraments. Who is Melchizedek?… Without father, he said, without mother, without genealogy, having neither beginning of days nor end of life: this is said in the Epistle to the Hebrews… Similar to whom? The Son of God.

— On the Sacraments (De sacramentis) IV, NPNF, reproduced online. Here Ambrose draws the bread and wine of Genesis 14:18 into full Eucharistic figure: the sacraments the newly baptized receive are “more ancient” than those of Moses because their figure “preceded” in Melchizedek, who is thus called “the author of the sacraments” and declared “similar in all ways to the Son of God.” The Eucharistic-type reading is traced on the real presence and ministerial priesthood timelines; here it stands as part of Ambrose’s reading of the figure.4

Notes

  • Placement on the timeline. Ambrose (c. 380) follows Cyprian (c. 253) and precedes Jerome (c. 398) and Augustine (c. 419). He is the earliest Latin witness on this timeline to state the identity explicitly: the priest-king is a man, not an angel, and the relation to Christ is prefiguration rather than identification.
  • “As some Jewish triflers do.” Ambrose’s aside rejects the reading of “some Jewish triflers” that Melchizedek was an angel. He does not argue against it at length; he sets it outside the Church’s reading. Jerome’s Letter 73 (c. 398) surveys the several views, leaving the judgement to his correspondent and dismissing the Holy-Spirit thesis.
  • The angel reading vs. the “made like the Son of God.” For Ambrose the phrase Hebrews 7:3 uses of Melchizedek (“made like unto the Son of God”) is what shows he is not divine but a human figure of the divine — a likeness, a shadow, a type.
