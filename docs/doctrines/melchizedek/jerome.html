Jerome on Melchizedek: the survey of the opinions in Letter 73

Dates: c. AD 347 – 420. The key text is Letter 73 (To Evangelus, Ad Evangelum), written c. AD 398; the same material recurs in his Hebrew Questions on Genesis · Wikipedia
Summary: Letter 73 is the earliest extant survey of the patristic opinions on Melchizedek. Asked for his view of an anonymous treatise arguing that Melchizedek was “of a more divine nature” — even the Holy Spirit — Jerome searches the earlier writers and reports two camps: Origen and Didymus made him an angel; Hippolytus, Irenaeus, Eusebius of Caesarea, Eusebius of Emesa, Apollinaris, and Eustathius all held that he was “a Canaanite man, the king of the city of Jerusalem.” He then sets out, with its chronological reckoning, “the opinion of the Hebrews” that Melchizedek was Shem son of Noah, and closes by leaving the judgement of the witnesses to his correspondent while dismissing the anonymous author’s Holy-Spirit thesis.

Where it appears

The occasion of Letter 73 is a request from a certain Evangelus, who had sent Jerome an anonymous tract — “the volume… anonymous and authorless” — contending that the priest who blessed Abraham “was of a more divine nature and should not be considered among men,” and even “that the Holy Spirit appeared to Abraham” as Melchizedek. Jerome answers by searching “the books of the Ancients to see what each one said,” and replies “as it were, with the counsel of many.” The same doxographic material, compressed, appears in his Hebrew Questions on Genesis at Genesis 14:18.1

Claims & evidence

Origen and Didymus: Melchizedek an angel

And immediately on the first page of Genesis I found an exegesis by Origen concerning Melchizedek, in which he discusses the matter in many words and comes to the conclusion that he was an angel. With almost the same arguments your author has employed on the Holy Spirit, he speaks on the heavenly powers. I went on to his follower Didymus, and came to a like result.

— Letter 73 (To Evangelus) §2, in the translation reproduced at Letter 73. Jerome records the “heavenly being” reading in Origen and his follower Didymus and observes that the anonymous author’s Holy-Spirit thesis uses “almost the same arguments.” The exalted Melchizedek of 11Q13 is an earlier Jewish attestation of a heavenly Melchizedek.2

The Greek writers’ consensus: “a Canaanite man, the king of the city of Jerusalem”

I turned to Hippolytus, Irenaeus, Eusebius of Caesarea, and Emisenus, Apollinaris also, and our Eustathius, who first sang the war trumpet against Arius as the Bishop of Antioch’s Church: and I found that all of their opinions, with different arguments and byways, had arrived at one intersection, to say that Melchizedek was a Canaanite man, the king of the city of Jerusalem, which was first called Salem, then Jebus, and finally Jerusalem.

— Letter 73 §2, online. On this reading, which Josephus also gives, Melchizedek was a real man, a Canaanite priest-king of the city later called Jerusalem. The letter adds that it is “not surprising if a priest of the most high God is described without circumcision and legal ceremonies,” since Abel, Enoch, Noah and Job also offered sacrifice; and in setting out what he has “read… in the books of the Greeks” (§§2–5) it glosses the agenealogētos of Hebrews 7:3 as meaning that Melchizedek “suddenly appears in Genesis” and is not mentioned afterwards.3

“The opinion of the Hebrews”: Melchizedek as Shem son of Noah, with the reckoning

Jerome then relays, at length and in the third person, the Jewish identification of Melchizedek with Shem, together with its supporting chronology: reckoning the generations, “from the birth of Shem to the generation of Abram, three hundred and ninety years,” so that “Shem, his tenth-degree grandson, survived Abraham by thirty-five years.” His Hebrew Questions on Genesis gives the same in brief — “They say that this man is Shem, the son of Noah, and by reckoning up the years of his life they show that he lived until Isaac” — before turning to the Eucharistic reading (“not at all through Aaron, by sacrificing irrational victims, but by the offering of bread and wine, that is, the body and blood of the Lord Jesus”).4 The letter presents the Shem identification as what “they say”; Jerome closes it by telling Evangelus, “It was my responsibility to cite (or repeat) witnesses; it is your responsibility to judge the credibility of the witness,” while dismissing the anonymous author who “pronounced with such arrogance and authority that Melchizedek had the Holy Spirit.” The introductory note printed with the letter summarizes Jerome as repudiating the Holy-Spirit theory “as absurd” and insisting “that Melchisedek was a real man, possibly, as the Jews said, Shem the eldest son of Noah.” The identification is set out as a disputed reading on Was Melchizedek Shem son of Noah?, and the rabbinic development on the rabbinic-tradition witness page.5

Reception and placement

  • Placement on the timeline. Jerome (c. 398) follows Cyprian (c. 253) and Ambrose (c. 380) and precedes Augustine (c. 419). His Letter 73 is a survey: the earliest extant document to lay the competing readings of Melchizedek side by side.
  • Two uses of Letter 73. Letter 73 is cited on the rabbinic-tradition page and on Was Melchizedek Shem son of Noah? for the Hebrew computation it preserves. This page records Jerome as a witness in his own right — his doxography of the opinions.
  • The location of Salem. The consensus Jerome reports from the Greek writers (§2), like Josephus, places Salem at Jerusalem, “first called Salem, then Jebus, and finally Jerusalem,” and the Hebrew Questions on Genesis gives “the king of Salem means the king of Jerusalem” among what the Hebrews hold. §7 of the same letter, in the translation cited, places Salem at “a town next to Scythopolis, which until today is called Salem,” where “the palace of Melchizedek is shown,” in the region of Shechem. The letter thus records both locations.6
