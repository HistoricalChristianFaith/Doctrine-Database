Jerome: the survey of the opinions — Melchizedek was a Canaanite man, not an angel and not the Holy Spirit

Dates: c. AD 347 – 420. The key text is Letter 73 (To Evangelus, Ad Evangelum), written c. AD 398; the same material recurs in his Hebrew Questions on Genesis · Wikipedia
Summary: Jerome gives the tradition its first scholarly doxography. Asked to adjudicate an anonymous treatise arguing that Melchizedek was “of a more divine nature” — even the Holy Spirit — he combs the earlier writers and reports two camps: Origen and Didymus made him an angel; Hippolytus, Irenaeus, Eusebius of Caesarea, Eusebius of Emesa, Apollinaris, and Eustathius all held that he was “a Canaanite man, the king of the city of Jerusalem.” Jerome sides with the second, rejects the divinizing readings as absurd, and reports — without endorsing it as his own conclusion — “the opinion of the Hebrews” that Melchizedek was Shem son of Noah.

Where it appears

The occasion of Letter 73 is a request from a certain Evangelus, who had sent Jerome an anonymous tract — “the volume… anonymous and authorless” — contending that the priest who blessed Abraham “was of a more divine nature and should not be considered among men,” and even “that the Holy Spirit appeared to Abraham” as Melchizedek. Jerome answers by searching “the books of the Ancients to see what each one said,” and replies “as it were, with the counsel of many.” The same doxographic material, compressed, appears in his Hebrew Questions on Genesis at Genesis 14:18.1

Claims & evidence

Origen and Didymus: Melchizedek an angel — a reading Jerome catalogues to reject

And immediately on the first page of Genesis I found an exegesis by Origen concerning Melchizedek, in which he discusses the matter in many words and comes to the conclusion that he was an angel. With almost the same arguments your author has employed on the Holy Spirit, he speaks on the heavenly powers. I went on to his follower Didymus, and came to a like result.

— Letter 73 (To Evangelus) §2, in the translation reproduced at Letter 73. Jerome here documents the “heavenly being” reading at its most reputable source — Origen — and notes that the anonymous author’s Holy-Spirit thesis is only a variation on it. This is the earliest interpretive strand on this timeline, the one running from 11Q13; Jerome names it precisely in order to set it aside.2

The Fathers’ consensus: “a Canaanite man, the king of the city of Jerusalem”

I turned to Hippolytus, Irenaeus, Eusebius of Caesarea, and Emisenus, Apollinaris also, and our Eustathius, who first sang the war trumpet against Arius as the Bishop of Antioch’s Church: and I found that all of their opinions, with different arguments and byways, had arrived at one intersection, to say that Melchizedek was a Canaanite man, the king of the city of Jerusalem, which was first called Salem, then Jebus, and finally Jerusalem.

— Letter 73 §2, online. This is Jerome’s own settled position, and it aligns him squarely with Josephus against the exalted readings: Melchizedek was a real man, a Canaanite (Jebusite) priest-king of the city later called Jerusalem. He adds that it is “not surprising if a priest of the most high God is described without circumcision and legal descent” — the silences of Hebrews 7:3 need no supernatural explanation.3

“The opinion of the Hebrews”: Melchizedek as Shem son of Noah — reported, with the reckoning

Jerome then relays, at length and in the third person, the Jewish identification of Melchizedek with Shem, together with its supporting chronology: reckoning the generations, “from the birth of Shem to the generation of Abram, three hundred and ninety years,” so that “Shem, his tenth-degree grandson, survived Abraham by thirty-five years.” His Hebrew Questions on Genesis gives the same in brief — “They say that he is the son of Shem… by calculating the years of his life, they show that he lived until Isaac” — before turning to the Eucharistic reading (“not by offering irrational victims through Aaron, but by offering bread and wine: that is, the body and blood of the Lord Jesus”).4 Jerome carries “the opinion of the Hebrews” into Latin Christianity as a reported view, not as his verdict — his own conclusion is the Canaanite-man reading above. Notes

  • Where it sits on this timeline. Jerome (c. 398) follows Cyprian (c. 253) and Ambrose (c. 380) and precedes Augustine (c. 419). His distinctive contribution is not a fresh interpretation but a survey: he is the first witness to lay the competing readings side by side and adjudicate among them, which is why his Letter 73 is the standard early map of the whole question.
  • Two functions on this wiki, kept distinct. Letter 73 is cited on the rabbinic-tradition page and the Shem argument only for the Hebrew computation it preserves. This page records Jerome as a witness in his own right — his doxography of the opinions and his own Canaanite-man conclusion.
  • Salem = Jerusalem, with a rival. Like Josephus, Jerome identifies Salem with Jerusalem (“first called Salem, then Jebus, and finally Jerusalem”). Elsewhere, however, he records the competing tradition that placed Salem near Scythopolis (Beth-shan) in the Jordan valley — noting that ruins of Melchizedek’s palace were shown there — so he is also our witness that the location was not universally agreed.
