Eusebius of Caesarea: a real, non-Levitical priest of the nations — the type of Christ’s gentile-embracing priesthood

Dates: c. AD 260/265 – 339 (bishop of Caesarea in Palestine from c. 314). The Melchizedek passages fall in the Demonstratio Evangelica (Proof of the Gospel), composed c. 314–320 · Wikipedia
Summary: Eusebius gives the tradition its first sustained apologetic treatment of Melchizedek as the type of a non-Levitical, non-Aaronic priesthood. Melchizedek is a real priest — not chosen by men, not of Aaron’s tribe, uncircumcised, offering neither the sacrifices nor the temple-service of the Law — who nonetheless blesses Abraham as his better. Precisely because he stands outside the Levitical order, he prefigures the priesthood of Christ, which is likewise not by descent and embraces the nations; and his bread and wine foreshadow the “spiritual sacrifice” the Church now offers. Eusebius does not read him as an angel or a divine being — he is the human, priest-of-the-Gentiles pole later cited by Jerome for the “Canaanite man” consensus.

Where it appears

Eusebius handles Melchizedek chiefly in the Demonstratio Evangelica (Proof of the Gospel), his great apologetic against Jewish objections to Christ. In Book 4.15 he uses Psalm 110 to argue that the priest there sworn in “for ever” must be more than human — an “eternal priest… greater than human and mortal nature.” In Book 5.3 he turns to Melchizedek himself and reads the Genesis figure as the type that makes Christ’s priesthood intelligible: a priest with none of the marks of the Aaronic order, whose office and offering point forward to the Church’s. The whole treatment serves Eusebius’s larger thesis that the gospel restores the ancient, pre-Mosaic religion of the patriarchs, of which Melchizedek — a priest before Sinai, before circumcision, before the tribe of Levi existed — is the standing proof.1

Claims & evidence

A priest with none of the marks of Aaron — yet Abraham’s better

But he that is named Melchizedek, which in Greek is translated “king of righteousness,” who was king of Salem, which would mean “king of peace,” without father, without mother, without line of descent, not having, according to the account, “beginning of years, nor end of life,” had no characteristics shared by the Aaronic priesthood. For he was not chosen by men, he was not anointed with prepared oil, he was not of the tribe of those who had not yet been born; and strangest of all, he was not even circumcised in his flesh, and yet he blesses Abraham, as if he were far better than he.

— Demonstratio Evangelica (Proof of the Gospel) V.3, in the W. J. Ferrar translation (1920), reproduced online. Every clause is turned to one end: Melchizedek shares nothing with the Levitical priesthood — not its lineage, not its anointing, not its circumcision, not its sacrifices — and yet is manifestly greater than Abraham, from whom Aaron himself descends. The silences of Hebrews 7:3 (“without father, without mother”) Eusebius reads with the Epistle as marks of a priesthood defined not by birth but by God’s appointment.2

The bread and wine as the Church’s “spiritual sacrifice”

For just as he, who was priest of the Gentiles, is not represented as offering outward sacrifices, but as blessing Abraham only with wine and bread, in exactly the same way our Lord and Saviour Himself first, and then all His priests among all nations, perform the spiritual sacrifice according to the customs of the Church, and with wine and bread darkly express the mysteries of His Body and saving Blood.

— Demonstratio Evangelica V.3 (Ferrar), online. Here Eusebius, a full generation before the Latin sacramental readings of Cyprian and Ambrose had crystallized in the West, already draws the bread and wine of Genesis 14:18 forward to the Christian offering — but pointedly as a spiritual sacrifice offered “among all nations,” keyed to his theme that Melchizedek was “priest of the Gentiles.” This Eucharistic-type reading is weighed as such on this wiki’s real presence and ministerial priesthood timelines; here it stands as part of Eusebius’s argument that Christ’s is a priesthood after Melchizedek’s order, not Aaron’s.3

“A priest for ever” — the eternal priest of Psalm 110 who is more than human

Now this would transcend human nature. For it is not in man to last for ever, since our race is mortal and frail. Therefore the Priest of God, spoken of in this passage, Who by the confirmation of an oath received a perpetual and limitless priesthood from God, was greater than man.

— Demonstratio Evangelica IV.15 (Ferrar), online. The argument here is about the reality, Christ — the “priest for ever after the order of Melchizedek” of Psalm 110:4 whose perpetual priesthood shows him to be Son of God, not merely man. Note the careful asymmetry that keeps Eusebius on the human side for Melchizedek and the divine side for Christ: it is Christ, the antitype, whose eternity is real; Melchizedek is the type in whom the record merely omits beginning and end.4

Notes

  • Where it sits on this timeline. Eusebius (c. 318) stands between Cyprian (c. 253) and Ambrose (c. 380). He is the earliest of the Greek exegetes on this timeline and the first to make Melchizedek carry a full apologetic argument for a non-Levitical, gentile-embracing priesthood.
  • Cited by Jerome for the “Canaanite man” camp. When Jerome surveys the opinions in Letter 73, he lists “Eusebius of Caesarea” among those — with Hippolytus, Irenaeus, Apollinaris and Eustathius — who held Melchizedek to be “a Canaanite man, the king of the city of Jerusalem,” against the angel-reading of Origen and Didymus. Eusebius’s Demonstratio bears this out: his Melchizedek is a real human priest, not a heavenly being.
  • “Priest of the Gentiles.” The phrase is load-bearing for Eusebius. Because Melchizedek served God outside Israel, before the Law and the covenant of circumcision, he proves for Eusebius that true worship predates and outlasts the Mosaic economy — the very point his Proof of the Gospel presses against Jewish objectors. The exalted, heavenly Melchizedek of 11Q13 plays no part here; Eusebius needs a man of the nations for his argument to work.
