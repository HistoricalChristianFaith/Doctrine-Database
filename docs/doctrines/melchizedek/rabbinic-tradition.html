The rabbinic tradition: Melchizedek was Shem son of Noah

Dates: a reception-stratum drawn from the classical rabbinic corpus, c. AD 200–600 — traditions attributed to the tannaim (Rabbi Yishmael, early second century) and set down in the Palestinian Targums, the Babylonian Talmud (redacted c. AD 500–600), and the midrash Genesis Rabbah (c. AD 400–450) · Wikipedia
Summary: The dominant reading of the ancient synagogue is that Melchizedek was no gentile stranger but Shem, the eldest son of Noah, still living in Abram’s day on the long antediluvian lifespans. The identification keeps the priesthood inside the chosen line and removes the scandal of an uncircumcised gentile priest greater than the patriarch. But the same tradition also has Melchizedek lose the priesthood to Abram for blessing Abram before he blessed God (b. Nedarim 32b, on the word-order of Genesis 14:19–20) — a homily that reads Psalm 110:4 as the priesthood passing to Abraham’s line, the exact counter to the Christian reading of that verse. This is the Jewish counter-tradition to the christological Melchizedek of the Epistle to the Hebrews. Whether Melchizedek really was Shem is weighed on the argument page, Was Melchizedek Shem son of Noah?

Claims & evidence

The Targums gloss “Melchizedek” as “Shem son of Noah”

And Malka Zadika — who was Shem bar Noah, the king of Yerushalem — came forth to meet Abram, and brought forth to him bread and wine; and in that time he was ministering before God Most High.

— Targum Pseudo-Jonathan on Genesis 14:18, Sefaria. Where the Hebrew names only “Melchizedek king of Salem,” the Aramaic paraphrase read in the synagogue supplies the equation outright: Malka Zadika (“righteous king,” the Aramaic for Melchizedek) “was Shem bar Noah.” The gloss identifies Salem as Jerusalem and Melchizedek as the surviving patriarch-priest of the antediluvian line.1

The chronology: Shem, on the long lifespans, outlived Abraham

The identification rests on a genealogical calculation: on the lifespans of Genesis 11, Shem was still alive throughout Abram’s life. Jerome, surveying “the opinion of the Hebrews” for a Latin readership, reproduces their reckoning exactly:

And they say that this was Shem, the first son of Noah, and at the time when Abram was born, he was 390 years old… Calculate the number of years through each age, and you will find from the birth of Shem to the generation of Abram, three hundred and ninety years. Now Abraham died in the hundred and seventy-fifth year of his age. By calculation, it is found that Shem, his tenth-degree grandson, survived Abraham by thirty-five years.

— Jerome, Letter 73 (To Evangelus) §5, reporting the Hebrew computation, Letter 73. The same instinct animates the midrash, which pictures Shem and Abraham as the two conspicuous righteous men of the age — “just as islands are conspicuous in the sea, so Abraham and Shem were conspicuous in the world” — each wary of the other after Abram slew the descendants of Shem’s son Elam at the battle of the kings.2

Melchizedek forfeits the priesthood to Abram — the counter-reading of Psalm 110:4

Rabbi Zekharya said in the name of Rabbi Yishmael: The Holy One, Blessed be He, wanted the priesthood to emerge from Shem, so that his children would be priests, as it is stated: “And Melchizedek king of Salem brought forth bread and wine; and he was priest of God the Most High” (Genesis 14:18). Once Melchizedek, traditionally identified as Shem, placed the blessing of Abraham before the blessing of the Omnipresent, He had the priesthood emerge from Abraham in particular, and not from any other descendant of Shem.

… Abraham said to him: And does one place the blessing of the servant before the blessing of his master? … Immediately the Holy One, Blessed be He, gave the priesthood to Abraham, as it is stated… “The Lord has sworn, and will not repent: you shall be a priest forever, because you are a king of righteousness [al divrati malki tzedek]” (Psalms 110:4), which is explained homiletically to mean: Due to the improper words [divrati] of Melchizedek, the offspring of Abraham shall be priests of God forever.

— Babylonian Talmud, Nedarim 32b, Sefaria. The Gemara clinches the point by reading the singular of the verse against Melchizedek’s heirs: “‘And he was priest of God the Most High’ (Genesis 14:18)… emphasizes that he, Melchizedek, is a priest, but his children will not be priests.” The reading turns the very verse the Epistle to the Hebrews makes the ground of an eternal Melchizedek priesthood into an etiology of the priesthood leaving Melchizedek for the line of Abraham (and so, eventually, Levi and Aaron).3

Abraham the high priest “after the order of Melchizedek”

Rabbi Yishmael says: Abraham was a High Priest, as it is stated: “The Lord has taken an oath and will not renounce it: You are a priest forever…” (Psalms 110:4)…

— Genesis Rabbah 46:5, Sefaria. The midrash independently attaches Psalm 110:4 to Abraham rather than to Melchizedek or to a coming messiah, confirming how firmly the rabbinic reading redirected the psalm’s oath into the patriarchal line — the mirror-image of the Christian application of the same verse to Christ.4

Notes

  • Why the synagogue read Melchizedek as Shem. The identification does two things at once. It removes the scandal that the Genesis scene poses to a covenantal reading — an uncircumcised gentile priest of El Elyon who is greater than Abram, blesses him, and takes his tithe — by making the priest a member of the chosen antediluvian line; and it keeps the priesthood, and the tithe, inside Israel’s own genealogy. It is best understood as a Jewish answer to the Christian use of Genesis 14 and Psalm 110:4 in the Epistle to the Hebrews.
  • A polemical inversion, not merely an identification. The striking move of Nedarim 32b is not that Melchizedek was Shem but that he lost the priesthood — a reading that cuts directly against Hebrews 7, which builds an unending priesthood “after the order of Melchizedek” precisely on the figure’s having “neither beginning of days, nor end of life.” Where Hebrews reads permanence, the Gemara reads forfeiture; the same psalm-verse anchors opposite conclusions.
  • Jerome is the bridge into Latin Christianity. Jerome’s Letter 73 (To Evangelus, c. AD 398) surveys the whole field of opinion — Origen and Didymus (an angel), the chain of Greek fathers (a Canaanite man), and “the opinion of the Hebrews” (Shem) — and carries the Jewish computation into the West; his own Hebrew Questions on Genesis repeats it (“They say that he is the son of Shem… and by calculating the years of his life, they show that he lived until Isaac”). Jerome does not firmly adopt the Shem reading — he leans, with the Greek fathers, to a real Canaanite priest-king — but he preserves the Hebrew view rather than suppressing it. His fuller treatment belongs on his own timeline entry; this page cites Letter 73 only for the Hebrew computation it reports.5
  • Whether the identification holds. The historical claim — that Melchizedek was Shem — is weighed adversarially on the argument page Was Melchizedek Shem son of Noah?, which rates it weak: it collides with the Epistle’s insistence that Melchizedek is “without father, without mother, without descent” (Shem being the most genealogically anchored man in Genesis), rests on no textual datum, and its one positive support — the chronology — depends on the shorter Masoretic reckoning and fails on the Septuagint’s longer one.
