Philo of Alexandria: Melchizedek as the Logos / “right reason”

Dates: c. 20 BC – AD 50 (this treatise, Legum Allegoriae III, composed in the early first century AD) · Wikipedia
Summary: The first interpretive reworking of the Genesis priest-king. Where Qumran built a heavenly judge from other texts entirely, the Jewish philosopher Philo reads the Genesis 14 scene itself allegorically: parsing “Melchizedek” as “righteous king” and “king of Salem” as “king of peace,” he makes Melchizedek a figure of the Logos — the “right reason” (orthos logos) that rules the soul as a lawful king rather than a tyrant, and pours out “bread and wine” (nourishment and glad, sober intoxication) to the soul. His etymological reading anticipates the gloss of Hebrews 7:2 and forms the bridge from the priest-king to the later Logos/christological readings of the Fathers.

Where it appears

Philo treats Melchizedek in his allegorical commentary on Genesis, Legum Allegoriae (“Allegorical Interpretation”) III, §§79–82 (chapters XXV–XXVI), expounding Genesis 14:18. As with everything in the Allegory, the surface narrative is a cipher for the life of the soul: the persons and places of the text stand for faculties, virtues, and dispositions of the mind. Melchizedek — whose name and city Philo glosses etymologically, exactly as Hebrews later will — becomes the ruling principle of reason within us. The passage is brief but weighty: it is the earliest surviving attempt to say what Melchizedek means by reading the Genesis scene itself, and it fixes two moves the Christian tradition would inherit — the “righteous king / king of peace” etymology and the assimilation of Melchizedek to the divine Word.1

Claims & evidence

Melchizedek is the “righteous king” and “king of peace” — a king, not a tyrant

Moreover, God made Melchisedek, the king of peace, that is of Salem, for that is the interpretation of this name, “his own high Priest,” without having previously mentioned any particular action of his, but merely because he had made him a king, and a lover of peace, and especially worthy of his priesthood. For he is called a just king, and a king is the opposite of a tyrant, because the one is the interpreter of law, and the other of lawlessness.

— Allegorical Interpretation III.79, in the C. D. Yonge translation, reproduced online. Philo reads the Hebrew name Malki-tsedeq as “righteous king” and the city name Salem as “peace” — the very double etymology Hebrews 7:2 gives (“King of righteousness… King of peace”), and here attested independently in a Jewish source of the same era. On this reading Melchizedek is “especially worthy of his priesthood” not for any recorded deed but for what his name discloses about his character: he is the lawful king, the antithesis of the tyrant.2

The kingly mind is guided by “right reason” — the pilot of the soul

Therefore the tyrannical mind imposes violent and mischievous commands on both soul and body, and such as have a tendency to cause violent suffering, being commands to act according to vice, and to indulge the passions with enjoyment. But the other, the kingly mind, in the first place, does not command, but rather persuades, since it gives recommendations of such a character, that if guided by them, life, like a vessel, will enjoy a fair voyage through life, being directed in its course by a good governor and pilot; and this good pilot is right reason.

— Allegorical Interpretation III.80, Yonge, online. Philo allegorizes the “just king / tyrant” contrast into two dispositions of the mind. The tyrannical mind rules by force and drives the soul into vice; the kingly mind rules by persuasion and steers “like a vessel” toward a good end — and its pilot is right reason (orthos logos). Melchizedek, the righteous king, thus figures the reason that governs the well-ordered soul.3

Salem is peace, and the “bread and wine” is the food and gladness reason brings the soul

We may therefore call the tyrannical mind the ruler of war, and the kingly mind the guide to peace, that is Salem. And this kingly mind shall bring forth food full of cheerfulness and joy; for “he brought forth bread and wine,” which the Ammonites and Moabites were not willing to give to the beholder, that is Israel; by reason of such unwillingness they are shut out from the companionship and assembly of God.

— Allegorical Interpretation III.81, Yonge, online. The bread and wine of Genesis 14:18 become the nourishment and joy the rational mind supplies to the soul. Philo contrasts this with the Ammonites and Moabites, who (per Deuteronomy 23:3–4) “did not come to meet you with bread and water” and so are barred from the assembly of the Lord — for him they figure the dispositions that reckon mind and sense, rather than God, the source of all things. Note that Philo’s bread-and-wine is a psychological allegory of the soul’s food, not a Eucharistic type; the Eucharistic reading of the same verse is a distinct, later Christian development weighed on this wiki’s real presence and ministerial priesthood timelines.4

“Reason is a priest” — the wine of sober, divine intoxication

But Melchisedek shall bring forward wine instead of water, and shall give your souls to drink, and shall cheer them with unmixed wine, in order that they may be wholly occupied with a divine intoxication, more sober than sobriety itself. For reason is a priest, having, as its inheritance the true God, and entertaining lofty and sublime and magnificent ideas about him, “for he is the priest of the most high God.”

— Allegorical Interpretation III.82, Yonge, online. This is the pivotal identification: “reason is a priest” (Greek logos hiereus) — Melchizedek the priest of “the most high God” is the Logos, whose “inheritance” (cf. the priest’s having no earthly portion but God alone) is the true God himself. The wine he pours is a “divine intoxication, more sober than sobriety itself” — Philo’s recurring figure of ecstatic, God-filled contemplation. It is this line that later readers cite as making Melchizedek a figure of the divine Word, and so the bridge to the Logos-christology the Fathers would draw from Hebrews.5

Notes

  • Where it sits on this timeline. Philo is the first witness to interpret the Genesis 14 scene itself allegorically, and he is placed after 11QMelchizedek (c. 100 BC) and before Josephus. The two Second-Temple readings are strikingly different: Qumran builds a heavenly Melchizedek from the Jubilee and Psalm texts and never touches Genesis 14, while Philo works from Genesis 14 to an allegory of the rational soul. Neither is the source of the other.
  • Did Philo mean the divine Logos? Since the mid-seventeenth century it has been standard to read §82 as identifying Melchizedek with the divine Logos — God’s Word, the mediating principle so central to Philo’s thought elsewhere. That is the mainstream interpretation and the reason Philo matters for the later christological tradition. But it is not beyond dispute: Philo uses logos here in senses that shade from “reason/right reason” (the ruling faculty of the soul) toward the cosmic divine Word, and some scholars argue the passage means only the former — the human rational principle — not a hypostatized divine Logos. The etymological and allegorical machinery is certain; the precise metaphysical weight of logos in §82 is debated.
  • Melchizedek elsewhere in Philo. Philo mentions Melchizedek only in passing outside this passage — e.g. in De congressu eruditionis gratia 89 and De Abrahamo 235 (on Abraham’s tithe) — but nowhere else develops the figure. Legum Allegoriae III.79–82 is his one sustained treatment, and the one the tradition remembers.
  • Bearing on Hebrews. Philo and the Epistle to the Hebrews independently give the same double etymology (“king of righteousness” / “king of peace,” Hebrews 7:2), evidence that the reading was current in first-century Judaism. But they diverge sharply on the point: Hebrews makes Melchizedek a type “made like unto the Son of God” who prefigures Christ’s eternal priesthood, while Philo makes him an allegory of reason within the soul. The common etymology is shared Second-Temple exegesis, not literary dependence in either direction.
