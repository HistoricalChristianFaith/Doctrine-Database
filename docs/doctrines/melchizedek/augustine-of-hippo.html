Augustine of Hippo: Melchizedek’s offering as the first appearance of the Christian sacrifice

Dates: AD 354 – 430 (bishop of Hippo from 395/396). The principal Melchizedek passages are in The City of God 16.22 and 17.17, written c. AD 419–420 · Wikipedia
Summary: Augustine reads the Genesis scene within the sacred history of Abraham: in Melchizedek’s bread and wine “first appeared the sacrifice which is now offered to God by Christians in the whole wide world.” Of Melchizedek “many and great things are written” in Hebrews; the psalm-oath (“a priest for ever after the order of Melchizedek”) means, on his reading, that the Aaronic order was to give way to Christ’s. He treats Melchizedek as a human, historical priest and concentrates on the offering and the order.

Where it appears

Augustine handles Melchizedek chiefly in The City of God: at 16.22, retelling Genesis 14 in the course of the sacred history of Abraham, and at 17.17, expounding Psalm 110 and its “priest for ever.” He also uses the tithe of Hebrews 7:9–10 (Levi paying tithes “in the loins of Abraham”) in his anti-Pelagian writings on original sin. Throughout, the exegesis is christological and ecclesial rather than speculative about the man’s nature.1

Claims & evidence

“For then first appeared the sacrifice which is now offered to God by Christians in the whole wide world”

He was then openly blessed by Melchizedek, who was priest of God Most High, about whom many and great things are written in the epistle which is inscribed to the Hebrews, which most say is by the Apostle Paul, though some deny this. For then first appeared the sacrifice which is now offered to God by Christians in the whole wide world, and that is fulfilled which long after the event was said by the prophet to Christ, who was yet to come in the flesh, “Thou art a priest for ever after the order of Melchizedek,” — that is to say, not after the order of Aaron, for that order was to be taken away when the things shone forth which were intimated beforehand by these shadows.

— The City of God 16.22, in the Marcus Dods translation. In this passage the bread and wine are “the first appearance” of the very sacrifice the Church now offers everywhere. He notes in passing that the authorship of Hebrews is disputed (“most say is by the Apostle Paul, though some deny this”) and draws on its testimony to Melchizedek — “many and great things are written” of him there. The Eucharistic reading of the offering is traced across witnesses on the real presence and ministerial priesthood timelines.2

The order of Melchizedek and the order of Aaron in City of God 17.17

…there is nowhere a priesthood and sacrifice after the order of Aaron, and everywhere men offer under Christ as the Priest, which Melchizedek showed when he blessed Abraham.

— The City of God 17.17, Dods, expounding Psalm 110:4. Augustine reads the situation of his own day as the fulfilment of the psalm: the Aaronic priesthood is gone, yet “everywhere men offer”; on his reading the psalm speaks of a different, abiding priesthood, the one “which Melchizedek showed.” The eternal priest, he goes on, “furnishes with His own body and blood” the table foreshadowed by Melchizedek’s wine and bread.3

Levi paid tithes to Melchizedek “in the loins of Abraham”

Levi did not on this account subsequently not pay tithes, because he had paid tithes already in the loins of Abraham, but because he was ordained to the office of the priesthood in order to receive tithes, not to pay them… because they too, whilst in the loins of Abraham, had already paid tithes to Melchisedec.

— On the Merits and Forgiveness of Sins, and on the Baptism of Infants 2.39. Augustine takes up the argument of Hebrews 7:9–10 — that Levi, still “in the loins” of his forefather, paid tithes to Melchizedek and so ranks beneath him — and turns it in the debate over original sin and infant baptism. Augustine here adopts the Epistle’s reasoning about the greater priesthood: Melchizedek is above Levi, and so above Aaron, as Hebrews argues.4

Reception and placement

  • Placement on the timeline. Augustine (c. 419) is the last of the Latin Fathers on this timeline, after Cyprian (c. 253), Ambrose (c. 380), and Jerome (c. 398). Where Jerome surveyed the identity question, Augustine assumes a human priest of the Most High and concentrates on the sacrifice and the order.
  • The identity question. Unlike Jerome, Augustine does not rehearse the competing identifications; his interest is not who Melchizedek was but what his offering foreshadowed. He assumes, as Cyprian, Ambrose and Jerome’s Greek sources do, a real man, and reads the Genesis silences through Hebrews as a type of Christ.
  • The authorship note. Augustine’s remark that Hebrews is ascribed to Paul by “most… though some deny this” is an early-fifth-century witness to the state of the question in the Latin West; he cites the epistle’s testimony about Melchizedek in either case.
