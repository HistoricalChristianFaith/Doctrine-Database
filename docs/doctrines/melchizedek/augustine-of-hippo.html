Augustine of Hippo: Melchizedek’s offering as the first appearance of the Christian sacrifice

Dates: AD 354 – 430 (bishop of Hippo from 395/396). The principal Melchizedek passages are in The City of God 16.22 and 17.17, written c. AD 419–420 · Wikipedia
Summary: Augustine reads the Genesis scene as the hinge of salvation-history: in Melchizedek’s bread and wine “first appeared the sacrifice which is now offered to God by Christians in the whole wide world.” Of Melchizedek “many and great things are written” in Hebrews; the psalm-oath (“a priest for ever after the order of Melchizedek”) proves the Aaronic order was to give way to Christ’s. He takes for granted the human, historical Melchizedek of the Latin consensus, and puts the whole weight of the figure on the offering and the order.

Where it appears

Augustine handles Melchizedek chiefly in The City of God: at 16.22, retelling Genesis 14 in the course of the sacred history of Abraham, and at 17.17, expounding Psalm 110 and its “priest for ever.” He also uses the tithe of Hebrews 7:9–10 (Levi paying tithes “in the loins of Abraham”) in his anti-Pelagian writings on original sin. Throughout, the exegesis is christological and ecclesial rather than speculative about the man’s nature.1

Claims & evidence

“For then first appeared the sacrifice which is now offered to God by Christians in the whole wide world”

He was then openly blessed by Melchizedek, who was priest of God Most High, about whom many and great things are written in the epistle which is inscribed to the Hebrews, which most say is by the Apostle Paul, though some deny this. For then first appeared the sacrifice which is now offered to God by Christians in the whole wide world, and that is fulfilled which long after the event was said by the prophet to Christ, who was yet to come in the flesh, “Thou art a priest for ever after the order of Melchizedek,” — that is to say, not after the order of Aaron, for that order was to be taken away when the things shone forth which were intimated beforehand by these shadows.

— The City of God 16.22, in the Marcus Dods translation. This is Augustine’s most influential sentence on Melchizedek: the bread and wine are “the first appearance” of the very sacrifice the Church now offers everywhere. He notes, in passing, the contested authorship of Hebrews (“most say is by the Apostle Paul, though some deny this”) but treats its testimony to Melchizedek as authoritative — “many and great things are written” of him there. The Eucharistic reading of the offering is developed as an argument on this wiki’s real presence and ministerial priesthood timelines; here it stands as Augustine’s reading of the scene.2

The order of Melchizedek is now universal; the order of Aaron has ceased

…there is nowhere a priesthood and sacrifice after the order of Aaron, and everywhere men offer under Christ as the Priest, which Melchizedek showed when he blessed Abraham.

— The City of God 17.17, Dods, expounding Psalm 110:4. Augustine makes the historical situation itself the proof of the prophecy: the Aaronic priesthood is gone, yet “everywhere men offer” — so the psalm must speak of a different, abiding priesthood, the one “which Melchizedek showed.” The eternal priest, he goes on, “furnishes with His own body and blood” the table foreshadowed by Melchizedek’s wine and bread.3

Levi paid tithes to Melchizedek “in the loins of Abraham”

Levi did not on this account subsequently not pay tithes, because he had paid tithes already in the loins of Abraham, but because he was ordained to the office of the priesthood in order to receive tithes, not to pay them… because they too, whilst in the loins of Abraham, had already paid tithes to Melchisedec.

— On the Merits and Forgiveness of Sins, and on the Baptism of Infants 2.39. Augustine takes up the argument of Hebrews 7:9–10 — that Levi, still “in the loins” of his forefather, paid tithes to Melchizedek and so ranks beneath him — and turns it in the debate over original sin and infant baptism. The point for the Melchizedek tradition is that Augustine treats the Epistle’s reasoning about the greater priesthood as a fixed premise: Melchizedek is above Levi, and so above Aaron, exactly as Hebrews argues.4

Notes

  • Where it sits on this timeline. Augustine (c. 419) closes the run of the Latin Fathers here — after Cyprian (c. 253), Ambrose (c. 380), and Jerome (c. 398). Where Jerome surveyed the identity question, Augustine largely leaves it settled (a human priest of the Most High) and concentrates on the sacrifice and the order — the lines the Western Church would carry forward.
  • Silent on Shem and the angel. Unlike Jerome, Augustine does not rehearse the competing identifications; his interest is not who Melchizedek was but what his offering foreshadowed. He assumes, with the Latin tradition, a real man, and reads the Genesis silences through Hebrews as a type of Christ.
  • The authorship note. Augustine’s remark that Hebrews is ascribed to Paul by “most… though some deny this” is a useful early-fifth-century witness to the unsettled state of the question in the Latin West — and it does not weaken his use of the epistle, whose testimony about Melchizedek he treats as canonical either way.
