John Chrysostom: “without father, without mother” read as Scripture’s silence

Dates: c. AD 349 – 407 (presbyter at Antioch from 386; archbishop of Constantinople from 398). The Melchizedek material falls in the Homilies on Genesis (Antioch, c. 386–388) and the Homilies on Hebrews (delivered at Constantinople, published posthumously, c. 403–407) · Wikipedia
Summary: Chrysostom expounds Hebrews 7:3 phrase by phrase. On his reading Melchizedek is called “without father, without mother… having neither beginning of days nor end of life” not because he literally lacked parents or was immortal, but because Scripture, by design, records none of them — so that in this silence he can prefigure the Son, of whom these things are true not in the record but “in reality.” With this type-and-reality distinction Chrysostom keeps Melchizedek a man, “possibly self-appointed” as priest, and reserves the absence of beginning and end to Christ.

Where it appears

Chrysostom treats Melchizedek twice. In Homily 35 on Genesis, expounding Genesis 14:18 verse by verse, he identifies the priest-king of Salem, parses his name with Hebrews 7:2, and already flags that “in a particular manner he was to prove a type of Christ.” Then in the great Homilies on the Epistle to the Hebrews — above all Homily 12, on Hebrews 7:1–3 — he works phrase by phrase through the Epistle’s reading of the figure. In both treatments Melchizedek is a real man and a type, and Chrysostom distinguishes what Scripture omits from what actually exists.1

Claims & evidence

“Without father, without mother” — from its not being expressed

I do not assert this in fact (he says); the meaning is, we do not know when or what father he had, nor what mother, nor when he received his beginning, nor when he died. And what of this (one says)? For does it follow, because we do not know it, that he did not die, or had no parents? Thou sayest well: he both died and had parents. How then was he “without father, without mother”? How “having neither beginning of days nor end of life”? How? Why from its not being expressed.

— Homilies on Hebrews 12, in the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers translation, reproduced online (also in the source edition). Chrysostom reads the phrases of Hebrews 7:3 as statements about the silence of the text, not about Melchizedek’s nature: he had parents and he died, but because Genesis names none of it, he can stand in Scripture as a figure of the one who truly has no beginning.2

Type and reality: “not because they have not been written, but because they do not exist”

As in case of this man, we know not either “beginning of days,” or “end of life,” because they have not been written; so we know them not in the case of Jesus, not because they have not been written, but because they do not exist. For that indeed is a type, and therefore we say “because it is not written,” but this is the reality, and therefore we say “because it does not exist.”… But made like unto the Son of God. Where is the likeness? That we know not of the one or of the other either the end or the beginning.

— Homilies on Hebrews 12 (NPNF), online. Chrysostom guards the analogy on both sides. If the likeness held “in all respects, there would no longer be type and reality; but both would be type.” The comparison — which he likens to a painter’s sketch, alike in outline, unlike once the colours are laid on — keeps Melchizedek a man (whose beginning and end are unrecorded) and reserves eternity for Christ (whose beginning and end, in Chrysostom’s words, “do not exist”). On this reading Melchizedek is neither deathless nor more than human.3

A real priest of Salem, “possibly self-appointed” — and a type of Christ

On the other hand, he was a priest, possibly self-appointed, this being the way with the priests of the time, you see. So in fact his peers had either accorded him the honor on account of his preeminence in age, or he had made it his business to act as a priest, like Noah, like Abel, like Abraham when they used to offer sacrifices. In a particular manner he was to prove a type of Christ.

— Homilies on Genesis 35.16, on Genesis 14:18, in the Robert C. Hill translation (Fathers of the Church 82), reproduced online. Here Chrysostom sets out the human footing before drawing the type: Melchizedek is a priest of the pre-Mosaic pattern, as Noah, Abel, and Abraham sacrificed without a Levitical order. On the etymology he follows Hebrews 7:2: “King of righteousness… King of Peace… who is ‘King of Righteousness and of Peace’? None, save only our Lord Jesus Christ.”4

Reception and placement

  • Placement on the timeline. Chrysostom (his earliest Melchizedek treatment, the Homilies on Genesis, c. 388) falls between Ambrose (c. 380) and Jerome (c. 398); his fuller exposition in the Homilies on Hebrews comes c. 403. His reading is of a human priest-king made, by the silences of the record, a type of the Son.
  • Method. Chrysostom grounds the type in the text’s omissions (“from its not being expressed”). Philo, by contrast, reads Melchizedek as an allegory of reason within the soul; Chrysostom keeps him a man and lets the narrative’s gaps carry the type.
  • The exalted readings. Chrysostom does not name the angelic, Holy-Spirit, or “power above Christ” readings (for the heavenly Melchizedek see 11Q13); on his reading Melchizedek is “without beginning” only because Genesis is silent. Theodoret (c. 447) uses the image-and-archetype argument against the Melchizedekians by name.
