Cyprian of Carthage: Melchizedek’s bread and wine as the type of the Lord’s sacrifice

Dates: c. AD 200/210 – 258 (bishop of Carthage from c. 248/249; martyred 14 September 258). The Melchizedek passage is in Epistle 62 (to Caecilius, on the sacrament of the cup), written c. AD 253 · Wikipedia
Summary: Cyprian is the fountainhead of the Latin reading of the bread and wine of Genesis 14:18 as a type of the Eucharist. For him Melchizedek “bore a type of Christ”: the priest of the Most High who “offered wine and bread” and blessed Abraham prefigures the one greater priest, Jesus, who “offered that very same thing which Melchizedek had offered — that is, bread and wine, to wit, his body and blood.” The identity question is answered christologically (Melchizedek is a shadow of the coming Christ), and the whole force of the reading falls on the offering, not the man.

Where it appears

The text is Epistle 62, addressed to a fellow bishop, Caecilius, and written to insist that the cup of the Lord be mixed with wine (against certain who used water only) — the letter often called Ad Caecilium de sacramento dominici calicis. In the numbering of the Ante-Nicene Fathers this is Epistle 62; in the older Oxford and the modern critical (CSEL) editions the same letter is numbered Epistle 63. It dates to c. AD 253, in the last years of Cyprian’s episcopate.1 Marshalling the Old Testament figures of the Eucharist, Cyprian turns first to Melchizedek: because he brought out bread and wine and blessed Abraham, he is read as the earliest “image” of the sacrifice that Christ would institute and the Church would offer.

Claims & evidence

Melchizedek “bore a type of Christ”: the bread and wine prefigure the Eucharistic sacrifice

Also in the priest Melchizedek we see prefigured the sacrament of the sacrifice of the Lord, according to what divine Scripture testifies, and says, “And Melchizedek, king of Salem, brought forth bread and wine.” Now he was a priest of the Most High God, and blessed Abraham. And that Melchizedek bore a type of Christ, the Holy Spirit declares in the Psalms, saying from the person of the Father to the Son: “Before the morning star I begat Thee; Thou art a priest for ever, after the order of Melchizedek.”

— Epistle 62.4 (to Caecilius), in the Ante-Nicene Fathers translation, reproduced online. Cyprian fuses the two texts that anchor the whole tradition — Genesis 14:18 and Psalm 110:4 (in his Latin, Ps. 109:3–4, “Before the morning star I begat Thee”) — and reads the priest-king not as an allegory of the soul (as Philo) nor as a bare historical figure (as Josephus), but as a type of Christ. The silences of the Genesis scene are not his interest; the offering is.2

Christ offered “that very same thing which Melchizedek had offered… his body and blood”

That order is assuredly this, coming from that sacrifice and thence descending, that Melchizedek was a priest of the Most High God; that he offered wine and bread; that he blessed Abraham. For who is more a priest of the Most High God than our Lord Jesus Christ, who offered a sacrifice to God the Father, and offered that very same thing which Melchizedek had offered, that is, bread and wine, to wit, His body and blood?

— Epistle 62.4, ANF, online. This is the interpretive core: the “order of Melchizedek” is defined for Cyprian by the offering — “the order proceeds first from the sacrifice.” What Melchizedek did in figure (bread and wine), Christ did in truth (his body and blood); and the letter closes the parallel: “the Lord, completing and fulfilling, offered bread and the cup mixed with wine, and so He who is the fulness of truth fulfilled the truth of the image prefigured.”3

Notes

  • Where it sits on this timeline. Cyprian (c. 253) opens the line of the Latin Fathers on this timeline — followed by Ambrose (c. 380), Jerome (c. 398), and Augustine (c. 419). He is the earliest surviving witness to the specifically Eucharistic reading of the bread and wine, a reading that would become the dominant Western understanding of the scene.
  • The bread-and-wine typology is weighed elsewhere. Whether Melchizedek’s offering is rightly read as a type of the Eucharistic sacrifice is a distinct question, treated on this wiki’s real presence and ministerial priesthood timelines, where Cyprian’s letter is a foundational witness. This page records only his reading of who Melchizedek was — a type of Christ — and how he handles the Genesis scene.
  • Not an identity in the ontological sense. Cyprian does not ask whether Melchizedek was Shem, an angel, or a Canaanite; the “type of Christ” language is typological, not an identification of the man with the Son. That later Latin sharpening — explicitly ruling out the angel reading and affirming a “holy man” who prefigures Christ — comes with Ambrose and Jerome.
