Was Melchizedek Shem son of Noah?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: weak — the dominant rabbinic identification is chronologically possible only on the shorter Masoretic reckoning, rests on no datum of the Genesis text, and collides head-on with the one New Testament passage that theologizes Melchizedek · advanced by the rabbinic tradition and, in the church, by Ephrem the Syrian

Related arguments: the identity of Melchizedek — see also a Canaanite priest-king · an angel or heavenly being · the Holy Spirit · a pre-incarnate Christ

The claim

And Malka Zadika — who was Shem bar Noah, the king of Yerushalem — came forth to meet Abram, and brought forth to him bread and wine.

— Targum Pseudo-Jonathan on Genesis 14:18, Sefaria. The claim is that the “king of Salem, priest of the most high God” who meets Abram is not an unknown gentile but Shem, the eldest son of Noah — still living, on the long antediluvian lifespans, into the age of the patriarchs.1

Who advances it

This is the standard reading of the ancient synagogue. It is stated outright in the Palestinian Targums (Pseudo-Jonathan on Genesis 14:18 glosses “Melchizedek” as “Shem bar Noah”), assumed in the Babylonian Talmud (Nedarim 32b, “the priesthood… to emerge from Shem”), and woven through Genesis Rabbah. Jerome carried it into Latin Christianity as “the opinion of the Hebrews” in Letter 73 and his Hebrew Questions on Genesis, reproducing the Jewish chronological calculation in full, though only to reject the equation as his own verdict. One Father, however, adopted it outright: Ephrem the Syrian (c. 306–373), who in his Commentary on Genesis XI states flatly that “this Melchizedek is Shem” — a king and priest by inheritance from Noah, still living into the days of Jacob and Esau. What drew the rabbis and Ephrem alike was the same gain: it removes an uncircumcised gentile priest, greater than the patriarch, from the story. The rabbinic reception is documented on the witness page, the rabbinic tradition.2

The evidence for

The strongest case has three legs. First, the chronology is genuinely open on the received Hebrew lifespans. Reckoning the begettings of Genesis 11, Shem — who lived 600 years — was still alive in Abram’s day; Jerome reproduces the Jewish computation exactly (“from the birth of Shem to the generation of Abram, three hundred and ninety years… Shem… survived Abraham by thirty-five years”). Since this wiki does not discount a claim merely for requiring long lifespans — the text itself supplies them — a living Shem meeting Abram is not to be ruled out on that ground. Second, the identification does real explanatory work. It resolves the difficulty a covenantal reading feels most sharply — an uncircumcised gentile priest of El Elyon, outside the promise, who is nonetheless greater than Abram, blesses him, and receives his tithe — by keeping the priest, the blessing, and the tithe inside the chosen line. Third, it is the majority Jewish reading, attested early (the tannaitic attribution to Rabbi Yishmael) and consistently across Targum, Talmud, and midrash — not a one-off fancy.3

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The chronological premise is sound on its own terms: on the Masoretic numbers Shem does outlive Abraham, so the identification is not impossible, and it should not be dismissed as requiring a miracle — it requires only the longevity the text already grants. As a piece of inner-Jewish exegesis with a clear motive, it is coherent and long-standing.

What is overstated or imprecise. Three defeaters, none of which the identification survives well. (1) It contradicts the one text that makes Melchizedek matter. Hebrews 7:3 defines Melchizedek as “without father, without mother, without descent [agenealogētos], having neither beginning of days, nor end of life” — yet Shem is the single most genealogically anchored man in Genesis: named son of Noah, head of the Table of Nations, furnished with a father, sons, a 600-year lifespan, and a death within the primeval record (Genesis 11:10–11). To identify the priest “without genealogy” with the very patriarch whose genealogy frames the primeval history is to assert the opposite of the datum the Epistle builds on. Jerome himself flags the point (agenealogētos). A proponent can soften this by reading agenealogētos as a statement about the record’s silence rather than an ontological lack — the argument-from-silence reading Ephrem himself held — on which a man with a recorded pedigree could still be “without genealogy” just where Genesis 14 omits it. But that only blunts the edge, not the thrust: the Epistle’s whole rhetoric trades on Melchizedek’s genealogical blankness, and to answer it by naming the single most fully-pedigreed man of the primeval line runs against the grain of the argument Hebrews is making. This defeater is reckoning-independent. (2) Its one positive support is reckoning-dependent. The chronology works only on the shorter Masoretic figures Jerome used. On the Septuagint long chronology — which this wiki rates arguably the stronger reckoning — the post-Flood begetting ages are each some hundred years higher and an extra generation (Kainan) is inserted, stretching the span from Shem to Abram by roughly eight centuries. On those numbers Shem is dead long before Abram is born, and the identification is not merely unlikely but chronologically impossible. So the reading is load-bearing on the short reckoning and collapses on the long one — the reverse of a robust argument. (3) It rests on no textual datum. Genesis never calls Melchizedek “Shem,” never calls Shem a king or a priest, and introduces Melchizedek abruptly as a stranger to the narrative. The equation is an inference drawn to remove a difficulty, not a reading the text offers. And its home context is polemical: Nedarim 32b deploys the identification to have Melchizedek forfeit the priesthood to Abram — the direct counter to the Christian “priest for ever after the order of Melchizedek.” That motive does not make it false, but it means the tradition is doing theological work in an inter-religious debate, not reporting an identification.4

Assessment

Weak. As a historical identification the reading does not hold on its best available case: it contradicts the agenealogētos of Hebrews 7:3 (Shem being the most fully-pedigreed figure of the primeval line), it rests on no datum of the Genesis text, and its sole positive support — the chronology — is possible only on the shorter Masoretic numbers and impossible on the longer Septuagint reckoning the wiki elsewhere rates stronger. It is not unsupported: it has a coherent motive, a genuine (if fragile) chronological toehold, and the weight of the dominant Jewish tradition behind it — which is why it earns a rated page rather than a footnote. The weakness is not the longevity (the text grants long lifespans and the wiki does not penalize a claim for that); it is Hebrews, the silence of Genesis, and the reckoning-dependence of the one thing the reading has going for it. The tradition’s more durable contribution is not the identification itself but its polemical inversion of Psalm 110:4 — the Melchizedek who loses the priesthood — documented on the rabbinic tradition page.
