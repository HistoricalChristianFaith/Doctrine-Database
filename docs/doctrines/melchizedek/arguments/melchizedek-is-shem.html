Was Melchizedek Shem son of Noah?

Disputed reading · on Who Was Melchizedek? · witnesses: the rabbinic tradition · Ephrem the Syrian · Josephus

The passage

And Malka Zadika — who was Shem bar Noah, the king of Yerushalem — came forth to meet Abram, and brought forth to him bread and wine.

— Targum Pseudo-Jonathan on Genesis 14:18, Sefaria.1

The question

Genesis introduces the “king of Salem” and “priest of the most high God” who blesses Abram and receives his tithe without naming his origin. The dispute is whether he is Shem, the eldest son of Noah, as the Targum, the Talmud and Ephrem the Syrian hold, or a Canaanite priest-king outside Abram’s line, as Josephus and the Greek writers Jerome lists hold; the answer bears on whether priesthood, blessing and tithe stay within the line of Shem, and on how Jewish and Christian readers used Psalm 110:4. No modern scholar holding the Shem identification was located in the sources opened for this page.

A Canaanite priest-king of Salem, outside Abram’s line

In brief: Melchizedek is a Canaanite king of the city later called Jerusalem, a gentile priest of El Elyon who is not of Abram’s family.

Held by: Josephus (Jewish historian, Antiquities I.180 and Jewish War VI.438, c. 75–94); Eusebius of Caesarea (Demonstratio Evangelica V.3, c. 318); E. A. Speiser (Genesis, Anchor Bible, 1964); Nahum M. Sarna (Jewish, Genesis, JPS Torah Commentary, 1989).2

Josephus names the founder of Jerusalem as “a potent man among the Canaanites,” called in his tongue the Righteous King, who “was the first priest of God, and first built a temple, and called the city Jerusalem, which was formerly called Salem.” Jerome, gathering the Greek writers for his correspondent Evangelus, found that “all of their opinions, with different arguments and byways, had arrived at one intersection, to say that Melchizedek was a Canaanite man, the king of the city of Jerusalem.”3

For the Christian holders of this reading his standing outside Israel is the point of the type. In the material Jerome gathers from the Greeks, “Melchizedek, because he was a Canaanite and not of the Jewish race, offered a type of the priesthood of the Son of God”; a priest without circumcision is no anomaly, “since Abel, Enoch, and Noah also pleased God and offered sacrifices.” Eusebius stresses that “he was not even circumcised in his flesh, and yet he blesses Abraham, as if he were far better than he.” Jerome’s Hebrew Questions sets beside the Hebrew report the Apostle’s use of the figure: “having the foreskin, he blessed Abraham the circumcised.”4

Speiser and Sarna, who hold the reading, set it in the chapter’s Canaanite background. Studies of that background supply its pieces: El Elyon, “God Most High, possessor of heaven and earth,” is read by Frank Moore Cross as old Canaanite religious language; J. A. Emerton identifies Salem with Jerusalem; and H. H. Rowley sets the name beside that of the Jebusite king Adoni-zedek (Joshua 10:1). On this reading Melchizedek is a priest-king of a pre-Israelite Jerusalem, serving its high god.5

Shem, the eldest son of Noah, still living in Abram’s day

In brief: The priest-king of Salem is Shem himself, priest by inheritance from Noah, who on the lifespans of Genesis 11 was still alive when Abram returned from battle.

Held by: Rabbi Yishmael (tannaitic, early second century), as the Babylonian Talmud reports him; Targum Pseudo-Jonathan on Genesis 14:18; the Babylonian Talmud (Nedarim 32b, redacted c. 500–600); Ephrem the Syrian (Syriac, Commentary on Genesis XI, c. 363–373).6

Targum Pseudo-Jonathan glosses Malka Zadika as “Shem bar Noah”: “Malka Zadika, who was Shem bar Noah, the king of Yerushalem.” The Talmud, in the name of Rabbi Yishmael, says that God “wanted the priesthood to emerge from Shem,” citing Genesis 14:18, and that because Melchizedek blessed Abraham before he blessed God, “He had the priesthood emerge from Abraham in particular, and not from any other descendant of Shem.” On this reading the priest, the blessing and the tithe all stay within the chosen line.7

The chronology of Genesis 11 carries the identification. As Jerome sets out “the opinion of the Hebrews,” Shem was 390 years old at Abram’s birth and “survived Abraham by thirty-five years”; the Hebrews also hold that “all the firstborn of the race of Noah” were priests until Aaron. Ephrem argues the same from the lifespans: “Because of the length of Melchizedek’s life extended to the time of Jacob and Esau, it has been stated, with much probability, that he was Shem.”8

Ephrem reads the patriarchs’ deference as pointing the same way. Abraham would not have given “a tenth of everything” to a mere Canaanite, and Rebekah would not have passed over Isaac and Abraham to consult a stranger about the twins in her womb. Shem was the greatest man then living: “This Melchizedek is Shem, who became a king due to his greatness; he was the head of fourteen nations. In addition, he was a priest. He received this from Noah, his father, through the rights of succession.” Settled at Salem, he stood “like a partition” between the sons of Ham and his own sons, guarding the blessed line from idolatry.9
