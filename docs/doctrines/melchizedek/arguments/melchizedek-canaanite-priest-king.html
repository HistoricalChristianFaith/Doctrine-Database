Was Melchizedek a Canaanite priest-king of El Elyon?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: sound — that Genesis presents Melchizedek as a real, human priest-king of (Jeru)Salem who served El Elyon, “God Most High,” is both the plain sense of the text and the reading of the patristic majority and the modern scholarly mainstream; what is genuinely contested is a further step — the historicity of the surrounding four-kings campaign and the date the passage entered Genesis — not the identification of the figure himself · advanced by Josephus and Jerome’s patristic consensus

Related arguments: the identity of Melchizedek — see also Shem son of Noah · an angel or heavenly being · the Holy Spirit · a pre-incarnate Christ

Related arguments: the God-fearing Gentile — on the Canaanite-priest reading Melchizedek is the archetype of the Hebrew Bible’s recurring outsider who already worships (or comes to confess) the God of Israel, the faith the New Testament reads as a foretype of the Gentile church (Matthew 8:11; Luke 4:27; 11:31); the theme runs across several timelines — see also Jethro’s conversion · Rahab in the line of Christ · the queen of the south as the Gentile church · Naaman’s confession · Nebuchadnezzar’s confession · the type as a canonical whole.

The claim

He who first built it was a potent man among the Canaanites, and is in our own tongue called [Melchisedec], the Righteous King… on which account he was the first priest of God, and first built a temple, and called the city Jerusalem, which was formerly called Salem.

— Josephus, Jewish War VI.10.1 (§438), William Whiston translation, reproduced online. The claim is the “plain historical” reading: the priest-king who met Abram was neither a heavenly being nor a patriarch in disguise but exactly what Genesis 14:18 says — a real man, king of Salem and “priest of the most high God” (El Elyon), a Canaanite (later called Jebusite) ruler of the city that would become Jerusalem.1

Who advances it

This is the tradition’s oldest and most durable reading. Josephus states it outright (Antiquities I.180–181; Jewish War VI.438), making Melchizedek a Canaanite founder-king of Salem = Jerusalem. It is the patristic majority: Jerome, surveying the opinions in Letter 73, reports that Hippolytus, Irenaeus, Eusebius of Caesarea, Eusebius of Emesa, Apollinaris, and Eustathius “had arrived at one intersection, to say that Melchizedek was a Canaanite man, the king of the city of Jerusalem,” and sides with them against the angel-reading of Origen and Didymus. Eusebius reads him as a real “priest of the Gentiles”; Ambrose as “a holy man and priest of God, not an angel”; Theodoret as a real Canaanite priest-king. And it is the modern scholarly mainstream: the standard Genesis commentaries read Melchizedek as a Canaanite or Jebusite priest-king of Salem worshiping the high god El Elyon — whether they judge the figure historical (Speiser, Sarna) or a literary construct inserted to link Abraham to the Jerusalem cult (Gunkel, von Rad, Westermann).2

The evidence for

The case is the strongest of any Melchizedek reading because it simply follows the text and its ancient Near-Eastern setting. First, the plain sense. Genesis 14:18–20 introduces him with no fanfare of divinity: a king of a named city who brings out provisions, blesses Abram, and receives a tithe — the ordinary acts of a priest-king, requiring no supernatural explanation any more than the priestly offerings of Abel, Enoch, Noah, or Job (as Jerome notes). Second, El Elyon is Canaanite religious language. “God Most High, possessor [qoneh] of heaven and earth” (Genesis 14:19) matches the vocabulary of the Canaanite high god: Elyon (“Most High”) is an ancient epithet of El, and the pairing recurs in the eighth-century Aramaic Sefire inscription (“El and Elyon”) and in the “creator of earth” formula known from Ugaritic and Phoenician. A priest of El Elyon in pre-Israelite Canaan is exactly what the reading supposes. Third, Salem is Jerusalem, and the city had a pre-Israelite dynasty. Psalm 76:2 sets “Salem” parallel to “Zion,” and the city appears as Uru-salim in the fourteenth-century Amarna letters — a Canaanite city-state centuries before David took it; its later king Adonizedek (Joshua 10:1), whose name shares the “-zedek” element, suggests ṣedeq (“righteousness”) was a standing feature of the Jebusite royal titulary — so “Melchi-zedek” (“my king is righteousness” / “king of righteousness”) fits a real dynastic name, not an invented one. On these grounds a broad range of scholars — Cross and Day on the El background, Emerton on the Salem = Jerusalem identification and a defensible traditional core, Rowley on the Jebusite priesthood — read the figure as a historical Canaanite priest-king.3

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The identification of the figure as a historical (or at least intended-as-historical) Canaanite/Jebusite priest-king of El Elyon at (Jeru)Salem is the plain sense of Genesis and the reading held across otherwise-opposed camps — the ancient historicizers (Josephus, the patristic majority) and modern critical commentators alike (Speiser, Sarna, Wenham, von Rad, Westermann, Cross, Day, Emerton all read the figure this way, whether or not they think the episode happened). It needs no speculation to fill the silences of the record and no appeal to the supernatural; it is the default from which every other identification is a departure.

What is overstated or imprecise — and what is not a defeater. Three limits, none of which unseats the identification. (1) The portrait is spare. Three verses give his city, his god, his gifts, and nothing else — no genealogy, birth, or death. The reading is genuinely thin, which is precisely the vacuum the later Shem, angel, Holy-Spirit, and Christophany readings rushed to fill; but thinness is a limit on how much we can say, not a reason to doubt what the text does say. (2) The Hebrews typology is not a rival. Hebrews 7:3 builds theology on the very silence of the record (“without father, without mother, without descent”) — but it does so by reading the absence of data as typologically significant, not by denying that a real man stood behind it. The historical question (“who was the man?”) and the typological one (“what does the text’s reticence signify?”) operate on different registers and need not conflict; the Fathers who most pressed the type — Chrysostom, Theodoret — are the same who most insisted he was a real man. (3) What is really contested is the episode, not the figure. The four-kings campaign of Genesis 14 (Chedorlaomer and his allies) has no external confirmation and reads to many critics like an antiquarian composition; and the Melchizedek unit is widely regarded as a later insertion legitimating the Jerusalem priesthood and tithe (Gunkel, von Rad, Westermann). Those debates — the historicity of the raid and the redaction-date of the pericope — are live, but they concern whether and when the scene was written down, not what kind of being it depicts: on the identification of the figure as a Canaanite priest-king, the “late insertion” critics and the “old tradition” defenders agree.4

Assessment

Sound. That Genesis presents Melchizedek as a real, human Canaanite (Jebusite) priest-king of El Elyon at Salem = Jerusalem is the plain sense of the text and the near-consensus reading of both the ancient church (the patristic majority against the exalted speculations) and modern scholarship (from the Anchor Bible and JPS commentaries to the critical Genesis commentators). Its one real limit is thinness — the record says very little, which is why the tradition kept reaching past it — and the genuinely open questions around Genesis 14 (the historicity of the eastern-kings campaign, the date the pericope was composed or inserted) concern the episode, not the identification of the figure, on which the otherwise-divided scholarship converges. The Hebrews typology is not a competitor to this reading but a second-order use of it: it reads the silence of the historical record, and so presupposes rather than displaces the man. This is the baseline against which the Shem, angel, Holy-Spirit, and pre-incarnate-Christ readings are all measured — and it is the one that holds.
