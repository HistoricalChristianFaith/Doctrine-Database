Ephrem the Syrian: “This Melchizedek is Shem” — the one Father who adopts the identification

Dates: c. AD 306 – 373; the Commentary on Genesis is a late work, c. 363–373 · Wikipedia
Summary: Ephrem the Syrian, the great Syriac poet-exegete, is the one Father on this timeline who takes the rabbinic identification of Melchizedek with Shem son of Noah as his own reading rather than reporting it to reject it. Where Eusebius, Ambrose, and Jerome held to a human Canaanite priest-king — and Jerome relayed the Shem equation only as “the opinion of the Hebrews” before declining it — Ephrem states flatly in his Commentary on Genesis XI that “this Melchizedek is Shem,” a king and priest by inheritance from Noah, still living into the days of Jacob and Esau. He grounds it in Shem’s longevity, in Abraham’s tithe, and in Rebekah’s consulting him. The identification is weighed and rated weak on its own page; Ephrem is its most important early Christian witness.

Where it appears

Ephrem treats the figure in his prose Commentary on Genesis, section XI, expounding the Genesis 14 meeting of Abram and Melchizedek. The same work is the sober, literal-historical Flood commentary (section VI) read elsewhere on this wiki — so the identification is not an isolated flourish but part of a running, matter-of-fact reading of the primeval history. Ephrem read the Syriac Peshitta, whose Genesis 11 genealogy carries the shorter (Masoretic-type) begetting-ages on which Shem’s six hundred years overlap the patriarchs.1

Claims & evidence

“This Melchizedek is Shem” — king and priest by succession from Noah

This Melchizedek is Shem, who became a king due to his greatness; he was the head of fourteen nations. In addition, he was a priest. He received this from Noah, his father, through the rights of succession.

— Commentary on Genesis XI.2, trans. Edward G. Mathews Jr., St. Ephrem the Syrian: Selected Prose Works, FOTC 91 (CUA Press, 1994), p. 151, Internet Archive. Ephrem does not hedge: the “king of Salem, priest of the most high God” simply is Shem, the eldest son of Noah. The priesthood is a patriarchal one — held before Aaron and passed by inheritance from Noah — and the kingship is that of “the head of fourteen nations,” i.e. of the branch of the Table of Nations that descends from Shem. This is the same equation the ancient synagogue made outright — “Malka Zadika, who was Shem bar Noah” (Targum Pseudo-Jonathan on Genesis 14:18) — here received into Syriac Christian exegesis.2

The chronology — Shem living into the days of Jacob and Esau

Because of the length of Melchizedek’s life extended to the time of Jacob and Esau, it has been stated, with much probability, that he was Shem.

— Commentary on Genesis XI.2, FOTC 91, p. 151, Internet Archive. The whole weight of the reading rests here. On the received Hebrew (and Peshitta) numbers Shem’s six hundred years reach down past Abraham — the same computation Jerome reproduces for “the opinion of the Hebrews” (Shem “survived Abraham by thirty-five years”) — so a still-living Shem can meet Abram, and even, as Ephrem presses it, reach the grandsons. The support is real but reckoning-dependent: on the Septuagint long chronology this wiki rates arguably the stronger reckoning, the higher post-Flood begetting-ages and the extra Kainan generation stretch the span so that Shem is dead centuries before Abram is born, and the identification becomes not merely unlikely but impossible.3

The corroborating arguments — Abraham’s tithe and Rebekah’s inquiry

Ephrem reasons from the deference the patriarchs show Melchizedek to his greatness, and from his greatness to his identity. It was to Melchizedek, he holds, that Rebekah went to enquire and was answered, “Two nations are in your womb… and the elder shall serve the younger” (Genesis 25:23) — and she would not have bypassed her husband Isaac, or her father-in-law Abraham “to whom revelations of the divinity came continually,” to consult a stranger, unless she had learned of his greatness from them. Nor, he adds, would Abraham have given “a tenth of everything” to a mere Canaanite or Sodomite. The reasoning is an inference from the tithe of Genesis 14:20 and the oracle of Genesis 25:23 to the conclusion that this priest must be the greatest man then living — Shem himself.4

Melchizedek a “partition” between the sons of Ham and the sons of Shem

His father Noah was dwelling in the east and Melchizedek was dwelling between two tribes, that is, between the sons of Ham and his own sons. Melchizedek was like a partition between the two, for he was afraid that the sons of Ham would turn his own sons to idolatry.

— Commentary on Genesis XI.3, FOTC 91, p. 151, Internet Archive. Ephrem gives the identified Shem a geography and a mission: settled at Salem between the Hamite and Shemite lines, he stands as a guard against the spread of idolatry from the sons of Ham — the elect priest keeping the blessed line (the line the wiki traces on the Shem timeline, “the tents of Shem”) from corruption. The Genesis silence about Melchizedek’s origins is thus filled in from Shem’s known place in the primeval history.5

Notes

  • Where it sits on this timeline. Ephrem (c. 370) follows Eusebius of Caesarea (c. 318) and precedes Ambrose (c. 380). He is the lone patristic adopter of the Shem identification on this timeline — against the Latin and Antiochene consensus (Eusebius, Ambrose, Chrysostom, Jerome, Augustine, Theodoret) for a human Canaanite priest-king — and so the argument’s most important early Christian voice.
  • Coherence with Hebrews 7:3. Ephrem also reads “without father, without mother… neither beginning of days, nor end of life” as said only by the silence of the record, not as a claim that Melchizedek literally lacked parents — the argument-from-silence reading catalogued on Does Hebrews 7:3 claim Melchizedek had no parents, birth, or death? That is precisely what lets him identify the priest “without genealogy” with a man (Shem) whose genealogy is recorded: for Ephrem the two readings cohere.
  • Why the wiki rates the identification weak. Ephrem’s endorsement raises the identification above a mere rabbinic curiosity, but does not carry it: the agenealogētos of Hebrews 7:3 tells against equating the priest “without descent” with the most fully-pedigreed man of the primeval line; the equation rests on no datum of the Genesis text (which never calls Shem a king or a priest); and its one positive support, the chronology, holds only on the shorter reckoning. The claim is weighed and rated on Was Melchizedek Shem son of Noah? (assessment: weak).
  • Its source. Ephrem inherits the equation from the Jewish tradition — the Palestinian Targums, Nedarim 32b, and Genesis Rabbah, set out on the rabbinic-tradition page — and passes it into Syriac Christianity a generation before Jerome would report and reject it.
