Ephrem the Syrian: “This Melchizedek is Shem” — the one Father who adopts the identification

Dates: c. AD 306 – 373; the Commentary on Genesis is a late work, c. 363–373 · Wikipedia
Summary: Ephrem the Syrian, the Syriac poet and exegete, is the one Father on this timeline who takes the rabbinic identification of Melchizedek with Shem son of Noah as his own reading. Where Eusebius and Ambrose held to a human priest-king, and Jerome reported the Shem equation as “the opinion of the Hebrews”, Ephrem states in his Commentary on Genesis XI that “this Melchizedek is Shem,” a king and priest by inheritance from Noah, still living into the days of Jacob and Esau. He grounds it in Shem’s longevity, in Abraham’s tithe, and in Rebekah’s consulting him. Whether Melchizedek was Shem is set out as a disputed reading on its own page, where Ephrem is the principal early Christian holder of the identification.

Where it appears

Ephrem treats the figure in his prose Commentary on Genesis, section XI, expounding the Genesis 14 meeting of Abram and Melchizedek. The same work contains Ephrem’s literal-historical commentary on the Flood (section VI), so the identification sits within a running literal reading of the primeval history. Ephrem read the Syriac Peshitta, whose Genesis 11 genealogy carries the shorter (Masoretic-type) begetting-ages on which Shem’s six hundred years overlap the patriarchs.1

Claims & evidence

“This Melchizedek is Shem” — king and priest by succession from Noah

This Melchizedek is Shem, who became a king due to his greatness; he was the head of fourteen nations. In addition, he was a priest. He received this from Noah, his father, through the rights of succession.

— Commentary on Genesis XI.2, trans. Edward G. Mathews Jr., St. Ephrem the Syrian: Selected Prose Works, FOTC 91 (CUA Press, 1994), p. 151, Internet Archive. For Ephrem the “king of Salem, priest of the most high God” is Shem, the eldest son of Noah. The priesthood is a patriarchal one — held before Aaron and passed by inheritance from Noah — and the kingship is that of “the head of fourteen nations,” i.e. of the branch of the Table of Nations that descends from Shem. The same identification appears in the Targum: Targum Pseudo-Jonathan on Genesis 14:18 glosses Malka Zadika as “Shem bar Noah”.2

The chronology — Shem living into the days of Jacob and Esau

Because of the length of Melchizedek’s life extended to the time of Jacob and Esau, it has been stated, with much probability, that he was Shem.

— Commentary on Genesis XI.2, FOTC 91, p. 151, Internet Archive. Ephrem grounds the identification in the length of Shem’s life. On the received Hebrew (and Peshitta) numbers Shem’s six hundred years reach down past Abraham — the same computation Jerome reproduces for “the opinion of the Hebrews” (Shem “survived Abraham by thirty-five years”) — so a still-living Shem can meet Abram, and, as Ephrem reads it, reach the grandsons. The Septuagint’s Genesis 11 gives different begetting-ages; the bearing of the rival reckonings on the identification is set out on Was Melchizedek Shem son of Noah?3

The corroborating arguments — Abraham’s tithe and Rebekah’s inquiry

Ephrem reasons from the deference the patriarchs show Melchizedek to his greatness, and from his greatness to his identity. It was to Melchizedek, he holds, that Rebekah went to enquire and was answered, “Two nations are in your womb… and the elder shall serve the younger” (Genesis 25:23) — and she would not have bypassed her husband Isaac, or her father-in-law Abraham “to whom revelations of the divinity came continually,” to consult a stranger, unless she had learned of his greatness from them. Nor, he adds, would Abraham have given “a tenth of everything” to a mere Canaanite or Sodomite. The reasoning is an inference from the tithe of Genesis 14:20 and the oracle of Genesis 25:23 to the conclusion that this priest must be the greatest man then living — Shem himself.4

Melchizedek a “partition” between the sons of Ham and the sons of Shem

His father Noah was dwelling in the east and Melchizedek was dwelling between two tribes, that is, between the sons of Ham and his own sons. Melchizedek was like a partition between the two, for he was afraid that the sons of Ham would turn his own sons to idolatry.

— Commentary on Genesis XI.3, FOTC 91, p. 151, Internet Archive. Ephrem gives the identified Shem a geography and a mission: settled at Salem between the Hamite and Shemite lines, he stands as a guard against the spread of idolatry from the sons of Ham — the elect priest keeping the blessed line (the line traced on the Shem timeline, “the tents of Shem”) from corruption. The Genesis silence about Melchizedek’s origins is thus filled in from Shem’s known place in the primeval history.5

Notes

  • Placement on the timeline. Ephrem (c. 370) follows Eusebius of Caesarea (c. 318) and precedes Ambrose (c. 380). He is the one patristic adopter of the Shem identification on this timeline; the other Fathers here (Eusebius, Ambrose, Chrysostom, Augustine, Theodoret) read Melchizedek as a human priest-king, while Jerome reports both the Canaanite and the Shem identifications and leaves the judgement to his correspondent.
  • Coherence with Hebrews 7:3. Ephrem is reported to read “without father, without mother… neither beginning of days, nor end of life” as said only by the silence of the record, not as a claim that Melchizedek literally lacked parents ⚠ unverified. On that reading he identifies the priest “without genealogy” with a man (Shem) whose genealogy is recorded: for Ephrem the two readings cohere.
  • Reception and debate. The readings of the identification, including the place of the agenealogētos of Hebrews 7:3 and of the rival chronologies, are set out on the disputed-reading page Was Melchizedek Shem son of Noah?
  • The Jewish witnesses. The same identification appears in the Targum, b. Nedarim 32b and Genesis Rabbah, set out on the rabbinic-tradition page. Jerome reported it in Letter 73 (c. 398) as “the opinion of the Hebrews”.
