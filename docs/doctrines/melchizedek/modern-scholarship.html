Modern scholarship — what critical study makes of Melchizedek

Dates: a survey of modern critical scholarship (chiefly 20th–21st century) · representative works: F. M. Cross, Canaanite Myth and Hebrew Epic (1973); H. H. Rowley, “Melchizedek and Zadok” (1950); J. A. Emerton, “The Riddle of Genesis XIV” (1971); A. S. van der Woude, editio princeps of 11QMelchizedek (1965); J. A. Fitzmyer, “Further Light on Melchizedek from Qumran Cave 11” (1967); P. J. Kobelski, Melchizedek and Melchireša‘ (1981); F. L. Horton, The Melchizedek Tradition (1976); H. W. Attridge, Hebrews (Hermeneia, 1989) · Horton’s survey (full text)
Summary: This page gathers what modern epigraphic, philological, and source-critical study has said about Melchizedek. Four threads recur across the literature. First, the Canaanite background: on Frank Moore Cross’s reconstruction, El Elyon, “God Most High, possessor of heaven and earth” (Genesis 14:18–22), is an old West-Semitic divine title with close Phoenician and Ugaritic parallels, and Melchizedek is read as a priest-king of a pre-Israelite (Jebusite) Salem = Jerusalem, his “-zedek” name of a piece with the later Jebusite king Adoni-zedek. Second, the textual question: the debate over whether Genesis 14 is archaic or late (the “erratic block” that fits no source), whether the Melchizedek scene is a still-later insertion, and whether Psalm 110 is a pre-exilic royal enthronement psalm that legitimated a non-Levitical Davidic priest-kingship “after the order of Melchizedek.” Third, the two exaltation trajectories — the heavenly elohim-judge of 11QMelchizedek and the miraculous birth of 2 Enoch — in which the priest-king becomes a heavenly being, and the question whether the Epistle to the Hebrews drew on that tradition (Fred Horton concluded that it largely did not; Paul Kobelski holds that it presupposes a heavenly Melchizedek). Fourth, the typology in Hebrews: Harold Attridge, F. F. Bruce, and David deSilva read Hebrews 7:3 as a rhetorical argument from the silences of the Genesis record rather than a claim that Melchizedek literally lacked parents or death. The disputed questions about the figure are listed on the Melchizedek timeline.

Claims & evidence

The Canaanite background: El Elyon, and a Jebusite priest-king of Salem

Melchizedek is “priest of the most high God” — El Elyon — and blesses Abram by “the most high God, possessor of heaven and earth” (El Elyon qōnēh šāmayim wā-’areṣ, Genesis 14:19, 22). Modern study reads both the divine name and the epithet as old West-Semitic religious language rather than an Israelite coinage: the title “creator/possessor of earth” recurs almost word-for-word in the eighth-century Phoenician portal inscription from Karatepe (’l qn ’rṣ, “El, creator of earth”), in later Neo-Punic, and behind the Hittite theonym Elkunirša — and a fragmentary Jerusalem ostracon of about 700 BC appears to carry the same phrase in the city itself. On Frank Moore Cross’s reconstruction in Canaanite Myth and Hebrew Epic, El is the aged creator-patriarch of the Canaanite pantheon, and Elyon (“Most High”) an old cult-title later absorbed into the worship of YHWH; on that reading the Genesis narrator presents Abram’s blessing by a Canaanite priest of El Elyon and then, in Abram’s reply (“the LORD, the most high God, the possessor of heaven and earth,” 14:22), identifies that Most High God with YHWH.1

The same background shapes the reading of Melchizedek himself as a Jebusite priest-king of Salem = Jerusalem. The equation Salem = Jerusalem is old (already Psalm 76:2, and Josephus) and is defended by J. A. Emerton against the rival identifications; on it Melchizedek is a king of the pre-Davidic city. His name — “my king is ṣedeq” (righteousness, or the name of a deity Ṣedeq) — is of a piece with that of the later Jebusite king of Jerusalem, Adoni-zedek (“my lord is ṣedeq,” Joshua 10:1), suggesting, on this reading, a dynastic -ṣedeq naming pattern in the city’s priest-kingship. On the “Jebusite hypothesis” of H. H. Rowley, this old Jerusalemite priesthood did not vanish when David took the city: Zadok, listed among David’s priests as “Zadok the son of Ahitub” (2 Samuel 8:17), was the pre-Israelite Jebusite priest of Salem co-opted into the new royal cult — Melchizedek’s successor — and Genesis 14:18–20 was drawn into the Abraham story to root that Jerusalem priesthood in the patriarch. Roland de Vaux judged Rowley’s thesis “an interesting hypothesis, but without foundation.” Rowley’s thesis presents Melchizedek as a real Canaanite priest-king and Genesis 14:18–20 as the legitimating charter of a non-Levitical Jerusalem priesthood.2

Genesis 14 and Psalm 110 as texts: the dating debate

The two texts on which the whole tradition rests are also two that critical study of the Pentateuch and Psalter has found hard to place. Genesis 14 is called the “erratic block” of the patriarchal narratives — a chapter of annalistic style, a coalition of otherwise-unknown eastern kings, and archaic-looking names that fits none of the usual pentateuchal strands (J, E, P). Hermann Gunkel, and in a different key W. F. Albright, treated it as preserving ancient material; J. A. Emerton’s two 1971 studies, clearing away what he called the “false clues” of a poetic or cuneiform original, and Gard Granerød’s full-length treatment read it as a late, learned composition, with the Melchizedek scene of verses 18–20 a still-later insertion that assimilates the chapter to Psalm 110. Gerhard von Rad, Claus Westermann, and E. A. Speiser all read the exchange as a legitimation, attaching Abraham (and so Israel) to the priest-king of Salem/Jerusalem and its worship of El Elyon. The questions at issue in this debate are the date and setting of the passage; von Rad, Westermann, and Speiser read the figure himself as the priest-king of a Canaanite city.3

Hans-Joachim Kraus and Sigmund Mowinckel read Psalm 110 as a pre-exilic royal psalm — an enthronement or coronation oracle for the Davidic king — in which the fourth verse, “Thou art a priest for ever after the order of Melchizedek” (Psalm 110:4), swears the king into a priesthood that is not Aaronic. Since David was of Judah, not Levi, they read the oath as legitimating the king’s cultic role by grounding it in the old Jerusalemite (Melchizedek) priest-kingship he had inherited with the city — the same legitimation logic von Rad and Westermann find in the Genesis scene. Another, late-dating reading hears the verse as Hasmonean propaganda, legitimating the priest-kings of the Maccabean house (⚠ unverified — no named proponent cited here). On either dating, the Epistle to the Hebrews received a royal oath of an eternal, non-Levitical priesthood tied to a figure whose priesthood the Genesis record leaves without pedigree.4

The two exaltation trajectories: from priest-king to heavenly being

A distinct strand of scholarship traces how, in the Second Temple period, the priest-king became a more-than-human being along two routes. The first runs through a heavenly courtroom. In the Qumran scroll 11QMelchizedek (11Q13), edited by A. S. van der Woude in 1965, Melchizedek is an exalted elohim who presides over the eschatological Day of Atonement and executes God’s judgment on Belial — a heavenly redeemer-judge. Van der Woude identified him with the archangel Michael; Joseph Fitzmyer’s re-edition noted that “the author of the text seems to refer to Melchizedek as Elohim,” seated among the divine beings of Psalm 82:1; Paul Kobelski’s monograph set this heavenly Melchizedek against his dark counterpart Melchireša‘ (“king of wickedness”). The second route runs through a birth-legend: the closing chapters of the Slavonic 2 Enoch (71–72) tell of Melchizedek’s miraculous birth, marked from the womb with “the badge of priesthood,” and his rescue by the archangel Michael before the Flood — a tradition Andrei Orlov reads as a development of the same impulse to make the priesthood, and the priest, more than human. The surviving text of 11Q13 does not cite Genesis 14 or Psalm 110.5 The later Christian identifications of Melchizedek as an angel (Origen and Didymus, as Jerome reports), a heavenly power above Christ (the Melchizedekians reported by Epiphanius), or the Holy Spirit (the tract Jerome answered) appear after this Jewish material.

Did the Epistle to the Hebrews draw on the heavenly-Melchizedek tradition?

The discovery of 11QMelchizedek made this a central modern question about the New Testament’s Melchizedek. Fred L. Horton’s The Melchizedek Tradition (1976) surveyed every source to the fifth century and set out expecting to confirm a close dependence of Hebrews 7 on the Qumran heavenly Melchizedek; he reported: “As regards my initial assumptions the results of my study have been largely negative.” Horton concluded that the author of Hebrews did not regard Melchizedek as a heavenly being, and that the Epistle rests not on the Qumran tradition but on an independent reading of the Old Testament passages themselves. Paul Kobelski holds the contrary position, that Hebrews presupposes a heavenly Melchizedek; the two works mark the range of the modern debate on the question.6

The typology in Hebrews read as an argument from silence

Hebrews 7:3 calls Melchizedek “without father, without mother, without descent [agenealogētos], having neither beginning of days, nor end of life; but made like [aphōmoiōmenos] unto the Son of God.” Harold Attridge, F. F. Bruce, and David deSilva read this not as an ontological report — that Melchizedek had no parents and never died — but as a rhetorical argument from silence: because the Genesis record, which elsewhere fixes the patriarchs’ lineage, birth, and death, says nothing of Melchizedek’s, the Epistle can present him as a standing figure of an unoriginate, deathless priesthood. Attridge’s Hermeneia commentary grounds the move in the rabbinic exegetical maxim quod non in Thora non in mundo — “what is not in the Torah is not in the world” — whereby what Scripture omits can be treated as if it did not exist; F. F. Bruce writes that “it is when the writer bases his exposition on the silence of Scripture that his method of exegesis seems strangest to modern readers,” glossing agenealogētos as “of unknown or obscure birth”; David deSilva reads chapter 7 as a Greco-Roman encomium exploiting the same silence. On this reading the word aphōmoiōmenos, “made like unto,” makes Melchizedek the copy and Christ the archetype. Attridge also allows that Hebrews may be aware of a tradition of a heavenly Melchizedek. Among the Fathers a similar reading appears in Chrysostom (“from its not being expressed”) and Theodoret (“if he had really been without father… he would not have been an image, but a reality”).7
