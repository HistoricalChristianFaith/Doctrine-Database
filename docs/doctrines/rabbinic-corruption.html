The Charge of Rabbinic Corruption of Scripture (Septuagint vs. Masoretic Text)

From the second century on, Christian writers noted that their Greek Old Testament — the Septuagint (LXX), the translation of "the seventy" — diverged from the Hebrew text the synagogue used, including at passages they cited as prophecies of Christ: Isaiah 7:14 ("virgin" vs. "young woman"), Psalm 96:10 ("the Lord reigned from the wood"), and the chronology of Genesis 5 and 11. Some of them charged that Jewish teachers after the time of Christ (later identified with Aquila's revision and with the proto-Masoretic Hebrew) had deliberately altered or cut the text to deny Christ, while the older, pre-Christian LXX preserved the original reading. This page records the witnesses to that charge, earliest extant first, together with writers who reported the charge without adopting it, the rabbinic accounts of the Greek translation, and chronographers who tabulated the divergences without charging alteration. Its subject is the charge of deliberate alteration after AD 70, not textual transmission in general. Modern textual critics read the same divergences in more than one way — Emanuel Tov explains the Septuagint's differences as renderings of a different Hebrew Vorlage, drawing on the Hebrew manuscripts from Qumran, while Henry B. Smith Jr. holds that the shorter Hebrew chronology of Genesis 5 and 11 was deliberately reduced — and these readings are set out under Disputed readings. Many of the witnesses ground the Septuagint's authority on the account of its divinely guided translation, whose growth is traced on the companion page Origin and Inspiration of the Septuagint. (A related dispute concerned language rather than text: a strand of the medieval Christian and Muslim East held that Syriac, not Hebrew, was the primeval tongue — in one telling, the original language into which the Law was first given; that dispute is traced on the first-language timeline.)

Timeline

Witnesses are listed by the date of their statement, earliest extant first. Questions that run across several witnesses are listed below under Disputed readings.

Justin Martyr (c. 165)

Justin Martyr, debating the Jew Trypho, gives the earliest extant form of the charge: the Jewish teachers both disparage the Septuagint of "your seventy elders that were with Ptolemy" as "untrue in certain respects" and "have altogether taken away many Scriptures" from it — precisely those "by which this very man who was crucified is proved to have been set forth expressly as God, and man, and as being crucified, and as dying."1 He names particular excisions — a Jeremiah logion ("Come, let us lay on wood on His bread"), a one-line descent oracle, and the passover saying of Esdras — and says the cutting is recent and incomplete, "still written in some copies… in the synagogues of the Jews (for it is only a short time since they were cut out)."2 His most-cited single example is the deletion of the words "from the wood" from Psalm 96:10, to which Trypho answers that the erasure "seems incredible."3 Justin charges intent as well as deed: had the teachers grasped the force of the texts they still admit, "be well assured they would have deleted them," and where they did not delete they altered the reading (the Septuagint's "take away the righteous" against their "bind the righteous").4

Irenaeus of Lyons (c. 189)

Irenaeus of Lyons, in Against Heresies III.21.1, is the earliest extant witness after Justin, and the earliest extant to fasten the charge on named later revisers and a single verse. He attacks "Theodotion the Ephesian… and Aquila of Pontus, both Jewish proselytes," for rendering Isaiah 7:14 "a young woman shall conceive" where the Septuagint had "a virgin," and the Ebionites who follow them in denying the virgin birth. He grounds the older Greek's authority on its pre-Christian date: the prophecy "was interpreted into Greek by the Jews themselves, much before the period of our Lord's advent, that there might remain no suspicion that perchance the Jews, complying with our humour, did put this interpretation upon these words" — adding that, had they foreseen the Christian use of the text, they "would themselves never have hesitated to burn their own Scriptures."5 He locates the divergence in the later Greek versions (Aquila and Theodotion) rather than in the Hebrew text. How his Isaiah 7:14 case, which turns on the rendering of the Hebrew 'almah, has been read is set out on Aquila's revision: anti-Christian distortion, or literalizing toward the Hebrew?

Tractate Megillah (c. 200)

A rabbinic account of the divergence. The tradition in b. Megillah 9a states that the Greek diverges from the Hebrew, and credits the divergence to the seventy-two translators: shut in separate chambers for Ptolemy and translating "from memory," "the Holy One, blessed be He, sent into the heart of each of them a counsel, and they all agreed to have one mind, and changed" about fifteen specific passages.6 The listed changes are anti-anthropomorphic, anti-polytheistic, and court-diplomatic — "God created the world in the beginning" (against a separate creator), "I will make a man in an image" (singular, against "let us make"), and "the beast that has small feet" for the hare of Lev 11:6, because "Ptolemy's wife was named" Arnebeth; none of the listed passages is among those Justin says were cut out.7 The account places the alteration in the Greek translation and ascribes it to divine prompting, rather than in the Hebrew text. Its relation to the Letter of Aristeas legend is set out on The Septuagint legend: were the seventy-two divinely moved to change the text?

Origen of Alexandria (c. 240)

Origen of Alexandria, replying in his Letter to Africanus to Julius Africanus — who had objected that the Greek story of Susanna could be no true Scripture, standing in no Hebrew Daniel — grants the Hebrew omission but draws the opposite inference: in his view the Greek tradition preserves what the Hebrew custodians dropped. The Jewish leaders, he charges, "hid from the knowledge of the people as many of the passages which contained any scandal against the elders, rulers, and judges, as they could, some of which have been preserved in uncanonical writings (Apocrypha)," and the tale of Isaiah sawn asunder survives only in a work "which probably the Jews have purposely tampered with, introducing some phrases manifestly incorrect, that discredit might be thrown on the whole."8 The same letter describes his Hexaplaric work on the Greek–Hebrew divergences — "the difference between our copies and those of the Jews did not escape me" — marking with obelisk and asterisk the passages each version has and the other lacks. He declines to "reject as spurious the copies in use in our Churches" or to "coax the Jews… to give us copies which shall be untampered with." His charge concerns suppressed passages (scandal-narratives against the elders), not whole books and not the christological proof-texts; how it bears on a charge that the Jews removed Scripture is set out on the "removed, not merely rejected" page.9

Eusebius of Caesarea (c. 325)

Eusebius of Caesarea, in Book 1 of his Chronicon, applies the charge to the chronology. Setting the Septuagint, Hebrew, and Samaritan reckonings of Genesis 5–11 side by side, he finds the Greek and the Hebrew agreeing in each patriarch's total lifespan but disagreeing — by a hundred years apiece — in the begetting-ages before the flood, so that the period from Adam to the Flood comes to 2,242 years in the Septuagint against 1,656 in the Hebrew, a 586-year gap.10 Because the begetting-age and the years-lived-after sum to the same total in both versions, and because the Hebrew matches the Greek precisely at Jared, Methuselah, and Lamech, he reasons that the Septuagint preserves the original and "is the best."11 The shorter Hebrew figures he suspects to be a deliberate abridgement — "we suspect that this was something which the Jews did. They made bold to shorten the time before the fathering of children to encourage early marriages" — the earliest extant statement of the chronology strand of the charge; the Genesis 5/11 question is set out on its own page.12

Epiphanius of Salamis (c. 392)

Epiphanius of Salamis, in his Treatise on Weights and Measures, directs the charge at a single named agent: Aquila, whose hyper-literal second-century Greek revision the synagogue preferred to the Septuagint. Epiphanius narrates Aquila's career as an apostasy — a baptized Christian expelled from the church for clinging to astrology, who turned Jew "embittered in mind… puffed up with vain jealousy" and learned Hebrew to make his version.13 That version, he charges, was made "not by the right motive, but (by the desire) so to distort certain of the words occurring in the translation of the seventy-two that he might proclaim the things testified to about Christ in the divine Scriptures to be fulfilled in some other way," and he calls it "incorrect and perversely translated."14 He is the principal patristic witness cited for the reading of Aquila's version as anti-Christian, set out on Aquila's revision: anti-Christian distortion, or literalizing toward the Hebrew? The same treatise elsewhere uses Aquila's version to recover Hebrew words the Seventy had omitted.

Philastrius of Brescia (c. 397)

Philastrius of Brescia, in his Book of Diverse Heresies (Diversarum Hereseon Liber), the earliest extant Latin heresy-catalogue, carries the Aquila-versus-Septuagint dispute into the Latin West and lists rejection of the Seventy among the heresies. He names "heretics who like the Jews spit out the translation of the Seventy-Two saintly and wise men, and prefer to use the text of a certain Aquila, a man of Pontus" — naming Aquila, as Epiphanius did in the Greek East. The authority he sets against Aquila is the inspiration account: under Ptolemy the seventy-two were each "secluded in a cell" with only a scribe, and "the versions of all of them agreed completely."15 He names no altered verse and charges no excision from the Hebrew. The separate-cells account he relays is traced on the companion page, the Origin and Inspiration of the Septuagint, and the reading of Aquila he shares is set out on Aquila's revision: anti-Christian distortion, or literalizing toward the Hebrew?

Jerome (c. 400)

Jerome, setting aside the Septuagint as his base and translating the Old Testament afresh "from the Hebrew words," appeals throughout his Vulgate prefaces to the Hebraica veritas — the "truth of the Hebrew" — ranks the versions Hebrew over Greek over Latin, and makes the synagogue's text the court of appeal, telling the doubter to "ask any one of the Hebrews, in whom you might place better faith" and protesting that he is "not at all aware of anything of the Hebrew to have been changed by me."16 He rejects the account of the seventy translators shut in separate cells as the construction of an unknown author's "lying," noting that "many after the time of Josephus" reported no such thing, and holds that the Seventy "translated those things [they] understood" rather than prophesied, "for it is one thing to be a seer, another to be an interpreter."17 The word "corrupted" he applies to the Greek, meaning its splintering into rival recensions in transmission ("this genuine and ancient translation is corrupted and violated"), the remedy for which he finds in the Hebrew.18 He allows that the Jews may have "afterward removed the testimonies used by the Apostles against them," and relays their own account of the Greek's softening of the Trinitarian texts for Ptolemy, placing that alteration in the translators' discretion rather than in the Hebrew text. His view of the legend is set out on The Septuagint legend: were the seventy-two divinely moved to change the text?

John Chrysostom (c. 407)

John Chrysostom, preaching at Antioch on Matthew 1:22–23, states the charge in a dating form. When the synagogue appeals to the later Greek translators — who at Isaiah 7:14 read "young woman" where the Septuagint had "virgin" — he answers by appeal to their date and allegiance: those versions were "made… after Christ's coming, continuing to be Jews, and may justly be suspected as having spoken rather in enmity, and as darkening the prophecies on purpose," whereas the Seventy, having translated "an hundred years or more before the coming of Christ, stand clear from all such suspicion."19 He grounds the contrast on the Septuagint's pre-Christian, providential origin — the Jews brought from Jerusalem under Ptolemy Philadelphus "a few years before the birth of Jesus Christ," their work "a particular disposition of Providence."20 On this reasoning only a version made after Christ falls under suspicion, which places the charge on the post-Christian revisions (Aquila and the rest) rather than on the Septuagint. His example turns on the rendering of the Hebrew 'almah; how it has been read is set out on Aquila's revision: anti-Christian distortion, or literalizing toward the Hebrew?

Augustine of Hippo (c. 420)

Augustine of Hippo, discussing in The City of God the Septuagint's longer antediluvian begetting-ages of Genesis 5 against the synagogue's shorter Hebrew, reports the tampering explanation as the opinion of others ("they say… the Jews, envying us our translation of their Law and Prophets, have made alterations in their texts so as to undermine the authority of ours") and leaves it to the reader — "this opinion or suspicion let each man adopt according to his own judgment."21 For his own part he will not believe that "the Jews, however malicious and wrong-headed, could have tampered with so many and so widely-dispersed manuscripts," and attributes the hundred-year pattern "neither to the malice of the Jews nor to… the seventy translators, but to the error of the copyist" who first transcribed the Greek from Ptolemy's library — an addition on the Greek side, where Eusebius had suspected a Hebrew shortening. The divergence "has not been reckoned a falsification; and for my own part I am persuaded it ought not to be reckoned so," and where the versions cannot both be true he takes "the real facts from that text out of which our own version has been translated" — the Hebrew; differences that are not copyists' errors he ascribes to the Seventy's "liberty of prophesying."22 His account of the Seventy's inspiration ("the one Spirit had been in them all") is traced on the companion Origin and Inspiration of the Septuagint page.

Philoxenus of Mabbug (c. 523)

Philoxenus of Mabbug, the miaphysite bishop who commissioned the Philoxenian Syriac Bible, is reported to have given a dating argument for the charge — known only secondhand, through Moses bar Kepha's later survey of the Greek versions. He is reported to rate the Septuagint best because it is the text "Our Lord and his disciples quote in the New Testament," and because, being made "long before the appearance of Our Lord," it gave the Jews "neither reason nor pretext to be moved by envy (to corrupt the text); but in the other translations the relevant passages were corrupted through the envy of the Jews."23 On this reasoning only a version made after Christ could have been bent against him, so the charge falls on the later revisions (Aquila and the rest), the same target as Epiphanius's. ⚠ The attribution is unverified: it rests on bar Kepha as quoted by the Wassersteins, no direct Philoxenus text for it having been located.

Isidore of Seville (c. 636)

Isidore of Seville, in the Bible-book of his Etymologies (Book VI), relays the inspiration account and levels no corruption charge. The seventy, sealed "each apart in his own chamber," rendered the text "through the Holy Spirit… in such a way that nothing was found in the manuscript of any of them that was different from the rest, even in the order of words."24 He lists the later versions (Aquila, Symmachus, Theodotion) without comment; he names one textual "corruption" and assigns it to the gentiles (healed by Ezra, "inspired with the divine spirit"); and he ranks Jerome's Hebrew-based Vulgate "deservedly preferred over the others… truer."25 He appears also as a witness to the inspiration account on the companion page, the Origin and Inspiration of the Septuagint.

Julian of Toledo (c. 686)

Julian of Toledo, archbishop of the Visigothic capital, takes up the chronology strand of the charge in an anti-Jewish treatise, the De comprobatione aetatis sextae, written c. 686 against Jews who reckoned from the shorter Hebrew totals that the world had not yet reached the sixth age in which Messiah comes. His program, stated in the preface: "because of this difference of years which is found between the Hebrew and our codices, it is not the books of the Hebrew translation, but the codices of the Seventy translators, that are to be followed" — for the Seventy "translated… by the office of prophesying rather than of translating, the Lord revealing it to them."26 He calls the shorter Hebrew reckoning the "falsity of the Jews" (falsitas Judaeorum); his argument in Book III also holds that the ages of the world are fixed by a "prefixed limit of generations," not by summed years, so that the Hebrew year-totals do not decide the question.27 The tampering motive on his pages he quotes from Augustine — the Jews "have changed certain things in their own codices, that our authority might be diminished" — introducing it as Augustine's "reportable opinion" (relatione opinabili); he also quotes Augustine twice declining to call the divergence a falsification and reading the extra hundred years as added on the Greek side, and enlists Jerome on the Greek's side on this chronological point.28

Jacob of Edessa (c. 692)

Jacob of Edessa, Syriac scholar and reviser of the Syriac Old Testament, states the chronology charge in the Syriac East with an explicit motive. In a scholion on Genesis he charges that the Hebrews, "wanting to pervert the computation of years, in order to show that Christ had not yet arrived," cut a hundred years from each antediluvian patriarch before his son was born. The textual observation is the one Eusebius made (Adam begets Seth at 130 in the Hebrew, 230 in the Greek); where Eusebius suggested a motive of encouraging early marriage, Jacob names an anti-messianic one.29 He adds that he had found the longer figure (230) "in some sufficiently accurate Hebrew histories."30 The charge reaches us through a Genesis scholion printed by Assemani within his dissertation on Ephraem the Syrian and translated by Adler; Assemani filed it under Ephraem, and modern scholars (Wacholder, ter Haar Romeny, Adler) assign it to Jacob, as set out on his page.

Tractate Soferim (c. 750)

A second rabbinic account of the Greek translation, in a different key from Tractate Megillah's. The minor post-Talmudic tractate Soferim 1:7 records that when "five elders wrote the Torah for King Ptolemy in Greek," that day "was as ominous for Israel as the day on which the golden calf was made, since the Torah could not be accurately translated."31 The later medieval liturgy made the lament a fast (the eighth of Tevet, when "darkness was in the world for three days").32 It charges no alteration of the Hebrew and does not address the Christian charge; its lament concerns the translation of the Torah into Greek. The tractate was redacted c. 750; the older Talmudic material also carries favourable accounts of the Greek (b. Megillah 9a's providential account; the Palestinian Talmud's "the Torah may be correctly translated only into Greek"), and the same paragraph of Soferim relays the seventy-two-in-separate-chambers account as well.

George Syncellus (c. 810)

George Syncellus, the Byzantine monk and chronographer, builds the Eusebian chronology comparison into his universal history, the Ecloga Chronographica. Setting the Septuagint, the Jewish Hebrew, and the Samaritan reckonings of Genesis 5–11 side by side after Eusebius — the tables headed "From Eusebios" — he concludes: "there is no choice but to acknowledge that… the text of scripture used by the Jews has erred in its chronology from Adam to Abraham."33 "The Hebrew text used by the Jews is wrong," he adds; the Septuagint was made from "an ancient and a strictly accurate Hebrew text," and "it is this version that we too have used in our own chronography," the text "the Church of Christ… is devoted to."34 His charge is chiefly of error — that the Jewish numbers are mistaken and the Greek original — and at the recapitulation he suspects the Jews "devised" the shorter figures. The phrase "criminal act of the Jews," sometimes quoted in his name, comes from Gustav Seyffarth's 1859 rendering rather than from the Adler–Tuffin translation of his text, as set out on his page.

Hrabanus Maurus (c. 845)

Hrabanus Maurus, abbot of Fulda and archbishop of Mainz, copies the inspiration account almost verbatim from Isidore into the Carolingian schools' standard encyclopedia, the De rerum naturis (De Universo). In Book V's chapter "On the library" he reproduces it — the seventy, "separated each apart in his own cell… translated everything through the Holy Spirit in such a way that nothing was found in the manuscript of any one of them that differed from the rest, even in the order of the words."35 Like Isidore he levels no corruption charge: the later versions (Aquila, Symmachus, Theodotion) are listed without comment, the one "corruption" he names is by the gentiles (healed by Ezra), and Jerome's Hebrew-based Vulgate is "deservedly preferred" to the others.36 Where Isidore states the separate-cells account as fact, Hrabanus inserts "as some assert" (ut quidam asserunt). He appears also on the companion page, the Origin and Inspiration of the Septuagint.

Moses bar Kepha (c. 903)

Moses bar Kepha, the ninth-century Syriac Orthodox bishop, is the channel through which the Philoxenus argument survives, and a witness in his own right. In a survey listing the ancient translations of the Bible into Greek he reports a version of the Septuagint legend (the seventy-two in separate chambers) and then, in a note of his own, ranks the Septuagint best of the versions — first on his own ground, "as is proven by the fact that it is this that Our Lord and his disciples quote in the New Testament," and then "according to Philoxenus of Mabbug," whose dating argument he quotes: the LXX "was made long before the appearance of Our Lord," so the Jews "had neither reason nor pretext to be moved by envy… but in the other translations the relevant passages were corrupted through the envy of the Jews."37 His ranking sets the Septuagint above Aquila and the other post-Christian revisions, so that the legend, the ranking, and Philoxenus's dating argument stand together in one survey. The survey is ch. 44 of his Hexaemeron Commentary (ed. and German trans. L. Schlimme, Der Hexaemeronkommentar des Moses bar Kepha, Wiesbaden, 1977, 1:173), which has no critical Syriac edition; the English follows the Wassersteins' rendering, the page not independently collated.

Agapius of Hierapolis (c. 942)

Agapius of Hierapolis, the Melkite bishop of Manbij — the old Mabbug, see of Philoxenus — gives the charge a narrative form in his Christian world history in Arabic, the Kitāb al-ʿUnwān. There the two named high priests of the Gospel, Annas and Caiaphas, frightened after the Resurrection that elders of their own people had owned the Messiah, "hid the translation of the Torah, translated by the Seventy commentators… which were translated under King Ptolemy Philadelphus in the town of Alexandria" and "changed and mutilated in the Books of the Prophets all that they could, of what referred to the prophecies on the Messiah" — while the same passages "in the books of the Seventy, are, on the contrary, faithful and clear."38 The same conspirators, "having the libraries in their possession," he says, "cut off from the history from the world, from Adam to the time of Abraham, 1389 years," so that the period from Adam to the flood stands at 2,256 years in the Septuagint against 1,656 in "the Torah mutilated and reduced by the Jews" — the deletion made to argue that the Messiah "would come only in the last days… we are still in the middle of the days of the world."39 Agapius joins both strands of the charge — the messianic proof-texts and the Genesis chronology — in one narrative set after the Resurrection, naming the agents, a motive, and a method (he narrates the excision of the "second Kainan," with the disciples demanding of his erased years, "where did you put them?"); the pre-Christian Greek, finished "approximately 300 years before the Advent of Christ," he holds free of the charge on the ground of its date, the argument reported of Philoxenus.40

Bar Hebraeus (c. 1286)

Bar Hebraeus, the Syriac Orthodox maphrian and polymath, follows in his Chronography the Septuagint's longer reckoning of Genesis 5–11 and tabulates the divergent "Syrian and Hebrew" totals beside it; the passages surveyed here state the difference without a charge of deliberate Jewish alteration.41 Henry B. Smith Jr. (2018) lists him among the chronographers who held that the rabbis "deliberately deflated" the chronology; the one note in Budge's edition explaining the divergences is by the editor Paul Bedjan, who ascribes them to the versions the author followed.42

Disputed readings

Questions about the corruption charge and the textual evidence it cites, each set out with its rival readings on its own page.

The corruption charge

How the divergence between the Septuagint and the rabbinic Hebrew, and the handling of each text by its custodians, have been explained.

  • Did the Jews deliberately corrupt the Hebrew Old Testament text? (the charge as a whole, read with the Hebrew manuscripts from Qumran)43
  • The Septuagint legend: were the seventy-two divinely moved to change the text? (b. Megillah 9a and the Letter of Aristeas)44
  • Aquila's revision: anti-Christian distortion, or literalizing toward the Hebrew?45
  • The reverse charge: did the Jews accuse the Christians of corrupting Scripture?46
  • The tikkune soferim: did the rabbis admit they altered the wording of Scripture? (the "emendations of the scribes")47
  • The rabbinic text-apparatus: did the scribes purge the variant readings, or preserve them? (the three scrolls of the Temple court and the Severus Scroll)48

The Genesis chronology

How the divergent numbers and names of the Genesis 5 and 11 genealogies have been explained.

  • Were the Genesis 5/11 patriarchal lifespans shortened (MT) or inflated (LXX) — and was it tampering? (the Masoretic, Samaritan and Septuagint figures)49
  • The "second Cainan" (Genesis 11 LXX / Luke 3:36) — original reading the Jews deleted, or a secondary plus?50

Other disputed readings

Whether writings outside the Hebrew Bible were removed from it, and whether a reading of Daniel 7 was suppressed.

  • Did the Jews remove writings from Scripture, or not receive them (Wisdom 18:15)? (the counterpart of "did the Jews reject the deuterocanon?" on the Old Testament canon timeline)51
  • Did rabbinic Judaism suppress a divine-Messiah ("two powers in heaven") reading of Daniel 7? (b. Ḥagigah 14a and b. Sanhedrin 38b)52
