John Chrysostom on infant baptism

Dates: c. 390 (Chrysostom, c. 347–407; preacher at Antioch, later archbishop of Constantinople. The passage is from his Third Baptismal Instruction — part of the eight-homily “Stavronikita series” recovered by Antoine Wenger and first published in 1957 — delivered to catechumens at Antioch; the series is dated to the period 389–398, most likely c. 390) · Wikipedia
Summary: Chrysostom takes infant baptism entirely for granted — “we baptize even infants” — and gives a rationale for it: he says infants are baptized although they are sinless, and does not list the remission of sins among what baptism gives them, naming instead the positive gifts of grace (sanctification, adoption, membership in Christ). How far that sets him apart from the Western tradition of Cyprian and Augustine, which ties infant baptism to inherited sin, is read differently: Augustine took “sinless” of personal sins; Antoine Wenger found in the passage “some foundation” for holding that Chrysostom considered infants free of original sin as well; and the Orthodox historian John Meyendorff places Chrysostom in a Greek tradition that sees the inheritance from Adam as mortality rather than guilt.

Claims & evidence

“We baptize even infants, although they are sinless” — for the further gifts of grace (Third Baptismal Instruction 3.6)

Having enumerated the many things baptism confers — not remission of sins only but a whole train of honors — Chrysostom turns to infants to prove the point that the rite gives far more than forgiveness:

Blessed be God, who alone does wonderful things! You have seen how numerous are the gifts of baptism. Although many men think that the only gift it confers is the remission of sins, we have counted its honors to the number of ten. It is on this account that we baptize even infants, although they are sinless, that they may be given the further gifts of sanctification, justice, filial adoption, and inheritance, that they may be brothers and members of Christ, and become dwelling places for the Spirit.

— John Chrysostom, Baptismal Instructions (Stavronikita series) 3.6, trans. Paul W. Harkins.1 The “ten honors” are the ones he has just been reciting: in the preceding section he tells the newly baptized they are “not only free, but also holy; not only holy, but also just; not only just, but also sons; not only sons, but also heirs; not only heirs, but also brothers of Christ; not only brothers of Christ, but also joint heirs; not only joint heirs, but also members; not only members, but also the temple; not only the temple, but also instruments of the Spirit.”2

Notes

  • The witness to the practice is incidental. Chrysostom is not arguing for infant baptism; he assumes it, and reasons from it. His point is about the wealth of what baptism gives, and infants are his proof: since, on his account, they have no sins to remit, the fact that the Church baptizes them anyway shows that baptism must confer much more than remission. That the custom can be used as a settled premise in an Antiochene catechetical homily around 390 places it as firmly in the fourth-century East as Gregory of Nazianzus (c. 381) does.
  • The rationale, and how it has been read. The feature most discussed is the ground Chrysostom gives, and the one he does not give. He calls infants “sinless” and lists what baptism gives them without including the remission of sins at all. The translator’s note draws out the significance: “sinless” renders a phrase literally meaning “having no sins”; if that refers to actual sins there is no difficulty (this is how Augustine read the text), but Wenger observes that Chrysostom omits remission of sin from the benefits granted to infants and takes this as “some foundation for the position that Chrysostom considered infants free from all sins, actual and original.”3 Augustine himself quoted the sentence against Julian of Eclanum (Contra Julianum I.21, as cited by Newman).4 John Meyendorff (Orthodox, Byzantine Theology, 1974) reads the Greek tradition as identifying “the inheritance of the Fall as an inheritance essentially of mortality rather than of sinfulness,” finds the idea in Chrysostom, and contrasts Augustine’s argument for infant baptism from “the remission of sins” with the Eastern view that “the Church baptizes children not to ‘remit’ their yet nonexistent sins but in order to give them a new and immortal life,” citing Theodoret of Cyrus.5 On that reading Cyprian’s council, which roots the newborn’s baptism in the “contagion of the ancient death” contracted from Adam, and Augustine, who made original guilt the reason for the rite, give the same practice a different theology of why.
  • Inherited fallenness. The passage is not a statement that Chrysostom held no doctrine of inherited fallenness: Wenger’s inference is offered as a tendency the passage “gives some foundation” to, and Meyendorff places Chrysostom among the Greek writers for whom the inheritance from Adam is mortality, with sinfulness its consequence.5 The passage shows that in his practice and preaching infants are baptized, and that the remission of personal sin is not the reason he gives for baptizing them.
