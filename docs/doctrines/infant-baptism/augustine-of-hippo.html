Augustine of Hippo on infant baptism

Dates: c. 412 (Augustine, 354–430; bishop of Hippo Regius in Roman North Africa. The passages below span three works over about two decades: the anti-Donatist On Baptism (c. 400–401), Letter 98 to Boniface (408), and the anti-Pelagian On the Merits and Forgiveness of Sins, and on the Baptism of Infants (412) — c. 412 is used here as the timeline anchor, the year of the treatise in which he makes infant baptism the hinge of his case for original sin) · Wikipedia
Summary: Augustine sets out the practice’s theological rationale at greater length than any earlier witness on this timeline. Against the Donatists he treats infant baptism as the settled custom of the whole Church — held “not as instituted by Councils, but as a matter of invariable custom,” and therefore “rightly held to have been handed down by apostolical authority.” Against the Pelagians he turns that universal practice into the central argument for original sin: since the Church everywhere baptizes infants for the remission of sins, and infants have committed no actual sins of their own, what the rite washes away in them can only be the guilt inherited from Adam. In Letter 98 he explains how an infant who cannot yet believe is nonetheless truly baptized — a believer “through the sacrament of faith,” whose sponsors answer for him, and who is thereby delivered “from that condemnation which by one man entered into the world.”

Claims & evidence

Infant baptism as the universal custom of the whole Church, handed down from the apostles (On Baptism 4.24.31)

Arguing against the Donatists over the authority of custom, Augustine states in passing — as a premise both sides grant — that infant baptism is the “firm tradition of the universal Church”:

… this is the firm tradition of the universal Church, in respect of the baptism of infants, who certainly are as yet unable “with the heart to believe unto righteousness, and with the mouth to make confession unto salvation,” as the thief could do… and yet no Christian will say that they are baptized to no purpose.

In the next breath he gives his rule for identifying apostolic tradition, and applies it to infant baptism:

And if any one seek for divine authority in this matter, though what is held by the whole Church, and that not as instituted by Councils, but as a matter of invariable custom, is rightly held to have been handed down by apostolical authority…

— Augustine, On Baptism, Against the Donatists 4.24.31.1 The logic is one of antiquity: a usage found everywhere, never traceable to the council that first imposed it, must reach back to the apostles themselves. Augustine deploys the same principle in the same book against Cyprian’s rebaptism of heretics; here he turns it in favor of paedobaptism.

From the universal practice to original sin (On the Merits and Forgiveness of Sins, Book 1)

The center of this witness is Augustine’s anti-Pelagian argument, written for the tribune Marcellinus in 412. The Pelagians granted that infants are baptized but denied they carry any inherited sin; some proposed that infants are baptized not for remission of sin at all but merely to enter the kingdom. Augustine answers from the Church’s own baptismal practice. Since, he argues, infants commit no sins in their “own actual life,” the remission their baptism confers can only be of inherited guilt:

Now, inasmuch as infants are not held bound by any sins of their own actual life, it is the guilt of original sin which is healed in them by the grace of Him who saves them by the laver of regeneration.

— On the Merits and Forgiveness of Sins, and on the Baptism of Infants 1.24.2 He presses the point as an either/or: infants are carried to “the Physician Christ” for “the sacrament of eternal salvation,” yet “they that are whole need not a physician, but they that are sick” — so, he argues, if the newborn were “hurt by no malady of original sin,” the practice would make no sense.3 Addressing two rival positions — that infants have their own actual sins, and that they have none at all — he draws the conclusion he holds both must accept:

… then both sides must agree in conceding to us, that nothing remains but original sin, which can be remitted in baptism to infants.

— On the Merits and Forgiveness of Sins 1.64.4 The chapter just before makes the same case from the rite itself: the infant is exorcised and, through his sponsor, renounces the devil, which would be empty “if he be not held in the devil’s family” (1.63). Augustine’s arguments from the exorcism and from the Church’s prayers are gathered on his lex orandi page.

Infant faith and the sponsors’ answer (Letter 98 to Boniface)

Boniface, a fellow bishop, had pressed a hard question: how can sponsors truthfully say of a speechless infant that “he believes”? Augustine’s reply grounds the whole rite in the notion of a sacrament as an efficacious sign that bears the name of the reality it conveys — “as… the sacrament of Christ’s body is Christ’s body… in the same manner the sacrament of faith is faith”:

Therefore an infant, although he is not yet a believer in the sense of having that faith which includes the consenting will of those who exercise it, nevertheless becomes a believer through the sacrament of that faith… if before he arrives at the use of reason he depart from this life, he is delivered by Christian help, namely, by the love of the Church commending him through this sacrament unto God, from that condemnation which by one man entered into the world.

— Augustine, Letter 98 (to Boniface, A.D. 408), §§9–10.5 The closing phrase — “that condemnation which by one man entered into the world” (Romans 5:12) — again ties the infant’s baptism to Adam’s inheritance, the same thread that runs through the anti-Pelagian treatise.

Notes

  • The practice is a premise, the theology an inference from it. Augustine never sets out to prove that infants are baptized; he assumes it as the invariable, apostolic custom of the whole Church and argues from it, making the doctrine of original sin rest on infant baptism as its firmest ground, an argument that presupposes the practice was not in dispute. The Pelagians conceded the rite and disputed only what it accomplishes, as the earlier disputes (Tertullian, Cyprian’s council) had concerned its timing.
  • Augustine’s rationale beside Chrysostom’s. John Chrysostom (c. 390) calls infants “sinless” and lists the gifts their baptism confers without naming the remission of sins; Augustine makes inherited guilt the reason for the rite. Augustine himself read Chrysostom’s “sinless” as referring to personal sins; Antoine Wenger saw in the omission “some foundation” for holding that Chrysostom considered infants free of original sin as well; and John Meyendorff (Orthodox, 1974) sets a Greek tradition that sees the inheritance from Adam as mortality against the Western framing of Cyprian and Augustine (see Chrysostom’s page).6 Augustine’s framing shares the language of Cyprian’s council (c. 253), which spoke of the newborn’s “contagion of the ancient death” contracted from Adam.
  • The corollary on unbaptized infants, and its reception. Because Augustine grounds infant baptism in an inherited Adamic guilt the rite remits, he draws the corollary Pelagius resisted: the infant who dies unbaptized is delivered from no condemnation and so is lost (softened only to a “mildest” punishment, mitissima poena). Others answered the question more mildly: Ambrose had left it open, Gregory of Nazianzus held such infants “neither glorified nor punished,” Latin theologians from Peter Abelard on read Augustine’s “mildest punishment” as the privation of the beatific vision, and Thomas Aquinas concluded that such infants do not suffer from that privation — the theory of limbo, which the International Theological Commission (2007) describes as having “never entered into the dogmatic definitions of the Magisterium” and as “a possible theological hypothesis”; the same document sets out grounds for hope that such infants are saved.7
  • The apostolic-authority claim is an argument from antiquity, not a documentary citation. Augustine does not point to a New Testament command to baptize infants; his rule infers apostolic origin from custom — a universal custom not imposed by any council is “rightly” presumed apostolic. Calvin made the same antiquity appeal in 1536; the inference turns on the premise that so widespread a usage could not have arisen after the apostles without leaving a trace of its introduction. Whether the practice reaches unbroken to the apostles, or spread early and silently, is disputed, and the earlier witnesses (from Irenaeus onward) are marshaled on both sides; see Do the spread of infant baptism and the patristic delay of baptism show whether the practice was apostolic?
