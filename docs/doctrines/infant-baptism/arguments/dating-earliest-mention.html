How far back does the earliest evidence trace infant baptism?

Disputed reading · on Infant Baptism · witnesses: Tertullian · Justin Martyr · the Didache

Related readings: Do the spread of infant baptism and the patristic delay of baptism show whether the practice was apostolic? · Does Irenaeus’s “born again to God” attest infant baptism? · Does Polycarp’s “eighty-six years” of service show he was baptized as an infant?

The passage

And so, according to the circumstances and disposition, and even age, of each individual, the delay of baptism is preferable; principally, however, in the case of little children. For why is it necessary—if (baptism itself) is not so necessary—that the sponsors likewise should be thrust into danger? Who both themselves, by reason of mortality, may fail to fulfil their promises, and may be disappointed by the development of an evil disposition, in those for whom they stood? The Lord does indeed say, "Forbid them not to come unto me." Let them "come," then, while they are growing up; let them "come" while they are learning, while they are learning whither to come; let them become Christians when they have become able to know Christ. Why does the innocent period of life hasten to the "remission of sins?" More caution will be exercised in worldly matters: so that one who is not trusted with earthly substance is trusted with divine! Let them know how to "ask" for salvation, that you may seem (at least) to have given "to him that asketh."

— Tertullian, On Baptism (De Baptismo) 18, c. 203, source.1

As many as are persuaded and believe that what we teach and say is true, and undertake to be able to live accordingly, are instructed to pray and to entreat God with fasting, for the remission of their sins that are past, we praying and fasting with them. Then they are brought by us where there is water, and are regenerated in the same manner in which we were ourselves regenerated.

— Justin Martyr, The First Apology 61, c. 155, source.2

The question

The earliest extant explicit discussion of the baptism of little children, Tertullian’s of c. 203, argues that it should be delayed; the fuller baptismal descriptions that survive from before c. 200, in the Didache and Justin Martyr, portray instructed, professing candidates and do not mention infants.3 How far back these data trace the practice, whether to an inherited custom reaching into the second century and the apostolic generation or to a development that took hold around c. 200, is disputed. Among Orthodox writers, the treatment of this question located here is a priest’s essay holding the first reading (Lawrence Farley, 2018);4 no Orthodox academic study of it was found.

An inherited custom, older than its first mention

In brief: Tertullian argues against a custom already in place, so infant baptism was inherited from the second century and the apostolic mission.

Held by: the New Testament scholar Joachim Jeremias, Infant Baptism in the First Four Centuries (1958; ET 1960) and The Origins of Infant Baptism (1962; ET 1963);5 the church historian Philip Schaff, History of the Christian Church vol. 2 and The Oldest Church Manual (1885);6 the Reformed theologian John Murray, Christian Baptism (1952);7 and the Catechism of the Catholic Church §1252 (1992), for which there is “explicit testimony to this practice from the second century on.”8 Earlier holders, from the third century on, are listed in the note.9

The earliest explicit voice is a critic, and on this reading a critic is a witness that the thing exists. Tertullian is trying to curb a going practice, with reasons (the risk to the sponsors, the innocence of childhood) why it should be deferred; one does not write to slow a custom that has not started. His protest places infant baptism in the church of c. 200 and implies an origin earlier still.1 The explicit chain that follows runs the same way: a rubric c. 215 in the Apostolic Tradition (“baptize the little children first… if they cannot [answer for themselves], let their parents or other relatives speak for them”), Origen’s c. 248 claim that “the Church received from the apostles the tradition of giving baptism even to little children,” and Cyprian’s council of c. 253, which debates only the day.10 No writer records the practice as new: Schaff held that the apostolic fathers’ silence proves nothing and that “no time can be fixed at which it was first introduced.”6 The Didache and Justin, on this reading, are a church order and an apology describing the conversion of adult pagans, so their focus on professing converts is what their genre predicts, and the fourth-century delayers are a theologically motivated cohort, a temporary decline from an earlier custom.11 For the apostolic period itself, Murray points to how few baptisms the New Testament records at all: of these, three are household baptisms, and “analogy would require us to believe that household baptism was quite common”; the evidence for infant baptism “falls into the category of good and necessary inference.”7 Jeremias adds indirect data that would put children among the baptized from the mission period: the New Testament household baptisms, Jewish proselyte baptism (which took in a convert’s minor children), the Polycarp “eighty-six years” datum, early funerary inscriptions, and Justin’s Christians “discipled from childhood”; the reading of Irenaeus that counts infants among those “born again to God” (c. 180) would add an implicit witness a generation before Tertullian. On this reading the custom Tertullian opposed was already inherited in his day, and its roots run back through the second century into the households of the apostolic mission.

A practice first attested c. 200, spreading thereafter

In brief: the record before c. 200 describes the baptism of professing believers, and infant baptism, first attested around 200, became general only in the following centuries.

Held by: the New Testament scholar Kurt Aland, Did the Early Church Baptize Infants? (1961; ET 1963), written in reply to Jeremias, and the church historian Everett Ferguson, Baptism in the Early Church (2009);12 the church historian David F. Wright, “The Origins of Infant Baptism — Child Believers’ Baptism?” (1987) and “One Baptism or Two?” (1988);13 and Thomas R. Schreiner and Shawn D. Wright, eds., Believer’s Baptism (2006), with earlier holders listed in the note.14

On this reading the pre-200 record is not silent about baptism; it is fairly full, and what it describes is adult, faith-professing baptism. The Didache presupposes a candidate taught the “Two Ways” who fasts before the rite, and Justin describes those who “are persuaded and believe” and then “are regenerated.”3 Aland pressed these descriptions against Jeremias, and read Tertullian’s protest as resistance to a spreading practice. Each item of the indirect evidence, on this reading, can be taken another way: the household formulae name no infant and repeatedly specify hearing, believing members; the bearing of proselyte baptism on Christian practice is disputed; Polycarp’s words need mean only a lifelong Christian; and the datable infant inscriptions cluster later and often record the emergency baptism of a dying child, which is the setting Aland and Ferguson give for the practice’s spread. Ferguson finds firm evidence only from c. 200 and treats general infant baptism as a third-century-and-later development tied to emergency baptism and the theology of original sin. Wright notes that “the very first attestation of infant baptism as the normal practice (in Hippolytus’ Apostolic Tradition, c.215) is in the form of an instruction how to baptize—first those who can answer for themselves and then those who cannot,” and reads much of the earliest evidence as “patient of the interpretation that quite young children were baptized on their own profession.”13 In the fourth century Basil, Gregory of Nazianzus, Ambrose, Rufinus, Jerome and Augustine, all reared by Christian parents, were baptized as adults, which Aland read as showing that infant baptism of Christian-born children had not yet become the norm, so that the practice first attested c. 200 became general only later.15
