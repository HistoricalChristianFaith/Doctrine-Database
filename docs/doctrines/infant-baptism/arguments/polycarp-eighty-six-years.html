Does Polycarp’s “eighty-six years” of service (Martyrdom of Polycarp 9) show he was baptized as an infant?

Disputed reading · on Infant Baptism · witness: Polycarp of Smyrna

Related readings: How far back does the earliest evidence trace infant baptism? · Do the spread of infant baptism and the patristic delay of baptism show whether the practice was apostolic? · Does Irenaeus’s “born again to God” attest infant baptism?

The passage

Eighty and six years have I served Him, and He never did me any injury: how then can I blaspheme my King and my Saviour?

— Polycarp of Smyrna, at his martyrdom, Martyrdom of Polycarp 9.1 The Greek states the duration of his service (edouleusa, from douleuō, to serve as a bondservant); the text does not state his age.

The question

Polycarp, bishop of Smyrna, was martyred about A.D. 155–156 on the traditional dating. If his “eighty and six years” of service span his whole life, he was a Christian from birth about A.D. 69–70, in the apostolic generation. Whether the words carry evidence that he was baptized as an infant, and so of infant baptism in the first century, is disputed.

Evidence of a Christian life, and initiation, from infancy

In brief: Polycarp’s eighty-six years of service span his whole life, so he was a Christian, and baptized, from infancy.

Held by: the New Testament scholar Joachim Jeremias, Infant Baptism in the First Four Centuries (1958; ET 1960), who adduced the datum as one strand in his cumulative case for the continuity of infant baptism from the apostolic mission;2 and the popular paedobaptist testimony lists, which cite it in the sharper form that Polycarp was “baptized as an infant.”3

The most natural way to hear “eighty and six years have I served Him” from an aged bishop about to die is that it counts his years, and that his service of Christ filled them. On that reading Polycarp knew no stretch of his life that was not Christ’s, which fits a man born into the faith rather than converted out of paganism. The verb strengthens the point: douleuō is the language of a whole life given over as a bondservant, and reads easily as co-extensive with that life rather than dating from a later adult decision. A man who served Christ from infancy was, on this reading, brought into Christ in infancy, and the rite of entry is baptism. Polycarp’s standing gives the datum its weight. Irenaeus, who knew him, reports that he had been “instructed by apostles” and “appointed bishop of Smyrna,” and Jerome calls him “a disciple of the Apostle John and by him ordained bishop of Smyrna.” A Polycarp raised as a Christian from birth therefore documents children formed in the faith from infancy in a household of the apostolic age, the natural soil for household initiation. Jeremias set the datum beside the New Testament household baptisms, the analogy of Jewish proselyte baptism, and the earliest funerary inscriptions of baptized infants, offering it as corroboration within that larger argument rather than as a proof on its own.

A confession of lifelong fidelity, silent on the date of baptism

In brief: the words give the length of Polycarp’s faithful service, not the date of his baptism.

Held by: the New Testament scholar Kurt Aland, Did the Early Church Baptize Infants? (1961; ET 1963), answering Jeremias point for point, who held that the “eighty-six years” need mean only that Polycarp had been a Christian all his life; and the church historian Everett Ferguson, Baptism in the Early Church (2009), who does not count the datum among the firm evidence for the practice.4

On this reading the sentence gives the length of Polycarp’s service, not his age. The eighty-six years may count his whole life, or they may count from his conversion and baptism, in which case they say nothing about infancy and could make him an adult convert of the first century. If they span his whole life, douleuō is the language of loyalty, spoken under interrogation to explain why he cannot now blaspheme the Master he has served all his days. A man raised from the cradle in a Christian home speaks exactly this way whether he was baptized in infancy or came to the font as a believing child or youth; the words fix his lifelong fidelity, not the date of his baptism. In credobaptist practice a child may be reared in the faith and baptized later on profession, so a Christian-from-birth Polycarp is consistent with either practice. The arithmetic is also open: the birth-year is back-calculated from a martyrdom date that the dating debate places anywhere from the traditional 155–156 to as late as 167 or 177.4 On this reading what the datum attests is a lifelong Christian, raised in the faith from birth, whose words leave the date of his baptism open.
