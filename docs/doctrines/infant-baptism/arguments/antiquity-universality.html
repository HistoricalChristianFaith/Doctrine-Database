Do the spread of infant baptism and the patristic delay of baptism show whether the practice was apostolic?

Disputed reading · on Infant Baptism · witnesses: Origen · Augustine · Gregory of Nazianzus

Related readings: How far back does the earliest evidence trace infant baptism? · Does Irenaeus’s “born again to God” attest infant baptism? · Does Polycarp’s “eighty-six years” of service show he was baptized as an infant? · Did the medieval Waldenses reject infant baptism?

The passage

And if any one seek for divine authority in this matter, though what is held by the whole Church, and that not as instituted by Councils, but as a matter of invariable custom, is rightly held to have been handed down by apostolical authority…

— Augustine, On Baptism, Against the Donatists 4.24.31 (c. 400–401), on the baptism of infants, source.1

My cleansing was deferred, as if it were necessary that I should, if I lived, be further polluted; because, forsooth, the guilt contracted by sin would, after baptism, be greater and more perilous

— Augustine, Confessions 1.11, on the baptism his mother Monica prepared when he fell gravely ill as a boy and then put off on his recovery, source.2

The question

Infant baptism is discussed as an existing custom by c. 200, claimed as a tradition from the apostles by the mid-third century, and held in Augustine’s day as the custom of the whole church, with no surviving record of a controversy over introducing it. Through the fourth century, many children of devout Christian families, Augustine among them, were baptized only as adults. Whether this record shows a practice received from the apostles, a post-apostolic introduction that became universal, or a usage whose general observance became a duty only over time, is disputed. Among Orthodox writers, the treatment of this historical question located here is a priest’s essay (Farley, 2018); no Orthodox academic study of it was found.

An apostolic custom, received everywhere

In brief: children were baptized from the apostles’ time, which is why the practice is found everywhere with no record of its introduction.

Held by: the New Testament scholar Joachim Jeremias, Infant Baptism in the First Four Centuries (1958; ET 1960) and The Origins of Infant Baptism (1962; ET 1963);3,4 the Catechism of the Catholic Church §1252 (Catholic, 1992), which calls the practice “an immemorial tradition of the Church” and holds it “quite possible” that infants were baptized “from the beginning of the apostolic preaching”;5 Lawrence Farley (Orthodox priest; essay, 2018);6 and, earlier, Origen (c. 246), the earliest extant writer to call the custom apostolic, with later holders listed in the note.7

The attestation is early and dense: explicit discussion by c. 200 (Tertullian), a rubric prescribing it c. 215 (the Apostolic Tradition), the apostolic-tradition claim c. 248 (Origen: “the Church received from the apostles the tradition of giving baptism even to little children”), a unanimous conciliar assumption of it c. 253 (Cyprian), and by the fourth century the settled custom of East and West. The early disputes were over when, not whether: Tertullian argues for delay, Cyprian’s council rejects an eighth-day delay that Fidus proposed while himself assuming infant baptism, and Gregory counsels waiting to about age three absent danger.8 The early church left tracks wherever it adopted or fought over a practice (the Quartodeciman Easter dispute, the rebaptism controversy, the Arian crisis); infant baptism left none on the question of whether to do it: no named innovator, no region that held out, no synod to decide on receiving it.3 For Jeremias the fourth-century deferrals are a temporary decline from that earlier custom,9 a preference within churches that had the rite: Gregory of Nazianzus, in the passage that counsels waiting, prescribes immediate baptism “if any danger presses,” since “it is better that they should be unconsciously sanctified than that they should depart unsealed and uninitiated.”10 Its motive was fear of post-baptismal sin, the Shepherd of Hermas’s teaching that after baptism “there is but one repentance,” which presupposes that baptismal grace was on offer to the child.11 The same churches resisted the delay: Cyprian’s sixty-six bishops refused an eight-day postponement, Basil and Nazianzus preached against adult procrastination, and the deathbed vogue receded.12 Farley describes infant baptism as an apostolic “exception to the rule of convert baptism” that “at very least… went back beyond living memory in Origen’s day.”6 On this reading a usage with no recorded point of entry, practised in every region, and traced to the apostles by the earliest writers to explain it, is an inheritance from the apostolic church.13

A usage whose general observance became a duty over time

In brief: infant baptism was an early usage, but baptizing every Christian child became the firm rule only between the third and fifth centuries.

Held by: John Henry Newman (Catholic), An Essay on the Development of Christian Doctrine (1845; rev. ed. 1878), which takes infant baptism and its fourth-century deferral as one of its instances of development.14

Newman counts infant baptism among the usages that are “not gained by the direct use and immediate application of Scripture” but “by the unconscious growth of ideas suggested by the letter and habitual to the mind,” naming it beside the observance of the first day of the week and the duty of public worship.14 He sets the fourth-century deferral out in full. Gregory of Nazianzus, dedicated to God by his mother at birth, was baptized only on landing from a perilous voyage to Athens; Basil, the son of confessors, not until “he had come to man’s estate”; Augustine, made a catechumen at birth, at thirty-three; Ambrose when he was chosen bishop; Jerome only after he “had reached man’s estate and had travelled.” Among the motives he names “a keen sense and awe of the special privileges of baptism which could only once be received” and a reluctance to be bound to “a strict rule of life.” Chrysostom, by contrast, “had a clear view of the importance and duty of infant baptism.” Newman concludes that infant baptism “was less earnestly insisted on in early times”: “neither in Dalmatia nor in Cappadocia, neither in Rome, nor in Africa, was it then imperative on Christian parents, as it is now, to give baptism to their young children.” The rule came with time: “It was on retrospect and after the truths of the Creed had sunk into the Christian mind, that the authority of such men as St. Cyprian, St. Chrysostom, and St. Augustine brought round the orbis terrarum to the conclusion, which the infallible Church confirmed, that observance of the rite was the rule, and the non-observance the exception.”15 On this reading what grew between the third and fifth centuries was the duty to baptize every Christian child, an instance, for Newman, of a usage held implicitly that came in time to be fully stated and kept.

A post-apostolic development that became universal

In brief: infant baptism began after the apostolic age, growing out of the baptism of believing children and of the dying, and then became the universal rule.

Held by: the New Testament scholar Kurt Aland, Did the Early Church Baptize Infants? (1961; ET 1963), in reply to Jeremias, and the church historian Everett Ferguson, Baptism in the Early Church (2009);16 the church historian David F. Wright, “One Baptism or Two? Reflections on the History of Christian Baptism” (1988);17 and the essays in Thomas R. Schreiner and Shawn D. Wright, eds., Believer’s Baptism (2006).18

On this reading the silence runs the other way. The New Testament names no baptized infant and describes the baptism of those who hear and believe, no direct evidence of infant baptism survives before c. 200, and the earliest writer to mention it, Tertullian, argues against it. Aland read that protest as resistance to a spreading practice and re-read Jeremias’s indirect evidence (Polycarp, the household formulae, the tomb inscriptions); Ferguson finds the firm evidence beginning c. 200, with general infant baptism developing in the third century and later around the emergency baptism of the sick and the theology of original sin. Universal custom need not be apostolic: in De Corona Tertullian lists rites “maintained throughout” the churches “from tradition” without scriptural warrant (triple immersion, tasting milk and honey, not kneeling on the Lord’s day, signing the forehead).19 The deferral is evidence in its own right. Tertullian judged “the delay of baptism… preferable; principally, however, in the case of little children,” wanting children to “become Christians when they have become able to know Christ.”20 Basil, from a devout Christian family, and his brother Gregory of Nyssa were baptized as adults, as were Ambrose, Jerome, Rufinus and Chrysostom; Monica deferred her own son’s baptism.2 Eusebius, quoting Clement of Alexandria, tells of a presbyter who “reared, kept, cherished, and finally baptized” a youth committed to him,21 and the ninth-century abbot Walafrid Strabo recalled that “in ancient times” baptism was given to those already matured.22 Aland read the deferral among the Christian-born as evidence that general infant baptism was a later development; Ferguson ties that development to the emergency baptism of the sick, a thesis Wright summarizes as “baby baptism was perhaps at first given only to dying babies, while others were baptized as and when they could answer for themselves.” Wright judges that “it is unlikely that in the first few decades of the church babies were baptized,” reads much of the earliest evidence as “patient of the interpretation that quite young children were baptized on their own profession,” and adds that baby baptism, “when it did develop, seems to have been accepted with little or no protest.” On this reading the general baptism of infants grew, within a century or so of the apostles, out of the baptism of believing children and of the dying, and became the church’s rule after the apostolic age.17
