Does Irenaeus's “born again to God” (Adv. Haer. II.22.4) attest infant baptism?

Disputed reading · on Infant Baptism · witness: Irenaeus of Lyons

Related readings: How far back does the earliest evidence trace infant baptism? · Do the spread of infant baptism and the patristic delay of baptism show whether the practice was apostolic? · Does Polycarp’s “eighty-six years” of service show he was baptized as an infant?

The passage

For He came to save all through means of Himself—all, I say, who through Him are born again to God—infants, and children, and boys, and youths, and old men.

— Irenaeus, Against Heresies II.22.4, New Advent.1 The Latin behind “born again to God” is renascuntur in Deum.

The question

Irenaeus, writing c. 180, names infants among those “born again to God” through Christ, and elsewhere calls baptism “the new birth unto God.” Whether this sentence attests that infants were being baptized in his day, which would make it the earliest extant witness to the practice, a generation before Tertullian’s explicit discussion (c. 203), or states the reach of the redemption Christ brought to every age of life, is disputed.

An implicit witness to the baptism of infants

In brief: “born again to God” is Irenaeus’s own name for baptism, so the infants he names among the reborn were baptized.

Held by: the nineteenth-century editor of the Ante-Nicene Fathers translation, who annotated the phrase “renascuntur in Deum” as referring “doubtless… to baptism”;2 the New Testament scholar Joachim Jeremias, Infant Baptism in the First Four Centuries (1958; ET 1960), who counted Irenaeus among the second-century evidence for the antiquity of the practice;3 and the popular testimony catalogs of Catholic Answers and churchfathers.org, which open their lists with this passage.4

The case rests on Irenaeus’s own vocabulary. “Born again to God” translates renasci in Deum, and Irenaeus defines that very phrase as baptism: in the Demonstration of the Apostolic Preaching 3 he says “this baptism… is the new birth unto God, that we should no longer be the sons of mortal men, but of the eternal and perpetual God,” and in §7 he speaks of “the baptism of our regeneration.”5 In Against Heresies III.17.1 “the power of regeneration into God” is glossed directly by the baptismal command of Matthew 28:19, which is the parallel the Ante-Nicene Fathers editor cites.6 A surviving fragment on Naaman’s sevenfold washing makes the same equation with John 3:5: “we are made clean, by means of the sacred water and the invocation of the Lord… being spiritually regenerated.”7 On this reading, new birth to God is Irenaeus’s settled name for baptism, not a looser metaphor, so when he lists infants first among those “born again to God” he is using baptismal language and placing infants inside the class it describes. Read this way, the sentence carries the evidence back a generation before Tertullian, whose objection to the baptism of little children already presupposes a living custom: the two fit together, Irenaeus showing the assumption that infants belong among the reborn already in place c. 180.

A statement about Christ sanctifying every age

In brief: the sentence says that Christ, by living through every age, opened new birth to infants too; it does not record who was brought to the font.

Held by: the New Testament scholar Kurt Aland, Did the Early Church Baptize Infants? (1961; ET 1963), who judged this text not decisive evidence for the practice;8 and the church historian Everett Ferguson, Baptism in the Early Church (2009) (grants that it “could be the earliest reference to infant baptism”; reads it in its recapitulation context).9

On this reading the sentence belongs to Irenaeus’s argument for recapitulation. Against opponents who confined the Saviour’s work to a narrow compass, he is proving that Christ “passed through every age, becoming an infant for infants, thus sanctifying infants; a child for children,” and so on to the old.10 The list of ages is there to enumerate the life-stages Christ redeemed by living through them, and “all who are born again to God… infants… and old men” states the scope of that redemption rather than recording who was brought to the font. The infants are “born again” in the sense that the incarnation opened new birth to their age too, a claim about what Christ made possible that need not presuppose that the church was baptizing babies as a matter of custom. Because salvation and baptism are so closely joined for Irenaeus, the same words can carry both senses; the sentence reaches baptism through the lexical bridge, and narrates no infant’s baptism. On this reading the earliest explicit evidence for the practice remains Tertullian’s objection of c. 203, and Irenaeus is a possible, implicit earlier witness rather than a documentary one.
