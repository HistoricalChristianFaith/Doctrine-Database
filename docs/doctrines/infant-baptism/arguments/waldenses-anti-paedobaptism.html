Did the medieval Waldenses reject infant baptism?

Disputed reading · on Infant Baptism · witness: the Waldenses, as reported by Reinerius Saccho (1254)

Related readings: Do the spread of infant baptism and the patristic delay of baptism show whether the practice was apostolic? · see also the Baptist successionism timeline

The passage

Secondly, they condemn all the Sacraments of the Church; in the first place, as to baptism, they say that the Catechism is nothing — also, that the ablution which is given to infants profits nothing.

— Reinerius Saccho, Of the Sects of Modern Heretics (Summa de Catharis et Pauperibus de Lugduno, 1254), on the “Leonists” (Waldenses), trans. S. R. Maitland, source.1

The question

The Waldenses, the dissenting movement that grew from the twelfth-century preaching of Valdes of Lyon and survived to the Reformation, are reported by a thirteenth-century inquisitor to have held that the washing given to infants “profits nothing.” Whether the Waldenses, as a body or in some strand, rejected infant baptism, and so form a link in a continuous believers’-baptism witness across the medieval centuries, is disputed.

An anti-paedobaptist strand of Waldensian dissent

In brief: some Waldenses, at least in some regions, denied the value of infant baptism or re-baptized converts, and so belong in a line of believers’-baptism dissent.

Held by: the Reformed historian Pierre Allix (1690) and the Baptist-successionist writers who followed him, among them J. M. Carroll (d. 1931), whose “Trail of Blood” chain of dissenting churches includes the Waldenses;2 the church historian Philip Schaff, History of the Christian Church, vol. 5 (for some quarters only);3 and Leonard Verduin, The Reformers and Their Stepchildren (1964) (holds that the Waldensians re-baptized converts; grants that they baptized their own infants).4

The case starts from a well-placed witness. Reinerius Saccho was a former Cathar turned Dominican inquisitor, unusually well informed about the groups he prosecuted, and his report that the Leonists held “the ablution which is given to infants profits nothing” is an authentic thirteenth-century record.5 Schaff, surveying the sources, finds among the medieval Waldenses “a disposition, in some quarters at least, to deny infant baptism and to some extent the opinion seems to have prevailed that infants were saved without baptism,” and notes that Rainerius “declares without qualification that the Poor Men of Lombardy hold to the salvation of infants not baptized.”6 The strand is located chiefly in the more radical Italian “Poor of Lombardy,” who had contact with the Bohemian reform, and in Alpine communities in touch with the Hussites. Verduin cites a fourteenth-century Strasbourg manuscript reporting that the Waldensians “have themselves rebaptized by their own clergy,” and Luther’s report of 1522 that “They baptize little ones … and rebaptize those who come to them from us”; on his reading what they rejected was “the Constantinian overtones” of the established rite, and they were “forerunners” of the Anabaptists.4 In its narrow form the reading holds that this later, regional Waldensianism questioned the necessity of infant baptism, holding unbaptized infants saved. In its broad, successionist form it gathers the Waldenses, with the Paulicians, Bogomils, Albigenses and Cathars (the dualist groups among them refused infant baptism), into a chain of pre-Reformation dissent transmitting “the true New Testament gospel” outside Rome. On this reading the Waldenses, in some regions at least, belong among the medieval dissenters who questioned the baptism of infants, in a line of believers’-baptism witness that runs to the Anabaptists and Baptists.

A movement that kept infant baptism

In brief: the Waldenses, from their founder on, were a movement that baptized its infants.

Held by: the historians Euan Cameron, The Reformation of the Heretics (1986) and Waldenses (2000), and Gabriel Audisio, The Waldensian Dissent (1999);7 Walter L. Wakefield and Austin P. Evans, Heresies of the High Middle Ages (1969);8 and Sam Young, “Waldensianism Before Waldo” (2022);9 with further holders in the note.10

On this reading the founder’s own profession states the movement’s original position. Valdes subscribed a Profession of Faith c. 1180: “We approve, therefore, of the baptism of infants, for we confess and believe that they are saved if they shall die after baptism before they commit sin.” The reconciliation of his companion Durand of Huesca (1207) contains no rejection of the rite.8 Saccho’s line, on this reading, denies that the washing saves an infant, not that the Waldenses re-baptized on profession, and the movement’s own Rescriptum “declares that baptism was regarded as necessary for all,” with practice differing between the Italian and French wings.6 Cameron and Audisio find the late-medieval Alpine Waldenses, served by itinerant barbes, outwardly conforming to the Catholic sacraments and taking their own infants to the parish priest to be baptized; Reformed, still paedobaptist, theology entered only after contact with Geneva and the Synod of Chanforan (1532).7 Young traces the “proto-Protestant” Waldensian genealogy to nineteenth-century American anti-Catholic writing, and Wakefield and Evans and Cameron trace part of the chain to Allix’s rendering of Saccho’s ablutio (“washing”) as absolutio (“absolution”) and to error-lists from the Coussord (1548) and Gretser (1613) editions that they judge interpolated or misattributed.9 The twelfth-century movement that did make baptism a believer’s rite, and with which the Waldenses are often merged in this literature, is the Petrobrusians; the dualist Cathars replaced water baptism with the consolamentum, from which children were debarred and which was normally given to adults near death, and some Paulicians let Catholic clergy baptize their infants.11 On this reading the Waldenses, from Valdes’s profession of c. 1180 to the Alpine communities served by the barbes, were a movement that baptized its infants.
