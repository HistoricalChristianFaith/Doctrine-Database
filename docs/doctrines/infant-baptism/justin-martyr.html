Justin Martyr on infant baptism

Dates: c. 155 (Justin Martyr, c. 100–c. 165 — the Palestinian-born philosopher who became a second-century Christian apologist and was martyred at Rome under Marcus Aurelius; his First Apology is usually dated c. 155–157 and the Dialogue with Trypho a few years later) · Wikipedia
Summary: Justin gives the fullest surviving pre-Tertullian account of Christian baptism, and on the infant-baptism question he is cited by both sides. His set-piece description of the rite (First Apology 61) portrays instructed adults who “are persuaded and believe” and are then “regenerated,” a description Kurt Aland pressed against Joachim Jeremias; Jeremias adduced his mention of those who “have been Christ’s disciples from childhood” (First Apology 15) as an early hint the other way, and Justin also frames baptism as the “spiritual circumcision” that replaces the carnal one (Dialogue 43). Justin fixes what the standard rite looked like around the mid-second century and does not say whose children were brought to it.

Claims & evidence

The baptismal set-piece describes instructed, professing believers

Justin’s First Apology 61 is the most detailed second-century description of Christian initiation we possess, written to explain the rite to a pagan emperor. It makes the candidate’s own persuasion and resolve the entry-point of the whole process:

As many as are persuaded and believe that what we teach and say is true, and undertake to be able to live accordingly, are instructed to pray and to entreat God with fasting, for the remission of their sins that are past, we praying and fasting with them. Then they are brought by us where there is water, and are regenerated in the same manner in which we were ourselves regenerated.

— The First Apology 61, trans. Dods & Reith (Ante-Nicene Fathers vol. 1), full text (cf. New Advent).1 The candidate is one who is “persuaded and believe[s],” who “undertake[s]” a manner of life, and who fasts and prays for the remission of past sins before the water — each of them an act a newborn cannot perform. This is the same portrait the earlier Didache paints of a catechized candidate who fasts before the font. Kurt Aland pressed this material against Joachim Jeremias: the direct record before Tertullian is not silent about baptism but fairly full, and what it describes is adult, faith-professing baptism.2

“Disciples from childhood” (First Apology 15) and its readings

Defending the moral seriousness of Christians, Justin points to lifelong believers who have kept chastity into old age:

And many, both men and women, who have been Christ’s disciples from childhood, remain pure at the age of sixty or seventy years; and I boast that I could produce such from every race of men.

— The First Apology 15, full text (cf. New Advent).3 Jeremias adduced this line (1 Apology 15.6) as an early hint that children were incorporated into the church from the first — if these sexagenarians and septuagenarians had been Christ’s “disciples from childhood,” some, on his reading, were baptized as children. Aland read “disciple from childhood” (ἐκ παίδων μαθητεύω) as catechized and raised in the faith from a young age, discipleship being a matter of teaching, and so, consistently with 1 Apology 61, as describing those brought up as Christians who professed and were baptized when able.4

Baptism as “spiritual circumcision” (Dialogue 43)

In the Dialogue with Trypho, arguing against his Jewish interlocutor that the fleshly circumcision of the Law has given way to a spiritual one, Justin identifies that spiritual circumcision with baptism:

And we, who have approached God through Him, have received not carnal, but spiritual circumcision, which Enoch and those like him observed. And we have received it through baptism, since we were sinners, by God’s mercy; and all men may equally obtain it.

— Dialogue with Trypho 43, full text (cf. New Advent).5 This is the earliest extant explicit equation of baptism with circumcision. The circumcision–baptism parallel appears in later writers on this timeline, Cyprian (c. 253), Ambrose (c. 387) and Calvin (1536), the last arguing that because circumcision was given to infants the covenant sign belongs to a believer’s children; Justin draws no inference about infants. In his own gloss, those who receive this spiritual circumcision are said to have “approached God through Him” and to have received it “since we were sinners,” the language of a believing convert, and the infant-circumcision half of the analogy is left implicit. The line does not mention the baptism of infants.6

On the subjects of baptism he is silent — and both sides cite him

Justin nowhere raises the question whether the infant children of Christians were baptized; the controversy had not yet been joined (the earliest explicit discussant, Tertullian, is a half-century later). What he describes — a persuaded, believing candidate who fasts before the water — fits an adult convert. Aland pressed it against Jeremias as evidence of the original norm, and Jeremias answered from First Apology 15 (above). On this axis Justin is the same kind of witness as the Didache, whose readings are set out on its page: a mid-second-century description of the standard rite, adduced by both sides; see How far back does the earliest evidence trace infant baptism?7

Notes

  • As a c. 155 witness Justin sits between the Didache (c. 100) and Irenaeus (c. 180) on this timeline, before the earliest explicit discussant, Tertullian (c. 203). He is a witness to the rite as practised in the mid-second century, not to a position in a debate not yet begun.
  • The Dialogue 43 “spiritual circumcision” line is the earliest extant baptism–circumcision equation; it does not extend the sign to infants.
