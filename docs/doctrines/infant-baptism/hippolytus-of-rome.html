Hippolytus of Rome (the Apostolic Tradition) on infant baptism

Dates: c. 215 (the conventional date of the Apostolic Tradition, the church order traditionally ascribed to Hippolytus of Rome, c. 170–235; a presbyter and the most learned theologian of the early-third-century Roman church, later reckoned a martyr) · Wikipedia
Summary: The Apostolic Tradition §21, describing the order of a baptism, directs that the little children be baptized first — those old enough answering the interrogations for themselves, and those too young having their parents or relatives answer in their stead. It is one of the earliest descriptions of infants being baptized with sponsors speaking on their behalf, set out as part of the order of the rite. The document's date, authorship, and unity are disputed.

Claims & evidence

“First baptize the little ones… if not, their parents or other relatives shall speak for them”

In its description of the paschal baptismal rite — the blessing of the water at cockcrow, the renunciation of Satan, the stripping and threefold immersion with its interrogations — the Apostolic Tradition lays down the order in which the candidates are to be baptized. Children come first, and where they cannot yet answer the credal questions themselves, a family member answers for them:

And first baptize the little ones; if they can speak for themselves, they shall do so; if not, their parents or other relatives shall speak for them. Then baptize the men, and last of all the women…

— The Apostolic Tradition of Hippolytus §21.4, trans. Burton Scott Easton (Cambridge, 1934).1 The much-quoted Dix–Chadwick rendering carries the same sense: “And they shall baptize the little children first. And if they can answer for themselves, let them answer. But if they cannot, let their parents or someone from their family answer for them.”2 Two features of the passage. First, it is descriptive, not polemical: unlike Tertullian a decade or so earlier, who argues that little children's baptism should be delayed, the Apostolic Tradition records infant baptism as part of the liturgical order, without argument for it. Second, it already presupposes the institution of sponsors: because an infant cannot make the baptismal renunciations and profession, “their parents or other relatives shall speak for them” — the very machinery of proxy-answering that Tertullian had worried put the sponsors “into danger.” The two witnesses thus corroborate each other: one opposing the custom, one prescribing it, both attesting that infants were being brought to the font in the early third century.

Notes

  • Authorship and date are disputed. The Apostolic Tradition is only traditionally ascribed to Hippolytus; the ascription rests on a reconstruction, and the Greek original is lost, the text surviving mainly in a fourth-century Latin (Verona) palimpsest and in Coptic, Arabic, and Ethiopic versions. Paul Bradshaw, Maxwell Johnson and L. Edward Phillips treat it as a composite “living” church order, layered from the mid-second into the fourth century rather than the work of one author at one date — David Wright's summary is that “almost everything concerning this text remains the subject of lively scholarly argument.” On this reading the c. 215 date is a convention, and this particular rubric may belong to a later stratum.3
  • Dating the rubric. The directive presents infant baptism, complete with proxy sponsors, as an established practice in the community whose rite this document preserves. It does not by itself date that establishment: if the layer is early-third-century it corroborates Tertullian; if later, it shows the practice fixed by the time the text reached its surviving form.
  • Readings of the rubric. The church historian David F. Wright notes that “the very first attestation of infant baptism as the normal practice (in Hippolytus’ Apostolic Tradition, c.215) is in the form of an instruction how to baptize—first those who can answer for themselves and then those who cannot,” and asks “At what age would children in a newly converted family be able to answer for themselves?”; he reads much of the earliest evidence as “patient of the interpretation that quite young children were baptized on their own profession.”4 How far back the custom reaches is disputed; see How far back does the earliest evidence trace infant baptism?
  • Place on the timeline. At c. 215 the Apostolic Tradition falls just after Tertullian (c. 203) and a generation before Origen's claim (c. 248) that the church received infant baptism “from the apostles” — the earliest extant descriptive prescription of the rite, between the earliest recorded opponent of the custom and the earliest extant appeal to apostolic warrant.
