Tertullian on infant baptism

Dates: c. 203 (the likely date of On Baptism / De Baptismo; Tertullian, c. 155 – c. 220, the earliest major Christian theologian whose Latin works survive, traditionally called a presbyter of Carthage — though his own works rather class him among the laity, the title descending only from Jerome; see the argument) · Wikipedia
Summary: Tertullian is the earliest Christian writer to discuss the baptism of little children explicitly — and he argues against it, counselling that baptism be delayed until a person can ask for it and knows Christ. His objection is the earliest extant evidence that infants were being brought for baptism in the church of his day.

Claims & evidence

Baptism should be delayed — "principally… in the case of little children"

Near the close of On Baptism, having defended the sacrament itself against a woman of the Cainite sect who denied it, Tertullian turns to the questions of who should baptize and when. He warns that baptism is "not rashly to be administered," and, weighing "the circumstances and disposition, and even age, of each individual," recommends postponement — above all for young children:

And so, according to the circumstances and disposition, and even age, of each individual, the delay of baptism is preferable; principally, however, in the case of little children. For why is it necessary—if (baptism itself) is not so necessary—that the sponsors likewise should be thrust into danger? Who both themselves, by reason of mortality, may fail to fulfil their promises, and may be disappointed by the development of an evil disposition, in those for whom they stood? The Lord does indeed say, "Forbid them not to come unto me." Let them "come," then, while they are growing up; let them "come" while they are learning, while they are learning whither to come; let them become Christians when they have become able to know Christ. Why does the innocent period of life hasten to the "remission of sins?" More caution will be exercised in worldly matters: so that one who is not trusted with earthly substance is trusted with divine! Let them know how to "ask" for salvation, that you may seem (at least) to have given "to him that asketh."

— Tertullian, On Baptism (De Baptismo) 18, trans. S. Thelwall (ANF vol. 3), full text (cf. New Advent).1 Two reasons drive the counsel. The first is the sponsors (godparents), who make the baptismal promises on the child's behalf and are "thrust into danger" — put at spiritual risk — should the child later fall away or should they themselves die before fulfilling what they vowed. The second is Tertullian's estimate of infancy itself: the "innocent period of life" has, on his reckoning, no urgent need to "hasten to the remission of sins," so baptism is better deferred until a person can "know Christ" and "ask" for salvation for himself. He applies the same logic of delay to the unmarried, "in whom the ground of temptation is prepared."

What the objection implies about the practice

For the dating of the practice the passage is read as evidence that it existed. Tertullian is the earliest named opponent of infant baptism — but to argue that it should be delayed, he must be arguing against something that was actually being done: an existing custom of bringing "little children" (with their sponsors) to the font. So the earliest explicit written reference to infant baptism is this argument against it, attesting the practice in North Africa by c. 200. Joachim Jeremias read the protest as aimed at an established, inherited custom; Kurt Aland, as resistance to a spreading practice; see How far back does the earliest evidence trace infant baptism?2

Notes

  • Tertullian's counsel of delay was not followed in the witnesses that come after him: within two generations infant baptism is attested as settled custom and claimed as apostolic (the Apostolic Tradition ascribed to Hippolytus, c. 215; Origen, c. 248) — witnesses later on this timeline.
  • Note the tension he embodies: he is simultaneously a dissenting witness (against early/infant baptism) and the earliest witness that the practice existed. His page therefore bears on any argument about how early infant baptism can be traced.
  • His reason for delay is prudential and pastoral (the risk to sponsors; the "innocent" state of childhood), not a denial that baptism could be given to infants — he nowhere calls the practice invalid, only inadvisable.
  • The delay-counsel is sometimes ascribed to Tertullian's Montanism — the rigorist "New Prophecy" he embraced later in life — and from there pressed into a broader claim that the Montanist movement itself was anti-paedobaptist (a staple of Baptist-successionist genealogies, which enlist the Montanists as early believers'-baptism dissenters). The chronology printed in the Corpus Christianorum edition (after Harnack and Monceaux) places On Baptism after 198 and before Tertullian's Montanist period, whose sympathies surface in works from c. 204–207; Andrew Koperski, drawing on Everett Ferguson, reads the treatise, which is directed against a sect, as giving mainstream reasons for delay (the risk to sponsors, the "innocence" of childhood) rather than a tenet of the New Prophecy.3 The tertullian.org edition notes that the reading in which the work's opening rebukes a Montanist prophetess "Quintilla" rests on a conjecture introduced by the 1550 Gelenius edition, without manuscript support. Tertullian's broader rigorism about post-baptismal sin is a separate question from any denial that infants may be baptized.4 The charge is set out in full, in the frame of the judgements passed on Tertullian, on the page “Was Tertullian’s opposition to infant baptism a Montanist or rigorist position?”
  • How far back the custom he opposes reaches, and whether it goes back to the apostles, are disputed; see How far back does the earliest evidence trace infant baptism? and Do the spread of infant baptism and the patristic delay of baptism show whether the practice was apostolic?
  • Whether he was a heretic. The New-Prophecy question that the first note sets aside for this treatise is the whole subject of the timeline “Was Tertullian a heretic?” — where his rigorism, his never-conciliar “condemnation,” and even the presbyter-of-Carthage title of the head-note (see the argument) are set out.
