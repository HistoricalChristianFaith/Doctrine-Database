Which text did the New Testament quote?

When a New-Testament writer cites the Old Testament, which text-form is he quoting — the Greek Septuagint, the proto-Masoretic Hebrew that later stabilized into the medieval Jewish Bible, an already-revised Greek (the kaige / proto-Theodotion recensions), an older Hebrew now recovered at Qumran, or a free rendering owing to none of these? This is a question about the shape of the text, not about the canon (which books count): a writer can quote a book everyone receives while following one wording of it rather than another. Justin Martyr (c. 155) charged the synagogue's teachers with cutting passages out of the Scriptures; Jerome (c. 400) held that the apostles quoted the Hebrew wherever the Greek diverged; Augustine (c. 425) held that they appealed to the Septuagint as well as to the Hebrew. Modern surveys (Archer & Chirichigno, 1983; Jobes & Silva; Fernández Marcos) report that where the Greek and the Hebrew part ways the New Testament more often follows the Greek, and textual critics such as Emanuel Tov describe the Scriptures of the Second-Temple period as textually plural, with Hebrew manuscripts from Qumran that at points agree with the Septuagint against the later Masoretic Text. The timeline below traces that discussion; the catalog sets out the quotations book by book; and the disputed readings give particular verses, and whole books, whose text-form or source interpreters explain in different ways.

Timeline

Justin Martyr (c. 155)

Debating the Jew Trypho, Justin Martyr treats the Greek of "the seventy elders" as the true text and charges "your teachers" with having physically cut passages out of the Scriptures — the "from the wood" of Psalm 96:10, a Jeremiah descent-oracle — and with misreading the christological proof-texts, above all Isaiah 7:14's "virgin" (παρθένος) against their "young woman."1 He presents the divergences as recent, deliberate removals. A. and D. J. Wasserstein (2006) note that the words "from the wood" are absent from the Hebrew and from the main Septuagint manuscript tradition and appear in a branch of Christian-transmitted copies and in Christian writers, and that several of the other texts Justin names as excised appear in no extant manuscript.2

Origen of Alexandria (c. 240)

In the Hexapla Origen set six columns side by side — the Hebrew, the Hebrew in Greek letters, Aquila, Symmachus, the Septuagint, and Theodotion — marking with obelisk and asterisk every place the Greek had more or less than the Hebrew: the earliest extant systematic collation of the Greek against the Hebrew, recording verse by verse where the two traditions differ. Origen kept the text the churches used rather than re-editing the Bible to the synagogue's Hebrew, and where he charges suppression, in the Letter to Africanus, he names particular passages (the Susanna narratives), withheld, he says, to spare the elders' shame.3

Jerome (c. 400)

Where the Greek Fathers before him had used the Septuagint, Jerome set the Greek aside as his base and translated the Old Testament afresh "from the Hebrew," appealing throughout his Vulgate prefaces to the Hebraica veritas, the "truth of the Hebrew." He ranked the versions Hebrew > Greek > Latin, rejected the story of the seventy translators working in separate cells, and argued that the evangelists and apostles, wherever the Septuagint diverged from the Hebrew, had themselves quoted the Hebrew and not the Greek. Augustine answered him on this point (next entry).4

Augustine of Hippo (c. 425)

Augustine defended the Septuagint as itself apostolically authoritative and inspired. In City of God XVIII.42–43 he argues that the same Spirit who spoke through the Hebrew prophets spoke through the Seventy, so that where the Greek differs from the Hebrew both may stand as prophetic; and he states that "the apostles, in their writings, appeal not only to the Hebrew, but also to the Septuagint." He also gave a pastoral reason: Jerome's new-from-the-Hebrew Psalter would unsettle congregations who had prayed the Greek for centuries. In their exchange Jerome argued for the priority of the Hebrew original and Augustine for the churchly authority of the Greek the apostles quoted.5

Swete and the modern enumerations (c. 1900)

Around 1900 the question was taken up in systematic tabulation. H. B. Swete's Introduction to the Old Testament in Greek (1900) and the Hatch–Redpath Concordance to the Septuagint (1897–1906) tabulated the New Testament's Old-Testament quotations and their agreement with the Greek. Swete noted two cautions: that "what counts as a quotation" is a soft edge (totals range from ~300 to ~400 by how one draws it), and that "the Septuagint" the apostles used was not one fixed book but a family of Greek texts, some already revised (kaige, proto-Theodotion) back toward the Hebrew.6

Qumran and the recovery of a plural text (post-1947)

Among the Qumran biblical manuscripts are Hebrew copies that agree with the Septuagint against the later Masoretic Text — a shorter Hebrew Jeremiah, a fuller Samuel (4QSamuela), a Deuteronomy 32 reading "sons of God" where the Masoretic Text has "sons of Israel" (32:8), and with worship-summons lines the Masoretic Text lacks (32:43). Emanuel Tov and Natalio Fernández Marcos read these manuscripts as evidence of a pluriform first-century text: on their account, places where the New Testament "follows the Greek against the Hebrew" may be cases of the Greek preserving an older Hebrew that a different Jewish text-tradition carried, and divergence between the traditions reflects competing textual families.7

Archer & Chirichigno; Jobes & Silva; Fernández Marcos (1983–)

Gleason Archer & Gregory Chirichigno's Old Testament Quotations in the New Testament: A Complete Survey (1983) prints the Masoretic Hebrew, Septuagint, and Greek New Testament in parallel for each quotation and sorts them into categories A–F; the textbooks of Karen Jobes & Moisés Silva (Invitation to the Septuagint) and Natalio Fernández Marcos (The Septuagint in Context) place that data in the wider study of the Greek Bible. These surveys report that most quotations follow "the LXX in one of its known forms." Most of the frequently cited "340 of 386" Septuagint-following quotations fall in passages where Hebrew and Greek agree; on Archer and Chirichigno's categories, where the two diverge, the New Testament follows the Greek roughly twice as often as the Hebrew.8

The catalog

Below is a reference table of Old-Testament quotations in the New Testament, each tagged for which text-form its wording matches where the Greek and Hebrew diverge. It covers the four Gospels, the Acts of the Apostles, Paul's Epistles to the Romans, 1–2 Corinthians, Galatians, Ephesians, and the Pastorals (1–2 Timothy, Titus), the Epistle to the Hebrews, the General Epistles (James, 1–2 Peter, Jude), and the Revelation of John; Philippians, Colossians, 1–2 Thessalonians and 1–3 John carry no explicit Old-Testament citation. Each row compares the New Testament wording with the Septuagint and the Masoretic Hebrew (and, where a divergence turns on it, with the Qumran evidence), with the standard surveys named in the note as controls. Most rows are tagged agree: Greek and Hebrew coincide, so the citation does not discriminate between them.

The Gospels. The divergences cluster in Matthew's fulfilment (“formula”) quotations and in a handful of citations whose wording is distinctively Greek — or, in Mark, distinctively Aramaic-Targumic. John's two most discussed citations follow the Hebrew against the Greek (Zechariah 12:10's “whom they pierced,” Psalm 41:9's “lifted up his heel”). Acts. The apostolic speeches quote the Septuagint at points where its wording carries the argument: Peter's and Paul's shared resurrection proof from Psalm 16's “see no corruption,” Stephen's “Remphan,” the eunuch's Isaiah 53, Paul's “despisers” of Habakkuk.

Romans, the densest quotation-field in the Pauline corpus: most of its citations are agree rows or catenae whose parts coincide, and its distinctively Septuagintal cases fall where the Greek word carries the argument (Isaiah 28:16's “shall not be ashamed” at Romans 9:33 / 10:11, Psalm 19:4's “their sound” at 10:18, Isaiah 40:13's “mind of the Lord” at 11:34, Isaiah 45:23's “every tongue shall confess” at 14:11). Job 41:11 at Romans 11:35 tracks the Hebrew against a wholly different Greek (MT-distinctive), and three citations match neither received text: “the just shall live by faith” of Habakkuk 2:4 (Romans 1:17), without the possessive both traditions carry; the Deliverer coming “out of Sion” of Isaiah 59:20 (Romans 11:26), an ἐκ Σιων that the critical Septuagint editions (Ziegler, Rahlfs) do not print and no Hebrew witness supports, though Jerome reports the Seventy as reading “from Zion”; and the Targum-shaped “vengeance is mine” of Deuteronomy 32:35 (Romans 12:19). 1–2 Corinthians and Galatians: most rows are agree rows or free conflations; Septuagintal forms appear at Isaiah 40:13's “mind of the Lord” (1 Corinthians 2:16), the universalizing “all” the Septuagint adds to Deuteronomy 27:26's curse (Galatians 3:10), and the causal “therefore” of Psalm 116:10 (2 Corinthians 4:13). 1 Corinthians 3:19 renders Job 5:13 closer to the Hebrew than to the wholly different Greek of Job (MT-distinctive); 1 Corinthians 14:21 quotes Isaiah 28:11–12 in a form owing to neither received text; and the resurrection taunt of 1 Corinthians 15:54–55 couples Isaiah 25:8 and Hosea 13:14 in a shape agreeing with a revised Greek (a Theodotion/kaige-type text) against the Old Greek. The “eye hath not seen” of 1 Corinthians 2:9 matches no extant Old-Testament text; its source is among the disputed readings below. Ephesians and the Pastorals: Ephesians 4:8 reads “he gave gifts unto men” where both the Hebrew and the Greek Psalter read “thou hast received gifts,” a “gave” shared with the Aramaic Targum (a switch Jerome and Theodoret each noticed); Ephesians also follows the Greek at Psalm 4:4's imperative “be angry” (Ephesians 4:26, where the Hebrew reads “tremble”) and the “two shall be one flesh” of Genesis 2:24 (Ephesians 5:31). The Pastorals cite thinly and mostly agree (Deuteronomy 25:4's un-muzzled ox at 1 Timothy 5:18; the Numbers 16:5 “seal” at 2 Timothy 2:19).

Hebrews, the New Testament's most quotation-saturated book, reproduces distinctively Greek wording in its opening catena (Psalm 104:4's angel-“spirits,” Psalm 102's vocative “Lord” addressed to the Son, Psalm 8's made “lower than the angels”) and at later turns of its argument (the Septuagintal new-covenant oracle of Jeremiah 31 with its “I regarded them not” for the Hebrew “I was their husband,” the personalized “coming one” who must not “draw back” of Habakkuk 2:3–4, the “yet once more” of Haggai 2:6 on which 12:27 turns, the “scourgeth every son” of Proverbs 3:12). Its Deuteronomy 32:43 at 1:6 follows a Greek reading that a Hebrew scroll from Qumran also carries (older-Hebrew), and its “vengeance is mine” at 10:30 follows the same Targum-shaped Deuteronomy 32:35 form Paul uses, matching neither received text. The General Epistles — James, 1–2 Peter, and Jude — cite thinly; most rows are agree rows, and the Septuagintal forms fall where the Greek word carries the point — the "God resisteth the proud" of Proverbs 3:34 (James 4:6 / 1 Peter 5:5), the "righteous scarcely saved" of Proverbs 11:31 (1 Peter 4:18), the "shall not be ashamed" of Isaiah 28:16 (1 Peter 2:6), the "royal priesthood" of Exodus 19:6 (1 Peter 2:9), the shortened Isaiah 40 with its "glory of man" (1 Peter 1:24–25). Bede, alone among the Fathers surveyed here, twice names the "ancient Editio" (the Septuagint) as the apostles' source over against his own Hebrew-based Vulgate (on James 4:6 and 1 Peter 4:18). 1 Peter 4:8's "charity shall cover the multitude of sins" follows the Hebrew of Proverbs 10:12 against a wholly different Greek (MT-distinctive), and the stone-of-stumbling of Isaiah 8:14 at 1 Peter 2:8 follows a revised Hebraizing Greek against an Old Greek that negated the image — the catalog's second revised-Greek row, a testimonium 1 Peter shares with Paul (Romans 9:33). Jude carries no Old-Testament-canon quotation to tag: its two citations — the dispute over Moses' body (Jude 9, from the Assumption of Moses) and Enoch's prophecy (Jude 14–15, quoting 1 Enoch 1:9) — are drawn from Second-Temple works outside the Hebrew canon, so they raise a canon question set out on its own page, not a text-form one.

The Revelation of John carries no citation formula and, on the standard counts, no formal quotation — only a dense weave of allusion and echo (Daniel, Ezekiel, Isaiah, Zechariah, the Psalms) — so only its handful of near-quotations are tagged (see the note on quotation versus allusion below the tag key). At Revelation 1:7 the pierced-Messiah allusion to Zechariah 12:10 reads “they… which pierced him,” with the Hebrew against the Old Greek's “they danced” — the same form as John 19:37 — while at Revelation 2:26–27 (repeated at 12:5 and 19:15) the ruler who will “rule” (ποιμανεῖ, shepherd) the nations “with a rod of iron” follows the Septuagint of Psalm 2:9 against the Masoretic “thou shalt break them.” Whether Revelation's wording reflects a Hebraizing Vorlage or the Greek is set out as a disputed reading.9

How to read the tags

  • agree — Hebrew, Greek, and New Testament substantially coincide; the quotation is evidentially inert for the "which text?" question (it fits every answer equally).
  • LXX-distinctive — the New Testament follows the Septuagint's Greek precisely where it diverges from the Masoretic Hebrew; its wording does not match the Hebrew as transmitted.
  • MT-distinctive — the New Testament stands closer to the Hebrew than to the Septuagint, consistent with use of a Hebrew (or Hebraizing) text.
  • older-Hebrew — the New Testament "follows the Septuagint," but the Septuagint here preserves an older Hebrew reading now attested at Qumran against the Masoretic Text — so it is not Greek-over-Hebrew but Hebrew-over-Hebrew. (Archer's A–F scheme does not isolate this category.)
  • revised-Greek — the New Testament agrees not with the "Old Greek" Septuagint but with an early Jewish revision of it (kaige, proto-Theodotion, Aquila-like), which had pulled the Greek back toward the Hebrew.
  • neither / free / targumic — the quotation matches neither the Septuagint nor the Masoretic Text precisely: a paraphrase, a conflation, or a form shared with the Aramaic Targum tradition.
  • ambiguous — the source or the text-form cannot be securely fixed (a loose allusion, an unidentified source, or a rendering compatible with more than one Vorlage).

A note on the boundary — quotation versus allusion. The rows below are quotations: places where a writer reproduces an Old-Testament passage closely enough that its wording can be measured against a text-form. That boundary is deliberately soft — the standard tallies swing from roughly 300 to 400 quotations by how one draws it — and one book sits almost wholly on its far side. The Revelation of John carries no citation formula (“as it is written,” “that it might be fulfilled”) and, on the standard counts, no formal quotation at all — yet it is the most Old-Testament-saturated book in the New, a continuous weave of allusion and echo. Loose allusions are not tagged here, because a paraphrase seldom preserves enough wording to fix a Vorlage; the catalog admits only the handful of Revelation passages that come near enough to a quotation to carry a text-form signal, and leaves the rest as allusions. Of that handful, the two closest to quotation (Rev 1:7 and Rev 2:27) point in opposite directions.

Disputed readings

Particular quotations, and whole books, whose text-form or source interpreters explain in different ways, each set out with its rival readings on its own page.

Quotations closer to the Hebrew

How citations whose wording stands closer to the Hebrew than to the Old Greek have been explained.

  • Matthew’s formula quotations — do they evidence direct use of a Hebrew (non-Septuagintal) text? (Jerome’s “from the Hebrew,” a revised Greek, or a testimonia source)10
  • Zechariah 12:10 at John 19:37 — “whom they pierced”: how did John come by a Hebrew-aligned form?11

Single quotations: text-form and source

Individual citations whose wording matches neither received text, or matches a Greek or Hebrew form other than the Old Greek and the Masoretic Text.

  • Does Hebrews 1:6 (“let all the angels of God worship him”) quote Deuteronomy 32:43 or Psalm 97:7? (the Qumran scroll 4QDeutq and the Septuagint against the Masoretic Text)12
  • Isaiah 25:8 at 1 Corinthians 15:54 — did Paul quote a revised Greek (a Theodotion/kaige-type text) against the Old Greek?13
  • Isaiah 28:11–12 at 1 Corinthians 14:21: “with men of other tongues” — did Paul quote a revised, Hebraizing Greek, or render the Hebrew himself?14
  • 1 Corinthians 2:9 — “eye hath not seen”: a free rendering of Isaiah, or a lost source Paul quoted? (Jerome’s Isaiah 64:4 and Origen’s “Secrets of Elijah”)15
  • Why does Paul quote Habakkuk 2:4 (“the just shall live by faith”) without a possessive? (one verse quoted in three forms in the New Testament)16
  • Where does Paul’s “out of Zion” at Romans 11:26 (Isaiah 59:20) come from? (Septuagint “for the sake of,” Masoretic “to,” Paul “out of”)17
  • Ephesians 4:8 at Psalm 68:18 — “he gave gifts unto men”: a targumic text-form, or Paul’s own reversal? (the Targum’s “gave” against the “received” of Hebrew and Greek)18
