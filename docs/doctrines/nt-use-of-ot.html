Which text did the New Testament quote?

When a New-Testament writer cites the Old Testament, which text-form is he quoting — the Greek Septuagint, the proto-Masoretic Hebrew that later stabilized into the medieval Jewish Bible, an already-revised Greek (the kaige / proto-Theodotion recensions), an older Hebrew now recovered at Qumran, or a free rendering owing to none of these? This is a question about the shape of the text, not about the canon (which books count): a writer can quote a book everyone receives while disagreeing with everyone about how its words run. The honest verdict, stated plainly and not to be laundered upward, is two-part. Where the Greek and the Hebrew part ways the Greek New Testament predominantly follows the Septuagint — the working Bible of the Greek-speaking apostolic churches — and a solid block of quotations reproduces the Greek precisely at the point it diverges from the Hebrew. But the deeper finding is that Second-Temple Judaism was textually plural: there was no single standardized Hebrew or Greek "Bible," the Greek itself circulated in divergent forms and early revisions, and in a number of places the New Testament's "Septuagintal" reading is really an older Hebrew the Greek happened to preserve and the rabbinic line later lost. The Dead Sea Scrolls are what turned that second claim from a guess into a demonstrable fact — which is why the modern picture is not "Greek over Hebrew" but "a plural text, quoted plurally."

Timeline

Justin Martyr (c. 155)

The earliest witness reads the divergence as a moral fact, not a textual one. Debating the Jew Trypho, Justin Martyr treats the Greek of "the seventy elders" as the true text and charges "your teachers" with having physically cut passages out of the Scriptures — the "from the wood" of Psalm 96:10, a Jeremiah descent-oracle — and with misreading the christological proof-texts, above all Isaiah 7:14's "virgin" (παρθένος) against their "young woman."1 It is the seed of the whole later tradition — but framed as deliberate, recent, anti-Christian tampering, a diagnosis the text-critical evidence would not sustain: his flagship "from the wood" runs the other way (a Christian addition to some Greek copies, not a Jewish deletion), and several "excised" texts appear in no manuscript at all.2

Origen of Alexandria (c. 240)

The first scholar to map the divergences instead of moralizing them. In the Hexapla Origen set six columns side by side — the Hebrew, the Hebrew in Greek letters, Aquila, Symmachus, the Septuagint, and Theodotion — marking with obelisk and asterisk every place the Greek had more or less than the Hebrew. It is the first systematic collation of the Greek against the Hebrew, and its very method concedes the point the corruption-polemic denied: the two traditions genuinely differ, verse by verse. Yet Origen's governing instinct was conservative — keep the text the churches actually use rather than re-edit the Bible down to the synagogue's Hebrew — and where he does charge suppression he charges narrow passages (the Susanna scandal-narratives), to the elders' shame, not the wholesale loss of the messianic proof-texts.3

Jerome (c. 400)

The polarity reverses. Where the Greek Fathers trusted the Septuagint and suspected the Hebrew, Jerome abandoned the Greek as his base and translated the Old Testament afresh "from the Hebrew," appealing throughout his Vulgate prefaces to the Hebraica veritas, the "truth of the Hebrew." He ranked the versions Hebrew > Greek > Latin, debunked the legend of the seventy inspired cells, and — most pointedly for this question — argued that the evangelists and apostles, wherever the Septuagint diverged from the Hebrew, had themselves quoted the Hebrew and not the Greek. That is the exact counter-thesis to the modern consensus, and it flags from the start that "which text did the New Testament use?" was contested inside the church, not a settled datum.4

Augustine of Hippo (c. 425)

Against Jerome, Augustine defended the Septuagint as itself apostolically authoritative — even inspired. In City of God XVIII.42–43 he argues that the same Spirit who spoke through the Hebrew prophets spoke through the Seventy, so that where the Greek differs from the Hebrew both may stand as prophetic; and he notes the plain fact that "the apostles, in their writings, appeal not only to the Hebrew, but also to the Septuagint" — indeed usually to the Greek. His worry was pastoral: Jerome's new-from-the-Hebrew Psalter would unsettle congregations who had prayed the Greek for centuries. The Jerome–Augustine exchange fixes the two poles the later debate would swing between — the priority of the Hebrew original versus the churchly authority of the Greek the apostles actually quoted.5

Swete and the first modern enumerations (c. 1900)

The question passes from polemic to statistics. H. B. Swete's Introduction to the Old Testament in Greek (1900) and the Hatch–Redpath Concordance to the Septuagint (1897–1906) gave scholarship its first systematic tabulations of the New Testament's Old-Testament quotations and their agreement with the Greek — the ancestor of every later "how many follow the Septuagint?" count. Swete already framed the two cautions the modern field keeps: that "what counts as a quotation" is a soft edge (totals range from ~300 to ~400 by how one draws it), and that "the Septuagint" the apostles used was not one fixed book but a family of Greek texts, some already revised (kaige, proto-Theodotion) back toward the Hebrew.6

Qumran and the recovery of a plural text (post-1947)

The Dead Sea Scrolls settled the underlying question the Fathers could only guess at. Among the Qumran biblical manuscripts are Hebrew copies that agree with the Septuagint against the later Masoretic Text — a shorter Hebrew Jeremiah, a fuller Samuel (4QSamuela), a Deuteronomy 32 with the "sons of God" line the Masoretic dropped — proving that many places where the New Testament "follows the Greek against the Hebrew" are really cases of the Greek preserving an older Hebrew that a different, equally Jewish text-tradition once carried. The Scrolls thereby dissolve the ancient corruption-charge in both directions: the first-century text was plural, no side had "the" Hebrew, and divergence is a fact about competing textual families, not about malice.7

Archer & Chirichigno; Jobes & Silva; Fernández Marcos (1983–)

Modern synthesis sets the columns side by side and does the arithmetic honestly. Gleason Archer & Gregory Chirichigno's Old Testament Quotations in the New Testament: A Complete Survey (1983) prints the Masoretic Hebrew, Septuagint, and Greek New Testament in parallel for each quotation and sorts them into categories A–F; the standard textbooks of Karen Jobes & Moisés Silva (Invitation to the Septuagint) and Natalio Fernández Marcos (The Septuagint in Context) place that data in the wider picture. Their agreed result: most quotations follow "the LXX in one of its known forms," but the much-cited headline "340 of 386 from the Septuagint" is rhetorically inflated — most of that 340 is passages where Hebrew and Greek simply agree and so prove no preference. The probative residue is smaller and points a real but modest lean: where the two diverge, the New Testament follows the Greek roughly twice as often as the Hebrew.8

The catalog

Below is a working reference table of Old-Testament quotations in the New Testament, each tagged for which text-form it reflects where the Greek and Hebrew diverge. The four Gospels, the Acts of the Apostles, Paul's Epistles to the Romans, 1–2 Corinthians, Galatians, Ephesians, and the Pastorals (1–2 Timothy, Titus), the Epistle to the Hebrews, and the General Epistles (James, 1–2 Peter, Jude) are now worked row by row — every one of their explicit quotations adjudicated against the Septuagint and the Masoretic Hebrew (and, where a divergence is load-bearing, the Qumran evidence), with the standard surveys re-checked rather than copied; the honest result is that most rows come out agree (Greek and Hebrew coincide, so the citation decides nothing), and the real divergences cluster in Matthew's fulfilment (“formula”) quotations, in a handful of citations that turn on a distinctively Greek — or, in Mark, distinctively Aramaic-Targumic — reading, and above all in Acts, whose apostolic speeches lean on the Septuagint at their argumentative hinges (Peter's and Paul's shared resurrection proof from Psalm 16's “see no corruption,” Stephen's “Remphan,” the eunuch's Isaiah 53, Paul's “despisers” of Habakkuk) — and, running the other way, in John, whose two sharpest citations follow the Hebrew against the Greek (Zechariah 12:10's “whom they pierced,” Psalm 41:9's “lifted up his heel”), the Gospels' clearest MT-distinctive counterweight to that Septuagintal lean. Romans — the densest quotation-field in the Pauline corpus — confirms the same divided picture: most of its citations are agree rows or catenae whose parts coincide, its distinctively-Septuagintal cases cluster where the Greek word carries the argument (Isaiah 28:16's “shall not be ashamed” at Romans 9:33 / 10:11, Psalm 19:4's “their sound” at 10:18, Isaiah 40:13's “mind of the Lord” at 11:34, Isaiah 45:23's “every tongue shall confess” at 14:11), and — the honest counterweight — a real residue runs the other way: Job 41:11 at Romans 11:35 tracks the Hebrew against a wholly different Greek (MT-distinctive), while three of Paul's sharpest citations match neither received text — the famous “the just shall live by faith” of Habakkuk 2:4 (Romans 1:17), where Paul drops the possessive both traditions carry; the Deliverer coming “out of Sion” of Isaiah 59:20 (Romans 11:26), an ἐκ Σιων found in no Greek or Hebrew copy; and the Targum-shaped “vengeance is mine” of Deuteronomy 32:35 (Romans 12:19). 1–2 Corinthians and Galatians extend the same divided picture into Paul's most sharply-argued letters: again most rows are agree rows or free conflations, Paul's Septuagintal lean surfaces where the Greek word carries the argument (Isaiah 40:13's “mind of the Lord” at 1 Corinthians 2:16, the universalizing “all” the Septuagint adds to Deuteronomy 27:26's curse at Galatians 3:10, the causal “therefore” of Psalm 116:10 at 2 Corinthians 4:13), and the honest counterweights are real: 1 Corinthians 3:19 renders Job 5:13 closer to the Hebrew than to the wholly different Greek of Job (MT-distinctive), 1 Corinthians 14:21 quotes Isaiah 28:11–12 in a form owing to neither received text, and above all the resurrection taunt of 1 Corinthians 15:54–55 couples Isaiah 25:8 and Hosea 13:14 in a shape agreeing with a revised Greek (a Theodotion/kaige-type text) against the Old Greek — the catalog's clearest instance of the Greek itself circulating in more than one form. The disputed “eye hath not seen” of 1 Corinthians 2:9, matching no extant Old-Testament text, is flagged where it stands. Ephesians and the Pastorals close out Paul: the shorter captivity letters (Philippians, Colossians) and 1–2 Thessalonians carry no explicit Old-Testament citations, but Ephesians supplies one of the corpus's most-discussed text-forms — the “he gave gifts unto men” of Ephesians 4:8, which reverses the “received gifts” of both the Hebrew and the Greek Psalter to a “gave” shared with the Aramaic Targum (a switch Jerome and Theodoret each noticed) — alongside a modest Septuagintal lean (Psalm 4:4's imperative “be angry” at Ephesians 4:26, where the Hebrew reads “tremble”; the “two shall be one flesh” of Genesis 2:24 at Ephesians 5:31), while the Pastorals cite thinly and mostly agree (Deuteronomy 25:4's un-muzzled ox at 1 Timothy 5:18; the Numbers 16:5 “seal” at 2 Timothy 2:19). Hebrews — the New Testament's most quotation-saturated book — is the corpus's strongest single case for an apostolic theology done from a Greek Bible: its opening catena (Psalm 104:4's mutable angel-“spirits,” Psalm 102's vocative “Lord” addressed to the Son, Psalm 8's made “lower than the angels”) and its later argument-hinges (the Septuagintal new-covenant oracle of Jeremiah 31 with its “I regarded them not” for the Hebrew “I was their husband,” the personalized “coming one” who must not “draw back” of Habakkuk 2:3–4, the “yet once more” of Haggai 2:6 the argument of 12:27 hangs on, the “scourgeth every son” of Proverbs 3:12) all lean on distinctively Greek wording — the book-level thesis weighed as its own argument below. The honest counterweights are real too: Hebrews carries the catalog's one older-Hebrew row (Deuteronomy 32:43's angels at 1:6, the Greek preserving a reading now attested at Qumran, not inventing one), and its “vengeance is mine” at 10:30 follows the same Targum-shaped Deuteronomy 32:35 form Paul uses, matching neither received text. The General Epistles — James, 1–2 Peter, and Jude — cite thinly and extend the same divided picture into the catalog's last worked field: most rows are agree rows, and the Septuagintal lean surfaces where the Greek word carries the point — the "God resisteth the proud" of Proverbs 3:34 (James 4:6 / 1 Peter 5:5), the "righteous scarcely saved" of Proverbs 11:31 (1 Peter 4:18), the "shall not be ashamed" of Isaiah 28:16 (1 Peter 2:6), the "royal priesthood" of Exodus 19:6 (1 Peter 2:9), the shortened Isaiah 40 with its "glory of man" (1 Peter 1:24–25) — and Bede, uniquely among the Fathers surveyed here, twice names the "ancient Editio" (the Septuagint) as the apostles' source over against his own Hebrew Vulgate (on James 4:6 and 1 Peter 4:18). The honest counterweights run both ways: 1 Peter 4:8's "charity shall cover the multitude of sins" follows the Hebrew of Proverbs 10:12 against a wholly different Greek (MT-distinctive), and the stone-of-stumbling of Isaiah 8:14 at 1 Peter 2:8 follows a revised Hebraizing Greek against an Old Greek that negated the image — the catalog's second revised-Greek row, a fixed testimonium 1 Peter shares with Paul (Romans 9:33). Jude carries no Old-Testament-canon quotation to tag: its two famous citations — the dispute over Moses' body (Jude 9, from the Assumption of Moses) and Enoch's prophecy (Jude 14–15, quoting 1 Enoch 1:9) — are drawn from Second-Temple works outside the Hebrew canon, so they raise a canon question worked on its own page, not a text-form one. With Paul's corpus, Hebrews, and now the General Epistles complete (1–3 John carry no explicit Old-Testament citation), the one remaining field — the Revelation of John — remains seeded, not yet adjudicated — drawn from the standard baselines (Archer & Chirichigno's category survey, the Kalvesmaki quotation table, and the loci citati apparatus of the Nestle–Aland / UBS Greek New Testament) and provisional until each is weighed on its own evidence. Treat an un-worked tag as a first sorting, not a verdict.9

How to read the tags

  • agree — Hebrew, Greek, and New Testament substantially coincide; the quotation is evidentially inert for the "which text?" question (it fits every answer equally).
  • LXX-distinctive — the New Testament follows the Septuagint's Greek precisely where it diverges from the Masoretic Hebrew; the citation cannot have come from the Hebrew as we have it.
  • MT-distinctive — the New Testament stands closer to the Hebrew than to the Septuagint, implying direct use of a Hebrew (or Hebraizing) text.
  • older-Hebrew — the New Testament "follows the Septuagint," but the Septuagint here preserves an older Hebrew reading now attested at Qumran against the Masoretic Text — so it is not Greek-over-Hebrew but Hebrew-over-Hebrew. (The category Archer's flat A–F scheme does not isolate.)
  • revised-Greek — the New Testament agrees not with the "Old Greek" Septuagint but with an early Jewish revision of it (kaige, proto-Theodotion, Aquila-like), which had pulled the Greek back toward the Hebrew.
  • neither / free / targumic — the quotation matches neither the Septuagint nor the Masoretic Text precisely: a paraphrase, a conflation, or a form shared with the Aramaic Targum tradition.
  • ambiguous — the source or the text-form cannot be securely fixed (a loose allusion, an unidentified source, or a rendering compatible with more than one Vorlage).

Arguments

The load-bearing sub-claims — how the raw counts should be weighed, and how to tell a genuine "Greek over Hebrew" case from an "older Hebrew the Greek preserved" — are argued off this timeline. The per-row adjudications that populate the catalog above are the argument pages this section grows.

Related arguments — the Septuagint's interpretive gloss (over a stable Hebrew)

Three of the catalog's rows are the same species of divergence and are best read together: the New Testament follows the Septuagint's Greek at a point where the Hebrew is stable — the same consonants stand in the Masoretic Text and, where attested, at Qumran — so the Greek reads an identical Hebrew its own way, not preserving an older Hebrew the Masoretes later lost (the fork worked at Goliath's height and Hebrews 1:6). The mechanism varies — a lexical choice (Isaiah's "virgin"), an interpretive rendering or inner-Greek variant (Hebrews' "body"), a revocalization plus a dālet/rêš confusion (Amos' "mankind … seek" for "possess … Edom") — but all three sit on the same side of the fork. Each row is adjudicated on its own page.

Isaiah 7:14 at Matthew 1:23 — is the "virgin" a Septuagintal gloss, and does Matthew depend on it? (assessment: sound)

The catalog's first row, adjudicated. Matthew's virgin-birth proof-text reproduces the Septuagint's παρθένος ("virgin") verbatim and turns on it, where the standing Hebrew ʿalmah ("young woman") and the three later Jewish revisers (νεᾶνις) do not by themselves denote virginity — so the "LXX-distinctive" tag holds. Two refinements keep it honest: the divergence is a gloss over an identical Hebrew (the consonantal ʿalmah stands unchanged in the Masoretic Text and in the c. 125 BC Great Isaiah Scroll, 1QIsaiaha), so unlike Hebrews 1:6 or Goliath's height the Greek preserves no older Hebrew here — the whole divergence is translational; and the gloss is a defensible pre-Christian Jewish reading (virginity connoted in the word's ordinary use, chosen c. 150 BC), not a Christian fabrication.10

Psalm 40:6 at Hebrews 10:5 — a Septuagintal "body," and does the argument depend on it? (assessment: sound)

The catalog's Hebrews-10:5 row, adjudicated. Hebrews quotes Psalm 40:6 with the Septuagint's σῶμα ("a body hast thou prepared me") where the Hebrew reads "ears thou hast dug for me," and the author reuses the Greek word at 10:10 ("the offering of the body of Jesus Christ") — so the "LXX-distinctive" tag holds. Two refinements: the Hebrew "ears" is textually stable (no variant Vorlage to recover), so like Isaiah 7:14 this is a Greek gloss over an identical Hebrew, not an older-Hebrew case; and the argument's spine is the antithesis "sacrifice thou wouldest not … I come to do thy will," present in both text-forms — so σῶμα is exploited (a bridge to "the offering of the body") rather than the foundation the argument would collapse without. Whether the Greek reached "body" by interpretive synecdoche (the fashioned obedient person for the fashioned ear) or by an early inner-Greek variant from ὠτία, the divergence is Greek-internal, not Hebrew-based.11

Amos 9:11–12 at Acts 15:16–18 — a Septuagintal "seek the Lord," and does the Council's decision depend on it? (assessment: sound)

The Jerusalem Council's proof-text, adjudicated. James confirms the Gentile mission from Amos in the Septuagint's form — "that the residue of men might seek after the Lord" — where the Hebrew reads "that they may possess the remnant of Edom," so the "LXX-distinctive" tag holds. Two refinements: the Hebrew is stable (the Wadi Murabba'at scroll reads "Edom" with the Masoretic Text; no Hebrew witness attests the Greek), so like Isaiah 7:14 the divergence is Greek-side — a revocalization (ʼĕdôm "Edom" → ʼādām "mankind") plus a dālet/rêš confusion ("possess" → "seek") — not an older-Hebrew case; and the Council's decision does not strictly hang on the Greek, because the load-bearing clause "all the nations upon whom my name is called" (v. 12b) stands in both text-forms, so the Greek sharpens James's argument (nations as seekers, not conquered vassals) rather than being its foundation.12

Deuteronomy 32:43 at Hebrews 1:6 — “let all God’s angels worship him,” the Septuagint preserving an older Hebrew? (assessment: sound)

The catalog’s one older-Hebrew row, adjudicated — the honest counterweight to the gloss group above. When Hebrews 1:6 proves the Son greater than the angels from “let all the angels of God worship him,” it quotes a clause the Masoretic Deuteronomy 32:43 does not contain but the Septuagint does — and which a Hebrew scroll from Qumran (4QDeutq, c. 100 BC) also carries (“bow down to him, all gods”). So unlike Isaiah 7:14, Psalm 40:6, and Amos 9:12 this is not the Greek reading a stable Hebrew but the Greek preserving an older Hebrew the Masoretes lost (Hebrew-over-Hebrew, the same phenomenon made christologically-neutral at Goliath’s height) — the finding that turns the ancient corruption-charge on its head. Two refinements keep it honest: the word narrows down the chain (Qumran “gods” → Greek “sons/angels of God” → Hebrews “angels of God”), so Qumran vindicates the line, not the exact christological word; and 4QDeutq is a distinct textual form and the extant Greek is itself conflate, so the Septuagint preserves an older Hebrew, not demonstrably the one behind the Greek.13

Matthew’s formula quotations — do they evidence direct use of a Hebrew (non-Septuagintal) text? (assessment: sound)

The catalog’s Matthew rows, adjudicated together — the doctrine’s diversity counterweight. Matthew’s distinctive “formula quotations” (the fulfilment citations “that it might be fulfilled which was spoken by the prophet”) are genuinely non-Septuagintal where the Greek and Hebrew part ways, and at Matthew 2:15 the citation follows the Hebrew of Hosea 11:1 (“my son,” singular) against the Septuagint’s “his children” — so the “MT-distinctive” tag holds and Matthew is the standing counter-case to any “the New Testament = the Septuagint” picture. This is the mirror image of the gloss group: not the Greek reading a stable Hebrew, but the New Testament standing closer to the Hebrew than to the Greek. Three refinements keep it honest: the proven claim is the text-form (non-Septuagintal, Hebrew-aligned), not the mechanism — a fresh Hebrew translation, a Hebraizing/revised Greek (Menken), or a testimonia source all fit, and Matthew’s agreement with the Hebrew is also agreement with Aquila/Symmachus/Theodotion, so the strong “the apostle translated the Hebrew” is possible, not demonstrated; the phenomenon is confined to the fulfilment citations (Matthew’s other quotations are largely Septuagintal), so Jerome’s universal “wherever the Evangelist quotes… he follows the Hebrew” is an overstatement, flagged on his detail page; and the three verses usually cited together fall into three classes — Hosea 11:1 MT-distinctive, Jeremiah 31:15 at Matthew 2:18 neither/free, the “Nazarene” of Matthew 2:23 a Hebrew-only word-play — so the bundle evidences Hebrew attention, not one uniform text-form.14

Does the Epistle to the Hebrews argue from a Greek Old Testament? — the book-level synthesis (assessment: sound)

The doctrine’s one book-level argument, gathering the per-row adjudications into a single question. Hebrews is the most quotation-saturated book in the New Testament, and it is the strongest single-book case that the apostolic church did its theology from a Greek Bible: its citations reproduce a Greek text-form throughout, show no independent recourse to the Hebrew, and a whole chain of its arguments turns on distinctively Greek wording — the Son made “a little lower than the angels” (Psalm 8:5 LXX for Hebrew “than God”), “the coming one” who will not “draw back” (Habakkuk 2:3–4), the vocative “Lord” addressed to the Son (Psalm 102:25), and the “body” of Psalm 40:6. Three refinements keep it honest: the dependence is graded (sometimes the Greek word is the proof, as at Psalm 8 and Habakkuk 2; sometimes only a bridge the argument survives without, as at Psalm 40:6); “Septuagint-shaped” is not “Greek-invented,” since at Hebrews 1:6 the Greek preserves an older Hebrew now attested at Qumran; and “the Septuagint” was a plural family of Greek texts, so the verdict is reached citation by citation, not by a blanket “LXX-only.”15

Their yield is a handful of genuine cruxes among many agree rows. At 1 Corinthians 3:19 Paul renders Job 5:13 ("he taketh the wise in their own craftiness") closer to the Hebrew than to the wholly different extant Greek of Job — a Pauline MT-distinctive twin to Job 41:11 at Romans 11:35; at 1 Corinthians 14:21 the "other tongues" citation of Isaiah 28:11–12 follows neither received text, standing closer to the Hebrew than to the divergent Old Greek; and the resurrection taunt of 1 Corinthians 15:54–55 couples Isaiah 25:8's "swallowed up in victory" — a passive form agreeing with the Theodotion/kaige-type revised Greek against the Old Greek's "death, having prevailed, swallowed [them]" — with a Hosea 13:14 reshaped so that "victory" and "death" replace the Septuagint's "penalty" and "Hades," the catalog's showcase revised-Greek row and its clearest evidence that the Greek itself was textually plural. Galatians adds the theologically load-bearing pair on which Paul's "curse of the law" turns: the universalizing "all" the Septuagint (with the Samaritan Pentateuch) reads into Deuteronomy 27:26 at Galatians 3:10, and the pointed dropping of the "of God" both text-forms carry from Deuteronomy 21:23 at Galatians 3:13. These load-bearing cruxes — the revised-Greek resurrection citation, the Isaiah-28 tongues text, the curse-of-the-law pair, and the source of the "eye hath not seen" of 1 Corinthians 2:9 (matching no extant Old-Testament text) — are being expanded into their own adjudications. The General Epistles — James, 1–2 Peter, and Jude — are worked on the same basis, taking D. A. Carson's James, 1–2 Peter, and Jude sections in the same Beale–Carson volume and Karen H. Jobes, 1 Peter (Grand Rapids: Baker Academic, 2005), as the scholarly controls, and re-checking rather than transcribing the Archer–Chirichigno calls. They cite thinly, and most rows are agree rows (the royal-law Leviticus 19:18 at James 2:8, the "be ye holy" of Leviticus 11:44 at 1 Peter 1:16, the rejected-stone Psalm 118:22 at 1 Peter 2:7, the Psalm 34 of 1 Peter 3:10–12). Their Septuagintal lean surfaces where the Greek word does the work: the "God resisteth the proud" of Proverbs 3:34 (James 4:6 / 1 Peter 5:5), the "righteous scarcely saved" of Proverbs 11:31 (1 Peter 4:18), the "shall not be ashamed" of Isaiah 28:16 (1 Peter 2:6, with Romans 9:33 / 10:11), the "royal priesthood" of Exodus 19:6 (1 Peter 2:9), the shortened Isaiah 40:6–8 with its "glory of man" (1 Peter 1:24–25), the passive "it was reckoned" of Genesis 15:6 (James 2:23), and the LXX Decalogue order — adultery before murder — at James 2:11. Jerome's heir Bede, uniquely among the Fathers surveyed here, twice names the "ancient Editio" (the Septuagint) as the apostles' source over against the Hebrew of his own Vulgate — on James 4:6 ("this statement regarding the proud and humble was placed by the blessed James from the proverbs of Solomon according to the ancient Translation, just as Peter did in his Epistle. In our Edition, which descends from Hebrew truth, it is said thus: He will mock the mockers, and He will give grace to the meek") and again on 1 Peter 4:18 — the general epistles' clearest patristic flags. The honest counterweights are real and two-directional: 1 Peter 4:8's "charity shall cover the multitude of sins" follows the Hebrew of Proverbs 10:12 against a wholly different Greek ("friendship covereth all who are not contentious") — MT-distinctive, a proverbial form shared with James 5:20; and the stone-of-stumbling of Isaiah 8:14 at 1 Peter 2:8 follows a revised Hebraizing Greek (Aquila/Theodotion-type) against the Old Greek, which negated the image ("not as a stumbling-stone shall ye meet him") — the catalog's second revised-Greek row after 1 Corinthians 15:54–55, and a fixed testimonium 1 Peter shares with Paul at Romans 9:33. James's "the spirit… lusteth to envy" (James 4:5), cited as scripture, matches no identifiable Old-Testament text and is flagged ambiguous. Jude carries no Old-Testament-canon quotation to tag: its two famous citations — the dispute over Moses' body (Jude 9, from the Assumption of Moses) and Enoch's prophecy (Jude 14–15, quoting 1 Enoch 1:9) — are drawn from Second-Temple works outside the Hebrew canon, so they raise a canon question worked on its own page, not a text-form one. ↩
