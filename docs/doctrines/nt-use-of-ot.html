Which text did the New Testament quote?

When a New-Testament writer cites the Old Testament, which text-form is he quoting — the Greek Septuagint, the proto-Masoretic Hebrew that later stabilized into the medieval Jewish Bible, an already-revised Greek (the kaige / proto-Theodotion recensions), an older Hebrew now recovered at Qumran, or a free rendering owing to none of these? This is a question about the shape of the text, not about the canon (which books count): a writer can quote a book everyone receives while disagreeing with everyone about how its words run. The honest verdict, stated plainly and not to be laundered upward, is two-part. Where the Greek and the Hebrew part ways the Greek New Testament predominantly follows the Septuagint — the working Bible of the Greek-speaking apostolic churches — and a solid block of quotations reproduces the Greek precisely at the point it diverges from the Hebrew. But the deeper finding is that Second-Temple Judaism was textually plural: there was no single standardized Hebrew or Greek "Bible," the Greek itself circulated in divergent forms and early revisions, and in a number of places the New Testament's "Septuagintal" reading is really an older Hebrew the Greek happened to preserve and the rabbinic line later lost. The Dead Sea Scrolls are what turned that second claim from a guess into a demonstrable fact — which is why the modern picture is not "Greek over Hebrew" but "a plural text, quoted plurally."

Timeline

Justin Martyr (c. 155)

The earliest witness reads the divergence as a moral fact, not a textual one. Debating the Jew Trypho, Justin Martyr treats the Greek of "the seventy elders" as the true text and charges "your teachers" with having physically cut passages out of the Scriptures — the "from the wood" of Psalm 96:10, a Jeremiah descent-oracle — and with misreading the christological proof-texts, above all Isaiah 7:14's "virgin" (παρθένος) against their "young woman."1 It is the seed of the whole later tradition — but framed as deliberate, recent, anti-Christian tampering, a diagnosis the text-critical evidence would not sustain: his flagship "from the wood" runs the other way (a Christian addition to some Greek copies, not a Jewish deletion), and several "excised" texts appear in no manuscript at all.2

Origen of Alexandria (c. 240)

The first scholar to map the divergences instead of moralizing them. In the Hexapla Origen set six columns side by side — the Hebrew, the Hebrew in Greek letters, Aquila, Symmachus, the Septuagint, and Theodotion — marking with obelisk and asterisk every place the Greek had more or less than the Hebrew. It is the first systematic collation of the Greek against the Hebrew, and its very method concedes the point the corruption-polemic denied: the two traditions genuinely differ, verse by verse. Yet Origen's governing instinct was conservative — keep the text the churches actually use rather than re-edit the Bible down to the synagogue's Hebrew — and where he does charge suppression he charges narrow passages (the Susanna scandal-narratives), to the elders' shame, not the wholesale loss of the messianic proof-texts.3

Jerome (c. 400)

The polarity reverses. Where the Greek Fathers trusted the Septuagint and suspected the Hebrew, Jerome abandoned the Greek as his base and translated the Old Testament afresh "from the Hebrew," appealing throughout his Vulgate prefaces to the Hebraica veritas, the "truth of the Hebrew." He ranked the versions Hebrew > Greek > Latin, debunked the legend of the seventy inspired cells, and — most pointedly for this question — argued that the evangelists and apostles, wherever the Septuagint diverged from the Hebrew, had themselves quoted the Hebrew and not the Greek. That is the exact counter-thesis to the modern consensus, and it flags from the start that "which text did the New Testament use?" was contested inside the church, not a settled datum.4

Augustine of Hippo (c. 425)

Against Jerome, Augustine defended the Septuagint as itself apostolically authoritative — even inspired. In City of God XVIII.42–43 he argues that the same Spirit who spoke through the Hebrew prophets spoke through the Seventy, so that where the Greek differs from the Hebrew both may stand as prophetic; and he notes the plain fact that "the apostles, in their writings, appeal not only to the Hebrew, but also to the Septuagint" — indeed usually to the Greek. His worry was pastoral: Jerome's new-from-the-Hebrew Psalter would unsettle congregations who had prayed the Greek for centuries. The Jerome–Augustine exchange fixes the two poles the later debate would swing between — the priority of the Hebrew original versus the churchly authority of the Greek the apostles actually quoted.5

Swete and the first modern enumerations (c. 1900)

The question passes from polemic to statistics. H. B. Swete's Introduction to the Old Testament in Greek (1900) and the Hatch–Redpath Concordance to the Septuagint (1897–1906) gave scholarship its first systematic tabulations of the New Testament's Old-Testament quotations and their agreement with the Greek — the ancestor of every later "how many follow the Septuagint?" count. Swete already framed the two cautions the modern field keeps: that "what counts as a quotation" is a soft edge (totals range from ~300 to ~400 by how one draws it), and that "the Septuagint" the apostles used was not one fixed book but a family of Greek texts, some already revised (kaige, proto-Theodotion) back toward the Hebrew.6

Qumran and the recovery of a plural text (post-1947)

The Dead Sea Scrolls settled the underlying question the Fathers could only guess at. Among the Qumran biblical manuscripts are Hebrew copies that agree with the Septuagint against the later Masoretic Text — a shorter Hebrew Jeremiah, a fuller Samuel (4QSamuela), a Deuteronomy 32 with the "sons of God" line the Masoretic dropped — proving that many places where the New Testament "follows the Greek against the Hebrew" are really cases of the Greek preserving an older Hebrew that a different, equally Jewish text-tradition once carried. The Scrolls thereby dissolve the ancient corruption-charge in both directions: the first-century text was plural, no side had "the" Hebrew, and divergence is a fact about competing textual families, not about malice.7

Archer & Chirichigno; Jobes & Silva; Fernández Marcos (1983–)

Modern synthesis sets the columns side by side and does the arithmetic honestly. Gleason Archer & Gregory Chirichigno's Old Testament Quotations in the New Testament: A Complete Survey (1983) prints the Masoretic Hebrew, Septuagint, and Greek New Testament in parallel for each quotation and sorts them into categories A–F; the standard textbooks of Karen Jobes & Moisés Silva (Invitation to the Septuagint) and Natalio Fernández Marcos (The Septuagint in Context) place that data in the wider picture. Their agreed result: most quotations follow "the LXX in one of its known forms," but the much-cited headline "340 of 386 from the Septuagint" is rhetorically inflated — most of that 340 is passages where Hebrew and Greek simply agree and so prove no preference. The probative residue is smaller and points a real but modest lean: where the two diverge, the New Testament follows the Greek roughly twice as often as the Hebrew.8

The catalog

Below is a working reference table of representative Old-Testament quotations in the New Testament, each tagged for which text-form it reflects where the Greek and Hebrew diverge. It is seeded, not adjudicated: the classifications are drawn from the standard baselines (Archer & Chirichigno's category survey, the Kalvesmaki quotation table, and the loci citati apparatus of the Nestle–Aland / UBS Greek New Testament) and are marked provisional until each row is weighed on its own evidence in a dedicated argument. Treat the tags as a first sorting, not a verdict.9

How to read the tags

  • agree — Hebrew, Greek, and New Testament substantially coincide; the quotation is evidentially inert for the "which text?" question (it fits every answer equally).
  • LXX-distinctive — the New Testament follows the Septuagint's Greek precisely where it diverges from the Masoretic Hebrew; the citation cannot have come from the Hebrew as we have it.
  • MT-distinctive — the New Testament stands closer to the Hebrew than to the Septuagint, implying direct use of a Hebrew (or Hebraizing) text.
  • older-Hebrew — the New Testament "follows the Septuagint," but the Septuagint here preserves an older Hebrew reading now attested at Qumran against the Masoretic Text — so it is not Greek-over-Hebrew but Hebrew-over-Hebrew. (The category Archer's flat A–F scheme does not isolate.)
  • revised-Greek — the New Testament agrees not with the "Old Greek" Septuagint but with an early Jewish revision of it (kaige, proto-Theodotion, Aquila-like), which had pulled the Greek back toward the Hebrew.
  • neither / free / targumic — the quotation matches neither the Septuagint nor the Masoretic Text precisely: a paraphrase, a conflation, or a form shared with the Aramaic Targum tradition.
  • ambiguous — the source or the text-form cannot be securely fixed (a loose allusion, an unidentified source, or a rendering compatible with more than one Vorlage).

Arguments

The load-bearing sub-claims — how the raw counts should be weighed, and how to tell a genuine "Greek over Hebrew" case from an "older Hebrew the Greek preserved" — are argued off this timeline. The per-row adjudications that populate the catalog above are the argument pages this section grows.

Related arguments — the Septuagint's interpretive gloss (over a stable Hebrew)

Two of the catalog's rows are the same species of divergence and are best read together: the New Testament follows the Septuagint's Greek at a point where the Hebrew is stable — the same consonants stand in the Masoretic Text and, where attested, at Qumran — so the Greek is glossing an identical Hebrew, not preserving an older Hebrew the Masoretes later lost (the fork worked at Goliath's height and Hebrews 1:6). Each row is adjudicated on its own page.

Isaiah 7:14 at Matthew 1:23 — is the "virgin" a Septuagintal gloss, and does Matthew depend on it? (assessment: sound)

The catalog's first row, adjudicated. Matthew's virgin-birth proof-text reproduces the Septuagint's παρθένος ("virgin") verbatim and turns on it, where the standing Hebrew ʿalmah ("young woman") and the three later Jewish revisers (νεᾶνις) do not by themselves denote virginity — so the "LXX-distinctive" tag holds. Two refinements keep it honest: the divergence is a gloss over an identical Hebrew (the consonantal ʿalmah stands unchanged in the Masoretic Text and in the c. 125 BC Great Isaiah Scroll, 1QIsaiaha), so unlike Hebrews 1:6 or Goliath's height the Greek preserves no older Hebrew here — the whole divergence is translational; and the gloss is a defensible pre-Christian Jewish reading (virginity connoted in the word's ordinary use, chosen c. 150 BC), not a Christian fabrication.10

Psalm 40:6 at Hebrews 10:5 — a Septuagintal "body," and does the argument depend on it? (assessment: sound)

The catalog's Hebrews-10:5 row, adjudicated. Hebrews quotes Psalm 40:6 with the Septuagint's σῶμα ("a body hast thou prepared me") where the Hebrew reads "ears thou hast dug for me," and the author reuses the Greek word at 10:10 ("the offering of the body of Jesus Christ") — so the "LXX-distinctive" tag holds. Two refinements: the Hebrew "ears" is textually stable (no variant Vorlage to recover), so like Isaiah 7:14 this is a Greek gloss over an identical Hebrew, not an older-Hebrew case; and the argument's spine is the antithesis "sacrifice thou wouldest not … I come to do thy will," present in both text-forms — so σῶμα is exploited (a bridge to "the offering of the body") rather than the foundation the argument would collapse without. Whether the Greek reached "body" by interpretive synecdoche (the fashioned obedient person for the fashioned ear) or by an early inner-Greek variant from ὠτία, the divergence is Greek-internal, not Hebrew-based.11
