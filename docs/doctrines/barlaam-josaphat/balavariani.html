The Georgian Balavariani: the tale made Christian

Dates: Old Georgian, c. 9th–10th c. (its earliest form disputed) · Lang, The Balavariani (1966)
Summary: In the Georgian Balavariani the Indian wisdom-romance is an explicitly Christian saint’s life: the prince Iodasaph is converted to Christianity by the ascetic Balahvar. David Marshall Lang treats it as the point at which the tale became Christian; Korneli Kekelidze posited an earlier, lost Syriac Christian version.

Claims & evidence

The tale as a Christian saint’s life

Where the Arabic Bilawhar wa-Būdhāsaf is a wisdom-tale of a prince and his teacher, the Georgian Balavariani makes the teacher a Christian monk and the prince a convert to the Gospel. The prince Iodasaph — sheltered by his father King Abenes from all sight of suffering, awakened to mortality, and drawn to the ascetic life as the Buddha had been — is now instructed and baptized by Balahvar, and dies a Christian hermit. The plot of the Buddha-legend remains; its teaching is Christian.

— David Marshall Lang, The Balavariani (Barlaam and Josaphat): A Tale from the Christian East (London/Berkeley, 1966), with introduction by Ilia V. Abuladze; and Lang, The Wisdom of Balahvar (1957).

The manuscript and the two recensions

Lang translated the long recension, edited by Ilia Abuladze from a unique full-length manuscript in the library of the Greek Patriarchate at Jerusalem (Jer. 140), arguing that it, rather than the shorter and better-known Wisdom of Balahvar, preserves the original Georgian text; the shorter form he took to be a later abridgement. On Lang’s account Euthymius the Athonite, a Georgian of Mount Athos, translated this Georgian text into Greek (the Greek’s authorship is disputed).

Reception and debate

  • The dating. Lang, Korneli Kekelidze, and Ilia Abuladze — the three scholars who edited and studied the Georgian texts — independently placed the full recension in the ninth or tenth century, citing among other things its New Persian loanwords and its dependence on the eighth-century Arabic book. The Georgian scholar Shalva Nutsubidze (1956) argued instead for a seventh-century original, identifying its author with the Byzantine writer John Moschus (whose surname he read as “the Georgian”); Simon Qaukhchishvili adopted the view with modifications, and Paul Devos answered it in 1957 (see when the Balavariani was composed). Its editors place the Georgian stage after the Arabic and before the Greek; this timeline places it at the ninth to tenth century, the dating its three editors give.
