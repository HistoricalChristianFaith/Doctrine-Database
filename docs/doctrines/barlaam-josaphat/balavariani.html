The Georgian Balavariani: the tale made Christian

Dates: Old Georgian, c. 9th–10th c. (its earliest form disputed) · Lang, The Balavariani (1966)
Summary: The hinge of the whole history. In the Georgian Balavariani the Indian wisdom-romance becomes, for the first time, an explicitly Christian saint’s life: the prince Iodasaph is converted to Christianity by the ascetic Balahvar, and the Buddhist matter is baptized into the hagiography that the Greek and Latin worlds would inherit.

Claims & evidence

The first Christianization

Where the Arabic Bilawhar wa-Būdhāsaf is a wisdom-tale of a prince and his teacher, the Georgian Balavariani makes the teacher a Christian monk and the prince a convert to the Gospel. The prince Iodasaph — sheltered by his father King Abenes from all sight of suffering, awakened to mortality, and drawn to the ascetic life exactly as the Buddha had been — is now instructed and baptized by Balahvar, and dies a Christian hermit. The Buddhist skeleton is intact; the flesh is Christian. This is the version in which the two figures first become candidates for a Christian calendar.

— David Marshall Lang, The Balavariani (Barlaam and Josaphat): A Tale from the Christian East (London/Berkeley, 1966), with introduction by Ilia V. Abuladze; and Lang, The Wisdom of Balahvar (1957).

The manuscript and the two recensions

Lang edited the long recension from a unique full-length manuscript in the library of the Greek Patriarchate at Jerusalem, arguing that it, rather than the shorter and better-known Wisdom of Balahvar, preserves the original Georgian text; the shorter form he took to be a later abridgement. It was this Georgian text that Euthymius the Athonite — himself a Georgian of Mount Athos — would translate into the Greek that carried the legend to the rest of Christendom.

Notes

  • The dating. Lang, Korneli Kekelidze, and Ilia Abuladze — the three scholars who edited and studied the Georgian texts — independently placed the full recension in the ninth or tenth century. The Georgian scholar Shalva Nutsubidze argued instead for a seventh-century original, identifying its author with the Byzantine writer John Moschus (whose surname he read as “the Georgian”); that thesis overreaches and is now abandoned — its etymology is false, its New-Persian loanwords bar a pre-ninth-century date, and the Georgian descends from an eighth-century Arabic book, which makes a seventh-century Georgian chronologically impossible (the dating argument weighs this in full, assessment sound). The relative position of the Georgian stage — after the Arabic, before the Greek — is not in doubt; what stays genuinely open is only the choice within the ninth-to-tenth-century window, and this timeline places the stage by its safer, later bound.
  • Why Georgia. Medieval Georgia sat on the seam between the Islamic and Byzantine worlds, with monks fluent in Arabic and Greek alike; it was exactly the milieu in which an Arabic wisdom-romance could be read, recognized as edifying, and rewritten as Christian hagiography.
