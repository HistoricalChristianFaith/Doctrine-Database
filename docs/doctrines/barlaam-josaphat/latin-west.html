Summary: How the legend conquered Western Christendom. A Latin translation of the Greek made in 1048 opened the door; in the thirteenth century the tale was gathered into the two great reference-works of the age — Vincent of Beauvais’s universal history and Voragine’s Golden Legend — and from there passed into every European vernacular, and even into Shakespeare.

Claims & evidence

The 1048 Latin translation

The Greek Barlaam and Ioasaph was first rendered into Latin in 1048. This anonymous translation was the gateway of the legend into the Latin West: as the standard scholarship observes, every later European-language version derives — “without exception” — from either the Greek or this Latin. The foundational study of the first Latin text and its Greek exemplar is Paul Peeters’s “La première traduction latine de ‘Barlaam et Josaphat’ et son original grec” (Analecta Bollandiana 49, 1931).

— see N. H. Levine, “Barlaam and Josaphat,” in Brill’s Encyclopedia of Buddhism (2019); and the Fordham entry on Barlaam et Josephat.

Vincent of Beauvais and the Golden Legend

In the thirteenth century the tale entered the reference-shelf of Christendom. The Dominican Vincent of Beauvais gave the story in full in his Speculum Historiale — the historical part of the vast Speculum Maius — and his fellow Dominican Jacobus de Voragine included it, in abridged form, among the saints’ lives of the Legenda Aurea (Golden Legend). It was the Golden Legend, copied and printed across Europe (William Caxton Englished it in 1483), that made Barlaam and Josaphat a standard saint’s life — read, preached, and painted like any other.

— Jacobus de Voragine, the Golden Legend, tr. Caxton, vol. 7; the identification of the Buddhist origin is set out in the 1913 Catholic Encyclopedia, “Barlaam and Josaphat.”

Into the vernaculars — and into Shakespeare

From the Latin and the Greek the legend spread through medieval Europe in dozens of forms: Rudolf von Ems’s Middle High German verse Barlaam und Josaphat (c. 1230), the Old French poems of Gui de Cambrai and Chardri, an Old Norse Barlaams saga made for the Norwegian royal court, and versions in Middle English, Old Swedish, and Icelandic. One of its embedded parables — the test of the three caskets of gold, silver, and lead — reached Shakespeare’s Merchant of Venice through Caxton’s Golden Legend.

— the Old Norse text: Barlaams ok Josaphats saga, ed. R. Keyser & C. R. Unger (Christiania, 1851).

Notes

  • The patron of the Norse saga is disputed. The Old Norse Barlaams saga is credited by one medieval source to King Haakon Sverresson (d. 1204) and by later scholarship (Gustav Storm) to Haakon the Young (d. 1257) at the translation-court of Haakon IV; the attribution is unsettled.
  • A saint without a canonization. Neither Barlaam nor Josaphat was ever put through a formal process of canonization; their sainthood is a fact of the calendars and the legendaries, not of a papal or conciliar act — which is exactly why a Buddhist prince could be venerated for centuries before anyone noticed.
