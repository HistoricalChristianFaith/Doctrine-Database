The first Christian notices of the Buddha: Clement and Jerome

Dates: Clement of Alexandria, c. 200; Jerome, c. 393 · Clement, Stromata 1.15 · Jerome, Against Jovinianus 1.42
Summary: Centuries before the Buddha-legend reached the Christian world in narrative form, two of its most learned Fathers already knew that a teacher called the Buddha was revered in India — Clement noting that the Indians paid him “divine honours,” and Jerome reporting the Indian tradition of his birth “through the side of a virgin.” These are not links in the legend’s transmission but a measure of how early the raw material was known in the West.

Claims & evidence

Clement of Alexandria: the Indians worship the Buddha (c. 200)

Clement, cataloguing the “barbarian philosophers” from whom (he argues) the Greeks learned, places the Indian sages among them and distinguishes their two orders.

The Indian gymnosophists are also in the number, and the other barbarian philosophers. And of these there are two classes, some of them called Sarmanae, and others Brahmins.

A few lines later he names the Buddha directly:

Some, too, of the Indians obey the precepts of Buddha; whom, on account of his extraordinary sanctity, they have raised to divine honours.

— Clement of Alexandria, Stromata (Miscellanies) 1.15 (tr. ANF; the Greek names him Βούττα, “Boutta”). Text also at The Stromata, Book 1.

Jerome: the Buddha born of a virgin’s side (c. 393)

Jerome, marshalling pagan precedents for the honour of virginity in his treatise Against Jovinianus, reports the Indian tradition of the Buddha’s miraculous birth.

To come to the Gymnosophists of India, the opinion is authoritatively handed down that Budda, the founder of their religion, had his birth through the side of a virgin.

— Jerome, Against Jovinianus 1.42 (tr. NPNF). Text also at Against Jovinianus, Book 1. Jerome couples it with the Greek legends of Minerva sprung from the head of Jove and Bacchus from his thigh — birth-marvels of the “barbarians” and the cultured alike.

Notes

  • Awareness, not transmission. Neither Father is a carrier of the Barlaam-and-Josaphat story; the legend reached the Christian world much later, by the Manichaean and Arabic route. What these notices show is that the Greco-Roman Christian world had known of the Buddha since at least the second century — so the eventual arrival of his life-story in Christian dress fell on ground long prepared.
  • The virgin-birth motif. Jerome’s report that the Buddha was “born through the side of a virgin” reflects the Indian tradition of Queen Māyā’s conception and the birth from her side; that a Latin Father should record it in 393, as a datum about “the Gymnosophists of India,” is a small but striking witness to the traffic of religious motifs across the ancient world.
