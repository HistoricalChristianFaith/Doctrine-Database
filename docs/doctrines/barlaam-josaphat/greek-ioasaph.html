The Greek Barlaam and Ioasaph and its attribution

Dates: late 10th / early 11th c. (before 1028), on the attribution to Euthymius · Loeb text (Woodward & Mattingly, 1914)
Summary: The Greek version is the one from which every later European telling descends, and its authorship is disputed. Lang and Robert Volk’s critical edition attribute it to the Georgian monk Euthymius the Athonite (d. 1028), translating and elaborating the Georgian Balavariani, and Volk classes it among the spurious writings of John of Damascus; the Roman Martyrology and the older editions ascribe it to the Damascene, as Franz Dölger did in 1953; Hermann Zotenberg (1886) assigned it to a seventh-century Sabaite monk.

Claims & evidence

The attribution to Euthymius the Athonite

Euthymius the Iberian (the Georgian), a monk of the Iviron monastery on Mount Athos who died in 1028, translated a great body of Christian literature between Georgian and Greek. His biographer George the Athonite lists the Balahvar romance among his translations from Georgian into Greek, and on this attribution the Greek Barlaam and Ioasaph is his rendering and reworking of the Georgian Balavariani. It is a polished Byzantine spiritual novel, complete with long doctrinal discourses (including the Apology of Aristides embedded in Barlaam’s teaching), and it became enormously popular; from it descend, directly or through the Latin, all the later European versions.

The attribution to John of Damascus

Most Greek manuscripts credit the work only to “John the Monk” (Iōannēs monachos). Later scribes identified this John with the most famous monastic John of the Greek East, John of Damascus (c. 675–749) — an attribution carried into the Roman Martyrology under Pope Sixtus V and preserved on the title-page of the standard Loeb edition of 1914, which prints the work under the Damascene’s name. In 1953 the Byzantinist Franz Dölger argued that the Greek version “in the form in which we possess it” is the Damascene’s, and Henri Grégoire and Gérard Garitte upheld at least some of his conclusions (as reported by Ilia Abuladze in his introduction to Lang, The Balavariani, 1966).

— [St John Damascene:] Barlaam and Ioasaph, tr. G. R. Woodward & H. Mattingly, Loeb Classical Library (1914).

A seventh-century Sabaite monk

Hermann Zotenberg (1886) argued that the work reflects the religious disputes of early seventh-century Syria and that its Greek text was edited by a monk of St Saba named John; the 1913 Catholic Encyclopedia describes the Greek as “written probably by a monk of the Sabbas monastery near Jerusalem at the beginning of the seventh century.”

— 1911 Encyclopædia Britannica, “Barlaam and Josaphat”; Francis Mershman, “Barlaam and Josaphat,” Catholic Encyclopedia.

Volk’s critical edition: spuria

Robert Volk’s critical edition of the Greek text, published within the collected Schriften des Johannes von Damaskos, classes Barlaam and Ioasaph among the spuria and attributes it to Euthymius the Athonite. Among those who hold the Euthymian attribution, how much Euthymius translated and how much he freely composed is discussed.

— Robert Volk, Die Schriften des Johannes von Damaskos VI: Historia animae utilis de Barlaam et Ioasaph (spuria), Patristische Texte und Studien 60–61 (De Gruyter, 2006); cf. “Barlaam and Josaphat,” Gorgias Encyclopedic Dictionary of the Syriac Heritage.

Reception and debate
