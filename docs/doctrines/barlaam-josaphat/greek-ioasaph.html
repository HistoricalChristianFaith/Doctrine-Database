The Greek Barlaam and Ioasaph and its false attribution to John of Damascus

Dates: late 10th / early 11th c. (before 1028) · Loeb text (Woodward & Mattingly, 1914)
Summary: The Greek version is the fountainhead from which every later European telling flows — and the one whose authorship the church got wrong for centuries. It was translated and elaborated from the Georgian Balavariani by the Georgian monk Euthymius the Athonite (d. 1028), but came to be ascribed to the theologian John of Damascus. Modern scholarship has restored Euthymius and marks the work among the Damascene’s spurious writings.

Claims & evidence

Euthymius the Athonite, the real author

Euthymius the Iberian (the Georgian), a monk of the Iviron monastery on Mount Athos who died in 1028, translated a great body of Christian literature between Georgian and Greek. Among his works was the Greek Barlaam and Ioasaph, rendered and reworked from the Georgian Balavariani. It is a polished Byzantine spiritual novel, complete with long doctrinal discourses (including the Apology of Aristides embedded in Barlaam’s teaching), and it became enormously popular; from it descend, directly or through the Latin, all the later European versions.

How John of Damascus got the credit

Most Greek manuscripts credit the work only to “John the Monk” (Iōannēs monachos). Later scribes identified this John with the most famous monastic John of the Greek East, John of Damascus (c. 675–749) — and the attribution stuck: it was carried into the Roman Martyrology under Pope Sixtus V and preserved on the title-page of the standard Loeb edition of 1914, which prints the work under the Damascene’s name.

— [St John Damascene:] Barlaam and Ioasaph, tr. G. R. Woodward & H. Mattingly, Loeb Classical Library (1914).

The modern verdict: spuria

Critical scholarship rejects the Damascene authorship. Robert Volk’s critical edition of the Greek text — published within the collected Schriften des Johannes von Damaskos precisely so as to settle the question — classes Barlaam and Ioasaph among the spuria and confirms Euthymius the Athonite as its author. What remains debated is only the finer measure of how much Euthymius translated and how much he freely composed.

— Robert Volk, Die Schriften des Johannes von Damaskos VI: Historia animae utilis de Barlaam et Ioasaph (spuria), Patristische Texte und Studien 60–61 (De Gruyter, 2006); cf. “Barlaam and Josaphat,” Gorgias Encyclopedic Dictionary of the Syriac Heritage.

Notes

  • An authorship error atop an identity error. The Greek text sits at the meeting of two misattributions: the hero who is really the Buddha, and the author who was really Euthymius, not John of Damascus. Both were corrected only by modern research — the second by textual scholarship, the first by the recovery of the Buddhist source.
  • Why it mattered so much. Because the Greek was the immediate parent of the 1048 Latin translation, and the Latin the parent of the vernacular Europe-wide diffusion, Euthymius’s book is the single most consequential link in the chain after the Georgian Christianization.
