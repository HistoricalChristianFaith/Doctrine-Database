The Sanskrit life of the Buddha

Dates: c. 1st–3rd c. AD (Aśvaghoṣa’s Buddhacarita, early 2nd c.; the Lalitavistara compiled c. 1st–3rd c.) · Cowell’s Buddhacarita, SBE vol. 49
Summary: The ancestor of the whole tradition. The Sanskrit lives of Gautama Siddhārtha narrate a sheltered prince, the “four sights” that awaken him to suffering and death, and his renunciation of throne and family for the ascetic path — the exact narrative skeleton that survives, a millennium later and dressed as Christian, in the story of Prince Josaphat.

Claims & evidence

The sheltered prince and the four sights

The core of the Buddha-legend is a king’s son kept from all knowledge of suffering, who on his rare excursions beyond the palace is confronted by four sights that overturn his sheltered world — a man broken by age, a sick man, a dead man, and a serene religious mendicant. Aśvaghoṣa’s Buddhacarita, a Sanskrit court-epic life of the Buddha, sets out the encounters in order (Book III); the prince, learning that age, sickness, and death come to all, resolves to seek the way beyond them. The fuller and more legendary Lalitavistara tells the same arc at greater length. (Aśvaghoṣa, Buddhacarita III, tr. E. B. Cowell, in Buddhist Mahâyâna Texts, SBE 49.)

The Great Renunciation

Awakened to the impermanence of all he has, the prince abandons palace, wife, child, and kingdom by night to become a homeless seeker — the Great Renunciation, the pivot of every telling. This is the scene the Christian legend keeps: Josaphat too is a prince, sheltered by a father who would keep him from all sorrow, awakened to mortality by the sight of the afflicted, and drawn away from his throne to the ascetic life — only now the teacher who awakens him is the Christian hermit Barlaam, and the release he seeks is the salvation of the Gospel.

Notes

  • Why it heads this timeline. This page is not a claim about the Buddha but the first rung of a transmission-history: the datable Sanskrit texts are the source from which the later Manichaean, Arabic, Georgian, Greek, and Latin versions all ultimately descend. The parallel of plot is not a coincidence noticed by moderns alone — it is a genealogy the sources themselves let us trace.
  • The name. The Buddha’s title bodhisattva (“a being destined for enlightenment”) is the very word that, worn down through the languages of transmission, becomes the saint’s name Josaphat — the surest single proof of the descent.
