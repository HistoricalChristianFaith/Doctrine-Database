The Manichaean bridge and the Arabic Book of Bilawhar and Budhasaf

Dates: Manichaean stage placed from the 3rd c. onward by Alfaric and Asmussen (surviving Turfan fragments later); the Arabic Kitāb Bilawhar wa-Būdhāsaf current in Baghdad by the 8th c. · Encyclopædia Iranica, “Barlaam and Iosaph”
Summary: The stretch of the road where the Buddha’s life left the Buddhist world. On Jes P. Asmussen’s account the Manichaeans carried the tale out of India and preserved it in their own script, and from Manichaean Middle Persian it was rendered into Arabic as the Book of Bilawhar and Budhasaf; how far the surviving fragments document that stage is disputed. Asmussen and the 1911 Britannica place in the copying of Arabic letters the slip by which the Buddha’s title bodhisattva became the name of a saint.

Claims & evidence

The Manichaean carriers (Turfan fragments)

Fragments of the story survive among the Manichaean manuscripts recovered from Turfan in Central Asia, in Uighur, Parthian, and Middle Persian. Asmussen, in the Encyclopædia Iranica, concludes from their existence that “it was the Manicheans who transmitted this Indian tale to the West,” and that “from Manichean Middle Persian the story was then translated into Arabic.” Asmussen places the Middle Persian form Bōdisav / Bwdysdf of the Buddha’s title bodhisattva among these Manichaean forms, directly behind the Arabic (de Blois treats that form as reconstructed; see the transmission-route page).

— Jes P. Asmussen, “Barlaam and Iosaph,” Encyclopædia Iranica III/8 (1988), 801.

Mani’s line of messengers

Mani (d. 274/277), in the Šābuhragān — the book he dedicated to the Sasanian king Shapur I — set the Buddha at the head of the line of messengers he claimed to complete. Al-Bīrūnī preserves the passage:

Wisdom and deeds have always from time to time been brought to mankind by the messengers of God. So in one age they have been brought by the messenger, called Buddha, to India, in another by Zarādusht to Persia, in another by Jesus to the West.

The passage goes on to name Mani himself as the bearer of this revelation in the last age, in Babylonia. The Manichaeans thus counted the Buddha among God’s messengers, a point cited in support of their role as carriers of the tale (see the transmission question). (This is the same movement that held the young Augustine as a “hearer” for nine years, from his nineteenth to his twenty-eighth year, before his conversion; see Confessions 4.1.)

— al-Bīrūnī, The Chronology of Ancient Nations, tr. C. Edward Sachau (London, 1879), p. 190, as quoted in Christiane Reck, “Šābuhragān,” Encyclopædia Iranica.

The Arabic Kitāb Bilawhar wa-Būdhāsaf (by the 8th c.)

In ʿAbbasid Baghdad the tale took Arabic form as the Kitāb Bilawhar wa-Būdhāsaf — the Book of Bilawhar (the sage) and Budhasaf (the prince). The bibliographer Ibn al-Nadīm connects it with the great translation-milieu of Ibn al-Muqaffaʿ (d. c. 757), and a recension of it entered Shīʿite devotional literature, embedded by the theologian Ibn Bābawayh (d. 991) in his Kamāl al-dīn. At this stage the story is a wisdom-romance of a prince and his ascetic teacher, not a Christian saint’s life; it is Christian in the Georgian adaptation. (For the Arabic tradition and its manuscripts, see the University of St Andrews project bibliography, Barlaam and Josaphat in Arabic Literature and Culture.)

The slip of the pen: Būdhāsaf → Yūdāsaf

Asmussen derives the prince’s name from an Arabic scribal slip. In the Arabic script the pointing that distinguishes an initial b (ـبـ) from a y (ـيـ) is a matter of dots below or beneath the letter; at some point Būdhāsaf was miscopied as Yūdāsaf. From that misreading descend the Georgian Iodasaph, the Greek Ioasaph, and the Latin Josaphat. As Asmussen puts it, “the name Iosaph is a corrupt arabicized form of bodhisattva, in which the initial b was misread as y.”

— Asmussen, Encyclopædia Iranica; cf. the same account in the 1913 Catholic Encyclopedia, “Barlaam and Josaphat.”

Reception and debate

  • The route. Asmussen takes the survival of fragments in three Manichaean languages as proof of the route; de Blois, Ronchey, and Deeg treat the pre-Arabic stage as reconstructed or open (see the transmission question).
  • The names. The chain bodhisattva → Bōdisav → Būdhāsaf → Yūdāsaf → Iodasaph → Ioasaph → Josaphat is given by Asmussen and the Catholic Encyclopedia. Barlaam descends from the Arabic Bilawhar; a further derivation from Sanskrit Bhagavan (“Lord,” an epithet of the Buddha) has been proposed and is not settled, and Almuth Degener has proposed purohita (see the names).
