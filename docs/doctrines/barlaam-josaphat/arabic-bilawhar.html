The Manichaean bridge and the Arabic Book of Bilawhar and Budhasaf

Dates: Manichaean transmission from the 3rd c. onward (surviving Turfan fragments later); the Arabic Kitāb Bilawhar wa-Būdhāsaf current in Baghdad by the 8th c. · Encyclopædia Iranica, “Barlaam and Iosaph”
Summary: The stretch of the road where the Buddha’s life left the Buddhist world and entered the orbit of the West. The Manichaeans carried the tale out of India and preserved it in their own scriptures; from Manichaean Middle Persian it was rendered into Arabic as the Book of Bilawhar and Budhasaf, and it is here — in the copying of Arabic letters — that the Buddha’s title bodhisattva mutated into the name of a saint.

Claims & evidence

The Manichaean carriers (Turfan fragments)

Fragments of the story survive among the Manichaean manuscripts recovered from Turfan in Central Asia, in Uighur, Parthian, and Middle Persian. The Encyclopædia Iranica draws the conclusion plainly: their existence proves that “it was the Manicheans who transmitted this Indian tale to the West,” and that “from Manichean Middle Persian the story was then translated into Arabic.” In these Manichaean forms the Buddha’s title bodhisattva already appears as Bōdisav / Bwdysdf — the form that stands directly behind the Arabic.

— Jes P. Asmussen, “Barlaam and Iosaph,” Encyclopædia Iranica III/8 (1988), 801.

The Arabic Kitāb Bilawhar wa-Būdhāsaf (by the 8th c.)

In ʿAbbasid Baghdad the tale took Arabic form as the Kitāb Bilawhar wa-Būdhāsaf — the Book of Bilawhar (the sage) and Budhasaf (the prince). The bibliographer Ibn al-Nadīm connects it with the great translation-milieu of Ibn al-Muqaffaʿ (d. c. 757), and a recension of it entered Shīʿite devotional literature, embedded by the theologian Ibn Bābawayh (d. 991) in his Kamāl al-dīn. At this stage the story is a wisdom-romance of a prince and his ascetic teacher, not yet a Christian saint’s life; the Christianizing would come in the Georgian adaptation. (For the Arabic tradition and its manuscripts, see the University of St Andrews project bibliography, Barlaam and Josaphat in Arabic Literature and Culture.)

The slip of the pen: Būdhāsaf → Yūdāsaf

The decisive corruption is an Arabic scribal accident. In the Arabic script the pointing that distinguishes an initial b (ـبـ) from a y (ـيـ) is a matter of dots below or beneath the letter; at some point Būdhāsaf was miscopied as Yūdāsaf. From that misreading descend the Georgian Iodasaph, the Greek Ioasaph, and the Latin Josaphat. As Asmussen puts it, “the name Iosaph is a corrupt arabicized form of bodhisattva, in which the initial b was misread as y.”

— Asmussen, Encyclopædia Iranica; cf. the same account in the 1913 Catholic Encyclopedia, “Barlaam and Josaphat.”

Notes

  • Where the tale becomes portable. The Manichaeans, a missionary religion spanning from the Roman Empire to China, were the natural carriers of an Indian story westward; that the fragments survive in three of their languages is the material proof of the route.
  • The etymology is the smoking gun. The chain bodhisattva → Bōdisav → Būdhāsaf → Yūdāsaf → Iodasaph → Ioasaph → Josaphat is what turns a plot-resemblance into a demonstrated genealogy. The derivation of Barlaam is less certain: most trace it to the Arabic Bilawhar, and a further link to Sanskrit Bhagavan (“Lord,” an epithet of the Buddha) has been proposed but is not settled.
