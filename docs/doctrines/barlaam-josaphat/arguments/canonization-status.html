Were Barlaam and Josaphat canonized?

Disputed reading · on Barlaam and Josaphat · witness: the Latin calendars and saints’-lives collections

The passage

The heroes of the romance have even attained saintly rank. Their names were inserted by Petrus de Natalibus in his Catalogus Sanctorum (c. 1380), and Cardinal Baronius included them in the official Martyrologium authorized by Sixtus V. (1585–1590) under the date of the 27th of November. In the Orthodox Eastern Church “the holy Josaph, son of Abener, king of India” is allotted the 26th of August.

— 1911 Encyclopædia Britannica, “Barlaam and Josaphat,” Wikisource.1

The question

Barlaam and Josaphat are commemorated in the Roman Martyrology on 27 November, in the Greek calendar on 26 August, and in the Slavic churches on 19 November, where the Orthodox calendar keeps “Sts. Barlaam, monk, Ioasaph, prince of India, and Abenner the King, father of St. Ioasaph (4th c.).”2,3 Writers differ on whether this standing amounts to canonization. The question turns partly on what the word names at a given date: the 1907 Catholic Encyclopedia records that bishops once permitted public veneration of saints in their own dioceses, that Alexander III (1159–81) asserted papal authority over it, that Urban VIII in 1634 reserved both canonization and beatification to the Holy See, and that an immemorial cult may be confirmed by “equivalent” canonization without a judicial process.4

Venerated through the martyrology, without a canonization

A martyrology entry is a liturgical commemoration, and inclusion in it is not by itself a canonization.

Held by: Benedict XIV (De servorum Dei beatificatione, as cited in the Catholic Encyclopedia, 1907), on the general rule.4 Its application to Barlaam and Josaphat: ⚠ unverified — no named proponent located yet.

The rule Benedict XIV states is that equivalent canonization “consists usually in the ordering of an Office and Mass by the pope in honour of the saint, and that mere enrollment in the Roman Martyrology does not by any means imply this honour.”4 On this reading the route by which the two reached the Western calendar matters. Their names were entered by Petrus de Natalibus in his Catalogus Sanctorum (c. 1380), a private compilation of saints’ lives, and from there Cardinal Caesar Baronius placed them, drawing on the life ascribed to John of Damascus, in the official Martyrologium Romanum of the 1580s, under 27 November: “Apud Indos, Persis finitimos, sanctorum Barlaam et Josaphat, quorum actus mirandos sanctus Joannes Damascenus conscripsit” (“Among the Indians, neighbours to the Persians, [the commemoration] of Saints Barlaam and Josaphat, whose wondrous deeds Saint John Damascene recorded”).2 No bull, cause, or processus for either figure is recorded in the sources cited here ⚠ unverified, so their standing in the West rests on that entry, and in the East on the synaxaria; on this reading they are saints of the calendar who were commemorated, not canonized.

Recognized as saints by their place in the calendars

Entry into the calendars of saints is itself the recognition of sainthood, so the two are canonized saints of both West and East.

Held by: the Encyclopædia Britannica (1911); Justin Popović (Serbian Orthodox, Lives of the Saints) ⚠ unverified; OrthodoxWiki.5

The 1911 Britannica writes that the two “have even attained saintly rank,” and that “thus unwittingly Gautama the Buddha has come to official recognition as a saint in two great branches of the Catholic Church.”1 OrthodoxWiki states that “Josaphat and Barlaam were canonized in the Roman Catholic Church (feast day November 27) and are recognized among the Eastern Orthodox.” Justin Popović included their life in the November volume of his Serbian Lives of the Saints.5 On this reading their record in the Martyrology of Rome and in the Greek and Slavic synaxaria is itself their recognition as saints.3
