When was the Georgian Balavariani composed?

Disputed reading · on Barlaam and Josaphat · witness: the Georgian Balavariani

Related readings: Who wrote the Greek Barlaam and Ioasaph?

The passage

…there was also formulated one entirely novel and individual conception of the origins of the Georgian version of Balavariani, which failed to attract much support among scholars. We refer to the theory of Professor Shalva Nutsubidze, a member of the Academy of Sciences of the Georgian S.S.R., according to whom the Georgian romance Balavariani (The Wisdom of Balavar) is a literary monument of the seventh century A.D. — an original composition written, like the Georgian Limonarion or ‘Spiritual Paradise’, by the Byzantine writer John Moschus.

— Ilia V. Abuladze, introduction to David Marshall Lang, The Balavariani (1966), reporting the thesis of Sh. Nutsubidze, K proiskhozhdeniyu grecheskogo romana “Varlaam i Ioasaf” (Tbilisi, 1956).1

The question

The Georgian Balavariani, in which the prince Iodasaph is converted to Christianity by the ascetic Balahvar, stands between the Arabic Kitāb Bilawhar wa-Būdhāsaf and the Greek Barlaam and Ioasaph on most reconstructions. Its absolute date is disputed: a seventh-century date would place the tale’s Christian form in the Caucasus of late antiquity, a ninth- or tenth-century date in the century or two before Euthymius the Athonite turned it into Greek. John Moschus (d. c. 619), the author named by the early dating, wrote the Pratum Spirituale (Leimonarion); Britannica describes him as a monk born at Damascus, surnamed Eucrates.2

A Georgian Christian adaptation of the Arabic book, of the ninth or tenth century

The Georgian is a Christian reworking of the eighth-century Arabic book, and its New Persian vocabulary places it no earlier than the ninth century.

Held by: N. Y. Marr; Korneli Kekelidze (1956); David Marshall Lang (1957, 1966); Ilia Abuladze (1966); Paul Devos (1957, against Nutsubidze).3

Marr, who first printed and studied the Georgian Balavar texts, observed that Persian words in the shorter Wisdom of Balavar — susti (“weak”), plasi (“hair shirt”), changi (“lyre”), pasukhi (“answer”), chabuki (“youth, knight”), laghi (“bold”) — “seemed to rule out any attempt to assign the work to a period prior to the ninth century,” New Persian vocabulary of this kind entering Georgian literature from the ninth century on. Abuladze, editing the full-length Jerusalem text, concluded that it “would be very difficult on this evidence to judge the Georgian version… to be a production of the earliest phase of Georgian literature, the period from the fifth to the eighth century.”4 The Georgian carries Arabic syntactic calques and an Arabic name-chain (Arabic Janaisar → Georgian Habeneser → Greek Abonner), and Paul Peeters (1931) held that it “indubitably stemmed from the Arabic”; Marr himself came to write that “it is a fact that it… is translated from the Arabic.” The Arabic book belongs to the eighth-century ‘Abbasid translation-milieu associated with Ibn al-Muqaffaʿ (d. c. 756–759), so on this reading its Georgian adaptation follows it.5 The unique full-length manuscript, Jerusalem Georgian codex 140, was copied in the second half of the eleventh century, and Euthymius had the Georgian in hand by around the 980s.6,7 Within this window its holders differ: Lang places the full-length version in the ninth century, Kekelidze on the border of the ninth and tenth, Abuladze in the ninth or tenth; and Lang, Kekelidze, and Abuladze take the full Balavariani as original and the Wisdom of Balahvar as its abridgement, with M. Tarkhnishvili dissenting.7

A seventh-century original by John Moschus “the Georgian”

The Balavariani is an original Georgian composition of the seventh century by John Moschus, read as a bilingual Georgian writer.

Held by: Shalva Nutsubidze (K proiskhozhdeniyu grecheskogo romana “Varlaam i Ioasaf”, 1956); Simon Qaukhchishvili, “with a few modifications.”8

Nutsubidze (1888–1969), Georgian philosopher and Rustaveli scholar, read the surname Moschus, in its Georgian form, as an ethnic label meaning “the Georgian,” so that John Moschus, author of the Pratum Spirituale, could have been “a Georgian bilingual author writing both in Georgian and in Greek”; the Balavariani then becomes his seventh-century Georgian composition, a companion to the Georgian Limonarion. He built on a current of Georgian priority in this legend: F. C. Conybeare (1896), working from Marr’s edition, held the Georgian to be the earliest Christian form of the story, ancestral to the Greek and through it the Armenian, and Marr’s teacher V. R. Rosen had considered it possible that the Greek was translated from an Old Georgian text. Nutsubidze treated the short Wisdom of Balavar as the authentic, more primitive core, and the long recension as secondary, so that behind the surviving manuscripts lies a text considerably older than they are. The thesis belongs to his wider work on Georgian authors behind the Greek Christian literature of late antiquity, including the identification of Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite with Peter the Iberian, which Ernest Honigmann reached independently.8,9
