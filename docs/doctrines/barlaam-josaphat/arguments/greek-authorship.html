Who wrote the Greek Barlaam and Ioasaph?

Disputed reading · on Barlaam and Josaphat · witness: the Greek Barlaam and Ioasaph

Related readings: When was the Georgian Balavariani composed?

The passage

…an edifying story… brought to the Holy City by John the Monk (an honourable man and a virtuous, of the monastery of Saint Sabas)…

— heading of the Greek Barlaam and Ioasaph, tr. G. R. Woodward & H. Mattingly (Loeb Classical Library, 1914).1

The question

Most Greek manuscripts credit the romance to “John the Monk” of the monastery of St Sabas, and it has been assigned to John of Damascus (d. c. 749), to an earlier Sabaite monk of around 600, and to the Georgian monk Euthymius the Athonite (d. 1028), working from the Georgian Balavariani. The answer fixes when and where the tale took Greek form: in eighth-century Palestine, in the seventh century, or on Mount Athos around the turn of the millennium.1

Euthymius the Athonite rendered it from the Georgian

The Greek is Euthymius’s translation and elaboration of the Georgian Balavariani, made at the Iviron monastery on Athos around the 980s.

Held by: George the Athonite (Life of John and Euthymius, c. 1045); David Marshall Lang (1955); Ilia Abuladze (1966); Robert Volk (critical edition, 2006–2009).2

Euthymius the Athonite was a Georgian monk of Iviron who translated a large body of literature between Georgian and Greek. His near-contemporary biography by his successor George the Athonite lists the Balahvar romance among the works he turned from Georgian into Greek.3 The Greek name-forms, plot, and sequence descend through the Georgian stage (Georgian Iodasaph → Greek Ioasaph; Balahvar → Barlaam), and Lang concluded in 1955 that “we are justified in naming Euthymius as the author of this Greek version… the oriental language from which he derived this version we can now identify as Georgian.”4 Volk’s two-volume critical edition, issued within the collected Schriften des Johannes von Damaskos, surveys the history of the attribution and concludes that the ascription to Euthymius the Georgian “has now become unavoidable,” classing the work among the Damascene’s spuria.5 Holders of this reading discuss two finer questions among themselves: whether the Iviron attribution formula makes the Greek the work of Euthymius (hypó) or something produced at his instance (pará, hypér Euthymíou), and how far he translated the Georgian and how far, as a metaphrast, he recomposed it as a Byzantine spiritual novel.6

John of Damascus wrote it

The “John the Monk of St Sabas” of the manuscripts is the Sabaite theologian John of Damascus, and the Greek romance in its present form is his.

Held by: the Roman Martyrology of Cardinal Baronius (1580s); Franz Dölger (Der griechische Barlaam-Roman ein Werk des H. Johannes von Damaskos, 1953); Henri Grégoire and Gérard Garitte, on at least some of Dölger’s conclusions.7

The manuscripts’ heading names a John of the monastery of St Sabas, and the most celebrated monk of Mar Saba named John is John of Damascus. Baronius’s Martyrology commemorates the two saints “quorum actus mirandos sanctus Joannes Damascenus conscripsit” (“whose wondrous deeds Saint John Damascene recorded”); the text is reproduced among the Damascene’s works in Migne’s Patrologia Graeca (vol. XCVI), and the Loeb edition of 1914 issues it under his name.8 The romance is a doctrinally developed Byzantine work, in which the hero is taught through long discourses on the faith that embed the second-century Apology of Aristides.1 In 1953 Dölger argued that the Greek version “in the form in which we possess it” belongs to John Damascene (c. 676–749), as the preambles of certain Greek manuscripts indicate, and he questioned the genuineness of George the Athonite’s statement that Euthymius translated “Balavar” from Georgian into Greek, as Abuladze reports his case.7

A Sabaite monk named John edited it in the early seventh century

The romance reflects the religious disputes of seventh-century Syria and Palestine, and its Greek text was the work of a monk John of St Sabas of that period.

Held by: Hermann Zotenberg (Notices sur le livre de Barlaam et Josaphat, 1886); the Encyclopædia Britannica (1911); Francis Mershman (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1913).8

The 1911 Britannica reports that “a strong case has been made out by Zotenberg that it reflects the religious struggles and disputes of the early 7th century in Syria, and that the Greek text was edited by a monk of Saint Saba named John, his version being the source of all later texts and translations.” Mershman’s article describes the Greek text as “written probably by a monk of the Sabbas monastery near Jerusalem at the beginning of the seventh century,” and records that the legend “cannot, however, have been a work of the great Damascene, as was proved by Zotenberg” in 1886 and by Hammel in 1888.8 On this reading the manuscripts’ “John the Monk” is a monk of St Sabas distinct from the Damascene. Lang in 1955 listed this attribution, to an anonymous author of about 600, among the three then current.1
