From what do the names Josaphat and Barlaam derive?

Disputed reading · on Barlaam and Josaphat · witness: the Arabic Kitāb Bilawhar wa-Būdhāsaf

Related readings: Did the tale reach the Arabic book through a Manichaean intermediary? · What does the parallel between Josaphat’s story and the Buddha’s life show?

See also (on another doctrine): a saint’s name or epithet shaped by a copying slip as a legend crossed languages — why is St Christopher dog-headed — and “Canaanite”?

The passage

The story is a Christianized version of one of the legends of Buddha, as even the name Josaphat would seem to show. This is said to be a corruption of the original Joasaph, which is again corrupted from the middle Persian Budasif (Budsaif = Bodhisattva).

— Francis Mershman, “Barlaam and Josaphat,” Catholic Encyclopedia (1913), New Advent.1

The question

The Georgian, Greek, and Latin names of the prince (Iodasaph, Ioasaph, Iosaphat/Josaphat) and of the hermit (Balahvar, Barlaam) descend from the Arabic Būdhāsaf/Yūdhāsaf and Bilawhar. What stands behind those Arabic names bears on how far the Christian romance’s names preserve its Indian source. The writers cited here derive the prince’s name from bodhisattva, and none of the sources surveyed here proposes another derivation for it; the ultimate origin of Bilawhar is given differently by different writers. Regula Forster (2013), as cited in Wikipedia’s survey, calls the long-standing derivation of Bilawhar from bhagavan “unproven” and notes that others have been proposed.2

Josaphat is the Buddha’s title bodhisattva, worn down across the languages

The prince’s name is the title bodhisattva, passed from Middle Persian into Arabic, where a one-dot copying slip turned its initial b into y.

Held by: the Encyclopædia Britannica (1911); Francis Mershman (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1913); Jes P. Asmussen (Encyclopædia Iranica, 1988).3

The 1911 Britannica puts it that “the very word Joasaph or Josaphat (Arabic, Yūdasatf) is a corruption of Bodisat due to a confusion between the Arabic letters for Y and B, and Bodisatva is a common title for the Buddha in the many birth-stories that clustered round the life of the sage.” Asmussen places the Middle Persian form Bōdisav/Bwdysdf in the Manichaean fragments recovered from Turfan, the community whose Uighur, Parthian, and Middle Persian copies of the tale he takes as the route west.4 In the Arabic script bāʾ (ﺑ) and yāʾ (ﻳ) share one skeleton and differ only in the dots below the line, and the manuscripts of the Kitāb Bilawhar wa-Būdhāsaf report both Būdhāsaf and Yūdhāsaf; it is the y-form that the Georgian, Greek, and Latin carry. Asmussen’s statement is that “the name Iosaph is a corrupt arabicized form of bodhisattva, in which the initial b was misread as y.”5 The later stages are fixed in datable texts: Iodasaph in the Georgian Balavariani, Ioasaph in Euthymius the Athonite’s Greek, Iosaphat in the Latin translation of 1048 and the Golden Legend. The western half of the hermit’s name runs through the same texts: Arabic Bilawhar, Georgian Balahvar, Greek Barlaam.

Bilawhar is the Buddha’s epithet bhagavan, “Lord”

The hermit’s Arabic name goes back to a second title of the Buddha, so that both names in the romance would preserve Buddhist epithets.

Held by: ⚠ unverified — no named proponent located yet.

This is the derivation that Wikipedia’s survey reports as the one historically given for the Arabic Bilawhar: Sanskrit bhagavan, “Lord,” an epithet of the Buddha. On this reading the chain bhagavan → Bilawhar → Balahvar → Barlaam runs through the same languages as the prince’s name, and the hermit, like the prince, bears a name drawn from the Buddha’s titles.2

Bilawhar is the priestly title purohita

The hermit’s Arabic name comes from the Sanskrit purohita, a priestly title, by way of a Middle Persian form.

Held by: Almuth Degener (“Barlaam the Priest,” Zeitschrift der Deutschen Morgenländischen Gesellschaft, 2014) ⚠ unverified.6

Degener derives Bilawhar from purohita through a hypothetical Middle Persian intermediate, so that the name of the prince’s teacher reflects a priestly office rather than an epithet of the Buddha. The western descent Bilawhar → Balahvar → Barlaam is common to this reading and the last; what this reading changes is the Indian word at the head of the chain, and with it the figure the Arabic name originally denoted.6
