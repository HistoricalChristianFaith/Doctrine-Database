What does the parallel between Josaphat’s story and the Buddha’s life show?

Disputed reading · on Barlaam and Josaphat · witness: the Sanskrit life of the Buddha and the Greek Barlaam and Ioasaph

Related readings: From what do the names Josaphat and Barlaam derive? · Did the tale reach the Arabic book through a Manichaean intermediary?

The passage

Now this story is, mutatis mutandis, the story of Buddha. It will suffice to recall the Buddha’s education in a secluded palace, his encounter successively with a decrepit old man, with a man in mortal disease and poverty, with a dead body, and, lastly, with a religious recluse radiant with peace and dignity, and his consequent abandonment of his princely state for the ascetic life in the jungle.

— 1911 Encyclopædia Britannica, “Barlaam and Josaphat,” Wikisource.1

The question

The Christian romance and the Sanskrit lives of the Buddha share a plot: a prophecy at a prince’s birth, a father who shuts him away from every sight of suffering, the encounters that awaken him, and his renunciation of the throne. The writers cited here take the Christian story to descend from the Buddha’s, and none of the sources surveyed here argues for independent origin; they differ on what the correspondence carried across — a story borrowed down to its details and parables, or a narrative whose teaching was transformed in passage.

The romance is the Buddha’s life, borrowed in detail

Josaphat’s story reproduces the Buddha-legend beat for beat, with its phrasing and its Indian parables, and is the Buddha’s life retold.

Held by: Diogo do Couto (historian of Portuguese India, 1542–1616); Édouard Laboulaye (1859); Felix Liebrecht (1860); Joseph Jacobs (Barlaam and Josaphat: English Lives of Buddha, 1896).1

In the Sanskrit lives — Aśvaghoṣa’s Buddhacarita (early 2nd c.) and the fuller Lalitavistara — the sage Asita foretells at the prince Siddhārtha’s birth that he will become either a world-ruler or a Buddha; his father Śuddhodana, choosing the throne, surrounds him with luxury and screens him from age, sickness, and death; on his drives beyond the palace the prince meets, in order, a man broken by age, a sick man, a corpse, and a wandering mendicant, and abandons palace, wife, and kingdom by night.2 In the Christian romance the astrologers warn King Abenner (Avenir) at Josaphat’s birth that the boy will embrace the Christian faith; the king shuts him in a palace where nothing sorrowful may be seen; on his outings Josaphat encounters a leper and a blind man, then a decrepit old man, and later learns of death; and, taught by the hermit Barlaam, he renounces his kingdom for the ascetic life.3 The 1911 Britannica adds that “some of the correspondences in the two stories are most minute, and even the phraseology, in which some of the details of Josaphat’s history are described, almost literally renders the Sanskrit of the Lalita Vistara.”1 The parables Barlaam tells travelled with the plot: “the man in the well” (a man fleeing a maddened elephant clings in a pit to a shrub gnawed by two mice, black and white, while honey drips onto his lips) has Indian analogues, and the three caskets reached Shakespeare’s Merchant of Venice through Caxton’s Golden Legend.4 On this reading the ordered plot, the Indian apologues, and the name that descends from bodhisattva (see the names) are independent lines that each lead back to the Buddha-legend.

The narrative crossed over; its Buddhist teaching did not

What passed from the Buddha-legend into the romance was a story, whose meaning was remade at each crossing, not a body of Buddhist teaching.

Held by: Lawrence Sutin, as quoted by Ed Simon (History News Network).5

Sutin, who traces the tale from Manichaean Persia through Georgia and Greece into Western Europe, emphasizes in Simon’s account that narrative similarity does not imply theological congruence: it “cannot be demonstrated that any distinct Buddhist teaching had survived in the oft-mutated… legend.” Simon draws the same line: “Religions aren’t easily reducible into one another.”5 Its holders point to the versions themselves: in the Christian romance the prophecy at the birth is that the boy will become a Christian, and the prince is awakened not by his own reflection on the four sights but by Barlaam’s catechesis.3 Rudolph of Ems, about 1220, expanded it into a German poem of 16,000 lines “celebrating the victory of Christian over heathen teaching.”1 The Hebrew Ben ha-Melekh ve-ha-Nazir of Abraham ibn Ḥisdai recast the same plot in a Jewish philosophical frame, with no Christian conversion in it (see the other branches). The same skeleton carries a different teaching in each community that retold it.
