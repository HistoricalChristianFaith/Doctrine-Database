Does the story of Josaphat trace the Buddha’s life closely enough to be borrowing, not coincidence?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: sound for the strong form — not that any single motif is uniquely the Buddha’s, but that the ordered cluster of motifs (sheltered prince → prophecy → isolating father → the four sights in sequence → Great Renunciation) together with the migrating Indian parables and the documented name- and text-chain jointly rules out independent origin · advanced by the same nineteenth–twentieth-century scholarship (Laboulaye, Liebrecht, Lang) that reconstructed the transmission.

Related arguments: the transmission — see also Is “Josaphat” a corruption of bodhisattva? and Did the tale reach Christianity through a Manichaean intermediary?

The claim

The identification of Josaphat with the Buddha does not rest on the name alone. The plot of the Christian romance reproduces the plot of the Buddha-legend beat for beat: a king’s son whose birth is met by a prophecy that he will forsake the world for the holy life; a father who, to defeat the prophecy, walls the boy up in pleasure palaces and keeps every sight of suffering from him; the prince’s excursions beyond the walls, on which he is confronted in turn by an old man, a sick man, a corpse, and a serene ascetic; and his consequent renunciation of throne and family for the religious life. The claim is that this correspondence — especially the ordered set of the four encounters — is too tight and too specific to be a coincidence of two independently-invented pious tales; it is a genealogy, not a resemblance.

The evidence for

The two arcs, laid side by side. In the Sanskrit lives — Aśvaghoṣa’s Buddhacarita (early 2nd c.) and the fuller Lalitavistara — the sage Asita foretells at the prince Siddhārtha’s birth that he will become either a world-ruler or a Buddha; his father Śuddhodana, choosing the throne, surrounds him with luxury and screens him from age, sickness, and death; on his drives beyond the palace the prince meets, in order, a man broken by age, a sick man, a corpse, and a wandering mendicant, and, awakened, abandons palace, wife, and kingdom by night — the Great Renunciation.1 The Christian romance keeps every joint of this: at Prince Josaphat’s birth the astrologers warn King Abenner (Avenir) that the boy will embrace the forbidden Christian faith; the king shuts him in a splendid palace where nothing sorrowful may be seen; on his outings Josaphat encounters a leper and a blind man, then a decrepit old man, and later learns of death, and — converted now not by inward reflection but by the hermit Barlaam, who takes the role the Buddha’s own insight had played — renounces his kingdom for the ascetic life.2 The skeleton is identical; only the theological clothing is changed.

The parables travelled with the plot — and they are demonstrably Indian. Independent of the main arc, the Greek and Latin romance carries a set of embedded apologues that Barlaam tells Josaphat, and several of them are traceable to Indian storytelling — most famously the parable of “the man in the well” (a man fleeing a maddened elephant falls into a pit, clings to a shrub gnawed by two mice, black and white, while honey drips onto his lips and he forgets his peril), a fable with clear Indian analogues; and the parable of the three caskets (gold, silver, and pitch), which reached Shakespeare’s Merchant of Venice through Caxton’s English Golden Legend.3 That such distinctively Indian material rides inside the same book is a second, independent line of descent: a coincidental look-alike biography would not also drag along a freight of Indian fables.

Adversarial assessment

Which motifs prove nothing on their own. Honesty requires conceding that several of the individual elements are hagiographic commonplaces that could arise independently. A prophecy at a hero’s birth, a father who tries to thwart it by confinement, a sheltered youth who breaks out to a higher calling — these recur across folklore and saints’ lives worldwide (the exposed-and-hidden royal child is a stock type from Cyrus to Moses to Oedipus). Taken singly, “a prophesied holy child” or “a resisting royal father” is a weak proof of borrowing. A further caution, pressed by historians of religion, is that narrative similarity is not doctrinal continuity: it “cannot be demonstrated that any distinct Buddhist teaching had survived in the oft-mutated legend,” so the parallel shows a story travelling, not a religion smuggled across.4 The strong form of the argument must therefore rest on the cluster, not on any loose single motif.

Why the cluster nonetheless holds. What lifts this above coincidence is the conjunction of specifics. It is not merely “a sheltered prince” but the ordered sequence of the four encounters — age, sickness, death, the ascetic — a signature so particular to the Buddha-legend that its survival intact is hard to explain by chance. That plot-signature does not stand alone: it arrives welded to a name that descends by a documented philological chain from bodhisattva (rated sound), and to a text-chain whose Manichaean, Arabic, Georgian, Greek, and Latin stages are each attested by surviving witnesses (rated plausible for the Manichaean link, the Buddhist origin standing either way), and to a cargo of unmistakably Indian parables. Four independent lines — ordered plot-motif, name, text-genealogy, and migrating fables — converge on the same conclusion. Any one might be explained away; their joint improbability under a coincidence hypothesis is what makes the derivation certain. The concession that individual motifs are generic, and that no doctrine came across, does not weaken this: the argument claims a story was borrowed, and on that claim the evidence is overwhelming.

Assessment

Sound in its strong form. The narrative parallel is not, by itself and motif-by-motif, a knock-down proof — a prophesied child and a walling-in father are stock hagiography, and the parallel carries no Buddhist teaching with it. But the argument as it should be stated is not motif-by-motif: it is that the ordered cluster (sheltered prince → prophecy → isolating father → the four sights in sequence → Great Renunciation), plus the freight of demonstrably Indian parables, plus the independently-documented name- and text-chains, jointly exclude independent origin. On that cumulative footing the derivation of Josaphat’s story from the Buddha’s is established, and this narrative evidence is a genuine third pillar alongside the name and the transmission route — not a decorative parallel but part of what makes the identification certain.
