Did the tale reach the Arabic book through a Manichaean intermediary?

Disputed reading · on Barlaam and Josaphat · witness: the Manichaean Turfan fragments and the Arabic Kitāb Bilawhar wa-Būdhāsaf

Related readings: From what do the names Josaphat and Barlaam derive? · What does the parallel between Josaphat’s story and the Buddha’s life show?

See also (on another doctrine): did the holy greyhound Guinefort’s cult descend from the Indian “faithful animal wrongly slain” fable?

The passage

“The fact that fragments of the tale have been preserved in Manichean texts in Uigur, Parthian, and Persian in Manichean script from Turfan proves that it was the Manicheans who transmitted this Indian tale to the West. From Manichean Middle Persian the story was then translated into Arabic.”

— Jes P. Asmussen, “Barlaam and Iosaph,” Encyclopædia Iranica.1

The question

The later stages of the romance’s road (Arabic → Georgian → Greek → Latin) survive text by text. The stage between the Indian Buddha-legend and the Arabic Kitāb Bilawhar wa-Būdhāsaf of eighth-century Baghdad does not survive as a dated text, and writers differ on whether a Manichaean Middle Persian version filled it and on what the Manichaean fragments from Turfan document.

A Manichaean Middle Persian version carried the tale into Arabic

Manichaean communities, who counted the Buddha among God’s messengers, held the tale in their own script, and the Arabic book was made from their Middle Persian version.

Held by: Prosper Alfaric (1917); David Marshall Lang (The Balavariani, 1966); Jes P. Asmussen (Encyclopædia Iranica, 1988); François de Blois (2009).2

Alfaric argued in 1917, from the newly recovered Turfan finds, that the tale reached the West through Manichaean hands, and not before the third century. Asmussen points to the name: the Arabic Būdhāsaf/Yūdhāsaf “is similar to and probably derives from the Manichean form Bwdysdf,” the Middle Persian Bōdisav of the Manichaean script.1 Manichaeism was a missionary religion whose network ran from the Roman Empire to China, and its founder Mani, in the Šābuhragān, named the Buddha as the first of the messengers whose line he claimed to complete, so a Buddhist sage’s life was not foreign matter to his followers. Fragments of this tale in Manichaean script were recovered at Turfan.3 Lang writes that “a particularly important role in this migration was played by the Manichaeans of Central Asia,” and reports W. B. Henning’s observation that the “prophetology” of Bilauhar’s teaching in the Arabic version “compellingly recalls authentic Manichaean writings,” a passage the Christian Georgian adaptation still carries.4 De Blois’s “tentative genealogy” of the whole tradition places a Manichaean stage between the Indian source and the Arabic recensions.2

The surviving Manichaean poem descends from the Arabic, and the earlier intermediary is a reconstruction

The one substantial Manichaean-script witness is a tenth-century reworking of the Arabic book, so the route from India into Arabic is reached by inference and remains an open problem.

Held by: François de Blois (2009), on the Persian poem; Silvia Ronchey (2015); Max Deeg (2024).5

The one substantial Manichaean fragment of the tale is the Early New Persian verse “Bilawhar,” preserved on a single damaged bifolio (catalogued as M 581, M 613, M 9010), which Henning, its editor, dated no earlier than the first half of the tenth century, after the Arabic book was already circulating in eighth-century Baghdad. De Blois identified an interpolated episode showing that this Persian poem was based on the Arabic short recension: it is a Manichaean reception of an Arabic text. The pre-Arabic Middle Persian stage, like the vocalised Bōdisav name-form, is then reconstructed rather than preserved.2,3 Ronchey (2015) describes the tale as reaching the West “presumably… possibly through eclectic Manichaean hands,” and Deeg’s 2024 study treats both the Indian template and its onward transmission as a problem still to be solved.5
