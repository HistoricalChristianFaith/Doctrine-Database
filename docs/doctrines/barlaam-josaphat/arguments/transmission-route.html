Did the tale reach Christianity through a Manichaean intermediary?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: plausible — the Manichaean intermediary is the leading and well-motivated reconstruction (the surviving name-form Bwdysdf is specifically Manichaean, and the Manichaeans were the attested trans-Asian carriers of Indian lore), but the early Manichaean bridge the theory needs is reconstructed, not preserved, and the one Manichaean-script manuscript of the tale that does survive is late and appears to descend from the Arabic; the broad Buddhist origin and the name-identity stand either way, so this is a question about the road, not the destination · advanced by the classic reconstruction (Alfaric, Henning, Asmussen, Lang; refined by de Blois).

Related arguments: the transmission — see also Is “Josaphat” a corruption of bodhisattva?

The claim

“The fact that fragments of the tale have been preserved in Manichean texts in Uigur, Parthian, and Persian in Manichean script from Turfan proves that it was the Manicheans who transmitted this Indian tale to the West. From Manichean Middle Persian the story was then translated into Arabic.”

— Jes P. Asmussen, “Barlaam and Iosaph,” Encyclopædia Iranica.1 The specific interpretation this page weighs is the route: that the Buddha-life did not merely end up in Christian hands but reached them by a particular chain whose load-bearing early link is Manichaean — Sanskrit Buddha-life → a Manichaean Middle-Persian intermediary → the Arabic Kitāb Bilawhar wa-Būdhāsaf → Georgian Balavariani → Greek → Latin. The end of that chain (Georgian → Greek → Latin) is documented text-by-text and the Buddhist origin overall is near-consensus; the genuinely contested piece is the Manichaean bridge between the Indian source and the Arabic book.

Who advances it

The Manichaean routing is the classic scholarly reconstruction, not a fringe guess. Prosper Alfaric argued already in 1917, from the newly-recovered Turfan finds, that the tale reached the West through Manichaean hands (and not before the 3rd century). W. B. Henning identified and edited the Persian Turfan fragment; Jes P. Asmussen stated the case flatly in the Encyclopædia Iranica; David Marshall Lang built it into his standard account of the chain. Its most careful modern statement is François de Blois’s “tentative genealogy” of the whole tradition, which still places a Manichaean stage between the Indian source and the Arabic recensions.2 The question this page tests is whether that Manichaean stage is documented or is a well-motivated inference filling the gap between the lost Indian original and the first Arabic text.

The evidence for

The name-form that reached Arabic is specifically Manichaean. The Arabic Būdhāsaf / Yūdhāsaf does not derive straight from Sanskrit bodhisattva; it stands one step downstream of the Middle Persian Bwdysdf (Bōdisav) attested in the Manichaean script. Asmussen puts it that the Arabic form “is similar to and probably derives from the Manichean form Bwdysdf.” That the specific spelling behind the Arabic is a Manichaean one is the strongest positive fingerprint of a Manichaean stage.1

The Manichaeans were the natural carriers, and demonstrably held the tale. Manichaeism was a missionary religion whose network ran from the Roman Empire to Tang China; its founder Mani drew openly on Indian material, and the community was precisely the sort of trans-Asian conduit that could move a Buddhist story westward. This is not idle plausibility: fragments of this very tale in Manichaean script were recovered at Turfan in Central Asia — including an Early New Persian verse rendering of “Bilawhar and Būdāsaf,” edited by Henning — so we know for a fact that Manichaean communities copied and transmitted it.3 Combined with the name-form, the reconstruction has real material anchors, not merely a convenient geography.

Adversarial assessment

What holds. Two things are secure. First, a Manichaean stage somewhere in the story’s westward life is well-supported: the name-form behind the Arabic is Manichaean, and surviving Manichaean-script copies prove the community held the tale. Second, the reading is the settled leading hypothesis, endorsed even by the specialist (de Blois) who has scrutinized the fragments most closely; nobody advances a rival non-Manichaean channel — the only competitor is honest agnosticism about the intermediary.

What is overstated. The word “proves” claims more than the artifacts deliver, on two counts. (1) The surviving Manichaean witness is late. The one substantial physical fragment — the Early New Persian verse “Bilawhar,” preserved on a single damaged bifolio (the fragments catalogued M 581, M 613, M 9010) — Henning himself dated no earlier than the first half of the tenth century, long after the Arabic Kitāb Bilawhar wa-Būdhāsaf was already circulating in eighth-century Baghdad. (2) It runs the wrong way. De Blois identified an interpolated episode showing that this Persian Manichaean poem was itself based on the Arabic short recension — i.e. it is a late Manichaean reception of an already-Arabic text, not the early Manichaean bridge into Arabic that the theory requires. So the fragment attests Manichaean involvement with the tale; it does not document the pre-Arabic Manichaean Middle-Persian intermediary, which remains reconstructed (like the vocalised Bōdisav name-form) rather than preserved.2 Even the roster of Manichaean-script witnesses has been questioned in recent reassessments, and careful writers now hedge — the intermediary is reached “presumably… possibly through eclectic Manichaean hands” — where the older handbooks said “proves.” Max Deeg’s 2024 study frames the Indian template and its onward transmission as a still-open problem.4

What it does not touch. None of this dents the identification the wiki rates sound. Whether the early bridge was Manichaean, some other Middle-Persian milieu, or a channel we can no longer name, the name still descends from bodhisattva and the plot is still the Buddha’s. The Manichaean question is about the road the legend took, not about where it started or where it arrived.

Assessment

Plausible. That a Manichaean intermediary carried the Buddha-life toward the Arabic world is the leading, coherent, and well-motivated reconstruction — anchored by the distinctively Manichaean name-form behind the Arabic and by the Manichaeans’ attested role as trans-Asian carriers who provably held the tale — and it is retained even in de Blois’s close analysis. But it stops short of demonstrated: the early Manichaean Middle-Persian stage the argument needs is inferred, not preserved, and the one substantial Manichaean manuscript that does survive is late (10th c.) and appears to derive from the Arabic rather than to feed it. Grade the two levels separately: Manichaean involvement in circulating the tale is well-attested (probable); Manichaean intermediation as the specific channel from Buddhist India into Arabic is plausible, the front-runner among reconstructions, but not proven. Either way the core identification — Josaphat is the Buddha — is untouched, since it rests on the name and the plot, not on which intermediary is named.
