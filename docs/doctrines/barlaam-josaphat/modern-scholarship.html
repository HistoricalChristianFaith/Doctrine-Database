The recovery of the Buddhist origin

Dates: identification worked out 1859–1860; stated flatly by the 1913 Catholic Encyclopedia; the full chain reconstructed in the 20th c. · Catholic Encyclopedia (1913)
Summary: After a millennium during which Barlaam and Josaphat were read as Christian saints, nineteenth-century scholars recognized the plot as the life of the Buddha and the name Josaphat as a worn-down bodhisattva. The twentieth century, above all David Marshall Lang, then reconstructed the whole language-by-language road from India to Rome.

Claims & evidence

The nineteenth-century identification (1859–1860)

The equation of Josaphat with the Buddha is generally credited to Édouard Laboulaye, who set it out in the Journal des Débats in 1859, and to Felix Liebrecht, who argued it the following year in “Die Quellen des Barlaam und Josaphat” (1860), building on earlier hints that the tale was of Indian origin. Within a generation the descent was scholarly common knowledge.

The Catholic Encyclopedia states it plainly (1913)

By the early twentieth century the identification had passed into the church’s own reference-works. The 1913 Catholic Encyclopedia opens its article by conceding the point:

The story is a Christianized version of one of the legends of Buddha, as even the name Josaphat would seem to show. This is said to be a corruption of the original Joasaph, which is again corrupted from the middle Persian Budasif (Budsaif = Bodhisattva).

— Francis Mershman, “Barlaam and Josaphat,” Catholic Encyclopedia (1913); also at Wikisource.

The transmission-chain reconstructed (20th c.)

The detailed route — Sanskrit → Manichaean Middle Persian → Arabic → Georgian → Greek → Latin — was worked out over the following century. The pivotal figure is David Marshall Lang, whose The Wisdom of Balahvar (1957) and The Balavariani (1966) established the Georgian stage as the point of Christianization and narrated the chain end to end. Earlier, F. C. Conybeare had demonstrated the Georgian and Armenian transmission (“The Barlaam and Josaphat Legend in the Ancient Georgian and Armenian Literatures,” 1896 — the Armenian branch is treated with the Hebrew and Ethiopic lines on the other-branches page); the Georgian text itself was edited by Ilia V. Abuladze, who introduced Lang’s translation. The comparativist Wilfred Cantwell Smith later used Josaphat-as-Bodhisattva as a set-piece in cross-traditional religious history — a Buddhist figure honoured for centuries as a Christian saint.

— David Marshall Lang, The Balavariani (1966); the Encyclopædia Iranica summary is Jes P. Asmussen, “Barlaam and Iosaph.”

Notes

  • What the identification does and does not overturn. That Josaphat “is” the Buddha is a claim about the literary descent of a story, not a claim that the church knowingly canonized a Hindu or Buddhist deity. For most of the legend’s Christian life its origin was simply unknown; the saints Barlaam and Josaphat were, to those who kept their feast, edifying Christian ascetics whose tale happened to have travelled further than anyone guessed.
  • The priority detail is worth a check. The Laboulaye (1859) and Liebrecht (1860) credit is the standard account in the handbooks; who first noticed the parallel, and how much each owed to earlier Orientalists, is the kind of question later scholarship has continued to refine.
