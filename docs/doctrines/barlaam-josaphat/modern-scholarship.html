The recovery of the Buddhist origin

Dates: identification worked out 1859–1860; stated by the 1913 Catholic Encyclopedia; the full chain reconstructed in the 20th c. · Catholic Encyclopedia (1913)
Summary: After a millennium during which Barlaam and Josaphat were read as Christian saints, nineteenth-century scholars recognized the plot as the life of the Buddha and the name Josaphat as a worn-down bodhisattva. Twentieth-century scholars, notably David Marshall Lang, reconstructed the whole language-by-language road from India to Rome.

Claims & evidence

The nineteenth-century identification (1859–1860)

The equation of Josaphat with the Buddha is generally credited to Édouard Laboulaye, who set it out in the Journal des Débats in 1859, and to Felix Liebrecht, who argued it the following year in “Die Quellen des Barlaam und Josaphat” (1860), building on earlier hints that the tale was of Indian origin. Within a generation the descent was scholarly common knowledge ⚠ unverified. The 1911 Encyclopædia Britannica credits an earlier recognition of “the identity of the stories of Buddha and St Josaphat” to the historian of Portuguese India Diogo do Couto (1542–1616), and gives the honour “in modern times” to Laboulaye, “Felix Liebrecht in 1860 putting it beyond dispute.”

— 1911 Encyclopædia Britannica, “Barlaam and Josaphat”.

The Catholic Encyclopedia (1913)

By the early twentieth century the identification appeared in Catholic reference works. The 1913 Catholic Encyclopedia article states:

The story is a Christianized version of one of the legends of Buddha, as even the name Josaphat would seem to show. This is said to be a corruption of the original Joasaph, which is again corrupted from the middle Persian Budasif (Budsaif = Bodhisattva).

— Francis Mershman, “Barlaam and Josaphat,” Catholic Encyclopedia (1913); also at Wikisource.

The transmission-chain reconstructed (20th c.)

David Marshall Lang, in The Wisdom of Balahvar (1957) and The Balavariani (1966), set out the route as Sanskrit → Manichaean Middle Persian → Arabic → Georgian → Greek → Latin (the Manichaean stage is disputed), argued that the Georgian stage was the point of Christianization, and narrated the chain end to end; Korneli Kekelidze posited an earlier Syriac Christian version. Earlier, F. C. Conybeare had argued for the Georgian and Armenian transmission (“The Barlaam and Josaphat Legend in the Ancient Georgian and Armenian Literatures,” 1896 — the Armenian branch is treated with the Hebrew and Ethiopic lines on the other-branches page); the Georgian text itself was edited by Ilia V. Abuladze, who introduced Lang’s translation. The comparativist Wilfred Cantwell Smith later used Josaphat-as-Bodhisattva as a set-piece in cross-traditional religious history — a Buddhist figure honoured for centuries as a Christian saint.

— David Marshall Lang, The Balavariani (1966); the Encyclopædia Iranica summary is Jes P. Asmussen, “Barlaam and Iosaph.”

Reception and debate

  • What the identification concerns. That Josaphat “is” the Buddha is a claim about the literary descent of a story. The 1911 Britannica describes the Buddha’s recognition as a saint as having come about “unwittingly”; for those who kept their feast, Barlaam and Josaphat were Christian ascetics.
  • Priority. The Laboulaye (1859) and Liebrecht (1860) credit is the one the 1911 Britannica gives “in modern times”; the same article credits Diogo do Couto (1542–1616) with an earlier recognition.
