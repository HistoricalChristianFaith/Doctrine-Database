The other branches of the legend

Dates: Hebrew, early 13th c. (ibn Ḥisdai); Christian Arabic → Ethiopic branch; Armenian (via a lost Syriac) off the Greek · Budge, Baralâm and Yĕwâsĕf (1923)
Summary: The westward Christian road — Arabic → Georgian → Greek → Latin → the European vernaculars — was not the story’s only route. From the same Arabic book the tale forked into at least three further branches: a Hebrew version that carried it into Judaism, a Christian Arabic recension that reached Ethiopia, and, off the Greek, an Armenian version descending through a lost Syriac. The legend did not travel a single line; it repeatedly forked — into another faith, down another continent, and along a second Christian road east.

Claims & evidence

The Hebrew branch: ibn Ḥisdai’s Ben ha-Melekh ve-ha-Nazir (early 13th c.)

The most distinctive of the other branches is Jewish, not Christian. Abraham ben Samuel ha-Levi ibn Ḥisdai of Barcelona — a Hebrew translator active about 1230, a staunch Maimonidean who also turned al-Ghazālī and Maimonides’ own Book of the Commandments from Arabic into Hebrew — adapted the Arabic Kitāb Bilawhar wa-Būdhāsaf into Ben ha-Melekh ve-ha-Nazir, “The Prince and the Nazirite” (or “the Hermit”). The plot is unmistakably the same: a king, to forestall a prophecy that his son will abandon the world, shuts the prince away in an island palace where every sight of suffering is hidden from him, until the young man meets an ascetic and is turned to the contemplative life. But ibn Ḥisdai recast it in a Jewish philosophical frame — thirty-five chapters of tales, fables, maxims, and versified aphorisms in intermingled prose and verse — and the Christian conversion of the Georgian and Greek versions simply drops away, replaced by the prince’s turn to ascetic wisdom. Because it descends straight from the Arabic rather than through the Christian Greek line, it is independent proof that the Indian tale crossed into a third religion on a road of its own; some of its parables, absent elsewhere, may even preserve matter close to the lost original. The Hebrew work proved enduringly popular among later moralists and was first printed at Constantinople in 1518.

— “Ḥasdai, Abraham ben Samuel Halevi,” Jewish Encyclopedia; “Ben ha-Melekh ve-ha-Nazir,” Encyclopaedia Judaica.

The Ethiopic branch: Baralām and Yěwāsěf

A separate Christian Arabic recension of the romance was carried south and translated into Ge’ez (classical Ethiopic) as Baralām and Yěwāsěf. It is a Christian form of the tale — the prince is converted, as in the Georgian and Greek — but it reaches Ethiopia through Arabic Christianity rather than through the Greek and Latin of the north, making it a distinct southern limb of the same tree. The complete Ethiopic text, with an English translation and a long introduction on the legend’s spread, was published by E. A. Wallis Budge in 1923; Budge counted, even then, “more than sixty translations, versions or paraphrases” of the story in the languages of the world.

— E. A. Wallis Budge, Baralâm and Yĕwâsĕf: Being the Ethiopic Version of a Christianized Recension of the Buddhist Legend of the Buddha and the Bodhisattva, 2 vols. (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1923).

The Armenian and Syriac branch: an abridgement off the Greek

Not every branch runs back to the Arabic. Frederick Cornwallis Conybeare, printing what survived of the old Georgian and Armenian texts in 1896, argued that the old Armenian version is not an independent descent from the East but an abridgement of a lost Syriac translation of the Greek — so this limb grows downstream of Euthymius’s Greek Barlaam and Ioasaph, not off the Arabic root like the Hebrew and Ethiopic. Conybeare’s essay is otherwise chiefly important for the Georgian: he took the Georgian to be the earliest Christian form of the story, from which the Greek (and thence the Syriac and Armenian) descended — the same relative ordering that later scholarship, above all David Marshall Lang, would confirm.

— F. C. Conybeare, “The Barlaam and Josaphat Legend in the Ancient Georgian and Armenian Literatures,” Folk-Lore 7, no. 2 (1896): 101–142.

Notes

  • Why the branches matter to the identification. The core claim — that Josaphat is the Buddha — rests on the documented language-by-language chain to the Latin West. The other branches strengthen it from the side: an independent Jewish rendering straight from the Arabic (with no Christian conversion in it), a separate Christian Arabic line reaching Ethiopia, and a downstream Armenian abridgement all trace to the same Arabic Bilawhar wa-Būdhāsaf that carries the corrupted name behind Josaphat. A single stray borrowing could be coincidence; a tale that forks repeatedly into three religions’ literatures cannot be.
  • Two roads out of the Arabic, one off the Greek. The Hebrew and Ethiopic branches fork from the Arabic itself; the Armenian (through its Syriac ancestor) forks later, from the Greek. It is a useful reminder that the transmission is a tree, not a queue: the same source could be adapted for a Jewish philosophical audience, a Ge’ez-reading Ethiopian church, and — on the main road — the calendars of Rome and Constantinople.
  • The Armenian’s exact date is left open. Conybeare fixed the Armenian’s position in the chain (an abridgement of a Syriac of the Greek) more firmly than its calendar date; the surviving witnesses are medieval, and a precise dating of the Armenian recension is not settled here.
