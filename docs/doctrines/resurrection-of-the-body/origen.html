Origen on the resurrection of the body

Dates: c. 230 (the resurrection chapter of On First Principles, written at Alexandria c. 229) · Wikipedia
Summary: The great third-century alternative to the same-flesh reading. Origen affirms a real, bodily resurrection — it is a body that rises — but locates the body's identity not in the return of the very same material particles (which flow and are scattered) but in a persisting bodily principle, the eidos or form, out of which the risen “spiritual body” is grown as a stalk from a seed. This is the pivot on which the later charge that he “denied the resurrection of the flesh” turns — a charge weighed on its own page and not re-argued here; this page states only his positive doctrine.

Claims & evidence

It is a body that rises — but a “spiritual” body grown out of the “animal” one

Let them answer us this, What is that which died? Was it not a body? It is of the body, then, that there will be a resurrection. … If it is true that these rise again, and that they arise “spiritual” bodies, there can be no doubt that they are said to rise from the dead, after casting away corruption and laying aside mortality… For it is out of the animal body that the very power and grace of the resurrection educe the spiritual body, when it transmutes it from a condition of indignity to one of glory.

— On First Principles (De Principiis) II.10.1, source. Against heretics who deny the resurrection altogether, Origen insists that what rises is genuinely a body; against crude literalists he insists that it is Paul's spiritual body, transmuted — not the animal body resuscitated unchanged. He anchors both moves in 1 Corinthians 15:44.

The identity of the risen body lies in a persisting “germ,” as the stalk in the seed

For in the same way also our bodies are to be supposed to fall into the earth like a grain; and (that germ being implanted in them which contains the bodily substance) although the bodies die, and become corrupted, and are scattered abroad, yet by the word of God, that very germ which is always safe in the substance of the body, raises them from the earth, and restores and repairs them, as the power which is in the grain of wheat, after its corruption and death, repairs and restores the grain into a body having stalk and ear.

— On First Principles II.10.3, source. The material scatters; what survives is the seminal principle of the body. On this ground Origen reads Paul's crux the way the anti-Gnostic fathers do — that a body raised “in glory, and power, and incorruptibility, has already become spiritual,” so that “flesh and blood shall not inherit the kingdom of God, nor shall corruption inherit incorruption” poses no barrier to the risen body he means (II.10.3).

Not “the same bodies” unchanged — a resurrection is a change to a higher condition

Neither we, then, nor the holy Scriptures, assert that with the same bodies, without a change to a higher condition, “shall those who were long dead arise from the earth and live again;” for in so speaking, Celsus makes a false charge against us.

— Against Celsus V.18, source. Answering the pagan Celsus, Origen presses Paul's seed-analogy: God “giveth it a body as it hath pleased Him, and to every seed his own body,” and there is sown “not that body that shall be” (V.18–19, quoting 1 Cor. 15:35–38).

He distances himself from the crude literalists, not from the Church's doctrine

Let no one, however, suspect that, in speaking as we do, we belong to those who are indeed called Christians, but who set aside the doctrine of the resurrection as it is taught in Scripture. … we, who believe that that which is “sown” is not “quickened” unless it die, and that there is sown not that body that shall be… we preserve both the doctrine of the Church of Christ and the grandeur of the divine promise.

— Against Celsus V.23, source. Origen is careful to place himself between two errors: the pagan mockery that Christians expect the same corpse revived, and the “very low and abject view of the resurrection of the body” held by simpler believers who imagine the risen flesh re-entangled with “the passions of flesh and blood” (On First Principles II.10.3).

The persisting form (eidos) — as reported by his critic Methodius

Origen therefore thinks that the same flesh will not be restored to the soul, but that the form of each, according to the appearance by which the flesh is now distinguished, shall arise stamped upon another spiritual body… it is necessary that the resurrection should be only that of the form.

— Methodius of Olympus, On the Resurrection (Aglaophon), Part III, reporting and then refuting Origen, source. Origen's own fullest statement of the eidos — the bodily form that individuates a person while its material substratum flows away and is replaced — is preserved chiefly through his opponents (Methodius here, later Jerome and the epitome in Photius), his commentary on Psalm 1 where he named the principle being lost. The report is cited honestly as hostile transmission; that Methodius takes the “form” for mere outward shape (and so mocks a resurrection of spheres) is itself part of what the dedicated argument page weighs.

Notes

  • Origen is the direct counter-pole to Tertullian: both read Paul's seed-analogy and “spiritual body,” but where Tertullian lodges identity in the same matter (changed only in glory), Origen lodges it in a persisting form, treating the material particles as a flux that need not return.
  • Whether this makes Origen a denier of “the resurrection of the flesh” — and whether he taught that risen bodies are spherical — is a contested question weighed on its own page: Did Origen deny the resurrection of the flesh? This page states his positive doctrine only and does not re-litigate the charge.
  • The transformed-body reading was carried further in the fourth century by Gregory of Nyssa and defended, against the worst forms of the charge, by Rufinus — while Jerome, Epiphanius, and the 553 anathemas pressed it in the opposite direction, condemning an Origenism that read the eidos as the abolition of the flesh.
