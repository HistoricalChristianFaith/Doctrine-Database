Cyril of Jerusalem on the resurrection of the body

Dates: c. 350 (the Catechetical Lectures delivered c. 348–350; bishop of Jerusalem c. 350–386) · Wikipedia
Summary: The mid-fourth-century catechetical witness to the same-flesh reading. Expounding the Jerusalem baptismal creed's article “the resurrection of the flesh,” Cyril devotes a whole lecture to the doctrine and states its identity plainly: the body raised is “the very same body,” only remade incorruptible — a resurrection of the flesh that lived, not of a fresh or spiritual substitute. He answers the scattered-body objection from God's power, grounds the hope in the seed of 1 Corinthians 15 and a train of nature-analogies, and ties it to Christ's own rising.

Claims & evidence

The resurrection is the “root of all good works” and a doctrine of the Catholic Church

The root of all good works is the hope of the Resurrection; for the expectation of the recompense nerves the soul to good works… Faith therefore in the Resurrection of the dead, is a great commandment and doctrine of the Holy Catholic Church; great and most necessary, though gainsaid by many, yet surely warranted by the truth. Greeks contradict it, Samaritans disbelieve it, heretics mutilate it; the contradiction is manifold, but the truth is uniform.

— Catechetical Lecture XVIII.1, source. Cyril makes the bodily hope the hinge of the moral life: because “his body shall remain to rise again,” the believer “is careful of his robe, and defiles it not.” He names the three camps that deny or distort the doctrine — pagan Greeks, Samaritans (who receive only the Law), and heretics — and answers each in turn across the lecture.

The same body is raised, only remade incorruptible — “as iron blending with fire becomes fire”

But especially mark this, how very pointedly Paul says, For this corruptible must put on incorruption, and this mortal must put on immortality. For this body shall be raised not remaining weak as now; but raised the very same body, though by putting on incorruption it shall be fashioned anew,—as iron blending with fire becomes fire, or rather as He knows how, the Lord who raises us. This body therefore shall be raised, but it shall abide not such as it now is, but an eternal body… for it shall be made spiritual, a marvellous thing, such as we cannot worthily speak of.

— Catechetical Lecture XVIII.18, source. This is Cyril's identity-of-substance formula in miniature, and it turns on Paul's own demonstratives (“this corruptible…this mortal,” 1 Cor. 15:53): it is the very same body that rises, changed in quality (“fashioned anew,” made “spiritual” and “eternal”) but not in identity. The iron-and-fire image marks the change as one of glory, not of substance — the iron is not annihilated but interpenetrated. Elsewhere he is equally blunt against the flesh-deniers: “Heed not those who say that this body is not raised; for it is raised.”

“Thou wilt be raised… to be judged with this body”

Be tender, I beseech thee, of this body, and understand that thou wilt be raised from the dead, to be judged with this body… Heed not those who say that this body is not raised; for it is raised.

— Catechetical Lecture IV.30–31, source. The earlier lecture “Of the Resurrection” ties the identity of the risen body to justice: it is this body — the one that acted, that sinned or served — that must rise to be judged. In Lecture XVIII Cyril presses the same point (XVIII.19–20): “we do nothing without the body… Since then the body has been our minister in all things, it shall also share with us in the future the fruits of the past,” so the body is to be kept pure, “nor misuse[d]… as though not our own” — a direct rebuke of the heretics who “say… that this vesture of the body belongs not to us.”

The scattered body is no obstacle to God's power

To thee, poor little feeble man, India is far from the land of the Goths, and Spain from Persia; but to God, who holds the whole earth in the hollow of His hands, all things are near at hand… Imagine a mixture of seeds of different plants… contained in thy single hand… Canst thou then separate the things in thine hand, and cannot God separate the things contained in His hand, and restore them to their proper place?

— Catechetical Lecture XVIII.2–3, source. Cyril states the era's stock objection in its sharpest form — bodies devoured by fishes, ground to powder by beasts, scattered as ash by wind, or carried off by birds to die “some in India, some in Persia, some in the land of the Goths” — and answers it, like Athenagoras before him, from divine omnipotence: the God who made the body from nothing can the more easily reassemble it, for “He who fashions that which is not, shall He not raise up that which is and is fallen?” (XVIII.9).

The resurrection read out of nature: the seed, the trees, the moon, the Phoenix

Wheat, it may be, or some other kind of grain, is sown; and when the seed has fallen, it dies and rots, and is henceforth useless for food. But that which has rotted, springs up in verdure; and though small when sown, springs up most beautiful… are then the things which were made for us quickened when they die, and do we for whom they were made, not rise again after our death?

— Catechetical Lecture XVIII.6–10, source. For the pagan “who receive[s] not what is written” Cyril argues “from the analogy of nature”: the buried grain that dies and rises (Paul's own image, 1 Cor. 15:36–38), the winter trees that “stand as if they were dead… but green in spring,” the moon that “vanishes completely… yet fills again, and is restored to its former state,” and the Phoenix “shewing forth the resurrection.” Against the Samaritans, who take only the Law, he argues instead from Moses — “I am the God of Abraham” (of the living), Aaron's budding rod, dust made flesh in Genesis 2:7 — and against believing objectors he marshals the prophets (Isaiah 26:19, Ezekiel 37, Daniel 12:2) and the raisings of Lazarus, the widow's son, and Christ himself (XVIII.11–16).

Expounding the creed: “the Resurrection of the Flesh”

The Faith which we rehearse contains in order the following, “…And in the Resurrection of the Flesh; And in Eternal Life.”… my present remarks concerning the resurrection of the dead have been made with reference to the Article “In the resurrection of the flesh.”

— Catechetical Lecture XVIII.22, source. Cyril's lecture is a catechesis on an article of the baptismal creed — the Jerusalem creed, like the Western form later expounded by Rufinus, confesses “the resurrection of the flesh” (ἀνάστασιν σαρκός), not merely “of the dead.” So the same-flesh reading is here not a private opinion but the plain sense of the word the candidates were about to profess at the font.

Notes

  • The Catechetical Lectures were delivered to baptismal candidates in Jerusalem during Lent, c. 348–350 (a year when Easter fell early — Cyril notes “the season is winter” as he speaks, XVIII.7); the resurrection is treated at length in Lecture XVIII and already broached in Lecture IV.30–31 (“Of the Resurrection”).
  • Cyril stands squarely in the identity-of-substance line with Justin, Athenagoras, Irenaeus, Tertullian, and Methodius. What he adds is the setting: not a treatise against Origen or the Gnostics but a bishop's baptismal catechesis, showing the same-flesh reading as the ordinary, creedal teaching handed to every convert — and a fuller battery of the era's stock proofs (the scattered-body objection, the nature-analogies, the Phoenix, the moon) than any earlier witness gathers in one place.
  • Like Augustine and Methodius he holds that all rise with eternal bodies but not alike: the righteous receive “a heavenly body… to hold converse with Angels,” the sinner “an eternal body, fitted to endure the penalties of sins” (XVIII.19; cf. IV.31) — the risen body's quality follows the soul's judgment.
  • He is cited in prose on the identity-of-substance argument page as a witness that the same-flesh reading is the catechetical mainstream.
