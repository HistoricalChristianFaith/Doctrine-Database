The medieval councils on the resurrection of the body

Dates: 1215 (Fourth Lateran) · 1274 (Second Lyons) · 1336 (Benedictus Deus)
Summary: Three medieval Latin texts, two conciliar and one papal, confess the bodily resurrection; the first of them defines the identity of the risen body with the present one. The Fourth Lateran Council (1215) defines that all will rise “with their own bodies which they now bear” (propriis… corporibus quae nunc gestant); the Second Council of Lyons (1274), in the profession of faith the emperor Michael VIII Palaeologus signed for the union with the Greeks proclaimed at the council, confesses that at the judgment all will appear “with their bodies” before the tribunal of Christ; and Benedict XII's Benedictus Deus (1336) fixes the sequence — the blessed see God immediately after death, but the bodily resurrection still comes at the last day. Lateran IV's wording on the identity of the risen body echoes the language Jerome and Rufinus had used.

Claims & evidence

Fourth Lateran (1215): all “will rise again with their own bodies which they now bear”

[Jesus Christ…] will come at the end of time to judge the living and the dead, and to render to each according to his works, to the reprobate as well as to the elect. All of them will rise with their own bodies, which they now wear, so as to receive according to their deserts, whether these be good or bad; for the latter perpetual punishment with the devil, for the former eternal glory with Christ.

— Fourth Lateran Council, Constitution 1, the confession of faith Firmiter credimus, source (Denzinger no. 429; DS 801). The Latin reads: qui omnes cum suis propriis resurgent corporibus quae nunc gestant, ut recipiant secundum opera sua, sive bona fuerint sive mala — “all of whom will rise again with their own bodies which they now bear.” The word propriis (“their own”) and the clause quae nunc gestant (“which they now bear”) define the identity of the risen body with this present flesh as an article of faith; Tertullian, Cyril, and Jerome had argued the same point. The confession was framed against the Cathar (Albigensian) dualists (compare Rufinus's account of the creed's word “flesh” against Valentinus and the Manicheans). The same constitution contains the council's other definitions — creation of all things from nothing, the one Church outside which there is no salvation, and the conciliar use of the word transubstantiation (see the real presence).

Second Lyons (1274): at the judgment all appear “with their bodies” before the tribunal of Christ

The same most holy Roman Church firmly believes and firmly declares that nevertheless on the day of judgment “all” men will be brought together with their bodies “before the tribunal of Christ” “to render an account” of their own deeds.

— Second Council of Lyons, the profession of faith of the emperor Michael VIII Palaeologus, source (Denzinger no. 464), citing Romans 14:10 and 2 Corinthians 5:10. Michael signed the profession for the union of the Greek and Latin churches proclaimed at the council — a union his own clergy repudiated within a few years, while the doctrinal formula was later prescribed (1385) to Greeks entering communion with Rome ⚠ unverified. The clause folds the bodily resurrection into the same articles that here define purgatory and the immediate judgment of souls: the saved cleansed of every stain are received “immediately into heaven,” those in mortal sin “immediately descend to hell,” yet all rise bodily for the last judgment.

Benedictus Deus (1336): the beatific vision precedes, but does not replace, the bodily resurrection

Since the passion and death of the Lord Jesus Christ, these souls have seen and see the divine essence with an intuitive vision and even face to face, without the mediation of any creature by way of object of vision; rather the divine essence immediately manifests itself to them, plainly, clearly and openly, and in this vision they enjoy the divine essence.

Nevertheless, on the day of judgment all men will appear with their bodies “before the judgment seat of Christ” to give an account of their personal deeds, “so that each one may receive good or evil, according to what he has done in the body” (2 Cor. 5.10).

— Benedict XII, Apostolic Constitution Benedictus Deus (1336), source (Denzinger no. 530–531; DS 1000–1002). The constitution addressed a controversy — Benedict's predecessor John XXII had preached that the saved do not see God until the general resurrection — by defining that the purified souls of the blessed enjoy the beatific vision immediately, before they take up their bodies again and before the general judgment. The constitution fixes the order: the soul's vision of God in the interval (see the intermediate state) is immediate, and all appear with their bodies at the day of judgment.

Notes

  • Earlier creeds (the Apostles', Nicene, and Athanasian, and the Eleventh Council of Toledo) also confess the resurrection of the body. Of the three texts here, Lateran IV's propriis… quae nunc gestant speaks of the risen body as this body, the reading that same-flesh writers had set against Origen's eidos (see did Origen deny the resurrection of the flesh?).
  • Fourth Lateran (1215) is also cited on other timelines — its confession Firmiter uses the word transubstantiation (see the real presence) and the annual-confession rule behind the practice of penance; Lyons II (1274) defined the Latin doctrine of purgatory. On the resurrection the three texts give definitions rather than arguments.
  • The block is dated c. 1215 (the earliest of the three) for its place on the timeline; Lyons II (1274) and Benedictus Deus (1336) are folded in as the same conciliar thread.
