The medieval councils on the resurrection of the body

Dates: 1215 (Fourth Lateran) · 1274 (Second Lyons) · 1336 (Benedictus Deus)
Summary: Between the last patristic witnesses and the scholastic synthesis, three medieval definitions raise the same-flesh reading from consensus to dogma. The Fourth Lateran Council (1215) defines that all will rise “with their own bodies which they now bear” (propriis… corporibus quae nunc gestant); the Second Council of Lyons (1274), in the profession of faith the emperor Michael VIII Palaeologus signed to seal the (short-lived) reunion with the Greeks, confesses that at the judgment all will appear “with their bodies” before the tribunal of Christ; and Benedict XII's Benedictus Deus (1336) fixes the sequence — the blessed see God immediately after death, but the bodily resurrection still comes at the last day. Together they are the creedal spine between John of Damascus and Aquinas, and they settle the identity question in the very idiom Jerome and Rufinus had fought for.

Claims & evidence

Fourth Lateran (1215): all “will rise again with their own bodies which they now bear”

[Jesus Christ…] will come at the end of time to judge the living and the dead, and to render to each according to his works, to the reprobate as well as to the elect. All of them will rise with their own bodies, which they now wear, so as to receive according to their deserts, whether these be good or bad; for the latter perpetual punishment with the devil, for the former eternal glory with Christ.

— Fourth Lateran Council, Constitution 1, the confession of faith Firmiter credimus, source (Denzinger no. 429; DS 801). The Latin is the phrase the whole later tradition quotes: qui omnes cum suis propriis resurgent corporibus quae nunc gestant, ut recipiant secundum opera sua, sive bona fuerint sive mala — “all of whom will rise again with their own bodies which they now bear.” The word propriis (“their own”) and the clause quae nunc gestant (“which they now bear”) make the identity of the risen body with this present flesh an article of faith, exactly the point Tertullian, Cyril, and Jerome had pressed. The confession was framed against the Cathar (Albigensian) dualists, who despised the material body — the same anti-dualist motive that had put the word “flesh” into the older creed (see Rufinus). The same constitution contains the council's other famous definitions — creation of all things from nothing, the one Church outside which there is no salvation, and the first conciliar use of transubstantiation (see the real presence).

Second Lyons (1274): at the judgment all appear “with their bodies” before the tribunal of Christ

The same most holy Roman Church firmly believes and firmly declares that nevertheless on the day of judgment “all” men will be brought together with their bodies “before the tribunal of Christ” “to render an account” of their own deeds.

— Second Council of Lyons, the profession of faith of the emperor Michael VIII Palaeologus, source (Denzinger no. 464), citing Romans 14:10 and 2 Corinthians 5:10. Michael signed the profession to seal the reunion of the Greek and Latin churches — a union his own clergy repudiated within a few years, but the doctrinal formula stood and was later prescribed (1385) to Greeks entering communion with Rome. The clause folds the bodily resurrection into the same articles that here define purgatory and the immediate judgment of souls: the saved cleansed of every stain are received “immediately into heaven,” those in mortal sin “immediately descend to hell,” yet all rise bodily for the last judgment. It is the same-flesh hope, now confessed by East and West together (however briefly).

Benedictus Deus (1336): the beatific vision precedes, but does not replace, the bodily resurrection

Since the passion and death of the Lord Jesus Christ, these souls have seen and see the divine essence with an intuitive vision and even face to face, without the mediation of any creature by way of object of vision; rather the divine essence immediately manifests itself to them, plainly, clearly and openly, and in this vision they enjoy the divine essence.

Nevertheless, on the day of judgment all men will appear with their bodies “before the judgment seat of Christ” to give an account of their personal deeds, “so that each one may receive good or evil, according to what he has done in the body” (2 Cor. 5.10).

— Benedict XII, Apostolic Constitution Benedictus Deus (1336), source (Denzinger no. 530–531; DS 1000–1002). The constitution settled a live controversy — Benedict's predecessor John XXII had suggested, as a private theologian, that the saved do not see God until the general resurrection — by defining that the purified souls of the blessed enjoy the beatific vision immediately, before they take up their bodies again and before the general judgment. This does not soften the bodily hope: it fixes the order. The soul's vision of God in the interval (see the intermediate state) is real and immediate, but the person is not yet complete; the resurrection of the body at the last day remains the true end, when the blessed will see God with the eyes of the risen flesh. The picture is exactly Augustine's — the interim vision awaiting the fuller joy of re-embodiment — now dogmatically fixed.

Notes

  • These are the three documents the manuals cite as the medieval conciliar witnesses to the resurrection of the body, alongside the earlier creeds (the Apostles', Nicene, and Athanasian, and the Eleventh Council of Toledo). All three sit firmly on the identity-of-substance side of the timeline's divide: the risen body is this body, remade, not a new one — the reading the fathers had defended against Origen's eidos and the Origenist controversy settled into.
  • Fourth Lateran (1215) recurs across this wiki as the council that fixed several medieval doctrines dogmatically — its confession Firmiter gave the church the word transubstantiation (see the real presence) and the annual-confession rule behind the practice of penance; Lyons II (1274) likewise fixed the Latin doctrine of purgatory. On the resurrection they add no new argument — the case was made by the fathers — but they convert the patristic consensus into conciliar definition, which is why the timeline records them.
  • The block is dated c. 1215 (the earliest of the three) for its place on the timeline; Lyons II and Benedictus Deus are folded in as the same conciliar thread carried forward to the eve of Aquinas, who systematizes precisely this defined doctrine.
