Jerome on the resurrection of the body

Dates: c. 397–400 · Wikipedia
Summary: The fiercest Latin champion of the resurrection of this same flesh against the Origenist “spiritual/transformed body.” In To Pammachius Against John of Jerusalem (Contra Ioannem Hierosolymitanum, c. 397) and Letter 84 to Pammachius and Oceanus (c. 400) — both products of the Origenist controversy — Jerome insists that the risen body keeps its very members: the same hands, feet, flesh, blood, and bones, and even the organs of sex, restored though put to no carnal use. His polemical wedge is the word itself: the Origenists will confess “the resurrection of the body” but never “of the flesh” — a slippage he says smuggles in an ethereal body under an orthodox-sounding word. He is the hinge tying this doctrine to the Origenist controversy.

Claims & evidence

“Body” is not “flesh”: the whole Origenist trick is a word

flesh is defined one way, the body another; all flesh is body, but not every body is flesh. Flesh is properly what is comprised in blood, veins, bones, and sinews. Although the body is also called flesh, yet sometimes it is designated ethereal or aërial, because it is not subject to touch and sight… A wall is a body, but is not flesh; a stone is a body, but it is not said to be flesh.

— Against John of Jerusalem 27, source. Jerome's central charge is philological. John of Jerusalem (defending Origen) confessed “the resurrection of the body” nine times in a sermon “and not even once of the flesh” — so that “the ignorant crowd” would hear flesh while “the perfect would understand that, when body was spoken of, flesh was denied.” The creed, he insists, says not “the resurrection of the dead” but concludes “with the resurrection of the flesh” (28), and the substitution of the vaguer word is the whole snare.

The same members return — the same hands, feet, flesh, blood, and bones

Similarly after the resurrection we shall have the same members which we now use, the same flesh and blood and bones, for it is not the nature of these which is condemned in Holy Scripture, but their works.

— Against John of Jerusalem 28, source. This is Jerome at his most concrete. Where Gregory of Nyssa strips away the “coats of skins” and Origen lodges identity in a persisting form, Jerome will have the buried members themselves — hands, feet, flesh, blood, bones — back in the risen body. Scripture condemns not their substance but their works; the flesh is glorified, not abolished (29: “the flesh will be glorious, but without destroying its reality”).

The members return though they are to have no office — sex without its functions

The reality of a resurrection without flesh and bones, without blood and members, is unintelligible. Where there are flesh and bones where there are blood and members, there must of necessity be diversity of sex. Where there is diversity of sex, there John is John, Mary is Mary… Likeness to the angels is promised us, that is, the blessedness of their angelic existence without flesh and sex will be bestowed on us in our flesh and with our sex… Nor will the resurrection of the members all at once seem superfluous, because they are to have no office, since, while we are still in this life, we strive not to perform the works of the members.

— Against John of Jerusalem 31, source. The famous, and to later readers startling, corollary: because the risen body keeps its members, it keeps its sex — “John is John, Mary is Mary” — and the organs of generation return, though put to no use. Jerome answers the Origenist mockery (why teeth if there is no eating, why the organs of sex if there is no marriage?) not by conceding the members away but by severing member from function: “sex can exist without the functions,” just as the continent already live in their sex without exercising it. “Who ever crowned a stone for continuing a virgin?” — the reward of chastity presupposes a body that could have sinned. This is the same move Augustine would later make (both sexes kept, “the sex of woman is not a vice, but nature”), against the Origenist and Gregorian instinct that sex is part of the mortal apparatus stripped away.

Job as the proof-text: “in my flesh shall I see God” — the shipwreck analogy

If he is not to rise again in his own sex, if he is not to have the same members which were then lying on the dunghill, if he does not open the same eyes to see God with which he was then looking at the worms, where will Job then be? You do away with what constituted Job, and give me the hollow phrase, Job shall rise again; it is as if you were to order a ship to be restored after shipwreck, and then were to refuse each particular thing of which a ship is made.

— Against John of Jerusalem 30, source. Jerome's favourite same-flesh text is Job 19:25–27 — “I know that my Redeemer liveth… and in my flesh shall I see God, Whom I shall see for myself, and my eyes shall behold, and not another.” “No one since the days of Christ speaks so openly concerning the resurrection as he did before Christ.” The reductio is the shipwrecked ship: a resurrection that keeps only the name “Job” while refusing his members and his sex has “done away with what constituted Job.” He backs it with Ezekiel's valley of dry bones (bone to bone, sinew, flesh, skin; 29), the graves opened at the crucifixion, and Christ's own handled, wound-bearing body (28, 34).

They confess the resurrection of the body as a whole, but deny the resurrection of its members

when we inquire whether the resurrection will exhibit anew the hair and the teeth, the chest and the stomach, the hands and the feet, and all the other members of the body, then no longer able to contain their mirth they burst out laughing and tell us that in that case we shall need barbers, and cakes, and doctors, and cobblers… Thus, while they maintain the resurrection of the body as a whole, they deny the resurrection of its separate members.

— Letter 84.5, to Pammachius and Oceanus, source. Letter 84 (c. 400), written after Jerome's own reputation as an Origenist had come under attack, gives the same argument in tighter, more mordant form. The Origenists, he says, hide behind the creed's very words — “We believe in the resurrection of the flesh” — but press them and they concede the resurrection “of the body as a whole” while denying “the resurrection of its separate members.” For Jerome that is no resurrection of the flesh at all. The letter is also where he half-concedes the Origenist defence's premise — that Origen affirmed the resurrection “in word” while “destroy[ing] the force of this language by other assertions” — a point weighed on the page on the charge that Origen denied the resurrection of the flesh.

“Flesh and blood cannot inherit” — the crux read as the carnal condition, not the substance

So long then as they remain mere flesh and blood, they shall not inherit the kingdom of God. But when the corruptible shall have put on incorruption, and the mortal shall have put on immortality, and the clay of the flesh shall have been made into a vessel, then that flesh which was formerly kept down by a heavy weight upon the earth, when once it has received the wings of the spirit—wings which imply its change, not its destruction—shall fly with fresh glory to heaven.

— Against John of Jerusalem 36, source. Reporting a debate with a disciple of Marcion who threw the crux at him — “Flesh and blood shall not inherit the kingdom of God” (1 Cor 15:50) — Jerome answers with the same reading Irenaeus and Methodius gave: the verse does not say the flesh “shall not rise” but that it “shall not inherit” — and only “so long as they remain mere flesh and blood,” i.e. in the carnal, corruptible condition. Once the same flesh “put[s] on incorruption,” the change is glorification, “its change, not its destruction.”

Notes

  • Both works are polemics of the Origenist controversy. Against John of Jerusalem (c. 397) answers Bishop John, a defender of Origen, whose evasive “Apology” Jerome dissects charge by charge; the fifth of the eight charges Epiphanius had pressed was that Origen “most openly denies the resurrection of the flesh and the bodily structure” (7). Letter 84 (c. 400) defends Jerome's own orthodoxy after his early debt to Origen was turned against him in the quarrel with Rufinus.
  • Jerome sits squarely on the same-substance side of this timeline, with Tertullian, Methodius, and Ambrose before him and Rufinus and Augustine just after — but he is the most insistently literal of them all on the return of the members, and the loudest on the “body”-vs.-“flesh” slippage.
  • He is a key hostile witness against Origen on this doctrine: much of what we know of Origen's eidos-body survives only through Jerome's Latin renderings of it to attack it, which is why his reliability is itself weighed on the resurrection-of-the-flesh page (“hostile, but reasonably close to the Greek where it can be checked”), and why his transformed-body opponent gets its own steelmanned page.
  • Jerome appears on this wiki also as a witness on the Old Testament canon, the perpetual virginity of Mary, and elsewhere, and as a party to the Origenist controversy and the Jerome–Augustine correspondence.
