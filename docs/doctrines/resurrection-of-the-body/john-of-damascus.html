John of Damascus on the resurrection of the body

Dates: c. 675–749 (the Exposition, c. 743) · Wikipedia
Summary: The last great father of the Greek East gathers the whole patristic tradition into the settled Byzantine statement of the hope. In An Exact Exposition of the Orthodox Faith IV.27 (“Concerning the Resurrection”) he defines resurrection as the re-union of soul and body and insists it is “this very body” — the corruptible, dissolved one — that rises incorruptible. He answers the flux/dispersal objection much as Athenagoras did, from the Creator's power and Paul's seed, and shuts the Origenist door explicitly: the change of Philippians 3:21 is “not…change into another form…but…from corruption into incorruption.” He is the eastern counterpart to Augustine's Latin synthesis, filling the long gap between the fourth-century Greek fathers and Aquinas.

Claims & evidence

The definition: resurrection is the re-union of soul and body — and it is "this very body" that rises

We believe also in the resurrection of the dead. For there will be in truth, there will be, a resurrection of the dead, and by resurrection we mean resurrection of bodies. For resurrection is the second state of that which has fallen… if they define death as the separation of soul and body, resurrection surely is the re-union of soul and body, and the second state of the living creature that has suffered dissolution and downfall. It is, then, this very body, which is corruptible and liable to dissolution, that will rise again incorruptible. For He, who made it in the beginning of the sand of the earth, does not lack the power to raise it up again after it has been dissolved again and returned to the earth from which it was taken.

— An Exact Exposition of the Orthodox Faith IV.27, source (NPNF ser. 2, vol. 9). This is the governing thesis, and it lands squarely on the same-substance side of the timeline's great divide: resurrection is by definition the raising of bodies, and specifically of “this very body…that will rise again incorruptible” — the one that decayed and returned to the earth, remade, not replaced. It is the Greek counterpart to the demonstratives Cyril and Tertullian pressed in the West.

The whole man is judged, so the whole man rises

if it is the soul alone that engages in the contests of virtue, it is also the soul alone that will receive the crown. And if it were the soul alone that revels in pleasures, it would also be the soul alone that would be justly punished. But since the soul does not pursue either virtue or vice separate from the body, both together will obtain that which is their just due.

— Exposition IV.27, source. The moral argument that runs from Athenagoras onward: because man acts as a soul-body composite, and God is just, the body must rise to share the judgment its deeds earned. A resurrection of the soul only would leave the partner in every virtue and vice unrewarded and unpunished.

The very same body, not another — the proof of Lazarus and the palpable Christ

First He raised up Lazarus, even after he had been dead four days, and was stinking. For He did not raise the soul without the body, but the body along with the soul: and not another body but the very one that was corrupt. For how could the resurrection of the dead man have been known or believed if it had not been established by his characteristic properties?

Handle Me and see, the Lord said to His own disciples when they were thinking that they saw a spirit, that it is I Myself, and that I am not changed: for a spirit hath not flesh or bones, as ye see Me have. And when He had said this He shewed them His hands and His side, and stretched them forward for Thomas to touch. Is not this sufficient to establish belief in the resurrection of bodies?

— Exposition IV.27, source. John argues the identity from the Gospel resurrections: Lazarus rose in “not another body but the very one that was corrupt” — recognizable by “his characteristic properties” (the individual idiotēs that makes a body this body) — and the risen Christ proves He is “not changed” by offering His flesh, bones, and wounds to be handled. The same handled, wound-bearing body that anchors Justin and the Latin tradition anchors the Byzantine one.

Not "change into another form," but from corruption to incorruption — against the transformed-body reading

The divine apostle, in truth, says, For our conversation is in heaven, from whence also we look for the Saviour, the Lord Jesus, Who shall change our vile body that it may be fashioned like unto His glorious body: not meaning change into another form (God forbid!), but rather the change from corruption into incorruption.

— Exposition IV.27, source. This is John's explicit closing of the door Origen had opened. He reads the “spiritual body” of 1 Corinthians 15 and the transfiguration of Philippians 3:21 exactly as the same-flesh tradition does: the difference between the buried body and the risen one is a difference of quality (corruption → incorruption), emphatically “not…change into another form.” The risen body of the Lord “passed through closed doors, was unwearying, had no need of food, or sleep, or drink” and lives “as the angels of God” — yet it is the same flesh, its powers glorified, not its substance exchanged. It is a Greek verdict against the reading a Greek father had founded, weighed on its own page.

The dispersed body answered from the Creator's power and the seed

But some one will say, How are the dead raised up? Oh, what disbelief! Oh, what folly! Will He, Who at His solitary will changed earth into body, Who commanded the little drop of seed to grow in the mother's womb and become in the end this varied and manifold organ of the body, not the rather raise up again at His solitary will that which was and is dissolved?… For that which thou sowest is not quickened except it die… But God giveth it a body as it hath pleased Him, and to every seed his own body. Behold, therefore, how the seed is buried in the furrows as in tombs… Believe, therefore, in this wise, even that the resurrection of the dead will come to pass at the divine will and sign. For He has power that is able to keep pace with His will.

— Exposition IV.27, source. The stock objection — how can a body scattered and dissolved be raised? — is met exactly as Athenagoras, Cyril, and Augustine meet it: from the divine power that already made the body from dust and grew it from a drop of seed, and from Paul's grain of wheat, which must die and be buried to rise as its own plant. The God who did the greater thing (creation from nothing) can do the lesser (re-gathering what once existed).

The settled conclusion: souls re-united to their bodies for the judgment

We shall therefore rise again, our souls being once more united with our bodies, now made incorruptible and having put off corruption, and we shall stand beside the awful judgment-seat of Christ.

— Exposition IV.27, source. The chapter closes where it began: the resurrection is the re-clothing of each soul in its own body, now incorruptible, for the final judgment. John's Exposition was the West's window onto Greek theology for centuries (translated into Latin in the twelfth century and drawn on by the scholastics), so this statement carried the eastern same-flesh consensus straight into the medieval synthesis that Aquinas would systematize.

Notes

  • John stands with the same-substance tradition without qualification — there is none of Gregory of Nyssa's “coats of skins” reserve about which features of the body return. What is stripped is corruption and the appetites of mortal life (“no longer marriage nor procreation of children”); the flesh itself, glorified, remains.
  • The Exposition of the Orthodox Faith is the third part of John's Fount of Knowledge and the first systematic summa of Greek theology; its authority in the East is roughly what Augustine's City of God and, later, Aquinas's Summa carry in the West. On this article it functions as a settled synthesis rather than a fresh argument — every point (the same body, the moral necessity, the seed, the palpable Christ) is inherited from the earlier fathers and welded into the Byzantine standard.
  • John also appears on this wiki as the classic defender of images and of the veneration of the Cross — see the veneration of images and icons and the veneration of the Cross. All three positions draw on the same Exposition.
