Rufinus of Aquileia and the creed's “resurrection of this flesh”

Dates: c. 404 · Wikipedia
Summary: The article of the creed. The baptismal creeds confess the resurrection as a rule of faith, and the older Western creed says not "the resurrection of the dead" but "the resurrection of the flesh" (carnis resurrectionem). Rufinus of Aquileia's Commentary on the Apostles' Creed (c. 404), an early full exposition of the Roman/Aquileian creed, explains why it says "flesh" — and why his own church sharpened it further, to "the resurrection of this flesh."

Claims & evidence

The two creed wordings: "flesh" (Apostles') vs. "the dead" (Nicene)

The article stands, in the Old Roman / Apostles' Creed, as the flesh:

remissionem peccatorum; carnis resurrectionem; vitam æternam. Amen. [… σαρκος ἀνάστασιν …] — "the resurrection of the flesh; the life everlasting."

— Apostles' Creed, final article, in Philip Schaff, The Creeds of Christendom II (source). The Niceno-Constantinopolitan Creed of 381, by contrast, confesses the resurrection of the dead: "expecto resurrectionem mortuorum" — προσδοκῶμεν ἀνάστασιν νεκρῶν (source). The Western baptismal creed says "flesh"; the Niceno-Constantinopolitan Creed says "the dead." Schaff's note on the creed calls "flesh" the word "by which the ancient Church protested against spiritualistic conceptions of the Gnostics" (see Notes).

Why "flesh": against those who deny it — Valentinus and the Manicheans

This last article, which affirms the Resurrection of the Flesh, concludes the sum of all perfection with succinct brevity. Although on this point also the faith of the Church is impugned, not only by Gentiles, but by heretics likewise. For Valentinus altogether denies the resurrection of the flesh, so do the Manicheans, as we shewed above.

— Rufinus, Commentary on the Apostles' Creed §41, NPNF ser. 2, vol. 3 (source). Rufinus sets the article against Valentinus and the Manicheans, who on his account deny the resurrection of the flesh. Irenaeus and Tertullian had written against Gnostic denials of the flesh's resurrection two centuries earlier.

"This flesh": the demonstrative that ties the promise to the believer's own body

to each soul will be restored, not a confused or foreign body but its own which it had when alive… And accordingly our Church, in teaching the faith instead of "the Resurrection of the flesh," as the Creed is delivered in other Churches, guardedly adds the pronoun "this"—"the resurrection of this flesh." "Of this," that is, no doubt, of the person who rehearses the Creed, making the sign of the cross upon his forehead, while he says the word, that each believer may know that his flesh, if he have kept it clean from sin, will be a vessel of honour…

— Rufinus, Commentary §43 (source). Rufinus's own church at Aquileia added the demonstrative "this" so that the confessor, signing his own forehead as he says the word, confesses the rising of the very body he is standing in — "not a confused or foreign body but its own which it had when alive." In Rufinus's exposition the numerically same flesh, not a replacement body, is what is promised.

The mechanism: an immortal principle in the flesh gathers its own scattered particles

the substance of each individual flesh, though its particles have been variously and diversely scattered, has within it an immortal principle, since it is the flesh of an immortal soul, and at the time which God in His good pleasure shall appoint, there will be collected from the earth and drawn to it, its own component particles, which will be restored to that form which death had formerly dissolved.

— Rufinus, Commentary §43 (source). Rufinus answers the standard "scattered body" objection as Athenagoras (c. 190) and Augustine (c. 426) also do: the dispersed particles are gathered back and restored to their form. For Rufinus the soul's immortality is the pledge; and Rufinus grounds the whole hope, as Paul does, on Christ's own rising — "if Christ be not risen, our preaching is vain" — "the first-fruits of them that sleep" (1 Corinthians 15).

Notes

  • Schaff's note on the creed observes that "flesh (caro, σάρξ)" was the word "by which the ancient Church protested against spiritualistic conceptions of the Gnostics," while cautioning it "may be misunderstood in a grossly materialistic sense" (Schaff, Creeds II, fn. 46).
  • Rufinus wrote in the years of the Origenist controversy, in which he and Jerome were opposing parties. The charge that Origen's eidos-doctrine emptied the resurrection of the flesh is the subject of did Origen deny the resurrection of the flesh?
  • Several witnesses on this timeline write as expositors of the creed's article: Cyril of Jerusalem (c. 350), Rufinus, and the Heidelberg Catechism (see the Reformed confessions).
