Rufinus of Aquileia and the creed's “resurrection of this flesh”

Dates: c. 404 · Wikipedia
Summary: The article of the creed. Long before the theologians systematized the doctrine, the baptismal creeds fixed it as a rule of faith — and the older Western creed says not "the resurrection of the dead" but "the resurrection of the flesh" (carnis resurrectionem). Rufinus of Aquileia's Commentary on the Apostles' Creed (c. 404), the earliest full exposition of the Roman/Aquileian creed, is the best primary witness to why the church chose "flesh" — and why his own church sharpened it further, to "the resurrection of this flesh."

Claims & evidence

The two creed wordings: "flesh" (Apostles') vs. "the dead" (Nicene)

The article stands, in the Old Roman / Apostles' Creed, as the flesh:

remissionem peccatorum; carnis resurrectionem; vitam æternam. Amen. [… σαρκος ἀνάστασιν …] — "the resurrection of the flesh; the life everlasting."

— Apostles' Creed, final article, in Philip Schaff, The Creeds of Christendom II (source). The Niceno-Constantinopolitan Creed of 381, by contrast, confesses the resurrection of the dead: "expecto resurrectionem mortuorum" — προσδοκῶμεν ἀνάστασιν νεκρῶν (source). The Latin Western tradition kept the more concrete, anti-spiritualizing word; the Greek conciliar tradition used the more general one. Both mean a bodily rising, but the "flesh" wording was a deliberate barricade.

Why "flesh": against those who deny it — Valentinus and the Manicheans

This last article, which affirms the Resurrection of the Flesh, concludes the sum of all perfection with succinct brevity. Although on this point also the faith of the Church is impugned, not only by Gentiles, but by heretics likewise. For Valentinus altogether denies the resurrection of the flesh, so do the Manicheans, as we shewed above.

— Rufinus, Commentary on the Apostles' Creed §41, NPNF ser. 2, vol. 3 (source). The word "flesh" is chosen precisely because it is the word the Gnostics and Manicheans could not say: they held salvation to belong to spirit and to release the soul from the body, so a creed that confessed the rising of the flesh ruled their teaching out at the font. This is the same anti-Gnostic front that Irenaeus and Tertullian had defended two centuries earlier, now crystallized into the baptismal rule itself.

"This flesh": the demonstrative that ties the promise to the believer's own body

to each soul will be restored, not a confused or foreign body but its own which it had when alive… And accordingly our Church, in teaching the faith instead of "the Resurrection of the flesh," as the Creed is delivered in other Churches, guardedly adds the pronoun "this"—"the resurrection of this flesh." "Of this," that is, no doubt, of the person who rehearses the Creed, making the sign of the cross upon his forehead, while he says the word, that each believer may know that his flesh, if he have kept it clean from sin, will be a vessel of honour…

— Rufinus, Commentary §43 (source). This is the sharpest point of the whole tradition. Rufinus's own church at Aquileia added the demonstrative "this" so that the confessor, signing his own forehead as he says the word, confesses the rising of the very body he is standing in — "not a confused or foreign body but its own which it had when alive." It is the liturgical form of the identity-of-substance reading: the numerically same flesh, not a replacement body, is what is promised.

The mechanism: an immortal principle in the flesh gathers its own scattered particles

the substance of each individual flesh, though its particles have been variously and diversely scattered, has within it an immortal principle, since it is the flesh of an immortal soul, and at the time which God in His good pleasure shall appoint, there will be collected from the earth and drawn to it, its own component particles, which will be restored to that form which death had formerly dissolved.

— Rufinus, Commentary §43 (source). Rufinus answers the standard "scattered body" objection just as Athenagoras and later Augustine do: the dispersed particles are gathered back and restored to their form. The soul's immortality is the pledge; and Rufinus grounds the whole hope, as Paul does, on Christ's own rising — "if Christ be not risen, our preaching is vain" — "the first-fruits of them that sleep" (1 Corinthians 15).

Notes

  • The "flesh" wording is not naive materialism. Schaff's note on the creed observes that "flesh (caro, σάρξ)" was the word "by which the ancient Church protested against spiritualistic conceptions of the Gnostics," while cautioning it "may be misunderstood in a grossly materialistic sense" — the point is the identity and reality of the risen body, not a crude reconstitution that ignores 1 Corinthians 15:50 (Schaff, Creeds II, fn. 46).
  • Rufinus wrote just as the Origenist controversy was breaking over him; his insistence on "this flesh" reads against the backdrop of the charge that Origen's eidos-doctrine emptied the resurrection of the flesh — the dispute weighed on the charge that Origen denied the resurrection of the flesh. Rufinus himself appears on this wiki as a party to the Origenist controversy with Jerome.
  • The creed article, not any one theologian, is what fixed the resurrection of the body as a non-negotiable of the faith; the theologians on this timeline are expounding an article the whole baptized church already confessed.
