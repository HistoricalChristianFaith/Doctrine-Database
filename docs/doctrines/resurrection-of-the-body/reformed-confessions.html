The Reformed confessions on the resurrection of the body

Dates: 1563 (Heidelberg Catechism) · 1646 (Westminster Confession)
Summary: The confessional documents of the Reformed churches state the same-flesh reading, which Calvin had set out, for catechesis and church order. The Heidelberg Catechism (1563), Q&A 57, confesses that "this my flesh, raised by the power of Christ, shall be reunited with my soul"; the Westminster Confession (1646), ch. 32, defines that the dead shall be raised "with the selfsame bodies, and none other."

Claims & evidence

Heidelberg Catechism (1563), Q&A 57: "this my flesh … reunited with my soul"

What comfort does the resurrection of the body offer you?
Not only shall my soul after this life immediately be taken up to Christ, my Head, but also this my flesh, raised by the power of Christ, shall be reunited with my soul and made like Christ's glorious body.

— Heidelberg Catechism, Lord's Day 22, Q&A 57, source (proof-texts: Luke 16:22; 23:43; Phil. 1:21–23; Job 19:25–26; 1 Cor. 15:20, 42–46, 54; Phil. 3:21; 1 John 3:2). The catechism expounds the Apostles' Creed's article "the resurrection of the body," and its demonstrative, "this my flesh," points to the present body, as Cyril and Calvin had pointed to it. The answer also confesses the interim: the soul is "immediately" taken up to Christ before re-embodiment (compare Benedictus Deus, 1336, on the medieval councils page, and the intermediate state).

Westminster Confession (1646), ch. 32.2: "the selfsame bodies, and none other"

At the last day, such as are found alive shall not die, but be changed: and all the dead shall be raised up, with the selfsame bodies, and none other (although with different qualities), which shall be united again to their souls forever.

— Westminster Confession of Faith, ch. 32 ("Of the State of Men after Death, and of the Resurrection of the Dead"), §2, source. The phrase "the selfsame bodies, and none other" states numerical identity; Tertullian ("in its own identity, in its absolute integrity") and Fourth Lateran ("their own bodies which they now bear," see the medieval councils) had used comparable language. The parenthesis "although with different qualities" distinguishes substance (unchanged) from quality (glorified). The next section keeps the two destinies distinct: "The bodies of the unjust shall, by the power of Christ, be raised to dishonour: the bodies of the just, by his Spirit, unto honour; and be made conformable to his own glorious body" (§3), drawing on Phil. 3:21, as John of Damascus had.

Notes

  • The block is dated c. 1563 (the earlier of the two, the Heidelberg Catechism) for its place on the timeline; the Westminster Confession (adopted by the Assembly in 1646, presented to Parliament in 1647) is folded in as the same confessional thread.
  • Both state the identity of the risen body with the present one. Like the medieval councils, they give confessional definitions rather than arguments, binding on the churches that adopted them.
  • The Westminster Confession is also cited on other timelines as a Reformed confessional standard — e.g. on the problem of evil (providence and the decree) and on divorce and remarriage (the Erasmian permission it enshrines).
