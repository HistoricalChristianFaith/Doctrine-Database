The Reformed confessions on the resurrection of the body

Dates: 1563 (Heidelberg Catechism) · 1646 (Westminster Confession)
Summary: The confessional documents of the Reformed churches codify Calvin's same-flesh reading for catechesis and church order. The Heidelberg Catechism (1563), Q&A 57, confesses that "this my flesh, raised by the power of Christ, shall be reunited with my soul"; the Westminster Confession (1646), ch. 32, defines that the dead shall be raised "with the selfsame bodies, and none other." They are the Protestant counterpart to the way the medieval councils had raised the patristic consensus into dogma.

Claims & evidence

Heidelberg Catechism (1563), Q&A 57: "this my flesh … reunited with my soul"

Q. What comfort does "the resurrection of the body" afford thee?
That not only my soul after this life shall be immediately taken up to Christ its head; but also, that this my body, being raised by the power of Christ, shall be reunited with my soul, and made like unto the glorious body of Christ.

— Heidelberg Catechism, Lord's Day 22, Q&A 57, source (proof-texts: Luke 23:43; Phil. 1:21–23; Job 19:25–26; 1 Cor. 15:20, 42–46, 54; Phil. 3:21; 1 John 3:2). The catechism expounds the Apostles' Creed's article "the resurrection of the body," and its demonstrative — "this my flesh," "this my body" — is the same finger Cyril, Jerome, and Calvin laid on the present body: the identical flesh, raised and made like Christ's glorious body, not a new one. The interim is folded in too — the soul "immediately taken up to Christ" before re-embodiment, the same sequence the intermediate state and Benedictus Deus (see the medieval councils) had fixed — but the resurrection of the body itself remains the comfort in view.

Westminster Confession (1646), ch. 32.2: "the selfsame bodies, and none other"

At the last day, such as are found alive shall not die, but be changed: and all the dead shall be raised up, with the selfsame bodies, and none other (although with different qualities), which shall be united again to their souls forever.

— Westminster Confession of Faith, ch. 32 ("Of the State of Men after Death, and of the Resurrection of the Dead"), §2, source. The phrase "the selfsame bodies, and none other" is the sharpest confessional statement of numerical identity anywhere in the tradition — the exact point Tertullian ("in its own identity, in its absolute integrity") and Fourth Lateran ("their own bodies which they now bear," see the medieval councils) had pressed, now in English. The parenthesis "although with different qualities" carries the whole patristic distinction between substance (unchanged) and quality (glorified). The next section keeps the two destinies distinct: "The bodies of the unjust shall, by the power of Christ, be raised to dishonour: the bodies of the just, by his Spirit, unto honour; and be made conformable to his own glorious body" (§3), reading Phil. 3:21 as John of Damascus and Calvin had.

Notes

  • The block is dated c. 1563 (the earlier of the two, the Heidelberg Catechism) for its place on the timeline; the Westminster Confession (adopted by the Assembly in 1646, presented to Parliament in 1647) is folded in as the same confessional thread carried forward — the mature Reformed codification of the doctrine Calvin had stated a generation before.
  • Both stand firmly on the identity-of-substance side of the timeline's divide. They add no new argument — the case was made by the fathers and sharpened by Calvin — but, like the medieval councils on the Catholic side, they convert the received reading into the binding confession of whole churches, which is why the timeline records them.
  • The Westminster Confession recurs elsewhere on this wiki as a Reformed confessional standard — e.g. on the problem of evil (providence and the decree) and on divorce and remarriage (the Erasmian permission it enshrines). The Heidelberg Catechism (1563) is the Reformed churches' most widely used catechism, one of the "Three Forms of Unity."
