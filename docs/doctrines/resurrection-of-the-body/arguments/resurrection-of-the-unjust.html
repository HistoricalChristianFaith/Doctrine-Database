Does everyone rise bodily, or only the saved? (the resurrection of the unjust)

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: sound — a general bodily resurrection of all the dead, just and unjust alike, unto a single last judgment is the plain sense of the New Testament's scope-texts (Acts 24:15; John 5:28–29; Daniel 12:2; Revelation 20:12–13), the settled creedal confession, and the near-unanimous reading of the tradition. It is well grounded and effectively undisputed: even the annihilationist camp, which contests the final fate of the risen wicked, overwhelmingly grants that they rise bodily to be judged before they are destroyed. The one genuinely-advocated denial — the minority teaching that only those "responsible" are raised, so the never-enlightened simply stay dead — reads against the texts' express universal scope ("all that are in the graves"). What is really disputed is not whether the unjust rise but what becomes of them once risen, which belongs to the final-state pages · the mainstream reading, held by Athenagoras (the wicked raised to judgment, not annihilation) and Augustine (City of God XX, the "second resurrection" of all the dead)

The claim

…there shall be a resurrection of the dead, both of the just and unjust.

— Paul before Felix, Acts 24:15 (KJV), link. The interpretation advanced here is the historic mainstream: at the last day every human being who has died is raised bodily — the wicked no less than the righteous — and only then, standing again in the body, is each brought to judgment and sent to his final state. The resurrection of the body is thus a universal event, not a privilege of the saved; the sorting of the risen into life and condemnation is a distinct, subsequent act. The timeline for this doctrine traces the nature of the risen body (is it the same flesh, or a transformed form?); this page adjudicates its scope.1

Who advances it

The universal-resurrection reading is not one theologian's thesis but the creedal mainstream. The Nicene Creed confesses simply "the resurrection of the dead" — the dead as such, undifferentiated — and the Athanasian Creed makes the scope explicit: at Christ's coming "all men shall rise again with their bodies; and shall give account of their own works," the good passing "into life everlasting, and they that have done evil into everlasting fire." On the timeline, Athenagoras already argues (c. 190) that the wicked are raised to judgment, not annihilation, since a just judgment must fall on the same soul-and-body composite that acted; and Augustine gives the reading its settling Latin form in City of God XX, distinguishing the "first resurrection" (of souls, in this life) from the "second…which is of the body" at the end of the world, a bodily raising of all the dead that "shall dismiss some into the second death, others into that life which has no death." Its later confessional heirs say the same: the Westminster Confession (already on the timeline) has "all the dead…raised up, with the selfsame bodies," the unjust to dishonour and the just to honour.2

The evidence for

Four scope-texts converge, and each of them is explicitly two-sided. The New Testament does not merely imply that the lost rise; it says so in the same breath as the saved. Paul stakes his defence on "a resurrection of the dead, both of the just and unjust" (Acts 24:15). Christ makes the scope maximal — "all that are in the graves shall hear his voice, and shall come forth" — and then, and only then, divides them: "they that have done good, unto the resurrection of life; and they that have done evil, unto the resurrection of damnation" (John 5:28–29). Daniel had already foreseen a double awakening: "many of them that sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake, some to everlasting life, and some to shame and everlasting contempt" (Daniel 12:2). And the throne-vision of Revelation gathers "the dead, small and great," from sea, death, and hell, to be "judged every man according to their works" (Revelation 20:12–13) — a judgment that presupposes the judged are raised to stand before it. The sheep-and-goats scene requires the same: "all nations" are gathered before the throne, and the sorting into "everlasting punishment" and "life eternal" is a second step after the gathering (Matthew 25:31–46). In each text the resurrection is the common antecedent; the division of destinies is the consequent. The scope is universal on the face of the words.3

The reason the tradition gives is the justice of embodied judgment. Athenagoras grounds the whole resurrection in the fact that man is "one living-being…formed from the two," soul and body, and that a just recompense must fall on the composite that acted, "not soul by itself." That argument applies to the wicked as much as to the righteous: if the body shared in the deeds, the body must share in the reckoning — so the wicked, too, must be raised in the body to be judged in it. This is why Athenagoras insists the lost are raised "to judgment, not annihilation." The same logic runs through Augustine, for whom the "second," bodily resurrection is precisely the presupposition of the "final retributive judgment" of all the dead, reading the throne-vision of Revelation 20 as the sketch of a bodily raising of "the great and small" alike. Structurally, Paul makes the resurrection in Adam / in Christ the hinge: "as in Adam all die, even so in Christ shall all be made alive" (1 Corinthians 15:22) — a text the traditional reading extends to the general raising, though its immediate horizon is the vivification of "them that are Christ's."4

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The universal scope is about as well-attested as any eschatological claim in the New Testament: four independent strands (Paul, John, Daniel, Revelation, with Matthew 25 confirming) each pair the resurrection of the wicked with that of the just, and each keeps the raising distinct from and prior to the sentencing. The creeds fix it, and the tradition is effectively unanimous from Athenagoras forward. Crucially, the scope reading survives even its natural pressure-point — the annihilationist objection — because that objection, properly stated, is not about scope at all.

Where the real dispute lies — and why it does not touch this page. The conditional-immortality tradition (weighed on its own timeline) is sometimes read as denying that the wicked rise. It does not — not in its mainstream form. Its leading voices affirm the general resurrection and locate the disagreement downstream of it: the wicked are raised bodily, judged, and only then destroyed (the "second death"). Edward Fudge frames the whole conditionalist thesis around "whatever degree and duration of conscious torment God might…impose" on the risen lost before they "become extinct" — a formula that presupposes they are raised. On this wiki's own reckoning of the shared shelf of texts, the resurrection of the unjust to judgment (John 5:29; Daniel 12:2; Acts 24:15; Matthew 25:46) is granted by the conditionalist as "God conferring existence even on the lost." So annihilationism contests the final fate of the risen wicked, not whether they rise; it is a dispute for conditional immortality and the final-state scenarios, not a rival to the universal-resurrection reading here. (The character of the wicked's risen body — whether a body raised only to be destroyed is "glorified" in any sense — is a genuine and separate question the tradition barely develops; the scope claim does not depend on settling it.)5

The one advocated denial of scope, and why it fails. The only tradition that genuinely denies a resurrection of all the dead is the Christadelphian doctrine of "resurrectional responsibility," which restricts the raising to those "responsible" — those who knew God's revealed will (or, on the narrower reading, the baptized) — so that the vast mass of humanity who never heard the gospel are "not raised at all," remaining in the dust. This is a real, historically-advocated position (it split the movement into "Amended" and "Unamended" fellowships in 1898), and it too rests its case on Acts 24:15, glossing "the just and unjust" as two classes of the responsible. But the gloss strains against the texts' express universality: John 5:28 says "all that are in the graves," Revelation 20:13 empties "the sea…death and hell" of their dead without remainder, and Matthew 25:32 gathers "all nations." To read "the unjust" as only the enlightened-unjust is to import a restriction the scope-language everywhere excludes. The mainstream reading takes the universals at face value.6

Assessment

Sound. A general bodily resurrection of all the dead, just and unjust alike, unto a single last judgment is the plain, converging sense of the New Testament scope-texts (Acts 24:15; John 5:28–29; Daniel 12:2; Revelation 20:12–13; Matthew 25:31–46), the explicit confession of the creeds, and the near-unanimous tradition from Athenagoras and Augustine onward — a mainstream reading with no serious countervailing case. Its apparent rival, annihilationism, turns out not to contest scope at all: its mainstream form affirms that the wicked rise bodily and locates its disagreement in their final fate, which is adjudicated elsewhere. The lone genuinely-advocated denial of universal scope, the Christadelphian "responsibility" limitation, reads against the texts' express universals and remains a distinct-minority view. What stays open is not the scope of the resurrection but the character of the risen body of the lost — a question the tradition leaves largely undeveloped, and which does not unsettle the article that all the dead rise. The resurrection of the body is, as the summary frames it, the hinge between the interval and the final state precisely because it is universal: the interval ends, all the dead rise, and only then is the final state entered.
