Does the "chain-consumption" (cannibalism) objection defeat the resurrection of the same body?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: weak — the objection that the same matter cannot be raised in two persons at once (flesh eaten by beasts, or by another man in famine, becomes part of a second body) is a real and ancient challenge, but it defeats only the crudest form of the doctrine — that identity consists in reassembling the numerically identical particles. Against that target it has genuine force; against the doctrine as the tradition actually held it, it fails, because the doctrine never needed the same atoms, only the same person's body. The patristic answer (assimilated matter is only "borrowed" and reverts to its first owner) blunts the objection; the scholastic answer (the identity of the risen body is secured by the returning soul, not by retained matter, so exact-particle recovery is not required) dissolves it by conceding the objection's premise while denying its conclusion · pressed by Celsus and the philosophers, and answered by Athenagoras, Augustine, and Aquinas

Related arguments: the identity of the risen body — the objection is aimed at the identity-of-substance reading and is the same flux problem the transformed-body reading presses as evidence for itself.

The claim

what kind of body is that which, after being completely corrupted, can return to its original nature, and to that self-same first condition out of which it fell into dissolution?

— the pagan critic Celsus, quoted in Origen, Against Celsus V.14, link. The advocated interpretation is that a resurrection of the numerically identical particles is impossible, and so the same-flesh doctrine fails. The sharpest form is not the mockery of a rotted corpse reassembled ("a hope…which might be cherished by worms") but the chain-consumption case: if a man's flesh is eaten — by fish, by beasts, or by another man in famine — so that his substance is assimilated into a second living body, then at the last day the same matter is claimed by two persons at once, and cannot rise in both. Either the first man's body is left incomplete, or the second's is; the particles cannot be in two places.1

Who advances it

The objection is a stock weapon of the doctrine's pagan critics, old enough that Athenagoras already treats it as the received difficulty "which seems to have exceedingly confounded some, even of those admired for wisdom." Celsus presses it in the 170s; the philosophers behind Athenagoras's treatise (c. 190) press it more precisely, tracing the eaten flesh through the food-chain — men eaten by fish, fish eaten by men, and "the devouring of offspring perpetrated by people in famine and madness," the "Median feast," and "the tragic banquet of Thyestes." Its target is the identity-of-substance reading, and it is the same material-flux problem that Origen turns the other way — making it the positive case for the transformed-body reading. So the objection is not a fringe jibe but the single hardest pressure-point on the same-flesh doctrine, felt from the second century on and never dropped: Augustine calls it, in City of God 22, "that question which seems the most difficult of all."2

The evidence for

Steelmanned, the objection is not really about cannibalism at all. The famine-cannibalism case is only the vivid extreme of a problem that holds for every body all the time. The matter of a living body is in ceaseless flux: the atoms of a man at his death are not the atoms of his youth, and over a lifetime the same particles pass in and out through food, air, and decay. Worse, matter is shared across bodies over time — the carbon and calcium in one man were, at earlier dates, in the soil, in plants, in animals, and in other human beings who died and returned to the ground. Across all of history the same finite stock of elements has cycled through countless bodies. So if the doctrine requires that each risen body be reconstituted from its own numerically identical particles, the demand is not merely hard to satisfy in the cannibal edge-case; it is systematically unsatisfiable, because a vast amount of matter has a genuine claim from more than one person. On that reading the doctrine asks for the metaphysically impossible: the same particle raised in two bodies.3

It lands cleanly on the crudest statements of the doctrine. And the doctrine was sometimes stated crudely enough to be hit. Justin speaks of God "collect[ing] again the decomposed members of the flesh"; Tertullian insists on "the same flesh and blood and bones"; Jerome demands the return of "the same members which we now use," down to the teeth and hair. Read as a claim about particles, these statements do owe the objection an answer — and the honest point in the objection's favour is that a same-flesh advocate cannot simply wave it away by asserting divine power to "gather the scattered body," because the difficulty is not that the particles are scattered (God could gather those) but that some of them are owed to two owners, and no amount of power reassembles a particle into two bodies without contradiction. To that precise form of the difficulty, a bare appeal to omnipotence is no answer, because omnipotence does not extend to the self-contradictory.4

Adversarial assessment

The first answer: assimilated matter never truly becomes the eater's own flesh. The oldest reply, Athenagoras's, denies the objection's factual premise. Not everything eaten is nourishment: "not everything which is taken for food under the pressure of outward necessity turns out to be suitable nourishment for the animal." Food against nature is corrupted, voided, or turned to "hurtful humours," and only what "has been purified by an entire digestion, and undergone a complete change for union with a particular body" is truly assimilated. Human flesh eaten in famine, on this view, is precisely the alien food that never coalesces into the eater's substance — so it never belonged to two bodies, and the dilemma never arises. Augustine reaches the same end by a different route: even flesh that is assimilated is only borrowed. "It must be looked upon as borrowed by the other person, and, like a pecuniary loan, must be returned to the lender" — the eaten flesh reverts to the man in whom it first became human flesh, and the famished eater's own lost flesh, "evaporated into the air," is restored to him by the God who can recover even that. This reply is coherent, and it dissolves the specific two-owners contradiction: on it, no particle is ever finally owned by two people, so none must rise in two bodies. Its weakness is that it still concedes the crude framing — it agrees the doctrine needs a rule assigning each particle to exactly one owner, and merely supplies the rule.5

The second answer: identity is secured by the soul, not the matter — so the premise is granted and the conclusion denied. The decisive reply refuses the crude framing outright. Aquinas holds that the risen body is numerically the same because the same rational soul returns to it — the soul, as the body's substantial form, is the principle of identity — and that a living body is already numerically one across a lifetime of total material turnover ("as fire…is called numerically one because its species persists, yet wood is consumed and new wood is applied"). If material identity is not what makes a man the same man during life, it need not be what makes his risen body his either; God need only restore matter enough "to complete the quantity due," informed by his soul. On the cannibal case Aquinas is explicit that not all of a body's matter belongs to "the truth of human nature," and that where a particle has a genuine double claim "each one will rise again in that wherein he approached nearest to the perfect participation of the…species," the shortfall in the other body made up by God from elsewhere — "nor will there be any need for that which was in one to rise in the other." This concedes the objection's whole premise — yes, the same particles cannot rise in two bodies — and denies its conclusion, because the doctrine never required the same particles. The same move is what the transformed-body reading reaches by lodging identity in a persisting form; identity by soul-or-form, not by atom-recovery, is the common solvent of the whole difficulty.6

What the objection actually shows. The two answers are not rivals so much as a weaker and a stronger form of one point: the doctrine is about the resurrection of the same person's body, not the same parcel of matter. Athenagoras and Augustine defend that by rationing the matter so no particle is doubly owned; Aquinas defends it by denying that particle-ownership was ever the issue. The stronger answer makes the weaker one unnecessary — if the soul secures identity, the elaborate bookkeeping of "borrowed" flesh is not needed at all. And crucially, the second answer does not lean on the supernatural to escape the objection: it grants the naturalist's whole point about flux and shared matter, and locates identity where personal identity is already located through ordinary life, in the persisting form rather than the changing stuff. So the objection succeeds only against a claim the mature doctrine does not make. It is a genuine defeater of "the numerically identical particles are reassembled" — and that crude claim deserves to fall — but not of "this person's own body rises, transfigured."

Assessment

Weak — as a defeater of the resurrection of the body. The chain-consumption objection is real, ancient, and, steelmanned into the general problem of material flux and matter shared across many bodies over time, genuinely fatal to one version of the doctrine: the crude claim that the risen body is the numerically identical particles reassembled. Against that target a bare appeal to omnipotence fails, because the same particle in two bodies is a contradiction, not merely a hard gathering job. But the doctrine as the tradition actually confessed it never asserted that. The patristic reply rations the matter so no particle is doubly owned (Athenagoras's un-assimilated food, Augustine's "pecuniary loan"), and the scholastic reply removes the premise entirely: the returning soul, not retained matter, secures the identity, and a body is already one and the same across total material turnover during life. Granting the objection's own premise — the same atoms cannot rise twice — the conclusion still does not follow, because exact-particle recovery was never what "the same body" required. The objection therefore refines the doctrine rather than defeating it; it is one of the reasons the tradition itself drifted from "the same matter returns" toward "the same person's body rises," which is the live question that divides the identity-of-substance and transformed-body readings. Its real payload — that material identity does not, in the end, matter to the doctrine — is a point the doctrine can grant and survive.
