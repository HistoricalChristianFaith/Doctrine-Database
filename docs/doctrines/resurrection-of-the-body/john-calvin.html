John Calvin on the resurrection of the body

Dates: c. 1559 · Wikipedia
Summary: Calvin, in the final (1559) Latin Institutes III.25 ("Of the Last Resurrection"), carries the same-flesh hope into the Reformation. The risen body is numerically this body — God will "fabricate" no new one — changed in quality but not in substance; and he refutes as "monstrous" the error of those (he names the ancient Manichees and the "spiritualist" deniers of his own day) who imagine the soul receives a new and different body.

Claims & evidence

The same flesh we now bear rises — "no new bodies are to be fabricated"

"This corruptible (says Paul) must put on incorruption, and this mortal must put on immortality," (1 Cor. 15:53.) If God formed new bodies, where would be this change of quality? … but here pointing with the finger to the bodies with which we are clothed, and promising that they shall be incorruptible, he very plainly affirms that no new bodies are to be fabricated.

— Institutes of the Christian Religion III.25.7 (Beveridge trans.), source. Calvin reads Paul's demonstratives exactly as Cyril and Jerome had: "this corruptible" is a finger laid on the body the reader now stands in, so a change of quality in this flesh, not the substitution of another, is what Scripture promises. The article rests, for Calvin, squarely on the identity-of-substance side of the timeline's divide.

The error of a new and different body is "monstrous"

Equally monstrous is the error of those who imagine that the soul, instead of resuming the body with which it is now clothed, will obtain a new and different body.

— Institutes III.25.7, source. The target is twofold: the ancient Manichees, who despised the flesh as too impure to rise (the same dualist denial Irenaeus and Tertullian had fought), and the "fanatical" spiritualizers of his own century who read the resurrection as the soul's release into a wholly new mode of being. Against both Calvin holds that the flesh is not replaced but "divested of corruption" and "clothed with incorruption" — the very body, remade.

Christ's own body is the pattern: the same body he had before, not a substitute

The mortal body which he had formerly carried he again received; for it would not have availed us much if a new body had been substituted, and that which had been offered in expiatory sacrifice been destroyed.

— Institutes III.25.7 (with the appeal to John 2:19, "Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up"), source. This is Calvin's decisive argument, the one Justin and John of Damascus had made from the palpable risen Christ: our resurrection mirrors his, and his was the raising of the identical body that hung on the cross, not the forming of a new one — else the sacrifice offered in that body would be undone. He closes the matter almost impatiently: "I am ashamed to waste so many words on so clear a matter; but my readers will kindly submit to the annoyance, in order that perverse and presumptuous minds may not be able to avail themselves of any flaw to deceive the simple" (III.25.8).

Notes

  • Calvin fills the long gap the timeline left between Aquinas (c. 1272) and the modern recovery in N. T. Wright (2003): the same-flesh reading is the unbroken Protestant inheritance too, not a peculiarly medieval or Catholic possession. The confessions of the Reformed churches then codify precisely Calvin's position — see the Reformed confessions (Heidelberg 1563, Westminster 1646).
  • Calvin appears across this wiki as a witness on several timelines — on infant baptism (the covenant defence of paedobaptism) and on the problem of evil (providence and reprobation in the same 1559 Institutes). On the resurrection he adds no new argument to the patristic case; his weight is that of the magisterial Reformation confessing, in its own idiom, the identity of this flesh raised.
  • His handling of "flesh and blood cannot inherit the kingdom of God" (1 Cor 15:50) is the same the tradition had settled on since Irenaeus: the phrase names the corruptible condition that is changed, not the substance of the flesh that is lost.
