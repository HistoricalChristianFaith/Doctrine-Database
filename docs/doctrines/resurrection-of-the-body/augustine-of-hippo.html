Augustine of Hippo on the resurrection of the body

Dates: c. 426 · Wikipedia
Summary: The settling Latin synthesis. In City of God Book 22 — the last book of the work, written c. 426–427 — Augustine takes over the identity-of-substance reading (the risen body is the same flesh, glorified into a "spiritual body") and, uniquely, presses it into a series of concrete case questions on the character of the risen body: the age all rise at, the retention of sex, the fate of bodily defects and monstrous births, the aborted and infants, the reabsorption of eaten flesh, hair and nails, and the scars of the martyrs. He answers each while repeatedly flagging where he speaks by conjecture, not certainty.

Claims & evidence

The same flesh rises, transformed into a spiritual body — still flesh, not spirit

Whatever, therefore, has been taken from the body, either during life or after death shall be restored to it, and, in conjunction with what has remained in the grave, shall rise again, transformed from the oldness of the animal body into the newness of the spiritual body, and clothed in incorruption and immortality. … The flesh shall then be spiritual, and subject to the spirit, but still flesh, not spirit, as the spirit itself, when subject to the flesh, was fleshly, but still spirit and not flesh.

— The City of God, Book XXII, ch. 21, source. Augustine reads Paul's "It is sown an animal body, it shall rise a spiritual body" exactly as Tertullian did — a change of quality, not of substance: "the same flesh shall have had that resurrection of which these words speak." He adds, honestly, that "what this spiritual body shall be and how great its grace, I fear it were but rash to pronounce, seeing that we have as yet no experience of it."

The disintegrated body is wholly reassembled — nothing is beyond the Creator's power to recall

Far be it from us to fear that the omnipotence of the Creator cannot, for the resuscitation and reanimation of our bodies, recall all the portions which have been consumed by beasts or fire, or have been dissolved into dust or ashes, or have decomposed into water, or evaporated into the air.

— The City of God, Book XXII, ch. 20, source. This is the old Athenagoras answer to the "scattered body" objection, now grounded in the definition of God as the mind that "perceives and moves all things": nothing "can either lie hid from Him who perceives all things, or irrevocably escape Him who moves all things."

The cannibalism puzzle: eaten flesh returns to its first owner, "like a pecuniary loan"

That flesh, therefore, shall be restored to the man in whom it first became human flesh. For it must be looked upon as borrowed by the other person, and, like a pecuniary loan, must be returned to the lender. His own flesh, however, which he lost by famine, shall be restored to him by Him who can recover even what has evaporated.

— The City of God, Book XXII, ch. 20, source. Augustine calls this "that question which seems the most difficult of all" — if a starving man eats another's flesh and assimilates it, whose is it at the resurrection? His answer: the borrowed matter reverts to its original owner, and God restores to the eater what famine took, recalling it from the air into which it evaporated.

All rise at the prime of life — the age of Christ, about thirty

all shall rise neither beyond nor under youth, but in that vigor and age to which we know that Christ had arrived. For even the world's wisest men have fixed the bloom of youth at about the age of thirty; and when this period has been passed, the man begins to decline towards the defective and duller period of old age.

— The City of God, Book XXII, ch. 15, source. He anchors this in Ephesians 4:13, "the measure of the age of the fullness of Christ" — carefully noting the phrase speaks not of bodily stature but of age, and offering (ch. 15–18) the alternative that the "perfect man" of that verse is really the corporate Christ, the Church grown to its fullness. Infants therefore "will not rise in that diminutive body in which they died, but shall receive by the marvellous and rapid operation of God that body which time by a slower process would have given them"; the aborted he "make[s] bold neither to affirm nor to deny," but if they rise, they rise as the infants do.

Both sexes rise: the female sex is nature, not a defect

For my part, they seem to be wiser who make no doubt that both sexes shall rise. … And the sex of woman is not a vice, but nature. It shall then indeed be superior to carnal intercourse and child-bearing; nevertheless the female members shall remain adapted not to the old uses, but to a new beauty, which, so far from provoking lust, now extinct, shall excite praise to the wisdom and clemency of God, who both made what was not and delivered from corruption what He made.

— The City of God, Book XXII, ch. 17, source. Against those who held that women rise as men, Augustine argues from Christ's answer to the Sadducees (Luke 20:35–36): the Lord denied there would be marriages in the resurrection, not women — "'They shall not be given in marriage,' which can only apply to females; 'Neither shall they marry,' which applies to males." "He, then, who created both sexes will restore both."

Bodily blemishes are removed while the substance is preserved — hair and nails redistributed, not restored to deformity

if the hair that has been cropped and the nails that have been cut would cause a deformity were they to be restored to their places, they shall not be restored; and yet no one will lose these parts at the resurrection, for they shall be changed into the same flesh, their substance being so altered as to preserve the proportion of the various parts of the body.

— The City of God, Book XXII, ch. 19, source. His governing image is the potter and the clay: as a workman can recast a deformed vessel of the same clay into a beautiful one, losing none of the material, so God removes "all deformities of the human body, whether common ones or rare and monstrous," redistributing rather than discarding the substance — "no part naturally belonging to the body can perish." "Not a hair of your head shall perish" he reads as the number, not the length, of the hairs.

The martyrs' scars are kept — as marks of honour, after the pattern of Christ's wounds

But the love we bear to the blessed martyrs causes us, I know not how, to desire to see in the heavenly kingdom the marks of the wounds which they received for the name of Christ, and possibly we shall see them. For this will not be a deformity, but a mark of honor, and will add lustre to their appearance, and a spiritual, if not a bodily beauty.

— The City of God, Book XXII, ch. 19, source. The one exception to the removal of all blemishes: the martyrs' wounds, retained "without any of the members being lost" — on the model of the risen Christ, who "allowed them to touch the marks of His wounds." "We are not to reckon or name these marks of virtue blemishes."

Notes

  • Augustine inherits the Tertullian identity-of-substance reading and gives it its Latin capstone; the whole of Book 22 assumes, against Origen, that it is the very flesh that rises — the dispute weighed on the charge that Origen denied the resurrection of the flesh.
  • The distinctive contribution is not the identity thesis but the casuistry of the risen body: age, sex, integrity, defects, reabsorbed matter, hair, nails, scars. This concrete cluster is weighed as its own question on the properties of the risen body.
  • Augustine is unusually candid about the limits of the exercise — "I make bold neither to affirm nor to deny" (abortions), "I fear it were but rash to pronounce" (the spiritual body) — and repeatedly concedes that if a reader insists each rises in the exact body he died in, "we need not spend much labor in disputing with him," provided only "there be no deformity, no infirmity, no languor, no corruption."
  • Augustine also appears on this wiki as the decisive witness on eternal conscious torment (the neighbouring City of God Book 21), and across the communion-of-the-saints cluster.
