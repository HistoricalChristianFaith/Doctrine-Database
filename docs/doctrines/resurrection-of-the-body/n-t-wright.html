N. T. Wright on the resurrection of the body

Dates: 2003 & 2008 · Wikipedia
Summary: The New Testament scholar and former Bishop of Durham who did most to recover the bodily Christian hope in the modern popular imagination — against a diffuse "going to heaven when you die." In the 800-page The Resurrection of the Son of God (2003) and its popular companion Surprised by Hope (2008) Wright argues that "resurrection" in the ancient world always meant new bodily life; that heaven is a temporary resting place, not the final destiny; and that Paul's "spiritual body" (sōma pneumatikon) means a body animated by the Spirit, not a body made of spirit. His slogan for the whole: "life after life after death."

Claims & evidence

The hope is bodily: "life after life after death"

Resurrection, in other words, means being given back one's body… It is, in fact, life after 'life after death.'

— "Jesus' Resurrection and Christian Origins," ntwrightpage.com; the theme is the central slogan of Surprised by Hope (2008). Wright distinguishes two stages of the post-mortem future: "life after death" is the intermediate state — being with Christ, "heaven" as a resting place — while "life after life after death" is the second and final stage, the raising of the body into God's renewed creation. The two must not be collapsed: the resurrection is not survival of the soul but the re-embodiment of the person after a period of being bodily dead.

"Resurrection" in the ancient world always meant new bodily life — and pagans denied it

the word anastasis refers to something that everybody knows doesn't happen… whenever the question of bodily resurrection is raised in the ancient world the answer is negative.

— "Jesus' Resurrection and Christian Origins," ntwrightpage.com. Wright's historical thesis is that "resurrection" was never a fuzzy word for the after-life in general: it denoted specifically the return to bodily life of those who had died, an event the whole pagan world agreed did not and could not happen — as Aeschylus has Apollo declare, that once a man's blood is spilt on the ground "there is no resurrection." The Jewish and then Christian claim was not an item within pagan religion but a frontal reversal of its universal "no": for Wright, resurrection "is the reversal of death, its cancellation, the destruction of its power" (The Resurrection of the Son of God, 2003).

Heaven is not the final destination; the goal is bodily resurrection into new creation

'Going to heaven when you die' is not held out in the New Testament as the main goal. The main goal is to be bodily raised into the transformed, glorious likeness of Jesus Christ… The use of the word 'heaven' to denote the ultimate goal of the redeemed… is severely misleading and does not begin to do justice to the Christian hope.

— "Rethinking the Tradition," ntwrightpage.com; the argument of Surprised by Hope. Heaven, for Wright, names the intermediate resting place — "not the final destiny for which they are bound, namely the bodily resurrection; it is a temporary resting place." The final hope is the new heavens and new earth, God's kingdom "on earth as in heaven," in which the dead are raised into renewed creation rather than removed from creation into a disembodied heaven.

Paul's "spiritual body" means a body animated by the Spirit, not made of spirit

Paul's language here, using Greek adjectives ending in –ikos, is not about the substance of which the body is composed, but about the driving force that animates it… It's the difference between, on the one hand, a ship made of steel or timber, and a ship powered by sail or steam.

— "Mind, Spirit, Soul and Body," ntwrightpage.com. Wright calls the standard rendering of 1 Corinthians 15:44–46 as "physical body" → "spiritual body" a "disastrous translation." The adjectives psychikon and pneumatikon answer the question "animated by what?", not "made of what?": the risen sōma pneumatikon is a genuinely physical body now empowered and sustained by God's Spirit — not an immaterial or ghostly body. Wright coins the term "trans-physical" for the resulting reality: transformed, but no less bodily. Read this way, Paul stands squarely in the same-substance tradition of 1 Corinthians 15 that the ancient church built on, against a modern instinct to spiritualize the hope into disembodiment.

Notes

  • Wright's polemic is against a modern disembodied piety, not against the patristic tradition: his reading of "spiritual body" lands close to Irenaeus's "flesh taken up by the Spirit" and the identity-of-substance line generally — a body animated by the Spirit, not exchanged for spirit. It is weighed as the mainstream reading on the identity-of-substance argument.
  • His –ikos argument for sōma pneumatikon is not uncontested: David Bentley Hart, among others, argues Wright over-reads a philologically thin distinction and imports a modern physicalism into Paul. The exegetical point — "animated by" vs. "made of" — is strong but not a settled consensus.
  • Wright's central diagnosis — that the New Testament hope is bodily resurrection, not the Greek immortality of the soul, and that "spiritual body" means Spirit-animated rather than immaterial — was stated a half-century earlier by Oscar Cullmann's Ingersoll Lecture (1955). Wright inherits and popularizes the thesis; Cullmann originated its sharp modern form.
  • Wright deliberately keeps the interval question (what the dead are doing now) subordinate to the body question; on the interval itself see the intermediate state, where his "temporary resting place" reading of heaven belongs.
  • Page citations here are to Wright's own essays at ntwrightpage.com, which restate the two books' theses in his words; the fuller scholarly case is in The Resurrection of the Son of God (2003).
