Tertullian on the resurrection of the body

Dates: c. 220 · Wikipedia
Summary: The fullest early treatise on the subject, and the classic statement of the identity-of-substance reading. Tertullian insists it is "the very same flesh" that rises — Paul's "spiritual body" being a change of glory, not of substance — on the ground that change is not destruction and that the justice of the judgment requires the same body that acted to be the body that is judged.

Claims & evidence

Paul's seed teaches the same flesh, changed in glory not substance

Cleave firmly then to the example, and keep it well in view, as a mirror of what happens to the flesh: believe that the very same flesh which was once sown in death will bear fruit in resurrection-life — the same in essence, only more full and perfect; not another, although reappearing in another form.

— On the Resurrection of the Flesh, ch. LII, source. On "it is sown a natural body; it is raised a spiritual body," Tertullian argues that Paul means "not to deny a community of substance, but a parity of prerogative — reducing the body to a difference of honour, not of nature"; the grain that comes up is the very grain that was sown, "changed not by abolition, but by amplification."

Change is not destruction: the same thing can be wholly changed and remain itself

A thing may undergo a complete change, and yet remain still the same thing. … So likewise changes, conversions and reformations will necessarily take place to bring about the resurrection, but the substance of the flesh will still be preserved safe.

— On the Resurrection of the Flesh, ch. LV, source. The decisive distinction against the spiritualizers: "to perish is altogether to cease to be what a thing once was, whereas to be changed is to exist in another condition." Moses' hand, Moses' shining face, the Transfiguration, Saul "changed into another man" — all are transformations that leave the substance intact.

Justice requires that the same body which acted be the body that is judged

For how absurd, and in truth how unjust, and in both respects how unworthy of God, for one substance to do the work, and another to reap the reward: that this flesh of ours should be torn by martyrdom, and another wear the crown…

— On the Resurrection of the Flesh, ch. LVI, source. The moral argument for numerical identity: if a different flesh were raised, "all the gain and fruit of the resurrection, and the permanent effect of God's judgment…would certainly fall to the ground." The risen body, though made impassible, "shall remain even after the resurrection…the same flesh too" (ch. LVII).

The flesh rises whole, in its own identity and integrity

And so the flesh shall rise again, wholly in every man, in its own identity, in its absolute integrity.

— On the Resurrection of the Flesh, ch. LXIII, source. The treatise's summary: even the flesh's every limb and characteristic returns, though freed from its former functions (chs. LX–LXII); wherever the body has been dispersed — "in waters, in fires, in birds, in beasts" — it is "in safe keeping in God's presence" until it stands forth again.

Notes

  • Tertullian's "identity of the very flesh" is the high-water mark of the same-flesh reading, pressed further than Justin or Athenagoras: he retains even the bodily members and sex, arguing their functions cease while their substance is preserved.
  • The closing chapter attributes the renewed clarity of the doctrine to "the new prophecy…from the Paraclete" — a mark of Tertullian's Montanist period; the argument for the flesh, however, is his throughout and was received by the catholic tradition.
  • His reading is the direct counter-pole to Origen's, in which identity is lodged in a persisting form rather than the same matter — weighed on whether Origen denied the resurrection of the flesh.
