Irenaeus on the resurrection of the body

Dates: c. 202 · Wikipedia
Summary: In Against Heresies Book 5 Irenaeus defends the resurrection of the flesh against the Gnostic denial. His two signature moves: the Eucharist proves the flesh is saveable (the body nourished by Christ's body and blood must rise), and the crux verse "flesh and blood cannot inherit the kingdom of God" is read to name the Spirit-less, not the bodily substance — the flesh is not what inherits but what is inherited.

Claims & evidence

The Eucharist proves the flesh is capable of resurrection

But vain in every respect are they who despise the entire dispensation of God, and disallow the salvation of the flesh… But if this indeed do not attain salvation, then neither did the Lord redeem us with His blood, nor is the cup of the Eucharist the communion of His blood, nor the bread which we break the communion of His body.

— Against Heresies V.2.2, source. The bodies that receive the Eucharist are "nourished from the body and blood of the Lord": "so also our bodies, being nourished by it, and deposited in the earth, and suffering decomposition there, shall rise at their appointed time, the Word of God granting them resurrection to the glory of God" (V.2.3).

God's power raises the mortal flesh; the flesh can partake of life

For if He does not vivify what is mortal, and does not bring back the corruptible to incorruption, He is not a God of power.

— Against Heresies V.3.2, source. Irenaeus argues (V.3.2–3) that the God who formed man from dust can far more easily "re-integrate again that which had been created and then afterwards decomposed"; and that flesh which is alive now plainly can partake of life — to deny it is like saying a sponge cannot hold water or a torch fire.

As Christ rose in the substance of flesh, so shall we

In the same manner, therefore, as Christ did rise in the substance of flesh, and pointed out to His disciples the mark of the nails and the opening in His side… so "shall He also," it is said, "raise us up by His own power."

— Against Heresies V.7.1, source. The "mortal bodies" God will "quicken" (Rom 8:11) can only be the flesh, Irenaeus argues — not the soul or the spirit, which do not die and decompose — so the resurrection is precisely of that which dies: the body.

"Flesh and blood cannot inherit the kingdom": the flesh is inherited, not excluded

Rightly therefore does the apostle declare, "Flesh and blood cannot inherit the kingdom of God"… not repudiating [by these words] the substance of flesh, but showing that into it the Spirit must be infused.

— Against Heresies V.10.2, source. This is Irenaeus's answer to the Gnostics' favourite proof-text (V.9–14). "Flesh and blood" names those "who have not the Spirit of God in themselves" — the works of the flesh that "bring death" — not the bodily substance. Strictly, he says, "the flesh does not inherit, but is inherited": as a bride is wedded rather than weds, so the flesh is "taken for an inheritance into the kingdom of God" by the life-giving Spirit (V.9.4).

Notes

  • Irenaeus's Eucharistic argument for the resurrection is one of the earliest, and it ties this doctrine to the real presence: the reality of the flesh's resurrection and the reality of Christ's flesh in the sacrament stand or fall together for him.
  • His reading of 1 Cor 15:50 is the direct answer to the crux that Paul's chapter poses, and the one the later same-flesh tradition (Tertullian) largely followed.
  • The same-flesh insistence he shares with Justin, Athenagoras, and Tertullian is what Origen's transformed-body account later strained against — the dispute weighed on whether Origen denied the resurrection of the flesh.
