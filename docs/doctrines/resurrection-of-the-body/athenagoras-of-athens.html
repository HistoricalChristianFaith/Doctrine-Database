Athenagoras of Athens on the resurrection of the body

Dates: c. 190 · Wikipedia
Summary: Author of the earliest surviving Christian treatise devoted wholly to the resurrection. He argues it from God's power and will (the God who created the body can reunite its scattered parts, and it is worthy of him to do so), meets the era's "chain-consumption" objection, and grounds the hope in the nature of man as a lasting soul-and-body composite who must rise entire to be justly judged.

Claims & evidence

The same body can be reconstituted from its dissolved elements

…that same power can reunite what is dissolved, and raise up what is prostrate, and restore the dead to life again, and put the corruptible into a state of incorruption.

— On the Resurrection of the Dead, ch. III, source. The argument from creation: the God who "made at their first formation the bodies of men" can raise them "with equal ease." He cannot be ignorant, Athenagoras insists (ch. II), of "whither each of the dissolved particles passes," and so can "unite and gather together again bodies that are dead, or even entirely dissolved into their elements, so as to constitute the same persons."

The resurrection is both within God's power and consonant with his will

…it is very evident that the resurrection of dissolved bodies is a work which the Creator can perform, and can will, and such as is worthy of Him.

— On the Resurrection of the Dead, ch. XI, source. Athenagoras structures his whole case around two questions — is the resurrection possible for God, and is it willed by him? — and answers that raising the body is neither beyond his power nor unjust nor unworthy of him (chs. X–XI).

The "chain-consumption" objection answered: alien matter never truly becomes another's body

…for the bodies that rise again are reconstituted from the parts which properly belong to them…

— On the Resurrection of the Dead, ch. VII, source. The stock pagan puzzle (chs. IV–VIII): if a body is eaten by fish, or — in famine or cannibalism — by another person, whose parts rise? Athenagoras answers physiologically: food "contrary to nature" is never assimilated into the body it enters but "wholly destroyed and carried off" as refuse, so no one's flesh ever really becomes part of another. Each person's own proper parts are therefore available to be reunited "for the exact construction and formation of the same body." (His chapter title "The Resurrection-Body Different from the Present" refers to the shedding of such superfluous, non-proper matter, not to a body of different substance.)

Man is a lasting composite of soul and body, who must rise as that composite

Man, therefore, who consists of the two parts, must continue for ever. But it is impossible for him to continue unless he rise again. For if no resurrection were to take place, the nature of men as men would not continue.

— On the Resurrection of the Dead, ch. XV, source. Because man is "one living-being…formed from the two" (soul and body), and God assigned to that composite "perpetual duration," the resurrection follows from the very nature and purpose of man's creation — and from justice, since a just judgment must fall on the composite that acted, "not soul by itself" (chs. XVIII–XX).

Notes

  • Athenagoras argues almost entirely from creation, nature, and providence rather than from Scripture — a philosophical apology aimed at educated pagans — though he cites Paul's "this corruptible…must put on incorruption" (1 Cor 15:53) at the climax of the judgment argument (ch. XVIII).
  • His raising of "the wicked…to judgment, not annihilation" also makes him an early witness on eternal conscious torment.
  • He shares the reconstitutionist mechanism with Justin; the same argument is later pressed to its limit by Tertullian and, on the transformed-body side, contested by Origen.
