Justin Martyr on the resurrection of the body

Dates: c. 165 · Wikipedia
Summary: An early and unambiguous witness to the identity-of-substance reading. In the fragments On the Resurrection Justin argues that "the resurrection is a resurrection of the flesh which died," that it rises whole and entire, that God can reconstitute the same dissolved body, and that the flesh — God's own image — is saved together with the soul.

Claims & evidence

The resurrection is of the flesh, which rises entire

The resurrection is a resurrection of the flesh which died. For the spirit dies not; the soul is in the body, and without a soul it cannot live. The body, when the soul forsakes it, is not.

— On the Resurrection, ch. X, source. Against those who "maintain the wrong opinion" and "say that there is no resurrection of the flesh," Justin makes the flesh — not the naturally-immortal soul — the very thing raised, because it is what died.

God can reconstitute the same body from its dissolved parts

For if on earth He healed the sicknesses of the flesh, and made the body whole, much more will He do this in the resurrection, so that the flesh shall rise perfect and entire.

— On the Resurrection, ch. IV, source. Justin answers the objection that the deformed would rise deformed: the same body rises, but restored to integrity. His account of the mechanism is thoroughly reconstitutionist — God, "who is Himself indestructible," can "make it anew again, and…make the same form just as it was before," gathering "the decomposed members of the flesh" back into "the same body as was formerly produced by Him" (chs. V–VI).

The flesh is God's own image, saved with the soul

For what is man but the reasonable animal composed of body and soul? Is the soul by itself man? No; but the soul of man. Would the body be called man? No, but it is called the body of man. If, then, neither of these is by itself man, but that which is made up of the two together is called man, and God has called man to life and resurrection, He has called not a part, but the whole, which is the soul and the body.

— On the Resurrection, ch. VIII, source. Because "man made in the image of God was of flesh" (ch. VII), the flesh is "a precious possession" to its Maker and cannot be left to annihilation; salvation is of the whole composite, not the soul alone.

Christ's bodily resurrection proves the body rises

Why did He rise in the flesh in which He suffered, unless to show the resurrection of the flesh? … and He let them handle Him, and showed them the prints of the nails in His hands.

— On the Resurrection, ch. IX, source. The risen Christ ate before the disciples and let them handle him, then "was taken up into heaven while they beheld" in the flesh — the pattern and pledge of the believer's own bodily resurrection.

Notes

  • These fragments are transmitted secondhand (in John of Damascus's Sacra Parallela) and the ANF editors note "these Fragments are Probably Genuine"; the reading here follows the printed text as we have it.
  • Justin's insistence that the soul is not immortal by nature also makes him a contested witness on conditional immortality — a separate question from the bodily resurrection argued here.
  • He shares the reconstitutionist mechanism with Athenagoras and the identity-of-flesh conclusion with Tertullian.
