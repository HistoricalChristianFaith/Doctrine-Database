Ambrose of Milan on the resurrection of the body

Dates: c. 378 (the funeral oration De excessu fratris II — On the Belief in the Resurrection, also called De fide resurrectionis — delivered on the seventh day after his brother Satyrus's death, c. 378; bishop of Milan 374–397) · Wikipedia
Summary: The Latin same-flesh witness one generation before Augustine, and the bridge from Tertullian's treatise to the City of God. Grieving his brother Satyrus, Ambrose turns the funeral oration into a sustained treatise On the Belief in the Resurrection. He lays out three grounds for the hope — reason, the universal analogy of nature, and the evidence that many have already risen — insists the risen flesh is the very same substance (“we shall be the same persons as we were”), reads the resurrection out of a long train of nature-analogies (the seasons, the sown seed, the vine, the Phoenix), answers the “how can scattered dust be gathered?” objection from God's creating power, and grounds the whole in Christ's own rising and the raisings He worked.

Claims & evidence

Three grounds for the resurrection: reason, the analogy of nature, and the evidence that many have risen

But belief in the resurrection is inferred most clearly on three grounds, in which all are included. These are reason, analogy from universal example, and the evidence of what has happened, since many have risen. Reason is clear. For since the whole course of our life consists in the union of body and soul, and the resurrection brings with it either the reward of good works, or the punishment of wicked ones, it is necessary that the body, whose actions are weighed, rise again. For how shall the soul be summoned to judgment without the body, when account has to be rendered of the companionship of itself and the body?

— On the Belief in the Resurrection (De excessu fratris II) 52, source. Ambrose gives the doctrine an ordered proof rather than a single argument: it is required by justice (the body acted with the soul, so the body must rise to be judged — “each should come into judgment, and each should be either given over to punishment or reserved for glory,” 88), demonstrated by the whole analogy of a nature that everywhere dies and rises, and confirmed by the fact that men have already been raised. The method — and much of the imagery — runs parallel to Cyril of Jerusalem a generation earlier.

The same flesh rises — “we shall be the same persons as we were”

And if one consider accurately, it is not the death of our being, but of evil, for being continues, it is evil that perishes. That which has been rises again… But this very thing is a proof that it is not the death of being, that we shall be the same persons as we were. And so we shall either pay the penalty of our sins, or attain to the reward of our good deeds. For the same being will rise again, now more honourable for having paid the tax of death.

— On the Belief in the Resurrection 48, source. This is Ambrose's identity-of-substance thesis: what rises is not a fresh or substitute body but “the same persons.” He presses it to the level of the material kinds — against those who deny that dry matter can revive, he argues from the fixity of seed to fixity of substance: “whatever has been sown rises again in its own nature… soft things do not spring from hard, nor hard from soft, nor is poison changed into blood; but that flesh is restored from flesh, bone from bone, blood from blood, the humours of the body from humours” (70). The end of the oration returns to Paul's demonstratives: the believer's one wish is that “this corruptible should put on incorruption, and this mortal put on immortality” (135).

The resurrection read out of nature: the seasons, the sown seed, the vine, the Phoenix

The first argument for the resurrection is the course of the world, and the condition of all things, the series of generations… the setting and rising of constellations, the ending of day and night, and their daily succession coming as it were again to life… Why should I speak of the fruits of the earth? Do they not seem to die when they fall, to rise again when they grow green once more? That which is sown rises again, that which is dead rises again, and they are formed once more into the same classes and kinds as before. The earth first gave back these fruits, in these first our nature found the pattern of the resurrection.

That bird in the country of Arabia, which is called the Phoenix, restored by the renovating juices of its flesh, after being dead comes to life again: shall we believe that men alone are not raised up again?

— On the Belief in the Resurrection 53, 59, source. Ambrose gathers the whole ancient stock of nature-proofs: the turning seasons and the rhythm of day and night, the buried grain that dies and springs green (Paul's own image, 1 Cor. 15:36–38, worked out at length in 54–57), the vine and grafted tree renewed, and — as Cyril, Tertullian, and others also do — the Phoenix of Arabia rising from its own remains. He turns the seed-image directly on the reader: “Thou too wast once seed, and thy body is the seed of that which shall rise again… It is sown a natural body, it shall rise a spiritual body” (60). He is careful to mark the fable's status — the Phoenix is known “by common report and the authority of writings,” i.e. of pagan naturalists, not of Scripture.

The scattered and devoured body is no obstacle to God's creating power

But you wonder how what has yielded to putrefaction can again become solid, how scattered particles can come together, those that are consumed be made good: you do not wonder how seeds broken up under the moist pressure of the earth grow green…

We come now to a point which much troubles the heathen, how it can be that the earth should restore those whom the sea has swallowed up, wild beasts have torn to pieces or have devoured… And if this were not so, I suppose we are to believe that it would not be difficult for God to join together what was dispersed, to unite what was scattered; God, Whom the universe obeys, to Whom the dumb elements submit and nature serves; as though it were not a greater wonder to give life to clay than to join it together.

— On the Belief in the Resurrection 55, 58, source. Ambrose states the era's stock objection in its hardest form — bodies rotted, dispersed as particles, swallowed by the sea or torn by beasts — and answers it exactly as Athenagoras and Cyril do, from the divine power the doctrine already presupposes: the God who made all things “out of nothing… whence He will and as He wills” can the more easily re-gather what already is (64), “for with God to will is to do” (114). He grants the objection is really about a part, not the whole — and dissolves even that part, closing with Revelation: “the sea gave up the dead which were in it, and hell gave up the dead which were in it… We must, then, not question how they shall rise again, whom hell gives up and the sea restores” (121).

Grounded in Christ's own rising and the raisings He worked

especially since this earlier resurrection is a pledge of that future resurrection, and a pattern of that reality Which is to come; indeed, it is rather itself truth than a pattern.

We have seen, then, how grave an offence it is not to believe the resurrection; for if we rise not again, then Christ died in vain, then Christ rose not again. For if He rose not for us, He certainly rose not at all, for He had no need to rise for Himself.

— On the Belief in the Resurrection 84, 102, source. The third ground — “the evidence of what has happened” — Ambrose fills with the Gospel raisings: Lazarus called from the tomb (77–80), the widow's son (81), Jairus's daughter (82), and the many bodies of the saints that rose when Christ died (83). These are “a visible proof… an example of the future resurrection.” He binds the whole hope to Christ as “the firstfruits of them that rest” — “the first-fruits are undoubtedly of the same nature and kind as the remaining fruits… as in Christ all die, so too in Christ shall all be made alive” (91) — so that the identity of the risen body is guaranteed by the sameness of Christ's own risen flesh.

Notes

  • Book II of De excessu fratris is Ambrose's second oration for his brother Satyrus, delivered on the seventh day after the burial; it is so fully a treatise on the doctrine that it circulated under the separate title De fide resurrectionis (“On the Belief in the Resurrection”). Book I is the funeral oration proper; Book II turns “from my brother to a general exhortation addressed to all” (2).
  • Ambrose stands squarely in the identity-of-substance line with Justin, Irenaeus, Tertullian, Methodius, and Cyril of Jerusalem, and is the immediate Latin forerunner of Augustine's City of God 22 — the bridge from Tertullian's On the Resurrection of the Flesh to the settled Western synthesis. He is cited in prose on the identity-of-substance argument page as a witness that the same-flesh reading is the fourth-century Latin mainstream.
  • Distinctive to Ambrose is the consoling frame: death is “not an evil… but even a good thing” (39), “a remedy” rather than a penalty (37–38, 47), the end of the miseries of this life — a pastoral theme he shares with the wider tradition on the intermediate state. Against the pagan alternatives he argues at length that the soul is not merely immortal (a “partial redemption” that leaves the body behind, 126) and rejects the transmigration of souls into beasts as a “ridiculous” poets' fable (65, 127–131).
  • Ambrose already appears on this wiki as a witness elsewhere (e.g. among the perpetual-virginity witnesses); this page slots his resurrection oration c. 378.
