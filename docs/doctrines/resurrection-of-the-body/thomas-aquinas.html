Thomas Aquinas on the resurrection of the body

Dates: c. 1272 · Wikipedia
Summary: The scholastic systematization. In Summa contra Gentiles IV.79–89 and the Summa Theologiae Supplement (qq. 75–86, compiled from his earlier Sentences commentary), Aquinas gives the same-flesh tradition its most rigorous form. The risen body is numerically identical to the one that died — and its identity is secured not by the return of the same particles of matter (which are in flux even during life) but by the return of the same soul, the substantial form. To this he adds the four dotes or "dowries" of the glorified body — impassibility, subtlety, agility, and clarity — and inherits from Augustine the questions of resurrection age and stature.

Claims & evidence

It must be the selfsame body — otherwise it is not resurrection but a new body

we cannot call it resurrection unless the soul return to the same body, since resurrection is a second rising, and the same thing rises that falls… And consequently if it be not the same body which the soul resumes, it will not be a resurrection, but rather the assuming of a new body.

— Summa Theologiae Suppl. q. 79 a. 1, source. Aquinas makes numerical identity a matter of the very meaning of the word: re-surrection is the same thing rising that fell. To deny it — to say the risen man is not the selfsame man — "is heretical, since it is contrary to the truth of Scripture which proclaims the resurrection" (q. 79 a. 2). The body rises "identically the same, but of a different condition, since it was mortal and will rise in immortality" (a. 1, ad 1).

The identity is secured by the soul, not by retained matter — the flux-of-matter answer

in the body of man, so long as he is alive, it is not with respect to matter that he has the same parts, but with respect to his species. In respect to matter, of course, the parts are in flux, but this is not an obstacle to his being numerically one from the beginning of his life to the end of it. An example of this can be taken from fire: While it continues to burn, it is called numerically one because its species persists, yet wood is consumed and new wood is applied.

— Summa contra Gentiles IV.81, source. This is Aquinas's decisive move on the hardest problem of the whole timeline — the same problem Gregory of Nyssa met with the flame illustration and Origen answered with a persisting eidos. Since a living body's matter is already in constant flux and yet it remains numerically one man, material continuity was never what made it "the same"; the rational soul, as the body's substantial form, is what individuates and secures identity. The soul's own act of being persists through death — "its being… is not merely in its concrete union with matter" — and "when the body is restored in the resurrection, it is returned to the same being which persisted in the soul" (IV.81). So God need not gather every particle a man ever had, only enough "to complete the quantity due."

Impassibility — the glorified body cannot suffer or be corrupted

the human body and all that it contains will be perfectly subject to the rational soul, even as the soul will be perfectly subject to God. Wherefore it will be impossible for the glorified body to be subject to any change contrary to the disposition whereby it is perfected by the soul; and consequently those bodies will be impassible.

— Summa Theologiae Suppl. q. 82 a. 1, source. The first of the four dotes. Grounded in Paul — "It is sown in corruption, it shall rise in incorruption" (1 Cor 15:42) — impassibility is the incapacity to undergo any "movement contrary to nature" (Aquinas takes the definition from John Damascene). Its root is the perfect subjection of body to soul, and of soul to God.

Subtlety — the body wholly subject to the soul, and so "spiritual," yet still palpable flesh

the aforesaid completeness by reason of which human bodies are said to be subtle will result from the dominion of the glorified soul (which is the form of the body) over the body, by reason of which dominion the glorified body is said to be "spiritual," as being wholly subject to the spirit.

— Summa Theologiae Suppl. q. 83 a. 1, source. The second dos. Crucially Aquinas rejects the idea that subtlety means the body is rarefied into something spirit-like: the risen body keeps flesh and bones and remains palpable, "for our Lord rose again with a glorified body; and yet His body was palpable, as appears from Luke 24:39" (q. 83 a. 6). "Spiritual body" (Paul's sōma pneumatikon) names a body ruled by the spirit, not a body made of spirit — the same reading N. T. Wright would later press against modern disembodiment.

Agility — the body promptly obedient to the soul as its mover

even as by the gift of subtlety the body is wholly subject to the soul as its form, whence it derives its specific being, so by the gift of agility it is subject to the soul as its mover, so that it is prompt and apt to obey the spirit in all the movements and actions of the soul.

— Summa Theologiae Suppl. q. 84 a. 1, source. The third dos: since the soul perfectly dominates the risen body as its mover, "there will be no labor in the saints' movements" (a. 1, ad 2). Where subtlety orders the body to the soul as form, agility orders it to the soul as mover.

Clarity — the overflow of the soul's glory into the body

this clarity will result from the overflow of the soul's glory into the body. For whatever is received into anything is received not according to the mode of the source whence it flows, but according to the mode of the recipient. Wherefore clarity which in the soul is spiritual is received into the body as corporeal.

— Summa Theologiae Suppl. q. 85 a. 1, source. The fourth dos, from "The just shall shine as the sun in the kingdom of their Father" (Matt 13:43): the risen body of the blessed will differ in brightness "according to the greater clarity of the soul by reason of its greater merit," as Paul says one star differs from another in glory (1 Cor 15:41).

Age and stature — all rise at the perfect youthful age, defects removed (after Augustine)

Man will rise again without any defect of human nature… human nature will be brought by the resurrection to the state of its ultimate perfection which is in the youthful age, at which the movement of growth terminates, and from which the movement of decrease begins.

— Summa Theologiae Suppl. q. 81 a. 1, source. Aquinas takes the questions of the risen body's character directly from Augustine, whom he cites by name: all rise at the "youthful age, which begins about the age of thirty years, as Augustine says (De Civ. Dei xxii)," on the strength of Ephesians 4:13; and each rises at "that quantity which would have been his at the end of his growth if nature had not erred or failed," God supplying or subtracting "what was excessive or lacking" (q. 81 a. 2).

Notes

  • Aquinas's central contribution is the hylomorphic solution to the identity problem: because the soul is the one substantial form of the body, sameness of soul (not sameness of matter) is what makes the risen body the same body. This dissolves the flux and cannibalism puzzles that had exercised Athenagoras and Augustine — God need only reunite the soul to matter sufficient to complete the body, since matter was never the principle of identity.
  • He stands squarely in the identity-of-substance line (Tertullian → Augustine) but reframes it philosophically; his reading of "spiritual body" as ruled-by-spirit-not-made-of-spirit keeps him clear of the Origenist alternative while conceding the transformation Origen was reaching for. Weighed on the identity-of-substance argument.
  • The four dotes became the standard Western catalogue of the glorified body's properties; several of the concrete determinations (the age of about thirty, the correction of every defect) descend from Augustine's City of God 22 and are weighed as their own question on the properties of the risen body.
  • Aquinas cites Augustine's De Civ. Dei xxii repeatedly — for the resurrection age (q. 81 a. 1), the retention of both sexes (q. 81 a. 3), and the martyrs' scars (q. 82 a. 1) — making explicit the Latin-synthesis lineage running from the Augustine block into the scholastic system.
