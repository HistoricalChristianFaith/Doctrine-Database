Gregory of Nyssa on the resurrection of the body

Dates: c. 380 · Wikipedia
Summary: The great Cappadocian statement, cast as a dialogue with his dying sister Macrina (On the Soul and the Resurrection, c. 380), with the physiology worked out in On the Making of Man. Gregory holds the same-substance hope — the very atoms of the dead body are reassembled — but frames the risen state not as the return of the corpse in its last condition but as the “reconstitution of our nature in its original form,” the Edenic pattern stripped of the “coats of skins.” The soul, which never wholly parts from its own matter, is what makes the reassembled body the same body.

Claims & evidence

The definition: the resurrection is "the reconstitution of our nature in its original form"

to sketch the outline of so vast a truth and to embrace it in a definition, we will say that the Resurrection is "the reconstitution of our nature in its original form." But in that form of life, of which God Himself was the Creator, it is reasonable to believe that there was neither age nor infancy nor any of the sufferings arising from our present various infirmities, nor any kind of bodily affliction whatever.

— On the Soul and the Resurrection, source. This is Gregory's governing thesis and it distinguishes him from the Latin same-flesh writers: what rises is not the body as it lay in the grave — aged, diseased, infant, mutilated — but the human being restored to the unfallen pattern God first made, "before his humanity got within reach of the assault of evil." The character of the risen body is Edenic, not a snapshot of the deathbed.

The very same atoms are reassembled — this is what "resurrection" means

all the souls in their entire number will come back out of their invisible and scattered condition into tangibility and light, the identical atoms (belonging to each soul) reassembling together in the same order as before; and this reconstitution of human life is called, in these Writings which contain God's teaching, the Resurrection, the entire movement of the atoms receiving the same term as the raising up of that which is actually prostrate on the ground.

— On the Soul and the Resurrection, source. For all his talk of a transformed, Edenic pattern, Gregory is on the same-substance side of the great divide: the scattered material of the very body that died is gathered back, "the identical atoms … in the same order as before." He is not proposing an Origenist body of new matter kept continuous by a bare form; the matter itself returns.

The soul keeps a bond with its own scattered elements and knows them again

the soul survives the dissolution of the composite body, whose scattered elements it will continue to accompany, as if watching over its property till the Resurrection, when it will clothe itself in them anew.

— On the Soul and the Resurrection (NPNF summary of the argument), source. Gregory's answer to the "scattered body" objection is that the simple, indivisible soul never wholly loses its recognizing bond with the matter it once informed — it "knows its elements amidst their kindred dust." This soul-side continuity is what makes the reconstituted body numerically the same body, and it anticipates the later scholastic solution (the soul as the form that individuates the body).

The flux objection answered by Paul's seed: the grain that dies rises as the ear

Just, then, as the "body" of the ear comes to light out of the seed, God's artistic touch of power producing it all out of that single thing, and just as it is neither entirely the same thing as that seed nor something altogether different, so … by these miracles performed on seeds you may now interpret the mystery of the Resurrection.

— On the Soul and the Resurrection, source. Gregory presses the hardest objection himself — that the living body is a flame or a stream, "always passing itself along and never remains the same," so that a resurrection of "the same" body would raise "a crowd of human beings" (infant, youth, old man at once). His answer is Paul's own 1 Corinthians 15 analogy: the grain must die and dissolve to rise as the ear, "neither entirely the same thing as that seed nor something altogether different" — sown in corruption, raised in incorruption.

The "coats of skins" — mortality and its appendages — are cast off, not raised

when we have cast off that dead unsightly tunic made from the skins of brutes and put upon us (for I take the "coats of skins" to mean that conformation belonging to a brute nature with which we were clothed when we became familiar with passionate indulgence), shall, along with the casting off of that tunic, fling from us all the belongings that were round us of that skin of a brute; and such accretions are sexual intercourse, conception, parturition, impurities, suckling, feeding, evacuation, gradual growth to full size, prime of life, old age, disease, and death.

— On the Soul and the Resurrection, source. Here Gregory diverges sharply from Augustine, who keeps sex, and even the digestive and generative members, as part of the nature God will restore. For Gregory the "coats of skins" (Genesis 3:21) are the whole apparatus of mortal, animal life — birth, growth, feeding, decay — added after the fall and stripped away in the resurrection; what rises is the Edenic form that had no need of them. The identity of substance is kept; the mortal accidents are not.

Notes

  • Gregory sits between the two camps of this timeline. On the material question he is firmly with the same-substance tradition (Tertullian, Methodius): the identical atoms are reassembled. On the character of the risen body he is closer to a restored-original-form reading — the deathbed condition is not what returns — which shares Origen's instinct that the risen state is a genuine transformation, without following Origen into lodging identity in a bare eidos apart from the matter.
  • The dialogue's frame is itself notable: Gregory reports the argument as spoken by his dying sister Macrina — "the Teacher" — whom he casts as the philosopher instructing him, on the model of Socrates and Diotima.
  • On the Making of Man (c. 379) supplies the anthropology behind the dialogue: man as a soul-body composite made in the image of God, whose resurrection is the recovery of that image. Gregory also appears on this wiki as a witness on the Old Testament canon.
  • Gregory's universal-restoration language in this same work — the healing "Fire," the final "one festival" in which even those "excluded by reason of their sin will at last be admitted" — belongs to the neighbouring question of the final state (universal restoration), not to the identity of the risen body treated here.
