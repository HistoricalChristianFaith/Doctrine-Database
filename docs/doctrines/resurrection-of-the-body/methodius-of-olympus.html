Methodius of Olympus on the resurrection of the body

Dates: c. 311 (martyred under the last persecution) · Wikipedia
Summary: The first sustained patristic rebuttal of Origen's resurrection doctrine. In his dialogue On the Resurrection (the Aglaophon) Methodius defends the identity of the same flesh: the risen body is the very body that died, remoulded and made incorruptible, not a persisting “form” stamped on a new spiritual body. He attacks the allegorizing of the flesh-deniers, re-reads the “coats of skins” as mortality rather than the body itself, and argues that a resurrection of the form is no resurrection of the flesh at all.

Claims & evidence

Against allegorizing away the flesh: the resurrection is of the flesh that died

For I cannot endure the trifling of some who shamelessly do violence to Scripture, in order that their opinion, that the resurrection is without flesh, may find support; supposing rational bones and flesh, and in different ways changing it backwards and forwards by allegorizing.

— On the Resurrection (Aglaophon), Part I.II, source. Methodius grounds the flesh in creation: Adam, before the “coats of skins,” already owns “bone of my bone and flesh of my flesh” (Gen. 2:23), and Christ confirms the literal reading of the making of male and female (Matt. 19:4–5) — so the flesh is no post-fall accretion to be shed.

The “coats of skins” signify mortality, not the body

God for this cause pronounced him mortal, and clothed him with mortality. For this is what was meant by the coats of skins, in order that, by the dissolution of the body, sin might be altogether destroyed from the very roots, that there might not be left even the smallest particle of root from which new shoots of sin might again burst forth.

— On the Resurrection I.IV (cf. the opening of I.II, “the ‘coats of skins’ are not bodies”), source. This is the counter-exegesis to the Origenist reading in which the “coats of skins” are the body itself, put on by a fallen soul. For Methodius death is God's remedy for sin, not a verdict against the flesh; God, like a craftsman recasting a marred statue, dissolves the body only to remould it “faultless” and incorruptible (I.VI).

Man is a composite of soul and body, so must rise as the composite

Now man is said to be composed of soul and body; he cannot then exist without a body, but with a body… The body then perishes not; for man is composed of soul and body.

— On the Resurrection I.XI, source. Against the claim that the risen will be “as the angels” and therefore bodiless, Methodius answers that each order of being keeps its own nature: angels do not become men, nor men angels, and man — a lasting union of soul and body — must be raised as man (I.X–XI). “As the angels” means not marrying, not fleshlessness (I.XII).

“Flesh and blood cannot inherit” names the carnal impulse, not the substance of the flesh

By flesh, he did not mean flesh itself, but the irrational impulse towards the lascivious pleasures of the soul. And therefore when he says, “Flesh and blood cannot inherit the kingdom of God,” he adds the explanation, “Neither doth corruption inherit incorruption.”

— On the Resurrection Part III.V, source. Methodius reads Paul's crux (1 Cor. 15:50) the way Irenaeus does: it is not the flesh that fails to inherit but life that inherits the body, “that the body may become the possession of incorruption, and not incorruption of the body.” He appeals explicitly to “Justin of Neapolis, a man not far removed either from the times or from the virtues of the apostles” for the same reading (III.VI).

Against Origen: a resurrection of the form without the flesh is no resurrection

Hence then, there is no resurrection of the form without the flesh. … It is, therefore, untenable to say, that the form shall arise in nowise corrupted, but that the body in which the form was stamped shall be destroyed.

— On the Resurrection Part III.XIII, XV, source. Two thrusts against Origen's eidos. First, a christological one: if the risen “form” could appear without flesh, then Moses and Elijah — who appeared in that form before Christ suffered — would already be raised, and Christ could not be “the first begotten of the dead” (Rev. 1:5). Second, a metaphysical one: the form is a quality of the flesh and cannot subsist once the flesh dissolves, “as the shape which disappears around the brass is separated from the melted statue.” He also rejects Origen's principle that “the body is not the fetter of the soul” (III.I).

Notes

  • The dialogue is named for Methodius's Origenist interlocutor Aglaophon; its full form survives partly in the original and partly in the epitome by Photius (Bibliotheca cod. 234), from which the refutation of Origen in Part III is drawn. Methodius is the earliest witness to read Origen's eidos as mere external shape — the reading that generates the “spherical bodies” caricature, examined on whether Origen denied the resurrection of the flesh.
  • His positive model is the recast statue and the remoulded potter's vessel (I.VI–VII, from Jer. 18): God dissolves the body in death to purge sin from it “by the roots,” then raises “the same parts, uninjured and immortal.” Identity is in the returning matter, remade — the antithesis of Origen's flowing substratum.
  • Methodius stands with Justin, Athenagoras, Irenaeus, and Tertullian in the identity-of-substance line, but is the first to frame it as a direct, systematic refutation of the transformed-body reading rather than a defence against Gnostic denial.
