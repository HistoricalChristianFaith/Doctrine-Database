Oscar Cullmann on the resurrection of the body

Dates: c. 1955 · Wikipedia
Summary: The Lutheran New Testament scholar (1902–1999) whose Ingersoll Lecture Immortality of the Soul or Resurrection of the Dead? The Witness of the New Testament (Harvard, 1955; published 1958) is the landmark twentieth-century statement that the New Testament hope is bodily resurrection — something entirely different from, and incompatible with, the Greek doctrine of the immortality of the soul. His diagnosis of that Greek intrusion, and his reading of death as a real enemy overcome only by God's new creative act, are the accent N. T. Wright later inherited and popularized.

Claims & evidence

Socrates dies serenely; Jesus trembles — two views of death

Like Jesus, Socrates has his disciples about him on the day of his death; but he discourses serenely with them on immortality. Jesus, a few hours before His death, trembles and begs His disciples not to leave Him alone.

— Immortality of the Soul or Resurrection of the Dead?, ch. 1 ("The Last Enemy"), Religion Online. Cullmann's famous opening: Socrates can be calm because on the Greek view the soul is immortal and death merely frees it from the body's prison — "death is the soul's great friend." Jesus recoils and is "troubled, even unto death" (Mark 14:33–34) because, on the biblical view, death is no friend but the real destruction of the whole man. The contrast is not incidental piety but a difference of doctrine: the two deaths dramatize two irreconcilable understandings of what death is.

Death is "the last enemy," not a natural release

This is really 'the last enemy' of God.

— Immortality of the Soul or Resurrection of the Dead?, ch. 1 ("The Last Enemy"), Religion Online. Cullmann takes the name from Paul: "The last enemy that shall be destroyed is death" (1 Corinthians 15:26). Death is not, as for Plato, the soul's liberation from the body but a hostile power that God must abolish — and abolishes only by raising the body, not by exempting an immortal part of the person from dying at all.

Resurrection is a new act of creation upon the whole man

Belief in the immortality of the soul is not belief in a revolutionary event. Immortality, in fact, is only a negative assertion: the soul does not die, but simply lives on. Resurrection is a positive assertion: the whole man, who has really died, is recalled to life by a new act of creation by God.

— Immortality of the Soul or Resurrection of the Dead?, ch. 1 ("The Last Enemy"), Religion Online. The person is a psychosomatic whole, not a soul that owns a body; so the person really dies, and the hope is not the survival of an undying part but God's fresh creative act raising the dead man entire. Resurrection is therefore an event — a miracle at the end — whereas immortality is merely the persistence of what never ceased.

The risen body is a "spiritual body" animated by the Holy Spirit

Wherever the Holy Spirit appears, the vanquished power of death recoils, even in the body.

— Immortality of the Soul or Resurrection of the Dead?, ch. 3 ("The First-Born from the Dead"), Religion Online. Cullmann reads Paul's sōma pneumatikon (1 Cor 15:44) as a body animated by the Holy Spirit — "a new creation, be it noted, not an immortality which the soul has always possessed." The Spirit already at work in believers is the down-payment and the power of the coming resurrection; the "spiritual body" is not an immaterial soul but the whole person re-created and quickened by God's Spirit.

The Greek immortality doctrine cannot be harmonized with the New Testament

The teaching of the great philosophers Socrates and Plato can in no way be brought into consonance with that of the New Testament. That their person, their life, and their bearing in death can none the less be honoured by Christians, the apologists of the second century have shown.

— Immortality of the Soul or Resurrection of the Dead?, Conclusion; cf. the Preface, "that is no reason for denying a radical difference between the Christian expectation of the resurrection of the dead and the Greek belief in the immortality of the soul." This is Cullmann's controversial edge: not merely that the two hopes differ, but that they are fundamentally incompatible — the doctrine of the soul's native immortality is a Greek intrusion the New Testament does not teach.

Notes

  • Cullmann's book set off a storm on publication precisely because his opposition of the two hopes is itself contested. The historic church did not simply choose resurrection over any survival of the soul: it held both a conscious intermediate state (the soul awaiting its body) and the bodily resurrection at the last day — the two are the successive stages Wright calls "life after death" and "life after life after death." Cullmann does keep an interval (the dead "sleep," yet already nearer to Christ through the Spirit), but critics charge that his flat antithesis under-reads the New Testament's own language of the soul's conscious rest with Christ. His enduring point is the one the whole timeline confirms: the centre of gravity of the Christian hope is bodily resurrection, not the soul's inherent deathlessness.
  • His reading of the "spiritual body" as a body animated by the Spirit rather than made of spirit is exactly the exegesis Wright would later press with the –ikos argument, and it lands close to Irenaeus's "flesh taken up by the Spirit" — the identity-of-substance line, against a spiritualizing of the hope into disembodiment.
  • Cullmann also appears on this wiki as the scholar behind the influential mid-century case for Peter's Roman ministry and martyrdom (Peter: Disciple, Apostle, Martyr, 1953).
