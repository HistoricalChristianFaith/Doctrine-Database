The Apostle Paul (1 Corinthians 15) on the resurrection of the body

Dates: c. AD 55 (1 Corinthians, written from Ephesus) · Wikipedia
Summary: The foundational text of the whole doctrine. Paul answers "with what body do they come?" by the analogy of the buried seed — the risen body is continuous with the body that died, yet transfigured: "sown a natural body…raised a spiritual body." Both the identity-of-substance and the transformed-body readings claim this chapter, and both must reckon with "flesh and blood cannot inherit the kingdom of God" (v. 50).

Claims & evidence

The dead are raised in a body continuous with the one sown, but transfigured

So also is the resurrection of the dead. It is sown in corruption; it is raised in incorruption: It is sown in dishonour; it is raised in glory: it is sown in weakness; it is raised in power: It is sown a natural body; it is raised a spiritual body. There is a natural body, and there is a spiritual body.

— 1 Corinthians 15:42–44, source. The four antitheses (corruption/incorruption, dishonour/glory, weakness/power, natural/spiritual) all predicate the raised body of the same subject that was sown — "it" is sown and "it" is raised — while insisting on a real transformation. The Greek contrast is sōma psychikon ("soul-ish," animate) → sōma pneumatikon ("spiritual"); the disputed question the later tradition inherits is whether "spiritual body" names a change of quality in the same flesh (Tertullian) or a change of the body's very mode of substance (Origen).

The crux: "flesh and blood cannot inherit the kingdom of God"

Now this I say, brethren, that flesh and blood cannot inherit the kingdom of God; neither doth corruption inherit incorruption.

— 1 Corinthians 15:50, source. The verse the Gnostics and later spiritualizers pressed against a fleshly resurrection, and which the fleshly-resurrection Fathers had to interpret. Irenaeus reads "flesh and blood" as a name for the unregenerate (those without the Spirit), not the bodily substance; the parallelism with "corruption…incorruption" points the same way — it is the corruptible mode, not the material body, that cannot enter.

The change is instantaneous, and swallows up death

In a moment, in the twinkling of an eye, at the last trump: for the trumpet shall sound, and the dead shall be raised incorruptible, and we shall be changed. For this corruptible must put on incorruption, and this mortal must put on immortality.

— 1 Corinthians 15:52–53, source. "This corruptible" and "this mortal" — the demonstratives point to the present body as the very thing that "puts on" incorruption, a phrase the same-flesh tradition (Tertullian, Irenaeus) leans on heavily: it is this body that is clothed anew, not replaced.

Notes

  • 1 Corinthians 15 is the single most-cited proof-text on this timeline: Justin, Irenaeus, and Tertullian all argue from the seed analogy, and Origen builds his transformed-body account on it as well.
  • The chapter also anchors a separate eschatological thread — "that God may be all in all" (15:28) — weighed under universal restoration.
  • Distinguish this doctrine (the nature of the risen body) from the intermediate state (the soul in the interval before the resurrection).
