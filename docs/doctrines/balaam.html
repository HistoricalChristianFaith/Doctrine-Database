Who Was Balaam son of Beor?

Balaam appears in three chapters of the Hebrew Bible (Numbers 22–24) as a seer fetched from Mesopotamia to curse Israel who, overruled by the God of Israel, blesses her four times instead and utters the oracle “there shall come a Star out of Jacob” — a verse that Christian writers on this timeline read alongside the star of the magi. Other passages of the same Scriptures remember him differently: it was “the counsel of Balaam” that led Israel into the sin of Peor (Numbers 31:16), he dies by Israel’s sword as a “soothsayer” (Joshua 13:22), and in the New Testament his name stands for false teaching — “the way of Balaam,” “the error of Balaam,” “the doctrine of Balaam” — and for one who “loved the wages of unrighteousness.” Was he a true prophet or a diviner, a villain or a reluctant instrument of grace? Where was “Pethor by the river,” and is he the Balaam whose name surfaced in 1967 on a plaster wall at Deir ‘Alla in Jordan? How did Jewish, Christian and Muslim writers remember him — the Mishnah, which denies him a share in the world to come, the Fathers who read his oracles of Christ, the legend that made him the magi’s ancestor?

This page is a biographical tradition-timeline: not a life of Balaam so much as a history of what was said and believed about him across the centuries, set out earliest extant source first — the Torah portrait and its Old Testament echoes, the New Testament’s three references, the extra-biblical inscription that names a Balaam son of Beor, and the retellings of Philo, Josephus, the rabbis, the Targums, the Fathers, the Islamic commentators and the Reformation preachers, down to modern critical scholarship. Its “witnesses” are the datable sources that report on or interpret Balaam. The disputed questions — who the Nicolaitans are, why 2 Peter calls him “son of Bosor,” how old the star oracle is, whether Numbers 22–24 is a composite, whether “Balaam” is a rabbinic cipher for Jesus — are set out reading by reading under Disputed readings.

Timeline

The Hebrew Bible (the Torah portrait and its echoes)

The later tradition draws on a single episode and a handful of backward glances at it. In Numbers 22–24, Balak king of Moab, dreading Israel encamped on his border, sends for “Balaam the son of Beor to Pethor, which is by the river” — Balaam himself names his home as “Aram, out of the mountains of the east” (Numbers 23:7) — to curse the people for pay. Warned by God, thwarted by his own speaking donkey and the angel in the road, Balaam can only bless: in four oracles he pronounces Israel un-cursable and foretells a ruler, “there shall come a Star out of Jacob, and a Sceptre shall rise out of Israel.”1 Other passages remember him otherwise. Numbers lays the apostasy of Baal-Peor at his door — “these caused the children of Israel, through the counsel of Balaam, to commit trespass against the LORD in the matter of Peor” — and reports him killed in Israel’s war on Midian; Joshua calls him “Balaam… the son of Beor, the soothsayer” (a diviner, qosem), slain with the sword; and Deuteronomy, Nehemiah, and Micah recall that Moab hired him to curse and that God “turned the curse into a blessing.”2 The Old Testament thus gives two portraits side by side — the speaker of the oracles and the counsellor of Peor — and the later writers on this timeline take up both.

The New Testament (three references)

The three New Testament references to Balaam all come in warnings against false teachers. 2 Peter describes them as “following the way of Balaam the son of Bosor, who loved the wages of unrighteousness” — rebuked by “the dumb ass speaking with man’s voice”; Jude sets them among those who “ran greedily after the error of Balaam for reward”; and in Revelation the letter to Pergamum, spoken in the voice of the risen Christ, charges the church with harbouring “them that hold the doctrine of Balaam, who taught Balac to cast a stumblingblock before the children of Israel, to eat things sacrificed unto idols, and to commit fornication.”3 The three phrases — the way, the error, and the doctrine of Balaam — present him as a prophet for hire and as a teacher who leads God’s people into idolatry and immorality; none of the three passages mentions his oracles of blessing.

The Deir ‘Alla plaster inscription (c. 800 BC) — the earliest extant non-biblical text naming a Balaam son of Beor

The oldest datable source outside the Bible to name a Balaam figure is not a text on parchment but a wall. Excavated in 1967 at Tell Deir ‘Alla in the eastern Jordan Valley — in the Transjordan region where Numbers sets the episode — a plaster inscription written in black and red ink, dated by its destruction layer to about 800 BC, opens as “the book of Balaam son of Beor… a seer of the gods,” giving the same name and patronymic as Numbers 22–24. It tells a different story: a night-vision in which the gods come to Balaam and a divine council of the Shaddayin resolves to darken the heavens, leaving him weeping and fasting — nothing of Balak, the donkey, or the blessing of Israel. Its Balaam is a visionary of a polytheistic council, yet it shares a term with Balaam’s biblical diction: the plural Shaddayin answers the Shadday (“the Almighty”) whose vision the seer receives in his oracles (Numbers 24:4, 16). Dated independently of the biblical text, it is the earliest witness on this timeline by the age of its evidence; it does not narrate the Numbers episode.4 Full detail »

Philo of Alexandria (Life of Moses I, c. AD 40) — the magos seized by the prophetic spirit

The earliest extant source on this timeline to retell and interpret the episode at length is the Alexandrian Jewish philosopher Philo (c. 20 BC – AD 50), whose Life of Moses I.263–304 retells it for a Greek readership. Philo casts Balaam as a magos — a Mesopotamian “magician” and master of “every branch of the soothsayers’ art” — who is seized by a prophetic spirit that “drove all his artificial system of divination and cunning out of his soul; for it was not possible that holy inspiration should dwell in the same abode with magic.” So in Philo the oracle comes not through his magic but by displacing it: he blesses Israel “without at all understanding the words which he uttered,” overruled against a will that “still desired… to curse this people.” Philo reads the star oracle as a coming king — “a man shall hereafter come forth out of thee who shall rule over many nations” — and dramatises the bare notice of Numbers 31:16 into a full scene in which Balaam, of his “own mind,” counsels Balak to seduce the Hebrews through the beauty of the Moabite women, the episode Revelation 2:14 calls “the doctrine of Balaam.” Raymond E. Brown discusses Philo’s magos label in connection with the wise men of Matthew 2.5 Full detail »

Pseudo-Philo (Biblical Antiquities 18, c. AD 70) — the prophet who foretells his own ruin

Between Philo and Josephus stands an anonymous first-century Jewish retelling, the Liber Antiquitatum Biblicarum (“Biblical Antiquities”), later mis-attributed to Philo and so credited to “Pseudo-Philo.” Its chapter 18 gives Balaam an inner voice. This Balaam is a seer who knows from the outset that his gift is only a loan — “the spirit which is given unto us is given for a time” — and who, even as he blesses Israel, narrates his own damnation: “I shall gnash my teeth because I was deceived and did transgress… for when I cursed I perished, and though I blessed I was not blessed.” Pseudo-Philo also addresses the episode’s question of free will and God’s permission: God frames the affair as a test of the human will (“Wherefore, Lord, dost thou tempt the race of man?”), permits the journey while branding it a sin (“Go with them, and thy journey shall be an offence”), and grounds Israel’s un-cursability not in Sinai but in the merit of Abraham’s binding of Isaac (“for the blood of him did I choose this people”). He mutes the speaking donkey to a silent one that merely sees the angel, and — like Josephus, and unlike Philo — drops the “star out of Jacob” entirely. With both of them he unpacks the Torah’s bare “counsel of Balaam” into a full scene of seduction by the women of Midian (“Choose out the most comely women… for otherwise thou canst not subdue them”), so that all three first-century Jewish sources on this timeline expand that counsel into the scene Revelation calls “the doctrine of Balaam.”6 Full detail »

Josephus (Antiquities of the Jews IV, c. AD 93) — a first-century historian’s retelling

A half-century after Philo, the priest-historian Flavius Josephus (c. AD 37 – c. 100) gives a detailed first-century narrative of Balaam, in Antiquities of the Jews IV.6. He calls him “the greatest of the Prophets at that time,” a seer whom God “had raised… to great reputation on account of the truth of his predictions” — and retells the speaking ass and the angel, expanding Israel’s forced blessing into a sweeping oration of her future glory. He does not reproduce the star: where Philo read the fourth oracle as a coming king, Josephus gives neither star nor sceptre of Numbers 24:17, reducing the prophecy to a notice that Balaam “foretold what calamities would befal the several Kings of the nations.” He stages Numbers 31:16 as a farewell scene: rebuffed and unrewarded, Balaam turns back and, unbidden, counsels Balak and the Midianites to send their most beautiful women to seduce the Hebrew youths into idolatry, “for by this means God will be angry at them.” He closes by noting that Moses honoured Balaam by setting down his prophecies in writing rather than claiming them as his own.7 Full detail »

The Aramaic Targums (Onkelos, Neofiti, Pseudo-Jonathan; redacted c. 2nd–8th c. AD) — the synagogue’s Balaam

After the first-century retellings comes not a single author but a whole reception stratum: the Aramaic Targums, the running paraphrases read alongside the Hebrew in the synagogue, whose written redaction spans several centuries (Onkelos the earliest, Pseudo-Jonathan the latest). They do two things to Balaam at once. First they read his fourth oracle messianically: where the Hebrew of Numbers 24:17 has “there shall come a Star out of Jacob, and a Sceptre shall rise out of Israel,” all three Torah Targums turn the star and sceptre into a king, and two of them (Onkelos, Pseudo-Jonathan) name him the Messiah — Onkelos, “when a king shall arise out of Jacob, and the Meshiha be anointed from Israel”; Neofiti, “a king… a redeemer and ruler from the house of Israel”; and Pseudo-Jonathan expanding it into the end-time war in which the Messiah destroys “the armies of Gog.” The reading appears in both the Babylonian (Onkelos) and the Palestinian (Neofiti, Pseudo-Jonathan) Targums; the verse is also read of a coming figure at Qumran (4Q175; CD 7:18–21) and in the name of the Bar Kokhba (“son of the star”) revolt. Second, the same paraphrases portray Balaam as a villain: Pseudo-Jonathan identifies him with Laban the Aramean (Jacob’s old enemy), calls him mad “from the vastness of his knowledge,” and stages his death as a duel of sorceries — Balaam flies off “by his magical arts” only to be run down and beheaded by Phinehas, who invokes “the Great and Holy Name,” for the counsel that killed twenty-four thousand. The Targums that name the Messiah in his oracle thus portray its speaker as a sorcerer.8 Full detail »

The rabbis (the Mishnah, c. AD 200, to the Babylonian Talmud, c. AD 500) — “Balaam the wicked”

Interwoven with the Targums, and reaching back to the same centuries, is the other synagogue stratum: the classical rabbinic literature, in which Balaam is a type of the wicked gentile. The Mishnah (redacted c. AD 200) names him first among the four commoners who “have no share in the World-to-Come” — at the head of the Mishnah’s short list — and the tractate Avot coins “the disciples of Balaam the wicked” (an evil eye, a haughty spirit, a limitless appetite) as the antitype of “the disciples of Abraham,” the one bound for Gehinnom and “the nethermost pit.” The long Talmudic sugya at Sanhedrin 105a–106b (c. AD 500) adds legendary material: a name-midrash reading “Balaam” as belo ‘am (“without a people”) or billa‘ ‘am (“he wore down the people”); a seer lame in one leg and blind in one eye; the planner of the seduction at Peor (the “enclosures… and prostitutes” scheme that dramatises “the counsel of Balaam,” Numbers 31:16); and a “lame” sorcerer of thirty-three cut down by Phinehas. The tannaitic Sifrei, glossing “there arose not a prophet since in Israel like unto Moses” (Deuteronomy 34:10), states that one did arise among the nations — Balaam — and ranks his prophecy in several respects above Moses’ own. These sources thus set his prophetic gift beside his exclusion from the World-to-Come, and share with the Targums both the messianic star oracle and the villain-portrait (the identification with Laban the Aramean, the death at the hands of Phinehas).9 Full detail »

The Catacomb of Priscilla fresco (Rome, c. 230–240) — a figure pointing to a star, beside a Virgin and Child

The earliest Christian image of Balaam on this timeline is a painting. On the plastered ceiling of a burial niche in the Catacomb of Priscilla on the Via Salaria in Rome, a fresco of the early third century — dated on style and position to about 230–240 (some place it late-second to early-third century) — shows a seated woman nursing an infant while a standing man in a philosopher’s pallium points up to a star above them. In the identification reported by Richard Stracke and Victoria Emily Jones, the pointing figure is Balaam, gesturing to the “Star out of Jacob” (Numbers 24:17), the oracle that Justin Martyr, Irenaeus, and, just below on this timeline, Origen read of Christ’s birth. Stracke and Jones read the image’s sense as christological, since it predates the Council of Ephesus: the prophecy of the star drawn beside the newborn king on his mother’s knee. On that identification it is the earliest datable image of Balaam, and Stracke and Jones describe it as the earliest image of the Virgin and Child independent of the Adoration of the Magi, gathering the Balaam, magi, and Marian threads into a single third-century picture. Jones judges the figure “most likely” Balaam; Isaiah (the prophet of “a virgin shall conceive”), a magus, and John the Baptist have also been proposed, and Pasquale Testini reads the figure as a personification of prophecy.10 Full detail »

Origen of Alexandria (the Commentary on John, the Homilies on Numbers, and Against Celsus, c. 240–248) — a diviner who prophesied

Writing at Caesarea roughly a generation after the Mishnah, amid the earliest Targumic traditions and more than two centuries before the Talmud’s Balaam sugya took its final shape, Origen is the earliest extant Christian author on this timeline to give Balaam a sustained reading, and he takes up both of the Torah’s portraits. He addresses the puzzle of how a pagan diviner could utter true prophecy, up to and including the messianic “star out of Jacob.” Starting from the high priest Caiaphas, who “prophesied” Christ’s saving death though he “did not say this on his own authority” (John 11:51), Origen draws a distinction: prophesying is not the same as being a prophet. “If anyone is a prophet, he certainly prophesies; but if anyone prophesies, he is not necessarily a prophet” — and Balaam “was not a prophet; for he is recorded as being a diviner.” The true word he spoke was, for Origen, not the fruit of an indwelling Holy Spirit but was supplied from outside him: put in his mouth by the angel of the road, or forced out by the circumstance of seeing Israel encamped — so that “even a wicked soul sometimes receives the gift of prophecy” without being made holy or a prophet. As a textual critic Origen athetized (marked with an obelus) the clause “and the spirit of God came upon him” where the Septuagint has it in Numbers 23. So where Philo had the prophetic spirit displace Balaam’s magic and Josephus called him “the greatest of the Prophets,” Origen withholds from him the office — a diviner throughout, past whom God speaks. He admires the donkey (“I marvel at Balaam’s ass… worthy… to see the angel of God”), and he takes up the star oracle: in Against Celsus and the Homilies on Numbers he makes the magi Balaam’s own “school” and “seed” in the East, custodians of his star-prophecy who read it fulfilled at Bethlehem.11 Full detail »

Ephrem the Syrian (the Hymns on the Nativity and The Pearl, c. 373) — the star Balaam spoke of, risen at Bethlehem

Ephrem takes up Balaam in hymns rather than commentary. His first hymn on the Nativity sets the star oracle (Numbers 24:17) in a roll of prophecies fulfilled on the feast, after Isaiah, David and Micah: “Lo! from Jacob shone the Star, and from Israel rose the Head. The prophecy that Balaam spake had its interpreting to-day!” A later hymn of the cycle pairs the foreign seer with the foreigners who saw the star: “that vision whereof Balaam spake; a stranger he who spread abroad concerning it,-strangers they who saw it.” In The Pearl, a cycle of hymns on the Faith, the speaking ass is a rebuke: “Because Balaam was foolish, a foolish beast in the ass spoke with him, because he despised God Who spoke with him.”12 Full detail »

Ambrose of Milan (the sermons and treatises of his episcopate, c. 378–397) — a covetous seer who prophesied Christ

The earliest Latin churchman on this timeline to dwell on Balaam, Ambrose (c. 339–397) mentions him across his Milan preaching and reads him differently from Origen. Where Origen had refused Balaam the name of prophet, Ambrose calls him a man “inspired by the spirit of prophecy” who, blessing Israel, saw Christ: he reads Balaam’s wish “let my soul die the death of the righteous” (Numbers 23:10) as a prophecy of the resurrection — “for as he saw the rising of Christ, so also he saw His triumph, he saw His death, but saw also in Him the everlasting resurrection of men.” With Origen he also makes the magi Balaam’s heirs (“draw their descent from Balaam, by whom it was prophesied: There shall rise a star out of Jacob… He saw the star in the spirit”). Ambrose also uses Balaam as a type of the prophet corrupted by greed — the seer Balak thought “could be tempted by rewards to curse,” whose avarice “would have won the day too, had not God bidden him hold back” — the sorcerer “seeking the aid of magic art” whom God overruled, and the source of the “doctrine of Balaam” that Revelation 2:14 condemns. The two portraits stand in different works: the prophet of Christ’s rising in his funeral oration for his brother Satyrus, the prophet-for-hire in his treatise on the duties of the clergy.13 Full detail »

Gregory of Nyssa (the Life of Moses, c. 390) — a diviner overruled into prophecy

The youngest of the Cappadocian Fathers treats Balaam in the Life of Moses — one of his last works — retelling the episode twice, once as history and once as spiritual contemplation. His answer to the puzzle of the true oracle: Balaam is a diviner whose ordinary power is demonic (“with the cooperation of demons”), but who is “moved by divine inspiration instead of by demonic power” and, “leaving divination aside,” is made to serve “as an interpreter of the divine will.” In Gregory’s account the oracle is not the fruit of his craft but a commandeering of it — God wringing truth from a hostile power, as the Gospel demons confess Christ against their will — and Gregory draws the lesson that magic and cursing are powerless against those who live in virtue, so “the curse turns into a blessing.” He alludes to the star oracle only obliquely (“a clear prophecy of better things which would later come to pass”), never quoting it; and in a dogmatic aside in On the Holy Trinity he cites Balaam — an augur “having obtained for himself the instruction of devils” who is yet “said in Scripture to receive counsel from God” — to argue that the title “God” alone settles no nature.14 Full detail »

Jerome (the commentaries and letters, c. 390–414) — a diviner who prophesied Christ

The Latin translator never devotes a treatise to Balaam, but touches him across his commentaries, letters and philological works, and takes up both of the Torah’s portraits. On one side he calls Balaam a diviner — a “false prophet, who is called a prophet συνωνυμῶς [by a shared name],” who “is sent by the Lord to deceive Balak,” grouped with the lying spirit of Ahab and the Satan of Job among the instruments God permits against the faithless. On the other, reporting Fabiola’s question, Jerome writes that “the soothsayer Balaam in prophesying of the future mysteries of Christ … spoke more plainly of Him than almost any other prophet.” From his Hebrew learning he also gives the etymology of the name — Balaam, “without a people” or “vain people” — the name-midrash the rabbis worked into belo ‘am, and turns it, with Beor (“in the skin”), into a running allegory of “the vain people of the nations” who nonetheless come to bless. He reads the oracles of Christ — “let my soul die the death of the righteous” spiritually, “out of Egypt” (Numbers 24:8) of the Incarnation — and repeats the tradition that the magi were Balaam’s successors (“whose successors these men were”), heirs of the star he foretold.15 Full detail »

Augustine of Hippo (the Questions on the Heptateuch IV, on Numbers, c. 419) — a reprobate compelled by the Spirit

Augustine (354–430) treats Balaam at length only once, in the terse exegetical notes of his Questions on the Heptateuch — and there, on the clause “the Spirit of God came upon him” (Numbers 24:2), he explains it grammatically: the Spirit was not made, “as though the Spirit of God were a created thing,” but “came to be over him.” His Balaam is a man “compelled by the power of the Spirit” to prophesy what he did not wish, who nevertheless “remained a reprobate” — one of those who “followed the way of Balaam … who loved the wages of iniquity” (2 Peter 2:15); in Augustine’s account his sin was to go back and consult God a second time when he already knew God’s will, his greed showing through. Augustine takes the speaking donkey as a literal miracle worked “to restrain his madness” (God giving the beast no rational soul, but making a voice sound from it, “prefiguring perhaps that God would choose the foolish things of the world to confound the wise”), and lays the Peor apostasy to Balaam’s “malign counsel” that Israel be lured into fornication “not only bodily but also spiritually, in worshipping the idol.” Like Josephus and Pseudo-Philo before him, and unlike the Latin Ambrose just before him, Augustine passes over the “star out of Jacob” altogether. His Balaam material survives in Latin only; the English here renders it.16 Full detail »

Cyril of Alexandria (the exegetical commentaries, c. 425) — a wicked augur who prophesied under constraint

Cyril of Alexandria calls Balaam “the wicked Balaam,” “a false prophet and an augur” hired from Mesopotamia to curse Israel by sorcery, whose curse God overruled into a blessing — and who, foiled, “again pursues his malice against them in another way,” the seduction at Peor. Cyril pairs Balaam with Caiaphas: like the high priest who “prophesied” Christ’s saving death without understanding it (John 11:51), Balaam spoke a true word “under constraint… not to him personally, but to the outward representative of the priesthood” — drawing on the same Caiaphas verse that Origen had used two centuries before. Where Ambrose and Jerome set the covetous diviner beside the clear prophet of Christ, Cyril in the passages cited here keeps to the dark portrait, acknowledging the true prophecy as something given under constraint rather than as a mark of the man’s standing before God.17 Full detail »

Theodoret of Cyrus (the Questions on Numbers, c. 453) — the Spirit in an unclean mouth

The Antiochene exegete Theodoret works through the episode’s puzzles one by one in his terse question-and-answer form. He calls Balaam a false seer whose “reputation, though widespread, was false” and whose divination was a “futile art” — and of the true prophecy he writes: “his unclean mouth received the power of the most Holy Spirit and uttered what he had not intended.” He makes Scripture’s “The spirit of God was in him” the crux of his answer. He answers two further questions: God forbade then permitted the journey not because he changed his mind (“God is not like a man”) but as a concession to Balaam’s greed (“Since you want to do it, go, but be sure to say whatever I choose”); and God emptied the curse of power so that Israel could never blame a seer for the punishments God himself sent. He reads the fourth oracle of Christ — not, however, from the “star,” which he never quotes, but from the line about “the children of Seth,” all humanity, whom the one “born of Judah according to the flesh” would win.18 Full detail »

The medieval legend (the Latin schools to the Golden Legend, c. 4th–13th c.) — the magi as Balaam’s descendants

In the Latin Middle Ages a further development concerned not the man but his afterlife: Balaam became the ancestor of the wise men. Earlier writers had made the magi his spiritual heirs — his “successors” or “school” in the East, custodians of the “star out of Jacob” (Irenaeus ties the magi’s star to Balaam’s oracle; Origen makes the magi his “seed,” and Eusebius repeats it as history); Latin writers word the relation in several ways. Ambrosiaster writes that “these Chaldean Magi … following, it may be supposed, the tradition from Balaam”; for Jerome the magi “knew that such a star would rise by the prophecy of Balaam, whose successors they were”; and the Carolingian schoolmaster Remigius of Auxerre writes that “another and more probable opinion is, that they were descendants of Balaam … as soon as they saw the star, would know that a King was born.” Aquinas’s Catena Aurea (c. 1263) gathers the chain in its comment on Matthew 2:1–2, and the Golden Legend (c. 1260) gives it in devotional form: “these kings were successors of Balaam, and came at the vision and sight of the star, by the prophecy of their father, which said that a star shall … spring out of Jacob.” Another Western tradition made the magi heirs of Seth instead. Whether the historical wise men possessed and preserved Balaam’s oracle is taken up on the magi timeline’s “heirs of Balaam?” page.19 Full detail »

The Qur’an and the Islamic exegetes (Bal‘am ibn Ba‘ura, c. 7th–14th c.) — the man given God’s signs

Islamic commentary also remembers Balaam, as the man given true knowledge from God who threw it away for the world. The Qur’an tells, without naming him, of “him to whom We gave Our signs, but he detached himself from them; so Satan pursued him… his example is like that of a dog” (7:175–176) — the gifted man who “clung to the earth.” A strand of classical commentary (tafsīr), on the authority of Ibn Mas‘ūd, Ibn ‘Abbās, Mujāhid, and ‘Ikrima, identifies the man as Bal‘am ibn Ba‘ūrā’ — Balaam son of Beor, the Arabic name giving both the seer and the patronymic of Numbers 22–24, where 2 Peter 2:15 has the Greek “Bosor.” The commentary also carries threads of the Numbers episode — a man of famed, answered prayer prevailed on to curse God’s people, a mount that “sat down and refused to proceed” on the road (the ass of Numbers 22:22–35), and a tongue overruled to bless Israel against his will — and ends with Balaam confessing, as his tongue lolls out, “Now I have lost this life and the Hereafter.” Ibn Kathīr calls it “well-known” that the verse was revealed about a man of the Children of Israel in ancient times; other exegetes referred it instead to a contemporary of Muḥammad (the poet Umayya ibn Abi al-Ṣalt) or to others, and treated the man as an archetype. Whether the verse originally refers to Balaam is taken up on its own interpretation page. The Samaritan stream is smaller: keeping only the Torah, the Samaritans did not expand Balaam into a villain or build their messianism on his star (which they read of the Taheb from Deuteronomy 18:18 instead), their Numbers 23:4 rendering the text with an angelic intermediary in the Samaritan Pentateuch’s usual way.20 Full detail »

The Byzantine Nativity services (the Menaion for 25 December; first canon ascribed to Cosmas of Maiuma, bishop from c. 743) — the star oracle read and sung at the feast

In the Byzantine-rite services for the Nativity of Christ, as the Menaion gives them, Balaam’s oracle is read at Vespers among the Old Testament readings of the feast, between Genesis and Micah: “The Spirit of God came upon Balaam, and he took up his parable … A Star shall rise out of Jacob; a Man shall spring forth from Israel, and shall crush the princes of Moab, and shall despoil the sons of Seth.” The first canon of Matins, “the composition of Cosmas of Maïuma” in the Menaion, addresses Christ in its fourth ode: “Shining forth, a Star out of Jacob, O Master, Thou hast filled with joy the wise watchers of the stars, the followers of the words of Balaam, the soothsayer of old.”21 Full detail »

The Reformation to the modern pulpit (Calvin, Luther, Butler; c. 1520–1900) — Balaam the mercenary preacher, and “Balaam’s ass” in the language

From the sixteenth century the figure appears chiefly in the pulpit and the moral essay, where Balaam serves as a type of the corrupt minister, the man who serves God with his lips for gain. John Calvin, expounding Numbers 22 in his Harmony of the Law, makes Balaam the portrait of the hypocrite whose show of scruple masks his greed: he “pretends a holy anxiety to obey … Yet secret covetousness influences him,” and in the end “sinned, in that he was attracted, like a hound, by the scent of gain, to sell his curses for money.” Martin Luther, expounding 2 Peter 2:15, applies the type to the clergy of his own day — “our ecclesiastics are specially Balaam’s children and scholars” — and coins a name for the venal churchman: “such Balaamites are our bishops and ecclesiastics, who are the throat of the devil.” English writers carry it into the eighteenth century. Matthew Henry glosses the whole man in a sentence — “the love of riches and honour turned Balaam out of the way of his duty, although he knew that the way he took displeased the Lord” — and Bishop Joseph Butler’s sermon “Upon the Character of Balaam” (1726) reads him as a case of self-deceit: the “most astonishing” spectacle of “a very wicked man, under a deep sense of God and religion, persisting still in his wickedness, and preferring the wages of unrighteousness,” a man who wished to “die the death of the righteous” (Numbers 23:10) without living the righteous life, and so “casts about for ways to reconcile this wickedness with his duty.” The figure also entered the language: in English usage “Balaam” came to mean “one who makes profession of religion for the sake of gain” (attested from the 1640s), while “Balaam’s ass” kept alive the donkey of Numbers 22:28 — the dumb beast given a voice to rebuke its master — and lent old printers a word, “balaam,” for the stray filler kept to plug a short column.22 Full detail »

Modern scholarship (a survey) — epigraphic, philological, source-critical and reception-critical study

Five lines of critical study recur in the works surveyed here. First, the epigraphic: the plaster inscription excavated in 1967 at Tell Deir ‘Alla, dated about 800 BC, names “Balaam son of Beor … a seer of the gods,” and its editors and interpreters — Hoftijzer & van der Kooij’s 1976 editio princeps, Hackett, McCarter, Levine — read it as attesting a Balaam tradition native to Iron-Age Transjordan, independent of Numbers, and so a storied regional figure rather than an author’s invention; the dialect classification and the finer reconstruction are debated among them. Second, the philological: W. F. Albright’s 1944 study dated the four poetic oracles very early (12th–10th c. BC, among the oldest Hebrew verse), older than the prose that frames them, with a bearing on the age of the “star out of Jacob”; the dating of the oracles is set out on its interpretation page. Third, the source-critical: Martin Noth, G. B. Gray and Alexander Rofé describe seams in Numbers 22–24 — the poetry against the prose, the talking-donkey pericope (22:22–35) against its frame (told to go, then punished for going), and the blesser of chs. 22–24 against the villain of 31:16 — the same tensions the Fathers addressed by exegesis. Fourth, the reception-critical: Geza Vermes traces how Second Temple and rabbinic tradition progressively hardened the ambivalent seer into “Balaam the wicked,” a reading of this timeline’s Targum and rabbinic strata. Fifth, Hackett, Michael S. Moore and John T. Greene treat the figure as irreducibly ambiguous — true prophet, mercenary diviner, and gentile foil at once — a feature of the traditions rather than a puzzle to be resolved into one “real” Balaam. The disputed questions are set out under Disputed readings.23 Full detail »

Disputed readings

How the passages and sources about Balaam’s name, oracles, legacy and later identifications have been read.

The doctrine of Balaam

  • Are the Nicolaitans of Revelation 2:15 the “doctrine of Balaam”? — the paired rebukes to Pergamum (Revelation 2:14–15), the name-calque proposal, and the early derivation of the sect from Nicolaus of Antioch (Acts 6:5).

The name and the text

  • Why does 2 Peter call Balaam “son of Bosor,” not “son of Beor”? — the manuscript readings of 2 Peter 2:15 and the explanations offered for the form.
  • Does the Septuagint read Balaam’s oracles more messianically than the Hebrew? (Numbers 24:7 and 24:17) — the Greek “a man” for the “Sceptre” and “Gog” for “Agag.”
  • Is the Balaam of Numbers 22–24 a literary composite? — the tensions within the pericope and how its composition has been reconstructed.

The star oracle and the book of Balaam

  • How old is the “Star out of Jacob,” and was it read messianically before Christianity? — the date of the poem and its pre-Christian Jewish readings; the oracle’s wider reception is traced on the Star Prophecy timeline.
  • Did a “book of Balaam” ever exist? — the Deir ‘Alla spr, the rabbinic “notebook of Balaam,” and the lost book postulated in Christian writers.

Later identifications

  • Is “Balaam” a rabbinic cipher for Jesus? — R. Travers Herford’s thesis and the Balaam passages of b. Sanhedrin 106b and Gittin 57a.
  • Is the man of Qur’an 7:175–176 Balaam son of Beor? — the classical commentators’ identification and the other referents they proposed.

On other timelines

  • Were the magi heirs of Balaam’s “star out of Jacob”? — on the magi timeline: the literary allusion behind Matthew 2 and the claim that the historical magi possessed and preserved the prophecy.
  • Were Jannes and Jambres the sons of Balaam? — on the Jannes and Jambres timeline: Targum Pseudo-Jonathan makes Pharaoh’s magicians Balaam’s “two young men” on the road to curse Israel (Numbers 22:22), and later legend his sons.
