Augustine of Hippo on the living heavens

Dates: c. 417–421 (City of God XIII, c. 417–418; the Enchiridion, c. 421); Augustine 354–430 · Wikipedia
Summary: Where Basil flatly denies that the stars are alive and Origen affirms it, Augustine deliberately refuses to decide. Twice over — in the Enchiridion and in the City of God — he professes not to know whether the sun, moon, and stars are ensouled intelligent beings or merely luminous bodies, and marks the question as one he “has not undertaken to discuss.” His studied agnosticism is what let the question stay formally open in the Latin tradition, and the scholastics (Aquinas) cite him as leaving the matter in doubt.

“I am not even certain” (Enchiridion 58)

Cataloguing the ranks of the angelic society, Augustine confesses he cannot map it — and among the open questions he places whether the luminaries belong to that company of spirits or are only lights:

I am not even certain upon this point: whether the sun, and the moon, and all the stars, do not form part of this same society, though many consider them merely luminous bodies, without either sensation or intelligence.

— Enchiridion 58, The Enchiridion. He records the majority opinion (“merely luminous bodies, without either sensation or intelligence”) without endorsing it, and withholds his own assent.1

“Another question… we have not undertaken to discuss” (City of God XIII.16)

In the City of God he faces the Platonist doctrine directly — that the heavenly globes have “intellectual and blessed souls” animating their bodies — and again declines to rule on it, refusing to grant Plato’s confident affirmation while pointedly not denying it either:

Whether this opinion of Plato's about the stars is true or not, is another question. For we cannot at once grant to him that these luminous bodies or globes, which by day and night shine on the earth with the light of their bodily substance, have also intellectual and blessed souls which animate each its own body, as he confidently affirms of the universe itself, as if it were one huge animal, in which all other animals were contained. But this, as I said, is another question, which we have not undertaken to discuss at present.

— City of God XIII.16, Book 13. The refusal is precise: he will not grant the animate-stars reading “at once,” but he brackets it as undecided rather than false.2

What he withholds assent from (City of God X)

The Platonist position Augustine keeps at arm’s length is the one he states, addressing the Platonists, in Book X: that the stars are not only bodies but “very blessed animals” — living, blessed beings eternal together with their bodies:

The sun, too, and the other stars, you not only acknowledge to be bodies, in which you have the cordial assent of all seeing men, but also, in obedience to what you reckon a profounder insight, you declare that they are very blessed animals, and eternal, together with their bodies.

— City of God X.29, Book 10. This is the ensouled-stars doctrine he neither embraces nor condemns — the very reading Philo had held and Origen had Christianized.3

Notes

  • Augustine appears on this wiki also as a witness on the astrology timeline and on the condemnation of Origen — but there his target is astral fate, not the prior question of whether the stars are alive. On the living-heavens question specifically he is the deliberate agnostic, the hinge between Origen’s affirmation and the later scholastic denial.
  • His caution is doctrinally load-bearing: because the West’s greatest authority left the question “in doubt,” neither the ensoulment reading nor its denial could claim him, and the medievals had to settle it on other grounds. Aquinas notes exactly this — “Augustine leaves the matter in doubt, without committing himself to either theory.”
