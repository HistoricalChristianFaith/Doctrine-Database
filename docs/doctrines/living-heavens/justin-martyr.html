Justin Martyr on angels appointed over the created order

Dates: c. 155 (the Second Apology) · Wikipedia
Summary: The earliest Christian witness on this timeline to the view that God administers the world He made through appointed angels. Writing around 155, Justin says God “committed the care of men and of all things under heaven to angels whom He appointed over them.” Justin sets his angels over “the things under heaven” — the earthly order — not expressly over the stars themselves, and the same sentence turns at once to the angels’ fall (the Genesis 6 Watchers). Athenagoras (c. 177) speaks of angels appointed about “the elements, and the heavens”; Clement (c. 200) of angels governing the stars.

God “committed the care… to angels whom He appointed over them” (Second Apology 5)

Justin’s theme in the fifth chapter is theodicy — why the righteous suffer — and his answer begins from the structure of providence: the God who ordered the world assigned its ongoing care to angelic ministers.

God, when He had made the whole world, and subjected things earthly to man, and arranged the heavenly elements for the increase of fruits and rotation of the seasons, and appointed this divine law—for these things also He evidently made for man—committed the care of men and of all things under heaven to angels whom He appointed over them.

— The Second Apology, Chapter V, source. The point that bears on the living-heavens question is the office it names: administration of the created order by appointed angels. God “arranged the heavenly elements for the increase of fruits and rotation of the seasons” — the ordered motions of the sky are for Justin a governed thing — and then handed the running of that order, and of “all things under heaven,” to angels set over them. In Justin the angelic office is broad and undifferentiated.1

Placement on the timeline

  • Angelic governance and the stars. Read against the timeline’s two readings, Justin belongs to neither outright. He does not make the stars living souls (the ensoulment reading), and he does not say an angel moves a star; he says angels are appointed over “the things under heaven.” Athenagoras (c. 177) and Clement (c. 200) speak more specifically of angels set over the heavens and the stars.
  • The Watcher turn. The same sentence continues, “But the angels transgressed this appointment, and were captivated by love of women…” — Justin’s Enochic reading of Genesis 6. That half of the chapter carries his account of the demonic; Justin is also a witness on the Nephilim timeline. For the living heavens, what bears on the question is the appointment that precedes the fall: for Justin the office of angelic administration is God-ordained, though these angels abandoned it.
