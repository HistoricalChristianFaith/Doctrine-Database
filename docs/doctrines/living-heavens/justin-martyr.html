Justin Martyr on angels appointed over the created order

Dates: c. 155 (the Second Apology) · Wikipedia
Summary: The earliest Christian statement of the principle the later angelic-mover reading would rest on — that God does not run the world He made directly but administers it through appointed angels. Writing around 155, Justin says God “committed the care of men and of all things under heaven to angels whom He appointed over them.” It is a seed, not yet the flower: Justin sets his angels over “the things under heaven” — the earthly order — not expressly over the stars themselves, and the very sentence turns at once to the angels’ fall (the Genesis 6 Watchers). Athenagoras a generation later extends the office to “the elements, and the heavens”; Clement brings it all the way to the stars. Justin supplies the governing idea beneath them both.

God “committed the care… to angels whom He appointed over them” (Second Apology 5)

Justin’s theme in the fifth chapter is theodicy — why the righteous suffer — and his answer begins from the structure of providence: the God who ordered the world assigned its ongoing care to angelic ministers.

God, when He had made the whole world, and subjected things earthly to man, and arranged the heavenly elements for the increase of fruits and rotation of the seasons, and appointed this divine law—for these things also He evidently made for man—committed the care of men and of all things under heaven to angels whom He appointed over them.

— The Second Apology, Chapter V, source. The point that carries into the living-heavens debate is the office it names: administration of the created order by appointed angels. God “arranged the heavenly elements for the increase of fruits and rotation of the seasons” — the ordered motions of the sky are for Justin a governed thing — and then handed the running of that order, and of “all things under heaven,” to angels set over them. This is the governing principle the scholastics would later specialize into the doctrine that an angel turns each sphere; in Justin it is still broad and undifferentiated.1

Notes

  • A seed of the angelic mover, not yet the star-mover. Read against the summary’s two rival readings, Justin belongs to neither pole outright. He does not make the stars living souls (the ensoulment reading), and he does not yet say an angel moves a star; he says angels are appointed over “the things under heaven.” That is the general angelic-governance principle in its earliest Christian form — the ground on which Athenagoras (c. 177) and Clement (c. 200) build the more specific claim that angels administer the heavens and the stars.
  • The Watcher turn. The same sentence continues, “But the angels transgressed this appointment, and were captivated by love of women…” — Justin’s Enochic reading of Genesis 6. That half of the chapter grounds his account of the demonic elsewhere on this wiki (Justin is a witness on the Nephilim and aliens-and-the-demonic timelines). For the living heavens, what matters is the appointment that precedes the fall: the office of angelic administration is real and God-ordained even though these particular angels abandoned it.
