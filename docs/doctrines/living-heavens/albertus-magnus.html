Albertus Magnus on the living heavens

Dates: c. 1255 (the natural-philosophy works, above all De caelo et mundo); Albert c. 1200–1280 · Wikipedia
Summary: Aquinas’ teacher holds that the spheres are moved by separate intelligences, which, on Takahashi’s and the Stanford Encyclopedia’s account, he does not finally identify with angels; on ensoulment he differs from his pupil. Albert holds that the celestial spheres are moved by separate immaterial intelligences ranked beneath God, and that this celestial motion is the channel through which form and causal power descend to the world below — his axiom, “every work of nature is the work of an intelligence.” Where Aquinas denies (c. 1270) the heavens any indwelling soul, Albert, reading Aristotle through Averroes, develops a doctrine of celestial souls: the mover of a sphere is an intellectual soul-form, not the vegetative-sensitive soul of an “animal.” He distinguishes the philosophers’ “intelligences” from the theologians’ angels and, on the account of Adam Takahashi and the Stanford Encyclopedia, does not finally settle whether to call the movers souls or to identify them with angels. Basil and Aquinas deny the heavens a soul.

Every work of nature is the work of an intelligence

Albert’s principle ties the whole visible order to the intellects that turn the heavens. Natural things are produced by those celestial intelligences as a house is produced by the architect who moves his tools; the form realized in nature descends from the intelligence that moves the spheres:

…quod totum opus naturae est opus intelligentiae: quia aliter non esset ad finem determinatum…

— “…that the whole work of nature is the work of an intelligence: because otherwise it would not be directed to a determinate end…” (Metaphysica IV, tract. 3, cap. 1, Logic Museum). The “intelligence” here is precisely the mover of the celestial spheres: on Albert’s cosmology the form conceived by “the intelligence of the universe, that is, the mover of the spheres,” is the primary formative principle in the generation of things below, flowing down as a virtus formativa through the celestial rays and motions. In this account the heavens are governed by mind, and their motion carries that governance to the earth.1

The mover of the sphere — and the celestial soul

In De caelo et mundo Albert takes up directly what moves the heaven and whether it is animate. Following Aristotle (Physics VIII, Metaphysics XII) but going beyond him, and drawing on Averroes and, behind him, Alexander of Aphrodisias, he gives an explicit account of a celestial soul — a mover that is intellectual, not the vegetative and sensitive soul that would make the heaven an “animal” in the crude sense. He keeps two vocabularies apart: the philosophers’ “intelligences,” the separate substances that move the spheres, and the theologians’ angels of the Pseudo-Dionysian hierarchy. On Takahashi’s reading and the Stanford Encyclopedia’s, he does not finally decide whether the celestial movers should be called souls, and whether they are to be identified with the angels; here he is more open to a form of animate heaven than his pupil.2

Placement on the timeline

  • Teacher and pupil diverge on ensoulment. On the mover, Albert and Aquinas both hold that separate intellectual substances move the spheres; Aquinas calls them angels, while Albert, on Takahashi’s and the Stanford Encyclopedia’s account, does not finally identify his “intelligences” with angels. On the soul they part. Aquinas denies the heavens an indwelling soul outright and calls the “living”-vs.-“inanimate” dispute merely verbal; Albert elaborates a celestial-soul doctrine and leaves the question open. Bonaventure calls the animate-heaven view “false and erroneous” (Sentences II, dist. 14).
  • Contemporaries. Albert and Bonaventure both work the mover-of-the-heavens question in the 1250s, before Aquinas’s ST I.70.3 (c. 1270).
  • Celestial souls and the condemned proposition. Albert’s celestial soul is an intellectual mover in the Aristotelian sense. The proposition the Origenist anathemas condemned concerned the rational, praying, morally culpable stars of Origen; the timeline keeps the two apart.
