Albertus Magnus on the living heavens

Dates: c. 1255 (the natural-philosophy works, above all De caelo et mundo); Albert c. 1200–1280 · Wikipedia
Summary: Aquinas’ own teacher is a full witness on the angelic-mover strand — and, on the ensoulment sub-question, the outlier on the other side of his pupil. Albert holds unambiguously that the celestial spheres are moved by separate immaterial intelligences ranked beneath God, and that this celestial motion is the channel through which form and causal power descend to the world below — his emblematic axiom, “every work of nature is the work of an intelligence.” But where Aquinas would deny the heavens any indwelling soul, Albert, reading Aristotle through Averroes, develops a doctrine of celestial souls: the mover of a sphere is an intellectual soul-form, not the vegetative-sensitive soul of an “animal.” He carefully distinguishes the philosophers’ “intelligences” from the theologians’ angels and, by the scholars’ account, never fully settles whether to call the movers souls or to identify them with angels. So he reinforces the mover consensus while softening the anti-ensoulment edge that Basil and Aquinas hold.

Every work of nature is the work of an intelligence

Albert’s signature principle ties the whole visible order to the intellects that turn the heavens. Natural things are produced by those celestial intelligences as a house is produced by the architect who moves his tools; the form realized in nature descends from the intelligence that moves the spheres:

…quod totum opus naturae est opus intelligentiae: quia aliter non esset ad finem determinatum…

— “…that the whole work of nature is the work of an intelligence: because otherwise it would not be directed to a determinate end…” (Metaphysica IV, tract. 3, cap. 1, Logic Museum). The “intelligence” here is precisely the mover of the celestial spheres: on Albert’s cosmology the form conceived by “the intelligence of the universe, that is, the mover of the spheres,” is the primary formative principle in the generation of things below, flowing down as a virtus formativa through the celestial rays and motions. The heavens are governed by mind, and their motion carries that governance to the earth.1

The mover of the sphere — and the celestial soul

In De caelo et mundo Albert takes up directly what moves the heaven and whether it is animate. Following Aristotle (Physics VIII, Metaphysics XII) but going beyond him, and drawing on Averroes and, behind him, Alexander of Aphrodisias, he gives an explicit account of a celestial soul — a mover that is intellectual, not the vegetative and sensitive soul that would make the heaven an “animal” in the crude sense. He keeps two vocabularies apart: the philosophers’ “intelligences,” the separate substances that move the spheres, and the theologians’ angels of the Pseudo-Dionysian hierarchy. On the standard reading of his corpus he does not finally decide whether the celestial movers should be called souls, and whether they are to be identified with the angels — the point at which he is genuinely more open to a form of animate heaven than his pupil.2

Notes

  • Teacher and pupil diverge on ensoulment. On the mover, Albert and Aquinas agree: separate intellectual substances (angels/intelligences) move the spheres. On the soul they part. Aquinas denies the heavens an indwelling soul outright and calls the “living”-vs.-“inanimate” dispute merely verbal; Albert elaborates a celestial-soul doctrine and leaves the question open. Where Bonaventure is firmer than Aquinas against ensoulment (the animate-heaven view is “false and erroneous”), Albert is softer — Aquinas sits between his teacher and his Franciscan contemporary.
  • He shows the settlement was a school consensus, not one article. With Albert and Bonaventure both working the mover-of-the-heavens question in the 1250s, the angelic-mover reading that Aquinas canonized (ST I.70.3, c. 1270) was already the common framework of the mid-thirteenth-century schools.
  • Not the condemned ensoulment. Albert’s celestial soul is an intellectual mover, not the rational, praying, morally-culpable star of Origen that the Origenist anathemas condemned. His openness is to Aristotelian sphere-souls, not to the sixth-century heresy — a distinction the timeline keeps throughout.
