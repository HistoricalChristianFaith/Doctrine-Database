Gregory of Nyssa on the living heavens

Dates: c. 380 (On the Making of Man and On the Soul and the Resurrection, both written c. 379–380, the first taking up his brother Basil’s Hexaemeron where death had left it); Gregory c. 335–394 · Wikipedia
Summary: Gregory never poses the question “are the stars alive?” in so many words, and he does not flatly call them “beings without souls” as Basil does. But on the axis the whole debate turns on — what moves the heavens — he gives a clear third answer: neither a soul in the sphere nor an angel over it, but God directly, “the Divine power and skill” that drives the sphere “like a wheel.” And he builds an express theological guard against ever taking the heaven for a god: God so ordered its nature, he argues, that it could not “be accounted as God.” His incidental physics treats the luminaries as plain bodies — the moon a mirror with no light of its own. So Gregory belongs on the negative side of the arc with Basil, reached from a different angle: not “the stars have no souls” but “the heaven is no god, and its Maker turns it.”

The heaven is deliberately made non-divine (On the Making of Man I.4)

Opening his completion of Basil’s six-days work, Gregory notes that God gave the heaven ceaseless motion and the earth fixity, and then draws a striking reason for the arrangement: motion and change are woven into the heaven precisely so that its otherwise God-like constancy should not tempt anyone to worship it. A thing that moves and changes cannot be mistaken for the unchanging Deity:

…the wisdom of God has transposed these properties, and wrought unchangeableness in that which is ever moving, and change in that which is immovable; doing this, it may be, by a providential dispensation, so that that property of nature which constitutes its immutability and immobility might not, when viewed in any created object, cause the creature to be accounted as God; for that which may happen to move or change would cease to admit of the conception of Godhead. Hence the earth is stable without being immutable, while the heaven, on the contrary, as it has no mutability, so has not stability either… and make them alien from the conception of Deity; for as has been said, neither of these (neither that which is unstable, nor that which is mutable) can be considered to belong to the more Divine nature.

— On the Making of Man I.4, newadvent.org. The heaven is expressly ruled out of “the conception of Deity.” This is the anti-deification guard — the same conviction that drives Basil and Ambrose — and it makes the ensouled-and-divine star of Philo and the Platonists impossible for Gregory, though he frames it as a denial of the heavens’ divinity rather than an explicit denial of their ensoulment.1

God’s power, not a soul or an angel, turns the sphere (On the Making of Man I.1)

Gregory’s account of what actually moves the heavens is the point most on-topic for this timeline. Where Philo and Origen posited an indwelling soul, and the later scholastics an angelic mover, Gregory ascribes the revolution straight to the Creator’s own operative power, working the cosmos mechanically — the sphere spun “like a wheel” against the fixed earth:

…as a sort of bond and stability for the things that were made, the Divine power and skill was implanted in the growth of things, guiding all things with the reins of a double operation (for it was by rest and motion that it devised the genesis of the things that were not, and the continuance of the things that are), driving around, about the heavy and changeless element contributed by the creation that does not move, as about some fixed path, the exceedingly rapid motion of the sphere, like a wheel… for neither is the earth shifted from its own base, nor does the heaven ever relax in its vehemence, or slacken its motion.

— On the Making of Man I.1, newadvent.org. The mover is “the Divine power and skill” itself; the heavens need no soul of their own and no angelic charioteer to keep turning. This is neither the ensoulment reading nor the angelic-mover reading, but the alternative both later crowded out — God as immediate cause of celestial motion.2

The moon is a mirror, not a living light (On the Soul and the Resurrection)

In a digression on how the mind, not the eye, is what truly sees, Gregory’s stock example is the moon — and his physics of it is thoroughly of the inanimate-body kind. The moon has no light of its own; it is a reflector, borrowing the sun’s rays as a mirror does:

…that it is in itself devoid of light, and that it revolves in the circle nearest to the earth, and that it is lit by light from the sun; just as is the case with mirrors, which, receiving the sun upon them, do not reflect rays of their own, but those of the sun, whose light is given back from their smooth flashing surface. Those who see this, but do not examine it, think that the light comes from the moon herself. But that this is not the case is proved…

— On the Soul and the Resurrection, newadvent.org. The luminary is a passive body — a mirror — whose apparent self-light is an error of the untrained observer. The point is epistemological, not a thesis about ensoulment, but its whole assumption is that the heavenly body is an object obeying physical regularities, not a living agent.3

Notes

  • What Gregory does not say. He never states, in either work, that the stars “are” or “are not” ensouled — the ensoulment question as such he leaves untouched. His witness is on the two adjacent axes the debate rides on: the heavens are not divine (against star-worship and the divine-star of the Platonists), and their motion is caused by God directly (against both the indwelling soul and the angelic mover). Read for the animate-heavens question, he is best classed with the negative pole, but by implication rather than by the flat denial Basil supplies.
  • Gregory wrote On the Making of Man as an express continuation of his brother Basil’s Hexaemeron, so the two Cappadocians stand together against the living, divine cosmos — Basil denying the stars are alive, Gregory denying the heaven is a god and assigning its motion to its Maker.
  • His direct-divine-mover answer is the road not taken by the Latin and scholastic mainstream, which after Pseudo-Dionysius and Aquinas settled instead on angels as the movers of the spheres. On Gregory’s account no intermediary is required: God turns the wheel.
