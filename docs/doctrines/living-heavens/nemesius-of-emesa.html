Nemesius of Emesa on the living heavens

Dates: c. 390 (On the Nature of Man / De natura hominis); Nemesius fl. late 4th century, bishop of Emesa · Wikipedia
Summary: Nemesius, whose treatise surveys the Greek philosophers’ doctrines of the soul, is the timeline’s clearest surveyor — and he tilts negative. He reports Plato’s world-soul, “the SOUL which turneth about the whole Globe,” as Plato’s opinion, without adopting it; and in his own voice he refers all the governance of the heavens to the one God, holding that the stars keep their courses by a necessity God laid on them, not by a will or soul of their own. Reviewing Plato’s tiered providence he expressly declines to credit the beings who “turne the heavens about,” doubting they even exist — so he is a witness for neither the ensouled stars nor the angelic movers, but for inanimate heavenly bodies carried by a divinely-imposed law.

Plato’s world-soul, reported not adopted (On the Nature of Man 2)

In the doxography of soul-theories that fills his second chapter, Nemesius lays out the Platonic world-soul — the one soul stretched through the whole cosmos that turns the globe and binds all bodies together. The framing is throughout “Plato is of opinion,” “saith hee”: it is a view surveyed, not confessed:

Plato is of opinion that there is both one generall soul, and many particular soules. One soule for the whole world altogether, and other soules for particular things… The soule… which pertaineth to the Vniverse is stretched forth from the center of the Earth to the uttermost limits of the Heavens… And hee saith, that this is the SOUL which turneth about the whole Globe, and which holdeth in, and bindeth together all such things as have bodily shape.

— On the Nature of Man ch. 2, trans. George Wither (1636). The doctrine that a cosmic soul turns the heavens is set out precisely so it can be examined; Nemesius attributes it to Plato and moves on.1

The stars are bound by a God-given necessity, not a soul (On the Nature of Man 38)

In his own voice, where Nemesius accounts for the regularity of the heavens, the cause is not an animating soul but a necessity the Creator imposed on the stars — and, in the same breath, God who made that necessity can suspend it (the sun standing still for Joshua) without the stars having any say:

It is hee [God] who laid that necessity upon the starres, by which they are constrained to keep the same course.

and, of the miracle at Gibeon, that God would “not dissolve that strong law which was made by him at the first, that the motion of the Starres should bee necessary.” — On the Nature of Man ch. 38, trans. Wither. The luminaries are constrained bodies obeying a law, not agents choosing their path.2

He will not credit the “heaven-turners” (On the Nature of Man 44)

The most on-point passage comes where Nemesius weighs Plato’s three-tiered providence. He praises Plato for referring all providence to God’s will — but pointedly refuses to endorse Plato’s second tier, the beings committed to “turne the heavens about.” His hedge “if any such were” doubts that such movers exist at all, and even granting them, their work would be mere necessity, not providence:

Now this is praise worthy in Plato, that he referreth all unto GOD, and affirmes all providence to depend upon his will. But whereas he sayes there is a second providence, committed unto those which turne the heavens about (as he hath said) we commend him not in so affirming. For (if any such were) that which they performed, is not a worke of providence; but rather, a worke of necessity, whereunto they were destinated.

— On the Nature of Man ch. 44, trans. Wither. In the same scheme Nemesius assigns Plato’s “certaine Angells (or spirits)” to the third, earthly tier — over “those things which men doe” — not to the motion of the heavens. So he gives no comfort to the angelic-mover reading either: the heavens are turned neither by a soul nor by an angel, but stand under God’s direct governance.3

Notes

  • Placement. Nemesius is the timeline’s agnostic surveyor, tilting toward the negative: he relays the world-soul as Plato’s, not his own, and in his own voice treats the stars as bodies moved by a divinely-imposed necessity — a witness against animate/rational stars, and against angelic movers, rather than for either. His “God lays a necessity on the stars” is close kin to Gregory of Nyssa’s “the Divine power… drives the sphere,” his near-contemporary: both make God the immediate governor of the heavens.
  • One secondary summary has claimed Nemesius held the world-soul “of the heavenly gods (the stars, planets, earth)” as his own theory. That misreads the treatise: the world-soul belongs to the Platonic survey of chapter 2, while Nemesius’s own providence chapters reduce the celestial movers to necessity and refer all rule to the one God’s will (ch. 44).
  • Nemesius appears on this wiki also as a witness on the Nephilim timeline, where the same treatise supplies his account of the fall of certain angels who “inclined to things below.”
