Philo of Alexandria on the living heavens

Dates: c. AD 30 (the exegetical treatises); Philo c. 20 BC–c. AD 50 · Wikipedia
Summary: The great Jewish Platonist of Alexandria is the timeline’s clearest early voice for the ensoulment reading. Fusing the Greek philosophers’ living cosmos with the Scriptures, Philo teaches that the stars are “animals… endowed with intelligence” — living, rational, divine minds, each wholly virtuous — and that the heaven is full of “entire souls… unadulterated and divine.” He is the seedbed from which Origen would draw the Christian doctrine the church later condemned.

The stars are living beings endowed with intelligence (On the Creation of the World 73)

Explaining the grades of created being, Philo places the stars in the highest rank — the only creatures that partake of virtue with no admixture of vice, because they are minds:

Some things again partake of virtue alone, being without any participation in any kind of vice; as for instance, the stars, for they are said to be animals, and animals endowed with intelligence; or I might rather say, the mind of each of them is wholly and entirely virtuous, and unsusceptible of every kind of evil.

— On the Creation of the World (De Opificio Mundi) 73, trans. C. D. Yonge, earlychristianwritings.com. The star is not a light but a mind — a living, intelligent, sinless being.1

The heaven full of divine souls (On the Giants 6–8)

In On the Giants Philo reads the “angels” of Scripture as the souls that fill the cosmos, and ranks the stars among them — the purest and most divine of the living beings that pervade the universe:

Those beings, whom other philosophers call demons, Moses usually calls angels; and they are souls hovering in the air.

…the heaven containing the stars: for these also are entire souls pervading the universe, being unadulterated and divine…

— On the Giants (De Gigantibus) 6–8, trans. Yonge, earlychristianwritings.com. On this scheme the whole air and heaven teem with living souls, and the stars are the divine ones among them.2

Living animals moving by their own energy (On Planting 12)

Philo restates the doctrine as the settled teaching of the philosophers, and specifies the mechanism — the planets move by their own intrinsic vital energy:

For those who have studied philosophy pronounce the stars also to be animals, being endowed with intellect, and pervading the whole universe; some being planets, and moving by their own intrinsic energy; and others, that is the fixed stars, being borne along with the revolutions of the universe; so that they likewise appear to change their places.

— On Planting (De Plantatione) 12, trans. Yonge, earlychristianwritings.com. That the planets move “by their own intrinsic energy” is the ensouled-star reading in its strong form: the light’s motion is the living creature’s own.3

Notes

  • Philo does not merely echo the Greeks; he weds their living cosmos to Genesis, and so hands the ensouled-heavens reading to Christian exegesis in a scripturally-framed form. Origen’s living, rational stars — later condemned in the Origenist anathemas — stand in this Alexandrian line.
  • His is the pole that Basil (“beings without souls”) and John of Damascus (“inanimate and insensible”) would later deny, and that Augustine would leave an open question.
