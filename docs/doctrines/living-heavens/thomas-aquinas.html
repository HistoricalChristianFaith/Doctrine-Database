Thomas Aquinas on the living heavens

Dates: c. 1270 (Summa Theologiae I, q. 70, a. 3); Aquinas 1225–1274 · Wikipedia
Summary: In Summa Theologiae I.70.3 Aquinas canvasses the whole prior tradition — Origen and Jerome for the living heavens, Basil and John of Damascus against, Augustine in doubt — and settles it by distinction: the heavenly bodies are not animated by a soul in the way plants and animals are, but they are moved by an intellectual substance, an angel. His answer is neither Origen’s ensoulment nor bare inanimate matter, but the angelic mover: an inanimate body governed by a separate spiritual intelligence.

The question and the divided tradition (ST I.70.3)

The article’s title states the question: “Whether the lights of heaven are living beings?” Aquinas opens by laying out the two poles — Anaxagoras’ bare fiery matter and the Platonists’ living heavens — and then the split among the Fathers themselves:

It was the belief of Origen (Peri Archon i) and Jerome that these bodies were alive, and the latter seems to explain in that sense the words (Ecclesiastes 1:6), “The spirit goeth forward, surveying all places round about.” But Basil (Hom. iii, vi in Hexaem.) and Damascene (De Fide Orth. ii) maintain that the heavenly bodies are inanimate. Augustine leaves the matter in doubt, without committing himself to either theory.

— Summa Theologiae I.70.3, newadvent.org. He has Origen’s affirmation, the Basil–Damascene denial, and Augustine’s suspended judgement before him.1 Whether Jerome belongs on the living side, as Aquinas pegs him to Ecclesiastes 1:6 (“the latter seems to explain in that sense”), is a disputed reading: Did Jerome hold that the sun, moon, and stars are living, ensouled beings, as Aquinas reports?

Moved by an intellectual substance (the angelic mover)

Aquinas’ own resolution is to deny the luminaries an animating soul of their own while giving them a separate mover — a spiritual intelligence that directs their motion:

Hence it follows that they are moved by some intellectual substances. Augustine appears to be of the same opinion when he expresses his belief that all corporeal things are ruled by God through the spirit of life (De Trin. iii, 4).

— ST I.70.3. This is the angelic-mover account: the sphere is an inanimate body, but an angel moves it. Dante’s Paradiso (c. 1320) gives spheres turned by Intelligences.2

“Not a difference of things but of words”

Aquinas closes by treating the controversy as verbal: once you distinguish being animated by a soul from being moved by a separate substance, the ancient quarrel dissolves into a disagreement over words:

It will also be seen that the difference of opinion between those who affirm, and those who deny, that these bodies have life, is not a difference of things but of words.

— ST I.70.3. On his analysis the “living” heavens of the Platonists and Origen and the “inanimate” heavens of Basil and Damascene can both be granted their truth: the bodies have no indwelling soul, yet a spiritual intelligence governs them.3

Placement on the timeline

  • Aquinas names earlier witnesses on both sides and answers by one distinction: not ensouled, but moved by an angel. The ensouled-stars proposition had been condemned in the Origenist anathemas.
  • He is also a witness on the astrology timeline, where he licenses divining by the stars only where it traces to God or a natural cause. In both treatments the stars are creatures under God’s governance, not living gods and not masters of the will.
  • The timeline closes with Kepler, who in the seventeenth century replaced a moving soul with a physical force.
