Thomas Aquinas on the living heavens

Dates: c. 1270 (Summa Theologiae I, q. 70, a. 3); Aquinas 1225–1274 · Wikipedia
Summary: Aquinas is the scholastic locus classicus. He canvasses the whole prior tradition — Origen and Jerome for the living heavens, Basil and John of Damascus against, Augustine in doubt — and settles it by distinction: the heavenly bodies are not animated by a soul in the way plants and animals are, but they are moved by an intellectual substance, an angel. So the mainstream medieval answer is neither Origen’s ensoulment nor bare inanimate matter, but the angelic mover: an inanimate body governed by a separate spiritual intelligence.

The question and the divided tradition (ST I.70.3)

The article’s title states the question exactly: “Whether the lights of heaven are living beings?” Aquinas opens by laying out the two poles — Anaxagoras’ bare fiery matter and the Platonists’ living heavens — and then the split among the Fathers themselves:

It was the belief of Origen (Peri Archon i) and Jerome that these bodies were alive, and the latter seems to explain in that sense the words (Ecclesiastes 1:6), “The spirit goeth forward, surveying all places round about.” But Basil (Hom. iii, vi in Hexaem.) and Damascene (De Fide Orth. ii) maintain that the heavenly bodies are inanimate. Augustine leaves the matter in doubt, without committing himself to either theory.

— Summa Theologiae I.70.3, newadvent.org. Aquinas is a faithful reader of the arc this timeline traces: he has Origen’s affirmation, the Basil–Damascene denial, and Augustine’s suspended judgement all before him.1 One name in his roster, though, is over-catalogued: pairing “Origen… and Jerome” on the living side rests, for Jerome, only on a cautious reading of Ecclesiastes 1:6 (“the latter seems to explain in that sense”) — and Jerome in fact reports the ensouled luminaries as an Origenist error to be condemned (Letter 124) and reads the star-agency texts as angels (Judges 5:20). That Jerome is not a genuine witness for ensoulment is weighed on the Jerome attribution argument.

Moved by an intellectual substance (the angelic mover)

Aquinas’ own resolution is to deny the luminaries an animating soul of their own while giving them a separate mover — a spiritual intelligence that directs their motion:

Hence it follows that they are moved by some intellectual substances. Augustine appears to be of the same opinion when he expresses his belief that all corporeal things are ruled by God through the spirit of life (De Trin. iii, 4).

— ST I.70.3. This is the settled medieval doctrine of the angelic movers: the sphere is an inanimate body, but an angel moves it — the reading Dante would render in verse.2

The dispute is verbal (the reconciling verdict)

Aquinas closes by defusing the whole controversy: once you distinguish being animated by a soul from being moved by a separate substance, the ancient quarrel dissolves into a disagreement over words:

It will also be seen that the difference of opinion between those who affirm, and those who deny, that these bodies have life, is not a difference of things but of words.

— ST I.70.3. On his analysis the “living” heavens of the Platonists and Origen and the “inanimate” heavens of Basil and Damascene can both be granted their truth: the bodies have no indwelling soul, yet a spiritual intelligence governs them.3

Notes

  • Aquinas is the synthesizing node of the timeline: he names the earlier witnesses on both sides and folds them into one distinction. His verdict — not ensouled, but moved by an angel — is the mature form of the angelic-mover settlement, and stands over against the ensouled-stars proposition condemned in the Origenist anathemas.
  • He appears on this wiki also on the astrology timeline, where he licenses divining by the stars only where it traces to God or a natural cause. The two treatments agree: the stars are creatures under God’s governance, not living gods and not masters of the will.
  • The angelic mover was not eternal: with the mechanical astronomy of the seventeenth century the separate intelligence gave way to a physical force — the point at which the timeline closes, in Kepler.
