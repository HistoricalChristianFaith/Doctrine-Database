Are the “waters above the firmament” themselves rational, ensouled essences? (the cognate to the ensouled stars)

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: weak — the reading that the supra-firmament waters of Genesis 1:6–7 are themselves living rational natures is a genuine limb of Origen’s densely-ensouled cosmos and was condemned alongside the ensouled stars in 543–553, but it is the weaker cousin of that reading: it lacks the stars’ one strong prop (the argument from ordered motion), its ontological form rests largely on a hostile summary that reifies what Origen’s own homilies offer only as allegory, and the patristic mainstream (Basil, Augustine, Aquinas) held the waters plainly material · advanced by Origen

A cognate of the ensoulment reading of the sun, moon, and stars — the same habit of finding a rational nature behind a piece of the cosmic furniture, applied to the waters “above the firmament.” It is weighed here on its own, and cross-links the ensoulment reading and the separate attribution question (whether Origen held the ensoulment scheme, rated sound there).

The claim

That the waters God set “above the firmament” on the second day (Genesis 1:6–7) are not physical water at all but rational, ensouled essences — spiritual powers in their own right, paired with the waters “below” as their contrary. It is the form the sixth-century condemnation names, bundling the supra-firmament waters with the luminaries as ensouled natures:

…heaven, sun, moon, stars, and the waters above the heavens are ensouled and rational powers…

— the sixth anathema of Justinian’s Edict to Menas (543), tertullian.org. Its sharpest statement is the hostile report of Epiphanius, who has Origen say the waters above the firmament “are not waters, but heroic beings of angelic power,” and the waters below “potencies of the contrary sort — that is, demons.” The interpretation weighed here is whether that reading — the physical waters as living rational natures — is true, a distinct question from whether Origen held the parallel ensoulment of the luminaries (he did).1

Who advances it

The reading is Origen’s, and a limb of his larger cosmos of pre-existent rational natures graded into varied bodies — the same scheme in which the sun, moon, and stars are ensouled minds. In his own Homilies on Genesis, however, Origen expounds the firmament-waters allegorically: the firmament is the “outer man” who “looks at things in a corporeal way,” the water above a figure of the “spiritual water” of eternal life, the water below a figure of the body’s sins — the reader is to become “a divider” of the two. That is spiritual exegesis: the waters signify spiritual realities. The stronger, ontological form — the waters are angelic beings above and demonic potencies below — is preserved chiefly in Epiphanius’ polemical summary (carried into Latin by Jerome), and it is that form the anathema condemns. Jerome’s separate digest of the First Principles (Letter 124) reports the genuine, first-person ensoulment of the celestial bodies (“the sun also and the moon and the rest of the constellations are alive”) but is silent on the waters — so the celestial-body ensoulment stands on Origen’s own words while the ensouled waters stand largely on the hostile digest.2

The evidence for

Coherence within a densely-ensouled cosmos. Granting Origen’s framework — rational natures that pre-exist their bodies and descend, by prior merit, into the whole graded furniture of the world — the supra-firmament waters are a natural candidate for a rational nature just as the stars are. If a luminary can be a mind clothed in a shining body, a “water above the heavens” can be a spirit clothed in a watery one. The reading is not an eccentric intrusion but an extension of a system that populates the cosmos with minds.3

Scripture’s agent-language for the waters. The reading claims the same warrant the ensouled-stars reading claims from Job 38:7: the Psalmist calls on “waters that be above the heavens” to praise the Lord (Psalm 148:4), and to invite a thing to praise God is, on this reading, to credit it with the mind that could. Where the luminaries “sing” and “worship,” the waters above are summoned to praise — volitional language pressed the same way.4

The pairing above/below. Genesis divides the waters, and the reading gives the division a moral shape: the waters above are the better powers, the waters below the worse. The very structure of the second day — a firmament set between two waters — invites a reading in which the two waters are two ranks of spirits, a cosmology whose upper and lower registers answer to good and evil.

Adversarial assessment

What holds. As allegory, Origen’s reading of the firmament-waters is a coherent and even fruitful piece of spiritual exegesis — the waters above as a figure of the spiritual life, the waters below as a figure of the flesh’s pull. Nothing here is refuted merely because it reads the cosmos as more than brute matter, and the biblical summons to the waters to praise God is real. The reading also fits its parent system tightly: within Origen’s ensouled cosmos it is no ad-hoc move.

What fails. Taken as the anathema takes it — the physical supra-firmament waters as living rational natures — the reading is the weaker cousin of the (already weak) ensoulment of the stars, and fails on four counts.

(1) It lacks the stars’ one strong prop. The ensouled-stars reading had a genuine cosmological argument behind it: the heavens move in perpetual, ordered, self-consistent circles, and on ancient physics “that cannot take place without a soul.” The waters above the firmament have no such motion to explain — they neither wheel nor keep courses — so the one inference that carried the stars does not transfer. What is left for the waters is only the agent-language, which is the weaker half of even the stellar case.5

(2) The ontological form outruns Origen’s own text. In the Homilies Origen reads the waters as a figure, not as living waters; the reification into “heroic beings of angelic power” and “demons” is Epiphanius’ hostile characterization. That an allegory (the waters signify spiritual powers) has been hardened into an ontology (the waters are spiritual powers) means the condemned proposition rests on a conflation — of Origen’s figurative reading of the waters with his separate, genuinely-held ensoulment of the celestial bodies. The charge that Origen ensouled the stars is sound; the charge that he taught the waters to be living natures is, at best, a strong reading of a polemicist’s paraphrase.

(3) The agent-language underdetermines it. That the “waters above the heavens” are invited to praise God no more makes them intelligent than the same summons makes dew, frost, cold, and heat intelligent — yet those, too, are “ordered to praise the Creator” (Daniel 3). Basil drew exactly this point: the praise-language is “only a figure, accepted as such by enlightened minds, to complete the glory of the Creator,” and to read it as ensoulment would turn every praising creature into an “intelligent and invisible nature.” Poetic personification fits Psalm 148 at least as well as ensoulment — the same defeater that sinks the star-agency proof-texts.6

(4) The tradition held the waters material. The Fathers who set the standard read the supra-firmament waters as real water, however its nature be construed. Basil rejects the spiritualizing reading outright — “let us reject these theories as dreams and old women’s tales; let us understand that by water water is meant” — naming and refuting precisely the “waters above = good powers, waters below = wicked spirits” scheme. Augustine insists that “whatever these waters are, and whatever their mode of existence, we cannot for a moment doubt that they are there” — real waters, not a figure dissolved into spirits. And Aquinas, canvassing Origen’s view that they are “spiritual substances,” concludes flatly: “we must hold, then, these waters to be material.” The reading was not merely unadopted but explicitly repudiated by the mainstream, and condemned with the ensouled stars in 543–553.7

Assessment

Weak. As an account of what the supra-firmament waters in fact are, the reading does not hold. It is weaker than the parallel ensoulment of the stars — itself rated weak — because it lacks that reading’s one genuinely strong prop (the argument from ordered motion), because its ontological form outruns Origen’s own text and survives mainly in a hostile paraphrase, and because its scriptural base is the same praise-and-personification language that underdetermines ensoulment everywhere it appears. It is weak, not unsupported: as a piece of spiritual allegory it is coherent, it is a real (if figurative) strand in Origen, and it follows naturally from his ensouled cosmos granting his premises. But its positive warrant as an ontological claim is essentially nil, the mainstream held the waters material, and the church condemned it. This verdict rates the interpretation, not whether Origen taught the ensoulment scheme (he did) nor whether God could set a rational nature above the firmament (a different question the evidence here does not reach).
