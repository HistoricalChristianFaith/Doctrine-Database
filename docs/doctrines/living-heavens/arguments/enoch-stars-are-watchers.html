Are 1 Enoch’s bound disobedient stars the Watcher-angels, or a distinct class of luminaries?

Disputed reading · on The Living Heavens · witness: 1 Enoch on the disobedient stars

Related readings: Did Jerome hold that the sun, moon, and stars are living, ensouled beings, as Aquinas reports? · the Watcher legend is followed on the Nephilim timeline (and its 1 Enoch detail page) and on the divine-council timeline.

The passage

And again I saw with mine eyes as I slept, and I saw the heaven above, and behold a star fell from heaven… And again I saw in the vision, and looked towards the heaven, and behold I saw many stars descend and cast themselves down from heaven to that first star, and they became bulls amongst those cattle and pastured with them.

— 1 Enoch 86:1–3, trans. R. H. Charles, source. Earlier in the book Enoch is shown “seven stars” bound in a fiery prison, “which have transgressed the commandment of the Lord” (18:13–16; 21:3–6).1

The question

1 Enoch describes seven stars bound and punished for transgressing the Lord’s commandment (chs. 18, 21) and, in the Animal Apocalypse, figures the descent of the Watchers of Genesis 6 as a fall of stars (ch. 86). The dispute is whether the bound stars are the Watchers under astral imagery or a distinct class of luminaries. It bears on whether the book’s treatment of the stars as moral agents is a separate witness on the living-heavens question or part of its Watcher story.

A distinct class of luminaries, kin to the Watchers in one tradition of revolt in heaven

On this reading the seven bound stars are luminaries that broke their appointed order, punished beside, but apart from, the fallen angels.

Held by: George W. E. Nickelsburg (critical scholarship, 1 Enoch 1, Hermeneia, 2001) ⚠ unverified.2

The case starts from Enoch’s tour in chapter 21. Enoch first sees a chaotic place holding “seven stars of the heaven bound together… which have transgressed the commandment of the Lord” (21:1–6). He then goes on: “from thence I went to another place, which was still more horrible than the former,” and Uriel names it, “This place is the prison of the angels” (21:7–10). The book gives the two groups two places of punishment.3

The offences differ in kind. The stars’ sin is calendrical: they “did not come forth at their appointed times” (18:15), and in the last days “many chiefs of the stars shall transgress the order… And not appear at the seasons prescribed to them” (80:6). The Watchers descend, take human wives, beget the giants and teach forbidden arts (chs. 6–8).4

The number matches the sky. The rebels are seven stars, while the Watchers are two hundred under twenty chiefs of tens led by Semjaza (ch. 6). Nickelsburg is reported to note that the seven correspond to the seven planets or wandering stars superintended in the Astronomical Book, and to set the punishment of the seven stars beside that of the Watchers as parallel developments of the “host of heaven” motif of Isaiah 24:21–22.5

Chapter 86 belongs to a dream-vision in which bulls and cattle stand for people and falling stars stand for the Watchers. Its imagery describes the Watchers by means of stars, while the luminaries of chapters 18, 21 and 80 keep their own place in the book’s cosmology. The two strands share a vocabulary of transgression, binding and fire, as kindred stories of revolt in heaven. On this reading the book’s treatment of the luminaries as responsible agents is a datum of its own, separate from the Watcher myth.6

The bound stars are the Watchers, told in the idiom of the sky

On this reading the seven disobedient stars are the fallen angels of Genesis 6 under astral names, and the disorder of the stars is the rebellion of the sons of God.

Held by: ⚠ unverified — no named proponent located yet.

The book treats star and angel as one vocabulary. Its Astronomical Book shows a patriarch taught the mechanics of the sky by Uriel, the angel who governs the luminaries and their “leaders,” so the stars appear as personnel; and the Animal Apocalypse figures the descending Watchers as falling stars (86:1–3). Where “star” and “angel” pass this freely into one another, an astral rebellion and an angelic one can be one event.7

Both groups receive the same punishment in the same register. The seven stars are “like great mountains and burning with fire,” bound “till ten thousand years… are consummated,” and both stars and angels are said to have transgressed. The prison of 18:14 is “a prison for the stars and the host of heaven,” holding the two names together, and Enoch’s tour describes the two prisons back to back (ch. 21).8

The scriptural background joins the two. In the Hebrew Bible “the host of heaven” names both the stars and the heavenly court (Deuteronomy 4:19; 1 Kings 22:19), and Isaiah 24:21–22 has “the host of the high ones that are on high” punished, “gathered together, as prisoners are gathered in the pit,” and “shut up in the prison” until a later visitation. If 1 Enoch is elaborating that single prophetic image, its bound stars and its bound angels are two refractions of one rebel host, punished in one fiery prison at the edge of the created order.9
