Did Jerome hold that the sun, moon, and stars are living, ensouled beings, as Aquinas reports?

Disputed reading · on The Living Heavens · witness: Jerome, as catalogued by Thomas Aquinas

Related readings: the companion question about Origen · Are 1 Enoch’s bound disobedient stars the Watcher-angels, or a distinct class of luminaries?

The passage

It was the belief of Origen (Peri Archon i) and Jerome that these bodies were alive, and the latter seems to explain in that sense the words (Ecclesiastes 1:6), “The spirit goeth forward, surveying all places round about.”

— Thomas Aquinas, Summa Theologiae I.70.3, source.1 The text of Jerome to which Aquinas points:

But he actually says “turning, revolving, the wind goes and returns upon its circuits” as if he calls the sun itself a breath, like an animal that breathes and lives, completing its annual orbit in its course, just like the poet Vergil says: “Meanwhile the sun flies around the great year”… “The breath nourishes within: and the intelligence stirs the whole mass infused through the limbs, and mingles itself with the mighty body.”

— Jerome, Commentary on Ecclesiastes, on 1:6 (c. 388–389), source.2

The question

In the article “Whether the lights of heaven are living beings?” Aquinas lists Jerome beside Origen among those who held the heavenly bodies to be alive. The dispute is whether Jerome’s own writings bear that attribution, which bears on how many patristic witnesses are counted for the ensoulment of the luminaries.

Jerome read Ecclesiastes 1:6 as speaking of a living sun

On this reading Jerome, glossing Ecclesiastes 1:6, took the “spirit” that goes round its circuits as treating the sun as a living, breathing being.

Held by: Thomas Aquinas (Catholic, Summa Theologiae I.70.3, c. 1270).3

Aquinas canvasses the divided tradition on the living heavens and sets the Fathers in three groups: Origen and Jerome for the living heavens, Basil and John of Damascus for inanimate bodies, and Augustine in doubt. He pegs Jerome’s place to a specific text, the gloss on Ecclesiastes 1:6, the verse on the course of the sun and the circuits of the wind, and he states the link with a measured “seems”: Jerome “seems to explain in that sense” the verse.4

The gloss supplies that sense. Jerome reads the verse “as if he calls the sun itself a breath, like an animal that breathes and lives,” and he reaches for the poets’ living cosmos to explain it: Virgil’s sun flying round the great year, and then, for “that bright sphere of the moon and Titan’s star,” the lines of Aeneid VI.726–727 in which “the intelligence stirs the whole mass infused through the limbs, and mingles itself with the mighty body,” the Virgilian world-soul. On this reading both senses he offers there treat the luminaries as alive, and the commentator who brings an ensouled cosmos to the verse is explaining Ecclesiastes’ “spirit” as the life of the heavenly bodies, and Aquinas records Jerome among the Fathers who read Scripture that way.5

Jerome reported the ensouled luminaries as an Origenist tenet and read the star-texts as angels

On this reading Jerome mentioned a living-sun sense of Ecclesiastes 1:6 among other construals, and elsewhere set out the ensouled stars as a doctrine of Origen’s that he rejected.

Held by: ⚠ unverified — no named proponent located yet.

The case reads the Ecclesiastes gloss as a commentator’s list of possible senses. The living-sun sense is introduced with “as if” and carried by the poet’s lines; Jerome then gives “another meaning of this verse” in physical terms, “when the sun moves to the South it is closer to the Earth; when it moves to the North it is raised to higher orbits,” and goes on to a spiritual sense of the sun of righteousness. On this reading the gloss canvasses poetic, physical and spiritual construals side by side, without adopting the first as his own.6

Where Jerome sets out the ensouled luminaries in full, he files them among Origen’s errors. His Letter 124 to Avitus (c. 409–410), an epitome of Origen’s First Principles sent to expose its errors, reports: “The sun also and the moon and the rest of the constellations are alive… The soul of the sun—or whatever else you like to call it—does not date its existence from the creation of the world,” and Jerome counts such passages among those that “deserve the church’s condemnation.” On the star-agency texts the commentary on the Song of Deborah transmitted under Jerome’s name reads angels: on “the stars in their courses fought against Sisera” (Judges 5:20) it says, “The stars are understood to be the angels who fought against Sisera from heaven.” On this reading Jerome is the reporter of the ensouled-stars doctrine, and the exegesis of the fighting stars under his name is the angels-reading.7
