John of Damascus on the living heavens

Dates: c. 743 (An Exposition of the Orthodox Faith II.7); John of Damascus c. 675–749 · Wikipedia
Summary: In the great summa of Greek patristic theology, John fixes the Eastern settlement on the luminaries: they are not endowed with life — “inanimate and insensible” — against the ensouled-stars reading of the Platonists and Origen. And they are signs of weather and change, not causes of what men do: the habits of the soul remain “in our own hands,” ruled by reason, so the stars neither live nor compel. The positive doctrine that the luminaries are moved by angels is not asserted here; that motif belongs to the later scholastic development (Aquinas, Dante).

The luminaries are not alive (Exposition II.7)

John states the denial plainly, at the head of his chapter on light and the heavenly bodies:

It must not be supposed that the heavens or the luminaries are endowed with life. For they are inanimate and insensible.

— An Exposition of the Orthodox Faith II.7, newadvent.org. This is Basil’s “beings without souls” carried forward into the settled dogmatic manual of the East.1

Signs, not causes — free will preserved (Exposition II.7)

The chapter’s second concern is astral fatalism. The Greeks make the stars the causes of human affairs; John allows them only to be natural signs and secondary influences on the body’s temperament, never masters of the will:

They are rather signs of showers and changes of air. But, perhaps, some one may say that though they are not the causes of wars, yet they are signs of them. And, in truth, the quality of the air which is produced by sun, and moon, and stars, produces in various ways different temperaments, and habits, and dispositions. But the habits are among the things that we have in our own hands, for it is reason that rules, and directs, and changes them.

— An Exposition of the Orthodox Faith II.7. The luminaries are neither living gods nor the arbiters of destiny: inanimate bodies that mark change and, at most, incline the body, while reason rules the soul.2

Notes

  • This chapter does not teach that angels move the stars — John rests the luminaries’ courses on divine command and the order of nature. The angelic-mover doctrine is a distinct claim, developed by the scholastics; John holds only the two negatives (not alive, not compelling) that the Eastern tradition had settled.
  • John of Damascus appears on this wiki also on the veneration of the cross timeline (the same Exposition, Book IV). On the living-heavens question he is the Eastern hinge between the patristic denial of ensoulment and the medieval Latin refinement of the angelic movers.
  • Aquinas cites “Damascene (De Fide Orth. ii)” alongside Basil as maintaining “that the heavenly bodies are inanimate” — making this chapter one of the two named authorities for the scholastic verdict.
