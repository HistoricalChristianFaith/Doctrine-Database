John of Damascus on the living heavens

Dates: c. 743 (An Exposition of the Orthodox Faith II.7); John of Damascus c. 675–749 · Wikipedia
Summary: In his Exposition of the Orthodox Faith, John states that the luminaries are not endowed with life — “inanimate and insensible.” And they are signs of weather and change, not causes of what men do: the habits of the soul remain “in our own hands,” ruled by reason, so the stars neither live nor compel. The positive doctrine that the luminaries are moved by angels is not asserted here. On this timeline angels set over the stars appear earlier in Athenagoras and Clement, and the angelic mover of the spheres in the thirteenth-century writers (Aquinas) and Dante.

The luminaries are not alive (Exposition II.7)

John states the denial at the head of his chapter on light and the heavenly bodies:

It must not be supposed that the heavens or the luminaries are endowed with life. For they are inanimate and insensible.

— An Exposition of the Orthodox Faith II.7, newadvent.org. Basil had called the stars “beings without souls” (Hexaemeron VI.7).1

Signs, not causes — free will preserved (Exposition II.7)

The chapter’s second concern is astral fatalism. The Greeks make the stars the causes of human affairs; John allows them only to be natural signs and secondary influences on the body’s temperament, never masters of the will:

They are rather signs of showers and changes of air. But, perhaps, some one may say that though they are not the causes of wars, yet they are signs of them. And, in truth, the quality of the air which is produced by sun, and moon, and stars, produces in various ways different temperaments, and habits, and dispositions. But the habits are among the things that we have in our own hands, for it is reason that rules, and directs, and changes them.

— An Exposition of the Orthodox Faith II.7. For John the luminaries are neither living gods nor the arbiters of destiny: inanimate bodies that mark change and, at most, incline the body, while reason rules the soul.2

Placement on the timeline

  • This chapter does not teach that angels move the stars — John rests the luminaries’ courses on divine command and the order of nature. Angelic governance of the stars is a distinct claim, found on this timeline earlier in Athenagoras and Clement and, as the angelic mover of the spheres, in the thirteenth-century writers; John holds the two negatives (not alive, not compelling).
  • John of Damascus is also a witness on the veneration of the cross timeline (the same Exposition, Book IV).
  • Aquinas cites “Damascene (De Fide Orth. ii)” alongside Basil as maintaining “that the heavenly bodies are inanimate” in Summa Theologiae I.70.3; Bonaventure quotes the same sentence.
