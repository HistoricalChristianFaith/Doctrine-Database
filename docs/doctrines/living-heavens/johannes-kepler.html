Kepler on the living heavens

Dates: c. 1609 (Astronomia Nova); Johannes Kepler 1571–1630 · Wikipedia
Summary: Kepler is the timeline’s closing witness — and the decisive one, because the shift that ends the whole “living heavens” question happens inside Kepler himself. In the Mysterium Cosmographicum (1596) he still explained the planets’ motion by a moving soul (anima motrix) seated in the sun, weakening with distance. Over the next decade — the 1605 letter likening the heavens to a clockwork, the Astronomia Nova of 1609 with its programme of a “celestial physics,” and finally the 1621 note in which he tells the reader to strike out the word soul and write force (vis) — he replaced the animate mover with a physical, magnetic force. When the star’s motion became the work of a force rather than a soul or an angel, neither the ensouled star of Origen nor the Intelligence of Dante had anything left to do.

The moving soul in the sun (Mysterium Cosmographicum, 1596)

Kepler’s first book already located the cause of planetary motion in the sun — but as a soul. Puzzling over why the outer planets move more slowly, he offered a choice, and took the second horn: a single anima motrix (“moving soul”) in the sun drives each planet, its power fading with distance:

…either the souls which move the planets are the less active the farther out the planet is removed from the sun, or there exists only one moving soul in the center of all of the orbits, that is the sun, which drives the planet the more vigorously the closer the planet is, but whose force is quasi-exhausted when acting on the outer planets because of the long distance.

— Mysterium Cosmographicum, ch. 20 (1596), trans. cited in a University of Virginia lecture (cf. A. M. Duncan, Mysterium Cosmographicum: The Secret of the Universe, 1981). At this stage the mover is still an ensouled principle — the very kind of animate cause the whole timeline turns on; Kepler has moved the soul to the sun and unified it, but he has not yet dispensed with it.1

The heavens as a clockwork, not a living being (letter of 1605)

The break is announced in a letter of 10 February 1605 to his patron Johann Georg Herwart von Hohenburg. Kepler states his programme in one sentence: the cosmos is not a divine animal but a mechanism, driven by a single physical force as a clock is driven by a weight:

My goal is to show that the heavenly machine is not a kind of divine living being but similar to a clockwork insofar as all the manifold motions are taken care of by one single absolutely simple magnetic bodily force, as in a clockwork all motion is taken care of by a simple weight.

— Kepler to Herwart von Hohenburg, 10 February 1605 (Gesammelte Werke 15:146), quoted in Max Caspar, Kepler (1959), via Wikiquote. “Not a kind of divine living being” (non… instar divini animalis) is the exact negation the living-heavens tradition had affirmed for fifteen centuries. (The line is commonly mis-attributed to the Astronomia Nova of 1609; it is this 1605 letter.)2

A “celestial physics” (Astronomia Nova, 1609)

The mature statement is the book itself. Kepler advertised the change of programme in its full title — Astronomia Nova… seu Physica Coelestis, “New Astronomy Based upon Causes, or Celestial Physics” — explicitly making astronomy a study of the physical causes of motion rather than a geometry of appearances. The force that moves the planets is now a species or virtue emanating from the rotating, magnetic body of the sun, sweeping the planets round; the soul has become a field of force.3

“Substitute the word force for the word soul” (the 1621 note)

Kepler made the retraction explicit a generation later. Re-issuing the Mysterium in 1621, he appended a note to his own youthful phrase about a “single moving soul,” instructing the reader to correct it — and thereby dating his own conversion from soul to force:

If for the word “soul” you substitute the word “force,” you have the very same principle on which the Celestial Physics is established in the Commentaries on Mars [i.e. the Astronomia Nova], and elaborated in Book IV of the Epitome of Astronomy.

For once I believed that the cause which moves the planets was precisely a soul, as I was of course imbued with the doctrines of J. C. Scaliger on moving intelligences.

— Mysterium Cosmographicum, 2nd ed. (1621), author’s note (KGW 8, p. 113; Eng. trans. Duncan, p. 203, n. 3), summarized in the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy. The second sentence names what he had left behind: the “moving intelligences” of the scholastic tradition — the same Intelligences that turn Dante’s spheres. Kepler had held a version of the animate-heavens reading and consciously abandoned it for a force.4

Notes

  • The interest of Kepler for this timeline is not that he denied the living heavens from the outside (as Basil did) but that he vacated the position from the inside, having first occupied it. His early anima motrix is a late, Copernican descendant of the animate-cosmos tradition; his mature vis motrix is its dissolution. The hinge is a single continuous line of physical reasoning — why do distant planets move slower? — that starts needing a soul and ends needing only a force.
  • Kepler did not thereby empty the heavens of God: he remained devout, and the sun-force is for him God’s instrument. What died was the specific claim the timeline tracks — that the luminary is itself a living rational being, or is turned by an angel set over it. Once the motion has a mechanical cause, the mover-soul and the mover-angel are alike superfluous — the outcome the summary’s two animate-heavens arguments record (the ensoulment reading weak, the narrow angelic-mover reading contested: strong as theology, superseded as celestial mechanics).
  • Chronology of the shift: anima motrix in the Mysterium (1596) → the clockwork letter (1605) → the “celestial physics” of the Astronomia Nova (1609) → the explicit “force for soul” retraction in the 1621 Mysterium note. The timeline anchors him at c. 1609, the mature vis motrix.
