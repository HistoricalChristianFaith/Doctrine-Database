Basil of Caesarea on the living heavens

Dates: c. 378 (the Hexaemeron, Homilies III and VI, preached in Lent); Basil 330–379 · Wikipedia
Summary: Against the Platonist and Stoic commonplace that the ordered heavens are a living, ensouled, divine animal, Basil flatly denies it: “the heavens are not alive,” the firmament is not “a sensible being,” and the stars are “beings without souls.” The luminaries are creatures — inanimate vehicles fashioned to carry light — not gods and not rational agents. This is the strong negative pole of the question, and it became the pattern the Latin West (Ambrose) and the scholastics (Aquinas, who cites this homily by name) took as the settled answer.

The heavens are not alive (Hexaemeron III.9)

In the third homily, expounding the firmament and the “waters above the heaven,” Basil pauses to head off the allegorists and the philosophers who read the praising heavens of the Psalms as literally conscious beings. Scripture’s poetry that the heavens “declare the glory of God” does not make them animate:

Although, however, waters above the heaven are invited to give glory to the Lord of the Universe, do not let us think of them as intelligent beings; the heavens are not alive because they “declare the glory of God,” nor the firmament a sensible being because it “sheweth His handiwork.”

— Hexaemeron III.9, newadvent.org. The praise of the heavens is a figure of speech; it confers no soul on the sky.1

The stars are “beings without souls” (Hexaemeron VI.7)

The sixth homily, on the fourth day, turns the astrologers’ own talk of “malignant” and “benevolent” stars against them — and in doing so states plainly that the stars have no souls at all. Either the evil in the “evil star” comes from its nature, which would blaspheme the Creator, or it comes from the star’s own choice, which would make it a rational animal — and it is neither, because the stars are not living things:

For the evil stars throw the blame of their wickedness upon Him Who made them. If evil is inherent in their nature, the Creator is the author of evil. If they make it themselves, they are animals endowed with the power of choice, whose acts will be free and voluntary. Is it not the height of folly to tell these lies about beings without souls?

— Hexaemeron VI.7, newadvent.org. The closing phrase — “beings without souls” (Greek ápsycha) — is the load-bearing denial: whatever else the luminaries are, they are not ensouled.2

The luminaries are vehicles for light, not living fires (Hexaemeron VI.2)

Basil’s positive account keeps the sun and stars firmly on the side of created instruments. Light was made on the first day; the luminous bodies were made on the fourth as its carriers — as a lamp is not itself fire but a device to bear a flame:

A lamp is not fire. Fire has the property of illuminating, and we have invented the lamp to light us in darkness. In the same way, the luminous bodies have been fashioned as a vehicle for that pure, clear, and immaterial light.

— Hexaemeron VI.2. On this account the shining thing is a made object put to a use, with no more inner life than a lamp.3

Notes

  • Basil appears on this wiki also as a witness on the astrology timeline, where the same sixth homily supplies the fullest Greek attack on genethlialogy. The two questions are distinct: the astrology homily denies that the stars compel human lives; this passage denies that the stars are alive at all. Both flow from the one conviction that the heavens are creatures, not gods.
  • His denial is aimed squarely at the Platonist “world-soul” and the Stoic living cosmos — the same tradition Philo had embraced and Origen had pressed into a Christian cosmology of pre-existent stellar souls. Basil shuts that door.
  • Aquinas cites “Basil (Hom. iii, vi in Hexaem.)” by name as the authority for the heavenly bodies being inanimate, pairing him with John of Damascus — so this homily is a direct source of the later scholastic settlement.
