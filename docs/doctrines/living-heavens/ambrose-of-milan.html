Ambrose of Milan on the living heavens

Dates: c. 387 (the Hexameron / Six Days of Creation, Book IV, on the fourth day); Ambrose 339–397 · Wikipedia
Summary: Carrying Basil’s dossier into the Latin West, Ambrose does not argue the ensoulment question in Basil’s terms, but reaches the same result from the other side: the sun, moon, and stars are creatures — servants made to govern day and night, “good helpers” but not gods, not creators, and not the authors of what they nourish. Made on the fourth day, after the earth had already greened, the luminaries are junior to the life they light. It is the inanimate-creature pole of the question expressed as a denial of the luminaries’ divinity.

Younger than the life they light (Book IV.1)

Ambrose first turns the order of creation against any worship of the luminaries. Because the lights were kindled only on the fourth day, the green world is older than they are — so they cannot be the divine source of its life:

The beetle is older than the sun, the grass is older than the moon.

— Six Days of Creation IV.1. The point is anti-idolatrous: the thing made later to serve cannot be the God who made the thing served.1

A good helper, not the creator or author (Book IV.1–2)

Speaking in the voice of the earth, Ambrose grants the sun every praise except divinity. It is a benefactor by service, not by sovereign command — a creature lent for use:

the sun is indeed good, but by service, not by command; he is a good helper of my fertility, but not the creator; he is a good nourisher of my fruits, but not the author.

I am not ungrateful for his preservation; he has been given to me for use, he is entrusted to my labor, he is subjected to my vanity, he is subjected to my corruption, and he serves me in slavery.

— Six Days of Creation IV.1. And in the following chapter he spreads the same verdict over the whole heavenly host, which was made to govern and therefore itself serves:

Indeed, if the day serves, how does the sun not serve, which was made to govern the day? How do the moon and stars not serve, which were made to govern the night?

Therefore, the sun or the moon are not the authors of fertility; but God the Father imparts the gift of fertility to all through the Lord Jesus.

— Six Days of Creation IV.2.2

Notes

  • Ambrose’s Hexameron follows Basil’s closely, and the same Book IV supplies the fullest Latin attack on astrology — where he appears as a witness on the astrology timeline. On the present question he does not take up Basil’s explicit “beings without souls,” but his relentless demotion of the luminaries to created servants (“not the creator… not the author… he serves me in slavery”) tells on the same side: they are things made, not living gods.
  • The English here renders Ambrose’s Latin (Migne, Patrologia Latina 14); the standard scholarly translation is J. J. Savage, Saint Ambrose: Hexameron, Paradise, and Cain and Abel (FOTC 42, Catholic University of America Press, 1961).
