Bonaventure on the living heavens

Dates: c. 1250 (Commentary on the Sentences II, dist. 14); Bonaventure 1221–1274 · Wikipedia
Summary: The Franciscan master and Aquinas’ contemporary holds the angelic-mover reading as the preferable of two options, and denies the ensoulment of the heavens. Asking outright “whether the motion of the heaven is from its own form or from an intelligence,” Bonaventure denies that the heaven is a living animal (that view is “false and erroneous”), quoting John of Damascus that “the heavens are inanimate and insensible.” For what does move it he allows two permissible readings — the heaven moved by its own form (God supplying what the form lacks), or God moving it through a created Intelligence or Angel — and inclines to the second. Aquinas, by contrast, calls the dispute between the living and inanimate heavens one of words.

The heaven is not a living animal (against ensoulment)

The question at II Sent. d. 14 is posed on the axis this timeline follows: what moves the heaven — a soul of its own, or an intelligence over it? Bonaventure sets out two “probable positions, in accord with reason and Scripture,” and expressly rules out a third — the philosophers’ claim that the heavens have souls and that a heaven is a “great animal”:

Sed haec positio falsa est et erronea. Sicut enim dicit Damascenus, “caeli sunt inanimati et insensibiles.”

— “But this position is false and erroneous. For as Damascene says, ‘the heavens are inanimate and insensible.’” (In II Sententiarum, d. 14, p. 1, a. 3, q. 2, Quaracchi text). Bonaventure cites John of Damascus, Exposition of the Orthodox Faith II.7, and calls the ensouled, living heaven an error.1

Moved by God through a created Intelligence or Angel (the angelic mover)

Having excluded the living heaven, Bonaventure states the two admissible accounts of celestial motion. The heaven either moves by its own form, with the divine power supplying whatever the form lacks — or God moves it through a created intelligence, that is, an angel:

Alia vero positio est, quod Deus movet caelum mediante Intelligentia creata sive mediante Angelo.

— “The other position is that God moves the heaven through the mediation of a created Intelligence, that is, through the mediation of an Angel.” (In II Sent. d. 14, p. 1, a. 3, q. 2, Quaracchi text.) His own summary folds both into one line — the heaven is not alive, but is moved either by its own form under God, or by God through an angel:

Caelum non est animatum, sed vel movetur a propria forma, Deo defectum virtutis supplente, vel a Deo mediante Angelo.

— “The heaven is not animate, but is moved either by its own form, God supplying the deficiency of its power, or by God through the mediation of an Angel.” He inclines to the angelic reading on the Augustinian principle that spirit presides over every body — the same principle (Augustine, De Trinitate III) that Aquinas invokes to the same end.2

The heavens in the Breviloquium: governed, not deified

Bonaventure’s handbook of doctrine treats the same corporeal heaven expositorily, and there he describes the luminaries’ work in wholly natural terms — they “influence terrestrial and elemental things” by their light, motion, and heat, as “signs of times… days, months, and years” — and denies them any compulsion over the will: they “are signs of times… in such a way that they are not certain signs of future contingent events, nor do they influence free will through the force of constellations, which certain philosophers called fate.” His angels, meanwhile, are the ranked spirits “sent in ministry and deputed to the guardianship of men… by purging, illuminating, and perfecting” — the Dionysian offices of mediation, not the turning of a sphere. The Breviloquium keeps angelology and celestial physics side by side and does not there make the angel the mover of the heaven; the mover-thesis lives in the disputed question at II Sent. d. 14.3

Placement on the timeline

  • Where he stands relative to Aquinas. Both deny the heavens are living animals and both allow the angelic mover; they differ in emphasis. Aquinas concludes that the quarrel between the “living” and “inanimate” camps “is not a difference of things but of words.” Bonaventure keeps a ranked choice open (own-form vs. angelic mover), and calls the animate-heaven view falsa et erronea.
  • The condemned proposition. The ensouled, rational luminary of Origen was anathematized in the Origenist condemnations of 543–553. Bonaventure grounds his rejection of the “great animal” heaven in John of Damascus.
  • Contemporaries. Albertus Magnus also treats the mover of the heavens in the 1250s, before Aquinas’s Summa (c. 1270); Bonaventure argues it on the Lombard’s Sentences, which every master was expected to gloss.
