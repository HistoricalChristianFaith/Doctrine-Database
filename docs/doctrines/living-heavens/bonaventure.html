Bonaventure on the living heavens

Dates: c. 1250 (Commentary on the Sentences II, dist. 14); Bonaventure 1221–1274 · Wikipedia
Summary: The Franciscan master and Aquinas’ contemporary is a firm witness on the angelic-mover strand — and, on the ensoulment sub-question, firmer than Aquinas. Asking outright “whether the motion of the heaven is from its own form or from an intelligence,” Bonaventure flatly denies that the heaven is a living animal (that view is “false and erroneous”), quoting John of Damascus that “the heavens are inanimate and insensible.” For what does move it he allows two permissible readings — the heaven moved by its own form (God supplying what the form lacks), or God moving it through a created Intelligence or Angel — and inclines to the second. So he holds the angelic mover as the better of two options, more cautiously than Aquinas, whose distinction dissolves the whole quarrel; and he rejects ensoulment more sharply than Aquinas does.

The heaven is not a living animal (against ensoulment)

The question at II Sent. d. 14 is posed exactly on the axis this timeline turns on: what moves the heaven — a soul of its own, or an intelligence over it? Bonaventure sets out two “probable positions, in accord with reason and Scripture,” and expressly rules out a third — the philosophers’ claim that the heavens have souls and that a heaven is a “great animal”:

Sed haec positio falsa est et erronea. Sicut enim dicit Damascenus, “caeli sunt inanimati et insensibiles.”

— “But this position is false and erroneous. For as Damascene says, ‘the heavens are inanimate and insensible.’” (In II Sententiarum, d. 14, p. 1, a. 3, q. 2, Quaracchi text). Bonaventure leans on the same authority the Eastern settlement rests on — John of Damascus, Exposition of the Orthodox Faith II.7 — and makes the ensouled, living luminary not a live option but an error.1

Moved by God through a created Intelligence or Angel (the angelic mover)

Having excluded the living heaven, Bonaventure states the two admissible accounts of celestial motion. The heaven either moves by its own form, with the divine power supplying whatever the form lacks — or God moves it through a created intelligence, that is, an angel:

Alia vero positio est, quod Deus movet caelum mediante Intelligentia creata sive mediante Angelo.

— “The other position is that God moves the heaven through the mediation of a created Intelligence, that is, through the mediation of an Angel.” (In II Sent. d. 14, p. 1, a. 3, q. 2, Quaracchi text.) His own summary verdict folds both into one line — the heaven is not alive, but is moved either by its own form under God, or by God through an angel:

Caelum non est animatum, sed vel movetur a propria forma, Deo defectum virtutis supplente, vel a Deo mediante Angelo.

— “The heaven is not animate, but is moved either by its own form, God supplying the deficiency of its power, or by God through the mediation of an Angel.” He inclines to the angelic reading on the Augustinian principle that spirit presides over every body — the same principle (Augustine, De Trinitate III) that Aquinas invokes to the same end.2

The heavens in the Breviloquium: governed, not deified

Bonaventure’s handbook of doctrine treats the same corporeal heaven expositorily, and its treatment is telling for what it does not say. He describes the luminaries’ work in wholly natural terms — they “influence terrestrial and elemental things” by their light, motion, and heat, as “signs of times… days, months, and years” — and denies them any compulsion over the will: they “are signs of times… in such a way that they are not certain signs of future contingent events, nor do they influence free will through the force of constellations, which certain philosophers called fate.” His angels, meanwhile, are the ranked spirits “sent in ministry and deputed to the guardianship of men… by purging, illuminating, and perfecting” — the Dionysian offices of mediation, not the turning of a sphere. So even where the Breviloquium keeps angelology and celestial physics side by side, it does not there make the angel the mover of the heaven; the mover-thesis lives in the disputed question at II Sent. d. 14.3

Notes

  • Where he stands relative to Aquinas. Both deny the heavens are living animals and both allow the angelic mover; but they diverge in emphasis. Aquinas concludes the ancient quarrel between the “living” and “inanimate” camps “is not a difference of things but of words” — dissolving it. Bonaventure keeps a genuine, if ranked, choice open (own-form vs. angelic mover), and states the anti-ensoulment side more sharply, branding the animate-heaven view falsa et erronea. His is the more Augustinian, more cautious cast the Franciscan school carried — the same reading, arrived at without Aquinas’ reconciling flourish.
  • The condemned reading is in view. The “great animal” heaven Bonaventure rejects is the ensouled, rational luminary of Origen, anathematized in the Origenist condemnations of 543–553; his verdict is the scholastic memory of that condemnation, made through the mouth of John of Damascus.
  • The scholastic settlement was broad, not one man’s. With Albertus Magnus a witness on the same side in the same decade, Bonaventure shows the angelic-mover consensus that Aquinas canonized was already the shared framework of the mid-thirteenth-century schools, argued out on the Lombard’s Sentences that every master was expected to gloss.
