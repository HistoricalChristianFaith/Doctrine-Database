Gregory the Great on the Living Heavens

Dates: c. 540–604 (pope 590–604; the Moralia in Job composed c. 578–595) · Wikipedia
Summary: Gregory does not argue the cosmology of the stars head-on, but he glosses two verses cited for living luminaries. In his Moralia in Job (“Morals on the Book of Job”) — a Latin commentary on Job composed c. 578–595 — Gregory glosses the “morning stars” that “sang together” (Job 38:7) as the angels, and the “stars” of Bildad’s speech (Job 25:5) allegorically as the souls of the righteous. On both verses he takes the angels-or-allegory route, not the ensoulment one. On the timeline he stands (c. 595) between Augustine’s suspended judgement and John of Damascus.

The passages

Job 38:7 — the “morning stars” are the angels

A verse cited for living luminaries is Job 38:7, “When the morning stars sang together, and all the sons of God shouted for joy.” Gregory reads the “morning stars” not as living luminaries praising God but as the angels, the “rational spirits” created first, and the parallel “sons of God” as those same angelic beings exulting at the mystery of the Incarnation:

For because the nature of rational spirits is believed to have been created first in time, the Angels are, not improperly, called ‘morning stars.’ But if this is so, whilst the earth was invisible, and in disorder, whilst darkness was over the abyss, they anticipated in their existence the coming day of the following age through the light of wisdom… But they are therefore perhaps also termed ‘morning stars,’ because they are frequently sent to exhort men, and while they announce the coming morn, they drive away from the hearts of men the darkness of the present life.

— Moralia in Job, Book XXVIII, on Job 38:7. The “singing” the verse ascribes to the stars is thus, for Gregory, the praise of angelic minds, not evidence that the physical star is itself a living soul.1

Job 25:5 — the “stars” are the souls of the righteous

Bildad’s “Behold even to the moon, and it shineth not; yea, the stars are not pure in His sight” (Job 25:5) was one of the texts Origen leaned on for the impurity, and so the moral fallibility, of the heavenly beings. Gregory takes the verse allegorically: the “moon” is the whole Church, and the “stars” the souls of the several righteous who shine in the darkness of a wicked world — the Elect, not living luminaries:

What is denoted by ‘the moon’ but the whole Church together? what by ‘the stars’ but the souls of the several persons that live rightly? who amidst the practices of wicked men, whilst they are conspicuous by rare virtues, as it were shine in the darkness of night. Whence likewise it is said by Paul to the disciples; Among whom ye shine like lights in the world… And that by the title of ‘stars’ the Elect are represented, Paul again informs us, by saying, For one star differeth from another star in glory.

— Moralia in Job, Book XVII, on Job 25:5. The star’s “impurity” is read as the residual sinfulness of the righteous soul before the strict Judge, and the luminary as a figure for the saint.2

Placement on the timeline

  • A witness by his exegesis, not a treatise on the heavens. Gregory nowhere sets out to answer “are the stars alive?” as Basil, Aquinas, and John of Damascus do. His place on this timeline is that, handling verses cited for living luminaries, he reads them as angels or allegory. The Moralia was composed c. 578–595, in the decades after the Origenist anathemas of 543–553.
  • Stars as angels and the angelic mover. Reading the “morning stars” as angels is the stars-as-angels figure (a star in a text stands for an angel), not the later scholastic claim that an angel moves the physical sphere. Gregory supplies the first; the angelic-mover account (Clement, Aquinas, Dante) is a distinct strand on this timeline.
  • Gregory on other timelines. Gregory’s Moralia is also a witness on the “Who was Job?” timeline, where his threefold (historical, allegorical, moral) method and his Christ-and-Church typology are set out in full.
  • Provenance. The English is the public-domain Library of Fathers translation (Morals on the Book of Job, 3 vols., Oxford: John Henry Parker, 1844–1850), cited by book and by the verse each passage expounds.
