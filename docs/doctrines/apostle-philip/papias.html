Papias on Philip and his daughters at Hierapolis

Dates: c. 110–130 (Papias’s Expositions of the Oracles of the Lord, surviving only in fragments quoted by later writers) · Wikipedia
Summary: Papias, bishop of Hierapolis in Phrygia, is the earliest extant witness to a Philip settled at Hierapolis. Writing of the apostolic memory he had gathered, he names Philip among the apostles whose sayings he sought, and — as Eusebius reports him — he had heard wonders directly from “the daughters of Philip,” his neighbours: a man raised from the dead, and Justus Barsabas surviving a draught of poison. Eusebius calls this Philip the apostle; whether he was the apostle or Philip the evangelist is disputed.

Claims & evidence

Philip among the apostles Papias sought out

In the preface to his lost work, Papias explains that he prized the living voice of those who had known the apostles, and lists the apostles whose words he pursued — Philip among them, in the company of Andrew, Peter, Thomas, James, John, and Matthew.

If, then, any one who had attended on the elders came, I asked minutely after their sayings,—what Andrew or Peter said, or what was said by Philip, or by Thomas, or by James, or by John, or by Matthew, or by any other of the Lord’s disciples… For I imagined that what was to be got from books was not so profitable to me as what came from the living and abiding voice.

— Papias, preface, in Eusebius, Ecclesiastical History 3.39, 4, in the Roberts–Donaldson translation of the Fragments of Papias (ANF vol. 1).

The daughters of Philip, and the wonders they told

Eusebius, drawing on Papias, reports that Philip and his daughters had lived at Hierapolis, and that Papias — their contemporary and townsman — had the stories from the daughters themselves. Eusebius calls this figure “the Apostle Philip”; which Philip is meant is disputed (see below).

The residence of the Apostle Philip with his daughters in Hierapolis has been mentioned above. We must now point out how Papias, who lived at the same time, relates that he had received a wonderful narrative from the daughters of Philip. For he relates that a dead man was raised to life in his day. He also mentions another miracle relating to Justus, surnamed Barsabas, how he swallowed a deadly poison, and received no harm, on account of the grace of the Lord.

— Eusebius, Ecclesiastical History 3.39, 9, in the Roberts–Donaldson translation of the Fragments of Papias (ANF vol. 1); the NPNF translation opens “That Philip the apostle dwelt at Hierapolis with his daughters has been already stated” (on Justus Barsabas, cf. Acts 1:23 and the promise of Mark 16:18).

Reception and debate

  • The earliest extant witness. Papias is the earliest extant witness to a Philip at Hierapolis, a resident of the same town who named the daughters as his informants. The report Eusebius gives from him speaks of “the daughters of Philip”; the title “Apostle” stands in Eusebius’s own sentence introducing it.
  • The daughters as tradents. Papias names Philip’s daughters as the source of stories he received at Hierapolis; Acts 21:9 gives four prophesying daughters to Philip the evangelist. Whether Papias’s Philip is the apostle or the evangelist is disputed; see the disputed-reading page.
  • No word of a death. Papias reports Philip’s residence and his daughters’ memories, and nothing of a martyrdom; the earliest extant account of Philip’s death is the fourth- or fifth-century Acts of Philip.
