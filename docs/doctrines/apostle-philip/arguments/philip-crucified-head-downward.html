Was Philip crucified head-downward at Hierapolis?

Disputed reading · on Philip · witness: the Acts of Philip

Related readings: Was the Philip remembered at Hierapolis the apostle or Philip the evangelist? · Was Peter crucified head-downward?

The passage

And he ordered to bring clubs and strong cords; and after piercing Philip’s ankles they brought hooks, and put the cords through his ankles, and hung him head downwards on a tree that was before the door of the temple…

— the Acts of Philip (ANF vol. 8, tr. Alexander Walker).1

For in Asia also great lights have fallen asleep… among these are Philip, one of the twelve apostles, who sleeps in Hierapolis, and his two aged virgin daughters, and another daughter who lived in the Holy Spirit and now rests at Ephesus.

— Polycrates of Ephesus (c. 190), in Eusebius, Ecclesiastical History 3.31, 3.2

The question

Papias, Polycrates and the Dialogue of Gaius place the grave of a Philip and his daughters at Hierapolis; the Acts of Philip, generally dated to the fourth or fifth century, narrate his death there, hung head-downward by his pierced ankles. The question is whether the account of a martyrdom, and of its manner, preserves the memory of how Philip died. No modern scholarly proponent of the martyrdom as the Acts narrate it is named in the sources surveyed here.

Philip died a martyr at Hierapolis, hung head-downward

In this reading the city that kept Philip’s grave also kept the memory of his death as a martyr, and the Acts record its manner.

Held by: the Acts of Philip (Greek, c. 350–450); the cult of the fifth-century martyrium of St Philip at Hierapolis, which honoured him as a martyr.3

The place rests on the earliest Asian witnesses: Papias (c. 110–130), Polycrates (c. 190) and the Dialogue of Gaius (c. 200) all set Philip’s grave at Hierapolis. By the fifth century a great octagonal martyrium stood on the hill above the city, and in 2011 Francesco D’Andria reported a first-century tomb within a church some forty metres from it. The shrine is a martyr’s shrine, and the church of Hierapolis honoured its Philip as a martyr. The early notice of a grave fits a martyr’s death: Polycrates says Philip “sleeps” (κεκοίμηται), and in the same letter says that Thraseas, “bishop and martyr from Eumenia,” and “the bishop and martyr Sagaris” “fell asleep,” as Acts says of Stephen at his stoning. The death the Acts narrate is a Roman one: a magistrate, the proconsul whose wife Nicanora Philip had converted, orders the execution. Crucifixion was a Roman penalty, and an inverted posture on the cross is described by Seneca. On this reading the Acts of Philip set down in narrative form the memory of the city where Philip was buried and venerated: that he died there as a martyr, hung head-downward by order of the proconsul.4

The martyrdom rests on late apocryphal sources; the early witnesses record a grave

In this reading the death-scene belongs to the fourth- or fifth-century Acts, and the earliest sources preserve a residence, daughters and a burial.

Held by: Sean McDowell (The Fate of the Apostles, 2015).5

The early Asian witnesses speak of Philip’s home, his daughters and his tomb. Papias reports stories he had from the daughters; Polycrates names the grave at Hierapolis and the resting-places of the daughters; Proclus, quoted in the Dialogue of Gaius, points to the tomb of the daughters and their father. The proconsul, his wife Nicanora, the pierced ankles, the head-downward tree and the preaching from it all appear first in the Acts of Philip, a narrative of journeys with Bartholomew and Mariamne, a talking leopard and kid, and a city that worships a viper. The head-downward posture had already been told of Peter in the second-century Acts of Peter, and on this reading the Acts of Philip give Philip the same death. The church’s memory of the manner was not single: McDowell reports traditions in which Philip dies of old age, and others in which he is beheaded. The martyrium belongs to the same fourth-to-fifth-century horizon as the Acts and records how Philip was venerated there. McDowell accordingly grades Philip’s martyrdom among the least secure of the apostolic-death traditions, resting on late apocryphal sources, and on this reading the early record of Philip at Hierapolis is the record of a residence, a family and a grave.6
