Was the Philip remembered at Hierapolis the apostle or Philip the evangelist?

Disputed reading · on Philip · witness: Polycrates of Ephesus and the Dialogue of Gaius

Related readings: Was Philip crucified head-downward at Hierapolis?

The passage

…among these are Philip, one of the twelve apostles, who sleeps in Hierapolis, and his two aged virgin daughters, and another daughter who lived in the Holy Spirit and now rests at Ephesus.

— Polycrates of Ephesus, letter to Victor of Rome (c. 190), in Eusebius, Ecclesiastical History 3.31, 3.1

After him there were four prophetesses, the daughters of Philip, at Hierapolis in Asia. Their tomb is there and the tomb of their father.

— Proclus, the Montanist, as quoted in the Dialogue of Gaius (c. 200), in Eusebius, Ecclesiastical History 3.31, 4.2

And the next day we that were of Paul’s company departed, and came unto Caesarea: and we entered into the house of Philip the evangelist, which was one of the seven; and abode with him. And the same man had four daughters, virgins, which did prophesy.

— Acts 21:8–9.

The question

The New Testament names two men called Philip: Philip of Bethsaida, one of the Twelve, and Philip the evangelist, one of the seven, whom Acts places at Caesarea with four prophesying virgin daughters. The earliest Asian sources place a Philip and his daughters at Hierapolis, and Polycrates calls him “one of the twelve apostles.” The question is which of the two men that Hierapolis tradition concerns. Its answer decides whether the Hierapolis notices belong to the history of the apostle at all.

The Hierapolis Philip was the apostle, one of the Twelve

In this reading the earliest local witnesses are reporting the grave and family of Philip of Bethsaida.

Held by: Polycrates of Ephesus (letter to Victor, c. 190); Eusebius of Caesarea (Ecclesiastical History, c. 325); J. B. Lightfoot (St. Paul’s Epistles to the Colossians and to Philemon, 1875).3

The identification with the apostle is what the Asian witnesses report, as Eusebius read them. Papias, bishop of Hierapolis c. 110–130, lived in the same town as Philip’s daughters and took stories from them; Eusebius, who had Papias’s whole work before him, reports this as “the residence of the Apostle Philip with his daughters in Hierapolis.” Polycrates, writing c. 190 as bishop of Ephesus and of a family that had given seven bishops, names this Philip “one of the twelve apostles.” He sets him at the head of Asia’s “great lights” beside John, who “reclined upon the bosom of the Lord” and “also sleeps at Ephesus,” so that the province’s apostolic graves are listed together. His account of the family is particular: three daughters, two who remained virgins and lie at Hierapolis, a third who rests at Ephesus. He does not call them prophetesses, and his description is not drawn from the wording of Acts 21. On this reading Polycrates’ distinct family details record a local memory of the apostle and his daughters, handed down in the province where they were buried, and Lightfoot’s study of the churches of the Lycus valley places the apostle’s later residence at Hierapolis on this testimony.4

The Hierapolis Philip was the evangelist, later identified with the apostle

In this reading the Philip of Hierapolis was the evangelist of Acts, and the title “apostle” was attached to him by later writers who joined the two men of the same name.

Held by: A. C. McGiffert (notes to Eusebius, Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, 2nd ser., vol. 1, 1890).5

The case rests on the daughters. Acts gives four prophesying virgin daughters to Philip the evangelist, “one of the seven,” and not to the apostle. Proclus, quoted in the Dialogue of Gaius, describes the Hierapolis family as “four prophetesses, the daughters of Philip,” the same number and the same gift that Luke records of the evangelist’s household. Eusebius, in the same chapter that quotes Polycrates and Proclus, goes on to cite Acts 21 on the evangelist’s daughters at Caesarea as further witness to the same family. On Polycrates’ “one of the twelve apostles” McGiffert comments that “Philip the apostle and Philip the evangelist are here confounded,” and he takes Eusebius’s identification of the two as showing “that in his time there was no separate tradition of the two men.” For McGiffert the identification needs no more explanation than this: “there is nothing more natural than that later generations should identify the evangelist with the apostle of the same name, and should assume the presence of the latter wherever the former was known to have been.” On this reading Papias valued the daughters of the evangelist as living links to the apostolic generation, a role in which they appear as his informants, and the apostolic title entered the tradition after Papias. Polycrates’ three daughters and Proclus’s four, in this reading, are two reports of the one household Acts describes, the family of the evangelist whose daughters prophesied.6
