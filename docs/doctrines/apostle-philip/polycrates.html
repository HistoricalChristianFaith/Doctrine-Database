Polycrates of Ephesus and the Dialogue of Gaius on Philip

Dates: c. 190–210 (Polycrates’s letter to Victor of Rome, c. 190; the Dialogue of Gaius, c. 200) · Wikipedia
Summary: Two early witnesses preserved by Eusebius place Philip’s home, grave, and daughters at Hierapolis. Polycrates, bishop of Ephesus, defending the Asian date of Easter, names “Philip, one of the twelve apostles,” buried at Hierapolis with his daughters; the anti-Montanist Dialogue of Gaius quotes the Montanist Proclus pointing to the tomb of Philip’s four prophesying daughters and their father there. The two accounts differ on the daughters, and which Philip is meant is disputed.

Claims & evidence

Polycrates: Philip “one of the twelve apostles,” asleep at Hierapolis

In the Quartodeciman controversy Polycrates wrote to Victor of Rome, appealing to the “great lights” of Asia who kept Easter on the fourteenth of the moon. He heads the roll with Philip — whom he calls one of the Twelve — and locates his grave and his daughters’ at Hierapolis and Ephesus.

For in Asia also great lights have fallen asleep… among these are Philip, one of the twelve apostles, who sleeps in Hierapolis, and his two aged virgin daughters, and another daughter who lived in the Holy Spirit and now rests at Ephesus.

— Polycrates of Ephesus, in Eusebius, Ecclesiastical History 3.31, 3 (the same letter is quoted again at 5.24, 2).

The Dialogue of Gaius: four prophetess-daughters, and their father’s tomb

Eusebius sets beside Polycrates a passage from the Dialogue of Gaius, a Roman churchman writing against the Montanists. In it Gaius’s opponent, the Montanist Proclus, speaks “concerning the death of Philip and his daughters” and points to their tomb, and their father’s, at Hierapolis.

After him there were four prophetesses, the daughters of Philip, at Hierapolis in Asia. Their tomb is there and the tomb of their father.

— Proclus, as quoted in the Dialogue of Gaius, in Eusebius, Ecclesiastical History 3.31, 4.

Reception and debate

  • Two early witnesses, one place. Polycrates, bishop of Ephesus, seven of whose kinsmen had been bishops (Ecclesiastical History 5.24, 6), and Proclus, the Montanist quoted by the Roman Gaius, both set the home and grave of Philip and his daughters at Hierapolis. Neither, as Eusebius quotes them, describes how Philip died.
  • The daughters. Polycrates gives three daughters — “two aged virgin daughters” at Hierapolis, and one who “lived in the Holy Spirit” and rests at Ephesus — and does not call them prophetesses. Proclus gives four, and calls them prophetesses — the number and gift Acts 21:9 records of the daughters of Philip the evangelist. The two also differ on the burials: Polycrates sets two daughters at Hierapolis and one at Ephesus, Proclus all four at Hierapolis. A. C. McGiffert, in his notes to Eusebius (1890), who identifies the Hierapolis Philip with the evangelist, prefers Polycrates’ account of where the daughters were buried: “the report of Polycrates deserves our credence rather than that of Proclus.” The two accounts are discussed under the two-Philips question.
  • “Sleeps.” Polycrates’s word for Philip (κεκοίμηται, “fallen asleep”) is the Christian term for death; in the same letter he uses it of Thraseas and Sagaris, whom he calls “bishop and martyr” (Ecclesiastical History 5.24, 4–5), and of the whole company of “great lights” who “have fallen asleep” in Asia. Whether the word bears on the manner of Philip’s death is discussed on the disputed-reading page linked below. Neither Papias nor Polycrates, as Eusebius quotes them, describes the manner of Philip’s death; the earliest extant account of it is the fourth- or fifth-century Acts of Philip. Whether that account preserves the memory of his death is disputed; see the disputed-reading page.
