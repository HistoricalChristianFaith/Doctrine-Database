Who Was the Queen of Sheba?

The Queen of Sheba is the great Gentile seeker of the Hebrew Bible — a foreign monarch who travels to the ends of the earth after a rumour of Israel’s God and goes home confessing Him. Scripture tells her story in a single passage, but a resonant one: hearing “of the fame of Solomon concerning the name of the LORD, she came to prove him with hard questions” (1 Kings 10:1), arriving at Jerusalem “with a very great train, with camels that bare spices, and very much gold, and precious stones.” Solomon answers everything she asks — “there was not any thing hid from the king, which he told her not” — and at the sight of his wisdom, his house, and “his ascent by which he went up unto the house of the LORD, there was no more spirit in her.” She breaks into blessing: “the half was not told me… Blessed be the LORD thy God, which delighted in thee, to set thee on the throne of Israel.” Gifts pass both ways — her hundred and twenty talents of gold and unmatched store of spices, and “all her desire, whatsoever she asked” from Solomon — and “she turned and went to her own country, she and her servants.” The Chronicler repeats the whole scene almost word for word (2 Chronicles 9:1–12).

Few unnamed figures of the Old Testament were made to carry so much. Jesus turns her into the standing type of the outsider whose faith condemns the faithless insider — “the queen of the south shall rise up in the judgment with this generation, and shall condemn it: for she came from the uttermost parts of the earth to hear the wisdom of Solomon; and, behold, a greater than Solomon is here” (Matthew 12:42; Luke 11:31). On that seed the later traditions grew luxuriant and divergent. Josephus made her “Nikaulis, queen of Egypt and Ethiopia”; the Ethiopian national epic, the Kebra Nagast, named her Makeda and told of her union with Solomon, the birth of their son Menelik, and the Ark of the Covenant carried from Jerusalem to Aksum — the charter myth of a dynasty that claimed her blood down to the twentieth century; the rabbinic Targum Sheni to Esther spun the hoopoe-messenger, the riddles, and the glass-floor that made her lift her skirt to reveal hairy legs, and other rabbinic strands cast her as a demon, Lilith-adjacent; and Islamic tradition, reading Qur’an 27:22–44, named her Bilqis and retold the throne and the glass pavilion. Meanwhile the Fathers read her spiritually: the queen who left the ends of the earth for Solomon’s wisdom became a figure of the Gentile church coming from afar to the true Solomon, Christ.

This page is a biographical tradition-timeline: not a life of the Queen of Sheba but a history of what was said and believed about her across the centuries, set out earliest source first. Its “witnesses” are the datable sources that report on or interpret her — the narrative of 1 Kings and its Chronicles parallel, the saying of Jesus, Josephus, the rabbinic Targums and legends, the patristic reading of her as the Gentile church, the Ethiopian Kebra Nagast, the Islamic Bilqis, and the modern historians and archaeologists who ask whether a real queen stands behind her. The genuinely contested questions — where Sheba lay (Sabaean South Arabia or Ethiopia/Aksum)? was she a historical tenth-century queen? does the Ethiopian Solomonic-descent claim hold? is the typological reading of “the queen of the south” sound? — are weighed on their own argument pages rather than settled by assertion. She is a prime instance of a recurring biblical type, the Gentile who comes to Israel’s God, and belongs with the other outsiders on this shelf who cross that threshold.

Timeline

The biblical portrait (1 Kings 10, with 2 Chronicles 9 and the saying of Jesus)

The whole later tradition grows from a single royal visit and one saying of Jesus that turns on it. 1 Kings 10 introduces her as a monarch drawn by report across a great distance: “when the queen of Sheba heard of the fame of Solomon concerning the name of the LORD, she came to prove him with hard questions.” She arrives in state — “a very great train, with camels that bare spices, and very much gold, and precious stones” — and lays before Solomon “all that was in her heart.” He satisfies her utterly: “Solomon told her all her questions: there was not any thing hid from the king, which he told her not.” It is the sight of his wisdom together with his court and his worship — the house he had built, the order of his table and servants, and “his ascent by which he went up unto the house of the LORD” — that overwhelms her: “there was no more spirit in her.” The Chronicler retells the same scene in almost identical words (2 Chronicles 9:1–12).1

What follows is why the episode mattered so much to the later tradition. The queen answers the vision of Solomon’s kingdom not with a treaty but with a confession that reaches past the king to his God. “It was a true report that I heard in mine own land… Howbeit I believed not the words, until I came, and mine eyes had seen it: and, behold, the half was not told me: thy wisdom and prosperity exceedeth the fame which I heard.” She pronounces Solomon’s servants happy to stand before him and hear his wisdom, and then blesses the LORD by name: “Blessed be the LORD thy God, which delighted in thee, to set thee on the throne of Israel: because the LORD loved Israel for ever, therefore made he thee king, to do judgment and justice.” Gifts pass in both directions — her hundred and twenty talents of gold and a store of spices such as never came again, and from Solomon “all her desire, whatsoever she asked” — and “she turned and went to her own country, she and her servants.” Centuries later Jesus, answering a generation that demanded a sign, makes her the standing type of the outsider whose faith will condemn the unbelieving insider: “The queen of the south shall rise up in the judgment with this generation, and shall condemn it: for she came from the uttermost parts of the earth to hear the wisdom of Solomon; and, behold, a greater than Solomon is here.” The Gentile queen, the long journey after wisdom, and the blessing of Israel’s God are the seeds of everything the tradition would make of her.2

Josephus makes her queen of Egypt and Ethiopia (c. AD 93)

The earliest post-biblical retelling already re-draws the map. In Antiquities of the Jews VIII.6.5–6 Flavius Josephus (c. AD 37 – c. 100) recasts the visitor as “a woman queen of Egypt and Ethiopia” — planting Sheba in Africa rather than the Sabaean South Arabia its name most naturally denotes, and becoming the first datable witness to the Ethiopian / African localization the Kebra Nagast would later make a national charter. A few sections earlier he had named a queen of Egypt “Nicaule” out of Herodotus (Whiston conjectures the historical Nitocris), the name tradition would fasten on her — though the visit-narrative itself leaves her unnamed. Josephus Hellenizes her: she is “inquisitive into philosophy” and comes to test Solomon “by her own experience, and not by a bare hearing.” He keeps the temple worship among the wonders that overwhelm her, softens her confession from a blessing of Israel’s covenant God into praise of the God “who hath so loved this countrey… as to make thee King,” and adds a piece of Judaean lore — the prized balsam of his own land, he says, grew from a root she gave.3 Full detail »
