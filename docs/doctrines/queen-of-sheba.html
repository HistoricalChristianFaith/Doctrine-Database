Who Was the Queen of Sheba?

The Queen of Sheba is the foreign monarch of 1 Kings 10 who comes to Jerusalem to test Solomon. Scripture tells her story in a single passage: hearing “of the fame of Solomon concerning the name of the LORD, she came to prove him with hard questions” (1 Kings 10:1), arriving at Jerusalem “with a very great train, with camels that bare spices, and very much gold, and precious stones.” Solomon answers everything she asks — “there was not any thing hid from the king, which he told her not” — and at the sight of his wisdom, his house, and “his ascent by which he went up unto the house of the LORD, there was no more spirit in her.” She breaks into blessing: “the half was not told me… Blessed be the LORD thy God, which delighted in thee, to set thee on the throne of Israel.” Gifts pass both ways — her hundred and twenty talents of gold and unmatched store of spices, and “all her desire, whatsoever she asked” from Solomon — and “she turned and went to her own country, she and her servants.” The Chronicler repeats the whole scene almost word for word (2 Chronicles 9:1–12).

Jesus names her in a saying about judgment — “the queen of the south shall rise up in the judgment with this generation, and shall condemn it: for she came from the uttermost parts of the earth to hear the wisdom of Solomon; and, behold, a greater than Solomon is here” (Matthew 12:42; Luke 11:31). Later traditions told her story in different ways. Josephus made her a “queen of Egypt and Ethiopia”; the Ethiopian national epic, the Kebra Nagast, named her Makeda and told of her union with Solomon, the birth of their son Menelik, and the Ark of the Covenant carried from Jerusalem to Aksum — the charter of a dynasty that claimed descent from her down to the twentieth century; the rabbinic Targum Sheni to Esther told of the hoopoe-messenger, the riddles, and the glass floor that made her lift her skirt to reveal hairy legs, and later Jewish sources identified her with the demoness Lilith; and Islamic tradition, reading Qur’an 27:22–44, named her Bilqis and retold the throne and the glass pavilion. The Fathers read her as a figure of the Gentile church coming from afar to Christ, whom they called the true Solomon.

This page traces what was said and believed about her across the centuries, earliest extant source first: the narrative of 1 Kings and its Chronicles parallel, the saying of Jesus, Josephus, the rabbinic Targums and legends, the Fathers, the Islamic Bilqis, the medieval Latin legend that made her foresee the Cross, the Ethiopian Kebra Nagast, and the modern historians and archaeologists who ask where Sheba lay and whether a historical queen stands behind 1 Kings 10. Whether the Kebra Nagast’s Solomonic-descent and Ark claims preserve older history is set out under Disputed readings. Jesus names her and Naaman the Syrian in separate sayings (Luke 11:31; 4:27); other foreign figures of the Hebrew Scriptures traced on this wiki include the Midianite priest Jethro, Balaam, and Jannes and Jambres.

Timeline

The biblical portrait (1 Kings 10, with 2 Chronicles 9 and the saying of Jesus)

1 Kings 10 introduces her as a monarch drawn by report across a great distance: “when the queen of Sheba heard of the fame of Solomon concerning the name of the LORD, she came to prove him with hard questions.” She arrives in state — “a very great train, with camels that bare spices, and very much gold, and precious stones” — and lays before Solomon “all that was in her heart.” He satisfies her utterly: “Solomon told her all her questions: there was not any thing hid from the king, which he told her not.” It is the sight of his wisdom together with his court and his worship — the house he had built, the order of his table and servants, and “his ascent by which he went up unto the house of the LORD” — that overwhelms her: “there was no more spirit in her.” The Chronicler retells the same scene in almost identical words (2 Chronicles 9:1–12).1

The queen answers what she has seen with a confession that names Solomon’s God. “It was a true report that I heard in mine own land… Howbeit I believed not the words, until I came, and mine eyes had seen it: and, behold, the half was not told me: thy wisdom and prosperity exceedeth the fame which I heard.” She pronounces Solomon’s servants happy to stand before him and hear his wisdom, and then blesses the LORD by name: “Blessed be the LORD thy God, which delighted in thee, to set thee on the throne of Israel: because the LORD loved Israel for ever, therefore made he thee king, to do judgment and justice.” Gifts pass in both directions — her hundred and twenty talents of gold and a store of spices such as never came again, and from Solomon “all her desire, whatsoever she asked” — and “she turned and went to her own country, she and her servants.” Centuries later Jesus, answering a generation that demanded a sign, names her as one who will rise up in the judgment against it: “The queen of the south shall rise up in the judgment with this generation, and shall condemn it: for she came from the uttermost parts of the earth to hear the wisdom of Solomon; and, behold, a greater than Solomon is here.”2

Josephus makes her queen of Egypt and Ethiopia (c. AD 93)

The earliest extant post-biblical retelling places the queen in Africa. In Antiquities of the Jews VIII.6.5–6 Flavius Josephus (c. AD 37 – c. 100) calls the visitor “a woman queen of Egypt and Ethiopia” — the earliest extant attestation of an African, rather than South-Arabian, localization of Sheba; the fourteenth-century Kebra Nagast also places her in Ethiopia. A few sections earlier he had named a queen of Egypt “Nicaule” out of Herodotus (Whiston conjectures the historical Nitocris), a name later attached to her — though the visit-narrative itself leaves her unnamed. Josephus presents her as a philosopher: she is “inquisitive into philosophy” and comes to test Solomon “by her own experience, and not by a bare hearing.” He keeps the temple worship among the wonders that overwhelm her, renders her confession as praise of the God “who hath so loved this countrey… as to make thee King,” and adds a piece of Judaean lore — the prized balsam of his own land, he says, grew from a root she gave.3 Full detail »

The rabbinic legend: the hoopoe, the riddles, the glass floor, and the demon strand (Targum Sheni; c. 3rd–13th c.)

The rabbinic sources tell the visit as a romance, and later Jewish sources identify the queen with a demon. The fullest telling is the Targum Sheni (“Second Targum”) to Esther, an expansive Aramaic paraphrase whose long digression on Esther 1:2 has Solomon rule the beasts, birds, and demons, and dispatch a missing wild-cock — the hoopoe — to carry his letter of summons to Queen “Saba” at Kitor, “whose dust is more precious than gold.” She comes; she poses riddles (a tube of paint, naphtha, flax); and Solomon seats her over a floor of glass she takes for water, so that she lifts her skirt to wade and bares hairy legs, drawing his rebuke that hair “is becoming to a man, but to a woman it is a shame.” Jacob Lassner reads the hairy leg as the folkloric mark of a demon. The Babylonian Talmud de-genders the queen (“not a woman but a kingdom,” Bava Batra 15b, reading malkat as malkhuta and pointing to the “Sheba” that raided Job), and the Targum on Job reads that raiding Sheba of Job 1:15 as Lilith. Gershom Scholem traces the identification of the Queen of Sheba with Lilith from that Targum: it was known to Moses de León, is only hinted at in the Zohar, and is stated outright in Joseph Angelino’s Livnat ha-Sappir; in the folklore he reports she is a jinn, “half human and half demon,” a snatcher of children and a demonic witch.4 Full detail »

The Fathers: “the queen of the south” as the Gentile church (Origen onward, c. 240)

The Christian Fathers read the queen through the saying of Jesus, “the queen of the south… came from the uttermost parts of the earth to hear the wisdom of Solomon; and, behold, a greater than Solomon is here” (Matthew 12:42; Luke 11:31), as a figure of the Gentile church: the nations, gathered from the ends of the earth, coming to the true Solomon, Christ. Origen (c. 240) gives the earliest extant form of this reading, and joins it to the Song of Songs, where the “black but comely” bride is the Ethiopian queen and “the Church came from the nations to hear the wisdom of the true Solomon… our Lord Jesus Christ.”5 Ambrose of Milan (“the mystery of the Church, which is gathered from the ends of the earth… there the type of Solomon, here Christ in His own body”), Gregory of Nyssa (the “queen of the Ethiopians” as the Gentiles once in darkness, bringing Christ their frankincense and gold), and Ephrem the Syrian (“she is a type of the church”) give the same reading; Jerome, Chrysostom, and Augustine stress that the outsider’s faith condemns the unbelief of Israel; Bede and Theophylact of Ohrid repeat it in the medieval West and the Byzantine East.6 Full detail »

The Islamic tradition: Bilqis, the hoopoe, the throne, and the glass pavilion (Qur’an 27:22–44; c. AD 610–632)

The Islamic retelling keys the story not to 1 Kings but to Qur’an 27:22–44 (Surat an-Naml, “The Ant”). A hoopoe in Solomon (Sulaymān)’s bird-army brings word of a queen of Sheba whose people “prostrate to the sun instead of Allāh”; Solomon sends her a letter — “Be not haughty with me but come to me in submission” — and, when she resolves to come, has her very throne fetched to his court in an instant by “one who had knowledge from the Scripture.” At her reception she is led onto a glass pavilion (ṣarḥ) she takes for water and “uncovered her shins [to wade through],” until Solomon tells her it is “a palace [whose floor is] made smooth with glass” — whereupon she submits: “my Lord, indeed I have wronged myself, and I submit with Solomon to Allāh, Lord of the worlds.” The Qur’an leaves her unnamed; the commentators (al-Tabari, al-Zamakhshari, al-Baydawi) and the qisas al-anbiya of al-Tha’labi and al-Kisa’i name her Bilqis, make her mother a jinni, and read the glass-floor trick as a test of a rumour that she had hairy, ass-hooved legs — the same glass-floor / exposed-legs motif the Jewish Targum Sheni tells of her, which Jacob Lassner treats as a single legend shared across the two traditions.7 Full detail »

The medieval Latin legend: the Queen foresees the wood of the Cross (the Golden Legend, c. 1260)

The Latin Middle Ages gave the queen a prophetic role. In the legend of the wood of the Cross — grown out of the apocryphal Gospel of Nicodemus and the twelfth- and thirteenth-century Latin tree-of-the-Cross tractates, and gathered into one of the most widely read books of the later Middle Ages, Jacobus de Voragine’s Golden Legend (c. 1260) — a single tree runs from a shoot Seth plants on Adam’s grave, through Solomon, to Calvary. Coming to visit Solomon, the Queen of Sheba alone perceives its future: “she worshipped this tree, because she said the Saviour of all the world should be hanged thereon, by whom the realm of the Jews shall be defaced and cease” — whereupon Solomon has the wood buried, to be dug up long after for the Cross. In the fuller tellings, and in the art the legend inspired, the timber is a beam or footbridge over a stream that the queen will not tread but kneels before — the stock scene painted across a whole cycle by Piero della Francesca at Arezzo (c. 1452–1466). In this legend the queen who came to Solomon for his wisdom is the one who foresees Christ’s Cross; the Fathers had read her as a figure of the Gentile church.8 Full detail »

The Ethiopian Kebra Nagast: Makeda, Menelik, and the Ark to Aksum (compiled c. 1314–1322)

The Ethiopian tradition makes the queen the mother of a dynasty. The Ethiopian national epic, the Kebra Nagast (“The Glory of the Kings”), names her Makeda, queen of ‘Azeb; tells how her merchant Tamrin’s report of Solomon’s wisdom draws her to Jerusalem; recounts the union Solomon wins from her by a trick of an oath and a cup of water (“after she had drunk water he worked his will with her and they slept together”); and follows their son Bayna-Lehkem — ‘Ebna Hakim, “the son of the wise man,” later fixed as Menelik I — back to his father, who anoints him king of Ethiopia under the throne-name David. As he departs, his companions carry off Zion, the Tabernacle of the Law of God (the Ark of the Covenant) by an angel’s command — “had it not been that God willed it Zion could not have been taken away” — and bring it to Ethiopia, where the book teaches God had willed it to dwell. On this narrative rests the charter of the Solomonic dynasty: a bloodline from Solomon and Sheba through Menelik, and the true Ark at Aksum — a charter written into the Ethiopian constitutions of 1931 and 1955 and running, by claim, to the deposition of Haile Selassie in 1974. The Ethiopic form is fourteenth-century; whether its dynastic and relic claims preserve older history is disputed (see the disputed reading).9 Full detail »

Modern scholarship (a survey)

Modern archaeological, historical, and reception-critical study of the Sheba tradition returns to four questions. On the kingdom, Kenneth Kitchen and Yigal Levin identify the biblical Sheba with Saba, the South-Arabian caravan state of Yemen — capital Marib, its great dam, its temples to the moon-god Almaqah, its own inscriptions — whose gold, spices, and gems match the queen’s gifts (the material record surveyed by the 2002 British Museum exhibition Queen of Sheba: Treasures from Ancient Yemen). On her historicity, Kitchen reads the visit against the documented incense trade and the wider pattern of long-distance royal exchange, and finds a South-Arabian monarch of Solomon’s era credible; Israel Finkelstein and Neil Asher Silberman hold that the Sabaean kingdom “began to flourish only in the eighth century B.C.,” some two centuries after Solomon. On the legend, Jacob Lassner reads the Jewish, Islamic, and Ethiopian retellings as one cross-fertilizing tradition that first demonized the queen (the shared glass-floor and hairy-legs motif, the Lilith identification) and then domesticated her as Bilqis and Makeda. On the Ethiopian charter, Stuart Munro-Hay dates the Solomonic-descent claim to the late thirteenth century and identifies the object at Aksum as a tabot; whether those claims preserve older history is set out under Disputed readings.10 Full detail »
