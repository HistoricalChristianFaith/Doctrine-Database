Hippolytus of Rome on eternal conscious torment

Dates: c. 170–235 · Wikipedia
Summary: Hippolytus, a Roman presbyter who wrote in Greek, describes the punishment of the wicked in the treatise Against Plato, on the Cause of the Universe: the unrighteous are sentenced after the resurrection to a fire “which is unquenchable and without end,” with a worm that “dieth not” and no death to deliver them.

Claims & evidence

A lake of unquenchable fire, reserved for the day of judgment

And in this locality there is a certain place set apart by itself, a lake of unquenchable fire, into which we suppose no one has ever yet been cast; for it is prepared against the day determined by God, in which one sentence of righteous judgment shall be justly applied to all. And the unrighteous, and those who believed not God, who have honoured as God the vain works of the hands of men, idols fashioned (by themselves), shall be sentenced to this endless punishment.

— Against Plato, on the Cause of the Universe, §1, source (Ante-Nicene Fathers, vol. 5). Hippolytus lays out the geography of Hades — a holding-place where the souls of righteous and unrighteous alike await the judgment — and within it the “lake of unquenchable fire” kept for the wicked. He calls their portion “this endless punishment.”

After the resurrection: “the fire which is unquenchable and without end”

…since to those who have done well shall be assigned righteously eternal bliss, and to the lovers of iniquity shall be given eternal punishment. And the fire which is unquenchable and without end awaits these latter, and a certain fiery worm which dieth not, and which does not waste the body, but continues bursting forth from the body with unending pain. No sleep will give them rest; no night will soothe them; no death will deliver them from punishment; no voice of interceding friends will profit them.

— Against Plato, on the Cause of the Universe, §3, source. Hippolytus has just argued that the body itself is raised — “the unrighteous will receive their bodies unchanged, and unransomed from suffering and disease” — so that the punishment falls on the whole person. In the passage the fire is “without end,” the worm “does not waste the body,” the pain is “unending,” and “no death will deliver them from punishment.”

Reception and debate

  • Attribution. The treatise was long transmitted under other names (including Josephus's), and its attribution to Hippolytus is not certain ⚠ unverified. It is printed among his fragments in the Ante-Nicene Fathers, vol. 5.
  • How widely unending punishment was taught before Nicaea, in Greek and in Latin, is discussed on its interpretation page; McClymond, counting from Brian Daley's survey, lists Hippolytus among the authors who affirm everlasting punishment (Theological Studies 76.4, 2015, p. 822).
  • Hippolytus is also a witness on other timelines (the intermediate state, the canon, and church order among them); his eschatology here is continuous with his account of Hades on the intermediate-state timeline.
