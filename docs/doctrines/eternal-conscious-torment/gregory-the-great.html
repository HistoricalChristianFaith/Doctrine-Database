Gregory the Great on eternal conscious torment

Dates: c. 540–604 · Wikipedia
Summary: Gregory the Great, bishop of Rome, treats the punishment of the damned in the fourth book of his Dialogues. In a dialogue with the deacon Peter he affirms that the damned “never [have] any release of their torments,” answers the objection that eternal pain for temporal sin is unjust, and says that the lost suffer “death without death… and end without end.”

Claims & evidence

“The wicked never any release of their torments”

Certain it is, and without all doubt most true, that as the good shall have no end of their joys, so the wicked never any release of their torments: for our Saviour himself saith: The wicked shall go into everlasting punishment, and the just into everlasting life. Seeing, then, true it is, that which he hath promised to his friends: out of all question false it cannot be, that which he hath threatened to his enemies.

— Dialogues, Book IV, ch. 44, source (trans. P. W., 1911). The chapter is headed “whether those that be in hell shall burn there for ever,” and Gregory answers it from the symmetry of Matthew 25:46: the endlessness of the reward and the endlessness of the penalty stand or fall together, for the same Lord promised the one and threatened the other.

The bodies of the damned “always die without end”

Neither shall the bodies of the damned, lying in torments, ever perfectly perish: for though they always decay, yet for ever shall they continue: and as they sinned both with soul and body, so living always in body and soul, they shall always die without end.

— Dialogues, Book IV, ch. 45, source. Gregory grounds the endlessness in the resurrection body: the damned are raised to a bodily existence that decays perpetually without ever being destroyed, so that they “always die without end.”

Why a temporal sin earns an eternal penalty

…the reason why wicked men made an end of sinning was, because they also made an end of their life: for willingly they would, had it been in their power, have lived without end, that they might in like manner have sinned without end. For they do plainly declare that they desired always to live in sin, who never, so long as they were in this world, gave over their wicked life.

— Dialogues, Book IV, ch. 44, source. To Peter's protest that a sin “which had an end when it was committed” cannot justly be “punished without end,” Gregory gives the will-based answer: the wicked stopped sinning only because death stopped them, and a will that would have sinned forever is justly requited forever. The answer measures the will of the sinner. Gregory adds that the lost are “enforced to suffer death without death, defect without defect, and end without end” (ch. 46).

Reception and debate

  • No prayer for the damned. Gregory draws the pastoral corollary that the church does not pray for those in hell, “seeing certain it is that they be condemned to endless pains” from which “no petition will be admitted of God, their just judge”. In the same book he teaches that prayer avails for some souls after death; see his page on the purgatory timeline.
  • Gregory is also a witness on other timelines (the canon, purgatory, and the living heavens among them); this eschatology is continuous with his account of the afterlife throughout Dialogues IV.
