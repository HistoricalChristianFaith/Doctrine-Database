Gregory the Great on eternal conscious torment

Dates: c. 540–604 · Wikipedia
Summary: Gregory the Great, the last of the traditional Latin Fathers and the first monk to become pope, handed Augustine's eternal-conscious-torment doctrine to the medieval West in the fourth book of his hugely popular Dialogues. In a set-piece dialogue with the deacon Peter he affirms that the damned “never [have] any release of their torments,” answers the objection that eternal pain for temporal sin is unjust, and coins the tradition's sharpest formula for the second death: the lost suffer “death without death… and end without end.”

Claims & evidence

“The wicked never any release of their torments”

Certain it is, and without all doubt most true, that as the good shall have no end of their joys, so the wicked never any release of their torments: for our Saviour himself saith: The wicked shall go into everlasting punishment, and the just into everlasting life. Seeing, then, true it is, that which he hath promised to his friends: out of all question false it cannot be, that which he hath threatened to his enemies.

— Dialogues, Book IV, ch. 44, source (trans. P. W., 1911). The chapter is headed “whether those that be in hell shall burn there for ever,” and Gregory answers it from the symmetry of Matthew 25:46: the endlessness of the reward and the endlessness of the penalty stand or fall together, for the same Lord promised the one and threatened the other. It is Augustine's argument, carried into the Middle Ages in Gregory's plainer voice.

The bodies of the damned “always die without end”

Neither shall the bodies of the damned, lying in torments, ever perfectly perish: for though they always decay, yet for ever shall they continue: and as they sinned both with soul and body, so living always in body and soul, they shall always die without end.

— Dialogues, Book IV, ch. 45, source. Gregory grounds the endlessness in the resurrection body, as the Latin apologists and later Aquinas do: the damned are raised to a bodily existence that decays perpetually without ever being destroyed, so that they “always die without end.” The paradox — a dying that never completes — is exactly the “second death” Augustine described.

Why a temporal sin earns an eternal penalty

…the reason why wicked men made an end of sinning was, because they also made an end of their life: for willingly they would, had it been in their power, have lived without end, that they might in like manner have sinned without end. For they do plainly declare that they desired always to live in sin, who never, so long as they were in this world, gave over their wicked life.

— Dialogues, Book IV, ch. 44, source. To Peter's protest that a sin “which had an end when it was committed” cannot justly be “punished without end,” Gregory gives the will-based answer: the wicked stopped sinning only because death stopped them, and a will that would have sinned forever is justly requited forever. It is a distinct move from Anselm and Aquinas's infinite-offense argument — measuring the will rather than the dignity of the one offended — and anticipates the modern free-will theodicy. Gregory completes the thought with his memorable formula for the deathless dying: the lost are “enforced to suffer death without death, defect without defect, and end without end” (ch. 46).

Notes

  • The bridge from Augustine to the schools. Between Augustine (c. 420) and Aquinas (c. 1274) the Latin West produced no comparably decisive treatment of the last things; Gregory's Dialogues — one of the most-copied and most-translated books of the early Middle Ages — carried the eternalist doctrine, together with a developed account of purgatorial cleansing for the saved, across that gap. His Moralia in Job teaches the same.
  • No prayer for the damned. Gregory draws the pastoral corollary that the church does not pray for those in hell, “seeing certain it is that they be condemned to endless pains” from which “no petition will be admitted of God, their just judge” — a line his medieval readers set carefully against his teaching that prayer does avail for souls being purged. The endlessness of hell is, for Gregory, precisely what marks it off from purgatory.
  • Gregory appears on this wiki as a witness on other timelines (the canon, purgatory, and the living heavens among them); this eschatology is continuous with his account of the afterlife throughout Dialogues IV.
