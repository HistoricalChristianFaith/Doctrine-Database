Jonathan Edwards on eternal conscious torment

Dates: c. 1741 · Wikipedia
Summary: Edwards (1703–1758), the New England Congregationalist theologian and preacher of the Great Awakening, gave eternal conscious torment its most searing preaching and one of its most sustained defenses. In the revival sermon Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God (1741) he set the wrath of God as present and the misery as endless; in the doctrinal sermon The Eternity of Hell Torments (on Matthew 25:46) he argued the case: that the threatened “eternal death” is not annihilation but an abiding, sensible misery, and that it is “absolutely without end” because the very word that measures the life of the blessed measures the punishment of the lost. He represents the doctrine at its rhetorical height, a century before it would be seriously challenged from within evangelicalism.

Claims & evidence

The misery of the lost is endless — suffered “to all eternity”

The famous Enfield sermon presses the endlessness of the wrath directly on the hearer. There is no relief, no mitigation, and no term:

It is everlasting wrath. It would be dreadful to suffer this fierceness and wrath of Almighty God one moment; but you must suffer it to all eternity. There will be no end to this exquisite horrible misery. When you look forward, you shall see a long for ever, a boundless duration before you, which will swallow up your thoughts, and amaze your soul; and you will absolutely despair of ever having any deliverance, any end, any mitigation, any rest at all.

— Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God (Enfield, 8 July 1741).1 He drives the point home with the image of ages upon ages that leave the sufferer no nearer the end: the lost “must wear out long ages, millions of millions of ages, in wrestling and conflicting with this almighty merciless vengeance,” and when so many ages have passed will “know that all is but a point to what remains.” So “your punishment will indeed be infinite.”2

Eternal death is not annihilation, but abiding sensible misery

In The Eternity of Hell Torments Edwards sets out the doctrine in three propositions, the second of which directly denies the annihilationist reading that later conditionalists would revive:

I. That it is not contrary to the divine perfections to inflict on wicked men a punishment that is absolutely eternal. II. That the eternal death which God threatens, is not annihilation, but an abiding sensible punishment or misery. III. That this misery will not only continue for a very long time, but will be absolutely without end.

— The Eternity of Hell Torments (on Matthew 25:46).3 He restates the third proposition as the sermon's central claim: “As the future punishment of the wicked consists in sensible misery; so it shall not only continue for a very long time, but shall be absolutely without end.”4

The grammar of Matthew 25:46: one word governs punishment and life alike

Edwards rests the endlessness on the parallel structure of Matthew 25:46 — the verse the tradition has always pressed (Cyprian, Augustine, Aquinas). The single word “eternal” measures the punishment, the happiness of the saints, and the very being of God:

The Scripture uses the same way of speaking to set forth the eternity of punishment and the eternity of happiness, yea, the eternity of God himself. Matt. xxv. 46. “These shall go away into everlasting punishment: but the righteous into life eternal.” The words everlasting and eternal, in the original, are the very same.

— The Eternity of Hell Torments.5 To make the punishment temporary, on this reading, is to make the life of the saints — and God's own eternity — temporary too. Whether the grammar of the sentence carries this weight is weighed on the aiōnios argument page.

The hopelessness of the endless state

The pastoral force of the doctrine, for Edwards, is that the lost will know their state is without term — that no number of ages advances them toward release:

after you shall have worn a thousand more such ages, you shall have no hope, but shall know that you are not one whit nearer to the end of your torments; but that still there are the same groans, the same shrieks, the same doleful cries, incessantly to be made by you, and that the smoke of your torment shall still ascend up for ever and ever.

— The Eternity of Hell Torments.6

Notes

  • The Reformed rhetorical apex. Edwards does not add new exegetical machinery to the doctrine so much as bring it to its highest pitch of vividness and urgency, wedding the traditional case (the Matthew 25:46 parallel, the denial of annihilation, the infinite-offense reasoning) to the psychology of the revival. His preaching became the best-known Protestant statement of eternal conscious torment.
  • The infinite-offense argument. Edwards's first proposition — that an absolutely eternal punishment is “not contrary to the divine perfections” — rests on the classic infinite-offense reasoning he shares with Anselm and Aquinas: sin against an infinitely worthy God incurs a guilt that justly draws an endless penalty. That reasoning is weighed adversarially on its own argument page (rated contested).
  • Provenance. Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God was preached at Enfield, Connecticut, on 8 July 1741, on Deuteronomy 32:35. The Eternity of Hell Torments, on Matthew 25:46, is printed in the second volume of Edwards's Works. Both are quoted here from the Christian Classics Ethereal Library texts.
