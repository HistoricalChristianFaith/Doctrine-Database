Athenagoras of Athens on eternal conscious torment

Dates: c. 177 (Athenagoras of Athens, c. 133–190; Wikipedia)
Summary: The Athenian Christian philosopher Athenagoras supplies not yet the developed doctrine of endless torment but the two premises it rests on: that the whole man, body and soul, is raised to be judged, and that the wicked are emphatically not annihilated. In his Plea he sets the two post-mortem destinies side by side — life “near God” or a “worse one and in fire” — and in his treatise on the resurrection he grounds the persistence of the person: man “must continue for ever,” each receiving in the body “what he has done.” The endless conscious duration of the fire is left for the later Latin apologists to spell out; Athenagoras lays the two pillars on which they build.

Claims & evidence

Two destinies after death — and the wicked are not annihilated

…since we know that God is witness to what we think and what we say both by night and by day, and that He, being Himself light, sees all things in our heart, we are persuaded that when we are removed from the present life we shall live another life, better than the present one, and heavenly, not earthly (since we shall abide near God, and with God, free from all change or suffering in the soul, not as flesh, even though we shall have flesh, but as heavenly spirit), or, falling with the rest, a worse one and in fire; for God has not made us as sheep or beasts of burden, a mere by-work, and that we should perish and be annihilated.

— A Plea for the Christians, ch. 31, source. Athenagoras is defending the Christians against the charge of secret vice: they will not sin, he argues, because they know a judgment awaits — either the heavenly life or “a worse one and in fire.” The clause that matters for the later doctrine is the reason he gives for the second fate’s permanence: God did not make man a disposable “by-work” destined to “perish and be annihilated.” The wicked, like the righteous, endure; only their state differs. This is the express denial of annihilation that the tradition would build the endless punishment on — a generation before Tertullian made the endlessness explicit.

The whole man must continue for ever, and be judged in the body

But that which has received both understanding and reason is man, not the soul by itself. Man, therefore, who consists of the two parts, must continue for ever. But it is impossible for him to continue unless he rise again. For if no resurrection were to take place, the nature of men as men would not continue.

— On the Resurrection of the Dead, ch. 15, source. The whole argument of the treatise is that man — the union of body and soul — was made for “perpetual duration” (ch. 12) and so “must continue for ever.” Because the deeds of this life are done by the man, not the soul alone, the man must be raised to answer for them: each “may, in accordance with justice, receive what he has done by the body, whether it be good or bad” (ch. 18). The persistence of the whole person and the bodily judgment are exactly the metaphysical groundwork the doctrine of eternal conscious torment requires — a raised body that can suffer, and a person who cannot lapse into non-existence.

Notes

  • Groundwork, not yet the doctrine. Athenagoras nowhere spells out that the fire is endless or that the suffering is conscious and unceasing; the “worse life in fire” is asserted, its duration left unstated. What he establishes are the two pillars the later doctrine stands on — the persistence of the whole person and the rejection of annihilation — so he opens the timeline as premise-supplier, not as first witness to the worked doctrine.
  • The premise the conditionalist shares. Athenagoras grounds man’s perpetual duration in God’s purpose in creating him, not in a Platonic natural immortality of the soul — a nuance that matters for the later debate, since the conditionalist premise (immortality is God’s to give or withhold) is one the earliest fathers largely shared. The parting of the ways is over the outcome: whether God sustains the lost forever to be punished, or lets them perish. Whether eternal conscious torment was in fact the ante-Nicene default is weighed on its own argument page.
  • Athenagoras appears on this wiki also as a witness on other timelines — on the Nephilim and divine council, on the Old Testament canon, and on divorce and remarriage.
