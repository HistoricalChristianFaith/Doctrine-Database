John Chrysostom on eternal conscious torment

Dates: c. 347–407 · Wikipedia
Summary: John Chrysostom, the great Antiochene preacher and later archbishop of Constantinople, is among the most emphatic patristic witnesses to eternal conscious torment — and, unusually, he argues its endlessness head-on against fellow Christians who taught a milder, terminating hell. From the pulpit he presses Paul's “eternal destruction” and the Matthew 25:46 parallel to insist that the punishment is as unending as the life set beside it, while teaching that its sharpest pain is the everlasting loss of God.

Claims & evidence

Against those who make hell “temporary, not eternal”

There are many men, who form good hopes not by abstaining from their sins, but by thinking that hell is not so terrible as it is said to be, but milder than what is threatened, and temporary, not eternal; and about this they philosophize much. … But that it is not temporary, hear Paul now saying, concerning those who know not God, and who do not believe in the Gospel, that they shall suffer punishment, even eternal destruction. How then is that temporary which is everlasting?

— Homilies on Second Thessalonians, Homily 3 (on 2 Thessalonians 1:9), source (Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, ser. 1, vol. 13). This is the decisive text: Chrysostom names the position he opposes — that hell is “temporary, not eternal” — and refutes it directly from Paul's phrase “eternal destruction” (the very verse the conditionalist reads for extinction, which Chrysostom instead reads as an everlasting ruin). His answer to the milder-hell party is a single question: “How then is that temporary which is everlasting?”

“If the life be eternal, the punishment is eternal”

But the case is not so with the other life. For that there, there will never be any release, hear from His own mouth, Their worm will not die, nor their fire be quenched. And these shall go into everlasting life, but these into everlasting punishment. Now if the life be eternal, the punishment is eternal.

— Homilies on Romans, Homily 25, source. Here Chrysostom joins the undying worm of Mark 9:48 to the grammar of Matthew 25:46, drawing the same inference the whole tradition draws from that verse: the one word “eternal” governs the life and the punishment alike, so the two must be equally endless. The flat clause “there will never be any release” rules out the restoration.

“That deathless punishment which has no end”

No one can escape His judgment, but all who in any way live in covetousness and rapine will undergo the punishment allotted by Him—that deathless punishment which has no end,—in the same way as also did this rich man.

— Four Discourses, chiefly on the parable of the rich man and Lazarus, Discourse 1, source. Expounding the rich man of Luke 16, Chrysostom draws the moral that the covetous face not a passing chastisement but “that deathless punishment which has no end.” The pairing of “deathless” with “no end” captures the doctrine exactly: the lost do not die out of their suffering, and the suffering does not cease.

Notes

  • The worst torment is the loss of God. Elsewhere in the same Homily 3 on 2 Thessalonians Chrysostom argues that the deepest anguish of hell is not the fire but exclusion from the presence of Christ — to fall from that glory, he says, is a heavier thing than the pains of hell itself. The endlessness he insists on is therefore an endless separation, not merely an endless burning; the two are of a piece.
  • An Antiochene, arguing the plain sense. Chrysostom belonged to the Antiochene school, which read Scripture grammatically rather than by Alexandrian allegory — the same school that produced named universalists (Diodore and Theodore). That he presses the endless reading from the grammar of Matthew 25:46, not from allegory, tells against the idea that eternalism was merely the literalists' default and universalism the sophisticated reading; the Antiochenes divided on the point.
  • Chrysostom appears on this wiki as a witness on many timelines; on the last things he is continuous with his teaching on the fate of the dead and the intermediate state.
