John Chrysostom on eternal conscious torment

Dates: c. 347–407 · Wikipedia
Summary: John Chrysostom, preacher at Antioch and later archbishop of Constantinople, argues the endlessness of hell against Christians who held a milder, terminating punishment. In his homilies he cites Paul's “eternal destruction” and the parallel of Matthew 25:46 to hold that the punishment is as unending as the life set beside it.

Claims & evidence

Against those who make hell “temporary, not eternal”

There are many men, who form good hopes not by abstaining from their sins, but by thinking that hell is not so terrible as it is said to be, but milder than what is threatened, and temporary, not eternal; and about this they philosophize much. … But that it is not temporary, hear Paul now saying, concerning those who know not God, and who do not believe in the Gospel, that they shall suffer punishment, even eternal destruction. How then is that temporary which is everlasting?

— Homilies on Second Thessalonians, Homily 3 (on 2 Thessalonians 1:9), source (Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, ser. 1, vol. 13). Chrysostom names the position he opposes — that hell is “temporary, not eternal” — and answers it from Paul's phrase “eternal destruction,” which he reads as an everlasting punishment; holders of conditional immortality read the same verse as extinction.

“If the life be eternal, the punishment is eternal”

But the case is not so with the other life. For that there, there will never be any release, hear from His own mouth, Their worm will not die, nor their fire be quenched. And these shall go into everlasting life, but these into everlasting punishment. Now if the life be eternal, the punishment is eternal.

— Homilies on Romans, Homily 25, source. Here Chrysostom joins the undying worm of Mark 9:48 to Matthew 25:46: the one word “eternal” governs the life and the punishment alike, so on his reading the two are equally endless.

“That deathless punishment which has no end”

No one can escape His judgment, but all who in any way live in covetousness and rapine will undergo the punishment allotted by Him—that deathless punishment which has no end,—in the same way as also did this rich man.

— Four Discourses, chiefly on the parable of the rich man and Lazarus, Discourse 1, source. Expounding the rich man of Luke 16, Chrysostom draws the moral that the covetous face not a passing chastisement but “that deathless punishment which has no end.” He pairs “deathless” with “no end.”

Reception and debate

  • The loss of God. In Homilies on Matthew, Homily 23, §9 (Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, ser. 1, vol. 10), Chrysostom distinguishes two punishments in the burning of the tree cut down: “For he that is burnt is also cast of course out of God's kingdom; and this latter punishment is more grievous than the other. Now I know indeed that many tremble only at hell, but I affirm the loss of that glory to be a far greater punishment than hell.”
  • Antioch. Philip Schaff names Chrysostom's teacher Diodore of Tarsus and Theodore of Mopsuestia among those who taught universal salvation, and reports that Nitzsch included “possibly Chrysostom” among universalists, while holding for his own part that “the passages on the other side are stronger” (History of the Christian Church, vol. II, §157 and n. 1157); McClymond lists Chrysostom among the authors who affirm everlasting punishment (Theological Studies 76.4, 2015, p. 822).
  • Chrysostom is also a witness on many timelines; on the last things he is continuous with his teaching on the fate of the dead and the intermediate state.
