Basil of Caesarea on eternal conscious torment

Dates: c. 330–379 · Wikipedia
Summary: Basil the Great, bishop of Caesarea, is cited for unending punishment; Michael McClymond counts him among the authors who affirm it, and Ilaria Ramelli counts “perhaps even Basil” among the authors she reads as universalists. In a pastoral letter to a fallen consecrated virgin he sets before her “anguish without end” from which “there is no release after death.” He is also cited from Shorter Rules 267, whose authenticity Ramelli disputes. His place in the restoration debate is set out on his apokatastasis page.

Claims & evidence

“Anguish without end… no release after death”

Where then shall that soul hide which in the sight of all these spectators shall suddenly be revealed in its fullness of shame? With what kind of body shall it sustain those endless and unbearable pangs in the place of fire unquenched, and of the worm that perishes and never dies, and of depth of Hades, dark and horrible; bitter wailings, loud lamenting, weeping and gnashing of teeth and anguish without end? From all these woes there is no release after death; no device, no means of coming forth from the chastisement of pain.

— Letter 46 (“To a Fallen Virgin”), §5, source (Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, ser. 2, vol. 8). Written to recall a consecrated virgin who had broken her vow, the letter presses the judgment as motive. In it the pangs are “endless,” the fire “unquenched,” the worm one that “never dies,” and “there is no release after death.”

Reception and debate

  • Shorter Rules 267. Basil is also cited for eternal punishment from Regulae brevius tractatae (Shorter Rules) 267 — “it is a demonic deceit … [to] invent a kind of an end of the punishments… if the punishment should once end, the heavenly life would necessarily have to end too.” (Q. 267, source; the fuller passage is quoted on the restoration timeline's Basil page.) Michael McClymond reports that “Ramelli also insists that Basil of Caesarea’s statements on everlasting punishment were likewise not original but probably were interpolated into the texts (354–58),” and replies that “Her claims of interpolation are designed to uphold Origen’s reputation and minimize disagreements between Origen and other ancient Christian authors” (Theological Studies 76.4, 2015, p. 818). Ramelli, in her reply, writes of “Basil’s problematic question-and-answer passage against apokatastasis, where he (if the passage is authentic) stated that his own brother, whom he appointed bishop, and saintly sister are inspired by the devil”: “Here I hypothesize not only—as McC has it—an interpolation (common in Basil’s question-and-answer works; moreover, anti-Origenian interpolations and glosses are abundantly attested in the case of Nyssen in the manuscripts themselves), but also pastoral concerns.” If the text is Basil’s, she holds, it “could be explained in light of the intended monastic, not scholarly, audience of that oeuvre. For Basil shared Origen’s own pastoral worries about the disclosure of the apokatastasis doctrine to simple or immature people”; and she cites Orosius, “who cannot be suspected of embracing apokatastasis, and who explicitly attributes this doctrine to Basil” (“Reply to Professor Michael McClymond,” Theological Studies 76.4, 2015, pp. 831–832).
  • The Philocalia and his family. With Gregory of Nazianzus he compiled the Philocalia of Origen; his sister Macrina and brother Gregory of Nyssa appear on the universal-restoration timeline, where Basil is also a witness (his apokatastasis page).
  • How he is counted. McClymond lists Basil among the authors who affirm everlasting punishment (p. 822); Ramelli counts “perhaps even Basil” among the authors she reads as universalists, and cites Basil with Augustine as attesting that the restoration was widely held in his day (“Reply to Professor Michael McClymond,” Theological Studies 76.4, 2015, pp. 833–834). How widely unending punishment was taught before Nicaea is discussed on its interpretation page.
