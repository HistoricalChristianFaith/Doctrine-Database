How widely was unending punishment of the lost taught in the church before Nicaea?

Disputed reading · on Eternal conscious torment · witnesses: Ignatius of Antioch, Justin Martyr, and the Christian writers before 325

Related readings: How widely was universal restoration held among the Church Fathers? · Did the second-century writers who denied the soul's natural immortality hold conditional immortality?

The passage

Such an one becoming defiled [in this way], shall go away into everlasting fire, and so shall every one that hearkens unto him.

— Ignatius of Antioch, Epistle to the Ephesians 16 (c. 108).1

And Plato, in like manner, used to say that Rhadamanthus and Minos would punish the wicked who came before them; and we say that the same thing will be done, but at the hand of Christ, and upon the wicked in the same bodies united again to their spirits which are now to undergo everlasting punishment; and not only, as Plato said, for a period of a thousand years.

— Justin Martyr, First Apology 8 (c. 155).2

The question

Christian writers before the Council of Nicaea (325) speak of the punishment of the wicked as “everlasting” or “unquenchable,” and several of them deny that the soul is immortal by its own nature. The dispute is how widely unending punishment was taught in this period: as the common teaching from the earliest writers on; as an open question in the earliest writers, beside a widely held hope of universal restoration; or as a later displacement of an original conditional immortality. Each side appeals to this record in claiming continuity with the earliest church.

An open question in the earliest writers, beside a widely held hope of restoration

On this reading the earliest writers' word “everlasting” does not settle the duration of punishment, and universal restoration was widely held in the early centuries; Ballou adds that endless punishment is first stated expressly by Tertullian.

Held by: Hosea Ballou 2d (Universalist pastor, The Ancient History of Universalism, 1829); Ilaria Ramelli (historian of ancient philosophy and patristics, The Christian Doctrine of Apokatastasis, 2013; reply to McClymond, 2015); David Bentley Hart (Orthodox, That All Shall Be Saved, 2019) ⚠ unverified.3

Ballou set out “in his own words, the opinion of every writer whose works are extant” to the end of the second century. Of the seven writers he finds calling it “the everlasting, the eternal fire or torment,” “there are three who certainly did not think it endless, as two of them believed the damned would be annihilated, and the other asserted their restoration to bliss,” so “their use of the word everlasting, is no criterion.” In the earliest writings, “that of Clemens Romanus and those of Ignatius,” future punishment is “either wholly omitted, or else expressed in the most indefinite manner.”4 For Ballou, Tertullian, c. 200–204, “is thought to have been the first christian writer who expressly asserted that the torments of the damned will be of ‘equal duration’ with the happiness of the blest.”5 Ramelli, drawing on her and David Konstan's study of the terms, holds that scholars should not list an author among the anti-universalists “just because he uses πῦρ αἰώνιον, κόλασις αἰώνιος, θάνατος αἰώνιος, or the like,” since universalists “from Origen to Gregory Nyssen to Evagrius” used the same phrases for “‘otherworldly,’ or ‘long-lasting,’ fire, educative punishment, and death.” On her reading “Ignatius, as others among ‘the 68,’ never mentions eternal punishment,” and before Origen the restoration is found in “Bardaisan, Clement, the Apocalypse of Peter's Rainer Fragment, parts of the Sibylline Oracles, and arguably of the NT, especially Paul's letters.”6 Hart, as quoted by Paul Axton, writes “that the universalist faction was at its most numerous at least as a relative ratio of believers, in the church's first half millennium,” for whom “hell was the fire of purification described by the Apostle Paul in the third chapter of I Corinthians.”7 On this reading unending punishment is one strand of early Christian teaching that grew more prominent from the end of the second century, beside a restoration taught by many of the period's leading theologians.

The common teaching of the church from the earliest writers on

On this reading the martyrs and Fathers of the first three centuries taught the unending punishment of the wicked, from the apostolic fathers onward, and universal restoration was a minority view first taught by Origen.

Held by: E. B. Pusey (Anglican, What is of Faith as to Everlasting Punishment?, 1880); Joseph Hontheim (Catholic, “Hell,” Catholic Encyclopedia, 1910); Kallistos Ware (Orthodox bishop, “Dare We Hope for the Salvation of All?”, 1998; on Christian history as a whole, not the pre-Nicene period); Michael McClymond (historian, Saint Louis University, Theological Studies, 2015).8

Pusey set out “the evidence that the eternity of punishment was held as matter of faith by the martyrs of the three first centuries, and also by the Fathers generally.” He gathers the early writers “not as authorities … but as Witnesses” to the belief of their times, under the Vincentian rule “‘what has been taught, always, every where, and by all’”, from Ignatius, Polycarp and Hermas through Justin (“12 statements”), Irenaeus and Tertullian, and Origen, “although his own speculations went against the belief which he attested.” The homily he attributes to Clement speaks of the ungodly “punished with grievous torments in fire unquenchable.”9 Schaff holds that “Everlasting Punishment of the wicked always was, and always will be the orthodox theory,” and that “Origen was the first Christian Universalist.”10 Hontheim writes that “the Fathers, from the very earliest times, are unanimous in teaching that the wicked will be punished after death,” and that on its eternity “The teaching of the fathers is not less clear and decisive.”11 McClymond, counting from Brian Daley's survey of patristic eschatology, finds “68 authors and texts that clearly affirm the eternal punishment of the wicked” against “perhaps four” universalists “in the Origenian sense,” the 68 coming “from each of the centuries surveyed, from both East and West.” He finds “no unambiguous cases of universalist teaching prior to Origen.”12 Ware, who hopes for the salvation of all, writes of the whole tradition that “in East and West alike—but more particularly in the West because of the influence of St Augustine of Hippo—the voices raised in favor of universal salvation remain a small minority. Most Christians, at any rate until the twentieth century, have assumed that the main part of the human race will end up in an everlasting hell.”13 On this reading the endless fire of Tertullian and his Latin successors states in fuller form what the church's earliest writers and martyrs already taught.

A later teaching that displaced an original conditional immortality

On this reading the earliest writers taught that immortality is God's gift to the saved and that the lost finally perish, and unending punishment came to prevail only as a doctrine of the naturally immortal soul spread in the church.

Held by: Emmanuel Pétavel-Olliff (conditionalist, The Problem of Immortality, English trans. 1892); John H. Roller (Advent Christian, The Doctrine of Immortality in the Early Church, rev. 2008).14

Pétavel reads Irenaeus as seeming “to exhaust the vocabulary at his disposal in order to deny the immortality of the unsaved,” and holds that this teaching “was, as it were, drowned in the rising tide of the Platonic theory which was made to triumph in the Church by the false Clementines, Tertullian, Minucius Felix, Cyprian, Jerome, and especially Augustine. Nevertheless, the primitive teaching was maintained here and there.”15 Roller, classifying thirty writers from the apostolic fathers to c. 310 as “Conditionalists” or “Naturalists,” counts “16 definite, 4 probable” conditionalists to “8 definite, 1 probable” naturalists, finds “that almost all of the Church Fathers who wrote before AD 200 were Conditionalists, and that most of those who wrote between then and AD 310 were Naturalists,” and concludes “that Conditional Immortality was the original, and predominant, doctrine of the early Church.”16 He presents the two positions as a debate within one church: “a doctrinal discussion between individuals, and among groups, all of whom were members of the same worldwide Body of Christ.” On this reading the end of the second century marks a change of doctrine, from immortality as God's gift to the saved to the unending punishment of an immortal soul.
