Tertullian on eternal conscious torment

Dates: c. 155–220 · Wikipedia
Summary: The North African apologist Tertullian describes a punishing fire that burns forever without consuming its object, and argues against reading the “destruction” of soul and body in Matthew 10:28 as their annihilation: an eternal penalty, he holds, requires that the substances punished be eternal. The Universalist historian Hosea Ballou 2d (1829) reports that he “is thought to have been the first christian writer who expressly asserted that the torments of the damned will be of ‘equal duration’ with the happiness of the blest” (The Ancient History of Universalism, ch. III, § V).

Claims & evidence

The “secret fire” (Apology 48)

The philosophers are familiar as well as we with the distinction between a common and a secret fire. Thus that which is in common use is far different from that which we see in divine judgments… for it does not consume what it scorches, but while it burns it repairs. So the mountains continue ever burning; and a person struck by lightning is even now kept safe from any destroying flame. A notable proof this of the fire eternal! a notable example of the endless judgment which still supplies punishment with fuel! The mountains burn, and last. How will it be with the wicked and the enemies of God?

— Apology, ch. 48, source. Tertullian argues from natural analogues — volcanoes that burn without exhausting themselves, bodies struck by lightning yet not destroyed — to a divine fire whose distinctive property is that it torments perpetually without annihilating. The same chapter states the fate of the impious directly: they “shall be consigned to the punishment of everlasting fire—that fire which, from its very nature indeed, directly ministers to their incorruptibility.” On his account the fire does not destroy the damned but preserves them for torment.

The “killing” of soul and body (On the Resurrection of the Flesh 35)

If, therefore, any one shall violently suppose that the destruction of the soul and the flesh in hell amounts to a final annihilation of the two substances, and not to their penal treatment (as if they were to be consumed, not punished), let him recollect that the fire of hell is eternal—expressly announced as an everlasting penalty; and let him then admit that it is from this circumstance that this never-ending “killing” is more formidable than a merely human murder, which is only temporal. He will then come to the conclusion that substances must be eternal, when their penal “killing” is an eternal one.

— On the Resurrection of the Flesh, ch. 35, source. Tertullian addresses the reading of Matthew 10:28 (“him which is able to destroy both soul and body in hell”) as annihilation. He grants that the verse speaks of “destruction,” but argues that an eternal killing is not a terminating one: for the killing to be endless, its object must endure to be killed. Reception and debate

  • Earlier writers. Whether writers before Tertullian already taught unending punishment is disputed. Ballou adds that his report “is, indeed, no positive proof that the same opinion had never been entertained before”; E. B. Pusey (What is of Faith as to Everlasting Punishment?, 1880) and Philip Schaff (History of the Christian Church, vol. II, §157) list earlier witnesses, from Ignatius on, to the belief in eternal punishment. The readings are set out on the interpretation page. Pétavel names Tertullian among those by whom, on his reading, “the Platonic theory” was “made to triumph in the Church” (The Problem of Immortality, 1892, p. 242).
  • Contemporaries. Minucius Felix describes an “intelligent fire [that] burns the limbs and restores them” (Octavius 35).
  • Tertullian is also a witness on other timelines.
