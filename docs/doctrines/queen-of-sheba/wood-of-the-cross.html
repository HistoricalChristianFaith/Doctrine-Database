The medieval Latin legend: the Queen of Sheba foresees the wood of the Cross

Dates: c. 12th–13th c. (a reception stratum, not a single source: the Latin wood-of-the-Cross legend, gathered and made famous in Jacobus de Voragine’s Legenda Aurea, c. 1260, whose English printing by William Caxton dates to 1483, and painted into a whole fresco cycle by Piero della Francesca at Arezzo, c. 1452–1466) · Golden Legend, “The Invention of the Holy Cross” (Caxton)
Summary: Where the Jewish and Islamic legends spun the queen’s riddles, her hairy legs, and her demon-blood, the Latin Middle Ages gave her a prophetic role of its own — and a Christian one. In the legend of the wood of the Cross, a beam kept in Solomon’s house (in the developed tale and in art, a plank or footbridge laid over a stream) is the very wood on which Christ will one day be crucified. Coming to visit Solomon, the Queen of Sheba perceives its future: she worships it and warns the king that “the Saviour of all the world should be hanged thereon,” by whom “the realm of the Jews shall be defaced and cease” — whereupon Solomon has it buried. She becomes, in this stratum, the Gentile who foresees the Cross — the Christian counterpart to the Jewish and Islamic legend-growth, and the pictorial complement to the Fathers’ reading of her as the Gentile church coming to the true Solomon. The episode was made famous by the most-read book of the later Middle Ages, the Golden Legend (c. 1260), and became a stock scene of Christian art, most memorably in Piero della Francesca’s Legend of the True Cross at Arezzo. It is presented here as legend and reception, not history.

Where it sits

This page is best read not as one dated witness but as a reception stratum — the deposit of what the Latin West made of the queen — slotted on the timeline at its medieval culmination. The wood-of-the-Cross legend is older than the book that made it famous: it grows out of the apocryphal Gospel of Nicodemus and the Latin tree-of-the-Cross tractates (the Post peccatum Adae and kindred texts of the twelfth and thirteenth centuries), which trace a single tree from a shoot Seth plants on Adam’s grave, through Solomon, to Calvary. What gathers the whole chain and lodges it in the devotional imagination is Jacobus de Voragine’s Legenda Aurea (the “Golden Legend,” c. 1260), whose chapters on the finding and the raising of the Cross carry the queen’s episode; and what fixes it in the eye is a century of Christian painting, above all Piero della Francesca’s Arezzo cycle. The stratum is therefore placed among the medieval reception traditions — after the patristic reading it completes and after the Islamic tale it parallels, and beside the roughly contemporary Ethiopian Kebra Nagast.1

Claims & evidence

The legend in the age’s most-read book: the Golden Legend (c. 1260)

The reading reaches its widest audience in the Legenda Aurea — compiled about 1260 and, in William Caxton’s English of 1483, one of the most widely read books of the later Middle Ages. Its chapter “The Invention [i.e. finding] of the Holy Cross” traces the wood of the Cross back through the centuries, and gives the Queen of Sheba a pivotal scene in that history:

When Seth came again he found his father dead and planted this tree upon his grave, and it endured there unto the time of Solomon. And because he saw that it was fair, he did do hew it down and set it in his house named Saltus. And when the Queen of Sheba came to visit Solomon, she worshipped this tree, because she said the Saviour of all the world should be hanged thereon, by whom the realm of the Jews shall be defaced and cease. Solomon for this cause made it to be taken up and dolven deep in the ground.

— Jacobus de Voragine, The Golden Legend, “The Invention of the Holy Cross” (trans. Caxton). The queen’s act is the hinge of the tale: she alone reads the future in the timber, worships it, and foretells both the crucifixion and its consequence for “the realm of the Jews” — and it is her prophecy that moves Solomon to bury the wood, which will later be dug up to make the Cross. Voragine underlines her place in the wood’s long history in the same chapter’s summing-up of why the day is called an “invention” (a finding): the Cross “was found of Seth in Paradise terrestrial, and also it was found of Solomon in the Mount of Lebanon, and of the Queen of Sheba in the temple of Solomon, and of the Jews in the water of Piscine, and on this day it was found of Helena in the Mount of Calvary.” The queen stands in that chain of five “finders” of the Cross, between Solomon and the Jews.2

The beam and the bridge: the fuller legend and its art

Caxton’s abridgement has the queen worship the tree where it stands in Solomon’s house; the fuller tellings of the True-Cross legend give the scene its most enduring form. There the rejected timber — too long, then too short, never fitting Solomon’s building — is finally laid across a stream as a plank or footbridge; and when the queen comes to it on her way to Solomon she will not set foot on it, but kneels and worships, perceiving that the Redeemer of the world will hang upon that very wood. This is the version that painters seized on. In Piero della Francesca’s fresco cycle The Legend of the True Cross (Basilica of San Francesco, Arezzo, c. 1452–1466), whose whole programme follows the Golden Legend, the panel The Queen of Sheba in Adoration of the Wood and the Meeting of Solomon and the Queen of Sheba shows her kneeling in reverence before the wooden beam — “she worshiped the beams made from the tree, and informed Solomon that the Saviour would hang from that tree” — before her formal reception at Solomon’s court. The Gentile queen who, in Scripture, comes to marvel at Solomon’s wisdom has become the Gentile prophetess who recognises Christ’s Cross centuries before Calvary.3

Notes

  • Reception, not history. Nothing in 1 Kings 10 or its Chronicles parallel knows of a sacred tree, a buried beam, or a prophecy of the Cross; the whole episode is a medieval Christian legend, an outgrowth of the apocryphal wood-of-the-Cross tradition, not a datum about the tenth century BC. Its interest is what the church made of the queen, not what she did.
  • The Christian counterpart to the Jewish and Islamic legends. Each later tradition gave the queen a new role: the rabbis a riddler and, at length, a demon; the Muslim commentators Bilqis, the jinn-born convert; and the Latin West a Gentile prophetess of the Cross. The Christian legend is the most theologically pointed of the three, and it dovetails with the Fathers’ older reading of her as a type of the Gentile church that comes from the ends of the earth to the true Solomon — here she does not merely typify the nations’ coming faith but is shown foreseeing its object, the crucified Christ.
  • “The realm of the Jews shall be defaced and cease.” The prophecy the legend puts in the queen’s mouth carries the supersessionist edge of much medieval Passion-devotion — the Cross as the end of the old covenant — the same edge the Fathers pressed in reading her as the outsider whose faith condemns unbelieving Israel (Matthew 12:42). It is reported here as part of the legend’s wording, not endorsed.
  • Editions. The Golden Legend is quoted in William Caxton’s English (1483), via the reader’s edition of Richard Stracke and the Fordham Medieval Sourcebook; Piero’s cycle is at the Basilica of San Francesco, Arezzo.
