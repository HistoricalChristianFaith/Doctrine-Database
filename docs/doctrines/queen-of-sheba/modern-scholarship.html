Modern scholarship — what critical study makes of the Queen of Sheba

Dates: a survey of modern critical scholarship and archaeology (chiefly 20th–21st century) · representative works: Kenneth A. Kitchen, Documentation for Ancient Arabia (1994–2000); Israel Finkelstein & Neil Asher Silberman, The Bible Unearthed (2001); St John Simpson (ed.), Queen of Sheba: Treasures from Ancient Yemen (British Museum Press, 2002); Jacob Lassner, Demonizing the Queen of Sheba (1993); Stuart Munro-Hay, The Quest for the Ark of the Covenant (2005); Yigal Levin, “The Queen of Sheba: Sabean or Ethiopian?”
Summary: This page surveys what modern archaeological, epigraphic, philological, and reception-critical study has said about the Queen of Sheba. Four questions recur. Kitchen and Levin identify the biblical Sheba with the South-Arabian kingdom of Saba (Yemen), whose material record is well documented. Whether a tenth-century queen stands behind 1 Kings 10 divides Kitchen, who finds her credible, from Finkelstein and Silberman, who date the Sabaean kingdom’s florescence to the eighth century BC. Lassner reads the queen’s later career in Jewish and Islamic legend as a single, cross-fertilizing tradition that first demonized and then domesticated her. Munro-Hay dates the Ethiopian Solomonic charter to the late thirteenth century; whether it preserves older history is set out on the disputed-reading page.1

Claims & evidence

The kingdom of Saba and the archaeology of Marib

The South-Arabian kingdom of Saba, in what is now Yemen, is documented by its own remains: its capital Marib, its colossal dam (a wonder of ancient engineering that watered the oasis for over a millennium), its temples to the moon-god Almaqah, and above all its own inscriptions in the Ancient South Arabian script; how early that record begins is part of the historicity debate below. The 2002 British Museum exhibition Queen of Sheba: Treasures from Ancient Yemen put that material record before the public: Saba was a wealthy caravan state astride the frankincense road, and the queen’s gifts of gold, spices, and precious stones in 1 Kings 10 match its trade. Kenneth Kitchen and Yigal Levin identify the biblical Sheba with this Saba rather than with Ethiopia across the Red Sea; Josephus and the Kebra Nagast place the queen in Ethiopia.2

The queen’s historicity

Whether a queen visited Solomon around 950 BC divides the scholars surveyed here. Kenneth Kitchen reads the episode against the documented incense trade and the wider ancient-Near-Eastern pattern of long-distance royal exchange, and holds a South-Arabian caravan monarch of Solomon’s era to be credible. Israel Finkelstein and Neil Asher Silberman note that continuous Sabaean epigraphy, monumental Marib, and the earliest external mentions of Saba all begin in the eighth century BC, and judge the Sabaean kingdom to have “flourished only… some two hundred years after” Solomon, reading the narrated visit as a later trade world set in Solomon’s reign. No contemporary inscription naming the queen or her journey has been reported. Assyrian records name “queens of the Arabs” (Zabibe, Samsi, and others), and the Sabaean ruler Itamra sent tribute to Sargon II c. 715 BC.3

The legend as one tradition: demonizing and domesticating the queen

The queen’s afterlife in the Targum Sheni, the Talmud, the medieval kabbalah, the Qur’an and its commentators, and the Ethiopic Kebra Nagast is read by Jacob Lassner, in Demonizing the Queen of Sheba, as a single, cross-fertilizing tradition, whose logic he traces: the shared glass-floor / exposed-legs motif (Jewish and Islamic alike), the hairy or ass-hooved legs read as the folkloric mark of a demon, the de-gendering of the queen in the Talmud (“not a woman but a kingdom”), and the kabbalistic fusion of the Queen of Sheba with Lilith together turn the dignified monarch of 1 Kings into a figure of anxiety about female power and the boundary between the human and the demonic — before Islamic and Ethiopian tradition domesticate her again as the convert Bilqis and the dynastic mother Makeda. Lassner reads the growth of the legend as a mirror of the cultures that retold her.4

The Ethiopian charter

The Ethiopian claim — that the queen was Makeda, bore Solomon the son Menelik I, and that Menelik carried the true Ark of the Covenant to Aksum, founding a Solomonic dynasty — is set out in the fourteenth-century Kebra Nagast. Stuart Munro-Hay dates the descent claim to the late thirteenth century, when the “restored” Solomonic line of 1270 took power, and the historians Harold Marcus and David Northrup are reported to read the book’s stories as legends created to legitimate that dynasty (⚠ unverified); the report that the preceding Zagwe king Lalibela had claimed descent from Moses rather than Solomon is ⚠ unverified. Munro-Hay’s investigation of the Ark tradition concludes that the Aksum sanctuary guards a tabot — an altar-tablet, as every Ethiopian church holds — not the biblical Ark. E. A. Wallis Budge (1922) held that the descent tradition was known in Ethiopia centuries before the book was compiled. The charter shaped the dynasty and its church from 1270 to 1974.
