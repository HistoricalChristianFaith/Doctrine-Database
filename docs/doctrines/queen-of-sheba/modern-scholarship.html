Modern scholarship — what critical study makes of the Queen of Sheba

Dates: a survey of modern critical scholarship and archaeology (chiefly 20th–21st century) · representative works: Kenneth A. Kitchen, Documentation for Ancient Arabia (1994–2000); Israel Finkelstein & Neil Asher Silberman, The Bible Unearthed (2001); St John Simpson (ed.), Queen of Sheba: Treasures from Ancient Yemen (British Museum Press, 2002); Jacob Lassner, Demonizing the Queen of Sheba (1993); Stuart Munro-Hay, The Quest for the Ark of the Covenant (2005); Yigal Levin, “The Queen of Sheba: Sabean or Ethiopian?”
Summary: This page gathers what careful modern study — archaeological, epigraphic, philological, and reception-critical — makes of the Queen of Sheba, and does so as a survey, not a verdict. Four things recur across the literature: the biblical Sheba is the South-Arabian kingdom of Saba (Yemen), not a legend, and its material record is now well documented; whether a real tenth-century queen stands behind 1 Kings 10 is genuinely disputed, since the monumental Sabaean state is best attested only from the eighth century BC; the queen’s later career in Jewish, Christian, and Islamic legend is a single, cross-fertilizing tradition that progressively demonized and then domesticated her; and the Ethiopian Solomonic charter is a medieval dynastic construction, not a tenth-century memory. The contested questions this survey opens — where Sheba lay, whether the queen was historical, whether the Ethiopian descent claim holds, whether the “queen of the south” typology is sound — are weighed, each on its own terms, on the argument pages of the Queen of Sheba timeline, not settled here.1

Claims & evidence

The kingdom is real: the archaeology of Saba and Marib

The first result of modern work is that Sheba is not a fairy-tale country. The South-Arabian kingdom of Saba, in what is now Yemen, is one of the best-attested civilizations of the ancient incense lands: its capital Marib, its colossal dam (a wonder of ancient engineering that watered the oasis for over a millennium), its temples to the moon-god Almaqah, and above all its own inscriptions in the Ancient South Arabian script give it a documented history reaching back into the early first millennium BC. The 2002 British Museum exhibition Queen of Sheba: Treasures from Ancient Yemen put that material record before the public: Saba was a wealthy caravan state astride the frankincense road, and the queen’s gifts of gold, spices, and precious stones in 1 Kings 10 name exactly its trade. That the biblical Sheba is this Saba — rather than Ethiopia across the Red Sea — is the mainstream philological and archaeological reading (Kitchen; Levin), weighed in full in the homeland argument.2

The queen’s historicity: a credible type, an unconfirmable token

Whether a real queen made a real visit to a real Solomon around 950 BC is where scholarship genuinely divides. The maximalist reads the episode against the documented incense trade and the wider ancient-Near-Eastern pattern of long-distance royal exchange, and finds a South-Arabian caravan monarch of Solomon’s era entirely plausible (Kitchen). The minimalist notes that continuous Sabaean epigraphy, monumental Marib, and the earliest external mentions of Saba all begin only in the eighth century BC — so that Finkelstein and Silberman judge the Sabaean kingdom to have “flourished only… some two hundred years after” Solomon, making the narrated visit a later trade world read back onto a golden age. No contemporary inscription names the queen or her journey. The honest middle grants that female Arabian rule and Sabaean long-distance embassy are both independently attested (the Assyrian “queens of the Arabs”; Saba’s tribute to Sargon II) — establishing the type without confirming the instance. The whole debate is set out on the historicity argument.3

The legend as one tradition: demonizing and domesticating the queen

The third result is reception-critical. The queen’s afterlife in the Targum Sheni, the Talmud, the medieval kabbalah, the Qur’an and its commentators, and the Ethiopic Kebra Nagast is not a scatter of unrelated tales but a single, cross-fertilizing tradition, and Jacob Lassner’s Demonizing the Queen of Sheba traces its logic: the shared glass-floor / exposed-legs motif (Jewish and Islamic alike), the hairy or ass-hooved legs read as the folkloric mark of a demon, the de-gendering of the queen in the Talmud (“not a woman but a kingdom”), and the kabbalistic fusion of the Queen of Sheba with Lilith together turn the dignified monarch of 1 Kings into a figure of anxiety about female power and the boundary between the human and the demonic — before Islamic and Ethiopian tradition domesticate her again as the convert Bilqis and the dynastic mother Makeda. The scholarship reads the growth as a mirror of the cultures that retold her, not as history recovered.4

The Ethiopian charter: a medieval construction, not a tenth-century memory

The fourth result concerns the most consequential of the legends. The Ethiopian claim — that the queen was Makeda, bore Solomon the son Menelik I, and that Menelik carried the true Ark of the Covenant to Aksum, founding a Solomonic dynasty — rests on a single fourteenth-century source, the Kebra Nagast, with no early or independent attestation. Modern study reads the descent claim as a medieval dynastic accretion, appearing exactly when the “restored” Solomonic line of 1270 needed a pedigree (the preceding Zagwe king Lalibela had claimed descent from Moses, not Solomon); and Stuart Munro-Hay’s investigation of the Ark tradition concludes that the Aksum sanctuary guards a tabot — an altar-tablet, as every Ethiopian church holds — not the biblical Ark. What is real is not the tenth-century history but the charter itself, which shaped a dynasty and a church for seven centuries. The full weighing is on the Solomonic-descent argument.5
