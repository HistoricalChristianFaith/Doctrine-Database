The Kebra Nagast: Makeda, Menelik, and the Ark carried to Aksum

Dates: compiled c. AD 1314–1322 (the Ethiopic Kebra Nagast, “The Glory of the Kings”; its colophon reports an Arabic version, claimed to descend from a Coptic archetype, made in the reign of ‘Amda Seyon I and then rendered into Ge‘ez by the scribe Isaac) · Wikipedia
Summary: The Ethiopian national epic gives the fullest Ethiopian account of the Queen of Sheba. It names her Makeda, queen of ‘Azeb; tells how her merchant Tamrin’s reports of Solomon’s wisdom draw her to Jerusalem; recounts the union Solomon obtains from her by a trick of an oath and a cup of water; and follows the son of that union, Bayna-Lehkem — ‘Ebna Hakim, “the son of the wise man,” better known as Menelik I — back to his father, where he is anointed king of Ethiopia under the throne-name David. As he departs, his companions carry off Zion, the Tabernacle of the Law of God (the Ark of the Covenant), by the command of an angel, and bring it to Ethiopia — where, the book teaches, God had willed it to dwell. On this narrative rests the charter of the Solomonic dynasty: the claim that Ethiopia’s kings descend from Solomon and Sheba through Menelik, and that the true Ark rests at Aksum. That charter was written into the Ethiopian constitutions of 1931 and 1955 and ran, at least by claim, down to the deposition of Haile Selassie in 1974.

Placement on the timeline

The Kebra Nagast (Ge‘ez for “The Glory of the Kings”) is a composite work of Ethiopian biblical narrative whose surviving Ethiopic form belongs to the fourteenth century. Its colophon, as Budge reports it, says that an Arabic version — itself claimed to have been made from a Coptic original — was produced in the reign of Gabra Maskal (‘Amda Seyon I), that is, between AD 1314 and 1344, and that the scribe Isaac (with several helpers) then rendered it into “the speech of Abyssinia”; Wikipedia reports a composition date of 1314–1321/22 on the colophon’s testimony. The king-list appended to one manuscript ends with Yekuno ‘Amlak, and on that ground W. Wright judged the book “a product of the fourteenth century” (Budge gives Yekuno ‘Amlak’s reign as 1270–1285, though in reporting Wright he says Yekuno ‘Amlak “died in 1344”). The book tells the dynasty’s account of its origins — that its blood ran back through Menelik to Solomon himself — and its age is disputed. Budge held the descent tradition to be centuries older than the book and the Solomonic line restored, not founded, when Yekuno ‘Amlak displaced the Zagwe kings in 1270; Stuart Munro-Hay dates the dynastic claim to the late thirteenth century, when Yekuno ‘Amlak took power, and Harold Marcus and David Northrup (⚠ unverified) are reported to read the book as legitimating that dynasty (see the disputed reading). The whole is quoted here from E. A. Wallis Budge’s 1922 English translation, The Queen of Sheba and Her Only Son Menyelek, made from Bezold’s edition of the Ethiopic text. The block is placed by the date of the Ethiopic text, among the medieval sources — after the Fathers’ reading of the queen as the Gentile church — not as a claim that the narrative is early.1

Claims & evidence

The queen is Makeda, drawn to Solomon by her merchant Tamrin

Where 1 Kings leaves the queen unnamed, the Kebra Nagast gives her a name, a country, and a reason for her journey. She is Makeda, queen of ‘Azeb (“the South”), and it is the return of her chief merchant — Tamrin, who traded with Solomon for the building of the Temple — that fires her longing to see the king’s wisdom for herself:

And that merchant, whose name was Tamrin, the merchant of the Queen of Ethiopia, went to Solomon the King; and Solomon took whatsoever he desired from him… Now that merchant was a man of great understanding, and he saw and comprehended the wisdom of Solomon, and he marvelled [thereat].

— Kebra Nagast 22–24 (Budge). The book opens its narrative of the queen under the heading “The Queen of the South,” keying the whole tradition to Jesus’ own phrase (Matthew 12:42; Luke 11:31). Makeda makes ready and travels to Jerusalem, where Solomon receives her, teaches her, and turns her from the worship of the sun to the God of Israel. The narrative keeps the biblical shape — the fame of Solomon, the great journey, the hard questions, the gifts — but fastens it to Ethiopia and gives the queen a name and lineage.2

The union: the oath, the cup of water, and the vision of the sun

The center of the epic is the night Solomon obtains the queen’s union by a stratagem. He swears he will not take her by force if she will swear not to take anything in his house by force; then he feeds her a thirst-making meal, sets a bowl of water where she will see it, and feigns sleep:

And Solomon seized her hand before she could drink the water, and said unto her, “Why hast thou broken the oath that thou hast sworn that thou wouldst not take by force anything that is in my house?”… And the Queen said, “Be free from thy oath, only let me drink water.” And he permitted her to drink water, and after she had drunk water he worked his will with her and they slept together.

— Kebra Nagast 30 (Budge). That night Solomon dreams of a brilliant sun that comes down over Israel and then withdraws to shine over Ethiopia for ever — the book’s sign that the glory is passing to the queen’s land. At her departure Solomon gives her a ring from his finger as a token: “if it happen that I obtain seed from thee, this ring shall be unto it a sign; and if it be a man child he shall come to me.”3

The birth of Menelik (Bayna-Lehkem), “the son of the wise man”

Homeward bound, the queen bears Solomon’s son:

And the Queen departed and came into the country of Bala Zadisareya nine months and five days after she had separated from King Solomon. And the pains of childbirth laid hold upon her, and she brought forth a man child.

— Kebra Nagast 32 (Budge). The boy is Bayna-Lehkem — the Ethiopic form the book glosses as ‘Ebna Hakim, “the son of the wise man,” the name Arab writers give as Ibn al-Hakim, and which later tradition fixed as Menelik I. Grown, he journeys to Jerusalem bearing his father’s ring; Solomon knows him at once by his resemblance to David and welcomes him. When Menelik insists on returning to his mother’s land, Solomon has him anointed king in the Holy of Holies and gives him the throne-name David — David II — and sends with him the firstborn sons of the elders of Israel to found a court in Ethiopia.4

The Ark of the Covenant carried to Ethiopia

The book’s climax — and the root of the Aksum claim — is the removal of the Ark. As Menelik prepares to leave, the young Azaryas (son of the priest Zadok) is told by an angel to take “Zion, the Tabernacle of the Law of God” with him; the four of them lift it from the sanctuary and leave carved wood in its place. The taking is presented as God’s own act:

And had it not been that God willed it Zion could not have been taken away forthwith. And the four of them carried Zion away…

— Kebra Nagast 48 (Budge). The Ark travels with Menelik’s company to Ethiopia, where “Zion, the habitation of the glory of God” becomes the guardian and blessing of the kingdom, and the queen charges her son to keep its service for ever, “from generation to generation.” The book reads the transfer as the fulfilment of Psalm 68:31 (“Ethiopia shall soon stretch out her hands unto God”): election has passed from an Israel that would reject its Messiah to an Ethiopia that keeps the Ark. The book thus claims both a Solomonic bloodline and the Ark for Ethiopia.5

The charter of a dynasty: from Menelik to Haile Selassie

The Kebra Nagast served as the founding document of Ethiopian kingship, grounding the ruling house’s legitimacy in descent from Solomon and Sheba through Menelik; Yekuno ‘Amlak had displaced the Zagwe in 1270, and the Ethiopic book was produced under his successors. Its authority was still live in the nineteenth century — King John IV wrote to Earl Granville on 10 August 1872 asking for the return of a copy taken from Magdala, “for in my Country my people will not obey my orders without it” — and it was written into modern law: the Ethiopian constitutions of 1931 and 1955 declared the emperor to descend “without interruption from the dynasty of Menelik I, son of the Queen of Ethiopia, the Queen of Sheba, and King Solomon of Jerusalem.” The dynasty it charters ended, at least by claim, with the deposition of Haile Selassie on 12 September 1974.6

Reception and debate

  • Two claims. The Kebra Nagast makes a genealogical claim (the kings of Ethiopia descend from Solomon and Sheba through Menelik I) and a relic claim (the Ark of the Covenant rests at Aksum). How each has been dated and read is set out on the disputed-reading page.
  • The age of the tradition. Budge (1922) held that the descent of the kings from Solomon “was certainly well known in Ethiopia for centuries before the Kebra Nagast was compiled.” Stuart Munro-Hay (2005) dates the claim that this dynasty descends from Solomon to the late thirteenth century, and identifies the object at Aksum as a tabot; the report that the Zagwe king Lalibela had claimed descent from Moses rather than Solomon is ⚠ unverified.
  • “Menelik” is a later name. The book itself calls the son Bayna-Lehkem / ‘Ebna Hakim (“son of the wise man”) and his throne-name David; the familiar form Menelik I is the name the tradition fixed on him and by which the dynasty and its founder-king are known.
  • Text and editions. Quoted throughout from E. A. Wallis Budge, The Queen of Sheba and Her Only Son Menyelek (London, 1922), the standard English translation, made from Carl Bezold’s critical edition of the Ethiopic (Munich, 1909). The Ark and descent claims are studied by Stuart Munro-Hay, The Quest for the Ark of the Covenant: The True History of the Tablets of Moses (London: I. B. Tauris, 2005).
