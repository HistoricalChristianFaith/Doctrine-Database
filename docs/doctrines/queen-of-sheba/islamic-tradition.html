The Islamic tradition: Bilqis, the hoopoe, the throne, and the glass pavilion

Dates: the Qur’an, revealed c. AD 610–632 — Surat an-Naml (“The Ant,” sūrah 27), a Meccan chapter — tells the story at 27:20–44; the later elaborations (the name Bilqis, the part-jinn genealogy, the hairy legs) belong to the Qur’anic commentators and the qisas al-anbiya (“Stories of the Prophets”) of the ninth–thirteenth centuries · Wikipedia
Summary: Islamic tradition gives the Queen of Sheba its own vivid retelling, keyed not to 1 Kings but to Qur’an 27:22–44. A hoopoe in Solomon (Sulaymān)’s bird-army brings word of a queen who rules a people that worship the sun; Solomon sends her a letter summoning her to submit to God; her throne is fetched to his court in the blink of an eye by “one who had knowledge from the Scripture”; and she is led across a glass pavilion (ṣarḥ) she takes for water, lifts her skirts to wade, and then submits — “my Lord, indeed I have wronged myself, and I submit with Solomon to Allāh, Lord of the worlds.” The Qur’an leaves her unnamed; later tradition names her Bilqis, makes her mother a jinni, and reads the glass-floor trick as a test of a rumour that she had hairy, ass-hooved legs — the very motif the Jewish Targum Sheni tells of her, marking a shared cross-tradition legend.

Where it sits

The Qur’anic narrative is the earliest of the great non-biblical retellings after the rabbinic strand, and by far the most widely read. Surat an-Naml is a Meccan chapter of the Qur’an, whose revelation Muslims place c. AD 610–632; on the timeline this block sits after the patristic reading of the queen as the Gentile church (whose sources run from Origen in the third century into the Byzantine and Latin Middle Ages) and before the fourteenth-century Ethiopian Kebra Nagast. As with the rabbinic legend, a distinction must be kept between the scriptural core — the Qur’anic story itself, which names neither the queen nor any demonic feature — and the exegetical accretions, gathered over the following centuries by the commentators (al-Tabari, al-Zamakhshari, al-Baydawi) and the qisas al-anbiya collections (notably al-Tha’labi, d. 1035, and al-Kisa’i), which supply the name Bilqis, the jinn-mother, the hairy legs, and the marriage. The whole is quoted here from the Saheeh International English translation, and its legend-development from the standard scholarship on the theme.1

Claims & evidence

The Qur’anic narrative: the hoopoe, the letter, the throne, and the glass pavilion

The story is told in Surat an-Naml as a chapter in the career of Solomon, to whom God has given “the language of birds.” Reviewing his ranks he misses the hoopoe, which returns with news of a distant queen:

But he [i.e., the hoopoe] stayed not long and said, “I have encompassed [in knowledge] that which you have not encompassed, and I have come to you from Sheba with certain news. Indeed, I found [there] a woman ruling them, and she has been given of all things, and she has a great throne. I found her and her people prostrating to the sun instead of Allāh, and Satan has made their deeds pleasing to them and averted them from [His] way, so they are not guided.”

— Qur’an 27:22–24 (Saheeh International). Solomon sends the hoopoe back with a letter, which the queen reads aloud to her council:

She said, “O eminent ones, indeed, to me has been delivered a noble letter. Indeed, it is from Solomon, and indeed, it is [i.e., reads]: ‘In the name of Allāh, the Entirely Merciful, the Especially Merciful, Be not haughty with me but come to me in submission [as Muslims].’”

— Qur’an 27:29–31. She tests him first with a gift, which he refuses; then, resolving to come herself, she becomes the occasion of two wonders. Before she arrives Solomon has her very throne brought to him instantaneously:

Said one who had knowledge from the Scripture, “I will bring it to you before your glance returns to you.” And when [Solomon] saw it placed before him, he said, “This is from the favor of my Lord to test me whether I will be grateful or ungrateful.”

— Qur’an 27:40. And at her reception she is led onto the floor of his palace — the ṣarḥ, a pavilion of glass — which she mistakes for water:

She was told, “Enter the palace.” But when she saw it, she thought it was a body of water and uncovered her shins [to wade through]. He said, “Indeed, it is a palace [whose floor is] made smooth with glass.” She said, “My Lord, indeed I have wronged myself, and I submit with Solomon to Allāh, Lord of the worlds.”

— Qur’an 27:44. In the Qur’an the point of the episode is her conversion: the sun-worshipping queen recognises the one God and submits (aslama) with Solomon. Nothing is said of her name, her ancestry, her legs, or any marriage — every one of those is a later growth.2

The name Bilqis and the part-jinn genealogy

The Qur’an calls her only “a woman ruling them.” The name Bilqis (Arabic بِلْقِيْس) is supplied by the commentators — al-Tabari, al-Zamakhshari, al-Baydawi — and is generally derived from Greek pallakís (or the Hebraised pilegesh), “concubine.” The Arabian genealogy makes her the daughter of a Himyarite king of Yemen, Hudhad (al-Hadhad); and the qisas al-anbiya add a supernatural mother. Al-Tha’labi’s ‘Ara’is al-majalis reports, on the authority of a companion of the Prophet, that one of Bilqis’s parents was a jinni — a hybrid origin invoked to explain her uncanny wisdom and, especially, the physical anomaly the legend fixes on: legs said to be excessively hairy or hooved like an ass’s. The same collections carry the story to its end: after the glass-floor exposure, Solomon has the jinn devise a depilatory (and, by tradition, the first bath-house), then marries her and restores her the kingdom of Yemen, which he visits thereafter.3

The glass floor and the hairy legs: a shared motif with the Targum Sheni

The most striking feature of the Islamic legend is not in the Qur’an at all. In the commentators’ telling, Solomon’s jinn — fearing he might marry the queen — whisper that under her skirts she hides hairy legs and the hooves of an ass; to test the rumour Solomon builds the glass pavilion, so that she, thinking it water, raises her skirts and reveals her legs. This is manifestly the same folk-motif as the Jewish Targum Sheni to Esther, where Solomon seats the queen over a floor of glass she takes for water, so that she bares hairy legs and draws his rebuke that hair “is becoming to a man, but to a woman it is a shame.” In both traditions the glass floor is a device for exposing a body coded as monstrous or demonic — the hairy leg being the folkloric mark of a demon, the thread that in the Jewish stream ends by fusing the queen with Lilith and in the Islamic one by giving her a jinn mother. The scholarship on the theme — Jacob Lassner’s Demonizing the Queen of Sheba the standard study — reads these shared motifs as a single cross-traditional legend of a powerful foreign woman “demonized” and mastered: her wisdom credited to jinn, her achievements (even the great Ma’rib dam) ascribed to their labour, and her hairy legs a body to be shaved and subdued before the king could take her.4

Notes

  • Scripture vs. commentary. The Qur’anic narrative (27:20–44) is spare and centres on the queen’s conversion; it names neither her nor any demonic feature. The name Bilqis, the jinn-mother, the hairy legs, and the marriage all come from the later exegetical and qisas tradition — a distinction this page keeps throughout.
  • “Sheba” in the Qur’an is Saba’. The queen’s land is Saba’ — the South-Arabian Sabaean kingdom (a separate sūrah, 34, is named for it) — so the Islamic tradition, unlike Josephus and the Kebra Nagast, locates her in Yemen rather than Africa; the Arabian genealogy makes her a Himyarite princess.
  • The glass pavilion (ṣarḥ). Qur’an 27:44’s ṣarḥ mumarrad min qawārīr is rendered by Saheeh International “a palace [whose floor is] made smooth with glass”; the exposure of her “shins” is in the text, but the hairy-legs reading that motivates the trick is the commentators’ gloss, carried over from the shared Near-Eastern legend.
  • Text and editions. The Qur’an is quoted from the Saheeh International translation (as displayed verse-by-verse at Quran.com). The legend’s development is set out in Jacob Lassner, Demonizing the Queen of Sheba: Boundaries of Gender and Culture in Postbiblical Judaism and Medieval Islam (Chicago, 1993); the classical elaborations are in al-Tha’labi, ‘Ara’is al-majalis fi qisas al-anbiya (“Lives of the Prophets”), trans. William M. Brinner (Leiden: Brill, 2002).
