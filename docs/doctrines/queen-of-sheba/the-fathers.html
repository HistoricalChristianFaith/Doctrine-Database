The Fathers on the Queen of the South

Dates: c. 240 – c. 1075 (the patristic and Byzantine chorus, from Origen’s Commentary on the Song of Songs to Theophylact of Ohrid) · Wikipedia
Summary: Where Josephus gave the queen a homeland and a name, the Christian Fathers gave her a meaning. Following the seed Jesus planted — “the queen of the south shall rise up in the judgment … for she came from the uttermost parts of the earth to hear the wisdom of Solomon; and, behold, a greater than Solomon is here” (Matthew 12:42; Luke 11:31) — they read her, almost with one voice, as a figure of the Gentile church: the nations, gathered from the ends of the earth, coming to the true Solomon, Christ, whose wisdom is greater. Origen sets the pattern (and weaves it into the Song of Songs, where the “black but comely” bride is the Ethiopian queen and the Church from the nations); Ambrose, Gregory of Nyssa, and Ephrem carry the same reading; Jerome, Chrysostom, and Augustine press its edge — that the outsider’s faith condemns unbelieving Israel; Bede and Theophylact hand it on to the medieval West and the Byzantine East. This page collects that chorus; whether the typology holds is weighed on its own argument page (sound).

Where it sits

The reading is not an invention of the Fathers but a development of a saying of Jesus. At the demand for a sign (Matthew 12:38–42; par. Luke 11:29–32), Jesus set two Gentiles over against his own generation — the men of Nineveh, who repented at Jonah, and the queen of the south, who crossed the earth for Solomon’s wisdom — and declared that “a greater than Solomon is here.” The typological engine is his own: Solomon is the type, Christ the antitype (“a greater than Solomon”), and the queen is the one who answers him. From Origen in the mid-third century onward, the Fathers drew the obvious corollary — if Christ is the true Solomon, the Gentile who comes from afar to his wisdom is the Church of the nations — and the reading became near-universal, East and West, in commentary, homily, and the Song-of-Songs mystical tradition. Chronologically it stands here after the biblical baseline and Josephus (c. AD 93), opening the specifically Christian interpretation of the figure.1

Claims & evidence

Origen of Alexandria (c. 240): the Church from the nations, and the Ethiopian bride

Now this history we have wished to repeat somewhat more at length, and to insert into this our exposition, knowing that these things so greatly befit the person of the Church which comes from the nations to Christ, that the Lord himself in the Gospels made mention of this queen, saying that she came from the ends of the earth to hear the wisdom of Solomon. And he calls her the queen of the South, in that Ethiopia lies in the parts of the South, and is set as it were at the utmost, at the ends of the earth. … She came then, this queen — nay rather, according to her figure, the Church came from the nations to hear the wisdom of the true Solomon and the true peaceable one, our Lord Jesus Christ.

— Commentary on the Song of Songs II (on Song 1:5, “I am black but comely”). Origen is the first and fullest Christian voice. Expounding the bride’s words “I am black, but comely, O ye daughters of Jerusalem,” he identifies her with the queen of Sheba, whom he reads through Jesus’ saying as the Church “which comes from the nations to Christ.” The bride herself takes up the confession: “I am that Ethiopian woman; I am black indeed for the ignobility of my race, but beautiful because of repentance and faith. For I have received into myself the Son of God.” The queen’s gifts are allegorized — the “sweetnesses of spices” are good works, the gold “rational teachings” — and she comes “not with one nation only, as the Synagogue before had the Hebrews alone, but with the nations of the whole world.” The whole Gentile-church typology of the later tradition is already here, fully formed.2

The church of the Gentiles, which in truth is gathered “from the ends of the earth,” fulfills the place of the queen of the south, not providing gifts in “perishable things, silver” and spices, but faith, the incense of knowledge, the outpouring of an offering, the sweat of the virtues and the blood of martyrdom. For with such gifts the true Solomon is pleased, who is Christ, “our peace.” For “Solomon” is interpreted as “peaceful.”

— Fragment on Matthew 277 (on Matthew 12:42). Origen’s comment on the Gospel saying itself makes the same identification tersely: the queen of the south is “the church of the Gentiles … gathered from the ends of the earth,” and the wisdom she seeks is Christ, “the true Solomon” — the name read, as often in the Fathers, through its Hebrew sense “peaceful” (cf. Ephesians 2:14, Christ “our peace”).3

Ambrose of Milan (c. 380): the mystery of the Church gathered from the ends of the earth

In this also is clearly expressed the mystery of the Church, which is gathered from the ends of the earth … in the seeking of wisdom by the Queen of the South: so that she may know the peaceful words of Solomon, the Queen, indeed, whose kingdom is indivisible, arising from diverse and distant peoples into one body. … this is a great Sacrament… of Christ and the Church: but yet this is greater; because that took place in figure before, but now the mystery is fulfilled in truth; for there the type of Solomon, here Christ in His own body.

— Exposition of the Gospel of Luke 7.96. Ambrose makes the reading explicit as type and fulfilment: the queen who comes “from diverse and distant peoples into one body” is the Church, and where Solomon was only the “type,” the truth is “Christ in His own body.” He pairs her with the repentant Ninevites to picture the two halves of the Church’s life — the queen’s pursuit of wisdom (“ignorance of sin… wisdom guards against it”) and the Ninevites’ penitence (“repentance blots out the offence”).4

Gregory of Nyssa (c. 380) and Ephrem the Syrian (c. 363): the Gentiles once in darkness, a type of the Church

Now as she was queen of the Ethiopians, and in a far distant country, so in the beginning the Church of the Gentiles was in darkness, and far off from the knowledge of God. But when Christ the Prince of peace shone forth, the Jews being still in darkness, thither came the Gentiles, and offered to Christ the frankincense of piety, the gold of divine knowledge, and precious stones, that is, obedience to His commands.

— Gregory of Nyssa, Homilies on the Song of Songs 7 (as preserved in Aquinas’s Catena Aurea on Luke 11). Gregory reads the queen’s Ethiopian darkness as the Gentiles’ former ignorance and her gifts as the graces the nations bring to Christ once he “shone forth.” Ephrem the Syrian says it as plainly as anyone: the queen “is a type of the church. Just as she came to Solomon, so too the church came to our Lord” — and, seeking a king who “does not pass away and wisdom which does not go astray,” the Church condemns the unbelieving synagogue all the more.5

Jerome (c. 398), Chrysostom (c. 390), and Augustine (c. 410): the outsider’s faith condemns unbelieving Israel

So the queen of the south will condemn the Jews in the same manner as the men of Nineveh will condemn unbelieving Israel. This is the queen of Saba, of whom we read in the book of Kings and Chronicles, who leaving her nation and kingdom came through so many difficulties to hear the wisdom of Solomon, and brought him many gifts. Also in these instances of Nineveh and the queen of Saba, the faith of the Gentiles is significantly set above that of Israel.

— Jerome, Commentary on Matthew (on 12:42). The Latin and Greek exegetes press the polemical edge of Jesus’ own use: the Gentile queen’s faith rebukes the unbelief of those who had the greater wisdom present among them. Chrysostom marvels that “she waited not for Solomon to come to her, but went herself unto him, although she was both a woman, and a barbarian, and at so great a distance… simply through the love of wise words” — and Christ, greater than Solomon, has come to them, speaking “of secret things, and most awful mysteries,” and is refused. Augustine puts the sting in a question: “who are those who looked askance at the presence of the Lord, whereas foreigners listened to his servants?”6

Bede (c. 735) and Theophylact of Ohrid (c. 1075): the reading handed on West and East

But if the queen of the South, who doubtless is of the elect, shall rise up in judgment together with the wicked, we have a proof of the one resurrection of all men, good as well as bad, and that not according to Jewish fables to happen a thousand years before the judgment, but at the judgment itself.

— Bede, On the Gospel of Luke (on 11:31). Bede takes the queen as “doubtless… of the elect,” the pattern of the barbarian who crossed the earth for wisdom while the Jews, having “wisdom and the power of God Himself” among them, “preferred not only not to listen but to blaspheme.” Theophylact, the timeline’s Byzantine coda, keeps the same christological point (Solomon spoke of “trees and woods… but though I came to you, speaking of ineffable things, you have not accepted Me”) and adds a moral-mystical layer: the “queen of the south” is also every soul strong in virtue that rises from the warm “south” of the spiritual life “to hear wisdom, that is, ascends to contemplation” of “the peaceful king” Christ.7

Notes

  • The dominical seed and the patristic step. The type’s core — Solomon→Christ, the queen→the responsive outsider — is Jesus’ own (“a greater than Solomon is here”). The Fathers’ identification of that outsider with the Gentile church is a development of the saying, coherent and near-universal, and is what the argument page weighs (sound).
  • Origen’s further allegory. Origen’s weaving of the queen into the Song of Songs — the “black but comely” bride as “that Ethiopian woman” — and his leaning on the geographical premise that “Ethiopia lies in the parts of the South” are a further, looser layer of allegory built on catchword links (south, Ethiopia, blackness). The Gentile-church type does not actually rest on the African localization: “the queen of the south… from the uttermost parts of the earth” carries the Gentile-from-afar point whether Sheba lay in Yemen or in Africa (the homeland question is weighed on its own page). The embroidery is traditional and coherent, but it is embroidery, not the load-bearing type.
  • Texts and editions. Origen’s Commentary on the Song of Songs was written c. 240 (begun at Athens, finished at Caesarea); the English is from the digitised text of Book II. Ambrose’s Exposition of the Gospel of Luke 7.96, Jerome’s Commentary on Matthew, Chrysostom’s Homily on Matthew 43, Augustine’s Sermon 72A.1, Bede’s Commentary on Luke, and Theophylact’s Commentary on Luke are quoted from their published translations; Gregory of Nyssa’s Homily on the Song of Songs 7 and one of Bede’s notices survive here in Aquinas’s Catena Aurea compilation. Ephrem’s comment is from his Commentary on Tatian’s Diatessaron 11.4.
