Josephus on the Queen of Sheba

Dates: c. AD 37 – c. 100 (Jewish priest, soldier, and historian; the Antiquities of the Jews completed c. AD 93–94) · Wikipedia
Summary: In Antiquities of the Jews VIII.6.5–6 (§§165–175) Josephus gives the earliest post-biblical interpretive retelling of the queen’s visit — and re-locates her. Where 1 Kings calls her only “the queen of Sheba,” Josephus makes her “a woman queen of Egypt and Ethiopia,” planting the visitor squarely in Africa rather than South Arabia; a few sections earlier he had named a queen of Egypt “Nicaule” out of Herodotus, the name later tradition would attach to her. He Hellenizes her motive — she is “inquisitive into philosophy” and comes to test Solomon “by her own experience, and not by a bare hearing” — expands her astonishment to take in the temple worship as well as the palace, softens her closing confession from a blessing of Israel’s God into praise of the God who made Solomon king, and adds a piece of Judaean lore: the balsam of his own country, Josephus says, grew from a root she gave. It is a key early witness to the African localization of Sheba, and the first data-point for the homeland question.

Where it sits

Flavius Josephus (c. AD 37 – c. 100), a priest of Jerusalem and general in the Jewish revolt who became a client of the Flavian emperors at Rome, completed his twenty-book Antiquities of the Jews about AD 93–94 — a paraphrase of Israel’s history from creation to his own day, aimed at a Graeco-Roman readership. The queen’s visit falls in Book VIII, chapter 6, sections 5–6 (Whiston’s divisions; §§165–175 in the Niese/Loeb numbering), immediately after the account of Solomon’s gold-fleet to Ophir and just before the notice of his decline. As the earliest surviving retelling of the episode — roughly a millennium after the events it narrates and a generation after the New Testament — it stands first on this timeline after the biblical baseline, and already shows the tradition doing to the queen what every later tradition would do: giving her a homeland, a name, and a character the terse text of 1 Kings withholds.1

Claims & evidence

“A woman queen of Egypt and Ethiopia” — the African localization

There was then a woman Queen of Egypt and Ethiopia: she was inquisitive into philosophy, and one that on other accounts also was to be admired.

— Antiquities VIII.6.5 (William Whiston). The single most consequential move Josephus makes is geographical. Scripture names the visitor only “the queen of Sheba” (1 Kings 10:1), and Jesus calls her “the queen of the south… from the uttermost parts of the earth” (Matthew 12:42) — a direction, not a country. Josephus fixes the direction into a place: Egypt and Ethiopia, the African south, not the Sabaean kingdom of South Arabia (Yemen) that the name “Sheba” most naturally denotes. He does not defend the identification; he simply assumes it, and in doing so he becomes the earliest witness to the Ethiopian / African reading that the Kebra Nagast would later make a national charter — a reading in tension with the epigraphic Saba of the Yemeni inscriptions. Whiston’s own note flags the passage as one of the more mistaken in all the Antiquities where Josephus touches the kings of Egypt, and Samuel Bochart had catalogued the errors before him.2

The name: “Nicaule,” the queen of Egypt out of Herodotus

For when after their death there was a Queen reigned, he calls her by her name, Nicaule: as thereby declaring, that while the Kings were of the male line, and so admitted of the same name, while a woman did not admit the same, he did therefore set down that her name, which she could not naturally have.

— Antiquities VIII.6.2 (Whiston). It is often said that Josephus “names the Queen of Sheba Nicaulis”; the truth is more oblique, and worth stating exactly. In the visit-narrative itself (§§165–175) Josephus gives her no personal name — only the title “queen of Egypt and Ethiopia.” The name Nicaule comes a few sections earlier, where Josephus, discussing why Herodotus lists no names for the 330 kings of Egypt after Menes, remarks that Herodotus did name the one queen who reigned among them — “he calls her by her name, Nicaule.” Because Josephus then makes his Solomon-visitor a queen of that same Egypt, later readers merged the two figures, and “Nicaule” (Whiston conjectures the historical Nitocris) became the name by which the tradition knew the Queen of Sheba. So the familiar “Josephus called her Nicaulis” is an inference from the juxtaposition, not a sentence he wrote — but the inference is a fair one, since on his telling the visitor simply is the queen of Egypt.3

Wisdom sought by trial, not by report — the philosopher-queen

When this Queen heard of the virtue and prudence of Solomon, she had a great mind to see him… she being desirous to be satisfied by her own experience, and not by a bare hearing: (for reports thus heard are likely enough to comply with a false opinion; while they wholly depend on the credit of the relators:)… and that especially in order to have a trial of his wisdom, while she proposed questions of very great difficulty, and intreated that he would resolve their hidden meaning.

— Antiquities VIII.6.5 (Whiston). Where 1 Kings says she “came to prove him with hard questions,” Josephus recasts the queen as a Greek-style seeker: “inquisitive into philosophy,” unwilling to trust hearsay, determined to verify Solomon’s reputation by first-hand experiment. Her “hard questions” become “questions of very great difficulty” whose “hidden meaning” she asks him to resolve — the germ of the riddle-contest that the later rabbinic and Islamic traditions would elaborate into set catalogues of enigmas. Solomon “easily comprehending in his mind the meaning of the curious questions she propounded to him… resolved them sooner than any body could have expected,” and she “was amazed at the wisdom of Solomon: and discovered that it was more excellent upon trial than what she had heard by report beforehand.”4

Astonished at the palace — and at the temple worship

But she was beyond measure astonished at the house which was called the forest of Lebanon: as also at the magnificence of his daily table… Nor was she less affected with those daily sacrifices which were offered to God; and the careful management which the Priests and Levites used about them. When she saw this done every day, she was in the greatest admiration imaginable.

— Antiquities VIII.6.5 (Whiston). 1 Kings had ended its catalogue of what overwhelmed her with “his ascent by which he went up unto the house of the LORD” (10:5) — the worship as the climax. Josephus keeps and amplifies that religious note: alongside the palace, the “forest of Lebanon” hall, the ordered table, and the liveried servants, it is “those daily sacrifices which were offered to God” and the priests’ and Levites’ careful ministry that leave her in “the greatest admiration imaginable.” The priest-historian, in other words, will not let the queen’s wonder rest on Solomon’s magnificence alone; the cult of the God of Israel remains, as in the biblical account, part of what conquers her.5

Her confession — and the gift of the balsam

One would therefore bless God who hath so loved this countrey, and those that inhabit therein, as to make thee King over them.”… For she gave him twenty talents of gold; and an immense quantity of spices, and pretious stones. They say also that we possess the root of that balsam which our countrey still bears by this woman’s gift.

— Antiquities VIII.6.5–6 (Whiston). Josephus rewrites the queen’s great confession. In 1 Kings she blesses the covenant God by name — “Blessed be the LORD thy God… because the LORD loved Israel for ever” (10:9). Josephus keeps the shape but blurs the theology to a form a Graeco-Roman reader could take: it is fitting that “one would… bless God who hath so loved this countrey… as to make thee King over them.” He then trims her gift from the biblical hundred-and-twenty talents of gold to “twenty talents” (with the “immense quantity of spices, and pretious stones” retained), and Solomon repays her so freely that “there was nothing that she desired which he denied her,” before “this Queen of Egypt and of Ethiopia… returned to her own Kingdom.” The vivid addition is Judaean lore: the prized balsam (opobalsamum) of his own country — the famous perfume-shrub of Jericho and En-Gedi — grew, Josephus reports, from a root the queen gave, one more strand tying her visit to the soil of the land.6

Notes

  • The seed of the homeland question. Josephus is the earliest datable voice for placing Sheba in Africa (“Egypt and Ethiopia”) rather than in the South-Arabian Saba of the Sabaean inscriptions — a millennium before the Kebra Nagast made the same identification the foundation of the Ethiopian Solomonic dynasty. Whether the African reading or the Sabaean one better fits the evidence is weighed on its own argument page (queued); Josephus is the first witness that the African localization is old, not a late Ethiopian invention — though his notorious looseness with the kings of Egypt (flagged by Whiston and Bochart) is a caution against treating him as an independent geographical source rather than an early interpreter.
  • “Nicaule” = Nitocris? The name later fastened on the Queen of Sheba is one Josephus supplies for a queen of Egypt out of Herodotus, not one he attaches to the Solomon-visitor in so many words (see the claim above). Whiston conjectures the historical Nitocris behind “Nicaule”; the identification of the two queens is Josephus’s implied doing, since both are “queen of Egypt,” and the tradition simply drew the line he left for it.
  • Josephus the Hellenizer. The recasting of the queen as one “inquisitive into philosophy” who distrusts hearsay and insists on personal verification is Josephus’s own colouring, part of his lifelong project of presenting Israel’s history in terms a cultured Greek or Roman would respect. It is a good specimen of his method, and a reminder that his “queen of Sheba,” like his Balaam, is a literary reconstruction shaped by his apologetic aims, not an independent record.
  • Text and edition. Greek in B. Niese, Flavii Iosephi Opera (Berlin, 1885–95); the English quoted here is William Whiston’s translation (1737), The Antiquities of the Jews VIII.6, as digitised at the University of Chicago’s Penelope archive. Whiston’s chapter-and-section divisions (VIII.6.2, 5–6) run alongside the Niese/Loeb continuous numbering (§§158, 165–175).
