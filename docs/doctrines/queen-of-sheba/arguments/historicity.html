Was the Queen of Sheba a historical tenth-century queen?

Type: historical argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — the episode is credible in its general shape: a wealthy South-Arabian caravan monarch, female rule attested for the region, and gifts of gold, spices, and gems that match the incense trade exactly. But no contemporary inscription names this queen or her visit, and the documented Sabaean state — its monumental building, its royal inscriptions, its incense monopoly — is best attested only from the eighth century BC, some two hundred years after Solomon. So a real queen behind 1 Kings 10 is possible and not implausible, but unattested and unproven; the strongest skeptical case (Finkelstein and Silberman) targets the tenth-century dating, and the strongest maximalist case (Kitchen) rests on general plausibility rather than direct evidence. Possibility is not proof, and absence of a contemporary record is not disproof — the honest verdict is genuinely disputed, strong by the trade historian’s lights and weak by the epigrapher’s

The claim

“And when the queen of Sheba heard of the fame of Solomon concerning the name of the LORD, she came to prove him with hard questions. And she came to Jerusalem with a very great train, with camels that bare spices, and very much gold, and precious stones.”

— 1 Kings 10:1–2. The question here is not where Sheba lay (that is weighed separately; the answer is South-Arabian Saba), nor whether the Ethiopian Solomonic-descent legend holds, but the prior one: did a real queen make a real state visit to a real Solomon around 950 BC, or is the episode a literary construction of the Solomon-glory cycle — the climactic proof of a wisdom and wealth so great that even the ends of the earth came to marvel? The two poles are the maximalist reading (the narrative preserves the memory of an actual South-Arabian embassy) and the minimalist reading (the Sabaean caravan kingdom, and Judah’s own capacity to attract such a visit, are anachronisms retrojected from a later age).1

Who advances it

The affirmative — that a real tenth-century queen stands behind the story — is argued most fully by Kenneth A. Kitchen, who reads the episode against the documented South-Arabian incense trade and the wider pattern of long-distance royal exchange in the ancient Near East, and it is the reading Ethiopian and Yemeni national tradition has always assumed. The skeptical case is put sharply by Israel Finkelstein and Neil Asher Silberman, for whom the Sabaean kingdom “began to flourish only in the eighth century B.C. — some two hundred years after” Solomon, so the visit as narrated is a seventh-century author’s projection of a trade world he knew onto a tenth-century golden age. Between them a broad middle grants that a caravan-kingdom monarch of Solomon’s era is not implausible while conceding the story cannot be confirmed.2

The case for historicity

Four lines make the episode credible. A real, wealthy kingdom in the right place. Saba was no legend: a South-Arabian caravan state on the frankincense road, capital Marib, whose great dam was one of the engineering wonders of the ancient world, and whose gold, gems, and aromatics were the wares of a genuine long-distance trade. A monarch arriving overland by camel bearing incense, gold, and precious stones is exactly a monarch of that road. The trade goods fit too well to be invented from nowhere. Frankincense and myrrh grew in South Arabia and the Horn and nowhere near Israel; the prophets fasten the same catalogue on Sheba as its byword — “the merchants of Sheba… with chief of all spices, and with all precious stones, and gold” (Ezekiel 27:22; Isaiah 60:6) — so the narrative’s incidental detail reflects a real economic geography. Female rule in the region is historically attested. The Assyrian royal annals of the eighth and seventh centuries BC name a whole series of Arabian queens — Zabibe, Samsi, Yatie, Te’elhunu, Tabua — who led their peoples, controlled trade, and treated with the great kings; some led the incense caravans in person. A queen of an Arabian people, negotiating with a distant throne, is precisely the kind of figure the record independently documents. Long-distance royal embassies to great kings are attested for Saba itself: Itamra (Yathaʿ Amar) of Saba paid tribute to Sargon II of Assyria about 715 BC — a Sabaean ruler dealing with a distant imperial court, the very sort of contact 1 Kings 10 describes, differing in date but not in kind.3

The case against — steelmanned

The skeptical case is not bare minimalism; it turns on dates and silence. No contemporary attestation. Not a single tenth-century inscription — Israelite, Sabaean, Egyptian, or Assyrian — names this queen, her visit, or a Solomonic court of the scale 1 Kings describes; the story survives only in the Hebrew narrative and its dependents. The Sabaean state looks too late. Continuous South-Arabian epigraphy begins only in the eighth century BC; the earliest substantial royal inscriptions (the mukarrib Yathaʿ Amar Watar, late eighth century) and the monumental florescence of Marib and its dam belong to the eighth–sixth centuries, and the earliest external mentions of Saba are the Assyrian references of 738 and c. 715 BC. On this reckoning a tenth-century Sabaean monarch wealthy enough to mount such an embassy is an anachronism — the trade world of the narrator’s own day read back two centuries. The attested queens are the wrong ones. The Assyrian “queens of the Arabs” are North-Arabian (Qedarite) rulers of the eighth–seventh centuries, not Sabaeans and not contemporaries of Solomon; they establish that female Arabian rule was possible, not that this queen existed. The narrative has a job to do. The episode is the climax of the Solomon-glory cycle — the queen exists in the text to be overwhelmed (“there was no more spirit in her”) and to pronounce the blessing that crowns Solomon’s wisdom and wealth; a figure so perfectly shaped to that function invites the suspicion that her function, not a court record, is her origin.4

Adversarial assessment

Where the skeptics overreach. The chronological objection is real but softer than it is often made to sound. There is a distinction between the documented florescence of the monumental Sabaean state (eighth century and after) and the existence of a settled, trading South-Arabian society, for which there are earlier, if sparser, traces; several standard references date the transition from tribal federation to a mukarrib-led polity to roughly 1000–800 BC, and the caravan trade in aromatics is older than the palaces and dams built on its profits. “Saba is only monumentally attested from the eighth century” is therefore an argument about the scale of a tenth-century Sabaean polity, not a proof that none existed — and absence of a contemporary inscription is weak evidence where the epigraphic habit itself only begins later. The maximalist need not claim a Marib at its later zenith; a wealthy caravan chiefdom capable of a prestige embassy is a far lower bar, and one the evidence does not exclude. Where the maximalists overreach. But the affirmative cannot be dressed as more than plausible. Every prop — the real kingdom, the fitting trade goods, the regional female rulers, the Sabaean embassy to Assyria — establishes that such a visit could have happened; none attests that it did. The closest analogues (the Arabian queens, the Sabaean tribute to Sargon) all fall two centuries too late and prove the type, not the instance. And the narrative’s theological shaping is genuine: whatever historical kernel it may hold has been worked into a set-piece in praise of Solomon, so its details serve the story before they serve the historian. Symmetry of skepticism. The honest position refuses both errors: it will not unseat the episode’s historicity on bare absence of evidence (the record is thin for the whole tenth century, and argues from silence), and it will not assert the visit as fact on the strength of general plausibility. What the evidence yields is a credible type without a confirmable token.

Assessment

Contested. That a real tenth-century South-Arabian queen stands behind 1 Kings 10 is genuinely disputed, with no clear winner — strong by the trade historian’s lights, weak by the epigrapher’s. In its favour: Saba was a real caravan kingdom, the queen’s gold-spices-gems gifts fit its trade exactly, female Arabian rulers are attested in the region, and Saba itself is documented sending embassies to a distant great king. Against it: no contemporary source names the queen or the visit, the monumental Sabaean state and its royal inscriptions are best attested only from the eighth century BC (some two centuries after Solomon), the attested Arabian queens are later North-Arabian rulers, not tenth-century Sabaeans, and the episode is the shaped climax of the Solomon-glory cycle. The evidence supports the plausibility of such a queen, not the fact of this one: possibility is not proof, and the absence of a contemporary record — in an age and region whose epigraphic habit begins later — is not disproof. A credible type; an unconfirmable token.
