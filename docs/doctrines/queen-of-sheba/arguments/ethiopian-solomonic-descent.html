Does the Kebra Nagast preserve a historical Solomonic descent of Ethiopia’s kings and the Ark’s transfer to Aksum?

Disputed reading · on Who Was the Queen of Sheba? · witness: the Kebra Nagast

The passage

…the descent of the Kings of Ethiopia from Solomon, King of Israel, and the “Queen of the South,” or the “Queen of Sheba.”

— the principal theme of the Kebra Nagast, in E. A. Wallis Budge’s words, The Queen of Sheba and Her Only Son Menyelek, Introduction, Internet Archive.1

…Azaryas found Zion, the Tabernacle of the Law of God; and it was taken away by them forthwith, in the twinkling of an eye, the Angel of the Lord being present and directing. And had it not been that God willed it Zion could not have been taken away forthwith.

— Kebra Nagast §48, trans. Budge, Internet Archive.2

The question

The Kebra Nagast makes two claims: that Ethiopia’s kings descend from Solomon and the Queen of Sheba (Makeda) through their son Menelik I, and that Menelik’s company brought the Ark of the Covenant to Aksum. Its surviving Ethiopic form is fourteenth-century. What is disputed is whether these claims hand down a memory older than the book, or were first formulated with the dynasty that took power in 1270. The answer bears on how the charter of the Solomonic dynasty and the Aksum tradition of the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church are dated. The sources gathered for this page include no modern academic study that defends the historical descent itself; the first reading is given from its traditional holders.

A preserved record: the Solomonic line and the Ark at Aksum

On this reading the Kebra Nagast records true history: Ethiopia’s kings descend from Solomon and the Queen of Sheba, and the Ark of the Covenant rests at Aksum.

Held by: Isaac, the book’s Ethiopic translator (colophon, fourteenth century); Emperor Yohannes IV (letter of 1872); the Ethiopian constitutions of 1931 and 1955; and the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church, which venerates the tabot at the Church of Our Lady Mary of Zion, Aksum, as the Ark.3

Its holders read the book as Ethiopia’s account of its own origins. It names the queen Makeda, queen of ‘Azeb, and opens her story under Jesus’ title “the Queen of the South” (Matthew 12:42). Her son by Solomon, Bayna-Lehkem, is recognized by his father, anointed king of Ethiopia in Jerusalem under the name David, and sent home with the firstborn sons of Israel’s elders to found a court. The removal of Zion is God’s act, done under an angel’s direction, and the book reads it as the fulfilment of Psalm 68:31, “Ethiopia shall soon stretch out her hands unto God.” Isaac writes in the colophon that he has “toiled much for the glory of the kingdom of Ethiopia”; Budge sums up his convictions as three: that the lawful kings of Ethiopia descended from Solomon, that the Ark had been brought from Jerusalem to Aksum by Menyelek, and that the God of Israel had moved His dwelling on earth to Aksum.4

Josephus had placed the queen in “Egypt and Ethiopia” a thousand years before the book, and the Solomon-and-Sheba story it tells had circulated long before its fourteenth-century redaction. The book was received as law: Yohannes IV wrote in 1872 that “in my country my people will not obey my orders without it,” and the constitutions of 1931 and 1955 declared that the emperor “descends without interruption from the dynasty of Menelik I, son of the Queen of Ethiopia, the Queen of Sheba, and King Solomon of Jerusalem.” On this reading the dynasty’s title rests on the history the book records, and the sanctuary at Aksum keeps the Ark that history brought there.5

An Ethiopian tradition older than the book

On this reading the descent of Ethiopia’s kings from Solomon was believed and written down in Ethiopia centuries before the fourteenth-century Kebra Nagast, which gathers the tradition rather than creating it.

Held by: E. A. Wallis Budge (Egyptologist and translator, The Queen of Sheba and Her Only Son Menyelek, 1922).6

Budge, who translated the whole book, judged the colophon’s account of its history “substantially correct”: a Coptic archetype, rendered into Arabic by ‘Abu ’l-‘Izz and ’Abu ’l-Faraj, and then into Ethiopic by Isaac. The theme of the descent, he wrote, “was certainly well known in Ethiopia for centuries before the Kebra Nagast was compiled.” He held that a Solomonic line had ruled until the Zagwe kings displaced it, and gives their rule as 914–1268; “written accounts of the descent of the kings of Ethiopia from Solomon must have existed in Ethiopia before the close of the ninth century,” and the Solomonic kings who withdrew to Shoa kept “chronological lists and other historical documents.”

The colophon, as Budge reads it, also explains the late Ethiopic text. Isaac asks why the Arabic version had not been translated sooner, and answers that it appeared while the Zagwe still reigned, when “the publication of any work that supported the claims of the Solomonic kings would meet with a very unfavourable reception.” Budge concluded that the book “existed in Arabic in some form during the three and a half centuries of the Zague rule” and was copied in Ethiopic only after Yekuno ‘Amlak restored the line in 1270. He also reports that “Ethiopian tradition asserts” the Ark survived the kingdom’s wars and “was preserved at Aksum until comparatively recent times.” On this reading the fourteenth-century book is the written form of a royal tradition Ethiopia had carried since the first millennium.7

A charter of the dynasty that took power in 1270

On this reading the descent claim was first formulated to legitimate the dynasty that took power in 1270, and the sacred object at Aksum is an altar-tablet (tabot) rather than the Ark of Moses.

Held by: Stuart Munro-Hay (historian of Aksum, The Quest for the Ark of the Covenant, 2005); Harold G. Marcus (A History of Ethiopia, 2002, ⚠ unverified); David Northrup (Seven Myths of Africa in World History, 2017, ⚠ unverified).8

Its holders date each claim by its earliest attestation. The Ethiopic book is dated 1314–1321/22 on the colophon’s testimony, more than two thousand years after Solomon. Munro-Hay is reported as finding no record of an Ethiopian monarch claiming descent from Solomon before the thirteenth century, so the claim first appears with Yekuno ‘Amlak’s displacement of the Zagwe in 1270. The Zagwe king Lalibela is said to have claimed descent from Moses, not Solomon (⚠ unverified). Marcus calls the book’s stories a “pastiche of legends” created to legitimate Yekuno ‘Amlak’s seizure of power, and Steven Kaplan notes that Solomon is dated to the tenth century BC, centuries before Aksum was founded. Scripture leaves the queen unnamed; “Makeda” and “Menelik” are the names this tradition gives her and her son.9

The relic claim is dated the same way. Munro-Hay’s book-length study traces the Aksum tradition and concludes that what the church at Aksum guards is a tabot, an altar-tablet of the kind every Ethiopian church possesses, and that the church “never possessed the ark” of Moses. Aksum’s ancient stelae and sacred stones, on his account, belong to a cult of sacred stones that the later Ark tradition drew on. On this reading the Kebra Nagast is the founding document of a medieval royal house, and its weight lies in what it did: it gave a dynasty and a church a shared account of their origins that lasted from 1270 to 1974.10
