Where was Sheba — Sabaean South Arabia (Yemen) or Ethiopia/Aksum?

Type: historical-geographic argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: the South-Arabian identification is sound — the biblical Sheba is the incense kingdom of Saba in modern Yemen (capital Marib), the reading the name, the genealogy, and the gold-spices-gems trade of 1 Kings 10 all point to; the Ethiopian/Aksum localization is a later tradition (Josephus onward), weak as a claim about the tenth-century queen’s actual homeland, though not baseless — the two are not cleanly exclusive, since Sabaean culture straddled the Red Sea into the Horn · the African reading is advanced by Josephus and the Kebra Nagast, the South-Arabian by the mainstream of philology and archaeology (Kitchen and others)

The claim

“When the queen of Sheba heard of the fame of Solomon… she came to prove him with hard questions… and she came to Jerusalem with a very great train, with camels that bare spices, and very much gold, and precious stones” — and she came “from the uttermost parts of the earth.”

— 1 Kings 10:1–2; Matthew 12:42. Scripture never says where Sheba lay, and two rival localizations have been urged. (A) South Arabia: the kingdom of Saba in what is now Yemen — a wealthy Sabaean caravan state on the frankincense road, capital Marib, whose gold, gems, and spices match the queen’s gifts exactly. (B) Ethiopia/Aksum: the African localization — already in Josephus (“queen of Egypt and Ethiopia”) and made a national charter in the Ethiopian Kebra Nagast, resting on Red-Sea trade and the later Aksumite kingdom. The question is which the biblical Sheba denotes; it is a distinct question from whether an actual tenth-century queen stood behind the story (weighed separately) and from whether the Ethiopian dynastic claim — Makeda, Menelik, and the Ark — holds as history.1

Who advances it

The South-Arabian identification is the mainstream of modern philology and archaeology: Sheba (Heb. Shəḇāʾ) is the kingdom of Saba attested in its own Ancient South Arabian inscriptions and by classical geographers, and scholars from Kenneth Kitchen onward read the biblical episode against the documented Sabaean incense trade. The Ethiopian localization is the older reception-tradition: Flavius Josephus (c. AD 93) already made her “a woman queen of Egypt and Ethiopia,” and the fourteenth-century Kebra Nagast made Ethiopia her home and the seat of a Solomonic dynasty — the reading still held in Ethiopian national and church tradition. The two need not be flatly opposed: Sabaean culture spanned both shores of the Red Sea, so an African setting for a Sabaean queen is not a contradiction in terms.2

The evidence for South Arabia

Three lines converge on Saba. The name. “Sheba” is the Sabaean kingdom’s own name; the vowelless South Arabian script leaves it read Sabaʾ / Shəḇaʾ alike, and the biblical Shəḇāʾ maps onto it directly. The genealogy. The Table of Nations places a Sheba among the sons of Joktan — the Semitic, Arabian line — alongside Hazarmaveth (Ḥaḍramawt) and other unmistakably South-Arabian names (Genesis 10:28), and Sheba recurs across the Hebrew Bible as a byword for the Arabian caravan trade. The trade. The queen’s gifts — “camels that bare spices, and very much gold, and precious stones” — are precisely the wares the prophets attach to Sheba: Ezekiel’s “merchants of Sheba… occupied in thy fairs with chief of all spices, and with all precious stones, and gold” (Ezekiel 27:22); Isaiah’s “all they from Sheba shall come: they shall bring gold and incense” (Isaiah 60:6); “the kings of Sheba and Seba shall offer gifts” (Psalm 72:10); “the companies of Sheba” (Job 6:19). Frankincense and myrrh grew in South Arabia and the Horn and nowhere near Israel; a monarch arriving overland by camel with incense, gold, and gems is a monarch of the incense road, and Saba — capital Marib, whose great dam is one of the engineering wonders of the ancient world — sat astride it. Saba is, moreover, richly attested archaeologically and epigraphically, and its inscriptions do record a queen (mlkt); the very personal name Ethiopian legend gives her, Makeda, has been read as a popular echo of the Sabaean official title mqtwyt.3

The evidence for Ethiopia — steelmanned

The African reading is not a mere pious invention. It is old: Josephus set the queen in “Egypt and Ethiopia” a full millennium before the Kebra Nagast, and the ambiguity is already in the Hebrew text — the Table of Nations lists a second Sheba on the Cushite (African-associated) side, a son of Raamah son of Cush (Genesis 10:7), so a reader could anchor Sheba on either shore. And there really was a Sabaean presence in the Horn: from about the eighth century BC an Ethio-Sabaean polity, Dʿmt, flourished in Tigray and Eritrea, its Great Temple at Yeha built in resolutely Sabaean style and dedicated to the South-Arabian moon-god Almaqah; one inscription even styles a ruler mukarrib of both Dʿmt and Saba. So the incense trade, the script, and the cult crossed the Red Sea, and “Sheba” on the African side is not geographically absurd. This is why the localizations are not cleanly exclusive: a Sabaean queen whose sphere reached into the Horn could be claimed, without contradiction, by both Yemen and Ethiopia.4

Adversarial assessment

The African reading’s weaknesses. Weighed as a claim about the queen’s actual homeland, the Ethiopian localization is late and secondary. Josephus’s “Ethiopia” reflects the elastic Greek usage in which Aithiopia could cover any southern land — and it runs together with the Cushite Sheba of Genesis 10:7 and the general ancient conflation of Cush/Nubia with the lands beyond Egypt; it is a Hellenistic re-mapping, not an independent memory. The Kebra Nagast is fourteenth-century and serves a dynastic purpose (see the descent argument). Decisively, the African polity that could host such a queen, Dʿmt, and the kingdom the tradition actually names, Aksum, both post-date Solomon: Dʿmt flourishes from roughly the eighth century BC and Aksum rises only around the turn of the era — centuries too late for a tenth-century visit. The Sabaean presence in the Horn, on current scholarship, was a limited trading/military contact, not a colonization, and it too belongs to the eighth–fifth centuries BC. So the material that makes Ethiopia possible is itself later than the episode it is invoked to explain.

A shared chronological caveat — and why it does not decide the geography. The same lateness cuts at Saba: on the standard reckoning the Sabaean kingdom, too, is best attested only from the eighth century BC, some two centuries after Solomon, which is why some (Finkelstein and Silberman) doubt a tenth-century queen existed at all. But that is a question about historicity — whether any such queen reigned in Solomon’s day — not about where the name Sheba points; it is weighed on its own. On the geographic question the asymmetry is plain: if the biblical Sheba names a real place, the name, the genealogy, and the trade-goods all name Saba in South Arabia, whereas the African localization rests on a later re-reading. And the caravan trade itself is older than the monumental Sabaean state, so “Saba is only attested from the eighth century” does not unseat South Arabia as the referent the way it would unseat a specific king-list date.

What is nonetheless true in the Ethiopian reading. Because Sabaean civilization straddled the Red Sea, the choice is not simply “Yemen, not Africa.” The honest verdict is that the biblical Sheba is Sabaean and Sabaean means, first and most naturally, South Arabia — but a Sabaean world that included the Horn is what let the African tradition grow, and that tradition became, in Ethiopia, one of the most consequential national myths in the world.

Assessment

Sound: the biblical Sheba is the South-Arabian kingdom of Saba (Yemen), the reading on which the name, the Joktanite genealogy, and the gold-spices-gems caravan trade of 1 Kings 10 converge, and which the epigraphic and archaeological record of Saba supports. The Ethiopian/Aksum localization is weak as a claim about the actual homeland of a tenth-century queen — it is a later, secondary re-mapping (Josephus → Kebra Nagast), and the African polities that could host it post-date Solomon — but it is not baseless, since Sabaean culture, script, and cult crossed into the Horn, so the two localizations are not cleanly exclusive. A separate chronological doubt (Saba, like Dʿmt, is best attested only from the eighth century BC) bears on the queen’s historicity, not on which land the name Sheba denotes.
