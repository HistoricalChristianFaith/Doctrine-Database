The rabbinic legend of the Queen of Sheba

Dates: c. 3rd–13th century AD (a reception stratum, not a single source: the Amoraic Talmudic haggadah of the 3rd–5th centuries, the fullest legend in the Targum Sheni to Esther whose redaction is placed in the late Byzantine to early-Islamic period, and the demonic / Lilith overlay of the medieval kabbalists, climaxing in the 13th-century Zohar) · Wikipedia
Summary: Where Scripture leaves the queen a dignified Gentile seeker and Josephus makes her a philosopher, the rabbis spun her visit into a full-blown romance and then, by degrees, into something sinister. The great text is the Targum Sheni (the “Second Targum”) to Esther, whose long expansion on Esther 1:2 has Solomon rule the beasts, birds, and demons; a missing wild-cock (the hoopoe) carry his letter of summons to Queen “Saba” at Kitor; and — most famously — the king seat her over a floor of glass she mistakes for water, so that she raises her skirt and bares hairy legs, drawing his rebuke that hair “is becoming to a man, but to a woman it is a shame.” She poses riddles; he solves them. Around this grew a darker reading: the Babylonian Talmud already de-genders her (“not a woman but a kingdom”), the Targum on Job identifies the raiding “Sheba” with Lilith, and the medieval kabbalists fused the two — the hairy-legged Queen of Sheba became a night-demon, a seductress, a child-snatching witch. The stately queen of 1 Kings is, by the close of this stratum, half woman and half jinn.

Where it sits

This is not one dated witness but a stratum — the deposit of what the synagogue and the study-house said about the Queen of Sheba across roughly a millennium, best read as a layer rather than a moment. Its earliest element is Amoraic: the Babylonian Talmud (redacted by the 6th century, but preserving 3rd–5th-century tradents) already turns her from a person into a polity. Its centrepiece is the Targum Sheni to Esther — an Aramaic paraphrase so expansive it is really a midrashic anthology hung on the book of Esther — whose Solomon-and-Sheba narrative is the fullest telling of the legend anywhere; nineteenth-century scholars dated it from the 4th century (Gelbhaus) to the 11th (Munk), Paulus Cassel to the 6th, and the present consensus places its redaction “somewhere between the late Byzantine period to the early Islamic period,” the Encyclopaedia Judaica favouring a 7th–8th-century date on the Galilean-Aramaic linguistic evidence. Its latest layer is medieval and mystical: the identification of the queen with the demoness Lilith, known to Moses de León, hinted at in the 13th-century Zohar, and stated outright in the kabbalah of his circle. Because a legend’s wording can be centuries younger than the tradition it carries, the block is slotted here among the Jewish reception strata — after Josephus, and before the Christian Fathers’ reading of her as the Gentile church — not as a claim that every motif is first-century.1

Claims & evidence

The hoopoe carries Solomon’s letter of summons (Targum Sheni)

The Targum Sheni opens by making Solomon lord of the whole creation — “the Holy One, blessed be He! made [him] to rule over all the beasts of the field, and over the fowls of the air, and over the creeping things of the earth, and over devils, demons, and spirits, whose language he understood as they also understood his.” At a feast, to overawe the visiting kings, he summons every creature to dance before him; the royal scribes call them by name and they come unbound. But one is missing:

At that time, the cock of the wood was missed among the fowls, and was not found. Then the king commanded in anger that he should appear before him, or else he would destroy him. Then the cock of the wood answered and said to King Solomon: ‘O lord of the earth, incline thine ears and hear my words… I have flown all over the world… Then I saw a certain country, the name of whose fortified town is Kitor, whose dust is more precious than gold, and where silver lies about like dung in the streets…’

— Targum Sheni to Esther (on Esther 1:2), in Paulus Cassel’s translation. The wild-cock — the Aramaic tarnegol bara, which Cassel renders “the cock of the wood” and other translators the hoopoe — reports that a woman rules that golden land, “her name is Queen of Saba,” and offers to fetch her. Solomon writes a letter, ties it to the bird’s wing, and it flies to Kitor at the head of a cloud of birds that darken the sun. The letter is a summons with a threat: come and be honoured, or “I shall send kings, legions, and riders against thee… My army consists of devils, demons, and spirits, who will strangle you in your beds.” The queen loads her ships with pearls and precious stones, sends six thousand boys and girls all born in the same hour and dressed in purple, and answers that though the journey is seven years she will come in three. This is the same beat the Qur’an would independently carry — there, too, it is a bird (the hoopoe) that tells Solomon of the queen and bears his letter — and the Jewish Encyclopedia notes the parallel directly.2

The floor of glass and the hairy legs

The legend’s most enduring image is the trap Solomon lays for the queen when she arrives:

Then Benayahu conducted her to the king, who, when he heard that she was coming, went and sat down in an apartment of glass. When the queen saw the king sitting there, she thought in her heart, and in fact said, that he was sitting in water, and she raised her dress to cross the water, when the king noticed that her foot was full of hair. He said to her, ‘Thy beauty is the beauty of women, and thy hair is the hair of men; hair is becoming to a man, but to a woman it is a shame.’

— Targum Sheni to Esther (Cassel). The floor of polished glass or crystal reads as a sheet of water; the queen lifts her skirt to wade; and the king sees what the trick was designed to expose — that her legs are covered in hair. On the surface it is a jest about an unwomanly blemish and a moral about vanity. Underneath, as the later tradition makes explicit, the hairy leg is a diagnostic: in Jewish folklore the mark of a demon — of Lilith and her kind — is that beneath a beautiful face the body is bestial, the feet clawed or shaggy. Solomon, the master of demons, is testing whether his visitor is wholly human. The Jewish Encyclopedia preserves the same scene almost word for word: on her arrival “he received her in a glass house. Thinking that Solomon was sitting in the water, she lifted up her skirt, whereupon he noticed hair on her feet… ‘Thy beauty is a woman’s beauty, but thy hair a man’s hair; hair adorneth man, but disfigureth woman.’”3

The riddles she posed

Scripture says only that the queen came “to prove him with hard questions” (1 Kings 10:1); the rabbis, like Josephus before them, filled the blank with actual enigmas. The Targum Sheni gives three, each a plain riddle with a homely answer:

She asked, ‘What is berries of wood and buckets of iron which draw up stones and pour out water?’ He answered, ‘A tube of paint.’ ‘What is,’ she asked again, ‘a thing which comes as dust from the earth, eats dust, is poured out as water, and sticks to a house?’ He answered, ‘It is naphtha.’ She further asked, ‘What is that which… goes at the head of all, cries loudly and bitterly with its head bowed down like a rush, is a cause of praise to the free, of shame to the poor, of honour to the dead, of disgrace to the living, of joy to the birds, and of grief to the fish?’ He answered, ‘It is flax.’

— Targum Sheni to Esther (Cassel). Cassel himself notes that these three are unlike the riddles Solomon solves in Midrash Mishlei (the Midrash on Proverbs), where the queen’s puzzles turn on Scripture and on the body. In the Buber recension of that midrash she asks, “What are the seven that issue and nine that enter, the two that offer drink, and the one that drinks?” and Solomon answers, “The seven that issue are the seven days of menstrual impurity. The nine that enter are the nine months of pregnancy. The two that offer drink are the breasts, and the child is the one who drinks.” The riddle-contest is a floating tradition attached to the visit in several forms — three enigmas in the Targum, a longer catalogue elsewhere — all of them elaborations of the terse “hard questions” of 1 Kings 10.4

“Not a woman but a kingdom” — the Talmud de-genders her

Alongside the romance runs a startling counter-current in which the rabbis deny that a Queen of Sheba ever visited Solomon at all. In the Babylonian Talmud, tractate Bava Batra, the point is made by reading the Hebrew malkat (“queen”) as malkhuta (“kingdom”):

Rabbi Shmuel bar Naḥmani says that Rabbi Yonatan says: Anyone who says that the queen of Sheba [malkat Sheva] who came to visit King Solomon… was a woman is nothing other than mistaken. What is the meaning of malkat Sheva? The kingdom [malkhuta] of Sheba, as is mentioned in Job: “And Sheba fell on them and took them away” (Job 1:15).

— Bava Batra 15b (William Davidson translation). The move is exegetical, not misogynistic in intent: the sage is resolving what he takes to be a difficulty by turning the visiting queen into a marauding realm, citing the “Sheba” that raided Job’s herds. But it does two things that matter for the later legend: it unsettles the queen’s identity, and it links her name to Job 1:15 — the very verse the Aramaic Targum on Job reads not as a human raider but as Lilith, “the Queen of Zemargad.” The bridge from “kingdom of Sheba” to “queen of demons” is built on this verse.5

The demonic overlay: the Queen of Sheba as Lilith

By the Middle Ages the hairy-legged queen and the raiding “Sheba” of Job had been fused into a single figure: the Queen of Sheba as Lilith, the arch-demoness. The identification runs through the kabbalists; Gershom Scholem traces its logic:

Widespread, too, is the identification of Lilith with the Queen of Sheba — a notion with many ramifications in Jewish folklore. It originates in the Targum to Job 1:15 based on a Jewish and Arab myth that the Queen of Sheba was actually a jinn, half human and half demon… This view was known to Moses b. Shem Tov de Leon and is also mentioned in the Zohar… Until recent generations the Queen of Sheba was popularly pictured as a snatcher of children and a demonic witch.

— Gershom Scholem, “Lilith,” Encyclopaedia Judaica. Scholem locates the identification’s origin not in the Zohar but in the Targum to Job 1:15 — the raiding “Sheba” read as Lilith, “queen of Zmargad” — and reports that the Zohar itself only hints at the demon-queen material; the identification is stated outright only in the later kabbalah of de León’s circle, above all Joseph Angelino’s Livnat ha-Sappir, which holds that the very riddles the queen posed to Solomon are “a repetition of the words of seduction which the first Lilith spoke to Adam.” The nineteenth-century commentator Paulus Cassel, whose translation carries the legend, had already drawn the same connection from the philology, tying the queen to Lilith “who is also understood as a night spectre, from laylah, night… Such a one appears here as queen and murderess.” The Jewish Encyclopedia’s article on Lilith records the endpoint of the tradition flatly: “In a certain legend she appears as the Queen of Sheba, who in the guise of a beautiful woman seduced a poor Jew of Worms.” So the same body of tradition that made her a golden-shipped monarch quizzing Solomon with riddles also made her a shape-shifting seductress with hairy legs — the two poles between which the whole rabbinic Queen of Sheba oscillates.6

Notes

  • Why the glass floor matters: the hairy leg is a demon-test. The trick reads on the surface as a joke about vanity, but its point in the tradition is diagnostic. In Jewish (and shared Near-Eastern) folklore the tell-tale sign of a demon — of Lilith and the lilin — is that a beautiful woman’s body is bestial beneath: shaggy, clawed, or bird-footed. Solomon, master of the demons, floors his hall with glass precisely to make the queen lift her skirt and betray whether she is wholly human. The motif is thus the hinge between the two halves of the legend — the courtly riddle-queen and the demoness — and it is why the medieval identification with Lilith could fasten on this scene.
  • “Queen of Sheba” → “queen of demons” is a trajectory, not a single pun. It is sometimes said the rabbis punned malkat Sheva (queen of Sheba) into malkat shedim (queen of demons). That neat wordplay is a modern summary of the tradition’s drift, not a sentence in any primary text: the Talmud only de-genders her to malkhuta, “kingdom” (Bava Batra 15b), and the demonic reading arrives later, by way of the Job 1:15 / Lilith link and the medieval kabbalah. The distinction is worth keeping so the legend’s real mechanism — a chain of exegetical steps — is not flattened into a coincidence of spelling.
  • There is no clean verse-anchored Zohar text naming the queen — the explicit locus is Livnat ha-Sappir. A firsthand hunt for a Zohar passage that plainly names the Queen of Sheba turns up none, and that is itself the finding: Gershom Scholem locates the identification’s origin not in the Zohar but in the Targum to Job 1:15 (already cited above), and reports that the Zohar itself only hints at the demon-queen material — the two harlots judged before Solomon read as Lilith and Naamah/Agrat — pinning the specifics to his own Hebrew study in Tarbiz 19 (1947/48), pp. 172–175. The queen is identified with Lilith outright only in the later kabbalah of de León’s circle: the graded demon-queen hierarchy systematized from Isaac b. Jacob ha-Kohen’s sources and the Tikkunei Zohar, and, most explicitly, Joseph Angelino’s Livnat ha-Sappir (early 14th c.), which makes her riddles a reprise of Lilith’s seduction of Adam. These kabbalistic loci are reported here through Scholem’s survey rather than read firsthand, so the strand is still cited at one remove — but the remove is now to named texts, not a vague “the Zohar.”
  • Islamic parallel. The hoopoe-messenger and the glass-pavilion (there a floor of crystal over water, at which the queen bares her legs) recur in the Qur’anic and later Islamic Bilqis legend (Qur’an 27:22–44) — a striking convergence with the Targum Sheni. Whether Jewish tradition fed the Islamic or the two drew on a common Near-Eastern stock is the subject of Jacob Lassner’s standard study; on this timeline the Islamic Bilqis has its own queued block, and only the Jewish side is treated here.
  • Text and editions. The Targum Sheni is quoted in Paulus Cassel’s English translation (An Explanatory Commentary on Esther, Edinburgh, 1888, Appendix I, “The Second Targum”), the fullest readily-linkable English of the whole Solomon–Sheba narrative; the standard critical edition is Bernard Grossfeld, The Two Targums of Esther (The Aramaic Bible 18, Collegeville, 1991). Bava Batra is quoted in the William Davidson translation. The demon strand is surveyed in Gershom Scholem’s Encyclopaedia Judaica “Lilith,” and the whole demonizing arc — Jewish and Islamic — in Jacob Lassner, Demonizing the Queen of Sheba: Boundaries of Gender and Culture in Postbiblical Judaism and Medieval Islam (Chicago, 1993).
