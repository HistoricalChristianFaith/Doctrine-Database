The Ministerial Priesthood — who may offer the Eucharist

Who may offer, or preside at, the Eucharist, and is that ministry a priesthood? This page traces, earliest extant attestation first, how Christian writers, church orders and councils have spoken of who may offer the Eucharist.

The communions answer in their own terms. The Council of Trent (1563) teaches that Christ instituted "a new, visible, and external priesthood," and that to the apostles and their successors "was the power delivered of consecrating, offering, and administering His Body and Blood"; the Second Vatican Council (1964) holds that the common priesthood of the faithful and the ministerial priesthood "differ from one another in essence and not only in degree" (see their entries below).1,2 Protopresbyter Thomas Hopko's The Orthodox Faith (Orthodox Church in America) states that "Christ is the only priest, pastor and teacher of the Christian Church," and that bishops, priests and deacons "have no other function or service than to manifest the presence and action of Christ to his people." It calls the sacrament of holy orders "the objective guarantee of the perpetual presence of Christ with his people," and adds that "through His chosen ministers, Christ exercises and realizes His unique and exclusive function as priest, perpetually offering Himself as the perfect sacrifice to the Father on behalf of His human brothers and sisters." Its priests "preside at the celebration of the liturgy," are "assigned by the bishop," and serve at an altar bearing "the antimension signed by the bishop which is the permission to the community to gather and to act as the Church of God."3 The Lutheran Apology of the Augsburg Confession (art. XIII) says that ministers "are called priests, not in order to make any sacrifices for the people as in the Law," but "to teach the Gospel and administer the Sacraments to the people," and adds: "Nor do we have another priesthood like the Levitical, as the Epistle to the Hebrews sufficiently teaches."4

Witnesses below connect the offering with three convictions: that the Eucharist is a true offering, the "pure offering" the prophet Malachi foresaw being made "in every place" among the nations (the Didache, Justin and Irenaeus); that Christ instituted it at the Last Supper and there charged the apostles, "this do in remembrance of me" (Thomas Aquinas); and that the power committed to the apostles passed to "their successors in the priesthood" (the Council of Trent, ch. 1). Leo the Great and Lumen Gentium, among others below, also speak of the priesthood of the whole baptized people. Trent's first canon on order anathematized the view that the New Testament has "only an office and bare ministry of preaching the Gospel" (see its entry below).5

The Scriptural texts

Four strands of biblical text are cited in the discussion:

  • The institution at the Last Supper — "when the hour was come, he sat down, and the twelve apostles with him … And he took bread, and gave thanks, and brake it, and gave unto them, saying, This is my body which is given for you: this do in remembrance of me" (Luke 22:14–19; cf. Matthew 26:26–28; Mark 14:22–24). Thomas Aquinas, below, reads the command "this do" as addressed to the apostles present.6
  • The commission and delegated authority — the risen Christ's "as my Father hath sent me, even so send I you … Receive ye the Holy Ghost: Whose soever sins ye remit, they are remitted unto them" (John 20:21–23), and the charge to the seventy, "He that heareth you heareth me; and he that despiseth you despiseth him that sent me" (Luke 10:16; cf. Luke 10:1). John Chrysostom, below, invokes John 20:23 for the authority of priests.7
  • The prophesied pure offering — "from the rising of the sun even unto the going down of the same my name shall be great among the Gentiles; and in every place incense shall be offered unto my name, and a pure offering" (Malachi 1:10–11), read in the Didache, Justin and Irenaeus as fulfilled in the Eucharist.8
  • The one sacrifice of Christ and the priesthood of the baptized — Christ, "after he had offered one sacrifice for sins for ever, sat down on the right hand of God… For by one offering he hath perfected for ever them that are sanctified" (Hebrews 10:12–14; cf. 7:27), and the baptized are "an holy priesthood, to offer up spiritual sacrifices" and "a royal priesthood" (1 Peter 2:5, 9). The Apology of the Augsburg Confession, above, appeals to Hebrews; Pius XII, below, explains the Mass as the same sacrifice as Calvary, since "the priest is the same, Jesus Christ"; and Lumen Gentium, below, calls the baptized "a spiritual house and a holy priesthood."9

Whether these texts establish a distinct ordained priesthood that alone may offer the Eucharist, and how such a ministry relates to the priesthood of all the baptized, the communions answer in the statements quoted above; see also the entry for the Council of Trent below and the Disputed readings.

Timeline

The witnesses are listed earliest first. This timeline is in its early stages and will be filled out as the doctrine's development across the centuries is traced.

Clement of Rome (c. 96)

Writing from Rome to quell a revolt at Corinth that had ejected its presbyters, Clement gives the earliest extant text to set the Levitical order of offerings beside the church's divinely ordered, appointed ministry. God himself, he argues, "has fixed by His own supreme will" both "where and by whom" the offerings are to be presented and at what times; as the old covenant assigned distinct ministrations to high priest, priests, and Levites while "the layman is bound by the laws that pertain to laymen," so each Christian must keep "the rule of the ministry prescribed to him" and not go beyond it (1 Clement 40–41).10 He traces the ministry's authority through a chain — God sent Christ, Christ the apostles, and the apostles: "preaching through countries and cities, they appointed the first-fruits… to be bishops and deacons of those who should afterwards believe" (1 Clement 42).11 Foreseeing "strife on account of the office of the episcopate," the apostles further "gave instructions, that when these should fall asleep, other approved men should succeed them in their ministry" — so the deposed Corinthian presbyters, he concludes, "cannot be justly dismissed from the ministry" (1 Clement 44).12 Clement does not call the Christian minister a "priest" (hiereus); the men he says the apostles appointed "bishops and deacons" (ch. 42) are those he describes as holding "the episcopate" and calls "presbyters" (ch. 44).13

The Didache (c. 100)

The church-order manual is the earliest extant post-apostolic text to call the Sunday Eucharist a sacrifice and to read it as the fulfilment of Malachi's "pure offering." The assembly gathers "every Lord's day" to "break bread and give thanksgiving," confessing its transgressions and being reconciled first, "that your sacrifice may be pure… For this is that which was spoken by the Lord: In every place and time offer to me a pure sacrifice; for I am a great King, saith the Lord, and my name is wonderful among the nations" (Didache 14).14 The next chapter directs the church to "appoint… bishops and deacons worthy of the Lord… for they also render to you the service of prophets and teachers" (Didache 15); the text does not say who presides at the offering.15

Ignatius of Antioch (c. 108)

Writing to the churches of Asia Minor on his way to martyrdom at Rome, Ignatius gives the earliest extant rule that a Eucharist is proper only when the bishop, or one he authorizes, presides: "Let that be deemed a proper Eucharist, which is administered either by the bishop, or by one to whom he has entrusted it… It is not lawful without the bishop either to baptize or to celebrate a love-feast" (Smyrnaeans 8).16 The congregation is to keep "but one Eucharist… one altar; as there is one bishop, along with the presbytery and deacons" (Philadelphians 4).17 Ignatius writes of one bishop with a presbytery and deacons in the churches he addresses, and ties the Eucharist to that bishop (whether this describes a settled or a newly urged order is a disputed reading: see “Do Ignatius’s letters describe an established episcopate, or urge a new one?”); the clause "or by one to whom he has entrusted it" provides for delegation. Whether a later Greek tradition that Ignatius was the child Jesus took in his arms preserves a memory of him is a disputed reading below.18

Justin Martyr (c. 155)

Justin gives the earliest extant detailed description of the rite in practice. At the Sunday assembly the people's gifts of bread and "a cup of wine mixed with water" are "brought to the president of the brethren," who "offers prayers and thanksgivings, according to his ability," the people answering "Amen"; then "those who are called by us deacons" distribute the elements "and to those who are absent a portion is sent" (First Apology 65, 67).19 The food so blessed is received "not as common bread and common drink" but "the flesh and blood of that Jesus who was made flesh," and no one is "allowed to partake" but the baptized believer in right standing (First Apology 66).20 In the Dialogue with Trypho he reads Malachi's "pure offering… in every place" as fulfilled "in the Eucharist of the bread and the cup… presented by Christians in all places throughout the world," against the rejected sacrifices of the Jewish priests (Dialogue 117).21 Justin does not call the president a "priest" or "bishop," and he calls the whole believing people "the true high priestly race of God" (Dialogue 116).21

Irenaeus of Lyons (c. 189)

Writing against the Gnostics, Irenaeus names the Eucharist "the new oblation of the new covenant" — the "pure sacrifice" Malachi foretold — "which the Church receiving from the apostles, offers to God throughout all the world," and writes that "the class of oblations in general has not been set aside… sacrifices there are, too, in the Church: but the species alone has been changed, inasmuch as the offering is now made, not by slaves, but by freemen" (Against Heresies IV.17–18).22 He makes the public succession of bishops his test of the apostolic tradition: one can "reckon up those who were by the apostles instituted bishops in the Churches, and [to demonstrate] the succession of these men to our own times" (III.3), so that "it is incumbent to obey the presbyters who are in the Church,—those who, as I have shown, possess the succession from the apostles; those who, together with the succession of the episcopate, have received the certain gift of truth" (IV.26.2).23 Like Justin before him and Tertullian after, he also calls "all the righteous" and "all the apostles of the Lord" priests (IV.8.3).24

Tertullian (c. 208)

Tertullian gives the earliest extant Latin statement of a distinction between the "Order" (ordo) and the laity, in a passage that also calls the laity priests. Arguing that clergy must be men of one marriage, he takes up the objection that what is barred to priests is open to laymen: "Are not even we laics priests?… It is the authority of the Church, and the honour which has acquired sanctity through the joint session of the Order, which has established the difference between the Order and the laity. Accordingly, where there is no joint session of the ecclesiastical Order, you offer, and baptize, and are priest, alone for yourself. But where three are, a church is, albeit they be laics" (On Exhortation to Chastity 7).25 In On Baptism he ranks the right to confer the sacrament: "the chief priest (who is the bishop) has the right: in the next place, the presbyters and deacons, yet not without the bishop's authority… Beside these, even laymen have the right" — in necessity, and without usurping "the specific function of the bishop," for "emulation of the episcopal office is the mother of schisms" (On Baptism 17).26 He grounds the distinction in the Church's authority and honour, and allows the layman to offer and baptize where no clergy are present.25

Hippolytus of Rome (c. 215)

The Apostolic Tradition preserves the earliest surviving ordination prayers. The bishop is consecrated in high-priestly terms — God is asked to grant him "to serve as thy high priest without blame… to offer thee the gifts of thy holy church," and "by the Spirit of high-priesthood to have authority to remit sins" (Apostolic Tradition 3).27 The deacon, by contrast, "is not ordained to the priesthood but to serve the bishop… He does not receive that Spirit that is possessed by the presbytery, in which the presbyters share"; presbyters join in laying hands on a new presbyter "because of the common and like Spirit of the clergy," while the deacon is ordained by the bishop alone (Apostolic Tradition 8–9).28 (The church-order is traditionally ascribed to Hippolytus and dated c. 215. Allen Brent (1995) and Alistair Stewart (2015) defend the ascription and a date before 235; Paul Bradshaw, Maxwell Johnson and L. Edward Phillips (2002) hold that its liturgical sections combine material of different origins, compiled c. 375–400.)29

Cyprian of Carthage (c. 253)

Cyprian writes that the eucharistic minister acts in the place of Christ. Correcting those who offered water alone in the cup, he reasons that since Christ "is Himself the chief priest of God the Father, and has first offered Himself a sacrifice… and has commanded this to be done in commemoration of Himself, certainly that priest truly discharges the office of Christ, who imitates that which Christ did; and he then offers a true and full sacrifice in the Church to God the Father" (Epistle 62, to Caecilius).30 The rite's form is "divinely appointed" and may not be "changed by human tradition into anything else," for "we may not break even the least of the Lord's commandments."31 In On the Unity of the Church he writes that "the flesh of Christ… cannot be sent abroad, nor is there any other home to believers but the one Church," and holds that there is one Eucharist within the one episcopal communion and none in schism.32

The First Council of Nicaea (325)

The first ecumenical council gives the earliest extant conciliar text reserving the offering of the Eucharist to the higher orders. Canon 18 corrects districts where deacons had been administering the Eucharist to the presbyters, and even handling it before the bishops: "neither canon nor custom permits that they who have no right to offer should give the Body of Christ to them that do offer." The deacons are to "remain within their own bounds, knowing that they are the ministers of the bishop and the inferiors of the presbyters," receiving the Eucharist after them, so that "either the bishop or the presbyter administer to them" (Canon 18).33 The canon appeals to existing "canon" and "custom," distinguishes offering (the bishop's and presbyter's right) from distributing (the deacon's role), and sets deposition as the penalty for deacons who disobey.33

Cyril of Jerusalem (c. 350)

Cyril's last mystagogic lecture gives a step-by-step liturgical description. Walking the newly baptized through the liturgy of the faithful, he names the celebrant throughout "the Priest" (ho hiereus) — where Justin had said "president" — and sets around him a graded ministry: the deacon serves and cries the directions, the presbyters "stand round God's altar," and the priest leads the thanksgiving (Mystagogic Catechesis 5.2–5).34 The Spirit is invoked upon the gifts — "we beseech the merciful God to send forth His Holy Spirit upon the gifts… that He may make the Bread the Body of Christ, and the Wine the Blood of Christ" (5.7) — the Eastern epiclesis; Ambrose (c. 390) locates the change in Christ's words spoken by the priest.35 The offering is called a sacrifice: "after the spiritual sacrifice, the bloodless service, is completed, over that sacrifice of propitiation we… offer this sacrifice," even "Christ sacrificed for our sins" (5.8–10).36 (The Mystagogical Catecheses are ascribed to Cyril, c. 350, or to his successor John of Jerusalem, c. 390; see the detail page.)

Aerius of Sebaste (c. 355)

Aerius is the earliest named figure on record to deny that bishop outranks presbyter. A presbyter of Sebaste passed over for its bishopric, he held, on Epiphanius's report, that "a bishop is no different from a presbyter… one order, and one honor and one rank," reciting the functions the two share: "A bishop lays on hands, but so does a presbyter… The bishop performs the eucharistic liturgy, the presbyter likewise" (Panarion 75.3, as Epiphanius quotes him to refute him).37 Every function on his list is assigned to presbyter and bishop alike; the quoted list does not mention the layman. Epiphanius answered by making ordination the bishop's distinguishing power (the episcopate alone "generates fathers… since he is not ordained for the purpose of ordaining, how could a presbyter consecrate a bishop?") and catalogued the position as the seventy-fifth heresy; Jerome (c. 393) also names ordination as the one function reserved to a bishop.38

The Apostolic Constitutions (c. 380)

The Syrian church-order sets the offering inside a developed eucharistic liturgy. Its Sunday assembly is the fulfilment of Malachi's "pure offering": the faithful gather "that your sacrifice may be unspotted, and acceptable to God, who has said concerning His universal Church: 'In every place shall incense and a pure sacrifice be offered unto me; for I am a great King, saith the Lord Almighty'" — an expansion of the Didache's reading (VII.30).39 The one who offers is the bishop, whom the book styles the "high priest": at the altar "let the high priest… put on his shining garment, and stand at the altar" and lead the great thanksgiving; at his consecration God is asked that he "may discharge the duty of a high priest… and present to Thee the gifts of Thy holy Church, and in the spirit of the high-priesthood have power to remit sins"; and those "who ought not to offer" the "sacrifices and eucharistical offices," usurping "the honour of the high-priesthood," are likened to Corah, Saul, and Uzziah (VIII; cf. II.27).40

Ambrose of Milan (c. 390)

Ambrose places the consecration in Christ's words of institution, spoken by the priest. Catechizing the newly baptized, he points them to the threefold ministry they have just seen — "You saw there the deacon, you saw the priest, you saw the chief priest [i.e. the bishop]" (On the Mysteries 2.6).41 The change, he says, is by no natural process but by the dominical word: "that sacrament which you receive is made what it is by the word of Christ… Before the blessing of the heavenly words another nature is spoken of, after the consecration the Body is signified" (On the Mysteries 9.52–54); and at the consecration "the priest uses not his own expressions, but he uses the expressions of Christ. Thus the expression of Christ performs this sacrament" (On the Sacraments IV.4.14).42 His change-of-nature language is also cited on the Real Presence page.

John Chrysostom (c. 392)

Chrysostom's Treatise on the Priesthood sets out the dignity of the priestly office. The priesthood "is indeed discharged on earth, but it ranks amongst heavenly ordinances," for "neither man, nor angel, nor archangel… but the Paraclete Himself, instituted this vocation"; and at the altar "there stands the priest, not bringing down fire from Heaven, but the Holy Spirit" (On the Priesthood 3.4).43 Priests, he writes, "have received an authority which God has not given to angels or archangels" — to bind and loose and to forgive sins — so that "what priests do here below God ratifies above"; and regeneration and "the flesh of the Lord" are "accomplished only by means of those holy hands, I mean the hands of the priest" (3.5–6).44 Chrysostom calls the Christian minister a "priest" (hiereus), a title Clement had not used.43,13 The same treatise is cited on the Real Presence page.

Jerome (c. 393)

Jerome holds that bishop and presbyter were originally one office. Expounding Titus and again in Letter 146 to Evangelus, he points to the New Testament's interchange of titles: "a presbyter… is the same as a bishop," since Paul calls the same men both (Philippians 1:1; Acts 20:28; Titus 1:5–7). The single bishop set over the presbyters is, he argues, a later remedy: earlier "the churches were governed by the common council of the presbyters," until "it was decreed throughout the whole world that one chosen from among the presbyters should be set over the rest… and the seeds of schisms be removed" — bishops are "greater than the presbyters more by custom than by the truth of the Lord's arrangement," and the one power reserved to a bishop is ordination.45 The occasion of Letter 146 is a man who ranked deacons above presbyters; Jerome protests that a deacon — "a mere server of tables" — should not exalt himself over the presbyters "through whose prayers the body and blood of Christ are produced."46 The same letter says that wherever there is a bishop, "his dignity is one and his priesthood is one… All alike are successors of the apostles."46 The letter closes by saying that "the traditions handed down by the apostles were taken by them from the old testament," bishops, presbyters and deacons occupying in the church "the same positions as those which were occupied by Aaron, his sons, and the Levites in the temple."46

Augustine of Hippo (c. 400)

Answering the Donatists, who held that a sacrament from an unworthy minister was void, Augustine places the sacrament's power in Christ rather than in the minister's holiness: "although many ministers, be they righteous or unrighteous, should baptize, the virtue of baptism would be attributed to Him alone… Peter may baptize, but this is He that baptizeth… Judas may baptize, still this is He that baptizeth" (Tractates on John 6).47 From the same controversy he draws a parallel between baptism and orders: as a validly baptized man who secedes is not rebaptized on return, so those "ordained before their secession, are certainly not ordained again… they retain the sacrament of their ordination; and hence… they are not ranked with the laity" (On Baptism I.1).48 In the City of God he names the offering the Church's own sacrifice, made through the one High Priest — "the sacrifice which the Church continually celebrates in the sacrament of the altar… in which she teaches that she herself is offered" (X.6).49 His eucharistic language is also cited on the Real Presence page.

Leo the Great (c. 450)

Preaching year by year on the anniversary of his consecration, Pope Leo I describes the priesthood as a Christ-derived office passing through Peter (the NPNF translation heads the section "From Christ and through S. Peter the priesthood is handed on in perpetuity"). Reading "Thou art a priest for ever after the order of Melchizedeck" of Christ, "in whom was prefigured the eternal High Priest," he says that "now that the mystery of this Divine priesthood has descended to human agency, it runs not by the line of birth… those men are received by the Church as its rulers whom the Holy Ghost prepares: so that in the people of God's adoption, the whole body of which is priestly and royal, it is not the prerogative of earthly origin which obtains the unction, but the condescension of Divine grace which creates the bishop" (Sermon 3).50 He speaks in one sentence of the corporate priesthood and the ordained ministry: all the regenerate "are made kings by the sign of the cross… consecrated priests by the oil of the Holy Spirit, so that beyond the special service of our ministry as priests, all… are a royal race and are sharers in the office of the priesthood" (Sermon 4).51 On the altar he sets the New-Covenant sacrifice: with "the variety of fleshly sacrifices" now ceased, "the one offering of… Body and Blood fulfils all those different victims," presented by "a nobler rank of Levites… priests of holier anointing" (Sermon 59).52

Thomas Aquinas (c. 1274)

In the Summa Theologiae Aquinas asks "whether the consecration of this sacrament belongs to a priest alone." He reasons that because the Eucharist "is performed only as in the person of Christ," and "whoever performs any act in another's stead, must do so by the power bestowed by such a one," the lay believer receives at baptism the power to receive the sacrament, whereas "the power of consecrating this sacrament on Christ's behalf is bestowed upon the priest at his ordination: for thereby he is put upon a level with them to whom the Lord said (Luke 22:19): 'Do this for a commemoration of Me'" (Summa Theologiae III, Q. 82, a. 1).53 He concludes: "it must be said that it belongs to priests to accomplish this sacrament."54 On Aquinas's account the consecration is an act done in persona Christi by a power Christ confers at ordination, and he reads "Do this in remembrance of me" (Luke 22:19) as addressed to the apostles and carried to the ordained by ordination.55

The Council of Trent (1563)

Trent's twenty-third session defines, against the Reformers, the Catholic doctrine of the sacrament of order. Because the Church has received "the holy visible sacrifice of the Eucharist," the council reasons, "it must needs also be confessed, that there is… a new, visible, and external priesthood," instituted by Christ, "and… to the apostles, and their successors in the priesthood, was the power delivered of consecrating, offering, and administering His Body and Blood, as also of forgiving and of retaining sins" (Decree on the Sacrament of Order, ch. 1).1 Canon 1 anathematizes the view "that there is not in the New Testament a visible and external priesthood… but only an office and bare ministry of preaching the Gospel"; the canons that follow define order as a true sacrament that imprints a character (so that "he who has once been a priest" cannot "again become a layman") and affirm "a hierarchy by divine ordination instituted, consisting of bishops, priests, and ministers," with bishops "superior to priests" and holding the power of ordaining.5 The council presents this as what "the tradition of the Catholic Church has always taught."1

Pius XII (1947)

In the encyclical Mediator Dei, Pius XII teaches that the priest offers in the person of Christ. Explaining why the Mass is the one sacrifice of Calvary, he locates the identity in the celebrant — "The priest is the same, Jesus Christ, whose sacred Person his minister represents," for the minister, "by reason of the sacerdotal consecration which he has received, is made like to the High Priest and possesses the power of performing actions in virtue of Christ's very person" (Mediator Dei 69) — and grounds that power in apostolic succession: "Only to the apostles, and thenceforth to those on whom their successors have imposed hands, is granted the power of the priesthood" (§ 40).56 The faithful, he teaches, truly "offer," yet "the sacrament of holy orders sets the priest apart from the rest of the faithful who have not received this consecration" (§ 43).57

The Second Vatican Council (1964)

The dogmatic constitution Lumen Gentium affirms the common priesthood of all the baptized — the regenerate are "consecrated as a spiritual house and a holy priesthood… they may offer spiritual sacrifices" — and distinguishes it from the ordained ministry: "Though they differ from one another in essence and not only in degree (essentia et non gradu tantum), the common priesthood of the faithful and the ministerial or hierarchical priesthood are nonetheless interrelated: each of them in its own special way is a participation in the one priesthood of Christ." The ministerial priest, "acting in the person of Christ… makes present the Eucharistic sacrifice, and offers it to God in the name of all the people," while the faithful "in virtue of their royal priesthood, join in the offering" (Lumen Gentium 10).2
