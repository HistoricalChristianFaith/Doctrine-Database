Eternal conscious torment

What is the final fate of the lost? This page traces the belief that the punishment of the lost is conscious and never ends, called in the modern debate eternal conscious torment. Two other Christian answers are traced on neighbouring timelines: universal restoration, the belief that God at the last restores all, and conditional immortality, the belief that the lost finally cease to exist. Those who hold eternal conscious torment hold that the lost exist without end in conscious suffering, whether the soul's immortality is held to belong to it by nature or to be conferred and sustained by God. They read the Bible's language for the fate of the lost accordingly: the fire is “unquenchable” and the worm “dieth not” (Mark 9:48), the smoke of the torment goes up “for ever and ever” (Revelation 14:11; 20:10), and the “eternal punishment” of Matthew 25:46 is as endless as the “eternal life” set beside it in the same sentence. The words for destruction and death are read not as extinction but as an unending ruin: a “second death” in which, as Augustine put it, the lost cannot die.

Accounts of the nature of the torment differ. The Supplement to Aquinas's Summa Theologiae holds that the damned live for ever so that they may be punished for ever, which “will serve the purpose of Divine justice.”1 The Catechism of the Catholic Church (1992) describes hell as a “state of definitive self-exclusion from communion with God and the blessed” (§1033), in which souls “suffer the punishments of hell, ‘eternal fire’” (§1035).2 Kallistos Ware (Orthodox bishop, 1998), who hopes for the salvation of all, follows Isaac the Syrian's “scourgings of love” and describes the torment as the experience of divine love by those who refuse it: “God does not inflict torment upon those in hell, but it is they who torment themselves through their willful refusal to respond to His love.”3

The page runs earliest extant attestation first: Athenagoras (the wicked raised to judgment, and not “annihilated”); the Latin apologists Tertullian, Minucius Felix, Cyprian and Lactantius, and Hippolytus of Rome, writing in Greek; the fourth-century bishops Cyril of Jerusalem, Basil and John Chrysostom; Augustine's City of God XXI; the anathemas against the restoration of 543; Gregory the Great and John of Damascus; Thomas Aquinas; the Fifth Lateran Council's definition of the soul's immortality (1513); Jonathan Edwards; and the late-twentieth-century defence of the view in the evangelical debate over conditional immortality.

Related doctrines: The living and the dead (the communion of the saints) — see also universal restoration (apokatastasis) · conditional immortality (annihilationism) · the intermediate state · purgatory and prayer for the dead · prayer to the saints · dream-apparitions of the dead · indulgences · the treasury of merit · the resurrection of the body (what finally becomes of the lost — whether they are restored, finally cease to be, or are tormented forever).

The three answers read the same texts: the “eternal” (aiōnios) punishment of Matthew 25:46, the “tormented for ever and ever” of Revelation and the undying worm of Mark, the vocabulary of destruction and death (Matthew 10:28; Revelation 20:14; John 3:16), and the fire of Gehenna and the lake of fire. Holders of eternal conscious torment read the fire as one that torments and never ends; holders of universal restoration read it as remedial and ending in restoration; holders of conditional immortality read it as consuming and ending in extinction.

Timeline

Athenagoras of Athens (c. 177) — two destinies after death

Athenagoras, an Athenian Christian philosopher, holds that man “shall live another life” after death: the good “near God, and with God,” the rest “a worse one and in fire; for God has not made us as sheep or beasts of burden, a mere by-work, and that we should perish and be annihilated.”4 In his treatise on the resurrection he argues that since man “consists of the two parts, [he] must continue for ever,” each receiving “what he has done by the body, whether it be good or bad.”5 These passages affirm the persistence of the whole person and deny that the wicked are annihilated; they do not state how long the punishment in fire lasts.

Tertullian (c. 197–210) — the “secret fire”

Tertullian, the North African apologist, describes a punishing fire that burns without consuming. In the Apology he appeals to a “secret fire” distinct from common fire, one that “does not consume what it scorches, but while it burns it repairs” — “a notable proof this of the fire eternal! a notable example of the endless judgment which still supplies punishment with fuel!” — so that the wicked are “consigned to the punishment of everlasting fire… which… directly ministers to their incorruptibility.”6 Against the reading of Matthew 10:28 as “a final annihilation of the two substances,” he argues that since “the fire of hell is eternal—expressly announced as an everlasting penalty,” and its “never-ending ‘killing’” is worse than a temporal murder, “substances must be eternal, when their penal ‘killing’ is an eternal one.”7 The Universalist historian Hosea Ballou 2d (1829) reports that Tertullian “is thought to have been the first christian writer who expressly asserted that the torments of the damned will be of ‘equal duration’ with the happiness of the blest”; whether earlier writers already taught unending punishment is disputed (see Disputed readings).8

Minucius Felix (c. 200)

Minucius Felix, a Latin Christian rhetorician, describes in the Octavius a tormenting fire that does not consume. “Nor is there either measure or termination to these torments,” he writes: “There the intelligent fire burns the limbs and restores them, feeds on them and nourishes them… that penal fire is not fed by the waste of those who burn, but is nourished by the unexhausted eating away of their bodies.”9

Hippolytus of Rome (c. 220)

Hippolytus, a Roman presbyter who wrote in Greek, describes in the treatise Against Plato, on the Cause of the Universe (printed among his fragments in the Ante-Nicene Fathers; its attribution is discussed on his detail page) “a lake of unquenchable fire” beneath the earth, reserved “against the day determined by God”; after the resurrection, “to the lovers of iniquity shall be given eternal punishment. And the fire which is unquenchable and without end awaits these latter, and a certain fiery worm which dieth not, and which does not waste the body, but continues bursting forth from the body with unending pain. No sleep will give them rest; no night will soothe them; no death will deliver them from punishment.”10

Cyprian of Carthage (c. 252)

Cyprian, bishop of Carthage, sets the doctrine before a pagan opponent as a warning. “An ever-burning Gehenna will burn up the condemned,” he writes, “and a punishment devouring with living flames; nor will there be any source whence at any time they may have either respite or end to their torments. Souls with their bodies will be reserved in infinite tortures for suffering.” He closes by pairing eternal punishment with eternal life: “Too late they will believe in eternal punishment who would not believe in eternal life.”11

Lactantius (c. 310)

Lactantius, tutor to Constantine's son, describes how, in his account, the lost burn without being consumed. “The same divine fire,” he teaches, “with one and the same force and power, will both burn the wicked and will form them again, and will replace as much as it shall consume of their bodies, and will supply itself with eternal nourishment… without any wasting of bodies, which regain their substance, it will only burn and affect them with a sense of pain.”12 The unrighteous, he adds elsewhere in the same book, “shall be burnt for ever with perpetual fire in the sight of angels and the righteous.”13

Cyril of Jerusalem (c. 350)

Cyril, bishop of Jerusalem, teaches endless conscious punishment in his baptismal Catechetical Lectures, grounding it on a resurrection body fashioned to suffer without perishing. At the resurrection, he tells the catechumens, “if a man is a sinner, he shall receive an eternal body, fitted to endure the penalties of sins, that he may burn eternally in fire, nor ever be consumed.”14 He closes the same lecture on Matthew 25:46, the one “eternal” governing the punishment and the life alike.

Basil of Caesarea (c. 373)

Basil, bishop of Caesarea, sets the endless fire before a fallen consecrated virgin in a pastoral letter. There she will face “those endless and unbearable pangs in the place of fire unquenched, and of the worm that… never dies… and anguish without end”; “from all these woes there is no release after death; no device, no means of coming forth from the chastisement of pain.”15 Ilaria Ramelli hypothesizes that the question-and-answer passage against the restoration ascribed to Basil (Shorter Rules 267) is an interpolation, or, if it is his, was written with “pastoral concerns” for its monastic audience; Michael McClymond rejects the interpolation claim.16 Basil is also a witness on the universal-restoration timeline.

John Chrysostom (c. 390)

John Chrysostom, preacher at Antioch and later archbishop of Constantinople, argues the endlessness of hell against Christians who held a milder, terminating punishment. “There are many men,” he says, “who… think… that hell is … temporary, not eternal”; against them he cites Paul's “eternal destruction” (2 Thessalonians 1:9): “How then is that temporary which is everlasting?”17 Elsewhere he rests the point on Matthew 25:46 — “if the life be eternal, the punishment is eternal” — and warns that the covetous face “that deathless punishment which has no end.”

Augustine of Hippo (c. 420)

Augustine argues for eternal punishment at length in City of God XXI. He answers the “tender-hearted” Christians who expected the damned to be released and Origen who expected even the devil freed, and grounds the endless penalty on Matthew 25:46: “as the eternal life of the saints shall be endless, so too the eternal punishment of those who are doomed to it shall have no end,” the same word governing both in one sentence.18 He reads the “second death” not as extinction but as a deathless dying: “death will not be abolished, but will be eternal, since the soul will neither be able to enjoy God and live, nor to die and escape the pains of the body” — a death whose severity is that “no death shall terminate it.”19 To the objection that eternal punishment for temporal sin is unjust he answers that no law “ever regulated the duration of the punishment by the duration of the offence punished”; the gravity of abandoning an infinite good, not the time taken, sets the penalty — “he who destroyed in himself a good which might have been eternal, became worthy of eternal evil.”20 (His engagement with the universalist side is traced on his apokatastasis page.)

Justinian's edict against the restoration (543) and the anathemas of 553

Justinian's edict to Menas (543) condemns the restoration by name: “If anyone says or thinks that the punishment of demons and of impious men is only temporary, and will one day have an end, and that a restoration (apokatastasis) will take place of demons and of impious men, let him be anathema.”21 The relation of the anti-Origenist anathemas to the Second Council of Constantinople (553) is disputed. E. B. Pusey (1880), as reported by Philip Schaff, holds that Origen was condemned by the fifth ecumenical council; Schaff, following Karl Joseph von Hefele, holds that the anathemas against Origen were passed by a local synod of Constantinople in 543.22 Kallistos Ware (Orthodox bishop, 1998) distinguishes the ten anathemas attached to Justinian's letter to Menas (543) from the fifteen attached to his letter of 553 to the bishops gathered before the council. He holds that Origen was “anathematized at the time of the Fifth Ecumenical Council”; that the fifteen “may have been endorsed by a lesser council, meeting in the early months of 553 shortly before the main council was convened,” and that “the Fathers of the Fifth Council were well aware of these fifteen anathemas and had no intention of revoking or modifying them”; and that the eleventh of the council's anathemas on the Three Chapters contains “a general condemnation of Origen, although without any specific reference to apocatastasis.”23 Michael Pomazansky (Orthodox protopresbyter, Orthodox Dogmatic Theology) holds that the Church “acknowledges the torments of gehenna to be eternal and unending, and therefore it condemned at the Fifth Ecumenical Council the false teaching of the Origenists that the demons and impious people would suffer in hell only for a certain definite time, and then would be restored to their original condition of innocence (apokatastasis in Greek).”24 Origen's own teaching and the history of the condemnation are traced on the apokatastasis timeline and the condemnation of Origen.

Gregory the Great (c. 593)

Gregory the Great, bishop of Rome, treats the question in the fourth book of his Dialogues. To Peter's question whether the damned “shall burn always, and never have any end of their torments,” Gregory answers: “Certain it is, and without all doubt most true, that as the good shall have no end of their joys, so the wicked never any release of their torments.”25 To the objection that eternal pain for a temporal sin is unjust, he answers that the wicked “desired always to live in sin,” so a will that would sin forever is punished forever; and he says that the lost suffer “death without death, defect without defect, and end without end.”

John of Damascus (c. 743)

John of Damascus writes in An Exposition of the Orthodox Faith that at the general resurrection “the devil and his demons and the man that is his, that is the Antichrist and the impious and the sinful, will be given over to everlasting fire: not material fire like our fire, but such fire as God would know,” while the righteous “shine forth as the sun with the angels into life eternal.”26

Thomas Aquinas (c. 1274)

The Supplement to Aquinas's Summa Theologiae treats the bodies of the damned as raised incorruptible so that their punishment may be endless: “the bodies of the damned will be incorruptible, and this will serve the purpose of Divine justice, since living for ever they will be punished for ever.”27 It argues from the infinite majesty of God offended that the penalty is eternal: “whoever sins mortally sins against God… But God's majesty is infinite. Therefore whoever sins mortally deserves infinite punishment… it seems just that for a mortal sin a man should be punished for ever.” Since “punishment cannot be infinite in intensity… it must needs be infinite at least in duration.”28

The Fifth Lateran Council (1513) — the soul's immortality defined

The eighth session of the Fifth Lateran Council (19 December 1513), in the bull Apostolici regiminis, condemned “all those who insist that the intellectual soul is mortal, or that it is only one among all human beings,” and defined that the soul “is also immortal,” individually multiplied for each body. Among its grounds the bull cites the Lord's promise of “eternal rewards and eternal punishments to those who will be judged according to the merits of their life.”29

Jonathan Edwards (1741)

Edwards, the New England Congregationalist theologian, preached the doctrine in “Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God” (1741): “It would be dreadful to suffer this fierceness and wrath of Almighty God one moment; but you must suffer it to all eternity. There will be no end to this exquisite horrible misery.”30 Expounding Matthew 25:46 in “The Eternity of Hell Torments,” he states that the wicked's “sensible misery… shall not only continue for a very long time, but shall be absolutely without end.”31

Robert A. Peterson (1995) — the evangelical debate over conditional immortality

In the late-twentieth-century evangelical debate over conditional immortality, the Presbyterian theologian Robert A. Peterson defended eternal punishment in Hell on Trial: The Case for Eternal Punishment (1995) and in a two-views exchange with the conditionalist Edward Fudge (2000). He describes the view as one that “holds that the wicked will suffer in hell forever,” and holds that “Jesus, Paul, and John teach that the suffering of the lost in hell will know no end.”32
