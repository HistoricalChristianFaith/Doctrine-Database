Who Was Nimrod?

Scripture gives Nimrod only a genealogical notice — “Cush begat Nimrod: he began to be a mighty one in the earth. He was a mighty hunter before the LORD” — and adds that “the beginning of his kingdom was Babel, and Erech, and Accad, and Calneh, in the land of Shinar”, whence went forth the building of Nineveh and its sister cities (Genesis 10:8–12). A prophet later calls Assyria “the land of Nimrod” (Micah 5:6), and the Chronicler repeats his birth (1 Chronicles 1:10). That is the whole of the biblical Nimrod. Later writers made of him the first king and the first tyrant, the giant-hunter who defied heaven, the architect of Babel and its tower, the founder of astrology and idolatry, the persecutor who cast Abraham into the fiery furnace — and, in a modern thesis, the hidden god behind the pagan mystery religions. Modern scholars have asked whether he preserves the memory of a real Mesopotamian king (Sargon of Akkad, Enmerkar, Tukulti-Ninurta, Gilgamesh and Amenhotep III have all been proposed) or is a legendary name, and readers since antiquity have asked whether Scripture presents him as a hero blessed by God or a rebel against Him.

The phrase “a mighty hunter before the LORD” (Hebrew lifnê YHWH) can be read as praise — a hunter by God’s favour, as Ephrem the Syrian took it — or, because the preposition and the Greek enantíon that renders it can bear a hostile sense, as defiance: a mighty hunter against the LORD. Much of the later tradition turns on that preposition, and the “mighty one” (gibbor) it makes of him is the word used of the “mighty men” of old just before the Flood. His Babel, in turn, is the city of the tower whose builders were scattered in the confusion of tongues; on the allotment of the nations at the dispersal (Deuteronomy 32:8), see the divine council.

This page is a biographical tradition-timeline: not a life of Nimrod, but a history of what was said and believed about him across the centuries, set out earliest source first. Its “witnesses” are the datable sources that report on or interpret him: the Genesis notice and its prophetic echoes, Josephus’s tyrant who raised the Tower in defiance of God, the Targums and the rabbinic legend of Nimrod and Abraham, the Fathers’ reading of Babel, and the medieval and modern reworkings, down to modern scholarship. How some of these sources have been read — whether Nimrod built Babel’s tower, whether he founded astrology and idolatry, and the Two Babylons equation of Nimrod with Tammuz and the “Babylon mystery religion” — is set out under Disputed readings.

Timeline

The biblical portrait (Genesis 10, Micah 5, and 1 Chronicles 1)

The later tradition grows from a single genealogical notice in the Table of Nations. In Genesis 10:8–12, among the sons of Cush son of Ham, “Cush begat Nimrod: he began to be a mighty one (gibbor) in the earth. He was a mighty hunter before the LORD: wherefore it is said, Even as Nimrod the mighty hunter before the LORD.” The notice then makes him the first empire-builder: “the beginning of his kingdom was Babel, and Erech, and Accad, and Calneh, in the land of Shinar. Out of that land went forth Asshur, and builded Nineveh, and the city Rehoboth, and Calah, and Resen between Nineveh and Calah: the same is a great city.” Scripture thus binds Nimrod to the two powers of Mesopotamia at once — Babylon in the south and Assyria in the north — and presents him as the first man to gather that kind of power.1 The rest of the canon mentions him twice: the Chronicler repeats the birth notice (1 Chronicles 1:10), and the prophet Micah, foretelling deliverance from Assyria, calls that empire “the land of Nimrod” in parallel with “the land of Assyria” — a poetic byword naming Nimrod with the Mesopotamian imperial power.2

Twice Nimrod is called “a mighty hunter before the LORD” (Hebrew lifnê YHWH). Read one way, the phrase is honour — a hunter of surpassing might in the sight of, and so by the favour of, God; this is how Ephrem the Syrian took it, glossing the proverb as a blessing (“May you be like Nimrod”). Read another way — because the Hebrew preposition, and the Greek enantíon that renders it in the old versions, can also mean “over against” or “in defiance of” — the phrase becomes an indictment: a mighty hunter against the LORD, the first rebel whose might was raised against heaven. The later readings divide on this preposition, into the hero and the tyrant; and the word Scripture uses for his greatness, “mighty one” (gibbor), is the word used of the “mighty men which were of old, men of renown” begotten just before the Flood.3 The witnesses on this timeline take up this figure — the first king, bound to Babel and Nineveh, called a mighty hunter “before the LORD” — in different ways.

Josephus — the tyrant who raised the Tower (c. AD 93)

The earliest extra-biblical witness on this timeline to give Nimrod a character is Josephus, whose Antiquities of the Jews (c. AD 93) portrays him as a villain. His Nimrod is “a bold man, and of great strength of hand” who taught the survivors of the Flood to trust their own courage rather than God, “gradually changed the government into tyranny,” and turned the people from the fear of God into a “constant dependence upon his power.” Josephus names Nimrod as the instigator of the Tower of Babel and gives the building a defiant motive that Genesis does not state — Nimrod would “be revenged on God” for the Flood by raising “a tower too high for the waters to be able to reach,” so that heaven could never again reach mankind — and the confusion of tongues follows as God’s answer to that defiance.4 Two readings appear here in their earliest form on this timeline: Nimrod is joined to a Tower at which Genesis 11 does not name him (Genesis gives the “mighty one” in chapter 10 and the unnamed builders in chapter 11), and the “mighty hunter before the LORD” is portrayed as a rebel against Him — the adversative reading of the crux.5

Theophilus of Antioch — the earliest Christian notice on this timeline (c. 185)

The earliest Christian notice of Nimrod on this timeline comes from Theophilus of Antioch, whose apology Ad Autolycum (c. 185) narrates “the history after the flood” about a century after Josephus. Following the Table of Nations, he lists Nimrod (“Nebroth”) as king of the first cities of Shinar — Babylon, Erech, Accad, Calneh — and credits him with building Nineveh, Rehoboth, Calah, and Resen in the north, the Nimrod-subject reading of Genesis 10:11 that gives him the whole Mesopotamian sweep. Two things mark his witness. He passes no moral judgement — Nimrod is the first king of the post-Flood cities, neither Josephus’s tyrant nor Ephrem’s blessed hunter, and the phrase “a mighty hunter before the LORD” goes unmentioned. And, unlike Josephus, he narrates Nimrod and the tower separately: the tower is raised not by a named king but by “men” who “took counsel of their own judgment,” for “a glorious name to themselves,” with no revenge-on-God motive.6

The Targums — the rebel written into the verse (c. 2nd–8th century)

The Aramaic Targums read alongside the Hebrew in the synagogue divide on the crux. The restrained Babylonian Onkelos keeps the neutral proverb — Nimrod “mighty” and “a mighty hunter before the Lord,” no rebellion implied — while the expansive Pseudo-Jonathan resolves the ambiguity against him inside the translation itself: Nimrod “began to be mighty in sin, and to rebel before the Lord,” a “mighty rebel” the like of whom “there hath not been… from the day that the world was created.” Rebellion is here written into the wording of the verse, in line with the midrashic pun that ties the name to Hebrew marad, “he rebelled.” Pseudo-Jonathan also preserves a strand favourable to Nimrod: at Genesis 10:11 Nimrod leaves Babel and reigns in Assyria “because he would not be in the counsel of a divided generation” — the two Nimrods, hero and rebel, side by side in one text.7

The rabbinic legend — the name, the garments, and the furnace of Ur (c. 3rd–13th century)

Classical rabbinic tradition filled the villain out into a biography. His name is read as an indictment — from marad, “because he caused the entire world to rebel against God during his reign” (b. Eruvin 53a) — and Nimrod is identified with the “Amraphel” of Genesis 14, the arch-rebel whose “disciple in corruption” is Nebuchadnezzar (b. Pesachim 94b). Pirqe de-Rabbi Eliezer explains how a hunter became the first king: he inherited the coats of skin God made for Adam, at whose sight the beasts fell down before him — and he is the ringleader who proposed the Tower of Babel.8

The best-known legend is his clash with Abraham. Reading “Ur of the Chaldees” (Genesis 15:7) as “fire of the Chaldees,” Genesis Rabbah stages Nimrod as the idol-king who, when the young Abraham smashed his father’s idols and out-argued him (fire is quenched by water, water borne by clouds…), threatened, “I will cast you into it, and let that God to whom you bow down come and rescue you from it” — and cast him into a fiery furnace from which God delivered him unburnt. The medieval Sefer ha-Yashar expands this into a full war between king and patriarch, opening with the star Nimrod’s astrologers see at Abraham’s birth. The strata differ in date: the furnace duel is classical amoraic midrash; the garments and the war-saga are later.9

The Pseudo-Clementine literature — Nimrod is Zoroaster, the first magus (c. 3rd–4th century)

A different strand runs alongside the Babel-tyrant tradition, and its fullest ancient statement is the Pseudo-Clementine literature — the fourth-century Christian romance ascribed to Clement of Rome. Here Nimrod is not the builder of a tower but the founder of magic, star-worship, and idolatry. In the Greek Homilies (IX.4–6) the man is named “Nebrod” — the Septuagint’s spelling of Nimrod — and identified outright with Zoroaster: a giant who “chose, giant-like, to devise things in opposition to God,” a great magician who compelled a star to grant him sovereignty, was struck dead by the fire he had called down, and was then buried, given a temple among the Persians, and worshipped as a god — the first case, in this telling, of the deified dead, and so the root of Persian fire-worship and of idolatry itself. The Latin Recognitions (IV.27–29) tell the same “first magician consumed by star-fire” myth but assign the Zoroaster role to Ham’s son Mesraim and keep Nimrod separate — “a certain king Nimrod… whom the Greeks also called Ninus.” The explicit Nimrod = Zoroaster equation is the Homilies’; the Recognitions give the same primeval origin-of-idolatry story without it. These are among the earliest extant sources for the tradition that Nimrod founded astrology and idolatry, a charge distinct from Babel; see how that tradition has been read and the astrology timeline.10

Ephrem the Syrian — the hero blessed “in the battles of the Lord” (c. 373)

The Syriac exegete Ephrem the Syrian gives the earliest full statement on this timeline of the favourable reading of the crux. In his Commentary on Genesis he takes “a mighty hunter before the Lord” as praise: Nimrod fought “according to the will of the Lord,” driving the scattered nations out to “the regions that had been set apart for them by God” — the executor of the God-decreed dispersal, not the ringleader of Babel. The proverb the Latins read as a byword for infamy, Ephrem reads as a blessing: “One used to bless a chief or a ruler by saying, ‘May you be like Nimrod, a mighty hunter who was victorious in the battles of the Lord.’” Ephrem read Genesis in the Syriac (Peshitta) tradition, and takes “before the LORD” in the honourable sense where Josephus, Jerome, and Bede take it in the hostile one.11

John Chrysostom — the birth of tyranny, moralized (c. 388)

Preaching through Genesis at Antioch, John Chrysostom reads Nimrod as a moral example of ambition. Nimrod “in imitation of his forebear did not take due advantage of his natural preeminence but hit upon another form of servitude in endeavoring to become ruler and king” — the invention of domination, subjecting free people to one man’s power. The fault is not strength but strength “not keeping to its limits… constantly lusting after more and clutching for glory,” and the cities of his kingdom are raised “with a view to ruling over the enemy.” In the next homily Chrysostom widens the same vice into the diagnosis of Babel — the race that “always longs for more… and topples into the very depths” — and presses it on his own hearers who build “splendid homes, baths, porches and avenues” for an “undying reputation.” Where Josephus tells the story of the adversative reading, Chrysostom draws its moral.12

Jerome — the first tyrant and the hunter “never read favourably” (c. 391–392)

At Bethlehem Jerome gave the hostile portrait in Latin and argued it from the Hebrew. In the Hebrew Questions on Genesis Nimrod “first seized an unprecedented tyranny among the people.” In the Commentary on Micah, on the prophet’s “land of Nimrod,” he rests the adversative reading on the name and the trade: Nimrod means “descending temptation,” he is “the giant and hunter and one who proudly opposes the Lord,” fallen “like lightning from heaven.” His best-known line on the subject: “As far as I can recall… I have never read favorably about a hunter” — pairing Nimrod with the two other biblical hunters, Ishmael and Esau, as a single type of the reprobate.13

Augustine — Nimrod at the head of the earthly city (c. 420)

Augustine reads Nimrod in the City of God. Working through the Table of Nations and Babel as one movement, he makes Nimrod the emblem of the earthly city — the society founded on domination and pride, set against the pilgrim city of God traced through Shem to Abraham. Babylon, whose name means “Confusion,” is the head of that earthly city, and “we conclude that the giant Nimrod was its founder… its proud and impious founder,” the architect of the tower meant to “reach the sky.” On the crux Augustine is the earliest extant voice on this timeline to name the ambiguity by its Greek word — enantion “means both ‘before’ and ‘against’” — and he decides it: those who render “a mighty hunter before the Lord” have “misunderstood” the word, and Nimrod “is to be recognized as a ‘hunter against the Lord,’” a “hunter” being “deceiver, oppressor, and destroyer.”14

Bede — the crux named, and Nimrod made a figure of the devil (c. 720)

The Venerable Bede, in his Commentary on Genesis, gathers the adversative tradition into one commentary and names the crux by its Greek term, as Augustine had done. “Before the Lord,” he explains, renders enantion, from which “both can be interpreted”: it means “before the Lord who made us” in Psalm 95 but “against the Lord” in Job, and “another version has it that Nimrod was a giant hunter against the Lord.” Bede adopts the hostile reading — the phrase “was added to magnify the crime” of a tyrant who, “neglecting heavenly matters,” sought “lowly things” — while stating that the Greek will bear either. He then reads Nimrod allegorically: Nimrod’s name means “tyrant, or fugitive, or transgressor,” and so “figuratively indicates the devil,” the hunter who “sets traps… in the forest of this world” and hunts souls to their death, set against the apostles made “fishers of men.”15

The Middle Ages — the proud giant-king, from Isidore’s encyclopedia to Dante’s hell (c. 7th–14th century)

The medieval sources on this timeline carry the patristic Nimrod — the “against the Lord” giant-tyrant, founder of Babylon and its tower — into encyclopedias, school-texts, chronicles and poetry. Isidore of Seville, in the Etymologies (c. 615–630), gives the Fathers in two dictionary sentences: “Nembroth is interpreted ‘tyrant.’ For he first seized an unaccustomed tyranny… and he himself undertook, in impiety, to build a tower against God” (7.6.22), and “first after the flood, Nembroth the giant founded the city of Babylon” (15.1.4) — Jerome’s wording joined to Augustine’s reading in one entry. Peter Comestor’s Historia Scholastica (c. 1173) and the Syriac Book of the Bee made Nimrod the tyrant who compelled fire-worship and was schooled in the stars — the giant-tyrant-astrologer; see how that tradition has been read. Dante lodges Nimrod among the giants ringing the pit of hell (Inferno 31), his mouth babbling the line “Raphèl maì amècche zabì almi” — punished by the confusion of tongues his tower provoked, so that, in Longfellow’s translation, “even such to him is every language / As his to others, which to none is known.”16 Full detail »

Modern scholarship — the name, the historical-identity theories, and the reception

Four lines of modern study recur. On the name, Christoph Uehlinger and Karel van der Toorn treat the marad (“rebel”) derivation as a later Hebrew pun and connect “Nimrod” with the Mesopotamian warrior-hunter god Ninurta, relating it to the “mighty hunter” epithet and the Assyrian geography. On the historical identity, Yigal Levin notes that no Nimrod appears in any Mesopotamian record and reads him as a legendary composite of Sargon of Akkad and Naram-Sin; Tukulti-Ninurta I (E. A. Speiser), Gilgamesh (Friedrich Delitzsch, John Skinner), an Egyptian king (Kurt Sethe, per van der Toorn and van der Horst), and Enmerkar (⚠ unverified — no named proponent located yet) have also been proposed. On the text, Speiser reads the notice as an insertion into the Table of Nations whose geography — Babel, Erech, and Accad in Shinar, Asshur, Nineveh, and Calah in the north — is authentic Mesopotamian memory. On the crux, the commentaries of Westermann, Wenham, and Hamilton read “a mighty hunter before the LORD” as a superlative of renown, neither Ephrem’s blessing nor Jerome’s curse; and K. van der Toorn and P. W. van der Horst, noting that “the biblical information concerning the figure of Nimrod is scarce,” trace the arch-rebel, the idolater, and the persecutor of Abraham as additions of post-biblical interpretation.17 Full detail »
