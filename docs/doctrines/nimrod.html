Who Was Nimrod?

Few names built on so few verses have grown so large as Nimrod. Scripture gives him only a genealogical notice — “Cush begat Nimrod: he began to be a mighty one in the earth. He was a mighty hunter before the LORD” — and adds that “the beginning of his kingdom was Babel, and Erech, and Accad, and Calneh, in the land of Shinar”, whence went forth the building of Nineveh and its sister cities (Genesis 10:8–12). A prophet later calls Assyria “the land of Nimrod” (Micah 5:6), and the Chronicler repeats his birth (1 Chronicles 1:10). That is the whole of the biblical Nimrod. Yet from this seed the tradition raised a towering figure: the first king and the first tyrant, the giant-hunter who defied heaven, the architect of Babel and its tower, the founder of astrology and idolatry, the persecutor who cast Abraham into the fiery furnace — and, in a much-repeated modern thesis, the hidden god behind every pagan mystery religion. Who, then, was he? A memory of a real Mesopotamian king — Sargon of Akkad, Gilgamesh, Tukulti-Ninurta, Amenhotep III? A merely legendary name? And what does Scripture itself say of him — a hero blessed by God, or a rebel against Him?

The crux is already latent in the text. The phrase “a mighty hunter before the LORD” (Hebrew lifnê YHWH) can be read as praise — a hunter by God’s favour, as the earliest readers took it — or, because the preposition and the Greek enantíon that renders it can bear a hostile sense, as defiance: a mighty hunter against the LORD. On that single ambiguous preposition much of the later tradition turns, and the “mighty one” (gibbor) it makes of him echoes the “mighty men” of old from just before the Flood.

This page is a biographical tradition-timeline: not a life of Nimrod — the sources for that are far too thin — but a history of what was said and believed about him across the centuries, set out earliest source first. Its “witnesses” are the datable sources that report on or interpret him: the Genesis notice and its prophetic echoes, Josephus’s tyrant who raised the Tower in defiance of God, the Targums and the rabbinic legend of Nimrod and Abraham, the Fathers’ reading of Babel, and the medieval and modern reworkings — down to the scholarship that weighs it all. The genuinely contested questions — was he a historical king, and which one? did he found Babel, astrology, and idolatry? does the popular Two Babylons equation of Nimrod with Tammuz and the “Babylon mystery religion” hold up? — are weighed on their own argument pages rather than settled by assertion.

Timeline

The biblical portrait (Genesis 10, Micah 5, and 1 Chronicles 1)

The whole later tradition grows from a single genealogical notice in the Table of Nations. In Genesis 10:8–12, among the sons of Cush son of Ham, “Cush begat Nimrod: he began to be a mighty one (gibbor) in the earth. He was a mighty hunter before the LORD: wherefore it is said, Even as Nimrod the mighty hunter before the LORD.” The notice then makes him the first empire-builder: “the beginning of his kingdom was Babel, and Erech, and Accad, and Calneh, in the land of Shinar. Out of that land went forth Asshur, and builded Nineveh, and the city Rehoboth, and Calah, and Resen between Nineveh and Calah: the same is a great city.” So Scripture binds Nimrod to the two great powers of Mesopotamia at once — Babylon in the south and Assyria in the north — and marks him as the first man to gather that kind of power.1 The rest of the canon glances back at him only twice, and briefly: the Chronicler repeats the birth notice verbatim (1 Chronicles 1:10), and the prophet Micah, foretelling deliverance from Assyria, calls that empire “the land of Nimrod” in parallel with “the land of Assyria” — a poetic byword fixing Nimrod as the eponymous founder of the Mesopotamian imperial power that would one day carry Israel away.2

The crux that drives every later reading is already latent in the one repeated phrase. Twice Nimrod is called “a mighty hunter before the LORD” (Hebrew lifnê YHWH). Read one way, the phrase is honour — a hunter of surpassing might in the sight of, and so by the favour of, God; this is how the earliest readers, and the Syriac tradition of Ephrem, took it, glossing the proverb as a blessing (“May you be like Nimrod”). Read another way — because the Hebrew preposition, and the Greek enantíon that renders it in the old versions, can also mean “over against” or “in defiance of” — the phrase becomes an indictment: a mighty hunter against the LORD, the first rebel whose might was raised in the teeth of heaven. On that single ambiguous preposition the two Nimrods of the later tradition divide, the hero and the tyrant; and the word Scripture chose for his greatness, “mighty one” (gibbor), is the very word used of the “mighty men which were of old, men of renown” begotten just before the Flood — a verbal echo the tradition would not miss.3 It is this double figure — the first king and the first rebel, bound to Babel and Nineveh, and the suspicion of defiance built into the very praise of him — that every witness on this timeline inherits and reworks.

Josephus — the tyrant who raised the Tower (c. AD 93)

The first extra-biblical witness to give Nimrod a character is Josephus, whose Antiquities of the Jews (c. AD 93) fixes the villain-portrait the whole later tradition builds on. His Nimrod is “a bold man, and of great strength of hand” who taught the survivors of the Flood to trust their own courage rather than God, “gradually changed the government into tyranny,” and turned the people from the fear of God into a “constant dependence upon his power.” To this Josephus adds the move Genesis itself does not make: he names Nimrod as the instigator of the Tower of Babel and gives the building a defiant motive found nowhere in the text — Nimrod would “be revenged on God” for the Flood by raising “a tower too high for the waters to be able to reach,” so that heaven could never again reach mankind — and the confusion of tongues follows as God’s measured answer to that defiance.4 Two things enter the tradition here for good, and neither is in the plain text: Nimrod is fused with a Tower that Genesis 11 never names him at (Scripture keeps the “mighty one” of chapter 10 and the nameless builders of chapter 11 apart), and the ambiguous “mighty hunter before the LORD” is now read flatly as a rebel against Him — Josephus standing at the head of the adversative pole of the crux the timeline inherits.5
