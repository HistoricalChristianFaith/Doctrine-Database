Did Thomas evangelize India?

Disputed reading · on Thomas · witness: the Acts of Thomas

Related readings: Was Thomas the twin brother of Jesus? · Did Bartholomew preach in India or in Armenia? · Was Matthew’s Gospel first written in Hebrew? (the last two turn on Pantaenus’s “India” report in Eusebius, Ecclesiastical History 5.10, and on the same ancient sense of “India”)

The passage

Parthia, according to tradition, was allotted to Thomas as his field of labor…

— Origen, in Eusebius, Ecclesiastical History 3.1.1

The question

Among the earliest notices of Thomas’s field, Origen’s (c. 230) names Parthia; the Acts of Thomas (c. 220–240), fourth-century Greek, Latin and Syriac writers, the Edessa cult and the Saint Thomas Christians of Kerala name India and place his death there. The question is whether that India tradition preserves the memory of a first-century mission, and how its sources relate to one another.

Thomas preached in India and was martyred there

In this reading the India tradition, anchored by a real king and carried by a living church, preserves a genuine first-century mission.

Held by: the Acts of Thomas (c. 220–240); Ephrem the Syrian (c. 306–373); the Saint Thomas (Mar Thoma) Christians of Kerala, in their foundation tradition; Sean McDowell (evangelical, The Fate of the Apostles, 2015), who rates the martyrdom in India “more probable than not.”2

The anchor is a king’s name. The Acts has Thomas sold to serve Gundaphar, king of India; in the nineteenth century coins and the Takht-i-Bahi inscription revealed a historical Gondophares, an Indo-Parthian king who ruled the north-west of the subcontinent in roughly the first half of the first century. A third-century narrative that preserves the name of an obscure ruler, unknown to every other surviving text, in the right region and era, is on this reading a real memory.3 By the last quarter of the fourth century a mission to India is named across the church and in three languages: Gregory of Nazianzus (“…Thomas with India, Marc with Italy”), Ambrose (“India to Thomas, Persia to Matthew”), Jerome (“he was with Thomas in India”), Paulinus of Nola and Gaudentius of Brescia; several set India beside Persia as a distinct field.4 The Saint Thomas Christians, a community attested on the Malabar coast from an early date, hold that Thomas landed at Muziris (Cranganore) about AD 52 and was martyred at Mylapore, near Chennai, about AD 72, where his tomb is shown.5 At Edessa a whole liturgical cult carried the India martyrdom: Ephrem’s hymns, a Syriac calendar keeping 3 July, and Egeria’s visit about 384 to “the church and memorial of saint Thomas.”6 The voyage itself was ordinary commerce: the Periplus of the Erythraean Sea (c. AD 40–70) describes the annual monsoon crossing from Egypt’s Red Sea ports to Muziris, Pliny calls Muziris primum emporium Indiae, and coin-hoards and the excavations at Pattanam (widely identified with Muziris) attest heavy Indo-Roman traffic in the apostolic decades.7 Holders of this reading may combine the sources as a two-stage mission: the Parthian north-west first, matching Origen and Gundaphar, and then by sea to Malabar, matching the trade route and the Kerala church (⚠ unverified — no named proponent of the two-stage form located yet).

An ancient tradition whose first-century origin lies beyond the documents

In this reading the India tradition is old and widely held, but its documentation begins with a third-century narrative and the cult it shaped, so a first-century mission remains undemonstrated.

Held by: Robert Eric Frykenberg (historian, Christianity in India: from Beginnings to the Present, 2008), ⚠ unverified, who, allowing that “dubious historicity may be attached” to the local traditions, stresses their “great antiquity.”8

The narrative source is third-century encratite hagiography, with talking animals, resurrections and set speeches; a real king in its setting establishes the backdrop, and Gundaphar’s name could have reached its author through trade memory or a lost source. Every fourth-century writer who names India — Gregory, Ambrose, Jerome, Paulinus, Gaudentius — writes after the Acts and after the rise of the Edessa cult, and so may be repeating what the book and the shrine had made common knowledge; no first-century witness names Thomas’s field. Origen’s notice names Parthia, and ancient writers used “India” for Arabia Felix, Ethiopia and the far East.9 The geography divides: Gundaphar ruled the north-west, in Gandhara and the Punjab, while the living tradition is in the far south, in Kerala and at Mylapore, and a two-stage mission is a reconstruction nowhere attested as a unit. The Saint Thomas Christians are an old community, but their written claim to a first-century foundation — the seven and a half churches, the Muziris landing, the Mylapore martyrdom — is later than the apostolic age; Encyclopædia Britannica’s article on them states that the apostolic advent “cannot be verified” while noting Christians on the Malabar coast from an early date.10 A tomb attests veneration and a sea-lane attests a possible voyage. On this reading the record documents a third-century narrative, a fourth-century cult and an Indian church of great antiquity, all of which commemorate a mission older than any of the documents.
