Origen and Eusebius: Parthia and Edessa

Dates: c. 230 (Origen’s notice) · c. 325 (Eusebius’s History) · Eusebius, Church History 3.1
Summary: Two early datable notices of where Thomas went are preserved by Eusebius. Origen, in a lost commentary on Genesis, assigns Thomas Parthia, the Persian East; other sources name India. And the Edessene Abgar legend, which Eusebius gives from a Syriac document, makes “Judas, who was also called Thomas” the apostle who sent Thaddaeus to King Abgar, a notice tying Thomas to the Syriac world.

Claims & evidence

Origen: Parthia was allotted to Thomas

Writing about 230, Origen set out the apostles’ “division of the world” in his commentary on Genesis. The work is lost, but Eusebius quotes the passage in the third book of his Ecclesiastical History. It is a geographical allotment without narrative or miracle, and among the earliest extant notices of Thomas’s field.

…the holy apostles and disciples of our Saviour were dispersed throughout the world. Parthia, according to tradition, was allotted to Thomas as his field of labor, Scythia to Andrew, and Asia to John, who, after he had lived some time there, died at Ephesus.

— Origen, quoted in Eusebius, Ecclesiastical History 3.1. “Parthia” is the Arsacid Persian empire east of the Euphrates. The notice names Persia; later sources name India.

Eusebius: “Judas, who was also called Thomas” and the Edessa legend

Eusebius also preserves, from what he says was a Syriac document in the archives of Edessa, the famous legend of King Abgar — the ailing king who wrote to Jesus and received a promise that a disciple would be sent. In Eusebius’s telling it is Thomas who dispatches that disciple after the resurrection.

After the ascension of Jesus, Judas, who was also called Thomas, sent to him Thaddeus, an apostle, one of the Seventy.

— Eusebius, Ecclesiastical History 1.13. The double name “Judas, who was also called Thomas” is the Syriac form, Judas Thomas, used in the eastern church and in the Acts of Thomas. The notice also connects Thomas with Edessa: he does not go himself but sends Thaddeus, and the legend places the origin of the Edessene church under his authority. Thomas’s relic and cult at Edessa are attested in the fourth century by Ephrem and Egeria.

Reception and debate

  • Parthia and India. Parthia and the Indo-Parthian kingdoms adjoined, and antiquity used the name “India” widely; Origen’s notice names Parthia, while the Acts of Thomas and later sources name India. How the two relate is discussed on the disputed-reading page.
  • The Abgar legend’s reception. The Decretum Gelasianum condemned the correspondence between Jesus and Abgar (⚠ unverified). Eusebius’s notice is cited here for what it reports of Thomas: that by Eusebius’s day the Edessene document traced the Syriac church’s mission to him.
  • The notices and the Acts. Origen’s notice (c. 230) falls within the period to which the Acts of Thomas is dated (c. 220–240), and neither it nor the Edessa document Eusebius used contains the Acts’ narrative; both send Thomas, or his envoy, to the East.
