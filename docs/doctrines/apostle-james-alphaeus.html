James, son of Alphaeus

James the son of Alphaeus heads the third group of four in every list of the apostles, and the New Testament records nothing else about him: no saying, deed, mission or death. For the first Christian generation the New Testament uses three designations: James son of Zebedee, John’s brother, beheaded by Herod Agrippa (he has his own page); James son of Alphaeus, of the Twelve; and James the brother of the Lord, called the Just, leader of the church at Jerusalem, martyred there about the year 62. Whether the second and third are one man or two has been answered in two ways. Jerome (c. 383) identified them, and J. B. Lightfoot (1865) lists “Western Services” and “Later Latin Writers” with Jerome; on that reading the life, see and death of James the Just are the apostle’s. The Greek, Syrian and Coptic calendars, Lightfoot reports, “assign a separate day to each,” and the son of Alphaeus has there his own commemoration.1 This page sets out what the sources report about him, earliest extant source first: the Gospel notices, the apostle-lists that give him a death, Jerome’s identification of the two Jameses, the medieval Golden Legend, and modern scholarship. It is a biographical tradition-timeline: its “witnesses” are the datable sources that report on James. The identification question is also set out, with its witnesses, on the perpetual-virginity timeline. For the shared ladder of sources on all the apostles, see the apostles hub.

Timeline

The New Testament (c. 30–90)

The New Testament names James the son of Alphaeus four times — in the lists of Matthew, Mark, Luke, and Acts — and nowhere else. In each list he stands ninth, heading the third group of four; the lists record nothing he said or did, nor where he went or how he died. The Gospels also name “James the less” (or “the little,” ho mikros), son of a Mary who watched the crucifixion, whom Jerome identified with the son of Alphaeus. Paul names James the brother of the Lord (Galatians 1:19), who led the Jerusalem church; John 7:5 says the Lord’s brethren did not yet believe in him, and Acts 1:14 lists “his brethren” with the apostles after the Ascension.2 Lightfoot (1865) read these notices as distinguishing the brethren from the Twelve (“though with the Twelve, they are distinguished from the Twelve”); Jerome, as Lightfoot reports, held that James “was not one of those that disbelieved.” How these Jameses relate is the question on which the later tradition about the apostle divides.3

The apostle-lists (c. 3rd–8th centuries)

In the apostle-list literature — Pseudo-Hippolytus On the Twelve Apostles, Pseudo-Dorotheus, the Byzantine and Latin catalogues — the apostle is given a death. Pseudo-Hippolytus reports that “James the son of Alphaeus, when preaching in Jerusalem, was stoned to death by the Jews, and was buried there beside the temple”: the place, manner and burial that Hegesippus reports for James the Just, stoned at Jerusalem and buried by the temple. Whether that shared manner of death supports identifying the two Jameses is set out on its own page. A second strand of the same literature sends him instead to Egypt, crucified at Ostrakine. The two strands differ on whether he died at Jerusalem or in Egypt.4

Jerome identifies the two Jameses (c. 383)

In his reply to Helvidius (c. 383) Jerome argued that the “brethren of the Lord” were Jesus’ maternal cousins, sons of “Mary the wife of Alphaeus” the Virgin’s sister, and so identified James the Lord’s brother with “Mary’s” son, the apostle James son of Alphaeus. He restated it in 392: James “called the brother of the Lord, surnamed the Just… the son of Mary sister of the mother of our Lord… after our Lord’s passion at once ordained by the apostles bishop of Jerusalem.” The further equation that the father Alphaeus was one man with the “Clopas” of John 19:25 was, Lightfoot reports, “unknown to Jerome himself”; Lightfoot believed it occurs first in Chrysostom and then, more explicitly, in Theodoret, both on Galatians 1:19, and reports that the theory “as worked out by other writers and as generally stated” includes it. Jerome’s argument and its reception are set out on the perpetual-virginity timeline. On his identification, the life, office and death recorded of James the Just are those of the apostle.5

The Golden Legend (c. 1260)

Jacobus de Voragine’s Golden Legend follows the identification. Its life of “St James the Less” explains his titles in turn: he “was also called James the son of Alpheus”; “called also the brother of our Lord, because he resembled much well our Lord in body, in visage”; “called James the Just for his right great holiness”; “he sang in Jerusalem the first mass that ever was sung therein, and he was first bishop of Jerusalem.” Its account of his death follows Hegesippus’s account of James the Just — cast down from a height, stoned, and struck on the head with a fuller’s club — and the club is the apostle’s emblem in Western art. Hegesippus (in Eusebius) and Jerome report the same holiness, bishopric and martyrdom of James the brother of the Lord.6

Modern scholarship

Lightfoot, in his essay “The Brethren of the Lord” (1865), concluded that “taking the scriptural notices alone, the Hieronymian account must be abandoned,” while leaving the identification of Clopas and Alphaeus “an open question”; he reported the case made for it (“it is said”), that the two words render one Aramaic name, Chalphai, and set out objections to it. Richard Bauckham (1990) distinguishes the three Jameses and holds that Clopas (QLWPY) and Alphaeus (HLPY) render distinct Aramaic names. Sean McDowell (2015) surveys the martyrdom traditions of the apostles. The Eastern churches commemorate James son of Alphaeus (9 October) and James the brother of the Lord (23 October) on separate days.7
