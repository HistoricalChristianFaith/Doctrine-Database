The Biblical Canon

This timeline records the extant witnesses to which books the churches received as New Testament Scripture, earliest first: Marcion's collection (c. 144), Justin Martyr, the Muratorian Fragment, Irenaeus, Clement of Alexandria, Tertullian, and Origen; Eusebius's sorting of the books into "acknowledged," "disputed," and "spurious" (Church History 3.25); Cyril of Jerusalem and the Council of Laodicea, whose lists end without the Apocalypse; Athanasius's Festal Letter 39 of 367; the list ascribed, on disputed grounds, to a Roman synod under Damasus (382); the African councils of Hippo (393) and Carthage (397); Jerome and Innocent I; the rejection-lists of the sixth to ninth centuries; and Luther's German New Testament of 1522. Several of these witnesses also list Old Testament books; the standing of the books Catholics call deuterocanonical, Orthodox writers anagignoskomena, and Protestants the Apocrypha is traced on the companion Old Testament canon timeline. The popular claim that the canon was decided at the Council of Nicaea in 325, and narrower scholarly arguments for a Nicene action on the canon, are set out with the other disputed questions under Disputed readings.

Timeline

The witnesses, earliest first. How particular texts, witnesses, and claims have been read is set out under Disputed readings.

Marcion of Sinope (c. 144)

Around 144, after his break with the church at Rome, Marcion of Sinope issued a fixed, closed collection of Christian scriptures, the earliest such collection on record: a single Gospel — a shortened Luke, without the nativity and without the passages tying Jesus to the Creator-God, and carrying no evangelist's name — together with ten edited letters of Paul (his Apostolikon); the Old Testament, the other three Gospels, Acts, and the Pastoral epistles were left out. Tertullian charged that he "used the knife, not the pen," and Irenaeus that he "mutilates the Gospel which is according to Luke" and "dismembered the Epistles of Paul." Adolf von Harnack (1921) and Hans von Campenhausen (1972) argued that Marcion's list provoked the church to fix and publish a broader one — a fourfold Gospel against his one, a full Paul against his ten, the Old Testament retained; John Barton (1997) holds that the fourfold Gospel and a Pauline collection were already forming independently, so that Marcion accelerated a process he did not begin. What is known of Marcion survives in the writings of those who opposed him.1

Justin Martyr (c. 155)

At Rome around 155, Justin Martyr refers to the apostles' "memoirs composed by them, which are called Gospels," citing them for the words of institution (First Apology 66), and reports that in the Sunday assembly "the memoirs of the apostles or the writings of the prophets are read, as long as time permits" (67). He does not enumerate the Gospels or name the evangelists, and some of his Gospel citations harmonize the texts.2

The Muratorian Fragment (c. 170)

The Muratorian Fragment — an eighty-five-line Latin catalogue, mutilated at both ends — lists New-Testament books: the four Gospels (Matthew and Mark presumed in the broken opening, since it numbers Luke "the third book" and John "the fourth"), Acts, the thirteen letters of Paul ("to seven churches"), Jude, two epistles of John, and the Apocalypse of John, some twenty-two of the books of the present New Testament. It does not list James, either epistle of Peter, Hebrews, or 3 John; it counts the Old-Testament Wisdom of Solomon among the received books; it accepts the Apocalypse of Peter while noting "some of us are not willing that it be read in church"; and it allows the Shepherd of Hermas to be read privately but not publicly "among the Apostles, for it is after [their] time". It closes by rejecting two epistles "forged in Paul's name" for the Marcionites (to the Laodiceans and Alexandrians) and the books of Valentinus, Basilides, and others, "for it is not fitting that gall be mixed with honey." Its date is disputed: A. C. Sundberg (1973) and G. M. Hahneman (1992) place it in the fourth-century East; B. M. Metzger (1987) and E. Schnabel (2014) hold a late-second-century date, resting on the fragment's notice that the Shepherd was written "very recently" under bishop Pius of Rome (c. 140–154). On the second-century date it is the oldest surviving enumeration of New-Testament books.3

Irenaeus of Lyons (c. 180)

Around 180, Irenaeus argues that the Gospels are exactly four — "neither more nor fewer in number than they are," matched to the four zones of the world and the four faces of the cherubim, the fourfold "Tetramorph" (Against Heresies 3.11.8), the earliest extant argument for the number — and narrates each evangelist's apostolic origin: Matthew among the Hebrews, Mark "the interpreter of Peter," Luke "the companion of Paul," John at Ephesus (3.1.1). Across his five books he quotes as Scripture some twenty-one of the twenty-seven New-Testament books; Philemon, 3 John, and Jude are not cited.4 He calls 1 Clement a "most powerful" letter (3.3.3) and cites the Shepherd of Hermas as "Scripture" (4.20.2, where the word may mean simply "writing"), both books outside the later New Testament; he files the tale of the boy Jesus and his alphabet-teacher among the Marcosians' "apocryphal and spurious writings" (1.20.1) and calls the Gospel of Judas a "fictitious history" (1.31.1). Whether his argument for four attests a fourfold Gospel already closed before him is taken up under Disputed readings.5

Clement of Alexandria (c. 200)

At Alexandria around 200, Origen's teacher Clement of Alexandria quotes in the Stromata the Epistle of Barnabas as "the Apostle Barnabas," the Shepherd of Hermas as words spoken "divinely" by revelation, the letter of Clement of Rome as "the Apostle Clement," and the Preaching of Peter for the risen Lord's commission — books outside the later New Testament — and draws on the Traditions of Matthias and the Gospel according to the Egyptians. Eusebius, reading Clement's lost Hypotyposeis, reports abridged commentary on "all canonical Scripture, not omitting the disputed books… Barnabas and the so-called Apocalypse of Peter." Clement also speaks of "the four Gospels that have been handed down to us" (Stromata 3.13), setting a saying aside because it stands outside them. Metzger (1987) reads Clement's wide citation of books outside the later canon as a mark of how open the boundary still was at Alexandria.6

Tertullian (c. 207)

In the Latin West, Tertullian answers Marcion's single, unattributed, edited Gospel of Luke with the fourfold Gospel: “the evangelical Testament has apostles for its authors” (Matthew, John) and “apostolic men” who wrote with apostolic authority (Luke the companion of Paul; Mark, of Peter, “whose interpreter Mark was”), and he argues that Marcion's one edited book is later than the four (Against Marcion IV.2, 5). For the apostles' writings he directs inquirers to “the apostolic churches … in which their own authentic writings are read” — Corinth, Philippi, Ephesus, Rome (Prescription 36). He calls the Shepherd of Hermas “apocryphal and false” (On Modesty 10), defends the book of Enoch as inspired (Apparel of Women I.3), and ascribes Hebrews to Barnabas (On Modesty 20).7

Origen of Alexandria (c. 248)

Origen enumerates the New Testament: casting the apostles as priests sounding the trumpets of Jericho, he runs out the four Gospels, the Acts, "two" epistles of Peter, James and Jude, the epistles of John "[and Revelation]," and Paul's "fourteen" letters (the thirteen plus Hebrews), the Apocalypse bracketed because some manuscripts of the homily omit it (Homilies on Joshua 7.1). A terser version names the evangelists and apostles as the diggers of "the wells of the New Testament" (Homilies on Genesis 13.2), and he states that John "wrote also the Apocalypse" (Commentary on John 5.3). Michael J. Kruger holds that "Origen likely produced a complete list of all 27 New Testament books," and so that "Athanasius' list is not the earliest complete list we possess." Origen also names rival gospels and sets them outside the four: reading Luke's "many have taken in hand to draw up a narrative" as a veiled criticism, he ranks them as "attempts": "those who wrote the Gospel according to the Egyptians and the Gospel according to the Twelve 'attempted' to write … for there is also the Gospel according to Thomas and the Gospel according to Matthias and several others. These are attempts; the church of God has approved only the four" (Homilies on Luke 1).8 In the Commentary on Matthew he cites "a tradition in the Gospel according to Peter … or The Book of James" — the Protoevangelium — for the view that the Lord's brethren were Joseph's sons by an earlier marriage, reporting what "some say" without calling the book Scripture (X.17, on Matt 13:55).9 In these passages the ground he gives for the boundary is that the church "has approved only the four"; his list names a "Gospel according to Thomas" without describing it.

Eusebius of Caesarea (c. 324)

Eusebius sorts the books into the acknowledged, the disputed but widely recognized, and the rejected, and sets a further class apart from even the rejected: among "those that are cited by the heretics under the name of the apostles" are "the Gospels of Peter, of Thomas, of Matthias, or of any others besides them, and the Acts of Andrew and John," books that "no one belonging to the succession of ecclesiastical writers has deemed worthy of mention" and whose style and matter "clearly show themselves to be the fictions of heretics," "to be cast aside as absurd and impious" (Church History 3.25.6–7). Eusebius attended the Council of Nicaea in 325; this chapter recurs in the questions about that council gathered under Disputed readings.10

Cyril of Jerusalem (c. 350)

Catechizing candidates for baptism, Cyril of Jerusalem tells them to learn the canon "from the Church," read "the twenty-two books of the Old Testament" and the recognized New, and "read none of the apocryphal writings … for why dost thou, who knowest not those which are acknowledged among all, trouble thyself in vain about those which are disputed?" (Catechetical Lecture 4.33). His New-Testament list runs the four Gospels — "for the rest have false titles and are mischievous" — Acts, the seven Catholic Epistles, and "the fourteen Epistles of Paul" (Hebrews counted with Paul, as Origen and Athanasius also count it): twenty-six of the twenty-seven books, without the Apocalypse. He names one apocryphal example, a "Gospel according to Thomas" he ascribes to the Manichaeans (4.36). The apocrypha are to be "put aside in a secondary rank," and "whatever books are not read in Churches, these read not even by thyself" (4.35–36).11

The Council of Laodicea (c. 363)

A regional council at Laodicea in Phrygia rules that "only the Canonical Books… of the New and Old Testament" may be read in church (canon 59), and canon 60 appends a catalogue whose New Testament is the present twenty-seven without the Apocalypse: four Gospels, Acts, the seven Catholic Epistles, and "fourteen Epistles of Paul" (Hebrews counted with Paul, as Cyril and Athanasius also count it), closing at Titus and Philemon without the Revelation of John.12 The genuineness of canon 60 is doubted: H. R. Percival heads it "of most questionable genuineness," noting its absence from older collections of the synod's canons, and B. F. Westcott held "the external evidence… decidedly against the authenticity" of the enumeration.13 (Its Old Testament — the Hebrew twenty-two, without the deuterocanonical books — is treated on the Old Testament canon page.)

Athanasius of Alexandria (367)

In his thirty-ninth Festal (Easter) Letter of 367, Athanasius gives a catalogue whose New Testament is exactly the present twenty-seven — the four Gospels, Acts, the seven Catholic Epistles, "fourteen Epistles of Paul" (Hebrews counted with Paul, as Origen and Cyril also count it), "and besides, the Revelation of John" — with the formula "let no man add to these, neither let him take ought from these" (Festal Letter 39.5–6). Unlike Cyril's and Laodicea's lists, his includes the Apocalypse. He presents the catalogue as written "of necessity" and urged by "true brethren," of the books "included in the Canon, and handed down, and accredited as Divine" (39.3). Athanasius had attended the Council of Nicaea in 325; how his letter bears on the claim that the council fixed the canon is among the Disputed readings. Alongside the New Testament he sets a twenty-two-book Hebrew Old Testament and, in a middle rank, books "not indeed included in the Canon, but appointed by the Fathers to be read" — Wisdom, Sirach, Esther, Judith, Tobit, the Teaching of the Apostles, and the Shepherd (39.4, 39.7).14

The Council of Rome under Damasus (382)

A synod met at Rome in 382 under Pope Damasus I, after the Council of Constantinople (381) and occupied with the Antiochene schism, with Jerome newly arrived as Damasus's secretary. A canon-list, the "Damasine list" is ascribed to this Roman synod: it runs out the present twenty-seven-book New Testament (Hebrews counted in "fourteen" of Paul) and the longer Septuagint Old Testament. The list is preserved as a chapter of the Decretum Gelasianum, the compilation this timeline meets again below among the rejection-lists, whose opening chapter quotes a work of Augustine from 416 and which Ernst von Dobschütz (1912) dated as a whole to the sixth century. Faustino Arévalo (1794) proposed that its canon-chapters are the decrees of a Roman council under Damasus; C. H. Turner edited them as "The Roman Council under Damasus," and W. A. Jurgens defends them as authentic acts of the council of 382. G. M. Hahneman points to the silence of Jerome, present at Rome, about any Roman conciliar canon. Whether the council promulgated the list is set out on its interpretation page.15

The African councils: Hippo (393) and Carthage (397)

The North-African synods gathered around Augustine and Aurelius of Carthage — Hippo in 393, Carthage in 397, reaffirmed at Carthage in 419 — each enact the rule "that besides the Canonical Scriptures nothing be read in church under the name of divine Scripture," then list the books. The New Testament is the present twenty-seven, with Revelation; Carthage lists Paul's "thirteen" epistles and then "one epistle … to the Hebrews" separately. These were regional African synods, and their canon regulates what is read in church "under the name of divine Scripture"; the note appended in 419 refers the list to Boniface of Rome and "the Church beyond the sea" for confirmation. On the Old-Testament side the lists place Tobit, Judith, the two books of Maccabees, and the Wisdom books among the Scriptures, where Athanasius had set Wisdom, Sirach, Judith, and Tobit in a rank of books "appointed by the Fathers to be read". A single later codex sets this African list, Hippo's canon 36, under a heading naming the Council of Nicaea; how that heading is read is taken up under Disputed readings.16

Jerome (c. 394)

Jerome, secretary to Damasus and translator of the Vulgate, both transmits the twenty-seven-book New Testament and records the disputes over it. Revising the Gospels for Pope Damasus he sets them in the order "Matthew, Mark, Luke, John" (Vulgate preface, 383), and in Letter 53 to Paulinus he runs the whole New Testament book by book — the fourfold Gospel, Paul "to seven churches," the seven Catholic Epistles of James, Peter, John, and Jude, and the Apocalypse. He records the doubts his sources carried: Hebrews denied to Paul in the West (Tertullian ascribing it to Barnabas), 2 Peter and James doubted, the two shorter Johannine epistles assigned to "John the presbyter," the Apocalypse doubted in the East. Of Hebrews and Revelation he tells Dardanus, "yet we receive them both," on "the authority of the ancient writers". What Jerome's writings attest about conciliar action on the canon at Rome or at Nicaea is taken up under Disputed readings.17

Innocent I of Rome (c. 405)

Answering an inquiry from Exuperius of Toulouse, Innocent I appends a canon-list matching the present twenty-seven-book New Testament, then turns to the apocrypha: "the rest of the books, which appear under the name of Matthias … or under the name of Peter and John (which were written by a certain Leucius) … or under the name of Andrew … or under the name of Thomas, and whatever others there may be," he writes, "you should know they are not only to be rejected but also condemned" (Ep. 6, 405). He names the same apostolic-pseudonym gospels and acts that Eusebius had set apart and assigns them to reputed authors (the Petrine and Johannine acts to "a certain Leucius," the Andrean to two named philosophers). His verb is damnanda ("condemned"). (The "apocrypha" he condemns are the pseudo-apostolic New-Testament writings, not the Old-Testament deuterocanonical books, which his list includes.)18

The rejection-lists (c. 500–810)

By the sixth century formal catalogues of titles "not to be received" appear. The Western Decretum Gelasianum (De libris recipiendis et non recipiendis, c. 500) names, among some sixty rejected apocrypha, "the book on the infancy of the saviour" (the boy-Jesus material of the Infancy Gospel of Thomas) and "the book of the nativity of the saviour and of Mary or the midwife" (the Protoevangelium of James), listed separately from "the Gospel in the name of Thomas which the Manichaeans use," each tagged "apocryphal" and "to be avoided by catholics." The Greek List of Sixty Books (c. 650) names no Thomas title and no infancy gospel among its apocrypha; the Stichometry of Nicephorus (c. 810) lists a "Gospel of Thomas" of 1,300 lines and a "Circuit of Thomas" without naming the infancy gospels or the Protoevangelium. Of these surviving lists, only the Western Decretum names the infancy texts, and the lists on both sides speak of books "to be avoided" or "not received".19

Martin Luther (c. 1522)

Translating the New Testament into German in 1522, Martin Luther distinguished a core of "true and certain chief books" from a disputed remainder, and printed the distinction into the volume. He translated and bound all twenty-seven books, but placed the four he most doubted, Hebrews, James, Jude, and Revelation, at the end, unnumbered in a table of contents that numbers the other twenty-three 1–23, prefacing the group with the note that these "have from ancient times had a different reputation." His prefaces give his reasons — James "an epistle of straw… for it has nothing of the nature of the gospel about it" and "not… the writing of an apostle"; Jude "an extract or copy of St. Peter's second epistle"; Revelation, in 1522, "neither apostolic nor prophetic" — his criterion being apostolicity and whether a book "preaches Christ." The "epistle of straw" line was dropped from later editions, and in 1530 he gave Revelation a new preface. The four still stand last in German-language Luther Bibles. Whether his arrangement amounts to removing the four books is set out on its interpretation page.20

Disputed readings

How particular claims, texts, and witnesses in this history have been read, each question set out on its own page with the readings its proponents hold.

Claims that the Council of Nicaea settled the canon

How the record has been read on the claim that the canon was decided at Nicaea in 325, on a later account of a sign at the council's altar, and on the claim that excluded gospels were destroyed.

  • Did the Council of Nicaea (325) decide the contents of the Bible?
  • Is the Synodicon Vetus's account (c. 900) of the books sorted by a sign at the altar of Nicaea historical?
  • Were the infancy gospels suppressed — banned, voted out, gathered up and burned?

Rudolph Cornely's texts for a Nicene action on the canon

Three texts the Jesuit scholar Rudolph Cornely adduced for the view that the council of 325 enumerated the sacred books, and how each has been read.

  • What does Jerome mean in writing that Judith "is found by the Nicene Council to have been counted among the number of the Sacred Scriptures"?
  • What does the "Council of Nicaea" heading over Hippo's canon-list in the Vallicellian codex (B.58) attest?
  • Does Cassiodorus's naming of Nicaea among those who divided the sacred books (Institutiones I.14) attest a Nicene action on the canon?

The New Testament's formation

How the timeline's witnesses have been read on the pace and shape of the New Testament's reception.

  • How did the churches come to know the New Testament canon, and what settled its disputed books?
  • Does Irenaeus's argument for four Gospels (Against Heresies 3.11.8) attest a fourfold Gospel already closed by c. 180?

Other disputed readings

  • Did the Roman synod of 382 under Damasus promulgate the canon-list preserved in the Decretum Gelasianum?
