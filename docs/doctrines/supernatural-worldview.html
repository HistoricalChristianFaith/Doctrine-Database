The Supernatural Worldview: How the Threads Connect

Scattered across this wiki are a dozen separate timelines and scores of individual arguments — the divine council, the sons of God and the Nephilim, the Flood, Babel and the disinherited nations, the giants of the conquest, the "aliens" of the modern age. Read one at a time they can look like unrelated curiosities. Read together they are pieces of a single story — the account of an "unseen realm" revived in our day by Michael Heiser and Fr. Stephen De Young and reaching back through the Fathers to the Second-Temple world. This page tells that story as one arc, earliest root first.

It is a synthesis, not an assessment. Everywhere else on this wiki each claim is weighed adversarially and rated; here the aim is different — to grant the whole reading and simply narrate the coherent picture its threads make when they are laid end to end. So this page renders no verdicts of its own and leaves the narrative unhedged. That is deliberate: a story told whole is worth seeing whole. The critical weighing has not gone away — it lives on the individual pages, linked at every step, several of which come out weak or unsupported — and the full price of holding the picture as history is tallied honestly at the foot, under What you would have to hold. A cohesive story can feel more likely precisely because it is cohesive; the cost ledger is the counterweight, and it should be read as part of the page, not an afterthought. This is one worldview told in its own voice; the naturalistic reading of the same evidence is not argued down here — it earns its own telling elsewhere.

A populated heaven: the divine council

The story begins not with humanity but with a heaven already full. Before Genesis reaches the garden it shows Yahweh at work amid a court of other beings — "let us make man in our image" — and the rest of the Hebrew Bible keeps returning to that court. Yahweh presides over an assembly of lesser, created divine beings, the divine council: he "stands in the divine assembly" and "among the gods (elohim) he holds judgment" (Psalm 82:1); "the sons of God came to present themselves before the Lord" (Job 1:6); the seraphim and the "host of heaven" ring his throne (Isaiah 6; 1 Kings 22:19). On this reading the plural is real, not a figure of speech and not a roster of idols. These are genuine spirits — created, subordinate, and answerable to the one God who made them.

Calling them elohim does not make the Bible polytheist. The word marks a being as an inhabitant of the unseen realm, not a rank shared with Yahweh: "Yahweh was an elohim, but no other elohim was Yahweh." He alone is uncreated and without peer; the council serves at his pleasure. And it is not silent scenery. It deliberates and it acts — its decrees are announced ("the sentence is by the decree of the watchers," Daniel 4:17), one of its members volunteers to go out from the throne (1 Kings 22:19–22), and, most consequentially of all, the whole map of the nations will one day be drawn along its lines: the "sons of God" of Deuteronomy 32:8 are the very beings to whom, at Babel, the peoples of the earth will be handed.

The frame runs deeper into the text than a first reading notices. One member of the council stands out from the rest — the Angel of Yahweh who is spoken of both as Yahweh and as sent by him, the visible Name-bearer whom the earliest Church read as the Son before his incarnation. And when the New Testament lifts the curtain on heaven a last time, the throne is still ringed by a council — the enthroned elders and living creatures of Revelation 4–5, the Old Testament's assembly restaged at the end of the story as it stood at the beginning. This populated heaven is the ground the whole rest of the arc is built on: everything that follows is a story about what some of these beings did.

The rebellion in Genesis 6: the sons of God and the Watchers

Some of those "sons of God" rebel. Genesis 6:1–4 reports, tersely, that they "saw the daughters of men that they were fair," took wives of them, and that in those days the Nephilim — the word the Septuagint renders gigantes, "giants" — "were on the earth." The Second-Temple tradition tells the same event at length and names the rebels the Watchers: two hundred of them, in the days of Jared, descended on the summit of Mount Hermon, bound themselves by oath to the deed, took human wives, and "bare great giants." This is the world of 1 Enoch and the Book of the Giants — the antediluvian apocalypse the whole angelic reading leans on, quoted as prophecy by Jude and echoed by 2 Peter, and remembered in its own hero: Enoch, "the seventh from Adam," who walked with God and lived 365 years, standing in the same seventh slot as the sun-priest of the Sumerian King List.

How can spirits beget children on women? The tradition does not answer with crude biology. On the reading that recovers its own mechanism, the begetting ran through a pagan sacral-kingship fertility rite — a divinized king embodying a god on a cultic bed, the child reckoned to have "two fathers" — the ideology behind Gilgamesh, "two-thirds divine," and behind the giant-kings of the earliest cities. (A rival account keeps it simpler: the rebels took on bodies, "leaving their own habitation" to do it.) However the crossing is construed, its fruit was a corrupting one, for the Watchers came bearing knowledge. They taught mankind the forbidden arts — the working of metals into swords and ornaments, the mixing of cosmetics and poisons, sorcery, root-cutting, and the reading of the stars — so that violence and idolatry spread with civilization itself. Heaven had been robbed to arm the earth, and the earth "was filled with violence." The stage was set for a judgment.

The Nephilim and their afterlife: giants, demons, and the gods

The Nephilim leave a double trace on the world, one in the flesh and one in the spirit. In the flesh they are the giant clans the Bible keeps meeting — the Rephaim, the Anakim, the Emim — and their memory clings to real places and objects. Og, king of Bashan, "of the remnant of the Rephaim," is remembered by his enormous iron bed, which reads as an echo of the cultic bed of Marduk at Babylon; and Og's own two royal cities, Ashtaroth and Edrei, appear in a pre-biblical Canaanite text as the cult-seat of a deified Rephaim-king. For the Rephaim were the divinized royal dead — the spirits of dead kings, summoned in Ugaritic funeral liturgy to bless the living dynasty, the shades Isaiah 14 pictures rising to greet a fallen tyrant. The giants are not merely big men; they are a lineage the surrounding cultures worshipped.

In the spirit the trace is darker still. When a giant died, the tradition taught, its hybrid soul did not go down to rest with the human dead but stayed on the earth as a disembodied, homeless spirit — and these, on the Enochic reading, are the demons, the "unclean spirits" the Gospels show Christ casting out, still bound to the ground and begging not to be sent to the abyss "before the time." Their fathers and their worshipped dead became the gods of the nations — the deified dead men whom the peoples served as deities, the "demons" the Septuagint says stand behind the idols. And the cult built on them was not benign: the Wisdom of Solomon charges it, of the Canaanites by name, with "merciless murderers of children" and "devourers of man's flesh, and the feasts of blood." The giants pass, but the powers they set loose — demons below, false gods above, a bloody worship between — remain the spiritual furniture of the world the rest of the story moves through.

The Flood: judgment, and its trace in the ground

A corrupted world, its blood-line and its worship both poisoned at the source, is what the Flood comes to answer. Genesis frames it exactly so: "the earth was filled with violence," and God resolved to blot out what the rebellion had made — the giants, their cult, and the humanity entangled with them — sparing one righteous line through Noah and the ark. On this reading the Flood is real history, not myth and not allegory laid over an empty event; it is the hinge of the primeval age, the judgment that closes the world of the Watchers. The New Testament takes it exactly this way, setting "the days of Noah" beside the coming of the Son of Man and the water-judgment of the past beside the fire-judgment still to come.

If it happened, it happened in real ground and real time, and the granted-premise reconstruction asks what that ground can be made to show. Does any datable horizon record a world-covering deluge? Do the giants leave authenticated bones, the survival leave a genetic bottleneck, the memory leave its print across the flood stories of the nations? And when did it fall — against which of the disputed biblical chronologies, and which of the datable strata? Those questions are walked through, artifact by artifact and rated on their own merits, on the companion pages: the material record, the dating, and the same evidence read under competing scenarios. This narrative does not relitigate those fits — many of them are thin, and the cost ledger below says so plainly. Here the Flood is simply the story's great divide: after it, the world begins again, and the next rebellion is already waiting at Babel.

Babel and the disinherited nations

After the Flood the earth begins again, and its first great act is another rebellion. The nations, still of one language, gather on the plain of Shinar to build a tower "whose top may reach unto heaven" — a bid to make a name and refuse the scattering God had commanded — and God answers by confusing their one tongue into many and dispersing them into the seventy nations of Genesis 10. But the dispersal is not only linguistic. According to the Song of Moses, "when the Most High divided to the nations their inheritance, when he separated the sons of Adam, he set the bounds of the people according to the number of the sons of God" (Deuteronomy 32:8) — apportioning the disinherited nations to the members of his council, while keeping one nation, Israel, back as "the Lord's portion … Jacob the lot of his inheritance" (v. 9). Babel is the hinge: at it the Most High hands the peoples of the earth over to the very beings the first stage of the story introduced, and reserves a single people, yet unborn, for himself — the people he will call out of Abraham.

This is the frame's cosmic geography: every nation but Israel stands under a spirit-patron drawn from the council, so that the "gods of the nations" are not empty idols but real, created powers set in genuine authority over their peoples. The rest of the Old Testament presupposes it — Daniel's angel is delayed twenty-one days by "the prince of the kingdom of Persia" and must pass on to fight "the prince of Grecia," while "Michael your prince" contends for Israel (Daniel 10): a war among the heavenly patrons standing behind the earthly empires. The Second-Temple writings say it plainly — over the nations God "placed spirits in authority to lead them astray," but "over Israel He did not appoint any angel or spirit, for He alone is their ruler" (Jubilees 15). And the appointment goes wrong. The patron-gods do not rule justly; they draw the worship of their nations to themselves, and so they merge with the other dark inheritance of the primeval age — the deified dead of the Nephilim line whom the peoples already served as gods. The council-members set over the nations and the worshipped giant-dead become, together, the "gods of the nations" that Scripture will finally unmask as demons — and for that corruption they stand under sentence, arraigned in the divine courtroom of Psalm 82: "ye are gods … but ye shall die like men."

The giants after the Flood: the conquest and "Reversing Hermon"

The scattering does not end the giants. When Israel comes up out of Egypt to take the land apportioned to it, it finds that land held by giant clans — the Anakim, before whom the spies felt "as grasshoppers," and the Rephaim, Emim, and Zamzummim of the country east of Jordan. If the Flood had drowned the Nephilim and their line, how are their like on the earth again? The frame offers more than one answer. The boldest takes Genesis 6:4's note that the Nephilim were on the earth "and also after that" to mean a second incursion — the sons of God descending again after the Flood to renew the corruption; a soberer reading treats "giant" as a category of oversized, warlike peoples rather than a bloodline, so that nothing needed to survive the water at all; and an older tradition imagined the line carried through the ark itself. However the recurrence is explained, the frame reads what follows the same way: the conquest is not an ordinary war of settlement but the cleansing of a land re-infected at its source.

So Israel's conquest of Canaan becomes, on this telling, holy war on the giant lineage — the ban (ḥerem) that devotes the Anakim and the Rephaim to destruction. Its emblem is Og, king of Bashan, "the last of the remnant of the Rephaim," whose two royal cities, Ashtaroth and Edrei, appear in a pre-biblical Canaanite tablet as the cult-seat of a deified Rephaim-king; for the Rephaim were the divinized royal dead, the shades summoned in Ugaritic funeral liturgy and pictured in Isaiah 14 rising in Sheol to greet a fallen tyrant. Bashan sits at the foot of Mount Hermon — the very summit where the Watchers first descended — and on the boldest reading of all, Jesus himself carries the campaign back to that ground. His ministry traces a deliberate "Reversing Hermon" path: he goes north to Caesarea Philippi, built at the grotto the pagans called a gate of the underworld, and there declares that "the gates of hell shall not prevail"; and "after six days" he is transfigured on a high mountain read as Hermon itself — reclaiming the mountain of the rebellion and undoing, on the enemy's own ground, the sin sworn there in the days of Jared.

The gods change costume: from gods to demons to "aliens"

The gods of the nations do not disappear when the gospel goes out to those nations; they change costume. The earliest Church did not treat the pagan deities as fictions but as the demons behind the idols — and it knew where they had come from. Justin Martyr taught that the fallen angels "begat children who are those that are called demons"; Athenagoras that "the souls of the giants" are "the demons who wander about the world"; Eusebius that the giants' spirits "have been deified by the subsequent generations of men." The gods of the earlier stages — the deified Nephilim dead and the disinherited council-patrons of Babel — are, in the Fathers' account, one company of deceiving spirits wearing the mask each age will believe. On the modern extension of that ancient demonology, the mask has simply changed again: the beings once worshipped as gods, then feared as fairies and elves, now appear as "aliens" — the same phenomenon, the same strategy of impersonation, dressed for a technological age that will no longer credit a god or a fairy but will credit a visitor from the stars.

Read this way, the modern UFO becomes the newest form of a very old deception. The entities are deceiving spirits in a technological costume, behaving in the reports far more like interdimensional intelligences than interstellar biology — craft that appear and vanish and pass through matter, "occupants" ill-suited to any voyage between stars. The abduction accounts, in which experiencers describe reproductive procedures and presented "hybrid" children, are read as the Watchers' program resumed — a recurrence of Genesis 6, "as it was in the days of Noah"; and the reports that an abduction stops at the name of Jesus are taken as proof that these visitors answer, as demons must, to Christ's authority. At the end of the arc stands its darkest expectation: that a coming, stage-managed "disclosure" of the aliens' reality will be the end-times "strong delusion" — the lie a deceived world is given up to believe (2 Thessalonians 2). The story that began with a populated heaven and a rebellion on a mountain thus closes in our own sky: the same beings, this worldview holds, still at their first work of impersonation, and the last act of the deception nearly upon us.

What you would have to hold

The picture above is a chain, and a chain is only as strong as the conjunction of its links. To hold the whole of it as history — not merely to find one thread suggestive — you would have to accept all of the following together. That is the honest price, and several of these the wiki rates weak or unsupported on their own pages. A cohesive story feels likelier because it coheres; the length of this list is the correction.

  • …that the Bible's elohim and "sons of God" are real created divine beings — a literal council, not metaphor or idols — including the disputed reading of Psalm 82 (contested), resting on the text-critically stronger Deuteronomy 32:8 (sound).
  • …that the "sons of God" of Genesis 6 were rebel divine beings and the Nephilim their offspring, begotten through a sacral-kingship rite (sound) — while the mechanism of angelic embodiment it needs remains thinly grounded (weak).
  • …that the disembodied Nephilim became the demons — a specifically Enochic identification (contested), not the plain New Testament datum.
  • …that the "gods of the nations" the peoples worshipped were these same beings — the deified Nephilim dead and the disinherited council-patrons — and not mere idols or invented myths (contested).
  • …that the Flood was a real event whose model prediction of a worldwide sediment horizon and a recent eight-person bottleneck the ground should confirm (both weak).
  • …that at Babel the Most High disinherited the nations and parcelled them out to the sons of God, keeping Israel as his own portion, so that a real cosmic geography of spirit-patrons stands behind the nations (plausible — resting on the text-critically sound reading of Deuteronomy 32:8, but overstated in its strong territorial form).
  • …that giants recurred after the Flood by a second angelic incursion (plausible) — rather than by the soberer reading of "giant" as a category of tall peoples (sound) — and that any of it left authenticated material remains (unsupported).
  • …that Israel's conquest was holy war on that giant lineage and that Jesus's ministry deliberately retraced and reversed the Watchers' descent-site in a single "Reversing Hermon" geography (plausible — anchored by the sound Hermon/ḥerem wordplay, but weakest at the joints that make it read as one deliberate design).
  • …that the modern UFO / "alien" phenomenon is this same company of deceiving spirits in a new costume — gods to fairies to "aliens" (plausible), interdimensional rather than interstellar (contested) — and, on the tail's most fragile claims, that abduction "hybridization" is a Genesis-6 reprise, that encounters halt at the name of Jesus, and that a coming "disclosure" is the end-times "strong delusion" (all three weak).
