Baptized for the Dead (1 Corinthians 15:29)

Related doctrines: Hard passages — see also Who Killed Goliath? (2 Samuel 21:19) · How Tall Was Goliath? (six or four cubits) · Solomon's Stalls: 40,000 or 4,000? (1 Kings 4:26 vs. 2 Chronicles 9:25) · Ahaziah's Age: 22 or 42? (2 Kings 8:26 vs. 2 Chronicles 22:2) · The Bronze Sea: 2,000 or 3,000 Baths? (1 Kings 7:26 vs. 2 Chronicles 4:5) · What Jesus Wrote on the Ground (John 8:6) · The Two Genealogies of Jesus (Matthew 1 vs. Luke 3) · The Death of Judas (Matthew 27 vs. Acts 1) · Naming the Wrong Prophet (Mark 1:2; Matthew 27:9)

In his argument for the resurrection Paul asks: “Else what shall they do which are baptized for the dead (οἱ βαπτιζόμενοι ὑπὲρ τῶν νεκρῶν), if the dead rise not at all? why are they then baptized for the dead?” (1 Corinthians 15:29). He names the practice and does not describe it further: the letter does not say what the rite was, who performed it, or what Paul thought of it. Hans Conzelmann (1975) counted over two hundred interpretations of the verse, and Michael F. Hull (2005) “about forty general hypotheses.”1

This timeline records how the verse has been read, earliest extant witness first. The Valentinian Theodotus (c. 170) reads the words of a vicarious baptism, and Tertullian, Epiphanius, Ambrosiaster, Didymus and Chrysostom report proxy baptisms, Epiphanius among the Cerinthians and Didymus and Chrysostom among the Marcionites. Tertullian, Chrysostom, Theodoret and the later Greek commentators read the phrase of the baptized person's own body; the interpreters Epiphanius cites read it of catechumens baptized near death. The Council of Carthage of 419 forbade presbyters to baptize the dead. Aquinas set out two senses of the verse, Calvin read it of the dying catechumen, and modern commentators have proposed further readings, which are set out under Disputed readings.

Timeline

The witnesses are listed earliest first. Each reports or construes the verse.

Clement of Alexandria, quoting Theodotus the Valentinian (c. 170)

The earliest extant interpretation, in the excerpts Clement made from the Valentinian teacher Theodotus, reads the verse of a baptism of angels on behalf of the spiritual seed. The verse is glossed: “the angels of whom we are portions were baptised for us.” On this reading the “dead” are the spiritual seed deadened by bodily existence, and their heavenly counterparts — the angels — are baptized on their behalf, so that when the initiate receives “the Name” he may pass the Limit and enter the Pleroma. Theodotus thus takes “baptized for the dead” as a proxy rite, set within the Valentinian account of redemption.2

Tertullian (c. 200)

Tertullian knows a vicarious-baptism practice and, in his treatise on the resurrection, presses the verse for a bodily rising: some “are baptized for the dead,” he grants, “with such a presumption as made them suppose that the vicarious baptism… would be beneficial to the flesh of another in anticipation of the resurrection” — for why be baptized for the dead “unless the bodies rise again which are thus baptized?” But in Against Marcion he holds the practice at arm's length — “never mind that practice, whatever it may have been” — and offers his own gloss, that since it is the body that dies, “to be ‘baptized for the dead’… means, in fact, to be baptized for the body.” Both a reported rite and a second reading of the phrase appear in his works.3

No Greek comment on the verse survives from the third century. Origen treats the neighbouring words “that God may be all in all” (15:28) in On First Principles and the Commentary on Matthew, but no comment of his on 15:29 survives, and none from another pre-Nicene Greek writer has been found here; in the Greek catena the verse is glossed by Chrysostom, Theodoret and Photius. The next surviving comments date from c. 375–395.4

Epiphanius of Salamis (c. 375)

Epiphanius reports a practice in some detail. Refuting the Cerinthians, he records a “tradition” he had heard current in Asia and Galatia: “when some of their people died too soon, without baptism, others would be baptized for them in their names, so that they would not be punished for rising unbaptized at the resurrection.” The tradition he reports traces the verse to this proxy baptism; Epiphanius then gives a second explanation, which he calls satisfactory: “others explain the text satisfactorily by saying that, as long as they are catechumens, the dying are allowed baptism before they die because of this hope”.5

Ambrosiaster (c. 380)

The anonymous Latin commentator on Paul known as Ambrosiaster reports the same motive without naming a sect: “It seems that some people were at that time being baptized for the dead because they were afraid that someone who was not baptized would either not rise at all or else rise merely in order to be condemned.” He takes the practice as one at Corinth, driven by fear for the fate of the unbaptized dead.6

Didymus the Blind (c. 380)

Didymus, head of the catechetical school at Alexandria, pins the practice on Marcion: “The Marcionites baptize the living on behalf of dead unbelievers, not knowing that baptism saves only the person who receives it.” Didymus reads the verse of a proxy baptism and holds that such a baptism does not benefit the dead, because baptism saves only its recipient.7

John Chrysostom (c. 395)

Chrysostom describes the Marcionite rite in detail and gives his own reading of the verse. Among the Marcionites, he reports, “when any Catechumen departs… having concealed the living man under the couch of the dead, they approach the corpse and talk with him, and ask him if he wishes to receive baptism; then when he makes no answer, he that is concealed underneath saith in his stead that of course he should wish to be baptized; and so they baptize him instead of the departed, like men jesting upon the stage.” This he ridicules. Paul, he insists, means something else: at baptism the candidate confesses “I believe in the resurrection of the dead,” so that to be “baptized for the dead” is to be baptized in hope of the rising of “thy dead body” — the immersion and emergence themselves “a symbol of the descent into Hades and return thence.”8

The Council of Carthage of 419 (canon 18)

Among the African canons the council rules that “the Eucharist should not be given to the bodies of the dead” — for the dead can neither “take” nor “eat” — and, in the same breath, “Nor let the ignorance of the presbyters baptize those who are dead.” The canon does not cite 1 Corinthians 15:29; it legislates against giving either sacrament to the bodies of the dead.9

Theodoret of Cyrus (c. 435)

The Antiochene bishop and exegete of the next generation, in the earliest extant complete Greek commentary on the whole Pauline corpus, reads the verse of ordinary baptism but gives it a turn of his own, distinct from Chrysostom’s: not the confession said at the font but the rite itself as a burial with Christ. In his words, “the one being baptized is buried together with the Master, so that having shared in His death he may become a partaker also of the resurrection; but if the body is dead and does not rise, why on earth is he even baptized?” “The dead,” then, is the mortal body (Romans 6:4), and on his reading to deny its rising empties the buried-and-raised symbolism of the font.10

Cosmas Indicopleustes (c. 550)

The Alexandrian merchant-turned-monk reads the verse wholly of ordinary baptism, understood as an enacted death and rising: “Since we are baptized mystically for our dead bodies, being submerged in the holy water and emerging therefrom, thus imitating death and resurrection, from the hope and promise of the resurrection from the dead, why… do we perform these acts in vain?” Like Chrysostom and Theodoret, Cosmas takes “the dead” as the baptized person's own mortal body.11

Photius of Constantinople (c. 875)

The patriarch and scholar reads the verse without a proxy rite, as a debater’s reductio: if there is no resurrection, those “baptized for the dead” are washed only to remain forever dead and “held in Hades” — so that, on his argument, the deniers’ own baptism tells against their denial. He also comments on Paul’s third-person “they who are baptized,” reading it as deliberate tact — Paul “directs the argument under other persons, and not against those very men, so that he might not, by pressing them too hard, provoke them to despair.”12

Theophylact of Ohrid (c. 1075)

The Byzantine archbishop transmits Chrysostom almost verbatim. He retells the Marcionite rite — a living person hidden under the dead man's bed to answer for him — calls those who appeal to Paul for it “fools,” and gives the creedal reading: those who “believed in the future resurrection of dead bodies and were baptized in such hope” are the ones the verse concerns. 13

Thomas Aquinas (c. 1270)

Aquinas sets out two senses of the verse. “Baptized for the dead” can mean baptized to wash away “dead works,” that is, sins; or it can mean that “some at that time” were re-baptized “for some dead relative, so that he too would be freed from sins after death.” The second group he both commends and faults — commends “in the fact that they seemed to have faith in the resurrection,” but reprehends “in the fact that they believed that one can be baptized for another,” since baptism profits only its recipient. Aquinas distinguishes prayer, which he says “can help those in purgatory,” from baptism, which cannot be transferred (see purgatory, where the verse is discussed in connection with prayer and offering for the dead, and the intermediate state).14

John Calvin (1553)

Calvin rejects the vicarious reading: “I cannot by any means be persuaded to believe this.” Had such a “magical abuse” of baptism prevailed at Corinth, he argues, the apostle who reproved their lesser faults and “inveighed” against their handling of the Supper would not have suffered it “to be openly carried on in the Church,” still less have cited it “without any word of reproof.” Calvin reads instead that those are “baptized for the dead” “who are looked upon as already dead, and who have altogether despaired of life” — the practice, which Calvin dates “from the very commencement of the Church,” by which a catechumen in mortal danger asked baptism, “that they might not leave this world before they had made a profession of Christianity.”15

Modern scholarship (19th–21st century)

Gordon Fee and Jerome Murphy-O'Connor, as reported by Matthew Michael Connor (2010), identify a vicarious baptism — some living Corinthians undergoing the rite on behalf of specific dead — as the majority position, and Joel R. White (1997) calls it “the majority interpretation” while arguing for another. C. K. Barrett (1968) reads the verse as an ad hominem argument from a practice that was “not Paul's view” of baptism. Anthony Thiselton (2000) surveys the field; Richard DeMaris (1995) sets the rite within a Corinth preoccupied with the dead, reading it as a ritual to integrate the deceased into the world of the dead. Other modern readings — Bernard Foschini's repunctuation (1950–51), Maria Raeder's baptism “for the sake of” the dead (1955), J. C. O'Neill's “dying bodies” (1980), Murphy-O'Connor's Corinthian slogan (1981), White's baptism on account of the apostles (1997) and William O. Walker's interpolation thesis (2007) — are set out on the interpretation page. The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints practises proxy baptism for the dead; Joseph Smith's 1842 epistle, now Doctrine and Covenants 128, quotes this verse (see the great-apostasy timeline). R. P. Carlson (1993), quoted by DeMaris, writes that “the reference itself is simply so obscure and our knowledge so limited that we cannot discern just what this rite actually involved or meant.”16

Disputed readings

How the verse's phrase, and the Corinthian practice behind it, have been read, each reading set out with its proponents.

  • How has “baptized for the dead” (1 Corinthians 15:29) been read? (as a proxy baptism, as baptism for the baptized person's own body, of the dying catechumen, of “dead works,” as baptism over the graves, as repunctuated questions, as baptism for the sake of the dead, as a slogan about the apostles, as baptism on account of the apostles, or as an interpolation).
  • What was the character and origin of the Corinthian “baptism for the dead”? (a rite of Christian hope for the resurrection, a phrase with no rite behind it, or a Christian form of the Greco-Roman care of the dead).
