Baptized for the Dead (1 Corinthians 15:29)

Related doctrines: Hard passages — see also Who Killed Goliath? (2 Samuel 21:19) · How Tall Was Goliath? (six or four cubits) · Solomon's Stalls: 40,000 or 4,000? (1 Kings 4:26 vs. 2 Chronicles 9:25) · What Jesus Wrote on the Ground (John 8:6) · The Two Genealogies of Jesus (Matthew 1 vs. Luke 3) · The Death of Judas (Matthew 27 vs. Acts 1)

In the middle of his great resurrection chapter Paul throws off a single, unexplained line: “Else what shall they do which are baptized for the dead (οἱ βαπτιζόμενοι ὑπὲρ τῶν νεκρῶν), if the dead rise not at all? why are they then baptized for the dead?” (1 Corinthians 15:29). He names a practice, uses it as a lever for the bodily resurrection — and says not one word more: not what the rite was, not who did it, not whether he approved. It has become one of the most contested verses in the New Testament, with interpreters counting dozens of proposed meanings. The writing-on-the-ground DNA is here too: the text withholds what it means, and every reading below is a reconstruction from the setting rather than a report of the thing itself.

Two features frame the whole history. First, the earliest readers who take the words at face value locate the practice not in the mainstream church but among heretics — Valentinian Gnostics, Marcionites, Cerinthians — and the orthodox tradition then works hard to read the verse a different way. Second, Paul's own grammar keeps its distance: he asks what “they” will do, never “you” or “we,” so that even the ancients who granted a real vicarious baptism did not suppose Paul endorsed it — only that its practitioners, by their own act, testified to the resurrection they were now denying. What follows are the readings actually advanced, earliest first: from the Gnostic and Marcionite rites the fathers report, through the orthodox re-readings (the confession of the resurrection at one's own baptism; the deathbed baptism of catechumens already “as good as dead”), the conciliar prohibition of literal corpse- and proxy-baptism, and the schoolmen's menu, to the modern verdict that Paul does after all point to a genuine vicarious rite he neither shares nor explains.

Timeline

The witnesses are listed earliest first. Each reports or construes the verse; none can recover with certainty the practice Paul left unexplained.

Clement of Alexandria, quoting Theodotus the Valentinian (c. 170)

The oldest surviving interpretation is a Gnostic one, and it already reads the verse as a literal vicarious baptism — though of a mythological kind. In the excerpts Clement made from the Valentinian teacher Theodotus, the verse is glossed: “the angels of whom we are portions were baptised for us.” On this reading the “dead” are the spiritual seed deadened by bodily existence, and their heavenly counterparts — the angels — are baptized on their behalf, so that when the initiate receives “the Name” he may pass the Limit and enter the Pleroma. The earliest witness thus takes “baptized for the dead” as a real proxy rite, and builds an entire redemption-myth on it.1

Tertullian (c. 200)

Tertullian knows a vicarious-baptism practice and, in his treatise on the resurrection, presses the verse for a bodily rising: some “are baptized for the dead,” he grants, “with such a presumption as made them suppose that the vicarious baptism… would be beneficial to the flesh of another in anticipation of the resurrection” — for why be baptized for the dead “unless the bodies rise again which are thus baptized?” But in Against Marcion he holds the practice at arm's length — “never mind that practice, whatever it may have been” — and offers his own gloss, that since it is the body that dies, “to be ‘baptized for the dead’… means, in fact, to be baptized for the body.” The verse's whole later career — a reported rite, then an orthodox re-reading — is already visible in him.2

Epiphanius of Salamis (c. 375)

Epiphanius supplies the fullest ancient report of an actual practice. Refuting the Cerinthians, he records a “tradition” he had heard current in Asia and Galatia: “when some of their people died too soon, without baptism, others would be baptized for them in their names, so that they would not be punished for rising unbaptized at the resurrection.” This is a straightforward proxy baptism, and Epiphanius traces the verse to it. But he immediately offers the orthodox alternative: “others explain the text satisfactorily by saying that, as long as they are catechumens, the dying are allowed baptism before they die because of this hope” — the reading that would dominate the later East.3

Ambrosiaster (c. 380)

The anonymous Latin commentator on Paul known as Ambrosiaster reports the same motive without naming a sect: “It seems that some people were at that time being baptized for the dead because they were afraid that someone who was not baptized would either not rise at all or else rise merely in order to be condemned.” He takes the practice as a real (if mistaken) one at Corinth, driven by anxiety over the fate of the unbaptized dead.4

Didymus the Blind (c. 380)

Didymus, head of the catechetical school at Alexandria, pins the practice on Marcion: “The Marcionites baptize the living on behalf of dead unbelievers, not knowing that baptism saves only the person who receives it.” The verse is again read as literal proxy baptism — and again the orthodox writer's point is that the rite is worthless, because baptism benefits none but its recipient.5

John Chrysostom (c. 395)

Chrysostom gives the most vivid description of the Marcionite rite — and the classic orthodox re-reading. Among the Marcionites, he reports, “when any Catechumen departs… having concealed the living man under the couch of the dead, they approach the corpse and talk with him, and ask him if he wishes to receive baptism; then when he makes no answer, he that is concealed underneath saith in his stead that of course he should wish to be baptized; and so they baptize him instead of the departed, like men jesting upon the stage.” This he ridicules. Paul, he insists, means something else: at baptism the candidate confesses “I believe in the resurrection of the dead,” so that to be “baptized for the dead” is to be baptized in hope of the rising of “thy dead body” — the immersion and emergence themselves “a symbol of the descent into Hades and return thence.”6

The Council of Carthage of 419 (canon 18)

That a literal baptism of the dead was no mere heretical curiosity but a temptation inside the Latin church appears from its conciliar prohibition. Among the African canons the council rules that “the Eucharist should not be given to the bodies of the dead” — for the dead can neither “take” nor “eat” — and, in the same breath, “Nor let the ignorance of the presbyters baptize those who are dead.” The bishops legislate against exactly the corpse-directed sacraments the verse was being read to license.7

Cosmas Indicopleustes (c. 550)

The Alexandrian merchant-turned-monk reads the verse wholly of ordinary baptism, understood as an enacted death and rising: “Since we are baptized mystically for our dead bodies, being submerged in the holy water and emerging therefrom, thus imitating death and resurrection, from the hope and promise of the resurrection from the dead, why… do we perform these acts in vain?” The Chrysostomic line — “the dead” are the baptizand's own mortal body — here hardens into settled Greek exegesis.8

Theophylact of Ohrid (c. 1075)

The Byzantine archbishop transmits Chrysostom almost verbatim. He retells the Marcionite ruse — a living person hidden under the dead man's bed to answer for him — dismisses the heretics' appeal to Paul as the folly of “fools,” and gives the creedal reading: those who “believed in the future resurrection of dead bodies and were baptized in such hope” are the ones the verse concerns. The medieval Greek tradition closes ranks behind the orthodox gloss.9

Thomas Aquinas (c. 1270)

Aquinas lays out the medieval Latin options and weighs them. “Baptized for the dead” can mean baptized to wash away “dead works,” that is, sins; or it can mean that “some at that time” were re-baptized “for some dead relative, so that he too would be freed from sins after death.” The second group he both commends and faults — commends “in the fact that they seemed to have faith in the resurrection,” but reprehends “in the fact that they believed that one can be baptized for another,” since baptism profits only its recipient. Here the verse brushes the doctrine of help for the dead: prayer, Aquinas grants, “can help those in purgatory,” but the sacrament of baptism cannot be transferred — a distinction that bears on purgatory and the intermediate state.10

John Calvin (1553)

Calvin rejects the vicarious reading outright as psychologically and morally impossible for Paul. Had such a “magical abuse” of baptism prevailed at Corinth, he argues, the apostle who reproved their lesser faults and “inveighed” against their abuse of the Supper would never have “openly” passed it over — still less cited it approvingly. Calvin reads instead that those are “baptized for the dead” “who are looked upon as already dead, and who have altogether despaired of life” — the ancient practice by which a catechumen in mortal danger asked baptism, “that they might not leave this world before they had made a profession of Christianity.”11

The modern verdict (19th–21st century)

Modern commentators, having catalogued the readings — upward of forty distinct interpretations, by one much-repeated tally more than two hundred — have largely swung back toward the ancient heretics' plain sense: that Paul refers to a genuine vicarious baptism, some living Corinthians undergoing the rite on behalf of specific dead, which he neither shares nor commends but presses as an ad hominem for the resurrection (his third-person “they” marking the distance). Anthony Thiselton surveys the whole field; Richard DeMaris sets the rite within a Corinth preoccupied with the passage of the dead, reading it as a ritual to integrate the deceased into the world of the dead. The practice has a modern afterlife as well: the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints revived proxy baptism on the strength of this verse, a development that belongs with the wider apostasy-and-restoration claim. What has not returned is any confidence that the Corinthian rite can be recovered in detail — on that, Paul's silence holds.12

Arguments

The timeline records who read the verse how. This section weighs the pivotal interpretive question on its own merits.

Related arguments — reading “baptized for the dead”

The whole dispute turns on one clause — οἱ βαπτιζόμενοι ὑπὲρ τῶν νεκρῶν, “those baptized for the dead” — and two rival readings, each independently advocated and best read together, divide the interpreters: does the phrase describe a real proxy baptism on behalf of the dead, or the ordinary baptism of the living, professing the resurrection? Weighed adversarially on their own pages.

Does 1 Corinthians 15:29 attest a vicarious “baptism for the dead” at Corinth? (the vicarious reading) — plausible

The reading of the ancient heretics and the modern majority: the words mean what they most obviously say — some Corinthians were baptized on behalf of particular dead persons — and Paul throws the practice at the resurrection-deniers without owning or explaining it. Strongest on the grammar (the natural force of ὑπὲρ + genitive and of “the dead” as dead people) and on Paul’s distancing third-person “they.” What keeps it short of sound is everything the words withhold — the shape and theology of the rite, and whether the practitioners were proto-orthodox or a fringe (plausible).

Do the words mean ordinary Christian baptism, not a proxy rite? (the non-vicarious readings) — plausible

The orthodox family of readings, flagship the Greek East’s creedal sense (Chrysostom, Cosmas, Theophylact): to be “baptized for the dead” is to be baptized with a view to the resurrection of the dead confessed at the font, so denying the resurrection empties one’s own baptism. Coherent, anciently and widely held, and free of the vicarious reading’s embarrassment about an unrebuked rite — but the less-probable reading of the Greek, since it must bend “the dead” away from the dead persons the chapter is about and cannot easily account for Paul’s third-person “they” (plausible).

Does the verse warrant proxy baptism as a divinely-mandated ordinance? (the Latter-day Saint reading) — weak

The reading that takes the further step: not merely that some Corinthians were baptized for the dead (the vicarious question, conceded plausible), but that Paul thereby endorses proxy baptism as a real, salvific, standing ordinance of the apostolic church — the biblical warrant for the Latter-day Saint temple practice. It starts from the grammar it shares with the vicarious reading and adds a genuine pastoral appeal (God’s fairness to the unevangelized dead, 1 Peter 3:19; 4:6), but fails past the grammar: Paul reports without instituting, his third-person aside commends nothing, the sole proxy rite the ancient record names it condemns as heretical, the mainstream church never practised it, and the reading rests finally on the wider apostasy-and-restoration claim (weak).
