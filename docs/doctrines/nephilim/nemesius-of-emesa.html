Nemesius of Emesa on the Nephilim

Dates: c. 390 Summary: Nemesius of Emesa (fl. c. 390) describes a fall of angels. Among the incorporeal beings, he says, only some angels fell — those who "inclined to things below" and set their desire on earthly things, withdrawing from God. He does not name Genesis 6 in this passage; it is placed here beside the angelic reading of the "sons of God" who took the daughters of men.

Claims & evidence

Only some angels fell — those who inclined toward earthly things

Of the incorporeal beings, only angels fell away, and not all of them, but some only, that inclined to things below and set their desire on things of earth, withdrawing themselves from their relations with things above, even from God.

— On the Nature of Man 58, trans. W. Telfer (Library of Christian Classics 4, 1955); the full work is online in George Wither's 1636 translation, archive.org. Nemesius frames the fall of angels as a downward inclination toward earthly things.

Notes

  • Nemesius describes a fall of certain angels who turned toward earthly things; the angelic reading of Genesis 6 reads the "sons of God" as such fallen angels.
  • He treats the angelic fall philosophically (incorporeal beings inclining to the corporeal) rather than narrating the Genesis 6 union itself.
  • Nemesius appears also as a witness on the living heavens timeline, where the same treatise surveys (without adopting) Plato's world-soul and refers the motion of the stars to a necessity God laid on them — not to a soul or an angel.
