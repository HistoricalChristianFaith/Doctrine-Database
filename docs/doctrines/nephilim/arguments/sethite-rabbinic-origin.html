Did the Sethite reading originate as a rabbinic reaction against the Enochic reading?

Disputed reading · on The Sons of God and the Nephilim (Genesis 6) · witness: Julius Africanus

Related readings: Is Augustine's case against the angelic reading (City of God XV.23) circular? · Are the Sethite Homilies on Genesis Chrysostom's own? · Was 1 Enoch received as Scripture in the early churches?

The passage

Rabbi Shimon ben Yoḥai called them … "the children of judges." Rabbi Shimon ben Yoḥai used to curse anyone who would call them … "children of God."

— Bereshit Rabbah 26:5, on Genesis 6:2, Sefaria.1

The earliest extant Christian statement of the Sethite reading is Julius Africanus's (d. c. 240): "the descendants of Seth are called the sons of God … the descendants of Cain are named the seed of men."2

The question

P. S. Alexander (1972) dates the angelic reading of the "sons of God" as effectively unanimous until the mid-second century AD, when Jewish exegesis exchanged it for a human reading; the Targumim render "sons of the great ones / nobles" (Onqelos, Pseudo-Jonathan) or "sons of the judges" (Neofiti). What is disputed is how the Christian Sethite reading relates to that Jewish turn: whether it was a rabbinic repudiation of the Enochic reading taken over by the Fathers, or whether the rabbinic and Christian turns away from the Enochic tradition ran on separate courses and for separate reasons. The answer bears on how the history of the two readings of Genesis 6 is told. The sources gathered for this page include no academic study that addresses the origin of the Christian Sethite reading directly; the second reading draws on Reed's study of the Enochic tradition.3

A rabbinic repudiation of the Enochic reading, adopted by the Fathers

On this reading the Jewish community, rejecting the Enochic books once Christians had made them their own, devised the Seth-and-Cain reading, which the Church Fathers then took over.

Held by: Fr. Stephen De Young and Fr. Andrew Stephen Damick (Orthodox priests, Lord of Spirits, "Land of Giants," c. 2020); Tertullian (c. 200), on the Jewish rejection of 1 Enoch only.4

the Jewish community repudiated that [the Enochic literature] and came up with what's called the Sethite hypothesis sometimes in the literature, that it's the line of Seth and the line of Cain. This gets adopted by Church Fathers.

De Young holds that the angelic reading is the original. It is the reading of 1 Enoch, Jubilees, the Septuagint, Philo and Josephus, and of the ante-Nicene Church. He places the first datable turn against it on the Jewish side, in the second century: R. Simeon b. Yohai's "sons of judges" and his curse on anyone who says "sons of God." On his account the Enochic books were by then Christian reading. Tertullian, around 200, gives this as the reason for their Jewish rejection: "By the Jews it may now seem to have been rejected for that (very) reason, just like all the other (portions) nearly which tell of Christ." De Young dates the Christian Sethite reading from Julius Africanus, then Ephrem and Augustine, in the late fourth and early fifth centuries. On his reading the Fathers who adopted it rejected a misunderstanding of the angelic claim, not the ancient claim itself. On this reading the human reading of Genesis 6 began on the Jewish side, in a turn bound up with the increasingly Christian career of 1 Enoch, and passed from there into the Church.4,5

A parting of the ways on separate timelines

On this reading the rabbinic movement set aside the Enochic myth earlier and more sharply, for reasons of its own after 70, while Christians adopted the Watcher tradition and turned from it later and gradually.

Held by: Annette Yoshiko Reed (Fallen Angels and the History of Judaism and Christianity, 2005), on the reception of the Enochic tradition.6

Reed traces the Book of the Watchers from its wide influence in pre-rabbinic Judaism, through its rejection by the rabbinic movement and its adoption by early Christians, to its later Christian marginalization and its loss to the West. She places the rabbinic move within post-70 boundary-drawing and identity formation. The Jewish and Christian rejections run on different timelines, the rabbinic sharper and earlier, the Christian more gradual. The angelic myth was never fully purged from Judaism: it survives in Targum Pseudo-Jonathan (Shemḥazai and Azael) and in the Talmud (Uzza and Azael), and it re-emerges in post-Talmudic mysticism. The rabbinic "sons of judges / nobles" reading turns on the double sense of ʾĕlōhîm, "God" and "judge." The curse in Genesis Rabbah 26:5 rests on the Aramaic Elohaya, which means only "God." Alan F. Segal (1977) documents the rabbinic polemic against "two powers in heaven," directed at exalted semi-divine figures, with the transformed Enoch-Metatron of b. Ḥagigah 15a among them. On the Christian side, pagan critics, Celsus in the second century and later the emperor Julian, mocked the angel–human breeding story. The explicitly Sethite reading first appears in Julius Africanus around 240, who also records the angelic view. Ephrem (c. 363) sets Seth against Cain within the Syriac tradition of the Cave of Treasures. Augustine (c. 420) builds the reading from the Septuagint's "sons of God / angels of God" with Psalm 82. On this reading the Jewish turn and the Christian Sethite reading are distinct developments within one long parting of the ways.6,7,8
