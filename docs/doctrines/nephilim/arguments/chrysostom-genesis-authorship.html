Are the Sethite Homilies on Genesis Chrysostom's own?

Disputed reading · on The Sons of God and the Nephilim (Genesis 6) · witness: John Chrysostom

Related readings: Did the Sethite reading originate as a rabbinic reaction against the Enochic reading? · Is Augustine's case against the angelic reading (City of God XV.23) circular?

The passage

If he does take that view, he's taking that view around 412, 413. … This is after. This is post-St. Ephraim, post-St. Augustine. … he's got two sets of homilies on Genesis, essentially, and scholars disagree about how those relate to each other. … The dating in the early fifth century is secure, but there are also a significant number of people — and these people include St. Photius the Great, his notes on the library at Constantinople — who think that what are now considered to be the later homilies weren't St. John Chrysostom, or at least reflect another hand. That someone else came and revised them. … It's also very possible that we don't know what view he held, and this was sort of expanded upon by a later editor of his homilies.

— Fr. Stephen De Young, Lord of Spirits, "Land of Giants," on the Sethite reading of Genesis 6 in Homily 22 on Genesis.1

The question

Chrysostom left two overlapping works on Genesis: 67 Homilies on Genesis (CPG 4409), of which Homily 22 reads the "sons of God" as Seth's line, and nine Sermons on Genesis (CPG 4410), whose first eight cover the same early chapters, "and in places the resemblance is verbatim." W. A. Markowicz set out the relation of the two series as "a problem" in 1963; the theories since include that one series revises the other, that only the sermons were preached, or that one preserves an auditor's notes. What is disputed is when and where the homilies were given and fixed in writing, and whether their Sethite reading is Chrysostom's own. The answer decides whether Chrysostom is counted among the Sethite witnesses.2

Preached by Chrysostom at Antioch, c. 385–388

On this reading the 67 homilies, Homily 22 included, are Chrysostom's own sermons from his years as a priest at Antioch.

Held by: De Montfaucon (editor of Chrysostom, as reported by Hill); J. Quasten (Patrology III); R. C. Hill (Fathers of the Church 74, 1986); Cyrille Crépey (Revue d'études augustiniennes et patristiques, 2009).3

Quasten, Cayré and Altaner–Stuiber date the series to Antioch, probably 388. Crépey argues in detail for 388 and for Antiochene provenance. Hill places the homilies at Antioch, "perhaps as early as the year 385." Photius's Bibliotheca counts 61 homilies, 27 of them delivered in Lent. De Montfaucon, as Hill reports, "debates the testimony of Photius… to the number 61." The present Homily 33 records the break from Holy Week to Pentecost, which "suggests that Photius has lost five homilies." On this account Photius's notice concerns the count of homilies in his copy. CPG lists both Genesis series under Chrysostom's name. Homily 22 also argues for the Sethite reading in three connected ways. It holds that "while human beings are called sons of God, angels are nowhere so called." It cites Matthew 22:30, that at the resurrection "they neither marry nor are given in marriage." And it traces Seth and Enosh against the line of Cain. On this reading the Sethite exegesis belongs to the homily's argument as Chrysostom preached it.3,4

A late text, perhaps expanded by a later editor

On this reading the Sethite material is late, after Ephrem and Augustine, and may reflect a reviser's hand, so it cannot settle what Chrysostom himself held.

Held by: Fr. Stephen De Young and Fr. Andrew Stephen Damick (Orthodox priests, Lord of Spirits, "Land of Giants," c. 2020).1

Asked whether the Sethite passage in the Genesis homilies is Chrysostom's view, De Young answers that it is "hard to know for sure." He dates the Sethite material to "around 412, 413," after Ephrem and Augustine, and calls that dating "secure." He points to the two sets of homilies, whose relation scholars dispute. He cites "a significant number of people," St. Photius among them, who hold that the later homilies "weren't St. John Chrysostom, or at least reflect another hand." Photius's Bibliotheca transmits a count of 61 homilies against the 67 of the received text, so his manuscript was not the text read today. On this reading the two-series problem points to a complicated history of transmission for the written Genesis corpus. On this reading a single proof-text from such a corpus cannot carry the weight put on it: "it's not a slam-dunk to use this text from Chrysostom and say: Well, that's what Chrysostom's view was and that's it." It remains "very possible that we don't know what view he held."1,2

Preached at Antioch, revised in writing at Constantinople after 401

On this reading the homilies are Chrysostom's, but their written text was reworked in a literary recension during his years at Constantinople.

Held by: ⚠ unverified — no named proponent located yet.5

The study "Revisiting the Date of Chrysostom's Homilies on Genesis" discusses internal evidence in the eighth homily that has been read as pointing to a literary recension after 401, in the wake of the anthropomorphite controversy at Constantinople. Chrysostom was bishop there from 398 until his exile; he died in 407. On this reading the homilies were first preached at Antioch, and the text that survives was fixed in a recension made within his lifetime, during his episcopate, so that the Genesis corpus carries both stages.5
