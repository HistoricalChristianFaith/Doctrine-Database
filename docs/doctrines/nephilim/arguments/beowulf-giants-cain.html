Does Beowulf witness the angelic reading of Genesis 6?

Disputed reading · on The Sons of God and the Nephilim (Genesis 6) · cited by: Doug Wilson

The passage

Since the Creator had proscribed him
with the kin of Cain—that killing avenged
eternal God, for that he Abel slew! …
Of Cain awoke all that woful breed,
Etins and elves and evil-spirits,
as well as the giants that warred with God
weary while: but their wage was paid them!

— Beowulf, ll. 106b–114, trans. Francis B. Gummere, The Oldest English Epic (1909), elfinspell.com. The Old English makes Grendel one "in Caines cynne" ("in Cain's kin," l. 107), and says that þanon ("thence") awoke "eotenas ond ylfe ond orcneas, / swylce gigantas, þa wið Gode wunnon" ("ettins and elves and evil-spirits, likewise giants, who strove with God").1

where was etched the rise
of that far-off fight when the floods o'erwhelmed,
raging waves, the race of giants
(fearful their fate!), a folk estranged
from God Eternal: whence guerdon due
in that waste of waters the Wielder paid them.

— Beowulf, ll. 1688b–1693, on the hilt of the giant-forged sword, trans. Gummere, elfinspell.com; the Old English "giganta cyn" ("the race of giants," l. 1690), "fremde þeod / ecean Dryhtne" ("a people estranged from the eternal Lord").1

The question

The Old English epic Beowulf (c. 700–1000) twice places its giants in biblical history: as broods descended from Cain, and as a race destroyed by the Flood. What is disputed is whether the poem belongs among the witnesses to the angelic reading of Genesis 6, in which the giants are the offspring of fallen "sons of God" and human women. The answer bears on how far the angelic reading's giant-lore lasted in the medieval Latin West. The sources gathered for this page include no academic study that enlists the poem for the angelic reading; that reading is given from its modern popular holder.

Cain's kin: the giants drawn into a human genealogy

On this reading the poet derives Grendel and the giants from Cain, a man, and fuses the Genesis-6 giants and Enochic giant-lore into that Cainite descent.

Held by: R. E. Kaske (Speculum, 1971); Ruth Mellinkoff (Anglo-Saxon England, 1979–80); Andy Orchard (Pride and Prodigies, 1995).2

Both passages anchor the monsters in Cain, the fratricide of Genesis 4. Grendel is "in Caines cynne," and from Cain "awoke" ettins, elves, evil-spirits and the gigantas who strove with God. The giants drowned in the Flood are the same giganta cyn. Kaske argued that the Enochic giant-tradition supplies the framework that lets the poet join Cain's progeny, the gigantas and the Genesis-6 giants into one monstrous descent. Mellinkoff takes the Cainite descent as given and studies the puzzle it raises: how a line of Cain survived the Flood that drowned all flesh but Noah's. She surveys the medieval traditions of post-diluvian survival that allowed it. Orchard, in "The Kin of Cain," describes how the poet built his monsters from the Cain genealogy, conflating biblical and apocryphal strands of patristic teratology. The Latin background is Augustine's City of God XV.23, which reads the "sons of God" as Seth's line, the "daughters of men" as Cain's, and the giants as human. The Vulgate an Anglo-Saxon poet knew calls them gigantes (Gen 6:4). On this reading the poem carries the giants, their war with God and the Flood, and sets them in the line of Cain.2,3

A witness to the ancient world's agreement on a celestial–earthly union

On this reading Beowulf stands with Josephus and 1 Enoch as an ancient witness that the giants came from a union of heavenly beings with women.

Held by: Douglas Wilson (Reformed pastor, sermon "The Nephilim, Hades, and Other Oddments," c. 2021).4

why on earth would such unions result in giants? And why would the entire ancient world concur with the reading that this was a perverse celestial/earthly connection, from Josephus to Beowulf to the Book of Enoch—from responsible histories to oddball books?

Wilson reads the "sons of God" as celestial beings and the Nephilim as their giant offspring. He asks why mere human unions would produce giants, and answers from the breadth of the witnesses: Josephus and 1 Enoch among the ancients, and Beowulf in the Christian Middle Ages. The poem knows the antediluvian giants as real; it says they "warred with God" and were "a folk estranged from God Eternal"; and it makes the Flood God's judgement on them. Its giant-lore was traced by Kaske to the Book of Enoch, the third name on Wilson's list. On this reading the poem attests how widely, and how long, the giants of Genesis 6 were remembered as a race at war with God, the picture the celestial reading gives of them.4
