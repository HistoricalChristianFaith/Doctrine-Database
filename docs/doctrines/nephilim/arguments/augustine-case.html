Is Augustine's case against the angelic reading (City of God XV.23) circular?

Disputed reading · on The Sons of God and the Nephilim (Genesis 6) · witness: Augustine of Hippo

Related readings: Did the Sethite reading originate as a rabbinic reaction against the Enochic reading? · Are the Sethite Homilies on Genesis Chrysostom's own? · Was 1 Enoch received as Scripture in the early churches?

The passage

Let us omit, then, the fables of those scriptures which are called apocryphal, because their obscure origin was unknown to the fathers from whom the authority of the true Scriptures has been transmitted to us by a most certain and well-ascertained succession… But it is not without reason that these writings have no place in that canon of Scripture which was preserved in the temple of the Hebrew people by the diligence of successive priests; for their antiquity brought them under suspicion, and it was impossible to ascertain whether these were his genuine writings, and they were not brought forward as genuine by the persons who were found to have carefully preserved the canonical books by a successive transmission.

— Augustine, The City of God XV.23 (trans. Marcus Dods), historicalchristian.faith. In the same chapter he adds that the writings under Enoch's name "which contain these fables about the giants, saying that their fathers were not men, are properly judged by prudent men to be not genuine."1

The question

In City of God XV.23 Augustine sets aside the books under Enoch's name and reads the "sons of God" of Genesis 6 as men of Seth's line. What is disputed is the ground of that judgement: whether he set the books aside on grounds independent of the angelic reading, whether he did so without the book itself before him, or whether he judged the Watchers story spurious because it was the claim in dispute. The answer bears on how the turn of the Latin West to the Sethite reading is described. The sources gathered for this page include no modern academic study of the circularity charge; the readings are given from Augustine's own text and from their popular modern holders.

Grounds independent of the giants: provenance, the Hebrew canon, and the Genesis text

On this reading Augustine sets the Enochic books aside for their uncertain origin and their absence from the Hebrew canon, and draws the Sethite reading from the canonical text of Genesis itself.

Held by: Augustine (City of God XV.23, c. 420), in the grounds he states.2

Augustine gives his reasons as matters of provenance and canon-history. The books are of "obscure origin… unknown to the fathers." They have "no place in that canon… preserved in the temple of the Hebrew people by the diligence of successive priests." They "were not brought forward as genuine" by the custodians of the canonical books. Their "antiquity brought them under suspicion," and it was "impossible to ascertain" whether they were Enoch's. He grants that Enoch wrote something inspired: "We cannot deny that Enoch, the seventh from Adam, left some divine writings, for this is asserted by the Apostle Jude in his canonical epistle." His target is the writings circulating under that name, "just as many writings are produced by heretics under the names both of other prophets."

The Sethite reading he builds separately, from the Septuagint's own "angels of God / sons of God." He reads them of men graced and then fallen: "by the Spirit of God they had been made angels of God, and sons of God; but declining towards lower things, they are called men, a name of nature, not of grace," with Psalm 82, "I have said, You are gods." Behind the judgement on the giants stands his conviction about the angels: "certainly I could by no means believe that God's holy angels could at that time have so fallen." On 1 Peter 3:18–21, in Letter 164 (414), he calls the question "intricate," declines "to pronounce any definite opinion," and reads the "spirits in prison" as the human dead. On this reading the argument rests on canon-history, on the Genesis text and on his angelology, each held on its own ground.3,4

A case made without the book

On this reading Augustine, and Aquinas after him, read Genesis 6 without access to 1 Enoch, whose full text and early spread came to light only in modern times.

Held by: Taylor Marshall (Catholic author; blog, 2018; podcast, 2023).5

Marshall writes that "Saint Augustine and Saint Thomas Aquinas (both had not read 1 Enoch)" read the passages in Jude and 2 Peter as they did, and frames his 2023 podcast on the same premise. The complete text of 1 Enoch survived only in Ge'ez. It was lost to the Latin West until the Scottish explorer James Bruce brought three Ethiopic manuscripts to Europe in 1773. Richard Laurence, Regius Professor of Hebrew at Oxford, published the first English translation in 1821. The Aramaic fragments from Qumran Cave 4, found in 1952 and edited by J. T. Milik in 1976, carry the work back before the Christian era and attest its wide circulation in the Second Temple period. Marshall treats the angelic reading as the older and near-unanimous one before Augustine. On this reading the Sethite turn was made on a narrower body of evidence than is now available.6

A circular case

On this reading Augustine, unable to tell which parts of the Enochic books are genuine, marks the Watchers story as the spurious part because it is the very claim in dispute.

Held by: Fr. Stephen De Young and Fr. Andrew Stephen Damick (Orthodox priests, Lord of Spirits, "Land of Giants," c. 2020).7

St. Augustine was saying this text we have now must represent Enoch's work, but he says: Look, that makes it so old. We can't verify what in this text we have now actually comes from Enoch and what doesn't. And then he makes this circular argument where he says you can't trust this story about the watchers, about these sinful angels, in the book of Enoch, because not everything in the book of Enoch is true and goes back to Enoch. So you say to him: Well, what's an example of something that's not true and doesn't go back to Enoch? And he says: Well, the watchers story. … "It's untrustworthy because it has stuff like this story in it, so you can't believe this story." This is circular argument. So he has some problems even getting this view forward.

On De Young's account, Augustine grants that the books are old and that he cannot sort Enoch's genuine words from the rest. He then lists the content of the Watchers story among the marks of a spurious book: the writings "which contain these fables about the giants, saying that their fathers were not men," are "judged… to be not genuine." In that clause the angelic origin of the giants is treated as already false. De Young also holds that the angelic reading was the original one, held across the Church's first centuries, and that the standing of 1 Enoch and the fortunes of that reading rose and fell together. On this reading the Fathers who turned against the Watchers rejected a misunderstanding of the ancient claim, and Augustine's own case for the turn takes its conclusion as its premise.7
