Does 2 Peter's "Tartarus" (tartaróō) borrow from the Greek Titanomachy?

Disputed reading · on The Sons of God and the Nephilim (Genesis 6) · witness: the New Testament (Jude & 2 Peter)

Related readings: Is 1 Enoch's Watcher legend a Maccabean allegory modelled on the Greek Titanomachy?

The passage

For if God spared not the angels that sinned, but cast them down to hell, and delivered them into chains of darkness, to be reserved unto judgment;

— 2 Peter 2:4 (KJV), historicalchristian.faith; "cast them down to hell" renders the Greek participle tartarṓsas, from tartaróō, "cast into Tartarus."1

And the angels which kept not their first estate, but left their own habitation, he hath reserved in everlasting chains under darkness unto the judgment of the great day.

— Jude 6 (KJV), the parallel verse, historicalchristian.faith.2

The question

Tartaróō occurs in the New Testament only at 2 Peter 2:4. In Homer and Hesiod, Tartarus is the gulf "as far beneath Hades as heaven is above earth" into which Zeus casts the Titans. What is disputed is what the word brings to 2 Peter's account of the angels that sinned: a Greek name for the deepest prison, received through Jewish writings; a deliberate echo of the Titans' punishment for Greek-speaking readers; or a conscious identification of the Watchers with the Titans. The answer bears on how far 2 Peter joins the Enochic Watcher tradition to Greek myth.

A Greek name for the deepest prison, received through Jewish apocalyptic

On this reading 2 Peter uses the current Greek word for the lowest hell, already at home in Jewish writings about the fallen angels, without taking up the Titan myth as belief.

Held by: J. N. D. Kelly (The Epistles of Peter and of Jude, 1969); Richard Bauckham (Word Biblical Commentary, 1983); Peter H. Davids (Pillar, 2006); Gene L. Green (BECNT, 2008).3

Bauckham reads 2 Peter 2:4 with Genesis 6:1–4 and notes its overlap with "early Greek theogonic myths, in which the ancient giants, the Cyclopes and Titans, were imprisoned in Tartarus, the lowest part of the underworld." He holds that the term reached 2 Peter "through the mediation of Jewish apocalyptic." The noun τάρταρος was already naturalized in Hellenistic-Jewish Greek: the Septuagint uses it at Job 40:20, 41:24 and Proverbs 30:16, as do Philo and Josephus. Greek 1 Enoch 20:2 sets the archangel Uriel "over Tartarus," placing the fallen angels' prison there before 2 Peter wrote. The verse itself keeps to the Enochic frame: the angels who sinned stand beside Noah and the Flood and beside Sodom and Gomorrah, and they are held in "chains of darkness" for judgment. On this reading 2 Peter took over "Tartarus as the angels' prison" from that Jewish vocabulary. The commentators named above read tartaróō as adopting the Greek word for the deepest hell while carrying none of the myth as doctrine.3

A deliberate echo of the Titans' punishment, for Greek-speaking readers

On this reading 2 Peter chose a word tied to the Titans' fall so that his readers would hear the Greek story of rebel powers cast down.

Held by: Jerome H. Neyrey (Anchor Bible, 1993); Bradly S. Billings (The Expository Times, 2008).4

Neyrey holds that the verse is tailored "to evoke a reminiscence of the Greek story of punishment of the Titans in Tartarus." Billings holds that the author is "appropriating… the particular language of Graeco-Roman mythology" to form a "linguistic bridge" into his readers' world. Neyrey and Billings point to the word's associations with the Titans. Tartarus is, in Homer (Iliad 8.13–16) and Hesiod (Theogony 717–735), the gulf into which Zeus hurls the defeated Titans. Liddell–Scott–Jones lists the verb-family at four witnesses across a millennium, including a Townley scholion on the Iliad about the gods "below with Kronos." A. T. Robertson notes the verb is found "here alone save in a scholion on Homer," and the cognate katatartaróō is the standard verb for the Titans' fall: "Zeus cast Kronos down to Tartarus." Jude 6, the parallel verse, has the angels kept "in everlasting chains under darkness" and does not use the word; Bauckham reads 2 Peter as reworking Jude's material,3 so that on his account the verb is 2 Peter's own choice at that point. On this reading the choice is a rhetorical one, made for a Hellenistic audience.5

The Watchers mapped onto the Titans

On this reading 1 Enoch and 2 Peter deliberately blend the Greek Titan-myth with the Hebrew Watcher legend, reading God against the Watchers through Zeus against the Titans.

Held by: Michael S. Heiser (Reversing Hermon, 2017); Taylor Marshall (blog, 2018; podcast, 2023).6

Marshall writes that Jude and 2 Peter "allude to the fantastical events of 1 Enoch, namely the sexual encounters of fallen angels with human women," and that 2 Peter 2:4 "explicitly uses Enoch's word 'Tartarus'." In his 2023 podcast he adds that the word is Greek, not Hebrew or Aramaic, drawn from Homer's Tartarus and the Titanomachy, so that its use in 1 Enoch and 2 Peter signals a "judaizing" blend of the two stories. Heiser presses a chain from the Rephaim to the Titans to Tartarus. Its links are in Jewish Greek. The Septuagint renders the Nephilim gígantes (Gen 6:4) and renders "the valley of Rephaim" as "the valley of the Titans" (2 Sam 5:18), while Chronicles uses Gigántōn; Heiser treats the two words as interchangeable for the Rephaim. Judith 16:6 sets "the sons of the Titans" beside "lofty giants." The Egyptian-Jewish third Sibylline Oracle works Kronos and the Titans into the Genesis history. In the second, Uriel leads up "the shapes of Titans born of old, and giants, and all whom the deluge whelmed," and Greek 1 Enoch had set that same Uriel "over Tartarus." Josephus compares the Watchers' sons to the Greek giants. On this reading, when 2 Peter names Tartarus for the angels' prison, he joins a Jewish practice of naming the Watchers and their giants in the language of the Titans.7
