Was 1 Enoch received as Scripture in the early churches?

Disputed reading · on The Sons of God and the Nephilim (Genesis 6) · witness: the Book of Enoch

Related readings: Is Augustine's case against the angelic reading (City of God XV.23) circular? · Did the Sethite reading originate as a rabbinic reaction against the Enochic reading?

The passage

And Enoch also, the seventh from Adam, prophesied of these, saying, Behold, the Lord cometh with ten thousands of his saints,

— Jude 14 (KJV), quoting 1 Enoch 1:9, historicalchristian.faith.1

The question

Jude quotes 1 Enoch as prophecy. R. H. Charles and the Catholic Encyclopedia describe the book as highly esteemed in the early centuries, "mainly owing to the quotation in Jude," until "in the fourth century the Henoch writings lost credit and ceased to be quoted." What is disputed is the standing the early churches gave it: Scripture, a cited book outside the churches' divine writings, or an apostolic tradition kept apart from public reading. The answer bears on how the Fathers' use of the angelic reading of Genesis 6 is described. The same Jude datum is discussed under whether the Old Testament canon was open in the apostolic age, and Tertullian's defence of the book is set out on the canon timeline.2

Scripture and prophecy for its early Christian users

On this reading 1 Enoch was cited and defended as Scripture by early Christian writers, and it remains Scripture in the Ethiopian and Eritrean churches.

Held by: the Epistle of Barnabas (c. 130); Tertullian (On the Apparel of Women, c. 200); Clement of Alexandria (c. 215); the Ethiopian and Eritrean Orthodox Tewahedo churches, in whose canon it stands.3

A New Testament author quotes Enoch as one who "prophesied." The Epistle of Barnabas introduces an apocalyptic oracle with "it is written, as Enoch says," and quotes the Animal Apocalypse (1 Enoch 89) as what "the Scripture saith"; the Catholic Encyclopedia reports that it "twice cites Henoch as Scripture." Tertullian defends the book outright: "every Scripture suitable for edification is divinely inspired." He answers the objection that it could not have survived the Flood, and holds that the Jews rejected it "for that (very) reason, just like all the other (portions) nearly which tell of Christ." He adds that "Enoch possesses a testimony in the Apostle Jude." Clement of Alexandria, glossing Jude's quotation in his Adumbrationes, writes: "In these words he verifies the prophecy." In his Eclogae Propheticae 53.4 he cites Enoch by name as an authority on the Watchers: "Enoch already said that the angels who transgressed taught humanity astronomy, divination, and the other arts." The book's full text survived in Ge'ez, in Ethiopian Bibles, and it is canonical in Ethiopia and Eritrea. On this reading these writers received Enoch's words as genuine prophecy and as Scripture, the standing the book keeps in those churches.3,4

A cited book, not among the churches' divine writings

On this reading 1 Enoch was known and quoted, but did not circulate in the churches as a divine book, and the canon lists of the fourth and fifth centuries left it out.

Held by: Origen (Against Celsus V.54, c. 248); Athanasius (Festal Letter 39, 367); Jerome (Lives of Illustrious Men; Commentary on Titus); Augustine (City of God XV.23, c. 420).5

Origen cites "the book of Enoch" in De Principiis IV, and also writes that "the books which bear the name Enoch do not at all circulate in the Churches as divine." Tertullian, defending the book, grants that it "is not received by some, because it is not admitted into the Jewish canon either." Athanasius's Festal Letter 39 lists the canonical books and a second tier "appointed by the Fathers to be read," and calls everything else "apocryphal… an invention of heretics"; 1 Enoch is in neither list. Jerome calls it "the apocryphal book of Enoch." He holds that quotation does not confer canonical standing: Paul quotes the poet Epimenides (Titus 1:12), and "those who think that the whole book ought to be followed by the one who has made use of a part of the book, seem to me to admit the Apocryphal Book of Enoch." Augustine grants that Enoch "left some divine writings," on Jude's word, and judges the writings under his name "not genuine." The Stichometry of Patriarch Nicephorus (d. 828) lists Enoch among the apocrypha of the Old Testament. On this reading Jude's quotation gave the book honour as a source, and the churches' reading-lists kept it outside the canon.5,6

An apostolic apocryphon: apostolic tradition, not read publicly

On this reading 1 Enoch is "hidden" because it is not read in public worship, yet "apostolic" because it carries in writing traditions that formed part of the apostles' teaching.

Held by: Fr. Stephen De Young (Orthodox priest and biblical scholar, The Whole Counsel Blog, 2020), after Patriarch Nikephoros I (9th c.).7

De Young describes the book as "a collection of ancient apocalyptic traditions transmitted for some centuries orally, then as independent written documents, and finally as one collected text." He writes that "outside of Ethiopia and Eritrea, 1 Enoch is not canonical, it is not read publicly in the worship of the Church." He holds that these traditions "shaped major portions of the New Testament, such as St. Matthew's Gospel, the Johannine corpus, the Petrine epistles, and Jude." So it is "best seen, then, as St. Nikephoros I, Patriarch of Constantinople stated in the 9th century, as an apostolic apocryphon." It is apostolic, he writes, because it preserves traditions "which formed a part of the apostolic teaching, as attested by St. Irenaeus of Lyons and others." In the Lord of Spirits podcast he holds that the angelic reading the book carries was "the universal view of the first three centuries, almost four centuries, of the Church." On this reading the early Fathers who used Enoch drew on apostolic tradition, and the book is "a vital part of the tradition in which the Scriptures were passed on to the fathers."7,8
