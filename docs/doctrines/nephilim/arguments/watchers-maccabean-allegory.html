Is 1 Enoch's Watcher legend a Maccabean allegory modelled on the Greek Titanomachy?

Disputed reading · on The Sons of God and the Nephilim (Genesis 6) · witness: the Book of Enoch (1 Enoch)

Related readings: Does 2 Peter's "Tartarus" (tartaróō) borrow from the Greek Titanomachy?

The passage

And the angels, the children of the heaven, saw and lusted after them, and said to one another: 'Come, let us choose us wives from among the children of men and beget us children.' … And they were in all two hundred; who descended in the days of Jared on the summit of Mount Hermon … And they became pregnant, and they bare great giants, whose height was three thousand ells: Who consumed all the acquisitions of men.

And Azazel taught men to make swords, and knives, and shields, and breastplates, and made known to them the metals of the earth and the art of working them, and bracelets, and ornaments, and the use of antimony, and the beautifying of the eyelids, and all kinds of costly stones, and all colouring tinctures.

— 1 Enoch 6–7 and 8:1, the Book of the Watchers, tr. R. H. Charles, ccel.org.1

The question

The Book of the Watchers tells how angels took human wives, fathered devouring giants and taught forbidden arts, and the Greek texts that carry the story call the giants gígantes and name the fallen angels' prison Tartarus, the pit of the Titans in Greek myth. Interpreters differ over what stands behind the legend and its likeness to the Greek myths of Titans and Giants: a memory of the primeval events kept in the Greek legends, a mythic treatment of the wars or the priestly marriages of the early Hellenistic age, a Jewish reworking of Greek and older Near Eastern myth, or a coded allegory of the Maccabean crisis. The date of the Book of the Watchers bears on several of these readings; another part of 1 Enoch, the Animal Apocalypse (chs. 85–90, within the Book of Dream Visions, chs. 83–90), is dated to the late 160s BCE from its allusions to Judas Maccabeus.2

The Greek legends remember the giants before the Flood

On this reading the Greek stories of the wars of gods, Titans and Giants are later recollections, in distorted form, of the giants of Genesis 6.

Held by: Eusebius of Caesarea (d. c. 339), Preparation for the Gospel V.4.3

Eusebius takes up Plutarch's view that "the deeds of Giants and Titans celebrated in song among the Greeks are also stories about daemons," and sets beside it the Genesis account of the giants born to "the angels of God" and the daughters of men. He proposes "that these daemons are those giants, and that their spirits have been deified by the subsequent generations of men, and that their battles, and their quarrels among themselves, and their wars are the subjects of these legends that are told as of gods."3 On this reading the shape of the Greek story answers to the Hebrew one. In Hesiod the defeated Titans are sent beneath the earth and bound in Tartarus (Theogony 717–735), as the Watchers are kept "in everlasting chains under darkness" (Jude 6; 1 Enoch 10); a monstrous brood is destroyed in a divine war, as the Nephilim perish in the Flood. Greek-speaking Jews and Christians joined the two vocabularies. The Septuagint renders the Nephilim gígantes (Gen 6:4) and "the valley of Rephaim" as the valley of the Titans (2 Sam 5:18, 22); Judith 16:6 sets the sons of the Titans in parallel with lofty giants; Josephus writes that the angels' sons "did what resembled the acts of those whom the Grecians call Giants" (Antiquities 1.73); and Hippolytus warns of "fallen angels chained in Tartarus" (Refutation X).4 On this reading those writers named in Greek terms the same primeval events that the Greek poets had kept as tales of gods.

A mythic reflection of the wars of Alexander's successors

On this reading the giants' violence in 1 Enoch 6–11 reflects, in mythic form, the wars of the Diadochi, the generals who divided Alexander's empire.

Held by: George W. E. Nickelsburg (Journal of Biblical Literature, 1977; 1 Enoch 1, Hermeneia, 2001).5

Nickelsburg reads the giants' insatiable violence as a mythologized reflection of the wars of the Diadochi (c. 323–281 BC). The warlords who claimed divine descent appear as the monstrous offspring of the Watchers, and the arts the Watchers teach (metallurgy, weaponry, sorcery, astrology, cosmetics) as a jaundiced view of Hellenistic "civilization." He reads the critique as thematic, aimed at warfare, hubris and claims of divine birth in general.5 On this reading the dating fits that setting. Nickelsburg places the Shemihazah-and-giants core (chs. 6–11) around the end of the 4th century BC and the assembled Book of the Watchers in the 3rd. J. T. Milik dated the oldest Aramaic copy from Qumran Cave 4, 4QEna (4Q201), to the first half of the 2nd century BC, judged that "from the first half of the second century B.C. onwards the Book of Watchers had essentially the same form as that in which it is known through the Greek and Ethiopic versions," and placed a 3rd-century exemplar behind it. James VanderKam (4Q201 c. 200–150 BCE) and John J. Collins also date the composition to the 3rd century, and Seth L. Sanders (2022) argues for a date earlier still.6 On this reading the Watcher myth is the theodicy of a generation that had lived through the successor wars, telling their violence as the work of giants born of rebel heaven.

A polemic about priests and their marriages

On this reading the Watchers' unions with women are a coded polemic against priests who marry in breach of the rules of family purity.

Held by: David W. Suter ("Fallen Angel, Fallen Priest," Hebrew Union College Annual, 1979).7

Suter reads the Watchers' illicit unions in chapters 6–16 as a coded polemic against priestly intermarriage and the violation of endogamy and family-purity rules. The fallen angels stand for fallen priests, and their marriages for the marriages of the Jerusalem priesthood. The text frames the Watchers' sin in the language of holiness and defilement. In 1 Enoch 15 God asks them: "Wherefore have ye left the high, holy, and eternal heaven, and lain with women, and defiled yourselves with the daughters of men and taken to yourselves wives, and done like the children of earth, and begotten giants (as your) sons?" The charge continues: "though ye were holy, spiritual, living the eternal life, you have defiled yourselves with the blood of women."8 On this reading the legend addresses its own community: holy beings who defile themselves by marriage stand for a priesthood whose marriages, in the author's eyes, defile the holy order it serves.7

A Hellenistic Jewish reworking of Greek and older Near Eastern myth

On this reading Jewish writers of the Hellenistic age drew Titan and Prometheus motifs from Greek myth, and both they and the Greek poets inherited an older Near Eastern story of revolt in heaven.

Held by: Jan N. Bremmer ("Remember the Titans!", 2004); Brian R. Doak (The Last of the Rephaim, 2012); Michael S. Heiser (More Unseen Realm).9

Bremmer finds behind both the Greek myths and the Watcher legend a common Near Eastern tradition of revolt and succession in heaven, known from Atrahasis, the Ugaritic Baal Cycle and the Hittite Kumarbi cycle. He adds a Prometheus typology for the instructing angel: like Prometheus, Asael defies the chief god, gives humans forbidden arts and is punished. The Greek coloring of the Jewish story, its giants and its Tartarus, is on this reading a Hellenistic Jewish borrowing at the level of motif and vocabulary.9 The Greek side of the lineage is traced by Hans G. Güterbock (1948), Peter Walcot (1966), Walter Burkert (1992) and M. L. West (1997). West sets the successions Anu–Kumarbi–Teshub, Anu–Ea–Marduk and Ouranos–Kronos–Zeus side by side and calls the Hurro-Hittite storm-god's war on the older gods "evidently the model for Hesiod's Theogony."10 The Greek myth keeps its own genealogy for the combatants: Earth bears "the great Giants with gleaming armour" from the blood of Ouranos (Hesiod, Theogony 183–187), and in Apollodorus "Earth, vexed on account of the Titans, brought forth the giants, whom she had by Sky" (Bibliotheca 1.6.1).11 Doak finds the same direction of borrowing in the Septuagint, whose Giant and Titan vocabulary "can only, in effect, serve to make the Greek Giants and Titans part of the biblical story." Heiser, quoting Doak, judges it "likely that the translators of the Septuagint were influenced to varying degrees by Greek mythological Titan traditions" in rendering the Rephaim.9 On this reading Jews writing and translating in Greek gave their own giants the names of the Greek ones, over a shared Near Eastern inheritance.

A coded allegory of the Maccabean crisis

On this reading the Watcher legend is a Jewish "judaizing" of the Greek Titanomachy, in which the Watchers stand for the Greek invaders and the giants for the children of Greek–Jewish unions.

Held by: Taylor Marshall (Dr Taylor Marshall Podcast, ep. 968, 2023).12

In his 2023 podcast Marshall reads the Watcher-and-Nephilim legend of 1 Enoch as a coded allegory of the Hellenistic crisis in Judea, produced "in the Maccabean period" and "likely" a Jewish appropriation, a "judaizing," of the Greek Titanomachy, the war of Zeus and the Olympians on the Titans. He lays out a six-step historicizing map:

  1. Greek/Gentile invaders enter the Holy Land from 312 BC;
  2. the Greeks impose pagan customs on the Jews;
  3. Greek men marry and couple with Jewish women;
  4. producing "hybrid … Greek/Jewish children … considered monstrous" — the Nephilim;
  5. the need to drive the Greeks out through the Maccabean Revolt (167–160 BC);
  6. so the "titanomachy" is really Judah Maccabeus against the invading pagan Greeks — a Greek myth "judaized" — not Zeus-versus-Titans and not Yahweh-versus-Watchers.

On this reading the Watchers are the Hellenizers, the Nephilim their mixed-marriage offspring, and the Flood and the war the purging of Hellenism from the land.12 Marshall's reading of the word Tartarus in 1 Enoch and 2 Peter as a mark of the same borrowing is set out on the related page on 2 Peter's "Tartarus". On his reading the Jewish story takes the Greeks' own myth of a war against monstrous rebels and retells it as Israel's war against the Greeks, so that its giants carry a people's horror at foreign rule and mixed marriage.13
