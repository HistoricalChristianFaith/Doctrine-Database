Michael S. Heiser on the Nephilim

Dates: c. 2015 (b. 1963 – d. 2023) · Wikipedia
Summary: Michael S. Heiser (1963–2023), an Old-Testament and Semitic-languages scholar (PhD, University of Wisconsin–Madison), set out a "Divine Council" reading of the Bible and of the angelic interpretation of Genesis 6; Fr. Stephen De Young and Doug Wilson, also on this timeline, likewise read the sons of God as divine beings. In The Unseen Realm (2015) and Reversing Hermon (2017) he argues that the "sons of God" of Genesis 6 are divine beings (not the line of Seth), that the Nephilim are their giant offspring (the word, on his derivation, meaning "giants," from Aramaic naphila), that the Babylonian apkallu sages are the background of 1 Enoch's Watchers, and that the later "giant clans" (Rephaim, Anakim, Og of Bashan) are Nephilim of the same kind — produced, on his preferred solution to the post-Flood problem, by a second incursion rather than by a line that survived the Flood — so that, on his reading, the conquest of Canaan is in part a holy war to exterminate them. Like De Young he holds that the sons of God are real spirit beings; in The Unseen Realm he holds that reading ancient kingship into Genesis 6 is anachronistic. De Young's later sacral-kingship mechanism (c. 2020) is a kingship reading of Genesis 6.

Claims & evidence

The "sons of God" as divine beings

The Sethite view was incoherent before. Now it's become the position for ostriches.

— The Ancient Near Eastern Context for Genesis 6:1–4, drmsh.com. Heiser holds that the plural phrase bene elohim ("sons of God") denotes divine beings everywhere else in the Hebrew Bible (Job 1:6; 38:7; Ps 29:1; 89:6), so Genesis 6 is describing a transgression by spirit beings, not the intermarriage of two human lines; he judges the Sethite reading "deeply flawed" and without support in the text (The Unseen Realm, Lexham 2015, chs. 12–13).

The Babylonian apkallu are the background of the Watchers

This article deals a death blow to any non-supernatural (or non-mythic as some prefer) interpretation … Annus' article is the most current study on the Mesopotamian apkallu.

— The Ancient Near Eastern Context for Genesis 6:1–4, drmsh.com. Following Amar Annus's 2010 study "On the Origin of Watchers," Heiser derives the Watchers of 1 Enoch from the divine apkallu sages of Mesopotamia — antediluvian bringers of civilization, some of them evil, whose post-flood offspring were reckoned part-human and "two-thirds apkallu" — making the giant-begetting sons of God of Genesis 6 the Israelite counterpart of that tradition. De Young also draws on the apkallu background.

Heiser on "Nephilim" as "giants" (Aramaic naphila)

I can make a workable morphological argument for nephilim being from Aramaic naphila and meaning giants … the pointing for that meaning should be nephulim or nophelim – the latter which occurs in Ezekiel but not in Gen 6.

— The Naked Bible: Nephilim, drmsh.com. Heiser argues that if nephilim derived from the Hebrew verb naphal ("to fall"), the expected form would be nophelim or nephulim; the actual spelling with the long i (as at Numbers 13:33) he explains as a borrowing of the Aramaic noun naphila, "giant." The reading matches the Septuagint's gígantes and the Numbers 13:33 link between the Nephilim and the giant Anakim. (Other derivations are noted under Notes.)

The giant clans and the conquest

Heiser reads Numbers 13:32–33 (the giant Anakim called "descendants of the Nephilim") as evoking Genesis 6:1–4, and argues that the later giant peoples of the Transjordan — the Rephaim, the Anakim, the Emim, and above all Og of Bashan, "the last of the Rephaim" — carry the Nephilim pedigree forward past the Flood. On his reading, this is why certain conquest texts use ḥerem (kharam, "devote to destruction"): Israel is at war not merely with rival nations but with populations "spawned by rival divine beings," so coexistence was impossible and the ban fell on the giant-clan cities specifically (The Unseen Realm, chs. 23 "Giant Problems" and 25 "Holy War").

Heiser's own account of how the giant clans relate to the Flood is more specific. On his account no Nephilim survived the Flood at all: he canvasses the options — a regional flood, "the 'bad seed' … carried through the flood in Noah's bloodline," and "the behavior of Gen 6:1–4 happened after the flood as well" — and prefers the last, resting it on the ʾasher clause of Genesis 6:4, where reading "whenever the sons of God used to go in" rather than "when" "creates continuity in the sense that the behavior recurred." The conquest-era clans are, for him, the product of a fresh incursion rather than a surviving line.1

Elsewhere Heiser treats the Numbers 13:33 clause linking the Anakim to the Nephilim ("which come of the giants") as an exilic scribal gloss: "the scribe thinks that the word nephilim solves the problem of the giants being there, so he creates the link to Gen 6 via the gloss."2

Og's iron bed as an echo of Marduk's cultic bed at Babylon

its dimensions (9 x 4 cubits) are precisely those of the cultic bed in the ziggurat called Etemenanki—which is the ziggurat most archaeologists identify as the Tower of Babel referred to in the Bible.

— The Unseen Realm (Lexham, 2015), ch. 23; cf. the companion note crediting Maria Lindquist's peer-reviewed argument, moreunseenrealm.com. Heiser drew a link between Og's oversized "iron bed" (Deut 3:11) and the bed on which Marduk and his consort Zarpanitu were thought to meet at Babylon, tagging Og as an heir of Babylonian sacral kingship. He qualifies it: the bed's dimensions "cannot be taken as a precise indication of Og's own dimensions," and the Marduk/Amorite name-wordplay he floats he flags as "only speculation."3 The parallel is argued by Maria Lindquist, whom Heiser credits, and is also adduced by De Young.

"Reversing Hermon": the New Testament uses 1 Enoch and reads Genesis 6 supernaturally

what cannot be coherently denied is that Peter and Jude have divine beings as the offenders, not mere humans … It is obvious to those who study all these texts, especially in Greek, that Peter and Jude knew 1 Enoch very well.

— Reversing Hermon Footnotes, drmsh.com. Heiser's second book argues that Jesus's ministry consciously reverses the sin of the Watchers at Mount Hermon, and that the New Testament writers (Jude quoting 1 Enoch, 2 Peter's verb tartaróō — "cast into Tartarus," the classical destination of the Titans) presuppose the Enochic Watcher-and-giant story, which on his account forecloses an anti-supernatural reading of Genesis 6. Whether 2 Peter's "Tartarus" borrows from the Titanomachy is set out on the interpretation page Does 2 Peter's "Tartarus" (tartaróō) borrow from the Greek Titanomachy?

The book sets out an integrated sacred geography: Mount Hermon (the Watchers' oath, 1 Enoch 6) → Bashan (Og's kingdom, land of the Rephaim) → Caesarea Philippi at Hermon's foot (the grotto of Pan, where Jesus answers that "the gates of hell shall not prevail") → the Transfiguration, which Heiser places on Hermon — read by him as Jesus deliberately reclaiming the Watchers' mountain. Heiser connects the Watchers' oath to the Hermon name through the root ḥ-r-m, and reads Bashan as "serpent" country and Matthew's "gates of Hades" as an allusion to the Pan grotto. Other scholars read two of these links differently: G. del Olmo Lete distinguishes bāšān I, "fertile, stoneless ground," from bāšān II, "serpent," in discussing the place-name, and R. T. France and Craig Keener read "gates of Hades" as an idiom for death (cf. Isa 38:10; Wisdom 16:13) — France of "the imprisoning power of death," Keener of "a common ancient expression for the realm of the dead."4

Ancient kingship and Genesis 6

Heiser and De Young agree that the sons of God are spirit beings and share the apkallu background. In The Unseen Realm (2015) Heiser holds that reading ancient kingship into Genesis 6 is anachronistic: divine-sonship language for a king is singular in the ancient Near East, whereas Genesis 6's plural "sons of God" refers to divine beings, so "it is flawed hermeneutics to read either ancient kingship or the glorification of believers back into Genesis 6" (The Unseen Realm, ch. 12, "Divinized Human Rulers"). For Heiser the offenders are rebel divine beings, not human rulers. De Young's later explanation of the begetting (c. 2020), a pagan sacral-kingship fertility ritual in which a divinized king unites with a spirit-as-woman, is a kingship reading of Genesis 6.5

UFOs and the abduction phenomenon

Heiser's Genesis-6 work carries over into his writing on UFOs and the claim that alien encounters are demonic. In Aliens & Demons: Evidence of an Unseen Realm (2017, a book plus a 90-minute documentary) he engages the phenomenon at length, both arguing for an unseen realm and criticizing writers who link UFOs and the Nephilim. He distinguishes two questions: the open question of biological life elsewhere, and the narrower one of the anomalous abduction experience.

Biological aliens would not overturn the Bible; the abduction experience is the real concern

I don't think the question of an ET reality is a problem for Christian theology.

what passes for contactee messaging and alien abductions is, in my view, quite sinister, and demonization is one (strong) possibility in my mind for explaining those phenomena.

— "Aliens and Demons: New 90-Minute Documentary," drmsh.com. Heiser does not read every sighting or the mere possibility of extraterrestrial life as demonic. Genuine biological life elsewhere would be part of creation and no threat to the faith; only the anomalous abduction/contactee subset — with its deceptive "messaging" — does he read as a doorway to the unseen realm of angels and demons.6

"Real space aliens cannot be demons" — but interdimensional ones could

if there are real space aliens (beings that meet the above criteria), then they cannot be demons, since demons do not conform to these criteria in biblical theology.

If aliens are interdimensional, though, then things change. The demon equation goes back on the table.

— "The Christian View of Aliens, Part 4," Michael S. Heiser Foundation. The whole demonic reading, on Heiser's terms, turns on what the entities are. If they are embodied biological creatures crossing physical space, the biblical category "demon" does not fit them. It is only if the intelligences are interdimensional — moving between the seen and unseen realms rather than merely across the galaxy — that they could be the fallen spirits of Scripture. His argument thus turns on a "spirit or spacecraft?" question.7

The bridge to Genesis 6: demons as the disembodied spirits of the Nephilim

What lets Heiser connect abduction accounts to Genesis 6 at all is the Second-Temple identification he defends in Reversing Hermon: the "demons" of the Gospels are the disembodied spirits of the dead Nephilim. The earliest source for it is 1 Enoch 15, where the giants, being born of both flesh and spirit, become "evil spirits upon the earth" at their death — a reading found also at Qumran (11Q11 = 11QApocryphal Psalms, col. V). On Heiser's framework, the beings operating behind a deceptive contactee phenomenon could thus be the surviving spirits of the Watchers' offspring.8

Against the "ancient astronaut" theorists: von Däniken, Sitchin, and Ezekiel's "saucer"

Heiser is also a critic of the ancient-astronaut writers — Erich von Däniken and especially Zecharia Sitchin — whose claim that the Mesopotamian Anunnaki were flesh-and-blood extraterrestrials he treats as paleobabble: pseudo-scholarship built on manipulated texts.

what Zecharia Sitchin has written about Nibiru, the Anunnaki, the book of Genesis, the Nephilim, and a host of other things has absolutely no basis in the real data of the ancient world … Because I don't like ancient texts manipulated to promote false claims.

— sitchiniswrong.com, the site Heiser built specifically to answer Sitchin from the cuneiform sources. He gives Ezekiel's "wheel within a wheel" the same treatment: not a spacecraft but a divine throne-theophany whose imagery a sixth-century exile would already know from Near-Eastern royal iconography. The ancient-astronaut books, he argues, ignore the actual vocabulary of Ezekiel 1 — the throne is never described as disk-shaped or metallic; that has to be imported — and read a modern machine back into a text that explains its own cherubim.9 Heiser thus combines a supernatural reading of the abduction experience with criticism of ancient-astronaut and UFO-Nephilim claims.

Notes

  • Heiser and other modern proponents. Doug Wilson and De Young, also on this timeline, share with Heiser the supernatural identity of the sons of God, and De Young also adduces the Og-bed link and the apkallu background; they differ from him on the mechanism (Wilson's literal-embodiment account; De Young's ritual one).
  • Three rebellions, not one — the nations' gods are Babel, not Genesis 6. Heiser's larger architecture in The Unseen Realm spreads the supernatural revolt across three distinct events — Eden (the nachash, Gen 3), the sons of God (Gen 6:1–4), and Babel (Gen 11 read through Deut 32:8–9, his "Deuteronomy 32 worldview") — and it is at Babel, not in Genesis 6, that Yahweh disinherits the nations and hands them to the created rebel elohim who become "the gods of the nations." Those national gods are of the same kind as the Genesis-6 "sons of God" but a different group and a different rebellion: the Genesis-6 Watchers are bound to await judgment (2 Peter 2:4; Jude 6; 1 Enoch 10), whereas the nations' gods still rule and are arraigned in Psalm 82. (Heiser's divine-council reading, and the reading of Psalm 82's elohim as real divine beings rather than human judges, are traced across time on the Divine Council timeline.) On Heiser's scheme the nations' gods are real rebel elohim, distinct from the Genesis-6 beings; the identification of the pagan gods with the fallen angels and their giant offspring is made instead by ante-Nicene writers on this timeline such as Justin and Athenagoras.
  • Other derivations of nephilim. Heiser derives nephilim from Aramaic naphila ("giant"). A derivation from naphal in the sense "fallen ones" (those who fell, or who fell upon others) lies behind Jerome's and Bede's "fallen ones," and standard lexica list the word's origin as uncertain (⚠ unverified). Heiser's case rests on the vocalization and on the Numbers 13:33 giant link; the etymology bears on the meaning of "Nephilim" rather than on the identity of the sons of God.
  • His 2015 view that reading ancient kingship into Genesis 6 is anachronistic sets him apart from De Young's later (c. 2020) sacral-kingship mechanism, and, unlike De Young, he does not tie the tradition to a surviving cult; his emphasis falls on the textual and comparative-Semitic case for the supernatural reading.
  • "UFO religion." Heiser treated the modern belief in benevolent "space brothers" as a post-modern religion in its own right, not a science. The religious-studies scholar Diana Walsh Pasulka also studies UFO belief as an emerging religion (American Cosmic: UFOs, Religion, and Technology, Oxford, 2019). He reads the abduction subset supernaturally and treats the space-brother belief as a religion.
