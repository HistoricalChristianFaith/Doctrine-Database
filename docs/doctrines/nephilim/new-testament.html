The New Testament (Jude & 2 Peter) on the Sons of God

Dates: c. AD 60–90 (apostolic era) · verse pages: Jude 1:6 · Jude 1:14 · 2 Peter 2:4 Summary: Two short epistles — Jude and 2 Peter — refer to "the angels which kept not their first estate" being held "in everlasting chains under darkness unto the judgment," and Jude names Enoch, "the seventh from Adam," as a prophet, quoting 1 Enoch directly. Early Christian writers (Clement, Justin, Tertullian, Origen, Hippolytus) read these passages of the Genesis 6 / Watchers event, as Fr. Stephen De Young also does. This page collects that witness and the patristic reception surveyed here, and notes a disputed passage, 1 Peter 3:19, whose "spirits in prison" the patristic commentators surveyed here read as human, and De Young and a number of modern commentators read as the imprisoned Watchers.

Claims & evidence

Jude: the angels who left their estate, and Enoch as prophet

Two passages of Jude are cited for the angelic reading. First, the imprisoned angels:

And the angels which kept not their first estate, but left their own habitation, he hath reserved in everlasting chains under darkness unto the judgment of the great day. (Jude 6, KJV)

Second, Jude quotes 1 Enoch, naming Enoch as one who prophesied:

And Enoch also, the seventh from Adam, prophesied of these, saying, Behold, the Lord cometh with ten thousands of his saints, To execute judgment upon all… (Jude 14–15, KJV)

De Young uses the phrase "the seventh from Adam" for a Sumerian-King-List parallel (the seventh antediluvian king); see De Young's page.

2 Peter: angels that sinned, cast into Tartarus, beside the flood

2 Peter places the sinning angels immediately next to Noah and the flood, the sequence of Genesis 6:

For if God spared not the angels that sinned, but cast them down to hell, and delivered them into chains of darkness, to be reserved unto judgment; And spared not the old world, but saved Noah the eighth person… bringing in the flood upon the world of the ungodly. (2 Peter 2:4–5, KJV)

The verb behind "cast them down to hell" is tartaróō ("consigned to Tartarus") — a term De Young and Damick discuss. Whether its use here (where Jude 6 has only "chains and darkness") borrows from the Greek Titanomachy is set out on the interpretation page Does 2 Peter's "Tartarus" (tartaróō) borrow from the Greek Titanomachy? Hippolytus of Rome (c. 235) uses the imagery of "fallen angels chained in Tartarus as punishment for their sins."1

The earliest Fathers read Jude/2 Peter as the Genesis 6 angels

Ante-Nicene writers on this timeline read these epistles of the angelic/Watchers event:

  • Clement of Alexandria (c. 215), on Jude 6: "the angels who had obtained the superior rank, having sunk into pleasures, told to the women the secrets which had come to their knowledge."
  • Justin Martyr (c. 165), on Jude 6: the angels "were captivated by love of women, and begat children who are those that are called demons."
  • Tertullian (c. 220), on Jude 6: "those angels… who rushed from heaven on the daughters of men," who disclosed metallurgy, herb-lore, enchantments, and astrology.
  • Origen (c. 253), on Jude 6: the "sons of God" who "took for themselves wives" are among those who "left their own dwelling" and "did not preserve their own principality."
  • Tertullian and Clement, on Jude 14, treat Enoch as genuine prophecy — Tertullian arguing the book survived (or was restored after) the flood, "since Enoch… has preached likewise concerning the Lord… Enoch possesses a testimony in the Apostle Jude."

Each is quoted in full and sourced on that Father's own detail page; they are gathered here because their comments bear on Jude/2 Peter, which they read as teaching the angelic origin of the giants.

A disputed passage: 1 Peter 3:19's "spirits in prison"

De Young also enlists 1 Peter 3:19–20 ("he went and preached unto the spirits in prison; which sometime were disobedient… in the days of Noah") as part of the angelic chain, reading the spirits as the imprisoned Watchers. The patristic commentators surveyed here read the spirits as human — the dead to whom Christ descended (the Descensus), or the generation to whom Christ preached through Noah. Among modern commentators, William J. Dalton, Peter Davids and Thomas Schreiner read the spirits as fallen angels to whom the risen Christ proclaimed his victory or judgment;2 Richard Bauckham is also named as holding that reading (⚠ unverified).
