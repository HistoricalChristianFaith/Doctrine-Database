Margaret Barker on the Nephilim

Dates: c. 1988 (b. 1944) · margaretbarker.com
Summary: Margaret Barker (b. 1944), a British Methodist biblical scholar, past President of the Society for Old Testament Study (1998) and holder of a Lambeth Doctorate of Divinity (2008), is the founder of "Temple Theology." She treats 1 Enoch as a survival of the older, pre-exilic temple religion, and reads the "sons of God" of Genesis 6 as fallen angels, of whom Genesis preserves only a fragment: the "fuller version" of the creation-and-fall story is, for her, the fallen-angel story. She further identifies those fallen angels with the apostate priesthood ("the priests were the angels in the temple"). Taylor Marshall paraphrases her as reading the Nephilim symbolically (see Notes).

Claims & evidence

Genesis 6 preserves a fragment of a larger fallen-angel story

In Genesis we have one episode of a longer story. The human pair are tempted with knowledge, and the result is a corrupted world of dust, thorns, thistles and mortality. The fuller version is preserved in many other sources as the story of the fallen angels. Led by Azazel, 200 angels rebelled against the great Holy One and came to earth. They made a counter covenant, and revealed their angelic knowledge to the people on earth, having first seduced human women. The serpent tempting Eve is one element in the larger picture.

— Creation Theology (Wakefield, 2004), margaretbarker.com. Barker treats the descent of the Watchers as the fuller narrative — the pre-exilic myth of which the Eden story and Genesis 6 are surviving fragments — and holds that the biblical compilers truncated a fuller angelic account.

The "sons of God" of Genesis 6 are the fallen angels

The fallen angels were known as the sons of God. That is how they are described in Genesis 6, and the Book of Isaiah also begins with the sons of God: 'Sons have I reared and brought up, but they have rebelled against me.' These were the fallen angels.

— Creation Theology (2004), margaretbarker.com. This is an angelic identification of the sons of God, the reading of the earliest Fathers on the timeline, which she reaches through her reconstruction of temple angelology and its Enochic sources.

The fallen angels are the apostate priesthood

The priests, as we have seen, were the angels in the temple. Their role was to maintain the eternal covenant by their teaching, and then, if it was damaged by human sin, to renew it with their prayers and ritual.

— Creation Theology (2004), margaretbarker.com. Barker maps the "fall of the angels" onto the historical corruption of the temple establishment: reading 1 Enoch's history (chapter 93), she dates the "fall" to the 8th–7th centuries BC, when "the priests in the temple abandoned Wisdom and lost their spiritual sight," and calls the builders of the second temple the "impure apostates" of the Enoch tradition.1 On her reading the fallen "sons of God" and their corruption of the earth correspond to apostate priests in Israel's history.

The sons of God "became the fathers of the giants"

This is the account of the sons of God who fell from heaven and became the fathers of the giants. … A longer version of the tale appears in I Enoch, and this may be an expansion of the biblical fragment, or it may be the fuller form of the story from which the Genesis account was taken.

— The Great Angel (1992), from the opening chapter "The Sons of God," commenting on Genesis 6:2, 4.2 Here Barker ties the fallen sons of God to the giants of Genesis 6, and treats the Genesis notice as a "fragment" of a fuller angelic tale.

Notes

  • Taylor Marshall's paraphrase. In a 2023 podcast Taylor Marshall paraphrases Barker as reading the Nephilim symbolically, as a metaphor for the wickedness and violence of the pre-flood world. In the essays quoted on this page Barker does not call the fallen angels or their offspring a "symbol," "metaphor," or "figure"; she uses narrative language ("rebelled," "came to earth," "seduced human women") and holds that the angelic account was edited out of the canonical text. She also reads Genesis 3 and Genesis 6 as two fragments of a single fallen-angel myth about corrupting knowledge. See Marshall's page.
  • Barker is a modern witness to the angelic reading (alongside Heiser, De Young, and Wilson), with her own thesis: the fallen "sons of God" encode the corruption of the priesthood (the "fallen angel, fallen priest" reading also associated with D. Suter and G. Nickelsburg's work on 1 Enoch).
  • Her wider "Temple Theology" reconstruction has been taken up in Latter-day Saint scholarship (⚠ unverified).
