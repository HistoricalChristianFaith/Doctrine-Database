Fr. Stephen De Young on the Nephilim

Dates: 2018–2026 · The Whole Counsel Blog · Lord of Spirits podcast · Tucker Carlson interview Summary: Fr. Stephen De Young, an Orthodox priest, is a modern proponent of the supernaturalist reading of Genesis 6. He holds that the Nephilim are giants and the offspring of demonic fornication, draws on the Second Temple and Babylonian background (1 Enoch, the Book of the Giants, the apkallu, Gilgamesh), and reads Israel's conquest as a war against demonic powers — Og of Bashan, the Anakim, and Goliath being surviving Rephaim/Anakim. In the Lord of Spirits episode "Land of Giants" (co-hosted with Fr. Andrew Stephen Damick, c. 2020) he develops the mechanism of the Nephilim's begetting (a sacral-kingship fertility ritual, not literal angel–human breeding), the "demonization" theology behind the giants, and the New Testament and historical-reception case for the angelic reading. He frames the rival Sethite reading as a later Rabbinic-Jewish development that entered Christianity only at the end of the 4th century.

This page merges three sources by the same author: the 2018 blog essay "Here There Be Giants," the c. 2020 Lord of Spirits podcast "Land of Giants" (with Fr. Andrew Stephen Damick), and a 2026 long-form interview on the Tucker Carlson Show ("Young giants"). All three are firsthand and are cited per claim. The Tucker interview restates much of the earlier material for a general audience but adds several new strands (the Sumerian King List ↔ Enoch parallel, the nous and the "ladder" of demonization, the Ugaritic funerary cult of the Rephaim, Mastema and the 10% reprieve, Wisdom of Solomon on the ongoing cult, and a "covert-paganism" reading of the modern West); those are gathered under "From the Tucker Carlson interview (2026)" below.

Claims & evidence

The Nephilim as giants begotten by demonic fornication

The perspective, then, of the Biblical writers, as well as later Jewish and Christian interpreters, is that these so called 'gods' were in fact demonic spirits (Deut 32:16-17), the wisdom which they taught was actually depravity and corruption, and that these heroes were petty tyrants produced by demonic fornication.

— Here There Be Giants, The Whole Counsel Blog. De Young identifies the "gods" of Genesis 6 as demonic spirits and the heroic offspring as tyrants produced by demonic fornication.

The Second Temple and Babylonian background

Later Second Temple Jewish literature such as 1 Enoch and the Book of the Giants discovered among the Dead Sea Scrolls at Qumran preserve the ancient Babylonian traditions which formed the background for the genealogies and narratives of Genesis 4-6. In Babylonian tradition, there were a group of seven gods called the 'apkallu'. … The Book of the Giants from the Dead Sea Scrolls identifies Gilgamesh as one of the Nephilim. Genesis can therefore be seen to be interpreting what was, for its original hearers, the historical record of gods and kings through a very different theological lens.

— Here There Be Giants, The Whole Counsel Blog. He grounds the giant traditions in Mesopotamian sources (the apkallu, Gilgamesh as "2/3 apkallu") that Genesis reframes theologically.

The mechanism as a sacral-kingship ritual rather than angel–human breeding

the king, as the high priest of the religion, would ritually embody the god whom they were worshiping … And then would go in and ritually have intercourse with a temple slave or a temple prostitute on this bed. … If a child was conceived to said king out of this ritual, that child would essentially have two fathers, the king who was regarded as divine himself [and] the god, and then one human mother, the temple slave who actually gave birth to him.

— Lord of Spirits, "Land of Giants." De Young offers this as his answer to the objection that "demons can't interbreed with humans": the ritual involves two humans (the divinized king and the temple slave) and the worshiped spirit, so the offspring is reckoned "two-thirds divine" — as Gilgamesh is "two-thirds divine, one-third human." On De Young's reading the Sethite objection rejects a misunderstanding of what the tradition claimed. In Mesopotamian tradition the "two-thirds" formula is used of Gilgamesh ("two-thirds divine") and of the apkallu-descended sage Lu-nanna ("two-thirds apkallu").

The giants as "demonized" men, an inverse of theosis

To be a giant is to be one of these fully demonized people. It's like the opposite of a saint. It's the opposite of someone who's achieved theosis and become like Christ. It's someone who's become like a demon, with all of their lusts and fury and violence and destructive capacity and spite.

— Lord of Spirits, "Land of Giants." Just as the saint is divinized by imaging God, the giant is "demonized" by imaging the demonic father he is ritually bound to — an anti-theosis.

The unclean spirits of the Gospels as the spirits of the dead Nephilim

these unclean spirits, the spirits we see in the synoptic gospels, who are the subject of Christ's exorcism, are the spirits of these dead nephilim who have become demonic and demonized. 90% of them are in the abyss according to the book of Jubilees; the other 10% are still roaming around, and that's why they're unclean spirits, because they're a mixture.

— Lord of Spirits, "Land of Giants." De Young distinguishes "unclean spirits" (dead, demonized Nephilim) from the fallen angels proper — following 1 Enoch 15 and Jubilees.

The "giant clans" as ritual social units in De Young's reading of the conquest

this is going to be important when we get to talking about Joshua, which people today want to present as genocide. They say, "Oh, well, this is going and murdering people because of their race or ethnicity," and, no, there's not a concept of race or ethnicity. … this is the idea of wiping out these ritual and cultural practices. This is what has to stop.

— Lord of Spirits, "Land of Giants." On De Young's account membership in a giant clan (Amorites/Martu, Anakim, Rephaim, Emim, Horites, Avim) is constituted by participation in its rituals, not descent; so a clan is "eradicated" when its members are killed or assimilated into another community — which is why Deuteronomy's rules of war single these clans out (cf. Amalek, born of Eliphaz and Timna the Horite concubine, Gen 36).

The conquest as war against demonic powers

The text of Numbers, Deuteronomy, and Joshua do not describe a 'holy war' or genocide directed at a particular ethnicity of human beings, but of a war waged by the worshippers of Yahweh, the God of Israel, against his spiritual enemies, demonic powers that had come to dominate the region of Canaan and the Transjordan.

— Here There Be Giants, The Whole Counsel Blog. The targeted destruction in the conquest, on De Young's reading, falls precisely where the Anakim/giants dwell (Og of Bashan; Goliath of Gath, Joshua 11:22), framing it as spiritual warfare; David completes the gigantomachy.

The New Testament and early-Church witnesses De Young cites for the angelic reading

2 Peter 2:4-9 talks about angels who sinned and who are imprisoned in chains of gloomy darkness. That 'chains of gloomy darkness' phrase is essentially a phrase from 1 Enoch, talking about the imprisonment of the angels who created the nephilim.

— Lord of Spirits, "Land of Giants." De Young cites the Septuagint (Gen 6 "angels of God" / "giants"), 1 Peter 3:19–20, 2 Peter 2:4–9 (note tartaróō), and Jude 6–7 as witnesses, arguing the angelic reading "is the universal view of the first three centuries, almost four centuries, of the Church."

De Young on a rabbinic origin and late Christian entry of the Sethite reading

the Jewish community repudiated that [the Enochic literature] and came up with what's called the Sethite hypothesis sometimes in the literature, that it's the line of Seth and the line of Cain. This gets adopted by Church Fathers.

— Lord of Spirits, "Land of Giants." De Young dates the Christian Sethite reading to Julius Africanus first, then Ephrem and Augustine (late 4th/early 5th c.), and argues later Fathers rejected a misunderstanding (literal inter-species breeding) because, unlike the ante-Nicene Fathers, they no longer witnessed living pagan fertility cult — "it wasn't happening in the Provence" where John Cassian lived. (Other accounts of the reading's origin are given in the note below and set out on Did the Sethite reading originate as a rabbinic reaction against the Enochic reading?)

Technology and wisdom as "revealed" by spirits before humanity is ready

De Young develops the apkallu theme into a general principle. The post-flood Babylonian kings did not claim to possess texts that survived the flood:

they're not saying we have some texts or something that survived the flood; they're saying that they're in contact with spirits who were active before the flood … and it's the same spirits who are now revealing this wisdom to them.

The revealed "technology" is metallurgy, sorcery, divination, and the like — the same advances Genesis assigns to the line of Cain — and the mechanism is the demonic gift of knowledge given prematurely:

these demonic spirits that are antithetical to humanity bring knowledge to humans before they're ready for it … a time would come when humanity had reached a level of spiritual maturity when they could receive this knowledge … And that begins with the serpent in the garden.

He reads the Tree of the Knowledge of Good and Evil the same way — not an evil thing, but a maturity withheld until humanity was ready, which the serpent offers as a "shortcut." His modern analogy is the atomic bomb: knowledge revealed to man that "we use … to make a weapon," whose first use "wipe[d] out most of the Christian population of Japan" at Nagasaki. The strand extends his apkallu theme into a general principle — knowledge as a demonic gift given before humanity is ready, with the serpent and the bomb as its bookends.

Nagasaki, and its Urakami district in particular, was the historic centre and largest concentration of Japanese Christianity: of Japan's roughly 100,000 Catholics, some 50,000–60,000 were in the Nagasaki region, mostly descendants of the Kakure Kirishitan ("hidden Christians") who had survived centuries of persecution. The "Fat Man" bomb detonated about 500 m from Urakami Cathedral (then the largest church in East Asia), and the Nagasaki Atomic Bomb Museum estimates that of ~15,000 Christians in the area more than 10,000 were killed (other accounts give ~8,500 of the parish's 12,000).

The Sumerian King List and the antediluvian pattern of Genesis

De Young holds that the patriarchal ages are not naïve literalism but encode a pattern shared with Mesopotamian king-lists, partly through Babylonian base-60 mathematics:

if you look at those ages as sets of 60 there are … certain things encoded about the different kings and the different generations … nobody's totally cracked the code in Genesis.

His worked example ties Enoch to the seventh antediluvian Sumerian king (traditionally Enmeduranki of Sippar, associated with the sun-god and divination):

the seventh king in the [Sumerian King] list is the one who creates the solar calendar … And if you look at Genesis and the Epistle of Jude … he refers to Enoch as the seventh from Adam … in the genealogies of Seth in Genesis, he lives for 365 years. So you have … the solar-calendar year as his age. He's the seventh person. The seventh person in the Sumerian Kings list creates the solar calendar.

The seventh antediluvian king, traditionally Enmeduranki of Sippar, the sun-god's city, is remembered in Mesopotamian tradition as the founder of divination; in Genesis the 365 years, the length of the solar year, are Enoch's age.

The mind (nous) as receiving thoughts from outside

A theological premise behind the "demonization" claim. Following the Orthodox/Greek sense of nous, De Young treats the mind as a perceiving organ rather than a generator:

they thought about the mind more as a sensory organ like your eye … the eye of the heart or the eye of the mind … ideas are like sights or sounds or smells. They're not produced by the brain … they come from outside.

This grounds his account of how a man comes to "image" a spirit (good or demonic): thoughts are received and then entertained, not manufactured.

The "ladder" of sin: how a man becomes "demonized" (a giant)

To the "demonization / anti-theosis" theology of "Land of Giants" the interview adds an explicit ladder. A thought arrives (not yet sin); it is entertained, becomes a plan, then an action, then a habit — at which point it becomes a passion ("the sins as the passions because they make us passive"), then possession, and finally a free, deliberate agreement with the spirit:

the furthest you can go … is that spirit isn't in control of you anymore. You just agree with it. You're on board with that sort of demonic spirit deliberately … you're rejoicing and enjoying … the chaos and the destruction.

when you … have a person who we're calling a … nephilim, we're talking about … a fully demonized human.

A spirit as a collective consciousness above the individual

De Young offers a definition of "spirit" (used of both the Holy Spirit and demonic spirits): a level of consciousness above the individual that humans participate in and embody but do not create:

a spirit is a sort of collective consciousness at a level … above human … They exist separate and apart … we participate in them … but we don't create them. We sort of embody them in the world.

He illustrates it with the body's sub-organisms ("gut flora … having chemical conversations") summed under a higher human consciousness — and with mass evil "within living memory" that cannot be explained as a coincidence of individuals making the same bad choices.

Og, the Rephaim, and the Ugaritic funerary cult of the dead kings

The interview expands the blog's treatment. Og of Bashan is "the last of the Rephaim," and the Rephaim are illuminated by the mid-20th-century discovery of Ugarit (Ras Shamra), destroyed in the Bronze Age collapse c. 1200 BC:

one of the texts there is a ritual text for when the king died … they did this funerary ritual where they … did … ritual magic to try to ward off the Rephaim — who were the spirits of these dead ancient kings — so that the king who had just died could pass by them safely to get into the underworld.

This, he says, is what Isaiah 14 and Psalm 88 mean by the Rephaim. In the royal funerary liturgy KTU 1.161, the "document of the sacrifice of the shades," the rpʾum (Rephaim) and named dead kings are summoned, and the rite closes in a blessing on the living king and his house. He reaffirms the mechanism behind Og's gigantic iron bed (Deut 3:11):

we found a ritual bed of the same measurements in the great ziggurat of [Etemenanki] in Babylon … that ritual bed was used in these sexual rituals to produce the next king.

Maria Lindquist (Catholic Biblical Quarterly 73, 2011) connects the dimensions of Og's bed with the bed of Marduk, the bīt erši ("house of the bed") in the summit temple of Etemenanki, known from a cuneiform description of a furnished cultic bed and echoed by Herodotus's summit couch. She reads Deuteronomy 3:11 as an allusion to that bed that "likens Og to a god," reads the Rephaim/Nephilim as semidivine, and argues against the older reading of the bed as a sarcophagus on lexical, material and dimensional grounds. Laura Quick takes up her proposal and Ronald Hendel cites it. She shares the literal "iron bed" reading with Millard, Weinfeld (1991), Tigay (1996) and Block, whose commentaries do not make the Marduk connection. The akītu festival to which the bed belongs sets Marduk beside his son Nabû; in Mesopotamian royal ideology Šulgi is "born of Ninsun," and Psalm 2:7 has the king "begotten" by decree.

A related Ugaritic text is the Rapiu libation-prayer KTU 1.108, which hails Rapiu, King of Eternity, the divine eponym of the rpʾum (Rephaim), as enthroned at ʿṯtrt and hdrʿy. B. Margulis (1970), del Olmo Lete, Mark S. Smith and Lindquist identify these with Ashtaroth and Edrei (Deut 1:4; Josh 12:4), Og's two cities; the underworld-king Milku is seated at Ashtaroth in RS 24.244. De Young does not cite this text.

The unclean spirits, Mastema, and the 10% reprieve (Jubilees)

"Land of Giants" identifies the unclean spirits with the dead Nephilim (1 Enoch 15 / Jubilees); the interview names the spirits' leader and the theodicy of their reprieve:

at the time of the flood the Nephilim are wiped out … they have … a leader who's named Mastema … Mastema comes to God and wants to strike this bargain … "Let some of us stay on the earth. We promise … we'll only torment bad people" … God agrees to allow 10% of them to remain … but … God's motive is he wants to use that to bring wicked people to repentance.

He ties this to the demons' cry "Have you come to torment us before the time?" and the Legion-into- swine-into-the-abyss episode. Mastema's bargain and the 90% / 10% split are in Jubilees 10:8–11 (see the Book of Jubilees page).

Peoplehood as constituted by ritual rather than genetics, and the Wisdom of Solomon on the ongoing cult

This develops his account of the "giant clans" as ritual social units. Membership in a people, he says, is two things — "participating in their initiation rituals" and "the ongoing ritual life of the community" — paralleled across Israelite (circumcision + Passover), Christian (baptism + Eucharist), and pagan practice. On the pagan side he points to the "ongoing ritual life":

according to the book of wisdom — or the Wisdom of Solomon … makes this explicit. Other texts outside of the scriptures make this explicit, that the ongoing ritual life of these people involved human sacrifice, and human sacrifice involved cannibalism.

Wisdom 12:3–6 names the Canaanites' "wicked sacrifices," "merciless murderers of children," and "devourers of man's flesh, and the feasts of blood" (the last clause at 12:5); Wisdom 14:23 and 14:27 tie idolatry to child-sacrifice. See the Wisdom of Solomon page.

His example of malleable descent is Caleb the Kenizzite — by descent a member of a Canaanite/giant clan Israel was to wipe out, yet "listed as an elder of the tribe of Judah … because he was incorporated." He dates matrilineal Jewish descent ("son of a Jewish mother") to after the exile (Ezra–Nehemiah), as a later guarantee of genetics, not the original rule.

How tall were the giants? De Young on giant skeletons

As in the blog's opening reference to "doctored photos" of giant skeletons, De Young places the giants in the realm of ritual symbolism rather than literal 50-foot men. Standard depictions of 15 feet are "a deliberate coding" reflecting the gods' "ritual body":

in the ancient Near East, they believed that their gods had a ritual body … about 15 ft tall … excavated temples of Baal … [have] footprints … based on him being about 15 foot tall.

On Goliath he notes the textual spread — "according to the Greek Old Testament tradition, he was 6'6" … in the Hebrew text he's 9'9", which is more clearly a … supernatural … height" — against an average man of "like 5'5"" in the early Iron Age. And he denies that giant fossils have been verified:

there have never been found on Earth fossils of … [giant] human beings? Not verifiably … a million reports from the 19th century in North America … finding 9-foot-tall skeletons … all destroyed by the Smithsonian … not as far as we could verify … some … were proven to be … the Cardiff Giant … hoaxes.

He attributes the 19th-century "giant skeleton" lore to the anti-modernist impulse in nascent evangelicalism — "we need to scientifically prove that everything in the Bible is literally true." On his account the giants are demonized men and ritual kings, not crypto-archaeological fossils.

The ritual's continuation in De Young's account: Japan, the Epstein files, and "covert" paganism

De Young restates that the sacral-kingship ritual persisted into living memory — the Japanese emperor's accession rite, in which he "ritually and ceremonially spends the night with the sun goddess," its sexual element officially "removed after World War II," so "Hirohito would have been the last one." The Daijōsai secludes the emperor overnight with a bed-like god-seat (the shinza) to commune with the sun goddess Amaterasu. R. S. Ellwood (The Feast of Kingship, 1973) reads it as a hieros gamos; Blacker (1990) judges Ellwood's argument "speculative and unconvincing," and others read the rite as a communion meal (Holtom), an incubation and rebirth (Orikuchi Shinobu), or a welcome-and-communion rite (Okada Shoji). The emperor renounced his divinity in 1946; Akihito performed the Daijōsai in 1990 and Naruhito in 2019. Alongside Japan he adduces a Khmer parallel from the Lord of Spirits material — the king of Angkor who "spends the night" with a serpent-spirit. The thirteenth-century envoy Zhou Daguan (at Angkor 1296–97) reports that in a gold tower the Khmer king coupled nightly with a nine-headed nāga serpent-spirit, "the lord of the earth for the entire country," appearing as a woman, his life and the realm resting on the union; Zhou reports it as what "the local people all say" of a secret tower. A related foundation myth makes the Khmer royal line the offspring of a brahmin's marriage to the nāga king's daughter (the Kauṇḍinya–Somā myth), a descent claim made in Khmer inscriptions from 658 onward. He reads the Epstein files as a modern glimpse of the same complex — "a fixation on blood, on genetics … human sacrifice, sex … as a ritual." And he argues the secular West has not escaped paganism but driven it underground:

the way most people talk about the economy is essentially the way ancient people would talk about a god … we don't call it Mammon … [or] Ares … if you remove overt religion, you just get covert … religion … It's paganism. It's human sacrifice … whether it's abortion or war.

He distinguishes pagan religion (the gods remain free and may refuse) from magic (ritual that compels the spirit to do one's will) — and warns that "name it and claim it" prayer is functionally the latter. De Young offers the Epstein-files and "covert religion" material as instances of his thesis that the Nephilim pattern is ongoing.

UFOs and “aliens” as spiritual beings, not extraterrestrials (2022)

Apart from the “Land of Giants” and blog material, De Young — with his Lord of Spirits co-host Fr. Andrew Stephen Damick — has turned the same demonology on the modern UFO phenomenon. On the live call-in episode “Pantheon and Pandemonium II” (13 January 2022) a caller (Brian) asked directly how the Orthodox Church regards the possibility of intelligent extraterrestrial life, and whether Sasquatch is related to the giants, pre- or post-flood. Quotations below are transcribed from the episode recording; obvious verbal fillers are elided. Damick began by granting that the Church has issued no dogma on extraterrestrials as such —

the Orthodox Church, as the Orthodox Church, has not put forth any statements at all about the possibility of the way we would normally think of extra-terrestrial intelligent beings.

— but insisted that the biblical world already carries a category for non-human intelligent visitors, which the modern word “alien” merely relabels:

we're visited by beings that are not human but are intelligent… that's in the Bible… We understand that in terms of the engagement of mankind with spiritual beings such as angels and demons and dead people on some occasions. This is totally a thing.

The caution he presses is against auto-classifying an unknown intelligence as a biological alien — smuggling in, unnoticed, a whole science-fiction picture of what is actually being encountered:

if you encounter something that you don't know what it is and it seems intelligent, to immediately classify it in terms of the way that Star Trek encounters these alien life forms—that's making a whole big set of assumptions about what it is that someone is encountering.

And he rejects the claim that contact would upend Christian theology; on his reading the unseen-realm framework already has room for it:

When someone says, “Oh, if we really encounter this, it's just going to blow theology wide open” or whatever, I think that's garbage.

— Lord of Spirits, “Pantheon and Pandemonium II: Live Q&A January 2022” (Ancient Faith Radio; video on YouTube). On their reading, phenomena labelled “aliens” or UFOs belong to the biblical and patristic category of spiritual beings — the deceiving “gods of the nations” — rather than to literal visitors from other planets. De Young and Damick are Orthodox holders of the “UFOs are demonic” reading, which Protestant writers (Bates, Missler, Marzulli) also hold; they leave the bare existence of biological extraterrestrial life an open question while reading the abduction / contact phenomenon through the unseen realm.

  • De Young (an Orthodox priest) explicitly frames his reading as a recovery of the ancient context against the "de-mythologized" reading "begun by St. Augustine" — see the timeline and Augustine of Hippo's page.
  • De Young's is the modern supernaturalist reading: the sons of God are angelic/demonic beings and the Nephilim are literal giants/"demonized" men, set against the Sethite tradition of Augustine, Cyril, Philoxenus, Bede, and the Homilies on Genesis under Chrysostom's name.

Andrew Stephen Damick) supplies De Young's historical thesis about how the patristic disagreement arose; the specific reception claims he makes about individual Fathers (Irenaeus, Jerome, Chrysostom, Augustine, Julius Africanus, and George Syncellus) are recorded on those pages.
  • The standing of 1 Enoch. De Young's appeal to the Fathers treats 1 Enoch as authoritative in the early Church. Jude quotes it as prophecy, and Tertullian defends it as inspired Scripture while reporting that it "is not received by some"; Origen writes that Enoch's books do "not at all circulate in the Churches as divine," and Athanasius, Jerome and Augustine exclude it. The readings are set out on Was 1 Enoch received as Scripture in the early churches?
  • De Young on Augustine's case as "circular." In "Land of Giants" De Young charges that Augustine's rejection of the angelic reading in City of God XV.23 "is circular argument": having granted he cannot verify what in the book of Enoch is genuinely Enoch's, Augustine then brands the watchers story the spurious part — with, De Young says, no criterion beyond its being the disputed claim ("it's untrustworthy because it has stuff like this story in it, so you can't believe this story"). In XV.23 Augustine cites the giants-from-non-men content among the marks of a spurious book, and also gives as grounds the books' non-reception by the priestly custodians of the Hebrew canon, the Genesis text itself, and an angelology in which holy angels did not so fall. The readings are set out on Is Augustine's case against the angelic reading (City of God XV.23) circular?
  • De Young on Chrysostom and "demonization." In the Tucker interview De Young cites Chrysostom — "it is people who live like demons who become demons," against the folk belief that a violent death turns one into a demon — for the "demonization" theology, as "an actual metaphysical transformation of who you are," and for the reading that the dead Nephilim became the Gospels' unclean spirits. In the homily Chrysostom writes against the belief that the souls of the dead become demons: "not the souls of those who die a violent death become demons… not that their nature is changed, but that in their desires they imitate the evil nature of demons." Earlier witnesses on this timeline identify the giants' spirits with the demons: Justin, Athenagoras ("the souls of the giants… are the demons who wander about the world"), Commodian, and Lactantius, after 1 Enoch 15 and Jubilees 10.
  • De Young on the date and authorship of the Genesis homilies. In "Land of Giants" De Young dates the Sethite material in Chrysostom's Genesis homilies to c. 412–413, "post-St. Ephraim, post-St. Augustine," and reports that "St. Photius the Great… [thought] the later homilies weren't St. John Chrysostom, or at least reflect another hand." Quasten, Cayré, Altaner–Stuiber, Crépey (2009) and Hill (FOTC 74) place the 67 Homilies on Genesis (with the Sethite Homily 22) at Antioch, c. 388; Photius's Bibliotheca records a count of 61 homilies, which Hill, after De Montfaucon, takes as homilies lost from his copy; W. A. Markowicz (1963) set out the relation of the two Genesis series as "a problem." The readings are set out on Are the Sethite Homilies on Genesis Chrysostom's own?
  • De Young on the rabbinic origin of the Sethite reading. In "Land of Giants" De Young holds that "the Jewish community repudiated [the Enochic literature] and came up with what's called the Sethite hypothesis… that it's the line of Seth and the line of Cain. This gets adopted by Church Fathers." Tertullian reports that the Jews rejected Enoch "just like all the other portions… which tell of Christ." P. S. Alexander (1972) dates the angelic reading as effectively unanimous until the mid-second century AD, when Jewish exegesis exchanged it for a human one; Genesis Rabbah 26:5 records that R. Shimon ben Yoḥai called them "the children of judges" and cursed anyone who would call them "children of God," and the Targumim render "sons of the great ones / nobles" or "sons of the judges." The earliest extant Christian statement of the Sethite reading is Julius Africanus's. Annette Yoshiko Reed reads the rabbinic and Christian turns away from the Enochic tradition as running on separate courses.
