The Septuagint (LXX) on the Nephilim

Dates: c. 250 BC Summary: The Septuagint — the Greek translation of the Torah made in Alexandria, traditionally c. 250 BC — is the earliest extant translation of Genesis 6 and the version the New Testament authors quote. It renders the Nephilim as gígantes ("giants"), the origin of the English word, and in a number of copies renders the sons of God as "angels of God" — so in those copies the passage reads as angelic beings and giants. Fr. Stephen De Young calls the LXX the "first canonical witness" against the later Sethite reading.

Claims & evidence

The translators read "angels of God" and rendered the Nephilim "giants"

when they translated this passage, they translated 'the sons of God' as 'the angels of God,' and they translated 'nephilim' as 'giants.'

— Fr. Stephen De Young & Fr. Andrew Stephen Damick describing the LXX of Genesis 6:1–4, Lord of Spirits, "Land of Giants." The Greek gígantes stands at Gen 6:4; the "angels of God" rendering at Gen 6:2 is a manuscript reading (e.g. Codex Alexandrinus), the variant noted below by Augustine.

Augustine on the Septuagint's text of Genesis 6:2

The Septuagint indeed calls them both angels of God and sons of God, though all the copies do not show this, some having only the name sons of God.

— Augustine, The City of God XV.23, historicalchristian.faith. Augustine, who himself rejects the angelic reading, reports that copies of the Septuagint in his day carried the "angels of God" rendering, while others had only "sons of God." See Augustine on the Nephilim.

Notes

  • The LXX is a text, not a person, but it is included on the timeline (like 1 Enoch and Jubilees) because it is a datable, citable interpretive witness — and the one the New Testament writers used.
  • The "angels of God" reading at Gen 6:2 is reported by Augustine (above). The verses can be read at Genesis 6:2 and Genesis 6:4.
