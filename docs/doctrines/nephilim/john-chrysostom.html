John Chrysostom on the Nephilim

Dates: c. 407 Summary: In Homily 22 of the Homilies on Genesis transmitted under his name, Chrysostom argues for the Sethite reading and against the angelic one. He calls the angelic interpretation a "blasphemy" and "the height of absurdity," arguing that angels are nowhere in Scripture called sons of God, and that incorporeal beings cannot marry (citing Christ's word that at the resurrection they "neither marry nor are given in marriage"). He identifies the "sons of God" as the godly descendants of Seth through Enosh, and the "sons of men" as the line of Cain.

Claims & evidence

Homily 22: whom Scripture calls "sons of God"

You see, there is need to make a careful study of this passage and confute the fanciful interpretations of those people whose every remark is made rashly… so that you will not lend your ears idly to people uttering those blasphemies… I mean, they claim that this remark is made not about human being but about angels; these (they say) he called sons of God. Let them demonstrate firstly where angels are called sons of God; they would not, however, be able to show this anywhere. While human beings are called sons of God, angels are nowhere so called.

— Homily 22 on Genesis, books.google.com. Chrysostom holds that Scripture calls human beings, and not angels, "sons of God."

Homily 22: the incorporeality of angels and Matthew 22:30

Is it not a particular hallmark of folly to claim that angels descended to have intercourse with women, and that incorporeal nature of theirs was reduced to association with corporeal creatures? Or do you not hear the words of Christ about the being of angels, "At the resurrection they neither marry nor are given in marriage, but are like angels of God"? After all, it is not for that kind of incorporeal creature to ever feel the onset of desire. In response to these people we have to reach the same conclusion, that to admit this notion into one's mind is the height of absurdity.

— Homily 22 on Genesis, books.google.com. He appeals to the incorporeality of angels and to Matthew 22:30 against the idea of angels' bodily intercourse with women.

Homily 22: the sons of God as the descendants of Seth and Enosh

So since these people took their origin from Seth and from his son named Enosh… those descended from him in the future were called sons of God by Sacred Scripture for the reason of their imitation of the virtue of their ancestors up to his time. On the other hand, he gave the name sons of men to those born after Seth, the descendants of Cain and those taking their descent from him.

— Homily 22 on Genesis, books.google.com. The "sons of God" are the virtuous Sethite line; the "sons of men" are the Cainites.

On Lazarus: the souls of the dead and the demons

For it is a fact that many of the less instructed think that the souls of those who die a violent death become wandering spirits, (demons.) … But this is not so. I repeat it is not so. For not the souls of those who die a violent death become demons, but rather the souls of those who live in sin; not that their nature is changed, but that in their desires they imitate the evil nature of demons.

— On Lazarus, Discourse 2 (Four Discourses on the Rich Man and Lazarus), historicalchristian.faith. Chrysostom rejects a popular belief that the violently killed turn into demons; on his account one becomes demonic not by manner of death but by imitating the demons' evil will in a life of sin — a change of desire, not of nature.

The same point in the Matthew homilies

But what can be the reason that they love also to dwell in the tombs? They would fain suggest to the multitude a pernicious opinion, as though the souls of the dead become demons, which God forbid we should ever admit into our conception.

— Homily 28 on Matthew §3, historicalchristian.faith. Here the opinion is said to be spread by the demons themselves to win credence; Chrysostom rejects the idea that the dead become demons.

Notes

  • Homily 22 frames the angelic reading as "blasphemies" as well as folly.
  • The Sethite case in Homily 22 rests on lexical (who Scripture calls "sons of God"), christological (angels neither marry nor desire), and genealogical (Seth/Enosh vs. Cain) arguments.
  • His appeal to Matthew 22:30 — angels "neither marry," so they could not have fathered the giants — is answered by Doug Wilson, who reads the saying as describing what the angels "in heaven" do not do.
  • De Young on the Lazarus homily. Independently of the Genesis homilies, Chrysostom teaches that one becomes demonic not by manner of death but by imitating the demons in a life of sin — "not that their nature is changed, but that in their desires they imitate the evil nature of demons" — citing Christ's "you are of your father the devil" (John 8:44) as a sonship of imitation. Fr. Stephen De Young adduces this homily (Tucker Carlson Show, "Young giants," 2026) for his "demonization / anti-theosis" theology and, by extension, the reading that the post-flood "unclean spirits" are the spirits of the dead Nephilim. The homily denies that the souls of the dead become demons and locates the change in the will, "not… of nature"; and in Homily 22 on Genesis Chrysostom makes the giants the offspring of two human lines. The identification of demons with the spirits of the dead giants appears on this timeline in Justin, Athenagoras, Commodian and Lactantius. (De Young assigns the sermon to Constantinople; the Lazarus discourses and the Matthew homilies are usually placed in Chrysostom's Antioch years, c. 388–390, ⚠ unverified.)
  • Authorship and date of the Genesis homilies. In Lord of Spirits ("Land of Giants") De Young questions counting Chrysostom a firm Sethite witness: he dates the Sethite material to c. 412–413 (after Ephrem and Augustine) and reports that St. Photius the Great's library notes question whether the relevant homilies are Chrysostom's own or "another hand." Quasten, Cayré, Altaner/Stuiber, Crépey (REAug 2009) and R. C. Hill (FOTC 74) place the 67 Homilies on Genesis, which carry the Sethite Homily 22, at Antioch, c. 385–388; Chrysostom died in 407. Photius's Bibliotheca gives a count of 61 homilies rather than 67, which Hill, following De Montfaucon, explains as homilies lost from Photius's copy. Chrysostom left two overlapping Genesis series whose relation W. A. Markowicz set out as a problem (Theological Studies 24 [1963]), and a post-401 literary recension of the written text has been discussed.
