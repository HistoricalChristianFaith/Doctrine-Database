Clement of Alexandria on the death of James

Dates: c. 200 (Clement's Hypotyposes; the words survive only as quoted by Eusebius, c. 325) · Wikipedia
Summary: The earliest extant tradition about James's death beyond the notice in Acts. Clement, in the seventh book of his lost Hypotyposes, told — as received “from those who had lived before him” — that the man who brought James to the judgment-seat was converted by his testimony, confessed himself a Christian, and was beheaded together with him; James, asked for forgiveness on the way, answered “Peace be with thee” and kissed him. Eusebius preserves the passage in Book 2 of his Church History.

Claims & evidence

The accuser converted, and beheaded with James

Eusebius, recounting the death of James under Herod Agrippa, pauses to relay a story he found in Clement. It is the earliest extant expansion of the martyrdom, and Eusebius presents it as a tradition Clement received: “Clement, in the seventh book of his Hypotyposes, relates a story which is worthy of mention; telling it as he received it from those who had lived before him.”

He says that the one who led James to the judgment seat, when he saw him bearing his testimony, was moved, and confessed that he was himself also a Christian. They were both therefore, he says, led away together; and on the way he begged James to forgive him. And he, after considering a little, said, “Peace be with thee,” and kissed him. And thus they were both beheaded at the same time.

— Clement of Alexandria, Hypotyposes Book 7, quoted in Eusebius, Ecclesiastical History 2.9.

Notes

  • The source. Clement (c. 150–215) wrote the Hypotyposes, which is lost, so the story reaches us only through Eusebius; Clement frames it as a tradition handed down rather than as first-hand knowledge, and no other early source for it is known. How it has been read is set out on Was James's accuser converted and beheaded with him?
  • The name Josias. Clement gives the accuser no name; the later Golden Legend calls him Josias and weaves him into a longer story of James in Spain and a magician named Hermogenes. The name and the longer story appear in the medieval text, not in Clement's notice.
