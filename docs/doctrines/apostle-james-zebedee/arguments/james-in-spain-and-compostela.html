Did James preach in Spain, and do his relics lie at Compostela?

Disputed reading · on James, son of Zebedee · witness: the Latin apostle-lists, the Breviarium Apostolorum, and the Compostela tradition

Related readings: Was James's accuser converted and beheaded with him?

The passage

Now about that time Herod the king stretched forth his hands to vex certain of the church. And he killed James the brother of John with the sword.

— Acts 12:1–2.

According to this tradition St. James the Greater, having preached Christianity in Spain, returned to Judea and was put to death by order of Herod; his body was miraculously translated to Iria Flavia in the northwest of Spain, and later to Compostela, which town, especially during the Middle Ages, became one of the most famous places of pilgrimage in the world.

— Achille Camerlynck, summarizing the Compostelan tradition, “St. James the Greater,” Catholic Encyclopedia (1910).1

The question

Acts records James's death at Jerusalem under Herod Agrippa I, about 44, and no first-century text names a mission field for him. From the seventh century Latin apostle-lists send him to Spain, and from the ninth a tomb in Galicia was venerated as his, the origin of the shrine and pilgrimage of Santiago de Compostela. Two linked questions are disputed: whether James preached in Spain, and whether the relics at Compostela are his.2

A Spanish mission, and the apostle's tomb at Compostela

In this reading James preached in Spain before returning to Jerusalem to die, and his body was carried back to Galicia, where it lies at Compostela.

Held by: the Breviarium Apostolorum (seventh century); Jacobus de Voragine (Golden Legend, c. 1260); the Bollandists (Acta Sanctorum, July) ⚠ unverified; Leo XIII (bull Omnipotens Deus, 1884) ⚠ unverified.3

From the seventh-century Latin apostle-lists onward the sources that name a field for James name Spain, and by about 700, Camerlynck reports, the tradition that he founded an apostolic see there was current. From the ninth century Compostela was venerated as the site of his grave, and around it grew a road, an architecture, a liturgy, a military order founded in the twelfth century, and a national patron, Santiago; the vow of dispensation from a vow of pilgrimage to the shrine was reserved to the pope. The Golden Legend joins the strands in one life: James preaching in Spain, his return to Judea, his death, and the voyage of his body to Galicia. No early source names a field for James, and the Spanish tradition supplies one. The Bollandists defended the tradition in the Acta Sanctorum, and Leo XIII's bull of 1 November 1884 is counted by Camerlynck “a strong argument in favour of the authenticity of the sacred relics of Compostela.” On this reading a veneration so long, so widely shared, and so solemnly confirmed preserves the memory of the apostle's labours in the West and of his resting-place.4

The body brought to Compostela, with or without a Spanish mission

In this reading the question of the relics stands apart from the question of the mission: James's body may rest at Compostela even if he never preached in Spain.

Held by: Notker of St Gall (Martyrology, ninth century), as reported by Camerlynck; Achille Camerlynck (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1910).5

Camerlynck sets out the difficulties raised against the preaching and then separates the two claims: “Even if St. James the Greater did not preach the Christian religion in Spain, his body may have been brought to Compostela, and this was already the opinion of Notker.” The oldest witness to a tomb in Spain speaks of a translation, not a mission: the Martyrology of Usuard of Saint-Germain-des-Prés, completed about 865, says the apostle's remains were translated from Jerusalem to Spain and “deposited in its uttermost region.” Camerlynck notes a rival tradition that the relics lie in the church of St-Saturnin at Toulouse, and answers that “it is not improbable that such sacred relics should have been divided between two churches.” He counts Leo XIII's bull Omnipotens Deus (1884) a strong argument for the authenticity of the relics. On this reading the relics are a question of their own, carried by the ninth-century witness to the tomb and the later confirmation of the cult, whatever is decided about a mission.6

A tradition formed between the seventh and ninth centuries

In this reading the Spanish mission and the Compostela tomb are later traditions, formed in the Latin West long after James's death at Jerusalem.

Held by: Louis Duchesne (“Saint Jacques en Galice,” 1900) ⚠ unverified; R. A. Fletcher (Saint James's Catapult, 1984).7

Fletcher, following Duchesne, finds “no sign whatsoever” that anyone in the first six centuries believed James had preached or been buried in Spain. The early division of mission fields that Eusebius reports from Origen names fields for Thomas, Andrew, John, Peter and Paul and none for James. Camerlynck lists the difficulties others have raised: by the tradition of the early Church, James had not yet left Jerusalem when he was killed (Clement, Stromata VI; Apollonius), and Paul, writing to the Romans about 57–58, plans to go to Spain on the principle of not building “upon another man's foundation.” Fletcher dates the Breviarium Apostolorum to the seventh century and describes it as a Latin translation and amplification of a Greek list, whose editor “brought Philip to Gaul and James to Spain”; it has James preach in Spain but not be buried there, as does the passage on James interpolated, before about 750, into the De ortu et obitu patrum ascribed to Isidore. An Asturian hymn of 783–8 honours James without naming a burial place. The tomb appears in the record in the first half of the ninth century, under Alfonso II and Bishop Theodemir of Iria, in accounts that survive from the late eleventh and early twelfth centuries; the story of disciples carrying the body by sea to Padrón Fletcher calls “in the highest degree unlikely.” On the identity of the remains he holds that “we cannot establish certainties” and reports Henry Chadwick's cautious suggestion of Priscillian of Avila. On this reading the cult is a history of what Christians of the ninth century and after came to believe about James.8
