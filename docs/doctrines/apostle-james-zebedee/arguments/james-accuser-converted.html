Was James's accuser converted and beheaded with him?

Disputed reading · on James, son of Zebedee · witness: Clement of Alexandria

Related readings: Did James preach in Spain, and do his relics lie at Compostela?

The passage

He says that the one who led James to the judgment seat, when he saw him bearing his testimony, was moved, and confessed that he was himself also a Christian. They were both therefore, he says, led away together; and on the way he begged James to forgive him. And he, after considering a little, said, “Peace be with thee,” and kissed him. And thus they were both beheaded at the same time.

— Clement of Alexandria, Hypotyposes Book 7, in Eusebius, Ecclesiastical History 2.9.1

The question

Acts 12:2 records only that Herod Agrippa I “killed James the brother of John with the sword,” about the year 44. Clement, writing about 200 in a work now lost, adds that the man who brought James to judgment was converted, asked his forgiveness, and was beheaded with him; Eusebius quotes the story as one Clement told “as he received it from those who had lived before him,” and no other early source for it is known. The question is whether the story preserves a memory of how James died or took shape as an edifying account of a martyr's death.2

A tradition handed down from those before Clement

In this reading the conversion and joint beheading of James's accuser is a memory of the martyrdom passed down to Clement by earlier Christians.

Held by: Clement of Alexandria (Hypotyposes VII, c. 200); Eusebius of Caesarea (Ecclesiastical History 2.9, c. 325); Jacobus de Voragine (Golden Legend, c. 1260).3

Clement wrote around the year 200, within reach of the sub-apostolic age, and he presents the story not as his own conjecture but as one received from those who had lived before him, a chain of witnesses running back toward the event. Eusebius, who gathered the early notices of the apostles for his history, set it beside the words of Acts as “a story which is worthy of mention.” The story fastens onto the martyrdom that Acts records, the beheading of James under Herod about 44, and adds nothing chronologically or geographically out of place: a Jerusalem trial, a man of the court who led the prisoner, and an execution by the sword. It fits a period in which the composure of the martyrs was widely reported to have moved bystanders and officials to confess the faith, and the word of forgiveness and the kiss of peace are the gestures the early churches prized. The account is spare: it names no one but James and claims no wonder. Later tradition kept it; the Golden Legend names the accuser Josias and sets the conversion on the road to execution.4

An edifying account in the form of a martyr story

In this reading the story was told to draw out the meaning of James's death and is a received tale rather than a report of what happened at the execution.

Held by: ⚠ unverified — no named proponent located yet.

The story survives in a single sentence of a lost work, quoted by one later author, and Clement offers it as something received rather than known. Its shape is one that recurs in the literature of the martyrs: the hostile agent turned by the witness of the condemned, the plea and the grant of forgiveness, and two deaths together. The later course of the tradition fits this: the Golden Legend gives the accuser a name, Josias, and sets him within a longer story of James in Spain and the magician Hermogenes, the kind of growth that a brief edifying account invites. On this reading the story's value lies in what it shows of how Christians around 200 remembered James — as a martyr whose death reconciled and converted — and of how the earliest expansions of an apostolic death were formed.5
