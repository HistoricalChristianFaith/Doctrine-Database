Theotokos (Mary as Mother of God)

Theotokos (Θεοτόκος, “God-bearer” or “Mother of God”) is a title given to Mary. This timeline lists, earliest extant attestation first, the writers, councils and documents that used, defended, qualified or questioned it, from the Christological controversies of the fourth and fifth centuries to a 1994 ecumenical declaration.

The title was at the centre of the Nestorian controversy. Nestorius, patriarch of Constantinople, ranked it below Christotokos (“Christ-bearer”), writing that he did not oppose it “unless it should advance to the confusion of natures”; Cyril of Alexandria made confession of it the first of his Twelve Anathematisms. The Council of Ephesus (431) deposed Nestorius, and the Council of Chalcedon (451) confessed Mary “the Mother of God according to his manhood.” Whether the title is chiefly a confession about Christ, a title of honour for Mary, or both, is itself among the disputed readings listed below. Mary’s perpetual virginity and her sinlessness are separate questions, traced on their own timelines.

Related doctrines: The Virgin Mary — see also the perpetual virginity of Mary · Mary as the New Eve · the Immaculate Conception · the Assumption / Dormition · the Tomb of the Virgin

Timeline

Tertullian (c. 210)

Tertullian, arguing against Marcion that Christ took real flesh, reads Isaiah’s sign of the virgin conceiving as fulfilled in “Emmanuel, God with us”: “a virgin did conceive and bear ‘Emmanuel, God with us.’ This is the new nativity… in this man God was born, taking the flesh of an ancient race.”1 The word Theotokos does not occur in the passage; whether it was in use before the fourth century is a disputed reading.

Alexander of Alexandria (c. 324)

Alexander of Alexandria, in a letter against Arius preserved by Theodoret, writes that Christ “in very deed, and not in appearance merely, carried a body, of Mary Mother of God,” in a passage arguing that the Son suffered no “detriment to His divinity.” It is the earliest literary use of the word whose attribution is not disputed in the sources surveyed here.2

Athanasius of Alexandria (c. 340)

Athanasius of Alexandria, Alexander’s deacon and successor, uses the title in his discourses against the Arians. Setting out Scripture’s “double account of the Saviour” — that he “was ever God… and that afterwards for us He took flesh of a Virgin, Mary Bearer of God, and was made man” — he renders Theotokos “Bearer of God” in this translation.3

Ambrose of Milan (c. 380)

Ambrose of Milan, writing in Latin against the Arians, expounds Paul’s “God sent His Son, made of a woman” (Galatians 4:4): the one born of Mary is the Father’s own Son, of whose “nature… His generation is eternal,” and the making is to be understood “not of the Godhead, but of the putting on of a body.”4 He does not use the Greek word here.

Gregory of Nazianzus (c. 382)

Gregory of Nazianzus, in Letter 101 to Cledonius, against Apollinarius, writes: “If anyone does not believe that Holy Mary is the Mother of God, he is severed from the Godhead.”5 He joins it to the statement that the Son was “at once divinely and humanly formed in her,” not passed through her “as through a channel.”

Nestorius (c. 429)

Nestorius, patriarch of Constantinople, ranked Theotokos below Christotokos (“Christ-bearer”): “I am not opposed to those who want to say it… Nonetheless, I have no doubt that the term Theotokos is inferior to the term Christotokos, as the latter is mentioned by the angels and the gospels.”6 Cyril and his supporters read that ranking as a division of the one Christ into two sons;7 whether Nestorius held that Christology is a disputed reading.

Cyril of Alexandria (c. 430)

Cyril of Alexandria grounds the title in the Incarnation: “since the holy Virgin brought forth corporally God made one with flesh according to nature, for this reason we also call her Mother of God, not as if the nature of the Word had the beginning of its existence from the flesh.”8 He made confession of it the first of his Twelve Anathematisms: whoever will not call the Virgin “Mother of God (Theotokos), inasmuch as in the flesh she bore the Word of God made flesh… let him be anathema.”9

Proclus of Constantinople (430)

Proclus of Constantinople preached a feast of the Virgin in the capital’s great church — with Nestorius present, according to the title the homily carries — in his Homily 1, On the Holy Virgin Theotokos: “The Virgin’s festival, my brethren, summons us today to words of praise.”10

The Council of Ephesus (431)

The Council of Ephesus deposed Nestorius, resolving “with many tears… that Nestorius be excluded from the episcopal dignity, and from all priestly communion.”11 At its first session the bishops declared Cyril’s Second Letter to Nestorius in accord with the creed of Nicaea, and Cyril’s letter carrying the Twelve Anathematisms was read into the acts.12

Theodoret of Cyrus (c. 431)

Theodoret of Cyrus, at John of Antioch’s request, wrote a point-by-point Reproof of Cyril’s Twelve Anathemas, objecting to Cyril’s language of a “natural union,” and in it confessed the Virgin Theotokos on his own terms: “we style that holy Virgin θεοτόκος, not because she gave birth in natural manner to God, but to man united to the God that had fashioned Him.”13 He is credited with drafting the creed of the Formula of Reunion (433),14 and at Chalcedon (451), under the bishops’ shouts, anathematized Nestorius by name before being restored to his see.15

The Formula of Reunion (433)

The Formula of Reunion ended the mutual excommunication between Cyril and the Antiochene bishops under John of Antioch that followed Ephesus, when Cyril accepted an Antiochene creed in his letter Laetentur caeli (“Let the heavens rejoice”). The creed confesses the title: “we confess the holy Virgin to be Mother of God; because God the Word was incarnate and became Man, and from this conception he united the temple taken from her with himself.”16 It also speaks of “a union of two natures” and confesses “one Christ, one Son, one Lord.”

Leo the Great (c. 449)

Leo the Great, bishop of Rome, calls Mary “the Mother of God” in his Christmas preaching: she “does not believe it loss of honour that she is soon to be the Mother of God,” for what is wrought in her is “of the Holy Ghost.”17 His Tome to Flavian (449) states that “in consequence of this unity of person which is to be understood in both natures… the Son of God took flesh from the Virgin who bore Him,” and that the Son of God suffered “not… in His Divinity… but in His weak human nature.”18

The Council of Chalcedon (451)

The Council of Chalcedon confesses in its Definition the one Christ “begotten of his Father before the worlds according to his Godhead; but in these last days… born… of the Virgin Mary, the Mother of God according to his manhood.” The Definition receives Cyril’s “synodical letters… addressed to Nestorius and the Easterns” and Leo’s letter to Flavian.19 At the council’s second session, after Leo’s letter was read, the bishops cried out: “Peter has spoken thus through Leo… Leo and Cyril taught the same thing.”20

John of Damascus (c. 730)

John of Damascus, in his Exact Exposition of the Orthodox Faith, writes: “we call the holy Mary the Mother of God. For this name embraces the whole mystery of the dispensation. For if she who bore Him is the Mother of God, assuredly He Who was born of her is God and likewise also man.”21 In the same chapter he declines the title “Mother of Christ,” which he attributes to Nestorius.

The Common Christological Declaration (1994)

On 11 November 1994 Pope John Paul II and Mar Dinkha IV, Catholicos-Patriarch of the Assyrian Church of the East — a church that honours Nestorius in its liturgy — signed a Common Christological Declaration. In it the two churches “confess one Lord Jesus Christ, the only Son of God,” note that “the Catholic tradition addresses the Virgin Mary as ‘the Mother of God’ and also as ‘the Mother of Christ’” while the Assyrian Church prays to her as “the Mother of Christ our God and Saviour,” and “recognize the legitimacy and rightness of these expressions of the same faith.” The Declaration states that “the controversies of the past led to anathemas, bearing on persons and on formulas.”22

Disputed readings

How the title’s origins, its meaning, and the condemnation of its chief objector have been read.

The origins of the title

Where the word came from before the fourth-century controversies.

  • Was “Theotokos” in use before the fourth century? — turns on reports concerning Origen, Hippolytus, Dionysius and Pierius of Alexandria, Peter of Alexandria, and the dating of the Sub Tuum Praesidium papyrus.
  • Did the title “Theotokos” derive from the cult of Isis?

The meaning of the title and the Nestorian controversy

  • Was “Theotokos” a confession about Christ, a title of honour for Mary, or both?
  • Was Nestorius condemned at Ephesus for a Christology he did not hold? — turns largely on the Bazaar of Heracleides, his apologia recovered in the twentieth century.
