The Apostles

The apostles were the men Jesus chose to be with him and to be sent out in his name — the word apostolos (ἀπόστολος) means “one sent.” The Gospels name twelve; Acts records that after one of them, Judas Iscariot, betrayed Jesus and died, the remaining eleven chose Matthias by lot to fill his place; and Paul, by his own account, was afterward called by the risen Christ, “as of one born out of due time.” This page introduces the group and opens onto a separate timeline for each figure, setting out, earliest extant source first, what the sources report of who he was, where he preached, and how he died.

Unlike the belief-timelines elsewhere on this site, each apostle's page follows a person rather than a doctrine: what it charts is the tradition about him as it appears in successive sources, from the New Testament notices through the Fathers, the apocryphal Acts, the Byzantine and Latin apostle-lists, and the medieval legendaries, down to modern scholarship. Where the sources conflict or are read differently — was Bartholomew the Nathanael of John's Gospel? did Thomas reach India? was Peter crucified at Rome? — the question has its own page setting out each reading and its proponents.

The four lists of the Twelve

The New Testament gives four lists of the Twelve — in Matthew, Mark, Luke, and Acts. They agree in dividing the names into three groups of four, each group always headed by the same man (Peter, Philip, and James son of Alphaeus), and they agree that Judas Iscariot comes last. Two names vary: where Matthew and Mark have “Thaddaeus” (some manuscripts “Lebbaeus”), Luke and Acts have “Judas son of James”; and the Simon whom Matthew and Mark call “the Cananaean” Luke and Acts call “the Zealot.” Acts, listing the Eleven after the resurrection, omits Judas Iscariot.1

Judas's place refilled: Matthias

Acts records that between the Ascension and Pentecost Peter stood up among the believers and proposed that another take the “office” (ἐπισκοπή) left empty by Judas. Two men were put forward; the company prayed and cast lots, “and the lot fell upon Matthias; and he was numbered with the eleven apostles.”2

Paul, born out of due time

Paul is not named in the lists of the Twelve. Acts tells of his encounter with the risen Christ on the road to Damascus, and he called himself an apostle “as of one born out of due time” — “the least of the apostles, that am not meet to be called an apostle” — who “laboured more abundantly than they all.”3

The apostles, one by one

The Twelve as the Gospels name them, followed by Matthias and Paul. The notes below draw on the New Testament notices; where a later identification is read differently by different writers (Bartholomew as Nathanael, Matthew as Levi, James son of Alphaeus as “James the less”), the writers on each side are named on that apostle's own timeline.4

  • Simon Peter — the fisherman of Bethsaida and Capernaum, named first in each list.
  • Andrew — Simon Peter's brother, who first brought him to Jesus.
  • James, son of Zebedee — with his brother John, surnamed by Jesus a “son of thunder”; the one of the Twelve whose martyrdom Acts records, “killed … with the sword” by Herod.
  • John — son of Zebedee, brother of James; remembered in later tradition as “the disciple whom Jesus loved” and as the last of the Twelve to die, an old man at Ephesus, while a report ascribed to Papias says he was killed.
  • Philip — of Bethsaida, the town of Andrew and Peter.
  • Bartholomew — whom some writers identify with the Nathanael of John's Gospel, and others, Augustine among them, distinguish from him.
  • Thomas — called Didymus, “the Twin.”
  • Matthew — the tax collector (publican); Jerome calls him “Matthew, also called Levi,” while Heracleon, quoted by Clement of Alexandria, lists the two names separately.
  • James, son of Alphaeus — whom Jerome identified with the “James the less” of Mark 15:40.
  • Thaddaeus / Jude — the name Matthew and Mark give as Thaddaeus (or Lebbaeus) stands in the place where Luke and Acts have Judas son of James.
  • Simon the Zealot — called “the Cananaean” in Matthew and Mark.
  • Judas Iscariot — who betrayed Jesus, and whose place among the Twelve Matthias took.

And, added to the Twelve:

  • Matthias — chosen by lot to replace Judas Iscariot.
  • Paul — who called himself the apostle of the Gentiles, called last of all on the Damascus road.

Beyond the Twelve

Luke's Gospel records that Jesus also appointed “other seventy” (seventy-two, in many manuscripts) and sent them out ahead of him, two by two, into every town he meant to visit.5 Near the same circle stand figures early Christian writers remembered as co-workers of the apostles — Mark and Luke, the evangelists; Barnabas, Paul's first companion; and James the brother of the Lord, who led the church at Jerusalem. Their traditions may in time receive timelines here.

The layers of sources

The apostles' timelines draw on the same body of sources, written across more than a millennium. They are set out here once, earliest first, so that each timeline can refer back to them by layer.

The New Testament

The four Gospel lists, the Acts of the Apostles, and Paul's own letters (especially Galatians 1–2) form the New Testament layer. Of the Twelve, they record the deaths of Judas Iscariot and of James son of Zebedee, killed by Herod Agrippa; of the rest they report where they were commissioned, not where they died.6

The early Fathers (2nd–4th c.)

Between the New Testament and the later compilers stand scattered notices, many surviving only as fragments quoted by later writers. Papias of Hierapolis (c. 100–130) preserves an account of Judas's swollen body and death.7 Clement of Alexandria (c. 150–215), in the lost Hypotyposes known through Eusebius, gives the story of the aged John at Ephesus and the note that several apostles were married.8 Writers of the late 2nd and early 3rd centuries locate several apostles: Polycrates of Ephesus places Philip and John in Asia, Irenaeus places Peter and Paul at Rome and John at Ephesus, and Tertullian places Peter, Paul, and John at Rome.9 Eusebius, in a passage he credits in part to Origen's Commentary on Genesis (early 3rd c.), records the apostles' fields — Thomas to Parthia, Andrew to Scythia, John to Asia, Peter to Rome and crucified head-downward.10

Eusebius (c. 325)

Eusebius's Ecclesiastical History, especially Book 3, gathers much of the foregoing into the earliest extant connected account of where the apostles went and how they ended. It preserves several of the earlier witnesses named above — Papias, Clement, Polycrates, Origen — by quotation.11

The apocryphal Acts (2nd–3rd c.)

The “Leucian” corpus — the Acts of Peter, of Paul (with Paul and Thecla), of Andrew, of John, and of Thomas — together with the later Acts of Philip, contains many of the best-known scenes: Peter's inverted cross, Andrew's X-shaped cross, Thomas in India, John and the poisoned cup. They narrate the apostles' travels, miracles, and deaths, and several of them urge sexual continence (encratism). Where a scene in them is disputed as history, the readings are set out on the apostle's own timeline.12

Jerome, On Illustrious Men (393) — a compact Latin summary

Jerome's short lives of Peter, James the Lord's brother, Matthew, Jude, Paul, and John condense the Greek tradition for a Latin readership, drawing on Eusebius and other earlier writers.13

The apostle-lists (uncertain date)

A family of late catalogues — Pseudo-Hippolytus's On the Twelve Apostles and On the Seventy Apostles, Pseudo-Dorotheus of Tyre, the Latin Breviarium Apostolorum (c. 600, which fixes feast-days and burial sites), the Byzantine Chronicon Paschale, and Isidore of Seville's De ortu et obitu patrum — assign each apostle a field and a manner of death. Several are of uncertain authorship, and they share much of their material; they record how the apostles' missions and deaths were remembered and commemorated in the churches that used them.14

The Golden Legend (c. 1260) and later hagiography

Jacobus de Voragine's Golden Legend gives the fullest lives — Bartholomew flayed in Armenia, Simon and Jude martyred in Persia. It was compiled in the thirteenth century, more than a millennium after the apostles' lifetimes.15

Modern scholarship

Where a mission or a martyrdom is disputed, the apostles' timelines report recent studies of the evidence — Sean McDowell's The Fate of the Apostles (2015), which grades each martyrdom account on a probability scale (on which only Peter, Paul, James son of Zebedee, and James the Lord's brother rate at its highest level); Candida Moss's more skeptical study of early martyrdom traditions; and studies such as F. F. Bruce's on the leading apostles.16
