Who Was Cyrus the Great?

Cyrus II of Persia is the one Gentile the Hebrew Bible calls God’s mashiach — “his anointed” (Isaiah 45:1) — and God’s “shepherd” (44:28): the pagan king who overthrows Babylon, ends the exile, and decrees the temple rebuilt. No other foreign ruler is given these titles. Isaiah names him outright, twice, and stakes God’s uniqueness on it — “I have surnamed thee, though thou hast not known me” (45:4) — while the closing chapters of Chronicles and the opening of Ezra record the decree itself: “The LORD God of heaven hath given me all the kingdoms of the earth; and he hath charged me to build him an house at Jerusalem” (Ezra 1:2). The destroyer’s captives go home; the vessels Nebuchadnezzar carried off are restored; and Jeremiah’s seventy years reach their term.

On those few verses two very different afterlives grew. In one, Cyrus is the anointed deliverer read as a figure of Christ — the outsider given the Messiah’s own title, who liberates the captive people and rebuilds the house of God. In the other, Cyrus is the great critical crux of the book that names him: because Isaiah 40–55 speaks from within the exile and names a king who would not rise for 150 years, the naming of Cyrus became the classic linchpin of the argument for a sixth-century “Second Isaiah.” And around him stands the most famous archaeological object tied to any biblical decree — the Cyrus Cylinder, a clay barrel from Babylon in which Cyrus credits Marduk with choosing him and describes restoring peoples and sanctuaries, often paralleled with (and often overstated against) the decree of Ezra.

This page is a biographical tradition-timeline: not a life of Cyrus but a history of what was said and believed about him across the centuries, set out earliest source first. Its “witnesses” are the datable sources that report on or interpret him — the biblical portrait; the Greek historians Herodotus and Xenophon; Josephus, who has Cyrus read his own name in Isaiah and set out to fulfil it; the Fathers who wrestled with a pagan called “anointed” (Tertullian, who read it straight to Christ; Jerome, who corrected him; Cyril and Theodoret, who found the middle way); the rabbis, who made him the “upright king” who later soured; Cosmas and Bede; and modern scholarship, where the dating crux and the Cylinder are weighed. The genuinely contested questions — does the naming of Cyrus require a “Second Isaiah”? does the Cylinder corroborate the Ezra decree? is Cyrus a type of Christ? — are weighed on their own argument pages. Cyrus belongs to a recurring biblical type, the Gentile brought into relation with Israel’s God, whose kin are the Syrian commander Naaman, the humbled emperor Nebuchadnezzar, and the Queen of Sheba — but he is the only one Scripture calls anointed.

Timeline

The biblical portrait (Isaiah 44–48; 2 Chronicles 36; Ezra 1 and 6)

Everything the later tradition made of Cyrus grows from a cluster of oracles in Isaiah and the decree that answers them. Isaiah names him where no prophet names a foreign king: “That saith of Cyrus, He is my shepherd, and shall perform all my pleasure: even saying to Jerusalem, Thou shalt be built; and to the temple, Thy foundation shall be laid”; and then, astonishingly, “Thus saith the LORD to his anointed, to Cyrus, whose right hand I have holden, to subdue nations before him.” The surrounding chapters point to the same rising conqueror without yet naming him — the “righteous man from the east” who is given “the nations before him” (41:2), the one “from the rising of the sun” who tramples princes like clay (41:25), the “ravenous bird from the east, the man that executeth my counsel from a far country” (46:11). And the honour is laid on a king who does not know the God who crowns him: “I have surnamed thee, though thou hast not known me.”1

The decree fulfils the word. “Now in the first year of Cyrus king of Persia, that the word of the LORD by the mouth of Jeremiah might be fulfilled, the LORD stirred up the spirit of Cyrus… and he hath charged me to build him an house at Jerusalem.” Chronicles and Ezra give the proclamation; a second, drier Aramaic version in Ezra 6 — temple dimensions, timber and stone, the restored gold and silver vessels — reads like the memorandum from the royal archive. So Cyrus ends Jeremiah’s seventy years (Jeremiah 25:11–12), and Daniel dates his own old age to “the reign of Cyrus the Persian.” The pagan destroyer of no temple, but the pagan restorer of the one that mattered: that is the portrait every later witness is trying to read.2

The Greek historians — Herodotus and Xenophon (c. 430–360 BC)

The earliest datable sources about Cyrus outside the Bible are Greek, and they already split into the two poles the later tradition would inherit. Xenophon’s Cyropaedia makes him the model king, whose empire of willing obedience proves “whether the government of men is after all an impossible or even a difficult task” — the very work Jerome would cite to show Isaiah’s prophecy fulfilled. Herodotus tells the harder story: born under his grandfather Astyages’ ominous dreams, Cyrus rises to found the Persian empire but dies in defeat far to the north, killed by the Massagetae under Queen Tomyris, who dips his head in a skin of blood. Neither Greek knows anything of the Isaiah oracle; theirs is the secular memory of the founder against which the biblical reading stands out.3 Full detail »

Josephus (Antiquities 11.1, c. AD 93) — the king who read his own name

The earliest post-biblical treatment makes the move the whole predictive tradition would inherit: Cyrus acted because he read the prophecy that named him. “This was known to Cyrus by his reading the book which Isaiah left behind him of his prophecies… when Cyrus read this, and admired the divine power, an earnest desire and ambition seized upon him to fulfil what was so written.” Josephus dates the prophecy well before the exile and expands the terse edict into a confession: “he foretold my name by the prophets, and that I should build him a house at Jerusalem.” It is a first-century witness not to Cyrus’s motives but to how the tradition was already being read.4 Full detail »

Tertullian (An Answer to the Jews 7, c. AD 200) — the oracle read straight to Christ

The earliest surviving Christian handling of Isaiah 45:1 does not treat it as a word to Cyrus at all. Reading the Greek “to my anointed, [the] Lord,” Tertullian applies the whole oracle directly to Christ: “whose right hand does God the Father hold but Christ’s, his Son?” The opened gates and shattered bars become the spread of the gospel through the nations, unbarring “the hearts of individuals… by the faith of Christ.” It is the head of a stream the later tradition would have to discipline — the christological over-reading that Jerome corrects.5 Full detail »

Jerome (Commentary on Isaiah; Prologue to Ezra, c. AD 408) — “it is said to Cyrus”

Jerome carries the figure into Latin and does the tradition’s hard philological work. Against the christological reading he insists on the plain addressee: “not only many Latin but also Greek people greatly err, thinking that it is written: Thus says the Lord to my Christ, the Lord… it is not κυρίὶ, which means Lord, but it is said to Cyrus… the king of the Persians.” The title “anointed” marks kingship, as it did for Saul. He points to the pagan record for the fulfilment — “the history of Cyrus the Great, consisting of eight books by Xenophon” — and ties “my shepherd” (44:28) to the edict of Ezra, noting carefully that Cyrus authorized the rebuilding but the temple itself rose under Darius.6 Full detail »

Cyril of Alexandria (Commentary on Isaiah, c. AD 428) — anointed to a kingdom, anticipating Christ

Cyril takes the middle path the tradition would settle on. Cyrus really is the “anointed” of Isaiah 45:1 — but not as a saint: “it calls Cyrus ‘Christ’… as anointed by God, in that God was in the habit of anointing those called to the kingdom even if they were not saints or worshipers… So Cyrus is the anointed one, anticipating Christ as king.” The word marks divine appointment to rule, not holiness — and precisely so it becomes a shadow of the true Anointed King, laid on a man “crowned… with glory, even though you do not know that I am the Lord.”7 Full detail »

Theodoret of Cyrus (Commentary on Isaiah, c. AD 435) — anointed for a required function

Theodoret reads “anointed” as a mark of divine commissioning, grounded in canonical usage: Scripture calls “anointed” not only those literally given unction but any whom God singles out for a task — as it called the patriarchs “my anointed ones” though they had no oil. “Here… he has given to Cyrus the title ‘anointed,’ in order to announce that it is [God] who has chosen him as king in such a way as to defeat the empire of the Babylonians, to make an end to the captivity of the Jews and to [re]construct the temple of God.” He converges exactly with Cyril: the title names an office, not a merit.8 Full detail »

The rabbinic tradition (Babylonian Talmud; traditions from c. AD 200, redacted by c. 500) — the upright king who soured

Jewish tradition holds Cyrus in a careful ambivalence. He was “a virtuous king” whose very name (Koresh) the Gemara reads from kasher, “upright” — and yet one who later “became corrupt,” so that his years are counted like an Israelite king’s only “before he became corrupt.” The next folio hunts the proofs that he soured. The title “anointed” the rabbis take in a purely functional sense — a commission, not a redeemer — the same distinction the Fathers reached from the other side. The warm name and the later fault are held together, as the Greek portrait held the paragon and the mortal conqueror.9 Full detail »

Cosmas Indicopleustes (Christian Topography, Book 8, c. AD 550) — the fullest retelling of the wonder

The Alexandrian merchant-monk gives the fullest Christian version of Josephus’s reading. Quoting Isaiah 45:1–13 in full, he pictures the king turning to the very page: “Cyrus, struck with wonder at the foreknowledge and the prediction, and finding his own name expressly announced by the Prophet beforehand, ordered the people to go up to Jerusalem… and not only did he do this, but those also who came after him did the same until the City and the Temple were finished.” Reading Ezra 1, Cosmas draws the same inference — that Cyrus “had read the book of Isaiah, and believed in what was written.”10 Full detail »

Bede (Commentary on Ezra and Nehemiah, c. AD 725) — the faith and piety of a pagan king

Reading the edict of Ezra 1, Bede praises “great” faith and “great” piety in Cyrus — that a Gentile king should confess Israel’s God as Lord of heaven who is yet present in Jerusalem, “a Spirit present everywhere,” and should free the captives “without exception… as free men.” Then he turns the whole return into a figure of the soul’s passage “from the confusion of sins… as if from Babylonian captivity… to Jerusalem to freedom.” With Bede the western tradition lands where Cyril and Theodoret led: Cyrus is a true instance of the Gentile who confesses Israel’s God, kin to Naaman and Nebuchadnezzar.11 Full detail »

Modern scholarship (19th–21st centuries) — the dating crux and the Cylinder

Two modern questions dominate the figure. The first is the dating crux: because Isaiah 40–55 speaks from within the exile and names Cyrus 150 years early, the naming became the linchpin of the case — systematized by Bernhard Duhm in 1892 — for an exilic “Second Isaiah,” against which conservatives urge the book’s own claim to predict and the unity of its manuscript transmission. The second is the Cyrus Cylinder, unearthed at Babylon in 1879: a Marduk-propaganda inscription describing Cyrus’s restoration of peoples and sanctuaries, hailed in the twentieth century as “the first charter of human rights” — a framing scholars from Amélie Kuhrt to Irving Finkel reject — and often adduced, more soberly, as corroboration of the policy behind the Ezra decree. Both are weighed below.12

Arguments

Cyrus named a century and a half early — predictive prophecy or a sixth-century “Second Isaiah”? — contested

Isaiah names Cyrus some 150 years before his rise (44:28; 45:1), the classic linchpin of the case that Isaiah 40–55 is an exilic “Second Isaiah.” The verdict is contested, and the honest result is not symmetrical mush. The strong critical plank is not the name but the pervasive exilic standpoint of chapters 40–55 — a fallen Jerusalem addressed as past, a Babylonian horizon, exiles spoken to as present — which is independent of the mere fact of the name and commands a scholarly majority. The strong conservative plank is equally internal: chapters 40–48 make prediction itself the proof of God’s deity (46:9–10), so a wholly after-the-fact reading has to explain a predictive contest it rigged. Each side’s bare-name form is the weak form — the critic’s if it presupposes that prophecy is impossible (which this wiki does not grant a priori), the conservative’s if it treats the Qumran scroll as evidence of eighth-century composition. And the fault line is literary-historical, not faith-versus-unbelief: Deutero-Isaiah is a dating judgment, not a disproof of inspiration; and the possibility of prediction is not proof that this passage is predictive.

Does the Cyrus Cylinder corroborate the restoration decree of Ezra? — sound as historical plausibility / overstated in its maximal forms

The Cyrus Cylinder describes Cyrus restoring sanctuaries and returning displaced peoples to their homes — the kind of act the Bible reports him doing for Judah (Ezra 1; 6:3–5). That the Cylinder, together with Cyrus’s documented repatriation policy, makes the biblical decree historically credible and consistent with his known practice is sound — and it survives the deflationary scholarship, since Kuhrt’s point that such repatriation was standard royal policy strengthens the “consistent with practice” reading even as it deflates the “uniquely tolerant liberator” one. Two framings are overstated and should be dropped: that the Cylinder is the Jewish decree or directly documents the return (it never names Judah, Jerusalem, the Jews, or Yahweh), and that it is a “first charter of human rights” (a twentieth-century myth; it is a traditional Marduk-propaganda foundation inscription). Its crediting of Marduk, not Yahweh, is not evidence against the Bible but the same audience-tailored self-presentation Ezra 1 reflects.

Is Cyrus a type of Christ, the anointed deliverer? — sound as typology / weak as direct prophecy of 45:1

Cyrus is the one Gentile called God’s mashiach (Isaiah 45:1), and two christological claims rest on it. That Cyrus — the anointed deliverer who frees captive Israel and rebuilds the temple — is a type of Christ the Anointed is sound and mainstream: the correspondence is exact and text-driven, and Cyril and Theodoret read it so while keeping the literal referent the Persian king. That Isaiah 45:1 speaks directly of Christ, as Tertullian took it, is weak: it depends on hearing the Greek “to Cyrus” as “to the Lord,” a confusion Jerome corrects and the Hebrew forecloses (“to Cyrus… the king of the Persians”), and 45:4’s “though thou hast not known me” cannot be said of the eternal Son. Keep the type; drop the identification.
