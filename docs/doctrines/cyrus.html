Who Was Cyrus the Great?

Cyrus II of Persia is the one Gentile the Hebrew Bible calls God’s mashiach — “his anointed” (Isaiah 45:1) — and God’s “shepherd” (44:28): the Persian king who, in the biblical account, overthrows Babylon, ends the exile, and decrees the temple rebuilt. Isaiah names him outright, twice — “I have surnamed thee, though thou hast not known me” (45:4) — while the closing chapters of Chronicles and the opening of Ezra record the decree itself: “The LORD God of heaven hath given me all the kingdoms of the earth; and he hath charged me to build him an house at Jerusalem” (Ezra 1:2). In Ezra’s account the captives go home and the vessels Nebuchadnezzar carried off are restored, and Chronicles presents the decree as the fulfilment of Jeremiah’s seventy years.

Later readers took those verses in several directions. Christian commentators read the anointed deliverer in differing ways: Tertullian applied the oracle to Christ; Cyril called Cyrus the anointed one “anticipating Christ as king”; Jerome and Theodoret read it of Cyrus’s own commission to rule. Modern scholars debate the date of Isaiah 40–55, in which the naming of Cyrus has a central place. And around him stands the Cyrus Cylinder, a clay barrel from Babylon in which Cyrus credits Marduk with choosing him and describes restoring peoples and sanctuaries, often compared with the decree of Ezra.

This page is a biographical tradition-timeline: not a life of Cyrus but a history of what was said and believed about him across the centuries, set out earliest source first. Its “witnesses” are the datable sources that report on or interpret him: the biblical portrait; the Greek historians Herodotus and Xenophon; Josephus, who has Cyrus read his own name in Isaiah and set out to fulfil it; the Fathers who commented on a Persian king called “anointed” (Tertullian, Jerome, Cyril and Theodoret); the rabbis, who made him the “virtuous king” who later became corrupt; Cosmas and Bede; and modern scholarship on the dating of Isaiah 40–55 and on the Cylinder. Other Gentiles brought into relation with Israel’s God have their own timelines: the Syrian commander Naaman, the king Nebuchadnezzar, and the Queen of Sheba.

Timeline

The biblical portrait (Isaiah 44–48; 2 Chronicles 36; Ezra 1 and 6)

The later tradition about Cyrus draws on a cluster of oracles in Isaiah and on the decree recorded in Chronicles and Ezra. Isaiah names him: “That saith of Cyrus, He is my shepherd, and shall perform all my pleasure: even saying to Jerusalem, Thou shalt be built; and to the temple, Thy foundation shall be laid”; and then “Thus saith the LORD to his anointed, to Cyrus, whose right hand I have holden, to subdue nations before him.” The surrounding chapters speak of a conqueror without naming him — the “righteous man from the east” who is given “the nations before him” (41:2), the one “from the rising of the sun” who tramples princes like clay (41:25), the “ravenous bird from the east, the man that executeth my counsel from a far country” (46:11). The king so honoured is one who does not know the God who names him: “I have surnamed thee, though thou hast not known me.”1

Chronicles and Ezra present the decree as the fulfilment of Jeremiah’s word: “Now in the first year of Cyrus king of Persia, that the word of the LORD by the mouth of Jeremiah might be fulfilled, the LORD stirred up the spirit of Cyrus… and he hath charged me to build him an house at Jerusalem.” A second, Aramaic version in Ezra 6 gives temple dimensions, timber and stone, and the restored gold and silver vessels. Cyrus’s first year thus closes Jeremiah’s seventy years (Jeremiah 25:11–12) in the Chronicler’s framing, and Daniel 6:28 says Daniel prospered “in the reign of Cyrus the Persian.” Later witnesses interpret this portrait of a foreign king remembered as the restorer of the Jerusalem temple.2

The Greek historians — Herodotus and Xenophon (c. 430–360 BC)

The earliest datable sources about Cyrus outside the Bible are Greek, and they give two different portraits. Xenophon’s Cyropaedia makes him the model king, whose empire of willing obedience leads Xenophon to ask “whether the government of men is after all an impossible or even a difficult task.” Herodotus gives a different account: born under his grandfather Astyages’ ominous dreams, Cyrus rises to found the Persian empire but dies in defeat far to the north, killed by the Massagetae under Queen Tomyris, who dips his head in a skin of blood. Neither Greek historian mentions the Isaiah oracle.3 Full detail »

Josephus (Antiquities 11.1, c. AD 93) — the king who read his own name

Josephus gives the earliest extant account that Cyrus acted on a prophecy naming him: Cyrus acts because he read it. “This was known to Cyrus by his reading the book which Isaiah left behind him of his prophecies… when Cyrus read this, and admired the divine power, an earnest desire and ambition seized upon him to fulfil what was so written.” Josephus dates the prophecy well before the exile and expands the edict into a confession: “he foretold my name by the prophets, and that I should build him a house at Jerusalem.”4 Full detail »

Tertullian (An Answer to the Jews 7, c. AD 200) — the oracle read of Christ

The earliest Christian treatment of Isaiah 45:1 surveyed here reads it as a word to Christ rather than to Cyrus. Reading the Greek “to my anointed, [the] Lord,” Tertullian applies the whole oracle directly to Christ: “whose right hand does God the Father hold but Christ’s, his Son?” The opened gates and shattered bars become the spread of the gospel through the nations, unbarring “the hearts of individuals… by the faith of Christ.”5 Full detail »

Jerome (Commentary on Isaiah; Prologue to Ezra, c. AD 408) — “not κυρίῳ, which means Lord, but Cyrus”

Jerome treats the figure in Latin with attention to the Hebrew. He holds that the oracle is addressed to Cyrus, and names the reading he rejects: “very many, not only of the Latins but of the Greeks, err badly, thinking that it is written: Thus says the Lord to my anointed, the Lord… For it says not κυρίῳ, which means Lord, but Cyrus… the king of the Persians.” For Jerome, the title “anointed” marks kingship, as it did for Saul. He points to the pagan record for the fulfilment — “Xenophon's history of the elder Cyrus in eight books” — and ties “my shepherd” (44:28) to the edict of Ezra, noting that Cyrus authorized the rebuilding but the temple itself rose under Darius.6 Full detail »

Cyril of Alexandria (Commentary on Isaiah, c. AD 428) — anointed to a kingdom, “anticipating Christ as king”

For Cyril, Cyrus is the “anointed” of Isaiah 45:1, but not as a saint: “it calls Cyrus ‘Christ’… as anointed by God, in that God was in the habit of anointing those called to the kingdom even if they were not saints or worshipers… So Cyrus is the anointed one, anticipating Christ as king.” In Cyril’s reading the word marks divine appointment to rule, not holiness, laid on a man “crowned… with glory, even though you do not know that I am the Lord.”7 Full detail »

Theodoret of Cyrus (Commentary on Isaiah, c. AD 435) — anointed for a required function

Theodoret reads “anointed” as a mark of divine commissioning, grounded in canonical usage: Scripture calls “anointed” not only those literally given unction but any whom God singles out for a task — as it called the patriarchs “my anointed ones” though they had no oil. “Here… he has given to Cyrus the title ‘anointed,’ in order to announce that it is [God] who has chosen him as king in such a way as to defeat the empire of the Babylonians, to make an end to the captivity of the Jews and to [re]construct the temple of God.” Like Cyril, he takes the title to name an office rather than personal holiness.8 Full detail »

The rabbinic tradition (Babylonian Talmud; traditions from c. AD 200, redacted by c. 500) — the upright king who soured

The Babylonian Talmud gives a mixed portrait. Cyrus was “a virtuous king” whose very name (Koresh) the Gemara reads from kasher, “upright” — and yet one who later “became corrupt,” so that his years are counted like an Israelite king’s only “before he became corrupt.” The next folio gives proofs that he soured. Medieval Jewish commentators (Rashi and Ibn Ezra, as surveyed by Lisbeth Fried in 2002) take the title “anointed” in a functional sense: a commission, not a redeemer.9 Full detail »

Cosmas Indicopleustes (Christian Topography, Book 8, c. AD 550) — the king who found his name in Isaiah

The Alexandrian merchant-monk gives an extended Christian retelling of the account found in Josephus. Quoting Isaiah 45:1–4 and 13 in full, he pictures the king turning to the very page: “Cyrus, struck with wonder at the foreknowledge and the prediction, and finding his own name expressly announced by the Prophet beforehand, ordered the people to go up to Jerusalem… and not only did he do this, but those also who came after him did the same until the City and the Temple were finished.” Reading Ezra 1, Cosmas draws the same inference — that Cyrus “had read the book of Isaiah, and believed in what was written.”10 Full detail »

Bede (Commentary on Ezra and Nehemiah, c. AD 725) — the faith and piety of a pagan king

Reading the edict of Ezra 1, Bede praises “great” faith and “great” piety in Cyrus — that a Gentile king should confess Israel’s God as Lord of heaven who is yet present in Jerusalem, “a Spirit present everywhere,” and should free the captives “without exception… as free men.” Then he turns the whole return into a figure of the soul’s passage “from the confusion of sins… as if from Babylonian captivity… to Jerusalem to freedom.” Bede reads the edict as a Gentile king’s confession of Israel’s God; other Gentiles read in this way appear on the timelines of Naaman and Nebuchadnezzar.11 Full detail »

Modern scholarship (19th–21st centuries) — the dating of Isaiah 40–55 and the Cylinder

Two modern questions attach to the figure. The first is the date of Isaiah 40–55. Bernhard Duhm (Das Buch Jesaia, 1892) assigned these chapters to an exilic “Second Isaiah,” and S. R. Driver read chapters 40–66 as a prophecy on Israel’s restoration from exile in Babylon; on this reading the chapters address Jerusalem as fallen and speak to exiles as Cyrus rises, and the naming of Cyrus, some 150 years after the traditional date of Isaiah ben Amoz, is part of that case. O. T. Allis (1950), John Oswalt and J. Alec Motyer hold the book a unity of predictive prophecy: the trial-of-the-nations passages offer the foretelling of events, Cyrus among them, as proof of the LORD’s deity, and Motyer argues that no manuscript transmits the proposed divisions separately. The second is the Cyrus Cylinder, unearthed at Babylon in 1879: an Akkadian inscription in which Marduk chooses Cyrus and Cyrus restores peoples and sanctuaries. It was promoted as “the first charter of human rights” by Mohammad Reza Shah Pahlavi around the 1971 imperial anniversary (⚠ unverified), a framing Amélie Kuhrt (1983) rejects, reading it as a traditional Mesopotamian foundation inscription. Its restoration passage is also compared with the Ezra decree, whose Aramaic memorandum in Ezra 6 Elias Bickerman (1946) defended as a genuine Persian chancellery document.12
