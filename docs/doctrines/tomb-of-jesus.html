The tomb of Jesus (Golgotha & the Holy Sepulchre)

The Gospels give the crucifixion and burial a very particular setting and then leave it. The place is called Golgotha, “a place of a skull”; it is near the city but outside the gate; the tomb is rock-cut, brand-new, never used, and it stands in a garden a few steps from where the cross was raised. No evangelist gives a street, a landmark, or a distance. This page traces how Christian writers, pilgrims, builders and, later, archaeologists answered the question the Gospels leave open — where is it? — and how that answer was carried, buried, recovered, monumentalized, destroyed, rebuilt, met by a rival site, and examined by excavation.

It is a reception-history timeline: not a survey of who believed a doctrine, but a chronicle of how one identification was made, kept and debated across eighteen centuries. Its “witnesses” are therefore datable sources and datable events — a second-century homily, a pagan temple, a pilgrim's diary, an emperor's biographer, a catechist pointing at the rock, a nun's liturgical journal, a Persian sack, a caliph's demolition, a Victorian general's identification, and a run of modern excavations — ordered earliest-first and each footnoted to its source. Questions this history raises — whether the site lay outside the wall of Jesus's day, whether the location was remembered locally across the period of Hadrian's colony, the claims of the Garden Tomb, and what the 2016–17 mortar date bears on — are reported at the entries where they arise; how the finding of the True Cross has been read is set out under Disputed readings.

Related doctrines: Sites of the Passion — see also the Garden of Gethsemane · Bethany & Bethphage (the tomb of Lazarus & the Palm Sunday sending-place) · the site of the Ascension

Timeline

The New Testament gives the setting (1st century)

The Gospels, with Hebrews 13:12, supply five data about the place. The execution place is “a place called Golgotha, that is to say, a place of a skull.”1 It is “nigh to the city,” yet Jesus “suffered without the gate” — near but outside the wall.2,3 The tomb is a fresh rock-cut chamber, “his own new tomb, which he had hewn out in the rock,” “wherein was never man yet laid.”4,5 And it stands close by, “in the place where he was crucified there was a garden; and in the garden a new sepulchre… for the sepulchre was nigh at hand.”6 These five data — a skull-named execution ground, near but outside the wall, a rock-cut and never-used tomb, set in a garden, adjacent to the cross — are the terms in which later identifications have been discussed. The “outside the wall” datum figures in the modern debate because the traditional site lies inside today's walls.

Hadrian builds over the spot in Aelia Capitolina (c. 130s)

When Hadrian refounded ruined Jerusalem as the pagan colony Aelia Capitolina after the Bar Kokhba war, a pagan temple was raised over the ground later shown as the tomb. Eusebius, the earliest source for it, says “impious and godless persons had thought to remove [the sacred cave] entirely from the eyes of men” and “covered the entire spot… then … a gloomy shrine of lifeless idols to the impure spirit whom they call Venus.”7 Jerome, about sixty years later, reports the same suppression but names different gods — “the spot which had witnessed the resurrection was occupied by a figure of Jupiter; while on the rock where the cross had stood, a marble statue of Venus was set up.”8 The two accounts differ on the deities (Venus alone in Eusebius, writing in Constantine's lifetime; Jupiter over the tomb and Venus over the cross in Jerome). Both report a pagan monument standing on the spot until Constantine, a period Jerome puts at about 180 years.

Melito of Sardis: the crucifixion “in the centre of Jerusalem” (c. 160–170)

The earliest extant non-biblical writer read as pointing to the place is Melito, bishop of Sardis, who travelled to Palestine and whose Paschal homily says the killing happened “in the very centre of Jerusalem… in the middle of the main street, even in the centre of the city, while all were looking on.”9 The homily names no tomb, no Golgotha, and no shown site. Joan Taylor (1998) reads this topographically: after Hadrian's building the old quarry lay inside the city, and a site in the middle of Jerusalem was pointed out to Melito as the place where Jesus died; she adds that his words fit her New Testament-based placement of the crucifixion “if Melito’s plateia is, in fact, the Decumanus.” U. C. von Wahlde reads the phrases as rhetoric about the public enormity of the crime, as prophets were killed in the city ⚠ unverified.

The Bordeaux Pilgrim: the earliest extant itinerary naming the sites (333)

The earliest surviving pilgrim itinerary, written by an anonymous traveller from Bordeaux, describes the site as identified and being built: “On the left hand side is the little hill of Golgotha, where the Lord was crucified. About a stone's throw from there is the crypt where his body was buried… at the same place, on the command of the Emperor Constantine, a basilica was built.”10 It is the earliest extant source to call Golgotha a “little hill” (monticulus); the Gospels do not call it a hill. It records the state of the site in 333, with the basilica under construction.

Eusebius: the demolition and the tomb “contrary to all expectation” (c. 337)

The fullest ancient account is Eusebius's, in his Life of Constantine, written within a decade of the events. When Hadrian's fill was cleared, “immediately, and contrary to all expectation, the venerable and hallowed monument of our Saviour's resurrection was discovered”; Constantine then raised over it a church, adorning “the sacred cave itself, as the chief part of the whole work.”11 The emperor's own letter, quoted by Eusebius, marvels that “the monument of his most holy Passion, so long ago buried beneath the ground, should have remained unknown for so long a series of years.”12 Eusebius does not say that the local church or its bishop, Macarius, pointed out where to dig; Macarius appears as the recipient of Constantine's building letter, and the finding is described as “contrary to all expectation.” Joan Taylor (1998) calls it “very possible” that the church remembered the tomb as lying under the precinct of the Temple of Venus, “even though no extant source ever mentions this or even alludes to it in covert form”; her concluding reconstruction states that Christians in Palestine knew where the emperor had covered the tomb over.13

Cyril of Jerusalem: the on-site catechist pointing at the rock (c. 350)

Cyril catechized on the spot about fifteen years after the basilica's dedication. He appeals to the site as visible: “here is Golgotha to confute me, near which we are now assembled,” and “this holy Golgotha, which stands high above us, and shews itself to this day… the sepulchre nigh at hand where He was laid; and the stone which was laid on the door, which lies to this day by the tomb.”14 On the garden he notes “a garden was the place of his burial, and a vine was what was planted there.”15 His lectures are addressed to catechumens; in them he also calls Golgotha “the very centre of the earth.”

Egeria: the site woven into the Jerusalem liturgy (c. 384)

By the 380s the Spanish (or Gaulish) pilgrim Egeria finds the site embedded in daily and Holy-Week worship, three linked stations: “the greater church, built by Constantine, which is situated in Golgotha behind the Cross, where the Lord suffered,” the Anastasis “in the place where the Lord rose after His Passion,” and “the Cross” itself, where the evening lucernare was sung.16 She describes the site in liturgical use half a century after the identification. She also records that the dedication feast was kept on the day “the Cross of the Lord was found”; the finding of the True Cross is absent from Eusebius, and how its narrative arose is disputed (see the readings).

Jerome: the statue on the spot, and Golgotha outside the gate (c. 395)

Jerome, writing from Bethlehem, reports Hadrian's suppression and places Calvary outside the gate. In Letter 58 he dates the pagan occupation “from the time of Hadrian to the reign of Constantine — a period of about one hundred and eighty years,” the resurrection spot under a Jupiter and the cross-rock under a Venus.17 In his commentary on Matthew he rejects the identification of Golgotha as Adam's grave and writes instead: “Without the city outside the gate are the places where criminals are executed, and these have got the name of Calvary… And Jesus was crucified there.”18 In the same Letter 58 Jerome discourages Paulinus from pilgrimage: “Access to the courts of heaven is as easy from Britain as from Jerusalem.”

Destroyed and rebuilt (614 – 1149)

The church was destroyed and rebuilt more than once. In 614 the Sasanian Persians sacked the basilica and carried off the relic of the Cross; Modestus repaired it and Heraclius restored the site by c. 630.19 In 1009 the Fatimid caliph al-Hakim ordered the church systematically demolished and the rock-cut tomb itself hacked down toward bedrock; only portions of the burial bench survived. The Byzantine rebuild under Constantine IX Monomachos (completed c. 1048) and the Crusaders' unified Romanesque church (consecrated 1149) gave the building roughly its present shape.20

A second proposed site: Gordon's Calvary and the Garden Tomb (1883)

In 1883 the British general Charles Gordon identified a skull-shaped escarpment north of the Damascus Gate as Golgotha, and an adjacent rock-cut tomb as the burial place, a garden setting outside the Old City walls. The identification was older than Gordon (Thenius, Howe, Conder), but he popularized it, and the Garden Tomb Association purchased the site in 1894.21 Gabriel Barkay (1986) dated the burial cave to Iron Age II (late 8th–7th c. BC) on its architecture, centuries before the first century, and argued on that basis against its being the “new sepulchre, wherein was never man yet laid” of the Gospels; a Hellenistic date proposed by Lufrani also places the cave before the first century.22

Excavation and scientific dating (1961 – 2025)

Three modern lines of excavation and dating bear on the traditional site. Kathleen Kenyon's 1961–67 excavations found no first-century occupation at the site, which she read as consistent with its being an extramural quarry-and-cemetery outside the “second wall” of Jesus's day — “the walls of the present Old City are not those of the time of the Gospels”; the line of that wall is reconstructed rather than fully excavated.23 In the 2016–17 restoration of the shrine, mortar between the original limestone burial bed and its marble slab was dated by optically stimulated luminescence to c. AD 345, in the Constantinian period and some 700 years earlier than the previous earliest scientific date for the shrine; Martin Biddle called it “spot-on for whatever Constantine did.”24 In 2022–25 Francesca Stasolla's excavation beneath the church reported cultivated soil with olive and vine remains over an Iron Age quarry, which press reports connected with “in the place where he was crucified there was a garden”; the plant material is placed by context and has not yet been radiocarbon-dated.25
