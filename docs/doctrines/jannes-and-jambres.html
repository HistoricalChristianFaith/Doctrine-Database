Who Were Jannes and Jambres?

Jannes and Jambres are the two magicians of Pharaoh’s court who matched Moses sign for sign — and then could not. In Exodus 7–8 Pharaoh summons “the wise men and the sorcerers” and “the magicians of Egypt” to counter Aaron’s rod: they cast down their own rods to serpents (only for Aaron’s to swallow them), they turn water to blood and bring up frogs “with their enchantments” — but at the plague of lice their art fails, and the magicians themselves tell Pharaoh, “This is the finger of God” (Exodus 8:19). The striking thing is what Exodus does not say: it never names them. Through the whole contest they are simply “the magicians of Egypt.”

Their names surface centuries later, and not in the Old Testament at all. Paul, warning Timothy of deceivers who resist the truth, reaches for a pair of figures his readers evidently knew: “Now as Jannes and Jambres withstood Moses, so do these also resist the truth… But they shall proceed no further: for their folly shall be manifest unto all men, as theirs also was” (2 Timothy 3:8–9). Paul cites the names as common knowledge, drawing not on Exodus — where they are anonymous — but on the flourishing Jewish legend that had by his day given Pharaoh’s sorcerers names, a genealogy, and a fate. That gap — unnamed in the Torah, named as household figures in a first-century apostle — is the hinge of everything on this page: it opens straight onto the vast extra-biblical tradition (the Damascus Document, the Targums, a whole lost apocryphal Book of Jannes and Jambres, the pagan magicians’ handbooks of Pliny and Apuleius, and the Fathers who tried to say where Paul got the names).

This page is a biographical tradition-timeline: not a life of the two magicians but a history of what was said and believed about them across the centuries, set out earliest source first. Its “witnesses” are the datable sources that name or interpret them — the anonymous magicians of Exodus, the naming in 2 Timothy, the Qumran “Yohana and his brother,” the Targums that make them Balaam’s own sons, the lost apocryphon the church weighed and finally shelved, the classical writers who ranked “Jannes” among history’s great mages, and the Fathers on Paul’s source. The genuinely contested questions — where the names come from and why they vary (Jambres / Mambres); whether they were sons of Balaam; what the lost Book of Jannes and Jambres contained and whether it was Scripture; and how legend disposed of them (converts? drowned in the Red Sea? killed in the golden-calf apostasy?) — are weighed on their own argument pages rather than settled by assertion. They are the prime instance of a recurring biblical type, the pagan magician who opposes God’s messenger, and stand as the dark mirror of Balaam, the pagan seer overruled to bless.

Timeline

The biblical portrait (Exodus 7–8, named in 2 Timothy 3:8–9)

The whole later tradition grows from a contest in which the two central figures are never named. In Exodus 7–8, when Aaron’s rod becomes a serpent before Pharaoh, “Pharaoh also called the wise men and the sorcerers: now the magicians of Egypt, they also did in like manner with their enchantments. For they cast down every man his rod, and they became serpents: but Aaron’s rod swallowed up their rods.” The magicians keep pace through the first plagues — they “did so with their enchantments” when the water turned to blood, and again “brought up frogs upon the land of Egypt” — so that the early wonders are a genuine duel of powers. Then the imitation breaks. At the plague of lice “the magicians did so with their enchantments to bring forth lice, but they could not… Then the magicians said unto Pharaoh, This is the finger of God” — the point at which Egypt’s sorcery confesses the limit of its craft before the God of Israel. Throughout, Exodus calls them only “the wise men,” “the sorcerers,” and “the magicians of Egypt”: they are anonymous.1

The names appear only centuries later, and outside the Old Testament. Writing to Timothy of the deceivers of the last days — “men of corrupt minds, reprobate concerning the faith” — Paul reaches for a pair of figures his readers plainly recognised: “Now as Jannes and Jambres withstood Moses, so do these also resist the truth… But they shall proceed no further: for their folly shall be manifest unto all men, as theirs also was” (2 Timothy 3:8–9). Paul supplies no explanation, as if the names needed none — and indeed they were current in the Jewish tradition of his day, where Pharaoh’s sorcerers had long since been given names (and much else besides). Because the Torah leaves them unnamed, the naming in 2 Timothy is itself the first datable witness to that extra-biblical tradition: the New Testament here preserves a piece of Second-Temple lore rather than a text of the Hebrew Bible. Everything the church, the synagogue, and the pagan mages later said of “Jannes and Jambres” hangs on this pair of anonymous opponents and the one apostle who named them.2
