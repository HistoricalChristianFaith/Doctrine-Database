Who Were Jannes and Jambres?

Jannes and Jambres are the two magicians of Pharaoh’s court who matched Moses sign for sign — and then could not. In Exodus 7–8 Pharaoh summons “the wise men and the sorcerers” and “the magicians of Egypt” to counter Aaron’s rod: they cast down their own rods to serpents (only for Aaron’s to swallow them), they turn water to blood and bring up frogs “with their enchantments” — but at the plague of lice their art fails, and the magicians themselves tell Pharaoh, “This is the finger of God” (Exodus 8:19). Exodus never names them: through the whole contest they are simply “the magicians of Egypt.”

Their names surface centuries later, and not in the Old Testament at all. 2 Timothy, a letter ascribed to Paul, warning of deceivers who resist the truth, names a pair of figures: “Now as Jannes and Jambres withstood Moses, so do these also resist the truth… But they shall proceed no further: for their folly shall be manifest unto all men, as theirs also was” (2 Timothy 3:8–9). The letter names them without explanation, and the names are not in Exodus; a century and a half earlier the Damascus Document names “Yannes and his brother,” a passage reference works cite as a naming of one of the magicians. That gap — unnamed in the Torah, named as familiar figures in a first-century letter — opens onto the extra-biblical tradition (the Damascus Document, the Targums, a whole lost apocryphal Book of Jannes and Jambres, the pagan magicians’ handbooks of Pliny and Apuleius, and the Fathers who asked where Paul got the names).

This page is a biographical tradition-timeline: not a life of the two magicians but a history of what was said and believed about them across the centuries, set out earliest source first. Its “witnesses” are the datable sources that name or interpret them — the anonymous magicians of Exodus, the naming in 2 Timothy, the Qumran “Yohana and his brother,” the Targums that set them at Balaam’s side, the lost apocryphon that the Gelasian Decree lists among the apocrypha, the classical writers who ranked “Jannes” among history’s great mages, and the Fathers on Paul’s source. Four disputed questions — where the names come from and why they vary (Jambres / Mambres); whether they were sons of Balaam; whether 2 Timothy took the names from the lost Book of Jannes and Jambres; and how the traditions end their story (converts? drowned in the Red Sea? killed after the golden calf?) — are set out under Disputed readings. The pair are figures of the foreign magician who opposes God’s messenger, set beside Balaam, the foreign seer overruled to bless — and, further off, in contrast with the Gentiles who cross the threshold the other way and confess Israel’s God: Naaman the Syrian, the Midianite priest Jethro, and the Queen of Sheba.

Timeline

The biblical portrait (Exodus 7–8, named in 2 Timothy 3:8–9)

The whole later tradition grows from a contest in which the two central figures are never named. In Exodus 7–8, when Aaron’s rod becomes a serpent before Pharaoh, “Pharaoh also called the wise men and the sorcerers: now the magicians of Egypt, they also did in like manner with their enchantments. For they cast down every man his rod, and they became serpents: but Aaron’s rod swallowed up their rods.” The magicians keep pace through the first plagues — they “did so with their enchantments” when the water turned to blood, and again “brought up frogs upon the land of Egypt” — so that the early wonders are a genuine duel of powers. Then the imitation breaks. At the plague of lice “the magicians did so with their enchantments to bring forth lice, but they could not… Then the magicians said unto Pharaoh, This is the finger of God” — the point at which Egypt’s magicians name the limit of their craft before the God of Israel. Throughout, Exodus calls them only “the wise men,” “the sorcerers,” and “the magicians of Egypt”: they are anonymous.1

The names appear only centuries later, and outside the Old Testament. Writing to Timothy of the deceivers of the last days — “men of corrupt minds, reprobate concerning the faith” — the letter ascribed to Paul names a pair of figures without introducing them: “Now as Jannes and Jambres withstood Moses, so do these also resist the truth… But they shall proceed no further: for their folly shall be manifest unto all men, as theirs also was” (2 Timothy 3:8–9). The letter supplies no explanation of the names. Because the Torah leaves the magicians unnamed, the naming in 2 Timothy is the earliest extant witness to both names of the pair (the older Damascus Document’s “Yannes and his brother” gives only one name). Where the letter found the names is disputed; see “Did 2 Timothy 3:8 take the names from the lost Book of Jannes and Jambres?”2

The Damascus Document (“Yohana and his brother,” c. 100 BC)

The Damascus Document is a Jewish sectarian text a century and a half older than 2 Timothy. Phillip J. Long calls it “the first reference to one of the magicians by the name,” and the Encyclopaedia Judaica lists its “Jannes and his brother” among the Second Temple sources of the legend; Albert Pietersma cautions that the passage’s brothers have been equated with Jannes and Jambres “without proper qualification.” Composed about 100 BC and known both from the mediaeval Cairo Genizah manuscript A and from a fragment among the Dead Sea Scrolls (6Q15 from Qumran’s Cave 6), the Document reads Israel’s deliverance from Egypt as a front in a cosmic war: “For in times past Moses and Aaron stood in the power of the Prince of Lights and Belial raised up Yannes and his brother in his cunning when seeking to do evil to Israel the first time.” The pair is framed dualistically — Moses and Aaron under the Prince of Lights against Belial’s two agents. Only one magician is named here (the Hebrew yḥnh, Yohana, behind the Greek “Jannes”): his companion is simply “his brother.” The couplet “Jannes and Jambres” appears in later sources. In strict composition-order this passage predates 2 Timothy; it is placed just after the scriptural baseline because that baseline — anonymous in Exodus, named in 2 Timothy — is the frame of the whole timeline.3 Full detail »

The pagan writers (Pliny c. AD 77, Apuleius c. AD 158, Numenius) — “Jannes” among the great magicians

The pair’s fame did not stay inside the synagogue and the church. Within a generation of 2 Timothy the name turns up in pagan Rome, in the literature of magic. In the history of the magic art that opens Book 30 of his Natural History (c. AD 77), the elder Pliny lists a Jewish “branch of magic… who derive their origin from Moses, Jannes, and Lotapea, Jews by birth” — ranking “Jannes” with Moses and Zoroaster among the very founders of the craft, though Pliny himself despised all magic as “an utter falsity.” Two generations later the orator Apuleius, on trial for sorcery about AD 158, dares his accusers to call him “that Carmendas or Damigeron or Moses or Iohannes… or whoever else has been celebrated among the mages since Zoroaster” (Apologia 90) — “Iohannes” being the same Jannes, a name he expects a Roman court to know at once. Both Latin writers name only Jannes, not Jambres — the “only one named” pattern of the Damascus Document again, and a datum for where the names came from. The third pagan notice, from the second-century Platonist Numenius of Apamea (preserved a century and a half later by Eusebius), instead retells the contest itself, naming “Jannes and Jambres, Egyptian sacred scribes, men judged to have no superiors in the practice of magic,” and — in the magicians’ favour — crediting them with power to blunt the worst of Moses’ plagues. All three report the legend from outside Jewish and Christian sources.4 Full detail »

The lost Book of Jannes and Jambres (composed c. 1st–3rd c. AD) — the apocryphon of the magicians’ penitence

A whole book about the pair once circulated: the Book (or Apocryphon) of Jannes and Jambres, known to the Latin West as the Paenitentia Iamnis et Mambre, “the Penitence of Jannes and Mambres.” Composed in Greek in Roman Egypt — in Albert Pietersma’s dating perhaps as early as the first century AD, and before Origen quotes it about 250 — it now survives only in fragments: Greek papyri (the Vienna scroll, 3rd c.; the Chester Beatty codex, late 4th c.; Michigan and Heidelberg scraps), an eleventh-century Latin-and-Old-English leaf whose picture shows Jambres raising his dead brother’s ghost, and Ethiopic fragments. What the pieces preserve is a full legend of the magicians’ fate: their careers as Balaam’s sons, their defeat by Moses, Jannes’ deathbed confession that “Moses’s power is of God” (the “penitence” that titles the book), his violent death and descent to Hades, and Jambres’ necromancy summoning his shade to describe the torments of hell. Ancient writers took notice of it: Origen held that Paul drew the names of 2 Timothy 3:8 straight from this “secret book,” and the Gelasian Decree (later fifth or sixth century) lists the book among the apocrypha. The legend it carried was also known to the pagan Platonist Numenius (preserved by Eusebius), who ranked Jannes and Jambres among Egypt’s unrivalled sacred scribes. Where 2 Timothy found the names is set out at its own page. In composition-order the book falls after the Damascus Document’s naming and before the fully-expansive Pseudo-Jonathan Targum; it is dated on this timeline by that composition, not by its later surviving copies.5 Full detail »

The Aramaic Targums (Pseudo-Jonathan; redacted c. 2nd–8th c. AD) — named, and tied to Balaam

After the sparse early namings comes not a single author but a whole reception stratum: the Aramaic Targums, the running synagogue paraphrases read alongside the Hebrew, whose written redaction spans several centuries. It is the latest and most expansive of them, Targum Pseudo-Jonathan, that weaves the pair into the story — and it does so three times. It supplies a scene Exodus lacks, in which Pharaoh dreams of a lamb outweighing all Egypt and “Jannis and Jambres, the chief of the magicians” read the omen and set the killing of the Hebrew boys in motion (Exodus 1:15); it names them again at the rod-contest itself, where “Janis and Jamberes, magicians of Mizraim,” match Moses “by their burnings of divination” (Exodus 7:11); and — the verse that binds this page to Balaam’s — it makes the two magicians the “two young men” who ride at Balaam’s side on the road to curse Israel (Numbers 22:22). The naming is distinctive to Pseudo-Jonathan: the restrained Onkelos keeps the anonymous “wise men and magicians” of the Hebrew. Two features bear on the disputed readings. First, the spelling is unstable even within one translation — “Jannis and Jambres,” “Janis and Jamberes,” “Jannes and Jambres” in successive verses — a datum for the name-origin question. Second, Numbers 22:22 calls them Balaam’s “two young men” — his servants in Kaufmann Kohler’s rendering (“two youths”), while the Encyclopaedia Judaica cites the verse among the sources that call them his sons; “sons of Balaam” is the wording of the apocryphon and Sefer ha-Yashar, and the International Standard Bible Encyclopedia (1915) notes the forty years that separate the Exodus contest from Balaam’s journey. This Targum verse is the primary text behind that Balaam-connection question.6 Full detail »

The Fathers on Paul’s source (Tertullian to Aquinas, c. 200–1274) — an unwritten tradition, or the lost Book?

The last stratum of the tradition is Christian commentary, and it keeps returning to the puzzle this page began with: 2 Timothy names two magicians the Old Testament never names. Read across the centuries — from Tertullian about 200 to Thomas Aquinas about 1270 — the Fathers make two recurring moves. The first is to ask where Paul got the names, and on this they divide into two camps. The Antiochene and Byzantine commentators answer “unwritten Jewish tradition,” or else direct inspiration: John Chrysostom — “either they were handed down by tradition, or it is probable that Paul knew them by inspiration” — is echoed almost verbatim by Oecumenius and Theophylact of Ohrid, and stated flatly by Theodoret of Cyrus (“not from holy Scripture but from an unwritten Jewish tradition”); Theodore of Mopsuestia joins them by rejecting the alternative, calling “particularly absurd… the notion that [Paul] had access to some information or writing now lost to us.” The Latin tradition holds that alternative: Ambrosiaster says the example is drawn “from the apocrypha,” Aquinas that Paul “perhaps obtained their names from certain Jewish writings,” and both stand with Origen, who had named the lost Book of Jannes and Jambres as Paul’s source outright. The second move is typological — the pair as the standing figure of counterfeit power that mimics the truth and then collapses: Tertullian files them under “that other species of magic which operates by miracles” against Moses; the Apostolic Constitutions pairs them with Annas and Caiaphas as the two Testaments’ opponents; and Augustine and Fulgentius turn the verse against the heretics of their own day, whose “folly shall be manifest to all men, as theirs also was.” Gathered here as one patristic stratum, these witnesses span more than a millennium and are dated on this timeline by the earliest of them (Tertullian, c. 200); whether 2 Timothy took the names from the book is set out on its own page.7 Full detail »

The medieval reception (the Acts of Pilate to the Zohar, c. 4th–13th c.) — the flying sorcerers and the necromancer’s book

Long after the Fathers had finished asking where Paul got the names, the two magicians went on living — in the church’s apocryphal tales and the synagogue’s legends alike. Christian writers press them into fresh service: in the Gospel of Nicodemus (Acts of Pilate) Nicodemus warns the council not to treat Jesus as Pharaoh treated Moses, since “Pharaoh had also two magicians, Jannes and Jambres… but not such as Moses did” — a test of divine origin (what is of God endures, what is mere magic collapses); and in the Acts of Peter and Paul the apostles warn Nero that Simon Magus, “as the Egyptians Jannes and Jambres led Pharaoh and his army astray until they were swallowed up in the sea,” will end the same way. Jewish tradition, meanwhile, keeps embroidering their fate: they join the mixed multitude, instigate the golden calf, turn proselyte — and die, in differing versions, drowned in the Red Sea, slain after the calf, or (in the Zohar) flying “up into the air before the sword of Phinehas… until, by the power of the Ineffable Name, they were caught and slain.” And in the Latin West the pair, now fixed as “Jamnes and Mambres,” become bywords of the master necromancer — their book copied out as late as eleventh-century England and their legend, in Albert Pietersma’s account, a backdrop to the Faust saga. The endings are set out on their own page.8 Full detail »

Modern scholarship (a survey) — the lost book reassembled, the names traced, the questions opened

Critical study has edited and traced the tradition, and several themes recur. First, the lost Book of Jannes and Jambres has been reassembled: Albert Pietersma’s Apocryphon of Jannes and Jambres the Magicians (Brill, 1994) edits it from fragments in half a dozen languages and dates the composition “perhaps as early as the first century C.E.,” tracing a legend told “from the Syriac east to the Latin west and from Egypt’s deserts to King Alfred’s court.” Second, the names are traced to a Jewish onomastic tradition — Yoḥana (a by-form of John) behind “Jannes,” an Aramaic root “to oppose” behind “Jambres”/“Mambres” — by A. J. Maclean (1909) and the International Standard Bible Encyclopedia (1915), while Ewald, Lauth and Freudenthal proposed an Egyptian derivation; the earliest sources name only one of the pair. Third, the earliest naming, the Damascus Document’s “Yannes and his brother,” is cited as evidence of a pre-Christian Jewish tradition, with Pietersma’s caution about equating its brothers with Jannes and Jambres. Fourth, the reference works distinguish the Targum’s “two young men” from the later “sons of Balaam,” and on whether 2 Timothy drew on the book, J. H. Charlesworth and D.-A. Koch allow possible dependence while Stephen Gero holds that it “cannot be regarded as certain.”9 Full detail »

Disputed readings

How the sources on the magicians’ names, their kinship, their book and their end have been read.

  • Where do the names “Jannes and Jambres” come from, and why does Jambres also appear as Mambres? — Egyptian and Hebrew-Aramaic derivations of the names.
  • Were Jannes and Jambres the sons of Balaam? — the Targum’s “two young men,” the apocryphon’s genealogy and Sefer ha-Yashar.
  • Did 2 Timothy 3:8 take the names from the lost Book of Jannes and Jambres? — Origen’s “secret book” and the Antiochene “unwritten tradition.”
  • What became of Jannes and Jambres, according to the traditions about them? — repentance, the Red Sea, the golden calf and the flight before Phinehas.
