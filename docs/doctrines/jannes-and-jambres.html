Who Were Jannes and Jambres?

Jannes and Jambres are the two magicians of Pharaoh’s court who matched Moses sign for sign — and then could not. In Exodus 7–8 Pharaoh summons “the wise men and the sorcerers” and “the magicians of Egypt” to counter Aaron’s rod: they cast down their own rods to serpents (only for Aaron’s to swallow them), they turn water to blood and bring up frogs “with their enchantments” — but at the plague of lice their art fails, and the magicians themselves tell Pharaoh, “This is the finger of God” (Exodus 8:19). The striking thing is what Exodus does not say: it never names them. Through the whole contest they are simply “the magicians of Egypt.”

Their names surface centuries later, and not in the Old Testament at all. Paul, warning Timothy of deceivers who resist the truth, reaches for a pair of figures his readers evidently knew: “Now as Jannes and Jambres withstood Moses, so do these also resist the truth… But they shall proceed no further: for their folly shall be manifest unto all men, as theirs also was” (2 Timothy 3:8–9). Paul cites the names as common knowledge, drawing not on Exodus — where they are anonymous — but on the flourishing Jewish legend that had by his day given Pharaoh’s sorcerers names, a genealogy, and a fate. That gap — unnamed in the Torah, named as household figures in a first-century apostle — is the hinge of everything on this page: it opens straight onto the vast extra-biblical tradition (the Damascus Document, the Targums, a whole lost apocryphal Book of Jannes and Jambres, the pagan magicians’ handbooks of Pliny and Apuleius, and the Fathers who tried to say where Paul got the names).

This page is a biographical tradition-timeline: not a life of the two magicians but a history of what was said and believed about them across the centuries, set out earliest source first. Its “witnesses” are the datable sources that name or interpret them — the anonymous magicians of Exodus, the naming in 2 Timothy, the Qumran “Yohana and his brother,” the Targums that make them Balaam’s own sons, the lost apocryphon the church weighed and finally shelved, the classical writers who ranked “Jannes” among history’s great mages, and the Fathers on Paul’s source. The genuinely contested questions — where the names come from and why they vary (Jambres / Mambres); whether they were sons of Balaam; what the lost Book of Jannes and Jambres contained and whether it was Scripture; and how legend disposed of them (converts? drowned in the Red Sea? killed in the golden-calf apostasy?) — are weighed on their own argument pages rather than settled by assertion. They are the prime instance of a recurring biblical type, the pagan magician who opposes God’s messenger, and stand as the dark mirror of Balaam, the pagan seer overruled to bless — and, further off, of the Gentiles who cross the threshold the other way and confess Israel’s God: Naaman the Syrian, the Midianite priest Jethro, and the Queen of Sheba.

Timeline

The biblical portrait (Exodus 7–8, named in 2 Timothy 3:8–9)

The whole later tradition grows from a contest in which the two central figures are never named. In Exodus 7–8, when Aaron’s rod becomes a serpent before Pharaoh, “Pharaoh also called the wise men and the sorcerers: now the magicians of Egypt, they also did in like manner with their enchantments. For they cast down every man his rod, and they became serpents: but Aaron’s rod swallowed up their rods.” The magicians keep pace through the first plagues — they “did so with their enchantments” when the water turned to blood, and again “brought up frogs upon the land of Egypt” — so that the early wonders are a genuine duel of powers. Then the imitation breaks. At the plague of lice “the magicians did so with their enchantments to bring forth lice, but they could not… Then the magicians said unto Pharaoh, This is the finger of God” — the point at which Egypt’s sorcery confesses the limit of its craft before the God of Israel. Throughout, Exodus calls them only “the wise men,” “the sorcerers,” and “the magicians of Egypt”: they are anonymous.1

The names appear only centuries later, and outside the Old Testament. Writing to Timothy of the deceivers of the last days — “men of corrupt minds, reprobate concerning the faith” — Paul reaches for a pair of figures his readers plainly recognised: “Now as Jannes and Jambres withstood Moses, so do these also resist the truth… But they shall proceed no further: for their folly shall be manifest unto all men, as theirs also was” (2 Timothy 3:8–9). Paul supplies no explanation, as if the names needed none — and indeed they were current in the Jewish tradition of his day, where Pharaoh’s sorcerers had long since been given names (and much else besides). Because the Torah leaves them unnamed, the naming in 2 Timothy is itself the first datable witness to that extra-biblical tradition: the New Testament here preserves a piece of Second-Temple lore rather than a text of the Hebrew Bible. Everything the church, the synagogue, and the pagan mages later said of “Jannes and Jambres” hangs on this pair of anonymous opponents and the one apostle who named them.2

The Damascus Document (“Yohana and his brother,” c. 100 BC) — the earliest naming

The scriptural baseline heads this page because it frames the whole tradition, but the oldest datable source actually to put a name on one of Pharaoh’s magicians is not 2 Timothy — it is a Jewish sectarian text a century and a half older. The Damascus Document, composed about 100 BC and known both from the mediaeval Cairo Genizah manuscript A and from a fragment among the Dead Sea Scrolls (6Q15 from Qumran’s Cave 6), reads Israel’s deliverance from Egypt as a front in a cosmic war: “For in times past Moses and Aaron stood in the power of the Prince of Lights and Belial raised up Yannes and his brother in his cunning when seeking to do evil to Israel the first time.” Two things stand out. First, the pair is already framed dualistically — God’s Moses and Aaron under the Prince of Lights against Belial’s two agents — the same key in which 2 Timothy will cast “Jannes and Jambres” as men who “resist the truth.” Second, and crucially for the origin of the names, only one magician is named here (the Hebrew yḥnh, Yohana, behind the Greek “Jannes”): his companion is simply “his brother,” still nameless. The fixed couplet “Jannes and Jambres” is thus not the tradition’s starting point but a later development that would fill in the anonymous brother. In strict composition-order this naming predates Paul’s; it is placed just after the scriptural baseline because that baseline — anonymous in Exodus, named as household figures by Paul — is the frame the whole timeline hangs on.3 Full detail »

The pagan writers (Pliny c. AD 77, Apuleius c. AD 158, Numenius) — “Jannes” among the great magicians

The pair’s fame did not stay inside the synagogue and the church. Within a generation of 2 Timothy the name turns up in pagan Rome, in the literature of magic. In the history of the magic art that opens Book 30 of his Natural History (c. AD 77), the elder Pliny lists a Jewish “branch of magic… who derive their origin from Moses, Jannes, and Lotapea, Jews by birth” — ranking “Jannes” with Moses and Zoroaster among the very founders of the craft, though Pliny himself despised all magic as “an utter falsity.” Two generations later the orator Apuleius, on trial for sorcery about AD 158, dares his accusers to call him “that Carmendas or Damigeron or Moses or Iohannes… or whoever else has been celebrated among the mages since Zoroaster” (Apologia 90) — “Iohannes” being the same Jannes, a name he expects a Roman court to know at once. Both Latin writers name only Jannes, not Jambres — the “only one named” pattern of the Damascus Document again, and a datum for where the names came from. The third pagan notice, from the second-century Platonist Numenius of Apamea (preserved a century and a half later by Eusebius), instead retells the contest itself, naming “Jannes and Jambres, Egyptian sacred scribes, men judged to have no superiors in the practice of magic,” and — in the magicians’ favour — crediting them with power to blunt the worst of Moses’ plagues. Three outsiders, then, attest the same legend from beyond its Jewish and Christian homes: the figure had become common cultural property.4 Full detail »

The lost Book of Jannes and Jambres (composed c. 1st–3rd c. AD) — the apocryphon the church shelved

Behind the sparse early namings there once stood a whole book about the pair: the Book (or Apocryphon) of Jannes and Jambres, known to the Latin West as the Paenitentia Iamnis et Mambre, “the Penitence of Jannes and Mambres.” Composed in Greek in Roman Egypt — perhaps as early as the first century AD, and certainly before Origen quotes it about 250 — it now survives only in fragments: Greek papyri (the Vienna scroll, 3rd c.; the Chester Beatty codex, late 4th c.; Michigan and Heidelberg scraps), an eleventh-century Latin-and-Old-English leaf whose picture shows Jambres raising his dead brother’s ghost, and Ethiopic fragments. What the pieces preserve is a full legend of the magicians’ fate: their careers as Balaam’s sons, their defeat by Moses, Jannes’ deathbed confession that “Moses’s power is of God” (the “penitence” that titles the book), his violent death and descent to Hades, and Jambres’ necromancy summoning his shade to describe the torments of hell. It carried real weight in antiquity: Origen held that Paul drew the names of 2 Timothy 3:8 straight from this “secret book”; the pagan Platonist Numenius (preserved by Eusebius) ranked Jannes and Jambres among Egypt’s unrivalled sacred scribes; and about the sixth century the church formally listed the book among the rejected apocrypha in the Gelasian Decree — the act that shelved it and hastened its loss. Whether Paul quoted this book or the broader legend behind it, and whether it ever held anything like scriptural standing, are contested questions weighed on their own rather than settled here. In composition-order the book falls after the Damascus Document’s naming and before the fully-expansive Pseudo-Jonathan Targum; it is dated on this timeline by that composition, not by its later surviving copies.5 Full detail »

The Aramaic Targums (Pseudo-Jonathan; redacted c. 2nd–8th c. AD) — named at last, and tied to Balaam

After the sparse early namings comes not a single author but a whole reception stratum: the Aramaic Targums, the running synagogue paraphrases read alongside the Hebrew, whose written redaction spans several centuries. It is the latest and most expansive of them, Targum Pseudo-Jonathan, that first weaves the pair fully back into the story — and it does so three times. It supplies a scene Exodus lacks, in which Pharaoh dreams of a lamb outweighing all Egypt and “Jannis and Jambres, the chief of the magicians” read the omen and set the killing of the Hebrew boys in motion (Exodus 1:15); it names them again at the rod-contest itself, where “Janis and Jamberes, magicians of Mizraim,” match Moses “by their burnings of divination” (Exodus 7:11); and — the verse that binds this page to Balaam’s — it makes the two magicians the “two young men” who ride at Balaam’s side on the road to curse Israel (Numbers 22:22). The naming is distinctive to Pseudo-Jonathan: the restrained Onkelos keeps the anonymous “wise men and magicians” of the Hebrew. Two cautions carry into the argument pages. First, the spelling is unstable even within one translation — “Jannis and Jambres,” “Janis and Jamberes,” “Jannes and Jambres” in successive verses — a datum for the name-origin question. Second, Numbers 22:22 calls them Balaam’s “two young men” — his servants, not his sons; the familiar “sons of Balaam” is a later hardening of the link, and runs into the forty years that separate the Exodus contest from Balaam’s journey. This Targum verse is the primary text behind that Balaam-connection question.6 Full detail »

The Fathers on Paul’s source (Tertullian to Aquinas, c. 200–1274) — an unwritten tradition, or the lost Book?

The last stratum of the tradition is the church’s own, and it keeps returning to the puzzle this page began with: Paul names two magicians the Old Testament never names. Read across the centuries — from Tertullian about 200 to Thomas Aquinas about 1270 — the Fathers make two recurring moves. The first is to ask where Paul got the names, and on this they divide into two camps. The Antiochene and Byzantine commentators answer “unwritten Jewish tradition,” or else direct inspiration: John Chrysostom — “either they were handed down by tradition, or it is probable that Paul knew them by inspiration” — is echoed almost verbatim by Oecumenius and Theophylact of Ohrid, and stated flatly by Theodoret of Cyrus (“not from holy Scripture but from an unwritten Jewish tradition”); Theodore of Mopsuestia joins them by ridiculing the alternative, calling “particularly absurd… the notion that [Paul] had access to some information or writing now lost to us.” The Latin tradition holds precisely that alternative: Ambrosiaster says the example is drawn “from the apocrypha,” Aquinas that Paul “perhaps obtained their names from certain Jewish writings,” and both stand with Origen, who had named the lost Book of Jannes and Jambres as Paul’s source outright. The second move is typological — the pair as the standing figure of counterfeit power that mimics the truth and then collapses: Tertullian files them under “that other species of magic which operates by miracles” against Moses; the Apostolic Constitutions pairs them with Annas and Caiaphas as the two Testaments’ opponents; and Augustine and Fulgentius turn Paul’s verse against the heretics of their own day, whose “folly shall be manifest to all men, as theirs also was.” Gathered here as one patristic stratum, these witnesses span more than a millennium and are dated on this timeline by the earliest of them (Tertullian, c. 200); the deeper question of whether Paul actually quoted the book is weighed on its own.7 Full detail »

The medieval reception (the Acts of Pilate to the Zohar, c. 4th–13th c.) — the flying sorcerers and the necromancer’s book

Long after the Fathers had finished asking where Paul got the names, the two magicians went on living — in the church’s apocryphal tales and the synagogue’s legends alike. Christian writers press them into fresh service: in the Gospel of Nicodemus (Acts of Pilate) Nicodemus warns the council not to treat Jesus as Pharaoh treated Moses, since “Pharaoh had also two magicians, Jannes and Jambres… but not such as Moses did” — a test of divine origin (what is of God endures, what is mere magic collapses); and in the Acts of Peter and Paul the apostles warn Nero that Simon Magus, “as the Egyptians Jannes and Jambres led Pharaoh and his army astray until they were swallowed up in the sea,” will end the same way. Jewish tradition, meanwhile, keeps embroidering their fate: they join the mixed multitude, instigate the golden calf, turn proselyte — and die, in versions that cannot be reconciled, drowned in the Red Sea, slain after the calf, or (in the Zohar) flying “up into the air before the sword of Phinehas… until, by the power of the Ineffable Name, they were caught and slain.” And in the Latin West the pair, now fixed as “Jamnes and Mambres,” become bywords of the master necromancer — their book copied out as late as eleventh-century England and their legend feeding, in the long run, the Faust saga. How legend finally disposed of them is one of the contested questions this tradition leaves open.8 Full detail »

Modern scholarship (a survey) — the lost book reassembled, the names traced, the questions opened

Set against the ancient witnesses, critical study grades the whole tradition, and several results recur. First, the lost Book of Jannes and Jambres has been reassembled: Albert Pietersma’s Apocryphon of Jannes and Jambres the Magicians (Brill, 1994) edits it from fragments in half a dozen languages and dates the composition “perhaps as early as the first century C.E.,” tracing a legend told “from the Syriac east to the Latin west and from Egypt’s deserts to King Alfred’s court.” Second, the names are traced to a Jewish onomastic tradition — Yoḥana (a by-form of John) behind “Jannes,” an Aramaic root “to oppose” behind “Jambres”/“Mambres” — though a rival Egyptian derivation is still argued, and the tell-tale datum is that the earliest sources name only one of the pair. Third, the earliest naming, the Damascus Document’s “Yannes and his brother,” is read with care: it attests the pre-Christian Jewish substrate Paul draws on, but the leap to the fixed couplet is itself part of the tradition’s growth. Fourth, the “sons of Balaam” link is a hardening of the Targums’ looser “two young men,” and whether Paul quoted the book (Charlesworth, Koch) or merely the widespread legend behind it (Gero’s caution) remains divided. This survey opens the contested questions — where the names came from, whether the Damascus Document’s brother-pair is this pair, the Balaam sonship, and Paul’s source — but settles none of them; each is weighed on its own, on the evidence rather than on any prior assumption.9 Full detail »

Arguments

Where do the names “Jannes and Jambres” come from — and why does Jambres become Mambres? — plausible

The Old Testament never names Pharaoh’s magicians; Paul does. Where did the names come from, and why do they vary — the Latin West writing “Mambres” for Jambres? The verdict is plausible, with a sound core. That the names descend from a genuine, pre-Christian Jewish onomastic tradition — not a late or arbitrary invention — is well-grounded: the Damascus Document names one of the pair (“Yannes and his brother”) about 100 BC, a century and a half before 2 Timothy, and by Paul’s day the names travel without gloss into Pauline Greek and pagan Latin alike. The Western Mambres is a regular variant of Jambres (an initial j-/y- → m- shift; the rabbinic “Yoḥana and Mamre” already carries the Mamre form), not a second name. What falls short of probability is the specific etymology: the “Jannes = Yoḥana/John” identification is likely but is a match of form, not a proof of meaning; the “Jambres/Mambres = the opposer/rebel” reading is attractive largely because it fits the figure’s role; a rival Egyptian derivation (Ewald, Lauth, Freudenthal) is still argued; and the earliest sources name only one magician — so the fixed couplet, and the second name’s origin, are themselves a later, unstable development. Sound that the tradition is early and Jewish; contested on exactly where the names come from and what they mean.

Were Jannes and Jambres the sons of Balaam? — weak

A Jewish tradition makes the two magicians the kin of Balaam son of Beor — his “two young men” at Numbers 22:22 in Targum Pseudo-Jonathan, and his sons outright in the lost Book of Jannes and Jambres and the mediaeval Book of Jasher — knitting the Torah’s two great pagan opposers of God’s messenger into one family. The verdict is weak for the literal kinship. It is confined to the tradition’s latest and most legendary layers — unknown to the Damascus Document, to Paul, to the pagan writers, and to the Fathers (only the most expansive Targum names the link at all; Onkelos and Neofiti leave the magicians anonymous); its own proof-text calls them Balaam’s servants, not his sons; and the wilderness chronology runs so hard against it — the magicians are grown sorcerers at the Exodus, Balaam’s journey falls forty years later — that the Book of Jasher has to relocate Balaam into Pharaoh’s court to save it. What is sound is only the thinner thing the legend dramatizes: a genuine, old tradition deliberately paired the two figures because they are twin instances of one recurring type — the pagan magician who opposes God’s messenger and is overruled. The thematic kinship is real; the family tree read out of it is a downstream knitting-together, not a recoverable fact.

The lost Book of Jannes and Jambres: did Paul quote it, and was it ever Scripture? — contested / unsupported

Behind Paul’s bare pair of names once stood a whole apocryphon, the Book of Jannes and Jambres. Two questions ride on it, and they must be kept apart. Did Paul (2 Timothy 3:8) draw the names from that written book? The verdict is contested. Origen (c. 250) said he did — the phrase is “not found in the public books, but in the secret book… entitled the Book of Jannes and Mambres” — and Ambrosiaster and Aquinas followed; but Theodoret and Theodore of Mopsuestia held that Paul used “an unwritten Jewish tradition,” and the modern debate splits the same way (Charlesworth and Koch vs. Gero’s caution). The case for is real (the earliest reader to address it says yes; a genuine, early book on exactly these figures existed, and a Latin Father knew its plot); but so are the defeaters — the names were common lore a century and a half before Paul and independent of the book, the book’s date is loose enough that it may even post-date him, and Origen routinely assigned every unlocatable citation to a “secret book.” The datings are too soft to settle it. Was the book ever Scripture? Here the answer is a settled no (rated unsupported for any canonical status): no canon received it, and the Gelasian Decree lists it among the rejected apocrypha. And a “yes” to the first question would not change this — an inspired author’s use of a non-canonical source does not canonise the source, as the firmer Jude/1 Enoch case shows. The historical question (where did the names come from?) is live; the canonical one is not.

What became of Jannes and Jambres — converts, drowned, or slain at the calf? — weak

Scripture leaves the magicians’ end a blank: 2 Timothy 3:9 says only that “their folly shall be manifest… as theirs also was,” and Exodus stops at their confession of “the finger of God.” Later tradition rushed to fill the blank, and did so in at least four irreconcilable ways: they repented and turned proselyte (the strand that titles the lost apocryphon, the Paenitentia); they drowned with Pharaoh’s host in the Red Sea (the Acts of Peter and Paul); they instigated the golden calf and were slain in its purge (Tanhuma / Yelammedenu); or they flew up before Phinehas’ sword and were struck down by the Ineffable Name (the Zohar). The verdict is weak as history: the endings are mutually exclusive (one cannot both repent-and-live and drown, both drown before Sinai and die at the calf after it, still less fly before Phinehas forty years on), each is late and downstream of the legend it elaborates, and none reaches the event. The one honest positive is narrower: the penitence strand is the earliest and most distinctive of the four — a fact about how the tradition first told the story, not about two historical men.
