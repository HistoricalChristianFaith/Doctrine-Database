The Fourth Lateran Council on the Real Presence of Christ in the Eucharist

Dates: 1215 (reckoned by the Catholic Church the twelfth ecumenical council; convoked by Innocent III) · Wikipedia
Summary: In the opening confession of faith, Firmiter credimus (Canon 1), the Fourth Lateran Council uses the verb transsubstantiare, its earliest extant use in a conciliar decree: Christ's body and blood are "truly contained in the sacrament of the altar under the forms of bread and wine," the bread "being changed by divine power into the body, and the wine into the blood." In the same sentence it reserved the confecting of the sacrament to "the priest who has been duly ordained."

Claims & evidence

"Changed by divine power" (Canon 1, Firmiter credimus)

The council's profession of faith affirms the one Church "in which the priest and the sacrifice is Jesus Christ himself," and then states the eucharistic conversion with the technical verb:

whose body and blood are truly contained in the sacrament of the altar under the forms of bread and wine; the bread being changed (transsubstantiatio) by divine power into the body, and the wine into the blood, so that to realize the mystery of unity we may receive of Him what He has received of us. And this sacrament no one can effect except the priest who has been duly ordained in accordance with the keys of the Church, which Jesus Christ Himself gave to the Apostles and their successors.

— Fourth Lateran Council (1215), Canon 1 (Firmiter credimus), Fordham Medieval Sourcebook.1 This is the earliest extant use of the verb in a conciliar decree. The Catholic Encyclopedia ("Berengarius of Tours") credits the first use of the noun transubstantiatio to Hildebert of Lavardin, a contemporary of Berengar (c. 1079), while Philip Schaff (1884) credited its introduction to Paschasius Radbertus in the ninth century. It was used in a letter of Innocent III in 1202, before the council he convoked. The canon gives no Aristotelian substance/accidents analysis of the mode; Thomas Aquinas sets one out in the Summa Theologiae (III, q. 75).2

Reception and debate

  • Dated sequence. The council adopted as its name for the eucharistic change a term already in use in the schools. Entries on the timeline before and after it: the ninth-century dispute between Paschasius and Ratramnus; the oaths of 1059 and 1079 imposed on Berengar, the second speaking of "substantial conversion"; Lateran IV (1215); Aquinas's Aristotelian exposition; and the Council of Trent (1551), which defines the conversion as "suitably and properly called Transubstantiation."
  • Real Presence and ministerial priesthood in one clause. The decree's next words — "no one can effect [this sacrament] except the priest who has been duly ordained" — reserve the confecting of the sacrament to the ordained in the same sentence as the conversion of the elements; see also ministerial priesthood.
  • What the canon states. The council states that the bread and wine are changed into the body and blood by divine power, and names the change transsubstantiatio; it does not set out how. The metaphysics of substance and accidents is set out by Aquinas. How the doctrine relates to patristic teaching is disputed; see How does the transubstantiation defined in 1215 and 1551 relate to the Fathers' teaching on the eucharistic change?.
