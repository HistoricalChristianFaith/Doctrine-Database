John Chrysostom on the Real Presence

Dates: c. 392 · Wikipedia
Summary: The Antiochene preacher uses concrete, physical language of the Eucharist. In his Treatise on the Priesthood he stands the reader before the altar where "the Lord [is] sacrificed" and "He who sitteth on high with the Father is at that hour held in the hands of all"; in his homilies on 1 Corinthians he tells his hearers that "this which is in the cup is that which flowed from His side," and that the bread they receive is "the Body of Christ." In the same treatise his eucharistic language is joined to his account of the priest's office — the treatise is also cited on the ministerial priesthood timeline — and throughout to his calls for worthy reception.

Claims & evidence

"He who sitteth on high with the Father is at that hour held in the hands of all" (On the Priesthood 3.4)

For when thou seest the Lord sacrificed, and laid upon the altar, and the priest standing and praying over the victim, and all the worshippers empurpled with that precious blood, canst thou then think that thou art still amongst men, and standing upon the earth? … Oh! what a marvel! what love of God to man! He who sitteth on high with the Father is at that hour held in the hands of all, and gives Himself to those who are willing to embrace and grasp Him. And this all do through the eyes of faith!

— Treatise on the Priesthood (De Sacerdotio) 3.4, source (NPNF Series I, vol. 9). Comparing the eucharistic rite to Elijah's fire from heaven, Chrysostom writes: "There stands the priest, not bringing down fire from Heaven, but the Holy Spirit" (3.4). Compare Cyril of Jerusalem on the Spirit invoked on the gifts.

"This which is in the cup is that which flowed from His side" (Homily 24 on 1 Corinthians)

"The cup of blessing which we bless, is it not a communion of the Blood of Christ?". Very persuasively spake he, and awfully. For what he says is this: "This which is in the cup is that which flowed from His side, and of that do we partake."

Expounding Paul's "communion of the body," Chrysostom continues:

For what is the bread? The Body of Christ. And what do they become who partake of it? The Body of Christ: not many bodies, but one body.

— Homilies on First Corinthians, Homily 24 (on 1 Cor 10:16–17), source (NPNF Series I, vol. 12; cf. New Advent). Chrysostom also expounds Paul's verb "break": "that which He suffered not on the cross, this He suffers in the oblation for thy sake, and submits to be broken, that he may fill all men."

Reception and debate

  • Physical idiom. Where Origen and Clement write allegorically and Cyril of Jerusalem uses "type," Chrysostom uses physical language: the worshippers "empurpled with that precious blood," the enthroned Lord "held in the hands," the draught in the cup "that which flowed from His side." He sets out no theory of how the change occurs; the language comes in homilies and exhortations to awe and to worthy communion.
  • Presence, ethics and unity. Chrysostom turns this language to pastoral ends. From "we are that self-same body" he argues at once to charity — "why do we not also show forth the same love, and become also in this respect one?" — and elsewhere he warns that to approach with a defiled conscience is to be "guilty of the body and blood of the Lord." He links the presence both to the danger of unworthy communion and to the demand of mutual love.
  • Because the Treatise on the Priesthood makes the Eucharist the ground of the priest's dignity — "what priests do here below God ratifies above" — Chrysostom is also a witness on the ministerial priesthood timeline; there the emphasis falls on who may offer, here on what is offered.
