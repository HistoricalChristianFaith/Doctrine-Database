The Council of Trent on the Real Presence of Christ in the Eucharist

Dates: 11 October 1551 (Session 13 of the Council of Trent, reckoned by the Catholic Church the nineteenth ecumenical council; the third session under Pope Julius III; the Decree Concerning the Most Holy Sacrament of the Eucharist) · Wikipedia
Summary: Meeting during the Reformation controversies, Trent's thirteenth session defines transubstantiation — the substance of bread and wine changed into the body and blood of Christ — and draws from it a worship corollary: because, the decree states, Christ is wholly and truly present, the sacrament is owed the worship of latria, the adoration due to God alone, and may be reserved, venerated, and carried in procession.

Claims & evidence

Transubstantiation (Decree, ch. 4)

Chapter 4 of the decree defines the change of substance:

by the consecration of the bread and of the wine, a conversion is made of the whole substance of the bread into the substance of the body of Christ our Lord, and of the whole substance of the wine into the substance of his blood; which conversion is, by the holy Catholic Church, suitably and properly called Transubstantiation.

— Council of Trent, Session 13 (1551), Decree Concerning the Most Holy Eucharist, ch. 4, trans. J. Waterworth.1 The timeline sets this account beside the other views. How the definition relates to the Fathers' teaching is disputed; see How does the transubstantiation defined in 1215 and 1551 relate to the Fathers' teaching on the eucharistic change?. In the decree, the presence is the ground of the worship set out in chapter 5.

The worship of latria (Decree, ch. 5, and canon 6)

Chapter 5 draws the adoration of the sacrament from the presence:

Wherefore, there is no room left for doubt, that all the faithful of Christ may, according to the custom ever received in the Catholic Church, render in veneration the worship of latria, which is due to the true God, to this most holy sacrament.

— Council of Trent, Session 13 (1551), Decree Concerning the Most Holy Eucharist, ch. 5, trans. Waterworth.2 Latria is the technical term for the adoration due to God alone, as distinct from the dulia (honour) shown to the saints. The same session attaches an anathema to its denial:

If any one saith, that, in the holy sacrament of the Eucharist, Christ, the only-begotten Son of God, is not to be adored with the worship, even external of latria; and is, consequently, neither to be venerated with a special festive solemnity, nor to be solemnly borne about in processions… or, is not to be proposed publicly to the people to be adored, and that the adorers thereof are idolators; let him be anathema.

— Council of Trent, Session 13 (1551), Canon 6, trans. Waterworth.3 The canon names the practices the worship issues in — the feast of Corpus Christi ("a special festive solemnity"), eucharistic processions, and public exposition for adoration — and anathematizes the assertion that the adorers of the sacrament are idolaters.

Reservation (Decree, ch. 6)

Chapter 6 treats the keeping of the consecrated host beyond the liturgy as an ancient custom to be retained:

The custom of reserving the holy Eucharist in the sacrarium is so ancient, that even the age of the Nicene Council recognised that usage… (the holy Synod) declares that this custom, as most salutary and necessary, is to be retained.

— Council of Trent, Session 13 (1551), Decree, ch. 6, trans. Waterworth.4 The chapter's title joins reservation to "bearing it to the sick." Around 440, Cyril of Alexandria had written of the consecrated Eucharist kept to a later day that "the power of the consecration and his life-giving grace is perpetual in it."

Reception and debate

  • The Canon of the Mass. The same council’s Session 22 (1562) defended the Roman Canon against the Reformers’ cuts as “so pure from every error”; see Trent on lex orandi, lex credendi.
  • Earlier entries on the timeline. Tertullian (c. 211) writes of distress if any of the bread or cup falls to the ground, and Cyril of Alexandria (c. 440) holds that the reserved sacrament keeps its power; in 1551 Trent's Session 13 defines the worship of latria due to the sacrament and attaches anathemas to its denial.
  • The Reformers' objection. Canon 6 answers the Reformed objection that adoring the consecrated bread is creature-worship — idolatry. In the decree, the object of adoration is Christ present in the sacrament, and the worship rests on the prior definition of the real, substantial presence. Whether the presence is real, and in what sense, is disputed; see Disputed readings.
  • Appeal to received custom. The council presents adoration and reservation as the received custom of the church ("according to the custom ever received in the Catholic Church"; reservation "so ancient, that even the age of the Nicene Council recognised that usage"), not as innovations.
  • Translation provenance. The English is J. Waterworth's, The Canons and Decrees of the Sacred and Oecumenical Council of Trent (London: Dolman, 1848), a nineteenth-century English edition of the conciliar acts.
