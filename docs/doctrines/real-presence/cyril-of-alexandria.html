Cyril of Alexandria on the Real Presence of Christ in the Eucharist

Dates: c. 440 (the Letter to Calosyrius; Cyril d. 444) · Wikipedia
Summary: Against those who held that the consecrated Eucharist lost its power if it were kept and consumed on a later day, Cyril writes that Christ's body is not changed, and "the power of the consecration and his life-giving grace is perpetual in it."

Claims & evidence

The Eucharist reserved to a later day (Letter to Calosyrius §6)

In a letter to Calosyrius, bishop of Arsinoe, written against the Anthropomorphites, Cyril addresses the opinion that the Eucharist, if reserved to the following day rather than consumed at once, ceased to be efficacious. He calls the opinion senseless and answers:

Christ is not altered, nor is his holy body changed, but the power of the consecration and his life-giving grace is perpetual in it.

— Cyril of Alexandria, Letter to Calosyrius, Bishop of Arsinoe (Letter 83; Greek in Patrologia Graeca 76, 1075–76, and in Pusey’s edition), §6.1 The wording quoted here is that quoted by the U.S. Conference of Catholic Bishops (2001, citing PG 76, 1076); Wace’s Dictionary renders the sentence “Christ’s holy Body is not changed; but the power of consecration and the life-giving grace still remain in it.” The letter is also translated in full as Letter 83 in J. I. McEnerney’s Fathers of the Church volume, which renders the closing clause “the power of the consecration and his life-giving grace is perpetual in his body.”2 Cyril places the power of the consecration in Christ's unchanging body rather than in the moment of reception.

Placement on the timeline

  • Neighbouring entries. Earlier entries on the timeline speak of the elements becoming the body and blood (Ignatius, Irenaeus, Origen); Cyril's letter addresses whether the consecration keeps its power when the sacrament is reserved. On the handling of the consecrated elements, Tertullian writes of guarding them from spillage (c. 211), and the Council of Trent defines the worship of latria due to the sacrament (1551).
  • Source provenance. The Letter to Calosyrius is the same work as Cyril's tract Against the Anthropomorphites (its title runs “against those saying God is anthropomorphic”; §§1–5 refute the Anthropomorphites, §6 answers the eucharistic error, §7 the idle monks). It is numbered Letter 83 (= CPG 5383): the Greek is edited by P. E. Pusey (Sancti patris nostri Cyrilli… in D. Joannis Evangelium…, vol. iii, Oxford, 1872, pp. 603–607) and reprinted in Patrologia Graeca 76, 1075–76; and it is translated in full as Letter 83 in J. I. McEnerney, St. Cyril of Alexandria: Letters 51–110 (Fathers of the Church 77, 1987), pp. 109–111, and again in L. R. Wickham, Cyril of Alexandria: Select Letters (Oxford, 1983), pp. 214–221. The rendering quoted above is the one quoted in modern Catholic catechetical sources (e.g. the U.S. bishops' The Real Presence of Jesus Christ in the Sacrament of the Eucharist, citing PG 76, 1076); Wace's Dictionary gives a different rendering (note 1).3
