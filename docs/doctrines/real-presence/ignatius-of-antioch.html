Ignatius of Antioch on the Real Presence

Dates: c. 108 · Wikipedia
Summary: The bishop of Antioch, writing on his way to martyrdom (c. 108), is the earliest post-apostolic writer on this timeline: he calls the Eucharist "the flesh of our Saviour Jesus Christ," desires "the bread of God … which is the flesh of Jesus Christ" and "the drink of God, namely His blood," and names the one bread "the medicine of immortality." He describes the Docetists, who deny that Christ had real flesh, as abstaining from the Eucharist because they do not confess it to be "the flesh of our Saviour Jesus Christ."

Claims & evidence

The Docetists' abstention from the Eucharist (Smyrnaeans 7)

They abstain from the Eucharist and from prayer, because they confess not the Eucharist to be the flesh of our Saviour Jesus Christ, which suffered for our sins, and which the Father, of His goodness, raised up again.

— Epistle to the Smyrnaeans 7 (shorter recension), source (cf. New Advent). Ignatius identifies the Docetists by their abstention from the Eucharist, and gives the reason: because they will not "confess … the Eucharist to be the flesh of our Saviour."

"The bread of God … which is the flesh of Jesus Christ" (Romans 7)

I have no delight in corruptible food, nor in the pleasures of this life. I desire the bread of God, the heavenly bread, the bread of life, which is the flesh of Jesus Christ, the Son of God, who became afterwards of the seed of David and Abraham; and I desire the drink of God, namely His blood, which is incorruptible love and eternal life.

— Epistle to the Romans 7 (shorter recension), source (cf. New Advent). Longing for martyrdom, Ignatius disclaims earthly food and reaches instead for "the bread of God … which is the flesh of Jesus Christ" and "the drink of God, namely His blood" — phrases that echo John 6 ("the bread of life," "my flesh … my blood").

The one bread is "the medicine of immortality" (Ephesians 20)

… breaking one and the same bread, which is the medicine of immortality, and the antidote to prevent us from dying, but [which causes] that we should live for ever in Jesus Christ.

— Epistle to the Ephesians 20 (shorter recension), source (cf. New Advent). The single loaf broken in the one assembly, under the one bishop, is "the medicine of immortality" (pharmakon athanasias) and "the antidote to prevent us from dying."

Reception and debate

  • Text. Ignatius is the earliest post-apostolic writer on this timeline; the middle recension of his seven letters is followed throughout (the longer recension's expansions are later interpolations, ⚠ unverified, and are not relied on here).
  • One Eucharist under the one bishop. Ignatius's eucharistic language is joined to his insistence that there is one Eucharist under the one bishop. That ecclesial / ministerial strand ("Let that be deemed a proper Eucharist, which is [administered] either by the bishop, or by one to whom he has entrusted it," Smyrnaeans 8) bears on the ministry of the Eucharist rather than the Real Presence as such, and is treated separately.
  • How Ignatius's "flesh and blood" language bears on the literal-or-figurative reading of the Eucharist is disputed; see Disputed readings. His letters do not address the mode of the presence, the question of the later scholastics.
