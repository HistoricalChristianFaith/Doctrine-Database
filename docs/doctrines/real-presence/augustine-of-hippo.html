Augustine of Hippo on the Eucharist

Dates: c. 412 · Wikipedia
Summary: Augustine (354–430), bishop of Hippo, is cited by later parties on both sides of the eucharistic debate. To the newly baptized he says that "the bread is the body of Christ, the cup is the blood of Christ," and in the Expositions on the Psalms he writes that "no one eateth that flesh, unless he hath first worshipped … we sin in not worshipping." In On Christian Doctrine he gives John 6's "eat my flesh" as an example of a figure; in the Tractates on John he reads the eating as incorporation into the body of Christ (the Church) and tells his hearers "believe, and thou hast eaten already." How these passages relate is disputed; see How is Augustine's eucharistic language of sign and figure to be read?.

The sermons and the Expositions on the Psalms

To the newly baptized: "the bread is the body of Christ, the cup is the blood of Christ"

Therefore, what you see is bread and a cup; your eyes also report this to you: but what your faith demands to be instructed, the bread is the body of Christ, the cup is the blood of Christ. … These, brothers, are called Sacraments, because in them one thing is seen, another is understood. What is seen has a corporeal appearance, what is understood bears spiritual fruit. … your mystery lies on the Lord's table: you receive your mystery. … Be what you see, and receive what you are.

— Sermon 272, preached on Pentecost to the newly baptized, historicalchristian.faith.1 In the same sermon Augustine calls what lies on the altar the communicants' own "mystery," for they are the body of Christ (1 Corinthians 12:27), and tells them, "Be what you see, and receive what you are." His Easter sermon to the newly baptized says: "That Bread which you see on the altar, consecrated by the word of God, is the Body of Christ … If you have received worthily, you are what you have received" (Sermon 227).2

"No one eats that flesh unless he has first worshipped … we sin in not worshipping"

For He took upon Him earth from earth; because flesh is from earth, and He received flesh from the flesh of Mary. And because He walked here in very flesh, and gave that very flesh to us to eat for our salvation; and no one eateth that flesh, unless he hath first worshipped: we have found out in what sense such a footstool of our Lord's may be worshipped, and not only that we sin not in worshipping it, but that we sin in not worshipping.

— Expositions on the Book of Psalms, on Psalm 98(99), §8 (the Vulgate Enarratio in Psalmum 98.9), on "fall down before His footstool," historicalchristian.faith.3 Here the flesh given to eat is the flesh received from Mary, and Augustine says that it is sin not to worship it. The passage is cited for the adoration of the sacrament (see "Later citation" below). The same exposition continues: "But doth the flesh give life? Our Lord Himself … said, 'It is the Spirit that quickeneth, the flesh profiteth nothing.'" The disciples who left "thought of it carnally, and imagined that the Lord would cut off parts from His body."

On Christian Doctrine and the Tractates on John

"Except ye eat the flesh of the Son of man" as a figure (On Christian Doctrine III.16.24)

If the sentence is one of command, either forbidding a crime or vice, or enjoining an act of prudence or benevolence, it is not figurative. If, however, it seems to enjoin a crime or vice, or to forbid an act of prudence or benevolence, it is figurative. "Except ye eat the flesh of the Son of man," says Christ, "and drink His blood, ye have no life in you." This seems to enjoin a crime or a vice; it is therefore a figure, enjoining that we should have a share in the sufferings of our Lord, and that we should retain a sweet and profitable memory of the fact that His flesh was wounded and crucified for us.

— On Christian Doctrine (De Doctrina Christiana) III.16.24, historicalchristian.faith.4 Augustine states a hermeneutical rule — a command that would be a crime taken literally must be figurative — and applies it to John 6:53, glossing the "eating" as sharing in Christ's Passion and holding "a sweet and profitable memory" of the crucifixion. The passage is cited for a figurative reading (see "Later citation" below); how it relates to the sermons and the Psalms is disputed (see the Summary).

Eating as dwelling in Christ: the sacrament and the thing signified (Tractates on John 26)

This it is, therefore, for a man to eat that meat and to drink that drink, to dwell in Christ, and to have Christ dwelling in him. Consequently, he that dwelleth not in Christ, and in whom Christ dwelleth not, doubtless neither eateth His flesh [spiritually] nor drinketh His blood [although he may press the sacrament of the body and blood of Christ carnally and visibly with his teeth], but rather doth he eat and drink the sacrament of so great a thing to his own judgment.

— Tractates on the Gospel of John 26.18 (on John 6:56), historicalchristian.faith.5 In this Tractate Augustine distinguishes the outward sacramentum from the inward res (the thing signified). The unworthy communicant "presses the sacrament … with his teeth" but does not eat Christ's flesh, because to eat it is to "dwell in Christ." The same Tractate exclaims: "O mystery of piety! O sign of unity! O bond of charity! He that would live has where to live, has whence to live. Let him draw near, let him believe; let him be embodied, that he may be made to live"; and it says that the Lord "would have this meat and drink to be understood as meaning the fellowship of His own body and members, which is the holy Church."

"Believe, and thou hast eaten already"

This is then to eat the meat, not that which perisheth, but that which endureth unto eternal life. To what purpose dost thou make ready teeth and stomach? Believe, and thou hast eaten already.

— Tractates on the Gospel of John 25.12 (on John 6:29), historicalchristian.faith.6 Here the eating that gives eternal life is believing. The line belongs to Augustine's comment on "the work of God, that ye believe on Him whom He hath sent" (John 6:29), earlier in the discourse than the "flesh" sayings of 6:53–56. It is cited by the Reformers (see "Later citation" below).

"The flesh profiteth nothing" (Tractates on John 27)

"It is the Spirit that quickeneth; the flesh profiteth nothing." … It profiteth nothing, but only in the manner in which they understood it. They indeed understood the flesh, just as when cut to pieces in a carcass, or sold in the shambles; not as when it is quickened by the Spirit. … "The words that I have spoken to you are spirit and life." … Hast thou understood spiritually? "They are spirit and life." Hast thou understood carnally? So also "are they spirit and life," but are not so to thee.

— Tractates on the Gospel of John 27.5 (on John 6:63), historicalchristian.faith.7 Augustine applies "the flesh profiteth nothing" (John 6:63) to the flesh as the hearers understood it, "as when cut to pieces in a carcass"; joined to the Spirit, he says, the same flesh "profiteth very much," for "the Word was made flesh" and "it is by the flesh that the Spirit has done somewhat for our salvation." In his reading the words are "spirit and life" to the one who has "understood spiritually."

Reception and debate

  • Later citation. Medieval and Catholic writers cite the footstool passage (Enarr. in Ps. 98.9) for the adoration of the sacrament; Ratramnus of Corbie, Berengar, Wycliffe, and the Reformers (Zwingli, Calvin, Cranmer) cite On Christian Doctrine III.16 and the Tractates for a figurative-spiritual reading (⚠ unverified).
  • The disputed reading. How Augustine's language of sign and figure is to be read is disputed; see How is Augustine's eucharistic language of sign and figure to be read? and Disputed readings.
