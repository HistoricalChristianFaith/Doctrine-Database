Gregory of Nyssa on the Real Presence

Dates: c. 383 (The Great Catechism, Oratio Catechetica Magna, c. 383–386; Gregory's lifespan c. 335–395) · Wikipedia
Summary: In chapter 37 of The Great Catechism Gregory writes that the bread consecrated by the Word "is changed into the Body of God the Word," and God by the benediction "transelements" (metastoicheiōsis) the natural quality of the visible things into the immortal Body. The change is worked "at once … by means of the Word," not by digestion. The editor of the NPNF translation compares this teaching to "the later transubstantiation."

Claims & evidence

The consecrated bread "is changed into the Body of God the Word" — "at once … by means of the Word" (Great Catechism 37)

Rightly, then, do we believe that now also the bread which is consecrated by the Word of God is changed into the Body of God the Word. For that Body was once, by implication, bread, but has been consecrated by the inhabitation of the Word that tabernacled in the flesh. … not that it advances by the process of eating to the stage of passing into the body of the Word, but it is at once changed into the body by means of the Word, as the Word itself said, "This is My Body."

— The Great Catechism 37, source (NPNF Series II, vol. 5; cf. New Advent). Gregory argues that as the body Christ took was maintained by bread and yet "was transmuted to the dignity of Godhead" by the indwelling Word, so now the consecrated bread is changed into that same Body. He denies that the change is the ordinary conversion of food into flesh by eating: it happens at once, by the Word and the "sacred Benediction," on the model of Christ's own "This is my body."

God "transelements" the visible things into the immortal Body (Great Catechism 37)

He gives these gifts by virtue of the benediction through which He transelements the natural quality of these visible things to that immortal thing.

— The Great Catechism 37, source. The verb Gregory uses here is metastoicheioō, "to transelement," to change the underlying elements. The NPNF editor notes that if the received text is sound, "Gregory distinctly teaches a transmutation of the elements very like the later transubstantiation," and "distinctly teaches that the words of consecration effect the change."

Reception and debate

  • The "antidote." Gregory reaches this through his "antidote" theme: humanity, poisoned by sin and death, needs the immortal Body taken in as medicine so that, "like a little leaven," it may assimilate the mortal body to its own incorruption.
  • Vocabulary. Gregory speaks of "transelementing" the quality of the visible things; he does not use the scholastic distinction of substance and accidents found in the decree of the Council of Trent (1551). How this change-language bears on the reading of the institution texts is disputed; see Disputed readings.
  • Word and benediction. Gregory locates the change in the Word and the benediction ("This is My Body"). Compare Ambrose (c. 390) on the words of Christ and Cyril of Jerusalem (c. 350) on the invocation of the Spirit.
