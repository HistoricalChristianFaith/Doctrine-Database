John Calvin on the Real Presence of Christ in the Eucharist

Dates: c. 1541 (the Short Treatise on the Lord's Supper sets out his mediating doctrine; the mature statement quoted here is the 1559 Institutes IV.17; Calvin 1509–1564) · Wikipedia
Summary: The Geneva reformer took a position between Zwingli's reading of "is" as "signifies" and Luther's bodily presence. Christ, he taught, is truly received in the Supper — but spiritually: the Holy Spirit lifts the believer up to feed on the true body and blood of the ascended Christ in heaven, so the bread and wine remain bread and wine yet are, for Calvin, not bare signs but efficacious instruments through which the reality is given. Against Zwingli he insisted the sacrament "truly exhibits" what it signifies; against Catholic teaching and Luther he denied that Christ's body is affixed to or enclosed in the bread, or present in a manner he called carnal; and of the mode he confessed he "rather feel[s] than understand[s] it." Reformed confessions from the Belgic Confession (1561) to the Westminster Confession (1646) teach a spiritual feeding on Christ by faith.

Claims & evidence

Two faults to avoid (Institutes IV.17.5)

Calvin frames his doctrine as the avoidance of two opposite faults — setting too little value on the signs, and extolling them immoderately:

Moreover, two faults are here to be avoided. We must neither, by setting too little value on the signs, dissever them from their meanings to which they are in some degree annexed, nor by immoderately extolling them, seem somewhat to obscure the mysteries themselves.

— Institutes of the Christian Religion IV.17.5.1

Calvin on the signs as not empty: the Spirit "truly exhibits" what they signify

Calvin holds that the Supper does not merely picture a grace received elsewhere; it conveys it, "by there exerting an efficacy of the Spirit":

That sacred communion of flesh and blood by which Christ transfuses his life into us, just as if it penetrated our bones and marrow, he testifies and seals in the Supper, and that not by presenting a vain or empty sign, but by there exerting an efficacy of the Spirit by which he fulfils what he promises.

So the sign and the thing are not to be pulled apart: "if by the breaking of bread the Lord truly represents the partaking of his body, there ought to be no doubt whatever that he truly exhibits and performs it" (Institutes IV.17.10).2

The Spirit as the bond: feeding on the ascended Christ in heaven

Luther held the body present in the bread; Calvin held that the believer is lifted up to the body. Christ's flesh remains in heaven, seated at the Father's right hand; the Holy Spirit is the "channel" who bridges the distance:

The bond of that connection, therefore, is the Spirit of Christ, who unites us to him, and is a kind of channel by which everything that Christ has and is, is derived to us.

Hence the ancient sursum corda — "lift up your hearts" — is for Calvin the whole posture of the rite: not Christ pulled down to the altar, but the communicant's mind raised to heaven.

And for no other reason was it formerly the custom, previous to consecration, to call aloud upon the people to raise their hearts, sursum corda… whenever it makes mention of him, enjoins us to raise our minds upwards and seek him in heaven, seated at the right hand of the Father.

— Institutes IV.17.12 and IV.17.36.3

The mode is a mystery he feels rather than understands

Pressed on how Christ's flesh, in heaven, becomes the food of the soul on earth, Calvin declines to theorize and confesses the limit:

Now, should any one ask me as to the mode, I will not be ashamed to confess that it is too high a mystery either for my mind to comprehend or my words to express; and to speak more plainly, I rather feel than understand it.

— Institutes IV.17.32.4 For Calvin it is a true reception without a local presence: "He declares that his flesh is the meat, his blood the drink, of my soul; I give my soul to him to be fed with such food… I have no doubt that he will truly give and I receive."

Reception and debate

  • Between Zwingli and Luther. Calvin's "spiritual real presence" stands between the two other Reformation positions on this timeline. Against Zwingli he denies the signs are bare tokens of an absent Christ; against Luther and Catholic teaching he holds that the presence of Christ in the Supper must be "such as neither affixes him to the element of bread, nor encloses him in bread, nor circumscribes him in any way" (Institutes IV.17.19).5 For Calvin the reality is received — by the Spirit, and by faith, the believer feeding on the Christ who remains in heaven.
  • Consensus Tigurinus (1549). The "Zurich Agreement," negotiated chiefly by Calvin and Heinrich Bullinger (Zwingli's successor), reconciled Geneva and Zurich on the Supper into a common Reformed position — rejecting any local presence or grace "enclosed" in the elements. It was followed in the 1550s by renewed controversy with the Lutheran Joachim Westphal.
  • Confessional reception. The Belgic Confession (1561, art. 35),6 the Heidelberg Catechism (1563, QQ. 75–79),7 the Church of England's Thirty-Nine Articles (1571, art. 28 — the body of Christ "is given, taken, and eaten… only after an heavenly and spiritual manner"),8 and the Westminster Confession (1646, ch. 29)9 all teach a feeding on Christ that is spiritual and by faith.
  • Translation note. The renderings above are the Beveridge translation of the Institutes. The Battles translation gives IV.17.32 as "a secret too lofty… I rather experience than understand it"; the sense is the same.
