Berengar of Tours on the Real Presence of Christ in the Eucharist

Dates: c. 1070 (De Sacra Coena, his reply to Lanfranc; Berengar c. 999–1088) · Wikipedia
Summary: Berengar, master of the school of Tours, drew on the treatise of Ratramnus and denied a material change of the elements: after the consecration the bread and wine remain bread and wine, and are the sign or sacrament of Christ's body and blood, received in spirit and by faith. His teaching was condemned at a series of synods; in 1059 he was made to swear an oath that the body of Christ is crushed by the teeth of the faithful, and in 1079 a second oath affirming a "substantial conversion" of the bread and wine.

Claims & evidence

The bread remains bread and is the sign of Christ's body — received spiritually, by faith

Against Lanfranc of Bec, Berengar argued in De Sacra Coena ("On the Holy Supper") that the consecration does not annihilate or physically transmute the bread; it makes it a sacrament — a sign that truly conveys the reality it signifies to the believing soul. He distinguishes the outward sign received in the mouth from the inward reality (the res sacramenti) received by faith:

Eternal salvation is given us if we receive with a pure heart the body of Christ, that is, the reality of the sign [rem sacramenti], while we are receiving the body of Christ in sign [in sacramento], that is, in the holy bread of the altar, which belongs to the temporal order.

— Berengar of Tours, De Sacra Coena adversus Lanfrancum (ed. Beekenkamp, 1941, p. 158), as translated in "Berengar of Tours," New Catholic Encyclopedia.1 The bread "belongs to the temporal order" — visible, changeable — whereas Christ's body, Berengar urged, cannot be subjected to time or change; so the sacrament is Christ's body in sign, not by a physical presence of the flesh on the altar.

Berengar's account of the sign and its reality

Berengar rejected the charge that he reduced the sacrament to a mere token. He would, he insisted, venerate the consecrated bread as the body of Christ — it is bread, "but not bread alone" — for through consecration the element becomes the efficacious sign of Christ's body, received in the soul. Charles E. Sheedy, in his 1947 study of the controversy, sums up his position: for Berengar "the Holy Eucharist, after the consecration as before, is bread and wine, not the Body of Christ, but the sign of the Body of Christ," the material elements receiving "a 'conversion,' not of their natural being, but of their spiritual significance."2

The oaths of 1059 and 1079

Synods condemned Berengar's teaching at Rome and Vercelli (1050), Tours (1055), Rome (1059), Poitiers (1075) and Rome (1078–79). At the Roman synod of 1059, under Nicholas II, he was made to swear an oath drafted by Cardinal Humbert of Silva Candida that the bread and wine after consecration are "the true body and blood of our Lord Jesus Christ, and are sensibly (sensualiter)… handled and broken by the hands of the priests, and crushed by the teeth of the faithful." The oath of 1079, under Gregory VII, states that the bread and wine are "substantially converted" (substantialiter converti) into Christ's flesh and blood — described as the earliest extant appearance of that phrase in an official ecclesiastical document (⚠ unverified).3

Reception and debate

  • Ratramnus, the oaths, and 1215. Berengar drew on the treatise of Ratramnus, which circulated under the name of John Scotus Eriugena; Philip Schaff records that at the synod of Vercelli (1050) Berengar was condemned and "the book of Ratramnus on the eucharist was burned".4 The synods that condemned Berengar issued the "crushed by the teeth" oath of 1059 and the "substantial conversion" formula of 1079; the Fourth Lateran Council defined transsubstantiatio in 1215. How this sequence relates to the Fathers' teaching is disputed; see How does the transubstantiation defined in 1215 and 1551 relate to the Fathers' teaching on the eucharistic change?.

Beekenkamp, De Sacra Coena adversus Lanfrancum (The Hague, 1941), re-edited as Rescriptum contra Lanfrannum (CCCM 84, ed. R. B. C. Huygens).
