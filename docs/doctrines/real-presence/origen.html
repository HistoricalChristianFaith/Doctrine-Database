Origen on the Real Presence

Dates: c. 248 · Wikipedia
Summary: In Against Celsus (Contra Celsum, c. 248), Origen answers the pagan Celsus — who held that men must offer thanks and first-fruits to the demons set over the things of this world — by insisting that Christians give thanks instead to the Creator. In that context he writes that "we also eat the bread presented to us; and this bread becomes by prayer a sacred body, which sanctifies those who sincerely partake of it." In this wording the bread becomes "a sacred body," it does so by prayer, and the result sanctifies those who partake. Elsewhere, in commenting on John 6 and on Leviticus, Origen reads the eating of Christ's flesh allegorically, as the feeding of the soul on the Word.

Claims & evidence

The bread "becomes by prayer a sacred body, which sanctifies those who sincerely partake of it" (Against Celsus VIII.33)

For this reason, then, let Celsus, as one who knows not God, give thank-offerings to demons. But we give thanks to the Creator of all, and, along with thanksgiving and prayer for the blessings we have received, we also eat the bread presented to us; and this bread becomes by prayer a sacred body, which sanctifies those who sincerely partake of it.

— Against Celsus (Contra Celsum) VIII.33, source (cf. CCEL, ANF vol. 4; New Advent). The argument is anti-pagan: Celsus urged thank-offerings to the demons who govern earthly provision, and Origen counters that Christians receive their food with thanksgiving to its Maker. The eucharistic bread is his chief example: it "becomes" a "sacred body" by prayer, and it "sanctifies those who sincerely partake of it."

Reception and debate

  • Three features of the wording: the verb, the bread becomes ("γίνεται") a sacred body; the agency, by prayer, an act of the gathered Church; and the effect, it sanctifies the communicant who "sincerely" partakes.
  • Origen's exegesis is often allegorical. In commenting on John 6 and on Leviticus he repeatedly turns the "eating" of Christ's flesh toward the feeding of the soul on the Word, warning against a literal construal.
