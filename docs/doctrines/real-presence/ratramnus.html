Ratramnus of Corbie on the Real Presence of Christ in the Eucharist

Dates: c. 843 (De Corpore et Sanguine Domini, written for Charles the Bald; Ratramnus d. c. 868) · Wikipedia
Summary: Asked by the emperor Charles the Bald whether the faithful receive Christ's body in the Eucharist "in a mystery" or "in very truth," Ratramnus — a monk of the same abbey of Corbie as Paschasius Radbertus — wrote that "that Bread and that Wine, figuratively, are the Body and Blood of Christ," distinguished "the Sacrament of the Body" from "the substance of the Body," and denied that the eucharistic body is in every respect the body born of Mary; the elements, he adds, are "not mere empty figures, or naked signs." His treatise has been read in different ways: the Protestant church historian Philip Schaff read it as making the elements Christ's body and blood "only in a spiritual sense to the faith of believers," and Thomas Cranmer credited it in his own change of view, while Michael Ott (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1913) and, earlier, John Fisher read it as consistent with Catholic doctrine.

Claims & evidence

Ratramnus on the elements as Christ's body "figuratively"

Charles the Bald put two questions to Ratramnus: whether the body and blood of Christ are received in the Eucharist in mysterio (in a mystery) or in veritate (in very truth), and whether it is the same body that was born of Mary and suffered. Ratramnus answers:

that Bread and that Wine, figuratively, are the Body and Blood of Christ… there is a great difference betwixt the visible appearance, and the invisible substance; that is, betwixt the Sacrament of the Body, and the substance of the Body.

— Ratramnus, De Corpore et Sanguine Domini, as translated in the parallel Latin/English edition Bertram, or Ratram, concerning the Body and Blood of the Lord (Oxford, 1687), Oxford Text Archive.1 Where Paschasius taught a strict identity of the sacramental and the historical body, Ratramnus distinguishes the visible sacrament from the "invisible substance" of the body, and denies that the eucharistic body is, without qualification, "the very body which was born of Mary, suffered, died, and was buried."

A figure, yet "not mere empty figures, or naked signs"

Ratramnus qualifies his "figure": the bread and wine are figures, but not empty ones:

not the True Body of Christ, but the Figure, or Sacrament of it… and yet not mere empty figures, or naked signs.

— Bertram, or Ratram, concerning the Body and Blood of the Lord (Oxford, 1687), Oxford Text Archive.2 He equates the "figure" with the "sacrament" of the body, while denying Paschasius's identity of the sacramental and the historical body.

Reception and debate

  • The Carolingian dispute — two monks of one house. Ratramnus and Paschasius Radbertus were both monks of Corbie, both wrote a De Corpore et Sanguine Domini for Charles the Bald, and their answers differ. The two treatises are cited in the disputed reading How does the transubstantiation defined in 1215 and 1551 relate to the Fathers' teaching on the eucharistic change?
  • A treatise with a long afterlife. The treatise came to circulate under the name of Ratramnus's contemporary John Scotus Eriugena, and Berengar of Tours drew on it in the eleventh century; Philip Schaff records that at the synod of Vercelli (1050) Berengar was condemned and "the book of Ratramnus on the eucharist was burned."3
  • How the treatise has been read. Michael Ott (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1913) reports that "a few Catholics and most Protestants" took Ratramnus to teach "a merely symbolic presence," a view that "Various German, French, and English translations made by the Sacramentarians" reinforced; Ott attributes that view to "a wrong interpretation of the words 'figura' and 'veritas'," reads Ratramnus's "invisible substance" as truly the body and blood of Christ, and records that John Fisher, bishop of Rochester, "cited it in defence of the Catholic doctrine on the Eucharist" (1527). Ott also records that the treatise was first printed at Cologne in 1532, placed on the Index of Prohibited Books in 1559, and removed in 1900.4 Philip Schaff (Protestant church historian, History of the Christian Church, vol. IV) reads Ratramnus as teaching that the elements "remain in reality as well as for the sensual perception what they were before the consecration," and are "the body and blood of Christ only in a spiritual sense to the faith of believers"; Ratramnus "calls the consecrated bread and wine figures and pledges of the body and blood of Christ." Schaff records that the tract, with Bullinger's writings and the influence of Ridley, Peter Martyr and Bucer, "produced a change in Archbishop Cranmer," and that "Mabillon, Natalis Alexander, and Boileau have defended the catholic orthodoxy of Ratramnus."5 The Reformed historical theologian John W. Riggs (The Lord's Supper in the Reformed Tradition, 2015) is reported as placing Ratramnus, with Hrabanus Maurus, among defenders of "Augustine's view of nonmetabolic real presence" (⚠ unverified).6
  • Figure vocabulary. Ratramnus's in figura shares its vocabulary with Cyril of Jerusalem's "type"/"antitype" and Augustine's "sign." How Augustine's "sign" and "figure" language is to be read is disputed; see How is Augustine's eucharistic language of sign and figure to be read?
