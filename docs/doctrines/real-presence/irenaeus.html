Irenaeus of Lyons on the Real Presence

Dates: c. 180–189 (Against Heresies, composed at Lyons) · Wikipedia
Summary: Writing against the Gnostics, who despised the flesh and denied that the material creation could be redeemed, Irenaeus argues from the Eucharist to the goodness of the body. His argument runs the opposite way to theirs: because the bread and the cup become the body and blood of Christ, and because our flesh is nourished by them, the flesh is capable of resurrection. The earthly bread, "when it receives the invocation of God, is no longer common bread, but the Eucharist, consisting of two realities, earthly and heavenly"; and our bodies, fed by it, "are no longer corruptible, having the hope of the resurrection." He speaks explicitly of a change in the elements worked by the word or invocation over them, and ties that change to the resurrection of the body.

Claims & evidence

The bread, "when it receives the invocation of God, is no longer common bread, but the Eucharist, consisting of two realities, earthly and heavenly" (Against Heresies IV.18.5)

But our opinion is in accordance with the Eucharist, and the Eucharist in turn establishes our opinion. For we offer to Him His own, announcing consistently the fellowship and union of the flesh and Spirit. For as the bread, which is produced from the earth, when it receives the invocation of God, is no longer common bread, but the Eucharist, consisting of two realities, earthly and heavenly; so also our bodies, when they receive the Eucharist, are no longer corruptible, having the hope of the resurrection to eternity.

— Against Heresies IV.18.5, source (cf. New Advent). The bread, after the invocation of God spoken over it, "is no longer common bread." He calls the result a thing "consisting of two realities, earthly and heavenly," and draws a parallel: as the bread is no longer common bread, so the bodies that receive the Eucharist "are no longer corruptible, having the hope of the resurrection to eternity."

"The Eucharist of the blood and the body of Christ is made" (Against Heresies V.2.2–3)

He has acknowledged the cup (which is a part of the creation) as His own blood, from which He bedews our blood; and the bread (also a part of the creation) He has established as His own body, from which He gives increase to our bodies. When, therefore, the mingled cup and the manufactured bread receives the Word of God, and the Eucharist of the blood and the body of Christ is made, from which things the substance of our flesh is increased and supported, how can they affirm that the flesh is incapable of receiving the gift of God, which is life eternal, which [flesh] is nourished from the body and blood of the Lord, and is a member of Him?

— Against Heresies V.2.2–3, source (cf. New Advent). The cup is acknowledged "as His own blood," the bread "established as His own body," and when they "receive the Word of God" the "Eucharist of the blood and the body of Christ is made." The passage is directed against the Gnostics: our flesh, Irenaeus argues, which is "nourished from the body and blood of the Lord," cannot be incapable of the gift of eternal life, since it is fed on the body and blood of the Lord and is "a member of Him."

Reception and debate

  • Two features of these passages. First, the change: the bread, after the invocation/word over it, "is no longer common bread," and "the Eucharist … is made." Second, the agency: the change is tied to "the invocation of God" and to the bread's "receiv[ing] the Word of God." Origen (c. 248) likewise speaks of bread that "becomes by prayer a sacred body."
  • The "two realities, earthly and heavenly" phrase is cited in the later debate over how the two relate: whether the earthly substance remains (the Lutheran position) or is changed beneath the appearances (the doctrine of transubstantiation). Irenaeus does not address that question; his argument in these chapters concerns the goodness of the flesh, which receives the heavenly gift.
  • The same stretch of the work (IV.17–18) carries Irenaeus's statement on the Eucharist as the Church's offering. Christ "took that created thing, bread, and gave thanks, and said, 'This is My body.' And the cup likewise … He confessed to be His blood, and taught the new oblation of the new covenant; which the Church receiving from the apostles, offers to God throughout all the world" — the "pure sacrifice" offered "in every place" that Malachi foretold (Against Heresies IV.17.5).1 Irenaeus adds that God "stood in need" of nothing: the oblation is the Church's grateful offering of creation's first-fruits, not a gift that supplies a divine lack. The eucharistic sacrifice is a distinct doctrine from the presence in the elements; it is noted here because Irenaeus treats the offering and the presence in the same chapters.
  • The sentence “our opinion is in accordance with the Eucharist, and the Eucharist in turn establishes our opinion” is also cited on the lex orandi timeline, on the relation of worship to belief.
