Tertullian on the Real Presence of Christ in the Eucharist

Dates: c. 211 (the likely date of De Corona) · Wikipedia
Summary: Tertullian witnesses less to a theory of the elements than to how the early North-African church handled them: the consecrated bread and wine were guarded so carefully that to let a crumb or a drop fall to the ground was felt as a grief. Against Marcion he also writes that Christ made the bread "His own body, by saying, 'This is my body,' that is, the figure of my body."

Claims & evidence

The consecrated bread and wine are guarded from falling to the ground

In The Chaplet (De Corona), arguing that many settled Christian practices rest on unwritten custom and tradition rather than on an explicit command of Scripture, Tertullian runs through a list of such customs — the baptismal renunciations, the milk and honey given to the newly baptized, offerings for the dead, the sign of the cross — and among them sets the care taken with the Eucharist:

We take also, in congregations before daybreak, and from the hand of none but the presidents, the sacrament of the Eucharist, which the Lord both commanded to be eaten at meal-times, and enjoined to be taken by all alike. As often as the anniversary comes round, we make offerings for the dead as birthday honours… We feel pained should any wine or bread, even though our own, be cast upon the ground.

— Tertullian, The Chaplet (De Corona) 3, trans. S. Thelwall (ANF vol. 3), full text (cf. New Advent).1 The bread and wine were the worshippers' own offerings ("even though our own"). Tertullian lists the care taken with them among the customs of the church of his day, not as a private scruple.

"This is my body," that is, "the figure of my body" (Against Marcion IV.40)

Elsewhere, against Marcion (who denied that Christ had real flesh), Tertullian argues from the Words of Institution to the reality of Christ's body: a figure presupposes "a veritable body."

Then, having taken the bread and given it to His disciples, He made it His own body, by saying, "This is my body," that is, the figure of my body. A figure, however, there could not have been, unless there were first a veritable body.

— Tertullian, Against Marcion IV.40, trans. P. Holmes (ANF vol. 3), New Advent.2

Reception and debate

  • Placement on the timeline. The timeline places Tertullian for the handling of the consecrated elements described in De Corona 3. He does not give a worked-out account of how Christ is present.
  • Later witnesses to the handling of the elements. Later entries on the timeline record related practice: Cyril of Alexandria (c. 440) insists the elements keep their power when reserved to the next day, and the Council of Trent (1551) defines the worship of latria due to them.
  • Translation note. A common older rendering of the clause reads "We feel uneasy / we are anxious lest aught of our Cup or Bread should fall upon the ground"; the substance — distress at a fallen fragment — is the same. The quotation above follows Thelwall's Ante-Nicene Fathers text.
  • The Montanist years. The De Corona cited here is a work of Tertullian's Montanist years. How later writers judged his turn to the “New Prophecy” is traced on the timeline “Was Tertullian a heretic?”, which records no synod as having tried him.
