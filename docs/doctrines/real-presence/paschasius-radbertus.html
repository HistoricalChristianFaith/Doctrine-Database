Paschasius Radbertus on the Real Presence of Christ in the Eucharist

Dates: c. 831 (De Corpore et Sanguine Domini, revised for Charles the Bald c. 844; Paschasius, abbot of Corbie, c. 785–865) · Wikipedia
Summary: Paschasius wrote the earliest extant full-length Latin treatise devoted to the Eucharist, and taught a strict identity: the body received on the altar is the very body of Christ "which was crucified and buried" — the same flesh born of the Virgin, now made present by the priest's consecration through the Spirit. His book set the terms of the ninth-century Carolingian dispute, drawing the reply of his fellow Corbie monk Ratramnus. His treatise is cited in the dispute over how transubstantiation relates to patristic teaching; see How does the transubstantiation defined in 1215 and 1551 relate to the Fathers' teaching on the eucharistic change?

Claims & evidence

Figure and reality: "the true body of Christ, which was crucified and buried"

In De Corpore et Sanguine Domini (c. 831, revised and presented to Charles the Bald c. 844) — the earliest extant Latin monograph on the Eucharist — Paschasius distinguishes the outward "figure" from the inward "reality," and identifies the reality as Christ's own historical body:

What is perceived externally is a figure or mark, but what is perceived internally is entirely reality and no figure at all; and therefore nothing else is here revealed but reality and the sacrament of the body itself—the true body of Christ, which was crucified and buried, surely the sacrament of his body, which is divinely consecrated by the priest above the altar with the word of Christ through the Spirit: whence the Lord Himself exclaims, "this is my body" (Luke 22:19).

— Paschasius Radbertus, De Corpore et Sanguine Domini, as translated in Bengt Hägglund, History of Theology (St. Louis: Concordia, 1968), p. 156.1 In this passage "figure" names what is perceived externally and "reality" what is perceived internally, which Paschasius identifies with Christ's own body, "crucified and buried," not a distinct sacramental body. Ratramnus (c. 843) denied this identity.

The eucharistic flesh and the flesh born of Mary — the Incarnation as the pattern of the change

Paschasius grounds the change on the Incarnation: as the Word once took flesh of the Virgin without seed by the power of the Holy Spirit, so the same Spirit makes Christ's body present from the substance of the bread and wine at the priest's word. He teaches that the flesh received in the sacrament is identical with the flesh "born of the Virgin Mary, that suffered on the cross and rose from the grave" — a daily miracle, invisible to sight and taste, worked by God.2 Paschasius uses neither the term transubstantiation nor the Aristotelian analysis of substance and accidents.

Reception and debate

  • The Carolingian dispute. Paschasius's treatise drew the reply of Ratramnus, a monk of the same abbey of Corbie writing at the same king's request. The two treatises are cited in the dispute over how transubstantiation relates to patristic teaching (see the page linked in the summary above).
  • Comparison with earlier witnesses. His Incarnation-warrant parallels Justin Martyr's (as the Word truly took flesh, the consecrated food truly is that flesh), and his change-language parallels that of Gregory of Nyssa and Ambrose. His treatment is monograph-length, and identifies the sacramental with the historical body.
  • Source provenance. There was no complete English translation of the treatise until Mark G. Vaillancourt's On the Body and Blood of the Lord (Corpus Christianorum in Translation 34, Brepols, 2023); the Latin is in Patrologia Latina 120 and the critical edition of Bede Paulus (CCCM 16, 1969). The verbatim passage above is given in Hägglund's History of Theology; the identity of the eucharistic flesh with the flesh of Mary, crucified and risen, is at De Corpore 4 and 7 (so the standard reference works: Catholic Encyclopedia; Encyclopedia.com).3
