Was the earliest Christian Eucharist a psychoactive sacrament continuous with the pagan Mysteries?

Disputed reading · on The Real Presence of Christ in the Eucharist · witness: Irenaeus of Lyons, on the Marcosian cup

The passage

Pretending to consecrate cups mixed with wine, and protracting to great length the word of invocation, he contrives to give them a purple and reddish colour, so that Charis, who is one of those that are superior to all things, should be thought to drop her own blood into that cup through means of his invocation, and that thus those who are present should be led to rejoice to taste of that cup, in order that, by so doing, the Charis, who is set forth by this magician, may also flow into them.

— Irenaeus, Against Heresies I.13.2, on the second-century teacher Marcus.1

The question

The witnesses on the timeline describe the Eucharist as the body and blood of Christ. A separate question concerns the rite's origin: whether the cup of the earliest Christians was a psychoactive drink inherited from the pagan mystery cults, as Brian C. Muraresku argued in The Immortality Key (2020), or a meal whose meaning its participants located in Jesus' Last Supper. The question turns on the ergot hypothesis for the Eleusinian kykeon, on archaeochemical finds, and on the Marcosian cup above. The published responses located for this page come from classicists, archaeologists and historians of religion; a response framed from within the Catholic, Orthodox or Protestant traditions as such was not located among the sources surveyed.

A visionary sacrament inherited from the Mysteries

On this reading the earliest Christian cup continued a psychoactive ritual drink of the Greek mysteries, later removed from the rite.

Held by: Carl A. P. Ruck (classicist, The Apples of Apollo: Pagan and Christian Mysteries of the Eucharist, 2001, ⚠ unverified); Chris Bennett (Sex, Drugs, Violence and the Bible, 2001); Brian C. Muraresku (The Immortality Key, 2020).2

The reading builds on the hypothesis of R. Gordon Wasson, the chemist Albert Hofmann and Ruck in The Road to Eleusis (1978): that ergot (Claviceps purpurea) on the barley of the kykeon supplied a lysergic-acid alkaloid that produced the Mystery's vision.3 Muraresku's thesis has three parts: the kykeon was an ergot-based psychedelic; a "religion with no name" of visionary ritual ran through Greco-Roman antiquity; and earliest Christianity, especially in female-led house-churches and under the Dionysian colouring he finds in the Gospel of John, continued it as a sacramental wine later suppressed by the institutional Church.4 The physical evidence it cites comes from Mas Castellar de Pontós, an Iron-Age sanctuary near Girona associated with Demeter and Persephone, where excavators reported traces of ergot in a miniature ritual chalice and in the dental calculus of a human jaw; laboratory work has since shown that toxic ergot peptides can be converted by an ancient ash or lye treatment into water-soluble lysergic-acid amides.5 Ancient wine was often infused, and could carry psychoactive additives (Homer's nepenthe, the medicated wines of the pharmacological writers); the Supper arose among cults that promised immortality through a sacred meal or drink; Ignatius calls the bread the "medicine of immortality", and fourth-century Fathers use the vocabulary of the Mysteries (⚠ unverified). Jacopo Bizzotto (Medicina Historica, 2018) describes the ergot hypothesis for the kykeon as "still inducing a lively discussion among scholars, divided between those who accept it enthusiastically and those who reject it without compromises".6 For a Christian-adjacent text, Bennett reads Irenaeus's account of Marcus as "a clear entheogenic reference": the cup was "definitely eucharistic in quality", carrying Charis's blood, and he cites the charge that Marcus compounded philtres and love potions. Bennett quotes Tom Hatsis (2018) that Charis "would spill her blood – her essence – into the Eucharist, making it accessible to anyone who partook, a use of pharmaka every bit as entheogenic as oracles 'inhaling' Apollo or Bacchants 'drinking' Dionysus".7

A rite rooted in the Last Supper, not in a drug

On this reading the Eucharist began as the meal in which Jesus' followers received bread and cup as his body and blood.

Held by: Nathan Nadeau (Academia Letters, 2021); Patrick McGovern (biomolecular archaeologist, Penn Museum, 2025); Kevin Clinton (classicist, Cornell, 2025); Jan Bremmer (historian of the mystery cults, 2025).8

The case starts from how the sources frame the rite: the New Testament and the Fathers ground it in the Last Supper, the body and blood, the new covenant and the Incarnation; Justin's warrant is that, as the Word was made flesh, the consecrated food "is the flesh and blood of that Jesus who was made flesh".9 Bremmer, a historian of the mystery cults, writes that "No cult is known that used mushrooms in antiquity, and Christianity was not a mystery cult". For Eleusis, Clinton draws on the sanctuary's own record, its annual financial accounts, "publicly audited and published on stone", which list no expense for drugs or ergot. Nadeau places the Eucharist's origins within modern scholarship that has set aside the pagan-continuity assumption, and treats the derivation of Iēsous from a word for "drug" as an etymological root fallacy.10 On the Marcosian cup, Irenaeus presents the colour-change as a trick by a sectarian teacher ("pretending to consecrate"), and Charles Stang, as quoted by Bennett, notes that the charge laid against Marcus was "not with a psychedelic Eucharist, but the use of a love potion".11 On this reading the Eucharist's meaning lies, from its earliest witnesses, in the Last Supper and in the bread and cup received as Christ's body and blood.
