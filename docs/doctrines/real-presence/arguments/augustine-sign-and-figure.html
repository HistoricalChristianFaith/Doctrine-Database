How is Augustine's eucharistic language of sign and figure to be read?

Disputed reading · on The Real Presence of Christ in the Eucharist · witness: Augustine of Hippo

Related readings: How does the transubstantiation defined in 1215 and 1551 relate to the Fathers' teaching on the eucharistic change?

The passage

If, however, it seems to enjoin a crime or vice, or to forbid an act of prudence or benevolence, it is figurative. "Except ye eat the flesh of the Son of man," says Christ, "and drink His blood, ye have no life in you." This seems to enjoin a crime or a vice; it is therefore a figure, enjoining that we should have a share in the sufferings of our Lord, and that we should retain a sweet and profitable memory of the fact that His flesh was wounded and crucified for us.

— Augustine, On Christian Doctrine III.16.24.1

Therefore, what you see is bread and a cup; your eyes also report this to you: but what your faith demands to be instructed, the bread is the body of Christ, the cup is the blood of Christ.

— Augustine, Sermon 272, to the newly baptized at Pentecost.2

The question

Augustine (354–430) calls the consecrated bread the body of Christ, and he also calls the eating of Christ's flesh in John 6 "a figure" and the sacrament a "sign". The dispute is whether, for Augustine, the body his sign conveys is received spiritually by faith, the bread remaining its sign, or is given sacramentally in the consecrated elements themselves. Philip Schaff (1884) wrote that Augustine "has been an apple of contention among the different confessions in all controversies on the doctrine of the Supper", so the answer bears on how the early Latin church is read. J. N. D. Kelly (1958) surveyed the modern classifications: F. Loofs read Augustine as "the exponent of a purely symbolical doctrine", A. Harnack made incorporation into the Church "the core of his sacramental teaching", and "others have attributed receptionist views to him".3 For the first reading below, a modern Lutheran or Reformed scholarly statement later than Schaff that could be opened was not located; a 2015 Reformed study, reported at second hand, is described in that reading's note.

The body received spiritually by faith, the bread bearing its name as sign

On this reading Augustine's "body" on the altar is a sign that bears the name of what it signifies, and the true eating of Christ is spiritual, by faith.

Held by: Huldrych Zwingli (Reformed, Fidei Ratio, 1530); John Calvin (Reformed, Institutes, 1559); Philip Schaff (Protestant church historian, History of the Christian Church, vol. III, 1884).4

The case starts from Augustine's own rule of interpretation: a command that seems to enjoin a crime is figurative, and he applies it to "Except ye eat the flesh of the Son of man", reading the command as sharing in Christ's sufferings and keeping "a sweet and profitable memory" of his wounded flesh. Zwingli took a second text, Against Adimantus 12, as "a key for us whereby we can unlock all the declarations of the ancients concerning the Eucharist": there Augustine writes that "the Lord did not hesitate to say: 'This is my body,' when He was giving a sign of His body". Zwingli adds Augustine's thirtieth tractate on John, that the risen body "must be in one place", and concludes that the Fathers "thought not of the natural but of the spiritual eating of Christ's body".5 Calvin reads Augustine's letter to Boniface the same way: sacraments take the names of the things they resemble, so that "in a certain manner the sacrament of Christ's body is Christ's body"; on that basis Calvin claims that "without all controversy he is wholly ours".6 The tractates on John supply the spiritual eating: "Believe, and thou hast eaten already"; the unworthy may "press the sacrament of the body and blood of Christ carnally and visibly with his teeth" and yet not eat Christ's flesh; and the meat and drink are to be understood as "the fellowship of His own body and members, which is the holy Church".7 Schaff places Augustine with Tertullian and Cyprian in "a symbolical theory of the Supper, which however includes a real spiritual participation of the Lord by faith", standing "nearest to the Calvinistic or Orthodox Reformed doctrine". On this reading the realist sermons use the sacrament's name for the thing it signifies, as the letter to Boniface explains, and Augustine's sign-theology is the governing category.8

The body given sacramentally in the consecrated elements

On this reading the consecrated bread is Christ's body, and Augustine's "sign" and "figure" describe the sacramental manner in which it is given and received.

Held by: Joseph Pohle (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1909); J. N. D. Kelly (Oxford patristic historian, Early Christian Doctrines, 1958); Raniero Cantalamessa (Catholic, Preacher to the Papal Household, 2022).9

The case rests on the sermons in which Augustine tells the newly baptized what the bread is: "the bread is the body of Christ, the cup is the blood of Christ", and "That Bread which you see on the altar, consecrated by the word of God, is the Body of Christ".10 Pohle and Kelly both point to the exposition of "worship his footstool": Christ "gave that very flesh to us to eat for our salvation; and no one eateth that flesh, unless he hath first worshipped", so that "we sin in not worshipping". Kelly reads this as "the eucharistic body which demanded adoration", and Pohle adds Augustine's teaching that the true Body and Blood are offered in the Mass.11 On this reading Augustine's sign-theology is his account of a sacrament, in which "one thing is seen, another is understood", and the letter to Boniface describes the bread as a physical object without denying what faith receives: Kelly judges that "Augustine's language is fully consistent with his recognition of its reality and actual presence". Augustine glosses "the flesh profiteth nothing" as aimed at those who "understood the flesh, just as when cut to pieces in a carcass, or sold in the shambles; not as when it is quickened by the Spirit".12 Kelly calls the resulting doctrine "realist through and through" and "also frankly spiritualizing": the body and blood "are veritably received by the communicant, but are received sacramentally or, as one might express it, in figura". Cantalamessa states the same reading in Catholic terms: Augustine made it clear "that the presence of Jesus in the Eucharist is 'in the sacrament'", "a sacramental and not a physical presence, mediated by signs", in which "the sign does not exclude the reality but makes it present to us".13
