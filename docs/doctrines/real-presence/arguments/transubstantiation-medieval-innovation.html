How does the transubstantiation defined in 1215 and 1551 relate to the Fathers' teaching on the eucharistic change?

Disputed reading · on The Real Presence of Christ in the Eucharist · witnesses: the Council of Trent, the Fourth Lateran Council

Related readings: How is Augustine's eucharistic language of sign and figure to be read?

The passage

by the consecration of the bread and of the wine, a conversion is made of the whole substance of the bread into the substance of the body of Christ our Lord, and of the whole substance of the wine into the substance of His blood; which conversion is, by the holy Catholic Church, suitably and properly called Transubstantiation.

— Council of Trent, Session 13 (1551), ch. 4.1

The question

How does the conversion of "the whole substance" defined at Trent relate to the Fathers' language of change? The readings below differ on whether the Fathers taught that conversion, on what the medieval term adds to their teaching, and on whether any term defines the manner of the change.

The dated record the readings cite runs as follows. At Corbie, Paschasius Radbertus (c. 831, revised c. 844) taught that the sacramental body is the flesh "born of Mary, that suffered on the cross and rose from the grave", and Ratramnus (c. 843) called the elements "not the True Body of Christ, but the Figure, or Sacrament of it", and "not mere empty figures, or naked signs".2,3 The oath imposed on Berengar of Tours at Rome in 1059 had the body "sensibly (sensualiter) … handled and broken by the hands of the priests, and crushed by the teeth of the faithful"; a second oath, in 1079, had the bread and wine "substantially converted" (substantialiter converti).4,5 The noun transubstantiatio is credited to Hildebert of Lavardin, c. 1079, by the Catholic Encyclopedia, and to Paschasius Radbertus by Philip Schaff (1884).6 The Fourth Lateran Council (1215) professed "the bread being changed (transsubstantiatio) by divine power into the body", and Thomas Aquinas (Summa Theologiae III, q. 75) taught that the whole substance of the bread is converted while the accidents remain without a subject.7,8 Trent's decree (1551) speaks of the "species" (appearances) remaining, not of "accidents".9

A modern Lutheran or Reformed scholarly statement of the first reading that could be opened was not located; the 2015 Reformed study named in its note is reported at second hand. The Catholic answers to the Theodoret and Gelasius passages are reported from a secondary survey of the scholastic theologians; an openly accessible modern Catholic scholarly treatment of those passages was not located.

A late scholastic doctrine, unknown to the Fathers

On this reading the Fathers did not teach a conversion of the bread's substance, and that teaching is medieval.

Held by: Martin Luther (Lutheran, The Babylonian Captivity of the Church, 1520); John Calvin (Reformed, Institutes, 1559); Philip Schaff (Protestant church historian, History of the Christian Church, vol. III, 1884).10

Luther dated the doctrine by the arrival of Aristotle: "the Church had the true faith for more than twelve hundred years, during which time the holy Fathers never once mentioned this transubstantiation — certainly, a monstrous word for a monstrous idea — until the pseudo-philosophy of Aristotle became rampant in the Church these last three hundred years".11 Calvin granted that "some of the ancients occasionally used the term conversion, not that they meant to do away with the substance in the external signs, but to teach that the bread devoted to the sacrament was widely different from ordinary bread"; the doctrine itself was "not so long since it was devised", and "in the age of Bernard, though a harsher mode of speech had prevailed, transubstantiation was not yet recognised".12 Schaff adduces two fifth-century texts for this reading. Theodoret of Cyrus, arguing against Eutyches that Christ's manhood remains after its union with the Godhead, used the consecrated elements as the parallel:

You are caught in the net you have woven yourself. For even after the consecration the mystic symbols are not deprived of their own nature; they remain in their former substance figure and form; they are visible and tangible as they were before. But they are regarded as what they are become, and believed so to be, and are worshipped as being what they are believed to be.

In the first dialogue Theodoret says the Lord honoured the symbols with the names of body and blood, "not because He had changed their nature, but because to their nature He had added grace". Gelasius I of Rome (c. 496), in the same anti-Eutychian argument, wrote that "the substance or nature of the bread and wine does not cease".13,14 Schaff read the change-language of Hilary, Ambrose and Gaudentius as containing "at most a dynamic, not a substantial, change of the elements", and took the near absence of adoration from the ancient liturgies as showing "that the doctrine of transubstantiation was not yet fixed in the consciousness of the church". On this reading the Corbie dispute, the 1059 and 1079 oaths and the twelfth-century spread of the noun mark the period in which the doctrine took shape.15

The ancient conversion of the elements, named with precision

On this reading the Fathers already taught that the bread and wine are changed into Christ's body and blood, and the medieval term names that belief without adding to it.

Held by: the Council of Trent (Catholic, 1551); Joseph Pohle (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1909); the Catechism of the Catholic Church (Catholic, 1992, §1375); Raniero Cantalamessa (Catholic, Preacher to the Papal Household, 2022).16

Trent claimed continuity for the conversion and called the word the Church's apt name for it: the conversion "is, by the holy Catholic Church, suitably and properly called Transubstantiation". The case rests on Fathers who speak of a change. Gregory of Nyssa (c. 383) wrote that "the bread which is consecrated by the Word of God is changed into the Body of God the Word", and said God "transelements" (metastoicheiōsis) the nature of the visible things. Ambrose of Milan (c. 390) taught that "by blessing nature itself is changed" and that "before it is consecrated, it is bread; but when the words of Christ have been spoken, it becomes the body of Christ". Cyril of Jerusalem told the newly baptized to regard the elements "not as bare elements, for they are … the Body and Blood of Christ".17 Catholic theologians have read the "nature" and "substance" that Theodoret and Gelasius say remain as the properties or sensible species of the elements: for Franciscus Sylvius, Gelasius "calls those accidents substance"; for Honoré Tournely, "By nature, Gelasius clearly understands the properties of nature"; for J. B. Franzelin (1868), Theodoret distinguishes "the sensible species, which is real and remains the same as it had been before" (⚠ unverified; as quoted in English translation).18 Pohle states the Catholic account of the relation: transubstantiation and the Real Presence "have been handed down together from generation to generation, though we cannot entirely ignore a dogmatico-historical development"; "complete clearness on the subject had been attained" by the Greek Fathers he names and by Ambrose in the West, while the earliest witnesses had not considered the manner of the conversion. He explains the imprecision of some patristic language by the Fathers' secure possession of the faith, the Discipline of the Secret, and the Alexandrian preference for allegory. The Catechism of the Catholic Church (1992) states that "It is by the conversion of the bread and wine into Christ's body and blood that Christ becomes present in this sacrament", and adduces Chrysostom and Ambrose for "this conversion".19 Cantalamessa describes Trent as having given "a more precise explanation" of the West's "Christological realism", in three adverbs: Jesus is present "vere, realiter, substantialiter". On this reading Paschasius, the Berengarian oaths, Lateran IV and Aquinas are stages in the West's articulation of a belief it already held.20

The patristic change, whose manner is left undefined

On this reading the change of the gifts is the ancient faith of the Church, and no term, including "transubstantiation", explains how it happens.

Held by: the Synod of Jerusalem under Patriarch Dositheus (Eastern Orthodox, 1672); Kallistos Ware (Eastern Orthodox, The Orthodox Church, 1963); John Breck (Eastern Orthodox priest and theologian, 2007).21

The Synod of Jerusalem confessed that the bread is "transmuted, transubstantiated, converted and transformed into the true Body Itself of the Lord", called this the "most ancient tradition of the Catholic Church concerning this Mystery", and added that "by the word 'transubstantiation' the manner is not explained, by which the bread and wine are changed into the Body and Blood of the Lord, — for that is altogether incomprehensible and impossible, except by God Himself".22 Ware states the reading: "while Orthodoxy has always insisted on the reality of the change, it has never attempted to explain the manner of the change: the Eucharistic Prayer in the Liturgy simply uses the neutral term metaballo, to turn about, change, or alter." He records that seventeenth-century Orthodox writers and councils used the Latin term with the scholastic distinction of substance and accidents, and that the Russian Church's 1838 translation of the Acts of Jerusalem kept the word but paraphrased the passage "in such a way that the technical terms Substance and Accidents were not employed".23 Breck writes that "Orthodox tradition speaks of 'change' or 'transformation,' (metamorphôsis; in the Eucharistic Divine Liturgy metabalôn, 'making the change') but always with a concern to preserve the mystery from the probings of human reason".24 The patristic warrant is the Greek tradition of the change at the invocation of the Spirit: Cyril of Jerusalem's prayer that the Spirit "make the Bread the Body of Christ", and John of Damascus's teaching that the bread and wine are "supernaturally changed by the invocation and presence of the Holy Spirit into the body and blood of Christ", not "merely figures of the body and blood of Christ (God forbid!)".25 On this reading the change itself belongs to the patristic faith, and the substance-and-accidents analysis is one Western way of speaking about it that the Orthodox may use or decline.
