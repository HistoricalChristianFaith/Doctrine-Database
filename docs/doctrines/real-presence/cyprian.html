Cyprian of Carthage on the Real Presence

Dates: c. 253 · Wikipedia
Summary: In his letter to Caecilius (Epistle 62; numbered 63 in some editions), written against the "Aquarians" who used water alone in the cup, Cyprian argues that the mingled cup of wine and water is the blood of Christ, so that "the blood of Christ is not offered if there be no wine in the cup." In the same letter he calls the priest's offering a "true and full sacrifice" made in imitation of what Christ did.

Claims & evidence

Wine in the cup: "the blood of Christ is not offered if there be no wine in the cup" (Epistle 62.9)

For, taking the cup on the eve of His passion, He blessed it, and gave it to His disciples, saying, "Drink ye all of this; for this is my blood of the New Testament, which shall be shed for many, for the remission of sins." … In which portion we find that the cup which the Lord offered was mixed, and that that was wine which He called His blood. Whence it appears that the blood of Christ is not offered if there be no wine in the cup, nor the Lord's sacrifice celebrated with a legitimate consecration unless our oblation and sacrifice respond to His passion.

— Epistle 62 (to Caecilius), On the Sacrament of the Cup of the Lord, §9, source (ANF vol. 5; cf. New Advent). The letter answers bishops who offered water only in the cup (whether from ascetic scruple or fear of the wine's smell betraying Christians under persecution). Cyprian argues that because the cup is Christ's blood, and Christ's blood was shown forth in wine, a cup without wine is not his blood. Earlier in the letter he writes: "the blood of Christ is assuredly not water, but wine; neither can His blood by which we are redeemed and quickened appear to be in the cup, when in the cup there is no wine whereby the blood of Christ is shown forth" (§2).

The "true and full sacrifice" (Epistle 62.14, 17)

For if Jesus Christ, our Lord and God, is Himself the chief priest of God the Father, and has first offered Himself a sacrifice to the Father, and has commanded this to be done in commemoration of Himself, certainly that priest truly discharges the office of Christ, who imitates that which Christ did; and he then offers a true and full sacrifice in the Church to God the Father, when he proceeds to offer it according to what he sees Christ Himself to have offered.

— Epistle 62.14, source. Cyprian calls the Eucharist a sacrifice — "the Lord's passion is the sacrifice which we offer" (§17) — offered by the priest who does what Christ did. Of the bread he writes: "the body of the Lord cannot be flour alone or water alone, unless both should be united and joined together and compacted in the mass of one bread" (§13). The sacrificial and priestly language of the same Epistle 62, read for the minister who "discharges the office of Christ," is treated on Cyprian's ministerial-priesthood page.

Further features of the letter

  • Types of the cup. Cyprian reads Melchizedek's bread and wine (Gen 14), the wine of Prov 9, and the "blood of the grape" of Gen 49 as types of the cup, and uses them to argue that the cup must hold wine because the wine is the blood. He reads the water mixed in the cup as the people united to Christ.
  • A disciplinary dispute. The letter makes its eucharistic argument in answer to a practical question of discipline: wine or water in the cup. How its language bears on the real-presence question is disputed; see Disputed readings.
  • On the numbering: the letter to Caecilius is Epistle 62 in the ANF (Oxford) series followed here, and Epistle 63 in the later Migne/CSEL numbering used by much modern scholarship — the same letter under two conventions.
