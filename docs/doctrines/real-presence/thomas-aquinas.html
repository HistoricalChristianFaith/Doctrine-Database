Thomas Aquinas on the Real Presence of Christ in the Eucharist

Dates: c. 1274 (the Summa Theologiae, Third Part, left unfinished at his death) · Thomas Aquinas, c. 1225–1274; Dominican friar and scholastic theologian · Wikipedia
Summary: Aquinas gives an Aristotelian exposition of transubstantiation, the term the Fourth Lateran Council (1215) had used in its profession of faith. In Summa Theologiae III, q. 75 he holds that the substance of the bread and wine does not remain after the consecration; that the whole substance of each is converted into the whole substance of Christ's body and blood — a conversion so unlike any natural change that it must be "called by a name of its own, 'transubstantiation'"; and that the sensible accidents (appearances) remain.

Claims & evidence

Whether the substance of the bread remains (Summa Theologiae III, q. 75, a. 2)

Aquinas rejects the view that the bread's substance persists alongside Christ's body. Answering whether the substance of the bread and wine remains after the consecration, he holds that it does not, and argues that the substance is neither annihilated nor dissolved but converted:

the substance of the bread and wine does not remain in this sacrament.

— Summa Theologiae III, q. 75, a. 2, New Advent (Fathers of the English Dominican Province translation).1 On this account, what was bread is no longer bread in substance once the words of consecration are spoken.

The conversion of the whole substance: "transubstantiation" (III, q. 75, a. 4)

The conversion, he argues, is unlike any natural change, in which one form succeeds another in an abiding matter; here the whole substance passes into another whole substance. This unique kind of change requires a name of its own:

the whole substance of the bread is changed into the whole substance of Christ's body, and the whole substance of the wine into the whole substance of Christ's blood. Hence this is not a formal, but a substantial conversion; nor is it a kind of natural movement: but, with a name of its own, it can be called "transubstantiation."

— Summa Theologiae III, q. 75, a. 4, New Advent.2 Here the change is analysed in Aristotelian terms, as a conversion of one substance into another. How this analysis relates to the Fathers' teaching is disputed; see How does the transubstantiation defined in 1215 and 1551 relate to the Fathers' teaching on the eucharistic change?

The remaining accidents (appearances) (III, q. 75, a. 5)

If the substance of the bread is gone, what is seen, touched, and tasted? Aquinas answers that the sensible qualities — the "accidents" — remain, so that the sacrament neither deceives the senses nor repels the communicant with the visible eating of flesh:

It is evident to sense that all the accidents of the bread and wine remain after the consecration.

— Summa Theologiae III, q. 75, a. 5, New Advent.3 Aquinas states that the change "has not properly a subject," and treats in III, q. 77 how the remaining accidents subsist without one. The Council of Trent (1551) speaks of the species (appearances) remaining while the substance is wholly converted.

Reception and debate

  • Earlier and later texts on the timeline. Gregory of Nyssa (c. 383) and Ambrose (c. 390) speak of a change of the elements; Lateran IV (1215) named it transubstantiatio; Aquinas explains it in Aristotelian terms — whole substance converted, accidents remaining. The Council of Trent (1551) defines the conversion "which the Catholic Church suitably and properly calls transubstantiation."
  • Same author, two questions, two pages. This page treats Aquinas's account of the eucharistic change (Summa III, q. 75). His account of who may consecrate — the priest alone, acting in persona Christi by a power conferred at ordination (Summa III, q. 82) — is on his ministerial-priesthood page.
  • Provenance. The Summa Theologiae is quoted from the standard Fathers of the English Dominican Province translation as published online; its Third Part was left unfinished at Aquinas's death in 1274.
