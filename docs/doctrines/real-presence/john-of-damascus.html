John of Damascus on the Real Presence

Dates: c. 743 (An Exact Exposition of the Orthodox Faith, De Fide Orthodoxa, book IV, ch. 13; John's lifespan c. 675–749) · Wikipedia
Summary: Writing the great summa of Greek patristic theology, John gathers the Eastern tradition into its most emphatic pre-medieval statement of the Real Presence. In chapter 13 of the fourth book, "Concerning the holy and immaculate Mysteries of the Lord," he teaches that "the bread itself and the wine are changed into God's body and blood" by the invocation of the Holy Spirit, denies outright that they are "merely figures," and answers the "antitype" language of the earlier fathers by confining it to the elements before consecration. He is the Eastern realist bridge between Cyril of Alexandria and the Carolingian West of Paschasius.

Claims & evidence

"The bread itself and the wine are changed into God's body and blood" — by the Spirit's invocation (Exposition IV.13)

The body which is born of the holy Virgin is in truth body united with divinity, not that the body which was received up into the heavens descends, but that the bread itself and the wine are changed into God's body and blood. But if you enquire how this happens, it is enough for you to learn that it was through the Holy Spirit … the manner of this cannot be searched out.

… the bread of the table and the wine and water are supernaturally changed by the invocation and presence of the Holy Spirit into the body and blood of Christ, and are not two but one and the same.

— An Exact Exposition of the Orthodox Faith IV.13, source (NPNF Series II, vol. 9; cf. New Advent). John grounds the change in the same power that worked the Incarnation and the first creation: "if God the Word of His own will became man … can He not then make the bread His body and the wine and water His blood?" The change is effected not by eating but "through the invocation" (epiclesis) of the Spirit, "the rain to this new tillage" — the Eastern consecratory emphasis of Cyril of Jerusalem carried forward.

"Not merely figures … but the deified body of the Lord itself" (Exposition IV.13)

The bread and the wine are not merely figures of the body and blood of Christ (God forbid!) but the deified body of the Lord itself: for the Lord has said, "This is My body," not, this is a figure of My body: and "My blood," not, a figure of My blood.

— Exposition IV.13, source. John presses the words of institution against a figurative reading, and reaches for the same Isaian image Cyril of Jerusalem used: as the live coal of Isaiah 6 "is not plain wood but wood united with fire," so "the bread of the communion is not plain bread but bread united with divinity." He backs the realism with John 6 ("Except ye eat the flesh of the Son of Man … ye have no life in you") and warns that the worthy receive it "for life everlasting," the unworthy "for chastisement" — the Pauline discernment of the body.

The "antitype" language belongs to the offering before consecration (Exposition IV.13)

But if some persons called the bread and the wine antitypes of the body and blood of the Lord, as did the divinely inspired Basil, they said so not after the consecration but before the consecration, so calling the offering itself.

— Exposition IV.13, source. This is John's own handling of the very "type"/"antitype" vocabulary that later memorialist readers press as a symbolic counter-witness (Basil's liturgy, Cyril of Jerusalem's "antitype"). John does not deny that the earlier fathers used it; he restricts it to the unconsecrated gifts, so that after the invocation the elements are the body and blood without qualification. His reading of the patristic "figure" language is weighed on the medieval-innovation argument page.

Notes

  • John's Exposition is the closing synthesis of Greek patristic theology, the standard textbook of Eastern Orthodoxy and, once translated by Burgundio of Pisa in the twelfth century, a source for the Western schoolmen — including Aquinas. His change-of-substance language ("changed into God's body and blood," "not plain bread but bread united with divinity") is as strong as any Eastern witness and later Latin writers read it, like Gregory of Nyssa's "transelementing," as anticipating transubstantiation — though John, writing in Greek, has neither the term nor the Aristotelian substance/accidents apparatus.
  • He fills the "long silence" the summary timeline notes between Cyril of Alexandria (c. 440) and Paschasius Radbertus (c. 831): the silence is one of surviving Western treatises, not of the doctrine, which the Greek East continued to teach without interruption. On John's own showing the realist reception was the settled mind of the Church he inherited.
  • His careful confinement of the "antitype" vocabulary to the pre-consecration offering makes him an early witness to the reading the "figure" did not mean "mere symbol" — a caution that cuts against enlisting the fathers' type-language flatly for the memorialist side. It sits in real tension, however, with the strand of the tradition — Theodoret and Pope Gelasius — that held the elements' natural substance remains after consecration; that these are hard to reconcile is part of why the precise later dogma is contested as a reading of the fathers.
