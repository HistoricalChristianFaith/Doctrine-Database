John of Damascus on the Real Presence

Dates: c. 743 (An Exact Exposition of the Orthodox Faith, De Fide Orthodoxa, book IV, ch. 13; John's lifespan c. 675–749) · Wikipedia
Summary: In his Exposition, a compendium of Greek patristic theology, John states the Eastern teaching on the Real Presence. In chapter 13 of the fourth book, "Concerning the holy and immaculate Mysteries of the Lord," he teaches that "the bread itself and the wine are changed into God's body and blood" by the invocation of the Holy Spirit, denies that they are "merely figures," and answers the "antitype" language of the earlier fathers by confining it to the elements before consecration. On the timeline he falls between Cyril of Alexandria (c. 440) and the Carolingian West of Paschasius (c. 831).

Claims & evidence

"The bread itself and the wine are changed into God's body and blood" — by the Spirit's invocation (Exposition IV.13)

The body which is born of the holy Virgin is in truth body united with divinity, not that the body which was received up into the heavens descends, but that the bread itself and the wine are changed into God's body and blood. But if you enquire how this happens, it is enough for you to learn that it was through the Holy Spirit … the manner of this cannot be searched out.

… the bread of the table and the wine and water are supernaturally changed by the invocation and presence of the Holy Spirit into the body and blood of Christ, and are not two but one and the same.

— An Exact Exposition of the Orthodox Faith IV.13, source (NPNF Series II, vol. 9; cf. New Advent). John grounds the change in the same power that worked the Incarnation and the first creation: "if God the Word of His own will became man … can He not then make the bread His body and the wine and water His blood?" The change is effected not by eating but "through the invocation" (epiclesis) of the Spirit, "the rain to this new tillage" — compare the consecratory emphasis of Cyril of Jerusalem.

"Not merely figures … but the deified body of the Lord itself" (Exposition IV.13)

The bread and the wine are not merely figures of the body and blood of Christ (God forbid!) but the deified body of the Lord itself: for the Lord has said, "This is My body," not, this is a figure of My body: and "My blood," not, a figure of My blood.

— Exposition IV.13, source. John presses the words of institution against a figurative reading, and reaches for the same Isaian image Cyril of Jerusalem used: as the live coal of Isaiah 6 "is not plain wood but wood united with fire," so "the bread of the communion is not plain bread but bread united with divinity." He cites John 6 ("Except ye eat the flesh of the Son of Man … ye have no life in you") and warns that the worthy receive it "for life everlasting," the unworthy "for chastisement" — the Pauline discernment of the body.

John on the "antitype" language: said of the offering before consecration (Exposition IV.13)

But if some persons called the bread and the wine antitypes of the body and blood of the Lord, as did the divinely inspired Basil, they said so not after the consecration but before the consecration, so calling the offering itself.

— Exposition IV.13, source. Here John addresses the "type"/"antitype" vocabulary of earlier writers (Basil's liturgy, Cyril of Jerusalem's "antitype"). He does not deny that the earlier fathers used it; he restricts it to the unconsecrated gifts, so that after the invocation the elements are, for him, the body and blood without qualification. How the patristic "figure" language should be read is disputed; see How does the transubstantiation defined in 1215 and 1551 relate to the Fathers' teaching on the eucharistic change?

Reception and debate

  • John's Exposition is a summary of Greek patristic theology. It was translated into Latin by Burgundio of Pisa in 1150, and the translation was used by Peter Lombard and Aquinas ("St. John Damascene," Catholic Encyclopedia). Its change language ("changed into God's body and blood," "not plain bread but bread united with divinity") stands in Greek; John uses neither the Latin term transubstantiatio nor the Aristotelian substance/accidents apparatus. Compare Gregory of Nyssa's "transelementing."
  • Placement on the timeline. He falls between Cyril of Alexandria (c. 440) and Paschasius Radbertus (c. 831), an interval for which the timeline records no Latin eucharistic treatise.
  • His confinement of the "antitype" vocabulary to the pre-consecration offering, and the statements of Theodoret and Pope Gelasius that the elements' natural substance remains after consecration, are both cited in the dispute over how the patristic "figure" language should be read; see How does the transubstantiation defined in 1215 and 1551 relate to the Fathers' teaching on the eucharistic change?
