Clement of Alexandria on the Real Presence

Dates: c. 198 · Wikipedia
Summary: In The Instructor (Paedagogus, c. 198), Clement of Alexandria ascribes effects to the eucharistic cup: "the blood of the Lord is twofold," and "to drink the blood of Jesus, is to become partaker of the Lord's immortality." The mingled cup of water and the Word "is called Eucharist," and "they who by faith partake of it are sanctified both in body and soul." Clement reads the wine, the water, the blood, and the Word as layered symbols, and ascribes to the Eucharist immortality and the sanctification of the whole person.

Claims & evidence

To drink the blood of Jesus is to partake of His immortality (Instructor II.2)

And the blood of the Lord is twofold. For there is the blood of His flesh, by which we are redeemed from corruption; and the spiritual, that by which we are anointed. And to drink the blood of Jesus, is to become partaker of the Lord's immortality; the Spirit being the energetic principle of the Word, as blood is of flesh.

— The Instructor (Paedagogus) II.2, "On Drinking," source (cf. New Advent). Clement distinguishes a "twofold" blood of the Lord — the blood of His flesh that redeems and the spiritual blood that anoints — and says that "to drink the blood of Jesus, is to become partaker of the Lord's immortality."

The mingled cup "is called Eucharist," and those who partake "are sanctified both in body and soul" (Instructor II.2)

And the mixture of both-of the water and of the Word-is called Eucharist, renowned and glorious grace; and they who by faith partake of it are sanctified both in body and soul. For the divine mixture, man, the Father's will has mystically compounded by the Spirit and the Word.

— The Instructor (Paedagogus) II.2, "On Drinking," source (cf. New Advent). The mingled cup — water and the Word together — "is called Eucharist," and those who partake of it by faith "are sanctified both in body and soul."

Reception and debate

  • Allegorical reading. In the same chapter Clement reads the wine of the Supper, its mingling with water, and the "blood of the grape" as layered symbols of the Word, the Spirit, and faith. He speaks of what the cup confers (immortality, sanctification) and gives no account of the mode of Christ's presence.
  • How this language bears on the literal-or-figurative reading of the Eucharist is disputed; see Disputed readings.
