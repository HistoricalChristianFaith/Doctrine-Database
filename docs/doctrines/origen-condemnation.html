The Condemnation of Origen

Origen of Alexandria (c. 185–c. 253), teacher at Alexandria and later a presbyter at Caesarea in Palestine, was imprisoned and tortured in the Decian persecution and died at Tyre a few years later. Three centuries after his death his name stands last in the eleventh canon of the council of Constantinople of 553, after Arius, Eunomius, Macedonius, Apollinarius, Nestorius and Eutyches. This page records the judgements passed on him and on his writings between those two dates and after.

The subject here is not Origen's theology as such but the judgements passed on it: a timeline of acts and documents, earliest extant first — who censured him or his books, on what charges, on whose authority, and how the charges changed between Demetrius of Alexandria in the 230s and the emperor Justinian in the 540s, and after. Its “witnesses” are therefore dated acts and documents — a bishop's synod, a dialogue, a defence written from prison, a heresy-catalogue, a papal letter, an imperial edict, a conciliar anathema, a chanted liturgical list, a schoolman's textbook, a humanist's thesis, a printed edition, a modern monograph — each footnoted to its source. Most of the charges are drawn from one book, Peri Archōn (On First Principles), which survives whole only in Rufinus's Latin translation of 397 and in Greek chiefly in excerpts quoted by its critics.

The Latin quarrel between Jerome and Rufinus in 393–411 is told on its own timeline, and the Eastern events of the same years — Theophilus of Alexandria's conflict with John Chrysostom and Chrysostom's deposition — on another; this page gives those years a shorter account. The pre-existence of souls is traced as a belief in its own right on the origin of the soul, and the final restoration of all things (apokatastasis), which the anathemas condemned with it, on universal restoration. Origen also appears as a witness on other timelines, from the canon and purgatory to astrology and the Nephilim.

Timeline

The preface to Peri Archōn (c. 229)

Most of the propositions later condemned are drawn from one work, written at Alexandria before Origen left it. In its preface Origen divides Christian teaching in two. On some points “the holy apostles, in preaching the faith of Christ, delivered themselves with the utmost clearness”; on others they stated only that a thing is so, “leaving, however, the grounds of their statements to be examined into by those who should deserve the excellent gifts of the Spirit.” Into the second class he explicitly puts the origin of the soul — whether it “is derived from the seed by a process of traducianism… or whether it has any other beginning” is, he says, “not distinguished with sufficient clearness in the teaching of the Church” — and the nature of the devil and his angels, of whom “the teaching of the Church has laid down that these beings exist indeed; but what they are, or how they exist, it has not explained with sufficient clearness.”1 Whether the propositions later anathematized were offered as inquiry into questions left open or as assertions is disputed; see the interpretation page.

Demetrius of Alexandria deposes him (c. 231–233)

The earliest recorded sentence against Origen followed his ordination. Sent to Greece “on account of a pressing necessity in connection with ecclesiastical affairs”, he travelled through Palestine “and was ordained as presbyter in Caesarea by the bishops of that country” — without the consent of his own bishop, Demetrius of Alexandria. Eusebius, who had read the papers and with Pamphilus wrote a defence of Origen in six books, then declines to narrate what followed: “The matters that were agitated concerning him on this account, and the decisions on these matters by those who presided over the churches… demand a separate treatise.”2

Eusebius records an earlier dispute, some fifteen years before. On an earlier stay in Palestine, Origen — then a layman — had been asked by the bishops there to preach; Demetrius objected, in a letter they answered, “that such a thing was never heard of before, neither has hitherto taken place, that laymen should preach in the presence of bishops.” Alexander of Jerusalem and Theoctistus of Caesarea replied with precedents: “I know not how he comes to say what is plainly untrue. For whenever persons able to instruct the brethren are found, they are exhorted by the holy bishops to preach to the people.” Demetrius recalled him to Alexandria by letter, and he went back.3

The proceedings themselves are known through Photius's ninth-century summary of Pamphilus's lost defence, which reports two sittings: the ordination was “contrary to the rule of the Church”, the bishop's “love… changed to hate”, and “a synod of bishops and some presbyters was summoned to condemn Origen. According to Pamphilus, it was decided that he must not remain in Alexandria or teach there, but that he should be allowed to retain his priesthood. But Demetrius and some Egyptian bishops, with the assent of those who had formerly supported him, also deprived him of his sacred office.”4 In that summary the first sitting expelled him and the second deposed him, and the ground stated is the ordination.

Eusebius records a further charge: the self-castration which, he reports, Origen had performed as a young catechist. Demetrius had at the time “admired greatly the daring nature of the act” and urged him on in his teaching; now, “overcome by human weakness, [he] wrote of his deed as most foolish to the bishops throughout the world” — having, Eusebius adds, “nothing else that he could say against him, save this deed of his boyhood.”5 Jerome, writing in 392, attributes the quarrel to Demetrius's anger: Origen “offended Demetrius, who was so wildly enraged at him that he wrote everywhere to injure his reputation.”6 Origen left Egypt for Caesarea; Demetrius died within about two years, after forty-three in the see, and the school Origen had built passed to his own pupil Heraclas, who succeeded him in it.7 The sentence was issued from Alexandria and circulated by letter to other churches. The surviving accounts of it come from Eusebius and from Pamphilus's defence. Whether the sentence was disciplinary only or also concerned his teaching is disputed; see the interpretation page.

Palestine declines to receive the sentence (from 231)

The Alexandrian sentence was not received in Palestine. Alexander of Jerusalem and Theoctistus of Caesarea, the bishops who had ordained him, “attended on him constantly, as their only teacher, and allowed him to expound the Divine Scriptures, and to perform the other duties pertaining to ecclesiastical discourse”; Firmilian of Caesarea in Cappadocia summoned him into his own province “for the benefit of the churches” and afterwards travelled to Judea to sit under him.8 For the remaining two decades of his life Origen taught, preached, and wrote as a presbyter of the Palestinian church. Jerome, counting the provinces a century and a half later, reckons that Origen “stands condemned by his bishop, Demetrius, only the bishops of Palestine, Arabia, Phenicia, and Achaia dissenting”, adding that “imperial Rome consents to his condemnation, and even convenes a senate to censure him, not… because of the novelty or heterodoxy of his doctrines, but because men could not tolerate the incomparable eloquence and knowledge which, when once he opened his lips, made others seem dumb.”9

Tortured under Decius; death at Tyre (250 – c. 253)

Eusebius records that in the Decian persecution Origen was arrested and tortured — “bonds and bodily tortures and torments under the iron collar and in the dungeon; and how for many days with his feet stretched four spaces in the stocks he bore patiently the threats of fire” — under a judge who “strove eagerly with all his might not to end his life.” In Eusebius's account he endured without recanting.10 He survived, and died a few years later at Tyre, where he was buried, in his sixty-ninth year.11 Epiphanius, writing about 375, reports a different story, of a forced offering of incense under persecution (see the entry for 375–377 below). Among the next generation of Greek theologians, Dionysius of Alexandria, Gregory Thaumaturgus, Pamphilus and Eusebius were his pupils or his pupils' pupils.

Methodius of Olympus writes against Origen's doctrines (c. 290 – 311)

The earliest extant treatises written against Origen's teaching are those of Methodius, bishop of Olympus in Lycia, martyred at the end of the last persecution. Jerome's catalogue lists three anti-Origen works among his six titles — “an excellent work On the resurrection, against Origen and On the Pythonissa and On free will, also against Origen”.12 The chief work, a dialogue on the resurrection called the Aglaophon, argues against the pre-existence of souls — Origen “said that the body was given to the soul as a fetter after the fall, and that previously it lived without a body”, and held that “the soul alone is man, as did Plato” — and, above all, against Origen's account of the risen body: that what rises is the persisting form of the flesh “stamped upon another spiritual body”, the material substrate being in flux and so no part of what is raised. Methodius answers that a form has no existence apart from the matter it shapes and perishes with it, as the shape of a melted statue “has no longer a substantial existence.”13 A companion treatise, On Things Created, attacks the argument that God cannot have become a Creator — “since there is no workman without some work… so neither is there an Almighty without an object of His power” — and so that the world is co-eternal.14

Methodius writes in the form of a philosophical dialogue. In the surviving extracts he does not name Origen a heretic, appeals to no synod or ecclesiastical authority, and at one point cites him with approval on a point of etymology.15 Eusebius replied: Jerome reports that in the sixth book of the Apology for Origen Eusebius “makes the same complaint against Methodius the bishop and martyr” that was later made against Jerome himself — that he had drawn on Origen before writing against him.16 Much of the Aglaophon survives in Greek because Epiphanius later copied it into his heresy-catalogue (see the entry for 375–377).

Pamphilus and Eusebius answer the charges from prison (c. 307–310)

Pamphilus of Caesarea, presbyter and keeper of the library in which Origen's books had been collected, was arrested in November 307 and beheaded on 16 February 310; he spent the intervening months composing, with his pupil Eusebius, an Apology for Origen in five books, to which Eusebius added a sixth after his death. Only the first survives, in Rufinus's Latin of 397, and it is the earliest surviving inventory of the charge-sheet. Pamphilus enumerates nine accusations before answering them: that Origen holds the Son unbegotten; that the Son was emitted, as Valentinus taught; that Christ is a mere man, as Paul of Samosata taught; that Christ's acts were done “only in appearance, and by allegory”; that he preaches two Christs; that he dissolves the historical narratives of Scripture; that he denies the punishment of sinners and the resurrection of the dead; that his account of the soul is erroneous; and that he teaches transmigration into animals.17 He first observes that they cannot all be true together — the first four contradict one another, “for malice is blind” — and his method throughout is to answer not in his own words but by quoting Origen at length, chiefly out of the book the accusations come from. The accusations were thus in circulation some seventy years before Epiphanius wrote.

Epiphanius lists Origen in his catalogue of heresies (c. 375–377)

In the Panarion, his catalogue of eighty heresies, Epiphanius of Salamis gives Origen an entry of his own — heresy 64 — alongside the Gnostics and the Arians. It is the earliest extant heresy-catalogue to list Origen under his own name. The catalogue's own index states the charges in one sentence: the Origenists “reject the resurrection of the dead, represent Christ and the Holy Spirit as creatures, allegorize Paradise, the heavens and all the rest, and foolishly say that Christ's kingdom will come to an end.” The chapter prints passages of what it attacks — Origen's now-lost commentary on the first psalm, quoted “word for word, so that no one may call my attack on him vexatious,” followed by some fifty chapters of Methodius's dialogue, which survives in Greek through this copy. It also gives an account of Origen's life not found in Eusebius: a forced offering of incense under persecution, an expulsion, and a silent weeping sermon at Jerusalem, and, later in the chapter on other reports about him, the words “though I have no faith in the exaggerated stories about him, I have not neglected to report what is being said.”18 Whether the incense story is credible is disputed; see the interpretation page. Epiphanius pressed the same case in a letter to John of Jerusalem which Jerome translated into Latin, and which is why it survives: “The Son, he tells us, cannot see the Father, and the Holy Spirit cannot see the Son”; souls “were once angels in heaven, and… having sinned… have been cast down into this, and have been confined in bodies”; and Origen “denies the resurrection of this flesh, as he most clearly does in his book of explanations of the first psalm.”19

Evagrius Ponticus and the Kephalaia Gnostica (c. 383–399)

Evagrius Ponticus passed no judgement on Origen; he appears on this timeline because modern scholars, beginning with Antoine Guillaumont, have connected his writings with the anathemas of 553. A deacon of the Cappadocian circle, ordained by Basil and Gregory of Nazianzus and present at the Council of Constantinople in 381, he left the capital in 382 and settled among the monks of the Egyptian desert until his death in 399, writing there the Kephalaia Gnostica, a set of numbered chapters drawing on the speculations of Peri Archōn.20 In the unrevised Syriac recension published by Guillaumont in 1958, the chapters set out an original unity of minds, whose “first movement… is the separation of the nous from the Unity”; a fall by degrees into graded bodies; a restoration in which come “the destruction of worlds, the dissolution of bodies, and the abolition of names”; and a Christ who is not simply the Word but the one created mind that did not fall — the nous “by them called Christ” of the twelfth anathema of 553, a proposition found in the Kephalaia Gnostica and not in the surviving text of Peri Archōn.21

Guillaumont argued that the fifteen anathemas of 553 follow this system closely; Ilaria Ramelli argues that the condemned propositions belong to later radicalizers of Evagrius rather than to Evagrius himself. Whether the anathemas condemn a sixth-century Evagrian Origenism rather than Origen is disputed; see the interpretation page.22 His treatise On Prayer circulated under another name, printed among the works of St Nilus of Ancyra and read as Nilus's in the Philokalia, until Irénée Hausherr restored it to Evagrius in 1934.23

Theophilus of Alexandria and the synods of 400 (399–403)

Theophilus of Alexandria had preached against the monks who held that God has a human body — a view Origen's books were used to oppose — and in 399, Socrates records, a crowd of them came to Alexandria and threatened his life over it. Socrates reports that he placated them by saying: “I myself also disapprove of Origen's works, and consider those who countenance them deserving of censure.”24 He then expelled the Origenist monks from the monasteries of Nitria, and in 400 convened a council at Alexandria whose synodical letter Jerome translated: “The books of Origen have been read before a council of bishops and unanimously condemned.” Eight errors are enumerated, said to be drawn mainly from Peri Archōn — among them that “the Son compared with us is truth, but compared with the Father he is falsehood”, that “Christ's kingdom will one day come to an end”, and that Christ, having suffered once for men, “will suffer again for the demons.”25 He asked Epiphanius of Salamis for a parallel synod on Cyprus, and got one; Epiphanius “assembled the bishops of Cyprus together, and prohibited the examination of the books of Origen.”26

These are the earliest extant synodical decrees against Origen's teaching. Socrates, who saw the copy carried to Constantinople in 403, records that in it “they did not excommunicate Origen himself but condemned his books” — though Theophilus had asked for more than that, writing to Epiphanius that a synodical letter should go round “that by universal consent Origen himself may be expressly condemned and also the infamous heresy of which he was the author.”27 No acts of either synod survive; what survives is the correspondence. A Gallic traveller who reached Alexandria in the middle of it reports the bishops pressing for the wider sentence — that Origen's writings, “along with the author himself, and even his whole works, be condemned” — the Origenist monks answering with the thesis that the offending passages had been interpolated by heretics, and the governor of the city called in to disperse them.28 Socrates records that when Epiphanius read the decree to the bishops at Constantinople, Theotimus of Scythia refused to subscribe: “I neither choose, Epiphanius, to insult the memory of one who ended his life piously long ago; nor dare I be guilty of so impious an act, as that of condemning what our predecessors did not reject.”29

At Rome in 400, with Rufinus's Latin Peri Archōn in hand, Pope Anastasius I held a synod at Theophilus's prompting and wrote to Simplicianus of Milan: “everything written in days gone by Origen that is contrary to our faith is even by us rejected and condemned… If Origen has put forth any other writings, you are to know that they and their author are alike condemned by me.” Writing to John of Jerusalem the following year he added that Origen's “object was to disintegrate our faith, which is that of the Apostles”, and noted the imperial decree “by which every one who serves God is warned against the reading of Origen.”30 In the same letter the pope wrote that he neither knew nor wished to know who Origen was. Whether the Roman sentence reached Origen's person or only his books is disputed; see the interpretation page. Within three years John Chrysostom had been deposed as bishop of Constantinople — told on the Theophilus–Chrysostom timeline — and Jerome's friendship with Rufinus had broken, told on the Origenist controversy. The surviving decrees of 400 cite doctrines drawn mainly from Peri Archōn; they do not take the form of a numbered list of anathemas. Socrates records that Theophilus afterwards read Origen again: asked why he countenanced what he had publicly condemned, he answered that “Origen's books are like a meadow enameled with flowers of every kind. If, therefore, I chance to find a beautiful one among them, I cull it: but whatever appears to me to be thorny, I step over, as that which would prick.”31

Augustine on Origen (414–428)

The question reached Augustine from Spain. In 414 the priest Paulus Orosius came to Hippo to report that Priscillianism had been answered in his province with books of Origen's, and that their readers had taken up Origen's own opinions along with them; he set out six articles of what was being taught, among them that the world was made last of all so that souls which had sinned beforehand might be purged in it, and that the eternal fire is neither real fire nor eternal. Augustine grants that the province got “no small good from those books, in the very matter where the error is mortal” — the Trinity — and then forbids one thing: “do not dare to think anything about the correction of the devil and his angels and their restoration to their former state… because we ought to add nothing of our own presumption to the final sentence of the highest and most truthful Judge.”32

The City of God states the case twice, and both statements passed into the Latin inheritance. In XI.23 he names the pre-existent fall — souls which “sinned by abandoning God” and “merited different degrees of debasement from heaven to earth, and diverse bodies as prison-houses” — and cites his source: “in the books which he entitles peri archōn… this is his sentiment, this his utterance.” His objections are that Genesis gives creation one cause, that a good God should make good creatures; and if bodies were graded penalties the devils would have the grossest bodies of all, whereas “the wickedest devil possesses an ethereal body, while man… received even before his sin a body of clay.”33 In XXI.17, arguing with those who expect the damned to be released, he adds that Origen went further than they, expecting the devil himself to be freed, “but the Church, not without reason, condemned him for this and other errors, especially for his theory of the ceaseless alternation of happiness and misery” — a doctrine in which, Augustine says, Origen “lost even the credit of being merciful”, since a blessedness that can be lost again is no blessedness.34 Book XXI was written in the 420s; the proceedings against Origen then on record were the synods and papal letters of 400–401.

His catalogue of heresies, compiled about 428, takes up the inherited charges. Entry 43 opens by quoting Epiphanius verbatim — the resurrection denied, Christ and the Spirit made creatures, paradise and the heavens allegorized — and then reports the defence on three of the four: “those who defend him say that he taught the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit to be of one and the same substance, and did not reject the resurrection of the dead; though those who have read more of him try to convict him on these points too.” What he states without qualification is the purgation and release of all the damned including the devil, followed after immense ages by a relapse of the freed into the same evils, “these alternating turns of blessedness and misery of the rational creation” running on for ever.35 Whether Origen taught such a succession of worlds, and whether it entails a renewed fall, is disputed; see the interpretation page. On where any soul comes from, Augustine declined all his life to decide, as that timeline records.

Vincent of Lérins: “a great Trial to the Church” (434)

Vincent of Lérins uses Origen as a teaching example. He heads a chapter of his Commonitorium “The Error of Origen a great Trial to the Church” and presents him as a learned teacher whose gifts made his error a greater trial: “this Origen, such and so great as he was, wantonly abusing the grace of God, rashly following the bent of his own genius…” — with the result that “very many were led astray from the integrity of the faith, not by any human excellencies of this so great man, this so great doctor, this so great prophet, but, as the event showed, by the too perilous trial which he proved to be.”36 Vincent also records the suggestion, already current, that the texts in question had been tampered with — the thesis Rufinus had argued a generation earlier. Whether the passages were interpolated is disputed; see the interpretation page.

The Latin book-lists: Gennadius and the Decretum Gelasianum (c. 480 – c. 550)

In the West Origen next appears in two handbooks. Gennadius, presbyter of Marseilles, states the charges as doctrinal articles in his Book of Church Doctrines: the risen body will not be exchanged for another, “not according to Origen”, but the same corruptible flesh will rise incorruptible; after the resurrection and the judgement we are not to believe in a restoration to come, “which Origen raves about”, whereby demons or wicked men, purged as it were by torments, return to the angelic dignity in which they were created; and the souls of men are not from the beginning among the other intellectual natures nor created all at once, “as Origen imagines.”37 The three articles concern the resurrection body, the restoration, and the pre-existence of souls, and are stated without argument. The treatise circulated for centuries under Augustine's name, as did its ruling on the origin of the soul, told on his page there. In his catalogue of writers Gennadius records, from Sulpicius Severus, that a synod at Alexandria had decreed “that Origen is to be read, though cautiously, by those who are wise, for the good that is in him, and is to be rejected by the less able on account of the evil.”38

The other Latin list is the so-called Decretum Gelasianum, a register of books to be received and not received which travelled under the name of Pope Gelasius I and was for centuries read as a papal decree. Ernst von Dobschütz, in his edition of 1912, argued that it is an anonymous private compilation of the first half of the sixth century, most probably Italian, and F. C. Burkitt, reviewing it, summarized the case — the section ascribed to Damasus quotes Augustine, who was writing about 416, so the ascription “is of no historical value”, and “had it been an official decree of Gelasius it would have been known and used by Dionysius Exiguus and Cassiodorus. Thus these famous Lists represent no Papal ordinance, but are the production of an anonymous scholar of the sixth century.”39 Its ruling on Origen reads: “likewise some works of Origen, which the blessed man Jerome does not reject, we receive to be read, but we say that the rest with their author must be refused.” Eusebius survives the same list only in part, having “wrote one book in praise and to excuse Origen the schismatic”; and a work called the Repentance of Origen is listed among the apocrypha.40 The clause extends the rejection to the author as well as the books — “the rest with their author” — and makes Jerome's approval the criterion for which works may be read.

Justinian's edict to Menas and its nine anathemas (543)

The sixth-century controversy arose among the monks of Palestine. In the account of Cyril of Scythopolis, a monk of the anti-Origenist party, a party of monks called Origenists had settled in the Judaean lauras; Sabas of the Great Laura went to Constantinople in 531 to ask for imperial help against it and got nothing; his successor Gelasius expelled the Origenists from the Great Laura in 537 and they regrouped at the New Laura, while two of their party, Domitian and Theodore Askidas, were made bishops and stayed at court. The anti-Origenist monks drew up a memorandum, the papal representative at Constantinople carried it to the emperor, and in 543 Justinian issued an edict addressed to Menas, patriarch of Constantinople, quoting Peri Archōn at length and appending nine anathemas — the earliest extant numbered list of anathemas against Origenist teaching.41 The first condemns anyone who “says or holds that the souls of human beings pre-exist, as previously minds and holy powers, but that they reached satiety with divine contemplation and turned to what is worse… and were made to descend into bodies as a punishment”; the ninth, anyone who holds “that the punishment of demons and impious human beings is temporary and that it will have an end at some time.”42

The bishops resident in the capital subscribed the edict; in Richard Price's summary its canons were sent to the patriarchs “not for their confirmation but for their circulation.”43 Theodore Askidas signed too, and remained at court as an influential churchman for another fifteen years. How far the nine anathemas correspond to Peri Archōn is disputed article by article: see the interpretation pages on the ensouled luminaries (the sixth anathema; Peri Archōn I.7), the resurrection body (the fifth), a crucifixion for the demons (the seventh) and God's power and an eternal creation (the eighth). Because the edict quotes his Greek at length, Paul Koetschau's edition of 1913, and the English translation made from it, used it as a source for the text of Peri Archōn; John Behr's edition of 2017 prints such material in an appendix.44

The council of 553: the fifteen anathemas and the eleventh canon (5 May – 2 June 553)

Three distinct things are connected with Origen's name and the council of 553. The council was summoned for other business: its subject was the Three Chapters — Theodore of Mopsuestia, Theodoret of Cyrrhus, Ibas of Edessa — which Justinian had already condemned by edict and now wanted endorsed, and which Pope Vigilius, brought to Constantinople under guard in 546, would not endorse. Eight sessions ran from 5 May to 2 June 553 under Eutychius of Constantinople, with the pope absent and his name struck from the diptychs; the bishops issued fourteen anathemas against the Three Chapters, and Vigilius accepted the council's decisions the following December.45 Origen is not in the summons, and not in any of the correspondence surrounding it.46

The first of the three things is a set of fifteen anathemas against Origen and the Origenists — fuller and more explicitly Christological than Justinian's nine of 543. Its first article anathematizes anyone who “asserts the fabulous pre-existence of souls, and shall assert the monstrous restoration which follows from it”; its last, anyone who says “that the life of the spirits shall be like to the life which was in the beginning… so that the end and the beginning shall be alike, and that the end shall be the true measure of the beginning.”47 Between those two the fifteen describe a whole system — undifferentiated minds, a fall by degrees into graded bodies, ensouled stars, a spherical resurrection body, an end in which every spirit is equal to Christ. Guillaumont identified this as the system of the sixth-century Palestinian monks called Isochristoi (see the entry on modern scholarship below); whether the anathemas therefore condemn an Evagrian system rather than Origen is disputed; see the interpretation page. Origen's own name occurs in none of them. Whether the council in session passed them is also disputed: the text survives under a seventeenth-century Vienna manuscript's caption ascribing it to “the 165 holy Fathers of the holy fifth Synod”, and the nineteenth-century editors of the acts in English wrote that “after all is said the matter remains involved in the greatest obscurity”.48 Franz Diekamp's study of 1899 assigned them to the bishops already gathered at Constantinople in the weeks before the council opened, a dating Georges Florovsky follows while counting the condemnation the council's; the Pravoslavnaya Entsiklopediya places them at a sitting of the council whose record is lost; and Norman Tanner's edition prints the acts of 553 without them, noting that “recent studies have shown that these anathemas cannot be attributed to this council.”49 Cyril of Scythopolis, a Judaean monk writing within five years, whose account Ilaria Ramelli describes as “the position of the Sabaite opponents of Origen”, reports that the assembled fathers anathematized Origen together with Theodore, Evagrius and Didymus.50 Whether Origen himself was anathematized by an ecumenical council is disputed; see the interpretation page.

The second is the council's eleventh canon, which does contain Origen's name: “If anyone does not anathematize Arius, Eunomius, Macedonius, Apollinarius, Nestorius, Eutyches and Origen, as well as their heretical books… let him be anathema.”51 The canon requires the anathematizing of persons already condemned; Origen stands last on its list, though he is the earliest of the seven: the others run from the 320s to the 440s, Origen from the 230s. Whether the name stood there in 553 is disputed. The minutes of the sessions survive in a Latin version, the original Greek acts being lost; the anathemas survive also in Greek, which Tanner's edition translates as “the more authoritative version”, and the Pravoslavnaya Entsiklopediya notes that Origen's name stands in the Greek list as well. The parallel roll in Justinian's Homonoia omits Origen, and Vigilius's own documents never name him, which Ilaria Ramelli takes as evidence of later insertion; the Pravoslavnaya Entsiklopediya judges the interpolation hypothesis unconvincing, and the NPNF editors saw “no possible reason to doubt” that he was condemned by name in the canon.52 The third is the reception of the council, recorded in the entries that follow.

Maximus the Confessor's Ambiguum 7 (c. 628)

Writing about 628, some seventy-five years after the anathemas, Maximus the Confessor took up a phrase of Gregory Nazianzen — that we are “a portion of God” who have “flowed down from above” — which he found being read in the sense the anathemas had condemned. He states that reading at length before answering it: “According to the opinion of these people, there once existed a unity of rational beings, by virtue of which we were connatural with God, in whom we had our remaining and abode. In addition to this they speak of a ‘movement’ that came about, as a result of which the rational beings were variously dispersed, prompting God to look toward the creation of this corporeal world, so that He could bind them in bodies as a punishment for their former sins.”53 He answers by argument rather than by citing the council, reversing the order of the terms: coming-into-being precedes motion, so motion cannot be a fall away from a prior static unity but is the created nature tending toward an end it has not yet reached. “If, then, coming into being must necessarily be posited before beings can begin to move, it follows that motion is subsequent to the manifestation of being”; and therefore “it cannot be maintained that a movement of rational beings previously at rest in a primordial unity subsequently brought corporeal bodies into being.”54

He makes two further moves. Against the fall through satiety, Maximus argues that the Good cannot cloy: “satiety by definition is the quenching of appetite, and this happens either because appetite desired things that were trivial, or because it was repulsed and nauseated by things that were base and repugnant… It is obvious that neither of these can apply to God, who by nature is infinite and infinitely attractive, and who rather increases the appetites of those who enjoy Him owing to their participation in that which has no limit.” And while rejecting the pre-existence of minds, he retains a pre-existence: not of souls but of the logoi, the divine intentions of each creature, “steadfastly fixed” in God from eternity while the creatures themselves are made in time. That lets Gregory's phrase stand: “We are, then, and are called ‘portions of God’ because of the logoi of our being that exist eternally in God. Moreover, we are said to have ‘flowed down from above’ because we have failed to move in a manner consistent with the logos according to which we were created and which preexists in God.”55 Of the use made of Gregory, Maximus writes that “their behavior is unethical and irreligious”; throughout the discussion his opponents are only “some people” and “certain individuals”, and Origen's name does not occur. Nicholas Constas, who edited and translated the work, judges that the Origenism refuted here comes not from a live seventh-century party but “from the conciliar documents of the preceding century, especially the edicts of Justinian”, and that because those documents “simply condemned Origenism without addressing the deeper conceptual issues, Maximos was impelled to dive down into the wreckage of Origen's system, in order to reconstruct it on sound philosophical and theological principles” — “overcoming the weaknesses of Origen's theology from within and preserving its essential truths for the Christian tradition.”56

The name in the later councils and the Byzantine lists (680–787 and after)

Two later councils refer to the condemnation. Constantinople III (680–681) refers to “the fifth holy synod, the latest of them, which was gathered here against Theodore of Mopsuestia, Origen, Didymus and Evagrius”;57 Nicaea II (787) anathematizes “the mythical speculations of Origen, Evagrius and Didymus, as did the fifth synod, that assembled at Constantinople.”58 Both refer the condemnation to the fifth synod rather than conducting a new examination, and both name Origen together with Didymus and Evagrius.

Photius reads the whole dossier (c. 845)

About 845 Photius — later patriarch of Constantinople, then a layman compiling reading notes for his brother — devoted three notices of his Bibliotheca to Origen's books and the defences of them. His notice on Peri Archōn is brief, giving a book-by-book table of contents with the objections inserted as they arise: “Read Origen's four books On First Principles… In this his statements are often blasphemous; thus, he asserts that the Son was created by the Father, the Holy Ghost by the Son… He also makes other strange and impious statements, indulging in frivolous talk about the migration of souls, the stars being alive, and the like.”59 An anonymous five-book apology for Origen gets a fuller notice, and with it a fresh list of fifteen counts on which the apologist says Origen was falsely accused — that prayer should not be offered to the Son, that rational natures enter the bodies of irrational beings, that there is neither eternal punishment nor resurrection of the flesh, that the soul of the Saviour was the same as the soul of Adam, and the rest. Photius reports the apologist's two lines of defence — that the offending passages were “only intended as a rhetorical exercise, or that they were foisted into his writings by certain heterodox persons” — and closes the notice: “But, my dear sir, if any one is shown to be not altogether impious, this is no reason why he should escape punishment for obvious blasphemies.”60

The notice on Pamphilus and Eusebius's Apology — the source from which this page's account of Demetrius's synods is drawn — is biographical: Photius sets the tradition that Origen was martyred under Decius beside the tradition that he died at Tyre in his sixty-ninth year and adjudicates between them (“This is the truer account, unless the letters supposed to have been written by him after the Decian persecution are spurious”), and narrates the Alexandrian quarrel with Demetrius, not Origen, as the man whose “love… changed to hate.”61 Elsewhere in the same collection he transcribes Stephen Gobar's sixth-century pro-and-con dossier on Origen, which records that Athanasius and Titus of Bostra approved him on many points, that Gregory Nazianzen called him “friend of beauty and goodness” and Gregory of Nyssa remembered him with praise, and that “Theophilus and Epiphanius detest Origen”; Adolf von Harnack observes that Photius did not repudiate as heretical any of the fathers Gobar cites.62

The Synodikon of Orthodoxy and the chapters against John Italos (843; 1082)

The Synodikon of Orthodoxy, chanted every first Sunday of Lent from 843 onward, names those it anathematizes at length — Arius “the first fighter against God and leader of the heresies”, Nestorius, Valentinus, Paul of Samosata, Sabellius, Eutyches, Dioscorus, Severus, Sergius, Pyrrhus, “and simply all heretics”. In the received text Origen is not among them, and neither are Didymus or Evagrius.63 In March 1082 the philosopher John Italos was tried at Constantinople, and eleven chapters against him were added to the Synodikon — the first major accretion to it since 843. They anathematize those who “receive the Platonic ideas as true”; those who say that at the last and common resurrection human beings “will rise and be judged with other bodies, and not with those in which they lived the present life”; and, in the tenth chapter, those “who receive and hand on the vain and Greek sayings: that there is a pre-existence of souls, and that all things were not made and brought forth out of nothing, and that there is an end to punishment or a restoration again of the creation and of human affairs.”64 The pre-existence of souls, a resurrection in other bodies, an end to punishment and a restoration also figure in the anathemas of 543 and 553; the chapters of 1082 are directed against a living philosopher, and Origen is not named in them.

The Latin schools: Abelard's Sic et Non (12th century)

In the Latin West Origen's homilies and commentaries continued to be read in the translations Rufinus and Jerome had made of them — as W. H. Fremantle puts it in the NPNF edition of Rufinus, “a considerable part of the works of the great Alexandrian have floated down across the ocean of the Dark Ages, and, while lost in their native Greek, have in their Latin garb come to enrich the later civilization of the West.”65 Peter Abelard's Sic et Non, a twelfth-century textbook of theological method, both reprints the Latin ruling against him and quotes him. Its prologue prints the Decretum Gelasianum's ruling verbatim under the heading “From the decrees of Pope Gelasius on authentic books” — “certain small works of Origen, which the most blessed man Jerome does not reject, we receive to be read; but all the rest, with their author, we say must be refused” — and lists the Repentance of Origen among the apocrypha, exactly as the sixth-century compiler had. In the same prologue Abelard defends the practice of quoting him, by way of Jerome: “If this is a crime, let the confessor Hilary be accused, who translated the interpretation of the psalms and the homilies on Job out of his books” — and cites Origen's own disclaimer, as Jerome reports it, that the offending speculations “are not, in our judgement, dogmas, but questions only, and put forward, so that they should not seem entirely untouched.”66

In the body of the work Abelard cites Origen as an authority fourteen times, including on whether Christ in descending to hell freed all, and on whether God punishes the same sin both here and hereafter. In the Historia calamitatum, writing of his own castration, he cites Origen's as a precedent and calls him “the greatest of Christian philosophers” (maximum illum Christianorum philosophorum, summum Christianorum philosophorum).67 Dante does not name Origen in the Commedia; the Enciclopedia Dantesca records, and rejects, a proposal by N. Netri to identify him with an unnamed figure in Paradiso X.68

“Is Origen saved?”: Pico, Merlin, and the printed Origen (1486–1574)

In December 1486 Giovanni Pico della Mirandola published nine hundred theses for public disputation at Rome. The last of the twenty-nine he offered on his own theological opinion reads: “It is more reasonable to believe that Origen is saved than to believe that he is damned.”69 A commission appointed by Innocent VIII in February 1487 indicted thirteen of the nine hundred, and this was among them; Pico reports the censure in his own words — the masters said the conclusion was “rash and reprehensible, and savouring of heresy, and contrary to the determination of the universal Church” — and answered it with a treatise, the Disputatio de salute Origenis, laid out in seven articles of which the fifth is the question itself. He argues from anachronism and from the absence of obstinacy: on the origin of the soul, “Origen was not bound in his own time to believe explicitly all that we are now bound to believe about the soul, because it was neither set down plainly in Scripture nor yet determined by the Church as it was afterwards”; and no one can produce evidence that he repented, because no one can produce evidence that he thought he had erred. The pope condemned all nine hundred theses in a bull of 4 August 1487 — which does not name Origen — and Alexander VI absolved Pico in 1493.70

In 1512 Jacques Merlin brought out at Paris the first collected Latin Origen, in four folio tomes, and prefaced the third with an Apologia for him; Noël Béda, the syndic of the Paris faculty of theology, attacked it and wrote a refutation, though the faculty itself seems never to have pronounced on the matter.71 Erasmus prepared an edition for Froben at Basel; it appeared in September 1536, two months after his death, and his short preface to the reader ends: “Nowhere is Origen not aflame, but nowhere is he more aflame than where he handles the words and deeds of Christ.” In a letter of 1518 to Johann Eck he wrote: “A single page of Origen teaches me more Christian philosophy than ten of Augustine” — a sentence he quotes as a saying of his own, and immediately qualifies with the news that he intends to edit Augustine too. On the condemnation he wrote, in the preface to a fragment of Origen on Matthew in 1527: “We weigh Origen by the manners, rites and dogmas of these times. If you want to judge how absurd that is, reverse the scene” — and imagine the schoolmen judged by the third century.72 Martin Luther, who had already written that Jerome and “his Origen” “filled the world with these trifles”, told his table in 1532 that “in the whole of Origen there is not one word about Christ”, and elsewhere that “Origen I have already excommunicated.”73 Gilbert Génébrard's Paris edition of 1574 announces on its title page that its author is “the great and ancient interpreter of the Scriptures and the second master of the Church after the apostles.”74

Defences, a censure, and the Maurist edition (1648–1759)

In 1648 the Jesuit Pierre Halloix published Origenes defensus (Liège), a defence of Origen; it was placed on the Index of Prohibited Books donec corrigatur, the decree communicated to its author in 1650 and published in 1655. The roster of Origen's modern defenders drawn up later in the century by the Dominican Noël Alexandre names Pico, Merlin, Génébrard, Étienne Binet and Halloix, all of them Catholics.75 In England, writers around the Cambridge Platonists defended some of the condemned doctrines themselves. An anonymous Letter of Resolution concerning Origen and the Chief of his Opinions (London, 1661), traditionally ascribed to George Rust, defends the pre-existence of souls and concludes that “the punishment of the damned, as it implies the sense of pain, shall not be eternal in the highest sense of the word”, and argues from the Church of England's own silence — the Thirty-Nine Articles dropped the Edwardine article condemning the restorers of that opinion. Henry More, who publicly denied writing it, called Origen “that Miracle of the Christian World” and wrote of the Fifth Council that the particulars of his condemnation were removed from the records by the same power that occasioned it, “for it was their Wrong, not their Fault, that they were misinformed.”76

Pierre-Daniel Huet's edition of the Greek exegetical remains (Rouen, 1668) carried a three-part study of Origen's life, doctrine and writings, the Origeniana, which Huet undertook precisely because the thing had been done “sparingly by some, carelessly by others, and fully, with a mind free of either love or hatred, by no one at all”. Huet concluded that Origen held the orthodox doctrine of the Trinity, and “besmirched it with Platonic ravings.”77 Charles Delarue of the Congregation of Saint-Maur brought out the first two volumes of the Opera omnia at Paris in 1733, dedicated to Clement XII, the third in 1740, and the fourth — begun by him and finished after his death by his nephew Charles-Vincent — in 1759, with Huet's Origeniana reprinted in it. Migne reprinted that edition in 1857 as volumes 11–17 of the Greek Patrologia.78

Modern scholarship and reception (1900–present)

Antoine Guillaumont argued that the anathemas of 553 describe an Evagrian Origenism; Henri Crouzel and Ilaria Ramelli argued further that the propositions condemned in 543 and 553 are not Origen's own and that Peri Archōn is written as inquiry. Others hold otherwise: Georges Florovsky reads the system condemned in the sixth century as Origen's own scheme, Michael McClymond holds against Ramelli that Origen taught the restoration as doctrine, and Franz Diekamp, Richard Price and the Pravoslavnaya Entsiklopediya hold Origen's name in the council's eleventh canon authentic.79 The entries below record that work and the editions on which it turned.

In 1913 Paul Koetschau published Peri Archōn in the Berlin corpus of the early Greek Christian writers. Rufinus had admitted omitting passages he took to be heretical interpolations; Koetschau concluded that Rufinus could therefore not be trusted at all, and that the reports of Origen's opponents preserved what the translator had suppressed. He accordingly broke up Rufinus's Latin wherever he thought he could detect a gap, and filled the gaps from Jerome's letter to Avitus, from Justinian's edict, and from the anathemas of 553. G. W. Butterworth's English of 1936 announced its dependence on its own title page — Being Koetschau's Text of the De Principiis Translated into English — and was the standard English version for some eighty years.80 John Behr's Oxford edition of 2017 describes the method — Koetschau was “convinced that the accounts of Origen's teaching given by his opponents, especially the letter of Jerome to Avitus, the reports of Justinian, and even the Anathemas of 553, do in fact represent Origen's authentic teaching”, and some of the resulting passages are ones he “literally ‘made up,’ by stitching together sentences from various anti-Origenist writers” — and prints the disputed material in an appendix.81

In 1958 Antoine Guillaumont published the editio princeps of the Syriac recension (S2) of Evagrius of Pontus's Kephalaia Gnostica preserved in a single British Library manuscript, which he judged the unrevised text, the long-known version (S1) being in his judgement a doctrinally revised one. He then argued, in a paper of 1961 and the book of 1962 that made the case in full, that the fifteen anathemas track propositions standing in S2, and quote Peri Archōn nowhere.82 On this reading the system anathematized — the pre-existent equality of minds, the fall by satiety, the spherical resurrection body, the final equality of every spirit with Christ — is Evagrian and monastic, refracted through the sixth-century Palestinian lauras. Whether it follows that the anathemas condemn Evagrian Origenism rather than Origen is disputed; see the interpretation page. Daniel Hombergen argued that anathemas framed a century and a half after Evagrius's death are a poor blueprint for Evagrius either, and Augustine Casiday and Ilaria Ramelli have pressed the same point further.83

Henri de Lubac's Histoire et Esprit (1950) presented Origen as an interpreter of Scripture — a reviewer at the time, John L. McKenzie, called it “the most weighty contribution to the discussion of early Christian exegesis” and noted its central complaint, that “most of Origen's critics have not read his exegetical works.”84 Hans Urs von Balthasar, whose anthology of Origen had appeared in German in 1938, opens its introduction: “It is all but impossible to overestimate Origen and his importance for the history of Christian thought. To rank him beside Augustine and Thomas simply accords him his rightful place in this history.”85 Jean Daniélou's Origène (1948) and Henri Crouzel's Origène (1985) followed; on the council Crouzel wrote that the fifteen anathemas “do not appear in the official minutes of that Council: so they are not canonically the work of an ecumenical council.”86 Ilaria Ramelli has argued that Origen's name in the eleventh canon is a later insertion (see the entry for 553). From 1973 the field has met in the international Origen colloquia, begun at Montserrat that September and held every few years since.87

Mark Edwards's Origen against Plato (2002) argues that Origen is not a Platonist — his is “an autonomous philosophy, designed to answer, not to flatter, the teaching of the schools”, and he “roundly declares the Ideas or Forms of Plato to be chimerical.”88 Elizabeth Clark's study of the first controversy (1992) argued that already around 400 “Origenism” was a construct, each of its opponents building a different version of it and using the charge to prosecute quarrels that were not principally doctrinal.89

No papal or conciliar act reversing the condemnations of 543, 553 or 787 has been traced here. Benedict XVI devoted two general audiences to Origen in April and May 2007, calling him “a figure crucial to the whole development of Christian thought” and “a true ‘maestro’”, and closing with an invitation to “welcome into your hearts the teaching of this great master of faith”; neither audience mentions the condemnation.90 The Catechism of the Catholic Church quotes him on prayer, from De oratione: “He ‘prays without ceasing’ who unites prayer to works and good works to prayer.”91 Particular questions this history raises are recorded below as disputed readings.

Disputed readings

How the charges against Origen, and the judgements passed on him, have been read. Three sets of questions are grouped: the charges concerning the fall and restoration of rational minds, the charge that he taught the devil's salvation, and whether the condemnations reached Origen himself or only his books; the rest stand on their own.

The fall and restoration of the rational minds

Charges concerning where souls came from, whether this world is one of a succession of ages, and whether all rational creatures are finally restored.

  • Was the pre-existence of souls an open question when Origen taught it?
  • Did Origen teach an endless succession of worlds, and does it entail a second fall?
  • Did Origen teach universal restoration (apokatastasis) as doctrine, and was it condemnable in his day?

The devil’s salvation

Readings of Peri Archōn I.6 and III.6.5 and of Origen’s letter denying the charge.

  • Did the charge that Origen taught the devil’s salvation represent his teaching?
  • Did Origen teach that Christ will be crucified again in the heavens to save the demons?

The condemnation of the man and of his books

Whether the judgements of 400–401 and 553 fell on Origen himself or on his writings and doctrines.

  • Did Anastasius I’s sentence of 400–401 condemn Origen’s books only, or Origen himself?
  • Was Origen himself anathematized by an ecumenical council? — on the fifteen anathemas and the eleventh canon of 553.

Other disputed readings

  • Was Demetrius’s sentence (c. 231–232) disciplinary only, or did it also concern Origen’s teaching?
  • Did Origen offer incense under persecution, as Epiphanius reports?
  • Was Origen judged by standards defined after his lifetime?
  • Were Origen’s condemned opinions offered as inquiry or as assertion?
  • Did the fifteen anathemas of 553 condemn sixth-century Evagrian Origenism rather than Origen?
  • Were the views condemned in Origen also held, uncondemned, by others?
  • Was the plea of heretical interpolation applied alike to Gregory of Nyssa and to Origen?
  • What does the condemnation of Origen and Theodore of Mopsuestia by the same council of 553 indicate about its aims?
  • Can Origen’s teaching be recovered, given how the text of Peri Archōn has come down?
  • Was Origen’s condemnation driven by ecclesiastical politics or by his doctrine?
  • Was Origen’s subordination of the Son pre-Nicene idiom, or a teaching of the kind later condemned in Arius?
  • What did Origen mean by identifying the two Seraphim of Isaiah 6 as the Son and the Holy Spirit?
  • Did Origen teach that God and all rational creatures are “of one and the same essence”?
  • Did Origen deny the resurrection of the flesh, and did he teach that risen bodies are spherical?
  • Did Origen teach that hellfire is the fire of a guilty conscience rather than a material, external fire?
  • Did Origen teach that the sun, moon and stars are living, ensouled rational beings?
  • Did Origen teach that the stars cause events? — a charge in the list Photius transmits.
  • Did Origen teach the transmigration of souls?
  • Did Origen teach that “magic is not an evil”, and that certain names have a natural power?
  • Did Origen’s allegorical exegesis set aside the literal and historical sense of Scripture?
  • Did Origen teach that the “coats of skins” (Genesis 3:21) are the mortal human body?
  • Did Origen teach that the image of God was lost at the fall?
  • Did Origen teach that God’s power is finite and that creation is coeternal with God? — the eighth anathema of 543.
  • Did Origen teach that the Son’s kingdom will come to an end?
