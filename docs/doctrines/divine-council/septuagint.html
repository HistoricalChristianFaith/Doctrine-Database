The Septuagint on the Divine Council

Dates: c. 250–100 BC (the Septuagint, the Greek translation of the Hebrew Bible made by Alexandrian Jews).

Summary: At the level of the text itself, the Greek translators preserved — and in places sharpened — a cosmology of real, lesser heavenly beings gathered around Yahweh. Where later Hebrew tradition muted the plurality of "gods" and "sons of God," the Septuagint kept an assembly of divine beings among whom God stands to judge, apportions the nations to heavenly administrators, and treats the gods of the pagans as genuine (malevolent) spiritual powers rather than as nothing at all.

Claims & evidence

Deuteronomy 32:8 — the nations apportioned "according to the number of the angels of God"

ἤστησεν ὅρια ἐθνῶν κατὰ ἀριθμὸν ἀγγέλων Θεοῦ

he set the bounds of the nations according to the number of the angels of God.

— Deuteronomy 32:8 (Septuagint), Greek text and English at Elpenor. Where the received Hebrew (Masoretic) text reads that God fixed the nations' boundaries "according to the number of the sons of Israel," the Greek reads "angels of God," and a Dead Sea Scrolls fragment of the same verse (4QDeutj) reads "sons of God" (υἱῶν θεοῦ). On this reading the seventy nations of the Table of Nations (Genesis 10) are parcelled out among heavenly beings — the divine council administering the world under the Most High. This is the text-critical linchpin of the divine-council reading.

Psalm 82(81):1 — God stands in the assembly of gods

Ο ΘΕΟΣ ἥστη ἐν συναγωγῇ θεῶν, ἐν μέσῳ δὲ θεοὺσ διακρινεῖ.

God stands in the assembly of gods; and in the midst [of them] will judge gods.

— Psalm 81:1 (Septuagint numbering = Hebrew Psalm 82:1), Greek text and English at Elpenor; cf. the verse Psalm 82:1. The translators rendered the Hebrew ‘adat ’el straightforwardly as a "συναγωγῆ θεῶν" — an assembly of gods — within which the God of Israel rises to pass judgment. The plural θεοί ("gods") is kept as the object of that judgment, not explained away.

Psalm 96(95):5 — the gods of the nations are demons

ὅτι πάντες οἱ θεοὶ τῶν ἐθνῶν δαιμόνια, ὁ δὲ Κύρι&omicromicron;ς τοὺς οὐρανοὺς ἐποίησεν.

For all the gods of the heathen are devils: but the Lord made the heavens.

— Psalm 95:5 (Septuagint numbering = Hebrew Psalm 96:5), Greek text and English at Elpenor. Where the Hebrew has the nations' gods as ’elilim ("worthless idols," "nothings"), the Greek reads δαιμόνια — "demons." The translators did not flatten the pagan gods into empty statues but read them as real, malevolent spiritual powers: idols with something behind them. The same Greek word carries into the New Testament's treatment of idol-worship (1 Corinthians 10:20).

Notes

  • Taken together, these three renderings show that Second-Temple Greek-speaking Jews carried a divine-council cosmology at the level of their Scripture itself: an assembly of gods around Yahweh (Ps 82), heavenly beings set over the nations (Deut 32), and the nations' gods understood as real demonic powers (Ps 96).
  • The Deuteronomy 32:8 reading is now text-critically favored by many scholars: the combined witness of the Septuagint ("angels of God") and the Qumran fragment 4QDeutj ("sons of God") is widely judged to preserve an older text than the Masoretic "sons of Israel," which looks like a later theological smoothing away of the other divine beings. The variant is adjudicated in full — steelman, Masoretic-priority counter-case, and rating (assessment: sound) — on the argument page Deuteronomy 32:8: "sons of God," "sons of Israel," or "angels of God"?; see also the survey at TheTorah.com, "The Sons of Israel or God? – Deuteronomy 32:8", and Michael Heiser's text-critical study, "Deuteronomy 32:8 and the Sons of God," Bibliotheca Sacra 158 (2001). This is the reading on which the modern divine-council argument was rebuilt.
