Michael S. Heiser on the Divine Council

Dates: c. 2015 (b. 1963 – d. 2023) · Wikipedia
Summary: Michael S. Heiser (1963–2023), an Old-Testament and Semitic-languages scholar (PhD, University of Wisconsin–Madison), is the modern popularizer of the "divine council" reading of the Hebrew Bible. Against the long-dominant tradition that read the Bible's "gods" and "sons of God" as human beings, idols, or mere metaphors, Heiser argues that Scripture takes for granted a populated spiritual world of real, created, lesser elohim — Yahweh's heavenly council — while insisting that Yahweh alone is God by nature, "species-unique among all residents of the spiritual world." Psalm 82 and Deuteronomy 32:8 are the load-bearing texts, and this "Deuteronomy 32 worldview" is the frame behind his work on the Nephilim, the gods of the nations, and the unseen realm.

Claims & evidence

Psalm 82's elohim are real divine beings, not human judges

Seeing these elohim as divine beings is viewed as a threat to monotheism, the heart of biblical theology… The point of verse 6 is that, in response to the corruption of the elohim, they will be stripped of their immortality at God's discretion and die like humans die.

— The Plural Elohim of Psalm 82: Gods or Men?, drmsh.com. Heiser reads the assembly of Psalm 82:1 as a real heavenly court and holds that "the plural elohim in Psalm 82 cannot be humans": the sentence "you shall die like men" (82:7) is a demotion, empty if pronounced on beings already mortal. Whether this single psalm bears the weight he places on it is weighed on the Psalm 82 identity argument (assessment: contested).

"Divine council" is standard scholarly vocabulary, not a novelty

The term divine council is used by Hebrew and Semitic scholars to refer to the heavenly host, the pantheon of divine beings who administer the affairs of the cosmos.

— The Unseen Realm: Recovering the Supernatural Worldview of the Bible (Lexham, 2015), ch. 2. Heiser locates his reading in the mainstream of comparative Semitics (Mullen, Cross, M. S. Smith), where the Ugaritic "assembly of El" stands behind the biblical throne-council of 1 Kings 22, Job 1, and Psalm 89. His distinctive move is not the council itself but insisting that the Old Testament writers meant it literally while refusing to make Yahweh one god among peers.

Yahweh is an elohim, but no other elohim is Yahweh

The Old Testament writers understood that Yahweh was an elohim—but no other elohim was Yahweh. He was species-unique among all residents of the spiritual world.

— The Unseen Realm (Lexham, 2015), ch. 3. This is Heiser's answer to the charge that a real council of elohim dissolves monotheism: elohim, on his account, is a "place-of-residence" term for any inhabitant of the spiritual world (God, angels, the demonic, the human dead of 1 Sam 28:13), not a statement of rank. Yahweh's uniqueness is one of nature — uncreated, omnipotent, Creator of the other elohim — not of category. The move mirrors the patristic distinction (Athanasius, Jerome) that others are "gods" by grace and He alone God by nature, arriving at a similar guard from the opposite direction. Whether this semantics actually holds — and where Heiser overstates it — is weighed on the "realm of residence, not rank" argument (assessment: plausible): the monotheism-defense conclusion is well-grounded, but the strong lexical claim that elohim simply means "resident of the spiritual plane" is over-precise — the standard lexica carry no locative sense, and the word is used functionally of embodied humans (Moses "made elohim to Pharaoh," Exod 7:1), which a pure residence-rule cannot absorb. Trust the conclusion; flag the claim that Hebrew semantics proves it.

The nations were disinherited to rebel elohim at Babel (Deut 32:8–9)

When the Most High divided to the nations their inheritance, when he separated the sons of Adam, he set the bounds of the people according to the number of the sons of God.

— Heiser's preferred text of Deuteronomy 32:8, following the Septuagint and 4QDeutj ("sons of God") against the Masoretic "sons of Israel"; the text-critical case is his Bibliotheca Sacra article, weighed and rated (assessment: sound) on the argument page Deuteronomy 32:8: "sons of God," "sons of Israel," or "angels of God"? On his reading the Babel judgment (Gen 11) hands the seventy nations over to created elohim who then rebel and become "the gods of the nations," keeping Israel as Yahweh's own portion — and Psalm 82 is the scene of their arraignment for misrule. This "Deuteronomy 32 worldview" is the architecture beneath his Nephilim work and his reading of the gods of paganism.1

The nations' gods are territorial spirit-princes (Daniel 10)

The gods of the nations were real. They had been assigned to their nations by Yahweh himself… This is why we read about the "prince of Persia" and the "prince of Greece" who oppose God's angel in Daniel 10.

— The Unseen Realm (Lexham, 2015), chs. 14–15. This is the payoff of the Deuteronomy 32 worldview: if the nations were parcelled out to lesser elohim, those beings should surface elsewhere as powers over particular nations, and Heiser finds them in the angelic "prince of Persia" and "prince of Greece" who resist Gabriel while "Michael your prince" fights for Israel (Daniel 10:13, 20–21), echoed at Deuteronomy 4:19–20. The core of this reading is strong and mainstream — that Daniel 10's princes are the angelic patrons of the nations, grounded in the Deut 32:8 allotment, is the majority reading of the critical commentaries. But two overstatements should be flagged. First, Heiser's popular framing (and the "territorial spirits" spirituality downstream of it) slides from national to territorial — a spirit bound to turf — where the texts bind the spirits to peoples (Michael tracks Israel into exile, not a plot of ground), a slide even sympathetic supernaturalist scholars decline. Second, Deuteronomy 4:19–20 is pressed as an independent proof of ruling spirit-beings, though its strongest philology reads it the other way — a demotion of the sun, moon, and stars to created objects given to the nations. Both are weighed on the argument page Cosmic geography: do Daniel 10 and Deuteronomy 4:19 confirm territorial national elohim? (assessment: plausible): trust "real spirit-powers over the nations, shown in conflict in Daniel 10"; flag the "territorial" strong form and the Deut 4:19 leg.2

The Old Testament carries "two Yahwehs" — a visible God and an invisible one

The ancient Israelite knew two Yahwehs—one invisible, a spirit, the other visible, often in human form.… There was no sense of a violation of monotheism since either figure was indeed Yahweh.

— The Unseen Realm (Lexham, 2015), the "Godhead" chapters (chs. 16–18). Heiser argues that the "Angel of Yahweh" who bears the divine Name (Exod 23:21, "my name is in him"), speaks and acts as Yahweh, and yet is distinguished from a sending Yahweh — seen at Mamre, in the bush, in Jacob's wrestling, and above all in the two Yahwehs of Genesis 19:24 ("the LORD rained… from the LORD out of heaven") — is a genuine Old-Testament "second Yahweh" figure, the seedbed the New Testament identifies with Christ (the pre-incarnate Word the Fathers read into the theophanies). To his credit, Heiser is guarded: he calls this a binitarian "Godhead" backdrop, explicitly not the Trinity taught in the Old Testament. The visible-Yahweh strand and its ancient patristic reading (Justin, Irenaeus, Tertullian, Novatian, Eusebius) are real and strong; what to flag is (a) the slide, in the popular reception more than in Heiser's own wording, from a distinct figure to a distinct divine being — a "binitarian godhead" as settled Old-Testament ontology, where the mainstream reads the messenger as Yahweh's self-manifestation or a plenipotentiary agent, and Hurtado and Bauckham relocate the data to "principal agent" and "divine identity"; and (b) the further popularizing slide into "the Trinity is in the Old Testament," which overstates even Heiser. All weighed on the argument page The two Yahwehs: is the Angel of Yahweh a second, visible Yahweh figure? (assessment: plausible).3

Notes

  • Three rebellions, not one. Heiser's larger scheme spreads the supernatural revolt across three distinct events — Eden (the nachash, Gen 3), the sons of God (Gen 6:1–4), and Babel (Gen 11 read through Deut 32:8–9). A precision the popular presentations blur: the "gods of the nations" arraigned in Psalm 82 are, in his own architecture, the Babel rebels, of the same kind as the Genesis-6 "sons of God" but a distinct group — the Genesis-6 Watchers being bound to await judgment (2 Peter 2:4; Jude 6; 1 Enoch 10) while the nations' gods still rule. So Psalm 82 establishes rebel elohim over the nations without, by itself, identifying them as the Genesis-6 beings; that further identification is the ante-Nicene euhemerist step, weighed on the Nephilim timeline's pagan-gods argument (assessment: contested).
  • The linchpin is a single psalm carrying a system. The Psalm 82 identity claim is genuinely the stronger reading and now the majority critical one (see the argument page), but Heiser at times writes as though it settles the whole framework by itself. It does not: the human-judges reading has real support in the judicial charges of 82:2–4 and in Israel's elohim-as-judges case-law (Exod 21:6; 22:8–9), and John 10:34–36 cuts for neither side. The council reading earns "the powers over the nations are real"; it does not, on its own, earn every later step built on it.
  • Also a witness on other timelines. Heiser appears on this wiki as the modern anchor of the Nephilim and UFOs-and-the-demonic readings, and on the Goliath-slayer question — the divine-council frame is the common root of the first two.
