Michael S. Heiser on the Divine Council

Dates: c. 2015 (b. 1963 – d. 2023) · Wikipedia
Summary: Michael S. Heiser (1963–2023), an Old-Testament and Semitic-languages scholar (PhD, University of Wisconsin–Madison), set out the "divine council" reading of the Hebrew Bible for scholarly and general readers. Against readings that take the Bible's "gods" and "sons of God" as human beings, idols, or metaphors, Heiser argues that Scripture takes for granted a populated spiritual world of real, created, lesser elohim — Yahweh's heavenly council — while insisting that Yahweh alone is God by nature, "species-unique among all residents of the spiritual world." Psalm 82 and Deuteronomy 32:8 are his central texts, and this "Deuteronomy 32 worldview" is the frame behind his work on the Nephilim, the gods of the nations, and the unseen realm.

Claims & evidence

Psalm 82's elohim are real divine beings, not human judges

Seeing these elohim as divine beings is viewed as a threat to monotheism, the heart of biblical theology… The point of verse 6 is that, in response to the corruption of the elohim, they will be stripped of their immortality at God's discretion and die like humans die.

— The Plural Elohim of Psalm 82: Gods or Men?, drmsh.com. Heiser reads the assembly of Psalm 82:1 as a real heavenly court and holds that "the plural elohim in Psalm 82 cannot be humans": the sentence "you shall die like men" (82:7) is a demotion, empty if pronounced on beings already mortal.

"Divine council" as a term of comparative Semitics

The term divine council is used by Hebrew and Semitic scholars to refer to the heavenly host, the pantheon of divine beings who administer the affairs of the cosmos.

— The Unseen Realm: Recovering the Supernatural Worldview of the Bible (Lexham, 2015), ch. 2. Heiser places his reading alongside the comparative Semitics of Mullen, Cross, and M. S. Smith, who set the Ugaritic "assembly of El" beside the biblical throne-council of 1 Kings 22, Job 1, and Psalm 89. He holds that the Old Testament writers meant the council literally, while refusing to make Yahweh one god among peers.

Yahweh is an elohim, but no other elohim is Yahweh

The Old Testament writers understood that Yahweh was an elohim—but no other elohim was Yahweh. He was species-unique among all residents of the spiritual world.

— The Unseen Realm (Lexham, 2015), ch. 3. This is Heiser's answer to the charge that a real council of elohim dissolves monotheism: elohim, on his account, is a "place-of-residence" term for any inhabitant of the spiritual world (God, angels, the demonic, the human dead of 1 Sam 28:13), not a statement of rank. Yahweh's uniqueness is one of nature — uncreated, omnipotent, Creator of the other elohim — not of category. Athanasius and Jerome, who read the "gods" of Psalm 82 as men, distinguish those who are "gods" by grace from Him who is God by nature.

The nations were disinherited to rebel elohim at Babel (Deut 32:8–9)

When the Most High divided to the nations their inheritance, when he separated the sons of Adam, he set the bounds of the people according to the number of the sons of God.

— Heiser's preferred text of Deuteronomy 32:8, following the Septuagint and 4QDeutj ("sons of God") against the Masoretic "sons of Israel"; the text-critical case is his Bibliotheca Sacra article; both readings of the variant are set out on Does Deuteronomy 32:8 originally end "sons of God" or "sons of Israel"? On his reading the Babel judgment (Gen 11) hands the seventy nations over to created elohim who then rebel and become "the gods of the nations," keeping Israel as Yahweh's own portion — and Psalm 82 is the scene of their arraignment for misrule. This "Deuteronomy 32 worldview" is the architecture beneath his Nephilim work and his reading of the gods of paganism.1

The nations' gods are territorial spirit-princes (Daniel 10)

The gods of the nations were real. They had been assigned to their nations by Yahweh himself… This is why we read about the "prince of Persia" and the "prince of Greece" who oppose God's angel in Daniel 10.

— The Unseen Realm (Lexham, 2015), chs. 14–15. On Heiser's reading, if the nations were parcelled out to lesser elohim, those beings should surface elsewhere as powers over particular nations, and he finds them in the angelic "prince of Persia" and "prince of Greece" who resist Gabriel while "Michael your prince" fights for Israel (Daniel 10:13, 20–21), echoed at Deuteronomy 4:19–20. John J. Collins (Daniel, Hermeneia, 1993) likewise reads Daniel 10's princes as the angelic patrons of the nations. David E. Stevens (Bibliotheca Sacra, 2000) and Clinton E. Arnold read such spirits as bound to peoples rather than to territory (Michael follows Israel into exile), against a "territorial spirits" framing. Jeffrey H. Tigay (Deuteronomy, JPS, 1996) reads Deuteronomy 4:19–20 as a demotion of the sun, moon, and stars to created objects given to the nations.2

The Old Testament carries "two Yahwehs" — a visible God and an invisible one

The ancient Israelite knew two Yahwehs—one invisible, a spirit, the other visible, often in human form.… There was no sense of a violation of monotheism since either figure was indeed Yahweh.

— The Unseen Realm (Lexham, 2015), the "Godhead" chapters (chs. 16–18). Heiser argues that the "Angel of Yahweh" who bears the divine Name (Exod 23:21, "my name is in him"), speaks and acts as Yahweh, and yet is distinguished from a sending Yahweh — seen at Mamre, in the bush, in Jacob's wrestling, and above all in the two Yahwehs of Genesis 19:24 ("the LORD rained… from the LORD out of heaven") — is an Old-Testament "second Yahweh" figure, whom he connects with the Christ of the New Testament; Justin, Irenaeus, Tertullian, Novatian, and Eusebius read the theophanies as appearances of the pre-incarnate Word. Heiser calls this a binitarian "Godhead" backdrop, explicitly not the Trinity taught in the Old Testament. Hurtado reads such data in terms of a "principal agent," and Bauckham in terms of "divine identity" (⚠ unverified — works not cited); Camilla von Heijne (The Messenger of the Lord in Early Jewish Interpretations of Genesis, 2010) finds early Jewish interpreters reading the messenger sometimes as an emissary distinct from God and sometimes as an extension of Yahweh, akin to a divine manifestation.3

Reception and debate

  • Three rebellions. Heiser's larger scheme spreads the supernatural revolt across three distinct events — Eden (the nachash, Gen 3), the sons of God (Gen 6:1–4), and Babel (Gen 11 read through Deut 32:8–9). In his scheme the "gods of the nations" arraigned in Psalm 82 are, in his own architecture, the Babel rebels, of the same kind as the Genesis-6 "sons of God" but a distinct group — the Genesis-6 Watchers being bound to await judgment (2 Peter 2:4; Jude 6; 1 Enoch 10) while the nations' gods still rule. The identification of the gods of the nations with the Genesis-6 angels and their offspring is made by Tertullian and Lactantius (see their entries).
  • Also a witness on other timelines. Heiser appears on this wiki as a witness on the Nephilim timeline and the UFOs-and-the-demonic reading, and on the Goliath-slayer question — he applies the divine-council frame to the first two.
