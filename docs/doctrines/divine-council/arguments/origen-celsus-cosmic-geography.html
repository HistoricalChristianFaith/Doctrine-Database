What did the allotment of the nations to "superintending spirits" mean to Celsus, to Origen (Against Celsus V.25–32), and to the modern Deuteronomy 32 reading?

Disputed reading · on the Divine Council and the Sons of God · witness: Origen of Alexandria

Related readings: Does Deuteronomy 32:8 originally end "sons of God" or "sons of Israel"?

The passage

…in all probability, the various quarters of the earth were from the beginning allotted to different superintending spirits, and were thus distributed among certain governing powers, and in this manner the administration of the world is carried on. And whatever is done among each nation in this way would be rightly done, wherever it was agreeable to the wishes (of the superintending powers), while it would be an act of impiety to get rid of the institutions established from the beginning in the various places.

— Celsus, as quoted by Origen, Against Celsus V.25.1

…our prophet of God and His genuine servant Moses, in his song in the book of Deuteronomy, makes a statement regarding the portioning out of the earth in the following terms: "When the Most High divided the nations, when He dispersed the sons of Adam, He set the bounds of the people according to the number of the angels of God; and the portion was His people Jacob, and Israel the cord of His inheritance."

— Origen, Against Celsus V.29.2

The question

Celsus and Origen both speak of the nations as set under overseeing spirits, and the exchange has been set beside the modern "Deuteronomy 32" reading of Michael Heiser as its ancient pedigree (⚠ unverified — no passage located in which Heiser cites Against Celsus). What each party takes the allotment to be — who the spirits are, why the nations were given to them, and whether the arrangement still binds — is given differently by each, and bears on how the third-century exchange and the modern reading are related. (The wording of Deuteronomy 32:8 that Origen quotes is itself disputed; see the related reading.)3

A standing order of the world, under which each nation keeps its ancestral worship

Celsus holds that the regions of the earth were assigned from the beginning to governing powers, whose established customs each people does right to keep.

Held by: Celsus (pagan philosopher, True Word, c. 178, as quoted by Origen).1

For Celsus the allotment is how "the administration of the world is carried on." The earth's quarters were "from the beginning allotted to different superintending spirits," and the "governing powers" so appointed are real; the gods of the nations are the powers to whom the earth was parcelled out. From this follows a rule of piety. Whatever is done among each nation in agreement with its presiding powers "would be rightly done," and to dissolve "the institutions established from the beginning in the various places" is impiety. Each people therefore does well to keep its ancestral worship, since that worship answers to the power set over it. On this ground Celsus counts the Jews' keeping of their own ancestral law as rightly done, so that Origen remarks he here "includes them in the general eulogy which he passes upon all who observe their ancestral customs." The blame falls instead on "those persons rather who have forsaken their own usages," since to get rid of "the institutions established from the beginning" is impiety.1 The allotment is permanent, and the order of the world is kept by each nation's fidelity to it.

A judgement at Babel, consigning the nations to angels under the one God until Christ reclaims them

Origen holds that the allotment is the one God's division of the nations among His angels at Babel, given as a discipline, with Israel kept as the Lord's portion and all nations later given to Christ.

Held by: Origen of Alexandria (Against Celsus V.25–32, c. 248; De Principiis I).4

Origen takes up Celsus's premise and reads it from Scripture. He asks "who it was that distributed the various quarters of the earth from the beginning among the different superintending spirits," and answers with the one God. He then offers "a mystical and secret view respecting the original distribution of the various quarters of the earth among different superintending spirits," quoting Deuteronomy 32:8 in its Greek form ("the number of the angels of God") and joining it to the confusion of tongues at Babel in Genesis 11. The spirits are created angels, set in the ordered administration of Providence.5

The allotment, for Origen, is penal. The builders of Babel were "handed over… to angels of character more or less severe, and of a nature more or less stern, until they had paid the penalty of their daring deeds," while Israel fell to a ruler "who did not receive those who were placed under him for the purpose of punishment, as was the case with the others."6 The arrangement is also provisional. Jesus "has rescued us from the present evil world, and from the princes of the world that come to nought," and to Him the Father says, "Ask of Me, and I shall give Thee the heathen for Thine inheritance, and the uttermost parts of the earth for Thy possession"; the customs the spirits sanctioned may therefore rightly be left behind.7 Origen builds the same reading into De Principiis, where "other nations are called a part of the angels" while "the portion of the Lord is His people Jacob."4

The nations disinherited to lesser elohim at Babel, an ancient reading revived

Heiser holds that at Babel the nations were handed over to created divine beings who became the gods of the nations, a cosmic geography he takes to be the ancient sense of Deuteronomy 32:8.

Held by: Michael S. Heiser (Bibliotheca Sacra, 2001; The Unseen Realm, 2015).8

On Heiser's reading the Babel judgment of Genesis 11 hands the nations over to created elohim, the "sons of God" of Deuteronomy 32:8, while Israel stays Yahweh's own portion. "The gods of the nations were real. They had been assigned to their nations by Yahweh himself"; those beings then turned to misrule and became the gods the nations worshipped, and Psalm 82 is the scene of their arraignment. They surface again as the "prince of Persia" and "prince of Greece" of Daniel 10.9 Heiser presents this "cosmic geography" as the sense of the Septuagint's Deuteronomy 32:8 as it was read in antiquity, not a modern construction. Read on these terms, the Celsus–Origen exchange is a third-century instance of that map: a pagan critic affirms the allotment of the earth to superintending spirits, and a Christian theologian answers him by quoting Deuteronomy 32:8 and tying it to Babel, the two sharing the map while disputing its consequences (⚠ unverified — no passage located in which Heiser himself cites Against Celsus).8
