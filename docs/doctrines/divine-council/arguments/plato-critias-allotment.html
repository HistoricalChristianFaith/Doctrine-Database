Plato's Critias: does the gods' allotment of the earth parallel the biblical apportionment of the nations?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: weak — as evidence that Greek tradition preserves the biblical “cosmic geography.” Plato's Critias really does open with the gods dividing the whole earth among themselves “by allotment,” each settling and tending his own district (109b), and Poseidon “receiving for his lot” the island of Atlantis (113b–c) — a genuine apportionment-of-domains-to-divine-powers motif that structurally rhymes with Deuteronomy 32:8. But as a match to the biblical picture, or a garbled memory of the real apportionment of the nations at Babel, it does not hold: the Greek lot-division of cosmic domains is a pervasive, independently-arising mytheme (already the Zeus–Poseidon–Hades three-way lot of Iliad 15), the structure differs at the load-bearing points (the gods distribute the earth among themselves, with no Most High apportioning it over them; they receive land and regions, not peoples; the allotment is a peaceful self-distribution, not a sovereign's disinheritance of the nations), and the near-universal polytheistic habit of assigning a patron god to each land explains the resemblance without any shared transmission — possibility ≠ proof. · adduced in comparative discussion of the Deuteronomy 32 worldview; weighed here on Plato's primary text.

The claim

The interpretation weighed here is a comparative-mythology thesis: that Greek tradition independently preserves the same apportionment-of-territories-to-gods motif that Deuteronomy 32:8 encodes — the Most High “set the bounds of the peoples according to the number of the sons of God” — so that a real, ancient conception of the world's regions divided among divine powers stands behind both. The primary datum is the opening of Plato's Critias, where Critias sets the stage for the Atlantis narrative:

In the days of old, the gods had the whole earth distributed among them by allotment. There was no quarrelling; for you cannot rightly suppose that the gods did not know what was proper for each of them to have, or, knowing this, that they would seek to procure for themselves by contention that which more properly belonged to others. They all of them by just apportionment obtained what they wanted, and peopled their own districts; and when they had peopled them they tended us, their nurselings and possessions, as shepherds tend their flocks, excepting only that they did not use blows or bodily force, as shepherds do, but governed us like pilots from the stern of the vessel… holding our souls by the rudder of persuasion according to their own pleasure;—thus did they guide all mortal creatures.

— Plato, Critias 109b–c (Jowett).1 The interest is not “who held it?” but “does the Greek parallel carry the weight the comparative argument asks of it?”

Who advances it

The parallel belongs to the comparative wing of the divine-council revival, which gathers extra-biblical apportionment motifs — the Ugaritic “seventy sons of El,” the Greek lot-division of the cosmos — to argue that Deuteronomy 32:8 taps an ancient, widely-attested conception rather than a late Israelite abstraction. On the strong end of that project the reading rests on tight West-Semitic evidence; Plato's Critias is a looser, later, and generically-Greek addition to it, and no standard scholarship adduces the Critias as evidence of the biblical apportionment. So the load-bearing material is not any modern proponent's gloss but Plato's own text, weighed against what it does and does not deliver.

The evidence for

The motif is really there, and it is more than a bare word-match. Plato does not merely mention a “lot”; he frames the whole primeval order around a just apportionment of the earth's regions to divine powers. The gods obtain their districts “by just apportionment,” each “peopled their own districts,” and then governed their allotted populations “as shepherds tend their flocks” — a pastoral governance of peoples by allotted divine powers that lands close to the biblical picture of the nations set under heavenly overseers, and even to the Second-Temple imagery of the “seventy shepherds” placed over the peoples in 1 Enoch. The apportionment recurs concretely: Athena and Hephaestus “both obtained as their common portion this land” of Attica, and Poseidon, “receiving for his lot the island of Atlantis,” settles his offspring there — each god administering the district that fell to him.2

The conception is genuinely ancient and not confined to Israel. The lot-division of the world's domains among divine powers is old and widespread in Greek tradition: already in the Iliad the three brothers Zeus, Poseidon, and Hades divide sky, sea, and underworld among themselves by lot, “and each was given his domain.” That an apportionment of the cosmos to divine powers surfaces independently in Homer and Plato supports the modest comparative claim the cosmic-geography reading cares about most — that “each realm (or land, or people) under its own divine power” is a real, ancient conception, not a late abstraction invented by Israel.3

Antiquity read pagan allotment beside the biblical one. This juxtaposition is not only a modern reflex: in the third century the pagan critic Celsus argued that “the various quarters of the earth were from the beginning allotted to different superintending spirits,” and Origen answered him not by denying the allotment but by granting it from Deuteronomy 32:8 — so a pagan-side apportionment premise was already being set beside the biblical text in antiquity. That reception is weighed on its own page, and it is where the genuinely ancient, cross-partisan pedigree of the reading actually lives.4

Adversarial assessment

Not dependence, only resemblance. Plato wrote in the fourth century BC, centuries after the milieu of the Song of Moses, with no transmission line in either direction, and no one claims literary borrowing. The most the parallel can be is a claim of shared archetype or a garbled common memory — the “pagan memory” register the wiki tests elsewhere and rates cautiously. So the burden is to show the resemblance is specific enough to need a shared source rather than independent origin, and here it fails on three counts.

The structure differs where it counts. First, in Plato the gods divide the earth among themselves: there is no supreme apportioner standing over them. Critias is explicit that they took “what was proper for each of them to have” with “no quarrelling” — a peer distribution, not a sovereign's decree. Deuteronomy 32:8 is the opposite shape: the Most High (Elyon) apportions the nations, and keeps Israel as his own portion. Second, Plato apportions land — “districts,” “portions differing in extent,” an island — whereas Deuteronomy 32:8 apportions peoples; the difference between binding a spirit to turf and binding it to a people is exactly the overreach flagged on this wiki's cosmic-geography page, and Plato sits on the turf side. Third, Plato's allotment is a just, peaceful, original ordering with the gods as benevolent shepherds; the biblical and Second-Temple development turns the allotted powers into rebels arraigned in Psalm 82 and the nations disinherited at Babel, with the Most High reclaiming them through Christ. Plato has no fall, no rebellion, no reclamation — the very features that make the biblical “cosmic geography” what it is.5

The Greek lot-motif is generic, and independent origin suffices. The three-way lot of Iliad 15 and the patron deities of Greek cities (Athena of Athens, Poseidon of the sea) show that dividing domains among gods by lot is a stock Greek mytheme, not a special echo of a biblical event. More broadly, the habit of assigning a divine patron to each land or people is near-universal in polytheism: it is exactly what human religiosity produces wherever gods and territory meet. A shared archetype of allotment is therefore the economical explanation, and it does not require a shared memory — still less a memory of the specific apportionment of Deuteronomy 32. Possibility ≠ proof: that a real primeval apportionment could underlie both traditions does not show that it does, and the ordinary explanation already accounts for the resemblance. Add that Plato's own point is a philosophical-patriotic prelude to the Atlantis war, not a cosmology of the nations, and the Critias turns out to be doing quite different work from Deuteronomy 32.

Assessment

Weak — as evidence for the biblical reading, though one sub-claim is worth banking. The bare observation is true and even striking: Greek tradition carries an apportionment-of-the-earth-to-divine-powers motif, and Plato's Critias 109b is a clear, unforced instance of it, complete with the allotted powers “shepherding” their peoples. That does modest work for the general comparative point — “each realm under a divine power” is an ancient, widespread conception, not a late abstraction. But the load-bearing interpretation — that Plato matches the biblical cosmic geography, or preserves a garbled memory of the real apportionment of the nations — does not hold on its best case: the structure differs at the decisive points (peers, not sovereign-and-subjects; land, not peoples; a benevolent original order, not rebellion-and-disinheritance), the Greek lot-division is a generic and independently-attested mytheme, and the universal patron-deity tendency explains the rhyme without any transmission. So trust the general point and no more; the tighter ancient analogue to Deuteronomy 32:8 is the Ugaritic seventy sons of El (plausible), and the reading's genuinely ancient, cross-partisan pedigree is carried on the reception side by the Origen–Celsus argument (sound), not by Plato.
