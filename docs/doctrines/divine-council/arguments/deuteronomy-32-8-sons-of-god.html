Does Deuteronomy 32:8 originally end "sons of God" or "sons of Israel"?

Disputed reading · on the Divine Council and the Sons of God · witness: the Septuagint

Related readings: What did the allotment of the nations to "superintending spirits" mean to Celsus, to Origen, and to the modern Deuteronomy 32 reading?

The passage

When the most High divided to the nations their inheritance, when he separated the sons of Adam, he set the bounds of the people according to the number of the children of Israel.

For the LORD's portion is his people; Jacob is the lot of his inheritance.

— Deuteronomy 32:8–9 (KJV, following the Masoretic Text), historicalchristian.faith.1

The last words of verse 8 survive in three forms:

  • "Sons of Israel" (bene yisrael) — the Masoretic Text, joined by the Samaritan Pentateuch. This is the reading behind most older English Bibles.
  • "Sons of God / gods" (bene elohim) — the Qumran fragment 4QDeutj (4Q37). A second scroll, 4QDeutq (4Q44), is broken at this word; P. W. Skehan, who first published it in 1954, judged from the spacing that its line was too short for "sons of Israel" and reconstructed bene el, "sons of El." (Heiser keeps the two scrolls distinct: it is 4QDeutj, not the fragmentary 4QDeutq, that carries the legible reading.)
  • "Angels of God" (ἀγγέλων θεοῦ) — the majority Greek of the Septuagint. A minority of Greek witnesses, including Papyrus Fouad 266 (Rahlfs 848), have "sons of God" (υἱῶν θεοῦ), and Wevers's Göttingen critical edition prints that as the Old Greek.

The question

Which Hebrew wording stood at the end of Deuteronomy 32:8 first: "sons of God / El," attested at Qumran and behind the Greek, or "sons of Israel," attested by the Masoretic Text and the Samaritan Pentateuch? The answer decides whether the verse, as first written, set the nations in relation to heavenly beings, and so bears on every later reading of the verse traced on the timeline.

"Sons of God" (or "sons of El") as the earlier text

This reading holds that the verse first named divine beings, and that "sons of Israel" is a later scribal change.

Held by: P. W. Skehan (BASOR, 1954); J. W. Wevers (Göttingen Deuteronomium, 1977); Jeffrey H. Tigay (JPS Deuteronomy, 1996); Michael S. Heiser (Bibliotheca Sacra, 2001).2

The case starts from transcriptional probability. Heiser frames it in his 2001 article:

It is far more likely both texts were intentionally altered in MT for the same reason: to eliminate a reference to divine beings in the name of expunging allegedly polytheistic language. It is inconceivable to suppose that a scribe would have done the reverse, altering an innocuous bene yisrael to a potentially explosive bene elohim. The LXX reading therefore sufficiently explains how the MT reading could have arisen, but the alternative fails this test.

On this view a reading is weighed, not counted: a scribe wary of polytheistic-sounding language had a motive to turn "sons of God" into "sons of Israel," and none to make the opposite change, so the harder reading accounts for its rival.3 Tigay adds a parallel eleven verses later. At 32:43 the Masoretic Text lacks a colon, "bow down to him, all you gods," which 4QDeutq and the Septuagint preserve; he reads the Masoretic wording of both verses as altered for one reason, to remove the divine beings, and Tov judges "sons of Israel" a theological correction.4 Wevers reads the majority Greek "angels of God" as a further softening, "a later attempt to avoid any notion of lesser deities."2

The sense of the passage is the third strand. Verse 8 divides the nations and verse 9 keeps Israel back as the Lord's portion, so Israel stands apart from the count. The seventy nations of the Table of Nations (Genesis 10) correspond, on this reading, to the seventy sons of El at Ugarit, where El is also titled "Most High," a correlation Heiser sets out after Cassuto and Ross. Heiser also holds that an accidental loss of letters could leave a bare bene el but not the full consonants of 4QDeutj's bene elohim. Within this reading the earliest form is still discussed: Tov holds that bene elohim "developed from an earlier bene el," and the NRSV prints "gods" while the ESV prints "sons of God."4

"Sons of Israel" as the original text

This reading holds that the Masoretic wording is original, and that "sons of God" arose by accident or by later interpretation.

Held by: David E. Stevens (Bibliotheca Sacra, 1997); Edward D. Andrews (Christian Publishing House, OTTC, 2019); Heath G. Henning (The Unbiblical Realm, 2023); the pseudonymous Reformed Presbyterian author of Reformed Masora (Substack), "The Masoretic Text Vindicated in Deuteronomy 32:8" (2025).5

The case starts from the witnesses. The great majority of Hebrew manuscripts read "sons of Israel," and the Samaritan Pentateuch, an independent Hebrew text tradition, agrees with them; on this view two separate Hebrew lines outweigh one Qumran fragment and a translation. Stevens offers a mechanism for the variant: a scribe's eye slipped, a parablepsis or homoioteleuton, dropping the letters of "Israel" and leaving a bare "sons of El," which later copyists then filled out. The reading in the Greek and at Qumran is, on this account, a copying accident followed by expansion rather than the earlier text.

Henning, the Reformed Masora author and Andrews add an argument from the history of ideas: the shorter "sons of God" reflects the developed angelology of the Hellenistic period and of the Qumran community, which read heavenly beings into the text. They apply lectio brevior and the conservatism of the Masoretic scribal tradition in the Masoretic Text's favour. The measure of the nations also fits Israel on this reading: the traditional correlation, as Heiser (2001) reports it, ties the number of the nations to the seventy souls of Jacob's household who went down to Egypt (Genesis 46:27; Exodus 1:5), so that the God who chose Israel set the bounds of the peoples with Israel in view, and verse 9 completes the thought by naming Jacob as the Lord's own lot.5
