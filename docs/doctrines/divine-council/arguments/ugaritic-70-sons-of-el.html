The seventy sons of El: does the Ugaritic parallel show Deuteronomy 32:8 is a real divine-council apportionment?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: plausible — the interpretation has a strong half and a soft half, and they must be told apart. The strong half is the comparative backdrop: at Ugarit the high god El — himself titled ʻlyn, "Most High," the very epithet (Elyon) that Deuteronomy 32:8 uses — presides over a divine assembly and has seventy sons (šbʿm bn ʾaṯrt, "the seventy sons of Athirat"), so that when Deuteronomy 32:8 in its stronger text apportions the nations "according to the number of the sons of God," it is speaking the ordinary West-Semitic idiom of a high god and his divine family, not coining a late abstraction. That the biblical apportionment shares the ancient council backdrop is close to consensus in comparative Semitics, and the Ugaritic seventy-fold pantheon is its tightest single analogue. The soft half is the exact-headcount reading pressed on top of it — "seventy sons of El = seventy nations, an encoded one-to-one census." Deuteronomy 32:8 states no number; the "seventy" is imported from the Table of Nations (Genesis 10) and later Jewish tradition; the count wobbles (70 in the Hebrew, 72 in the Greek); "seventy" is a stock ANE completeness-figure that also numbers Gideon's sons and Jacob's household; and Ugarit nowhere apportions nations to its seventy sons at all. So the parallel establishes a real, ancient divine-council conception behind the verse; it does not deliver a demonstrable transmitted headcount. · advanced by Michael S. Heiser, downstream of Mark S. Smith.

The claim

Ugaritic mythology plainly states that the head of its pantheon, El (who, like the God of the Bible, is also referred to as El Elyon, the "Most High") fathered 70 sons, thereby setting the number of the "sons of El" (Ugaritic, bn ʾil). An unmistakable linguistic parallel with the Hebrew text underlying the LXX reading was thus discovered… God (El Elyon in Deut. 32:8) divided the earth according to the number of heavenly beings who already existed from the time of creation.

— Michael S. Heiser, "Deuteronomy 32:8 and the Sons of God," Bibliotheca Sacra 158 (2001): 52–74, citing the Baal Cycle at KTU 1.4 VI 46.1 The interpretation weighed here is that this correspondence shows Deuteronomy 32:8 to encode a real divine-council apportionment of the peoples — the nations parcelled out among the "sons of God" — and not a late, abstract theological figure of speech. The interest is not "who held it?" but "does the Ugaritic parallel carry that weight?"

Who advances it

The popular form is Heiser's: the seventy sons of El at Ugarit are the "unmistakable" backdrop of Deuteronomy 32:8–9, on which he builds a "Deuteronomy 32 worldview" in which the seventy nations of Babel are each assigned to one of the (also seventy) "sons of God."2 He is downstream of the mainstream comparative work — above all Mark S. Smith, who models the Ugaritic pantheon as a four-tier divine family (El and Athirat at the head; beneath them "the seventy sons of Asherah/Athirat"; then craft-gods and messenger-gods), and reads Deuteronomy 32:8–9 in its "sons of God" form as preserving exactly that older theology: each divine son receives a nation as his patrimony, Yahweh receiving Israel. Smith notes the number "perhaps survives in the later Jewish notion of the seventy angels, one for each of the world's putatively seventy peoples."3 The comparative-Semitics substrate (F. M. Cross, E. T. Mullen, L. K. Handy) is broader still: a high god presiding over a council of divine "sons" is the common West-Semitic picture, and the Hebrew Bible shares it.

The evidence for

The Ugaritic datum is real and precise. In the Baal Cycle, after Baal's palace is built, he holds a banquet and "invites the seventy sons of Athirat" — šbʿm bn ʾaṯrt, KTU 1.4 VI 46, the one place an ancient Ugaritic text puts a number on the pantheon.4 El and his consort Athirat head that family, so the "seventy sons of Athirat" are equally the sons of El — a full divine council under the high god. This is not a strained retrofit onto Israel's text: it is the native religious vocabulary of Late Bronze Age Canaan, recovered from Ras Shamra, and it stands a few centuries before the Song of Moses.

The epithet lines up. The high god of Ugarit is called ʻlyn ("Most High"); Deuteronomy 32:8 has "the Most High" (Elyon) apportion the nations. And the beings by whose "number" he apportions them are, on the text-critically stronger reading (4QDeutj's bene elohim, the Septuagint's "angels/sons of God," against the Masoretic "sons of Israel"), precisely "sons of God / sons of El" — the same phrase, in the same divine-family idiom, as the Ugaritic bn ʾil. That variant is adjudicated and rated sound on its own page and is not re-argued here; this page grants the stronger text and asks what the Ugaritic parallel adds.5 Put together, the epithet and the phrase place Deuteronomy 32:8 inside a documented Canaanite conception rather than a late Israelite invention — which is exactly the "not a late abstraction" the interpretation most needs, and its firmest ground.

The later Jewish tradition reads it the same way. The apportionment of the nations to a set of national divine patrons is not a modern inference read back into a mute text: it is spelled out downstream in the Second-Temple and rabbinic sources — the seventy nations and seventy guardian angels/princes of the nations (Targum Pseudo-Jonathan to Deuteronomy 32:8; the seventy shepherds set over Israel and the nations in 1 Enoch 89–90; the seventy princes of 3 Enoch; b. Shabbat 88b; b. Sukkah 55b), with Deuteronomy 4:19 as its companion (the host of heaven "allotted" to the peoples). Whatever the verse's original headcount, ancient Jewish readers took Deuteronomy 32:8 to mean a real division of the peoples among heavenly powers — the very structure the argument claims.6

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The backdrop claim is strong and mainstream: Deuteronomy 32:8, in its stronger text, speaks the ordinary West-Semitic language of a "Most High" apportioning the peoples among the "sons of God," and the Ugaritic seventy-sons-of-El pantheon is the tightest single ancient-Near-Eastern analogue to it. On that much the comparative field is close to agreed, and it does the work the interpretation cares about most — it makes the apportionment a real, inherited divine-council conception, not a late abstraction. If the argument stopped there it would rate at or near sound.

What is overstated or imprecise — the exact-headcount reading. The soft half is the leap from "shared divine-council idiom" to "an encoded, one-to-one seventy-sons = seventy-nations census," and four things weigh against pressing the number that hard. First, Deuteronomy 32:8 states no number at all — it says "according to the number of [the sons of God]," and the "seventy" is imported from the Table of Nations (Genesis 10) and cemented by the later seventy-angels tradition, not read off the verse. Second, even that borrowed count wobbles: Genesis 10 yields seventy names in the Hebrew but seventy-two in the Greek (the same 70/72 split behind the seventy(-two) disciples of Luke 10 and the seventy(-two) Septuagint translators), so the "match" depends on which text one counts. Third, "seventy" is a stock ANE completeness-figure, not a census: it numbers Gideon's sons (Judges 8:30), Ahab's sons (2 Kings 10:1), and the household of Jacob that went down to Egypt (Genesis 46:27) — the last being the very number the Masoretic "sons of Israel" reading exploits — and at Ugarit itself no god-list actually enumerates seventy deities, so the figure there reads as rhetorical totality (10 × 7). A round completeness-number matching on both sides is weaker evidence of transmission than an odd number would be; it may signal a shared schema of fullness rather than a transmitted headcount. Fourth and most telling, the Ugaritic texts nowhere apportion nations to the seventy sons of El at all: they call the pantheon "the seventy sons of Athirat," but the assignment of peoples to divine patrons is a biblical and Second-Temple development (Deuteronomy 32:8–9 + 4:19 + the seventy-angels tradition), supplied from the Hebrew side of the comparison, not from KTU 1.4. So the "seventy sons rule seventy nations" equation is a synthesis built downstream of Ugarit, and even a sympathetic specialist such as N. Wyatt cautions that whether the Ugaritic phrase carried any such cosmological-political sense "they have no means of knowing on presently available evidence."7

Assessment

Plausible — and the two halves should be banked at different strengths. The sub-claim to trust is the backdrop: Deuteronomy 32:8's apportionment of the nations to the "sons of God" belongs to a real, ancient West-Semitic divine-council conception, and the Ugaritic seventy sons of El — under a high god titled "Most High," fathering a full divine family — is its tightest single analogue, so the verse is not a late Israelite abstraction. That much is close to consensus in comparative Semitics and is the argument at its best. The overreach to guard against is treating the number as a proof: Deuteronomy 32:8 supplies no count, the "seventy" is borrowed from Genesis 10 and later Jewish tradition and wobbles to seventy-two in the Greek, "seventy" is an ANE totality-idiom rather than a census, and Ugarit never apportions nations to its seventy sons — so the tidy "seventy sons of El = seventy nations" headcount is thematic resonance and downstream synthesis, not a demonstrable one-to-one transmission. Net: the parallel is real and load-bearing for the divine-council structure of the verse; it is softer as a numerological proof, and Heiser's "unmistakable" overstates that softer half. The reading's deep antiquity on the reception side is carried separately by the Origen–Celsus cosmic-geography argument (sound), and its text-critical base by the Deuteronomy 32:8 variant (sound); the seventy-nations count also bears on the Babel dispersion traced on the first language.
