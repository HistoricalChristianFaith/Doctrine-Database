Psalm 82: real divine beings, or human judges?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — as the claim that the elohim whom God judges in Psalm 82 ("God stands in the divine assembly; among the gods he holds judgment") are real, created, lesser divine beings — Yahweh's heavenly council, arraigned for misgoverning the nations — and not the long-traditional reading's human judges or magistrates of Israel called "gods" honorifically. This is the linchpin of Michael Heiser's whole "divine council" framework, and it is genuinely disputed. By the lights of comparative Semitics and the psalm's own internal logic the divine-being reading is the stronger, and it is now the majority reading in critical Old-Testament scholarship: verse 1's ‘adat-’el is the "assembly of El" of the Ugaritic pantheon-texts, and verse 7's sentence that the accused "shall die like men" only bites if the accused are not already men. What keeps it contested rather than settled is that the charges of verses 2–4 (judging unjustly, favoring the wicked, failing the poor) are the standard vocabulary of human magistrates, that elohim demonstrably means "judges/God-at-the-sanctuary" in the case-law of Exodus, and that the dominant Jewish and patristic tradition — with the Johannine citation (John 10:34–36) read its way — took the "gods" to be men. So: exegetically the stronger reading, and the majority critical one, but facing a live and non-frivolous rival it does not simply defeat.

The claim

Seeing these elohim as divine beings is viewed as a threat to monotheism, the heart of biblical theology… The point of verse 6 is that, in response to the corruption of the elohim, they will be stripped of their immortality at God's discretion and die like humans die.

— Michael S. Heiser, The Plural Elohim of Psalm 82: Gods or Men?, drmsh.com. Heiser reads the elohim of Psalm 82:1 as members of Yahweh's heavenly council — real created spirits set over the nations, here sentenced to lose their immortality for ruling them corruptly — and holds that "the plural elohim in Psalm 82 cannot be humans."1

Who advances it

The divine-being reading is not Heiser's invention; it is, as he insisted, the standard vocabulary of Semitic scholarship. "The term divine council," he wrote, "is used by Hebrew and Semitic scholars to refer to the heavenly host, the pantheon of divine beings who administer the affairs of the cosmos." Behind him stand the mid-twentieth-century studies of the Israelite assembly of the gods against its Canaanite background — H. Wheeler Robinson, Frank Moore Cross, E. Theodore Mullen's The Assembly of the Gods (1980), and Mark S. Smith's work on the Ugaritic parallels — for whom Psalm 82 is a scene of Yahweh judging, and demoting to mortality, the gods of the nations. Heiser is the popularizer who carried that scholarly reading to a wide lay audience and made it the keystone of a "supernatural worldview."2

The rival — the elohim are human judges, rulers, or magistrates of Israel (or Israel itself), styled "gods" because they wield God's delegated authority — is the older and long-dominant reading. It is the line of the Aramaic Targum ("judges"), of much rabbinic tradition, of the medieval commentators, and of a large part of the Christian exegetical tradition; among moderns it is defended by A. F. Kirkpatrick (who read the psalm as "the responsibility of earthly judges to the Supreme Judge") and by numerous conservative commentators who resist the divine-council frame as a concession to polytheism. Both readings are genuinely advocated today, which is why this is a rated argument rather than a settled datum.3

The evidence for

Verse 1 names a divine assembly in the vocabulary of the wider Semitic world. The psalm opens, "God has taken his stand in the assembly of El (‘adat-’el); in the midst of the elohim he holds judgment." That phrase is not a stray metaphor: the "assembly of El" (‘dt ’ilm) is the standing term for the divine council in the Ugaritic texts, where the high god El presides over a pantheon of lesser deities — the same institution the Hebrew Bible pictures as Yahweh surrounded by "the host of heaven" (1 Kings 22:19–23), the "sons of God" who present themselves before him (Job 1:6; 2:1), and "the council of the holy ones" (Psalm 89:6–7). Read against that background, the assembly of Psalm 82 is a heavenly court, not a courtroom in Jerusalem.4

The sentence "you shall die like men" only bites if the condemned are not men. This is the reading's sharpest internal argument. In verses 6–7 God declares, "I said, you are gods, sons of the Most High, all of you; nevertheless you shall die like men, and fall like any prince." To threaten a human being with dying "like men" is empty — it is the one thing a human is already bound to do. The clause carries force only if the addressees are beings for whom mortality is a demotion: created immortals stripped of their deathlessness as a judicial penalty. Heiser presses exactly this: on the human reading the sentence would be as empty as ordering a mortal to do the one thing he must already do; it makes sense only as the demotion of a being who was not going to die.1

Everywhere else in a heavenly setting, bene elohim / elohim denotes divine beings. The plural noun is grammatically flexible — it can mean the one God, angels, the dead (1 Sam 28:13), or false gods — so it is decided by context; and in every other passage where the term stands for a plurality gathered before the throne it denotes spiritual beings: the "sons of God" at creation who "shouted for joy" (Job 38:7) and who present themselves before Yahweh (Job 1:6), the "sons of God" summoned to ascribe glory (Psalm 29:1), the holy ones of the council (Psalm 89). Psalm 82 sits inside that pattern, not outside it.4

The Deuteronomy 32:8 frame supplies the charge. The reason these elohim are on trial is that they were given the nations to govern and governed them corruptly. That is precisely the structure of Deuteronomy 32:8–9 in its text-critically stronger form, where the Most High apportions the nations "according to the number of the sons of God" (so the Septuagint and a Dead Sea Scroll, against the Masoretic "sons of Israel"), keeping Israel as his own portion. Psalm 82 reads as the sequel: the heavenly powers set over the nations are arraigned for the injustice of their rule and sentenced to fall. The two texts interlock into one coherent divine-council cosmology — the strongest systematic point in the reading's favor.5

Adversarial assessment

What holds. As a reading of Psalm 82 in its ancient Near Eastern setting, the divine-being interpretation is strong and is now the majority position in critical scholarship. The ‘adat-’el of verse 1 really is the assembly-of-El terminology of the Ugaritic world; the "die like men" of verse 7 really does read most naturally as the demotion of an immortal; and the wider throne-council pattern (Job 1, Psalm 89, 1 Kings 22) really does supply the native idiom. None of this depends on ruling the supernatural in or out a priori — it is straightforward comparative and internal exegesis, and it is sound.

What the human-judges reading still has going for it. The rival is not a mere failure of nerve about polytheism; it has real textual purchase, which is why it survives. First, the charges in verses 2–4 — "How long will you judge unjustly and show partiality to the wicked? Give justice to the weak… rescue the poor and needy" — are the standard indictment of human magistrates; corrupt courts, bribery, and neglect of the poor are the prophets' recurring complaint against Israel's rulers, not a charge one expects to level at the gods of Assyria. Second, elohim demonstrably can mean "judges" or "God-at-the-sanctuary" in Israel's own case-law: at Exodus 21:6 and 22:8–9 a disputed case is brought "to the elohim," which the tradition (and many translations) render as the judges who render the verdict in God's name. Third, the reading has the weight of the receiving tradition — Targum, much of the rabbis, and a large patristic stream took the "gods" to be men — so it is not a modern rationalization but the older default. (Heiser answers that Exodus never calls the human judges elohim, reading those texts of God himself; that answer is contestable, and the dispute is live.)3

John 10:34–36 is claimed by both and decides neither. When Jesus, accused of blasphemy for making himself God, answers, "Is it not written in your Law, 'I said, you are gods'? If he called them gods, to whom the word of God came…", each camp hears its own reading. The divine-council side notes that Jesus takes the psalm's "gods" as a real, scripturally established category into which even his hearers might be admitted; the human-judges side notes that "those to whom the word of God came" most naturally means the human recipients of the Law at Sinai (or the Israelites addressed by it), and that the whole a-fortiori argument works by contrasting the Son with mere men honored with the title. The verse is a genuine datum, but it under-determines the identity question — it establishes that Scripture calls some party "gods," not which party.6

Where the church's later reading actually landed — and why it is not simply the human-judges reading. A caution against flattening the dispute: the mature patristic mainstream did read the elohim as men, but characteristically not as judges so much as men deified by grace and adoption — Athanasius, Jerome, and Augustine all take "you are gods" of human beings made sons of God through the Word, precisely to fence the Son's unique divinity against the Arians, who were themselves exploiting the verse. That is a third position, neither Heiser's council of created spirits nor the bare civic-magistrate reading, and it shows the identity question was contested inside the "gods = men" camp as well. It does not rescue the magistrate reading exegetically, but it explains why the traditional weight against the divine-council view is real and theologically motivated rather than merely exegetical.6

Assessment

Contested — as the claim that the elohim of Psalm 82 are real, created, lesser divine beings, Yahweh's heavenly council, rather than human judges.

The honest verdict is that the divine-being reading is the stronger of the two on the exegetical merits, and the majority reading in modern critical scholarship, without being uncontested. Its two best arguments — the ‘adat-’el council-terminology of verse 1 and the "die like men" demotion of verse 7 — are hard to answer on the human-judges reading, and the Deuteronomy 32:8 frame ties the psalm into a coherent cosmology of nations-under-powers that the magistrate reading cannot supply. That is enough to make it the reading to prefer, and to make Heiser's insistence on it defensible rather than fringe. What it is not is a knockout: the indictment of verses 2–4 is genuinely the language of human courts, elohim genuinely does the work of "judges" in Israel's case-law, and the Johannine citation cuts for neither side, so a competent and non-tendentious reader can still hold the human interpretation. Two precisions keep the rating honest. First, this argument settles only that Psalm 82's elohim are real divine beings; it does not by itself establish Heiser's further identification of them with the specific "gods of the nations" of the Babel disinheritance, still less with the Genesis-6 Watchers — those are additional steps weighed elsewhere. Second, the wiki does not rate the reading down merely because it posits real spirit-beings; it is weighed on the text, and on the text it comes out strong-but-disputed. The sub-claim to keep is "Psalm 82 depicts a council of real heavenly beings, not a bench of human judges"; the one to flag on the proponent's page is any suggestion that this single psalm, by itself, delivers the whole divine-council architecture.
