John Chrysostom on the Divine Council

Dates: c. 390 (John Chrysostom, c. 347–407; Wikipedia). Summary: The Antiochene preacher is a rejecter witness — a mainstream foil to the divine-council reading of Psalm 82. Expounding Christ's use of the psalm at John 10:34–36 (in his Homilies on the Gospel of John, preached at Antioch c. 390) and the verse itself in a homily on the Acts, Chrysostom takes the "gods" of Psalm 82:6 to be men — humans who "become God, and a child of God" by grace, set off against the Son who has divinity "by nature." He does not read the elohim as a council of created divine beings, nor even (unlike some of his fellow Antiochenes) as an office of human judges: for him the psalm is a text about the deification of humanity by grace and adoption.

Claims & evidence

The "gods" of Psalm 82 are men who have this honor "by grace," against the Son who has it "by nature"

When they said, "For a good work we stone thee not, but for blasphemy; and because that thou being a man makest thyself God"; hear His answer; "If the Scripture called them gods unto whom the word of God came, how say ye that I blaspheme, because I said, I am the Son of God?" What He saith is of this kind: "If those who have received this honor by grace, are not found fault with for calling themselves gods, how can He who hath this by nature deserve to be rebuked?" Yet He spake not so, but proved it at a later time, having first relaxed and yielded somewhat in His discourse…

— Homilies on the Gospel of John 61.2 (on John 10:33–36), source. The whole force of Christ's argument, for Chrysostom, is a contrast between two ways of holding the title "god": the men of Psalm 82 hold it by grace (a conferred honor), while the Son holds it by nature — so the verse's "gods" are creatures, not a standing assembly of divine beings. It is the same "by grace vs. by nature" logic Athanasius and Jerome deploy against the Arians, who were exploiting the verse to level the Son with the creatures.

On Psalm 82:6 itself: "the man can become God, and a child of God"

So then, it all depends upon the use… Why do I say, an angel? the man can become God, and a child of God. For we read, "I have said, Ye are gods, and all of you are children of the Most High." (Ps. lxxxii. 6.) And what is greater, the power to become both God and angel and child of God is put into his own hands. Yea, so it is, man can be the maker of an angel.

— Homilies on the Acts of the Apostles 32 (on Acts 15), source. Chrysostom quotes Psalm 82:6 to prove that a human being — by the use he makes of his freedom — "can become God, and a child of God." The "gods" and "children of the Most High" of the psalm are therefore men raised by grace, not lesser elohim of a heavenly council.

Notes

  • Chrysostom belongs with the "elohim = men, deified by grace" strand (Irenaeus, Athanasius, Jerome, Augustine) rather than the divine-council reading — a datable Greek-East foil for the divine-council timeline.
  • Notably he does not take the alternative Antiochene line that the "gods" are human judges or rulers; he reads them as humanity at large, exalted by grace to be "children of the Most High" — closer to Jerome, who likewise rejected restricting the "gods" to kings and princes.
  • He appears on this wiki also as a witness on the Serpent of Eden timeline and on the Nephilim, Flood, Old-Testament-canon, and astrology timelines.
