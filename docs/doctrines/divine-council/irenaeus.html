Irenaeus on the Divine Council

Dates: c. 180 (Against Heresies III.6; Irenaeus of Lyons, c. 130–202; Wikipedia) Summary: Irenaeus is a two-sided witness a century and a half before the Arian debates. Reading Psalm 82 he takes its “gods” to be the Father, the Son, and those who have received the adoption — the Church — so the “gods” are men deified by adoptive grace, the very reading later made an anti-Arian rule by Athanasius, Jerome, and Augustine, here far earlier and without that pressure. Yet on the gods of the nations he keeps the other strand: “the gods of the heathen are idols of demons” — barred from being gods “in every sense,” but real demonic powers behind the idols.

Claims & evidence

Psalm 82's “gods” are the Father, the Son, and those who received the adoption — the Church

And again: “God stood in the congregation of the gods, He judges among the gods.” He [here] refers to the Father and the Son, and those who have received the adoption; but these are the Church. For she is the synagogue of God, which God—that is, the Son Himself—has gathered by Himself… But of what gods [does he speak]? [Of those] to whom He says, “I have said, Ye are gods, and all sons of the Most High.” To those, no doubt, who have received the grace of the “adoption, by which we cry, Abba Father.”

— Against Heresies III.6.1, source. Irenaeus reads the elohim of Psalm 82 not as a council of created spirits but as the adopted sons of God — the Church, made “gods” by the grace of adoption. This is the anti-council reading in its earliest patristic form: the “gods” are men, deified by grace, not a class of divine beings.

The gods of the nations are “idols of demons” — no gods, yet real demonic powers

When, however, the Scripture terms them [gods] which are no gods, it does not, as I have already remarked, declare them as gods in every sense, but with a certain addition and signification, by which they are shown to be no gods at all. As with David: “The gods of the heathen are idols of demons;”… But as to what they are in their own person, he speaks concerning them; “for they are,” he says, “the idols of demons.”

— Against Heresies III.6.3, source. Irenaeus keeps the second strand alongside the first: the gods of the nations are “no gods at all,” yet the name is not empty — behind the idols stand real “demons.” He denies the elohim of Psalm 82 are a council of spirits while affirming that the powers of the nations' cult are real, malevolent beings, the same double judgment Justin and the Septuagint's Psalm 96:5 already carry.

Notes

  • Irenaeus is chronologically the first Father on this timeline to read Psalm 82's “gods” as men deified by adoptive grace — the anti-council reading that Athanasius (c. 340), Jerome, and Augustine later hardened into a fixed anti-Arian tool. He reaches it here c. 180, from the plain logic of adoption, long before the controversy that made it doctrine.
  • The whole passage is driven by a Christological monotheism: “no other is named as God, or is called Lord, except Him who is God and Lord of all” and His Son. The same pressure — guarding the one God (and, later, the Son's unique divinity) — is what drove the mainstream reading of Psalm 82 steadily inward, away from a populated council.
  • Yet he is genuinely two-sided: with Justin and the Septuagint (Psalm 96:5, “the gods of the nations are demons”) he holds that the gods of the nations are real demonic powers — a strand Tertullian and Lactantius develop into the full “pagan gods = fallen angels” thesis.
