Clement of Alexandria on the Divine Council

Dates: c. 215 (Clement of Alexandria, c. 150–215; Wikipedia) Summary: Clement is the earliest post-apostolic Father to state the cosmic-geography reading in so many words. In the Stromata he says plainly that “by an ancient and divine order the angels are distributed among the nations,” while believers are “the Lord's portion”—the two halves of Deuteronomy 32:8–9. He pairs it with Deuteronomy 4:19: God gave the sun, moon, and stars to the nations to worship as a first, permitted step toward the Maker. But for Clement these are created, ministering angels under the one Word and Providence, not rival gods—the mediating position later held by Origen, here a generation earlier.

Claims & evidence

“By an ancient and divine order the angels are distributed among the nations”; believers are the Lord's portion

It is He who also gave philosophy to the Greeks by means of the inferior angels. For by an ancient and divine order the angels are distributed among the nations. But the glory of those who believe is “the Lord's portion.”

— Stromata, Book VII, source. This is the Deuteronomy 32:8–9 structure in its cleanest patristic form: the nations parcelled out under angelic administrators (“an ancient and divine order”), with the believing remnant reserved directly to God as “the Lord's portion.” Clement takes the arrangement as a real division of oversight, not a figure.

Regiments of angels are set over nations and cities—and even individuals

For regiments of angels are distributed over the nations and cities. And, perchance, some are assigned to individuals.

— Stromata, Book VI, source. Clement extends the national allotment downward to cities and even to individual persons (the guardian-angel motif), showing that he understood the Deuteronomy 32:8 division as one tier of a whole hierarchy of ministering spirits set over the created order.

The host of heaven was given to the nations to worship (Deuteronomy 4:19)—a first, permitted step

And He gave the sun, and the moon, and the stars to be worshipped; “which God,” the Law says, made for the nations, that they might not become altogether atheistical, and so utterly perish… For this was the sway given to the nations to rise up to God, by means of the worship of the heavenly bodies. But those who would not abide by those heavenly bodies assigned to them, but fell away from them to stocks and stones, “were counted,” it is said, “as chaff-dust and as a drop from a jar,” beyond salvation.

— Stromata, Book VI, source. Clement supplies the Deuteronomy 4:19 pairing that so often travels with 32:8: the “host of heaven” was allotted to the nations, the sun and moon and stars given them to worship as a divinely-conceded first rung by which they might “rise up to God.” Those who then fell further—to idols of “stocks and stones”—stand condemned. The permitted star-worship of the nations is thus the mild, providential face of the same allotment Deuteronomy 32 records.

Notes

  • Clement is chronologically the first Father on this timeline to state the allotment-of-the-nations reading of Deuteronomy 32:8–9 outright, and to bind it to Deuteronomy 4:19—the two texts the modern cosmic-geography case rests on. He predates Origen, whom he taught at Alexandria.
  • His powers are created, ministering angels under the one Word and Providence, not an assembly of rival “gods.” Like Origen after him, Clement keeps the council's administrative shape while denying it any independent divinity—the mediating patristic position between the Second-Temple council and the later “elohim = men” reading.
  • His Deuteronomy 4:19 gloss reads the host-of-heaven cult as permitted—a concession to keep the nations from atheism, not a neutral original ordering and not (yet) a penalty. That “permitting” strand is developed much further by Eusebius, who couples the same texts to a rebel-power's later corruption of the arrangement.
