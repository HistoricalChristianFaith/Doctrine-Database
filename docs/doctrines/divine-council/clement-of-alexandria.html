Clement of Alexandria on the Divine Council

Dates: c. 215 (Clement of Alexandria, c. 150–215; Wikipedia) Summary: In the Stromata Clement states that “by an ancient and divine order the angels are distributed among the nations,” while believers are “the Lord's portion”—the two halves of Deuteronomy 32:8–9. He pairs it with Deuteronomy 4:19: God gave the sun, moon, and stars to the nations to worship as a first, permitted step toward the Maker. For Clement these are created, ministering angels under the one Word and Providence, not rival gods.

Claims & evidence

“By an ancient and divine order the angels are distributed among the nations”; believers are the Lord's portion

It is He who also gave philosophy to the Greeks by means of the inferior angels. For by an ancient and divine order the angels are distributed among the nations. But the glory of those who believe is “the Lord's portion.”

— Stromata, Book VII, source. Clement sets the nations under angelic administrators (“an ancient and divine order”), with the believing remnant reserved directly to God as “the Lord's portion,” the phrase of Deuteronomy 32:9. Clement takes the arrangement as a real division of oversight, not a figure.

Regiments of angels are set over nations and cities—and perhaps individuals

For regiments of angels are distributed over the nations and cities. And, perchance, some are assigned to individuals.

— Stromata, Book VI, source. Clement extends the national allotment downward to cities and, perhaps, to individual persons (the guardian-angel motif), a hierarchy of ministering spirits set over the created order, of which the national allotment is one tier.

The host of heaven was given to the nations to worship (Deuteronomy 4:19)—a first, permitted step

And He gave the sun, and the moon, and the stars to be worshipped; “which God,” the Law says, made for the nations, that they might not become altogether atheistical, and so utterly perish… For this was the sway given to the nations to rise up to God, by means of the worship of the heavenly bodies. But those who would not abide by those heavenly bodies assigned to them, but fell away from them to stocks and stones, “were counted,” it is said, “as chaff-dust and as a drop from a jar,” beyond salvation.

— Stromata, Book VI, source. Clement cites Deuteronomy 4:19: the “host of heaven” was allotted to the nations, the sun and moon and stars given them to worship as a divinely-conceded first rung by which they might “rise up to God.” Those who then fell further—to idols of “stocks and stones”—stand, in his reading, condemned.

Reception and debate

  • Clement is the earliest Father on this timeline whose extant works set angels over the nations in the terms of Deuteronomy 32:8–9 and cite Deuteronomy 4:19 with it; Michael Heiser's modern reading appeals to the same two texts. Origen, placed after him, was his pupil at Alexandria.
  • His powers are created, ministering angels under the one Word and Providence, not an assembly of rival “gods.”
  • His Deuteronomy 4:19 gloss reads the host-of-heaven cult as permitted—a concession to keep the nations from atheism. Eusebius (c. 320) couples the same texts with a rebel power's corruption of the arrangement.
