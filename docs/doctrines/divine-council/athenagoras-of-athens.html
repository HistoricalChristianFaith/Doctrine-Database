Athenagoras of Athens on the Divine Council

Dates: c. 177 (Athenagoras of Athens, c. 133–190; Wikipedia) Summary: In his Plea for the Christians (addressed to Marcus Aurelius and Commodus, c. 177), Athenagoras describes an administered heaven: God set a “multitude of angels” over the parts of creation, so that the “particular parts are provided for by the angels appointed over them,” while He keeps the “universal and general providence of the whole.” Over this graded oversight stands a rebel “prince of matter,” a created power gone “hostile to God.” The domain he names is the elements and the world at large; he does not speak of the nations or of Deuteronomy 32:8.

Claims & evidence

God set a “multitude of angels” over the elements, the heavens, and the world

Nor is our teaching in what relates to the divine nature confined to these points; but we recognise also a multitude of angels and ministers, whom God the Maker and Framer of the world distributed and appointed to their several posts by His Logos, to occupy themselves about the elements, and the heavens, and the world, and the things in it, and the goodly ordering of them all.

— A Plea for the Christians, ch. X, source.1 Athenagoras builds the point right into his defence of Christian monotheism: acknowledging one God does not deny a populated heaven of subordinate “angels and ministers,” each “appointed” by the Logos to a “post” over a part of the created order. The heaven he assumes is administered by appointed powers.

General providence is God's; the particular parts are governed by the angels appointed over them

For this is the office of the angels, to exercise providence for God over the things created and ordered by Him; so that God may have the universal and general providence of the whole, while the particular parts are provided for by the angels appointed over them.

— A Plea for the Christians, ch. XXIV, source.2 The sentence sets out a two-tier providence in which the one God retains the oversight of the whole while delegating the “particular parts” to “the angels appointed over them.” Athenagoras applies it to the elements and the world, not to the peoples.

Some angels fell; chief among them the rebel “prince of matter”

Some, free agents, you will observe, such as they were created by God, continued in those things for which God had made and over which He had ordained them; but some outraged both the constitution of their nature and the government entrusted to them: namely, this ruler of matter and its various forms, and others of those who were placed about this first firmament.

These angels, then, who have fallen from heaven, and haunt the air and the earth, and are no longer able to rise to heavenly things, and the souls of the giants, which are the demons who wander about the world… But the prince of matter, as may be seen merely from what transpires, exercises a control and management contrary to the good that is in God.

— A Plea for the Christians, chs. XXIV–XXV, source.3 He adds that some angels fell. Angels are “free agents”: some kept their posts, but some “outraged … the government entrusted to them” — foremost “this ruler of matter and its various forms,” the “prince of matter” whose management now runs “contrary to the good that is in God.” In the same passage he joins this to the Genesis-6 account: the angels who fell “into impure love of virgins” begat the giants, whose souls are “the demons who wander about the world.”

Placement on the timeline

  • On this timeline Athenagoras (c. 177) stands before Clement of Alexandria (c. 215). He describes a heaven of created “angels and ministers” appointed to govern the parts of creation under God's providence.
  • Scope. Athenagoras does not speak of the seventy angels of the nations, of a headcount, or of Deuteronomy 32:8; his “particular parts” are the elements, the heavens, and the world at large: a two-tier providence, God over the whole and appointed angels over the parts, with a rebel chief.
  • His powers are created angels under the one God — “whom God the Maker and Framer of the world distributed and appointed” — not an assembly of rival gods.
  • The same passage seats Athenagoras as a witness on the Nephilim timeline (the fallen angels who begat the giants, whose souls become the wandering demons).
