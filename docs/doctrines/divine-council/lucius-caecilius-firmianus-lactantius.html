Lactantius on the Divine Council

Dates: c. 310 (The Divine Institutes II.14–17; Lactantius, c. 250–325; Wikipedia) Summary: Lactantius gives the fullest early-fourth-century statement of the “pagan gods = fallen angels' offspring = demons” thesis. God sent angels to guard mankind; seduced by the devil, they “polluted [themselves] by intercourse with women,” and their mixed-nature offspring became “two kinds of demons; one of heaven, the other of the earth.” These earthbound demons “wish themselves to appear as [men's] guardians, that they themselves may be worshipped,” so men “worship the wise demons as gods of the earth.” Under exorcism they “utter their own names — those which are adored in the temples.” The gods of the nations are, for Lactantius, precisely these demons.

Claims & evidence

The fallen angels' mixed-nature offspring became the two kinds of demons

Therefore, while they abode among men, that most deceitful ruler of the earth, by his very association, gradually enticed them to vices, and polluted them by intercourse with women. Then, not being admitted into heaven on account of the sins into which they had plunged themselves, they fell to the earth… But they who were born from these, because they were neither angels nor men, but bearing a kind of mixed nature, were not admitted into hell, as their fathers were not into heaven. Thus there came to be two kinds of demons; one of heaven, the other of the earth.

— The Divine Institutes II.15, source. Lactantius grounds the demons in the Genesis-6 descent: the guardian angels God had sent are corrupted, and their “mixed nature” offspring — the giants of Genesis 6 — become the earthbound demons. He distinguishes two ranks: the fallen angels themselves (demons of heaven) and their offspring (demons of the earth).

Men worship these demons as gods

they themselves also, though they are the destroyers of men, yet wish themselves to appear as their guardians, that they themselves may be worshipped, and God may not be worshipped… They consecrate these in their houses, to these they daily pour out libations of wine, and worship the wise demons as gods of the earth, and as averters of those evils which they themselves cause and impose.

— The Divine Institutes II.15, source. The demons usurp the very role God gave the guardian angels: they “wish themselves to appear as [men's] guardians,” and so divert to themselves the worship owed to God. This is the identity of the gods of the nations — the demons posing as guardian-gods.

Under exorcism the demons confess the very names adored in the temples

they fear the righteous, that is, the worshippers of God, adjured by whose name they depart from the bodies of the possessed: for, being lashed by their words as though by scourges, they not only confess themselves to be demons, but even utter their own names—those which are adored in the temples—which they generally do in the presence of their own worshippers.

— The Divine Institutes II.16, source. The demons, forced to speak, name themselves — and the names are those “adored in the temples.” The gods of the pagan cult and the demons are, on Lactantius's argument, one and the same. It is the same exorcism-proof Tertullian uses in Apology 23.

Notes

  • Lactantius stands with Tertullian a century later: the same mechanism (the Genesis-6 angelic descent → demon offspring) and the same conclusion (the gods of the nations are these demons). The two together carry the “pagan gods = fallen angels” thread on this timeline, downstream of Justin and the Septuagint's Psalm 96:5.
  • Distinctive to Lactantius is the “two kinds of demons” taxonomy (the fallen angels = demons of heaven; their giant offspring = demons of the earth) and the guardian-turned-usurper move: the demons pose as the guardians God had actually appointed, so the counterfeit steals the office of the real ministering angels the cosmic-geography Fathers set over the nations.
  • Lactantius appears on this wiki also as a witness on the Nephilim and purgatory timelines.
