The gods of the nations: a catalog of the national patron deities

This page is a reference companion to the divine-council summary's argument group “the gods of the nations across cultures.” Where those argument pages weigh whether the Deuteronomy 32:8–9 apportionment — the peoples parcelled out to divine powers, with Israel kept as Yahweh's own portion — left a footprint in the cults of the nations, this catalog gathers, god by god, the concrete detail behind the roster. Each entry sets out the deity's names and epithets across the languages that record it, the people and territory bound to it, its legend cycle in text and inscription, and how Scripture and the Fathers place it.

One honesty note governs the whole catalog. The modern “gods of the nations” reading folds each patron into the disinherited elohim of Deuteronomy 32 — a real, lesser divine power set over its people at Babel. That cross-identification is flagged here as the reader's synthesis wherever the text does not state it: a real power behind an idol is possible, but possibility is not proof, and the canon itself is two-voiced (a patron who acts on his own turf in the older narrative; a wooden nothing in the prophetic idol-polemic). Whether any given god is a council-elohim, the Fathers' “idol of demons,” or a mere personified cult is the contested question the argument pages carry — this catalog reports the detail, not a verdict.

Chemosh — the god of Moab

Names and epithets. In Moab's own language the name is Kamōš (kmš, written without the waw on the Mesha Stele). The Hebrew Bible vowels it Kəmōš — pointed, it is widely held, after bəʾôš, “stench,” a polemical distortion — so the truer vocalization survives in the Greek Chamōs (Χαμώς, the Septuagint) and the Latin Chamos (the Vulgate). The etymology is genuinely unresolved: proposed links run to the Eblaite deity Kamiš, a Ugaritic divine name Kamāþu, or an epithet of the Mesopotamian Nergal, Kammuš, perhaps “bull.” On the Mesha Stele he appears also in the compound Ashtar-Chemosh, which ties him to ʿAþtar (Athtar), the Semitic deity of the planet Venus — whether as a paired consort-goddess (Ishtar / Astarte) or as an astral by-name of Chemosh himself is debated. His name was a stock theophoric element: the Moabite kings Kammusu-nadbi (“Chemosh is generous,” named on Sennacherib's Taylor Prism, 701 BC) and, on the Kerak inscription, Kemosh-yat (probably Mesha's own father); Akkadian records give dKa-am-muš and Kamušu-ilu (“Chemosh is god”). In the Graeco-Roman period he was identified with the war-god Ares, on which account Rabbat-Moab was renamed Areopolis, “city of Ares.”1

The people and territory bound to him. Chemosh's land is the Transjordan plateau east of the Dead Sea — Iron Age Moab, at its apogee in the ninth century BC. The bond of god to people is so close that Scripture calls Moab flatly “the people of Chemosh” (Numbers 21:29, in the ballad of Heshbon): the nation is named for its god. The cult outlived the kingdom — Chemosh persists as a theophoric element among people of Moabite descent living in Egypt and Babylonia, and an Aramaic inscription from al-Karak names him as late as the third century BC.2

The legend cycle — on the page and on the stone. Moab's own monument frames the nation's whole history in patron-god terms. The Mesha Stele (the Moabite Stone, c. 840 BC, Louvre AO 5066) — king Mesha's record of the revolt narrated in 2 Kings 3 — opens on defeat as divine anger: “Omri, king of Israel, had oppressed Moab many days, for Chemosh was angry with his land”; then, the god relenting, “Chemosh said to me, Go take Nebo against Israel…” and Mesha devotes the slain “to Ashtar-Chemosh.” The identical schema — a national god angry with his own land, giving it over to a foreign power, then delivering it back — is the Moabite mirror of Israel's way of speaking about Yahweh.3 The Bible preserves the grimmest scene of the same war: besieged and losing, the king of Moab “took his eldest son… and offered him for a burnt offering upon the wall. And there was great indignation against Israel” (2 Kings 3:27) — a child-sacrifice to Chemosh on the city wall, followed by a wrath that breaks the siege.4 Chemosh reached Jerusalem, too: Solomon built him a high place “in the hill that is before Jerusalem” — “Chemosh, the abomination of Moab” set beside Milcom of Ammon (1 Kings 11:7, 33) — a shrine that stood three centuries until Josiah defiled the high places on that same Mount of Corruption (2 Kings 23:13).5 The cycle closes in prophecy: Jeremiah's oracle against Moab sends the god himself into exile — “Chemosh shall go forth into captivity with his priests and his princes” (48:7), “Moab shall be ashamed of Chemosh” (48:13), and its people mourned one last time as “the people of Chemosh” (48:46).6

How Scripture and the Fathers place him. The Fathers did not read Chemosh as an empty name. Jerome, following a Hebrew tradition, identifies Chamos with Baal-Peor (Beelphegor): commenting on Isaiah's oracle against Moab he notes that “in Nabo there was consecrated the idol Chamos, which is called by another name Beelphegor,” and elsewhere, reading Baal-Peor as an obscene fertility cult, he glosses the same god as a Priapus — the Moabite idol “worshiped for the obscenity of his magnitude, whom we can call Priapus” — and turns Jeremiah 48:7 against him (“Chamos will go into captivity, along with its priests”). In Letter 129 he lists “Beelphegor… Beelzebub and Chamos” among the false gods Israel wrongly “worshipped… as Almighty God.” Bede, on Solomon's shrine for “Chamos, an offence to Moab,” reads the high places as places where they “sacrificed to demons.” So the patristic placement is consistent: a real spiritual power behind the idol, and a demonic one — not a neutral member of a heavenly council.7

The divine-council cross-identification (a synthesis). The modern “gods of the nations” reading folds Chemosh into the disinherited elohim of Deuteronomy 32:8–9: a real, lesser divine power set over Moab at Babel, mirror to Yahweh over Israel — and the Mesha Stele's own grammar (Chemosh angry with his land, then giving it back) is exactly what makes the reading live. But this join is the reader's synthesis. Scripture names Chemosh and, separately, describes an apportionment of the nations to heavenly powers; nowhere does it say in one breath that Chemosh is one of the apportioned sons of God. Whether he is a council-elohim, the Fathers' idol of demons, or a mere personified cult is precisely the contested question weighed on the argument page the named national gods (assessment: plausible) — possibility is not proof.8

Milcom — the god of Ammon (and the Molech question)

Names and epithets. Ammon's national god is Milkōm (mlkm) — in the Hebrew Bible Milcom (1 Kings 11:5, 33; 2 Kings 23:13), and behind two title-forms of the same root mlk, “king”: Malcham (malkām, “their king,” Zephaniah 1:5) and the “their king” who “shall go into captivity” in Jeremiah's Ammon-oracle (49:1, 3) and Amos 1:15. The name is transparently the West-Semitic word for king — the god is “the King” — and it was a live theophoric element in Ammonite personal names on seals and ostraca. The knot that gives this entry its second title is the vowel e in Molech: at 1 Kings 11:7 the Masoretic text has Solomon build a high place “for Molech, the abomination of the children of Ammon” — where the parallel verses 11:5 and 11:33 read Milcom for the same Ammonite god, and several Septuagint witnesses read Milcom at 11:7 too. On the traditional account the pointing Mōlek is the consonants of melek (“king”) revocalized after bōšet, “shame” — a polemical distortion (Abraham Geiger, 1857), the same move Hebrew is widely thought to have worked on Chemosh (see above) and on Astarte/bōšet. That vocalization theory is now contested (R. M. Kerr calls it an out-of-date proposal that never won factual support), and the Greek keeps the fuller Moloch (Μολοχ, the Septuagint and, quoting it, Acts 7:43), the Latin Moloch / Melchom (the Vulgate).9

Is Molech the same god as Milcom? — the open question. Whether the child-sacrifice “Molech” of the Torah and the prophets (Leviticus 18:21; 20:2–5; Jeremiah 32:35) is Ammon's national Milcom is genuinely unsettled, and three readings compete. (1) Same god. The 1 Kings 11:7 slip (“Molech … of the children of Ammon,” where the neighbours read “Milcom”) and the shared root mlk let the tradition simply equate them — the reading the Fathers inherited. (2) A distinct “King”-deity. Otto Eissfeldt argued in 1935 that mōlek was never a god's name at all but a type of sacrifice — the Punic molk/mulk offering attested in North-African inscriptions — so that “to give one's seed to mōlek” is to give it as a molk-offering, not to a deity called Molech (a reading Francesca Stavrakopoulou and Heath Dewrell still defend). Against this, John Day and George Heider replied that Leviticus 20:5's “go a whoring after Molech” reads far more naturally of a god, and reconstructed Molech as a distinct underworld deity Malik/Milku — a divine name attested at Ugarit and at Mari, twice equated in Mesopotamian god-lists with the death-god Nergal — whose cult the Judahite kings imported and whom the biblical writers then tarred with Ammon's similarly-named “King.” On this third view Molech and Milcom are near-homonyms drawn together, not simply one god. The catalog states the tangle and does not force it: the safe report is that Ammon's patron is Milcom the King, that a “Molech” child-cult ran in Judah under the same root, and that Scripture itself, at 1 Kings 11:7, already lets the two names slide into one.10

The people and territory bound to him. Milcom's land is Ammon — the Transjordan plateau around its capital Rabbath-Ammon (Rabbah, modern Amman), between the Jabbok and the Arnon east of the Jordan. The bond of god to territory is drawn most sharply in Jeremiah's oracle, where the god is spoken of as the nation's landholder: “why then doth their king [Milcom] inherit Gad, and his people dwell in his cities?” (49:1) — the Ammonite god possessing, through his people, the very Israelite Transjordan — and where his fall is the nation's: “their king shall go into captivity, and his priests and his princes together” (49:3). Solomon's indictment sets him beside the neighbouring patrons in a single line — “Ashtoreth the goddess of the Zidonians, Chemosh the god of the Moabites, and Milcom the god of the children of Ammon” (1 Kings 11:33) — the roster of one-god-per-people that the divine-council reading takes as its clearest biblical footprint.11

The legend cycle — the fire, Topheth, and Gehenna. Milcom has left no Mesha Stele of his own; his “cycle” in the record is the cult the Bible condemns, the rite of “passing children through the fire.” The Torah forbids it by name: “thou shalt not let any of thy seed pass through the fire to Molech” (Leviticus 18:21), on pain of being cut off for “whoredom with Molech” (20:2–5); Jeremiah locates it: they “built the high places of Baal … in the valley of the son of Hinnom, to cause their sons and their daughters to pass through the fire unto Molech” (32:35). The place is Topheth in the Valley of (the son of) Hinnom just outside Jerusalem, which Josiah defiled “that no man might make his son or his daughter to pass through the fire to Molech” (2 Kings 23:10). It is from this cursed valley — Hebrew Gê-Hinnōm — that the New Testament's word for hell, Gehenna, is drawn: Bede spells the derivation out, that as the idolaters “perished” in the Valley of Hinnom, so the name passed to “the punishment of hell.” The lurid picture familiar from later art — a hollow bronze idol with a calf's face and outstretched arms, heated red, children laid in its hands while drums drowned their cries — is not in Scripture or the Talmud: it first appears in the medieval midrash Tanhuma and in Rashi, built up out of the classical (Diodorus Siculus) reports of Carthaginian sacrifice to Kronos. It is a genuine tradition, but a late synthesis, not a datum of the biblical text.12

How Scripture and the Fathers place him. The Fathers read Molech as a real idol-power, and straightforwardly as Ammon's king-god. Jerome, on Jeremiah 32:35, writes that “Moloch is the idol of the Ammonites, which is turned into a king,” and that in the Valley of Hinnom the people “served… Moloch and all the other demons in that place”; in his onomastic handbook he glosses the name plainly — “Moloch: your king … whom they are accustomed to call both Melchom” — tying Molech, Milcom, and “king” into the one god; and on Amos he calls him “the idol of the Ammonites, called Molech or their king.” Bede, on Josiah's reform, reads Topheth as the place where “altars were set up to sacrifice to demons, and to consecrate their children to a wicked fire,” and names Solomon's shrine “for Melchon, an abomination to the children of Ammon.” And when Stephen indicts Israel with Amos's line — “ye took up the tabernacle of Moloch” (Acts 7:43) — Chrysostom takes it as the same king-idol. The patristic placement is therefore consistent with Chemosh's: a real and demonic power behind the idol, not a neutral member of a heavenly council.13

The divine-council cross-identification (a synthesis). The modern “gods of the nations” reading folds Milcom into the disinherited elohim of Deuteronomy 32:8–9: a real, lesser divine power set over Ammon at Babel, mirror to Yahweh over Israel — and Jeremiah 49:1's grammar (Ammon's “king” inheriting Gad, as Yahweh inherits Israel) is just the kind of language that keeps the reading live. But, as with Chemosh, the join is the reader's synthesis: Scripture names Milcom and, separately, describes the apportionment of the nations to heavenly powers; it nowhere says in one breath that Milcom is one of the apportioned sons of God. That Molech was worshipped with real horrors, and that the Fathers took a demon to stand behind him, does not settle which kind of being he is — council-elohim, idol of demons, or personified cult. That is the contested question weighed on the named national gods (assessment: plausible) — possibility is not proof.14

Dagon — the god of Philistia

Names and epithets. The Philistines' national god is Dāgôn (dgwn) — in the Septuagint Dagōn (Δαγών), in the Vulgate Dagon. Behind the coastal city-cult stands a far older and greater power: the West-Semitic Dagan, a grain-and-fatherhood god of inland Syria worshipped as “lord of the land,” attested from the third millennium BC at Ebla, at Mari on the middle Euphrates, at Emar, and at Ugarit, where the storm-god Baal-Hadad is repeatedly styled “son of Dagan” (bn dgn). The Philistines' patron is thus no local invention but a regional father-god of the Amorite–Canaanite world, worn down on the coast to a single people's deity. The etymology splits along two lines — and Jerome preserves both. The older and now-favoured derivation ties the name to dāgān, “grain”: Philo of Byblos already reports Sanchuniathon glossing Dagon as a word for grain (sitōn), and Jerome's own name-handbook renders Bethdagon as “house of wheat.” The rival — and far more famous — derivation ties it to dāg, “fish”: Jerome glosses “Dagon: fish of sadness,” and the medieval rabbis Rashi and David Kimhi built the gloss out into the familiar merman, an idol “half fish from the navel down, half man above.” Modern scholarship judges the fish etymology late and mistaken — a medieval reading imposed on a name that meant grain — so the “fish-god of the Philistines” of popular imagination rests on the weaker of the two ancient glosses.15

The people and territory bound to him. Dagon's people are the Philistines of the coastal Pentapolis — Ashdod, Gaza, Ashkelon, Gath, and Ekron — with his chief temples at Ashdod (where the captured ark was set beside him) and Gaza (where Samson died in his house). His name is stamped on the land in the place-name Beth-Dagon (“house of Dagon”), which appears twice in Joshua's boundary lists — one town in the lowland of Judah (Joshua 15:41), another on the border of Asher (19:27) — and again as the Bethdagon into which routed Seleucid cavalry fled in 1 Maccabees. (The caution the grain etymology forces: some Beth-Dagon names may honour the crop dagan, not the god.) The double range is the mark of this deity — a national patron on the Philistine seaboard, and behind him the trans-regional Dagan whose cult ran from the Euphrates to the Mediterranean centuries before the Philistines ever reached Canaan.16

The legend cycle — the idol that could not stand. Unlike Chemosh, Dagon left no monument in his own voice; every scene the record preserves is a scene of his humiliation, and that deflation is itself the point. When the Philistines captured the ark they “brought it into the house of Dagon, and set it by Dagon” at Ashdod (1 Samuel 5:2) — the trophy of a beaten god set beside the victor. But “when they of Ashdod arose early on the morrow, behold, Dagon was fallen upon his face to the earth before the ark of the LORD” (5:3). They stood him up again; the next morning he lay fallen once more, “and the head of Dagon and both the palms of his hands were cut off upon the threshold; only the stump of Dagon was left to him” (5:4) — the idol beheaded and dismembered, prostrate before the mercy-seat. From it the narrator draws an etiology of cult-custom: “therefore neither the priests of Dagon, nor any that come into Dagon's house, tread on the threshold of Dagon in Ashdod unto this day” (5:5), even as the LORD's “hand was sore upon” the Ashdodites “and upon Dagon our god” (5:7).17

The other scenes are of a piece. At Gaza the Philistine lords “gathered them together for to offer a great sacrifice unto Dagon their god… for they said, Our god hath delivered Samson our enemy into our hand” (Judges 16:23) — and the feast in Dagon's honour ends with the blinded Samson pulling the temple down on some three thousand of the god's worshippers. Ambrose retells it: Samson “shook the columns with great force, and broke them in pieces, whereon followed the downfall of the upper roof,” so that “the dying Samson obtained a triumph” and “slew more in his death than while in the light of life.” After Gilboa the Philistines “put [Saul's] armour in the house of their gods, and fastened his head in the temple of Dagon” (1 Chronicles 10:10) — the enemy king's head dedicated as the ark had been. And as late as the Maccabean wars the cult still stood: the routed horse “fled to Azotus” (Ashdod) “and went into Bethdagon, their idol's temple, for safety,” whereupon Jonathan “burned with fire” the temple of Dagon and all who had sheltered in it (1 Maccabees 10:83–84).18

How Scripture and the Fathers place him. The Fathers made the ark-scene a set-piece, and they read the toppled Dagon two ways at once — as a real power thrown down, and as nothing at all. Theodoret presses the nothing: “Dagon who was adored as God by them (although he was a dumb, senseless idol) was made to fall before the ark,” a “spectacle… so that the Philistines might perceive the difference between false god and true God.” Chrysostom, arguing that a holy object does not sanctify its place, notes that the captured ark “gave proof of its own power in an alien land by twice throwing the idol to the ground so that the idol was broken.” Bede allegorises the shattered statue into the fall of the devil himself — “the head of all sin, the pride of the devil” — the “truncated Dagon lying on the threshold” figuring the hour when “Now is the judgment of the world, now the prince of this world shall be cast out” (John 12); Gregory the Great reads the same scene as idolatry examined and condemned “the more subtly… the more truly.” So the patristic placement matches the OT's own two-voiced handling: a real power stands behind the idol, and that power is beaten and cast down — not a neutral member of a heavenly council.19

The divine-council cross-identification (a synthesis). The modern “gods of the nations” reading would fold Dagon too into the disinherited elohim of Deuteronomy 32:8–9 — a real, lesser power set over Philistia, mirror to Yahweh over Israel. But Dagon is the hardest case for that reading and its most honest test, because his entire biblical presence is a demonstration that he is no power at all before the ark: he cannot keep his own head on before Israel's God, and the deflationary voice here all but drowns out the patron voice. Whether the being behind the idol is a council-elohim, the Fathers' beaten demon, or (as the grain etymology would have it) a personified crop-cult is exactly the contested question weighed on the named national gods (assessment: plausible) — and Dagon's toppling is a standing reminder that, even granting a real power, Scripture's interest is in its subjection, not its rank. Possibility is not proof.20

Qos — the god of Edom (whom the Bible never names)

Qos is the catalog's odd entry, and its most telling one. Chemosh, Milcom, and Dagon are all named on the page of Scripture as the gods of their peoples; Edom's national god is never named in the Old Testament at all. Yet Edom plainly had one, and we know his name from everywhere but the biblical text — Assyrian tribute-lists, Edomite seals, Egyptian topographical inscriptions, Aramaic papyri, and the temples of Edom's Nabataean heirs. The one-god-per-nation structure the divine-council reading leans on holds even for the one neighbour whose god the biblical writers pass over in silence — which makes Qos, precisely because he is absent from the roster, the sharpest test of whether that roster is a feature of the ancient landscape or an artifact of the Bible's own polemic.

Names and epithets. The name is written qws (קוס) and its vocalization is debated and probably shifted over time: the older Edomite Qāws / Qaus (whence the common spelling Qaus/Qaush) later contracting to Qôs, with Akkadian scribes writing Qauš and the Greek keeping forms like Kôze. The favoured etymology derives the name from the Arabic qaus, “bow” — suggesting a hunter- or warrior-god, or a storm-god whose bow is the rainbow — and on that ground Javier Teixidor and others identify Qos with Quzah, “the archer,” a north-Arabian mountain-and-weather god (a memory of the “bow” survives in the Arabic names of the stars of Sagittarius — Kaus Australis, Kaus Media, Kaus Borealis). The link is attractive but not certain: John F. Healey cautions that the evidence for the Quzah identification is limited, so the “bow” sense is best stated as the leading proposal, not a settled fact. What the record does show plainly is his character: where he can be seen — on his Nabataean-era images — he is a weather-god, enthroned and wielding a thunderbolt (below).21

The one biblical trace — and the silence around it. Scripture names Qos exactly once, and never as a god: buried in the register of temple-servants (nəþînîm) who returned from Babylon is “the children of Barkos” (Ezra 2:53; Nehemiah 7:55) — Bar-Qos, “son of Qos,” a clan whose very name is a fossilized confession of the Edomite god, carried unremarked into a list of Jerusalem temple-helpers. That is the whole of it. Where the Bible tells us flatly that Chemosh is “the god of Moab” and Milcom “the god of the children of Ammon,” it says nothing of Edom's god by name — even in the long Edomite king-list of Genesis 36, even in the fierce oracles against Edom (Obadiah; Jeremiah 49; Ezekiel 25, 35). The silence is conspicuous rather than accidental: Edom is one of Israel's most closely-watched neighbours, its “brother” (Esau), and the omission of its patron sits oddly beside the Bible's readiness to name and mock every other nation's god.22

The people and territory bound to him. Qos's land is Edom — the rugged highlands of Seir south and south-east of the Dead Sea — and its Graeco-Roman continuation Idumaea, after Edomites pushed west into the southern hill-country of Judah. The cult's roots may reach further south still: a case is made that Qos-worship originated in the Ḥismā desert of southern Jordan and north-western Arabia, where a mountain, Jabal al-Qaus, still bears the name. From the eighth century BC the god is firmly Edom's own, and the bond of god to people is drawn exactly as it is for Chemosh and Milcom — only, in Edom's case, entirely outside the Bible.23

The record outside Scripture — kings, seals, and Egyptian lists. What the Bible withholds, the inscriptions supply in abundance. Two Edomite kings carry Qos in their names in the Assyrian tribute-records: Qausmalak (Qauš-malaku, “Qos is king”), a tributary of Tiglath-Pileser III (c. 734 BC), and Qausgabri (Qauš-gabri, “Qos is mighty”), a tributary of Esarhaddon and Ashurbanipal (7th c. BC) whose name recurs on a royal bulla found at Umm el-Biyara above Petra. The theophoric element runs on through the Persian-era Aramaic ostraca of Idumaea, the mixed-population papyri of Elephantine in Egypt, and down to the Herodian Costobarus. Some argue the god is far older: E. A. Knauf and others read certain place-names in the Ramesside topographical lists (Late Bronze Age Egypt) as theophoric compounds of Qos — a claim developed by B. Oded — which, if right, would set the first trace of Qos centuries before the earliest of Chemosh or Milcom. The reading of those Egyptian names is contested and should be held lightly, but the later evidence is not in doubt: from the eighth century BC on, Qos is beyond question “the patron deity of the Edomite people.”24

The legend cycle — a Nabataean afterlife, not a biblical story. Qos has no legend-cycle in text, because the one literature that would have preserved one — the Bible — is silent, and Edom left no Mesha Stele of its own. What survives instead is a rich material cult among Edom's Nabataean successors, who kept Qos alive for centuries after the kingdom fell. At the mountaintop sanctuary of Khirbet et-Tannur and the nearby village temple of Khirbet edh-Dharih in southern Jordan, the one named male god is Qos, shown as a weather-god enthroned between bulls, a many-forked thunderbolt in his left hand — and there he is thoroughly syncretized: identified with the Arab sky-god Quzah, drawn toward the supreme Nabataean god Dushara, and assimilated in the Graeco-Roman manner to Zeus (and the Syrian Zeus-Hadad), his cult set amid zodiac-friezes and astral imagery that mark the seasonal cycle. Judith McKenzie's study of the site reads its whole programme as a single storm-god and fertility-goddess ensuring the land's abundance. The cult even flickers back into political history: Josephus reports that Herod's brother-in-law Costobarus — “one whose ancestors had been priests to the Koze, whom the Idumeans had formerly esteemed as a god” — came of that priestly line and is thought to have leaned on the old loyalty when he plotted his defection. Qos then fades from the record in the first centuries AD.25

Why Scripture (and so the Fathers) pass him over. Here the entry cannot end as the others do, for there is no biblical naming for the Fathers to gloss: because Scripture never names Qos, the patristic tradition — which read Chemosh, Molech, and Dagon so vigorously as demons — leaves Edom's god unremarked too, following the text's own silence. Why that silence? Juan Manuel Tebes argues it is deliberate memory-work: in the Second-Temple centuries Judeans and Idumaeans lived intermingled and competed for the same land, and the Qos cult grew popular in Persian- and Hellenistic-era Idumaea just as the priestly circles of Yehud were pressing a robust monotheism and ethnic purity — so the biblical writers wrote Edom's god out rather than dignify a too-close rival. A second, older suggestion runs the other way: that Yahweh and Qos were felt to be too alike to name side by side. The Bible itself remembers Yahweh “coming from Seir… the field of Edom” and “from Teman” (Deuteronomy 33:2; Judges 5:4; Habakkuk 3:3), the very country of Qos; the Kenite/Midianite hypothesis of Yahwism's desert origin even floats the notion that “Qos” was once a title of Yahweh rather than a separate god. Tebes himself judges the outright identification of Qos with Yahweh mistaken, and this wiki weighs that Seir-origin material separately on the “Shasu of Yhw” question; the safe report here is that the silence is real, that it is probably purposeful, and that its cause is debated.26

The divine-council cross-identification (a synthesis). Qos's payload for the “gods of the nations” reading is not a proof-text but the silence itself. The modern reading folds each national patron into the disinherited elohim of Deuteronomy 32:8–9 — a real, lesser power set over its people at Babel — and the obvious objection is that the roster of named gods could be a mere by-product of the biblical writers' polemic, a list of whom they chose to mock. Qos answers that objection from the far side: here is a national god the writers did not name, whom we know only because the surrounding cultures recorded him anyway — so the one-god-per-people pattern is demonstrably a feature of the ancient Near-Eastern landscape, not an invention of Israel's controversialists. That strengthens the worldview the reading describes. It does not, however, settle what Qos is: whether the being behind the Edomite cult is a council-elohim, a demon, or a personified sky-and-bow cult is the same contested question weighed on the named national gods (assessment: plausible) — and the Seir overlap with Yahweh is a caution against reading the pattern too mechanically. Possibility is not proof.27

Ashtoreth (Astarte) — the goddess of the Zidonians (and Eshmun of Sidon)

Ashtoreth is the catalog's first goddess, and its cleanest case of a single deity known by other names. Where Chemosh and Milcom sit tight to one people, the Sidonians' great goddess runs the length of the ancient world under a chain of equivalents — the Mesopotamian Ishtar, the Greek Aphrodite, the Roman Venus — so that a Phoenician, a Babylonian, a Greek, and a Roman could each point to her and mean, by the reckoning of their own priests, the same power. That equivalence-chain — the ancient interpretatio, the “translation” of one nation's god into another's — is itself part of what the “gods of the nations” reading leans on, and part of what complicates it. Alongside her stands a second Sidonian deity the roster verse does not name: Eshmun, the healing god who was Sidon's own city-patron — so this entry carries two gods, the goddess of the Sidonians and the poliad god of Sidon.

Names and epithets. In Phoenician the name is ʿAštart (štrt), in Ugaritic ʿAþtart — the feminine of the common Semitic ʿAþtar, deity of the planet Venus. The Hebrew Bible vowels it Ashtoreth (ʿAštōret): on the widely-held account the consonants of ʿAštart here carry the vowels of bōšet, “shame” — the same polemical revocalization Hebrew is thought to have worked on Chemosh and on Molech (see above). The plural Ashtaroth serves both as her collective (paired with the Baalim) and as a place-name. Eastward she is the Akkadian Ishtar (Sumerian Inanna) — the grammatically masculine East-Semitic cognate that kept the older form without the feminine ending; westward the Greek Astarte (Αστάρτη), whom “the Phoenicians say is Aphrodite,” and the Roman Venus. Cicero already fixes that last link: distinguishing several goddesses named Venus he marks off a fourth “conceived in Syria and Tyre, who is called Astarte.” She is a goddess of love and war, imaged with lion, dove, and the star of the planet Venus — though whether she was properly a fertility goddess, and whether that astral identification is original to her, are questions now debated by specialists rather than settled.28

The Sidon binding. Scripture ties her to one people exactly as it ties Chemosh to Moab: she is “Ashtoreth the goddess of the Zidonians” (1 Kings 11:5), for whom Solomon built a high place east of Jerusalem; named again in his indictment beside her neighbours — “Ashtoreth the goddess of the Zidonians, Chemosh the god of the Moabites, and Milcom the god of the children of Ammon” (11:33) — and defiled at last by Josiah as “Ashtoreth the abomination of the Zidonians” (2 Kings 23:13). In the plural she heads the recurring apostasy-formula of Judges and Samuel: Israel “forsook the LORD, and served Baal and Ashtaroth” (Judges 2:13), “Baalim, and Ashtaroth, and the gods of Syria, and the gods of Zidon…” (10:6), until Samuel calls them to “put away… Ashtaroth from among you” (1 Samuel 7:3–4). The Sidonian bond ran straight into Israel's own royal house: Jezebel was daughter of the Sidonian king Ithobaal — remembered in the Tyrian king-list as a priest of Astarte who seized the throne — and it was her patronage that planted the coastal cult in Samaria (1 Kings 16:31).29

Eshmun — Sidon's own city-god. The goddess “of the Zidonians” was the nation's great patroness, but Sidon's poliad (city) deity proper was the male healing-god Eshmun, whose name (“the eighth”) marks him the eighth son of Sydyk, “the Just.” The Greeks read him without hesitation as Asclepius — a trilingual inscription flatly equates Eshmun, Asclepius, and the Latin Aesculapius — and his great sanctuary lay at Bostan esh-Sheikh a little north of Sidon, a healing-shrine with ritual ablution-basins fed by the river the Greeks renamed for Asclepius. Sidon's royal monuments set goddess and city-god side by side: on the sixth-century sarcophagus of King Eshmunazar II (“Eshmun helps”) the king declares that he and his mother, priests of Astarte, built temples to Baal of Sidon, to Astarte, and “a temple for Eshmun… on the mountain” — the civic triad of Sidon in a single inscription; the works were carried on by his successor Bodashtart, named on the temple's own foundation-stones. Behind the Greek dress this Asclepius is, in Damascius's words, “some native Phoenician divinity” — Sidon's own.30

The people and territory bound to them. The land is Sidon and the Phoenician seaboard — the coastal city-states of Sidon and Tyre and their hinterland (Tyre's own patron Melqart is a separate entry). Astarte's name is stamped on the map far inland as well: Ashtaroth was the royal city of Og king of Bashan in the Transjordan (Deuteronomy 1:4; Joshua 9:10), and Ashteroth-Karnaim, “Ashtaroth of the two horns” (Genesis 14:5), carried her horned image in its very name — a goddess whose cult-sites became toponyms, as Beth-Dagon did for Dagon. The bond of goddess to people is drawn most plainly in Solomon's roster (1 Kings 11:33) and in Judges 10:6, where “the gods of Zidon” stand in a single line beside the gods of Syria, Moab, Ammon, and Philistia — the one-god-per-people pattern the divine-council reading takes for its clearest biblical footprint.31

The legend cycle — the euhemerized queen of Philo of Byblos. Phoenicia's own theology of Astarte survives because a hostile Christian preserved it: the Phoenician History of Philo of Byblos (c. AD 100), claiming to render the ancient priest Sanchuniathon, is quoted at length by Eusebius in the Preparation of the Gospel — who cites it precisely to expose the gods as deified mortals. In that euhemerized genealogy Astarte is a queen: “Astarte set the head of a bull upon her own head as a mark of royalty; and in travelling round the world she found a star that had fallen from the sky, which she took up and consecrated in the holy island Tyre. And the Phoenicians say that Astarte is Aphrodite.” She is daughter of Ouranos, consort of Kronos (El), mother of Desire and Love; and by the same account Sidon's Eshmun appears as the Asclepius born to Sydyk, eighth of the Cabeiri. The Ras Shamra tablets have since shown that Sanchuniathon preserves genuine second-millennium Canaanite myth (Baal as Zeus Demarous, Dagon, Pontus), even as the antiquity of “Sanchuniathon” himself, and Philo's euhemerist frame, remain disputed. Two later strands round out the cult. The goddess honoured across Israel as the “queen of heaven,” for whom the women “knead their dough, to make cakes” and “burn incense” (Jeremiah 7:18; 44:17), is with fair probability this same Astarte (John Day so identifies her, though which goddess owns the title is contested). And the lurid “sacred prostitution” long attached to her cult on the authority of Herodotus and of Lucian's On the Syrian Goddess is now judged a scholarly myth built on misread and recycled sources, not a datum of the ancient record.32

The Bible's own scene of her is a scene of spoil. After Saul fell on Gilboa the Philistines “put his armour in the house of Ashtaroth: and they fastened his body to the wall of Beth-shan” (1 Samuel 31:10) — the dead king's arms dedicated in the goddess's temple, the counterpart to Saul's head fastened “in the temple of Dagon” in the parallel account (see above), and the mirror of Israel's own way of hanging a captured trophy before its God.33

How Scripture and the Fathers place her. The Fathers read Astarte, like her sisters, as a real power behind the idol — and they kept the interpretatio while turning it to demonology. Cyril of Alexandria, on Hosea, notes that “Astarte… they say is the one among the Greeks called… Aphrodite,” whose statue “stands naked, and as in the form of a prostitute” — the same equivalence Philo reports, now marshalled to shame the cult. Bede, on the temple where Saul's arms were hung, reads “Ashtaroth… the doctrine of malignant spirits, who, wandering around the folds of the faithful… explore if they can devour someone,” and on Samuel's reform names Baalim and Ashtaroth the “idols… from the Sidonians.” The historical cult is glimpsed too in Theophilus of Antioch, whose Tyrian king-list records a “Juthobalus, priest of Astarte” on the throne of Tyre — the very Ithobaal whose daughter was Jezebel. Even the pagan case knew the pattern: in Minucius Felix's Octavius the heathen spokesman lists, as proof that each people keeps its own gods, “the Chaldaeans, Belus; the Syrians, Astarte…” So the placement matches the rest of the catalog: a real, demonic power behind the idol — or a deified mortal — not a neutral member of a heavenly council.34

The divine-council cross-identification (a synthesis). Astarte offers the reading its most striking patristic near-parallel — and its subtlest caution. In the Apology Tertullian rehearses the ancient commonplace that deity is apportioned: “an imperial and supreme domination to one, while its offices are put into the hands of many… the procurators, prefects, and governors of the divine empire,” so that “Every province even, and every city, has its god. Syria has Astarte…” That is nearly the architecture of Deuteronomy 32:8–9 — one Most High over a tier of powers set each over a people — voiced by a Father, with Astarte named as Syria's. But Tertullian sketches the arrangement to argue for toleration, not to endorse real elohim; and Astarte herself supplies the counter-weight, for her interpretatio cuts both ways: a goddess who is Ishtar and Aphrodite and Venus at once looks less like one apportioned power set over one people than like a single cult diffusing under many names. Whether the being behind the Sidonian idol is a council-elohim, the Fathers' demon, or a personified cult carried across the languages is the contested question weighed on the named national gods (assessment: plausible) — possibility is not proof.35

Melqart — the god of Tyre (the Tyrian Heracles, and Jezebel's Baal)

Melqart is the catalog's dying-and-rising patron, and the one whose very name is a patron-god etymology. Where Chemosh and Dagon are proper names of obscure derivation, Tyre's god is called Melqart — milk-qart, “king of the city” — a title so transparent that the deity is, by his own name, the sovereign of a named town. He is also the catalog's most famous god under a borrowed face: to every Greek and Roman who met him he was the Tyrian Heracles / Hercules, and to Israel's historians he is very probably the Baal whom Jezebel of Tyre carried into Ahab's house. And he is the one patron with a resurrection — his central rite was the egersis, the annual “awakening” of a god who died and rose.

Names and epithets. In Phoenician the name is Milk-qart (mlqrt), the compound of milk, “king,” and qart, “city” (the same qart that names Carthage, Qart-ḥadašt, “New City”) — so “king of the city,” Tyre's own sovereign-god, whose standing title in the inscriptions is Baʿal Ṣūr, “Lord of Tyre.” In Scripture he is nowhere named as Melqart; he stands behind the bare Baal of Ahab and Jezebel — the Baal of the Zidonians and Tyrians. Westward, under the ancient interpretatio, he is the Tyrian Heracles — the equivalence Herodotus already takes for granted when he sails to Tyre to see “the temple of Heracles” (2.44) — and under the Roman dress Hercules; Augustine registers a “Hercules… held in honour in Tyre” distinct from the Greek one, noting that “several” figures bore the name. The Melqart–Heracles fusion carried the god's lion-skin, his labours, and (fatally for his standing) his funeral pyre into the reading of the Tyrian cult.36

The people and territory bound to him. Melqart's city is Tyre, the island fortress of the southern Phoenician coast, and through Tyre its whole colonial reach: the standard practice of a Tyrian foundation was to plant a temple of Melqart in the new town, so his cult ran the length of the Mediterranean — to Gades (Cádiz), to Lixus, and above all to Carthage, Tyre's greatest daughter. The bond of colony to mother-city was itself a cult of Melqart: Carthage sent a yearly tithe — a tenth of the public revenue — to the god's temple at Tyre, and Diodorus Siculus records that when the Carthaginians once let the custom lapse they read their disasters as the god's anger (20.14). The Barcid house was especially his: Hannibal swore his enmity to Rome by Melqart and named him in his treaty with Philip V of Macedon. One honest distinction the catalog must draw here: the notorious tophet child-sacrifice of Carthage was offered not to Melqart but to Baal Hammon and Tanit, the dominant Carthaginian pair — Melqart, for all his rank as ancestral patron, kept only a senior-but-secondary place in Carthage and had no part in the tophet rite. The western Phoenician child-cult belongs beside Chemosh and Molech (above), not here.37

The legend cycle — the awakening, the pillars, and the games. Alone among the catalog's gods Melqart has a death and a resurrection. His central rite was the egersis (Greek ἐγερσις, “awakening”), an annual festival in which the god died and was raised, kept at Tyre in the month Peritius (about February). The earliest notice of it is preserved by Josephus, who — citing the Tyrian court-records as rendered into Greek by Menander of Ephesus — reports that king Hiram, Solomon's ally, pulled down the old temples and “built the temple of Hercules [Melqart] and that of Astarte,” and “first … set up” (better, first celebrated the awakening of) “Hercules in the month Peritius” (Antiquities 8.5.3) — a tenth-century date for the resurrection-festival of Tyre's god, and the reason many read Melqart as a dying-and-rising deity of the Adonis / Tammuz type. Sailing to Tyre a few centuries later, Herodotus found the god's temple reckoned one of the oldest in the world — built “when Tyre had been founded,” the priests told him, 2,300 years before — and marvelled at its two votive pillars, “one of pure gold and one of an emerald stone… shining by night” (2.44). And the cult was still a public magnet in the second century BC: at the quinquennial games at Tyre, held in honour of Heracles, the Hellenizing high-priest Jason sent three hundred drachmas “to the sacrifice of Hercules” — money his own envoys balked at spending on so pagan a rite and turned instead to the building of warships (2 Maccabees 4:18–20).38

The Baal of Ahab and Jezebel — and Elijah's taunt. Scripture's likeliest encounter with Melqart is its most famous idol-drama. When Ahab “took to wife Jezebel the daughter of Ethbaal king of the Zidonians, and went and served Baal, and worshipped him” (1 Kings 16:31), the Baal he planted in Samaria was, on the reckoning of most modern scholars, Tyre's own Melqart — for Ethbaal (the Ithobaal remembered in the Tyrian king-list as a priest of Astarte turned king, see the Astarte entry above) was Tyre's ruler, and it was his daughter who brought the coastal state-cult east. A striking detail turns on the egersis: when Elijah mocks the prophets of Baal on Carmel — “Cry aloud: for he is a god; either he is talking, or he is pursuing, or he is in a journey, or peradventure he sleepeth, and must be awaked” (1 Kings 18:27) — several readers hear a direct jab at Melqart's cult: the god “on a journey” recalling the Heraclean wanderings, and the god who “sleepeth, and must be awaked” parodying the ritual awakening of the sleeping god. The parallel is attractive but not certain: the drought-and-rain setting of the chapter fits a storm-god (Baal-Shamem / Hadad) at least as well, so whether Carmel's Baal is specifically Melqart is debated — and it is worth noting that the Fathers who retell the scene name the enemy only as “Baal, the god of the Sidonians” (so Ephrem the Syrian), never as Melqart by name. The Melqart identification is the scholars' inference, not the text's word.39

How the Fathers place him. Because Scripture never gives the god his Tyrian name, the patristic handling reaches him under his two other faces — Hercules, and the “king of Tyre.” On the first the Fathers are of one mind: the Tyrian Hercules was a dead man deified. Augustine notes that “in those times Hercules was held in honour in Tyre, but that was not the same one” as the Greek, and that this Hercules “burned himself on Mount Œta, because he was not able… to endure the disease under which he languished” (City of God 18.12) — the pyre proving the god a mortal. Clement of Alexandria makes the same point from Homer (“Hercules… was known… as only a mortal man… after living fifty-two years… was burned in a funeral pyre in Oeta”), and Cyprian and Minucius Felix repeat the tag — “Hercules, that he may put off humanity, is burnt up by the fires of Oeta” — within the euhemerist thesis that the gods “were formerly kings, who on account of their royal memory… began to be adored by their people even in death,” and are now “impure and wandering spirits” (Cyprian). So under the name Hercules the Tyrian patron is placed exactly as Chemosh and Dagon were: a deified man and a real demon behind the idol.40

The god's second patristic face is the more consequential, for it is a genuine bridge to the divine-council material. The oracle against Tyre in Ezekiel 28 addresses first “the prince of Tyrus,” who says “I am a God, I sit in the seat of God… yet thou art a man, and not God” (28:2), and then “the king of Tyrus,” who “hast been in Eden the garden of God… the anointed cherub that covereth… upon the holy mountain of God” (28:12–14). That second lament a broad line of Fathers — Tertullian, Origen, Ambrose, Augustine, Jerome — read not of a man at all but of a fallen superhuman being: Tertullian says God “speaks of the devil there in the person of the prince of Tyre”; Ambrose, “the prince of Tyre stands for the devil”; Jerome, that “the one of whom it is written is by no means a human being, but a contrary fortitude, which formerly dwelt in God's paradise” — recording, and rejecting, the rival Jewish reading that took the king of Tyre for the historical Hiram. The mainstream grammatical-historical reading keeps the oracle on the human king; whether it also describes the Eden rebel is weighed on this wiki's argument page Ezekiel 28 and the anointed cherub (assessment: contested).41

The divine-council cross-identification (a synthesis). Melqart offers the reading its single most direct patristic bridge — and, like Astarte, its own built-in caution. The bridge is Origen: reading the two Ezekiel 28 oracles together, he concludes that the second “cannot possibly refer to a man but must be understood of some higher power that fell… some angel, to whom had been allotted the duty of supervising the Tyrian people, whose souls also were… committed to his care.” That is the cosmic-geography of Deuteronomy 32:8–9 stated of Tyre in as many words — a heavenly power allotted a single nation — voiced by a third-century Father over the very god this entry tracks. It is the closest the catalog comes to a Father saying, of a named nation, exactly what the modern reading says. But two things pull the other way. First, Origen means a created, fallen angel, not a legitimate council-elohim; and he is expounding Ezekiel's king, not identifying Melqart. Second — and as with Astarte's interpretatio — the very features that make Melqart vivid cut against reading him as one apportioned power set over one people: a god who is Heracles, who dies on a pyre and is awakened each spring, and whose temple holds his tomb, looks less like a disinherited son of God than like a euhemerized culture-hero diffusing under many names. Whether the being behind the Tyrian idol is a council-elohim, the Fathers' deified-mortal demon, or a personified civic cult is the contested question weighed on the named national gods (assessment: plausible) — possibility is not proof.42

Hadad (Rimmon) — the god of Aram-Damascus (the storm-god of the Ben-Hadad kings)

Hadad is the catalog's storm-god, and the one patron whose name is stamped, dynasty-deep, on the kings who bore it: the rulers of Damascus called themselves Ben-Hadad, “son of Hadad,” generation after generation, as if the storm-god fathered the royal line. To the Bible he is chiefly a shrine and a place-name — the “Rimmon” in whose house Naaman bowed — but on the Aramean kings' own victory-stones he is what Chemosh is on the Mesha Stele: the god who goes before the king and gives him the battle. And his greater self reaches back to the Ugaritic Baal-Hadad, the storm-god of the Canaanite myth-cycle (the Baal styled “son of Dagan” in the Dagon entry above), so this entry, like Astarte's, carries a deity at once national and trans-regional.

Names and epithets. The West-Semitic storm-god is Hadad (hd / hdd) — “the thunderer,” the name plausibly echoing the crash of thunder itself. Eastward he is the Akkadian Adad (Addu), Mesopotamia's storm-god, whose stock by-name Rammānu, “the Thunderer” (from ramāmu, “to roar, to thunder”), supplies the Hebrew Bible its Rimmon: the “house of Rimmon” at Damascus (2 Kings 5:18) is the temple of Hadad under his thunder-title. Zechariah preserves the fused form Hadad-Rimmon (12:11). Westward he is the great Baal-Hadad, the storm-and-fertility god who is the active Baal of the Ras Shamra (Ugaritic) tablets — “Baal” (Lord) his title, “Hadad” his name — so that the “Baal” fought across the Canaanite world is very often Hadad himself. His image is the bull and the forked lightning, the storm-god enthroned with a thunderbolt in his fist. Jerome's name-handbook glosses the components the Damascus kings carried into their thrones: Adad as “chief,” “cousin,” or “witness”; Remmon (Rimmon) as “loftiness, elevation”; the royal Benadad as “handsome son”; and Tabremmon — Tab-Rimmon, “Rimmon is good” — as “the good height, or the good sight of them.”43

The people and territory bound to him. Hadad's land is Aram-Damascus and the wider Aramean kingdoms of inland Syria, and the bond of god to people is written into the very names of its kings. The Damascene throne was a Hadad-dynasty: Ben-Hadad I, “the son of Tabrimon, the son of Hezion, king of Syria, that dwelt at Damascus” (1 Kings 15:18); the Ben-Hadad who besieged Samaria and warred with Ahab (1 Kings 20); the sick Ben-Hadad whom his officer Hazael smothered to seize the crown (2 Kings 8:7–15); and Hazael's own son, a third Ben-Hadad (2 Kings 13:24) — the storm-god's name renewed on the throne even by the usurper's line. Earlier the Aramean coalition-king is Hadadezer (“Hadad is help,” 2 Samuel 8). Scripture does set Aram's cult in the one-god-per-nation roster — among the gods Israel served it lists “the gods of Syria, and the gods of Zidon, and the gods of Moab … and the gods of the Philistines” (Judges 10:6) — but here a real difference from Chemosh must be marked: the Bible never says “Hadad the god of Syria” as it says “Chemosh the god of Moab.” Aram's patron is named only by his shrine (Rimmon) and by the kings' theophoric names, never in the roster line itself.44

The legend cycle — on the stone. Like Chemosh, Hadad speaks in the first person on his kings' monuments, and in exactly the same grammar. The Tel Dan Stele (Old Aramaic, c. 840–835 BC, most probably erected by Hazael of Damascus) frames an Aramean victory as the storm-god's gift: “Hadad made me king … and Hadad went before me,” whereupon the king slew “[Jeho]ram son of [Ahab] king of Israel” and a king “of the House of David” — the earliest mention of the Davidic dynasty outside the Bible, won under the banner of a national god who “goes before” his king precisely as Yahweh goes before Israel. The Zakkur Stele (c. 785 BC) shows the mirror image: Zakkur king of Hamath records his rescue from a siege-coalition headed by Bar-Hadad son of Hazael, king of Aram — another theophoric Hadad-name on the Damascus throne — though the delivered Hamathite credits Baal-shamayn, not Hadad, a reminder that these patrons overlapped and competed. So too does a votive stele from near Aleppo, the Melqart Stele of Bar-Hadad, on which an Aramean king “Bar-Hadad” dedicates his offering not to Hadad but to Melqart, Tyre's god (the entry above) — the borders between the nations' gods were freely crossed.45

In Scripture — Rimmon's house, and the mourning at Megiddo. The god's biblical scenes are three, and none is a monument in his own voice. The first is a scene of divided loyalty: the healed Syrian general Naaman asks the prophet's leave for a coming compromise — “when my master goeth into the house of Rimmon to worship there, and he leaneth on my hand, and I bow myself in the house of Rimmon … the LORD pardon thy servant in this thing” (2 Kings 5:18) — the one glimpse of Hadad's temple-cult from the inside. The second is a scene of grief: Zechariah likens the coming national lament to “the mourning of Hadad-rimmon in the valley of Megiddon” (12:11). The third is a scene of judgment on the dynasty: Amos's oracle sends “a fire into the house of Hazael, which shall devour the palaces of Ben-hadad,” and breaks “the bar of Damascus” and its idol-plain (1:4–5). Outside Scripture, Josephus adds a striking note on the Damascus cult of his own day: the Syrians, he says, “honour to this day as gods” the kings Benhadad and Hazael, “by reason of their benefactions and their building them temples, by which they adorned the city of the Damascenes” — a euhemerized dead-king cult grown up beside the storm-god's own.46

How Scripture and the Fathers place him. Here the catalog's pattern genuinely bends. The Fathers who read Chemosh, Molech, and Dagon so fiercely as demons handled Hadad far more lightly — because the two verses that name him invited other readings, and they took them. On Zechariah 12:11 Jerome reads Adadremmon not as a god at all but as a place: “a city … now called Maximianopolis in the field of Megiddo, where Josiah, a just king, was wounded by Pharaoh Nechao,” so that “the mourning of Hadad-rimmon” is the historic lament for the slain Josiah — itself a type of the mourning for the pierced Christ. On Amos 1:4–5 he reads the dynasty historically (Hazael dead, “his son Benadad succeeded him in the kingdom, from whom all the later kings of Aram … took the name Benadad”) and glosses Damascus's idol-plain as “the useless help of idols.” And the one text that puts a worshipper inside Hadad's temple — Naaman’s “house of Rimmon” — became for the tradition not a demonology-locus but the classic test-case of accommodation and a divided heart: reflecting on his own slow conversion, C. S. Lewis claimed Naaman's pardon for himself — “I am one of many who have bowed in the house of the real God when I believed Him to be no more than Rimmon.” So Hadad's patristic placement is lighter than the rest of the catalog: named only by shrine and dynasty, he is read as a town, a dead-king cult, or a moral problem more than as a living demon — a difference the honesty of the catalog should record.47

The divine-council cross-identification (a synthesis). Hadad hands the reading its single strongest inscriptional parallel and, in the same breath, its plainest reminder that the biblical roster is uneven. The Tel Dan Stele's “Hadad went before me” is the Aramean twin of the Mesha Stele's Chemosh — a national storm-god who makes his king and wins his wars, mirror to Yahweh over Israel — and that is just the cosmic-geography grammar the Deuteronomy 32:8–9 reading looks for, now attested on Aram's own stone rather than in Israel's polemic. But three things pull the other way, and Hadad displays all three. First, Scripture never enrolls him in the named roster (Judges 10:6's “gods of Syria” is generic); the footprint here is on the Aramean monuments, not in Israel's own list of apportioned patrons. Second — as with Astarte and Melqart — the very breadth of Hadad / Adad / Baal-Hadad, one storm-god diffusing under many names from Ugarit to Assyria, looks less like one power set over one people than like a single cult worn thin across the whole Semitic world. Third, the Damascenes of Josephus's day deified their kings Ben-Hadad and Hazael — a euhemerist face beside the storm-god one. Whether the being behind the house of Rimmon is a council-elohim, the Fathers' idol-power, a personified storm, or a deified dynasty is the contested question weighed on the named national gods (assessment: plausible) — possibility is not proof.48

Marduk (Bel) — the god of Babylon (and Nabu of Borsippa)

Marduk is the catalog's imperial god, and its clearest specimen of a patron whose rank among the gods can be watched rising with his city's rank among the nations. A minor local god of Babylon at the start of the second millennium BC, he climbs, as Babylon climbs, until Babylon's own creation-epic enthrones him king of the gods and heaps his fifty names upon him. To the Bible he is Bel and Merodach, a god carried off as dead weight when his city falls; to Herodotus he is the “Zeus Belus” of the greatest temple in the world; and in the Greek additions to Daniel he is the idol whose priests Daniel catches in fraud. Beside him stands his son Nabu — the biblical Nebo — scribe-god of neighbouring Borsippa, whose name the Babylonian kings carried into their thrones, and behind whose lord-title even the exiled Daniel was renamed Belteshazzar.

Names and epithets. The god's own name is Marduk (Sumerian Amar-utu, plausibly “calf of the sun”); the Hebrew Bible gives it once, as Merodach (Jeremiah 50:2), and folds it into the royal name Merodach-baladan (Isaiah 39:1). Far more often Scripture calls him by his title Bel (Akkadian Bēlu, “Lord” — the exact eastern cognate of Canaanite Baʿal): “Bel boweth down” (Isaiah 46:1), “Bel is confounded” (Jeremiah 50:2), “I will punish Bel in Babylon” (Jeremiah 51:44). The creation-epic Enūma Eliš closes by proclaiming his fifty names — the titles and functions of the other gods gathered onto Marduk as their powers are made his, the list running across its last two tablets to the culminating Bēl-mātāti, “lord of the lands.” His son is Nabu — the Hebrew Nebo (Isaiah 46:1) — god of writing, wisdom, and the scribal arts, worshipped at Borsippa (his temple Ezida); his name is the nabû (“herald, one who proclaims”) element behind the theophoric royal names Nebuchadnezzar (Nabû-kudurri-uṣur, “Nabu, guard the heir”), Nabonidus (Nabû-naʾid), and Nabopolassar, while Bel stands behind Belshazzar (Bēl-šarra-uṣur) and, by the same lord-title, behind the Babylonian name given the captive Daniel — Belteshazzar, a name Daniel 4:8 glosses as being “according to the name of my god” (Daniel 1:7).49

The people and territory bound to him — a god who rose with his city. Marduk's land is Babylon, and his cult-centre is the great temple Esagila (“house whose top is high”) at the city's heart, beside the towering ziggurat Etemenanki (“house of the foundation of heaven and earth”). What makes Marduk the catalog's plainest test-case for the patron-god thesis is that his standing among the gods tracks his city's standing among the nations so exactly. Through the third millennium he was a minor local deity; as Babylon rose — first under Hammurabi in the eighteenth century BC, and again as the seat of a world-empire under Nebuchadnezzar II — Marduk rose with it, displacing the older Enlil of Nippur to become king of the pantheon, so that the Enūma Eliš reads in part as a charter for exactly that promotion. Here a patron-god's theological rank so closely follows his people's political fortunes that his central myth is the story of his elevation. And when Babylon fell, the Bible marks the god's fall in the same breath as the city's: “Babylon is taken, Bel is confounded, Merodach is broken in pieces” (Jeremiah 50:2).50

The legend cycle — the creation-epic, the New Year, and the tower. Marduk has the richest legend of any god in this catalog, preserved in Babylon's own creation-epic, the Enūma Eliš (“When on high”), seven tablets recovered from the library of Ashurbanipal at Nineveh. Harried by the primeval salt-water chaos-goddess Tiamat and her monster-brood, the younger gods can find no champion until Marduk agrees to fight — on the condition that they enthrone him supreme; they acclaim him, “Marduk is king.” He goes out armed with the winds and the storm-flood, snares Tiamat in a net, drives the winds into her, and (in Speiser's rendering) splits her carcass “like a shellfish into two parts,” making the heavens of one half and the earth of the other; he sets the constellations, fixes the calendar, forms humankind from the blood of the rebel Qingu to bear the gods' labour, and builds Babylon and Esagila as his seat, whereupon the assembled gods proclaim his fifty names. The epic was no mere text: it was recited at the Akitu, Babylon's New-Year festival in the spring, when Marduk's kingship — and the human king's, who was ritually stripped, struck on the cheek, and made to declare he had not sinned against the god — was renewed for the year, and the god's image processed in triumph, Nabu brought up from Borsippa to join his father. Towering over the whole was Etemenanki, the ziggurat of Marduk, which many identify with the Tower of Babel of Genesis 11 — the city and tower on the plain of Shinar “whose top may reach unto heaven,” where “the LORD did there confound the language of all the earth.” Herodotus, who saw the sanctuary, describes “the temple of Zeus Belus” with its eight stacked towers and, at the summit, a shrine holding a great couch and a golden table, where (the priests told him) the god came to lie.51

Then the deflation. Where Chemosh speaks in the first person on the Mesha Stele, Babylon's god is heard in Scripture only being humbled. Second Isaiah's taunt makes the pageant-gods baggage: “Bel boweth down, Nebo stoopeth… they are a burden to the weary beast,” and “they could not deliver the burden, but themselves are gone into captivity” (Isaiah 46:1–2) — gods who cannot carry, but must be carried. Jeremiah turns Bel's own devouring against him: “I will punish Bel in Babylon, and I will bring forth out of his mouth that which he hath swallowed up” (51:44), the god made to disgorge the nations he had eaten. And the Greek additions to Daniel supply the sharpest scene of all: in Bel and the Dragon, the Babylonians spend on their idol “every day twelve great measures of fine flour, and forty sheep, and six vessels of wine,” believing he eats it, until Daniel scatters ashes to reveal the footprints of the seventy priests who consume it by night — so “the king slew them, and delivered Bel into Daniel's power, who destroyed him and his temple” — and Daniel then bursts the living dragon they worship with lumps of “pitch, and fat, and hair,” saying, “Lo, these are the gods ye worship.”52

How Scripture and the Fathers place him. The Fathers read Bel through the same lens they turned on Chemosh and Dagon — a real idol-power, cast down — but with a learned twist, folding him into the classical pantheon. Jerome, on Isaiah 46:1–2, identifies Bel with Saturn: “Bel has fallen… whom the Greeks call Belus and the Latins call Saturn,” to whom the ancients “not only offered… human sacrifices of captive and lowly mortals, but also sacrificed their own children,” while Nabo, “which is interpreted as prophecy and divination, signifies that it has fallen silent throughout the whole world after the truth of the Gospel”; the whole burden of idolatry, he adds, “made their souls captive to the devil and demons.” Theodoret agrees on the identification — “As for Bel, some claim that he was Kronos” — and Eusebius reads the toppled idols as “the heavy and burdensome and diabolic load of deceitful idolatry.” On the Bel and the Dragon story itself Jerome is famously cool: noting with Eusebius and Apollinarius that “the stories of Susanna and of Bel and the Dragon are not contained in the Hebrew,” he had marked them in his own translation with a critical symbol as no part of the Hebrew canon — so the most vivid patristic scene of Bel's exposure comes with the Fathers' own caution that it is not Scripture proper. The placement is nonetheless of a piece with the rest of the catalog: a real and demonic power behind the idol, beaten and unmasked, not a neutral member of a heavenly council.53

The divine-council cross-identification (a synthesis). Marduk is the catalog's strongest illustration of the patron-god pattern the divine-council reading leans on, and its most direct tie to the Babel thread that underwrites the whole Deuteronomy 32 allotment. The reading folds Bel into the disinherited elohim of Deuteronomy 32:8–9: the nations were parcelled out to divine powers at Babel — the very plain of Shinar where Marduk's tower stood and the languages were confounded (Genesis 11) — so that Babylon's own high god is, on this synthesis, one of the apportioned patrons, and his rise-with-his-city is what a real cosmic geography would look like from the inside. That Etemenanki is plausibly the Tower of Babel draws the knot tight: the place of the disinheritance and the seat of this patron would be one and the same. But, as everywhere in the catalog, the join is the reader's synthesis. Scripture names Bel and Merodach and Nebo; Scripture describes an apportionment of the nations at Babel; nowhere does it say in one breath that Marduk is one of the sons of God set over Babylon. And here the deflationary voice is at its loudest: Bel is confounded, Merodach broken in pieces, the pageant-gods hauled off as dead weight, the idol exposed as a priests' fraud. Whether the being behind Esagila is a council-elohim, the Fathers' demon-behind-the-idol, or a personified imperial cult is the contested question weighed on the named national gods (assessment: plausible) — and Babylon, the archetype of the proud city under judgment, is a standing reminder that Scripture's interest in these gods is in their downfall, not their rank. Possibility is not proof.54

Ashur — the god of Assyria (deity, city, and nation in one name)

Ashur is the catalog's imperial god of the north, and its tightest knot of all: with Ashur the deity, the city, and the nation are a single word. Chemosh is the god of Moab and Marduk the god of Babylon, but Assyria simply is the land of Ashur — god, capital, people, and state share one name, an identity found nowhere else in the ancient Near East. And yet, like Qos of Edom, this most totalizing of national gods is never named as a god in the Old Testament: “Asshur” there is a man and a place (Genesis 10:11), and “the Assyrian” is the rod of Yahweh's anger (Isaiah 10:5). So Ashur joins Qos as a case where the one-god-per-people pattern holds precisely where Scripture declines to name the god — only here the silence sits beside the loudest imperial theology in the whole roster.

Names and epithets. The name is Aššur (Assur, Ashur), and it began not as a personified force of nature but as the deified city itself — unusual in Mesopotamia, where god and city normally bear different names, and so close a fusion that oaths could be sworn by “Assur” as though the city were a god. Because he started as a place, Ashur originally had no myth, no genealogy, and no consort — he stood alone. As the cult drew on southern Mesopotamian theology he was assimilated to Enlil (Akkadian Bel), chief god of Nippur, absorbing Enlil's wife Ninlil (as Mullišu) and sons Ninurta and Zababa; and under the Sargonid kings his name came to be written with the signs AN.ŠÁR, so identifying him with Anšar, the primordial ancestor of the gods in the Babylonian creation-epic (see below). His epithets are those of a supreme god — bêlu rabû (“great lord”), ab ilāni (“father of the gods”), šadû rabû (“great mountain”) — and his name is stamped on his kings: Ashur-uballit, Ashurnasirpal, Ashurbanipal, Esarhaddon (Aššur-aḥa-iddina). His emblem is the winged sun-disk, often enclosing a “feather-robed archer” drawing his bow — the sign of war and kingship — though scholars debate whether the disk is properly Ashur's or the sun-god Shamash's, and his emblems seem in any case borrowed from other chief deities.55

The people and territory bound to him. Ashur's land is Assyria, the upper Tigris, and his cult-centre is the temple Ešarra (“House of the Universe”) on its terrace above the river in the city of Assur. The bond of god to people is tighter here than anywhere in the catalog, because it is an identity: the god is the city is the nation. That fusion shaped a distinctive imperial ideology. Where Babylon's king called himself Marduk's son, the Assyrian king claimed no divine parentage but styled himself iššiʾak Aššur, “vice-regent” (or steward) of Ashur — the god was the true king of Assyria, the human ruler merely his earthly deputy, and the throne-names confess it: Aššur-nārāri (“Ashur is my help”), Aššur-rēš-iši (“Ashur has lifted my head”), Aššur-rāʾim-nišēšu (“Ashur loves his people”). Wars were fought at the god's command to enlarge his dominion; conquered peoples were not compelled to worship Ashur but were declared to have been abandoned by their own gods and brought under his yoke — the empire as the extension of one patron deity's rule.56

The legend cycle — a god with no myth, who took another god's. Ashur's most telling feature is a lack: alone among the great gods of the catalog he has almost no legend of his own — no birth, no deeds, no cycle — precisely because he began as a place and not a personified power. So when Assyria wanted an imperial theology to rival Babylon's, it did not compose Ashur a myth; it took Marduk's. After Sennacherib sacked Babylon in 689 BC — diverting the Arakhtu canal over its ruins and carrying off Marduk's statue — Assyrian scribes produced an Assyrian recension of the Enūma Eliš in which Ashur simply replaces Marduk as the champion who splits Tiamat and makes the world, with Assur (Baltil) replacing Babylon and Lahmu and Lahamu replacing Ea and Damkina. The hinge of the whole substitution was the spelling AN.ŠÁR: by writing Ashur's name with the signs of Anšar — Marduk's own great-grandfather in the epic's genealogy — the scribes made their national god older than, and so senior to, the god he displaced. Sennacherib moved Babylon's Akitu New-Year festival to Assur and built a festival-house where Ashur, not Marduk, does battle with the chaos-dragon. W. G. Lambert judged the reworking “completely superficial,” leaving the plot “in chaos” by handing Marduk's part to his great-grandfather without ironing out the confusion — but the crudeness is exactly the datum this catalog wants: here is a national god literally rewritten into the head of the pantheon, a patron deity promoted over the reigning king of the gods by the fiat of the empire that worshipped him.57

How Scripture places Assyria — and why its god goes unnamed. Where the Bible names and mocks Chemosh, Molech, Dagon, and Bel, it never once names Ashur as a god. In Scripture “Asshur” is the eponymous son of Shem and the land itself — “out of that land went forth Asshur, and builded Nineveh” (Genesis 10:11) — and Assyria's god is passed over in the same conspicuous silence that surrounds Edom's Qos. But Assyria itself towers over the prophets, and there the theology is inverted: Assyria is not a rival kingdom under a rival god but Yahweh's own instrument. “O Assyrian, the rod of mine anger, and the staff in their hand is mine indignation” (Isaiah 10:5) — Assyria is a tool in Yahweh's hand against a faithless Israel; and when it forgets this and boasts, Yahweh breaks it: “shall the axe boast itself against him that heweth therewith? or shall the saw magnify itself against him that shaketh it?” (10:15). The rod does not lift itself.58

The clash of theologies is staged most sharply at Jerusalem's walls, where Sennacherib's Rabshakeh frames the siege exactly as a contest of national gods: “Hath any of the gods of the nations delivered his land out of the hand of the king of Assyria?… Who are they among all the gods of these lands, that have delivered their land out of my hand, that the LORD should deliver Jerusalem out of my hand?” (Isaiah 36:18–20; 2 Kings 18:33–35). Ashur's imperial creed — that no god withstands him — is put in the mouth of his own herald, and answered not by a rival patron but by the God who wields Assyria as a rod: Hezekiah prays “that all the kingdoms of the earth may know that thou art the LORD God, even thou only” (2 Kings 19:19), and “the angel of the LORD went forth, and smote in the camp of the Assyrians a hundred and fourscore and five thousand” (Isaiah 37:36; 2 Kings 19:35). The Fathers followed the text: because Scripture never names Assyria's god, they did not gloss him as they gloss Bel or Dagon, but read “the Assyrian” as the rod of God's anger and the Rabshakeh's boast as blasphemy against the living God — Jerome so treats the passage throughout his commentary on Isaiah.59

The divine-council cross-identification (a synthesis). Ashur's payload for the “gods of the nations” reading is double. On the one hand he is the catalog's tightest god↔nation identity and, in the Sennacherib recension, its most vivid picture of patron gods contending for supremacy — a national god literally rewritten over the reigning king of the gods, which is just what a real cosmic geography of jostling territorial powers might look like from the inside. The modern reading folds Ashur into the disinherited elohim of Deuteronomy 32:8–9 — a real, lesser power set over Assyria at Babel — and the empire's own theology of Ashur's supremacy is the kind of thing that keeps the reading live. But two features cut the other way, and both are honest tests. First, like Qos, Ashur is never named as a god in Scripture, so his place on the roster is inferred from the nations' own records, not from the biblical text — which strengthens the worldview (the one-god-per-people pattern is plainly a feature of the ancient landscape, not merely Israel's polemic) while saying nothing about what Ashur is. Second, and unlike every other entry, Scripture's handling of Assyria is not idol-polemic at all but instrumentalization: Ashur's people are Yahweh's rod, and the god who claims supremacy is answered by the God who claims to wield him. Whether the power behind Assyria is a council-elohim, the Fathers' demon, or a personified imperial cult is the contested question weighed on the named national gods (assessment: plausible) — and the Sennacherib narrative is a standing reminder that, on Scripture's telling, the loudest imperial god is a tool in a greater hand. Possibility is not proof.60

Inshushinak — the god of Elam (the Lord of Susa, whom the Bible never names)

Inshushinak is the catalog's third case of a national god the Old Testament passes over in silence — the companion of Edom's Qos and Assyria's Ashur, and the sharpest of the three, because his very name is a city Scripture knows well. Elam appears in the Bible from Genesis to Daniel; its capital, Susa, is the Shushan of Nehemiah, Esther, and Daniel, the winter palace of the Persian kings. Yet the god whose name means that city — Inshushinak, “Lord of Susa” — is never named as a god in the Old Testament at all. As with Qos and Ashur, the one-god-per-people pattern holds precisely where the biblical writers decline to name the god: we know Elam's patron only from Elam's own bricks and tablets, not from the text that knew his city by heart.

Names and epithets. The name is Inšušinak (also written Šušinak, Šušun) — a Sumerian loan, nin-šušinak, worn down by the loss of its first syllable, and meaning transparently “Lord of Susa.” The epithet is unusually explicit: where Chemosh is the god of Moab by a separate name, Elam's patron simply carries his city in his title, as Ashur carries his. He was “the main deity of the local pantheon” of Susa and closely bound to kingship — the Sukkalmah rulers styled themselves the king “whose kingdom Inshushinak loves.” His two great offices were justice and the underworld: he was the divine judge invoked in oath-formulas (parties swore by touching the kitin, the “divinely-enforced legal protection”), and he “was believed to reside in” the land of the dead as its lord and judge — an aspect that, by the Neo-Elamite period, “apparently overshadowed all his other functions.” Mesopotamian scribes, reaching for an equivalent, folded him into Ninurta: the god-list An = Anum explains Inshushinak as “Ninurta of silence,” and Ashurbanipal, carrying off his statue, called him a “mysterious god who dwells in seclusion.”61

The people and territory bound to him. Inshushinak's land is Elam — the south-western Iranian lowland and the Zagros highlands behind it — and his city is Susa (Shushan) on the river Ulai, the lowland capital. He did not stand alone: the high gods of Elam were Humban and the “Great God” Napirisha, the mother-goddess Kiririsha, and Inshushinak, and from about the nineteenth century BC the sources group Inshushinak with Napirisha and Kiririsha as a triad (an older handbook grouping instead pairs him with Humban and Kiririsha; the regions of Elam varied in which head-god they set first). His primacy was oldest and firmest at Susa itself, where the earliest datable act of his cult is recorded: about 2100 BC the ruler Puzur-Inshushinak — whose own name means “Inshushinak protects” — “instated daily offerings to Inshushinak in Susa, which constitutes the oldest known reference to such a practice.”62

The biblical footprint — the city Scripture keeps, the god it drops. Elam is one of the Old Testament's steady neighbours. Its king Chedorlaomer heads the eastern coalition that Abraham routs (“Chedorlaomer king of Elam,” Genesis 14:1, 9); Isaiah summons it to war (“Go up, O Elam: besiege, O Media,” 21:2); Jeremiah devotes a whole oracle to it (“against Elam … I will bring again the captivity of Elam,” 49:34–39); and its capital becomes, under Persia, one of the most famous addresses in the book: Daniel sees his vision “at Shushan in the palace, which is in the province of Elam … by the river of Ulai” (Daniel 8:2), Nehemiah serves the king “in Shushan the palace” (Nehemiah 1:1), and the whole drama of Esther plays out there (“Shushan the palace,” Esther 1:2). And in all of it — the wars, the oracle, the palace — the god whose name is that palace-city is never once named. The silence is the same conspicuous omission that surrounds Qos of Edom and Ashur of Assyria: the Bible knows Susa intimately and never mentions its Lord.63

The legend cycle — the ziggurat and the judgment of the dead. Inshushinak left no Mesha Stele narrating a war, but he left something no other god in the catalog can match: a mountain of his own. In the thirteenth century BC the Elamite king Untash-Napirisha raised for him the great ziggurat of Choga Zanbil — originally Dur-Untash (“town of Untash”), or Al-Untash-Napirisha — a walled sacred city some 30 km south-east of Susa whose stepped tower is “the best preserved example of the stepped pyramidal monument” anywhere and, in 1979, the first Iranian site inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List. Its dedicatory bricks put the god's ownership in the first person: “I gave it to Inšušinak of the Siyan-kuk, and I a temple tower erected.” His other “cycle” is not a myth but a courtroom in the underworld: the Akkadian Susa funerary texts (c. 1600–1400 BC) trace the dead soul's journey down, escorted by Inshushinak's two assistants Lagamal and Ishmekarab, to present itself before the Anunnaki and “receive Inshushinak's judgment.” He is, in the end, the god who weighs the dead — the judge of Susa in this world and the next.64

Why Scripture (and so the Fathers) pass him over. As with Qos, the entry cannot close on a line of patristic glossing, because there is no biblical naming to gloss: the Fathers who read Chemosh, Molech, and Dagon so fiercely as demons say nothing of Elam's god, following the text's own silence. Part of the reason is chronology. By the time Susa entered Israel's story as a household name — the Persian capital of Daniel, Nehemiah, and Esther — the old Elamite state was centuries dead, its native cult long since overlaid by Assyrian, Babylonian, and then Persian rule; the “Shushan” the exiles knew was an imperial city, not the seat of a living rival god. Inshushinak's heyday lay a thousand years before, in the world of Chedorlaomer and Untash-Napirisha, from which the Bible preserves only the king's name and the coalition's defeat. So Elam joins Edom and Assyria as a national god the biblical writers pass over — here not out of polemical suppression so much as distance in time, the patron of Susa having faded from the region before Scripture's Susa was ever built.65

The divine-council cross-identification (a synthesis). Inshushinak's payload for the “gods of the nations” reading is, like Qos's and Ashur's, the silence itself. The modern reading folds each national patron into the disinherited elohim of Deuteronomy 32:8–9 — a real, lesser power set over its people at Babel — and the standing objection is that the biblical roster of named gods might be a mere by-product of Israel's polemic, a list of whom the writers chose to mock. Elam answers that objection a third time from the far side: here is a national god the writers did not name — whose very name is a city they knew by heart — recovered only from Elam's own inscriptions. The one-god-per-people pattern plainly held for Susa too, independent of the Bible's notice, which strengthens the worldview the reading describes. It does not, however, settle what Inshushinak is: whether the power behind the Lord of Susa is a council-elohim, a demon, or a personified city-cult and judge of the dead is the same contested question weighed on the named national gods (assessment: plausible) — possibility is not proof.66

Amun-Ra — the god of Egypt (Thebes, and the gods of the nomes)

Egypt is the catalog's richest entry and its hardest one, because Egypt does not answer the pattern with a single patron at all. Moab has Chemosh, Ammon Milcom, Philistia Dagon — one god to one people. Egypt has a pantheon spread across a whole internal map: an imperial high god, Amun-Ra of Thebes, set over the land as “king of the gods,” and beneath him a resident patron for every province and every great city — Ptah at Memphis, Ra at Heliopolis, Thoth at Hermopolis, and a score more, Egypt's own cosmic geography drawn in temple-stone. So this entry carries the imperial god and the district-gods together, and it is the one place the one-god-per-people register plainly bends: here it is many gods per one people. That very abundance is why the Bible frames the Exodus not as a duel with a single idol but as judgment “against all the gods of Egypt” (Exodus 12:12).

Names and epithets. The imperial god is Jmn — Amun (also Amon, Amen), “the Hidden One,” the concealed creative power — whose cult-city was Thebes and whose vast temple stood at Karnak (Egyptian Ipet-isut, “the most select of places”). From the Middle Kingdom his priests fused him with the ancient sun-god Ra of Heliopolis to make Amun-Ra, and by the New Kingdom empire he was nsw nṯrw, “king of the gods,” the state god of Egypt at its imperial height, “lord of the thrones of the Two Lands.” The Greeks read him without hesitation as Zeus — his desert oracle at Siwa made “Zeus-Ammon” a power Greeks and Romans consulted (Alexander among them) — and Herodotus already gives the equation in Book 2. Scripture keeps his name once, bound to his city: Jeremiah's oracle against “Amon of No” (46:25, following the Hebrew) — Amon the god, No his Thebes.67

The internal map — a god for every nome and city. Beneath the imperial god runs a whole territorial pantheon, Egypt's most distinctive contribution to the catalog. The land was administered as some forty-two nomes (provinces), each with its own emblem-standard and resident deity, and every major city carried its poliad patron: Ptah the craftsman-god at Memphis (biblical Noph/Moph); Ra/Atum the sun-god at Heliopolis (biblical On — whose priest Poti-pherah gave Joseph his wife, Genesis 41:45 — also called Aven and Beth-shemesh, “house of the sun”); Thoth the god of writing at Hermopolis; Neith at Sais; the ram-headed Khnum at Elephantine; Set at Ombos; Horus at Edfu; the crocodile-god Sobek in the Faiyum; and Bastet at Bubastis (biblical Pi-beseth). The Greeks translated the whole roster by interpretatio graeca — Herodotus's Book 2 gives Amun as Zeus, Ptah as Hephaestus, Neith as Athena, Thoth as Hermes, Horus as Apollo, Bastet as Artemis, Set as Typhon — the same god-swapping the catalog meets with Melqart-Heracles and Astarte-Aphrodite. Whether this remarkably systematic patron-per-district habit actually corroborates the Deuteronomy 32 apportionment is a separate question, weighed (and found weak) on Egypt's nome- and city-gods; here the catalog simply records the map.68

The people and territory bound to him. Amun's own land is Thebes (Egyptian Waset, Greek Diospolis Magna, “great city of Zeus”), Upper Egypt on the Nile — the No (or No-Amon) of the prophets, whose colossal temple-city at Karnak and Luxor remains the largest religious complex of the ancient world. The bond of god to polity is as tight here as anywhere in the catalog: Amun's rise to supremacy tracks Thebes's own political rise through the Middle and New Kingdoms, and the Theban priesthood grew so rich that in the Third Intermediate Period the High Priests of Amun ruled Upper Egypt as a state within the state, the god's wealth become the god's kingdom. When Nahum wants an image of an unconquerable city brought low, he reaches for Amun's: “Art thou better than populous No, that was situate among the rivers … whose rampart was the sea?” (3:8) — Thebes sacked by Ashurbanipal in 663 BC, the fall of the god's own city.69

The legend cycle — rival creations, city by city. Egypt's territorial theology produced not one cosmogony but several, each centred on a city and exalting its patron to the role of creator. At Heliopolis the sun-god Atum-Ra generates himself out of the primeval waters and brings forth the Ennead, the Nine gods. At Memphis the Memphite Theology — preserved on the Shabaka Stone — has Ptah create all things by his heart (thought) and his tongue (word), the ancient world's nearest analogue to creation by the spoken word. At Hermopolis the Ogdoad, eight primeval deities, arise under Thoth. Threaded through the whole is the great Osiris myth: the good king Osiris murdered and dismembered by his brother Set, his body sought and reassembled by his wife Isis, and his death avenged by their son Horus, who takes the throne — Egypt's central story of kingship, death, and rising, told fullest by Plutarch. Each local system was, in effect, a bid to seat one city's god at the head of the divine world — the patron writ cosmic.70

How Scripture places them — the cities and their idols. Where the Bible names Chemosh “the god of Moab” outright, Egypt's gods reach Scripture through their cities and their idols. Jeremiah's oracle strikes “the multitude of No, and Pharaoh, and Egypt, with their gods, and their kings” (46:25). Ezekiel's oracle against Egypt marches city by city through the very seats of the nome-gods: “I will also destroy the idols … out of Noph” (Memphis, Ptah's city; 30:13), “execute judgments in No” (Thebes, Amun's; 30:14), “pour my fury upon Sin” (Pelusium; 30:15), and “the young men of Aven [On, Ra's Heliopolis] and of Pi-beseth [Bubastis, Bastet's city] shall fall by the sword” (30:17). Jeremiah promises the same fire elsewhere: Nebuchadnezzar “shall kindle a fire in the houses of the gods of Egypt … he shall break also the images of Beth-shemesh” — “house of the sun,” Heliopolis again — “that is in the land of Egypt” (43:12–13). And Isaiah's “burden of Egypt” opens on the LORD riding a swift cloud into Egypt, so that “the idols of Egypt shall be moved at his presence” (19:1). The gods enter the biblical text as idols to be shaken and burned.71

The plagues as a contest against the gods — and how the Fathers place them. The Bible's own frame for the Exodus makes it a reckoning with Egypt's whole pantheon. At the last plague Yahweh declares: “against all the gods of Egypt I will execute judgment: I am the LORD” (Exodus 12:12), and Numbers looks back on it, “upon their gods also the LORD executed judgments” (33:4). A long tradition, ancient and modern, reads the individual plagues as strokes against individual deities — the Nile turned to blood against Hapi and the ram-god Khnum, the frogs against the frog-goddess Heqet, the darkness against Ra, the death of the firstborn against Pharaoh the living Horus — though the pairings are the reader's construction; the text speaks of “all the gods” together, not a checklist. The Fathers took Egypt's idols as real but doomed powers. Jerome, on Isaiah 19:1, reads that “not … the Angels, but the Lord Himself” comes on a swift cloud to “make the idols of Egypt tremble,” and folds in Jeremiah's fire in “the temples of the gods of Egypt.” Augustine, turning the pagan Asclepius against itself in the City of God, presses that “the gods of Egypt were dead men” — deified mortals whose “most holy place of shrines and temples” the Hermetic writer himself foretells will one day be “full of sepulchres and dead men.” So the patristic placement matches the Old Testament's two-voiced handling: real powers stand behind the idols, and those powers are judged and thrown down — not neutral members of a heavenly council.72

The divine-council cross-identification (a synthesis). Egypt is at once the strongest and the most awkward case for the “gods of the nations” reading, and its honesty lies in holding both together. Strongest, because no nation tied gods to places more visibly — an imperial high god over the whole land and a resident patron for every nome and city — and because the Bible itself frames the Exodus as Yahweh's judgment “against all the gods of Egypt,” treating that pantheon as a real object of divine reckoning. Most awkward, because the shape does not sit neatly under Deuteronomy 32:8–9: Egypt is not one power set over one people but a whole pantheon over one people, its gods merge and absorb one another (Amun-Ra, Ptah-Sokar-Osiris) and its city-theologies compete to crown their own patron — the dynamics of syncretism and ambition, not of a sovereign parcelling out fixed portions. On that ground the nome-parallel is rated weak as corroboration of the apportionment (Egypt's nome- and city-gods), even as the prior question — whether a real power stands behind an idol like Amun's or Ptah's — is the same one weighed on the named national gods (assessment: plausible). Whether the being behind the Hidden One of Thebes is a council-elohim, the Fathers' beaten demon, or a personified imperial cult is the contested question the argument pages carry — and Egypt is the catalog's standing reminder that the pattern is a real ancient habit, not a tidy one. Possibility is not proof.73

El and Baal-Hadad — the gods of Canaan (Ugarit, the matrix of the pantheon)

Canaan is the catalog's source-entry, and its most consequential one. Chemosh, Milcom, Astarte, Melqart, Hadad, Dagon — nearly every patron already surveyed is a branch of a single Canaanite tree, and that tree is documented, root and trunk, in one place: the cuneiform library of Ugarit (Ras Shamra, on the north-Syrian coast), unearthed from 1929 and unread for three thousand years. There the whole pantheon stands in its own words — an aged high god, El, “father of the gods” and father of the seventy sons who are the divine family; and beneath him the vigorous storm-king Baal-Hadad, the active Baal (“Lord”) fought and served across the whole biblical world. So this entry carries two gods, the retired patriarch and the reigning storm-god, and it is different from the others in two ways. First, it is not one nation's patron but the matrix from which the neighbour-nations' patrons descend — Canaan was less a country than a family of city-states sharing this pantheon, and “Baal” is Israel's own besetting apostasy, not a foreign god across a border. Second, it is here that the divine-council reading meets its sharpest double edge: the very titles that make El look like a real high god over the nations — Elyon, “Most High,” and the rest — are titles the Old Testament also gives to Yahweh, so that the Canaanite matrix is at once the reading's strongest backdrop and the source of its hardest question (below).

Names and epithets — El, the aged head of the pantheon. The high god of Ugarit is ʾil (El) — the same word that in Hebrew is the generic ʾēl, “god,” and that stands inside a hundred Israelite names (Israel, Bethel, Daniel). At Ugarit it is also the proper name of a specific deity: a grey-bearded patriarch enthroned “at the source of the two rivers,” wise, remote, and kindly. His epithets are a gallery of age and fatherhood. He is “father of years” (ʾab šanima) — the Ancient One, an image the Bible echoes in the “Ancient of Days” and in ʾēl ʿôlām, “the Everlasting God.” He is “the Bull” (þr), the sign of his generative power — a title, tellingly, that the active Baal never bears (Baal is only ever “the bullcalf”). He is “father of the gods” (ʾab ilm) and “creator of creatures” (bny bnwt), and “the Kindly One, El the Compassionate” (lṭpn il d pʾid). His consort is the mother-goddess Athirat (ʾaṯrt) — the biblical Asherah — “Lady Athirat of the Sea,” “progenitress of the gods,” whose children number the seventy. And crucially for this catalog, El carries the epithet ʿlyn, “Most High” — the very word (Elyon) Deuteronomy 32:8 uses of the God who apportions the nations, a coincidence of title that is the hinge of the whole divine-council reading.74

Names and epithets — Baal-Hadad, the active storm-king. Where El has withdrawn into venerable age, the god who acts in the Ugaritic myth is Baal. Baʿl (bʿl) is not properly a name at all but a title — “Lord,” “Owner,” “Master” — borne pre-eminently by the storm-god Hadad (hd / hdd), “the thunderer,” so that the “Baal” whom Israel chased after is very often Hadad himself (his national cult as Aram's patron, and the Adad/Rimmon by-names, are the Aram-Damascus entry above; here he is the storm-god of the Canaanite myth). His signature epithet is “Rider on the Clouds” (rkb ʿrpt) — a storm-god riding the thunderhead — and he is “Prince, Lord of the Earth” (zbl bʿl arṣ), the zbl (“Prince”) that survives, twisted, in the biblical Baal-Zebul/Beelzebul. His weapons are the thunder and the forked lightning; his beast is the bull; his home is the northern mountain Zaphon; and in the myth he is “son of Dagan” (the trans-regional grain-god of the Dagon entry above). That “Rider on the Clouds” is not an idle detail: the Old Testament deliberately claims the same title for Yahweh — “extol him that rideth upon the heavens” (Psalm 68:4), “the LORD rideth upon a swift cloud” (Isaiah 19:1) — taking the storm-god's banner and planting it on Israel's God, a polemic that only works because Baal owned the phrase first.75

The local-Baal roster — a Lord for every place. Because baʿl means “lord/owner,” it attached to place after place, each “Baal of X” a locally-owned manifestation of the storm-god — so Scripture usually speaks of the Baalim, the Baals, in the plural. Three are named for their towns. Baal-Peor — the Baal of Mount Peor in Moab, to whom “Israel joined himself” in the apostasy of the Midianite women (Numbers 25:3; Hosea 9:10; Psalm 106:28), a cult the tradition read as grossly sexual. Baal-Zebub the god of Ekron — “Lord of the Flies,” the Philistine oracle Ahaziah consulted on his sickbed (2 Kings 1:2–3): almost certainly a Hebrew slur on the title Baal-Zebul, “Baal the Prince,” recoined as “lord of dung-flies” — and under the form Beelzebul/Beelzebub it becomes, in the Gospels, a name for “the prince of the devils” (Matthew 12:24). And Baal-Berith, “Lord of the Covenant,” the civic god of Shechem from whose temple Abimelech drew his war-chest (Judges 8:33; 9:4). One deity, then, splintered across the map into a roster of place-bound lords.76

The people and territory bound to them. Canaan's peculiarity in this catalog is that it has no single national border: the land from Ugarit down through Phoenicia and into the hill-country was a patchwork of city-states sharing one pantheon, and Israel itself grew up inside that world, speaking a Canaanite dialect and tempted, generation on generation, by Canaanite gods. So the bond here is not god-to-foreign-nation but god-to-Israel's-own-apostasy: the recurring formula of Judges and Samuel is that Israel “served Baal and Ashtaroth” (Judges 2:13; 10:6; 1 Samuel 7:3–4), the storm-god and his goddess together. The struggle runs through the whole monarchy — Jezebel's imported Baal-cult (1 Kings 16–18), Jehu's purge of the Baal-temple (2 Kings 10), Josiah's reforms — and the prophets make the covenant a marriage in which Baal is the rival: Hosea looks to the day Israel will call Yahweh “Ishi” (“my husband”) and “shalt call me no more Baali” (“my Baal,” Hosea 2:16), the very word for “lord” too dangerous to keep. Ugarit itself supplies the territory of the myth: El at the rivers' source, Baal on Mount Zaphon — a sacred geography a few centuries older than the Song of Moses.77

The legend cycle — the Baal Cycle of Ras Shamra. Alone among the catalog's gods, the Canaanite storm-god has a full epic in his own tongue. The Baal Cycle (KTU 1.1–1.6, copied in the thirteenth century BC by the scribe Ilimilku) runs across six tablets in three movements. First, Baal versus Yam: the sea-god Yam (“Sea,” also Nahar, “River”), the personified chaos-water, demands sovereignty and Baal as his slave; armed with two clubs forged by the craftsman-god Kothar-wa-Hasis, Baal strikes Yam down and is proclaimed king — the widespread chaoskampf of a storm-god defeating the Sea, of which the Bible's Yahweh-against-Leviathan/Rahab-and-the-sea is the Israelite counterpart. Second, the palace: the new king has no house, and, with Anat and Athirat interceding before El, Kothar-wa-Hasis builds him a cedar-and-lapis palace on Mount Zaphon in seven days, its window a slit through which Baal thunders. Third, Baal versus Mot: Baal is summoned down to Mot (“Death”), swallowed into the underworld, and dies — the fields wither; his sister-consort Anat hunts down Mot, splits, winnows, and grinds him like grain; the sun-goddess Shapshu searches, Baal revives, and the failed pretender Athtar vacates the throne he could not fill — a dying-and-rising storm-god whose return is the return of the rains. The whole cycle is the theology of a fertility-and-weather god: a divine kingship that must be won, lost to death, and won again, season upon season — and it is against exactly this that the biblical writers set a God whose sovereignty is never in doubt and who neither slumbers nor dies.78

The god in Scripture — Baal-Peor and the contest at Carmel. Baal's biblical scenes are scenes of defeat, as Dagon's were. At Baal-Peor Israel “joined himself” to the Moabite Baal and “the anger of the LORD was kindled” in a plague (Numbers 25:3) — the apostasy Hosea remembers as the hour Israel “separated themselves unto that shame” (9:10). The set-piece is Mount Carmel (1 Kings 18): Elijah sets the LORD against “Baal” before all Israel — “if the LORD be God, follow him: but if Baal, then follow him” (18:21) — and when the storm-god's four hundred and fifty prophets cry from morning to noon and cut themselves and get no fire, Elijah mocks the silence: “Cry aloud: for he is a god; either he is talking, or he is pursuing, or he is in a journey, or peradventure he sleepeth, and must be awaked” (18:27). Then the LORD's fire falls and consumes the drenched sacrifice, and the “Rider on the Clouds” who cannot make it rain is answered by the God who ends the drought that same day. The scene is Scripture's clearest verdict on Baal: a storm-god who fails at storms.79

How Scripture and the Fathers place him. The Fathers read Baal as a real but demonic power — the standard patristic placement of this catalog. Cyprian, cataloguing Israel's idolatry, chains the “served Baal” texts of Judges to the flat verdict of the Psalter: “All the gods of the nations are demons, but the Lord made the heavens” (Psalm 96:5, Septuagint). The name Beelzebub drew the sharpest comment, because the Gospels had already made it a name for Satan's chief: Thomas Aquinas, on Matthew 12:24, explains that “Beelzebub is the god of E[k]ron … called the lord of flies on account of the foul rite of the blood sacrificed, by reason of which many flies gathered,” so that “they believed that this demon was the prince of demons” — and Jerome's name-handbook fixes the same etymology, “Beelzebub: Having flies … For the fly is called Beelzebub.” On Baal-Peor Jerome reads the Moabite Baal as an obscene fertility idol “which we can call Priapus” (the same gloss the catalog's Chemosh entry weighs, since the Fathers identified Chemosh with Baal-Peor); on Jeremiah he points to “the temple of Baal, whom you have worshipped instead of God” in the valley of Hinnom; and Ambrose makes Carmel a proof that the LORD alone commands fire, the prophets of Baal crying in vain until “the fire fell from the Lord from heaven and consumed the burnt offering.” So the patristic placement matches the Old Testament's two-voiced handling: a real power stands behind the idol, and that power is a beaten demon — not a neutral member of a heavenly council.80

The El↔Yahweh titles question. This is the entry's hardest and most honest paragraph, and the reason Canaan is the catalog's double-edged case. The Old Testament gives the God of Israel a series of names built on El: El Elyon, “God Most High,” the God of Melchizedek whom Abram identifies with the LORD (Genesis 14:18–22); El Shaddai, “God Almighty”; El Olam, “the Everlasting God” — and it depicts Yahweh doing what El does (presiding over a divine council, Psalm 82) and wearing what Baal wears (“Rider on the Clouds”). A school of critical scholarship — Frank Moore Cross, and Mark S. Smith above all — reads this as a memory of religious history: that Israel's God absorbed the older Canaanite El (the two coalescing so completely that no biblical polemic is ever aimed at El, only at Baal), and that Deuteronomy 32:8–9, in its older text, even preserves a stage at which “the Most High” (El/Elyon) apportions the nations among his sons and Yahweh receives Israel as one such portion. Against that evolutionary reading, Michael Heiser and others argue that Elyon in Deuteronomy 32:8 simply is Yahweh — El and Elyon being titles Israel's God rightly bears — so that the verse shows Yahweh as the council's head, not a junior member. The debate is genuinely open, and the wiki weighs its two load-bearing pieces separately: the Deuteronomy 32:8 “sons of God” variant (assessment: sound) and the seventy sons of El (assessment: plausible). What the catalog records here is only the tension itself: the Fathers, reading Genesis 14 purely as Melchizedek and the Eucharist, saw no Canaanite deity behind “God Most High” at all — for them El Elyon was always simply God — while the comparative evidence makes the overlap of titles real. Which way it cuts is the contested question; the overlap is a fact, and it is the strongest single reason to hold the divine-council reading with the care it asks for.81

The divine-council cross-identification (a synthesis). Canaan is where the “gods of the nations” reading is at once best supplied and most exposed. Best supplied, because the Ugaritic pantheon hands the reading its tightest single backdrop: a high god titled Elyon, “Most High,” presiding over a full divine family of seventy sons — exactly the West-Semitic conception of a sovereign apportioning the peoples among his divine children that Deuteronomy 32:8–9 speaks in, and the reason that verse reads as inherited idiom rather than late invention. That backdrop is weighed, and rated plausible, on the seventy sons of El, and the catalog does not re-argue it here. Most exposed, because the same Canaanite matrix supplies the reading's two sharpest cautions. First, the El↔Yahweh overlap cuts both ways: the titles that make El look like a real Most High over the nations are titles Israel's own God bears, so the parallel that grounds the reading also feeds the rival reconstruction (Yahweh once a son of El) the reading must resist — possibility is not proof, and here even the direction of the inference is contested. Second, Baal is the catalog's plainest case of the two-voiced canon: a genuine cosmic power in the Ugaritic epic and a real snare in Israel's story, yet a god who cannot light his own altar at Carmel — Scripture's interest, as with Dagon, is in his defeat, not his rank. Whether the being behind the Rider on the Clouds is a council-elohim, the Fathers' beaten demon, or a personified storm is the contested question weighed on the named national gods (assessment: plausible) — and Canaan is the standing reminder that the pattern the reading traces is a real, documented, ancient one, told here in the gods' own words.82

Hubal — the god of Mecca (chief idol of the Kaʻbah, and the Allah question)

Hubal is the catalog's one entry from after the age of the Fathers, and its clearest test of whether the “gods of the nations” pattern reaches beyond the biblical world. He is the chief idol of pre-Islamic Mecca — the greatest of the images the Quraysh kept in and around the Kaʻbah, the sanctuary that a famous tradition says housed 360 idols — and the patron before whom the tribe cast the divining arrows. Two things set this entry apart from the rest. First, no Father ever glossed Hubal: the cult flourished into the seventh century AD and was thrown down not by the Church but by Islam, so the “how the sources place him” work here is done by the Qurʾan and the Prophet's biographers, not by Jerome or Bede. Second, Hubal carries this catalog's sharpest form of the title-overlap problem that Canaan raised with El: because the Kaʻbah was also the house of Allah, the “high god” of the Meccan cult, the relationship between the idol in the shrine and the God of the shrine is contested ground — the analogue, in Arabia, of the El↔Yahweh question above.

Names and epithets. The name is Hubal (هُبَل), and its origin is debated along the same seam as the deity itself. A long-standing proposal reads it as the Aramaic hū bel, “he is Baal” — tying the Meccan idol to the West-Semitic storm-lord of the Canaan entry above — while others derive it from Arabic roots for “vapour” or a slender build. His character is contested too: the older claim that Hubal was a moon-god (Hugo Winckler) is now generally rejected as speculation, and the surviving descriptions read him rather as a warrior- and rain-god (so David Leeming and Mircea Eliade) — the martial cast fitting the war-cry at Uhud (below) and the arrow-oracle fitting a god consulted for judgment. Unlike Chemosh or Milcom he has left no theophoric royal names on a monument; what fixes him is the one great image in the Kaʻbah and the rites performed before it.83

An imported god — the northern origin. The Arab tradition itself remembered Hubal as a foreign import, not a native Meccan deity. The idol-cult of the Kaʻbah is traced to ʻAmr ibn Luḥayy, the Khuzaʻite chief the sources blame for corrupting the pure monotheism of Abraham and Ishmael: falling sick, he was sent to a hot spring in al-Balqāʾ in Syria, found its people worshipping idols — “To them we pray for rain, and from them we seek victory over the enemy” — and, the account runs, “asked them to give him some of those idols, and they did. He took them back with him to Mecca and erected them around the Kaʻbah.” Al-Azraqī's history of Mecca specifies Hubal's provenance as the land of Hit in Mesopotamia, and modern historians (Philip Hitti) place the idol's homeland “in the north of Arabia, possibly from Moab or Mesopotamia”; Hubal even surfaces once in a Nabataean inscription, beside Dushara and Manawatu. That an outside god was carried in and set up as the sanctuary's chief image is striking for the reading this catalog serves: the pattern of “a real power behind this place and people” is here a thing consciously installed, and remembered as such.84

The people and territory bound to him. Hubal's people are the Quraysh, the tribe that held Mecca and its shrine, and through them the wider pilgrim-Arabia that converged on the Kaʻbah. Ibn al-Kalbī's Book of Idols makes the bond plain: the Quraysh “had several idols in and around the Kaʻbah. The greatest of these was Hubal… It stood inside the Kaʻbah,” and it was “the first to set it up… Khuzaymah ibn-Mudrikah,” whence “it used to be called Khuzaymah's Hubal.” So where Chemosh sits over Moab and Milcom over Ammon, Hubal sits at the centre of the Arabian cult-place itself — not a god of open territory so much as the presiding image of the one sanctuary the tribes shared, the seat from which the divining arrows spoke.85

The legend cycle — the image and the arrow-oracle. Hubal has no epic in his own voice; his “cycle” is a cult and its famous statue. Ibn al-Kalbī describes the image: Hubal was “of red agate, in the form of a man with the right hand broken off. It came into the possession of the Quraysh in this condition, and they, therefore, made for it a hand of gold” — the broken idol mended with a golden prosthesis (later writers call the stone carnelian, a near-kin of agate). Before it stood the oracle: “In front of it were seven divination arrows… On one… was written ‘pure’ (sarīḥ), and on another ‘consociated alien’ (mulṣaq). Whenever the lineage of a new-born was doubted, they would offer a sacrifice to it and then shuffle the arrows and throw them”; a third arrow served “divination concerning the dead,” a fourth “concerning marriage,” the remaining three left unexplained. The most consequential throw in the tradition is a family one: it was “before Hubal that ʻAbd-al-Muṭ̣alib shuffled the divination arrows in order to find out which of his ten children he should sacrifice in fulfilment of a vow he had sworn, and the arrows pointed to his son ʻAbdullah, the father of the Prophet” — the ransomed child who would become Muhammad's father. So the god who would be broken by Islam stands, in its own foundation-story, over the cradle of the Prophet.86

Hubal in the Prophet's story — the cry at Uhud and the cleansing of the Kaʻbah. Hubal enters the earliest Muslim narrative twice, and both scenes are of a god defeated — the same shape Dagon's and Baal's biblical scenes take. After the Muslims' reverse at Uhud (625), the Quraysh leader Abu Sufyan exulted from the field; the exchange is preserved in the tradition: “Abu Sufyan said, ‘Superior may be Hubal!’ ” (i.e. “Be exalted, Hubal!”), “On that the Prophet said ‘Reply to him.’ They asked, ‘What may we say?’ He said, ‘Say: Allah is More Elevated and More Majestic!’ Abu Sufyan said, ‘We have Al-ʻUzza, whereas you have no ʻUzza!’ ” — and the Prophet answered, “Say: Allah is our Helper and you have no helper.” The cult ended five years later at the conquest of Mecca (630): “The Prophet entered Mecca and there were 360 idols around the Kaʻbah. He started stabbing them with a stick… saying, ‘Truth has come and falsehood has vanished…’” (Qurʾan 17:81), and Hubal was broken with the rest and the shrine rededicated to Allah alone. The great idol of Arabia falls as Dagon fell before the ark — the god who could not keep his own right hand cannot keep his house.87

How the sources place him — idol, jinn, or high god. With no Father to gloss him, the placement is the Qurʾan's and the tradition's, and it runs two-voiced, exactly as the biblical handling of Chemosh and Dagon does. The dominant voice is deflationary: the gods of the Meccan cult are nothing — “names which ye have named, ye and your fathers, for which Allah hath revealed no warrant” (Qurʾan 53:23, of the goddesses al-Lāt, al-ʻUzzā, and Manāt), idols that “created nothing, but are themselves created” and cannot so much as make a fly (16:20; 22:73) — and Hubal's smashed statue is that polemic made visible. But a second voice grants the idols a real spiritual tenant: the Qurʾan says the associators in truth “invoke… Satan, a persistent rebel” (4:117) and that many “worshipped the jinn” (34:41), and Ibn al-Kalbī's own account of the cult's origin has ʻAmr ibn Luḥayy directed to the buried idols by “an oracle of the jinn.” So the Islamic placement lands where the patristic one did on the biblical gods: a beaten idol in the foreground, and behind it a rebel spirit — not a neutral member of a heavenly council.88

The Hubal↔Allah question. This is the entry's hardest paragraph, and its parallel to the El↔Yahweh problem of the Canaan entry. Because the Kaʻbah was both Hubal's house and the house of Allah — already, before Islam, the acknowledged “high god” of the Meccans, creator and Lord of the Kaʻbah — the two stand disconcertingly close, and scholars have read the closeness in opposite directions. Julius Wellhausen took Hubal to be an old name for Allah himself, the shrine's own god under an idol's form. Others read the reverse: Hubal an imported intermediary set up alongside a supreme Allah, so that a Meccan might pray to Allah the Most High while standing beside Hubal's image — the “imperfect monotheism” that G. R. Hawting argues the Qurʾan's opponents in fact held (fellow theists who acknowledged Allah's supremacy but fell short in practice), against the later tradition's picture of crude polytheism. Patricia Crone, differently again, presses toward a genuinely pagan Allah of Mecca worshipped with goddess-consorts. The debate is unsettled, and its stakes are the same as Canaan's: whether the shrine's high god absorbed a national idol, or presided over it, or simply was it, cannot be read off the sources cleanly — and each answer reshapes what the “gods of the nations” pattern is doing here.89

The divine-council cross-identification (a synthesis). Hubal is a Tier-B case: an Arabian tribal patron far from the Deuteronomy 32 horizon, reached not by any text that names him among the apportioned sons of God but by the bare recurrence of the shape — one presiding power bound to a people and its holy place. What that recurrence buys is real but limited. On the one hand it strengthens the worldview the reading describes: here, a full millennium past the Fathers and outside the biblical orbit, is the same one-power-per-sanctuary conception, and — more pointedly — a god the tradition itself frames as an imported spiritual tenant later exposed as a rebel jinn, the very grammar the “gods of the nations” reading uses. On the other hand the case cannot carry more than that: no source folds Hubal into the disinherited elohim of Deuteronomy 32 (that join is wholly the reader's synthesis), the Hubal↔Allah tangle warns against reading a national idol and a high god as tidily distinct powers, and possibility is not proof — a broken statue mended with a golden hand attests a belief, not the being behind it. Whether the tenant of the Kaʻbah was a council-elohim, the tradition's rebel jinn, or a personified tribal cult is the same contested question weighed on the named national gods (assessment: plausible) and across the wider “gods of the nations” group — here carried one step past the biblical world, and rated no higher for the distance.90

Al-Lāt, al-ʻUzzā, and Manāt — the goddesses of the Arabian tribes (“the daughters of Allah”)

These three are the catalog's set-piece of the pattern in its purest form: not one deity over one people, but a textbook spread of one-goddess-per-tribe across pre-Islamic Arabia — al-Lāt at Ṭāʾif among the Thaqīf, al-ʻUzzā at Nakhla among the Quraysh and Kināna, and Manāt on the coast road among the Aws and Khazraj of Yathrib (Medina) and the Hudhayl. Like Hubal they are post-patristic — no Father glossed them, and the cult was thrown down by Islam, not the Church, so the placement-work here belongs to the Qurʾan and the Prophet's biographers. But they add two things Hubal does not. They are the catalog's first cluster of goddesses, and, above all, they are the one set the sources themselves gather under a single divine-family title: “the daughters of Allah” (banāt Allāh) — three lesser powers said to intercede before the high god, which is as close as the Arabian record comes to speaking, in its own voice, of a council of subordinate deities under one Most High.

Names and epithets. Al-Lāt (al-Lāt, “the goddess,” the feminine of Allāh/al-ilāh) was called at Ṭāʾif ar-Rabba, “the Lady”; she is the oldest-attested of the three in the wider record, for Herodotus already names her: the Arabians, he writes, “have but these two gods, to wit, Bacchus and Urania… Bacchus they call in their language Orotalt, and Urania, Alilat” (Histories 3.8) — “Alilat” being al-Lāt, whom he equates with the Heavenly Aphrodite. Al-ʻUzzā is “the Mightiest” (the superlative feminine of ʻazīz), a warrior- and morning-star goddess whom the Nabataeans assimilated to Venus/Aphrodite. Manāt — whose name is built on manā, “to apportion, to allot” (kin to maniyya, “death, fate”) — is the goddess of fate and destiny, the doom-dealer who allots each life its span, and by the tradition's own reckoning the oldest of the three in cult. That an allotment-goddess should head a catalog built around Deuteronomy 32's allotment of the nations is a coincidence of vocabulary, not a proof-text — but a suggestive one.91

The people and territory bound to each — one goddess per tribe. The three map onto Arabia as neatly as Chemosh onto Moab. Al-Lāt belonged to Ṭāʾif and the Thaqīf, whose banū ʻAttāb ibn Mālik kept her shrine; al-ʻUzzā, “the greatest idol among the Quraysh,” stood in the valley of Nakhla east of Mecca and was the special goddess of the Quraysh and their kin the Kināna; Manāt was “erected on the seashore in the vicinity of al-Mushallal in Qudayd, between Medina and Mecca,” on the pilgrim road, and was the goddess of the Aws and Khazraj of Yathrib and of the Hudhayl and Khuzāʻa. Each tribe named its children for its own: Zayd-al-Lāt and Taym-al-Lāt, ʻAbd-al-ʻUzzā, ʻAbd-Manāt — the theophoric fossils that show how deep the bond of goddess to people ran. The result is the “gods of the nations” structure in miniature: a single sacred landscape parcelled out, one presiding power to each people, exactly the shape the divine-council reading takes for its footprint.92

The cult — a stone, three trees, and a shore. The fullest description is Ibn al-Kalbī's Book of Idols, and it fixes each goddess to a physical seat. Al-Lāt “stood in al-Ṭāʾif, and was more recent than Manāh. She was a cubic rock beside which a certain Jew used to prepare his barley porridge” — her custody “in the hands of the banū-ʻAttāb ibn-Mālik of the Thaqīf, who had built an edifice over her.” Al-ʻUzzā was worshipped in living wood: she “was a she-devil which used to frequent three trees in the valley of Nakhlah,” where the Quraysh “used to journey to her, offer gifts unto her, and seek her favours through sacrifice.” Manāt, the coastal shrine, was the most venerable: “the most ancient of all these idols was Manāh,” before whom the Medinan tribes would shave their heads to complete the pilgrimage. Three seats — a squared stone, a grove, a shore-stone — and three tribes, the whole cult of the “daughters” laid out across the Ḥijāz.93

“The daughters of Allah” — and the Qurʾan's rebuke. What makes this triad the catalog's most pointed entry is that the pagans themselves bound the three into a family under the high god. They “were also called the Daughters of Allāh… and were supposed to intercede before God,” and the pilgrims' chant named all three in one breath: “By Allat and al-ʻUzza, / And Manāh, the third idol besides. / Verily they are the most exalted females / Whose intercession is to be sought.” It is precisely this intercessor-daughters theology that the Qurʾan confronts in Sūrat an-Najm: “Have ye thought upon Al-Lāt and Al-ʻUzzā / And Manāt, the third, the other?” (53:19–20) — then the sarcasm, “Are yours the males and His the females? That indeed were an unfair division!” (53:21–22), and the verdict that governs this whole catalog's honesty note: “They are but names which ye have named, ye and your fathers, for which Allah hath revealed no warrant” (53:23). The Arabian record thus preserves, and Islam then denies, the one thing the divine-council reading most wants to find said out loud: subordinate powers ranked beneath a supreme God.94

The “Satanic Verses” episode. The three goddesses are also the pivot of the most contested incident in the Prophet's early Meccan career. Al-Ṭabarī's History, on the authority of Ibn Isḥāq, reports that as Muhammad recited Sūrat an-Najm and reached the naming of the three, Satan cast onto his tongue a concession to them: “These are the exalted gharānīq” (the exalted cranes), “whose intercession is to be hoped for” — briefly granting the daughters of Allah the very intercessory role the surrounding verses deny. The tradition has the concession swiftly abrogated, and reads Qurʾan 22:52 as its explanation: “never sent We a messenger or a prophet before thee but… Satan proposed… into that which he recited. But Allah abolisheth that which Satan proposeth.” The episode is transmitted by al-Ṭabarī, al-Wāqidī, and Ibn Saʻd but is absent from Ibn Hishām's recension of Ibn Isḥāq, and its historicity is fiercely disputed in both the Muslim and the modern-critical traditions — but disputed or not, it is a witness to how central these three intercessor-goddesses were to the Meccan cult the new monotheism had to displace.95

The destruction of the three shrines. The cult ended, as Dagon's did before the ark and Hubal's did in the Kaʻbah, in a scene of the goddess overthrown — here three scenes, in the year of Mecca's fall (630). The most vivid is al-ʻUzzā's: Muhammad sent Khālid ibn al-Walīd to Nakhla, where, Ibn al-Kalbī relates, he “found an Abyssinian woman with dishevelled hair… gnashing and grating her teeth,” the goddess made flesh; her custodian cried on her to “deal Khālid an unmistakable blow,” but Khālid answered, “O al-ʻUzzā! May thou be blasphemed, not exalted! Verily I see that God hath abased thee,” and “dealt her a blow which severed her head in twain, and lo, she crumbled into ashes.” He cut down the tree, killed the custodian, and the Prophet declared: “That was al-ʻUzzā. But she is no more. The Arabs shall have none after her.” Manāt's shrine on the coast at al-Mushallal was demolished in the same year (Ibn al-Kalbī credits ʻAlī; other early sources name Saʻd ibn Zayd al-Ashhalī — the point is disputed), and al-Lāt's at Ṭāʾif was burnt to the ground by al-Mughīra ibn Shuʻba once the Thaqīf submitted. Three goddesses, three ruined seats — the “daughters of Allah” unmade in a single campaigning season.96

The wider afterlife — interpretatio across the Arab world. Long before Islam these were not merely Ḥijāzī tribal idols but deities of the whole Arab and Aramaean north, translated into their neighbours' gods by the ancient interpretatio — the same cross-identifying reflex that ran Astarte into Aphrodite and Melqart into Heracles. At Palmyra al-Lāt was drawn first toward the Syrian Atargatis and then, by the second century AD, portrayed outright in the armed likeness of Athena — “Athena-Allāt,” helmeted and shield-bearing; the Nabataeans built her a temple at Iram (Wadi Ramm) and worshipped al-ʻUzzā as their Venus/Aphrodite, consort of their god. That an Arabian goddess could be the same power as Athena to a Palmyrene, as Venus to a Nabataean, and as one of Allah's daughters to a Meccan is itself a datum the “gods of the nations” reading leans on — the ancients took the divide-and-parcel of the peoples as a real spiritual geography — and, equally, a caution: an equivalence-chain that runs a single goddess across a dozen names is exactly what makes it hard to say she was ever a discrete being rather than a traveling cult.97

The divine-council cross-identification (a synthesis). Like Hubal these three are a Tier-B case, reached not by any text that folds them into the apportioned sons of God but by the recurrence of the shape — and yet they are the strongest Tier-B case in the catalog, for three reasons. They multiply the pattern (one goddess per tribe, the roster in miniature); they extend it to goddesses, showing the “gods of the nations” conception was not gendered; and, uniquely, the sources themselves rank them as intercessor-daughters beneath a high god — a native, un-coached statement of subordinate powers under one Most High that no biblical patron supplies so plainly. That is real weight for the worldview the reading describes. But it cannot be pressed past its limit. No source folds al-Lāt, al-ʻUzzā, or Manāt into the disinherited elohim of Deuteronomy 32 — that join is wholly the reader's synthesis; the Islamic placement runs two-voiced exactly as the biblical handling of Dagon does (the idols are “but names” in one breath, real jinn or Satan's proposal in the next); and the interpretatio that carries a single goddess across Athena, Venus, and “daughter of Allah” warns against reading each name as a separate being. Whether the power behind the three trees and the squared stone was a council-elohim, a rebel spirit, or a personified cult of fate and the morning star is the same contested question weighed on the named national gods (assessment: plausible) and across the wider “gods of the nations” group — carried here to the far edge of the biblical world, and rated no higher for the reach.98

Tarhunna (Teshub) — the Storm-god of Hatti (and the “Thousand Gods”)

The Hittite empire gives the catalog its Anatolian entry and, in one recurring treaty formula, its most systematic ancient statement of the whole pattern: not one god over one people only, but a sworn roster of every people's god at once — the “Thousand Gods of Hatti.” Two cautions frame it from the start. First, the “Hittites” of this cult — the Indo-European-speaking empire ruled from Hattusa in the second millennium BC — are almost certainly not the “Hittites” (Bene-Heth, the sons of Heth) among whom Abraham buried Sarah in Genesis 23; that identification, made when the empire was first rediscovered, is now doubted, and the two are best kept apart. Second, this is the one national god in the catalog whom no Church Father ever named, for the empire and its storm-god had dropped out of human memory altogether until the nineteenth and twentieth centuries dug them back up — so the patristic column here is a blank the modern spade fills.

Names and epithets. The storm-god of Anatolia wore a different name in each of the peoples the empire gathered: Tarhunna in Hittite, Tarhunz in Luwian, Taru in the older Hattian, and, under the Hurrian influence that reshaped the imperial cult, Teshub (Teššub). The Hittite name is transparent: it is built on the verb tarḥu-, “to prevail, conquer, be powerful,” from the Proto-Indo-European root *terḥ₂-, “to cross over, overcome” — “the Conqueror,” the Mighty One. In the cuneiform he is usually hidden behind the Sumerian storm-god logograms dU (“God 10”) and dIM / dIŠKUR (“God Storm”) — the very sign that also wrote the Semitic Hadad and Mesopotamian Adad, which is why the West-Semitic and Anatolian storm-gods (the Hadad of Aram-Damascus cataloged above among them) slide so easily into one another. He is styled “king of the gods” (Hurrian šarri ennāže) and “lord of heaven and earth,” his greatest local hypostasis the storm-god of Aleppo (Halab). The ancients cross-identified him readily: he is the Ugaritic Baal and the Semitic Hadad/Adad by function, was equated with Zeus in the Hellenized south-west, and his weather-god iconography traveled into the Roman world as Jupiter Dolichenus, whose cult-hill at Doliche was remembered as “the Hill of the Father” — though how much of that late Roman god is really Teshub is disputed.99 Beside him at the head of the pantheon stood his consort the Sun-goddess of Arinna (Hittite Arinniti; the Hattian Eštan, epithet Wurunšemu, “Mother of the land”), “Queen of all lands,” the deity who legitimized the throne — in Queen Puduḥepa's prayer, “the Sun-goddess of Arinna, my lady, mistress of the Hatti lands, queen of heaven and earth.” The king served as her priest and the queen as her priestess, the royal pair mirroring the divine one.100

The people and territory bound to him. Tarhunna's land is Anatolia, ruled from Hattusa (near modern Boğazkale, in the loop of the Kızılırmak), the capital to which king Labarna/Hattusili moved the seat around 1650 BC; the empire proper ran through its New-Kingdom height (c. 1400–1180 BC) until Hattusa was burnt to the ground amid the Late-Bronze collapse about 1180. The bond of god to state ran through the crown itself: the Hittite king acted as “high priest for the whole kingdom,” touring the holy cities in an annual round of festivals, so that the storm-god and the Sun-goddess of Arinna were the twin legitimizers of his rule — the god of the people and the god of the king. The empire was not a nation-state but a patchwork of absorbed peoples, and its religion followed suit: as Hatti conquered, it took the conquered cities' gods into its own pantheon rather than abolishing them — the mechanism that produced the “thousand gods” below.101

The legend cycle — the succession in heaven. The storm-god's great myth is the Kumarbi Cycle, a set of Hurrian songs preserved in Hittite that narrates how the kingship of heaven passed by violence from one god to the next. In the Song of Kumarbi (“Kingship in Heaven”) the first king Alalu is overthrown after nine years by his cupbearer Anu (the Sky), Anu in turn by his cupbearer Kumarbi — who, letting Anu flee, bites off and swallows his genitals and is thereby made pregnant with the storm-god Teshub and other deities, born at last by splitting Kumarbi's skull. The sequel, the Song of Ullikummi, has the deposed Kumarbi beget a blind, deaf stone monster, Ullikummi, on the shoulder of the giant Upelluri (an Atlas-like figure on whose back heaven and earth were once separated) to unseat Teshub — the monster cut loose at last with the primeval copper cutter “with which heaven and earth were cut apart.” A third song, the Illuyanka myth recited at the spring puruli festival, pits the storm-god against a great dragon. The succession-in-heaven pattern — sky-father castrated, a swallowing, a stone in place of a devoured heir — is so close to Hesiod's Theogony (Ouranos–Kronos–Zeus) that the Hittite-Hurrian song is commonly held to stand somewhere behind the Greek succession myth, and the Ullikummi combat behind the Zeus–Typhon battle.102

The “Thousand Gods of Hatti.” What sets the Hittite entry apart is not the storm-god alone but the list he heads. Hittite state treaties close by summoning the whole pantheon as sworn witnesses — the “thousand gods of the land of Hatti” — naming the great gods first and then the local deities of city after city, an explicit written map of one-power-per-place. The most famous instance is the Egyptian–Hittite peace treaty of Hattusili III and Ramesses II (c. 1259 BC), whose curse-and-blessing clause invokes both nations' gods at once: “as to him who shall not keep them, a thousand gods of the land of Hatti and a thousand gods from the land of Egypt shall destroy his house, his land and his servants,” and a like blessing on him who keeps them. The rock-cut sanctuary at Yazılıkaya outside Hattusa gives the roster a face: some sixty-plus deities carved in two converging processions, the storm-god Teshub and the sun-goddess Hepat meeting at the center — the assembled “thousand gods” in stone. Here a great power kept, as a matter of diplomatic record, a standing register of the gods of all its peoples, sworn together under its head storm-god.103

How Scripture and the Fathers place him. Here the Hittite entry is unlike every other in the catalog: neither Scripture nor the Fathers place Tarhunna at all, because the Anatolian empire was wholly lost. Its capital lay unread under the mound at Boğazköy until Charles Texier found the ruins in 1834, its archive of clay tablets was not excavated until Hugo Winckler's dig of 1906, and its language was only cracked when Bedřich Hrozný showed Hittite to be Indo-European in 1915 — so no ancient or medieval reader of the Bible ever knew the storm-god of Hatti existed. What the Fathers did know was the Bible's own “Hittites” — the sons of Heth, son of Canaan (Genesis 10:15), the people of Ephron among whom Abraham bought the cave of Machpelah, the “daughters of Heth” Esau married, Uriah the Hittite, one of the Canaanite nations Israel was to dispossess — and these they read, rightly, as a people of the land, not as the empire of Hattusa. The careful modern verdict keeps them apart: E. A. Speiser judged it “most unlikely that this group name has any direct connection either with the Hattians of Anatolia or with their ‘Hittite’ successors,” and Trevor Bryce separates a small Canaanite hill-people of the patriarchal narratives from the later neo-Hittite kingdoms of Syria that the empire's fall left behind. The modern name “Hittite,” borrowed from the Bible for the Anatolians, is a nineteenth-century convenience now under scrutiny — a caution this entry carries on its face.104

The divine-council cross-identification (a synthesis). The Hittite material is a Tier-B case — reached not by any text folding Tarhunna into the apportioned sons of God but by the recurrence of the shape — yet it contributes something no other entry does. The “thousand gods of Hatti” treaty-lists are the closest thing the ancient record offers to a written, official map of territorial deities: each people its god, all of them ranged and sworn together, a diplomatic mirror of the very picture the divine-council reading draws of the nations parcelled out to their powers. And the Kumarbi succession-in-heaven belongs to the same succession-of-gods and rebellion mytheme the Nephilim timeline tracks — the Greek Titanomachy weighed there as a possible garbled memory of the Genesis-6 revolt (the Titanomachy argument) — so the Hittite song, standing behind Hesiod, is a node on that thread; flagged as a cross-link at the myth tier, not merged into it. But the limits are as plain. The Hittite pantheon is a polytheism that absorbs conquered gods, with a storm-god elevated through combat at its head — not a sovereign Most High who apportions all the nations and keeps one people as his own portion (the load-bearing half of Deuteronomy 32:8–9); no head god divides the peoples, and no disinheritance-and-reclamation arc runs through it. Its structurally closest biblical-council cousin is not the storm-god at all but the apportioning Mesopotamian divine assembly. And the OT “Hittites” are a different people entirely, so the entry reaches the council frame by structural resonance, not by any line that folds Tarhunna into the disinherited elohim. Whether the power behind the storm-god of Hatti was a council-elohim, a rebel spirit, or a personified cult of sky and conquest is the same contested question weighed on the named national gods (assessment: plausible) and across the wider “gods of the nations” group — possibility is not proof.105

Ahura Mazda (Ohrmazd) — the god of Persia (and the “prince of Persia”)

Persia is the one entry in this catalog where Scripture itself names a spirit-power standing over the nation: Daniel's “prince of Persia” (Daniel 10:13, 20), a heavenly figure who withstands the angelic messenger twenty-one days until Michael comes to help. The nation is Persia; the prince is its patron-spirit in Daniel's vision — already weighed on its own page. This entry supplies the other half: the actual god the Persians themselves worshipped as sovereign of heaven — Ahura Mazda, the “Wise Lord” of the Zoroastrians. Two features set the entry apart from the rest of the catalog and must frame it from the start. First, Persian religion is not a plain polytheism with a chief idol but a dualism: a genuinely transcendent creator-god opposed by a cosmic adversary, with a structured heaven between them — the closest of all the nations' religions to the biblical shape, which (as we will see) cuts in more than one direction. Second, “the god of Persia” is really the god of the imperial ideology from Darius I onward: the empire's founder Cyrus never names Ahura Mazda at all, and whether the early Achaemenids were “Zoroastrian” in any developed sense is genuinely disputed — a caution this entry carries on its face.

Names and epithets. The name is a title: Ahura Mazdā (Avestan ahura- “lord” + mazdā- “wisdom, wise”) — the “Wise Lord” or “Lord of Wisdom.” It runs through the languages of the two great Persian empires: Old Persian Auramazdā in the Achaemenid inscriptions, contracted in Middle Persian to Ohrmazd (also Hormazd, Ormazd) under the Sasanians. His stock epithet is creator: the royal inscriptions open, again and again, “A great god is Ahuramazdā, who created this earth, who created yonder sky, who created man, who created happiness (šiyāti) for man” — a maker of heaven, earth, and humankind, not merely a storm- or war-patron. Under him stands a structured heaven that no other entry in this catalog matches for order: the six (or seven) Amesha Spentas (“Bounteous Immortals” — Good Purpose, Best Truth/Asha, Desirable Dominion, Holy Devotion, Wholeness, Immortality), a rank of lesser yazatas (“ones worthy of worship,” among them Mithra the god of covenant and Anahita the waters-goddess), and the fravashis, the guardian-spirits of the faithful — a graded host of divine beings ranged beneath one high god.106

The people and territory bound to him. Ahura Mazda is the god of the Persian crown, and the binding is written across the empire's own monuments. On the great rock relief at Behistun (c. 520 BC) Darius I grounds his whole right to rule in the Wise Lord: he is king “vashnā Auramazdāha” — “by the favour of Ahuramazdā” — a formula that recurs dozens of times across the inscription (Ahura Mazda's name appears some sixty-nine times; Darius claims the god's help over thirty), each victory and each act of order credited to the god who “bestowed the kingdom” on him. From Darius through Xerxes the royal texts name Ahura Mazda essentially alone — at most “the other gods who are.” That reticence breaks only with Artaxerxes II (r. 405–359 BC), the first Achaemenid to set two further deities beside the Wise Lord by name: his building inscriptions at Susa and Ecbatana (A²Sa, A²Ha, A²Sd) close by invoking “the favour of Ahuramazdā, Anāhitā, and Mithra,” and (by the report of Berossus) he was the first to raise cult-statues of Anahita in the cities of the empire — a widening of the imperial pantheon around its old sovereign head.107 The same god carried into the second Persian empire: under the Sasanians (3rd–7th c. AD) Ohrmazd was the state god of a Zoroastrian empire, his fire-temples and his Magi priesthood the official cult of Persia until the Arab conquest.108

The theology and legend — the two spirits and the cosmic war. What the Persians told of Ahura Mazda is less a birth-and-battle epic than a cosmic dualism, and it is the heart of the entry. In the oldest layer, the Gathas (the hymns ascribed to the prophet Zarathushtra/Zoroaster — whose own date is wildly contested, from the second millennium down to c. 600 BC) and the wider Avesta (Yasna, the Yashts, the Vendidad), the Wise Lord is the uncreated source of all good, truth (asha), and light, set against Angra Mainyu (Middle Persian Ahriman), the “Hostile Spirit” of the lie (druj), destruction, and darkness. Between them the whole of creation is a battlefield, and history a war moving toward Ahura Mazda's final victory and the renovation of the world (frashokereti). In the developed system the two are twin primordial mentalities — Ahriman set opposite the Wise Lord's own creative Bounteous Spirit (Spenta Mainyu) — and every human choice enlists on one side or the other. Of the yazatas beneath him, one traveled far past Persia: Mithra, the covenant-god, whose name stands behind the Roman mystery-cult of Mithras that spread through the legions in the first centuries AD — though how much of that western Mithras is really the Iranian Mithra, and how much a Roman re-invention, is much disputed.109

How Scripture and the Fathers place him. The classical and Christian witnesses knew the Persian cult better than they knew most of this catalog's gods. Herodotus (1.131–132) gives the famous Greek report: the Persians “have no images of the gods, no temples nor altars, and count the making of them folly,” but “sacrifice to Zeus going up to the tops of the mountains, and the whole circle of the heavens they call Zeus” — an imageless, mountain-top worship of a sky-sovereign, with a Magus chanting a theogony over each offering (his identification of the Persians' “Mitra” with a female Aphrodite is a well-known slip). The Fathers, in turn, met Persian religion as the great eastern dualism. Clement of Alexandria faults the Magi for worshipping the created elements — fire and water — as first principles, “ignorant of the great First Cause, the Maker of all things”; Eusebius preserves and weighs a Zoroastrian description of God; and the two-principles scheme — named plainly by the later Byzantine historian Agathias as the Persians' “Hormisdas” (Ahura Mazda) the good creator against “Arimanes” (Ahriman) the evil one — is the very dualism the Fathers fought at second hand in Manichaeism, the Persian Mani's fusion that Augustine and others spent whole treatises refuting. So the god of Persia was not, like the storm-god of Hatti, lost to memory: he was known, named, and argued against. Scripture's own contact runs the other way. When the Lord raises up Cyrus as his “anointed” and shepherd to free the exiles (Isaiah 44:28–45:1; Ezra 1), the text is emphatic that Cyrus does not know Yahweh (“though thou hast not known me,” Isaiah 45:4–5) — and, tellingly, Cyrus's own propaganda credits not Ahura Mazda but whichever local high god fit his audience: Marduk on the Babylonian Cyrus Cylinder, Yahweh in the decree of Ezra, never the Wise Lord. Ahura Mazda becomes the named sovereign of the Persian state only with Darius; whether Cyrus was a Zoroastrian at all is exactly the open question the sources leave.110

The divine-council cross-identification (a synthesis). Persia is the catalog's sharpest test case, because it is the one place where the two halves — a named nation and a named spirit set over it — are both already on the biblical page. Daniel 10 depicts the “prince of Persia” as a real heavenly power contending over the empire, and the cosmic-geography-of-Daniel-10 argument (assessment: plausible) rates that reading — national patron-spirits behind the nations, grounded in the Deuteronomy 32:8 allotment — as strong and mainstream. So here the “god of the nation” and its “prince” sit closer than anywhere else in the roster. But the honest synthesis pulls two ways, and the entry must say both. On one side, Zoroastrianism is the most biblical-looking of the nations' religions: a genuinely transcendent creator of heaven, earth, and man; an explicit cosmic adversary of the lie; a graded heavenly host of Amesha Spentas and yazatas beneath the high god — a structure that reads, at a glance, like a photographic negative of the Genesis–Deuteronomy picture, and it is no accident the Fathers heard in it a half-remembered truth turned to a dualist error. On the other side, that very closeness makes it fit the Deuteronomy 32 frame worst of any entry here. The apportionment sets lesser elohim over the nations beneath a sovereign Most High who keeps Israel as his own portion; but Ahura Mazda is not cast as a subordinate son apportioned under anyone — he is his own system's Most High, a rival sovereign creator, the least plausible candidate in the whole catalog for a “disinherited” council-elohim. The council-shaped elements are elsewhere: the yazatas and fravashis as a heavenly host (structurally like the sons of God), and — from the biblical side — the “prince of Persia” spirit, which the text need not identify with the head god the Persians worshipped at all. Whether that prince, or the power the Persians called the Wise Lord, was a council-elohim, the Fathers' spirit-behind-an-idol, or a personified cult of sky and truth is the same contested question weighed on the named national gods (assessment: plausible) and across the wider “gods of the nations” group; the nearest apportioning cousin, as ever, is the Mesopotamian divine assembly, not the sovereign Wise Lord. The underlying allotment is Deuteronomy 32:8–9 — possibility is not proof.111

The Lokapālas — the guardians of the directions (India, the Indo-European instance)

India supplies the catalog's Indo-European entry, and it differs from all the rest in what it partitions. Every god cataloged above is set over a people and a land; the Indian scheme parcels out the compass — a standing set of gods, one to each quarter and inter-quarter of space, called the Lokapālas (“world-guardians”) or Dikpālas (“guardians of the directions”). Two cautions frame the entry. First, like the storm-god of Hatti this is a national god no Church Father ever named: the Fathers knew of India's sages — Clement of Alexandria lists the Brahmins and the Sarmanae among the barbarian philosophers — but not one of them names a Lokapāla, so the patristic column here is again a near-blank. Second, what the Lokapālas guard is direction and cosmic space, not ethnic nations; that this makes them a weak match to the Deuteronomy 32 apportionment is weighed in full on the Hindu Lokapālas argument (assessment: weak). This catalog entry supplies the roster behind that verdict — the gods, their quarters, their epithets and legends — god by god.

Names and epithets — the eight (and ten) world-guardians. The term is transparent Sanskrit: loka-pāla, “guardian of the world” (loka “world” + pāla “protector”); the parallel dik-pāla substitutes diś, “direction, quarter.” In the developed Purāṇic and architectural tradition the set is fixed to the eight points of the compass — the Aṣṭa-dikpāla: Indra in the East, Agni the fire-god in the south-east, Yama the death-god in the South, Nirṛti (decay, misfortune) in the south-west, Varuṇa in the West, Vāyu the wind-god in the north-west, Kubera the wealth-lord in the North, and Īśāna (a form of Śiva; in some lists Soma, the Moon) in the north-east — expanded to ten (Daśa-dikpāla) by adding Brahmā for the zenith above and Viṣṇu (or the world-serpent Ananta) for the nadir below. Each carries his own epithets and vehicle — Indra on the elephant Airāvata, Yama on the buffalo, Varuṇa on the sea-monster makara, Kubera lord of the yakṣas — and each guards his quarter in temple iconography and in the Vāstu-puruṣa-maṇḍala, the metaphysical grid on which temples and towns were laid out. (The older Vedic reckoning already named a slightly different octad — Indra, Vāyu, Yama, Sūrya the Sun, Agni, Varuṇa, Candra the Moon, and Kubera — so the “eight guardians” come in two canonical lists, the point being the fixed number of directional powers, not one frozen roster.)112

The territory bound to them — the quarters, and the king who embodies them. The Lokapālas' “territory” is the region of space each presides over, but classical Hindu law bound them to the polity through a striking doctrine: the king himself is compounded of the world-guardians. The Laws of Manu (Mānava-Dharmaśāstra) states it twice. At 5.96 the king “is an incarnation of the eight guardian deities of the world, the Moon, the Fire, the Sun, the Wind, Indra, the Lords of wealth and water (Kubera and Varuṇa), and Yama”; and at 7.4–5, when the kingless creatures “through fear dispersed in all directions, the Lord created a king for the protection of this whole (creation), taking (for that purpose) eternal particles of Indra, of the Wind, of Yama, of the Sun, of Fire, of Varuṇa, of the Moon, and of the Lord of wealth (Kubera)… Because a king has been formed of particles of those lords of the gods, he therefore surpasses all created beings in lustre.” The sovereign of the land is thus a walking synthesis of the guardians of its quarters — one of the ancient world's tightest weldings of the god-of-the-directions to the god-of-the-realm. Beneath this cosmic tier Hindu practice layers tutelary powers down to the locality, the clan, and the person (the grāmadevatā, kuladevatā, and iṣṭadevatā) — a patron for every scale of life, treated at length on the argument page.113

The legend cycle. Three of the guardians carry great myths. Indra, king of the gods and guardian of the East, is the Vedic warrior par excellence: the Ṛg Veda's most-hymned deity, whose signal deed is the slaying of the drought-dragon Vṛtra — “I will declare the manly deeds of Indra… He slew the Dragon, then disclosed the waters, and cleft the channels of the mountain torrents” (RV 1.32) — the combat that frees the pent-up waters and orders the world, a storm-god's dragon-fight of the same family as Baal against Yam or Marduk against Tiamat. Yama, guardian of the South, is the first man to die and thereby the lord of the dead: “Yama, who first found out the way, the pasture that shall not be taken from us” — the pioneer who “travelled to the lofty heights above us” and became king of the departed, gathering the ancestors in his realm (RV 10.14). Varuṇa, guardian of the West, is the awful keeper of cosmic order (ṛta): the all-seeing sky-sovereign who “knows the pathway of the wind,” before whom “the two world-halves” tremble, who binds the wrongdoer with his noose (pāśa) and looses the penitent — the moral overseer of the Vedic pantheon, later demoted to god of the waters and the ocean-deeps. Agni (fire, the mouth of the gods and messenger between heaven and earth), Kubera (lord of wealth and the yakṣa-hosts, dwelling at Alakā on Mount Kailāsa), and Vāyu (the wind, breath of the cosmos) round out the better-storied quarters.114

How Scripture and the Fathers place them. As with the storm-god of Hatti, neither Scripture nor the Fathers ever place a Lokapāla, for India lay past the horizon of the biblical world and its gods were unknown to it by name. What the Fathers did know was India's reputation for wisdom. Clement of Alexandria, cataloguing the “barbarian philosophers,” names the Indian gymnosophists and divides them into two classes, “some of them called Sarmanae, and others Brahmins” — and elsewhere credits “the naked philosophers” and even Boutta (the Buddha), “whom… they honour as a god” — so the Greek and Christian world knew there were Indian sages and Indian gods, without ever meeting Indra or Varuṇa on the page. This is therefore the one national god in the catalog reached wholly through comparative religion rather than through any line of Scripture or patristic testimony: a fact the entry carries on its face.115

The divine-council cross-identification (a synthesis). The Lokapālas are a pure Tier-B case — reached not by any text folding them into the apportioned sons of God, but by the recurrence of a shape: a cosmos parcelled out to presiding powers, layered from the cosmic scale down to the village. As an Indo-European instance — a great tradition cousin to the Greek and Roman by language and inheritance — it shows that the “assigned guardian per portion of the world” conception is not peculiarly biblical. But the match is loose, and the entry says why plainly. What the headline Lokapāla scheme partitions is direction and cosmic space, not the ethnic nations of Deuteronomy 32:8–9 — the very axis-mismatch that holds the Greek division by lot to weak; the tutelary layer that does bind a spirit to place-and-people is intra-Indian cult-geography (villages, castes, lineages), not the foreign nations of the earth apportioned by a sovereign Most High who keeps one people as his portion; there is no head god who divides the peoples, no disinheritance, and no reclamation-arc; and a directional-guardian scheme is one of the most freely independently-invented mythemes there is (the Buddhist Four Heavenly Kings, and beyond), so its recurrence in India argues against a shared memory rather than for one. The full adversarial weighing — and the resulting weak rating — is on the Hindu Lokapālas argument; the structurally-closest apportioning cousin remains the Mesopotamian divine assembly, and the being-behind-the-cult question is the same one weighed on the named national gods (assessment: plausible) across the wider “gods of the nations” group. The underlying allotment is Deuteronomy 32:8–9 — possibility is not proof.116

The grāmadevatā and kuladevatā — India's village and clan tutelaries (the ground-floor of the pattern)

The Lokapālas above stand at the top of the Indian scheme, guarding the quarters of the cosmos; this entry supplies its bottom — the tier where “one presiding power per bounded community” is not a temple-wall abstraction but the lived religion of the village lane. Beneath the cosmic guardians Hindu practice layers tutelary powers down through the locality, the lineage, and the person: the grāmadevatā (the guardian-deity of a single village), the kuladevatā (the tutelary of a family or clan), and the iṣṭadevatā (the deity an individual chooses as a personal patron). If the Lokapālas partition direction and cosmic space, these partition ground and kin — a spirit fixed to this village's fields and boundaries, that lineage's ancestral line, this worshipper's own devotion. It is here, not at the compass-guardian tier, that the Indian material draws nearest to a patron bound to a place-and-a-people, so it earns its own roster depth beside the Lokapāla entry above. The same two cautions govern it: no Church Father ever named a village goddess — the whole tier is reached through ethnography, not Scripture — and how nearly (or poorly) it matches the Deuteronomy 32 apportionment of the nations is weighed in full on the Hindu Lokapālas argument (assessment: weak). This catalog entry supplies the roster — the terms, the named goddesses, their territories and legends — behind that verdict.117

Names and terms — the three tiers, and the goddesses who fill the lowest. The words are transparent Sanskrit compounds in -devatā, “deity”: grāma-devatā (“village deity”), kula-devatā (“clan/lineage deity,” from kula, family or gotra), and iṣṭa-devatā (“chosen deity,” from iṣṭa, “wished-for”). The grāmadevatās are overwhelmingly local goddesses — in the South the ammas or ammans (from the Tamil-Telugu amman / amma, “mother”) — and each region worships its own. The most widespread is Māriyamman, the mother-goddess of rain and of the pox-diseases (smallpox, chickenpox), invoked against epidemic across Tamil and Telugu country; Ellamma (identified with the beheaded-and-restored Reṇukā of the Paraśurāma legend) is patron of countless Deccan villages; Gangamma presides over Tirupati and its great jatara festival. Not every village guardian is female: the Tamil Aiyaṅār (Ayyanar) is a male boundary-guardian who rides the village edge by night on his terracotta horse. Alongside these run the household deities: a family's kuladevatā, inherited down the generations and consulted at every wedding and birth, and each devotee's own iṣṭadevatā — so a single Maharashtrian household may open an auspicious rite by worshipping clan-deity, village-deity, and chosen-deity together.118

The territory bound to them — a deity fixed to one village's fields and boundaries. This is the tier's defining trait and the reason it belongs in a catalog of territorial gods. The grāmadevatā is not a great pan-Indian deity worshipped locally; she is the tutelary of this village and no other, so identified with it that, as the classic ethnography records, she “is a representation of the village itself” — sometimes her head set at the village centre and the rest of the settlement reckoned her body. Henry Whitehead's 1921 survey found the cult tenaciously local: many goddesses were named for the very hamlet they belonged to (Addankamma, “the goddess from Addanki”; Pandilamma, “the goddess from Pandil”), and most were “unique to their village… worshipped only by members of a particular community.” Her jurisdiction is the village lands — she guards the fields from blight, the cattle from murrain, the people from plague and drought and bandit; propitiated she blesses, neglected she afflicts. The male boundary-guardians make the territoriality literal: Aiyaṅār and his host are stationed at the village frontier, rows of votive terracotta horses set at the edge of the settlement so the god may patrol its perimeter against the demons and dangers of the outside dark. One presiding power, one bounded jurisdiction, a spirit inseparable from its ground and folk — the ground-floor of the “patron per portion” pattern.119

The legend cycle. The village goddesses carry a distinctive kind of myth: many are the deified spirits of wronged women. The paradigm is Reṇukā / Ellamma: the wife of the sage Jamadagni, beheaded at her husband's command by their son Paraśurāma for a moment's impure thought, then restored — but with a low-caste woman's head joined to her body in the confusion of the resurrection, so that she is worshipped as two-natured, the high-born and the outcaste in one, patron of the marginal. Māriyamman's legends run the same way — a chaste woman betrayed and dying in wrath, her fury thereafter breaking out as the fever-heat of the pox until she is cooled by worship (her rites use neem leaves and water to “cool” the goddess and the disease alike). The pattern — a human wrong, a violent death, a deification into the guardian who both sends and averts the very calamity of her story — is the signature of the tier: these are not sky-gods but local powers grown from the soil and the memory of the community. The boundary-guardians carry the complementary myth of the nightly patrol: Aiyaṅār and his lieutenant Kāṭṭavārayāṅ ride the dark on horseback, warding the village edge — which is why the potter's terracotta horses are the goddess-village's standing army.120

How Scripture and the Fathers place them. Exactly as with the Lokapālas above — and even more completely — neither Scripture nor the Fathers ever place a village goddess. The cosmic guardians at least share a name-class with deities the Greeks half-knew; the grāmadevatās are village-scale cults invisible past the next district, let alone past the Indus. What the Fathers knew of India was its sages and its wealth, not the amman of any hamlet: Clement of Alexandria could name the Brahmins and the Sarmanae, but no line of patristic or biblical text names Ellamma or Māriyamman. This is therefore the catalog's purest ethnographic entry — a whole territorial-god tier documented not through Scripture or the Fathers but through the modern fieldwork of Whitehead and his successors, a fact the entry carries on its face.121

The divine-council cross-identification (a synthesis). Of the whole Indian scheme, this tier is the strongest structural match to the biblical picture and, at the same time, shows most plainly why the match falls short. A spirit bound to a place and its people, another to a lineage, another to a person is a real patron-per-portion structure — closer to a god set over Moab than a compass-guardian ever is. But two gaps hold even this half short of Deuteronomy 32. First, scope: the whole tutelary system parcels out India's own villages, castes, and lineages — internal cult-geography — not the foreign nations of the earth; there is no disinheritance of the peoples and no reclamation-arc, the elements that make the biblical allotment a moment in redemptive history rather than a static map. Second, structure: the village goddesses are not appointed by a single sovereign who assigns them their portions. They arise from the soil, the ancestral line, or an individual's choice — bottom-up and organic where the biblical allotment is top-down and sovereign; there is no Most High of the amman-cults dividing the peoples and delegating each to a guardian. So the tier is genuine corroboration of the bare “guardian-per-portion” conception — on India's own villages, where the biblical reading claims the gentile nations — and, being reached wholly by the recurrence of a shape rather than by any text folding these goddesses into the sons of God, it is a pure Tier-B case: possibility is not proof. The full adversarial weighing, and the resulting weak rating, is on the Hindu Lokapālas argument; the recurring one-power-per-portion conception and its gap to the specific biblical allotment are weighed across the “gods of the nations” group, and the being-behind-the-cult question on the named national gods (plausible). The underlying allotment is Deuteronomy 32:8–9.122

The City Gods (Chenghuang) and Tudi Gong — China's celestial civil service (deities as posted officials)

Of every entry in this catalog China is the one that reads least like a pantheon and most like a ministry. Chinese folk and imperial religion runs a celestial bureaucracy: under Heaven (Tian / Shangdi, and in the later folk pantheon the Jade Emperor) a spirit-official is appointed over each jurisdiction of the earth — a City God (Chenghuang 城隍, “god of the wall and moat”) over each walled city, and below him a Tudi Gong (土地公, “Lord of the Land”) over each village, street, or plot. What makes it belong in a catalog of territorial gods is not the mere fact of a local guardian — every culture here has that — but that the local guardian is a post in a chain of command: a jurisdiction defined on the administrative map, an officer who reports upward, and, uniquely among these parallels, a commission that can be audited, promoted, transferred, and revoked by decree. The striking datum this entry supplies is who fills those posts: not primordial powers but named, deified human beings — a dead and virtuous magistrate or loyal general appointed to the office — so that the roster of China's territorial gods reads like a civil-service register of the honoured dead. Whether that appointed-official structure corroborates the Deuteronomy 32 apportionment of the nations is weighed in full on the celestial-bureaucracy argument (assessment: plausible — on the sovereign-plus-audit axis the structurally cleanest parallel in the whole comparative set, short of a match to the apportionment of the foreign nations). This catalog entry supplies the roster behind that verdict: the named City Gods, their cities, and the appointment logic.123

Names and terms — the deified humans posted to the office. The word is a compound of the city's own defences: cheng (城), the wall, and huang (隍), the dry moat — the pair first appears in the Book of Changes, and the Chenghuang began as the divinized protection of a city's ramparts before the office was, over centuries, filled by persons. A City God is characteristically a named historical human raised to the post for distinguished or loyal service. The paradigm is Ji Xin (紀信), a general of the founding Han emperor who impersonated his cornered lord to let him escape and was burned alive by the victors — enshrined as City God at Xingyang, Zhenjiang, Xi’an, and many cities, and formally ennobled “Marquis of Xingyang” with the posthumous name Zhonglie (“loyal and valiant”) by imperial decree in 1438. Shanghai stacks three in one temple: Huo Guang (霍光), the Han general and regent (d. 68 BC), the temple’s original City God; Qin Yubo (秦裕伯, 1295–1373), a Yuan–Ming scholar-official posthumously appointed City God of Shanghai by the Ming founder, the Hongwu Emperor; and Chen Huacheng (陳化成, 1776–1842), a Qing general who died defending the city in the First Opium War. Other cities post their own: Hangzhou venerates Zhou Xin (周新), an upright Ming censor executed on a false charge; Suzhou Huang Xie, Lord Chunshen of the Warring States; Beijing Wen Tianxiang and Yang Jiaoshan; and the Song statesman Fan Zhongyan is honoured in this rank in his own country. The lower office is the Tudi Gong, the earth-god of the smallest unit — also frequently a deified local worthy — whose defining trait is that “the Tudigong of one location is not the Tudigong of another.” One office, one jurisdiction, filled by the honoured dead.124

The territory bound to them — a hierarchy of jurisdictions mapped onto the empire. The Chinese system is the catalog’s most literally administrative: the spirit-officials are ranked in tiers that mirror the levels of imperial government. The City God holds a single walled city or administrative seat — “both the magistrate and the god held sway over the same administrative area” — and beneath him the Tudi Gong holds the village, ward, or plot, “a subordinate of the City God… His superiors include the City God and the Jade Emperor, the Supreme God.” The City God is popularly the living magistrate’s “supernatural or divine counterpart,” keeping his own secret registers of the district’s affairs; a newly-arrived prefect ritually reported to him on taking up his post. The jurisdictions nest exactly as the bureaucracy does — empire, province, prefecture, county, ward — each with its resident divine officer, so the map of the gods is the map of the state. It is the pattern of “one presiding power per bounded portion” carried out with an administrator’s precision, down to the finest grain the earth can be divided into.125

The appointment logic — posted, ennobled, transferred, and cashiered by decree. This is the tier’s signature and the reason it is the notable echo of the divine-council idea that the Most High appointed the powers over the nations. In China divinity is, in Henri Maspero’s classic formulation, “a responsibility like a public function: the title endures but those who hold it succeed one another… functionary gods who receive a position, who lose it, who are promoted or demoted.” The City God’s term was even reckoned in office-years — “thought to change every three years, just as a living magistrate would change office every three years” — and the post could be re-staffed from below: a city could, in effect, retire one occupant and welcome a new patron into the same temple. The throne treated the offices as its own to fill and to grade: Ji Xin was ennobled “Marquis of Xingyang” by an emperor’s edict, Qin Yubo was posted to Shanghai by the Hongwu Emperor, and the Tudi Gong himself is judged on performance — “if misfortunes visit a locality, the Tudi Gong is judged to have lost interest and a new patron is chosen.” A spirit appointed to a territory, transferable and demotable by a higher authority: the bureaucratic mirror of the appointed-patron conception.126

Legends and the state cult — judge of the local dead, warden of the walls, and the Ming standardization. The City God’s duties join this world to the next. He guards the physical city — the wall and moat his name records — against flood, fire, drought, and enemy; and he presides over the local dead, an underworld magistrate who receives the newly deceased of his district, keeps the register of their deeds, and forwards their cases up the infernal bureaucracy, which is why his temple courts are hung with the imagery of judgement and his processions escort the wandering ghosts. The office was not merely folk belief but state cult: the Ming founder Zhu Yuanzhang gave the Chenghuang special attention, mandating a City God temple in every administrative seat and conferring graded titles that matched the level of the jurisdiction — standardizing across the empire the rank of a spirit to the rank of his territory, the clearest imperial statement that these were offices in a single graded system. The Qing continued the cult in province, prefecture, and county alike. The historian David Johnson’s study of the T’ang and Sung cults traces how the office grew from the divinized rampart into the personified magistrate of the dead that the Ming then regularized.127

The divine-council cross-identification (a synthesis). On the bare structure, this is the strongest parallel in the catalog: a supreme heaven that appoints a spirit-officer over each bounded jurisdiction, oversees him, and can promote, transfer, or cashier him — the nearest pagan echo not only of the appointed-patron conception but of Psalm 82’s arraignment of the powers who govern badly. It supplies the two things the Greek lot and Rome’s evocatio lack together — a Most High at the apex and a spirit bound to a place-and-its-people. Yet the same three gaps that hold the argument to plausible rather than sound hold here. First, scope: the whole system apportions China’s own cities and villages — internal cult-geography — not the foreign nations of the earth, and there is no disinheritance of the peoples and no reclamation-arc. Second, ontology: the officers are largely deified mortals on fixed terms — a loyal general or an honest magistrate raised to the post — a different category from the primordial council-elohim. Third, and cutting deepest, its very independence: developed a continent away from the Near East, it can be no memory of Babel and no matrix of the biblical council — only a demonstration that the “appointed-patron-per-place” shape is one human religiosity generates on its own, which is as much the naturalistic reading’s datum (the standard sinology reads the celestial bureaucracy as the “imperial metaphor,” heaven modelled on the civil service) as the supernaturalist’s. So bank it precisely: China is the cleanest evidence that the appointed-official conception recurs independently and in fine administrative detail, and the one pagan system that mirrors the audit of the powers — a genuine Tier-B corroboration, weighed to plausible on the celestial-bureaucracy argument. The recurring one-power-per-portion conception and its gap to the specific biblical allotment are weighed across the “gods of the nations” group, and the being-behind-the-cult question on the named national gods (plausible). The underlying allotment is Deuteronomy 32:8–9 — possibility is not proof.128

The ujigami — Japan's clan and birthplace guardian-kami (each lineage and locale under its own deity)

Where China (above) posts its territorial gods like officials, Japan binds them by blood and birthplace. Shintō carries the catalog's cleanest instance of “each clan and each place under its own deity” in the ujigami (氏神, literally “clan-kami”) — the guardian kami of a lineage (uji) and, as the system broadened, of a locality. What makes it belong in a catalog of territorial gods is not the bare fact of a local guardian — every culture here has that — but that the tie is ascribed, not chosen: those born within a kami's bounds are its ujiko (氏子, “children of the kami”), enrolled under its protection by birth and bound to its shrine, much as a Moabite was born under Chemosh or a Tyrian under Melqart. A person no more selects his ujigami than he selects his clan or his birthplace; he inherits it. That is the datum this entry supplies to the roster: a whole civilization that organizes its gods as one-guardian-per-people-and-place and assigns each person to his by the accident of birth. Whether that inherited-patron structure corroborates the Deuteronomy 32 apportionment of the nations is weighed, for the nearest rated East-Asian instance, on the celestial-bureaucracy argument (assessment: plausible); this entry gathers the Japanese detail behind that comparative verdict.129

Names and terms — three overlapping guardians and the parishioner born to them. Three words name the guardian from three angles. The ujigami proper is the deity of a lineage — its bond to its ujiko reckoned “based on bloodlines.” The ubusunagami (産土神, “birth-soil kami”) is the deity of the land where one was born, whose tie to its ubuko (産子) is instead “based on a faith linked to geographical location” and who is believed to protect a person “from before you are born until after you die … even if you move to another location.” The chinjugami (鎮守神) is the tutelary of a specific site — a manor, a village, and later a Buddhist temple compound. Historically the three converged: as the old clan system weakened after the Heian period, the manor-protecting chinju and the birthplace ubusunagami “came to be seen as the heart of the community, and were eventually referred to as ujigami,” so that the single word now covers all three — the god one is born under, whether by blood or by soil. A newborn is carried to the shrine in the ujiko-iri rite and formally entered on the kami's rolls.130

The named clan-kami and their shrines. The system's aristocratic layer preserves the clearest clan-god bonds. Ame-no-Koyane, “the ancestral god of the Nakatomi clan” and of Fujiwara no Kamatari's Fujiwara house, is enshrined at Kasuga Taisha (the Kasuga Grand Shrine) in Nara, the Fujiwara's tutelary sanctuary. Hachiman (八幡), “the divine spirit of the semi-legendary Emperor Ōjin,” became the tutelary kami of the Minamoto (Genji) warrior clan — “Because Emperor Ōjin was an ancestor of the Minamoto warrior clan, Hachiman became its tutelary kami” — and after Minamoto no Yoritomo founded the Kamakura shogunate the “protector of the warrior class” at large; some 2,500 shrines are dedicated to him, second in number only to Inari, headed by Usa Jingū. The Miwa clan's deity Ōmononushi is enshrined at Ōmiwa Shrine at the foot of Mount Miwa — “one of the oldest extant Shinto shrines,” which has no honden because the mountain itself serves as the shintai. Clan, kami, and shrine lock together, one to a lineage.131

The territory bound to them — from the clan altar up to the provincial patron. The Japanese pattern nests, though by lineage and land rather than by China's chain of command. At the base each uji and each village kept its own kami; above them sat a province-wide patron. Every old province had its ichinomiya (一宮, “first shrine”), “the Shinto shrine with the highest rank in a province,” dedicated to its most important local deity — Ōmiwa was the ichinomiya of Yamato. Under the Ritsuryō administration the incoming provincial governor (kokushi) made his first official call on the ichinomiya to announce his appointment to the local kami — the closest Japanese echo of China's magistrate reporting to the City God. The result is a territorial map of the sacred: the archipelago divided into provinces and localities, each with its resident patron, and every inhabitant assigned to his by birth — the pattern of one-presiding-power-per-bounded-portion, worked out through kinship and place.132

Legends — the kami's origins in the national myth-histories. These patrons are not faceless: the eighth-century Kojiki and Nihon Shoki give each a place in the story of the age of the gods. Ame-no-Koyane is the liturgist of the pivotal Ama-no-Iwato episode, reciting the ritual prayer (norito) that helps draw the sun-goddess Amaterasu from the heavenly rock-cave and restore light to the world — the mythic charter of the Nakatomi/Fujiwara priestly office. Ōmononushi appears in the Nihon Shoki when Emperor Sujin, beset by plague, appeals to the kami of Mount Miwa, who demands that his own half-human descendant Ōtataneko conduct his rites. And Hachiman's very identity is a deified emperor — the enshrined spirit of Ōjin — so that the Minamoto's war-god is at once their imperial ancestor. The clan's god and the clan's origin are told in one breath.133

The divine-council cross-identification (a synthesis). On the bare structure, the ujigami system is a strong Tier-B parallel — in one respect the closest in the catalog to the biblical shape. What Deuteronomy 32 pictures is not merely gods-over-places but peoples allotted to powers, each nation belonging to its god by no choice of its own; and the ubusunagami/ujigami bond is precisely an ascribed one — you are the ujiko of the kami you were born under, as Israel's neighbours were the people of Chemosh, Milcom, or Dagon by birth. Yet the same three gaps that hold the wider argument short of sound hold here. First, scope: the system apportions Japan's own clans and provinces — internal cult-geography — not the foreign nations of the earth; there is no Most High apportioning them, no Babel, and no disinheritance-and-reclamation arc (Amaterasu and the imperial line sit at the apex of Japan's own myth, not over the nations). Second, ontology: the kami are largely deified ancestors (Ame-no-Koyane forefather of the Fujiwara; Hachiman the divinized Emperor Ōjin) or nature-and-locale spirits (the mountain-kami of Miwa) — a different category from the primordial council-elohim. Third, its very independence: developed on the far edge of Asia with no Near-Eastern contact, it can be no memory of Babel and no matrix of the biblical council — only a demonstration that the “a-people-and-its-god” shape is one human religiosity generates on its own, which is as much the naturalistic reading's datum as the supernaturalist's. So bank it precisely: Japan is the catalog's clearest case of the inherited, birth-assigned patron-bond — an independent structural recurrence weighed comparatively to plausible alongside the East-Asian celestial-bureaucracy argument. The recurring one-power-per-portion conception and its gap to the specific biblical allotment are weighed across the “gods of the nations” group, and the being-behind-the-cult question on the named national gods (plausible). The underlying allotment is Deuteronomy 32:8–9 — possibility is not proof.134

Huitzilopochtli — the god of the Mexica (Tenochtitlan, and the altepetl patrons of Nahua Mexico)

Every god above belongs to the Old World, and most to a single connected web of Near-Eastern cult. This last entry crosses an ocean the pattern could not have crossed with it. The Mexica (Aztecs) of central Mexico, sealed off from the biblical world for the whole of their history, organized political life around exactly the shape this catalog tracks: a people bound to a patron power, a national god who led his tribe to its promised land, and a nested tier of civic and kin-group tutelaries beneath. Whether that independent recurrence corroborates the Deuteronomy 32 apportionment — or instead shows the “a-people-and-its-god” shape to be a human constant — is weighed, and rated plausible, on the Mesoamerican-patron-gods argument; this entry gathers the concrete detail behind that verdict — the names, the altepetl pairs, the birth-and-migration legends, and the Franciscan chroniclers' reading of these gods as devils.135

Names and epithets. Huitzilopochtli compounds Nahuatl huitzilin (“hummingbird”) and opochtli (“left”) — conventionally “Hummingbird of the South” or “Blue Hummingbird on the Left,” since in Aztec cartography south lay to the left and slain warriors were believed reborn as hummingbirds, so the name carries the sense of the resuscitated warrior of the southern sky. He is the Blue Tezcatlipoca, the southern member of the four Tezcatlipocas who quarter the cosmos; other names in his list are Xiuhpilli (“Turquoise Prince”), Totec (“Our Lord”), and Mexi / Mexitli — the form from which the Mexica, and Mexico itself, took their name. A war- and sun-god “robust, strong, warlike and a great destroyer of towns and men,” he shared the twin summit of the Templo Mayor with the rain-god Tlaloc; his own festival was Panquetzaliztli.136

Named patrons for named cities — and the tier beneath. Nahua political society was built out of the altepetl (from in atl, in tepetl, “the water, the mountain”), the ethnically-based city-state, and “a patron deity complete with state temple” was one of the defining marks of one — so the roster of one-god-per-polity is not a modern abstraction but the standing architecture of the state. Huitzilopochtli was the patron of the Mexica capital Tenochtitlan; Quetzalcoatl, the Feathered Serpent, of the great pilgrimage city Cholula; Camaxtli — a form of the Chichimec hunter-god Mixcoatl — of Tlaxcala and Huexotzinco, so distinctively theirs that his image had no place in Tenochtitlan or Texcoco; Tezcatlipoca and Xipe Totec presided over others still. Beneath each altepetl the pattern repeated at a finer grain: the city-state was subdivided into calpulli (kin-based wards), and each calpulli “worshiped its own patron deity and maintained a ceremonial district” with its own temple. A patron for the nation, a patron for the city, a patron for the kin-group — the one-power-per-bounded-portion conception stacked from the top of the state to the neighborhood.137

Legends — the migration, the founding sign, and the birth at Coatepec. Two great cycles carry the god. The first is the migration charter: by the tradition (Durán, Sahagún, and the pictorial Tira de la Peregrinación / Codex Boturini) the Mexica set out from Aztlán at Huitzilopochtli's command, his idol borne on the priests' shoulders in a sacred bundle; on the road “his voice was heard giving orders,” he gave the wandering tribe its name, and he promised them a homeland if they served him — ending in the land-grant sign of an eagle perched on a prickly-pear cactus, where they founded Tenochtitlan (traditionally 1325). The second is the Coatepec birth-myth (Sahagún, Florentine Codex, Book 3): on Serpent Mountain the earth-goddess Coatlicue, sweeping her shrine, is impregnated by a falling ball of hummingbird feathers; her daughter Coyolxauhqui and the four hundred southern-star gods, the Centzon Huitznahua, take the pregnancy as dishonor and march to kill her; forewarned by the defector Quauitlicac, Huitzilopochtli springs from the womb fully grown and armed with his fire-serpent weapon Xiuhcoatl, beheads Coyolxauhqui and hurls her dismembered body down the mountain, and scatters the four hundred — a solar allegory of the sun routing the moon and stars, and the mythic charter of the Templo Mayor, whose foot bore the great carved disk of the broken Coyolxauhqui.138

The Franciscan devil-reading — a “gods-of-the-nations-as-fallen-powers” reception at first contact. The sixteenth-century friars who recorded all of this did not read these patrons as empty personifications; they read them, in the plainest terms, as devils. Fray Bernardino de Sahagún, whose Historia general de las cosas de Nueva España (the Florentine Codex) is the primary source for the god, opens his account of Huitzilopochtli by calling him “otro Hércules” (another Hercules) for his warlike strength — but then a “nigromántico, amigo de los diablos… inventor de guerras” (“a necromancer, a friend of the devils… an inventor of wars”), the euhemerist's deified-sorcerer whom demons empowered. The Dominican Diego Durán read the migration itself in biblical terms, casting Huitzilopochtli as a Moses-figure leading a chosen people who carried their god's bundle “as the children of Israel did with the Ark of the Covenant.” That double gesture — classing the native patron with the demonic and yet unable to describe it except through the vocabulary of Scripture — is itself the “gods of the nations” reading enacted at first contact: the missionary took the god of an unreached people to be a real fallen power behind the idol, exactly the patristic “idol of demons” frame the argument pages weigh.139

The divine-council cross-identification (a synthesis). On the one axis that matters most — a divine power bound to a people and leading them to their land — Mesoamerica is the tightest Tier-B fit in the whole catalog: Huitzilopochtli does for the Mexica precisely what Chemosh, Milcom, or Dagon did for Moab, Ammon, or Philistia, and does it in a hemisphere no memory of Babel can reach. But the same three gaps that hold the wider argument short of sound hold here, and one of them cuts hardest of all. First, there is no Most High who apportions the nations: Huitzilopochtli settles his own tribe and does not parcel the Tlaxcalans to Camaxtli or the Cholulans to Quetzalcoatl — the patrons coexist as gods of rival, self-standing peoples, not as appointees of one sovereign over the board. Second, the scope is intra-Mesoamerican, with no dimension of the foreign nations of the earth and no disinheritance-and-reclamation arc — the migration mirrors the Exodus (a god delivering his people to their land) far more than Deuteronomy 32's apportionment of all peoples. Third, its very independence, the feature that makes the fit so striking, converts the parallel into a structural recurrence: a New-World culture that demonstrably shares no history with Israel shows the “a-people-and-its-god” shape to be one the religious imagination generates on its own — which is as much the naturalist's datum as the supernaturalist's (possibility ≠ proof). So bank it precisely: Mesoamerica is the catalog's clearest case of a national god who leads and settles his own people, weighed comparatively to plausible on the Mesoamerican argument, sitting with the parallels that bind a power to a people or place rather than a cosmic realm. The recurring one-power-per-portion conception and its gap to the specific biblical allotment are weighed across the “gods of the nations” group, and the being-behind-the-cult question on the named national gods (plausible). The underlying allotment is Deuteronomy 32:8–9 — possibility is not proof.140

Viracocha, Inti, and the huacas — the gods of the Andes (the Inca, and the ayllu tutelaries of Peru)

Like the Mexica entry above, the Andes lie on the far side of an ocean the Near-Eastern pattern could not have crossed with it — and there too a great civilization, sealed off from the biblical world for the whole of its history, organized religious life around exactly the shape this catalog tracks. But the Andean case adds a feature Mesoamerica lacks: a creator remembered as dividing humanity into its several nations and settling each in its own land — the nearest thing in the whole roster to a sovereign apportioner. Whether that independent recurrence corroborates the Deuteronomy 32 apportionment or instead shows the pattern to be a human constant is weighed, and rated plausible, on the Andean-huacas argument; this entry gathers the concrete detail behind that verdict — the names, the ayllu-and-pacarina architecture, the division-of-the-nations charter at Tiahuanaco, and the missionary “extirpators of idolatry” who read the huacas as devils.141

Names and epithets. The Andean high god is Viracocha (Quechua Wiraqucha) — the pan-Andean creator, so sacred that he was more often invoked by descriptive titles than his own name: the compound Con Ticci Viracocha Pachayachachic (also Kon-Tiki Wiraqucha), where Ticci carries “foundation, origin, beginning” and Pachayachachic means “teacher” or “maker of the world” — his formal Inca title being Viracocha Pachayachachi, “Creator of All Things.” Set beneath him in the imperial cult was Inti, the sun, dynastic patron of the Inca from whom the Sapa Inca claimed descent. On the coast the great creator-and-oracle was Pachacamac (Pacha Kamaq, “the one who animates the world,” the “Maker of the Earth”), “Lord of Earthquakes,” whom the Inca absorbed as an aspect or companion of Viracocha. And beneath these towered the countless named local powers — the mountain-huaca Pariacaca (Paria Caca) of the Huarochirí highlands foremost among them.142

Named patrons for named peoples — and the tier above them. Andean society was built out of the ayllu, the kin-based descent-group that reckoned itself from a common founding ancestor; each ayllu traced that ancestor to a pacarina — the specific lake, cave, or spring from which he had first emerged — and venerated both the pacarina and the ancestor's mummified body (the mallqui) as huacas, holy and powerful things fed and consulted across generations. So a real power stood behind this people in this place, named to it: the Huarochirí Manuscript preserves a whole local pantheon of such huacas — Pariacaca, born on the mountain Condor Coto as five eggs, the “fivefold father” and patron of the Huarochirí groups; the fire-god Huallallo Carhuincho whom he defeats; the goddess Chaupi Ñamca — each bound to and defining its own community, a roster of one-power-per-people that matches, from an independent hemisphere, the biblical habit of naming Chemosh of Moab or Dagon of Philistia. Over the whole the Inca laid a second tier: rather than abolish the conquered peoples' huacas, they subordinated them to the imperial cult of Inti, binding hundreds of shrines (some 328) into the ceque system of ritual pathways radiating from the Coricancha at Cusco — a supreme cult set over a graded order of still-local patrons.143

Legends — the division of the nations at Tiahuanaco, the emergence from the pacarinas, and the coastal creator. The charter that lifts the Andes above every other New-World parallel is Viracocha's division of humanity. By the tradition (Juan de Betanzos; Cristóbal de Molina; the Jesuit Bernabé Cobo) Viracocha made mankind at Tiahuanaco (Tiwanaku) and then parcelled it out: he shaped the several nations, “painting each nation with its own dress and language,” gave each its songs, customs, and seed-crops, and dispatched each to travel underground and emerge at its appointed pacarina to settle its allotted country — “these shall be called the people, and they shall come out of such a source in such a province… and shall populate in it.” A single high god who divides the one human stock into distinct peoples, assigns each its identity, and installs each in its own land is the catalog's closest analogue to Deuteronomy 32:8's “when the Most High divided to the nations their inheritance… he set the bounds of the people.” The coast kept its own creation cycle: Pachacamac, having defeated the rival creator Con, shaped the first man and woman from clay, and his oracle south of Lima drew pilgrims from across the Andes for answers spoken in his name. And the local huacas carry their own epics — above all the Huarochirí cycle of Pariacaca subduing the fire-god and ordering the waters of his valleys.144

The “extirpators of idolatry” — a gods-of-the-nations-as-devils reception at first contact. The chroniclers and missionaries who recorded all of this did not take the huacas for empty personifications; they took them for devils. Pedro de Cieza de León already reported at contact the veneration of the ancestor-mummies and a caste of priests who served the huacas, framing the cult as the work of the deceiver; Cristóbal de Molina's Relación de las fábulas y ritos de los Incas (c. 1575) gathered the Viracocha creation, the flood, and the meaning of the huacas under the same lens. The frame hardened into a program: the seventeenth-century visitas against native religion produced the Jesuit Pablo José de Arriaga's La extirpación de la idolatría del Pirú (1621), the classic manual for the “extirpators of idolatry,” which reads the huacas and mallquis throughout as the Indians' dealings with the demonio — the huacas to be found out, burned, and replaced. Here again the missionary took the god of an unreached people to be a real fallen power behind the idol, exactly the patristic “idol of demons” frame the argument pages weigh.145

The divine-council cross-identification (a synthesis). On the axis the biblical argument most needs — a sovereign who divides the peoples — the Andes are the tightest fit in the whole catalog: Viracocha does not merely patron one tribe (as Huitzilopochtli does for the Mexica) but parcels the one human stock into its several nations, tongues, and lands, closer to Deuteronomy 32:8's Most High than any other Tier-B parallel gathered here. Yet the two strong axes never join into the biblical shape, and that seam is what holds the case to plausible rather than sound. First, Viracocha distributes peoples, places, and tongues but assigns no guardian god over each nation — the huacas rise from below, as a people's own deified ancestors emerging from its own pacarina, not as powers appointed over it from above; so the load-bearing join of the allotment (a Most High parcelling the nations to their patrons, keeping one as his portion) is exactly what the Andean picture lacks. Second, the tempting two-tier Inti-over-huacas hierarchy is a mid-fifteenth-century conquest-era integration — local patrons first, imperial sun-cult imposed later — the reverse of a primeval apportionment, with no dimension of the foreign nations and no disinheritance-and-reclamation arc. Third, its very independence, the feature that makes the fit so striking, converts the parallel into a structural recurrence that fits a human-universal reading at least as well as a supernatural one (belief, not the beings; possibility ≠ proof). So bank it precisely: the Andes are the catalog's clearest case of a creator who divides the nations and settles each in its land, weighed comparatively to plausible on the Andean-huacas argument, sitting with the parallels that bind a power to a people or place rather than a cosmic realm. The recurring one-power-per-portion conception and its gap to the specific biblical allotment are weighed across the “gods of the nations” group, and the being-behind-the-cult question on the named national gods (plausible). The underlying allotment is Deuteronomy 32:8–9 — possibility is not proof.146

Teutates, the Matres, and the landvættir — the gods of the Celts and the north (tribe-bound and land-bound powers of Gaul, the Rhineland, and Iceland)

The Indo-European north-west — Gaulish and Germanic barbarian religion, largely outside the Near-Eastern orbit and recorded chiefly by the Romans who conquered it — frames the world exactly as this catalog tracks, in powers bound to a people or a place. It also carries the single most on-the-nose datum in the whole comparative set: a Gaulish god whose name simply means “god of the tribe.” Yet it is rated weak — the lowest fit gathered here — on the Germanic-and-Celtic tribal-gods argument, because its patrons are a people's own gods and its land-wights are the ground's own spirits, with no Most High who apportions the nations above them; this entry gathers the concrete detail behind that verdict — the names, the tribe-and-land binding, the bloody Gaulish triad and the Icelandic wights, and the missionaries who read them all as devils.147

Names and epithets. The Gaulish god is Teutates (also Toutatis, Toutiorix), named by the Roman poet Lucan in a triad of blood-fed Gaulish gods — Teutates, Esus, and Taranis (“the Thunderer,” from taran, thunder). Because the Gauls left almost no writing of their own, the classical witnesses render these powers through Roman names — the interpretatio romana Julius Caesar set out in his account of Gaul: the Gauls, he reports, honour Mercury above all, then Apollo, Mars, Jupiter, and Minerva, with much the same functions other nations give them. Where epigraphy lets the native and Roman names be matched, dedications pair Mars Teutates and Jupiter Taranis, anchoring two of Lucan's three. Alongside the named high gods stood a whole class of divine Mothers — the Romano-Celtic and Germanic Matres and Matronae, worshipped in triads on hundreds of votive altars across Gaul, Britain, the Rhineland, and Iberia. And in the north, resident in a country's hills, waters, and stones, were the Norse landvættir (“land-wights”), guardian-spirits of the land itself.148

Named patrons for named peoples — and a god who is his people. The sharpest datum is lexical. Teutates is derived by the standard etymology from Proto-Celtic *toutā (“tribe, people”; cf. Old Irish túath, Gothic þiuda, and the same root in Latin-era Toutiorix, “king of the people”), so the theonym reads “god of the touta” — the deity as the personified guardian of the community itself, the conception written straight into the divine name. The Matres/Matronae extend it to a whole class of deities patroned to peoples and lands by name: the Matres Gallaicae (“the Galician Mothers”) in Iberia; the British Matres Ollototae (plausibly “the Mothers of all the people / of the foreign folk”); the Matres Nemausicae, the Mothers of the people of Nemausus (Nîmes); and the great Rhineland cluster of Matronae — the Aufaniae and others — confined to the territory of the Ubii, whose votaries are depicted in that tribe's distinctive dress. Here a real power stands behind this tribe in this country, named to it — the divine mothers of the Ubii, the Galicians, the Nemausans — the very habit by which Scripture names Chemosh of Moab or Dagon of Philistia among the named national gods. The land-wights make the binding tighter still — not to a people but to the ground itself, quarter by quarter.149

Legends and cult — the bloody triad of Gaul and the guardian-wights of Iceland. Lucan reports the Gaulish triad as gods placated with human blood — “their harsh Teutates, their Esus whose savage shrines make men shudder, their Taranis whose altar is no less cruel than that of Scythian Diana” (Pharsalia 1.444–446) — and the medieval Berne Scholia on Lucan spell out three distinct modes of sacrifice: the victims of Teutates drowned head-down in a vat, those of Esus hung and bled on a tree, those of Taranis burned. In the north the land-wights were live stakes, not decoration. The Book of Settlements (Landnámabók) preserves an early Icelandic law — ascribed to Úlfljótr's code — that a ship must not approach land with a gaping dragon-head at the prow, “lest the land-wights be frightened”; Snorri Sturluson's Heimskringla tells how a Danish king's sorcerer, scouting Iceland in a whale's shape for invasion, found every hill and mountain full of landvættir and was driven off by four great guardian-spirits, one over each quarter of the island — the bull, the eagle/griffin, the dragon, and the rock-giant that stand to this day on Iceland's coat of arms. And in Egils saga, driven from Norway after his feud with King Eiríkr Bloodaxe, Egil Skallagrímsson raises a níðstöng (scorn-pole) topped with a horse's head and turns his curse past the king onto the spirits of the country itself: “This curse I turn also on the guardian-spirits [landvættir] who dwell in this land, that they may all wander astray… till they have driven out of the land king Eric and Gunnhilda” — the land-wights treated as a real constituency whose favour decides whether a ruler keeps his land.150

A gods-of-the-nations-as-devils reception — the missionaries of the north-west. The Christian witnesses who recorded this world did not take its powers for empty personifications; they took them for devils. In the very Gallaecia of the Matres Gallaicae, Bishop Martin of Braga's De correctione rusticorum (“On the correction of country folk,” c. 572) reads the rustic gods of sea, river, spring, and wood as demons cast out of heaven who lord it over the elements and are worshipped by the ignorant: “in the sea they call them Neptune, in the rivers Lamia, in the springs Nymphs, in the woods Diana, and they are all no more than evil demons and wicked spirits.” On the Germanic side the same frame turned to the axe: the English missionary Boniface, evangelizing Hesse, felled the great oak of the thunder-god (the “Oak of Jupiter/Donar”) at Geismar (c. 724) to prove the god behind it powerless — the tribal storm-god confronted as a defeated pretender. Here again the missionary took the god of an unreached people to be a real fallen power behind the idol, exactly the patristic “idol of demons” frame the argument pages weigh.151

The divine-council cross-identification (a synthesis). On the one axis where the Germanic-Celtic material is the tightest fit in the whole catalog — a god bound to a people — nothing else here is so on the nose: Teutates is, in so many words, “the tribe's god,” the conception carried in the theonym, and the Matres named for the Ubii and the Galicians extend it to a class. Yet the case is rated weak — below every other parallel gathered here — because the fit is all on the generic axis and none on the load-bearing one. First, “a people has its guardian god” is not “a Most High apportioned the nations to their gods”: the tribal patron is a tribe's own deity, locally generated, not a power handed down from a primeval division of humanity — and Teutates may even be a title (“the tribal god”) that different Gaulish peoples applied to different deities, evidence for the universal habit of having a tribe-god rather than for one roster of nations each under its allotted patron. Second, the Norse contribution is the wrong shape entirely: the landvættir are genius loci — spirits of the ground that rise from below and inhere in the land's own features, not powers a sovereign assigned from above — the same defeater that limits the Andean huacas, biting harder here because the wight is not even a people's deity but the land's. Third, the load-bearing half of Deuteronomy 32:8–9 is wholly absent: Germanic and Celtic religion has no high god who divides all the nations to guardian gods, no apportioning assembly, no horizon of the foreign nations being distributed, and no disinheritance-and-reclamation arc — a tribe-god and a land-wight are exactly what independent human religiosity produces everywhere, so the parallel is a structural recurrence that a human-universal reading explains at least as economically as a supernatural one (belief, not the beings; possibility ≠ proof). So bank it precisely: the north-west supplies the catalog's sharpest lexical fit and its clearest land-guardian conception, yet the least apportionment, weighed to weak on the Germanic-and-Celtic tribal-gods argument, sitting with the parallels that bind a power to a people or place without a sovereign who parcels the nations — the directions-only Greek lot, the Hindu Lokapālas, and Egypt's nome-gods. The recurring one-power-per-portion conception and its gap to the specific biblical allotment are weighed across the “gods of the nations” group, and the being-behind-the-cult question on the named national gods (plausible). The underlying allotment is Deuteronomy 32:8–9 — possibility is not proof.152

The poliad deities — the city-patrons of Greece (Athena of Athens, Artemis of Ephesus, and the gods who “held” the poleis)

The Greek city-state (polis) frames the very thing this catalog tracks. Every polis stood under a poliad deity — a theos poliouchos (“city-holding god”), titled Polias of a goddess and Polieus of a god — the patron who “held” the town, guarded its citadel, and received its chief festival, so that civic identity and divine patronage were one thing: to be an Athenian was to belong to Athena, an Ephesian to Artemis. This entry gathers the concrete roster behind the Greek instance — Athena of Athens, Hera of Argos and Samos, Artemis of Ephesus, Apollo of Delphi and Delos, Poseidon of the Isthmus, Zeus of Olympia — the founding contests that bound a god to a city, and the two occasions on which the New Testament itself walked into a named city-patron's cult and named it. One line of the Greek material — the myth of the gods dividing the cosmos by lot (dasmos) — is weighed as a possible memory of the Deuteronomy 32 apportionment, and rated weak, on the Greek division-by-lot argument; that is a different datum from the poliad pattern gathered here, which binds a power not to a cosmic realm but to a city and its people. This catalog reports that detail; it issues no separate verdict.153

Names and epithets. The generic term is poliouchos (“holding the city”) or polias/polieus (“of the city”), attached to whichever deity a polis owned as its own. The paradigm is Athena Polias, “Athena of the City,” whose priestess was Athens' senior religious officer and whose ancient olive-wood image was the holiest object in the state. Beside her the same civic titulature ran across Greece: Zeus Polieus kept his own altar and ox-sacrifice (the Bouphonia) on the Athenian Acropolis; Hera was Argeia, “the Argive,” patroness of Argos and, across the water, of Samos, each with a vast Heraion; Artemis Ephesia held Ephesus; Apollo owned Delphi (as Pythios) and his birth-island Delos (as Delios); Poseidon lorded the Isthmus by Corinth; and Zeus Olympios presided at Olympia. The bare habit — one named power set over one named town — is the grammar of the whole Greek religious map, and the topographer Pausanias, touring the country in the second century AD, records it city by city as a matter of course.154

A god won for a city — the contest for Attica, and the sky-fallen image. The binding of patron to polis was told as a founding event. For Attica, Athena and Poseidon contended for the land: Poseidon struck the Acropolis rock and brought forth a salt sea (or well), Athena planted the olive, and the olive's gift won her the city, Poseidon taking second place. Herodotus (writing c. 440 BC) reports both tokens as still shown on the citadel in his day — in the temple of Erechtheus, he says, are “an olive-tree and a sea” that Poseidon and Athena left when they strove for the country — and the later temple built over them, the Erechtheion, enshrined the contest in its very plan, an eastern cella for Athena Polias and a western shrine sharing Poseidon-Erechtheus. Athena's cult-image there was believed diipetes — “fallen from Zeus / from the sky” — a detail that reaches straight into the New Testament: the Ephesians said the same of their Artemis, an image “which fell down from Jupiter” (diopetous, Acts 19:35), the two great poliad goddesses of the Greek world sharing a claim that their idols came from heaven, not from human hands.155

Legends and cult — the Panathenaea, the Ephesian Wonder, and the Delphic oracle. Each poliad cult ran the city's largest rites. At Athens the Panathenaea, greatest of the four years, wound a procession up to the Acropolis to drape a freshly woven peplos on Athena's olive-wood statue, the state renewing its bond to its goddess. At Ephesus the Artemision — the temple of Artemis, four times the size of the Parthenon — was reckoned one of the Seven Wonders of the world, and the city styled itself the goddess's neokoros (“temple-warden”), the same word the town clerk uses in Acts 19:35. At Delphi Apollo's Pythia gave the oracles that steered Greek colonization and war, the shrine reckoned the omphalos, the navel of the earth; his island Delos was his birthplace and the treasury of a maritime league. Poseidon's Isthmian Games and Zeus's Olympic Games (whose temple held Pheidias' gold-and-ivory Zeus, a second of the Seven Wonders) were poliad festivals grown into panhellenic ones, and Zeus's oracle at Dodona spoke through a sacred oak. Every one of these was the cult of a particular god who owned a particular place.156

How Scripture places them — two confrontations and a “prince of Grecia.” Greece is one of the few nations whose city-cult the New Testament walks into and names. At Athens Paul's “spirit was stirred in him, when he saw the city wholly given to idolatry” (Acts 17:16), and on the Areopagus — the hill under the Acropolis of Athena Polias — he takes as his text an altar he found inscribed “TO THE UNKNOWN GOD” (Acts 17:23), preaching the Maker to a city that had a shrine for every power. At Ephesus the confrontation is with the poliad goddess by name: the silversmith Demetrius, whose trade was silver shrines of Artemis, raises the city against Paul lest “the temple of the great goddess Diana should be despised… whom all Asia and the world worshippeth,” and the theatre roars for two hours, “Great is Diana of the Ephesians” (Acts 19:27–28, 34) — the New Testament's most vivid scene of a named city-patron's cult defending its own. Behind these earthly cities Daniel's visionary names a heavenly counterpart: as the angel leaves off contending with “the prince of Persia,” he says, “lo, the prince of Grecia shall come” (Daniel 10:20) — a spirit-power set over Greece as the “prince of Persia” stands over Persia, the one place besides Persia where Scripture itself pairs a named nation with a named unseen power.157

…and how the Fathers place them — the gods of the cities as demons. The Christian witnesses who knew Greek religion best did not take its patrons for empty personifications; they took them for demons. Clement of Alexandria, himself steeped in Greek learning, devotes his Exhortation to the Greeks (Protrepticus, c. 195) to dismantling the city cults: the poliad gods, he argues, are dead men deified and living demons worshipped, and “demons who have obtained sacred honours may be discovered in crowds throughout your cities.” He gloats that the oracles have gone silent — the fountains of divination “are dead… shown… to have run dry,” the Pythian and Clarian and Didymaean springs stopped, Zeus's oak at Dodona decayed — the demons behind them, on his reading, driven out; and he even quotes a Sibylline oracle foretelling that the Ephesian Artemis's own temple would be swallowed by earthquake, so that “prostrate on the ground Ephesus shall wail.” This is the patristic “idol of demons” frame the argument pages weigh — the same reading Paul voices when he says the Gentiles “sacrifice to devils, and not to God,” and the Septuagint sharpens at Psalm 96(95):5, “all the gods of the nations are demons.”158

The divine-council cross-identification (a synthesis). On the axis this catalog cares about most — a real power bound to a people and place — the Greek poliad system is a tight fit: a named god owns a named city, receives its chief festival, and is felt to stand or fall with it (the tokens of the contest kept on the Acropolis, the two-hour riot for the “great goddess Diana”), a binding nearer the shape of Chemosh of Moab or Dagon of Philistia among the named national gods than the cosmic-realm lot the division-by-lot argument rates weak. And Daniel's “prince of Grecia” supplies, uniquely with Persia, a biblical-side unseen power over the very nation whose gods this entry rosters — so the “god of the city” and its heavenly “prince” sit closer here than almost anywhere in the catalog. But the load-bearing half of Deuteronomy 32:8–9 is missing all the same. There is no Most High who apportions the nations and keeps one as his portion: the Greek gods divide the world among themselves by lot, peers with no sovereign over them, and a polis chose or inherited its patron rather than being handed one from a primeval division of humanity — the same gap that limits Rome's evocatio, whose one-god-per-city cosmology the Greek poliad pattern most resembles. There is no apportioning assembly above the city-gods, no horizon of the foreign nations being distributed, and no disinheritance-and-reclamation arc; and a patron god for every town is exactly what independent human religiosity produces, so the parallel corroborates the general conception without proving a shared memory of the biblical allotment. Whether the power the Athenians called Athena, or Daniel's prince of Grecia, was a council-elohim, the Fathers' spirit-behind-an-idol, or a personified civic cult is the contested question carried on the named national gods (plausible) and across the wider “gods of the nations” group; this catalog banks the detail, and the apportionment reading of the Greek material stays where the division-by-lot argument leaves it. The underlying allotment is Deuteronomy 32:8–9 — possibility is not proof.159

Roma and the Capitoline Triad — the gods of Rome (the tutelary powers of the city, the guardian kept secret, and the gods that could be lured away)

Rome pressed the logic this catalog tracks harder than any other ancient state. It not only held that every city stood under a resident guardian — it built a whole apparatus around the premise: a triad of civic patrons enthroned over the city, a ritual (evocatio) for calling an enemy town's god out to desert it, and a state secret — the concealed name of Rome's own guardian — kept precisely so the rite could not be turned back on Rome. The evocatio datum is weighed on its own page — Rome's evocatio and the hidden name of its guardian, rated plausible as corroboration that a one-patron-power-per-polity cosmology was real, seriously held, and pitched (unlike the Greek cosmic lot) in the register of cities and peoples nearest Deuteronomy 32:8. This entry does not re-assess that; it gathers the concrete roster — the named guardians, the named cases, the secret name, the cult — and how Scripture and the Fathers place them.160

Names and epithets. Rome's own patrons were the Capitoline Triad — Jupiter Optimus Maximus (“Jupiter Best and Greatest”), with Juno Regina (“Queen Juno”) and Minerva beside him — enthroned in the great temple on the Capitoline Hill that was the religious heart of the state and the terminus of every triumph. Beneath that apex ran a dense grammar of guardian powers: the di tutelares (the tutelary deities of a place, people, or person), the genius loci (the guardian spirit of a place), and the Genius Populi Romani — the collective guardian spirit of the Roman people itself, given its own cult and image. Under the emperors this civic-guardian theology climbed onto the throne: the Genius Augusti, the guardian spirit of the reigning emperor, was sworn by and sacrificed to across the empire — the very oath and offering a Christian would die refusing. The bare habit — one named power (or a household of them) set over one named community — is the Roman religious map in miniature.161

A god bound so tightly to a city it could be lured away — the named cases. The sharpest proof that Rome treated the patron as a real asset of a people is evocatio: a besieging general summoning the enemy city's guardian to abandon it and cross to Rome before the walls fell. The earliest full narrative case is Juno Regina of Veii, called out of the Etruscan city by the dictator Camillus (traditionally 396 BC) — “Thee too, Queen Juno… I beseech, that thou wouldst follow us… to the City which is ours and which will soon be thine” — her cult-statue then escorted to a new temple on the Aventine. The fullest text preserves the formula spoken (by tradition) at the destruction of Carthage in 146 BC, addressed to the city's guardian “whether it be a god or a goddess” — later named as Tanit / Juno Caelestis — begging that power to “desert the people and community of Carthage, leave their sacred places, temples, and city” and “come to Rome.” The same treatment recurs on other Italic peoples: the chief Etruscan god Voltumna / Vortumnus was carried from Volsinii to Rome when that city fell. These named cases, and the question whether they carry weight for the biblical cosmic geography, are set out in full on the evocatio argument page; the catalog banks the roster.162

The guardian kept secret — and the goddess with the sealed mouth. The defensive corollary is the tell. Because Rome could summon out an enemy's god, it feared the rite turned against it, and so — Pliny reports as standing pontifical doctrine — “the tutelary deity of Rome has been kept a secret… to prevent any enemy from acting in a similar way,” and the city itself had a concealed sacred name it was religiously forbidden to utter, whose divulger, Valerius Soranus, “soon paid the penalty.” Antiquity attached this secret to Angerona, a goddess whose cult-statue in the temple of Volupia stood with her mouth bound and sealed (os obligatum et signatum), a finger raised to her lips — taken as the divine warden of the unspeakable name (some even guessed the hidden name was Angerona; a late source proposed Amor, “Roma” reversed). That a great state kept its guardian's identity as classified military intelligence is the hardest single sign of how literally the one-god-per-city cosmology was held: a patron you can lose is a patron worth hiding.163

Legend and cult — the captured gods in the triumph, and the gods that leave a doomed city. Roman practice made the traffic in patron powers visible. The triumph wound up the Capitoline to Jupiter's temple dragging the spoils of conquest — and among those spoils, the images of the conquered peoples' gods, carried captive through the streets, so that Rome literally paraded the deities of the nations it had beaten. And the belief that a city's guardians depart before it falls was Roman commonplace: the grammarian Servius, glossing Virgil's line that at Troy's fall “all the gods on whom this empire was stayed went forth, the shrines and altars left” (Aeneid 2.351), explains that the deities “were called away from the enemy before a siege” — exactly what evocatio was built to procure. The apex cult — Jupiter Optimus Maximus on the Capitol, where consuls sacrificed on entering office and generals discharged their vows — bound the fortunes of the state to the favour of a named resident god.164

How Scripture and the Fathers place them — the captive gods, and the gods that could not save Rome. The Christian apologists turned Rome's own theology against it. Tertullian, in his Apology (c. 197), takes up the boast “that, as a reward of their singular homage to religion, the Romans have been raised to such heights of power” and dismantles it: Rome grew great by sacrilege, not devotion, for empire is won by taking cities, and “that is a thing in which the gods have their share of calamity.” “They boast,” he says, “as many triumphs over the gods as over the nations; as many spoils of battle they have still, as there remain images of captive deities,” and “the poor gods submit to be adored by their enemies” — the evoked and conquered patrons of the nations, now Rome's captives. A century after Rome's own fall to Alaric (410), Augustine built the opening books of The City of God on the same point against those who blamed the sack on the abandoned old gods: Rome had entrusted itself to the vanquished gods of Troy, powers that could not even save their own city — “And these be the gods to whose protecting care the Romans were delighted to entrust their city!” he scoffs, and again, “Where, then, was the wisdom of entrusting Rome to the Trojan gods, who had demonstrated their weakness in the loss of Troy?” He piles up the swarm of petty Roman tutelaries, each assigned its scrap of function, precisely to show that a crowd of such gods had never protected anything. Behind both writers stands the “gods of the nations as demons” reading the argument pages weigh — Paul's word that the Gentiles “sacrifice to devils, and not to God,” and the Septuagint's Psalm 96(95):5, “all the gods of the nations are demons.”165

The divine-council cross-identification (a synthesis). On the axis this catalog cares about most — a real power bound to a people and place — Rome is the ancient world's most emphatic instance: not a myth about how the gods once divided the cosmos, but a living institution that only works if each city really has one resident guardian who can, in principle, change sides — and a state that hid its own guardian's name to keep it. That lands harder than the Greek cosmic lot, and in the Deuteronomy-32 register of cities and peoples rather than cosmic realms, which is why the evocatio argument rates it plausible as corroboration of the bare conception. But the load-bearing half of Deuteronomy 32:8–9 is missing all the same. There is no Most High apportioning the nations and keeping one as his portion: the Roman guardians are simply resident, with no sovereign parcelling them out; there is no disinheritance and no rebellion (the tutelary can be poached, but it is not a rebel arraigned as in Psalm 82); and the movement runs the wrong way for a “memory” of a fixed primeval allotment, since evocatio is precisely about reassigning a guardian by human petition. Independent Etruscan and Italic religion, plus the near-universal habit of tying a patron god to each land, produces the Roman rite with no transmission from the Song of Moses. So the parallel corroborates the general conception — and does so hard — without proving a shared memory of the biblical apportionment; whether the power the pontiffs called Juno Regina of Veii, or Rome's own hidden guardian, was a council-elohim, the Fathers' spirit-behind-an-idol, or a personified civic cult is the contested question carried on the named national gods (plausible) and across the wider “gods of the nations” group. This catalog banks the detail; the weighing stays where the evocatio argument leaves it. The underlying allotment is Deuteronomy 32:8–9 — possibility is not proof.166

The òrìṣà — the gods of the Yoruba (the city-state patrons of West Africa, beneath the supreme Olódùmarè)

West Africa lies on the far side of the Sahara from the biblical world, and the Yoruba city-states developed their religion in complete isolation from it — yet they framed the world in exactly the two-storey shape this catalog tracks: a supreme creator at the apex, and beneath him a pantheon of òrìṣà, many of them bound to a particular town, kingdom, or river. It is the catalog's widest-flung independent instance, and its clearest sub-Saharan one. Whether that independence corroborates the Deuteronomy 32 pattern or instead shows the pattern to be a human constant is weighed, and rated plausible, on the Yoruba-òrìṣà argument; this entry gathers the concrete detail behind that verdict — the supreme high god, the town↔òrìṣà roster, the deified king of Ọ̀yọ́, the cradle at Ilé-Ifẹ̀, the Ifá corpus, and the missionary who read the idols as things to be abolished.167

Names and epithets. At the apex stands Olódùmarè (also Olórun, “owner of heaven”), the supreme creator — so exalted that, unlike the òrìṣà, he has no temple, image, or priesthood of his own and is approached through the divinities as intermediaries. Beneath him is the pantheon of òrìṣà (the primordial divinities are the irúnmọlẹ̀, reckoned in tradition at a symbolic “four hundred and one,” a figure of innumerability rather than a census). Among them: Ọbàtálá, the arch-divinity and moulder of human bodies, oldest of the irúnmọlẹ̀; Ọ̀rúnmìlà, the òrìṣà of wisdom and the patron of Ifá divination; Ṣàngó (Shango), the òrìṣà of thunder and justice; Ògún, the god of iron, war, and the forge; and the river-goddesses Ọ̀ṣun, Ọya, and Yemọja. Each carries oral praise-poems (oríkì) that name its powers, its town, and its lineage. The Yoruba theologian E. Bọlaji Idowu called the resulting system a “diffused monotheism”: not a flat polytheism of rival gods but many powers understood as ministers and conceptualizations of the one Olódùmarè, supreme over them all.168

Named patrons for named peoples — and the tier above them. The Yoruba habit is to bind a named power to a named town, and to carry that place in the god's own praise-name. Ṣàngó is the patron of the imperial city of Ọ̀yọ́, whose fourth king he is remembered to have been. Ọ̀ṣun is the goddess of the river that bears her name and of the city of Òṣogbo on its bank, where the Osun-Osogbo Sacred Grove — a UNESCO World Heritage Site — is “regarded as the abode of the goddess.” Ògún carries the praise-name Ògún Onírè, “Ògún, proprietor of the town of Ire,” and is remembered to have sunk into the earth at Ire-Ekiti. Ọya is the goddess of the Niger (Odò-Ọya); Yemọja presides over the Ogun River with her chief shrine at Abeokuta; and Ọbàtálá belongs to Ilé-Ifẹ̀. So a real power stands behind this people in this place, named to it — the very habit by which Scripture names Chemosh of Moab or Dagon of Philistia among the named national gods. And over the whole roster stands the second tier: the territorial òrìṣà are not peers with no one above them but ministers beneath the supreme Olódùmarè — a remote high god set over a graded order of still-local patrons.169

Legends — the deified king of Ọ̀yọ́, the creation at Ilé-Ifẹ̀, and the divination corpus. The sharpest legend is Ṣàngó's, because the tradition remembers him as a historical ruler raised to divinity. Samuel Johnson's History of the Yorubas makes him “the fourth King of the Yorubas,” a restless warrior-king who reigned seven years, “was fond of making charms,” and was “said to have the knowledge of some preparation by which he could attract lightning”; by Johnson's account his reign ended in disgrace and he hanged himself, after which “he was afterwards deified, and is still worshipped by all of the Yoruba race as the god of thunder and lightning” — his devotees developing the counter-tradition Ọba kò so (“the king did not hang”), that he ascended rather than died. The charter of the world is set at Ilé-Ifẹ̀, the “cradle of the race”: Olódùmarè commissions the making of dry land upon the primordial water (by Ọbàtálá, or, when he fails, by Odùduwà, who descends from heaven on a chain), and from Ifẹ̀ the royal lineages disperse to found the several Yoruba kingdoms, each owing precedence to Ifẹ̀. And the theology is carried in a vast oral canon — Ifá, the divination system of Ọ̀rúnmìlà, organized into 256 Odù whose verses the babaláwo recite.170

A gods-of-the-nations-as-idols reception — the missionary of the Niger. The Christian witness who knew Yoruba religion best did not take its powers for empty personifications; he read them as idols to be abolished. Samuel Ajayi Crowther — a Yoruba, enslaved as a boy and freed by an anti-slavery patrol, who became the first African Anglican bishop and the translator of the Bible into Yoruba — in his Niger Mission (1872) writes of converts who had separated themselves from their former associates “in idolatry,” and looks for the day “when the idols shall be utterly abolished.” His Yoruba Scriptures put into the mouths of the Yoruba themselves the very verses behind that reading — Paul's word that the Gentiles “sacrifice to devils, and not to God,” and the Septuagint's Psalm 96(95):5, “all the gods of the nations are demons.” Here again a missionary took the gods of an unreached people to be powers to be displaced, the same “idol of demons” frame the argument pages weigh.171

The divine-council cross-identification (a synthesis). On the axes this catalog cares about most, the Yoruba supply two at once: a real power bound to each people and place (Ṣàngó of Ọ̀yọ́, Ọ̀ṣun of Òṣogbo, Ògún of Ire) and a single supreme creator set over the whole pantheon (Olódùmarè), all of it in a sub-Saharan hemisphere no memory of Babel can reach — a two-tier shape that places it with the stronger comparative parallels (China, the Andes, Mesopotamia) rather than the flat ones, and firmer at the apex than the peer-gods of the Greek lot or the sovereign-less guardians of Rome's evocatio. But the load-bearing half of Deuteronomy 32:8–9 is missing all the same. Olódùmarè is supreme over the òrìṣà but does not apportion the nations to them — the apex is a supremacy, not an act of dividing humanity and handing each people its god (weaker here, on that axis, than the Andean Viracocha, who at least divides the peoples). The territorial òrìṣà rise from within a people — a deified king, a personified river — not as powers assigned over it from above, the same defeater that limits the Andean huacas; the scope is intra-Yoruba, with no horizon of the foreign nations and no disinheritance-and-reclamation arc; and its very independence, the feature that makes the fit so striking, converts the parallel into a structural recurrence that a human-universal reading explains at least as well as a supernatural one (belief, not the beings; possibility ≠ proof) — while the New-World diaspora that carries the same òrìṣà (Santería's Changó and Ọ̀ṣun, Candomblé's Xangô and Oxum) is that one tradition's survival across the slave trade, not a fresh independent witness. So bank it precisely: the Yoruba are the catalog's widest-flung independent case of a power bound to a people and place beneath a supreme high god, weighed comparatively to plausible on the Yoruba-òrìṣà argument, sitting with the parallels that bind a power to a people or place under a sovereign apex. The recurring one-power-per-portion conception and its gap to the specific biblical allotment are weighed across the “gods of the nations” group, and the being-behind-the-cult question on the named national gods (plausible). The underlying allotment is Deuteronomy 32:8–9 — possibility is not proof.172
