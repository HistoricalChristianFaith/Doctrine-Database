Justin Martyr on the Divine Council

Dates: c. 160 (Justin Martyr, c. 100–165; Wikipedia) Summary: Justin is a two-sided witness. He affirms that God set angels over the nations and that real spiritual powers—fallen angels and the demons begotten of them—stand behind pagan cult, yet he holds that the “gods of the nations” are idols of demons and not gods. And when he comes to Psalm 82 itself, he reads its elohim not as a council of created divine beings but as men—human beings made in God's image for incorruption, who instead die like Adam.

Claims & evidence

God set angels over the nations; the transgressing angels begat the demons behind pagan cult

…committed the care of men and of all things under heaven to angels whom He appointed over them. But the angels transgressed this appointment, and were captivated by love of women, and begat children who are those that are called demons; and besides, they afterwards subdued the human race to themselves, partly by magical writings, and partly by fears and the punishments they occasioned, and partly by teaching them to offer sacrifices, and incense, and libations, of which things they stood in need after they were enslaved by lustful passions…

— The Second Apology, Chapter V, source. Justin preserves the administrative shape of the divine council—heavenly beings appointed by God over men—but the beings are created angels, and those who fell became the demonic powers masquerading behind the sacrifices of the nations.

The “gods of the nations” are idols of demons, and not gods

…show us that the Spirit of prophecy admits another God besides the Maker of all things, taking care not to speak of the sun and moon, which, it is written, God has given to the nations to worship as gods…For such expressions are used, not as if they really were gods, but because the Scripture is teaching us that the true God, who made all things, is Lord alone of those who are reputed gods and lords. And in order that the Holy Spirit may convince [us] of this, He said by the holy David, ‘The gods of the nations, reputed gods, are idols of demons, and not gods;’ and He denounces a curse on those who worship them.

— Dialogue with Trypho, Chapter LV, source. The words are Trypho's, but they state a scriptural maxim (from the Psalm) that Justin does not contest: whatever powers stand behind them, the nations' “gods” are not a rank of real deities but “reputed gods,” idols of demons. The one true God is “Lord alone of those who are reputed gods and lords.”

Psalm 82's “gods” are men—made for incorruption, who die like Adam

‘God standeth in the congregation of gods; He judgeth among the gods…I said, Ye are gods, and are all children of the Most High. But ye die like men, and fall like one of the princes…’ But in the version of the Seventy it is written, ‘Behold, ye die like men, and fall like one of the princes,’ in order to manifest the disobedience of men,—I mean of Adam and Eve,—and the fall of one of the princes, i.e., of him who was called the serpent, who fell with a great overthrow, because he deceived Eve. But as my discourse is not intended to touch on this point, but to prove to you that the Holy Ghost reproaches men because they were made like God, free from suffering and death, provided that they kept His commandments, and were deemed deserving of the name of His sons, and yet they, becoming like Adam and Eve, work out death for themselves…thereby it is demonstrated that all men are deemed worthy of becoming “gods,” and of having power to become sons of the Highest; and shall be each by himself judged and condemned like Adam and Eve.

— Dialogue with Trypho, Chapter CXXIV, source. This is Justin's own reading of Psalm 82, and it plants the pole opposite the “real created elohim” reading: the “gods” and “children of the Most High” of the psalm are human beings, made in God's image and meant for deathless sonship, who forfeit that immortality by imitating Adam and Eve's disobedience and so “die like men.”

Notes

  • Justin holds both halves at once: real demonic powers stand behind the pagan cult of the nations (Second Apology V; cf. the appointment of angels over men), and Psalm 82's “gods” are humans destined for deification who lost their immortality (Dialogue CXXIV). The two are not in tension for him—the first is about who animates idolatry, the second about who the psalm's elohim are.
  • So Justin does not read Psalm 82 as a council of created elohim. The council's administrative structure survives in his thought only as an order of created angels set over the nations, some of whom fell; the psalm's “gods” he assigns to the human, deification-and-fall register. He is thus an early witness to the patristic “the elohim of Psalm 82 are men” reading.
