Paul

Paul is the apostle about whom the New Testament says most and whose death it does not narrate. Almost half of Acts follows him, and thirteen letters carry his name, so that his origin (a Pharisee of Tarsus, a Roman citizen, “an Hebrew of the Hebrews” who had persecuted the church before his conversion on the Damascus road), his three missionary journeys, the Jerusalem council, and his arrest and voyage under guard to Rome are recorded at length. Acts stops with Paul in his own hired house at Rome, preaching “no man forbidding him,” and says nothing of how he died; the later tradition concerns what happened after Acts 28. Within a generation Clement of Rome writes that Paul, “having taught righteousness to the whole world,” had “come to the extreme limit of the west” and “suffered martyrdom under the prefects”; Ignatius names Peter and Paul as apostles to the Romans, Dionysius of Corinth has the two martyred “at the same time,” the Roman presbyter Gaius points to the apostles’ “trophies” on the Vatican and the Ostian way, and Tertullian, Eusebius and Jerome report that he was executed at Rome under Nero, and, from the Acts of Paul and Tertullian on, that he was beheaded, a citizen’s death by the sword rather than the cross. Clement speaks of “the extreme limit of the west,” the Muratorian canon of a journey to Spain (the mission announced in Romans 15), and Jerome of a mission “also in the West”; whether these refer to Spain is discussed on its own page below. Eusebius and Jerome speak of a release and a second imprisonment, the setting in which they place the Pastoral epistles. The second-century Acts of Paul supplies the earliest physical portrait — “small in size, bald-headed, bandy-legged… with eyebrows meeting, rather long-nosed” — along with the romance of Thecla and a baptized lion. This page sets out the sources, earliest extant source first — the New Testament, Clement of Rome, the Acts of Paul, Tertullian, Eusebius, Jerome, and modern scholarship — with the two disputed questions (did he reach Spain? was he beheaded at Rome under Nero?) on their own pages below. This is a biographical tradition-timeline: its “witnesses” are the datable sources that report on him. For the shared ladder of sources, see the apostles hub.

Timeline

The New Testament (c. 34 to the 60s)

The New Testament says more about Paul than about any other apostle. Saul of Tarsus in Cilicia — a Roman citizen by birth, of the tribe of Benjamin, “an Hebrew of the Hebrews,” a Pharisee schooled in Jerusalem “at the feet of Gamaliel,” a tent-maker by trade — first appears consenting to the stoning of Stephen; he “made havock of the church.” Acts narrates that on the road to Damascus the risen Christ struck him down and called the persecutor “a chosen vessel”; he became the apostle to the Gentiles. Three missionary journeys carried him across Asia Minor and Greece; the Jerusalem council (Acts 15; Galatians 2) settled that his Gentile converts need not be circumcised; and a riot at the Temple ended in his arrest, his appeal “unto Caesar” as a citizen, and a storm-wrecked voyage to Rome, where Acts leaves him under house-arrest preaching “with all confidence, no man forbidding him.” He was not one of the Twelve — he came to the risen Christ last of all, “as of one born out of due time” — yet by his own account he “laboured more abundantly than they all,” and he wrote to Rome of a further plan, to preach in Spain, at the western edge of the world; Acts does not narrate it.1

Clement of Rome (c. 96)

The earliest extant writer after the New Testament to speak of Paul’s end is Clement, bishop of Rome, writing to Corinth perhaps thirty years after the event, from the city where it happened. Setting Peter and Paul before his readers as “the greatest and most righteous pillars” martyred in “our own generation,” Clement writes that Paul, after being “seven times” imprisoned, exiled, and stoned, “gained the illustrious reputation due to his faith, having taught righteousness to the whole world, and come to the extreme limit of the west, and suffered martyrdom under the prefects.” Whether “the extreme limit of the west,” written from Rome, refers to Spain, and how far the later details of the Roman death go back to this notice, are taken up on the disputed-reading pages below.2

The Acts of Paul and Acts of Paul and Thecla (c. 160)

The second-century Acts of Paul is a narrative romance, whose best-known episode is the tale of Thecla, the noble virgin of Iconium who follows Paul, survives fire and beasts, and baptizes herself. Tertullian reports that its author, a presbyter in Asia, was convicted and removed from office, “confessing that he had done it from love of Paul.” It contains the earliest surviving physical portrait of the apostle, “a man small in size, bald-headed, bandy-legged, well-built, with eyebrows meeting, rather long-nosed, full of grace — for sometimes he seemed like a man, and sometimes he had the countenance of an angel.” Its closing Martyrdom has Paul beheaded at Rome under Nero, with milk spurting from the severed neck. Its portrait shaped how Paul was pictured in Western art (⚠ unverified).3

Tertullian (c. 200)

Writing from Carthage, Tertullian points inquirers to the churches the apostles founded, and to Rome above all — “how happy is its church, on which apostles poured forth all their doctrine along with their blood! where Peter endures a passion like his Lord’s, where Paul wins his crown in a death like John’s.” Tertullian does not say which John; the same sentence turns next to the Apostle John’s ordeal in boiling oil. A note to the Ante-Nicene Fathers translation identifies this John as “The Baptist’s” (n. 386); with John identified as John the Baptist, who was beheaded, the comparison sets Paul’s death by the sword, the citizen’s execution, beside Peter’s cross. In the Scorpiace he writes “that Paul is beheaded… a birth suited to Roman citizenship,” and names Nero as the emperor who first “stained with blood the rising faith” at Rome. The two works together have Paul beheaded and Peter crucified, both at Rome, under Nero.4

Eusebius (c. 325)

Eusebius gathers the threads and adds the physical monuments. Following Acts, he notes that Paul was “sent bound to Rome” under Festus and spent two years in open custody; but he then records the tradition — behind the Pastoral epistles — that Paul was acquitted at that first trial, “sent again upon the ministry of preaching,” and only on a second visit to Rome “suffered martyrdom.” For the death he reports that Paul “was beheaded in Rome itself,” and the Roman presbyter Gaius (c. 200) points to the apostles’ “trophies” (tropaia) — “But I can show the trophies of the apostles. For if you will go to the Vatican or to the Ostian way, you will find the trophies of those who laid the foundations of this church” — while Dionysius of Corinth attests that Peter and Paul “suffered martyrdom at the same time.” Quoting Origen, Eusebius sums up the mission: “Paul… preached the Gospel of Christ from Jerusalem to Illyricum, and afterwards suffered martyrdom in Rome under Nero.”5

Jerome (c. 393)

In his catalogue of Christian authors, Jerome opens the entry on Paul with a biography and closes it with the fullest early statement of the martyrdom. He dates the death — “in the fourteenth year of Nero,” the same day as Peter — and names the place of burial: Paul “was beheaded at Rome for Christ’s sake and was buried in the Ostian way,” where the basilica of San Paolo fuori le Mura stands over the traditional tomb. Jerome also states the release and second imprisonment — Paul was “dismissed by Nero, that the gospel of Christ might be preached also in the West” — and reads 2 Timothy’s “I was delivered out of the mouth of the lion” as Paul’s own word on Nero.6

Modern scholarship

Sean McDowell (The Fate of the Apostles, 2015) grades the evidence for the martyrdoms of Paul, Peter, and James son of Zebedee at the highest level of probability, and F. F. Bruce (Peter, Stephen, James, and John, 1979) treats the Roman deaths of Peter and Paul. No source surveyed here reports a different end for Paul than his death at Rome. Whether Paul was released between two Roman imprisonments and reached Spain, and how far the manner and the year of his death go back, are discussed on the disputed-reading pages below, with the Pastoral epistles, the silence of Acts, and the absence of a Spanish local tradition of Paul.7

Disputed readings

How the sources have been read on Paul’s last years and on the manner and date of his death.

  • Did Paul reach Spain? — Clement’s “extreme limit of the west,” the Muratorian canon, the Acts of Peter, and the release behind the Pastoral epistles.
  • Was Paul beheaded at Rome under Nero? — how far the sword, the year and the tomb on the Ostian way go back.
