The Council of Trent on the seven sacraments

Dates: 1547 (Session 7, under Paul III) · Wikipedia
Summary: Against Luther's reduction, the seventh session of the Council of Trent (3 March 1547) turned the enumeration into defended dogma: the sacraments of the new law are seven — "neither more nor less" — all instituted by Christ, and to deny it, or to deny that any one of them is truly a sacrament, is anathema. The number Peter Lombard first fixed and Florence first decreed is here made a test of orthodoxy.

Claims & evidence

Seven — neither more nor less — all instituted by Christ

If any one saith, that the sacraments of the New Law were not all instituted by Jesus Christ, our Lord; or, that they are more, or less, than seven, to wit, Baptism, Confirmation, the Eucharist, Penance, Extreme Unction, Order, and Matrimony; or even that any one of these seven is not truly and properly a sacrament; let him be anathema.

— Council of Trent, Session 7 (1547), Canons on the Sacraments in General, Canon 1, trans. J. Waterworth, source.1

Notes

  • The count becomes a boundary. Where Florence had stated the seven, Trent defends them: the canon is cast as an anathema precisely because there is now an organized denial to answer. Both halves of the medieval settlement are fixed — the definition (a true and proper sacrament, instituted by Christ) and the number (seven, closed at both ends). The later Session 14 (1551) went on to define penance in detail.
  • The unresolved question. Trent settles the count for the Roman communion but not the underlying dispute Luther raised: what makes a rite a sacrament — dominical institution, a visible sign, the conferring of grace — and whether all seven meet that test. That definitional question is the one on which the whole enumeration turns.
  • Trent across the wiki. The Council of Trent is also a witness on penance (Session 14), the Real Presence, justification, and the Old Testament canon.
