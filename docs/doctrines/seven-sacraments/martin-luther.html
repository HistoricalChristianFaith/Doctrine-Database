Martin Luther on the sacraments

Dates: 1520 · Wikipedia
Summary: In The Babylonian Captivity of the Church (1520), Luther broke the medieval consensus at its root. Applying his own criterion — a sacrament must have both a sign divinely instituted and a promise of forgiveness attached to it — he denied that there are seven, opened with three (baptism, penance, the bread), and closed strictly with two: baptism and the Lord's Supper. He also noted, tellingly, that the lone ancient witness cited for seven in fact counts only six.

Claims & evidence

"I must deny that there are seven sacraments"

To begin with, I must deny that there are seven sacraments, and for the present maintain that there are but three: baptism, penance, and the bread. All three have been subjected to a miserable captivity by the Roman curia, and the church has been robbed of all her liberty. Yet, if I were to speak according to the usage of the Scriptures, I should have only one single sacrament, but with three sacramental signs.

— Martin Luther, The Babylonian Captivity of the Church (1520), trans. A. T. W. Steinhäuser et al. (Luther's Works, American Edition, vol. 36), source.1

Strictly, only two

By the treatise's close, applying his sign-plus-promise test, Luther drops even penance from the strict count:

Hence there are, strictly speaking, but two sacraments in the church of God—baptism and the bread. For only in these two do we find both the divinely instituted sign and the promise of forgiveness of sins. The sacrament of penance, which I added to these two, lacks the divinely instituted visible sign, and is, as I have said, nothing but a way and a return to baptism.

— Luther, Babylonian Captivity (LW 36), source.2

The lone ancient witness for seven counts only six

Luther turns the historical argument against the number: the one father cited for the sevenfold scheme (Pseudo-Dionysius) does not, in fact, reach seven:

I am well aware that this is the one writer of antiquity who is cited in support of the seven sacraments, although he omits marriage and so has only six.

— Luther, Babylonian Captivity (LW 36), source.3

Notes

  • What Luther was rejecting. His target is exactly the definition Hugh and Lombard had built and Florence had ratified — a sacrament as any sign that confers grace — which had let the list grow to seven. Luther's narrower test (a dominically instituted sign joined to a promise of forgiveness) reduces the field to the two rites explicitly commanded by Christ with an attached word of promise. Confirmation, marriage, orders, and extreme unction fail the test for lack of a divinely instituted sign; penance, though close, lacks the outward sign.
  • The count as a battleground. Luther's reduction is precisely what Trent answers in 1547, defining "neither more nor less than seven" under anathema. The definitional dispute — what counts as a sacrament — is the real hinge of the whole disagreement over the number.
  • On sourcing. Wording varies by edition; the quotations above follow the revised Steinhäuser translation in Luther's Works vol. 36. The older 1915 Holman rendering of the opening reads "there are only three, baptism, penance, and the bread… only one sacrament, and three sacramental signs."
