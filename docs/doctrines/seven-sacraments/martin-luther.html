Martin Luther on the sacraments

Dates: 1520 · Wikipedia
Summary: In The Babylonian Captivity of the Church (1520), Luther rejected the count of seven. Applying his criterion — a sacrament must have both a sign divinely instituted and a promise of forgiveness attached to it — he denied that there are seven, opened with three (baptism, penance, the bread), and closed strictly with two: baptism and the Lord's Supper. He also noted that the one ancient writer cited for seven counts six.

Claims & evidence

"I must deny that there are seven sacraments"

To begin with, I must deny that there are seven sacraments, and for the present maintain that there are but three: baptism, penance, and the bread. All three have been subjected to a miserable captivity by the Roman curia, and the church has been robbed of all her liberty. Yet, if I were to speak according to the usage of the Scriptures, I should have only one single sacrament, but with three sacramental signs.

— Martin Luther, The Babylonian Captivity of the Church (1520), trans. A. T. W. Steinhäuser et al. (Luther's Works, American Edition, vol. 36), source.1

Strictly, only two

By the treatise's close, applying his sign-plus-promise test, Luther drops penance from the strict count:

Hence there are, strictly speaking, but two sacraments in the church of God—baptism and the bread. For only in these two do we find both the divinely instituted sign and the promise of forgiveness of sins. The sacrament of penance, which I added to these two, lacks the divinely instituted visible sign, and is, as I have said, nothing but a way and a return to baptism.

— Luther, Babylonian Captivity (LW 36), source.2

Luther on Pseudo-Dionysius

Luther answers the appeal to Pseudo-Dionysius, cited for the sevenfold scheme:

I am well aware that this is the one writer of antiquity who is cited in support of the seven sacraments, although he omits marriage and so has only six.

— Luther, Babylonian Captivity (LW 36), source.3

Notes

  • What Luther was rejecting. Luther sets his test (a divinely instituted sign joined to a promise of forgiveness) against the scholastic definition of a sacrament as a sign that confers grace, and argues that the scholastics "are compelled by their own definition either to admit that penance is not a sacrament and thus to reduce their number, or else to bring forth another definition of a sacrament." On his test confirmation, marriage, orders, and extreme unction lack a divinely instituted sign joined to a promise; penance, though close, lacks the outward sign. Of Dionysius's six he adds: "we read nothing at all about these 'sacraments' in the rest of the fathers; nor do they ever regard them as sacraments when they speak of these things. For the invention of sacraments is of recent date." He also writes: "I do not say this because I condemn the seven sacraments, but because I deny that they can be proved from the Scriptures."4
  • Trent's response. The seventh session of Trent (1547) anathematized anyone who says the sacraments "are more, or less, than seven." How Luther's count and Trent's relate to the earlier record is set out on the disputed reading.
  • On sourcing. Wording varies by edition; the quotations above follow the revised Steinhäuser translation in Luther's Works vol. 36. The older 1915 Holman rendering of the opening reads "there are only three, baptism, penance, and the bread… only one sacrament, and three sacramental signs."
