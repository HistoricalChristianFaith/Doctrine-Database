Were the seven always practiced and only later recognized, not invented? (the discovery/recognition reading)

Type: historical / doctrinal argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — the reading weighed here is the Catholic reply to the construction thesis: the seven were always practiced even where they were not yet enumerated, so the number Lombard fixed is a later recognition of an apostolic reality, not an invention — just as the church fixed the canon of Scripture late for books that were early. Strong where it rests on the genuine antiquity of the individual rites and the real force of the canon analogy; weak where the selection of exactly these seven, their closure, and their parity as grace-conferring turn out to depend on a definitional criterion that is itself twelfth-century. Genuinely disputed between the confessions, strong by one discipline's lights and weak by another's (contested) · advanced by the Council of Trent, the Catechism of the Catholic Church, and the development-of-doctrine tradition (Newman).

Related arguments: the sevenfold count — see also Is the sevenfold count a twelfth-century construction rather than an apostolic datum? (the construction reading)

The claim

The construction thesis is granted its narrow point: no closed list of seven appears before the twelfth century. The reading assessed here denies that this settles anything doctrinal. Its thesis is that the number is a discovery, not a construction — the church came to recognize, under the Spirit's guidance and over centuries, a sacramental treasure it had received from Christ and practised from the beginning. The Catechism states it exactly:

As she has done for the canon of Sacred Scripture and for the doctrine of the faith, the Church, by the power of the Spirit who guides her “into all truth,” has gradually recognized this treasure received from Christ and, as the faithful steward of God's mysteries, has determined its “dispensation.” Thus the Church has discerned over the centuries that among liturgical celebrations there are seven that are, in the strict sense of the term, sacraments instituted by the Lord.

— Catechism of the Catholic Church §1117.1 The load-bearing move is the canon analogy: that a late enumeration of pre-existing realities is exactly how the church has always worked, so lateness of the list is no argument against apostolicity of the things.

Who advances it

The reading is the Catholic tradition's answer to the Reformers. Trent supplies its dogmatic backbone — the seven were “instituted by Jesus Christ our Lord” — and Catholic reference works concede the late enumeration precisely in order to relocate the continuity in the practice: all seven “existed and were used by the Church for centuries” before Lombard drew the list.2 Its mature philosophical form is John Henry Newman's development of doctrine: a truth held implicitly from the start is later articulated, so definition follows possession. Against it stands the construction reading in its strong form, which holds that the late count reflects a late and partly-invented doctrine.

The evidence for

The individual rites are genuinely ancient. This is the reading's real strength, and it is considerable. Baptism and the eucharist are apostolic beyond dispute; the laying-on of hands for office appears in the New Testament and 1 Clement; the reconciliation of penitents is attested from the second century (see the penance timeline); the anointing of the sick is prescribed in James 5:14–15 (“let him call for the elders of the church; and let them pray over him, anointing him with oil”);3 the blessing of marriage and post-baptismal chrismation are early. So the sceptic who says “the seven were invented in 1150” is wrong about the rites: what was assembled late was largely practised early. Even Pseudo-Dionysius, whose catalogue reaches a different six, is evidence for this point — his rites were all in use; he simply grouped them differently.4

The canon analogy is not a dodge. The church did fix the biblical canon late (fourth century and beyond) for books that were first-century; no one infers from the lateness of the list that the Gospels are late. If a late enumeration is compatible with apostolic origin there, the same courtesy is owed here in principle. The recognition reading is therefore coherent: a settled number can be the terminus of a real development rather than a fresh creation.

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The antiquity-of-the-rites point holds firmly, and with it the refutation of the crude form of the construction thesis. The canon analogy also holds as far as it goes: lateness of a list does not by itself impeach the apostolicity of its contents. To this extent the recognition reading is on solid ground.

The selection, not just the count, was constructed. The analogy strains at the joint the construction thesis actually presses. The canon was closed by a criterion the church claimed to find (apostolic authorship / reception), and the disputed books were few. The sacramental seven were closed by a criterion Lombard sharpened — a sign that not merely signifies but efficiently causes the grace it signifies — and that criterion is itself twelfth-century.5 Run a different criterion (Pseudo-Dionysius's, or a looser Augustinian sign-definition) and the church had long “recognized” monastic consecration, funerals, the blessing of water, footwashing, and royal unction as sacred mysteries too. So the number seven is not read off the practice; it is produced by choosing which practices count and by what test. That is recognition doing the work of construction — the very thing the reading denies.

The parity claim is where it is thinnest. Recognition must show not merely that seven rites were practised but that they were held to be sacraments in one and the same strict sense — grace-conferring, Christ-instituted, on a par. Here the earliest evidence resists: the father who numbers them calls the sacraments of the new dispensation “very few” and names two, and marriage's admission to the strict list leans on the Vulgate's rendering of Ephesians 5:32, a proof-text rated weak. The ancient church practised marriage-blessing and anointing; that it ranked them alongside baptism as instrumental causes of grace is exactly what is not attested early — and that parity, not the bare practice, is what the sevenfold dogma asserts.

It can prove too much, or too little. If “recognized over the centuries” licenses closing the list at seven, the same logic once licensed the East's and Pseudo-Dionysius's other numbers; the reading needs an independent reason why seven is the recognition and the sixes and eights are not — and that reason is supplied by the Catholic magisterium's authority to define, which is precisely the further premise a Protestant denies. So the recognition reading does not stand on the historical evidence alone; it stands or falls with a prior doctrine of the church's authority to fix the number.

Assessment

Contested. The reading is strong exactly where it insists the individual rites are ancient — there the crude “invented in 1150” charge fails, and the canon analogy legitimately blocks the inference from a late list to late things. But it is weak where it needs to be strong: the selection of exactly these seven, their closure, and their parity as grace-conferring rest on a definitional criterion that is itself a twelfth-century development and on a subsequent claim of magisterial authority to define — not on a uniform apostolic reckoning the church merely read off. Whether that counts as recognizing an apostolic datum or as constructing one is genuinely disputed between the confessions, and the answer tracks a prior question (the church's authority to define doctrine) that this page cannot settle: strong by the development tradition's lights, weak by the strict enumeration-historian's, with no neutral tiebreaker — hence contested. What is not in doubt, and is rated separately, is that the count itself is late: see the construction reading (sound).
