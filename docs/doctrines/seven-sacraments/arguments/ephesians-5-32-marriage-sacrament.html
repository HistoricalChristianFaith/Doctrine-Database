Does Ephesians 5:32 make marriage a grace-conferring sacrament?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: weak as a proof-text — the verse soundly teaches that marriage signifies the union of Christ and the Church, but the further step to marriage as an instrumental cause of grace, numbered among the seven, is carried by the Latin word sacramentum, not by Paul's Greek · advanced by Peter Lombard (with Aquinas, Florence, and Trent).

The claim

When Paul, having quoted “a man shall leave his father and mother and be joined to his wife, and the two shall become one flesh” (Genesis 2:24), adds “This is a great mystery” (Ephesians 5:31–32), the Latin Vulgate renders his Greek mystērion by sacramentum: sacramentum hoc magnum est.1 A Latin reader thus met a verse apparently calling marriage “a great sacrament,” and the medieval West read it as scriptural warrant for numbering matrimony among the grace-conferring sacraments. Thomas Aquinas states the reading in its mature form:

He goes on to interpret this mystically, and he says This is a great sacrament, it is the symbol of a sacred reality, namely, the union of Christ and the Church… Notice here that four Sacraments are termed great. Baptism by reason of its effect… Confirmation by reason of its minister… the Eucharist because of what it contains, the Whole Christ; and Matrimony by reason of its signification, for it symbolizes the union of Christ and the Church.

— Thomas Aquinas, Commentary on Ephesians, ch. 5, lect. 10, source.2 The interpretation being assessed is the load-bearing one: that Ephesians 5:32 supports counting marriage as one of the seven sacraments — a divinely instituted sign that confers the grace it signifies.

Who advances it

The reading is the Latin sacramental tradition's, running from the Vulgate's sacramentum through Peter Lombard, who first fixed marriage in the sevenfold list in Book IV of the Sentences,3 to Aquinas's exegesis above, to its conciliar definition at Florence (1439) and Trent (1547). Against it stand the Greek fathers, who read the verse without any such inference, and the Reformers — Luther explicitly denies marriage a sacrament in The Babylonian Captivity of the Church.

The evidence for

What the verse genuinely establishes is strong and near-universally held: marriage is a God-given type of the union of Christ and the Church. Paul says so plainly (“but I speak concerning Christ and the Church”), and the fathers, East and West, amplify it. John Chrysostom calls the one-flesh union “a great mystery” precisely because it prefigures Christ, who “left the Father, He came down, and came to the Bride, and became one Spirit.”4 Jerome reports Gregory of Nazianzus calling the passage “full of ineffable promises… It requires a divine heart in its interpretation,” to be read “as Christ in relation to the church.”5 On this the medieval reading builds honestly: a rite that so richly signifies the redemptive union is a fitting candidate for a sacred sign, and Aquinas grounds matrimony's sacramentality exactly here, “by reason of its signification.” That marriage carries a real, Christ-directed sacred meaning is sound.

Adversarial assessment

What holds. Marriage signifies the union of Christ and the Church — that typological reading of Ephesians 5:31–32 is the plain sense of Paul's own gloss and the consensus of the fathers. Nothing below disturbs it. The question is only whether the verse carries the additional weight of numbering marriage among the grace-conferring rites.

The word sacramentum is a translation artifact, not an argument. Paul wrote mystērion, “mystery” — a hidden reality now disclosed — and the Vulgate renders mystērion by sacramentum in a whole string of places where no rite, still less a grace-conferring one, is in view: “great is the mystery of godliness” (magnum est pietatis sacramentum, 1 Timothy 3:16) and “the mystery of the seven stars” (sacramentum septem stellarum, Apocalypse 1:20), among others.6 No one reads “the mystery of godliness” or the seven stars as sacraments; the Latin word simply means “a sacred secret” there, as it does at Ephesians 5:32. So the verse cannot be made to prove marriage a sacrament in the technical, numbered sense merely because the same Latin noun later became the fixed term for the seven rites — that is reading the twelfth-century meaning back into a fourth-century translation of a first-century Greek word.

The Greek fathers who read the verse draw no such inference. Chrysostom, Theophylact, Theodoret, and Jerome all take the “great mystery” to be the thing signified — the Christ–Church union typified by marriage — not marriage as an instrumental cause of sanctifying grace. That distinction (sign of a mystery vs. numbered grace-conferring rite) is exactly what the verse leaves open and what the later Latin synthesis supplies from elsewhere.

Augustine's “great sacrament” is the bond's indissolubility, not a numbered rite. The father who most develops marriage's sacramentum means by it the bonum sacramenti — the good of the inseparable bond: “What, then, in Christ and in the Church is great, in the instances of each married pair it is but very small, but even then it is the sacrament of an inseparable union.”7 Augustine's sacramentum is the indissoluble covenant symbolized (the ground of the Latin case against remarriage — see the indissoluble-bond argument on the divorce timeline, and the deprecation of remarriage), not marriage's membership in a sevenfold list of grace-conferring sacraments. Conflating the two is the argument's central overreach.

The Reformers' denial turns on the same point. Luther grants the typology but rejects the sacrament, arguing that Scripture attaches to marriage no divine promise of grace, that the Vulgate's sacramentum mistranslates mystērion, and that marriage existed “from the beginning of the world” and among unbelievers — so it cannot be a sign instituted by Christ to convey new-covenant grace.8 Whatever one makes of his conclusion, his exegetical premise — that the word sacramentum here proves nothing — is correct.

Assessment

Weak, as a proof-text for numbering marriage among the grace-conferring sacraments. The verse robustly establishes that marriage signifies the union of Christ and the Church — a reading that is itself sound and near-universal — but the leap from “great mystery / sacred sign” to “instrumental cause of sanctifying grace, one of exactly seven” is not carried by Ephesians 5:32. It rests on the Vulgate rendering mystērion by sacramentum, a word the same Vulgate uses for “the mystery of godliness” and “the mystery of the seven stars” with no rite in view; even Aquinas grounds matrimony here only “by reason of its signification,” a weaker sense than the grace-causing definition Lombard applies to the other sacraments. The broader doctrine that marriage is a sacrament may stand or fall on other grounds (tradition, the fittingness of a sanctified sign, the covenant theology of Ephesians 5 as a whole); this page rates only the narrow, load-bearing proof-text argument, and on that the word-based case is weak. Peter Lombard's own note already concedes the counter — “the Greek 'mystery' does not by itself license numbering matrimony among the grace-conferring sacraments” — and that concession is flagged on his detail page.
