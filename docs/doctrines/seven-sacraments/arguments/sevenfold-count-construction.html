What does the late appearance of a list of seven sacraments (Peter Lombard, c. 1150) show about the origin of the seven?

Disputed reading · on The Seven Sacraments · witness: Peter Lombard

The passage

…there are seven sacraments, namely, baptism, confirmation, the bread of blessing (that is, the Eucharist), penance, extreme unction, orders, and marriage.

— Peter Lombard, The Sentences, Book IV, dist. 2, ch. 1, trans. Giulio Silano, source.1

The question

No extant text before the twelfth century gives a closed list of seven sacraments. The same seven, in the same order, as Peter Lombard's list were named by the Council of Florence (1439) and defined by the Council of Trent (1547), which anathematized anyone who says the sacraments "are more, or less, than seven."2 Writers of each tradition describe the earlier record in similar terms: the Catholic Pierre Pourrat writes that "History does not give us a definite list of the Sacraments until the 12th. century," the Protestant Philip Schaff that "This uncertainty as to the number of the sacraments continued till the twelfth century," and the Orthodox John Meyendorff that in the patristic period "there was no technical term to designate 'sacraments' as a specific category of church acts."3,4,5 The historian Philip L. Reynolds (2016) writes that the list's "first extant appearances date from the 1140s."6 What is disputed is what the late list shows about the seven rites themselves, and so how the medieval and Reformation-era decisions on the number relate to the practice of the early church. No openly accessible academic treatment of the question since 1950 by a Catholic or Protestant historian was located in the sources surveyed here.

A recent enumeration, not received from the ancient church

In brief: the early church did not count seven sacraments; the list was drawn in the twelfth century, and measured by Christ's own institution and promise only baptism and the Lord's Supper (in Luther's first count and in Melanchthon's Apology, also penance or absolution) are sacraments in the strict sense.

Held by: Martin Luther (Lutheran reformer, The Babylonian Captivity of the Church, 1520);7 John Calvin (Reformed, Institutes of the Christian Religion, 1559 ed.);8 Philip Schaff (Protestant church historian, History of the Christian Church, vol. III, 1867; rev. 1884).9,10

Luther answers the appeal to Pseudo-Dionysius, "the one writer of antiquity who is cited in support of the seven sacraments, although he omits marriage and so has only six": "we read nothing at all about these 'sacraments' in the rest of the fathers; nor do they ever regard them as sacraments when they speak of these things. For the invention of sacraments is of recent date." He states his aim: "I do not say this because I condemn the seven sacraments, but because I deny that they can be proved from the Scriptures." A sacrament, for him, needs "both the divinely instituted sign and the promise of forgiveness of sins," and so there are "strictly speaking, but two."7

Calvin writes: "This number seven is nowhere found in the ecclesiastical writers, nor is it well ascertained at what time it crept in." The fathers "sometimes use freedom with the term sacrament," applying it to "all ceremonies, external writs, and exercises of piety," but "when they speak of those signs which ought to be testimonies of the divine favour toward us, they are contented with those two, Baptism and the Eucharist," as in Augustine's sacraments "very few in number."8

Schaff gathers the varying counts, from two (Bede, Ratramnus, Ratherius) to twelve (Peter Damian). The number seven was "first introduced, as is usually supposed, by the bishop Otto of Bamberg (1124), more correctly by Peter Lombard," and "rationally and rhetorically justified by Thomas Aquinas and other scholastics."4 Melanchthon's Apology of the Augsburg Confession draws the conclusion for teaching: "Neither do we believe it to be of any consequence, though, for the purpose of teaching, different people reckon differently, provided they still preserve aright the matters handed down in Scripture. Neither have the ancients reckoned in the same manner." On this reading the count follows the definition: rites that have "the command of God, and to which the promise of grace has been added" are sacraments properly so called, and the twelfth-century list is one reckoning among several.10

Seven rites instituted by Christ and practised from the beginning

In brief: Christ instituted all seven rites, or mysteries, and the church has used them from the start; what came late was the general definition of a sacrament, and with it the count.

Held by: the Council of Trent (Catholic, Session 7, 1547);2 Patriarch Jeremias II of Constantinople (Orthodox, reply to the Lutheran theologians, c. 1576);11 the Synod of Jerusalem (Orthodox, Confession of Dositheus, 1672);12 Pierre Pourrat (Catholic, Theology of the Sacraments, Eng. tr. 1910).3,13

Trent defined that the sacraments of the New Law were "all instituted by Jesus Christ, our Lord," and are seven.2 Jeremias II, answering the Lutheran theologians who had sent him the Augsburg Confession, wrote that the Mysteries "are seven in number … just seven and no more."11 The Synod of Jerusalem holds that the Mysteries of the Gospel are seven: "For a less or a greater number of the Mysteries we have not in the Church," and "the seven of them were instituted in the Sacred Gospel, and are gathered from the same, like the other dogmas of the Catholic Faith."12 Pourrat explains why the count came late: "The definition of what a sacrament is had to be laid down before the number of Sacraments could be determined"; "from the fact that the Church did not have an explicit knowledge of the number of her Sacraments from the beginning, it does not follow she did not know them"; "she lived the dogma before she formulated it." Each of the seven, he notes, bore the name before anyone counted them: "Baptism, the Eucharist and Matrimony were called sacramenta from the time of Tertullian. St. Cyprian applied the name, sacrament, to Confirmation, St. Augustine to Holy Orders, Innocent to Extreme Unction, and St. Gregory the Great to Penance."3

D. J. Kennedy holds that "Our seven sacraments were all mentioned in the Sacred Scriptures, and we find them all mentioned here and there by the Fathers"; "Thus time was required, not for the development of the sacraments - except in so far as the Church may have determined what was left under her control by Jesus Christ — but for the growth and knowledge of the sacraments."14 The Catechism of the Catholic Church (1992) sets the count beside the canon: "As she has done for the canon of Sacred Scripture and for the doctrine of the faith, the Church … has gradually recognized this treasure received from Christ," and "has discerned over the centuries that among liturgical celebrations there are seven that are, in the strict sense of the term, sacraments instituted by the Lord."15 On this reading the seven are rites received from Christ, and the late list is the church's reflection reaching rites it already had.

Mysteries not bound to a fixed number

In brief: the seven mysteries belong to a wider sacramental life, and their number is not a dogmatic limit.

Held by: Timothy (Kallistos) Ware (Orthodox, The Orthodox Church, 1963; rev. ed. 1993);16 John Meyendorff (Orthodox, Byzantine Theology, 1974);5 the Orthodox Church in America (The Orthodox Faith, vol. II, Worship, online, n.d.).17

For Meyendorff the Byzantines understood the sacraments "more as the aspects of a unique mystery of the Church in which God shares divine life with humanity," and "Byzantine theology ignores the Western distinction between 'sacraments' and 'sacramentals' and has never formally committed itself to any strict limitation of the number of sacraments." Theodore the Studite (ninth century) lists six: illumination, synaxis, chrism, ordination, monastic tonsure and burial. The seven appear "for the first time" in the Profession of Faith required of Michael Palaeologus by Clement IV in 1267, which "had been prepared, of course, by Latin theologians"; their wide acceptance in the East afterwards came, in Meyendorff's account, "not so much from the influence of Latin theology as from the peculiarly Medieval and Byzantine fascination with symbolic numbers," seven evoking the gifts of the Spirit in Isaiah 11. The lists still differed: Joasaph of Ephesus wrote, "I believe that the sacraments of the Church are not seven but more," and named ten.5

Ware dates the fixing of the list later still: "Only in the seventeenth century, when Latin influence was at its height, did this list become fixed and definite." He holds that "all seven are true sacraments," ranked in a hierarchy with baptism and the Eucharist pre-eminent, and that they are not to be isolated from the sacramentals, among them monastic profession, the great blessing of waters and the burial service. "Even today the number seven has no absolute dogmatic significance for Orthodox theology, but is used primarily as a convenience in teaching."16 The Orthodox Church in America's The Orthodox Faith notes that "Usually seven sacraments are counted," and holds that "The practice of counting the sacraments was adopted in the Orthodox Church from the Roman Catholics. It is not an ancient practice of the Church"; "The more ancient and traditional practice of the Orthodox Church is to consider everything which is in and of the Church as sacramental or mystical."17 On this reading the seven name the chief mysteries of one sacramental life, and the number serves teaching rather than marking a limit.
