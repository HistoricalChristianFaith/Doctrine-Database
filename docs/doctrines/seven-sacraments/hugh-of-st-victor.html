Hugh of St Victor on the sacraments

Dates: c. 1134 · Wikipedia
Summary: A generation before Peter Lombard, Hugh wrote the first great scholastic synthesis on the subject — On the Sacraments of the Christian Faith. He sharpens the definition, insisting a sacrament must not only signify but contain grace; yet his own working set remains large and tiered, sorted into ranks rather than closed at any number. The count is not yet seven.

Claims & evidence

A sacrament contains grace, not merely signifies it

Hugh advances beyond Augustine's bare "sacred sign," requiring that a sacrament hold the grace it represents:

A sacrament is a corporeal or material element set before the senses without, representing by similitude and signifying by institution and containing by sanctification some invisible and spiritual grace.

— Hugh of St Victor, On the Sacraments of the Christian Faith (De sacramentis), Book I, Part 9, ch. 2, trans. Roy J. Deferrari (Mediaeval Academy of America, 1951), pp. 154–155, source.1

He rejects the loosest sign-definition — but still counts no total

Not every sacred sign is a sacrament, Hugh insists — and yet the concept he keeps is broad and layered, not a fixed list:

not every sign of a sacred thing can properly be called a sacrament… the letters of sacred expressions and statues or pictures are signs of sacred things, of which, however, they cannot reasonably be called the sacraments.

— Hugh of St Victor, De sacramentis, Book I, Part 9, ch. 2 (Deferrari), source.2 Across the two long books of the work he sorts many sacraments into kinds — those "in which salvation principally consists" (baptism, the eucharist), those that sanctify (holy water, ashes, blessings), and those "prepared" for the reception of others — a large, tiered array rather than an enumerated seven.3

Notes

  • Halfway to the count. Hugh supplies the crucial ingredient the fixing at seven will need — a sacrament causes, and does not merely picture, grace — and he narrows Augustine's over-broad "any sacred sign." But he does not draw the line at seven; his sacraments are still a graded multitude. It falls to Peter Lombard, a decade or so later, to apply the tighter definition to close the list.
  • On sourcing. Deferrari's translation is the standard English edition; the definition wording above is as it appears there (Book I, Part 9). The three-fold classification is summarized from the same work rather than quoted verbatim.
