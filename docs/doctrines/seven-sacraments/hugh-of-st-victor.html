Hugh of St Victor on the sacraments

Dates: c. 1134 · Wikipedia
Summary: A generation before Peter Lombard, Hugh wrote On the Sacraments of the Christian Faith. He sharpens the definition, insisting a sacrament must not only signify but contain grace; his working set is large and tiered, sorted into ranks rather than closed at any number.

Claims & evidence

A sacrament contains grace, not merely signifies it

Hugh adds to Augustine's "sacred sign" the requirement that a sacrament hold the grace it represents:

A sacrament is a corporeal or material element set before the senses without, representing by similitude and signifying by institution and containing by sanctification some invisible and spiritual grace.

— Hugh of St Victor, On the Sacraments of the Christian Faith (De sacramentis), Book I, Part 9, ch. 2, trans. Roy J. Deferrari (Mediaeval Academy of America, 1951), pp. 154–155, source.1

Not every sacred sign a sacrament

Not every sacred sign is a sacrament, Hugh insists; the concept he keeps is broad and layered, not a fixed list:

not every sign of a sacred thing can properly be called a sacrament… the letters of sacred expressions and statues or pictures are signs of sacred things, of which, however, they cannot reasonably be called the sacraments.

— Hugh of St Victor, De sacramentis, Book I, Part 9, ch. 2 (Deferrari), source.2 Across the two long books of the work he sorts many sacraments into kinds — those "in which salvation principally consists" (baptism, the eucharist), those that sanctify (holy water, ashes, blessings), and those "prepared" for the reception of others — a large, tiered array.3

Notes

  • Definition and classification. Hugh requires that a sacrament contain, and not merely picture, grace, and he narrows Augustine's broad definition of a sacrament as a sacred sign; he does not draw the line at a number, and his sacraments form a graded multitude. David S. Schaff writes that "Hugo wrote the first treatise on the sacraments, De sacramentis," and reports that "Hugo de St. Victor in his Summa also seems to recognize only five,—baptism, confirmation, eucharist, penance, and extreme unction," and that he "called the sprinkling with water a sacrament."4 Peter Lombard's Sentences (c. 1150) give a list of seven.
  • On sourcing. Deferrari's translation is the standard English edition; the definition wording above is as it appears there (Book I, Part 9). The three-fold classification is summarized from the same work rather than quoted verbatim.
