Thomas Aquinas on the number of the sacraments (why seven)

Dates: c. 1274 · Wikipedia
Summary: Aquinas supplies the mature scholastic rationale for the septenary that Peter Lombard had fixed (c. 1150) and the Council of Florence (1439) would decree. In the treatise on the sacraments in general (Summa Theologiae III, q. 65, "Of the number of the sacraments") he does not argue that seven can be counted from the apostolic record; he argues that exactly seven is fitting — by an analogy to the stages of natural and social life (birth, growth, nourishment, healing, convalescence, governance, propagation), and again as a set of remedies matched to the defects of sin. He then orders the seven, ranks the Eucharist first among them as containing Christ himself, and grades their necessity. This is the reasoned defence of the number, not its discovery: by his day the list was already "commonly adopted by all."

Claims & evidence

Why seven: the sacraments answer the stages of the life of the body

Aquinas's central argument for the count is an analogy between the spiritual life and the bodily life. A man is perfected in the corporeal life first in his own person (by generation, growth, nourishment, and by the removal of hindrances — healing and convalescence) and secondly in the community (by governance and propagation); seven sacraments answer, one to one, to these seven perfections:

For spiritual life has a certain conformity with the life of the body… Now the life of the body is perfected "directly," in three ways. First, by generation whereby a man begins to be and to live: and corresponding to this in the spiritual life there is Baptism… Secondly, by growth… and corresponding to this in the spiritual life there is Confirmation… Thirdly, by nourishment… and corresponding to this in the spiritual life there is the Eucharist… man needs a cure from his infirmity; which cure is twofold. one is the healing, that restores health: and corresponding to this in the spiritual life there is Penance… The other is the restoration of former vigor… and corresponding to this in the spiritual life there is Extreme Unction, which removes the remainder of sin, and prepares man for final glory… In regard to the whole community, man is perfected in two ways. First, by receiving power to rule the community… and corresponding to this in the spiritual life there is the sacrament of order… Secondly in regard to natural propagation. This is accomplished by Matrimony.

— Summa Theologiae III, q. 65, a. 1.1

The same seven, derived a second way: remedies for the defects of sin

Aquinas offers a converging derivation — the seven as a graded pharmacy against sin — and reports a third that pairs each sacrament with a virtue, showing the count "over-determined" rather than arbitrary:

We may likewise gather the number of the sacraments from their being instituted as a remedy against the defect caused by sin. For Baptism is intended as a remedy against the absence of spiritual life; Confirmation, against the infirmity of soul found in those of recent birth; the Eucharist, against the soul's proneness to sin; Penance, against actual sin committed after baptism; Extreme Unction, against the remainders of sins… order, against divisions in the community; Matrimony, as a remedy against concupiscence in the individual, and against the decrease in numbers that results from death.

— Summa Theologiae III, q. 65, a. 1. He also answers the objection, drawn from Augustine, that the sacraments of the New Law are "less numerous" than the Old — conceding the point about ceremonies while holding the seven as the settled New-Law count.2

The order of the seven

Their sequence is not accidental: the sacraments for the perfection of the individual precede those for the perfection of the community, and within each group the directly-perfecting come before the remedial. So the list runs baptism, confirmation, eucharist, penance, extreme unction — then orders and matrimony last:

the last place among the sacraments is given to order and Matrimony, which are intended for the perfection of the multitude: while Matrimony is placed after order, because it has less participation in the nature of the spiritual life… Of the remaining three, it is clear that Baptism which is a spiritual regeneration, comes first; then Confirmation… and after these the Eucharist which is ordained to final perfection.

— Summa Theologiae III, q. 65, a. 2. The order, he notes, "is commonly adopted by all."3

The Eucharist the greatest — the others ordained to it

Among the seven the Eucharist ranks first absolutely, because it contains Christ himself and not merely a share of his power, and because every other sacrament is ordered to it as to its end:

Absolutely speaking, the sacrament of the Eucharist is the greatest of all the sacraments… First of all because it contains Christ Himself substantially: whereas the other sacraments contain a certain instrumental power which is a share of Christ's power… For all the other sacraments seem to be ordained to this one as to their end. For it is manifest that the sacrament of order is ordained to the consecration of the Eucharist: and the sacrament of Baptism to the reception of the Eucharist.

— Summa Theologiae III, q. 65, a. 3. In the same article, explaining how matrimony touches the Eucharist "in its signification," Aquinas cites the Vulgate of Ephesians 5:32 — "This is a great sacrament: but I speak in Christ and in the Church" — the verse on which matrimony's place in the list leans.4

Necessity: three needed for salvation, four fitting

Fixing the number does not flatten the seven into equals. Aquinas distinguishes what is necessary for salvation without qualification from what is necessary only for its greater fitness:

In the first way, three sacraments are necessary for salvation. Two of them are necessary to the individual; Baptism, simply and absolutely; Penance, in the case of mortal sin committed after Baptism; while the sacrament of order is necessary to the Church… But in the second way the other sacraments are necessary. For in a sense Confirmation perfects Baptism; Extreme Unction perfects Penance; while Matrimony, by multiplying them, preserves the numbers in the Church.

— Summa Theologiae III, q. 65, a. 4.5

Notes

  • The argument defends the number; it does not count it. Aquinas's case for seven is one of fittingness (the analogy to bodily life, the remedies for sin, the virtues) reinforced by consensus — "the order of the sacraments… is commonly adopted by all." He nowhere claims a closed apostolic list of seven; the enumeration he defends is the one Peter Lombard had fixed a century before. This is exactly the distinction the timeline turns on — see the construction reading and the recognition reading.
  • Matrimony and Ephesians 5:32. The seventh item's scriptural warrant is the most contested, and Aquinas rests its link to the sacramental order on the Vulgate's sacramentum hoc magnum est at Ephesians 5:32. Whether that verse actually makes marriage a grace-conferring sacrament is weighed on its own page.
  • Provenance. Question 65 stands in the Tertia Pars proper — the part Aquinas wrote himself, before it broke off in the treatise on penance — not in the posthumous Supplement that carries his treatment of extreme unction and matrimony as separate rites. The translation quoted is that of the Fathers of the English Dominican Province.
  • Aquinas appears on this wiki also on the anointing of the sick timeline, where he gives the fullest scholastic treatment of the fifth sacrament as extreme unction.
