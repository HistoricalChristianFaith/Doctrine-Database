Peter Lombard on the seven sacraments

Dates: c. 1150 · Wikipedia
Summary: In Book IV of the Sentences — the theology textbook of the medieval West for four centuries — Peter Lombard did what no one before him had: he named the sacraments of the new law and fixed them at seven. Combining a tightened definition (a sacrament causes the grace it signifies) with this enumeration, he set the standard that Florence and Trent would later make dogma.

Claims & evidence

A sacrament is a sign that causes grace, not merely signifies it

Lombard closes off Augustine's over-broad "any sacred sign" by requiring that a sacrament effect what it represents:

a sign of the grace of God and a form of invisible grace, so that it bears its image and exists as its cause.

— Peter Lombard, The Sentences, Book IV, dist. 1, trans. Giulio Silano (Pontifical Institute of Mediaeval Studies, 2010), source.1 This "sign that also confers grace" is exactly what lets him separate the sacraments proper from the wider field of sacred signs — and so close the set.

The seven, named

…there are seven sacraments, namely, baptism, confirmation, the bread of blessing (that is, the Eucharist), penance, extreme unction, orders, and marriage.

— Peter Lombard, The Sentences, Book IV, dist. 2, ch. 1 (Silano), source.2 The Latin runs: Iam ad sacramenta novae legis accedamus, quae sunt baptismus, confirmatio, panis benedictio id est eucharistia, poenitentia, unctio extrema, ordo, coniugium. This is the first appearance of the exact sevenfold list that becomes the Western standard.

Notes

  • The hinge of the whole timeline. Everything before Lombard — Tertullian, Ambrose, Augustine, even the sixfold scheme of Pseudo-Dionysius — has a doctrine of the sacrament but no fixed number; everything after him inherits seven. Because the Sentences became the set text every theologian lectured on, Lombard's enumeration propagated with the curriculum, and by the thirteenth century it was simply assumed.
  • Marriage and Ephesians 5:32. Marriage's place on the list leans on the Vulgate of Ephesians 5:32, which renders Paul's Greek mystērion ("mystery") by Latin sacramentum — so a Latin reader met a verse apparently calling marriage "a great sacrament" — whereas the Greek "mystery" does not by itself license numbering matrimony among the grace-conferring sacraments.
  • On sourcing. The English wording above is from Giulio Silano's PIMS translation of Book IV; the exact quotations here are as reproduced in a peer-reviewed study of Lombard on marriage, and the Latin is the standard Quaracchi text (Sententiae IV, dist. 2). A print-page citation of Silano is The Sentences, Book 4 (Toronto, 2010), dist. 2.
