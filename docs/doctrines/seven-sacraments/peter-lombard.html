Peter Lombard on the seven sacraments

Dates: c. 1150 · Wikipedia
Summary: In Book IV of the Sentences — the theology textbook of the medieval West for four centuries — Peter Lombard defines a sacrament as a sign that causes the grace it signifies and names seven sacraments of the new law. His is the earliest such list in the record surveyed here; whether others gave the number before him is reported differently (see Notes).

Claims & evidence

A sacrament is a sign that causes grace, not merely signifies it

Lombard narrows Augustine's broad definition of a sacrament as a sacred sign by requiring that a sacrament effect what it represents:

a sign of the grace of God and a form of invisible grace, so that it bears its image and exists as its cause.

— Peter Lombard, The Sentences, Book IV, dist. 1, trans. Giulio Silano (Pontifical Institute of Mediaeval Studies, 2010), source.1 Pierre Pourrat (1910) connects the definition with the count: "The definition of what a sacrament is had to be laid down before the number of Sacraments could be determined."3

The seven, named

…there are seven sacraments, namely, baptism, confirmation, the bread of blessing (that is, the Eucharist), penance, extreme unction, orders, and marriage.

— Peter Lombard, The Sentences, Book IV, dist. 2, ch. 1 (Silano), source.2 The Latin runs: Iam ad sacramenta novae legis accedamus, quae sunt baptismus, confirmatio, panis benedictio id est eucharistia, poenitentia, unctio extrema, ordo, coniugium.

Notes

  • Placement on the timeline. The earlier witnesses on this timeline — Tertullian, Ambrose, Augustine, and the sixfold scheme of Pseudo-Dionysius — give no list of seven; Florence (1439) and Trent (1547) name the same seven, in the same order. The Sentences became the set text on which theologians lectured. D. J. Kennedy (1912) records that "According to some writers Otto of Bamberg (1139), the Apostle of Pomerania, was the first who clearly adopted the number seven," and writes that "Most probably this honour belongs to Peter Lombard"; for him the definition and enumeration "were at once universally accepted, proof positive that he did not introduce a new doctrine."4 David S. Schaff (1907) names "Rolandus (afterwards Alexander III.) in his Sentences and Otto of Bamberg in a sermon" as giving seven "about the time of Peter the Lombard."5 What the late list shows is a disputed reading.
  • Marriage and Ephesians 5:32. Luther (1520) argued that reading Ephesians 5:32 as a sacrament of the New Law was "a thing they would never have done if they had read mysterium, as it is in the Greek."6
  • On sourcing. The English wording above is from Giulio Silano's PIMS translation of Book IV; the exact quotations here are as reproduced in a peer-reviewed study of Lombard on marriage, and the Latin is the standard Quaracchi text (Sententiae IV, dist. 2). A print-page citation of Silano is The Sentences, Book 4 (Toronto, 2010), dist. 2.
