Augustine of Hippo on the sacraments

Dates: c. 410 · Wikipedia
Summary: Augustine gives the West its enduring definition of a sacrament — a visible sign of a sacred, invisible reality, the word joined to the element — and this definition is deliberately broad: a sacrament is any sacred sign, so many things can be called sacramenta. Yet where he counts at all, he says the sacraments of the New Testament are "very few in number," and names only baptism and the eucharist. There is a doctrine of the sacrament; there is no list of seven.

Claims & evidence

A sacrament is a sacred sign of an invisible thing

Augustine's compact formula, given while defining sacrifice, became the standard definition of a sacrament in general — a visible sign of an invisible reality:

A sacrifice, therefore, is the visible sacrament or sacred sign of an invisible sacrifice.

— Augustine, The City of God, Book X, ch. 5, trans. Marcus Dods (Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, 1st ser., vol. 2), source.1 The point recurs as his famous rule for how a rite becomes a sacrament: the word added to the visible element makes it a "visible word."

"The word is added to the element, and there results the Sacrament"

the word is added to the element, and there results the Sacrament, as if itself also a kind of visible word.

— Augustine, Tractates on the Gospel of John, Tractate 80, §3, trans. John Gibb (Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, 1st ser., vol. 7), source.2 Because a sacrament is simply a sacred sign, the term is elastic: Augustine can call the salt given to catechumens, the creed, and other holy signs sacramenta. The definition draws no boundary of number.

The sacraments of the New Testament are "very few in number"

Where Augustine does say how many, he says few — and names only two — contrasting the light yoke of the gospel with the many observances of the Law:

our Lord Jesus Christ has appointed to us a "light yoke" and an "easy burden," as He declares in the Gospel: in accordance with which He has bound His people under the new dispensation together in fellowship by sacraments, which are in number very few, in observance most easy, and in significance most excellent, as baptism solemnized in the name of the [Trinity], the communion of His body and blood…

— Augustine, Letter LIV (to Januarius), ch. 1, §1, trans. J. G. Cunningham (Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, 1st ser., vol. 1), source.3

Notes

  • Broad definition, no fixed count. Augustine's genius was to define the kind of thing a sacrament is (a sacred sign, a visible word) rather than to enumerate them. Precisely because the definition is so capacious, later ages could apply it to a great many rites — and the medieval task became not inventing sacraments but deciding which of the sacred signs deserved the name in its strict sense. When Augustine himself gives a number he says "very few," listing baptism and the eucharist; the fixing at seven is a scholastic achievement of the twelfth century (Peter Lombard).
  • Augustine across the wiki. He is a witness on infant baptism, the Real Presence, justification, and many other doctrines.
