Augustine of Hippo on the sacraments

Dates: c. 410 · Wikipedia
Summary: Augustine defines a sacrament as a visible sign of a sacred, invisible reality, the word joined to the element. The definition is broad: a sacrament is any sacred sign, so many things can be called sacramenta. In his letter to Januarius he says the sacraments of the new dispensation are "in number very few," and names baptism and the eucharist, "and such other things as are prescribed in the canonical Scriptures." He gives no count of the sacraments.

Claims & evidence

A sacrament is a sacred sign of an invisible thing

Augustine's compact formula, given while defining sacrifice, states a definition of a sacrament in general — a visible sign of an invisible reality:

A sacrifice, therefore, is the visible sacrament or sacred sign of an invisible sacrifice.

— Augustine, The City of God, Book X, ch. 5, trans. Marcus Dods (Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, 1st ser., vol. 2), source.1 The point recurs as his rule for how a rite becomes a sacrament: the word added to the visible element makes it a "visible word."

"The word is added to the element, and there results the Sacrament"

the word is added to the element, and there results the Sacrament, as if itself also a kind of visible word.

— Augustine, Tractates on the Gospel of John, Tractate 80, §3, trans. John Gibb (Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, 1st ser., vol. 7), source.2 Because a sacrament is a sacred sign, the term is broad: of what catechumens receive — identified in the translator's note as the blessed salt, the sacramentum salis — Augustine writes that it is "holier than any food which constitutes our ordinary nourishment, because it is a sacrament."3 He gives no count of the sacraments.

The sacraments of the new dispensation are "in number very few"

In his letter to Januarius Augustine says the sacraments are few, naming baptism and the eucharist, "and such other things as are prescribed in the canonical Scriptures," and contrasts the light yoke of the gospel with the many observances of the Law:

our Lord Jesus Christ has appointed to us a "light yoke" and an "easy burden," as He declares in the Gospel: in accordance with which He has bound His people under the new dispensation together in fellowship by sacraments, which are in number very few, in observance most easy, and in significance most excellent, as baptism solemnized in the name of the Trinity, the communion of His body and blood, and such other things as are prescribed in the canonical Scriptures…

— Augustine, Letter LIV (to Januarius), ch. 1, §1, trans. J. G. Cunningham (Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, 1st ser., vol. 1), source.4

Notes

  • Broad definition, no fixed count. Augustine defines the kind of thing a sacrament is (a sacred sign, a visible word) rather than listing the sacraments, and in Letter 54 he says "very few," naming baptism and the eucharist, "and such other things as are prescribed in the canonical Scriptures." His letter is read in more than one way. Calvin (1559) cites it for the view that the fathers, speaking of signs that testify to God's favour, "are contented with those two, Baptism and the Eucharist."5 Pierre Pourrat (1910) counts Augustine among the fathers who applied the name sacrament to each of the later seven, Augustine applying it "to Holy Orders."6 The list of seven appears in Peter Lombard (c. 1150); see the disputed reading.
  • Augustine across the wiki. He is a witness on infant baptism, the Real Presence, justification, and many other doctrines.
