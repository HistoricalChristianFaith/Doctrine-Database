Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite on the sacred rites

Dates: c. 500 · Wikipedia
Summary: The anonymous author who wrote under the name of Paul's Athenian convert composed, around 500, the first systematic treatise on the church's rites — The Ecclesiastical Hierarchy. It enumerates the sacred "mysteries," but its list is not the later seven: it counts baptism, the eucharist, the consecration of the ointment (chrism), the ordination of clergy, the consecration of monks, and the rites over the dead. That a careful sixth-century catalogue could arrive at a different six is the plainest proof that no fixed number of sacraments yet existed.

Claims & evidence

The eucharist — "initiation of initiations"

Dionysius ranks the Synaxis (Communion) first among the mysteries, as the rite that completes all the others:

it is "initiation of initiations"… For it scarcely ever happens, that any Hierarchical initiation is completed without the most Divine Eucharist, as head of the things done in each.

— Pseudo-Dionysius, The Ecclesiastical Hierarchy, ch. 3, trans. John Parker, source.1

The Muron (chrism) — a mystery in its own right

He devotes a whole chapter to the consecration of the ointment, treating it as a distinct sacred rite:

the perfecting unction of the Muron makes the man initiated of good odour, for the holy perfecting of the Divine birth unites those who have been perfected to the supremely Divine Spirit.

— Pseudo-Dionysius, The Ecclesiastical Hierarchy, ch. 4, source.2

The six mysteries, chapter by chapter

The treatise is organized around six sacred rites, one to a chapter: baptism (the "Divine Birth" / Illumination, ch. 2); the Synaxis or Communion — the eucharist (ch. 3); the consecration of the Muron, the holy ointment (ch. 4); the sacerdotal consecrations — the ordination of the three clerical orders, hierarch, priest, and minister (ch. 5, "the three Orders of the consecrators"); the consecration of monks (ch. 6, "Mysterion on Monastic Consecration"); and the rites over the dead (ch. 7, "Mysterion over those who have religiously fallen asleep").3

Notes

  • A different six. Set beside the later Roman seven, Dionysius's list overlaps in part (baptism, eucharist, chrism, ordination) but diverges sharply: he counts monastic consecration and funeral rites as mysteries, and does not enumerate penance, marriage, or the anointing of the sick as such. His scheme becomes foundational for later Eastern liturgical theology, yet it demonstrates that as late as c. 500 the "sacraments" were an open, differently-drawn set — a fixed count of seven was still centuries away.
  • Provenance. The works passed for centuries as those of the first-century Dionysius of Acts 17:34; they are now dated to c. 500 on the strength of their dependence on the Neoplatonist Proclus (d. 485), and the author is conventionally called Pseudo-Dionysius.
