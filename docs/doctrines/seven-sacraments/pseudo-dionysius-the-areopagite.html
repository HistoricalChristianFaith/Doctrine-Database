Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite on the sacred rites

Dates: c. 500 · Wikipedia
Summary: The anonymous author who wrote under the name of Paul's Athenian convert composed, around 500, a treatise on the church's rites — The Ecclesiastical Hierarchy. It devotes a chapter each to six sacred "mysteries": baptism, the eucharist, the consecration of the ointment (chrism), the ordination of clergy, the consecration of monks, and the rites over the dead. Its list differs from the Latin seven of the twelfth century; how it bears on that seven is part of a disputed reading.

Claims & evidence

The eucharist — "initiation of initiations"

Dionysius ranks the Synaxis (Communion) first among the mysteries, as the rite that completes all the others:

it is "initiation of initiations"… For it scarcely ever happens, that any Hierarchical initiation is completed without the most Divine Eucharist, as head of the things done in each.

— Pseudo-Dionysius, The Ecclesiastical Hierarchy, ch. 3, trans. John Parker, source.1

The Muron (chrism) — a mystery in its own right

He devotes a whole chapter to the consecration of the ointment, treating it as a distinct sacred rite:

the perfecting unction of the Muron makes the man initiated of good odour, for the holy perfecting of the Divine birth unites those who have been perfected to the supremely Divine Spirit.

— Pseudo-Dionysius, The Ecclesiastical Hierarchy, ch. 4, source.2

The six mysteries, chapter by chapter

The treatise is organized around six sacred rites, one to a chapter: baptism (the "Divine Birth" / Illumination, ch. 2); the Synaxis or Communion — the eucharist (ch. 3); the consecration of the Muron, the holy ointment (ch. 4); the sacerdotal consecrations — the ordination of the three clerical orders, hierarch, priest, and minister (ch. 5, "the three Orders of the consecrators"); the consecration of monks (ch. 6, "Mysterion on Monastic Consecration"); and the rites over the dead (ch. 7, "Mysterion over those who have religiously fallen asleep").3

Notes

  • Comparison with the Latin seven. Set beside the seven of Peter Lombard (c. 1150), Dionysius's list overlaps in part (baptism, eucharist, chrism, ordination) and differs in part: he counts monastic consecration and funeral rites as mysteries, and does not enumerate penance, marriage, or the anointing of the sick as such. John Meyendorff records that Theodore the Studite in the ninth century gives the same six4; the scheme's wider place in later Eastern liturgical theology is not yet sourced here (⚠ unverified). Luther (1520) called Dionysius "the one writer of antiquity who is cited in support of the seven sacraments, although he omits marriage and so has only six."5
  • Provenance. The works passed for centuries as those of the first-century Dionysius of Acts 17:34; Kevin Corrigan and L. Michael Harrington (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, 2004; rev. 2023) write that it is "generally accepted in modern times" that the author "must have lived in the time of Proclus," who died in 485; they note that "the first clear citation of Dionysius’ works is by Severus of Antioch between 518 and 528." The author is conventionally called Pseudo-Dionysius.6
