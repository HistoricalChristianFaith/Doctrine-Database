Penance & Confession (the forgiveness of post-baptismal sin)

What does the church do with a Christian who sins after baptism? The first answer was uncertain — some taught there was no second repentance at all. From that starting point the tradition developed in stages: a single, once-only "second repentance" (the Shepherd of Hermas); a public, graded, once-in-a-lifetime discipline of exomologesis and the orders of penitents (Tertullian, Cyprian, Nicaea, Basil), with a rigorist revolt denying the gravest sinners any return at all (the Montanist Tertullian, Novatian); then the quiet revolution of the Irish penitentials, which made confession private, tariffed, and — decisively — repeatable; its universalization as mandatory annual confession (Fourth Lateran Council, 1215); and its dogmatic definition as a sacrament, with contrition, confession, and satisfaction and a judicial absolution, at the Council of Trent (1551), against a Reformation that kept confession but denied it was a sacrament of satisfaction.

Timeline

The Shepherd of Hermas (c. 150)

The Shepherd of Hermas reports the rigorist teaching that baptism is the only repentance there is, and confirms it as "sound doctrine" — then softens it: for those already baptized the Lord has appointed one further repentance, but "he has opportunity to repent but once," for "there is but one repentance to the servants of God."1

Tertullian (c. 203 and c. 217)

Tertullian, as a churchman, gives the classic account of this "second repentance" — the "plank" for the shipwrecked sinner — and of its outward rite, exomologesis: confession, sackcloth and ashes, fasting, and prostration before the presbyters and the congregation.2 Later, as a Montanist, he reversed course: attacking a bishop's edict that forgave adultery and fornication, he divided sins into the remissible and the irremissible, denying the church any power over the graver kind.3

Cyprian of Carthage (c. 251)

After the Decian persecution left the church full of the lapsed, Cyprian set the mediating course: even those who only bought certificates (the libellatici) have sinned and must repent, and peace is not to be seized on the strength of martyrs' letters but granted by the bishop — yet the penitent, even the adulterer, is truly offered reconciliation, for to deny repentance its "fruit" only drives the fallen to despair.4 He records that Novatian himself had once endorsed peace for the dying lapsed.5

Novatian (c. 251)

Novatian, the rigorist pole, led a schism holding that the church cannot reconcile the lapsed — "as if there was no longer for them a hope of salvation, not even if they should do all things pertaining to a genuine and pure conversion"; his followers called themselves the Cathari, "the pure," and a Roman synod of sixty bishops condemned the position and reaffirmed healing the fallen "with the medicines of repentance."6

The Council of Nicaea (325)

The Council of Nicaea hardened the mediating discipline into church-wide law: the lapsed pass through graded orders of penitents — hearers, then prostrators, then those who stand with the faithful "but without oblation" — for fixed terms of years before full communion; and, against Novatian, no one at the point of death "must be deprived of the last and most indispensable Viaticum."7

Basil of Caesarea (c. 375)

Basil's canonical letters turned the graded system into a detailed penitential code, assigning each sin a fixed term distributed across the four orders — weepers, hearers, kneelers, and standers — the intentional murderer, for instance, drawing a full twenty years (four weeping, four hearing, seven kneeling, four standing) before the sacrament is restored.8

The Irish Penitentials (c. 525–650)

The Irish and Welsh monastic handbooks — Finnian, Columbanus, and Cummean — quietly reversed the ancient discipline: confession became private ("sins are to be absolved in secret"), each sin drew a fixed "tariff" of bread-and-water fasting graded to deed and rank, and, unlike the once-only public penance, it could be repeated — "there is no impediment to the iteration of penance."9 Carried to the Continent and to England by the Celtic and Anglo-Saxon missions, this form displaced the public discipline it could not otherwise be replaced.10

The Fourth Lateran Council (1215)

The Fourth Lateran Council made the now-universal private confession universal law: Canon 21, Omnis utriusque sexus, requires every Christian of "both sexes" past the age of discretion to "confess all their sins at least once a year to their own (parish) priest" and communicate at Easter, on pain of excommunication and denial of Christian burial — and in the same canon binds the priest to the seal of confession, never to "make known the sinner."11

The Council of Trent (1551)

Against the Reformers — who retained confession but denied it was a sacrament of satisfaction and read priestly absolution as a mere announcement of the gospel — the Council of Trent (Session 14) defined penance as a true sacrament instituted by Christ for sins after baptism (the "second plank after shipwreck"), whose parts are contrition, confession, and satisfaction, and whose absolution "is not a bare ministry only… but is after the manner of a judicial act, whereby sentence is pronounced by the priest as by a judge."12
