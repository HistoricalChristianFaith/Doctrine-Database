Penance & Confession (the forgiveness of post-baptismal sin)

What did the church do with a Christian who sinned after baptism? This timeline records the answers in the order of their earliest extant attestation: the Shepherd of Hermas (c. 150), which reports teachers who allowed no repentance but baptism and grants one further repentance; the public discipline of exomologesis and the orders of penitents (Tertullian, Cyprian, Nicaea, Basil); the Montanist Tertullian and Novatian, who refused reconciliation for the gravest sins, and the bishops and synods who granted it (Callistus, Cyprian, the Roman synod of 251, Pacian, Ambrose); the Irish penitentials (c. 525–650), with private confession, fixed tariffs, and repeatable penance; the Fourth Lateran Council's precept of annual confession (1215); Thomas Aquinas's treatise on the sacrament (c. 1274); and the Council of Trent's definitions of 1551. Whether the earliest church held the gravest sins to lie outside its power to reconcile is a disputed reading, recorded under Disputed readings.

Related doctrines: The sacraments — see also the seven sacraments · confirmation & chrismation · anointing of the sick

Timeline

The Shepherd of Hermas (c. 150)

The Shepherd of Hermas reports "some teachers" who held that baptism is the only repentance there is, and has the Shepherd confirm it as "sound doctrine" — then qualifies it: for those already baptized the Lord has appointed one further repentance, but "he has opportunity to repent but once," for "there is but one repentance to the servants of God."1

Tertullian (c. 203 and c. 217)

Tertullian, as a churchman, gives an account of this "second repentance" — the "plank" for the shipwrecked sinner — and of its outward rite, exomologesis: confession, sackcloth and ashes, fasting, and prostration before the presbyters and the congregation.2 Later, as a Montanist, he reversed course: attacking a bishop's edict that forgave adultery and fornication, he divided sins into the remissible and the irremissible, and denied that the bishop could remit the graver kind, whose pardon he reserved to God ("Who, moreover, was able to forgive sins? This is His alone prerogative").3

Callistus of Rome (c. 217)

The edict Tertullian mocks — "the bishop of bishops… I remit… the sins both of adultery and of fornication" — is assigned to Callistus, bishop of Rome, by Harnack and by Hanna, and to Agrippinus of Carthage by F. X. Murphy in the New Catholic Encyclopedia (see the detail page). Callistus's rival Hippolytus attacks him for the same leniency, complaining that Callistus reconciled the sexually sinful ("all had their sins forgiven by himself") and defended a mixed church — wheat and tares, the ark of clean and unclean — that holds sinners within it rather than expelling them.4

Cyprian of Carthage (c. 251)

After the Decian persecution left the church full of the lapsed, Cyprian set the course between those who would readmit the lapsed at once and those who would not readmit them: even those who only bought certificates (the libellatici) have sinned and must repent, and peace is not to be seized on the strength of martyrs' letters but granted by the bishop — yet the penitent, even the adulterer, is offered reconciliation, for to deny repentance its "fruit" only drives the fallen to despair.5 He records that Novatian himself had once endorsed peace for the dying lapsed.6

Novatian (c. 251)

Novatian, a Roman presbyter, was set up as a rival bishop of Rome to Cornelius and led a separate church. Socrates (c. 439) reports the letter he then sent to the churches: "they should not admit to the sacred mysteries those who had sacrificed; but exhorting them to repentance, leave the pardoning of their offense to God, who has the power to forgive all sin."7 Eusebius, quoting the synodical account, reports him as acting toward the lapsed "as if there was no longer for them a hope of salvation, not even if they should do all things pertaining to a genuine and pure conversion"; his followers called themselves the Cathari, "the pure," and a Roman synod of sixty bishops condemned the position and ruled that the fallen be healed "with the medicines of repentance."8

The Council of Nicaea (325)

The canons of the Council of Nicaea set graded orders and terms of years: the lapsed pass through graded orders of penitents — hearers, then prostrators, then those who stand with the faithful "but without oblation" — for fixed terms of years before full communion; and, under what Canon 13 calls "the ancient canonical law," no one at the point of death "must be deprived of the last and most indispensable Viaticum."9

Basil of Caesarea (c. 375)

Basil's canonical letters set out a detailed penitential code, assigning each sin a fixed term distributed across the four orders — weepers, hearers, kneelers, and standers — the intentional murderer, for instance, drawing a full twenty years (four weeping, four hearing, seven kneeling, four standing) before the sacrament is restored.10

Pacian of Barcelona (c. 380)

Pacian, bishop of Barcelona, answered a Novatianist named Sympronian in three Letters and a treatise, the Paraenesis ad Paenitentiam — against the Novatianists then active in Spain. The Novatian case, he reports, “amounts to this… That there is no room for repentance after Baptism; that the Church cannot remit mortal sin; that by the receiving of sinners she herself perishes” — and he answers that the power to bind and loose was not the Apostles’ alone but passed to their successors the bishops, so that “whether we baptize, whether we constrain to penance, or grant pardon to the penitent, we do this by the authority of Christ,” a power from which Christ, in the terms of the grant, “excepted nothing whatever… great or small.”11

Ambrose of Milan (c. 384)

Ambrose, writing his two books Concerning Repentance against the Novatianists, argues for the church’s power to reconcile even the gravest sinner: appealing to the keys and to Christ’s word “whosesoever sins ye forgive they are forgiven” (John 20:22–23), he holds that “when the Lord forgave all sins, He made an exception of none” and “granted to His priests the power of loosing without any exception” — the graver the sin, the heavier the penance, but the door is never shut — and reads the prodigal son, restored by his father to robe, ring, and feast, as the very pattern of the reconciled penitent.12

The Irish Penitentials (c. 525–650)

The Irish and Welsh monastic handbooks — Finnian, Columbanus, and Cummean — differ from the public canonical discipline on three points: confession is private ("sins are to be absolved in secret"), each sin draws a fixed "tariff" of bread-and-water fasting graded to deed and rank, and, unlike the once-only public penance, it can be repeated — "there is no impediment to the iteration of penance."13 Their editors McNeill and Gamer write that the penitentials spread because "the bishops had nothing practicable to offer in their place," and that "as a working system the public discipline could hardly have been imposed on the Germanic peoples," although the synods of Chalon-sur-Saône (813) and Paris (829) condemned them.14

The Fourth Lateran Council (1215)

The Fourth Lateran Council made annual confession a precept for all the faithful: Canon 21, Omnis utriusque sexus, requires every Christian of "both sexes" past the age of discretion to "confess all their sins at least once a year to their own (parish) priest" and communicate at Easter, on pain of excommunication and denial of Christian burial — and in the same canon binds the priest to the seal of confession, never to "make known the sinner."15

Thomas Aquinas (c. 1274)

In the Summa Theologiae Thomas Aquinas treats penance as a sacrament in its matter, form, parts, and minister. For Thomas, penance is a true sacrament, in which "the priest, by his deed and word … signifies the work of God Who forgives his sins"; its matter is the acts of the penitent (with sin the "remote matter… for detestation, and destruction"); its parts are contrition, confession, and satisfaction; its minister is a priest alone, wielding the "key of ministry"; and confessing every mortal sin to that priest is "necessary for the salvation of a man who has fallen into a mortal actual sin." He defends the indicative form — the sacrament "consists… in the removal of a certain matter, viz. sin… expressed by the priest saying: 'I absolve thee.'"16

The Council of Trent (1551)

In Session 14 the Council of Trent defined penance as a true sacrament instituted by Christ for sins after baptism (the "second plank after shipwreck"), whose parts are contrition, confession, and satisfaction, and whose absolution "is not a bare ministry only… but is after the manner of a judicial act, whereby sentence is pronounced by the priest as by a judge"; its Canon 9 anathematizes the view that absolution is "a bare ministry of pronouncing and declaring sins to be forgiven."17
