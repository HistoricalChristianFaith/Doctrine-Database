Who Was Og of Bashan?

Og is the king of Bashan whom Numbers and Deuteronomy record Israel defeating on the march to Canaan, and whose iron bedstead Deuteronomy measures. He “went out against” Israel “to battle at Edrei,” and the LORD told Moses, “Fear him not: for I have delivered him into thy hand… thou shalt do to him as thou didst unto Sihon king of the Amorites” (Numbers 21:33–35). His was a kingdom of “threescore cities… the region of Argob… which was called the land of giants” (Deuteronomy 3:4–13), ruled from Astaroth in Edrei. Deuteronomy adds: “For only Og king of Bashan remained of the remnant of giants; behold, his bedstead was a bedstead of iron… nine cubits was the length thereof, and four cubits the breadth of it, after the cubit of a man” (Deuteronomy 3:11).

Later books of the Hebrew Bible recall him among the kings overthrown. Og is “of the remnant of the giants” — the last of the Rephaim — who “dwelt at Ashtaroth and at Edrei” (Joshua 12:4; 13:12); the Psalms twice set him beside Sihon among the “great kings” and “famous kings” the LORD “slew” — “and Og the king of Bashan: for his mercy endureth for ever” (Psalm 136:20; 135:11); Nehemiah’s levites rehearse the same conquest (Nehemiah 9:22). Later sources say more: the rabbinic sources make Og an antediluvian who outrode the Flood on the roof of Noah’s ark and later tried to crush the camp of Israel under an uprooted mountain; Origen and Augustine read him figuratively, as one of the hostile powers and as a figure of the devil, and Eusebius places his cities at towns of Roman Arabia “where giants dwelled”; and, drawing on the Ugaritic rpʾum, Baruch Margulis (1970) linked Og’s Ashtaroth and Edrei to a Ugaritic text, and Michael Heiser (2015) read “the last of the Rephaim, who dwelt at Ashtaroth and Edrei” against a Canaanite cult of the deified royal dead, with Bashan as an underworld frontier.

This page is a biographical tradition-timeline: not a life of Og but a history of what was said and believed about him across the centuries, set out earliest extant source first. Its “witnesses” are the datable sources that report on or interpret him — the Torah and its Old Testament echoes, the Septuagint, Josephus, the Greek and Latin Fathers (Origen, Eusebius, Jerome, Augustine, Bede), and the rabbinic sources that tell of his survival of the Flood. Three questions about him are answered differently in the sources below: what the iron “bedstead” measures; whether “only Og… remained” refers to the conquest or, as Pirqei de-Rabbi Eliezer reads it, to the Flood; and whether “the last of the Rephaim” names a giant, a shade of the royal dead, or both, as Heiser reads it. Deuteronomy names other peoples of great stature beside him: the Anakim of Hebron and the Emim and Zamzummim of Transjordan (Deuteronomy 2:10–21); the Philistine champion Goliath of Gath has his own timeline.

Timeline

The biblical portrait (Numbers 21; Deuteronomy 1–3; Joshua 12–13; the Psalms)

In Numbers, Og first appears in a campaign report. On the road north Israel turns “by the way of Bashan,” and “Og the king of Bashan went out against them, he, and all his people, to the battle at Edrei.” The outcome repeats Sihon’s: “the LORD said unto Moses, Fear him not… So they smote him, and his sons, and all his people, until there was none left him alive; and they possessed his land.” Deuteronomy repeats it and enlarges the kingdom — “threescore cities… all the region of Argob, the kingdom of Og in Bashan… which was called the land of giants” — and the Psalms name him as one of the “great” and “famous kings” the LORD struck down: “Sihon king of the Amorites… and Og the king of Bashan… for his mercy endureth for ever.”1

The record also gives Og a measure and a lineage. He is “of the remnant of the giants” — the last of the Rephaim — “which dwelt at Ashtaroth and at Edrei,” and Deuteronomy points the reader to a surviving relic: “behold, his bedstead was a bedstead of iron; is it not in Rabbath of the children of Ammon? nine cubits was the length thereof, and four cubits the breadth of it, after the cubit of a man.” Nine cubits by four — on the ordinary cubit, some thirteen feet by six. Maria Lindquist compares it with the cultic bed of Marduk at Babylon ⚠ unverified. Amos, recalling the Amorite whom God destroyed before Israel, uses a simile of scale: “whose height was like the height of the cedars, and he was strong as the oaks.”2

The Septuagint (3rd–2nd century BC) — “of the Raphain… a bed of iron”

The Greek translators of Deuteronomy keep the measurement and transliterate the people’s name. “For only Og the king of Basan was left of the Raphain: behold, his bed was a bed of iron (klīnē sidēra)… the length of it is nine cubits, and the breadth of it four cubits, according to the cubit of a man.” Where the King James has “the remnant of giants,” the Septuagint transliterates Raphain here rather than using gigantes, the word it uses for the Nephilim and the Rephaim elsewhere. The bed, and its exact dimensions, pass into Greek unchanged.3

Josephus (Antiquities of the Jews 4.5.3, c. AD 93) — the giant king and his iron bed at Rabbath

Retelling the war for a Greco-Roman readership, Josephus makes Og a marvel of stature and bearing — “Og had very few equals, either in the largeness of his body, or handsomeness of his appearance… a man of great activity in the use of his hands” — and then reads his size directly off the relic Deuteronomy names: “men could easily guess at his strength and magnitude when they took his bed at Rabbath, the royal city of the Ammonites; its structure was of iron, its breadth four cubits, and its length a cubit more than double thereto.” He takes the “bedstead” as literal furniture.4 Full detail »

Origen of Alexandria (Homilies on Joshua 3, c. AD 240) — Og among the “kings of the hostile powers”

Preaching through Joshua, Origen contrasts the few kings felled east of the Jordan under Moses with the many that fall once “Jesus” (the Greek Joshua) leads: there were not “several kings of the hostile powers said to have been cast down by them. He says only that they cast down ‘Sihon, king of the Amorites,’ and ‘Og, king of Bashan,’ and the Amalekites across the Jordan. But Jesus, where he leads the army, now ‘five kings’ fall together.” Origen reads Og not for his size but as one of the “kings of the hostile powers,” a spiritual adversary overthrown.5

Eusebius of Caesarea (Onomasticon, c. AD 325) — Og’s cities, “where giants dwelt”

Eusebius, cataloguing the places of Scripture, locates Og’s kingdom on the map of Roman Arabia and records the giant-tradition attached to it. Of Astarōth: “the ancient city of Og… where giants dwelled… located in the Batanea six miles from Adraa.” Of Edrai: “Whence the gigantic (and powerful) Og, king of Basan… Now there is Adra a famous city of Arabia, twenty-four miles from Bostra.” The two towns Joshua gives “the remnant of the Rephaim” are, for Eusebius, places that can be visited, and still associated with giants.6

Jerome (Commentaries on Isaiah, Micah, and Amos, c. AD 393–410) — Bashan the land of “ignominy” and “confusion,” and the cedar-tall Amorite read as Sihon

Jerome explains Og’s kingdom by etymology. On Isaiah: “Bashan is a region of Arabia, over which Og ruled, who was called king of Bashan; and it means αἰσχύνη, that is, ignominy”; on Micah, there is “another confusion that leads to death, in which Og, king of Bashan, once dwelt, since Bashan means confusion.” Amos’s cedar-tall Amorite Jerome identifies not with Og but with Sihon, the king of Heshbon: God destroyed “Sihon king of the Amorites, who was as lofty and as strong as a cedar and an oak; and I crushed his fruit above, and his roots below.”7

Augustine of Hippo (Expositions on the Psalms 135–136, c. AD 415) — Og “the heaper-together,” a figure of the devil

Augustine, expounding the two psalms that name Og, applies the etymology of his name to the devil. On Psalm 136’s “And Og, the king of Basan”: “The ‘heaper-together,’ such is the meaning of Og, and, king of ‘confusion,’ which Basan signifies. For what else does the devil heap together but confusion?” On Psalm 135 he draws the moral from the slaughter of the mighty kings: “When you see what has been done to the wicked, take heed lest it be done to you… God… drove out the wicked.” Augustine reads the name allegorically: Og as a figure of the adversary who heaps together, whom God casts out.8

The Venerable Bede (Commentary on Genesis, on Genesis 14:5, c. AD 720) — “Rephaim in Greek are called Giants”

Bede, commenting on the raid of the eastern kings, equates the Rephaim with the Giants. At Ashtaroth-Karnaim — one of Og’s own cities — the kings “struck the Rephaim… Rephaim in Hebrew, in Greek are called Giants: in the singular number… a Rephaim is called a Giant. Now their land… Ashtaroth-karnaim… because indeed they were the dwellings of strong men.” Bede does not name Og here; the Rephaim he glosses as Giants are the people of whom Deuteronomy calls Og “the remnant,” and Ashtaroth is one of Og’s cities.9

The rabbinic tradition (Targum Pseudo-Jonathan; the Babylonian Talmud; Pirqei de-Rabbi Eliezer; traditions from c. AD 200, redacted c. 500–800) — the antediluvian who outrode the Flood

The rabbinic sources make Og a survivor of the Flood: an antediluvian, brother of Sihon and, in one strand, a descendant of the fallen Watchers: “Sihon and Og were brothers… sons of Ahijah, son of Shamhazai,” and Rabbi Yoḥanan reads the fugitive “one that was saved” of Genesis 14:13 as “Og, who was saved from the punishment of the generation of the flood.” The Targum tells how: “Og came, who had been spared from the giants that died in the deluge, and had ridden protected upon the top of the ark, and sustained with food by Noah”; Pirqei de-Rabbi Eliezer adds that Og “sat down on a piece of wood under the gutter of the ark… he swore to Noah… that he would be their servant for ever,” and Noah “bored an aperture in the ark… his food daily.” Pirqei de-Rabbi Eliezer cites Deuteronomy 3:11’s “only Og… remained” for the escape, and the Talmud asks how he stood in the Flood’s boiling water.10

Where Scripture measures his bed, the aggadah measures the giant himself in mountains. At the last, Og “said: How large is the camp of Israel? It is three parasangs. I will go and uproot a mountain three parasangs long and I will hurl it upon them and kill them.” God sent grasshoppers that “pierced the peak of the mountain and it fell on his neck… his teeth were extended to one side of his head and to the other and he was unable to remove it”; and Moses, “ten cubits tall,” “took an axe ten cubits long, jumped up ten cubits, and struck Og in the ankle and killed him.” In this story a ten-cubit Moses with a ten-cubit axe reaches only Og’s ankle.11 Full detail »

Og and the Ugaritic Rephaim (20th–21st century)

After the decipherment of the Ugaritic tablets from Ras Shamra, Baruch Margulis (1970) proposed a third reading of “the last of the Rephaim.” He reads the Ugaritic libation-prayer KTU 1.108 as enthroning the god Rapiu — divine patron of the rpʾum, the cognate of the biblical Rephaim — “in Ashtaroth… in Edrei,” the two cities Deuteronomy and Joshua assign Og; a second liturgy (KTU 1.161) summons the rpʾum as the deified spirits of dead kings. Michael Heiser (2015) developed the link: on his reading “Og… of the remnant of the Rephaim, who dwelt at Ashtaroth and Edrei” is at once a giant king and a shade, and Heiser reads the name Bashan as punning on the word for “serpent” and the region as a Canaanite frontier of the underworld.12
