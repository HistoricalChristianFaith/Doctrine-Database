John of Damascus on the Intermediate State

Dates: c. 749 · Wikipedia
Summary: John of Damascus (c. 675–749), monk and theologian, sets out in An Exact Exposition of the Orthodox Faith a systematic account of the soul. In Book II he defines the soul as "a living essence, simple, incorporeal … immortal, reasoning and intelligent," a substance that uses the body but is not dependent on it — so death does not extinguish the person but separates a still-living, still-rational soul. In Book III, on the Descent to Hades, he holds that Christ's deified soul went down to Hades and "freed those who had been bound for ages," bringing light to the dead and leading the captive out.

Claims & evidence

The soul as a living, simple, incorporeal, immortal, rational essence

The soul, accordingly, is a living essence, simple, incorporeal, invisible in its proper nature to bodily eyes, immortal, reasoning and intelligent, formless, making use of an organised body, and being the source of its powers of life, and growth, and sensation, and generation, mind being but its purest part and not in any wise alien to it; (for as the eye to the body, so is the mind to the soul); further it enjoys freedom and volition and energy, and is mutable, that is, it is given to change, because it is created.

— An Exact Exposition of the Orthodox Faith, Book II, ch. 12 ("Concerning Man"), historicalchristian.faith. Because the soul is an incorporeal, immortal, rational substance that merely "makes use of" the body, on this account it persists in conscious existence when the body dies.

The soul incorporeal "by free gift," not by nature

We understand two kinds of what is incorporeal and invisible and formless: the one is such in essence, the other by free gift: and likewise the one is such in nature, and the other only in comparison with the denseness of matter. God then is incorporeal by nature, but the angels and demons and souls are said to be so by free gift, and in comparison with the denseness of matter.

— An Exact Exposition of the Orthodox Faith, Book II, ch. 12, historicalchristian.faith. John distinguishes the incorporeality of the soul, which is "by free gift," from God's, which is "by nature." (Of the angels he says earlier in Book II, ch. 3, that each is "immortal, not by nature but by grace," historicalchristian.faith.)

The descent of Christ's deified soul to Hades

The soul when it was deified descended into Hades, in order that, just as the Sun of Righteousness rose for those upon the earth, so likewise He might bring light to those who sit under the earth in darkness and shadow of death … And thus after He had freed those who had been bound for ages, straightway He rose again from the dead, shewing us the way of resurrection.

— An Exact Exposition of the Orthodox Faith, Book III, ch. 29 ("Concerning the Descent to Hades"), historicalchristian.faith. In John's account Hades holds souls "bound for ages"; Christ's separated (and deified) soul enters it and frees them. Gregory the Dialogist (c. 594) describes the just as "kept back by the gates of hell" because "the intercession of the Mediator was not yet come."

Context

  • John's Exposition describes the soul as a substance in its own right (immortal, incorporeal, rational) that survives bodily death as a conscious agent. That the Exposition became the standard dogmatic summary for the Christian East is stated here ⚠ unverified.
  • Both quoted passages are firsthand from the Exposition (Book II ch. 12 and Book III ch. 29).
  • He states that souls are incorporeal "by free gift," and God alone "by nature."
  • Latin witnesses on this timeline describe the same event in terms of the "secret rest of the fathers" (see Gregory the Dialogist).
