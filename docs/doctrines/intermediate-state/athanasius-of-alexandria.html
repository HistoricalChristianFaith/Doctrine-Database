Athanasius of Alexandria on the Intermediate State

Dates: c. 371 · Wikipedia
Summary: Athanasius of Alexandria (c. 296–373), defending the integrity of Christ's incarnate body against those who said the Word was changed into flesh and bone, affirms that while the body lay in the tomb the Word — not parted from it — descended "to preach, as Peter says, also to the spirits in prison" (1 Pet 3:19), a fourth-century witness to the descent, given within a christological argument. Thomas Aquinas later cites the passage in an objection in Summa Theologiae III q.52 a.2, where it links 1 Peter 3:19 to the descent into hell.

Claims & evidence

The body in the grave and the Word preaching to the spirits in prison

But in the Body which was circumcised, and carried, and ate and drank, and was weary, and was nailed on the tree and suffered, there was the impassible and incorporeal Word of God. This Body it was that was laid in a grave, when the Word had left it, yet was not parted from it, to preach, as Peter says, also to the spirits in prison.

— Letter to Epictetus (Letter 59), §5, historicalchristian.faith. Athanasius's argument in the letter is that the body born of Mary is truly human, not the very Essence of the Word changed into "flesh, bones, hair." To make the point he distinguishes body from Word at death — the body alone is "laid in a grave," while the Word, though it had "left it," was "not parted from it" and went to the spirits in prison. The descent enters in passing, as a premise of his argument that the Word and the body are distinct.

The Word, not the body, preaching among the dead

And this above all shews the foolishness of those who say that the Word was changed into bones and flesh. For if this had been so, there were no need of a tomb. For the Body would have gone by itself to preach to the spirits in Hades. But as it was, He Himself went to preach, while the Body Joseph wrapped in a linen cloth, and laid it away at Golgotha.

— Letter to Epictetus (Letter 59), §6, historicalchristian.faith. Athanasius presses the same distinction one step further: the body lay in the tomb, while "He Himself" — the Word — "went to preach" among the dead. The reasoning does not argue for the descent; it uses it to distinguish the body in the tomb at Golgotha from the Word active among "the spirits in Hades" in the interval between death and resurrection.

Reception and context

  • The descent appears in Athanasius as a premise, not a thesis: the question in the letter is the nature of Christ's body, and Christ's preaching to the spirits in prison (1 Pet 3:19) is a point he does not stop to argue.
  • The creed quoted in De Synodis 8. De Synodis (On the Councils of Ariminum and Seleucia) §8 contains the words: "descended into the parts beneath the earth, and regulated the things there, Whom the gate-keepers of hell saw (Job xxxviii. 17, LXX.) and shuddered." The line belongs to the creed dated by consulate at Sirmium (359), which Athanasius quotes as the paper produced at Ariminum by Germinius, Auxentius, Valens, Ursacius and others, and argues against; it is not his own composition (historicalchristian.faith). The Letter to Epictetus passages above are his own words. On the creed clause itself, see the question of the descent clause in the Apostles' Creed.
  • Later citation. Thomas Aquinas quotes the Letter to Epictetus in Summa Theologiae III q.52 a.2 ("Whether Christ went down into the hell of the lost?"), objection 3: 1 Peter 3:19 "is understood of Christ's descent into hell, as Athanasius says (Ep. ad Epict.)." In his reply Aquinas writes that "these words of Peter are referred by some to Christ's descent into hell," and that "Augustine, however, furnishes a better exposition of the text in his Epistle to Evodius," referring the preaching not to the descent but to "the operation of His Godhead" (newadvent.org).
