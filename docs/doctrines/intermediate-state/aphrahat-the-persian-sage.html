Aphrahat the Persian Sage on the Intermediate State

Dates: c. 345 · Wikipedia
Summary: Aphrahat (c. 270–345), author of the Syriac Demonstrations, teaches a sleep of the dead: from death until the resurrection the dead "sleep this slumber without knowing good from evil," so that "as yet no one has received his reward" — neither the righteous their kingdom nor the wicked their torment. Alongside it he teaches that a heavenly "spirit," given to the righteous, goes to the Lord and waits there for the resurrection of its body.

Claims & evidence

Death as sleep, from Scripture's language for the dead

For such death is a sleep, as David said, I lay down and slept, and awoke. Again Isaiah said, They that sleep in the dust shall awake. And our Lord said concerning the daughter of the chief of the synagogue, The damsel is not dead, but sleeping a slumber. And concerning Lazarus, He said to His disciples:—Our friend Lazarus has fallen asleep; but I go to waken him. And the Apostle said:—We shall all sleep, but we shall not all be changed. And again he said:—Concerning those that sleep, be ye not grieved.

— Demonstration VIII, Of the Resurrection of the Dead, §18, historicalchristian.faith. Aphrahat builds the teaching on the Bible's "sleep" language for the dead (Ps 13:3; Isa 26:19; Mark 5:39; John 11:11; 1 Cor 15:51; 1 Thess 4:13), which he takes as a description of the dead's condition.1

The dead sleep "without knowing good from evil" until the Judge comes

But our faith thus teaches, that when men fall asleep, they sleep this slumber without knowing good from evil. And the righteous look not forward to their promises, nor do the wicked look forward to their sentence of punishment, until the Judge come and separate those whose place is at His right hand from those whose place is at His left.

— Demonstration VIII, §20, historicalchristian.faith. Here the righteous do not yet look forward to their promises nor the wicked to their sentence, until the general judgment. Just before it (§19) Aphrahat pictures the dead as a sleeping servant who dreams pleasantly or fearfully of his coming wages; §20 then states that they sleep "without knowing good from evil."2

"As yet no one has received his reward"; the virgins are "sleeping up to the present time"

From all these things, understand thou, my beloved, as it has been made certain for thee, that as yet no one has received his reward. For the righteous have not inherited the kingdom, nor have the wicked gone into torment. … And those virgins that are waiting the bridegroom are sleeping up to the present time, and are awaiting the cry when they will awake.

— Demonstration VIII, §22, historicalchristian.faith. Reward and torment are alike deferred to the resurrection: the harvesters have not been paid, the King has not yet returned, and the virgins are still asleep. The intermediate state, for Aphrahat, is an interval of waiting rather than a place of present recompense.3

The heavenly "spirit" that goes to the Lord until the resurrection

But recollect, my beloved … that the spirit which the righteous receive, according to its heavenly nature, goes to our Lord until the time of the Resurrection, when it shall come to put on the body in which it dwelt. And at every time it has the memory of this in the presence of God, and looks eagerly for the Resurrection of that body in which it dwelt.

— Demonstration VIII, §23, historicalchristian.faith. Answering those who cite Paul's "When we shall depart from the body, we shall be present with the Lord" (cf. 2 Cor 5:8), Aphrahat distinguishes: the man sleeps in the dust, while the heavenly spirit the righteous were given returns to God and waits there for the resurrection of the body in which it dwelt. The wicked, from whom "the Holy Spirit is far removed," "are buried after the manner of animals."4

Reception and context

  • Related positions on this timeline. Justin rejects those who say there is no resurrection of the dead and that souls "when they die, are taken to heaven" (a different position from Aphrahat's); Calvin wrote Psychopannychia against those who "maintain that the soul dies or sleeps," whom he identifies as some Anabaptists; and the Westminster Confession states that the souls of the dead "neither die nor sleep."
  • Sleep and the waiting spirit. Aphrahat predicates the sleep of the buried person, who knows neither good nor evil until the resurrection, while the heavenly spirit given to the righteous waits with God and "has the memory of this in the presence of God" (§23). He does not describe the soul as ceasing to exist.
  • The demonstration closes on the bodily resurrection: "in the day of the Resurrection thy body shall arise in its entirety" (§25).
  • Date: the Demonstrations were composed 337–345, a generation before his compatriot Ephrem, whose Hymns on Paradise also defer the entry into Paradise to the resurrection; Ephrem's souls of the just reside on the borders of Paradise and await their bodies.
