Martin Luther on the Intermediate State (the Descent into Hell)

Dates: c. 1533 (the Torgau sermon) · Wikipedia
Summary: Martin Luther (1483–1546), the Wittenberg reformer, gave the descent into hell a reading distinct both from the medieval scholastic one (on this timeline, Aquinas) and from Calvin's. In an Easter sermon preached in the castle at Torgau in 1533, he taught a real, victorious descent: the whole Christ — God and man — descended after his burial and destroyed hell and the devil's power, delivering believers from it. But Luther refused to speculate about the manner of it (whether, for instance, Christ descended according to the soul or only through his might and power): he pressed Christians to "stick to the words" in simple faith and leave the mechanics for the next world. The Lutheran Formula of Concord (1577) directs the churches to this sermon.

Claims & evidence

A victorious descent: hell "torn apart" and the devil's power destroyed

hell has been torn apart for us through Christ, and that the devil's kingdom and power have been destroyed, which is why Christ died, was buried, and descended, so that they should no longer harm us or subdue us

— Third Sermon for Easter Day, Torgau, 1533 (tr. Paul A. Rydecki from the Weimar edition, WA 37), godwithuslc.org. Luther presents the descent as an act of conquest: Christ went down to hell to destroy its power. The sermon does not discuss a place of waiting patriarchs (the limbus patrum); that Luther had already rejected it is stated here ⚠ unverified. The point of the article is its benefit — that hell and the devil can no longer hold the believer.

The descent as deliverance

He descended into hell, in order that He might also deliver us out from there who should have lain captive therein

— Third Sermon for Easter Day, Torgau, 1533, godwithuslc.org. Luther reads the descent soteriologically: Christ entered death and the grave, and then hell, precisely "that He might bring His own out from there." The accent falls on the believer's rescue rather than on the deliverance of the Old-Testament saints that the medieval scheme had foregrounded.

"Stick to the words": the manner left open

Whoever wishes to keep from veering off the straight path, let him only stick to the words and imagine them for himself in a simple way, as best he can.

— Third Sermon for Easter Day, Torgau, 1533, godwithuslc.org. Luther leaves the disputed questions — for instance whether Christ descended "according to the soul, or only through His might and power" — unanswered, treating them as beyond what the article requires; he says that the descent "did not happen bodily, as Christ surely remained in the grave for the three days," and that "the same Christ, God and man in one person, went to hell" (godwithuslc.org). It is this self-limiting "Christian simplicity" that the Formula of Concord singles out and adopts.

Context

  • The timeline carries three readings of the article from this period and before it. In Aquinas Christ descends to empty the limbus patrum of the Old Testament righteous; in Luther's 1533 sermon the descent is a victory over hell; in Calvin's Institutes (II.16) the "descent" is Christ's soul bearing divine judgment on the cross. Luther places the descent of the whole person, God and man, after the burial and before the resurrection, while the body lay in the grave.
  • The Torgau sermon was the third of Luther's Easter-Day sermons that year (1533). The Formula of Concord (Epitome and Solid Declaration, Art. IX) directs the churches to "Dr. Luther … in his sermon, delivered in the castle at Torgau in the year 1533."
  • Luther's sermon is cited on the disputed-reading page on the creed's descent clause (whether "he descended into hell" is original to the creed and should be retained).
