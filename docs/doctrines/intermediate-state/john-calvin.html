John Calvin on the Intermediate State

Dates: c. 1542 · Wikipedia
Summary: John Calvin (1509–1564), the Geneva reformer, makes two moves on the intermediate state. In his early treatise Psychopannychia (written 1534, published 1542) he argues against those who "maintain that the soul dies or sleeps," whom he identifies as some Anabaptists: the soul is a substance that, after the body dies, still lives — conscious, sensing, understanding — the believing dead resting with Christ and the wicked already suffering. He also rejects the scholastic geography of the intermediate state: the limbus patrum he calls a "fable," and the speculative mapping of the soul's "abode" he treats as idle curiosity. His account affirms conscious survival with Christ at death and rejects the limbo of the fathers and purgatory.

Claims & evidence

The soul's conscious survival after death in Psychopannychia

the soul, after the death of the body, still survives, endued with sense and intellect

— Psychopannychia, monergism.com. This is the thesis the whole treatise defends against those he calls "sleepers," whose view his preface (dated Basel, 1536; godrules.net) traces to "some dregs of Anabaptists." Calvin maintains the soul is "a substance, and after the death of the body truly lives, being endued both with sense and understanding" — so the title Psychopannychia (the all-night "wake" of the soul) marks his denial that the soul lies dormant between death and resurrection.

Inquiry into the soul's intermediate abode

it is foolish and rash to inquire into hidden things, farther than God permits us to know

— Institutes of the Christian Religion, III.25.6, ccel.org. While Calvin insists the soul lives and is held in joy or torment after death, he refuses to map its "abode" in detail, warning that to press beyond what Scripture reveals about the intermediate condition is presumption rather than piety. For Calvin, the believing dead are with Christ, and the precise topography is not for us to chart.

Calvin on the limbus patrum

Though this fable has the countenance of great authors, and is now also seriously defended by many as truth, it is nothing but a fable.

— Institutes of the Christian Religion, II.16.9 ("Absurd exposition concerning the Limbus Patrum. This fable refuted"), ccel.org. Calvin grants that the descent into hell "was of no little importance to the accomplishment of redemption," but rejects the reading that makes it a journey to a "subterraneous cavern" where the Fathers' souls were shut up awaiting release. The limbus patrum, which Aquinas taught, Calvin calls a "fable," despite "the countenance of great authors."

Context

  • Calvin's account has two sides: against those he calls "sleepers," whom he identifies as some Anabaptists, the soul does not sleep but consciously lives with Christ; against the scholastic teaching of Aquinas and the Catholic theologians of his day, there is no limbus patrum and no purgatory.
  • Psychopannychia was one of Calvin's earliest works (composed 1534; published 1542). The Westminster Confession (1646), later on this timeline, also affirms the conscious survival of the soul.
  • Calvin reads "He descended into hell" (Institutes II.16) as Christ's spiritual bearing of divine judgment rather than a journey to the limbus patrum, which he rejects in the same chapter.
