The Westminster Confession on the Intermediate State

Dates: 1646 · Wikipedia
Summary: The Westminster Confession of Faith (completed 1646 by the Westminster Assembly) is a Reformed confessional statement on the soul after death. Chapter XXXII, "Of the State of Men after Death, and of the Resurrection of the Dead," makes three claims in one paragraph: the soul neither dies nor sleeps but at death immediately returns to God; the righteous are received into the highest heavens and the wicked are cast into hell; and "besides these two places … the Scripture acknowledgeth none." The Confession thus acknowledges two places only for separated souls, entered at once, and no Abraham's bosom as a third waiting-place, no limbo and no purgatory.

Claims & evidence

The soul after death (XXXII.1)

The bodies of men, after death, return to dust, and see corruption; but their souls (which neither die nor sleep), having an immortal subsistence, immediately return to God who gave them.

— Westminster Confession of Faith, XXXII.1, creeds.net. The Confession affirms that the soul, having "an immortal subsistence," neither dies nor sleeps and "immediately" returns to God when the body dies. Calvin, earlier on this timeline, had argued for the soul's conscious survival against soul-sleep in Psychopannychia (1542).

The righteous and the wicked after death

The souls of the righteous, being then made perfect in holiness, are received into the highest heavens, where they behold the face of God in light and glory, waiting for the full redemption of their bodies; and the souls of the wicked are cast into hell, where they remain in torments and utter darkness, reserved to the judgment of the great day.

— Westminster Confession of Faith, XXXII.1, creeds.net. On the Confession's account the righteous behold God's face before the resurrection, while still "waiting for the full redemption of their bodies." They are "then made perfect in holiness" at death itself, with no purgatorial cleansing between.

"Besides these two places"

Besides these two places for souls separated from their bodies, the Scripture acknowledgeth none.

— Westminster Confession of Faith, XXXII.1, creeds.net. The clause leaves no place for the limbus patrum, the limbo of children, purgatory, or a third "Abraham's bosom" holding-place: the Confession names heaven and hell only, entered immediately at death.

Context

  • The Confession affirms that the soul is conscious after death (it "neither die[s] nor sleep[s]"), as Calvin had argued in Psychopannychia, and, as Calvin had rejected the limbus patrum and purgatory, it acknowledges no place for separated souls besides heaven and hell.
  • Where Aquinas distinguished five abodes — heaven, hell, purgatory, the limbus patrum, and the limbo of children — the Confession acknowledges two places only.
  • The clause "waiting for the full redemption of their bodies" keeps the condition of the blessed dead short of the final state: they already behold God, and the resurrection of the body, the subject of the chapter's remaining paragraphs, is still to come.
