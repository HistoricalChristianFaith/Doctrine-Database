An Ancient Homily on Holy Saturday on the Intermediate State

Dates: c. 403 (ascribed to Epiphanius of Salamis, d. 403; date and authorship disputed) · Wikipedia (Epiphanius of Salamis)
Summary: An anonymous Greek homily for Holy Saturday — transmitted under the name of Epiphanius of Salamis, its authorship and date disputed — gives the descent a dramatic voice. On the day Christ lies dead, "there is a great silence on earth," for the King "has fallen asleep in the flesh" and gone down to seek Adam "as for a lost sheep," to wake the imprisoned dead and lead them up. It narrates the harrowing of Hades as the rescue of the first parents.

Claims & evidence

The "great silence": God has died in the flesh and descended to the dead

Something strange is happening — there is a great silence on earth today, a great silence and stillness. The whole earth keeps silence because the King is asleep. The earth trembled and is still because God has fallen asleep in the flesh and he has raised up all who have slept ever since the world began. God has died in the flesh and hell trembles with fear.

— Homily for Holy Saturday (In sancto et magno Sabbato; Pseudo-Epiphanius), English from the Roman Office of Readings for Holy Saturday. In the homily Christ's body rests in the tomb while, in the realm of the dead, he frees the captives.

The search for Adam and the captive dead

He has gone to search for our first parent, as for a lost sheep. Greatly desiring to visit those who live in darkness and in the shadow of death, he has gone to free from sorrow the captives Adam and Eve … The Lord approached them bearing the cross, the weapon that had won him the victory. … He took him by the hand and raised him up, saying: "Awake, O sleeper, and rise from the dead, and Christ will give you light."

— Homily for Holy Saturday (Pseudo-Epiphanius), Office of Readings. Christ then commands "all who are held in bondage to come forth, all who are in darkness to be enlightened, all who are sleeping to arise," and promises Adam not the earthly paradise but a throne in heaven: "I will not restore you to that paradise, but I will enthrone you in heaven." In the homily the descent empties the place where the dead were held.

Authorship, date, and use

  • Author and date are disputed. The homily belongs to a collection of homilies ascribed to Epiphanius of Cyprus (Salamis, d. 403) and printed after his works; the Greek text is in Petau's edition (Paris, 1622), reprinted in Migne, Patrologia Graeca 43, cols. 439–464, and in Dindorf's Pseudo-Epiphanii homiliae (1859–62).1 It is read at the office of Holy Saturday in the Orthodox Church,1 and Holy Cross Monastery (Orthodox) publishes a complete English translation as the “Sermon of St. Epiphanius of Cyprus for Holy Saturday.”2 André Vaillant (Slavist, editor of the Old Slavonic version, 1958) holds that the attribution to Epiphanius, already contested by Petau, “n'est pas soutenable” (“is not tenable”), that the homily “est médiéval et byzantin,” and that its descent narrative reworks the Gospel of Nicodemus; reading in it a compliment to the emperor Leontius (695–698), he assigns it to “probablement un autre archevêque Épiphane, de la fin du VIIe siècle,” as Petau had supposed, noting an archbishop Epiphanius of Cyprus at the Council in Trullo (691).1 The “c. 403” placement here follows the ascription under which the homily is printed and read; on Vaillant's dating it belongs to the end of the seventh century. Roger Pearse (2025) summarises Vaillant's identification.3
  • The homily is read in the Roman Catholic Office of Readings on Holy Saturday. Its subject, Christ raising Adam and the dead from Hades, is the subject of the Eastern Anastasis (harrowing-of-Hades) icon. It states, as devotion rather than argument, that between cross and resurrection Christ went down to free Adam and the captive dead.
  • It gives in narrative form the descent-and-deliverance picture also found in Eusebius ("descended alone, but rose with many") and Cyril of Jerusalem (the redemption of the righteous imprisoned since Adam) (both dated earlier on this timeline).
