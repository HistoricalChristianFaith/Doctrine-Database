The Gospel of Nicodemus (Acts of Pilate) on the Intermediate State

Dates: c. 450 (apocryphal; date and strata disputed — see below) · Wikipedia
Summary: The Gospel of Nicodemus — its second part, the Descensus ad Inferos ("Descent into Hell") — is a sustained narrative of the harrowing of Hades. Where the Fathers state the descent in a sentence or a hymn, this apocryphon dramatizes it: the patriarchs and prophets waiting in the darkness, John the Baptist heralding the dayspring among the dead, Seth's report of the archangel Michael at paradise's gate, and Christ shattering the gates of brass to lead Adam and the righteous out of Hades into paradise. Its Latin form, in M. R. James's words, "has been the parent of versions in every European language."

Claims & evidence

The dead in "the shadow of death" and the coming light

The people that walked in darkness have seen a great light, and they that dwell in the land of the shadow of death, upon them did the light shine.

— The Gospel of Nicodemus (Acts of Pilate), Descent into Hell 2 (18), M. R. James translation, Wikisource. The narrator-witnesses are the dead themselves: as "a golden heat of the sun" breaks into the gloom, "the father of the whole race of men, together with all the patriarchs and prophets, rejoiced." The narrative places the Old Testament righteous in a holding-place where they wait, conscious and in darkness, for the One who will come down to them.

John the Baptist preaches in Hades as Christ's forerunner — "the dayspring from on high"

I am John, the voice and the prophet of the most High, which came before the face of his advent to prepare his ways … And now have I come before his face, and come down to declare unto you that he is at hand to visit us, even the dayspring, the Son of God, coming from on high unto us that sit in darkness and in the shadow of death.

— Descent into Hell 2 (18), Wikisource. John, slain by Herod, descends ahead of Christ to announce his coming to the dead — the same role he played on earth (Luke 1:78, "the dayspring from on high"). Hippolytus (On Christ and Antichrist 45), earlier on this timeline, also has the Baptist go before Christ to the dead.

Seth reports Michael at paradise's gate and the "oil of mercy"

Thou wilt not be able to receive it save in the last days and times, save when five thousand and five hundred years are accomplished: then shall the most beloved Son of God come upon the earth.

— Descent into Hell 3 (19), Wikisource. Adam bids his son Seth tell the patriarchs what the archangel Michael said when Seth came to the gate of paradise begging "the oil of the tree of mercy" to anoint his dying father: it cannot be had until the appointed years are fulfilled and the Son of God descends to raise Adam's body. The waiting dead "rejoiced with a great rejoicing" at the sign that their release is at hand.

The gates of Hades — "Lift up your gates … the King of glory shall come in"

Remove, O princes, your gates, and be ye lift up, ye everlasting doors, and the King of glory shall come in … The Lord strong and mighty, the Lord mighty in battle, he is the King of glory.

— Descent into Hell 5 (21), Wikisource. The voice of Psalm 24:7–9 thunders twice at the gates; Hades and Satan dispute in terror; the gates of brass are broken, the bars of iron ground to powder, and the bound dead loosed. This dialogue of Hell with Satan is the setting the medieval mystery plays of the Harrowing took up (stated here ⚠ unverified).

Adam and the saints led out of Hades into paradise

And the Lord stretching forth his hand … took the right hand of Adam and went up out of hell, and all the saints followed him.

— Descent into Hell 8 (24), Wikisource. "Peace be unto thee," Christ says to Adam, and signs the cross over him and the patriarchs; he commits them to the archangel Michael, who leads them into paradise. In the narrative the holding-place of the Old Testament righteous is emptied.

Character, date, and reception

  • An apocryphon. The Gospel of Nicodemus is a pseudonymous Passion-and-descent narrative, framed as the report of Nicodemus and of two sons of Simeon raised from the dead, and stands outside the New Testament canon; it appears on this timeline as a witness to belief about the intermediate state.
  • Date and strata are disputed. The work is composite. M. R. James (1924, Wikisource) judges that its first part, the Acts of Pilate (the Passion and Resurrection story), "is not earlier than the fourth century," and of the second part, the Descent into Hell quoted here, that "we do not yet know" when it was appended — "Not, I should conjecture, before the fifth century." Justin and Tertullian already appeal to "Acts of Pilate," lost in the form they knew (stated here ⚠ unverified). The Descent survives chiefly in Greek and Latin recensions that differ in wording; of the Latin, James writes that "it is in Latin that it has chiefly flourished, and has been the parent of versions in every European language." That the composite "Gospel of Nicodemus" reached its present form before c. 600 is stated here ⚠ unverified. The "c. 450" placement here is an approximate, conventional slot for an undatable text, set by the descent layer quoted here — like the Holy Saturday homily, the Odes of Solomon, and the hymn ascribed to Ambrose, it is listed under its work rather than a securely dated author.
  • Narrative and reception. Of the deliverance of the righteous fathers from Hades, M. R. James writes that "the central idea … is exceedingly ancient. Second-century writers are full of it." The apocryphon supplies narrative detail: the named patriarchs, the dialogue of Satan and Hades, the breaking of the brazen gates. That through it the harrowing entered the liturgy, the iconography of the Anastasis, and the Corpus Christi and mystery cycles of the medieval West is stated here ⚠ unverified. It narrates at length the picture stated catechetically by Cyril of Jerusalem and in homily by the Holy Saturday homily.
  • In the apocryphon the saints are led "up out of hell" and handed to Michael, who leads them into paradise: paradise here lies outside Hades, and the righteous pass to it at the descent.
