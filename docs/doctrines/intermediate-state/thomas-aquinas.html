Thomas Aquinas on the Intermediate State

Dates: c. 1274 · Wikipedia
Summary: Thomas Aquinas (c. 1225–1274), Dominican master and author of the Summa Theologiae, sets out the soul's condition between death and resurrection. The separated soul does not sleep: on his account it is at once assigned an abode answering to its state. The Supplement to the Summa (which, according to the editor's note in the English Dominican translation, was "compiled probably by his companion and friend Fra Rainaldo da Piperno" and "gathered from St. Thomas's commentary on the Fourth Book of the Sentences of Peter Lombard," newadvent.org) distinguishes five — paradise (the heaven of the blessed), the hell of the damned, purgatory (where the saved are cleansed), the limbo of the Fathers (limbus patrum, the Old-Testament "Abraham's bosom," now emptied), and the limbo of children. On this account the perfectly cleansed righteous enter the vision of God immediately; those still owing satisfaction for sin pass through purgatory first; and the holy Fathers who died before Christ waited in the limbus patrum until Christ's descent into hell set them free. (In 1336 Pope Benedict XII's constitution Benedictus Deus defined that the purified righteous see God at once, before the resurrection; see Benedict XII.)

Claims & evidence

Purgatory among the abodes of souls

those who die in grace with venial sins an abode is assigned where they are punished ere they receive their reward, which abode is purgatory

— Summa Theologiae, Supplement, q. 69, a. 7, obj. 9, newadvent.org. The sentence stands in an objection to the article "Whether so many abodes should be distinguished?"; in his reply Aquinas grants that "there must needs be a place where such persons are cleansed if they go hence without being perfectly clean." In the body of the article he defines purgatory as the place "where souls are detained from receiving their reward at once on account of the sins they have committed." On his account it is not a third destiny but a temporary station on the way to heaven: the soul is saved, but its remaining debt of temporal punishment is discharged before it enters the vision of God.

Separated souls and the places appointed to them

The separated soul receives nothing directly from corporeal places in the same way as bodies which are maintained by their respective places: yet these same souls, through knowing themselves to be appointed to such places, gather joy or sorrow therefrom

— Summa Theologiae, Supplement, q. 69, a. 1, ad 3, newadvent.org. For Aquinas the abodes order souls by state rather than by mere geography. In the next article ("Whether souls are conveyed to heaven or hell immediately after death?") he answers that "as soon as the soul is set free from the body it is either plunged into hell or soars to heaven," "unless it be held back by some debt, for which its flight must needs be delayed until the soul is first of all cleansed": a soul fully cleansed has nothing to detain it, so it is admitted at once to glory.

The holy Fathers detained in hell before Christ's descent

the holy Fathers were detained in hell for the reason, that, owing to our first parent's sin, the approach to the life of glory was not opened.

— Summa Theologiae III, q. 52, a. 5, newadvent.org. The Old-Testament righteous, free of personal guilt but barred from the vision of God by the inherited debt of original sin, waited in the upper part of hell — the limbus patrum, the "Abraham's bosom" of Luke 16. Aquinas notes that Christ's soul "descended only into that part of hell wherein the just were detained" (III, q. 52, a. 2).

The deliverance of the holy Fathers at the descent

when Christ descended into hell He delivered the holy Fathers from thence.

— Summa Theologiae III, q. 52, a. 5, newadvent.org. By the power of his Passion, Christ "delivered the saints from the penalty whereby they were excluded from the life of glory, so as to be unable to see God in His Essence." On this account the limbus patrum was emptied at the descent, so since Christ no one waits there — the fully-cleansed go straight to God, and those needing satisfaction to purgatory.

Context

  • The Supplement (q. 69, a. 7) distinguishes five abodes for souls after death: paradise; the hell of the damned; purgatory (temporary cleansing of the saved); the limbo of the Fathers (limbus patrum), now emptied by Christ; and the limbo of children (the unbaptised who die in original sin only, kept from the vision of God but spared sensible punishment — Supplement, Appendix I/II).
  • The limbus patrum is, for Aquinas, a feature of the past: it explains where the Old-Testament saints waited and what Christ's "descent into hell" accomplished. Calvin, later on this timeline, calls the limbus patrum a "fable"; the Westminster Confession (1646) acknowledges two places only for separated souls, heaven and hell.
