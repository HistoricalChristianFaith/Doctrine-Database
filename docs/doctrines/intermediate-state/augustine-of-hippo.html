Augustine of Hippo on the Intermediate State

Dates: c. 420 · Wikipedia
Summary: Augustine (354–430), bishop of Hippo, teaches that between death and the resurrection the soul waits in "hidden retreats" (abdita receptacula), resting or suffering in proportion to its earthly merit; Abraham's bosom is the hidden rest of the righteous, divided by a fixed gulf from torment; Tartarus is the prison of the fallen angels; and the dead can be helped by the prayers, alms, and the Eucharistic sacrifice of the living. In the Enchiridion he calls a purgatorial fire for some believers "not impossible," a matter that may be "either ascertained or left doubtful"; in the City of God he writes that "temporary punishments are suffered by some in this life only, by others after death, by others both now and then."

Claims & evidence

The interval in a hidden retreat: rest or affliction by merit

During the time, moreover, which intervenes between a man's death and the final resurrection, the soul dwells in a hidden retreat, where it enjoys rest or suffers affliction just in proportion to the merit it has earned by the life which it led on earth.

— The Enchiridion, ch. 109 ("The State of the Soul During the Interval Between Death and the Resurrection"), historicalchristian.faith. In this compressed statement the soul is conscious and located in the interval (Latin in abditis receptaculis, "in hidden receptacles / retreats"), and its rest or affliction is proportioned to the merit "earned by the life which it led on earth."

Abraham's bosom as the hidden rest of the righteous

If you would not fall into a childish mistake, you must understand Abraham's bosom to be a retired and hidden resting-place where Abraham is; and therefore called Abraham's, not that it is his alone, but because he is the father of many nations, and placed first, that others might imitate his preeminence of faith.

— Catena Aurea on Luke 16:22 (Augustine, De Origine Animae 4.16, as gathered by Aquinas), historicalchristian.faith. Rejecting a bodily reading as "a childish mistake," Augustine takes "Abraham's bosom" as a hidden resting-place holding many souls — the rest of the faithful, entered by imitating Abraham's faith. In his preaching on the same parable he warns that the rich man's torment is owed to "his impiety, infidelity, pride and cruelty," not his wealth, and points to the gulf the rich man begs across "too late in hell."

Tartarus as the prison of the fallen angels

It is a fixed and unchanging religious truth that the devil and his angels are never to return to the life and holiness of the saints. From Scripture we know that God's sentence implies that he dragged them down by infernal ropes to Tartarus and delivered them to be tortured and kept in custody for judgment. They will be received into everlasting fire and there tortured forever and ever.

— City of God 21.23, historicalchristian.faith. Reading 2 Peter 2:4 ("cast them down to Tartarus," tartarōsas), Augustine places the rebel angels in Tartarus as a present prison of "custody for judgment" — a holding-place for the fallen angels distinct from the rest of the righteous, before the "everlasting fire" into which they "will be received."

The dead are aided by the prayers, alms, and Eucharistic sacrifice of the living

Nor can it be denied that the souls of the dead are benefited by the piety of their living friends, who offer the sacrifice of the Mediator, or give alms in the church on their behalf. But these services are of advantage only to those who during their lives have earned such merit, that services of this kind can help them.

— The Enchiridion, ch. 110 ("The Benefit to the Souls of the Dead from the Sacraments and Alms of Their Living Friends"), historicalchristian.faith. The offerings, he adds, are "thank-offerings for the very good," "propitiatory offerings for the not very bad," and merely "a species of consolation to the living" for the very bad — their benefit being "either in obtaining a full remission of sins, or at least in making the condemnation more tolerable." In this chapter their benefit is bounded by the merit earned "during their lives."

Burial honors comfort the living; prayer and the sacrifice profit the dead

So, then, all these things, care of funeral, bestowal in sepulture, pomp of obsequies, are more for comfort of the living, than for help to the dead.

— On Care to Be Had for the Dead, §4, historicalchristian.faith. Augustine distinguishes the two: lavish burial does the dead no spiritual good (the poor Lazarus, carried off by angels, fared better than the rich man with his handsome obsequies), but supplications, he holds, reach them — "there is no doubt that it profits them who while living in the body merited that such things should profit them after this life" (§6). When even bodies cannot be buried, "yet should there be no pretermitting of supplications for the spirits of the dead."

A purgatorial fire for some believers: "not impossible"

And it is not impossible that something of the same kind may take place even after this life. It is a matter that may be inquired into, and either ascertained or left doubtful, whether some believers shall pass through a kind of purgatorial fire, and in proportion as they have loved with more or less devotion the goods that perish, be less or more quickly delivered from it.

— The Enchiridion, ch. 69 ("It is Not Impossible that Some Believers May Pass Through a Purgatorial Fire in the Future Life"), historicalchristian.faith. Reading 1 Corinthians 3:11–15 (the man "saved, yet so as by fire"), Augustine starts from the "fire" of adversity at work in this life and then raises the possibility of "something of the same kind" after death, for believers who will inherit the kingdom; in ch. 67–68 he rejects the opinion that the baptized who "persevere" in grave sin "to the last day of their lives" will be "saved by fire." Here he leaves the question open: it "may be inquired into, and either ascertained or left doubtful." In the City of God (21.13) he writes: "temporary punishments are suffered by some in this life only, by others after death, by others both now and then; but all of them before that last and strictest judgment" (historicalchristian.faith).

The descent into hell "established beyond question"; whom Christ delivered left open

It is established beyond question that the Lord, after He had been put to death in the flesh, descended into hell; for it is impossible to gainsay either that utterance of prophecy, You will not leave my soul in hell … Who, therefore, except an infidel, will deny that Christ was in hell?

— Letter 164 (to Evodius), §2–3, newadvent.org. Augustine treats the descensus — that Christ's soul went to the realm of the dead — as "established beyond question," grounded in Psalm 16:10 (Acts 2:27) and Peter's "loosed the pains of hell" (Acts 2:24), and asks "who, therefore, except an infidel, will deny that Christ was in hell?" What he leaves unsettled is exactly whom Christ delivered there, and whether 1 Peter 3:19's "spirits in prison" belong to the descent at all; he inclines to a non-literal reading, the spirits preached to "in the days of Noah" through Noah's own preaching. See his detail page on 1 Peter 3:19 / the Nephilim question.

Reception and context

  • Augustine's own settled language is the soul in abdita receptacula ("hidden receptacles / retreats"), conscious and located, with rest or affliction already proportioned to earthly merit (Enchiridion 109).
  • Questions left open. Augustine marks parts of the question as unresolved — the purgatorial fire is something that may be "left doubtful" (Enchiridion 69). In Letter 164 to Evodius he does not settle Christ's preaching to the "spirits in prison" (1 Peter 3:19) and entertains a non-literal reading of it. Thomas Aquinas, in Summa Theologiae III q.52 a.2, calls this Augustine's "better exposition of the text" (newadvent.org). See his detail page on 1 Peter 3:19 / the Nephilim question.
  • On the parable of Dives and Lazarus (Luke 16) Augustine insists the rich man is condemned for "impiety, infidelity, pride and cruelty," not for wealth as such (Sermon 299E.5; cf. Sermon 41.5–6 on almsgiving as "friends … with the mammon of iniquity" who "receive you in the eternal dwellings").
