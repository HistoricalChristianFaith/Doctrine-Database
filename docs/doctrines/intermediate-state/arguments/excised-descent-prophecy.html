Was the descent-prophecy that Justin and Irenaeus quote as Scripture (“The Lord God remembered His dead people of Israel”) a passage removed from the Jewish text of the prophets?

Disputed reading · on the intermediate state · witnesses: Justin Martyr, Irenaeus

Related readings: Is the creed clause “he descended into hell” an original article of the Apostles’ Creed, and should it be retained? · on the rabbinic corruption timeline, Did the Jews deliberately corrupt the Hebrew Old Testament text?

The passage

And again, from the sayings of the same Jeremiah these have been cut out: "The Lord God remembered His dead people of Israel who lay in the graves; and He descended to preach to them His own salvation."

— Justin Martyr, Dialogue with Trypho, ch. 72 (c. 165), in the chapter headed “Passages Have Been Removed by the Jews from Esdras and Jeremiah,” source.1

Irenaeus quotes the same saying in varying wording, under varying names:

  1. To Isaiah — "And that it was not a mere man who died for us, Isaiah says: 'And the holy Lord remembered His dead Israel, who had slept in the land of sepulture; and He came down to preach His salvation to them, that He might save them'" (Against Heresies III.20.4).2
  2. To Jeremiah — "As Jeremiah declares, 'The holy Lord remembered His dead Israel, who slept in the land of sepulture; and He descended to them to make known to them His salvation, that they might be saved'" (AH IV.22.1).3
  3. To no prophet by name — Christ "remembered His own dead ones who had formerly fallen asleep, and came down to them that He might deliver them" (AH IV.33.1); "Others … said, 'The holy Lord remembered His own dead ones who slept in the dust, and came down to them to raise them up, that He might save them'" (AH IV.33.12); and, of the three days in the place of the dead, "as the prophet says concerning Him: 'And the Lord remembered His dead saints who slept formerly in the land of sepulture; and He descended to them, to rescue and save them'" (AH V.31.1).4
  4. To Jeremiah — "And in Jeremiah He thus declares His death and descent into hell, saying: 'And the Lord the Holy One of Israel, remembered his dead, which aforetime fell asleep in the dust of the earth; and he went down unto them, to bring the tidings of his salvation, to deliver them'" (Demonstration of the Apostolic Preaching 78).4

No two of these quotations agree word for word; the prophet named is Isaiah once, Jeremiah twice, and no one three times.5 The saying stands in no surviving text of Jeremiah or Isaiah, Hebrew or Greek.6

The question

Justin presents the saying as a prophecy of Jeremiah that Jewish teachers had cut from the Scriptures; the parties below agree that no surviving manuscript of the prophets carries it. What is disputed is where the saying came from: a genuine prophecy that the received text has lost, or a Christian text that Justin and Irenaeus received as prophecy. The modern holders of the first reading located here are Latter-day Saint writers; no Catholic, Orthodox or Protestant scholar holding it, and no Orthodox treatment of the saying, was located.

A genuine prophecy, now missing from the received text of the prophets

On this reading the saying is a prophecy of Jeremiah that the received book does not carry, preserved by the second-century Church.

Held by: Justin Martyr (Dialogue with Trypho, c. 165); Irenaeus (Against Heresies; Demonstration), on its standing as prophecy; John A. Tvedtnes (Latter-day Saint, 1999); M. Catherine Thomas (Latter-day Saint, Brigham Young University, 2006).7

Justin charges that Jewish teachers “have altogether taken away many Scriptures from the translations effected by those seventy elders who were with Ptolemy, and by which this very man who was crucified is proved to have been set forth expressly as God, and man, and as being crucified, and as dying,” and offers the descent saying to Trypho as one of his examples. Irenaeus builds on the saying as prophecy: by it “Isaiah” shows “that it was not a mere man who died for us,” and “in Jeremiah He thus declares His death and descent into hell.” Tvedtnes holds that Jeremiah wrote more than the biblical book contains. That book “is a later collection of separate documents,” so “it is possible that some of Jeremiah’s writings never made it into the collection”; its Greek form is an eighth shorter than the Hebrew; and the Dead Sea Scrolls hold a Hebrew Jeremiah following the shorter Septuagint text along with “two texts about Jeremiah that are not in the Bible.” He takes the Isaiah ascription at III.20.4 as Irenaeus’s slip (“he mistakenly attributes it to Isaiah”), sets the saying beside other non-biblical Jeremiah traditions in the Lives of the Prophets and 4 Baruch, and finds such traditions confirmed by the Book of Mormon’s report that the brass plates held “many prophecies which have been spoken by the mouth of Jeremiah” (1 Nephi 5:13).8 Thomas counts the saying among “several apparently Jewish texts” describing the Lord’s work among the spirits of the dead, and cites J. H. Crehan’s judgement in A Catholic Dictionary of Theology (1967). Crehan, who finds it “strange that no trace of this text is found in the LXX of Jeremias, if what Justin alleges is true,” grants the tampering point: he writes that, “in the light of the Qumran scrolls,” “some tampering with controversial texts was practised by the Jews, for the Isaias scroll at 53:11 has a reading which favours the Christian argument (and which is found in the LXX), but this reading has disappeared from all later Hebrew mss.”9 On this reading a Christian-favourable reading can drop out of the later text, and the second-century writers who quote this one under a prophet’s name preserve a saying that the received book of Jeremiah does not.

A Christian text, absent from the Jewish Scriptures, received as prophecy

On this reading the saying arose on the Christian side, in Greek copies or proof-text collections, and Justin, finding it missing from the Jewish text, took the difference for removal.

Held by: H. B. Swete (Cambridge, 1900); Kaufmann Kohler and Samuel Krauss (Jewish Encyclopedia, 1901–1906), on Justin’s Bible text generally; Friedrich Loofs (Encyclopaedia of Religion and Ethics, 1911); M. R. James (The Lost Apocrypha of the Old Testament, 1920).10

Swete, surveying quotations in early Christian writers, finds that Justin’s “text of the LXX contained some remarkable interpolations”: the “from the wood” of Psalm 95 (96):10, the Esdras passover saying, and the words he ascribes to Jeremiah. “These passages appear to be of Christian origin, yet Justin is so sure of their genuineness that he accuses the Jews of having removed them from their copies.” Of “from the wood” he notes that no existing Greek manuscript of the Psalter carries it except one late copy, while a ligno stands in Old Latin texts.11 Kohler and Krauss read the Dialogue the same way: Justin “had matter in his Bible text which the Jews did not have in theirs; and he urged this as a reproach against them.” They see the pattern at Isaiah 7:14, where Justin opposed the rendering “young woman” to the Septuagint’s “virgin” but “did not know that the latter explanation originated with Aquila,” the Greek translator (fl. 130).12 J. Rendel Harris finds the same in the list that carries the descent saying: the Jeremiah 11:19 saying Justin calls cut out “is in all copies of Jeremiah in the Septuagint,” and the Esdras saying belonged to “Justin’s Testimony Book.”13 Loofs lists the descent saying among the Scripture proofs Irenaeus and Justin give for the descent as “an OT (apocryphal) passage.”14 James sets it beside other “obviously Christian” Jeremiah sayings in later Acts and, of the two explanations he weighs (an apocryphal book, or “Christian interpolations into the text of the LXX”), writes: “To the Justin passage this last explanation probably does apply.”15 On this reading the saying circulated in Christian Greek copies or collections, where Irenaeus met it under more than one prophet’s name, and its wording carries the second-century Christian confession of the descent to the dead.
