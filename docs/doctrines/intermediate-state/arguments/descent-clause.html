Is the creed clause “he descended into hell” an original article of the Apostles’ Creed, and should it be retained?

Disputed reading · on the intermediate state · witnesses: John Calvin, the Formula of Concord, the Catechism of the Catholic Church

Related readings: Was the descent-prophecy that Justin and Irenaeus quote as Scripture a passage removed from the Jewish text of the prophets?

The passage

But it should be known that the clause, "He descended into Hell," is not added in the Creed of the Roman Church, neither is it in that of the Oriental Churches. It seems to be implied, however, when it is said that "He was buried."

— Rufinus of Aquileia, A Commentary on the Apostles’ Creed §18 (c. 404), source.1

was crucified, and died and descended into the parts beneath the earth, and regulated the things there, Whom the gate-keepers of hell saw (Job 38:17) and shuddered

— the Fourth Creed of Sirmium, the “Dated Creed” (22 May 359), as quoted by Athanasius, De Synodis 8, source.2

The dated record of the clause is set out in the sources these readings share. The old Roman creed, in the Greek of Marcellus of Ancyra (340 or 341) and the Latin of Rufinus, runs from “buried” to the resurrection with no descent clause.3 A descent clause stands in three Eastern conciliar formulae of 359–360: the Dated Creed of Sirmium, a Homoian creed that Athanasius and Socrates quote in order to oppose it; the related formula of Nice (359); and the Constantinopolitan formula of 360. Among Latin baptismal creeds its earliest extant attestation is the creed of Aquileia that Rufinus expounds.4 The complete received Latin text, with descendit ad inferna, is first attested whole in the Scarapsus of Pirminius (eighth century).5

The question

The parties below agree on this record: the clause is absent from the old Roman creed and enters the Western creed later. What they read differently is what that history means for the clause’s standing: whether a later wording confesses what the Church already believed, whether its late entry is reason to drop it, and how communions that confess another creed regard it. The Orthodox reading below is drawn from a catechism, a lecture on the descent and a popular writer; an Orthodox scholar’s treatment of the Western clause itself was not located.

A later article confessing a belief older than its wording

On this reading the clause entered the creed after the apostles, but it confesses a belief the Church held from the beginning, and it keeps its place.

Held by: John Calvin (Reformed, Institutes II.16.8; first published 1536); the Formula of Concord (Lutheran, 1577); David P. Scaer (Lutheran, JETS, 1992); Matthew Y. Emerson (evangelical, 2019).6

Calvin grants the late entry and keeps the article: “although it is apparent from the writings of the ancient Fathers, that the clause which now stands in the Creed was not formerly so much used in the churches, still, in giving a summary of doctrine, a place must be assigned to it.” Since “there is none of the Fathers who does not mention Christ’s descent into hell,” “it is of little consequence by whom and at what time it was introduced.”7 The Formula of Concord, pointing to Luther’s Torgau sermon of 1533, holds burial and descent to be “distinguished as different articles” and confesses that “the entire person, God and man, after the burial descended into hell, conquered the devil, destroyed the power of hell.”8 Rufinus, who reads the clause as implied in “buried,” goes on in §28 to cite the Psalms and 1 Peter 3 for it, the latter being where “what He did in hell is declared.”9

Scaer opened his reply to Grudem:

I can respect Wayne Grudem's decision to omit "he descended into hell" from his recitation of the Apostles' Creed within the congregation … More importantly, there are good Biblical, historical and theological reasons for retaining the phrase.

Scaer grants the clause’s “weak attestation in the post-apostolic Church” and roots it in the apostolic writings: “1 Peter 3:18–22 has an outline strongly resembling our creed,” and if that passage was a Roman creedal formula, “we would have evidence for the early inclusion of the descent into hell in a creed of apostolic times.” He concludes: “By eliminating the descent from the creed we lose the one reference to the victory of Christ and the defeat of God's major opponent.”10 Emerson holds that where the clause is absent its content “was viewed as included in the phrase ‘he was buried,’” and that inferna then meant “place of the dead.”11 The clause stands in the Book of Common Prayer (1662) and in the Lutheran Church–Missouri Synod’s Lutheran Worship.12 Its holders differ on its sense: for Calvin, Christ’s bearing of God’s judgement; for the Formula, his victory over hell.

A late intruder to be dropped from the creed

On this reading the clause has no claim to be apostolic, entered the creed through a misunderstanding, and should be omitted.

Held by: American Methodists (dropped from worship by 1792); Wayne Grudem (evangelical, JETS, 1991); John Piper (Baptist, Desiring God, 2012).13

Grudem states his thesis:

In this article my purpose is to give reasons why it seems best to consider the troublesome phrase "he descended into hell" a late intruder into the Apostles' Creed that really never belonged there in the first place and that, on historical and Scriptural grounds, deserves to be removed.

Grudem rests the case on the pedigree: the phrase “was not found in any of the early versions of the Creed … until it appeared in one of two versions from Rufinus in A.D. 390. Then it is not included again in any version of the Creed until 650,” and Rufinus “understood the phrase simply to mean that Christ was ‘buried.’” Its later insertion after “and buried” led, on his account, to strained explanations (Calvin’s, the Heidelberg Catechism’s, the Westminster Larger Catechism’s). It “has no claim to being apostolic,” “no clear warrant from Scripture,” and “there would be all gain and no loss if it were dropped from the Creed once for all.”14 Piper reaches omission by exegesis. He reads 1 Peter 3:19 of Christ preaching through Noah to a generation now in prison, and 1 Peter 4:6 of those who heard the gospel and “have since died”:

With regard to 1 Peter 3:19, I take these words to mean that Christ, through the voice of Noah, went and preached to that generation, whose spirits are now "in prison," that is, in hell. In other words, Peter does not say that Christ preached to them while they were in prison. He says he preached to them once, during the days of Noah, and now they are in prison.

I would say, therefore, that there is no textual basis in the New Testament for claiming that between Good Friday and Easter Christ was preaching to souls imprisoned in hell or Hades … For these and other reasons, it seems best to me to omit from the Apostles Creed the clause, "he descended into hell," rather than giving it other meanings that are more defensible, the way Calvin does.

Christ’s promise to the thief, “today” in Paradise (Luke 23:43), gives Piper no impression of “a defective place from which the thief must then be delivered.”15 In Methodist practice John Wesley removed the Anglican Article III on the descent while keeping the clause in daily prayer; American Methodists dropped the phrase from worship by 1792.16

A Western baptismal creed; the East confesses the descent in its hymnody

On this reading the Apostles’ Creed is a Western creed outside Eastern use, so its clauses are not the measure; the Orthodox Church confesses the descent in its liturgy and catechesis.

Held by: the Longer Catechism of Philaret of Moscow (Russian Orthodox, 1830); Hilarion Alfeyev (Russian Orthodox, lecture, 2002); Robert Arakaki (Orthodox, Orthodox Bridge, 2018).17

The creed of the Orthodox Church is the Niceno-Constantinopolitan, which has no descent clause; the Lutheran historian Friedrich Loofs reports that the Eastern Churches do not recognize the Apostles’ and Athanasian creeds, and that the descent is “none the less” an element of Eastern tradition, citing Philaret’s catechism.4 Philaret’s catechism teaches the descent under the article on the resurrection, in the words of the Paschal hymn: “In the grave as to the flesh, in hades with thy soul, as God, in paradise with the thief, and on the throne wert thou, O Christ.” Christ descended “that he might there also preach his victory over death, and deliver the souls which with faith awaited his coming.”18 Alfeyev treats the descent as “an integral part of the dogmatic and liturgical tradition of the Christian Church”; for the Eastern Church it “remained first of all a mystery which could be praised in hymns.”19 Arakaki puts the same position in terms of the creeds: the Nicene Creed “has formal standing,” the Apostles’ Creed “is an informal creed that was never ratified by an Ecumenical Council,” and “the Orthodox Church has never opposed the Apostles Creed.” The Eastern churches “had no objections to Christ’s descent to Hell”; the line “was not included in the creedal confessions but was retained in the liturgical life of the early Church under other forms, e.g., the Holy Saturday services.” “For Orthodoxy, theology is not just creedal but also conciliar and liturgical.”20 On this reading the question of the clause’s date is a question about one Western formula; the confession of the descent itself is carried by the Holy Saturday and Paschal services.

An article of the Church’s received creed, a faithful summary of the apostles’ faith

On this reading the creed’s authority rests on the Church’s reception and exposition of it, so a clause that entered after the old Roman form belongs to the creed as received.

Held by: Herbert Thurston (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1907); the Catechism of the Catholic Church (1992).21

Thurston sets out the history: the old Roman form “does not contain” the clause; Rufinus “explicitly states that the words descended into hell were not in the Roman Creed, but existed in that of Aquileia”; “they are also in some Greek Creeds and in that of St. Jerome.” He accounts for their entry: “It was no doubt a remembrance of 1 Peter 3:19, as interpreted by Irenaeus and others, which caused their insertion.” The apostolic composition of the creed is, for him, an open question: the Catechism of the Council of Trent “apparently assumes the Apostolic origin of our existing Creed, but such a pronouncement has no dogmatic force and leaves opinion free.” What gives the creed its standing is its use: “the Apostles’ Creed has always been held to have the authority of an ex cathedra utterance,” and “Catholics have generally been content to accept the Creed in the form, and in the sense, in which it has been authoritatively expounded by the living voice of the Church.”22 The Catechism of the Catholic Church calls the Apostles’ Creed “a faithful summary of the apostles’ faith” and “the ancient baptismal symbol of the Church of Rome,” and expounds the article in its received form: Christ, like all the dead, “in his soul joined the others in the realm of the dead,” but “descended there as Saviour, proclaiming the Good News to the spirits imprisoned there,” “to free the just who had gone before him.”23
