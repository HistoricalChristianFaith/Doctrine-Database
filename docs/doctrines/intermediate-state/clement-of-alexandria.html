Clement of Alexandria on the Intermediate State

Dates: c. 200 · Wikipedia
Summary: Clement (c. 150–215), head of the Alexandrian catechetical school, pictures a conscious, divided Hades in which "the souls of the righteous and those of sinners" are emphatically not "in the same condemnation." He describes Christ's descent as an act of preaching: the Lord (and, after him, the apostles) "preached the Gospel to those in Hades," so that the righteous who died before the Incarnation — Jews under the Law and Gentiles who "lived rightly" under philosophy — might hear, repent, and be saved there.

Claims & evidence

The righteous and the wicked are not in one condition in Hades

It is not plainly the place, which, the words above say, heard the voice, but those who have been put in Hades, and have abandoned themselves to destruction, as persons who have thrown themselves voluntarily from a ship into the sea. They, then, are those that hear the divine power and voice. For who in his senses can suppose the souls of the righteous and those of sinners in the same condemnation, charging Providence with injustice?

— The Stromata, Book 6, historicalchristian.faith. Clement assumes the structured Hades of the earlier tradition: the dead are conscious and sorted, the righteous in one condition and sinners in another, so that it would charge God with injustice to lump them together. Hippolytus and Tertullian, on this timeline, also describe a divided underworld.

Christ preached the Gospel to those in Hades — and the apostles after him

Wherefore the Lord preached the Gospel to those in Hades. … Do not [the Scriptures] show that the Lord preached the Gospel to those that perished in the flood, or rather had been chained, and to those kept "in ward and guard"? [1 Peter 3:19-20] And it has been shown also, in the second book of the Stromata, that the apostles, following the Lord, preached the Gospel to those in Hades. … If, then, the Lord descended to Hades for no other end but to preach the Gospel, as He did descend; it was either to preach the Gospel to all or to the Hebrews only.

— The Stromata, Book 6, historicalchristian.faith. Reading 1 Peter 3:19 together with the descent, Clement describes the descensus as a mission: the preaching reaches "those who had lived in righteousness according to the Law and Philosophy, who had ended life not perfectly, but sinfully," and God saves "with a righteousness and equality which extend to all that turn to Him, whether here or elsewhere."

"For this cause was the Gospel preached also to the dead": the dead as former unbelievers

Therefore he adds, "For this cause was the Gospel preached also to the dead"—to us, namely, who were at one time unbelievers. "That they might be judged according to men," he says, "in the flesh, but live according to God in the spirit." Because, that is, they have fallen away from faith; whilst they are still in the flesh they are judged according to preceding judgments, that they might repent.

— Fragments: From the Latin Translation of Cassiodorus (on 1 Peter 4:6), historicalchristian.faith. In this fragment Clement reads "the dead" of 1 Peter 4:6 as "us, namely, who were at one time unbelievers," who "whilst they are still in the flesh … are judged according to preceding judgments, that they might repent." Here the judgments are those of this life, aimed "that they might repent" and "live according to God in the spirit"; the fragment does not apply the verse to the dead in Hades.

Reception and context

  • In Stromata 6 Clement reads 1 Peter 3:19–20 of the descent: Christ preaches to the dead in Hades, and the apostles after him. His account extends the preaching to some who "ended life not perfectly, but sinfully," and differs from accounts on this timeline that describe the deliverance as of the righteous dead (e.g. Cyril of Jerusalem; the Catechism of the Catholic Church, §633, "to free the just who had gone before him").
  • His divided, conscious Hades ("not … the same condemnation") places him with the accounts of a two-part Hades given by Hippolytus and Tertullian.
