Justin Martyr on the Intermediate State

Dates: c. 165 · Wikipedia
Summary: Justin Martyr (c. 100–165), philosopher and apologist, holds that the soul is not by nature immortal but survives death by God's will: at death the souls of the pious go to a better place and those of the unjust to a worse, both awaiting the resurrection and judgment. He writes that those who deny the resurrection and say that souls are taken to heaven at death are not to be imagined Christians.

Claims & evidence

The souls of the pious and the unjust in distinct places, awaiting the judgment

But I do not say, indeed, that all souls die; for that were truly a piece of good fortune to the evil. What then? The souls of the pious remain in a better place, while those of the unjust and wicked are in a worse, waiting for the time of judgment. Thus some which have appeared worthy of God never die; but others are punished so long as God wills them to exist and to be punished.

— Dialogue with Trypho, ch. 5, historicalchristian.faith. In the chapter headed "The Soul is Not in Its Own Nature Immortal," Justin (relaying the old man who converted him) denies the Platonic doctrine that the soul is unbegotten and inherently immortal: the soul lives only as a "partaker of life" because God wills it. On this footing the dead do not lapse into nothing, nor do they pass at once to their final reward; the pious are kept "in a better place" and the wicked "in a worse," both waiting for the time of judgment — a conscious, divided intermediate state short of the resurrection.

Those "who say there is no resurrection of the dead, and that their souls, when they die, are taken to heaven"

For if you have fallen in with some who are called Christians, but who do not admit this [truth], and venture to blaspheme the God of Abraham, and the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob; who say there is no resurrection of the dead, and that their souls, when they die, are taken to heaven; do not imagine that they are Christians, even as one, if he would rightly consider it, would not admit that the Sadducees, or similar sects of Genistae, Meristae, Galilaeans, Hellenists, Pharisees, Baptists, are Jews … But I and others, who are right-minded Christians on all points, are assured that there will be a resurrection of the dead, and a thousand years in Jerusalem, which will then be built, adorned, and enlarged, [as] the prophets Ezekiel and Isaiah and others declare.

— Dialogue with Trypho, ch. 80, historicalchristian.faith. Justin couples two positions: denying the resurrection and holding that souls are "taken to heaven" immediately at death, and he says their holders are not to be imagined Christians. Against this he ranks himself with the "right-minded Christians" who expect a bodily resurrection (and, here, a millennial reign in Jerusalem). In this polemic the intermediate state is an interim: the saved are not yet in heaven but wait, in a better place, for the resurrection to come.

Context

  • In Justin's account the dead neither lapse into nothing nor pass at once to heaven: they are conscious and sorted (pious vs. unjust), yet still awaiting the resurrection and judgment — an explicit two-place, interim geography rather than a final one.
  • The denial of the soul's natural immortality (ch. 5–6: the soul lives only as God wills, as a "partaker of life") is the metaphysical ground of his account: survival after death is a gift of God's will, not an inherent property of the soul.
  • Justin's expectation of "a thousand years in Jerusalem" (ch. 80) ties his intermediate-state doctrine to his chiliasm: the righteous dead wait for the resurrection that inaugurates the kingdom on earth.
  • In the same Dialogue (ch. 72) Justin lists, among Scriptures he says the Jews "cut out," a one-line descent oracle he ascribes to Jeremiah — "The Lord God remembered His dead people of Israel … and He descended to preach to them His own salvation." The saying stands in no surviving text of Jeremiah or Isaiah, Hebrew or Greek. Its origin is the subject of a disputed-reading page: John A. Tvedtnes and M. Catherine Thomas (Latter-day Saint writers) take it for a genuine prophecy the received book does not carry; H. B. Swete, Kaufmann Kohler and Samuel Krauss, Friedrich Loofs and M. R. James take it for a Christian text that Justin received as Scripture. On that page Swete counts Justin's "from the wood" (Ps 96:10) among Christian interpolations in his Septuagint text, J. Rendel Harris notes that the Jeremiah 11:19 saying Justin also calls cut out "is in all copies of Jeremiah in the Septuagint," and Kohler and Krauss relate Justin's quarrel over Isaiah 7:14 to Aquila's Greek translation.
