Mark of Ephesus on the Intermediate State

Dates: c. 1438 · Wikipedia
Summary: Mark, Metropolitan of Ephesus, wrote his first homily (reply) on purgatory at the Council of Ferrara–Florence in answer to the Latin report on purgatory. He affirms that the departed are helped by the prayers, Liturgies and almsgiving of the living, and describes the state of souls between death and the final Judgement: some are cleansed at the departure from the body, others after it, and those with graver sins are “kept in Hades” as in prison; he declines the Latin teaching of a cleansing purgatorial fire.

Claims & evidence

The departed are helped by the prayers of the living

Of the fact that those reposed in faith are without doubt helped by the Liturgies and prayers and almsgiving performed for them, and that this custom has been in force from antiquity, there is the testimony of many and various utterances of the teachers, both Latin and Greek, spoken and written at various times and in various places. But that souls are delivered thanks to a certain purgatorial suffering and temporal fire which possess such [purgatorial] power and has the character of a help — this we do not find either in the Scriptures or in the prayers and hymns for the dead, or in the words of the teachers.

— Mark of Ephesus, first homily on purgatory, §1, English translation (translator not named) at silouanthompson.net. Mark answers the Latin report’s statement that the souls of the penitent “are cleansed after death by means of purgatorial sufferings.”

The state of souls departed with unrepented or unexpiated faults

But some must be cleansed in the very departure from the body, thanks only to fear, as St. Gregory the Dialogist literally shows; while others must be cleansed after the departure from the body, either while remaining in the same earthly place, before they come to worship God and are honored with the lot of the blessed, or — if their sins were more serious and bind them for a longer duration — they are kept in Hades, but not in order to remain forever in fire and torment, but as it were in prison and confinement under guard. All such ones, we affirm, are helped by the prayers and Liturgies performed for them, with the cooperation of the divine goodness and love for mankind.

— Mark of Ephesus, first homily on purgatory, §1, silouanthompson.net. The cleansing he describes is “not by means of some purgatorial fire or a definite punishment in some place (for this, as we have aid, has not at all been handed down to us).” Some sins, he writes, the divine goodness “immediately disdains and remits,” while others it “either likewise releases and forgives — and that completely — or lightens the responsibility for them until that final Judgment.”

Placement on the timeline

  • The homily belongs to the debate on purgatory between the Greek and Latin delegations at the Council of Ferrara–Florence (1438–1439); the same text is cited on the purgatory and prayer-for-the-dead timeline. The timeline year, c. 1438, is the year of the council’s sessions at Ferrara.
  • Kallistos Ware (Orthodox bishop, The Orthodox Church, 1963) reports that the formula of union, under which the Orthodox “accepted the Roman teaching on Purgatory,” was “signed by all the Orthodox present at the Council except one,” Mark of Ephesus, and that purgatory, “as a point of dispute between east and west,” “only came into the open in the thirteenth century” (excerpt at fatheralexander.org).
