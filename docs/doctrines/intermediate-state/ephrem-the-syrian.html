Ephrem the Syrian on the Intermediate State

Dates: c. 373 · Wikipedia
Summary: Ephrem (c. 306–373), the great Syriac deacon and hymnographer, teaches in his Hymns on Paradise that the departed do not enter Paradise at death: since the soul "cannot have perception of Paradise without its mate, the body," the souls of the just wait "in the delightful mansions on the borders of Paradise … awaiting there the bodies they love," entering the Garden only at the resurrection, when body and soul are reunited and go in together. It is a conscious-but-diminished intermediate waiting — kin to the "sleep of the soul" of his compatriot Aphrahat, though Ephrem's own picture is of souls that reside and await, not of oblivion. His Nisibene Hymns place him equally in the descent tradition: Christ's death shatters the gates of Sheol.

Claims & evidence

The soul cannot perceive Paradise without the body

I imagined that he was already there, / but then I considered / how the soul cannot / have perception of Paradise / without its mate, the body, / its instrument and lyre.

— Hymns on Paradise VIII.2 (trans. Sebastian Brock). Musing on the penitent Thief's "to day shalt thou be with me in paradise" (Luke 23:43), Ephrem is checked by a difficulty: the soul is like a harpist without a harp. Body and soul are "companions"; each attests the other — "the body has need of the soul / in order to live, / and the soul too requires the body / in order to see and to hear" (VIII.4), so that once it has left the body the soul is "deficient in sensation and consciousness" (VIII.7). He does not deny the soul's survival; he denies it a full experience of Paradise on its own.1

Paradise is complete, but the soul cannot enter it alone — the body enters at the resurrection

That blessed abode / is in no way deficient, / for that place is complete and perfected / in every way, / and the soul cannot / enter there alone, / for in such a state it is in everything / deficient in sensation and consciousness; / but on the day of Resurrection / the body, with all its senses, / will enter in as well, once it has been made perfect.

— Hymns on Paradise VIII.7 (trans. Brock). The lack is on the soul's side, not Paradise's: the Garden is already perfect, but a disembodied soul cannot take it in. Ephrem grounds this in the primal history — when God set Adam in Paradise, "the soul could not enter there / of itself and for itself, / but together they entered, / body and soul … and together they left it, once they had become / sullied. / From all this we should learn / that at the Resurrection they will enter again / together" (VIII.9).2

The souls of the just reside on the borders of Paradise, awaiting their bodies

Thus in the delightful mansions / on the borders of Paradise / do the souls of the just / and righteous reside, / awaiting there / the bodies they love, / so that, at the opening / of the Garden's gate, / both bodies and souls might proclaim, / amidst Hosannas, / "Blessed is He who has brought Adam from Sheol / and returned him to Paradise in the company of many."

— Hymns on Paradise VIII.11 (trans. Brock). This is the load-bearing statement of Ephrem's intermediate state: a waystation "on the borders of Paradise" — an ante-Paradise distinct from Sheol proper — where the righteous dead consciously "reside" and "await" the bodies "they love," so that soul and body may enter the Garden together at the resurrection. Brock summarizes: "the soul cannot enter Paradise without the body, and so the righteous cannot in fact enter Paradise until the final Resurrection … in the meantime the disembodied souls await the Resurrection just outside the boundary of Paradise in a state that other Syriac writers describe as 'the sleep of the soul.'"3

The descent: Christ's death shatters the gates of Sheol

Death opened the gates of Sheol, and there shone from it the splendour of the face of our Lord; and like the men of Sodom they were smitten; they groped and sought the gate of Sheol, which they had lost.

— Nisibene Hymns XLI.16. In the great cycle of hymns "On our Lord, Death, and Satan," Ephrem stages Death and Sheol as boastful characters undone by Christ's descent: the Lord's face floods the underworld with light, and its denizens, dazzled like the Sodomites at Lot's door, cannot even find the gate. Ephrem thus joins the descent tradition that runs through this timeline — Christ enters the realm of the dead as victor and leads its captives out.4

Notes

  • Kin to Aphrahat, but not identical. Ephrem is the second Syriac witness on this timeline, a generation after Aphrahat (whose page already notes that in Ephrem "a similar 'sleep of the dead' recurs"). Both defer the reward of Paradise to the resurrection and refuse the disembodied soul its full inheritance. But where Aphrahat makes the buried man sleep "without knowing good from evil," Ephrem's souls of the just reside in "delightful mansions" and consciously await "the bodies they love" — a diminished, longing waiting rather than oblivion. Brock notes only that "other Syriac writers" call the state "the sleep of the soul"; Ephrem's own image is of residing, waiting souls.
  • An ante-Paradise, not Sheol. The "borders of Paradise" where the righteous wait is distinct both from the Garden's interior (which they cannot yet enter) and from Sheol (from which Christ's descent frees Adam, VIII.10–11; Nisibene Hymns XLI). The geography differs from the compartmented Hades of the Greek and Latin Fathers (Tertullian, Hippolytus): Ephrem locates the just not in a subterranean Abraham's bosom but on the outer slopes of the mountain of Paradise itself.
  • Body and soul are inseparable partners in salvation. The whole argument turns on Ephrem's refusal to let the soul be saved apart from the body — the resurrection of the flesh is the believer's hope, not a disembodied heaven. In this he stands with Justin, Irenaeus, and Aphrahat against a spiritualizing eschatology, though he affirms the soul's survival more warmly than Aphrahat's soul-sleep does.
  • Sources. The Hymns on Paradise are quoted from Sebastian Brock's translation (Popular Patristics 10, St Vladimir's Seminary Press). Ephrem appears on this wiki also as a witness on prayer to the saints, and on the Mary as the New Eve, perpetual virginity, and immaculate conception timelines.
