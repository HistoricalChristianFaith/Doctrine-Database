Tertullian on dream-apparitions of the dead

Dates: c. 210 (A Treatise on the Soul / De Anima, c. 210–213; Tertullian c. 155–220) · Wikipedia
Summary: The earliest extant sustained Christian treatment of the phenomenon, denying that the dead return. Because every soul is detained in Hades until the resurrection, no departed soul can be "brought up" by necromancy or seen as itself in a dream; what appears is a demonic counterfeit, and the test of a dream is not its vividness but whether the thing dreamed is "fully proved" true. He holds that the image of a dead man in a dream is no more the man than the image of a living one.

Claims & evidence

Hades and the summoning of the dead

Tertullian grounds his treatment in his eschatology: Hades is a real holding-place that detains every soul until the end (his fuller doctrine of the intermediate state is traced on that timeline). On his account, if no soul may leave, the souls that magicians and mediums claim to "call up" are not the dead. He grounds this on the rich man and Lazarus — the gulf none may cross — and makes the point directly against the necromancers:

Moreover, the fact that Hades is not in any case opened for (the escape of) any soul, has been firmly established by the Lord in the person of Abraham, in His representation of the poor man at rest and the rich man in torment. No one, (he said,) could possibly be despatched from those abodes to report to us how matters went in the nether regions…

— A Treatise on the Soul 57, source. He grants one exception — "The power of God has, no doubt, sometimes recalled men's souls to their bodies, as a proof of His own transcendent rights" — and distinguishes it from necromancy: a resurrection restores a soul to a solid, palpable body, and by that contrast, he argues, exposes "the fraudulence of every incorporeal apparition of dead persons."

The figure raised by the medium, and the case of Endor

Since on his account the dead cannot be summoned, Tertullian identifies the figure that answers the summons as a demon — the same unclean spirit "lying concealed in the persons of the dead" that exorcism unmasks — and he applies this to the witch of Endor calling up Samuel (1 Sam 28), which he reads as a demonic representation, not the prophet's returning soul:

…it was no less than this that was anciently permitted to the Pythonic (or ventriloquistic) spirit — even to represent the soul of Samuel, when Saul consulted the dead, after (losing the living) God. God forbid, however, that we should suppose that the soul of any saint, much less of a prophet, can be dragged out of (its resting-place in Hades) by a demon.

— A Treatise on the Soul 57, source. Satan "is transformed into an angel of light," so it was "easy" for one and the same spirit — in the sorceress and in the apostate king — to counterfeit Samuel and make Saul believe he saw him. Origen read the episode the other way, holding that the figure was Samuel himself.1

Dreams of the dead and dreams of the living

Tertullian then declines to grant that a dream of a dead person is any more "the person" than a dream of a living one, and moves the test of truth from the vividness of the vision to its verification:

Well, we admit apparitions of dead persons in dreams to be not more really true than those of living persons; but we apply the same estimate to all alike — to the dead and to the living, and indeed to all the phenomena which are seen. Now things are not true because they appear to be so, but because they are fully proved to be so. The truth of dreams is declared from the realization, not the aspect.

— A Treatise on the Soul 57, source. The tomb-oracles of the Nasamones and the Celts, who sleep at their ancestors' graves to receive dreams, Tertullian treats as the same imposture: on his account the dead do not come, whatever the sleeper sees.2

Notes

  • Tertullian's earlier chapters on dreams (De Anima 45–49) sort dreams into those from God, those from demons, and those arising naturally from the soul's own restlessness; within that framework, a dream can be sent, but not by the dead. Those chapters are treated on the sibling timeline the discernment of dreams, where the same book's fourfold division and the same epistemic rule — "things are not true because they appear to be so, but because they are fully proved to be so" — are treated in full.
  • Augustine's On Care to Be Had for the Dead (c. 421) makes parallel points: both writers deny that the dead are the agents of their own apparitions, and both compare the dream of the dead to the dream of the living. Tertullian grants that God has "sometimes recalled men's souls to their bodies"; Augustine, that God may send a soul from the dead. Tertullian assigns the apparition to a demonic counterfeit, Augustine to an "angelical operation" permitted by God.
  • He appears also as a witness on the timelines of the intermediate state (the conscious, structured Hades that his argument here presupposes) and to prayer and oblation for the dead. The De Anima belongs to his Montanist years; the question whether that turn to the “New Prophecy” made him a heretic is traced on the timeline “Was Tertullian a heretic?”.
