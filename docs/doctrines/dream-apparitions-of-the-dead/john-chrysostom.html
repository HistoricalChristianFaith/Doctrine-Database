John Chrysostom on dream-apparitions of the dead

Dates: c. 388 (the Four Discourses… on the parable of the Rich Man and Lazarus / De Lazaro, preached at Antioch; Chrysostom c. 349–407) · Wikipedia
Summary: The timeline's sharpest rejecter voice. Preaching on the rich man and Lazarus, Chrysostom denies outright that the souls of the departed remain among us: at death every soul is "forthwith led away" and does not linger, and God has deliberately "closed the portals" so that none of the dead return. It follows that a figure of the dead seen in a dream or answering a summons is not the departed but the tempter's counterfeit — "the forms of the departed… have deceived and ruined many" — and even a spirit that says "I am the soul of such-and-such" is to be disbelieved, since "evil spirits say so to deceive." Where Tertullian and Augustine relocate the agency (to a demon, or to a God-permitted angel), Chrysostom fastens on the prior point the folk phenomenon takes for granted: the dead simply do not come.

Claims & evidence

The souls of the dead do not linger here — they are "forthwith led away"

Chrysostom's whole rejection rests on where the soul goes at death. Expounding the angels who carry Lazarus, he insists the parable settles that no soul — righteous or wicked — stays behind among the living:

And not only from that which has just been said, but from the parable, it is quite certain that souls when they leave the body do not still linger here, but are forthwith led away. And hear how it is shown: "It came to pass," it is said, "that he died, and was carried away by the angels." Not the souls of the just only, but also those of sinners are led away.

— Second Discourse on Lazarus, tertullian.org. If neither the just nor sinners remain, then the whole premise of the folk stories — a dead soul near enough to appear and disclose — is denied at the root. The dead are gone the moment they die.

The souls of the violently dead do not become "wandering spirits"; a demon says "I am the soul of…" only to deceive

The Second Discourse is titled, in part, to correct exactly the belief that produces apparitions — that a soul torn from the body violently roams the world. Chrysostom flatly denies it, twice for emphasis, and traces the error to the devil's design; then he draws the practical corollary against any spirit that claims to be a particular dead person:

…many of the less instructed think that the souls of those who die a violent death become wandering spirits, (demons.) But this is not so. I repeat it is not so. For not the souls of those who die a violent death become demons, but rather the souls of those who live in sin; not that their nature is changed, but that in their desires they imitate the evil nature of demons.

What then if the demons say, "I am the spirit of such and such a monk"? Neither because of this do I credit the notion, since evil spirits say so to deceive those who listen to them.

— Second Discourse on Lazarus, tertullian.org. He grounds the caution on Paul silencing the true-speaking spirit at Philippi (Acts 16:17–18): a demon is not to be believed "even when speaking the truth," lest it "at another time mingle falsehood with the truth." The identity a spirit claims — even the identity of a holy departed soul — is worthless as evidence; the speaker is not the dead person it names.

God has "closed the portals": the dead do not return, and dream-forms of the departed are the tempter's snare

In the Fourth Discourse Chrysostom asks why, if the dead could profitably tell us of the next world, God does not let them come. His answer is that God has shut that door on purpose, precisely because an open traffic with the dead would be the tempter's opportunity — and he notes that even now, without any real return, apparitions of the departed in dreams already do harm:

For even now, when nothing of that kind takes place, the forms of the departed often have appeared in dreams, and have deceived and ruined many. Much more if such a state of things, namely, that many returned from the dead, existed, that subtle spirit would involve many in his wiles, and introduce great deception into our life. Therefore God has closed the portals, and does not permit any of those who have departed to return to tell us the things that take place there; so that the tempter cannot take advantage of such a state of things…

— Fourth Discourse on Lazarus, tertullian.org. The dream-figure of a dead man is here neither the man's soul nor an innocent "angelical operation" but a weapon: the "subtle spirit" feigning "appearances" and even readying "his ministers to feign death and burial… as having risen from the dead." The pastoral upshot is a blanket caution — do not trust a dream because it wears a dead loved one's face.1

Notes

  • Chrysostom sits between Tertullian and Augustine on this timeline and agrees with both that the apparition is not the departed — but his stress differs. Where Augustine's answer is the neutral, God-permitted likeness worked by an angel (leaving a careful opening for souls specially sent), Chrysostom's is uniformly negative and demonological: the door is shut, the dead do not come, and what wears their form is the deceiver. He is the timeline's plainest "no," and the natural counterweight to Gregory the Great's revenant catalogue, in which the dead appear as themselves to beg the living's prayers.
  • The argument turns on his doctrine of the intermediate state — that the soul is carried at once to its fixed abode — the same eschatology of a sealed Hades that underwrites Tertullian's verdict.
  • He appears on the sibling timeline the discernment of dreams on the God-sent side of the ledger (a true dream fitted to the recipient's faith); the two pages are complementary — a dream can be a genuine divine message, but a dream that presents a dead person is not to be trusted as that person.
