John Chrysostom on dream-apparitions of the dead

Dates: c. 388 (the Four Discourses… on the parable of the Rich Man and Lazarus / De Lazaro, preached at Antioch; Chrysostom c. 349–407) · Wikipedia
Summary: Preaching on the rich man and Lazarus, Chrysostom denies that the souls of the departed remain among us: at death every soul is "forthwith led away" and does not linger, and God has deliberately "closed the portals" so that none of the dead return. He concludes that a figure of the dead seen in a dream or answering a summons is not the departed but the tempter's counterfeit — "the forms of the departed… have deceived and ruined many" — and a spirit that says "I am the spirit of such and such a monk" is likewise to be disbelieved, since "evil spirits say so to deceive." Where Tertullian and Augustine assign the apparition to another agent (a demon, or a God-permitted angel), Chrysostom's emphasis falls on the soul's departure at death: on his account the dead do not come.

Claims & evidence

Where the soul goes at death: "forthwith led away"

Chrysostom grounds his position in where the soul goes at death. Expounding the angels who carry Lazarus, he holds that the parable shows that no soul — righteous or wicked — stays behind among the living:

And not only from that which has just been said, but from the parable, it is quite certain that souls when they leave the body do not still linger here, but are forthwith led away. And hear how it is shown: "It came to pass," it is said, "that he died, and was carried away by the angels." Not the souls of the just only, but also those of sinners are led away.

— Second Discourse on Lazarus, tertullian.org. On this account neither the just nor sinners remain near enough to appear and disclose; the dead depart at the moment of death.

The souls of the violently dead, and spirits that claim a dead person's identity

The Second Discourse's title announces that "the souls of those that die a violent death do not become wandering spirits." Chrysostom denies that such souls roam the world, repeating the denial, and traces the belief to the devil's design; then he draws the practical corollary against any spirit that claims to be a particular dead person:

…many of the less instructed think that the souls of those who die a violent death become wandering spirits, (demons.) But this is not so. I repeat it is not so. For not the souls of those who die a violent death become demons, but rather the souls of those who live in sin; not that their nature is changed, but that in their desires they imitate the evil nature of demons.

What then if the demons say, "I am the spirit of such and such a monk"? Neither because of this do I credit the notion, since evil spirits say so to deceive those who listen to them.

— Second Discourse on Lazarus, tertullian.org. He grounds the caution on Paul silencing the true-speaking spirit at Philippi (Acts 16:17–18): a demon is not to be believed "even when speaking the truth," lest it "at another time mingle falsehood with the truth." On his account the identity a spirit claims, including that of a holy departed soul, is no evidence that the speaker is the dead person it names.

The "closed portals" and dream-forms of the departed

In the Fourth Discourse Chrysostom asks why, if the dead could profitably tell us of the next world, God does not let them come. His answer is that God has shut that door on purpose, precisely because an open traffic with the dead would be the tempter's opportunity — and he notes that even now, without any real return, apparitions of the departed in dreams already do harm:

For even now, when nothing of that kind takes place, the forms of the departed often have appeared in dreams, and have deceived and ruined many. Much more if such a state of things, namely, that many returned from the dead, existed, that subtle spirit would involve many in his wiles, and introduce great deception into our life. Therefore God has closed the portals, and does not permit any of those who have departed to return to tell us the things that take place there; so that the tempter cannot take advantage of such a state of things…

— Fourth Discourse on Lazarus, tertullian.org. Chrysostom adds that, were the dead allowed to return, the tempter "would be able often to feign appearances, or by preparing his ministers to feign death and burial…" His pastoral counsel is not to trust a dream because it wears a dead loved one's face.1

Notes

  • Chrysostom sits between Tertullian and Augustine on this timeline and shares with both the view that the apparition is not the departed, with a different stress. Augustine assigns a true apparition to a God-permitted likeness worked by an angel (allowing that a soul may be specially sent); Chrysostom holds that the door is shut, the dead do not come, and what wears their form is the deceiver. Gregory the Great's Dialogues (c. 593) relate apparitions in which the dead appear as themselves to ask the living's prayers.
  • His argument draws on his doctrine of the intermediate state — that the soul is carried at once to its fixed abode — the same eschatology of a sealed Hades that Tertullian also invokes.
  • He appears on the sibling timeline the discernment of dreams as a witness to a God-sent dream (a true dream fitted to the recipient's faith). On his account a dream can be a genuine divine message, but a dream that presents a dead person is not to be trusted as that person.
