Gregory the Great on dream-apparitions of the dead

Dates: c. 593 (Dialogues Book IV; Gregory c. 540–604, pope 590–604) · Wikipedia
Summary: Gregory relates apparitions in which the departed appear as themselves. Book IV of the Dialogues is a catalogue of deathbed visions and revenant stories in which the departed appear as themselves — conscious of their own post-mortem state, naming the sins for which they suffer, and begging the living for the prayers and the Eucharistic sacrifice that will release them. Gregory counsels caution about dreams in general (IV.48), and of the cases he relates he writes that "the spirits of them that be dead desire it of the living."

Claims & evidence

The deacon Paschasius in the baths

One of Gregory's cases is Paschasius, a Roman deacon whom Gregory calls "a man of a wonderful holy life," who had taken part with Lawrence against Symmachus. After his death Bishop Germanus of Capua, taking the waters for his health, finds Paschasius himself standing in the hot baths, serving — and the dead man explains his own state and asks to be prayed for:

…he found there standing in those hot waters the foresaid Paschasius, ready to do him service. At which sight being much afraid, he demanded what so worthy a man as he was did in that place: to whom Paschasius returned this answer: "For no other cause," quoth he, "am I appointed to this place of punishment, but for that I took part with Lawrence against Symmachus: and therefore I beseech you to pray unto our Lord for me, and by this token shall you know that your prayers be heard, if, at your coming again, you find me not here."

— Dialogues IV.40, source. Germanus prays; returning, he finds Paschasius gone — "purged from that sin." In Gregory's telling the apparition is the deacon, aware of his state and asking for prayer.

The priest of Centumcellis, and Gregory's general statement

A parallel story follows. A priest of Centumcellis is repeatedly served at the baths by a helpful stranger; offering him blessed bread in thanks, he learns the man is dead and suffering, and is asked to offer the sacrifice for him. When the man later vanishes, Gregory draws a general moral:

…whereby it appeareth what great profit the souls receive by the sacrifice of the holy oblation, seeing the spirits of them that be dead desire it of the living, and give certain tokens to let us understand how that by means thereof they have received absolution.

— Dialogues IV.55, source. Gregory states as a rule that the dead themselves desire and solicit the living's prayers, and that their appearing is the "token" of it.1

Dreams and waking visions

Asked directly "whether dreams are to be believed," he sorts them into kinds — from the stomach's fullness or emptiness, from diabolical illusion, from thought mixed with illusion, from revelation, and from thought mixed with revelation — and warns that because they "grow from such divers roots, with so much the more difficulty ought we to believe them." Only the discerning can tell them apart: "Holy men, indeed, by a certain inward spiritual taste, do discern betwixt illusions and true revelations," for the deceiving spirit "sometime useth to foretell many true things, that, in the end, he may by some falsehood ensnare our soul."2 The waking apparitions and deathbed visions of Book IV he relates without this caution about dreams; there the dying see dead saints and kinsmen come to meet them, and the dead return to name their state. These stories sit within his teaching of an immediate particular judgment and a purifying fire before the last day — the setting in which souls have a state to report and a need the living can meet, traced on the purgatory timeline.

Notes

  • Gregory and Augustine answer the question differently: the apparitions are the dead themselves (Gregory), or likenesses worked by angels while the dead are unaware (Augustine). Where Augustine had written that the departed are "utterly unconscious" of their apparitions and that the agency is angelic, Gregory's IV.55 states that the dead themselves desire the living's prayers. Aquinas (c. 1274) cites both: he grants that by providence souls sometimes appear (citing Augustine's martyr Felix), and cites "the fourth book of the Dialogues" for souls in purgatory who appear "to seek our suffrages."
  • The six-kinds-of-dream classification (Dialogues IV.48) is treated on the sibling timeline the discernment of dreams, where the same passage is quoted for its warning that the wicked spirit "foretell[s] many true things, that, in the end, he may by some falsehood ensnare our soul." On Gregory's account dreams are a real channel that must be sifted, while he relates the waking deathbed apparitions of Book IV as the dead themselves.
  • The same Book IV is also a source for Gregory on purgatory and prayer for the dead (the cleansing fire and the profit of the oblation) and on the intermediate state (each soul judged at death, entering rest or punishment at once). The revenant stories here are told within that same eschatology.
