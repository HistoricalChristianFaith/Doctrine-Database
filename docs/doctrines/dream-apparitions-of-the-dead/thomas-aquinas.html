Thomas Aquinas on dream-apparitions of the dead

Dates: c. 1274 (Summa Theologiae, Supplement, compiled posthumously from earlier work; Aquinas 1225–1274) · Wikipedia
Summary: Aquinas answers the question by citing both Augustine and Gregory. He holds that "according to the natural course" the separated souls are "utterly cut off from communication with the living"; he grants that "according to the disposition of Divine providence" they sometimes appear (citing Augustine's account of the martyr Felix), and cites Gregory's Dialogues IV for souls in purgatory who appear "to seek our suffrages." And he distinguishes the saints from the damned: the saints "can appear when they will," the damned only when permitted.

Claims & evidence

The natural course and divine providence

The article asks "whether the souls who are in heaven or hell are able to go from thence." Aquinas answers by granting the ordinary rule and the extraordinary exception together:

…according to the natural course, the separated souls consigned to their respective abodes are utterly cut off from communication with the living. Nevertheless, according to the disposition of Divine providence separated souls sometimes come forth from their abode and appear to men, as Augustine, in the book quoted above, relates of the martyr Felix who appeared visibly to the people of Nola when they were besieged by the barbarians.

— Summa Theologiae, Supplement q. 69, a. 3, source. The structure follows Augustine's On Care to Be Had for the Dead, which Aquinas cites — the departed do not "by their proper nature" attend the living, but God may send them — stated as a rule and an exception. Aquinas adds that souls sometimes appear "to seek our suffrages, as to those who are detained in purgatory, as evidenced by many instances related in the fourth book of the Dialogues," citing Gregory's revenant stories as instances.

The saints and the damned

Aquinas then distinguishes the glorified dead from the rest (see also prayer to the saints):

There is, however, this difference between the saints and the damned, that the saints can appear when they will to the living, but not the damned…

— Summa Theologiae, Supplement q. 69, a. 3, source. The article continues that the saints, by a power belonging to their glory, may appear to the living when they will, while the damned may appear only when permitted; the souls in purgatory appear, as quoted above, "to seek our suffrages."1

Notes

  • Aquinas's reply draws on Augustine's account of the dead (in Aquinas's phrase, cut off "according to the natural course") and on Gregory's prayer-seeking apparitions, placing both under divine providence and sorting the cases by the state of the soul.
  • Aquinas's treatment of the saints and of the souls in purgatory is traced on the prayer-to-saints and purgatory timelines.
  • Compare his treatment of dreams on the sibling timeline the discernment of dreams (Summa II-II, q. 95, a. 6): there a dream's causes divide into inward/natural and outward, the outward "referable to God… by the ministry of the angels" or "to the action of the demons".
