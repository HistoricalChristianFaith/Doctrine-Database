Matthew the royal (legal) succession, Luke Joseph's natural line — both of Joseph

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: plausible — coherent, and it rests on a demonstrable feature of Matthew's list (a schematic throne-succession, not a strict pedigree), so it explains the lists' whole divergence where the levirate reading explains only the doubled father; but its final Joseph-link still needs a legal-father device, and it secures only Jesus's legal Davidic right, not his blood · classically formulated by J. Gresham Machen (1930), on a seed in Augustine

Related arguments: reconciling the two genealogies — see also one father natural, one legal (levirate/adoption) · Does Luke give Mary's genealogy?

The claim

"Matthew gives the legal descendants of David—the men who would have been legally the heir to the Davidic throne if that throne had continued—while Luke gives the descendants of David in that particular line to which, finally, Joseph, the husband of Mary, belonged."

— J. Gresham Machen, The Virgin Birth of Christ (1930), link. The thesis is not (as with the levirate reading) that a single marriage event doubled Joseph's father, but that the two lists differ along their whole length because they are two different kinds of list: Matthew traces the legal line of succession to David's throne — who at each step held the dynastic right — while Luke traces Joseph's actual biological descent. Both are Joseph's; they diverge wherever the crown passed otherwise than strict father-to-son, and touch again where the two happen to meet (Shealtiel and Zerubbabel, then Joseph himself). On this reading "who is the legal heir to David's throne?" and "who is Joseph's natural ancestor?" are simply two different questions, answered by two lists.

Who advances it

The recognition that Matthew's is the royal, kingly line is old — Augustine already reads Matthew as descending through the reigning kings and grants that Scripture's "begat" can carry legal, not only natural, generation (his own preferred resolution is adoption).1 But the distinctive modern form — that Matthew is a succession list of throne-heirs and Luke a natural pedigree, both Joseph's — is classically stated by the Princeton NT scholar J. Gresham Machen in The Virgin Birth of Christ (1930), who judged it the likeliest account, still supplying a levirate or collateral link (Jacob and Heli as kinsmen) only at the last step to Joseph.2 It is the framing carried by much conservative commentary since, and is regularly paired with the observation that it resolves the curse on Jeconiah (Jer. 22:30). It should be distinguished from Julius Africanus's explicit rejection of a royal-vs-priestly split (both lines run through the royal tribe of Judah): this reading proposes a royal-vs-natural distinction, not a royal-vs-priestly one, and so is untouched by Africanus's objection.3

The evidence for

The reading's real strength is that Matthew's list is demonstrably a schematic succession document, not a strict biological pedigree. To reach his announced pattern of three fourteens (Matt. 1:17), Matthew drops three reigning kings — Ahaziah, Joash, and Amaziah — between Joram and Uzziah ("Ozias"), writing "Joram begat Ozias" where the record of Kings and Chronicles has three generations; and he telescopes a further name at Josiah → Jeconiah.4 So Matthew's "begat" already skips links to track the crown, not each birth — exactly what a legal-succession list does and a bloodline does not. That grants the reading's central premise on the text's own showing, and yields two further advantages over the levirate solution. First, it explains the lists' whole divergence: if one traces the crown and the other the blood, they should differ at nearly every name between David and Joseph (as they do) and coincide only where the two lines cross — at Shealtiel and Zerubbabel, and finally at Joseph — rather than differing at one doubled father and mysteriously everywhere else.5 Second, it turns the curse on Jeconiah into a feature. Jeremiah swore that no offspring of Coniah would "prosper, sitting upon the throne of David"; yet Matthew runs the legal line straight through Jeconiah. If Matthew gives the legal heirship and the biological descent runs otherwise (through Nathan), Jesus can inherit David's throne-right legally through Joseph while not standing under the blood-curse as Jeconiah's physical seed — the two lists doing precisely different work.6

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The load-bearing premise is simply true: Matthew's genealogy is a selective, schematic list built to a numerical scheme, willing to omit reigning kings, so treating it as a legal/dynastic line rather than a birth register is warranted by the text, not imposed on it. Given that, the reading does something the levirate solution cannot — it accounts for why the two lists diverge throughout, not just at Joseph's father — and it makes coherent sense of the Jeconiah curse. As a demonstration that the two genealogies can both be Joseph's, doing two different jobs, it is coherent and unrefuted.

What is overstated or imprecise. Three limits keep it short of a probable reconstruction. (i) The final link still needs the levirate. The genre distinction explains the run of the lists, but not the last step — why Matthew names Joseph's father Jacob and Luke names him Heli. Machen resolves it exactly as Africanus did, by a levirate or collateral kinship between Jacob and Heli; so at the crux this reading inherits the levirate page's difficulty rather than escaping it, and the "two different kinds of list" thesis does not, by itself, deliver the doubled father. (ii) "Reigning kings" holds only to the exile. After Jeconiah none of the names Matthew lists ever sat on David's throne; from the exile onward Matthew's "succession" is a line of legal claimants to a vacant crown, not of kings — a looser notion than "who reigned," and one the texts nowhere spell out. (iii) It does not secure Jesus's Davidic blood. On this reading Luke's Nathan line is Joseph's natural descent, and Joseph is not Jesus's biological father; so the lists together give Jesus a legal throne-right but not the physical Davidic ancestry the creeds confess — that requires the separate (and weaker) move of assigning Luke's line to Mary. And the modern critical judgment (Brown) rejects every harmonization alike, holding that neither list is a biological record to begin with.7

Assessment

Plausible. This is the strongest of the harmonizations on the point where the levirate reading is weakest: it rests on a demonstrable fact about Matthew's list — a schematic throne-succession that omits real kings — and so explains the near-total divergence of the two lines and the Jeconiah curse together, rather than pinning everything on one unattested marriage. It is coherent and not ruled out. It falls short of probable because its own final link (Joseph's doubled father) still reaches for the levirate device it was meant to improve on, because "succession of kings" is literally true only down to the exile, and because — as here framed, both lists being Joseph's — it establishes Jesus's legal Davidic right but not his descent by blood. Keep two things separate: that Matthew's genealogy really is a legal/dynastic list (which the text supports), and that this fully reconstructs how the two lists relate at every step (which it does not settle).
