Both genealogies are Joseph's — one father natural, one legal

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — coherent and ancient, and it answers the pagan objection on its own terms, but the levirate form rests on an unrecorded tradition its own author calls unattested, and it explains only the doubled father, not the near-total divergence of the two lists · advanced by Julius Africanus (levirate) and Augustine (adoption)

Related arguments: reconciling the two genealogies — see also Matthew the royal succession, Luke Joseph's natural line · Does Luke give Mary's genealogy?

The claim

"Thus neither of the evangelists is in error, as the one reckons by nature and the other by law… Heli dying childless, Jacob raised up seed to him and begat Joseph, his own son by nature, but the son of Heli by law. Thus Joseph was the son of both."

— Julius Africanus, Epistle to Aristides §§ II, VI, link. The assertion is that both lists give Joseph's ancestry, and the two fathers named for him — Jacob in Matthew, Heli in Luke — are a natural father and a legal one. Africanus supplies the legal father by levirate marriage (Deut. 25:5); Augustine supplies him instead by adoption. Either way the contradiction dissolves: a single man could lawfully stand on the record as son of two fathers.

Who advances it

The levirate form is the dominant patristic solution: coined by Julius Africanus (c. 220), transmitted and endorsed by Eusebius (c. 320), certified by Jerome against the emperor Julian (c. 398), and carried whole into the Greek systematic tradition by John of Damascus (c. 743); it passes into the medieval West through Bede and the schoolmen. The adoption form is Augustine's preferred variant (c. 400) — a species of the same "legal father" genus, not a rival to it. Both take Matthew's line as the natural one and Luke's as the legal one.

The evidence for

The reading has real strengths. Israel's levirate law was a genuine institution that could give one man two fathers of record — the begetter and the dead brother whose "seed" he legally was — so the harmonization invokes a mechanism the Bible itself supplies, not an ad hoc invention. It answers the pagan objection (Julian's) on the objection's own ground: a Jew would not have found "two fathers" a contradiction at all. It fits two features of Luke's text that Matthew's lacks — Luke's guarded "as was supposed," and his conspicuous avoidance of the word "begat" throughout the list — which Africanus reads as deliberate markers of legal rather than natural generation. And it claims a line back to Jesus's own surviving kinsmen, the desposynoi, for the underlying genealogies.1 Augustine's adoption variant needs no special tradition at all: it rests only on the two Evangelists' diction and on Luke's God-ward, adoption-shaped genealogy (which ends "Adam, which was the son of God," a son "by grace").2

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The core thesis — both lists are Joseph's, and a doubled father can be legal rather than a contradiction — is coherent and is not refuted by anything in the texts. That a man could have a natural and a legal father is simply true of the levirate and of adoption. As a defeater of the bare charge "the Gospels contradict," the reading succeeds: it shows a way both statements can stand.

What is overstated or imprecise. The levirate form's specific machinery is where it strains. Its linchpin — the wife Estha married in succession by Matthan (Solomon's line) and Melchi (Nathan's), making Jacob and Heli half-brothers — is an otherwise-unrecorded tradition that Africanus himself concedes "is not supported by testimony." Africanus's own copy of Luke apparently lacked the names Matthat and Levi that stand in our text between Heli and Melchi, so his scheme fits his list but not the received one. More seriously, a single levirate marriage explains only the doubled father of Joseph; it does nothing to explain why the two lists diverge at nearly every name between David and Joseph, or why one runs through Solomon and the other through Nathan — a whole chain of difference, not one link. (Even the lists' one apparent point of contact, Shealtiel and Zerubbabel, reproduces the same doubled-father problem rather than escaping it — weighed on Are Matthew's and Luke's Shealtiel and Zerubbabel the same two men?) This is why Ambrosiaster rejected the levirate reading as early as c. 380 as "neither plausible nor useful," and why Brown judges it "solves so little… that it should be abandoned."3 Two further softness-points: the assignment of which line is natural and which legal is underdetermined by the texts (Africanus and Augustine pick Matthew-natural, but nothing forces it), and the adoption variant, while cheaper, still leaves the same wholesale divergence of the two chains unexplained.

Does the levirate really yield two fathers? The statute itself pulls the other way from Africanus's "son of both." The law of Deuteronomy 25:5–6 exists to continue the dead man's line: the firstborn of the levirate union "shall succeed in the name of his brother which is dead, that his name be not put out of Israel."4 On the strict rule the child is reckoned to the deceased — here Heli — and the natural begetter recedes; so it is Jacob's being named at all (Matthew's "Jacob begat Joseph") that would need explaining, not Heli's. Africanus's scheme needs Joseph to carry both fathers of record at once, a looser thing than the statute, whose whole point is to assign the child to one line (the dead brother's) precisely so it is not lost. The mechanism also demands a double convergence that the texts nowhere record: that Matthew's Solomon line (through Matthan) and Luke's Nathan line (through Melchi) both run down into a single half-brother pair, Jacob and Heli — which is exactly the otherwise-unattested coincidence the Estha tradition is invented to supply. Neither point is fatal — a levirate child genuinely had a begetter and a legal father and could be spoken of either way — but together they show the "two fathers" the reading trades on is not simply handed over by the law it appeals to.

Assessment

Contested. As a demonstration that the two genealogies can both be Joseph's without contradiction, the reading holds — and for that limited defensive purpose it is the strongest of the harmonizations, invoking a real biblical institution rather than an invented one. But it does not rise to a probable reconstruction of what actually happened: the levirate form depends on an unverifiable tradition its own author flags as unattested, doesn't fit the received text of Luke, and accounts for one doubled father while leaving two nearly-disjoint lists unexplained. It is genuinely disputed — a coherent possibility carried by weighty ancient authority, met by an equally old dissent (Ambrosiaster) and the modern critical verdict (Brown) that it is ad hoc. Keep two things separate: that the Gospels do not demonstrably contradict here (which this reading secures), and that this is the true account of the two lists (which it does not establish).
