Do the Gospel genealogies rest on a pedigree kept by Jesus's kinsmen (the desposynoi), as Julius Africanus reports?

Disputed reading · on the two genealogies of Jesus (Matthew 1 vs. Luke 3) · witness: Julius Africanus

The passage

“But as there had been kept in the archives up to that time the genealogies of the Hebrews as well as of those who traced their lineage back to proselytes, such as Achior the Ammonite and Ruth the Moabitess, and to those who were mingled with the Israelites and came out of Egypt with them, Herod, inasmuch as the lineage of the Israelites contributed nothing to his advantage, and since he was goaded with the consciousness of his own ignoble extraction, burned all the genealogical records, thinking that he might appear of noble origin if no one else were able, from the public registers, to trace back his lineage to the patriarchs or proselytes and to those mingled with them, who were called Georae.

“A few of the careful, however, having obtained private records of their own, either by remembering the names or by getting them in some other way from the registers, pride themselves on preserving the memory of their noble extraction. Among these are those already mentioned, called Desposyni, on account of their connection with the family of the Saviour. Coming from Nazara and Cochaba, villages of Judea, into other parts of the world, they drew the aforesaid genealogy from memory and from the book of daily records as faithfully as possible.… And let this suffice us, for, although we can urge no testimony in its support, we have nothing better or truer to offer.”

— Julius Africanus, Epistle to Aristides § V (c. 220), as preserved in Eusebius, Church History 1.7.13–15, source.1

The question

Africanus grounds his harmonization of Matthew's and Luke's lists in what Jesus's relatives, the desposynoi (“those belonging to the Master”), are said to have preserved and expounded. The dispute is whether a genealogy actually kept within Jesus's family lies behind the Gospel lists, or whether the lists are theological compositions of another provenance. The question is distinct from whether Africanus's levirate scheme (Estha, Matthan and Melchi, the childless Heli) reconciles the two lists, and from the use of the same desposynoi traditions on the perpetual-virginity timeline to ask how the “brethren” were related to Jesus.

A pedigree kept and handed down by Jesus's kinsmen

On this reading the Gospel genealogies, or the tradition behind them, go back to records and memory preserved by Jesus's own Davidic family.

Held by: Julius Africanus (Epistle to Aristides, c. 220); Eusebius (Church History 1.7, c. 320); John F. McCarthy (Catholic, “New Light on the Genealogies of Jesus,” 1987); Richard Bauckham (Jude and the Relatives of Jesus, 1990), on the list's origin in the kin-circle; he reads it as a deliberately composed seventy-seven-generation scheme.2

Africanus presents his account as family tradition: “Nor indeed is this incapable of proof, neither is it a rash conjecture. For the kinsmen of the Saviour after the flesh… have also handed down the following account.” Eusebius introduces it as the account Africanus “had received from tradition.” On this reading Africanus's closing admission concerns the explanation he offers, while the existence of the family's records is reported as fact.2

On this reading, the family's Davidic descent was a public matter in the first century. Hegesippus, a second-century writer gathering Palestinian traditions, reports that the grandsons of Jude, “the Lord's brother according to the flesh,” were brought before Domitian, asked whether they were descendants of David, “and they confessed that they were”; that Symeon son of Clopas was accused as “a descendant of David and a Christian”; and that Vespasian ordered “all that belonged to the lineage of David” sought out. A family examined on its pedigree, on this reading, knew and transmitted that pedigree. Bauckham identifies the travels of the desposynoi from Nazareth and Kokhaba with the missionary journeys of the Lord's brothers that Paul presupposes in 1 Corinthians 9:5, and argues that Luke's seventy-seven-generation list “derives, in virtually the form that Luke has preserved, from the circle of the first generation of the desposyni.”3

Josephus describes priestly families keeping and checking genealogies: priests taking a bride's genealogy “from the ancient tables” with “many witnesses,” diaspora priests sending “the ancient names of their parents in writing” to Jerusalem, and high-priestly names “from father to son set down in our records”; he gives his own descent from the public records. Paul, in the 50s, calls Jesus “of the seed of David according to the flesh” (Romans 1:3).4 McCarthy reads Matthew's opening “book of the generation” as “a written genealogical record to which he is referring - a document that he is quoting,” one Joseph “could well have registered” at the Bethlehem census. On this reading the Evangelists drew on documents of the kind the family is said to have kept.2

Theological compositions of popular, not archival, provenance

On this reading the two lists were composed or adapted to say who Jesus is, and are not transcripts of a family register.

Held by: Raymond E. Brown (Catholic, The Birth of the Messiah, 1977; rev. 1993).5

Brown holds that “the Lucan genealogy is no less theological in purpose than Matthew's and no freer of historical difficulties,” and that it, “while in some ways more plausible than Matthew's list, scarcely constitutes an exact record of Jesus' biological ancestry.” What can be said “with surety” of Luke's list, in his account, is that it is partly “artificially arranged in numerical patterns of seven” and “contains enough inaccuracies and confusions to suggest a popular provenance (rather than an archival provenance) among Greek-speaking Jews.” Luke “adopted this list and adapted it for theological purposes by placing it between the baptism of Jesus and his temptations.”5

On this reading the shape of the two lists reflects their purpose. Matthew runs from Abraham through Solomon and the kings of Judah to Jacob; Luke ascends from Heli through David's son Nathan to “Adam, which was the son of God.” Between David and Joseph they share only Shealtiel and Zerubbabel, and they name different fathers for Joseph. Brown reads each list by its message: “the two NT genealogies tell us how to evaluate Jesus, they tell us nothing certain about his grandparents or his great-grand-parents. The message about Jesus, son of Joseph, is not that factually he is also (grand)son of either Jacob (Matthew) or of Eli (Luke) but that theologically he is ‘son of David, son of Abraham’ (Matthew), and ‘Son of God’ (Luke).” The genealogies, on this reading, carry Christological claims in the form of descent.5
