Are Matthew's and Luke's Shealtiel and Zerubbabel the same two men?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — the pair's identity with the post-exilic Zerubbabel son of Shealtiel (the governor of Haggai and Ezra) is well-grounded, near-consensus; but their divergent fathers (Jeconiah in Matthew, Neri in Luke) and divergent sons (Abiud in Matthew, Rhesa in Luke) — together with the third variant of 1 Chronicles 3:19, where Zerubbabel is son of Pedaiah — reproduce in miniature the very doubled-father crux the harmonizations exist to solve, so the lists' one meeting-point is a genuine crossing rather than the independent confirmation the royal-succession reading takes it for · the identity is the unanimous ancient and modern reading; the reconciliation of the divergences is carried by the levirate / royal-succession harmonizers and by later conjecture (Lord Arthur Hervey)

The claim

"Jechonias begat Salathiel; and Salathiel begat Zorobabel" (Matthew 1:12).
"…Zorobabel, which was the son of Salathiel, which was the son of Neri" (Luke 3:27).
"…Zerubbabel the son of Shealtiel, governor of Judah…" (Haggai 1:1; cf. Ezra 3:2).

Between David and Joseph the two genealogies scarcely touch: they run through different sons of David (Solomon in Matthew, Nathan in Luke) and disagree at nearly every name. The single conspicuous exception is a Shealtiel (Salathiel) whose son is Zerubbabel — a pair standing in both lists at the same point, the hinge of the Babylonian exile and return. The natural reading, and the one on which the harmonizations lean, is that this is one historical pair, the Davidic governor Zerubbabel son of Shealtiel who led the return under Cyrus and Darius and rebuilt the temple, named across the post-exilic books.1 If so, the two lists really do cross here — which is exactly what the royal-succession harmonization needs. But the crossing is not clean: the two Evangelists give this Shealtiel different fathers and this Zerubbabel different sons, and a third biblical list makes Zerubbabel the son of someone else again. So the one meeting-point is itself a knot.

Who advances it

That the two Evangelists mean the same famous post-exilic pair is close to unanimous, ancient and modern: Zerubbabel son of Shealtiel is a fixed point of the return, named in Haggai, Zechariah, Ezra–Nehemiah and 1 Esdras, and no one proposes an obscure second Zerubbabel-son-of-a-Shealtiel to fill either Gospel slot. The work of this page is therefore not to defend the identity but to weigh what the shared pair does — and does not — prove. The reconciliation of the divergent fathers and sons is carried by the same hands that harmonize the rest of the lists: the levirate scheme of Julius Africanus and its heirs (one father natural, one legal), and above all the royal-succession reading classically stated by J. Gresham Machen (Matthew the royal line, Luke Joseph's natural descent), whose whole architecture requires the two lines to meet at Shealtiel/Zerubbabel and again at Joseph. The medieval West inherited a set of three stock answers to the surrounding tangle — transmitted in Thomas Aquinas's Commentary on Matthew from an earlier Gloss — and the nineteenth century added a text-critical conjecture, Lord Arthur Hervey's proposal that Luke's "Rhesa" is not a name at all.2

The evidence for

The identity is well-grounded. Both lists set the Shealtiel–Zerubbabel pair at precisely the exilic hinge — Matthew "after they were brought to Babylon," Luke at the corresponding depth of his ascending list — which is just where the historical Zerubbabel belongs. And one strand of the Old Testament record independently agrees with Matthew: 1 Chronicles 3:17 makes Shealtiel a son of "Jeconiah… the captive," exactly as Matthew does ("Jechonias begat Salathiel").3 So Matthew's Shealtiel is demonstrably the Chronicler's, i.e. the post-exilic figure — and Luke, naming the same son Zerubbabel, is naming the same man.

The crossing even has a ready-made mechanism on the royal-succession reading. Jeremiah had written the royal line "childless" at Jeconiah — "no man of his seed shall prosper, sitting upon the throne of David" — so a harmonizer can take the Solomonic royal line as failing at Jeconiah and the crown passing to a collateral heir out of David's other son Nathan: Luke's Neri, of Nathan's line, is the natural father of Shealtiel, who is reckoned the legal royal heir of the childless Jeconiah. On that reading the two lists are supposed to meet here precisely because this is where a Nathanic man steps into the Solomonic succession — the "legal vs. natural" device doing at Shealtiel exactly the work it does at Joseph.4 After Zerubbabel the lines are then free to diverge again — Matthew tracing one son (Abiud), Luke another (Rhesa) — each descending separately to Joseph, which a man with several sons can perfectly well support.

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The two men are the one post-exilic pair; there is no serious case for homonyms here, and the third-century scheme, Machen's reading, and the modern critics alike take the identity for granted. That much is sound. A father may have more than one son, so Matthew's Abiud and Luke's Rhesa need not contradict: they can be two lines off one Zerubbabel.

What is overstated or imprecise. The trouble is that the meeting-point is repeatedly cited — on the wiki's own royal-succession page among others — as if the bare fact of a shared pair confirmed the harmonization. It does not, for three reasons that make the crossing a further difficulty rather than a proof.

(i) The divergent fathers reproduce the doubled-father crux one tier up. To make Shealtiel a single man who is "son of Jeconiah" (Matthew) and "son of Neri" (Luke) at once is to invoke the very levirate-or-legal-succession device the harmonization was built to establish at Joseph. So the crossing is not independent evidence for that device: it is the same hypothesis applied a second time. Take the hypothesis away and the shared pair yields two men named Shealtiel with two different fathers — a duplicate of the original problem, not its solution.

(ii) The divergent sons are both unattested in the Old Testament Davidic line, and 1 Chronicles adds a third variant. Neither Matthew's Abiud nor Luke's Rhesa appears among Zerubbabel's sons in 1 Chronicles 3:19–20 (Meshullam, Hananiah, and the rest); and that same verse makes Zerubbabel the son not of Shealtiel but of Pedaiah, Shealtiel's brother — against Haggai, Ezra, and both Gospels, which call him "son of Shealtiel."5 The medieval Gloss transmitted by Aquinas already felt the whole weight of this and offered three escapes for the Pedaiah/Abiud tangle: that Chronicles is corrupt in "numbers and names"; that Shealtiel bore a second name; or — "the truer one" — that Shealtiel and his brother each fathered a Zerubbabel of his own, so the Evangelist's Zerubbabel is not the Chronicler's at all. The first is an appeal to textual damage, the second an unattested double name, the third a homonym move that quietly surrenders the very identity the crossing depends on.6 The most economical modern reconciliation of Pedaiah with Shealtiel is, once again, the levirate (Shealtiel childless, Pedaiah raising up seed to him) or adoption — the same device a third time.

(iii) The tidiest "solution" to Rhesa dissolves the crossing itself. Lord Arthur Hervey, to rid Luke's list of its unattested Rhesa, conjectured that "Rhesa" is no personal name but the title rēsh galutha, "head of the captivity" (prince) — so that Luke's "Zorobabel, Rhesa" would mean "Zerubbabel the prince," collapsing two Lukan links into one titled man.7 The conjecture is ingenious and would smooth one name, but it is unsupported by any manuscript — every witness reads Rhesa as a link in the chain — and if accepted it shortens Luke's list and shifts every generation below, so it buys relief at one name by disturbing the count the crossing is supposed to secure. It is a token of how much the "clean meeting" has to be constructed.

Behind all of this stands the modern critical judgment (Brown) that neither list is a documentary blood-record to begin with, so that a shared pair of famous names two-thirds of the way down is exactly what one expects when two theological genealogies both route through the one indispensable post-exilic Davidid — not a seam where a real pedigree shows through.8

Assessment

Contested. Keep the two questions the lead poses apart. Are they the same two men? — near-certainly yes: this is the one post-exilic Zerubbabel son of Shealtiel, and Matthew's Shealtiel is even confirmed as Jeconiah's son by 1 Chronicles 3:17; that identity is sound. Does the shared pair therefore support the royal-succession reconciliation? — no more than the reconciliation supports itself. The divergent fathers (Jeconiah vs. Neri) can be made one man only by the same legal/levirate crossing the harmonization is trying to prove, so the meeting-point is the hypothesis applied twice, not fresh evidence for it; the divergent sons (Abiud vs. Rhesa) are both absent from the Old Testament Davidic list; and 1 Chronicles 3:19 introduces a third father for Zerubbabel (Pedaiah), soluble only by yet another levirate or adoption or by a homonym that would break the identity. The honest verdict is that the lists' one point of contact is a real crossing but a contested one: it neither refutes the harmonizations (a man can carry a legal and a natural father, and can have several sons) nor confirms them (every knot in it is untied with the same disputed tools used everywhere else). The royal-succession page should treat Shealtiel/Zerubbabel as the place its reading expects a crossing, not as an independent datum that delivers one.
