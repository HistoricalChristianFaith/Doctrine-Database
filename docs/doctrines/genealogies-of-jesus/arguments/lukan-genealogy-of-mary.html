Does Luke give Mary's genealogy rather than Joseph's?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: weak — it removes the clash by handing Luke's list to Mary and gives the Virgin the Davidic blood the creeds confess, but Luke's own words make Joseph, not Mary, "the son of Heli," and the whole early church read the list as Joseph's · advanced by post-medieval and modern harmonizers (no early-church proponent)

Related arguments: reconciling the two genealogies — see also Both genealogies are Joseph's — one father natural, one legal · Matthew the royal succession, Luke Joseph's natural line

The claim

"And Jesus himself began to be about thirty years of age, being (as was supposed) the son of Joseph, which was the son of Heli."

— Luke 3:23 (KJV), link. The reading proposes that this verse is really the head of Mary's family tree: "Heli" is Mary's father, and Joseph is named only as his son-in-law ("son of Heli" = son by marriage). On this construction Matthew gives Joseph's own line (through Solomon) and Luke gives Mary's (through Nathan), so Jesus has a genuine biological Davidic descent — through his mother — and the two lists never really competed, because they are the pedigrees of two different people.

Who advances it

Unlike the legal-and-natural-father reading, this one has no ancient proponent: no father of the first eight centuries reads Luke's list as Mary's. Its systematic form has no attestation earlier than the late-fifteenth-century Dominican Annius of Viterbo (Giovanni Nanni, d. 1502), and it became widely popular after the Reformation and especially in modern evangelical study Bibles, prized because it secures Mary's Davidic descent directly from the text and dispenses with the levirate machinery.1 It is often confused with the older patristic point — made by Eusebius, Jerome, and John of Damascus — that Mary shared Joseph's tribe; but that point derives Mary's descent from the tribal-marriage law alongside a Joseph-genealogy, and John of Damascus even gives Mary a separate line (through Joachim), precisely because he takes Luke's list to be Joseph's.

The evidence for

The reading has a genuine attraction. It gives Mary the Davidic blood that the creed ("born of the Virgin Mary… of the seed of David according to the flesh," Rom. 1:3) seems to require Jesus to inherit biologically — and if Joseph was not the natural father, that blood can only come through Mary. It also fits the well-observed difference of character between the two lists: Matthew's runs through the reigning kings (a legal-royal claim to David's throne, passing through the line that held it), while Luke's runs through Nathan, a non-royal son of David, which reads more naturally as a plain blood descent. If Luke's is Mary's actual bloodline, that contrast is explained at a stroke.2

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The conclusion the reading is built to protect — that Mary was of David's house — is itself well-supported, affirmed across the tradition (Eusebius, Jerome, John of Damascus) and arguably required by Romans 1:3. And the observation that Matthew's kingly line and Luke's Nathan line do different work is sound; it is the backbone of the older harmonizations too.

What is overstated or imprecise. The specific mechanism — that Luke's list is Mary's — runs against the grammar and against the tradition. Luke says Joseph "was the son of Heli," not that Mary was Heli's daughter; the "son-in-law" gloss has to be read into the text, and Luke names no woman anywhere in the chain. The whole early church, including writers keen to honor Mary, took the list as Joseph's: Africanus builds his levirate scheme on Joseph's two fathers, Jerome answers Julian by insisting the line is Joseph's (Scripture "does not follow the female line"), and John of Damascus, who most wants a Marian pedigree, supplies one separately rather than finding it in Luke. The reading is, in short, largely reverse-engineered: it starts from a conclusion the tradition reached by other means (Mary's Davidic descent) and retrofits it onto Luke's list to avoid the harder harmonization — at the cost of making Luke say something he does not say.3

Assessment

Weak. The goal it serves — Mary's Davidic descent — is legitimate and well-attested, but the means are not: the "Heli = Mary's father, Joseph his son-in-law" construction contradicts Luke's plain statement that Joseph "was the son of Heli," names no woman, and was unknown to the entire early church, which read the list as Joseph's. It is best seen as a modern shortcut to a conclusion the older tradition secured differently (through the tribal-marriage law, or through a separate Marian line as in John of Damascus). For the defensive task of showing the Gospels need not contradict, the legal-and-natural-father reading does the job without rewriting Luke; this reading is dominated by it.
