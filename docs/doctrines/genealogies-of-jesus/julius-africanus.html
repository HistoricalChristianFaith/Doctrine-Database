Julius Africanus on the Two Genealogies of Jesus

Dates: c. 220 (the Epistle to Aristides) · Wikipedia
Summary: The fountainhead of the whole tradition. Africanus first demolishes the easy "royal line vs. priestly line" answer as a pious falsehood, then offers the solution the church would repeat for fifteen centuries: Jacob (Matthew) and Heli (Luke) were half-brothers by one mother; when Heli died childless, Jacob married his widow and raised up Joseph — Joseph's natural father, while Heli was his father "by law." So both Gospels are true, one reckoning by nature, the other by law. He claims the outline came from Jesus's own surviving kinsmen, while candidly admitting it rests on no documentary proof.

Claims & evidence

The "royal vs. priestly" harmonization is a pious lie, and must be rejected

"Some indeed incorrectly allege that this discrepant enumeration and mixing of the names both of priestly men, as they think, and royal, was made properly, in order that Christ might be shown rightfully to be both Priest and King… Let us not therefore descend to such religious trifling as to establish the kingship and priesthood of Christ by the interchanges of the names… The evangelists, therefore, would thus have spoken falsely, affirming what was not truth, but a fictitious commendation… Nor shall an assertion of this kind prevail in the Church of Christ against the exact truth, so as that a lie should be contrived for the praise and glory of Christ."

— The Epistle to Aristides, § I, source. Africanus refuses to solve the puzzle by supposing one Evangelist gave a "royal" and the other a "priestly" descent: both lines run through the royal tribe of Judah, and inventing a priestly pedigree to flatter Christ would make the Evangelists liars. His governing principle is that the harmonization must be true, not merely edifying.

Both lists are Joseph's; the levirate law gives him a father by nature and a father by law

"For whereas in Israel the names of their generations were enumerated either according to nature or according to law,—according to nature, indeed, by the succession of legitimate offspring, and according to law whenever another raised up children to the name of a brother dying childless… Thus neither of the evangelists is in error, as the one reckons by nature and the other by law."

— The Epistle to Aristides, § II, source. This is the hinge of the argument: Israel counted descent two ways, and the levirate marriage of Deuteronomy 25:5 could give one man two recorded fathers — the one who begot him and the dead brother whose "seed" he legally was.

The mechanism: Matthan and Melchi, one wife Estha, and the brothers Jacob and Heli

"Matthan and Melchi, having taken the same woman to wife in succession, begat children who were uterine brothers, as the law did not prevent a widow… from marrying another. By Estha, then—for such is her name according to tradition—Matthan first, the descendant of Solomon, begets Jacob; and on Matthan's death, Melchi, who traces his descent back to Nathan… had a son Heli. Thus, then, we shall find Jacob and Heli uterine brothers… And of these, the one Jacob having taken the wife of his brother Heli, who died childless, begat by her the third, Joseph—his son by nature and by account. Whence also it is written, 'And Jacob begat Joseph.' But according to law he was the son of Heli, for Jacob his brother raised up seed to him."

— The Epistle to Aristides, § III, source. Africanus even reads Luke's parenthetical "as was supposed" as governing not only "son of Joseph" but "son of Heli," so that Luke has deliberately "entirely omitted the word 'begat' to the very end" precisely to signal generation "according to law." His closing summary (§ VI) states it flatly: "Heli dying childless, Jacob raised up seed to him and begat Joseph, his own son by nature, but the son of Heli by law. Thus Joseph was the son of both."

The source: the Saviour's own kinsmen — but confessedly without documentary proof

"A few… of the studious, having private records of their own… pride themselves in preserving the memory of their noble descent; and among these happen to be those already mentioned, called desposyni, on account of their connection with the family of the Saviour… Whether, then, the case stand thus or not, no one could discover a more obvious explanation… And let this suffice us for the matter, although it is not supported by testimony, because we have nothing more satisfactory or true to allege upon it."

— The Epistle to Aristides, § V, source. Africanus grounds the outline in the desposynoi — relatives of Jesus around Nazareth who kept private genealogies after Herod (he says) burned the public archives. But he is scrupulous: the reconstruction "is not supported by testimony." That candor is the honest limit on his own solution, and the point later critics press.

Notes

  • The name-count problem in his own copy. Africanus places Melchi "third from the end" in Luke's list, whereas our text of Luke 3:23–24 has Matthat and Levi standing between Heli and Melchi. The likeliest explanation (so Westcott–Hort, and Bede long before) is that Africanus's manuscript of Luke omitted those two names; if so, his scheme fits his text but not ours — an early sign that the reconciliation does not map cleanly onto the received lists.
  • Transmission. The letter survives chiefly because Eusebius quoted it in Church History 1.7; the fuller text was recovered later from catenae and Cardinal Mai's fragments. Jerome sends readers to "Africanus the chronologist" for the full argument, and John of Damascus reproduces the scheme almost verbatim.
  • What it fixes and what it leaves. The levirate device explains the doubled father of Joseph; it does not by itself explain why the two lists diverge at nearly every name between David and Joseph, nor why one runs through Solomon and the other through Nathan. Those remain for the argument page on the legal-and-natural-father reading.
