Jerome on the Two Genealogies of Jesus

Dates: c. 398 (the Commentary on Matthew) · Wikipedia
Summary: Jerome names the enemy. He tells us the objection came from the emperor Julian — "the Apostate" — who catalogued the clashing fathers of Joseph among the Gospels' contradictions in a book against the Christians. Jerome's reply is the levirate solution in a sentence: "one was his father by nature, the other by law," and he sends the reader to Africanus and Eusebius for the full argument. To the further worry — why give Joseph's line at all, since he was not Jesus's father — Jerome answers that Scripture does not trace descent through women, and that Joseph and Mary were of one tribe.

Claims & evidence

The objection is Julian's; the answer is nature vs. law

"This passage is objected to us by the Emperor Julian in his Discrepancy of the Evangelists. Matthew calls Joseph the son of Jacob, Luke makes him the son of Heli. He did not know the Scripture manner, one was his father by nature, the other by law. For we know that God commanded by Moses, that if a brother or near kinsman died without children, another should take his wife, to raise up seed to his brother or kinsman. But of this matter Africanus the chronologist, and Eusebius of Cæsarea, have disputed more fully."

— Commentary on Matthew, on 1:16, as preserved in Aquinas, Catena Aurea, source. Jerome supplies what the earlier witnesses do not: the identity of the objector. Julian's lost polemic pressed the two fathers of Joseph as a Gospel self-contradiction; Jerome answers with Africanus's levirate reading and defers to the prior treatments rather than re-arguing them.

The same, in his own words, in the running commentary

"But Jacob begot Joseph. Here Julianus Augustus has presented to us the disagreement of the Evangelists, why the evangelist Matthew called Joseph the son of Jacob, and Luke called him the son of Heli; not understanding the custom of the Scriptures, that one is according to nature, the other according to the Law his father. For we know this by the command of God given through Moses, that if a brother or relative without children dies, another should take his wife to raise up offspring for his brother or relative. Both Africanus, a historian of the time, and Eusebius of Caesarea extensively debated in their books the discrepancies in the Gospels."

— Commentary on Matthew, on 1:16, source. The firsthand text of Jerome's commentary confirms the report: Julian is "Julianus Augustus," the objection is the Jacob/Heli clash, and the solution is the received nature-and-law reading with a nod to Africanus and Eusebius.

Why Joseph's line, not Mary's: Scripture does not follow the female line, and they were of one tribe

"The attentive reader may ask, Seeing Joseph was not the father of the Lord and Saviour, how does his genealogy traced down to him in order pertain to the Lord? We will answer, first, that it is not the practice of Scripture to follow the female line in its genealogies; secondly, that Joseph and Mary were of the same tribe, and that he was thence compelled to take her to wife as a kinsman, and they were enrolled together at Bethlehem, as being come of one stock."

— Commentary on Matthew, on 1:16, source. Notably, Jerome does not take the shortcut of saying Luke's list is Mary's. He answers the Mary-question the way Eusebius did — a genealogy of Joseph suffices because a lawful, within-the-tribe marriage carries Mary's Davidic descent with it — which is why the later "Luke traces Mary" reading cannot claim him as a witness.

Notes

  • Julian the Apostate. The emperor Julian (r. 361–363) wrote Against the Galileans, and evidently a piece specifically on the Gospels' discrepancies; both survive only in fragments quoted by their Christian answerers. Jerome's citation is a principal witness that the genealogy-clash was a set-piece of pagan anti-Christian argument, not a difficulty first felt inside the church.
  • Deference, not innovation. Jerome adds no new mechanism; his contribution is (a) naming the adversary and (b) certifying the levirate solution of Africanus and Eusebius as the church's standard reply. His refusal of the Mary-genealogy shortcut is itself significant for the argument page on that reading.
