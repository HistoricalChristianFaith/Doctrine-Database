Irenaeus of Lyons on the Genealogy of Jesus

Dates: c. 180 (Against Heresies; bishop of Lyons, d. c. 202) · Wikipedia
Summary: The earliest witness on this timeline — earlier by a generation than Julius Africanus, who opens the harmonizing tradition proper. Irenaeus does not yet wrestle with the clash between Matthew and Luke; his interest is in Luke's ascending line and where it ends. Luke runs the pedigree of Jesus back not merely to Abraham but all the way to Adam, "which was the son of God," and Irenaeus reads that Adam-ward reach as the hinge of his recapitulation theology: Christ the Second Adam "gathers up" into Himself the whole human race from Adam downward. He is also the first datable reader to count the generations in Luke's list — he reckons "seventy-two" — a figure shorter than the received text's, and so an early witness to the fluid textual state of Luke 3.

Claims & evidence

Luke's genealogy runs back to Adam — and Christ "sums up" all humanity from Adam onward

"Wherefore Luke points out that the pedigree which traces the generation of our Lord back to Adam contains seventy-two generations, connecting the end with the beginning, and implying that it is He who has summed up in Himself all nations dispersed from Adam downwards, and all languages and generations of men, together with Adam himself. Hence also was Adam himself termed by Paul 'the figure of Him that was to come,' because the Word, the Maker of all things, had formed beforehand for Himself the future dispensation of the human race, connected with the Son of God."

— Against Heresies 3.22.3, ANF 1, historicalchristian.faith (also newadvent.org). This is the theological reason Luke, unlike Matthew, climbs past Abraham to "Adam, which was the son of God" (Luke 3:38): the ascending list is not antiquarian but soteriological. Because the Word "recapitulates" — sums up, re-heads — the whole race in Himself, His pedigree must reach the whole race's head, Adam. Irenaeus even hears the generation-count as echoing the Table of Nations: the "seventy-two" answer to "all nations dispersed from Adam" and "all languages" — the seventy or seventy-two peoples of Genesis 10 (seventy in the Hebrew, seventy-two in the Septuagint tradition). The number and the theology are made to interlock.

Born of Mary, not begotten of Joseph: the Second-Adam analogy requires it

"…so did He who is the Word, recapitulating Adam in Himself, rightly receive a birth, enabling Him to gather up Adam [into Himself], from Mary, who was as yet a virgin. If, then, the first Adam had a man for his father, and was born of human seed, it were reasonable to say that the second Adam was begotten of Joseph. But if the former was taken from the dust, and God was his Maker, it was incumbent that the latter also, making a recapitulation in Himself, should be formed as man by God, to have an analogy with the former as respects His origin."

— Against Heresies 3.21.10, ANF 1, historicalchristian.faith (also newadvent.org). Irenaeus states, a generation before the harmonizers, the premise on which the whole later puzzle rests: Joseph is emphatically not Jesus's natural father. Precisely because the Second Adam had God, not a man, for His maker, "it were reasonable to say" — but false — "that the second Adam was begotten of Joseph." The genealogies through Joseph therefore cannot be birth registers of Jesus's own blood; they attach Him to the Davidic and Adamic line by another mode, a point the later tradition (levirate, adoption, legal succession, Mary's descent) would spend fifteen centuries trying to specify.

Notes

  • The generation-count and the text of Luke 3. Irenaeus reckons Luke's list at seventy-two generations; the received Greek text of Luke 3:23–38 yields seventy-seven names from Jesus up to God (seventy-six generations to Adam, seventy-seven counting "the son of God"). His shorter count is an early datum in the long argument over the exact contents of Luke's genealogy — the same fluidity that shows up in Africanus, whose copy seems to have lacked the Matthat and Levi that stand between Heli and Melchi in our text. Whether Irenaeus counted a shorter list, followed a symbolic "seventy-two" for its own sake (to match the nations of Genesis 10 / the seventy-two sent out in Luke 10:1), or simply miscounted, the figure shows the list was not yet fixed and reckoned uniformly by c. 180.
  • Recapitulation, not harmonization. Irenaeus never raises the discrepancy between Matthew's and Luke's lists that drives this page — his opponents were Gnostics who severed the Creator from the Redeemer, not skeptics counting Joseph's fathers. His contribution is to fix the meaning of Luke's Adam-ward direction, which the later tradition inherits: Augustine makes the same move when he reads Luke's ascent "up to God" as pointing to the atoning Priest and to adoption, and Ambrosiaster presses the ascent "past Adam to God" against Photinus. The royal-vs-natural-line argument likewise turns on Luke tracing an actual descent while Matthew traces a schematic royal succession.
  • Why he fronts the timeline. At c. 180 Irenaeus predates Africanus (c. 220) by a generation and is the earliest surviving Christian writer to comment directly on Luke's genealogy as a genealogy — its terminus, its length, and its Christological point — which is why the timeline now opens with him.
