The Venerable Bede on the Two Genealogies of Jesus

Dates: c. 710 (Commentary on Luke) · Wikipedia
Summary: The early-medieval Latin transmitter. Bede, gathering the fathers in his Commentary on Luke, adds no new solution but hands the whole harmonizing settlement on to the Latin West. To the "knotty question… how Joseph could have had two fathers" he gives Africanus's levirate scheme almost whole — and notices, in passing, that Africanus's own copy of Luke ran the names differently, an early recognition that the reconciliation depends on which text of Luke one holds. He keeps the received rule that Scripture does not genealogize women, yet reasons — with Eusebius and Jerome — that Joseph's pedigree exhibits Mary's tribe too; reads Luke's ascending, God-ward form with Augustine as a figure of adoption; and flags the "second Cainan" of Luke 3:36 as a Septuagint generation absent from the Hebrew.

Claims & evidence

The doubled father resolved by Africanus's levirate — and a note that Africanus's copy of Luke differed

"It rightly raises the question of how Joseph could have had two fathers coming from different lines of ancestors, one whom Luke mentions and the other whom Matthew mentions. For Matthew says: "And Mathan begot Jacob. And Jacob begot Joseph, the husband of Mary." But this knotty question, Africanus explained very clearly when writing about the harmony of the Gospels. He said, "Mathan and Mathat, at different times, each fathered children from the same wife named Estha. Because Mathan, who descended from Solomon, had first taken her as his wife, and upon leaving behind a son named Jacob, died. After his death, since the law does not forbid a widow from marrying another man, Mathat, who was descended from Nathan and of the same tribe but not of the same lineage, took the widow of Mathan as his wife, from whom he also fathered a son named Heli. Through this, Jacob and Heli are brothers, though from different fathers. Of these, Jacob, following the law, took the wife of his brother Heli, who died childless, and begot Joseph, who is naturally his son, which is why it is written: 'And Jacob begot Joseph.' But according to the law, Joseph is also considered the son of Heli, since Jacob, as his brother, had taken Heli's wife to raise up seed for him, as mandated by the law. Thus, both genealogies, Matthew's saying 'And Jacob begot Joseph', and Luke's with the proper observation stating 'He was supposed the son of Joseph, who was the son of Heli,' are validated and intact. Moreover, Africanus conveyed these same ideas in these very words, except he wrote 'Melchi' instead of 'Matthat,' because either his copy had it so, or in the history where he learned these things, he found the same Matthat had two names.""

— Commentary on Luke, on Luke 3:23 ("Who was Heli"), source. Bede takes over Africanus's levirate reconciliation entire — one wife, Estha, married in succession by a Solomon-line and a Nathan-line husband; Jacob and Heli half-brothers; Jacob raising up seed to the childless Heli — so that Matthew names Joseph's father by nature and Luke his father by law. What is distinctive is Bede's small text-critical aside. To make the scheme land on the received Luke (Heli, Matthat, Levi, Melchi…), he pairs the Nathan-line grandfather with "Mathat," the name standing next to Heli in our text; but he candidly reports that Africanus's own version wrote "Melchi" there instead, "because either his copy had it so." That is the observation later editors would repeat — that Africanus's manuscript of Luke apparently ran the list without the intervening names, so his reckoning fits his copy and not ours (weighed on the Africanus page and the argument for the natural-and-legal-father reading).

Luke's list is Joseph's, not Mary's — but Joseph's tribe exhibits Mary's

"Hence, if it troubles anyone that since Mary conceived Christ by the Holy Spirit, and Joseph is called his father not truly, but supposedly, why not rather is Mary's lineage described than Joseph's, who seemed to have no relation to him, let them first know that it is not customary in the Scriptures to set forth the order of women in genealogies. Next, Joseph and Mary were from one tribe, whence the law compelled him to take her as a relative. And that they are registered together in Bethlehem indicates they are born from one stock, and thus through Joseph's genealogy, the origin of Mary is also shown."

— Commentary on Luke, on Luke 3:23 ("As was supposed the son of Joseph"), source. Bede meets the natural objection — if Joseph is not the natural father, why trace his line at all? — with the standard answer he inherits from Eusebius and Jerome: Scripture does not genealogize women, and the within-the-tribe marriage law carries Mary's descent along with Joseph's. He is emphatically not reading Luke's list as Mary's own (the later, and weak, "Luke traces Mary" construction); Luke's pedigree stays Joseph's, and Mary's Davidic descent is a corollary drawn from the shared tribe, not a second list.

Luke ascends to God because his genealogy figures adoption and the forgiveness of sins

"Luke, however, did not recount the generations from the beginning of his Gospel, but from the baptism of Christ, not by descending but ascending, assigning Him more as a priest in expiating sins… By ascending, however, he passes from Abraham and arrives at God, to whom we are reconciled, purified and expiated. Rightly, he also took up the origin of adoption, because we are made sons of God by adoption through believing in the Son of God… But he sufficiently demonstrated that he did not call Joseph the son of Heli because he was begotten from him, but because he was adopted by him, since he also called Adam the son of God, even though he was made by God."

— Commentary on Luke, on Luke 3:23, source. Here Bede follows Augustine almost to the word (the Harmony of the Gospels reading that Luke gives the genealogy at the baptism, runs it up to God, and so "follow[s] the line of adoption"). Notably, Bede sets this beside Africanus's levirate account of the doubled father: the levirate answers how Joseph has two fathers, while the adoption reading supplies why Luke's inverse, God-ward list has the shape it does — the very question weighed as sound on the argument page for Luke's ascending structure. He also reproduces Augustine's count: not "forty-two generations, which make up thrice fourteen," but forty-one to Christ, "due to Jeconiah being counted twice."

The "second Cainan" of Luke 3:36 is a Septuagint generation absent from the Hebrew

"The name and generation of Cainan, according to Hebrew truth, is found neither in Genesis nor in the Words of the Days; but it is claimed that Arphaxad begat Sela or Sale as a son without any intermediary… Therefore, understand that blessed Luke took this genealogy from the edition of the seventy interpreters, where it is written that Arphaxad at the age of one hundred and thirty-five begat Cainan, and Cainan, when he was one hundred and thirty years old, begat Sela. But which of these is true, or if both can be true, God knows. We simply remind the reader that such a great discrepancy exists between the two codes in the series of time that from the flood to the birth of Abraham in Hebrew truth there are found to be 292 years, but in the translation of the seventy interpreters, 1,077 years are comprehended."

— Commentary on Luke, on Luke 3:36, source. Bede marks the internal wrinkle in Luke's own list: between Arphaxad and Shelah, Luke names a second Cainan that stands in the Greek Old Testament but not in the Hebrew of Genesis 11 or in 1 Chronicles. He rightly derives it from "the edition of the seventy interpreters" (the Septuagint), declines to adjudicate its originality ("which of these is true … God knows"), and even registers the chronological stakes — the Greek reckoning stretches the flood-to-Abraham interval nearly fourfold. This bears not on the Matthew–Luke divergence but on the New Testament textual crux weighed in full on the argument page for the "second Cainan" (where the bare question of originality is judged contested, the deliberate-deletion charge weak).

Notes

  • A compiler, not an innovator. Bede's method here is to gather and transmit: the doubled father from Africanus, the ascending-adoption reading and the 3×14 count from Augustine, the Mary-tribe corollary from Eusebius and Jerome. His value on this timeline is as the channel through which the whole Latin harmonization passed to the medieval schools — the counterpart in the West to what John of Damascus was doing for the Greek tradition in the same generation.
  • Levirate and adoption side by side. Bede juxtaposes two answers the tradition kept somewhat apart: Joseph is "the son of Heli" by levirate law (Africanus) and, a few lines later, "because he was adopted by him" (Augustine). He does not resolve the slight tension — a sign of the compilatory character of the commentary — but the two are close cousins: both make Joseph's Lukan fatherhood legal rather than natural.
  • The text-critical aside is the notable contribution. Bede's remark that Africanus "wrote 'Melchi' instead of 'Matthat'… because either his copy had it so" is an early recognition that the levirate reconciliation is anchored to a particular text of Luke — the point the Africanus detail page flags, that his manuscript apparently lacked the names our text carries between Heli and Melchi.
  • An Enochic footnote. On Luke 3:37 Bede observes that the ascending order "in the seventieth degree holds Enoch" — a count that turns, like Irenaeus's "seventy-two," on where one reckons from and which names one reads, and so brushes against the seventy-seven-generation reading of the list's length.
