Augustine on the Two Genealogies of Jesus

Dates: c. 400 (the Harmony of the Gospels) · Wikipedia
Summary: Augustine offers the tradition's second harmonization. In the Harmony of the Gospels he lists three ways the doubled father could resolve — a maternal grandfather, an adoptive father, or the levirate — and prefers adoption: Matthew's insistent "begat" marks Joseph's natural descent from Jacob, while Luke, calling Joseph "son of Heli," records an adoptive father. He finds it fitting that Luke gives the genealogy at the baptism and runs it up to God, since "by adoption we are made the sons of God." In his later Retractations he softened, no longer excluding Africanus's levirate account.

Claims & evidence

Three possible resolutions of the doubled father

"Or there occur three reasons, by one of which the Evangelist was led. For either one Evangelist has mentioned the father by whom Joseph was begotten, but the other his maternal grandfather, or some one of his ancestors. Or one of the fathers mentioned was the natural father of Joseph, the other his father who had adopted him. Or after the manner of the Jews, when a man has died without children, the next of kin taking his wife ascribes to his dead kinsman the son whom he has himself begotten."

— Questions on the Gospels 2.5, as preserved in Aquinas, Catena Aurea, source. Augustine sets out the field of solutions plainly: (1) different ancestors named; (2) one natural, one adoptive father; (3) the levirate. He then chooses among them.

The preferred reading: Luke records adoption

"It is most probable that Luke took the origin by adoption, as not being willing to say that Joseph was begotten by him whose son he related him to be. For more easily is a man said to be his son by whom he was adopted, than to be begotten by him from whose flesh he was not born. But Matthew saying, 'Abraham begat Isaac, and Isaac begat Jacob,' and continuing in the word 'begat,' until at last he says, but 'Jacob begat Joseph,' has sufficiently expressed that he has carried through the succession of the fathers, to that father by whom Joseph was not adopted, but begotten."

— Harmony of the Gospels 2.3, source. Augustine leans on the two Evangelists' diction: Matthew's chain of "begat" fixes Jacob as the natural father, so the differently-worded Luke ("son of Heli," with "begat" nowhere in the list) is best read as recording an adoptive one. He even allows that "begotten" could bear an adoptive sense ("not after the flesh, but by affection"), but does not need it.

Why adoption fits: Luke's priestly, upward, God-ward genealogy

"And suitably does Luke, who relates Christ's ancestry not in the opening of his Gospel, but at his baptism, follow the line of adoption, as thus more clearly pointing Him out as the Priest that should make atonement for sin. For by adoption we are made the sons of God, by believing in the Son of God. But by the descent according to the flesh which Matthew follows, we rather see that the Son of God was for us made man. Luke sufficiently shows that he called Joseph the son of Heli, because he was adopted by Heli, by his calling Adam the son of God, which he was by grace."

— Harmony of the Gospels 2.4, source. The theological fit is the clincher for Augustine: Luke's list climbs to "Adam, which was the son of God," and Adam was God's son "by grace," i.e. by a kind of adoption — so reading Heli's fatherhood as adoptive matches the whole grain of Luke's God-ward, atonement-shaped genealogy.

Later reconsideration: the levirate no longer excluded

In the Retractations, reviewing this section of the Harmony, Augustine withdrew the firmness with which he had set adoption against the levirate, conceding that Joseph could rightly be called son of Heli in the levirate sense as well. The connection is noted in the standard editorial apparatus to Africanus's letter, which cross-references "Augustine, Retract. ii. 7."1

Notes

  • A variant of the legal-father solution, not a rival to it. Adoption and the levirate are two species of one genus: both say both lists are Joseph's, and that his second "father" is legal, not biological. The wiki treats them together on the argument page for the legal-and-natural-father reading.
  • The direction question. Augustine (like Africanus) takes Matthew's line as the natural one and Luke's as the legal one. Some later writers reverse the assignment; nothing in the texts forces the direction, which is one of the softness-points of the whole harmonizing approach.
