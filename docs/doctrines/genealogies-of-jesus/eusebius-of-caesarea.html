Eusebius of Caesarea on the Two Genealogies of Jesus

Dates: c. 320 (the Church History) · Wikipedia
Summary: The transmitter. Eusebius preserves Africanus's letter by quoting it in full in Church History 1.7, endorses the levirate solution as the best available answer to a difficulty "many suppose" to be a contradiction, and draws from it the corollary the later tradition would prize: since the Law barred marriage across tribes, Joseph's Davidic descent "virtually" proves Mary's. In his own comment on Luke he reads the guarded "as was supposed" as the Evangelist voicing the common opinion, not his own.

Claims & evidence

The genealogies are widely thought to contradict; Africanus's is the account "which has come down to us"

"Matthew and Luke in their gospels have given us the genealogy of Christ differently, and many suppose that they are at variance with one another. Since as a consequence every believer, in ignorance of the truth, has been zealous to invent some explanation which shall harmonize the two passages, permit us to subjoin the account of the matter which has come down to us, and which is given by Africanus… After refuting the opinions of others as forced and deceptive, he give the account which he had received from tradition."

— Church History 1.7.1–2, source. Eusebius frames the problem candidly — the two lists look discrepant, and believers have improvised harmonies — and presents Africanus's levirate solution as the received answer. He then quotes the letter at length (the Matthan/Melchi–Estha mechanism, Jacob and Heli as uterine brothers, Joseph "his own son by nature, but by law the son of Eli").

Joseph's traced descent "virtually" shows Mary to be of the same tribe

"Thus far Africanus. And the lineage of Joseph being thus traced, Mary also is virtually shown to be of the same tribe with him, since, according to the law of Moses, inter-marriages between different tribes were not permitted. For the command is to marry one of the same family and lineage, so that the inheritance may not pass from tribe to tribe. This may suffice here."

— Church History 1.7.17, source. This is the seed of an idea that grows across the tradition: because a lawful marriage kept Mary within Joseph's tribe, proving his Davidic descent proves hers too — without Luke's list itself being Mary's. Jerome repeats the point; John of Damascus later supplies Mary an explicit line of her own.

"As was supposed" reports the common opinion; the shunning of Solomon's cursed line

"For if when Matthew affirmed Joseph to be the son of Jacob, Luke had in like manner affirmed that Joseph was the son of Eli, there would be some dispute. But seeing the case is that Matthew gives his opinion, Luke repeats the common opinion of many, not his own, saying, as was supposed… For since there were among the Jews different opinions of the genealogy of Christ… some shunned this opinion [that Christ came through Solomon and the kings] because of the many crimes related of their kings, and because Jeremiah said of Jechonias that 'a man should not rise of his seed to sit on the throne of David.'"

— on Luke 3:23, as preserved in Aquinas, Catena Aurea, source. Here Eusebius adds a second, exegetical layer: Luke's "as was supposed" signals that he is reporting how the descent was commonly reckoned; and there was a live Jewish motive to prefer Nathan's line over Solomon's — the curse of Jeremiah 22:30 on Jeconiah, that none of his seed should prosper on David's throne.

Notes

  • Why he matters here. Without Eusebius the Epistle to Aristides would be lost; his quotation is the channel through which the levirate solution reached the Latin and Greek fathers alike.
  • A caution about "tradition." Eusebius says Africanus "received [the account] from tradition," but Africanus's own words (§ V of the letter) state that his interpretation "is not supported by testimony." Modern editors (Spitta) note that Eusebius overstated the traditional pedigree of the solution — the genealogical lists were what the kinsmen preserved, not necessarily the levirate explanation of them. Whether even the lists rest on a genuine preserved family pedigree, or the whole desposynoi-archive claim is itself an unsupported tradition, is adjudicated on the desposynoi-genealogy argument page (assessment: contested).
  • The Jeconiah thread. The curse-of-Jeconiah motif (Jer. 22:30) reappears wherever the two lines are compared — some later writers use it to argue that the legal royal claim had to pass around the cursed Solomonic king through the Nathan line. It belongs to the wider case for reading Matthew's and Luke's lists as doing different theological work; see the summary's Arguments section.
