Raymond E. Brown on the Two Genealogies of Jesus

Dates: 1977 (The Birth of the Messiah) · Wikipedia
Summary: Brown, a Catholic biblical scholar and author of a commentary on the infancy narratives, holds that neither genealogy is a biographical record: both are theological constructions, and both carry historical difficulties. He judges Luke's list "no less theological" and no freer of problems than Matthew's, and denies that either tells us anything certain about Jesus's actual grandparents. He rejects the levirate-marriage harmonization that had begun with Africanus, holding it "solves so little" that it "should be abandoned."

Claims & evidence

Luke's genealogy is as theological, and as difficult, as Matthew's

"the Lucan genealogy is no less theological in purpose than Matthew's and no freer of historical difficulties."

— The Birth of the Messiah, p. 90, quoted with pagination here; the book itself is borrow-only at the Internet Archive. Brown does not hold that Luke preserved a more reliable list than Matthew: on his reading both Evangelists shape their genealogies to make Christological points, and both lists carry historical difficulties.

Neither list is an exact record of Jesus's ancestry

"the Lucan list, while in some ways more plausible than Matthew's list, scarcely constitutes an exact record of Jesus' biological ancestry."

— The Birth of the Messiah, p. 90, source. Brown allows that Luke's list is "in some ways more plausible" than Matthew's while denying that it is an exact record. Of Luke's list he writes that "(w)hat one may say with surety of Luke's list is that, in part, it is artificially arranged in numerical patterns of seven and that it contains enough inaccuracies and confusions to suggest a popular provenance (rather than an archival provenance) among Greek-speaking Jews."

The genealogies evaluate Jesus; they do not report his grandparents

"the two NT genealogies tell us how to evaluate Jesus, they tell us nothing certain about his grandparents or his great-grand-parents."

— The Birth of the Messiah, p. 94, source. This is Brown's own thesis: the genealogies are theological portraits — Matthew's structured in three fourteens around David, Luke's climbing through seventy-odd names to "the son of God" — whose function is to say who Jesus is, not to document his bloodline.

Brown on the levirate solution

"The theory of a levirate marriage solves so little and has so many difficulties that it should be abandoned as a solution in the problem of the two genealogies."

— The Birth of the Messiah, Appendix, p. 504, source. Brown rejects the levirate device of Africanus, which Ambrosiaster had also rejected in the fourth century as "neither plausible nor useful for anything."

Reception and debate

  • A reply. John F. McCarthy (Living Tradition 11, 1987) answers Brown, asking "Does a theological purpose need to conflict with an accurate historical presentation?" and reading Matthew's opening as the citation of a written genealogical record. The ancient witnesses on this timeline (Africanus, Eusebius, Jerome, John of Damascus) held that both lists trace Joseph's descent, reckoned by nature and by law. See the interpretation page on the desposynoi genealogy.
  • Shared data. Brown and the harmonizers start from the same data: two lists, both through Joseph, diverging widely. Africanus required that a harmonization be true and not merely edifying; Brown holds that the genealogies tell "nothing certain" about Jesus's grandparents.
