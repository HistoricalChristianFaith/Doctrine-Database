Raymond E. Brown on the Two Genealogies of Jesus

Dates: 1977 (The Birth of the Messiah) · Wikipedia
Summary: The modern critical closing witness. Brown, the leading Catholic scholar of the infancy narratives, holds that neither genealogy is a biographical record: both are theological constructions, and both carry historical difficulties. He judges Luke's list "no less theological" and no freer of problems than Matthew's, and denies that either tells us anything certain about Jesus's actual grandparents. He explicitly rejects the levirate-marriage harmonization that had begun with Africanus, holding it "solves so little" that it "should be abandoned."

Claims & evidence

Luke's genealogy is as theological, and as difficult, as Matthew's

"the Lucan genealogy is no less theological in purpose than Matthew's and no freer of historical difficulties."

— The Birth of the Messiah, p. 90, quoted with pagination here; the book itself is borrow-only at the Internet Archive. Brown rejects the older assumption that Luke, the "historian," preserved a more reliable list than Matthew: both Evangelists shape their genealogies to make Christological points, and both lists carry problems that no harmonization dissolves.

Neither list is an exact record of Jesus's ancestry

"the Lucan list, while in some ways more plausible than Matthew's list, scarcely constitutes an exact record of Jesus' biological ancestry."

— The Birth of the Messiah, p. 90, source. Brown grants Luke's list is in places more plausible — but "more plausible" is a long way from "exact record." The disagreement between the two chains, on his reading, is not a copyist's slip to be repaired but a sign that the genealogies were never functioning as biological registers.

The genealogies evaluate Jesus; they do not report his grandparents

"the two NT genealogies tell us how to evaluate Jesus, they tell us nothing certain about his grandparents or his great-grand-parents."

— The Birth of the Messiah, p. 94, source. This is Brown's positive thesis: the genealogies are theological portraits — Matthew's structured in three fourteens around David, Luke's climbing through seventy-odd names to "the son of God" — whose function is to say who Jesus is, not to document his bloodline.

The levirate solution should be abandoned

"The theory of a levirate marriage solves so little and has so many difficulties that it should be abandoned as a solution in the problem of the two genealogies."

— The Birth of the Messiah, Appendix, p. 504, source. Brown reaches, on critical grounds, the verdict Ambrosiaster had reached in the fourth century: the device of Africanus explains too little for its cost. A levirate marriage would account for one doubled father, but not for two lists that diverge at almost every name and run through different sons of David.

Notes

  • Not the last word, but the counterweight. Brown's judgment is the mainstream critical position, and this wiki weighs it as such — but it is a verdict, not a neutral fact. That the genealogies are theologically shaped does not by itself prove they contain no historical descent; a defender can grant the theology and still argue for a recoverable core. The rejoinders are set out on the argument page for the legal-and-natural-father reading, whose ancient defenders (Africanus, Eusebius, Jerome, John of Damascus) held exactly that both lists trace real, if differently-reckoned, descent.
  • Where the disagreement really lies. Brown and the harmonizers agree on the data — two lists, both through Joseph, diverging widely — and disagree on the demand made of them. The fathers required that a harmonization be true (Africanus's own principle); Brown holds that no proposed harmonization meets that bar and that the genealogies were never meant to be read as biology in the first place.
  • Citation note. The four quotations above are reproduced with Brown's pagination in a scholarly article (John F. McCarthy, Living Tradition 11) that argues against Brown's dismissal — a hostile-to-Brown source, so not cherry-picking in his favor. The book itself is available to borrow at the Internet Archive for readers who wish to check the pages in context.
