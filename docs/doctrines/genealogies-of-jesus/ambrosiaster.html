Ambrosiaster on the Two Genealogies of Jesus

Dates: c. 380 (the Questions on the Old and New Testaments) · Wikipedia
Summary: The tradition's early dissenter. The anonymous Roman commentator called Ambrosiaster devotes a whole Question to the two fathers of Joseph and, uniquely among the early Latins, rejects Africanus's levirate solution as "neither plausible nor useful." His own answer keeps both lists on Joseph but drops the levirate machinery: the two descents "fork" from David through Solomon and through Nathan, and Joseph and Heli are simply kinsmen of one stock — so that Jesus is "son" of Heli in the same loose sense he is called "son" of Joseph. His real quarry is the heretic Photinus, and the genealogy's ascent past Adam to God is his proof that the Son existed "before all flesh."

Claims & evidence

The question stated: does Joseph have two fathers?

"Why is it that in Matthew Joseph's father is written as Jacob, and in Luke as Heli, so that he seems to be ignorantly described as having two fathers, or at least so that who his true father is remains unknown? — There is no doubt that Jacob was Joseph's father. For the line from David through Solomon maintains its course and, proceeding straight, reaches Jacob, whose son is Joseph. Heli, however, the son of Matthat, holds the line from David through Nathan, David's son, down to the time when the Savior came."

— Questions on the Old and New Testaments, Q. 56, source. Ambrosiaster takes Matthew's "Jacob begat Joseph" as the plain, natural fatherhood, and treats Luke's Heli as belonging to a parallel Davidic branch through Nathan.

The lists "fork" from David; Joseph and Heli are brothers of one stock, not by levirate

"Therefore each Evangelist, following from David the line of different brothers, makes, as it were, a fork: so that Matthew descends from David through Solomon to Joseph, while Luke, starting from Heli… ascends through the stock of Nathan… and joins the tribe of both Heli and Joseph, showing that both are of one and the same stock; so that when he associates Joseph with Heli, he shows them not to differ in lineage, but to be brothers. And by this not only is the Savior shown to be the son of Joseph, but also of Heli. For by the very reasoning by which the Savior is called the son of Joseph, he is also the son of Heli, and of all the others who belong to the same tribe."

— Questions, Q. 56, source. On this reading "son of Heli" does not mean Joseph was begotten by Heli at all; it means Jesus is reckoned to the whole Davidic kindred, exactly as Paul says the fathers are "his" and Christ is "of them… according to the flesh" (Rom. 9:5). The doubled fatherhood dissolves into a shared ancestry.

The levirate solution rejected as "neither plausible nor useful"

"The opinion held by some, that Heli had taken the wife of Jacob, as the Law commanded… and that it therefore happened that Heli fathered Joseph for Jacob, whose wife he had taken, and that in this way the genealogies were joined, so that Joseph is rightly said to have Heli as father, is neither plausible nor useful for anything. Rather, what was said above joins by genealogy two brothers, sons of one man, and shows Christ to be the Son of God before every generation."

— Questions, Q. 56, source. This is the earliest surviving Latin voice against the levirate reading — the answer Jerome, Ambrose, and John of Damascus all adopt. Ambrosiaster thinks it explains too little and is not worth the trouble; more than a millennium later Raymond Brown reaches the same verdict on other grounds (see the Brown detail page).

The point of it all: against Photinus, the genealogy proves the Son existed before Mary

"But beyond Adam he joins Christ to God the Father, so that he who was called son of these, from Adam up to Joseph and Heli, may be understood to be before them all as the true Son of God, to the confusion of Photinus, who insists that Christ is only from Mary, and did not exist before. Of those whose son he was not, he was called son; yet of God, whose true Son he was, this was denied."

— Questions, Q. 56, source. For Ambrosiaster the genealogy is a weapon of Christology: Luke runs the list up to "the Son of God" precisely to show that the one called son of Joseph, of Heli, of Adam, is antecedently and truly the Son of God — against Photinus's denial of Christ's pre-existence.

Notes

  • The dissent that did not win. Ambrosiaster's "two brothers of one stock" reading never displaced the levirate solution in the tradition, but it anticipates the modern instinct that the levirate device is ad hoc. Its weakness is that a "fork" from David through two sons still leaves the two lists agreeing at only two names between David and Joseph — it explains the shared tribe, not the near-total divergence of the two chains.
  • A looser sense of "son." His key move — that "son of Heli" is no stronger than "son of Adam" or "son of God" in the same sentence — is grammatically shrewd, but it makes Luke's genealogy a chain of reckoned rather than begotten links, which sits awkwardly with a list evidently meant to record descent.
