The Assumption / Dormition of Mary

The belief traced here is that Mary, at the end of her earthly life, was taken up body and soul into heaven — her flesh not left to the corruption of the grave but glorified in anticipation of the general resurrection. In the Eastern churches it is the Dormition (“falling asleep,” Koimēsis), a name that presupposes she truly died; in the Latin West it came to be called the Assumption, which the Catholic Church defined as dogma in 1950. The two names mark the same event from two angles, and they are traced here as one doctrine.

The New Testament does not narrate how Mary’s life ended, and no extant writer before the late fourth century records a death, a tomb, or a translation. The earliest extant writer to raise the question, Epiphanius of Salamis (c. 377), says that no one knows her end and that Scripture “kept silence.” The bodily assumption is first narrated in the apocryphal Transitus Mariae (“Passing of Mary”) texts, which survive in fifth- and sixth-century manuscripts; one of the two oldest families, the Six Books, is dated to the fourth century by Shoemaker and Bauckham and after Chalcedon by Jugie and Mimouni. Shoemaker finds in the Six Books an advanced Marian piety in a broadly orthodox, monotheist setting, and places the oldest layer of the other family, the Palm-of-the-Tree-of-Life tradition, in a gnostic milieu. The belief is preached by Gregory of Tours in the Latin West (c. 590) and by the Greek homilists for the feast in the East. The timeline is laid out by earliest extant attestation.

One distinction runs through the page. The Dormition presupposes a real death and so a soul that survives it — the same middle state between death and resurrection traced on the intermediate-state timeline — while the Assumption is the claim that Mary does not wait for the general resurrection, her body raised and glorified ahead of everyone else’s. The 1950 definition does not state whether she died first; its phrase is “having completed the course of her earthly life.”

Timeline

Epiphanius of Salamis (c. 377)

Epiphanius of Salamis, in the long treatise against the Antidicomarianites that became Panarion 78 — the same anti-heretical work in which he defends Mary’s perpetual virginity — is the earliest extant writer to raise the question of Mary’s end, and he answers it with open agnosticism. “Let them search the Scriptures,” he challenges his opponents; “they will not find Mary’s death; they will not find whether she died or did not die.”1 He lays out three possibilities — that she died and was buried, that she was martyred, or that she remained alive — and settles on none: “I do not dare to speak, but I keep my own thoughts and I practice silence.”2 Philip Schaff (Protestant church historian) sets his indecision within “the entire silence of the apostles and the primitive church teachers respecting the departure of Mary,”3 and William Webster (Protestant) reads his words as evidence that no tradition about Mary’s end yet existed; Frederick Holweck and Tim Staples (Catholic) read them as consistent with one handed on unwritten (see the silence of the first centuries), and Staples reads his comparison of Mary with Elijah as an early form of the view that she did not die (see whether Mary died).

The Six Books Dormition apocryphon (c. 400; date disputed)

The Six Books apocryphon — the Syriac “Departure of my Lady Mary from this World,” one of the two oldest Dormition narrative families — is on its earlier datings the earliest extant text to tell the bodily assumption as a full story. Mary truly dies; then, at the Lord’s command, “the twelve apostles bore [her body] as it went to the Paradise of Eden,” where Christ calls “Mary, rise,” and “straightway she was restored to life” and is crowned in glory.4 Its surviving manuscripts are fifth- and sixth-century, and the date of composition is disputed: Shoemaker locates “a likely origin by the second half of the fourth century if not even earlier,” and Bauckham dates its concluding apocalyptic tour “from the fourth century at the latest, but perhaps considerably earlier”, while Jugie and Mimouni read it as post-Chalcedon.5 On the early datings the narrative circulated in Epiphanius’s own generation; on the late datings, after 451.

Pseudo-Melito, Transitus Mariae (the Latin Passing of Mary, c. 500)

The Transitus attributed to Melito of Sardis — in fact pseudonymous, a Latin narrative of the late fifth or sixth century — is the most widely copied Western telling of the bodily assumption and the fullest Latin text of the narrative family that Gregory of Tours’s account epitomizes. It narrates a real death and then a bodily translation: at the Lord’s word “Mary rose from the tomb,” and the angels bore her “into the paradise of God.”6 What sets it apart is its prologue, which presents the work as a correction of an earlier Transitus it calls heretical — the writing of one “Leucius,” who “vitiated, by his impious writing, the assumption of the blessed ever-virgin Mary … to such a degree that it would be impious not only to read it in the church of God, but even to hear it.”7 Like John of Thessalonica’s Greek homily, it presents itself as a purged version of an earlier, corrupted account; whether such texts are the source of the belief or witnesses to an older tradition is disputed (see the Transitus narratives).

Gregory of Tours (c. 590)

Gregory of Tours gives the earliest extant narrative affirmation in the Latin West. Retelling, in his Glory of the Martyrs, the account that had reached him through the Transitus tradition, he writes that when Mary’s earthly course was finished and the apostles had gathered, “the Lord … commanded the body of Mary be taken in a cloud into paradise; where now, rejoined to the soul, Mary dwells with the chosen ones.”8 Whether those narratives are the belief’s source or witnesses to an older tradition is disputed (see the Transitus narratives).

Pseudo-Evodius of Rome — the Coptic Falling Asleep of Mary (c. 590)

Pseudo-Evodius of Rome preserves the Egyptian branch of the tradition, and its most distinctive feature is a two-stage reckoning found nowhere in the Palestinian or Latin tellings. In this Coptic homily — pseudonymously fathered on Evodius, “the archbishop of the great city Rome … the second after Peter” — Mary dies on the 21st of Tobe (mid-January), her “soul leaped from her body into the bosom of her beloved Son”; then, after a deliberate seven-month interval, her body is raised on the 16th of Mesore (mid-August): “a great chariot of light … the holy Virgin Mary sitting upon it … And the Lord … raised the body of His virgin mother, and put her soul into her body again … raised by Me from the dead.”9 The homily is a pseudonymous Coptic composition generally placed in the later sixth century, its earliest form dated by Shoemaker to before 567.10 It is a regionally distinct witness: it shows the bodily assumption preached in sixth-century Egypt, and it supplies the primary text behind the next entry’s calendrical split — Egypt kept a January Dormition and an August Assumption as two moments of one story.

The feast fixed for 15 August — Emperor Maurice (582–602)

Emperor Maurice marks the point at which the commemoration became an empire-wide institution rather than a local Palestinian one. Later tradition — the Liber Pontificalis and the fourteenth-century historian Nikephoros Kallistos — credits him with fixing the feast of the Dormition on 15 August throughout the Greek Empire, ending a patchwork in which “some kept this feast in January, with the monks of Egypt; others in August, with those of Palestine.”11 The feast itself was older than the decree: a “Memory of Mary” is already kept on 15 August in the fifth-century Armenian Lectionary of Jerusalem.12 As reported, the decree standardized an existing observance; the attribution to Maurice rests on sources written centuries after his reign.

Theoteknos of Livias (c. 600)

Theoteknos of Livias, bishop of a see east of the Jordan, preached one of the two oldest surviving Greek homilies for the feast — and the earliest extant to call it an Assumption (Análēpsis, a “taking up”) rather than a Dormition. He affirms the bodily claim: “even though the God-bearing body of that holy one did taste death, it was not corrupted; for it was kept incorrupt and free of decay, and it was lifted up to heaven with her pure and spotless soul.”13

John of Thessalonica (c. 615)

John of Thessalonica, introducing the feast to a church that had not been keeping it, describes the state of its sources. The narratives of Mary’s end, he explains, had been so corrupted by heretics “adding words of their own” that “our ancestors distanced themselves from these accounts as not in accord with the catholic Church”; he preaches only after purging the apocrypha, “taking no account of fabricated stories.”14

Modestus of Jerusalem (c. 630)

Modestus of Jerusalem, patriarch of Jerusalem, is the name attached to what is often called the oldest surviving Greek homily for the feast. It affirms both a real death and an incorrupt, heaven-borne body: Mary “did not experience corruption in that life-sheltering body of hers, even in the tomb,” being “preserved [from it] by Christ the Savior,” who “called her to himself and invested her with an incorruptibility touching all her corporeal frame.”15 The attribution is disputed: Martin Jugie argued the homily is pseudepigraphic, since a reference to Christ’s two wills, defined only in 680–681, places the surviving text later in the seventh century, and its author is often called “pseudo-Modestus.”16

Germanus of Constantinople (c. 720)

Germanus of Constantinople, patriarch and Dormition homilist, preaches Mary’s genuine death together with her body’s incorruption: in his first homily her “flesh did not undergo the corruption of the tomb,” and it was “impossible that you, the vessel which bore God, should be dissolved and decomposed into the dust of death.”17 His second homily gives a different account: her bones had “become hard and shrivelled in decay” in the earth before being re-clothed with incorruptible flesh — a two-stage account of decay overtaken by restoration, unlike the incorrupt tomb of Andrew of Crete and John of Damascus.17 Pius XII quoted him by name in the 1950 constitution.

Andrew of Crete (c. 725)

Andrew of Crete, in three homilies for the feast, develops the bodily assumption at length. He holds Mary’s death to be real — a Koimēsis, a sleep unto resurrection — yet her tomb “allow[ed] no corruption,” and “today, Jesus transports the Queen of the human race outside of her earthly dwelling.”18 He argues from the empty tomb, in which only the grave-clothes remained.

John of Damascus (c. 730)

John of Damascus, in three homilies preached at Gethsemane, gives the Dormition its classic Eastern statement. He affirms the bodily glory outright — “Why do you seek in the tomb one who has been assumed to the heavenly courts?” — argues from fittingness, and sets the procession to the tomb in scriptural figures: Mary is “the Ark of God,” borne as Solomon’s elders bore the ark “to rest in the temple of the Lord.”19 An excerpt from the Euthymiac History, transmitted with his second homily (and usually regarded as a later interpolation into it), traces the account of the empty tomb to Juvenal of Jerusalem’s answer, at the Council of Chalcedon (451), when the Emperor Marcian and Empress Pulcheria asked for Mary’s relics: her tomb, opened after three days at the request of a late-arriving apostle, had been found empty, only the grave-clothes remaining.20 Whether that tradition reaches back to the apostles or into the fifth- and sixth-century apocrypha is disputed; see the tradition John of Damascus invokes.

Cosmas Vestitor (c. 800)

Cosmas Vestitor, a lay Byzantine homilist of the later eighth or early ninth century, wrote four homilies for the feast that consolidate the Byzantine narrative — the apostles gathered, Mary’s death and burial at Gethsemane, the tomb opened after three days and found empty, only the grave-clothes remaining — reworking Germanus of Constantinople and drawing openly on the older apocryphal Transitus material.21 The Greek is lost and the homilies survive only in an early Latin translation, and in that form they carried the consolidated Byzantine narrative into the West, feeding the medieval Latin reception of the Dormition down to the Golden Legend.22

Paschasius Radbertus (c. 860)

Paschasius Radbertus, abbot of Corbie, wrote the West’s most influential Assumption sermon — the letter Cogitis me, circulated for centuries under the name of Jerome — and it is marked by caution. He warns his readers against “that apocryphal work on the passing of the virgin,” the same Transitus narratives the Gelasian Decree had listed among the books “not to be received,”23 and declines to settle the bodily question: “many of our people doubt whether she was assumed with the body or left the body behind,” and “it is more fitting to hold an opinion with a pious desire than to define unadvisedly what is not known without danger.”24

Pope Pius XII — Munificentissimus Deus (1950)

Pope Pius XII defined the Assumption for the Catholic Church, declaring it “a divinely revealed dogma: that the Immaculate Mother of God, the ever Virgin Mary, having completed the course of her earthly life, was assumed body and soul into heavenly glory.”25 The phrase “having completed the course of her earthly life” does not state whether Mary died before she was assumed. John Paul II later explained that “Pius XII did not intend to deny the fact of her death, but merely did not judge it opportune to affirm solemnly the death of the Mother of God as a truth to be accepted by all believers”; Stephen Shoemaker writes that the definition left the question open “at the urging of Jugie in particular,” whom he describes as “a modern champion of Mary’s immortality.”26 Kallistos Ware (Orthodox, 1984) reports that “while most Orthodox are firmly convinced of the reality of the bodily Assumption, they do not consider that it is desirable or even possible to define it as a dogma.”27
