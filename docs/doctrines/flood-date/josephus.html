Josephus on the Dating of the Flood

Dates: c. 37–100 (the Antiquities of the Jews composed c. AD 93) · Wikipedia
Summary: Retelling the primeval history in Greek for a Roman readership, Josephus closes the antediluvian genealogy with an explicit interval from Adam to the Flood. The transmitted figure — "two thousand six hundred and fifty-six years" (Antiquities 1.82) — is a famous textual crux, and Josephus' own summed patriarch-ages point to 2,256, roughly six centuries longer than the Masoretic 1,656 and close to the Septuagint's long chronology. That makes him an early, first-century witness that the longer Genesis 5–11 numbers were already in circulation among Jews — yet he sides with the Hebrew and Samaritan against the Greek in omitting the second Cainan of Genesis 11, and his figures are internally inconsistent. His bearing on the dating question is therefore double-edged: a datable early witness to the long reckoning whose own numbers are far too unstable to fix a date.

Claims & evidence

The Flood "two thousand six hundred and fifty-six years from Adam" — a figure in crux

…this flood began on the twenty-seventh [seventeenth] day of the forementioned month: and this was two thousand six hundred and fifty six [one thousand five hundred and fifty six years] from Adam the first man…

— Antiquities of the Jews, I.iii.3 (§82), Whiston translation, source (the bracketed figures are Whiston's note of the manuscript variants). The transmitted 2656 is textually unstable — manuscripts and editors give 2262, 1656, and 1056 as well — and it should not be laundered as a settled date. Josephus' own list of begetting-ages, when summed, comes to 2,256, which is why the transmitted 2656 has long been read as a corruption. Isaac Vossius, in his De vera aetate mundi (1659), emended §82 to 2,256 precisely to bring Josephus into line with the Septuagint against the received text — an emendation with no support in the printed Greek editions, but arithmetically what Josephus' figures require.

The patriarch-ages follow the Septuagint's longer numbers

Whatever total is read, Josephus' individual antediluvian begetting-ages carry the same centenary additions that distinguish the Septuagint's long chronology from the Hebrew: his interval from Adam to the Flood runs roughly six hundred years longer than the Masoretic 1,656 and lands within a few years of the Greek total. He is thus, in effect, an early and independent-looking witness that the longer Genesis 5 figures were already current among first-century Jews — a datum in the case that the received Hebrew was later shortened, weighed on the transmission-of-the-text page. Felix Schlichter traces both the arithmetic (the ages summing to 2,256) and the early-modern controversy it fuelled — Vossius' campaign to prove Josephus followed the Septuagint throughout — in his study of Josephus in early modern biblical chronology.

But he omits the second Cainan of Genesis 11

…Sala was the son of Arphaxad: and his son was Heber…

— Antiquities of the Jews, I.vi.4, Whiston translation, source. Josephus runs the post-Flood line straight from Arphaxad to Sala (Salah), with no Cainan between them. This is the Hebrew and Samaritan sequence; the Septuagint's Genesis 11 inserts a second Cainan there, and Luke 3:36 repeats it. So Josephus is no clean copy of the Greek: he leans to its longer numbers but keeps the shorter Hebrew list — one of the several places his chronology proves internally mixed rather than a faithful witness to any single tradition.

Notes

  • Why he cannot fix a date. Josephus' figures are, in Whiston's phrase, "a law unto themselves": they conflict with the Septuagint, with the Masoretic Hebrew, and even with their own transmitted sum (2656 vs. the 2256 the ages require). His value on the dating question is as evidence that the long reckoning was early, not as a source that settles the interval — possibility that the longer numbers are original is not proof of a date. The rival reckonings themselves are weighed on the summary's argument pages: the Masoretic short chronology, the Septuagint long chronology, and the Samaritan Pentateuch.
  • A tendentious historian. Josephus wrote under Flavian patronage and was capable of bending scripture to his patrons (most famously referring the "ambiguous oracle" of a world-ruler to Vespasian). That caution tempers his rhetorical flourishes but does not touch a plain chronological datum like the sum of the patriarchs' ages; the instability of his numbers here is a transmission problem, not a tendentious one.
  • Josephus appears on this wiki also as a witness on the Nephilim, First Language, Old Testament Canon, Origin of the Septuagint, and Pillars of Seth timelines — the first four, with this one, all drawn from his primeval history in Antiquities Book I.
