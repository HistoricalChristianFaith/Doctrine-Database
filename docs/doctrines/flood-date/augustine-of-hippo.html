Augustine of Hippo on the Hebrew-vs-Septuagint Chronology of the Flood

Dates: c. 420 (City of God, Bk. XV, chs. 10–14) · Wikipedia
Summary: Augustine is the earliest surviving Latin voice to weigh, explicitly and at length, the very text-critical dispute this timeline turns on: the antediluvian genealogies of Genesis 5 differ by a uniform hundred years between the Hebrew manuscripts and the Septuagint (his own Latin's parent text), and the two reckonings cannot both be right. What makes him a striking witness is that Augustine, elsewhere the great champion of the Septuagint's inspired authority, comes down on the Hebrew side of the numbers. Forced by the Methuselah problem — on the Greek figures the patriarch would outlive the Flood he never boarded — he judges the Hebrew reading true, traces the Greek's extra centuries to a single copyist's deliberate scheme (not to Jewish tampering and not to the Seventy), and lays down the rule that where the versions diverge one should prefer "that language out of which the translation was made." He is thus an early proponent of the short-chronology side, and an ancient anticipation of the modern argument that the longer Greek numbers are a secondary inflation — the mirror image of Eusebius' charge that the Hebrew was later deflated.

Claims & evidence

The dispute stated: Hebrew and Greek diverge by a uniform hundred years

Augustine sets out the discrepancy generation by generation. Adam, in his Latin (Septuagint) copies, begets Seth at 230 and lives 700 years after; the Hebrew gives 130 and 800 — and so down the line, the pre-son figure "always made shorter by 100 years in the Hebrew" and the post-son figure longer by the same, so that each patriarch's total agrees across the two texts even where the internal split does not.1 He notes the exceptions precisely — the sixth generation (Jared) where the texts do not differ, the eighth (Methuselah) where the Greek runs 100 more before the son rather than less, and the ninth (Lamech) where the totals themselves part by twenty-four years. This is the same clean, directional offset the modern chronology debate turns on; Augustine describes it with more care than most of his successors, and treats it as a problem demanding a verdict, not a curiosity to be waved away.

The Methuselah crux forces a choice — and Augustine takes the Hebrew

Certain it is that Methuselah did not survive the flood, but died in the very year it occurred, if the numbers given in the Hebrew manuscripts are true.

— The City of God, Book XV, ch. 11, trans. Marcus Dods (NPNF1 2), newadvent.org / ccel.org.2 On the Septuagint figures Augustine's Latin copies carried, Methuselah begets at 167 and lives 802 years more, so that his 969-year life runs fourteen years past the Flood — an impossibility, since only the eight souls in the ark survived and he was not among them. Augustine records the strained rescue some proposed (that Methuselah spent those years "translated" with his father Enoch, off the earth) but does not take it; he reports that its motive was to avoid "cast[ing] a slight on the trustworthiness of versions which the Church has received into a position of high authority," and that its holders "believe that the Jewish manuscripts rather than our own are in error." His own conclusion is the opposite: the arithmetic resolves cleanly only on the Hebrew numbers, so the Hebrew is where the truth lies. It is a rare case of the great defender of the Septuagint conceding a point to the Hebrew text against his own version.

The Greek's extra centuries are a copyist's design, not Jewish malice

…that diversity of numbers which distinguishes the Hebrew from the Greek and Latin copies of Scripture, and which consists of a uniform addition and deduction of 100 years in each lifetime for several consecutive generations, is to be attributed neither to the malice of the Jews nor to men so diligent and prudent as the seventy translators, but to the error of the copyist who was first allowed to transcribe the manuscript from the library of the above-mentioned king.

— The City of God, Book XV, ch. 13, trans. Marcus Dods (NPNF1 2), newadvent.org / ccel.org.3 Augustine refuses both easy blame-narratives. The Jews, "scattered far and wide," could not have "unanimously conspired to forge this lie" across so many dispersed manuscripts; nor would the seventy translators, shut up together at Ptolemy's behest, have "by common consent inserted these errors." The regularity of the offset — a hundred years added before each son's birth and deducted after, "for several consecutive generations" — "savors not of accident, but of design," so it must trace to one copyist working from the Alexandrian library copy. Augustine even reconstructs the copyist's motive: he inflated each begetting-age by a century to insinuate that the antediluvians' "excessive" lifespans were really short years (so that "100 of their years equalled but ten of ours"), adjusting the figure precisely where a father would otherwise beget "too young." That is, in substance, the modern inflation thesis — the Greek numbers padded, the Hebrew original — argued sixteen centuries before its creationist revival, and reached by the very Father most invested in the Septuagint's authority.

Prefer the Hebrew: the language from which the translation was made

…I would have no manner of doubt that when any diversity is found in the books, since both cannot be true to fact, we do well to believe in preference that language out of which the translation was made into another by translators. For there are three Greek manuscripts, one Latin, and one Syriac, which agree with one another, and in all of these Methuselah is said to have died six years before the deluge.

— The City of God, Book XV, ch. 13, trans. Marcus Dods (NPNF1 2), newadvent.org / ccel.org.3 This is Augustine's governing rule for the whole dispute, and it points the same way as his Methuselah verdict: the Hebrew, as the source-language of the translation, is the more trustworthy where the copies clash. The closing detail is telling — he already knew manuscripts (three Greek, one Latin, one Syriac) carrying the corrected reading on which Methuselah dies six years before the Flood, the very reading modern defenders of the Greek text reconstruct as original (Methuselah begetting at 187, not the corrupt 167). So on both the specific crux and the general principle, Augustine lands on the side of the shorter Hebrew figures.

Notes

  • An honest complication for a simple "Augustine = Septuagint" story. Augustine is famous for defending the Septuagint's inspired authority — most sharply in City of God XVIII.42–43, where he treats the Seventy as prophetically guided and warns against correcting them by the Hebrew. But on the Genesis 5 chronology he makes an explicit exception: the numbers are a place where "both cannot be true to fact," and there he prefers the Hebrew. The two positions are not a contradiction so much as a scope-limit — he holds the Greek generally inspired yet concedes that a copyist corrupted its primeval figures — but it means he cannot be filed simply on the long-chronology side that the Septuagint tradition otherwise anchors.
  • Bearing on the argument pages. Augustine's copyist-inflation account is an ancient anticipation of the modern case for the Masoretic short chronology (the schematic hundred-year additions read as a secondary padding of the Greek), and he stands as a counter-witness to Eusebius' mirror-image charge that it was the Hebrew that was later shortened. His knowledge of the corrected six-years-before-the-Flood manuscripts is also cited on the Septuagint long chronology page for the resolution of the Methuselah variant. He does not, however, convert his verdict into a Flood date: like the reckonings themselves, his witness bears on which text is prior, not on whether either sum fixes the event — the distinction this timeline keeps throughout.
  • Augustine appears across this wiki on many timelines. On the primeval history of Genesis he is also the anchor witness for the historicity of Noah's Flood (reading Genesis 6–9 as history and allegory together, and defending the antediluvian 900-year lives as ordinary solar years in this same Book XV), the Nephilim (the Sethite reading of the sons of God), and the first language.
  • The chapter 10, 11, and 13 quotations follow the public-domain NPNF edition of The City of God (newadvent / ccel).
