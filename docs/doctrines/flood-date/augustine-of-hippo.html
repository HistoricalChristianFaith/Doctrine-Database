Augustine of Hippo on the Hebrew-vs-Septuagint Chronology of the Flood

Dates: c. 420 (City of God, Bk. XV, chs. 10–14) · Wikipedia
Summary: Augustine takes up, explicitly and at length, the text-critical dispute over the antediluvian figures: the genealogies of Genesis 5 differ by a uniform hundred years between the Hebrew manuscripts and the Septuagint (his own Latin's parent text), and the two reckonings cannot both be right. Augustine, who elsewhere defends the Septuagint's inspired authority, here sides with the Hebrew numbers. Taking up the Methuselah problem — on the Greek figures the patriarch would outlive the Flood he never boarded — he judges the Hebrew reading true, traces the Greek's extra centuries to a single copyist's deliberate scheme (not to Jewish tampering and not to the Seventy), and lays down the rule that where the versions diverge one should prefer "that language out of which the translation was made." Eusebius, a century earlier, had held the reverse: that the Hebrew figures had been shortened.

Claims & evidence

The dispute stated: Hebrew and Greek diverge by a uniform hundred years

Augustine sets out the discrepancy generation by generation. Adam, in his Latin (Septuagint) copies, begets Seth at 230 and lives 700 years after; the Hebrew gives 130 and 800 — and so down the line, the pre-son figure "always made shorter by 100 years in the Hebrew" and the post-son figure longer by the same, so that each patriarch's total agrees across the two texts even where the internal split does not.1 He notes the exceptions precisely — the sixth generation (Jared) where the texts do not differ, the eighth (Methuselah) where the Greek runs 100 more before the son rather than less, and the ninth (Lamech) where the totals themselves part by twenty-four years. He treats the discrepancy as requiring a choice between the texts.

The Methuselah crux, and Augustine's choice of the Hebrew

Certain it is that Methuselah did not survive the flood, but died in the very year it occurred, if the numbers given in the Hebrew manuscripts are true.

— The City of God, Book XV, ch. 11, trans. Marcus Dods (NPNF1 2), newadvent.org / ccel.org.2 On the Septuagint figures Augustine's Latin copies carried, Methuselah begets at 167 and lives 802 years more, so that his 969-year life runs fourteen years past the Flood, which Augustine treats as impossible, since only the eight souls in the ark survived and he was not among them. Augustine records the harmonization some proposed (that Methuselah spent those years "translated" with his father Enoch, off the earth) but does not take it; he reports that its motive was to avoid "cast[ing] a slight on the trustworthiness of versions which the Church has received into a position of high authority," and that its holders "believe that the Jewish manuscripts rather than our own are in error." His own conclusion is the opposite: on the Hebrew numbers Methuselah dies in the year of the Flood, and he takes the Hebrew reading as true.

Augustine on the source of the Greek's extra centuries

…that diversity of numbers which distinguishes the Hebrew from the Greek and Latin copies of Scripture, and which consists of a uniform addition and deduction of 100 years in each lifetime for several consecutive generations, is to be attributed neither to the malice of the Jews nor to men so diligent and prudent as the seventy translators, but to the error of the copyist who was first allowed to transcribe the manuscript from the library of the above-mentioned king.

— The City of God, Book XV, ch. 13, trans. Marcus Dods (NPNF1 2), newadvent.org / ccel.org.3 Augustine rejects both Jewish malice and the Seventy as the source. The Jews, "scattered far and wide," could not have "unanimously conspired to forge this lie" across so many dispersed manuscripts; nor would the seventy translators, shut up together at Ptolemy's behest, have "by common consent inserted these errors." The regularity of the offset — a hundred years added before each son's birth and deducted after, "for several consecutive generations" — "savors not of accident, but of design," so it must trace to one copyist working from the Alexandrian library copy. Augustine also reconstructs the copyist's motive: he inflated each begetting-age by a century to insinuate that the antediluvians' "excessive" lifespans were really short years (so that "100 of their years equalled but ten of ours"), adjusting the figure where a father would otherwise beget "too young."

Augustine's rule: prefer the language from which the translation was made

…I would have no manner of doubt that when any diversity is found in the books, since both cannot be true to fact, we do well to believe in preference that language out of which the translation was made into another by translators. For there are three Greek manuscripts, one Latin, and one Syriac, which agree with one another, and in all of these Methuselah is said to have died six years before the deluge.

— The City of God, Book XV, ch. 13, trans. Marcus Dods (NPNF1 2), newadvent.org / ccel.org.3 Augustine applies the rule to the whole dispute: the Hebrew, as the source-language of the translation, is to be preferred where the copies clash. He also reports manuscripts (three Greek, one Latin, one Syriac) in which Methuselah dies six years before the Flood; Henry B. Smith Jr., who defends the Septuagint chronology, reconstructs Methuselah's begetting-age in the Greek as 187 rather than 167, the figure on which he dies six years before the Flood.

Reception and debate

  • Augustine and the Septuagint's authority. Augustine defends the Septuagint's inspired authority — most sharply in City of God XVIII.42–43, where he treats the Seventy as prophetically guided and warns against correcting them by the Hebrew. But on the Genesis 5 chronology he makes an explicit exception: the numbers are a place where "both cannot be true to fact," and there he prefers the Hebrew. He holds the Greek generally inspired while assigning its primeval figures to a copyist.
  • Augustine and Eusebius. Augustine assigns the hundred-year additions to a copyist of the Greek; Eusebius had charged that it was the Hebrew that was later shortened. Augustine does not convert his reckoning into a calendar date for the Flood.
  • Augustine appears on many timelines. On the primeval history of Genesis he is also a witness for the historicity of Noah's Flood (reading Genesis 6–9 as history and allegory together, and defending the antediluvian 900-year lives as ordinary solar years in this same Book XV), the Nephilim (the Sethite reading of the sons of God), and the first language.
  • The chapter 10, 11, and 13 quotations follow the public-domain NPNF edition of The City of God (newadvent / ccel).
