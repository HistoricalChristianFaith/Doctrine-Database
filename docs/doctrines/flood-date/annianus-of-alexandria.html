The Alexandrian World-Era Chronographers on the Dating of the Flood

Dates: the Alexandrian monks Panodorus (fl. c. 400) and Annianus (fl. c. 400–412, under Patriarch Theophilus of Alexandria) · Wikipedia (Annianus), Wikipedia (Panodorus)
Summary: These two Alexandrian monks built the Alexandrian world era on the Septuagint figures of Genesis 5–11 and are the concrete institutional carriers of the long chronology between Eusebius (c. 320) and Ussher (1650). Reckoning on the Greek numbers, they placed the Flood at Anno Mundi 2242 — about 3250 BC — and fixed Creation at 25 March 5492 BC (Annianus) / c. 5493 BC (Panodorus). Their scheme's signature is the mystical 5500: exactly 5,500 years from Creation to the Incarnation, all turning on 25 March. That architecture is why they matter to the dating question — it is the clearest evidence that the long figures were carried by a chronology committed in advance to seating Christ at Anno Mundi 5500, the very motive the Septuagint-long-chronology page weighs against the Greek numbers.

Claims & evidence

The Flood at Anno Mundi 2242 — about 3250 BC on the Septuagint reckoning

Both chronographers reckoned the antediluvian interval on the Septuagint's longer begetting-ages, arriving at 2,242 years from Adam to the Flood — the total George Syncellus, preserving them, judged the more accurate on Eusebius' authority (Julius Africanus had reckoned 2,262).1 Set against Annianus' Creation of 25 March 5492 BC, Anno Mundi 2242 falls near 3250 BC; against Panodorus' c. 5493 BC, a year earlier. This is a genuine Flood date downstream of a genuine Creation date, computed — not the sexagesimal artifice of Berossus' 432,000 years, but a reckoning of the biblical genealogies in their Greek form, roughly a millennium before Ussher fixed the rival Hebrew figure at 2348 BC.

Creation fixed to seat the Incarnation at Anno Mundi 5500

The organizing principle of the Alexandrian era is not the Flood but the Incarnation. Annianus built his world era so that the years from Creation to Christ came to exactly 5,500, with Creation, the Annunciation, and the Resurrection all falling on 25 March; his stated aim — the aim Syncellus inherited and defended — was to confirm "the incarnation of Christ at the completion of the 5500th year from the creation of the universe."2 The scheme was not invented from nothing: Hippolytus of Rome had already placed Christ's birth at Anno Mundi 5500, reading the "five and a half days" and the dimensions of the ark typologically. What Annianus added was a rigorous calendar built to that total. The Creation date, in other words, was chosen so that the sum would land on 5,500 — the theological number is load-bearing, and the year of Creation is set to reach it.

Annianus against Panodorus: biblical numbers over secular king-lists

The two monks were rivals on method. Panodorus tried to harmonize the sacred chronology with the vast king-lists of Berossus and Manetho, straining the Egyptian and Babylonian dynastic years to fit within the biblical frame; Annianus criticized him precisely for "relying too much on secular sources rather than biblical sources for his dates" and rebuilt the era on the scriptural numbers alone.3 The quarrel matters here for two reasons. It shows the long chronology being defended, at its origin, as the biblical reckoning against pagan competitors — not padded from them. And it shows how tightly the whole system was engineered: Annianus' adjustments to Panodorus were driven by the need to make the numbers seat the Incarnation at Anno Mundi 5500, which tells us the total was the fixed point and the intervals were fitted to it.

The same scheme carried forward: the Byzantine world era and the Chronicon Paschale

The Alexandrian reckoning did not stay in Alexandria. Its heirs re-epoched the same Septuagint-based long chronology: the Chronicon Paschale (c. 630) set Creation at 21 March 5507 BC, and the Byzantine world era, standard across the Eastern empire by the mid-seventh century, fixed it at 1 September 5509 BC. Both keep the Flood at Anno Mundi 2242 — near 3265 BC and 3267 BC respectively.4 These are the same long-chronology family, differing only in where they anchor Anno Mundi 1 on the calendar; the 16-year gap between the Alexandrian 5492/5493 and the Byzantine 5509 turns on small manuscript and computational choices, not on a different reading of Genesis. So the institutional carrier of the LXX Flood date runs unbroken from Panodorus and Annianus (c. 400) through Byzantium to the eve of the modern debate.

Notes

  • Where they diverge from the modern LXX reconstruction, and why. The leading modern defender of the Septuagint chronology, Henry B. Smith Jr. of Associates for Biblical Research, restores Creation at c. 5554 BC and the Flood at c. 3298 BC — roughly 60 years earlier at Creation and 48 at the Flood than Annianus' figures. The gap is instructive. Smith reaches his numbers by reconstructing the "original" Greek readings from divergent manuscripts (e.g. Methuselah's begetting-age at 187 rather than the transmitted 167), with no external total to hit; the ancient Alexandrians used the transmitted Greek sum (Adam–Flood 2,242) and set Creation where it had to sit for the Creation-to-Incarnation span to come to exactly 5,500. Two reckonings of the "same" Septuagint chronology thus land ~50 years apart, one driven by text-criticism and one by a theological total — which is itself the point the Septuagint long chronology page presses: the long figures travel inside a system committed in advance to Anno Mundi 5500, so their originality cannot be taken as a settled premise.
  • Possibility is not proof of a date. That the Alexandrian chronographers computed a Flood at c. 3250 BC does not fix the event there any more than Ussher's 2348 BC fixes it on the Hebrew side. Their value on the dating question is as datable witnesses — the carriers who kept the long reckoning alive and institutional for over a millennium — and as evidence that the long chronology's numbers were embedded in a theological architecture. The rival reckonings themselves are weighed on the summary's argument pages: the Masoretic short chronology, the Septuagint long chronology, and the Samaritan Pentateuch.
  • Their work survives almost entirely through George Syncellus' Ecloga Chronographica (c. 808–810), who copied large blocks of Annianus and Panodorus verbatim; no complete text of either monk is extant. Syncellus also preserves, through Panodorus, the fragments of Berossus and Manetho that appear on this wiki's Flood-dating timeline as the datum a biblical reckoning must out-explain.
