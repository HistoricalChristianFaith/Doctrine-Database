The Alexandrian World-Era Chronographers on the Dating of the Flood

Dates: the Alexandrian monks Panodorus (fl. c. 400) and Annianus (fl. c. 400–412, under Patriarch Theophilus of Alexandria) · Wikipedia (Annianus), Wikipedia (Panodorus)
Summary: These two Alexandrian monks built the Alexandrian world era on the Septuagint figures of Genesis 5–11. Reckoning on the Greek numbers, they placed the Flood at Anno Mundi 2242 — about 3250 BC — and fixed Creation at 25 March 5492 BC (Annianus) / c. 5493 BC (Panodorus). Their scheme counts exactly 5,500 years from Creation to the Incarnation, all turning on 25 March. George Syncellus, through whom their work survives, states the aim as confirming the Incarnation at the completion of the 5500th year.

Claims & evidence

The Flood at Anno Mundi 2242 — about 3250 BC on the Septuagint reckoning

Both chronographers reckoned the antediluvian interval on the Septuagint's longer begetting-ages, arriving at 2,242 years from Adam to the Flood — the total George Syncellus, preserving them, judged the more accurate on Eusebius' authority (Julius Africanus had reckoned 2,262).1 Set against Annianus' Creation of 25 March 5492 BC, Anno Mundi 2242 falls near 3250 BC; against Panodorus' c. 5493 BC, a year earlier. It is a Flood date computed from a Creation date, reckoned on the biblical genealogies in their Greek form, about nine centuries earlier than the 2348 BC Ussher later reckoned on the Hebrew figures.

Creation fixed to seat the Incarnation at Anno Mundi 5500

The organizing principle of the Alexandrian era is not the Flood but the Incarnation. Annianus built his world era so that the years from Creation to Christ came to exactly 5,500, with Creation, the Annunciation, and the Resurrection all falling on 25 March. Syncellus, who inherited the scheme, states his purpose as confirming "the incarnation of Christ at the completion of the 5500th year from the creation of the universe."2 Hippolytus of Rome placed Christ's birth at Anno Mundi 5500, reading the "five and a half days" and the dimensions of the ark typologically; Annianus built a calendar to that total.

Annianus against Panodorus: the biblical numbers and the secular king-lists

The two monks were rivals on method. Panodorus tried to harmonize the sacred chronology with the vast king-lists of Berossus and Manetho, trying to fit the Egyptian and Babylonian dynastic years within the biblical frame; Annianus criticized him for "relying too much on secular sources rather than biblical sources for his dates" and rebuilt the era on the scriptural numbers alone.3 Annianus thus presented the long chronology as the biblical reckoning, against Panodorus' use of the pagan king-lists.

The same scheme carried forward: the Byzantine world era and the Chronicon Paschale

The Alexandrian reckoning did not stay in Alexandria. Later eras re-epoched the same Septuagint-based long chronology: the Chronicon Paschale (c. 630) set Creation at 21 March 5507 BC, and the Byzantine world era, standard across the Eastern empire by the mid-seventh century, fixed it at 1 September 5509 BC. Both keep the Flood at Anno Mundi 2242 — near 3265 BC and 3267 BC respectively.4 These are the same long-chronology family, differing only in where they anchor Anno Mundi 1 on the calendar; the 16-year gap between the Alexandrian 5492/5493 and the Byzantine 5509 turns on small manuscript and computational choices, not on a different reading of Genesis (⚠ unverified). The Septuagint Flood date at Anno Mundi 2242 is thus kept from Panodorus and Annianus (c. 400) in the Byzantine world era.

Reception and debate

  • A modern Septuagint reconstruction. Henry B. Smith Jr. of Associates for Biblical Research, who defends the Septuagint chronology, restores Creation at c. 5554 BC and the Flood at c. 3298 BC — roughly 60 years earlier at Creation and 48 at the Flood than Annianus' figures.5 Smith reaches his numbers by reconstructing the Greek readings from divergent manuscripts (e.g. Methuselah's begetting-age at 187 rather than the transmitted 167); the Alexandrians used the transmitted Greek sum (Adam–Flood 2,242) and set Creation where the Creation-to-Incarnation span came to 5,500.
  • The rival reckonings. The Masoretic, Septuagint, and Samaritan reckonings of the interval to the Flood are set out on the timeline, with the Samaritan question on its interpretation page.
  • Their work survives almost entirely through George Syncellus' Ecloga Chronographica (c. 808–810), who copied large blocks of Annianus and Panodorus verbatim; no complete text of either monk is extant. Syncellus also preserves, through Panodorus, the fragments of Berossus and Manetho that appear on the Flood-dating timeline.
