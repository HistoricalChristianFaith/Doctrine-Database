Does the Septuagint long chronology date the Flood to the fourth millennium BC?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: weak — reckoning the Greek (Septuagint) begetting-ages of Genesis 5 and 11 yields about 2,240–2,260 years from Creation to the Flood, pushing the event roughly a millennium earlier than Ussher, to c. 3300–3000 BC. The claim that these longer figures are the original ones, deflated in the later Hebrew text, is a genuinely live and defensible text-critical position — arguably the stronger of the two reckonings, since it also relieves the impossibly compressed post-Flood interval and lands the Flood in the proto-literate horizon before the king-lists that the short chronology collides with. But as a fixed date for a literal global deluge it still fails on its best case: the genealogies it sums are demonstrably open (the Greek text itself adds a generation the Hebrew lacks), the reckoning cannot claim the original text as a settled premise (the long figures may themselves have been shaped to seat Christ at Anno Mundi 5500), and even c. 3200 BC the continuous Naqada-Egyptian and Uruk-Mesopotamian sequences run across the horizon with no rupture. The failure is scoped to the young single-date global-Flood model, not to the Greek text or to a flood tradition with a real kernel.

Related arguments: the biblical chronologies — see also the Masoretic short chronology (the rival reckoning, dating the same event a millennium later) and the Samaritan Pentateuch chronology (the shorter third witness). The question of which text-form is prior is the same evidence worked on the "Scripture's text & canon" cluster: see also the Septuagint's origin and Eusebius' charge that the Hebrew figures were later shortened.

The claim

Take the same genealogies the short chronology sums, but read them in the Greek. The Septuagint gives most of the antediluvian patriarchs a begetting-age a full hundred years higher than the Hebrew — Adam begets at 230 rather than 130, Seth at 205 rather than 105, and so on down the line — and inserts a second patriarch (a Cainan) into the post-Flood list of Genesis 11 that the Hebrew omits. Summed on the standard Greek text, that is about 2,242–2,262 years from Creation to the Flood, and about 3,394 years from Adam to Abraham against the Masoretic 2,008.1 On the leading modern reconstruction those figures place Creation at c. 5554 BC and the Flood at c. 3298 BC — roughly a thousand years earlier than Ussher's 2348. This is the reckoning behind the Byzantine Anno Mundi world-era (Creation c. 5509 BC) and the whole Greek and Christian-East chronographic tradition; the interpretation assessed here is the composite one it serves: that the Septuagint preserves the original, un-deflated genealogical numbers, and that summing them yields the better, more defensible date for the Flood.

Who advances it

The long chronology is the older Christian reckoning, not a modern novelty. It is the chronology of the Greek chronographers — Julius Africanus, then Eusebius, whose Chronicon was the first to set the Hebrew, Greek, and Samaritan totals side by side and who preferred the longer Greek figures against the Hebrew — and it became the backbone of the Byzantine Anno Mundi and Alexandrian world-eras and of the "the world will last six thousand years" scheme that placed Christ's coming near Anno Mundi 5500.2 In the modern era it has been revived as a serious text-critical thesis, most fully by Henry B. Smith Jr. and the Associates for Biblical Research, who argue that the Septuagint preserves most of the original numbers and that the shorter Hebrew figures are the result of a deliberate, systematic deflation of the primeval chronology in the rabbinic era — pointing back to Eusebius as the earliest witness to that very charge.1 It is set here against its in-group rival, the Masoretic short chronology, which dates the same event to c. 2348 BC.

The evidence for

The divergence has a direction, and it can be read as deflation rather than inflation. The three traditions do not disagree randomly: the gaps cluster in the begetting-ages, mostly as a clean hundred-year offset. The usual charge is that the Greek added a century to each father to stop him begetting "too young." But the arrow can be reversed. Smith and others argue that the shared agreements between the Septuagint and the Samaritan against the Masoretic point to a Hebrew Vorlage that was later shortened, not a Greek text that was inflated — making the longer chronology the underlying one and the Masoretic figures the secondary, systematically reduced reading. This is not special pleading: it is a real text-critical argument that answers the inflation case point by point, and it has capable defenders.1

It has the earliest Christian chronographer on its side. Eusebius, the first to tabulate all three reckonings and choose among them, took the longer Greek figures as the better text and already accused the Jewish scribes of having curtailed the Hebrew numbers — the same charge the modern defenders make, seventeen centuries earlier. Whatever its merits, the long chronology is not a fringe reading forced onto the data; it is the reckoning the Greek-speaking church actually used, and the shorter Hebrew total only displaced it in the Latin West with Jerome and, decisively, with Ussher.2

It relieves strains the short chronology cannot carry. This is its strongest independent point. Ussher's figures leave scarcely three centuries between the Flood and Abraham — too little for the human race to repopulate, disperse from Babel, and found the Egyptian and Sumerian civilizations already flourishing in Abraham's day; on the Masoretic numbers several post-Flood patriarchs are still alive in Abraham's lifetime, and Noah himself dies only two years before Abraham's birth. The Septuagint's extra ~1,386 years open exactly the room that history seems to demand. And by moving the Flood back to c. 3300–3000 BC, the long chronology lands it in the proto-literate horizon — before the firm king-lists and pyramid-building of the literate third millennium — so that the sharpest form of the short chronology's collision, "why did the recording civilizations not notice being drowned," loses much of its bite.3

The long reckoning is attested before the polemic could have shaped it — in Hebrew-based Jewish sources, not only Greek ones. This is the strongest part of the textual case, and it is external rather than internal. A long, Septuagint-type primeval chronology is already on record generations before any Christian–Jewish dispute could have touched it: Demetrius the Chronographer (c. 220 BC) computes from the longer figures, Eupolemus (c. 160 BC) works from a similarly long chronology, the first-century Hebrew-composed Liber Antiquitatum Biblicarum (Pseudo-Philo) carries the longer Genesis 5 begetting-ages, and Josephus reports the longer ante-diluvian ages from a Hebrew Vorlage — with the earliest Christian chronographers (Theophilus of Antioch, Julius Africanus) inheriting the same long reckoning. This does not by itself prove the Greek numbers original — a longer Vorlage could already have been inflated by the second century BC, which is the text-critical mainstream's reading — but it shows the two chronologies were diverging, in Hebrew as well as Greek and in Jewish texts no one accuses of anti-Christian motive, long before the rabbis the deflation charge names could have altered anything. The full weighing of this evidence, and of the mainstream counter-case, is on the Genesis-chronology corruption argument.2

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The textual half of the claim is a genuine, unsettled contest, not a settled loss for the Greek. Which of the three traditions is prior is a real open question, and the case that the Hebrew figures were deflated — rather than the Greek inflated — has serious defenders answering the inflation hypothesis on its own ground, with the earliest Christian chronographer already on that side. Anyone who waves the Septuagint numbers away as an obvious secondary padding has overstated the matter. And the chronology's practical advantages are real: it removes the compressed post-Flood interval that embarrasses the short reckoning, and it escapes the most acute version of the collision with the datable record by predating the literate king-lists. Of the two rival reckonings in this group, the long chronology is the better-defended as a chronology.

But "the original figures" is contested here too — and the same charge cuts back. The long chronology cannot claim the original text as a settled premise any more than the short one can. Three traditions diverge — Masoretic about 2,008 years Adam-to-Abraham, Samaritan about 2,249, Septuagint about 3,394 — and the very fact that they were consciously harmonized in transmission tells against any of them being a pristine autograph. The deflation thesis has a mirror image that is at least as old: the longer Greek figures may themselves be a theological inflation, stretched to seat Christ near Anno Mundi 5500 in the "six thousand years" scheme the Greek chronographers were committed to. This is not a bare suspicion: the Alexandrian chronographer Annianus (c. 412) built his whole world era to that total, fixing Creation at 25 March 5492 BC precisely so that the span from Creation to the Incarnation would come to exactly 5,500 years — the theological number was the fixed point and the intervals were fitted to it, a commitment the Byzantine world era carried forward for a millennium. A number shaped to hit a providential total is no more a raw calendar than a number shortened to deflate one. The point is not that the Masoretic wins — it is that the Septuagint's originality is one side of a live dispute (worked in full on the Septuagint's origin and the rabbinic-corruption pages), so the reckoning cannot rest on it as established fact.4

The one notorious Greek variant is localized and recoverable, not a sign the numbers are mush. The Septuagint carries a real snag the Hebrew does not: in the standard printed (Vaticanus-based) text Methuselah begets at 167 and lives 802 years more, which places his death fourteen years after the Flood he did not board the ark for. But this is a single, well-understood scribal slip, not a general instability. The better-attested reading is 187 — carried by Codex Alexandrinus and other uncials (Cottonianus, Coislinianus), the third-to-fourth-century papyri 911 and 961, and over a dozen minuscules, and agreeing with both the Masoretic Text and Josephus — from which the 167 most likely arose by an early copyist's eye-slip. On 187 the arithmetic resolves cleanly: Methuselah dies six years before the Flood, exactly as Julius Africanus computed it (his pre-Flood total of 2,262 years) and as the manuscripts Augustine cited already reflected. The transmitted number is thus recoverable by ordinary text-critical means, and the reconstructed long chronology is internally consistent — the leading modern defender's choice of 187 is a normal manuscript judgment, not special pleading. What survives as a fair caution is narrower and sharper: the total the Greek-Christian tradition actually canonized — Eusebius' and Syncellus' Anno Mundi 2242, the backbone of the Byzantine world era — is the one that rests on the corrupt 167, while the internally-consistent reconstruction (Africanus' 2262) is the reading that predates it. That the historic long chronology's headline number sits on the inferior variant does not show the figures cannot be reconstructed; it fits, rather, the separate worry that the 2242-based Anno Mundi-5500 scheme was fitted to its theology.5

And a genealogy is still not a calendar — the Greek text proves it best. Summing begetting-ages assumes the Genesis 5 and 11 lists are closed father-to-son chains with no one omitted. But the biblical genealogies elsewhere skip generations, and the decisive demonstration lives inside the very tradition being defended: the Septuagint Genesis 11 inserts a second Cainan between Arphaxad and Shelah that the Hebrew lacks — and Luke's genealogy carries that Greek reading ("Sala, which was the son of Cainan, which was the son of Arphaxad"). A generation present in one line of transmission and absent in another is direct proof that the lists are not closed chains; if the number of names between two fixed points is itself variable, their ages cannot be a running clock. The long chronology, which retains that extra Cainan, is if anything the clearer witness against reading either genealogy as a total.6

The collision softens but does not clear. Moving the Flood to c. 3300–3000 BC does buy relief from the literate-king-list problem — but it does not deliver the universal rupture a literal worldwide deluge predicts. The Egyptian Naqada sequence runs continuously through the fourth millennium into the unified state of c. 3100 BC, and Mesopotamia's Uruk expansion spans the same centuries without a break, bottleneck, or eight-person restart. A global Flood at c. 3200 BC still lands in the middle of continuous, well-attested archaeological cultures that record no such event; the long chronology trades a collision with the written record for a milder but still-unmet collision with the material one, weighed for the short chronology on its own page and only pushed back a millennium here, not dissolved.3

Scope the failure honestly. What breaks is the young single-date global-Flood model — the reading that treats either genealogical sum as a fixed calendar date for a worldwide deluge. Two things are left standing. The Greek text and its priority are untouched by this page: whether the Septuagint or the Masoretic figures are original remains a fair contest, to be decided (if at all) on manuscript grounds, and the long chronology may well be the better chronology for relieving the compressed post-Flood interval. And the milder proposition that a real, remembered flood lies behind the tradition survives easily. The long chronology fails as a date for a global deluge; it does not drag the Greek text, or the historicity of a flood, down with it — and it does the short chronology one real service by showing how far the "biblical date of the Flood" moves when the text-form changes.

Assessment

Weak. As a computation the Greek reckoning is sound — about 2,240–2,260 years from Creation to the Flood, on the numbers the Septuagint gives — and the claim that those numbers are the more original is a defensible, genuinely contested text-critical position with the earliest Christian chronographer behind it, not a naïvety. It is, in fact, the better-defended of the two rival reckonings as a chronology: it relieves the impossibly compressed post-Flood interval the short chronology cannot carry, and by dropping the Flood into the proto-literate horizon it escapes the sharpest form of the collision with the record. But the interpretation the reckoning serves is that this fixes a real Flood date, and that does not hold on its best available case. The premise that the Greek figures are the un-deflated originals is one side of a live dispute (argued on its own pages), and the long chronology may itself be theologically shaped to seat Christ at Anno Mundi 5500 — the total the tradition actually canonized (Anno Mundi 2242) resting on the one demonstrable Greek scribal slip, the "167" for Methuselah that the better-attested "187" corrects; the summing assumes closed genealogies that the Greek text itself shows to be open (the extra Cainan it carries and Luke repeats); and even c. 3200 BC the continuous Naqada-Egyptian and Uruk-Mesopotamian sequences span the horizon with no rupture.

The verdict is scoped, not sweeping. It falls on the young single-date global-Flood model that reads a genealogical sum as a hard calendar fix — not on the Septuagint text (whose priority is argued honestly elsewhere) and not on the historicity of a flood tradition with a real kernel. Of the two rival reckonings in this group, the long chronology is the harder to dismiss and the easier to live with; its failure is the milder and the more instructive one, because it shows that the "biblical date of the Flood" is not a fixed point at all but a contested interval that slides a thousand years with the manuscript in hand.
