Does the Masoretic short chronology date the Flood to c. 2348 BC?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: weak — reckoning the received Hebrew (Masoretic) begetting-ages of Genesis 5 and 11 yields a clean, internally consistent 1,656 years from Creation to the Flood, and the claim that those figures are the more original (against the Septuagint's inflated ones) is a genuinely live, defensible position. But two things break it as a Flood date: even granting the numbers, the genealogies are plausibly open, so their sum need not be a calendar interval; and a literal global deluge fixed at c. 2348 BC collides with the continuous, unbroken record of Egypt and Mesopotamia straddling that date. The failure is scoped to the young single-date global-Flood model, not to the Hebrew text.

Related arguments: the biblical chronologies — see also the Septuagint long chronology (the rival reckoning, a millennium earlier) and the Samaritan Pentateuch chronology (the shorter third witness). For the collision with the datable record, see also dating against the ground and the continuous record of Egypt and Mesopotamia across c. 2350 BC.

The claim

Add up the numbers the Hebrew Bible actually gives. Genesis 5 records the age of each antediluvian patriarch when his named son was born — Adam 130, Seth 105, Enosh 90, Kenan 70, Mahalalel 65, Jared 162, Enoch 65, Methuselah 187, Lamech 182 — and Genesis 7 puts the Flood in Noah's six-hundredth year. Summed, that is 1,656 years from Creation to the Flood.1 Chaining that to the post-Flood ages of Genesis 11 and thence to Abraham and the dated monarchy, James Ussher fixed Creation at 4004 BC and the Flood at 2348 BC — the number most English study-Bible margins still carry and the one most readers mean by "the biblical date of the Flood."2 The interpretation assessed here is the composite one the reckoning serves: that the Masoretic figures preserve the original, un-inflated genealogical numbers, and that summing them yields a real, defensible date for the Flood.

Who advances it

The reckoning is Ussher's classic statement (1650), but it long predates him — it is the Hebrew total Jerome preferred against the longer Greek one — and, on this chronology specifically, the reading Augustine reached too, though he was the era's foremost champion of the Septuagint's authority: in City of God XV he traced the Greek's uniform extra century to a copyist's design rather than to Jewish tampering, and ruled that where the versions diverge one should prefer the Hebrew "language out of which the translation was made."3 It became the default of the Latin West and then of the English Reformation Bibles. In the modern era it is the chronology of young-earth creationism: the calculators behind the Answers in Genesis and Institute for Creation Research "biblical chronology," and the peer-reviewed defenders of the Masoretic figures as the textually superior reading against the Septuagint's higher numbers.3 It is set here against its in-group rival, the Septuagint long chronology, which pushes the same event a millennium earlier.

The evidence for

The arithmetic is clean and the text is the received one. Unlike most attempts to date a primeval event, this one asks nothing exotic: it reads the plainest numbers in the canonical Hebrew text and adds them. 1,656 is not a reconstruction; it is what Genesis 5 plus Genesis 7:6 says on its face, and it has the weight of the Masoretic tradition — the text the synagogue and the Reformation churches transmitted — behind it.1

The Masoretic figures have a serious text-critical defense. The three surviving traditions of Genesis 5 and 11 disagree, and the divergence is not random: many Septuagint patriarchs carry exactly a hundred years more before the firstborn, which looks like a schematic secondary inflation (to keep each father from begetting "too young"). If that added century is an editorial overlay, then the Masoretic numbers are the underlying, un-inflated ones — a position argued in detail in recent creationist textual scholarship, and one that turns the shorter chronology from a naïve literalism into a defensible claim about which manuscript family is prior.3

It lands near a real horizon. Of the datable Mesopotamian flood deposits, the ones the tradition's own reign-lengths point to — Shuruppak and the earliest at Kish, c. 2900–2800 BC — sit only a few centuries above Ussher's 2348. Short-chronology defenders reach for exactly this: alone among the biblical reckonings, the Masoretic one is close enough to a physical candidate that the gap looks bridgeable rather than absurd.4

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The computation is valid and the total is not in dispute: on the Masoretic figures the Flood falls 1,656 years after Creation. And the textual half of the claim is a real position, not special pleading — whether the Hebrew or the Greek numbers are prior is a genuinely open text-critical question, with capable defenders of Masoretic priority answering the case for the Septuagint point by point. Anyone who dismisses the short chronology as obviously corrupt has overstated the matter; that much of the argument stands.

But "the original figures" is contested, not established. The claim smuggles in a conclusion. Three traditions diverge — Masoretic 1,656, Samaritan 1,307, Septuagint about 2,242 years to the Flood — and the fact that they were consciously harmonized in transmission cuts against any of them being a pristine autograph. A live modern current argues the reverse of the short-chronology case: that the Septuagint's higher ages are the older reading and the Hebrew was deflated. Eusebius, the earliest Christian to tabulate all three, preferred the longer Greek reckoning against the Hebrew, and this wiki treats his choice as part of the case that the shorter Hebrew figures were later shortened.5 The point is not that the Septuagint wins — it is that "the Masoretic numbers are the original ones" is one side of an unsettled dispute (developed on its own page), so the reckoning cannot claim the original text as a settled premise.

Even granting the numbers, a genealogy is not a calendar. Summing begetting-ages assumes the Genesis 5 and 11 lists are closed father-to-son chains with no one omitted. But the biblical genealogies elsewhere demonstrably skip generations — Matthew 1 drops three kings to make its fourteens — and "begat" (hôlîd) can mean "became ancestor of." William Henry Green's 1890 Primeval Chronology, still the standard treatment, argued on those grounds that the antediluvian and post-diluvian lists were never meant to yield a total, so that the interval from Creation to the Flood cannot be fixed by addition at all.6 The traditions themselves prove the lists are fluid: the Greek Genesis 11 inserts a second Cainan between Arphaxad and Salah that the Hebrew lacks — and Luke's genealogy carries that very name ("Sala, which was the son of Cainan, which was the son of Arphaxad"), a generation present in one line of transmission and absent in another.7 If the number of names between two fixed points is itself variable, their ages cannot be a running clock.

And the date collides with the ground. This is the decisive external check. A literal, worldwide deluge in 2348 BC predicts a universal cultural and demographic rupture at that horizon. There is none. Egypt's Old Kingdom was building and continuously occupying its pyramids straight through the twenty-fourth century, and Mesopotamia's Early Dynastic, Akkadian, and Ur III sequences run unbroken across it — no gap, no eight-person bottleneck, no interrupted king-lists. That collision is weighed on its own page; here it is enough that the short chronology's headline date falls in the middle of two literate, well-attested civilizations that did not notice being drowned.4 The near-miss on the Mesopotamian silt cited above cuts the other way once examined: those deposits are non-synchronous local floods of a repeatedly-flooding plain, not one global event (weighed on the material-record page), and Shuruppak's flood is regional, dated by the same reign-lengths that put it six centuries above Ussher's number.

Scope the failure honestly. What breaks is the young single-date global-Flood model — the reading that treats 1,656 AM as a fixed calendar date for a worldwide deluge c. 2348 BC. Two things are left standing. The Masoretic text itself is untouched: whether its figures or the Greek ones are prior remains a fair contest, decided (if at all) on manuscript grounds, not by this page. And the milder proposition that a real, remembered flood lies behind the tradition survives easily — a regional Mesopotamian flood, or the Septuagint's older window, is not refuted by anything here. The short chronology fails as a date; it does not drag the Hebrew text or the historicity of a flood down with it.

Assessment

Weak. As a computation the reckoning is unimpeachable — 1,656 years, on the numbers the Masoretic text gives — and the claim that those numbers are the more original is a defensible, genuinely contested text-critical position, not a naïvety. But the interpretation the reckoning serves is that this fixes a real Flood date, and that does not hold on its best available case. The premise that the Hebrew figures are the un-inflated originals is one side of a live dispute, not a settled fact; the summing assumes closed genealogies that the biblical lists themselves show to be open (the Cainan the Greek Genesis and Luke carry but the Hebrew omits); and a literal global deluge at c. 2348 BC lands in the middle of the continuous, unbroken record of Old Kingdom Egypt and Early Dynastic–Akkadian Mesopotamia.

The verdict is scoped, not sweeping. It falls on the young single-date global-Flood model that reads 2348 BC as a hard calendar fix — not on the Masoretic text (whose priority is argued honestly elsewhere) and not on the historicity of a flood tradition with a real kernel. Of the two rival reckonings in this group, the short chronology is the one close enough to a physical horizon to tempt the identification; that proximity is also what exposes it, since the horizon it nears turns out to be a set of non-synchronous local floods, and the civilizations on either side of its date never broke stride.
