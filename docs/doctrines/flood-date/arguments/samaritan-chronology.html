Does the Samaritan Pentateuch preserve the original chronology of Genesis 5 and 11?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: weak — the Samaritan Pentateuch gives a third set of Genesis 5 and 11 figures, summing to 1,307 years from Creation to the Flood — shorter than the Masoretic 1,656 and far shorter than the Septuagint's ~2,242 — and a real minority (Bertheau, Jepsen, Larsson, a modern prime-number argument) has advocated these as the original, unaltered numbers from which the Hebrew derived. But the case fails on its best form: the Samaritan figures for Jared, Methuselah, and Lamech are cut by exactly the amounts needed to make all three die in the Flood year — a transparent schematic edit derived from the Book of Jubilees, and the cleanest single proof that at least one tradition was deliberately reworked. It is best read not as a rival Flood date (no proponent proposes one) but as a text-critical witness: a live but thin priority claim, and — because it is pre-rabbinic — a genuine datum in the deflation debate. The failure is scoped to the young single-date global-Flood model and to the "Samaritan-is-original" thesis, not to the value of the Samaritan text as a witness.

Related arguments: the biblical chronologies — see also the Masoretic short chronology (Flood c. 2348 BC) and the Septuagint long chronology (Flood a millennium earlier), the two reckonings this Samaritan witness sits between. The question of which text-form is prior is the same evidence worked on the "Scripture's text & canon" cluster: see also the Septuagint's origin and the charge that the Hebrew figures were later shortened.

The claim

There are not two sets of Genesis chronology numbers but three. The Samaritan Pentateuch — the Torah preserved by the Samaritan community, whose text-form was fixed by roughly the second or first century BC — reads Genesis 5 almost exactly like the Hebrew, with three pointed exceptions: it gives Jared a begetting-age of 62 rather than 162, Methuselah 67 rather than 187, and Lamech 53 rather than 182. Everything else in the antediluvian list matches the Masoretic figures. Summed with Noah's 600 years at the Flood, that yields 1,307 years from Creation to the Flood — the shortest of the three reckonings, against the Masoretic 1,656 and the Septuagint's roughly 2,242.1 In the post-Flood list of Genesis 11 the Samaritan text moves the other way, adding a hundred years to several begetting-ages as the Septuagint does, so its Adam-to-Abraham total (about 2,249 years) actually sits between the Masoretic (~2,008) and the Septuagint (~3,394). The interpretation assessed here is the one a minority has genuinely advanced: that the Samaritan figures of Genesis 5 preserve the original antediluvian numbers, and that the Hebrew (and the Greek) are secondary alterations of them.

Who advances it

The advocacy is real but thin, and it is a claim about textual priority, not a proposal of a Flood date. Ernst Bertheau (1878) noticed that the Samaritan figures form a self-consistent arithmetical system — one can recover each patriarch's total lifespan by adding, from the Flood, the ages at which each begat his firstborn — a regularity that holds only on the Samaritan numbers, and inferred from it that the Samaritan text is prior to the Hebrew. A. Jepsen (1929) argued the same priority on the ground that the Hebrew carries "the simpler figures." Gösta Larsson (1973) pressed the point sharply: "an alteration from the almost perfect Samaritan system to the irregular Hebrew system seems quite incredible."2 In the modern era the same intuition has been revived in a number-theoretic key: a self-published paper argues that the Samaritan antediluvian dates of birth and death are (nearly all) prime numbers once Adam and Eve's seven years in Eden are counted, that "such combination cannot occur by chance," and that the prime scheme "should be assumed as the progenitor one" — so "the chronology of the Masoretic text was developed from the Samaritan one and not vice versa" (it notes that even the Flood at Anno Mundi 1,307 is itself prime).3 It is set here alongside its in-group rivals, the Masoretic short chronology and the Septuagint long chronology.

The evidence for

The Samaritan system is internally regular, and regularity can look original. Bertheau's and Larsson's point is not nothing: the Samaritan numbers do interlock into a tidy scheme, and it is a fair instinct that a clean system is more likely to be the source and a ragged one the corruption. On that instinct the Hebrew's jagged figures (Jared 162, Methuselah 187, Lamech 182) look like the debris left after a neat original was disturbed. This is a genuine text-critical argument with named defenders across a century, not a fringe invention.2

It is demonstrably pre-rabbinic, which gives it real work to do. Whatever its priority, the Samaritan text-form was fixed centuries before the Christian era — well before the rabbinic period in which one leading thesis says the Hebrew antediluvian numbers were deliberately deflated. Yet the Samaritan already carries short antediluvian begetting-ages (shorter even than the Hebrew's). That makes the Samaritan a live datum against the crudest form of the deflation-after-Jesus charge: short primeval figures plainly existed in a Hebrew-based tradition long before any rabbi could have invented them. Defenders of the Masoretic text reach for exactly this.4

It rounds out the field. The dating question turns on which text-tradition is prior, and honest treatment of that question cannot quietly drop the third witness. The Samaritan is the reckoning Ussher, Eusebius, and every comparative chronographer since had to place beside the Hebrew and the Greek; leaving it off the table would misrepresent how open the "biblical date of the Flood" really is.1

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The Samaritan is a real third witness, its Genesis 5 numbers do form a consistent system, and its pre-rabbinic date is a fact with genuine force in the corruption debate — a point this page grants and even uses. Anyone who ignores the Samaritan tradition in weighing the priority question has left out evidence. That much stands.

But the "original" reading has the arrow backwards — and the text shows it. The three Samaritan departures from the Hebrew are not scattered; they are precisely and only the three cuts needed to make Jared, Methuselah, and Lamech all die in the year of the Flood. Jared (born Anno Mundi 460, lifespan reduced to 847), Methuselah (born 587, lifespan 720), and Lamech (born 654, lifespan 653) each expire in AM 1,307 — the Flood year — to a man. That is not what an untouched original looks like; it is what a scribe produces when he needs no antediluvian patriarch to survive a Flood he was not aboard the ark for. Gerhard Hasel's reply to Bertheau and Larsson is the decisive one: regularity is the fingerprint of editing, not of originality — "there is no reason why something regular is to become irregular," so the irregular Hebrew figures are the harder reading to explain as secondary, and "the Samaritan Pentateuch and the LXX version give evidence of schematizations."5

And the scheme is borrowed, so its antiquity proves nothing. The specific Samaritan figures for the three altered patriarchs — the begetting-ages (62, 67, 53), the remaining years, and the lifespans — are the very numbers the Book of Jubilees (c. 160 BC) had already produced when it deflated the primeval chronology to fit its own 50-jubilee, 2,450-year scheme, which likewise forced Jared, Methuselah, and Lamech to die before the Flood. The Samaritan did not independently preserve an original; it inherited Jubilees' engineered figures. This springs the circularity trap: appealing to the Samaritan's early date, or to Jubilees, to establish the "originality" of the short numbers begs the question, because Jubilees is the source of the scheme, not an independent witness to it.6

No one actually dates the Flood by it. Unlike the Masoretic (Ussher's 2348 BC) and the Septuagint (c. 3300–3000 BC on the leading reconstruction), the Samaritan 1,307 is never carried forward to a calendar date for the Flood by its own advocates. Bertheau, Jepsen, and Larsson argue textual priority; the modern prime-number proponent argues the scheme is the mathematical progenitor while openly conceding it is a "carefully constructed artificial" system, not literal history. The interpretation "the Samaritan preserves the original figures" therefore never even reaches the interpretation "and so the Flood happened in such-and-such a year" — and were it forced to, 1,307 years from Creation, anchored forward through the Samaritan's own inflated Genesis 11, lands the event in the same literate third-millennium horizon the other reckonings collide with, so it inherits their wall without relief.4

Scope the failure honestly. Two very different claims travel under one banner here, and they deserve different verdicts. As a thesis that the Samaritan figures are the original antediluvian chronology, the argument is weak: the flood-year-death schematization and the borrowing from Jubilees are strong, converging evidence that the Samaritan is secondary, and the mainstream text-critical judgment agrees. But the Samaritan text as a witness is untouched and even useful — it is real evidence that short primeval numbers circulated before the rabbis, a genuine check on the crudest deflation charge, and the clearest illustration that at least one tradition edited these lists on purpose. What fails is "Samaritan-is-original," not "the Samaritan matters."

Assessment

Weak. The advocacy is real — Bertheau, Jepsen, and Larsson argued Samaritan priority across a century, and a modern prime-number argument revives it — so the claim clears the bar of a genuinely held position and earns a rated page rather than a footnote. But it does not hold on its best case. The three Samaritan departures from the Hebrew are exactly the cuts that make Jared, Methuselah, and Lamech die in the Flood year (AM 1,307), a transparent schematic edit; those very figures are inherited from the Book of Jubilees, so the Samaritan's early date is no independent evidence of originality; and the mainstream principle that regularity marks editing while irregularity marks the older reading turns the proponents' best intuition against them. Add that no advocate ever converts 1,307 into an actual Flood date — the prime-number defender calls the scheme artificial outright — and the interpretation "the Samaritan preserves the original chronology" comes out weak.

The verdict is scoped, not sweeping. It falls on the "Samaritan-is-original" thesis and, a fortiori, on any young single-date global Flood read off the Samaritan numbers — not on the Samaritan Pentateuch as a text-critical witness, which retains real value: it is pre-rabbinic evidence that short antediluvian figures are old (a fair rebuttal to the crudest deflation charge) and the cleanest demonstration that these genealogies were, in at least one line of transmission, deliberately reworked. Of the three reckonings in this group it is the one no one dates the Flood by — and the one whose editing is easiest to catch in the act.
