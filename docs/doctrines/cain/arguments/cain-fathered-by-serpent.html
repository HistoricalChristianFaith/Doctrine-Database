Was Cain fathered by the serpent? The “serpent seed”

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: unsupported — the reading that Cain was begotten not by Adam but by the serpent (or the fallen angel Sammael) contradicts Genesis 4:1, which names Adam as Cain’s father in the very clause of his conception, rests on a minority aggadah read against its own text, and in its modern “two-seedline” form is a vehicle for explicit racism; the New Testament’s “of that wicked one” (1 John 3:12) is a moral, not a biological, filiation · the interpretation is weighed here, not any single proponent’s wording of it

The claim

“(Sammael) riding on the serpent came to her, and she conceived; afterwards Adam came to her, and she conceived Abel.”

— Pirqe de-Rabbi Eliezer 21, Sefaria. In its ancient form the claim is that Eve conceived Cain by a superhuman seducer — the serpent, or the angel Sammael riding it — so that Cain is not Adam’s son but the offspring of the Evil One. In its modern form (William Branham’s “Serpent’s Seed” teaching, and the Christian Identity “two-seedline” doctrine) the same idea is literalized into two biological races of mankind — the seed of the serpent through Cain, and the seed of Adam through Seth — and turned to racist ends.1

Who advances it

The seed of the idea is genuinely old. A strand of Jewish aggadah read the tempting of Eve as a seduction and made Cain its fruit: the Targum of Pseudo-Jonathan already hints that Eve “had desired the Angel” and bore Cain saying “I have acquired a man, the Angel of the LORD,” and Pirqe de-Rabbi Eliezer states outright that Sammael on the serpent fathered Cain. Related notions surface in some gnostic texts. But the reading was always a minority one, at odds with the plain sense of Genesis, and the mainstream Jewish and Christian traditions rejected it. Its modern revival is the work of the twentieth-century American preacher William M. Branham, whose “Serpent’s Seed” teaching held that Eve’s sin was sexual union with the serpent producing Cain, and of the “two-seedline” theology of Christian Identity, which made non-white peoples the literal descendants of Cain and thus “of Satan.”2

The evidence for

The case, such as it is, rests on three moves. (1) The ancient aggadah. That a stream of early Jewish interpretation made Cain the serpent’s or Sammael’s son is a real datum: Pirqe de-Rabbi Eliezer 21 says so plainly, and the seduction-motif is old. (2) The wording of Genesis 4:1. Proponents seize on Eve’s cry, “I have gotten a man from the LORD” (Hebrew qanîtî ʾîš ʾet-YHWH), and on the Targum’s “the Angel of the LORD,” to argue that Eve credits a heavenly being rather than Adam. (3) The contrast of “seeds.” They read the enmity between “thy seed and her seed” (Genesis 3:15) and John’s “Cain, who was of that wicked one” (1 John 3:12) as literal biological descent — two physical lineages, serpent’s and Adam’s, running through Cain and Seth respectively.3

Adversarial assessment

Each move fails. (1) Genesis 4:1 names Adam as the father, in the same breath. “And Adam knew Eve his wife; and she conceived, and bare Cain” — the verse could not be more explicit that Cain is conceived of Adam’s union with Eve. Eve’s cry “I have gotten a man from the LORD” credits God as the giver of the child (the ordinary biblical sense, as when children are “an heritage of the LORD,” Psalm 127:3), not as the biological father; and the very same chapter says “Adam knew his wife… and she bare Seth” in identical language, so if the formula makes God Cain’s father it makes Adam Seth’s — incoherent. (2) The Targum’s wording is a paraphrase, not a genealogy. Even where Pseudo-Jonathan reads “the Angel of the LORD,” it still opens “And Adam knew Hava his wife”; the seduction-motif is a midrashic elaboration of Eve’s temptation, not a claim overturning Cain’s stated paternity, and the tradition that carried it also carried “afterwards Adam came to her” — the very sentence undermining a single serpent-fathered lineage. (3) “Of that wicked one” is moral, not biological. 1 John 3:12 explains its own phrase: Cain was “of that wicked one” because “his own works were evil, and his brother’s righteous” — John’s whole argument is that being “of the devil” means doing the devil’s works (1 John 3:8–10), the same idiom Jesus uses of the Pharisees (“Ye are of your father the devil,” John 8:44) who were plainly Abraham’s physical seed. The “seeds” of Genesis 3:15 are likewise moral and spiritual lineages, as the whole tradition read them, not a claim that the serpent sired a physical race. (4) The reading has no surviving object. Even were it granted, Cain’s line perishes entirely in the Flood (Bede: “the entire cursed progeny was finished by the flood”), so no living people could be “serpent seed” — which is exactly why the modern two-seedline form has to graft the doctrine onto Ham or onto post-Flood peoples, abandoning the Genesis genealogy it claims to rest on. (5) The modern form is racist pseudo-exegesis. Branham’s and Christian Identity’s use of the doctrine to brand whole races as literally descended from Satan is not merely unsupported but a documented engine of hatred — the interpretive fruit by which this tree is known.4

Assessment

Unsupported. The serpent-seed reading contradicts the text it claims to expound: Genesis 4:1 names Adam as Cain’s father in the same clause as his conception, Eve’s “I have gotten a man from the LORD” credits God as giver in the ordinary biblical idiom (not as sire), and the identical formula used of Seth’s birth makes a serpent-paternity reading of the wording incoherent. Its scriptural props — the “seeds” of Genesis 3:15 and 1 John 3:12’s “of that wicked one” — are moral filiations that the passages themselves define by works, not bloodline. The ancient aggadah that seeded the idea (Pirqe de-Rabbi Eliezer 21) was always a minority elaboration read against its own “Adam knew Hava,” and even taken literally it leaves no surviving descendants, Cain’s whole line ending at the Flood. This is not a case of ruling out the supernatural: an angelic or diabolic seduction is not impossible in principle — the wiki weighs a genuine angelic-paternity reading on the Nephilim timeline — but here the specific claim is barred by the text’s own naming of Adam and by the New Testament’s own definition of its terms, and its modern form is an instrument of racial hatred. The reading has no real basis; rated unsupported. (For the identity of the serpent itself, see the serpent-of-Eden timeline.)
