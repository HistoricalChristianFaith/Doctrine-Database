The burial cloths of Christ (the Shroud of Turin & the Sudarium of Oviedo)

The Gospels bury Jesus in cloth and then, at the empty tomb, single the cloth out: the “linen clothes” lying, and “the napkin, that was about his head” — the sudarium — folded “in a place by itself.” Two objects venerated today are held by their custodians and devotees to be those cloths: the Shroud of Turin, a full-length linen bearing the faint front-and-back image of a scourged and crucified man, and the Sudarium of Oviedo, an image-less bloodstained face-cloth kept in northern Spain. This page traces how each relic entered the historical record and how bishops, a pope, and later laboratories examined the claim that they are first-century. It is a companion to the tomb of Jesus and the True Cross; where those turn on place, these turn on dating and forensics, which is why they are gathered here as relics rather than there as a site.

It is a reception-history timeline: not a survey of who believed a doctrine, but a chronicle of how two relic-claims surfaced, were disputed, and were examined by physical science, ordered by earliest extant attestation, each block footnoted. The earliest extant documentary notices are the opening of the Oviedo relic-chest in 1075 and the Shroud’s presence at Lirey c. 1354–1357; the earlier journeys of both cloths are told in traditions recorded later. In 1988 three laboratories dated the Shroud’s linen to AD 1260–1390, and the Sudarium’s radiocarbon results fall between c. AD 600 and 800, while the scientists of the 1978 STURP examination reported that how the Shroud’s image was formed remained unsolved. Whether a twelfth-century Hungarian miniature depicts the Shroud is set out on its own page under Disputed readings.

Timeline

The Gospels name the burial cloths (1st century)

The four evangelists agree that Jesus was wrapped for burial in linen. Matthew, Mark, and Luke use sindon, a single linen sheet: Joseph of Arimathea “wrapped it in a clean linen cloth.”1 John describes othonia, “linen clothes” bound “with the spices, as the manner of the Jews is to bury,” and then, at the empty tomb, distinguishes a separate head-cloth: Peter and John saw “the linen clothes lie, and the napkin, that was about his head, not lying with the linen clothes, but wrapped together in a place by itself.”2 The Latin of that “napkin” is sudarium (a sweat-cloth), the word the Oviedo relic takes its name from; the full-length sindon is what the Shroud is held to be. The Gospels do not record what became of the cloths.

Rival “shrouds” and the Eastern image-traditions (4th–13th centuries)

For a millennium no single object holds the field. Medieval Europe venerated many burial linens of Christ — John Calvin’s Treatise on Relics catalogued as many as forty-three in Europe — and the East told of a miraculous cloth image-of-Christ, the Mandylion or Image of Edessa, moved to Constantinople in 944 and lost in the sack of 1204. Ian Wilson identifies the Turin Shroud with the Edessa cloth folded to show only the face, and reads a twelfth-century Hungarian manuscript, the Pray Codex, as depicting it.3 The history of the Turin cloth before the fourteenth century is the point in dispute; whether the Pray Codex miniature depicts the Shroud is set out on its own interpretation page. The Mandylion had a second life apart from the Shroud question: as an image traditionally traced to Christ himself, it was cited by the defenders of icons in the Byzantine image controversy; that tradition is traced under image-veneration. Eusebius's fourth-century account of King Abgar's letter makes no mention of an image; the Syriac Doctrine of Addai has a portrait painted by the king's court painter, and Evagrius in the sixth century describes a “God-made image”, a miraculous imprint of Christ's face on a cloth.4

The Sudarium surfaces at Oviedo (11th–12th centuries)

The Sudarium's earliest extant documentary record is at the north-Spanish city of Oviedo, where it is kept in the Arca Santa, a relic-chest in the cathedral's Cámara Santa. The recorded event is the ceremonial opening of the chest on 14 March 1075 under King Alfonso VI, after which he had it clad in silver inscribed “the Holy Sudarium of Our Lord Jesus Christ.”5 The relic's earlier journey — carried from Jerusalem before the Persian sack of 614, via Alexandria and North Africa to Cartagena, Toledo, and at last Asturias, ahead of the Muslim advance — is told chiefly by Bishop Pelagius of Oviedo (d. c. 1153), whom the historian Peter Linehan (1982) called “the Prince of Falsifiers”.6 Unlike the Shroud, the Sudarium bears no image — only blood and serum stains — and the case made for its antiquity rests not on a picture but on a claimed match with the Shroud (see below).

The Shroud appears at Lirey; a bishop calls it painted (c. 1354–1390)

The Turin Shroud's earliest extant documentary record places it in the collegiate church of Lirey, in the Champagne, held by the French knight Geoffroi de Charny around 1354–1357. In 1389 Pierre d'Arcis, bishop of Troyes, addressed a memorandum, which survives as an unsigned, undated draft, to Clement VII (Avignon obedience), reporting that his predecessor Henri de Poitiers had investigated the cloth about 1355 and found it “cunningly painted, the truth being attested by the artist who had painted it, to wit, that it was a work of human skill and not miraculously wrought.”7 In 1390 Clement VII ruled that the cloth could be exposed only if it were publicly declared a “figure or representation” of the true shroud, not the original.8

Fire, the House of Savoy, and Turin (1453–1578)

Charny's granddaughter sold the cloth to the House of Savoy in 1453; it was kept at the ducal chapel in Chambéry, where a fire in 1532 scorched it through its folds and molten silver from the reliquary burned the symmetrical holes still visible, repaired by Poor Clare nuns. In 1578 Duke Emanuele Filiberto moved it to his new capital, Turin, where it has remained — giving the relic its enduring name and its home in the cathedral of San Giovanni Battista.9

Secondo Pia's negative (1898)

The modern scientific controversy begins with a photograph. When the lawyer and amateur photographer Secondo Pia was allowed to photograph the Shroud in May 1898, he found that his glass-plate negative showed a lifelike positive image — as though the cloth itself were a photographic negative, its lights and darks reversed. The photograph drew scientific attention to how the image had been made.10

STURP examines the cloth (1978)

In October 1978 the Shroud of Turin Research Project (STURP), a team of about thirty scientists, was given five days of direct access. Their 1981 summary concluded that the image is a superficial straw-yellow discoloration of only the topmost fibers, that “no pigments, paints, dyes or stains have been found on the fibrils,” that it “is not the product of an artist,” and that “the problem remains unsolved.”11 The microscopist Walter McCrone dissented, reporting iron-oxide and vermilion particles and arguing the image was painted about 1355; STURP's chemists attributed those particles to environmental contamination.12

Radiocarbon: the Shroud dated to 1260–1390 (1988)

In 1988 postage-stamp samples cut from one corner of the Shroud were dated independently by accelerator mass spectrometry at Arizona, Oxford, and Zurich, under British Museum coordination. The result, published in Nature, was a calibrated date of AD 1260–1390 at 95% confidence, which the laboratories described as “conclusive evidence that the linen of the Shroud of Turin is mediaeval”.13 Raymond Rogers argued in 2005 that the sampled corner was a later “invisible” repair unrepresentative of the main cloth; peer-reviewed statistical reanalyses of the raw data (Riani and colleagues 2013; Casabianca and colleagues 2019) reported the sub-samples to be measurably heterogeneous. Mechthild Flury-Lemberg, the textile conservator who led the Shroud’s 2002 restoration, reported finding no traces of such a repair.14

The Sudarium dated, and compared with the Shroud (1990s–2000s)

The Oviedo cloth was itself radiocarbon-dated (samples arranged by Pierluigi Baima Bollone, run at Tucson and Toronto, with a further 2007 test): the results fall between c. AD 600 and 800, most probably the eighth century, which the laboratories read as excluding a first-century origin.15 Alongside the dating, the Spanish Center for Sindonology, Mark Guscin, and Alan and Mary Whanger argued that the Sudarium and the Shroud wrapped the same head: a shared AB blood grouping, and dozens of geometric coincidences between the two patterns of bloodstains.16 Hugh Farey, against this, holds that the two cloths' own radiocarbon dates lie centuries apart and that the stain-matching is subjective.17
