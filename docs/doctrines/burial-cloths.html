The burial cloths of Christ (the Shroud of Turin & the Sudarium of Oviedo)

The Gospels bury Jesus in cloth and then, at the empty tomb, single the cloth out: the “linen clothes” lying, and “the napkin, that was about his head” — the sudarium — folded “in a place by itself.” Two objects venerated today claim to be those very cloths: the Shroud of Turin, a full-length linen bearing the faint front-and-back image of a scourged and crucified man, and the Sudarium of Oviedo, an image-less bloodstained face-cloth kept in northern Spain. This page traces how each relic entered the historical record and how the church, and then the laboratory, weighed the claim that they are first-century. It is a companion to the tomb of Jesus and the True Cross — but where those turn on place, these turn on dating and forensics, which is why they are gathered here as relics rather than there as a site.

It is a reception-history timeline: not a survey of who believed a doctrine, but a chronicle of how two relic-claims surfaced, were contested, and were finally tested by physical science — ordered earliest-attestation-first, each block footnoted. Two honest threads run throughout and are worth watching for. First, the documentary trail of each object begins late — the Sudarium at Oviedo in the eleventh century, the Shroud at Lirey in the mid-fourteenth — with the earlier journey resting on traditions no contemporary record confirms. Second, the two great scientific results point the same way and against the tradition: the Shroud's 1988 radiocarbon date falls in AD 1260–1390, the Sudarium's around the seventh century — centuries apart from each other and from the crucifixion, so that the popular claim that both wrapped the one body requires both laboratory results to be wrong. Against that stands one genuine anomaly the page does not paper over: no one has established how the Shroud's image was made. The questions this raises — whether the Shroud is authentic, what the radiocarbon date really settles, and whether the two cloths belong to one burial — are weighed on their own argument pages.

Timeline

The Gospels name the burial cloths (1st century)

The four evangelists agree that Jesus was wrapped for burial in linen. Matthew, Mark, and Luke use sindon, a single linen sheet: Joseph of Arimathea “wrapped it in a clean linen cloth.”1 John describes othonia, “linen clothes” bound “with the spices, as the manner of the Jews is to bury,” and then, at the empty tomb, distinguishes a separate head-cloth: Peter and John saw “the linen clothes lie, and the napkin, that was about his head, not lying with the linen clothes, but wrapped together in a place by itself.”2 The Latin of that “napkin” is sudarium (a sweat-cloth), the word the Oviedo relic takes its name from; the full-length sindon is what the Shroud claims to be. The Gospels describe the cloths but of course say nothing of any surviving them; every later relic-claim is a claim to continuity the text neither makes nor forbids.

Rival “shrouds” and the Eastern image-traditions (4th–13th centuries)

For a millennium no single object holds the field. Medieval Europe venerated many burial linens of Christ — a modern tally counts more than forty rival shrouds — and the East told of a miraculous cloth image-of-Christ, the Mandylion or Image of Edessa, moved to Constantinople in 944 and lost in the sack of 1204. Some modern authors (Ian Wilson above all) try to bridge the gap by identifying the Turin Shroud with the Edessa cloth folded to show only the face, and read a twelfth-century Hungarian manuscript as depicting it.3 These reconstructions are the strongest bid for a pre-1350 Shroud, but they are inferential and contested — the pre-fourteenth-century history of the Turin cloth is precisely what is disputed. The Pray-Codex reading is weighed on its own argument page. The Mandylion had a second life apart from the Shroud question: as an image traditionally traced to Christ himself, it was the iconophiles’ sharpest argument-from-relic in the Byzantine image controversy — whether Christ really left such a self-made image is weighed under image-veneration at “Did Christ leave a self-made image… authorizing icons?” (assessed weak as history: the “not made by hands” element is a sixth-century development, and Eusebius’s earliest report of Abgar has no image at all).

The Sudarium surfaces at Oviedo (11th–12th centuries)

The Sudarium's securely documented history begins in the north-Spanish city of Oviedo, where it is kept in the Arca Santa, a relic-chest in the cathedral's Cámara Santa. The pivotal recorded event is the ceremonial opening of the chest on 14 March 1075 under King Alfonso VI, after which he had it clad in silver inscribed “the Holy Sudarium of Our Lord Jesus Christ.”4 The relic's earlier journey — carried from Jerusalem before the Persian sack of 614, via Alexandria and North Africa to Cartagena, Toledo, and at last Asturias, ahead of the Muslim advance — is told chiefly by Bishop Pelagius of Oviedo (d. c. 1153), whose reputation as a fabricator of charters (his nickname is “the Prince of Falsifiers”) makes the transfer-narrative a weak historical support: the documented record effectively begins in the eleventh century, not the first.5 Unlike the Shroud, the Sudarium bears no image — only blood and serum stains — and its case for antiquity rests not on a picture but on a claimed match with the Shroud, weighed separately.

The Shroud appears at Lirey; a bishop calls it painted (c. 1354–1390)

The Turin Shroud enters certain history in the collegiate church of Lirey, in the Champagne, held by the French knight Geoffroi de Charny around 1354–1357. Within a generation the local bishop denounced it: in 1389 Pierre d'Arcis, bishop of Troyes, sent a memorandum to Antipope Clement VII reporting that his predecessor Henri de Poitiers had investigated the cloth about 1355 and found it “cunningly painted, the truth being attested by the artist who had painted it, to wit, that it was a work of human skill and not miraculously wrought.”6 In 1390 Clement VII resolved the dispute by permitting the cloth's exposition only if it were publicly declared a “figure or representation” of the true shroud, not the original.7 The d'Arcis memorandum survives only as an unsigned draft and hedges its charge (“as it is said”), so its evidentiary weight is itself disputed — but the plain fact stands that the Shroud's first documented owner met a formal ecclesiastical verdict of human manufacture, and that this earliest notice falls exactly where the radiocarbon date would later fall.

Fire, the House of Savoy, and Turin (1453–1578)

Charny's granddaughter sold the cloth to the House of Savoy in 1453; it was kept at the ducal chapel in Chambéry, where a fire in 1532 scorched it through its folds and molten silver from the reliquary burned the symmetrical holes still visible, repaired by Poor Clare nuns. In 1578 Duke Emanuele Filiberto moved it to his new capital, Turin, where it has remained — giving the relic its enduring name and its home in the cathedral of San Giovanni Battista.8

Secondo Pia's negative reopens the question (1898)

The modern controversy is born from a photograph. When the lawyer and amateur photographer Secondo Pia was allowed to photograph the Shroud in May 1898, he found that his glass-plate negative showed a startlingly lifelike positive image — as though the cloth itself were a photographic negative, its lights and darks reversed. The effect suggested a subtlety no medieval forger was thought to have intended, and it turned a fading relic into a scientific problem that has not since been closed.9

STURP examines the cloth: no paint, no mechanism (1978)

In October 1978 the Shroud of Turin Research Project (STURP), a team of about thirty scientists, was given five days of direct access. Their 1981 summary concluded that the image is a superficial straw-yellow discoloration of only the topmost fibers, that “no pigments, paints, dyes or stains have been found on the fibrils,” that it “is not the product of an artist,” and — candidly — that “the problem remains unsolved.”10 The microscopist Walter McCrone dissented, reporting iron-oxide and vermilion particles and arguing the image was painted about 1355; STURP's chemists rejected this as environmental contamination, and the dispute over the image's origin was never fully resolved — a genuinely open scientific question this page does not pretend is closed.11

Radiocarbon: the Shroud dated to 1260–1390 (1988)

In 1988 postage-stamp samples cut from one corner of the Shroud were dated independently by accelerator mass spectrometry at Arizona, Oxford, and Zurich, under British Museum coordination. The result, published in Nature, was a calibrated date of AD 1260–1390 at 95% confidence — “conclusive evidence that the linen of the Shroud of Turin is mediaeval” — matching, to within a few years, its first appearance at Lirey.12 The result did not end the debate. Raymond Rogers argued in 2005 that the sampled corner was a later “invisible” repair unrepresentative of the main cloth; peer-reviewed statistical reanalyses of the raw data (Riani and Atkinson 2013; Casabianca and colleagues 2019) found the sub-samples measurably heterogeneous, a real criticism of the sampling.13 But the conservators who examined the cloth found no mend, and no reanalysis yields a first-century date; the medieval result remains the scholarly consensus, and what it does and does not settle is weighed on its own page.

The Sudarium dated, and matched to the Shroud (1990s–2000s)

The Oviedo cloth was itself radiocarbon-dated (samples arranged by Pierluigi Baima Bollone, run at Tucson and Toronto, with a further 2007 test): the results cluster around the seventh century AD — roughly the sixth to ninth centuries, most probably the eighth — ruling out a first-century origin as the labs read it.14 Alongside the dating, the Spanish Center for Sindonology, Mark Guscin, and Alan and Mary Whanger pressed a forensic case that the Sudarium and the Shroud wrapped the same head: a shared AB blood grouping, and dozens of claimed geometric coincidences between the two patterns of bloodstains.15 The claim is the Sudarium's chief bid for antiquity — and its chief difficulty, since the two cloths' own radiocarbon dates lie centuries apart, so that “the same body” requires both to be wrong.16

An unresolved standoff (present)

The honest sum is a standoff of unequal parts. On one side, the two independent radiocarbon programmes and the documentary records agree in dating both relics well after the first century, and the Shroud's earliest notice is a bishop's finding of forgery. On the other, a real anomaly persists: no proposed mechanism — medieval artistry or otherwise — has been shown to reproduce the Shroud's image in all its features, and the sampling and statistics of the 1988 test remain a live methodological argument. The wiki takes the supernatural as genuinely possible and does not discount the image because it lacks a natural explanation — but an unexplained image is not by itself an ancient cloth (possibility ≠ proof). The weight of the physical and documentary evidence points medieval; the image keeps the question from being simply closed.17

Arguments

Related arguments — the Shroud’s antiquity

The physical and documentary bids to break the medieval verdict from the evidence itself rather than by faulting the laboratory — either by putting the Shroud before its 1988 radiocarbon window of 1260–1390, or by showing the image could not be a medieval manufacture at all. Each is weighed adversarially on its own page: the two dating bids come out weak, and the image-formation mechanism — the strongest live anomaly — comes out contested. They are best read together as one campaign against the date.

Does the Hungarian Pray Codex depict the Shroud before 1200? (assessment: weak)

The Pray Codex — a Hungarian manuscript firmly dated c. 1192–1195 whose entombment miniature is read (Wilson, Fanti) as portraying the Turin Shroud: a nude, low-crossed, thumbless Christ, a herringbone weave, and an L-shaped set of circles matching the Shroud’s “poker holes” (which predate the 1532 fire). If sound it would put the cloth before 1200 and break the radiocarbon window. Weighed adversarially it comes out weak: each feature has an independent twelfth-century explanation — the posture is a common entombment type, the thumbless hands a drawing-convention, the “weave” the standard rendering of a stone sarcophagus lid (a separate grave-cloth is drawn beside it), and the “poker holes” a selection from red circles that ornament the whole illumination, matched to a letter four random points readily form. Not unsupported — the convergence is a genuine coincidence and the argument is honestly advanced — but a cluster of conventional motifs is not a portrait, and even a real resemblance would show only that some image-cloth existed by 1195, not that this dated linen did.

Do pollen and plant-images tie the Shroud to first-century Palestine? (assessment: weak)

The pollen & plant-images — Max Frei’s claim of ~34 pollen taxa, some “exclusively” Palestinian/Anatolian, tracing a Jerusalem→Anatolia→Europe route, and Avinoam Danin’s faint plant-images (Gundelia tournefortii + Zygophyllum dumosum) said to co-occur only near Jerusalem and to bloom at Passover. Weighed adversarially it is weak: everything load-bearing — the “only near Jerusalem” geography, the spring season — needs species-level identifications the re-examiners could not confirm (Baruch verified 3 of Frei’s 34; Litt, finding the grains wax-obscured, doubted even the anchoring Gundelia), the method was uncontrolled and never reproduced, and the plant-images are too faint to argue from. Not unsupported — pollen really is present and the assemblage has a Near-Eastern cast — but a contested pollen smear does not date a linen, and a granted Levantine signature would show only where the cloth travelled, not when its threads were woven.

How was the Shroud’s image formed? (assessment: contested)

The image-formation mechanism — the strongest live pro-Shroud anomaly, and deliberately left open on the timeline above. Three accounts compete: an unexplained superficial anomaly (STURP’s 1978–81 finding — no pigment, not an artist, micron-deep, negative-like, 3-D-encoding, “the problem remains unsolved”); medieval / artefactual manufacture (McCrone’s paint; Nickell’s bas-relief rubbing; Garlaschelli’s 2010 full life-size frottage, which recovers the superficiality, negativity, and part of the 3-D effect); and a resurrection radiation burst (Jackson, Rucker, McAvoy 2025), which openly concedes no known physics produces it. Weighed adversarially it comes out contested: judged as chemistry the image is a real, still-unsolved anomaly no reproduction yet matches in every feature at once — but judged as evidence for antiquity it is weak, because “unexplained” is neither “ancient” nor “miraculous,” the image-chemistry encodes no date, and the radiation reading is reverse-engineered from the one object it explains. The wiki grants the anomaly without laundering it into a first-century cloth.

Is the Shroud of Turin the authentic burial cloth of Jesus? — weak

The flagship question, weighed adversarially and on its best available case. For authenticity: the image is a real and still-unexplained anomaly — superficial, negative-like, encoding three-dimensional relief, and not (STURP concluded) a painting — and no medieval reproduction matches it in every feature. Against it: the 1988 radiocarbon date (1260–1390), the total absence of any securely documented existence before Lirey c. 1350, the d'Arcis memorandum's verdict of manufacture, and the anatomical and iconographic marks of a High-Gothic image. The verdict is weak — authenticity does not hold on the evidence — but not unsupported: the unexplained image is a genuine residue, and the wiki does not rule the cloth ancient or discount its mystery by a naturalistic reflex. An unexplained image, though, is not an ancient cloth.

Does the 1988 radiocarbon test reliably date the Shroud to 1260–1390? — sound

The dating sub-question, held to the wiki's dating-method discipline. Three laboratories agreeing, coordinated by the British Museum, and a result that converges with the cloth's first documented appearance, make the medieval date strong. The counter-case — Rogers's “invisible reweave,” contamination, and the peer-reviewed heterogeneity of the sub-samples — is a legitimate criticism of a single, non-random, corner sample and is not dismissed here; but the conservators found no mend, and no reanalysis produces an ancient date. The reliability of the medieval result rates sound as the consensus of radiocarbon scientists, with the honest caveat that the sampling was methodologically imperfect and the call for a fresh, multi-site test is reasonable.

Do the Sudarium of Oviedo and the Shroud come from the same body? — weak

The claim that the two relics wrapped one crucified head — pressed via a shared AB blood grouping and scores of geometric coincidences between their bloodstains. It fails on its best case for one decisive reason and several supporting ones: the cloths' own radiocarbon dates (Shroud 13th–14th c., Sudarium 7th c.) are centuries apart, so “the same body” needs both labs to be wrong; AB typing on aged, degraded bloodstains is unreliable (recent work finds AB reactivity even on unstained fabric); and the stain-overlay coincidences are subjective and, critics show, inflated. Rated weak — the correspondence is not established, and with it the Sudarium's own bid for first-century antiquity falls away.
