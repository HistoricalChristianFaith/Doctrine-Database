The Two Genealogies of Jesus (Matthew 1 vs. Luke 3)

Related doctrines: Hard passages — see also Who Killed Goliath? (2 Samuel 21:19) · How Tall Was Goliath? (six or four cubits) · What Jesus Wrote on the Ground (John 8:6)

Two of the Gospels open the life of Jesus with a family tree, and the two trees do not match. Matthew begins from Abraham and climbs down the royal line — "David the king begat Solomon…" — through the kings of Judah to "Jacob begat Joseph the husband of Mary, of whom was born Jesus." Luke, at the Jordan, runs the other way, up from "Jesus… being (as was supposed) the son of Joseph, which was the son of Heli," through a different descent from David — not through Solomon and the kings but through David's other son Nathan — all the way back to "Adam, which was the son of God." Both lines pass through Joseph, whom both Evangelists are at pains to say was not Jesus's natural father. And between David and Joseph the two lists scarcely touch: Matthew gives Joseph's father as Jacob, Luke gives him as Heli; the intervening names differ almost entirely (only Shealtiel and Zerubbabel appear in both), and Luke's stretch from David to Jesus is nearly half again as long as Matthew's.1

So Joseph appears to have two fathers, Jesus two irreconcilable pedigrees. The difficulty was felt early and pressed hard: the pagan emperor Julian, in his book against the Christians, listed the clashing fathers of Joseph among the Gospels' self-contradictions, and Jerome answered him by name. The church's replies fall into a few enduring families — that the two lists are both Joseph's, one giving his father by nature and the other by law (the levirate-marriage solution of Julius Africanus, or Augustine's variant by adoption); that Luke was in fact tracing Mary's descent under her husband's name; or, in the modern critical reading, that neither genealogy is a biological record at all, and the two are not meant to be harmonized. This page gathers the witnesses to the crux, earliest first — the harmonizers and the doubters — and weighs the competing solutions under Arguments.

Timeline

Julius Africanus (c. 220)

Julius Africanus, the Christian chronographer, gives the solution that fathered all the rest. In his Letter to Aristides he first sweeps away the easy answer that Matthew traces a "royal" line and Luke a "priestly" one — both descents run through the royal tribe of Judah, so that reading "establish[es] the truth by a false statement."2 His own account rests on Israel's law of levirate marriage (Deut. 25:5): Jacob and Heli were half-brothers by one mother, Estha, but different fathers (Matthan of Solomon's line, Melchi of Nathan's); when Heli died childless, Jacob "raised up seed to him" and begot Joseph — "his own son by nature, but the son of Heli by law." So Matthew names the natural father, Luke the legal one, and both are true.3 Africanus claims the framework of the story came from the desposynoi, the surviving kinsmen of Jesus, though he candidly admits it "is not supported by testimony."4

Eusebius of Caesarea (c. 320)

Eusebius is the reason Africanus's letter survives: he quotes it at length in his Church History, prefacing it with the note that "many suppose" the two genealogies "are at variance with one another," and that "every believer, in ignorance of the truth, has been zealous to invent some explanation."5 He endorses the levirate solution as the best available and draws from it a further point the later tradition would seize on: since the Law forbade marriage across tribes, Joseph's Davidic descent "virtually" shows Mary too "to be of the same tribe with him."6 Elsewhere he reads Luke's guarded "as was supposed" as the Evangelist reporting "the common opinion of many, not his own," and notes that some Jews shunned the Solomon line because of Jeremiah's curse on Jeconiah.7

Ambrosiaster (c. 380)

The anonymous Latin commentator known as Ambrosiaster is the tradition's early dissenter: he examines the levirate solution and rejects it as "neither plausible nor useful for anything."8 On his reading the two lists "fork" from David: Matthew descends through Solomon to Jacob and Joseph, Luke ascends through Nathan from Heli, and Joseph and Heli are simply two men "of one and the same stock" — kinsmen, so that "in the same sense in which Jesus was called Joseph's son, he was also Heli's son," and of all their common tribe.9 His target is the heretic Photinus, who made Christ begin only with Mary; the genealogy, ascending past Adam to God, proves the Son "before all flesh."10

Jerome (c. 398)

Jerome, in his Commentary on Matthew, names the objector: "this passage is objected to us by the Emperor Julian in his Discrepancy of the Evangelists. Matthew calls Joseph the son of Jacob, Luke makes him the son of Heli."11 Julian, he replies, "did not know the Scripture manner — one was his father by nature, the other by law" — the levirate answer, for which Jerome points the reader to "Africanus the chronologist, and Eusebius of Caesarea," who "have disputed [it] more fully."12 To the further question why Joseph's line is given at all, since he was not Jesus's father, Jerome answers that Scripture does not trace genealogies through women, and that Joseph and Mary "were of the same tribe," enrolled together at Bethlehem "as being come of one stock."13

Augustine (c. 400)

Augustine, in the Harmony of the Gospels, lays out three ways the doubled father might be resolved — a maternal grandfather, an adoptive father, or the levirate — and prefers adoption: it is "most probable that Luke took the origin by adoption," for Matthew's insistent "begat… begat" marks natural descent to Jacob, while "more easily is a man said to be his son by whom he was adopted."14 He finds a fitting sense in it: Luke gives the genealogy at the baptism and runs it up to God, "as thus more clearly pointing Him out as the Priest that should make atonement," since "by adoption we are made the sons of God."15 (In his later Retractations Augustine withdrew his over-firm rejection of the levirate, conceding Africanus's reading as also admissible.)16

John of Damascus (c. 743)

John of Damascus, gathering the Greek tradition in An Exact Exposition of the Orthodox Faith, restates Africanus's levirate scheme almost verbatim — Matthan of Solomon's line and Melchi of Nathan's by one wife, Jacob and Heli brothers, Jacob raising seed to the childless Heli, so "Joseph… is by nature the son of Jacob, of the line of Solomon, but by law the son of Heli of the line of Nathan."17 But he adds what the Gospels leave silent: a descent for Mary, through Nathan's line by way of "Panther… Barpanther … Joachim," Mary's father — so that the Virgin herself is shown to be of David's house, and the demonstration of Joseph's tribe (whom the law required her to marry within) doubles as a demonstration of hers.18

Raymond E. Brown (1977)

Raymond E. Brown, the leading modern scholar of the infancy narratives, closes the timeline with the critical verdict. He holds that neither list is a biographical record: "the Lucan genealogy is no less theological in purpose than Matthew's and no freer of historical difficulties," and it "scarcely constitutes an exact record of Jesus' biological ancestry."19 The genealogies, he concludes, "tell us how to evaluate Jesus" but "nothing certain about his grandparents." And he dismisses the harmonization that began with Africanus: "the theory of a levirate marriage solves so little and has so many difficulties that it should be abandoned as a solution."20

Arguments

The proposed resolutions of the crux cut across the timeline — each is advocated across many centuries rather than sitting at one date — and are weighed adversarially on their own pages, each with an assessment of the reading it serves. All of them accept the same starting point: the two lists differ, and both run through Joseph. What they dispute is how (or whether) the difference resolves.

Related arguments — reconciling the two genealogies

The three readings below are the church's rival harmonizations. Two keep both lists on Joseph — one splitting his fatherhood into a natural line and a legal one, the other taking Matthew as the royal succession and Luke as Joseph's natural descent — while the third hands Luke's list to Mary. They answer the same question in incompatible ways and are best read together — against the modern critical judgment (Brown) that neither is a biological record and no harmonization succeeds.

Are Matthew and Luke both Joseph's line, the doubled father one legal and one natural? (assessment: contested)

Both lists are Joseph's; one father natural, one legal — the dominant patristic solution, in two forms: Africanus's levirate marriage (Jacob the natural father, Heli the legal, or the reverse) and Augustine's adoption. It reconciles the texts without emending either and answers the pagan objection on its own terms — a man really could have "two fathers" under Israel's law. The interpretation is contested: it is coherent and ancient, but the levirate form rests on an otherwise-unrecorded tradition (the wife Estha, the pairing of Matthan and Melchi) that Africanus himself grants is unattested, it does not account for the whole divergence of the two lists (which differ at almost every name, not only at Joseph's father), and modern critics from Brown on judge it an ad hoc device.21

Does Matthew give the royal (legal) succession and Luke Joseph's natural line, both through Joseph? (assessment: plausible)

Matthew the royal (legal) succession, Luke Joseph's natural line — a distinct harmonization, classically J. Gresham Machen's (1930): the two lists differ along their whole length because they are two kinds of list, Matthew tracing the legal succession to David's throne and Luke Joseph's actual biological descent, both meeting at Shealtiel/Zerubbabel and at Joseph. The interpretation is plausible: it rests on a demonstrable fact — Matthew's list is schematic, dropping three reigning kings (Ahaziah, Joash, Amaziah) to make its 3×14 — so treating it as a throne-succession rather than a birth register is warranted by the text; it thereby explains the lists' whole divergence (which the levirate reading cannot) and turns the curse on Jeconiah (Jer. 22:30) into a feature. It stays short of probable because its final link still borrows the levirate device, "reigning kings" holds only to the exile, and — with both lists on Joseph — it secures Jesus's legal Davidic right but not his descent by blood.22

Does Luke give Mary's genealogy rather than Joseph's? (assessment: weak)

Luke traces Mary, not Joseph — the reading (ancient in seed, but especially popular since the Reformation) that Luke's list is really the Virgin's, "Heli" being Mary's father and Joseph his son-in-law, so that Jesus's biological Davidic descent comes through his mother. It has an obvious appeal: it gives Mary the Davidic blood the creeds confess, and removes the clash by assigning each list to a different parent. The interpretation is weak: Luke's own words make Joseph, not Mary, "the son of Heli," name no woman in the list, and were read by the whole early church — Africanus, Eusebius, Jerome, even the Mary-minded John of Damascus — as Joseph's pedigree; the Mary-genealogy construction is largely reverse-engineered to secure a conclusion (Mary's Davidic descent) that the tradition reached by other means.23

Does the curse on Jeconiah (Jer. 22:30) bar the Messiah's throne-claim? (assessment: plausible)

The curse on Jeconiah and Matthew's 3×14 schema — a Matthew-specific crux (not a rival harmonization but a support for the royal-succession reading). Matthew runs the royal line through Jeconiah, on whom Jeremiah wrote "no man of his seed shall prosper, sitting upon the throne of David"; and he reaches his announced "fourteen generations" thrice only by omitting three reigning kings and padding the last set. The interpretation — that the curse is no bar, and the schematic count confirms Matthew's list is a theological throne-succession — is plausible: the schema is demonstrably deliberate (fourteen = the numerical value of David), and the curse dissolves on a close reading (Jeremiah's "childless" king in fact had sons, the sentence is time-bound to the pre-exilic throne, and the line is expressly rehabilitated when Haggai restores the "signet" to Jeconiah's grandson Zerubbabel). It stays short of sound because the popular resolution — that the virgin birth "breaks" a bloodline curse — is weak and self-undermining (legal succession is itself a throne-claim, and both genealogies converge on Jeconiah's grandson Zerubbabel), so the point is carried by reading Jeremiah rightly, not by severing the blood.24
