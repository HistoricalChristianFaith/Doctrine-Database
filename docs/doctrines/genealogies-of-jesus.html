The Two Genealogies of Jesus (Matthew 1 vs. Luke 3)

(1 Kings 4:26 vs. 2 Chronicles 9:25) · Ahaziah's Age: 22 or 42? (2 Kings 8:26 vs. 2 Chronicles 22:2) · The Bronze Sea: 2,000 or 3,000 Baths? (1 Kings 7:26 vs. 2 Chronicles 4:5) · What Jesus Wrote on the Ground (John 8:6) · The Death of Judas (Matthew 27 vs. Acts 1) · Naming the Wrong Prophet (Mark 1:2; Matthew 27:9) · Baptized for the Dead (1 Corinthians 15:29)

Two of the Gospels open the life of Jesus with a family tree, and the two trees do not match. Matthew begins from Abraham and climbs down the royal line — "David the king begat Solomon…" — through the kings of Judah to "Jacob begat Joseph the husband of Mary, of whom was born Jesus." Luke, at the Jordan, runs the other way, up from "Jesus… being (as was supposed) the son of Joseph, which was the son of Heli," through a different descent from David — not through Solomon and the kings but through David's other son Nathan — all the way back to "Adam, which was the son of God." Both lines pass through Joseph, whom both Evangelists are at pains to say was not Jesus's natural father. And between David and Joseph the two lists scarcely touch: Matthew gives Joseph's father as Jacob, Luke gives him as Heli; the intervening names differ almost entirely (only Shealtiel and Zerubbabel appear in both), and Luke's stretch from David to Jesus is nearly half again as long as Matthew's.1

So Joseph appears to have two fathers, and Jesus two pedigrees that do not match. The difficulty was felt early and pressed hard: the emperor Julian, in his book against the Christians, listed the clashing fathers of Joseph among the Gospels' self-contradictions, and Jerome answered him by name. Christian answers have taken several forms: that the two lists are both Joseph's, one giving his father by nature and the other by law (the levirate-marriage solution of Julius Africanus, or Augustine's variant by adoption); that Luke was tracing Mary's descent under her husband's name (a reading that Francis Xavier Patrizzi, as reported by John F. McCarthy, traces to Annius of Viterbo, c. 1490, and that McCarthy reports in Cornelius a Lapide)2; or, in the critical reading of Raymond Brown, that neither genealogy is a record of biological ancestry and the two are not meant to be harmonized. This page gathers the witnesses to the question, earliest first, and lists the disputed readings at the end.

Timeline

Irenaeus of Lyons (c. 180)

Irenaeus, the earliest witness here, does not discuss the clash between the two lists; his comment concerns where Luke's ascending line ends. Luke traces the pedigree of Jesus back past Abraham to "Adam, which was the son of God," and Irenaeus makes that Adam-ward reach the hinge of his recapitulation theology: Christ the Second Adam "has summed up in Himself all nations dispersed from Adam downwards, and all languages and generations of men, together with Adam himself" — on his reading, the reason Luke, unlike Matthew, climbs to the head of the race. His is also the earliest extant count of the list, reckoned at "seventy-two generations," fewer than the names in the received text.3 He also states that, because the first Adam had God, not a man, for his maker, the Second Adam was not "begotten of Joseph": for Irenaeus Joseph is not Jesus's natural father.4

Julius Africanus (c. 220)

Julius Africanus, the Christian chronographer, gives the earliest extant statement of the levirate solution. In his Letter to Aristides he first rejects the answer that Matthew traces a "royal" line and Luke a "priestly" one — both descents run through the royal tribe of Judah, so that reading "establish[es] the truth by a false statement."5 His own account rests on Israel's law of levirate marriage (Deut. 25:5): Jacob and Heli were half-brothers by one mother, Estha, but different fathers (Matthan of Solomon's line, Melchi of Nathan's); when Heli died childless, Jacob "raised up seed to him" and begot Joseph — "his own son by nature, but the son of Heli by law." So Matthew names the natural father, Luke the legal one, and on his account both are true.6 Africanus says the framework of the story came from the desposynoi, the surviving kinsmen of Jesus, and adds that it "is not supported by testimony."7

Eusebius of Caesarea (c. 320)

Eusebius is the reason Africanus's letter survives: he quotes it at length in his Church History, prefacing it with the note that "many suppose" the two genealogies "are at variance with one another," and that "every believer, in ignorance of the truth, has been zealous to invent some explanation."8 He presents the levirate solution as "the account of the matter which has come down to us" and draws from it a further point: since the Law forbade marriage across tribes, Joseph's Davidic descent "virtually" shows Mary too "to be of the same tribe with him."9 Elsewhere he reads Luke's guarded "as was supposed" as the Evangelist reporting "the common opinion of many, not his own," and notes that some Jews shunned the Solomon line because of Jeremiah's curse on Jeconiah.10

Ambrosiaster (c. 380)

The anonymous Latin commentator known as Ambrosiaster examines the levirate solution and rejects it as "neither plausible nor useful for anything."11 On his reading the two lists "fork" from David: Matthew descends through Solomon to Jacob and Joseph, Luke ascends through Nathan from Heli, and Joseph and Heli are simply two men "of one and the same stock" — kinsmen, so that "in the same sense in which Jesus was called Joseph's son, he was also Heli's son," and of all their common tribe.12 He writes against Photinus, who held, in Ambrosiaster's words, that Christ "is only from Mary, and did not exist before"; for Ambrosiaster the genealogy, ascending past Adam to God, assigns Christ to be the Son of God "before all flesh."13

Jerome (c. 398)

Jerome, in his Commentary on Matthew, names the objector as Julian: "this passage is objected to us by the Emperor Julian in his Discrepancy of the Evangelists. Matthew calls Joseph the son of Jacob, Luke makes him the son of Heli."14 Julian, he replies, "did not know the Scripture manner — one was his father by nature, the other by law" — the levirate answer, for which Jerome points the reader to "Africanus the chronologist, and Eusebius of Caesarea," who "have disputed [it] more fully."15 To the further question why Joseph's line is given at all, since he was not Jesus's father, Jerome answers that Scripture does not trace genealogies through women, and that Joseph and Mary "were of the same tribe," enrolled together at Bethlehem "as being come of one stock."16

Augustine (c. 400)

Augustine lays out three ways the doubled father might be resolved — a maternal grandfather, an adoptive father, or the levirate — and in the Harmony of the Gospels prefers adoption: it is "most probable that Luke took the origin by adoption," for Matthew's insistent "begat… begat" marks natural descent to Jacob, while "more easily is a man said to be his son by whom he was adopted."17 He finds a fitting sense in it: Luke gives the genealogy at the baptism and runs it up to God, "as thus more clearly pointing Him out as the Priest that should make atonement," since "by adoption we are made the sons of God."18 (Augustine later revisited the passage in his Retractations; John F. McCarthy reports that he there withdrew the adoption theory in favour of Africanus's levirate reading.)19

Bede (c. 710)

The Venerable Bede, in his Commentary on Luke, carries the harmonizing tradition into early-medieval Latin learning, gathering the earlier answers. To "the knotty question… how Joseph could have had two fathers," he gives Africanus's levirate solution almost whole (Estha married in succession by a Solomon-line and a Nathan-line husband, Jacob and Heli half-brothers, Jacob raising seed to the childless Heli), so that Matthew's "Jacob begot Joseph" and Luke's "son of Heli" stand "both… validated and intact"; and he notes that Africanus's own version named "Melchi" where the received Luke has Matthat, "because either his copy had it so, or in the history where he learned these things, he found the same Matthat had two names."20 With Eusebius and Jerome he holds that Luke's list is Joseph's, not Mary's, "for it is not customary in the Scriptures to set forth the order of women in genealogies," yet because Joseph and Mary "were from one tribe" and "registered together in Bethlehem," Joseph's pedigree exhibits Mary's stock as well; and Luke's ascending, God-ward form he reads with Augustine as "the origin of adoption," Joseph called "the son of Heli… because he was adopted by him."21

John of Damascus (c. 743)

John of Damascus, in An Exact Exposition of the Orthodox Faith, restates Africanus's levirate scheme almost verbatim — Matthan of Solomon's line and Melchi of Nathan's by one wife, Jacob and Heli brothers, Jacob raising seed to the childless Heli, so "Joseph… is by nature the son of Jacob, of the line of Solomon, but by law the son of Heli of the line of Nathan."22 He also gives a descent for Mary, over whose origin, he says, both Evangelists "pass in silence," through Nathan's line by way of "Panther… Barpanther … Joachim," Mary's father, presenting her as of David's house; and he holds that, since the law required marriage within the tribe, the demonstration of Joseph's descent suffices for hers.23

Raymond E. Brown (1977)

Raymond E. Brown, Catholic biblical scholar and author of The Birth of the Messiah, holds that neither list is a biographical record: "the Lucan genealogy is no less theological in purpose than Matthew's and no freer of historical difficulties," and it "scarcely constitutes an exact record of Jesus' biological ancestry."24 The genealogies, he concludes, "tell us how to evaluate Jesus" but "nothing certain about his grandparents." He rejects the levirate harmonization of Africanus: "the theory of a levirate marriage solves so little and has so many difficulties that it should be abandoned as a solution."25

Disputed readings

Questions about how the genealogies and their early witnesses have been read, each set out with its readings on its own page or on a related timeline.

  • Do the Gospel genealogies rest on a pedigree kept by Jesus's kinsmen (the desposynoi), as Julius Africanus reports? — the historical premise beneath Africanus's account, that the lists rest on a genealogy kept by Jesus's relatives after Herod (he says) burned the public registers.26

The "second Cainan" of Luke 3:36

Luke's list also holds an internal question of its own. Between Arphaxad and Shelah (Salah) it names a second Cainan — "which was the son of Cainan, which was the son of Arphaxad" (Luke 3:36) — a generation that stands in the Greek Old Testament (the Septuagint of Genesis 11 and Genesis 10:24) but is absent from the Hebrew of Genesis 11, which runs Arphaxad straight to Salah, and from the parallel lists in 1 Chronicles. The received text of Luke here agrees with the longer Greek reckoning. Whether that Cainan is original to Luke (and to Genesis 11) or a later addition to Luke's text, and whether the name was removed from the Hebrew, are set out on the "second Cainan" page of the rabbinic-corruption timeline.27
