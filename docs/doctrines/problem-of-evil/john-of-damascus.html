John of Damascus on the problem of evil

Dates: c. 743 (John of Damascus, monk of Mar Saba, c. 675–749; the key text is An Exact Exposition of the Orthodox Faith, Book II) · Wikipedia
Summary: The last of the Greek fathers gathered the settled Eastern answer into a textbook. In An Exact Exposition of the Orthodox Faith he states all three classical moves in crisp formulae: evil is no substance but the mere “absence of goodness, just as darkness… is absence of light”; moral evil arises from the free will of creatures — the devil “made good by the Creator, became of his own free-will the inventor of evil”; and God's providence governs all, working “partly according to good-will… and partly according to permission,” so that God is never the author of the evil He permits. His compact definitions became the fixed reference of Eastern theology and, through Latin translation, fed the Western schoolmen.

Claims & evidence

Evil is no substance — the absence of goodness, as darkness is the absence of light

For evil is nothing else than absence of goodness, just as darkness also is absence of light. For goodness is the light of the mind, and, similarly, evil is the darkness of the mind.

— An Exact Exposition of the Orthodox Faith, Book II, ch. 4, source.1 The darkness-and-light image is the Eastern shorthand for the privation thesis: evil is not a stuff God made but the failure of a good that ought to be present. John repeats the formula for moral evil in the same words at the end of Book II: “wickedness is nothing else than the withdrawal of goodness, just as darkness is nothing else than the withdrawal of light.”2 It is the same answer as Basil, Gregory of Nyssa, and Dionysius, condensed into a definition a student could memorize.

Moral evil originates in the free will of the creature

…the devil who, although made good by the Creator, became of his own free-will the inventor of evil, and by the powers who revolted with him, that is the demons, and by the other troops of angels who abode in goodness.

— An Exact Exposition of the Orthodox Faith, Book II, ch. 27, source.3 John grounds moral evil in freedom, which he ties to reason itself: “either man is an irrational being, or, if he is rational, he is master of his acts and endowed with free-will.” The rational creature “leads nature rather than nature him,” and so is justly praised or blamed. The devil received “no trace whatever of evil in himself” from his Creator but “of his free choice was changed” — the angelic fall, like every sin, is self-authored, so that God is in no way its cause.

Providence governs all — by good-will and by permission

Now the works of Providence are partly according to the good-will (of God) and partly according to permission. Works of good-will include all those that are undeniably good, while works of permission are… For Providence often permits the just man to encounter misfortune in order that he may reveal to others the virtue that lies concealed within him, as was the case with Job.

— An Exact Exposition of the Orthodox Faith, Book II, ch. 29, source.4 The distinction of good-will from permission is John's instrument for keeping God clear of authoring evil while remaining sovereign over it: God positively wills the good, and merely permits the evil, bending it to remedial ends — the testing of Job, the humbling of Paul, the correction of the proud. He sorts this into God's “antecedent will… which springs from Himself” (that all be saved) and His “consequent will and permission… which has its origin in us” (that sinners bear the consequences of their choices). And natural evil he lodges not in God but in the fall:

For God did not create death, neither does He take delight in the destruction of living things… But death is the work rather of man, that is, its origin is in Adam's transgression, in like manner as all other punishments.

— An Exact Exposition of the Orthodox Faith, Book II, ch. 28, source.5 As with Augustine, death and its train are penal — a consequence of the creature's own transgression, not a flaw in the Creator's work.

Notes

  • John's stress that God's “original wish was that all should be saved and come to His Kingdom” (Book II, ch. 29) frames providence as fundamentally benevolent: “it was not for punishment that He formed us but to share in His goodness.” The antecedent/consequent-will distinction he uses here became a standard tool of later dogmatics for reconciling God's universal saving will with the loss of the impenitent.
  • The Exact Exposition is the third part of John's Fount of Knowledge, the first full systematic theology of the Greek church; it was translated into Latin in the twelfth century (Burgundio of Pisa) and cited by Peter Lombard and Aquinas, so John's crisp privation-and-providence formulae passed into the Western synthesis alongside those of Dionysius.
  • John appears on this wiki as a witness on several timelines — among them the Theotokos, the veneration of images, and the perpetual virginity of Mary — reflecting his role as the great gatherer of the Eastern tradition.
