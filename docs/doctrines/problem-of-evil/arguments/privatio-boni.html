Evil as privation of good (privatio boni) — does it hold?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — as a metaphysics of evil the privation theory is coherent, elegant, and remains the standard patristic–Thomist account: evil is no substance God made but the lack or corruption of a due good, so God can be the author of all being without being the author of evil. It is strong by the lights of classical metaphysics and answers the question it was built for — what is evil? But it is genuinely disputed by the lights of the phenomenology of suffering: the “positive-evil” objection (pain, cruelty, and malice present themselves as vividly real qualia, not mere absences) is live and unsettled, defended and attacked in current literature. And a second limit is decisive for this timeline: the privation thesis is a piece of a theodicy, not a theodicy — it says what evil is, not why an omnipotent, wholly good God permits it. Rated on its best metaphysical case it is defensible; rated as an answer to the problem of evil it is, by itself, incomplete. · advanced by Basil, Gregory of Nyssa, Augustine, Pseudo-Dionysius, and Thomas Aquinas

See also: the problem-of-evil timeline, where the privation answer runs from Basil and Gregory of Nyssa through Augustine and Pseudo-Dionysius to its systematic form in Aquinas; and the constructive theodicies (greater good, soul-making, free will) that supply the why this argument leaves open, including the free-will defense.

The claim

The dominant patristic and scholastic metaphysics of evil holds that evil is not a positive thing but a privation of a due good — a lack or corruption parasitic on some prior good, with no substance, no nature, and no efficient cause of its own. Its classic Latin statement is Augustine’s, in the Enchiridion (c. 421):

For what is that which we call evil but the absence of good? In the bodies of animals, disease and wounds mean nothing but the absence of health… for the wound or disease is not a substance, but a defect in the fleshly substance,—the flesh itself being a substance, and therefore something good, of which those evils—that is, privations of the good which we call health—are accidents. Just in the same way, what are called vices in the soul are nothing but privations of natural good.

— Augustine, Enchiridion 11.1 The thesis does real theological work, and that is why it matters here. If evil were a thing, it would need a maker, and either God made it (and is its author) or a rival first principle did (Manichaean dualism). The privation account dissolves the dilemma: God is the cause of all being, being as such is good, and evil is precisely the absence of a being or good that ought to be present — so nothing God made is evil, and evil needs no positive cause. It lets the one good Creator stand as author of everything real without standing as author of evil.

Aquinas gives the thesis its most exact form, sharpening the fathers on one point: not every absence of good is evil, only the privation of a good that is due to a thing by its nature.

it must be that by the name of evil is signified the absence of good… But not every absence of good is evil. For absence of good can be taken in a privative and in a negative sense. Absence of good, taken negatively, is not evil … the absence of good, taken in a privative sense, is an evil; as, for instance, the privation of sight is called blindness.

— Aquinas, Summa Theologiae I, q. 48, aa. 1–3.2 A stone’s lack of sight is a mere negation and no evil; an animal’s is a privation of a due good, and so an evil. On this refinement evil is always the corruption or deficiency of some particular good in some particular subject — it cannot exist except in a good, since the good is what it deprives.

Who advances it

The reading is the common property of the Fathers, East and West. Basil denies evil is “a living animated essence”; his brother Gregory of Nyssa calls it “the non-existence of the good”; Augustine, a former Manichee who had once made evil a rival dark substance, makes the privation account the cornerstone of the Latin answer; Pseudo-Dionysius gives it its most rigorous ancient form, arguing that a cause produces only what is like itself (fire cannot make cold), so the Good cannot produce evil, which therefore “has not a subsistence, but a parasitical subsistence… being itself nothing in any manner of way whatever.”3 It was Dionysius’s treatment that Aquinas built the scholastic doctrine on, and through Aquinas the privation theory became the standard account of Catholic and much of classical Protestant theology. It has roots in the pagan Neoplatonism of Plotinus, who had already located evil in the privation of form — a philosophical inheritance the Fathers baptized and corrected (against Plotinus they refuse to identify evil with matter, which as created being is good).

The evidence for

It is metaphysically coherent and does the work asked of it. Grant the two premises the tradition argues for elsewhere — that being as such is good (the doctrine of the transcendentals), and that God is the one efficient cause of all being — and the privation account follows cleanly. Evil, having no positive being, needs no positive cause; it enters as a defect in a good, the way blindness enters an eye or rust a blade. This is not an evasion but a genuine explanatory economy: it accounts for the parasitic character everyone grants evil actually has — corruption always presupposes something good to corrupt, disease a body to sicken, a lie a truth to deny, malice a will and its goods to pervert. There is no such thing as a purely evil being, because total privation of good is total privation of being, i.e. nothing. That observation is hard to deny and the theory explains it.

It secures monotheism against dualism. The theory’s historical payload is decisive against the Manichaeans and every two-principle cosmology: one does not need an evil god or an eternal evil matter to explain evil, because evil is not the kind of thing that needs a source at all. Dionysius’s argument that like produces like — that a single good First Cause cannot be the origin of its own contrary — is a real result, and it is why the account was reached for precisely by thinkers (Augustine, Dionysius) fighting dualism.

A privation can have terrible positive effects. The theory is often caricatured as making evil trivial or unreal — “if evil is nothing, why fear it?” But privation of a due good is not nonentity; it is a real defect in a real thing, and its consequences are as real as a severed artery. A missing keystone is an absence, and the collapse it causes is catastrophic. The Fathers are explicit that evil, though no substance, is genuinely present and genuinely destructive; the ontological claim (“no positive being”) never implied a practical one (“nothing to worry about”).

Adversarial assessment

Two objections recur, and they cut in different directions. The first attacks the metaphysics; the second concedes it and denies that it settles the problem the timeline is about.

1. The positive-evil objection (does pain reduce to an absence?). The hardest challenge is that some evils seem to be positive realities, not mere lacks. A toothache, the felt agony of torture, the active relish of cruelty — these present themselves as vividly real, intense, present qualia, and it strains language to call a throbbing pain “the absence” of something. G. Stanley Kane and others press that pain is a positive experience that on the privation theory would have to be at once an evil (as pain) and a good (as a positive existent), which looks incoherent.4 The objection is serious and it is live — it is exactly why some philosophers abandon strict privation for a “corruption of the good” (corruptio boni) account that need not call every evil an absence.5 But the privationist has real replies, and the exchange is genuinely unresolved rather than a rout. (a) The evil in pain is not the sensation as such but the privation it registers or inflicts — disorder, damage, the loss of the good of health or wholeness; John Hick, who insists pain and suffering are ontologically positive, still locates their badness in a privation.6 (b) Modern defenders separate the sensory-somatic aspect of pain (real, positive, but not in itself the evil — pain can be useful, even good) from the affective “painfulness” whose badness is best cashed out as loss and negation; on that division the intuition “what is so real in pain is what is bad about it” comes apart.7 Neither side has carried the field: the metaphysician’s parsing is principled, the phenomenologist’s protest that it explains away what is most obvious about suffering is not silenced. This is the paradigm of a contested result — strong by one discipline’s lights, weak by another’s.

2. It answers “what is evil?” not “why does God allow it?” (a piece of a theodicy, not a theodicy). This is the limit that matters most for the problem of evil, and it is a defeater, not a rival thesis (so it is folded in here, not split to its own page): even granting the privation account entirely, it does no theodical work by itself. To say evil is a privation is to describe its metaphysical status; it is not yet a reason why a good, omnipotent God permits so much of it. Blindness is a privation — that classification does nothing to explain why God allows a child to be born blind. The privation theory clears God of being evil’s positive author (He causes only being, and being is good), which answers a real question about the source of evil; but the sharpest form of the problem is not “where does evil come from?” but “why is it permitted?” — and that question the privation account hands off, untouched, to the greater-good, soul-making, free-will, and providence answers the rest of the timeline supplies. Aquinas himself treats them as distinct moves: the privation analysis (q. 48) says what evil is; the separate claim that God “should allow evil to exist, and out of it produce good” (q. 2, a. 3 ad 1) is the theodicy.8 A proponent who offers “evil is just a privation” as an answer to the problem of evil has confused the two questions, and should be corrected on exactly this point — not because the metaphysics is wrong, but because it is answering a different question.

A note on scope. A third, weaker complaint — that the theory makes evil “unreal” and so trivial — rests on a confusion already addressed above: privation of a due good is a real defect with real and often devastating effects, and the Fathers never denied it. That objection does not lower the rating.

Assessment

Contested. Keep the two questions the tradition itself keeps distinct, and the verdict follows. As a metaphysics of evil—an account of what evil is—the privation theory is coherent, economical, and still the standard classical account; it explains the parasitic character evil actually has, it secures one good Creator against dualism, and its ontological modesty (“no positive being”) never entailed the false practical claim that evil is nothing to fear. Against the strongest attack on the metaphysics—that pain and positive cruelty are real qualia, not absences—it has principled replies (locate the evil in the privation the sensation registers or inflicts; split the sensory from the affective) that are defended in current literature but have not silenced the phenomenological protest; that live standoff, strong by metaphysics’ lights and weak by the phenomenology of suffering’s, is what makes the interpretation contested rather than sound. And rated as an answer to the problem of evil it is, by design, only a piece: it clears God of authoring evil’s positive being, but it does not say why He permits evil—that is the separate work of the greater-good, soul-making, free-will, and providence theodicies. The rating tracks the interpretation, not any one proponent; flag as overreach only the slide from “evil is a privation” (a metaphysical thesis it earns) to “so the problem of evil is solved” (a theodicy it never was).
