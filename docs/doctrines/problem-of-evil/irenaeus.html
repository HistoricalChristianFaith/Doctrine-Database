Irenaeus on the problem of evil

Dates: c. 180 · Wikipedia
Summary: Irenaeus, bishop of Lyons, gives the earliest sustained Christian answer surveyed here to why God permits evil: God made man free, so moral evil is the cost of a will that can love God rather than obey by compulsion; and God made man immature, an “infant” meant to grow toward perfection through experience — including the experience of evil, since one cannot knowingly choose the good without knowing its contrary. Against the Gnostics (who blamed a defective creator) he locates evil in the free creature and treats its permission as the schooling of a maturing race.

Claims & evidence

Man made free, to obey God voluntarily

God made man a free [agent] from the beginning, possessing his own power, even as he does his own soul, to obey the behests (ad utendum sententia) of God voluntarily, and not by compulsion of God. For there is no coercion with God, but a good will [towards us] is present with Him continually.

— Against Heresies IV.37.1, source. On this account the root of moral evil is the free will God gave and will not override. Basil and Augustine, later on this timeline, also lodge moral evil in the free will.

Man made an “infant,” to grow toward perfection

so also it was possible for God Himself to have made man perfect from the first, but man could not receive this [perfection], being as yet an infant.

— Against Heresies IV.38.1, source. Irenaeus answers the objection “why did God not make man incapable of falling?” with a developmental answer: perfection is a growth, not an initial endowment — a mother could give strong food to her infant, “as the child is not yet able to receive more substantial nourishment.” John Hick named his “soul-making” theodicy (1966) “Irenaean” after this answer (see the modern debate).

Knowledge of the good and of its contrary

But how, if he had no knowledge of the contrary, could he have had instruction in that which is good? For there is thus a surer and an undoubted comprehension of matters submitted to us than the mere surmise arising from an opinion regarding them.

— Against Heresies IV.39.1, source. For Irenaeus the permission of evil serves knowledge: experience of the bitter teaches the sweet more surely than opinion. Lactantius (c. 313) also argues that without evil the material of wisdom and virtue is taken away.

Reception and context

  • Irenaeus does not use the language of privation (evil as a lack of good) found in Basil and Augustine; his answer is anthropological and developmental, grounded in freedom and growth rather than metaphysics of the good.
  • His framing is polemical — aimed at the Gnostic claim that the material world and its evils betray a botched or malicious creator. By lodging evil in the free creature and its immaturity, he defends the goodness of the one Creator without positing a rival dark principle.
  • Irenaeus appears also as a witness on the original sin, Mary as the New Eve, and intermediate state timelines.
