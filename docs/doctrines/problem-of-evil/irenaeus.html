Irenaeus on the problem of evil

Dates: c. 180 · Wikipedia
Summary: Irenaeus, bishop of Lyons, gives the earliest sustained Christian answer to why God permits evil, and it is a “soul-making” answer before the name: God made man free, so moral evil is the cost of a will that can love God rather than obey by compulsion; and God made man immature, an “infant” meant to grow toward perfection through experience — including the experience of evil, since one cannot knowingly choose the good without knowing its contrary. Against the Gnostics (who blamed a defective creator) he locates evil in the free creature and turns its very permission into the schooling of a maturing race.

Claims & evidence

God made man free, so evil is the cost of a will not coerced

God made man a free [agent] from the beginning, possessing his own power, even as he does his own soul, to obey the behests (ad utendum sententia) of God voluntarily, and not by compulsion of God. For there is no coercion with God, but a good will [towards us] is present with Him continually.

— Against Heresies IV.37.1, source. The root of moral evil is the free will God gave and will not override: a love that could not refuse would not be love. This is the free-will strand the whole later tradition (Basil, Augustine) would carry.

Man was made an “infant,” meant to grow toward a perfection he could not be given ready-made

so also it was possible for God Himself to have made man perfect from the first, but man could not receive this [perfection], being as yet an infant.

— Against Heresies IV.38.1, source. Irenaeus answers the objection “why did God not make man incapable of falling?” with a developmental theodicy: perfection is a growth, not an initial endowment — a mother could give strong food to her infant, “as the child is not yet able to receive more substantial nourishment.” This is the ancestor of the modern “soul-making” theodicy (Hick).

The good cannot be knowingly chosen without knowledge of its contrary

But how, if he had no knowledge of the contrary, could he have had instruction in that which is good? For there is thus a surer and an undoubted comprehension of matters submitted to us than the mere surmise arising from an opinion regarding them.

— Against Heresies IV.39.1, source. The permission of evil serves knowledge: experience of the bitter teaches the sweet more surely than opinion. It is the same “contrast” principle Lactantius would state a century later — that without evil the very material of wisdom and virtue is taken away.

Notes

  • Irenaeus does not use the language of privation (evil as a lack of good) that Basil and Augustine would supply; his answer is anthropological and developmental, grounded in freedom and growth rather than metaphysics of the good.
  • His framing is polemical — aimed at the Gnostic claim that the material world and its evils betray a botched or malicious creator. By lodging evil in the free creature and its immaturity, he defends the goodness of the one Creator without conceding a rival dark principle.
  • Irenaeus appears on this wiki also as a witness on the original sin, Mary as the New Eve, and intermediate state timelines.
