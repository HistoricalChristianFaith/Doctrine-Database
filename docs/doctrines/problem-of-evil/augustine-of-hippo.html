Augustine of Hippo on the problem of evil

Dates: c. 420 · Wikipedia
Summary: Augustine gives the Latin West its enduring synthesis, and he does it as one who had once answered the problem the other way — the young Manichee who made evil a substance, a rival dark kingdom. Freed of that, he holds instead that evil is no nature at all but the corruption or privation of a good nature; and that God, being omnipotent and good, permits evil only because “He judged it better to bring good out of evil than not to permit any evil to exist.” The privation account answers what evil is; the greater-good account answers why God allows it — the seed of what later theology would call the felix culpa. Beneath both he keeps the free-will strand of the earlier Fathers, tracing moral evil to a deficient turning of the created will (nothing is its “efficient cause”), and he places natural evil — death and its train — as the just penalty of the first sin.

Claims & evidence

Evil is the corruption of a good nature, not a nature of its own

When accordingly it is inquired, whence is evil, it must first be inquired, what is evil, which is nothing else than corruption, either of the measure, or the form, or the order, that belong to nature. Nature therefore which has been corrupted, is called evil, for assuredly when incorrupt it is good; but even when corrupt, so far as it is nature it is good, so far as it is corrupted it is evil.

— Concerning the Nature of Good, Against the Manichaeans, ch. 4, source. Evil is parasitic: it has no independent existence but is the spoiling of something that, as a nature, remains good. This directly answers the Manichaean dualism Augustine had left — there is no co-eternal evil substance, only good natures more or less corrupted. It is the Latin form of Basil's “evil is no essence.”

God permits evil because He can bring a greater good out of it

For He judged it better to bring good out of evil, than not to permit any evil to exist.

— Enchiridion, ch. 27, source. The positive theodicy: an omnipotent goodness would not permit any evil “unless in His omnipotence and goodness, as the Supreme Good, He is able to bring good even out of evil.” God's answer to evil is not to have prevented it but to redeem it — the logic later crystallized in the Exsultet's “O happy fault” (felix culpa).

Moral evil originates in the created will — its cause is “deficient, not efficient”

For what is it which makes the will bad, when it is the will itself which makes the action bad? And consequently the bad will is the cause of the bad action, but nothing is the efficient cause of the bad will.

— The City of God XII.7, source. Here Augustine faces the sharper question — not what evil is, but where a good creature's evil will comes from — and refuses to hunt for a positive cause behind it. The evil will is not made evil by anything above, beside, or below it (a good cause could not produce it, and there is no evil substance to be the cause); it is self-originated, a falling away. So its cause is “not efficient, but deficient, as the will itself is not an effecting of something, but a defect” — to look for the cause of an evil will is “as if some one sought to see darkness, or hear silence.” This keeps God clear of authorship (He is not the cause of the defection) while leaving the creature justly answerable: the will “could not become evil, were it unwilling to become so.” It is the metaphysical backbone under the free-will answer that Irenaeus and Basil had stated more simply.

Natural evil — death and its train — is the just penalty of the first sin

the first men were indeed so created, that if they had not sinned, they would not have experienced any kind of death; but that, having become sinners, they were so punished with death, that whatsoever sprang from their stock should also be punished with the same death.

— The City of God XIII.3, source. The privation-and-freedom account handles moral evil, but Augustine must still place the suffering that merely befalls — disease, decay, death. He frames it as penal: not a flaw in creation as God made it, but a consequence God ordained as the just sentence on Adam's sin and inherited by his stock. Death is thus “not by the law of nature, by which God ordained no death for man, but by His righteous infliction on account of sin.” This is Augustine's most consequence-laden move and the one most in tension with later theodicy: it ties the whole weight of natural evil to a historical Fall (see original sin), and it stands opposite the developmental reading of Irenaeus, for whom mortality schools a race made to grow rather than punishing a race that fell. Whether the penal reading answers natural evil in general is weighed on the natural evil as the penalty of the human Fall argument page: as an account of human mortality it is scripturally grounded, but pressed (as the modern “no death before the Fall” form presses it) to explain the whole predatory order it comes out weak, since predation and death are attested for ages before any human — a slide from “human death is penal” (Augustine's warranted claim) to “all death entered at the Fall” that the evidence does not support.

Notes

  • The two threads lock together. Because evil is a privation and not a rival power, an omnipotent God is not defeated or implicated by it; and because God can wring good from it, its permission is not a failure of goodness but its instrument. This pairing — privation + providential greater good — became the standard Western frame, carried into scholasticism by Aquinas. Whether the privation half holds on its own is weighed on the evil-as-privation (privatio boni) argument page — strong as metaphysics, but a description of what evil is, not yet a reason God permits it.
  • The four moves lock into one system: evil is a privation (so no rival substance implicates God), moral evil arises from a deficient created will (so God is not its author), natural evil is penal (so the world's suffering is just, not arbitrary), and the whole is permitted only because omnipotent goodness can wring a greater good from it. Augustine argues the free-will half at length also in On Free Choice of the Will (De Libero Arbitrio); the deficient-cause formulation quoted above is its mature statement in the City of God.
  • His corpus holds two translations of the Enchiridion; the traditional chapter-27 line is quoted here from the NPNF (Shaw) text. The Outler translation renders the same sentence “For God judged it better to bring good out of evil than not to permit any evil to exist.”
  • Augustine appears across this wiki as a central witness — on original sin, predestination, universal restoration (as its decisive Latin opponent), and many other timelines.
