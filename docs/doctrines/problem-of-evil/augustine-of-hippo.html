Augustine of Hippo on the problem of evil

Dates: c. 420 · Wikipedia
Summary: Augustine gives the Latin West its enduring synthesis, and he does it as one who had once answered the problem the other way — the young Manichee who made evil a substance, a rival dark kingdom. Freed of that, he holds instead that evil is no nature at all but the corruption or privation of a good nature; and that God, being omnipotent and good, permits evil only because “He judged it better to bring good out of evil than not to permit any evil to exist.” The privation account answers what evil is; the greater-good account answers why God allows it — the seed of what later theology would call the felix culpa.

Claims & evidence

Evil is the corruption of a good nature, not a nature of its own

When accordingly it is inquired, whence is evil, it must first be inquired, what is evil, which is nothing else than corruption, either of the measure, or the form, or the order, that belong to nature. Nature therefore which has been corrupted, is called evil, for assuredly when incorrupt it is good; but even when corrupt, so far as it is nature it is good, so far as it is corrupted it is evil.

— Concerning the Nature of Good, Against the Manichaeans, ch. 4, source. Evil is parasitic: it has no independent existence but is the spoiling of something that, as a nature, remains good. This directly answers the Manichaean dualism Augustine had left — there is no co-eternal evil substance, only good natures more or less corrupted. It is the Latin form of Basil's “evil is no essence.”

God permits evil because He can bring a greater good out of it

For He judged it better to bring good out of evil, than not to permit any evil to exist.

— Enchiridion, ch. 27, source. The positive theodicy: an omnipotent goodness would not permit any evil “unless in His omnipotence and goodness, as the Supreme Good, He is able to bring good even out of evil.” God's answer to evil is not to have prevented it but to redeem it — the logic later crystallized in the Exsultet's “O happy fault” (felix culpa).

Notes

  • The two threads lock together. Because evil is a privation and not a rival power, an omnipotent God is not defeated or implicated by it; and because God can wring good from it, its permission is not a failure of goodness but its instrument. This pairing — privation + providential greater good — became the standard Western frame, carried into scholasticism by Aquinas.
  • Augustine keeps the free-will strand of Irenaeus and Basil alongside this (moral evil originates in the defective turning of the created will), most fully in On Free Choice of the Will; the privation-and-greater-good pages here state the metaphysical and providential half of his answer.
  • His corpus holds two translations of the Enchiridion; the traditional chapter-27 line is quoted here from the NPNF (Shaw) text. The Outler translation renders the same sentence “For God judged it better to bring good out of evil than not to permit any evil to exist.”
  • Augustine appears across this wiki as a central witness — on original sin, predestination, universal restoration (as its decisive Latin opponent), and many other timelines.
