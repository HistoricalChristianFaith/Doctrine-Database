Boethius on the problem of evil

Dates: c. 524 (Anicius Manlius Severinus Boethius, c. 477–524; The Consolation of Philosophy written c. 523–524) · Wikipedia
Summary: Awaiting execution on a false charge of treason, the Roman senator Boethius wrote the most-read theodicy of the Latin Middle Ages. In The Consolation of Philosophy, Lady Philosophy answers his complaint — that the wicked flourish while the innocent suffer — along two joined lines. First, metaphysically: because God is omnipotent yet can do no evil, “evil is nothing,” and the wicked, by forsaking the good that is the end of all things, forfeit real being — the good are powerful, the bad are powerless. Second, providentially: what looks like the disordered rule of chance is the unfolding in time of an order (Fate) that flows from a single changeless reason (Providence), so that nothing falls outside God's good government. Boethius carries the privation-plus-providence answer of the fathers into the Latin West in a form every medieval reader would know.

Claims & evidence

“Evil is nothing,” for the omnipotent God can do no evil

“But can God do evil, then?” “Nay; by no means.” “Then, evil is nothing,” said she, “since He to whom nothing is impossible is unable to do evil.”

— The Consolation of Philosophy, Book III, prose XII, source.1 The argument is compact: all things that truly are come from the Good, which is God; God is omnipotent; yet the one thing omnipotence cannot do is evil. So evil is not a thing God makes or could make — it is “nothing,” no positive reality. This is the privation thesis of Basil, Gregory, Augustine, and Dionysius, put as a single dialectical hammer-blow.

The wicked, forsaking the good, lose their very being

Just as we call a corpse a dead man, but cannot call it simply “man,” so I would allow the vicious to be bad, but that they are in an absolute sense I cannot allow. That only is which maintains its place and keeps its nature; whatever falls away from this forsakes the existence which is essential to its nature.

— The Consolation of Philosophy, Book IV, prose II, source.2 Since to exist is to hold to one's nature, and the end of every nature is the good, to turn from the good is to un-make oneself: “they who forsake the common end of all things that are, they likewise also cease to be at all.”3 From this Boethius draws his paradox — that the wicked have no real power, for power is the ability to reach what one wills, and what all truly will is happiness, which is the good the wicked precisely fail to gain. Hence “the power of the good, and the indubitable weakness of the bad.”4 The evildoer's apparent triumph is his deepest impotence.

Providence orders all; Fate is its unfolding in time

Providence is the Divine reason itself, seated in the Supreme Being, which disposes all things; fate is the disposition inherent in all things which move, through which providence joins all things in their proper order.

— The Consolation of Philosophy, Book IV, prose VI, source.5 Here Boethius meets the sharpest edge of the problem — not what evil is but why the innocent suffer and the wicked prosper. His answer is the distinction of Providence and Fate: the one changeless plan, seen whole in the divine mind, is Providence; that same plan spun out through the moving chain of causes in time is Fate. What looks from below like chance or injustice is a thread the reader cannot yet trace to its pattern; seen from above “there is nothing evil… in Providence.” God, being goodness itself, so governs that even the suffering of the just and the license of the wicked serve the health of the whole. Boethius's handling of how this changeless foreknowledge leaves the human will free runs on into the timeline on predestination, grace and free will.

Notes

  • Boethius writes as a philosopher, not an ecclesiastic — Lady Philosophy quotes no Scripture — yet his conclusions are the fathers' theodicy: evil as privation, providence as all-embracing, the free will spared by a foreknowledge that does not compel. He was a Christian (author also of theological tractates), and the Consolation was read throughout the Middle Ages as harmonious with the faith.
  • The doctrine that evil “is nothing” is not a denial that wickedness is real or blameworthy; it is the claim that evil has no substance of its own — it is a corruption or defect in something good, exactly as with Augustine and Dionysius. The wicked man's acts are real; what they lack is the being and power that only the good confers.
  • The Consolation was among the most copied, translated, and glossed books of the Latin Middle Ages (King Alfred, Chaucer, and Queen Elizabeth I each Englished it), which is why its privation-and-providence frame, inherited from the fathers, reached medieval readers as their common inheritance.
