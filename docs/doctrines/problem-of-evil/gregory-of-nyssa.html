Gregory of Nyssa on the problem of evil

Dates: c. 380 · Wikipedia
Summary: Gregory, Basil's younger brother, takes the two Greek moves his brother had stated — evil is no substance, and moral evil is self-authored — and welds them to a third: God, foreseeing the fall, ordered the whole toward a final healing. Evil, he argues, has no existence of its own; it is the “deprivation of the good,” standing to virtue as blindness stands to sight, or darkness to light. It arises not in God but in the creature's own free turning from the good. And because God foresaw that turning before He made man, creation was never a mistake: the same providence that permitted the fall devised “man's recall to good,” a remedial ordering that (on Gregory's fuller teaching) reaches toward the restoration of all things.

Claims & evidence

Evil is not a substance — it is the deprivation of the good

…they would have understood that there is no evil other than wickedness. Now all wickedness has its form and character in the deprivation of the good; it exists not by itself, and cannot be contemplated as a subsistence. For no evil of any kind lies outside and independent of the will; but it is the non-existence of the good that is so denominated. Now that which is not has no substantial existence, and the Maker of that which has no substantial existence is not the Maker of things that have substantial existence.… He Who formed the sight did not make blindness. He Who manifested virtue manifested not the deprivation thereof.

— The Great Catechism (Catechetical Oration), ch. 7, source.1 This is the Greek privation answer in its sharpest form: evil is not a rival stuff God made but a logical opposite of the good — the “non-existence” of virtue — so, as the maker of what has no substance, God is not evil's author. Gregory presses the argument explicitly against the Manichees, who inferred an evil creator from the pains of mortal life.

Evil originates not in God but in the free turning of the creature's will

No growth of evil had its beginning in the Divine will.… But the evil is, in some way or other, engendered from within, springing up in the will at that moment when there is a retrocession of the soul from the beautiful. For as sight is an activity of nature, and blindness a deprivation of that natural operation, such is the kind of opposition between virtue and vice.… you will find that it is not God Who is the author of the present evils, seeing that He has ordered your nature so as to be its own master and free; but rather the recklessness that makes choice of the worse in preference to the better.

— The Great Catechism, ch. 5, source.2 The free-will strand joined to the privation strand, exactly as in Basil: because God made the rational creature “its own master and free,” the cause of moral evil is the will that turns from the good, not the God who made only good. Gregory grounds freedom in the image of God — a nature under “necessity” could not be the image of a free Maker.

God foresaw the fall and ordered creation toward restoration

He knew what was going to be, yet did not prevent the tendency towards that which actually happened.… As, then, He had in sight the perversion, so He devised man's recall to good. Accordingly, which was the better way?—never to have brought our nature into existence at all, since He foresaw that the being about to be created would fall away from that which is morally beautiful; or to bring him back by repentance, and restore his diseased nature to its original beauty?

— The Great Catechism, ch. 8, source.3 This is Gregory's distinctive addition to the theodicy: divine foreknowledge does not make God the author of the evil He foresaw, because the same foresight framed the remedy. Evil is permitted within a providence that “devised man's recall,” a healing Gregory casts as a physician's painful but curative treatment of the soul — the seam at which his theodicy runs into his teaching on the final restoration of all things.

Notes

  • Gregory reads the “coats of skins” (Genesis 3:21) allegorically as the mortality God provided after the fall — not a punishment merely but a provision, so that the soil of sin might be shed and man “re-moulded… after the original pattern” through the resurrection. Natural evil (death, decay) is thus folded into the remedial order, as with Basil.
  • The claim that vice is only the logical opposite of virtue — “non-existence” opposed to existence as blindness to sight — is the same privation thesis Augustine would systematize in the Latin West a generation later. Gregory states it in Greek and against the Manichees; Augustine, a former Manichee, states it against the same error in Latin.
  • Gregory appears on this wiki also as the fullest patristic witness to universal restoration, where his providential-ordering argument here reaches its conclusion.
