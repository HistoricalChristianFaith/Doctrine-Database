Leibniz on the problem of evil

Dates: c. 1710 (Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, 1646–1716; the Essais de Théodicée were published at Amsterdam in 1710) · Wikipedia
Summary: Leibniz gives the constructive theodicy tradition its most ambitious modern statement — and its name. His 1710 Théodicée coins the very word “theodicy” (from Greek theos, God, and dikē, justice: the “justification of God”). Its argument is that God, surveying every possible world His wisdom could conceive, freely actualized the best of all possible worlds; the evils it contains are the unavoidable accompaniments of the greatest attainable good, so their permission is no stain on His goodness. And it sorts evil into three kinds — metaphysical (the mere finitude and imperfection built into any creature), physical (suffering), and moral (sin) — the last two flowing from the first. This is the greater-good answer of Lactantius, Augustine, and Aquinas pressed to its optimistic limit; it is also the statement Voltaire would lampoon in Candide and the moderns would argue with.

Claims & evidence

The word “theodicy” — a justification of God before the bar of evil

The title of the 1710 work — Essais de Théodicée sur la bonté de Dieu, la liberté de l’homme et l’origine du mal (“Essays of Theodicy on the goodness of God, the freedom of man, and the origin of evil”) — is the first appearance of the term, Leibniz’s own coinage.1 The neologism names exactly the enterprise the fathers had been about: not merely a defense that the charge is non-contradictory, but a positive vindication of God’s justice and goodness in the face of the world’s evil. The word has since become the standard name for the whole question this timeline traces, applied backward to answers given long before Leibniz gave them a title.

The best of all possible worlds

Now this supreme wisdom, united to a goodness that is no less infinite, cannot but have chosen the best. For as a lesser evil is a kind of good, even so a lesser good is a kind of evil if it stands in the way of a greater good; and there would be something to correct in the actions of God if it were possible to do better. As in mathematics, when there is no maximum nor minimum, in short nothing distinguished, everything is done equally, or when that is not possible nothing at all is done: so it may be said likewise in respect of perfect wisdom, which is no less orderly than mathematics, that if there were not the best (optimum) among all possible worlds, God would not have produced any.

— Theodicy, Essays §8.2 This is the load-bearing move. God’s intellect contains infinitely many possible worlds, complete down to the last detail; His goodness inclines Him to create, His wisdom to create the best; and since (Leibniz holds) there is a uniquely best among them, that is the one He made. Evil enters not because God willed it for its own sake but because the best whole is a fabric in which some evils are the necessary condition of goods that outweigh them — remove the thread and you unravel more than you mend. It is the classical greater-good theodicy of Lactantius (“no virtue without evils to overcome”) and Augustine (“better to bring good out of evil than to permit no evil”), now recast as a claim about God’s optimizing choice among the whole space of possibilities.

The three evils: metaphysical, physical, and moral

Evil may be taken metaphysically, physically and morally. Metaphysical evil consists in mere imperfection, physical evil in suffering, and moral evil in sin.

— Theodicy, Essays §21.3 The division organizes the whole answer. Metaphysical evil is the root: to be a creature at all is to be finite, limited, less than God — an unavoidable imperfection in anything that is not the infinite being itself. From this original limitation, Leibniz argues, the other two evils follow: the creature’s finite understanding and will can fail (moral evil, sin), and its finite, mutable nature is exposed to loss and pain (physical evil, suffering). God is the cause of the world’s perfection; the privation that limits every creature He merely permits, as the shadow that necessarily accompanies any finite light. This is the privation answer of the fathers — evil as the shortfall of a good, not a substance God made — given a metaphysical pedigree back to the bare finitude of the creature.

Notes

  • Leibniz stands with the constructive theodicists, not the sceptics: where Aquinas had assembled the inherited pieces into a system, Leibniz presses the greater-good answer to its most confident form — not merely that God could have a sufficient reason, but that He demonstrably chose the optimal whole. His statement is therefore the high-water mark of optimism that the modern critics take as their target.
  • The optimism drew fire almost at once. Voltaire’s Candide (1759), written after the Lisbon earthquake of 1755 killed tens of thousands on All Saints’ Day, satirized the “best of all possible worlds” through the relentlessly-battered Dr Pangloss — the popular counter-image that has clung to Leibniz ever since. Whether the “best possible world” thesis actually holds — whether there even is a uniquely best world, and whether ours could be it — is weighed on the best of all possible worlds argument page (rated weak), not settled by the fact that Leibniz held it; the more general greater-good move it presses to the limit is rated separately as plausible.
  • Leibniz’s reconciliation of God’s foreknowledge and choice with human freedom — the second theme of the Théodicée’s full title — runs on into the timeline on predestination, grace and free will, where the same problem of a sovereign God and a free creature is worked out.
