Leibniz on the problem of evil

Dates: c. 1710 (Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, 1646–1716; the Essais de Théodicée were published at Amsterdam in 1710) · Wikipedia
Summary: Leibniz’s 1710 Théodicée carries in its title the word “theodicy” (from Greek theos, God, and dikē, justice: the “justification of God”). Its argument is that God, surveying every possible world His wisdom could conceive, freely actualized the best of all possible worlds; the evils it contains are the unavoidable accompaniments of the greatest attainable good, so, Leibniz argues, their permission is no stain on His goodness. And it sorts evil into three kinds — metaphysical (the mere finitude and imperfection built into any creature), physical (suffering), and moral (sin) — the last two flowing from the first. Earlier witnesses here — Lactantius, Augustine, Aquinas — also answer from a greater good; Aquinas denies that there is a best possible world. Voltaire satirized the “best of all possible worlds” in Candide (1759).

Claims & evidence

The word “theodicy”

The title of the 1710 work — Essais de Théodicée sur la bonté de Dieu, la liberté de l’homme et l’origine du mal (“Essays of Theodicy on the goodness of God, the freedom of man, and the origin of evil”) — carries the term.1 The word names a positive vindication of God’s justice and goodness in the face of the world’s evil, as distinct from a defense that the charge is non-contradictory. It has since been used as a name for answers given before 1710 as well.

The best of all possible worlds

Now this supreme wisdom, united to a goodness that is no less infinite, cannot but have chosen the best. For as a lesser evil is a kind of good, even so a lesser good is a kind of evil if it stands in the way of a greater good; and there would be something to correct in the actions of God if it were possible to do better. As in mathematics, when there is no maximum nor minimum, in short nothing distinguished, everything is done equally, or when that is not possible nothing at all is done: so it may be said likewise in respect of perfect wisdom, which is no less orderly than mathematics, that if there were not the best (optimum) among all possible worlds, God would not have produced any.

— Theodicy, Essays §8.2 In Leibniz’s account God’s intellect contains infinitely many possible worlds, complete down to the last detail; His goodness inclines Him to create, His wisdom to create the best; and since (Leibniz holds) there is a uniquely best among them, that is the one He made. Evil enters, he argues, not because God willed it for its own sake but because the best whole is a fabric in which some evils are the necessary condition of goods that outweigh them — remove the thread and you unravel more than you mend. Leibniz casts the greater-good answer, found earlier in Lactantius and Augustine, as a claim about God’s optimizing choice among the whole space of possibilities.

The three evils: metaphysical, physical, and moral

Evil may be taken metaphysically, physically and morally. Metaphysical evil consists in mere imperfection, physical evil in suffering, and moral evil in sin.

— Theodicy, Essays §21.3 In Leibniz’s division metaphysical evil is the root: to be a creature at all is to be finite, limited, less than God — an unavoidable imperfection in anything that is not the infinite being itself. From this original limitation, Leibniz argues, the other two evils follow: the creature’s finite understanding and will can fail (moral evil, sin), and its finite, mutable nature is exposed to loss and pain (physical evil, suffering). On Leibniz’s account, God is the cause of the world’s perfection; the privation that limits every creature He merely permits, as the shadow that necessarily accompanies any finite light. Here Leibniz traces the privation account of earlier writers — evil as the shortfall of a good, not a substance God made — back to the bare finitude of the creature.

Reception and context

  • Leibniz offers a constructive theodicy rather than a skeptical reply: not merely that God could have a sufficient reason, but that He chose the optimal whole. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on Bayle states that the Théodicée sought mainly to refute Bayle.
  • Voltaire’s Candide (1759), written after the Lisbon earthquake of 1755 killed tens of thousands on All Saints’ Day, satirized the “best of all possible worlds” through the battered Dr Pangloss; see Voltaire.
  • Leibniz’s reconciliation of God’s foreknowledge and choice with human freedom — the second theme of the Théodicée’s full title — is traced on the timeline on predestination, grace and free will, where the same problem of a sovereign God and a free creature is worked out.
