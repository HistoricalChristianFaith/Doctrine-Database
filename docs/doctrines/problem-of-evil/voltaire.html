Voltaire on the problem of evil

Dates: c. 1759 (François-Marie Arouet, “Voltaire,” 1694–1778; Candide, ou l’Optimisme was published in 1759, the Poème sur le désastre de Lisbonne in 1756) · Wikipedia
Summary: Voltaire is the timeline’s great critic, not a theodicist. His Poème sur le désastre de Lisbonne (1756) and, above all, Candide (1759) mounted the most famous assault ever made on the constructive optimism of Leibniz and Alexander Pope — the doctrine that this is “the best of all possible worlds” and that “whatever is, is right.” Provoked by the Lisbon earthquake of 1755, which killed tens of thousands on All Saints’ Day, Voltaire pressed natural evil — the suffering that no human will chose — as the case optimism could not absorb. He offered no rival system in its place; Candide ends by turning from metaphysics to labour (“we must cultivate our garden”). His achievement was to hand the sharpened problem, with Bayle’s skepticism, on to Hume.

Claims & evidence

“The best of all possible worlds” held up to ridicule

The target of Candide is named in its subtitle, ou l’Optimisme. Its mouthpiece is Dr Pangloss, “professor of metaphysico-theologico-cosmolo-nigology,” a caricature of the systematic optimist who deduces the perfection of the whole from the armchair:

It is demonstrable, said he, that things cannot be otherwise than as they are; for all being created for an end, all is necessarily for the best end… He proved admirably that there is no effect without a cause, and that, in this best of all possible worlds, the Baron’s castle was the most magnificent of castles, and his lady the best of all possible Baronesses.

— Candide, ch. 1.1 The phrase “the best of all possible worlds” is Leibniz’s own (le meilleur des mondes possibles); Voltaire takes the constructive theodicy at its most confident and sets it to walk through a world of rape, war, slavery, disease, and judicial murder, letting the events refute the maxim while Pangloss goes on reciting it. The whole book is the reductio: after the destruction of Lisbon and his own flogging at an auto-da-fé, the battered hero can only ask — “If this is the best of possible worlds, what then are the others?”2

The Lisbon earthquake: optimism against a mass grave

The occasion for the whole attack was real. On 1 November 1755 an earthquake, fire, and tsunami levelled Lisbon and killed tens of thousands of worshippers at Mass. In Candide Voltaire drops Pangloss into the ruins to console the dying with system:

For, said he, all that is is for the best. If there is a volcano at Lisbon it cannot be elsewhere. It is impossible that things should be other than they are; for everything is right.

— Candide, ch. 5.3 Three years before Candide, Voltaire had made the argument in earnest in verse. The Poème sur le désastre de Lisbonne (1756) — subtitled, in English translation, An Inquiry into the Maxim, “Whatever Is, Is Right” (Pope’s optimist slogan) — refuses to let the doctrine be spoken over the rubble:

Oh wretched man, earth-fated to be cursed;
Abyss of plagues, and miseries the worst!
Horrors on horrors, griefs on griefs must show,
That man’s the victim of unceasing woe,
And lamentations which inspire my strain,
Prove that philosophy is false and vain…
Yet in this direful chaos you’d compose
A general bliss from individuals’ woes?
Oh worthless bliss! in injured reason’s sight,
With faltering voice you cry, “What is, is right”?…
All may be well; that hope can man sustain,
All now is well; ’tis an illusion vain.

— The Lisbon Earthquake (trans. William F. Fleming).4 The move is the one every theodicy of the will finds hardest to answer: Lisbon was natural evil, suffering that outran any human choice — not the wage of sin, not the price of a free will, just the earth killing the devout at prayer. To answer it, optimism must either deny the suffering is real evil or claim a hidden good it cannot exhibit; Voltaire’s verse makes both look like an insult to the dead.

Notes

  • Voltaire is a critic, not a rival theodicist. He does not deny God (he was a deist, and the poem still addresses God); he denies that the evil of the world can be reasoned into a system of optimal good. His two named targets are Leibniz’s “best of all possible worlds” and Alexander Pope’s “Whatever is, is right” (Essay on Man, 1733–34) — the same greater-good optimism in its French and English dress.
  • Candide ends not with a counter-theory but with a shrug at all theory: the survivors settle to work a small farm, and to Pangloss’s last effort to prove that all the calamities were links in the best chain of events, Candide replies only that “we must cultivate our garden.” The refusal of system is itself the position — a quietist retreat from theodicy rather than an answer to it.
  • Voltaire drew directly on the skepticism of Pierre Bayle, whose articles on the problem of evil he admired and mined; together they form the Enlightenment bridge from Leibniz (1710) to Hume’s Dialogues (1779), where the natural evil Voltaire pressed becomes the standing objection to natural religion. The problem of natural evil itself — whether nature’s disorder can be laid to a will at all — is weighed on this doctrine’s cosmic-fall argument page.
