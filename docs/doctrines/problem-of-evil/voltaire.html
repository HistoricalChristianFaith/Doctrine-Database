Voltaire on the problem of evil

Dates: c. 1759 (François-Marie Arouet, “Voltaire,” 1694–1778; Candide, ou l’Optimisme was published in 1759, the Poème sur le désastre de Lisbonne in 1756) · Wikipedia
Summary: Voltaire writes as a critic, not a theodicist. His Poème sur le désastre de Lisbonne (1756) and Candide (1759) attack the constructive optimism of Leibniz and Alexander Pope — the doctrine that this is “the best of all possible worlds” and that “whatever is, is right.” Provoked by the Lisbon earthquake of 1755, which killed tens of thousands on All Saints’ Day, Voltaire pressed natural evil — the suffering that no human will chose — against optimism. He offered no rival system in its place; Candide ends by turning from metaphysics to labour (“we must cultivate our garden”). He drew on the skepticism of Bayle; Hume’s Dialogues followed in 1779.

Claims & evidence

“The best of all possible worlds” satirized

The target of Candide is named in its subtitle, ou l’Optimisme. Its mouthpiece is Dr Pangloss, “professor of metaphysico-theologico-cosmolo-nigology,” the systematic optimist of the satire:

It is demonstrable, said he, that things cannot be otherwise than as they are; for all being created for an end, all is necessarily for the best end… He proved admirably that there is no effect without a cause, and that, in this best of all possible worlds, the Baron’s castle was the most magnificent of castles, and his lady the best of all possible Baronesses.

— Candide, ch. 1.1 The phrase “the best of all possible worlds” is Leibniz’s own (le meilleur des mondes possibles); Voltaire sets the optimist to walk through a world of rape, war, slavery, disease, and judicial murder while Pangloss goes on reciting the maxim. After the destruction of Lisbon and his own flogging at an auto-da-fé, the battered hero can only ask — “If this is the best of possible worlds, what then are the others?”2

The Lisbon earthquake

On 1 November 1755 an earthquake, fire, and tsunami levelled Lisbon and killed tens of thousands on All Saints’ Day. In Candide Voltaire drops Pangloss into the ruins to console the dying with system:

For, said he, all that is is for the best. If there is a volcano at Lisbon it cannot be elsewhere. It is impossible that things should be other than they are; for everything is right.

— Candide, ch. 5.3 Three years before Candide, Voltaire had made the argument in earnest in verse. The Poème sur le désastre de Lisbonne (1756) — subtitled, in English translation, An Inquiry into the Maxim, “Whatever Is, Is Right” (Pope’s optimist slogan) — refuses to let the doctrine be spoken over the rubble:

Oh wretched man, earth-fated to be cursed;
Abyss of plagues, and miseries the worst!
Horrors on horrors, griefs on griefs must show,
That man’s the victim of unceasing woe,
And lamentations which inspire my strain,
Prove that philosophy is false and vain…
Yet in this direful chaos you’d compose
A general bliss from individuals’ woes?
Oh worthless bliss! in injured reason’s sight,
With faltering voice you cry, “What is, is right”?…
All may be well; that hope can man sustain,
All now is well; ’tis an illusion vain.

— The Lisbon Earthquake (trans. William F. Fleming).4 Voltaire presents Lisbon as natural evil, suffering that outran any human choice — in his framing not the wage of sin, not the price of a free will, but the earth killing the devout at prayer — and his verse presents the optimist’s consolations as an insult to the dead.

Reception and context

  • Voltaire does not deny God (he was a deist, and the poem still addresses God); he denies that the evil of the world can be reasoned into a system of optimal good. His two named targets are Leibniz’s “best of all possible worlds” and Alexander Pope’s “Whatever is, is right” (Essay on Man, 1733–34) — the one written in French, the other in English.
  • Candide closes with the survivors settled to work a small farm; to Pangloss’s last effort to prove that all the calamities were links in the best chain of events, Candide replies only that “we must cultivate our garden.”
  • Voltaire drew on the skepticism of Pierre Bayle, whom he praises in the Lisbon poem (see Bayle). Between Leibniz (1710) and Hume’s Dialogues (1779), in which Philo presses the trilemma against natural religion, Voltaire’s poem and novel press natural evil.
