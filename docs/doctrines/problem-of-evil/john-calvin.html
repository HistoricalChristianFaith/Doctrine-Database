John Calvin on the problem of evil

Dates: c. 1559 (John Calvin, 1509–1564; the doctrine cited — Institutes of the Christian Religion I.16–18 on providence and III.23 on reprobation — reached its full form in the final 1559 Latin edition, the text the quotations below follow) · Wikipedia
Summary: Calvin fills the long gap the timeline leaves between Aquinas and Leibniz, and he sharpens the one question the fathers had kept softest — the difference between what God wills and what He merely permits. His doctrine of universal providence denies that God ever bare-permits anything: God “holds the helms and overrules all events,” so that “nothing happens but what he has knowingly and willingly decreed,” and “fortune and chance are heathen terms.” Yet against the obvious objection — that this makes God the author of sin — Calvin holds the classical line: God uses even the deeds of the wicked and of Satan for His own righteous ends while “he contracts no taint from their impurity.” On the origin of sin and reprobation he presses hardest of all: Adam fell “divine providence so ordaining, but he falls by his own fault,” and of the decree of reprobation Calvin makes his most-quoted confession — “the decree, I admit, is dreadful.” This is the Reformed answer to how a sovereign God relates to evil without authoring it; it overlaps directly with the timeline on predestination, grace and free will.

Claims & evidence

Providence is God's active government of all events — not mere foreknowledge, not idle looking-on

First, then, let the reader remember that the providence we mean is not one by which the Deity, sitting idly in heaven, looks on at what is taking place in the world, but one by which he, as it were, holds the helms and overrules all events.

— Institutes I.16.4, source (trans. Henry Beveridge, 1845).1 Calvin sets his doctrine against a diluted providence — a God who foresees but does not govern. Against that he insists the divine government is actual and total: “there is no random power, or agency, or motion in the creatures, who are so governed by the secret counsel of God, that nothing happens but what he has knowingly and willingly decreed,” so that “fortune and chance are heathen terms; the meaning of which ought not to occupy pious minds.”2 This is providence pushed past the softer patristic and scholastic formulations — past even Boethius's changeless reason unfolding in time — to a government from which nothing whatever is exempt.

Not “bare permission”: God does not merely allow evil, He orders it to His ends

…it is the merest trifling to substitute a bare permission for the providence of God, as if he sat in a watch-tower waiting for fortuitous events, his Judgments meanwhile depending on the will of man.

— Institutes I.18.1, source.3 Here is Calvin's hard edge, and the point where he most sharpens the timeline's running question. The fathers had softened the problem with a permission that fell short of positive will: John of Damascus had God govern “partly according to good-will… and partly according to permission,” and Aquinas had God permit the evils He could prevent. Calvin refuses to let “permission” do the work of insulating God: to say God only allows evil, as though standing by, is “the merest trifling.” The divine will is, in his phrase, active even here. This is the deliberate hardening that makes Calvin a distinct answer and not a mere restatement of the inherited one — and it is what makes the charge “then God is the author of sin” press on him harder than on any father before.

God employs the wicked and even Satan, yet “contracts no taint from their impurity”

For the carnal mind can scarcely comprehend how, when acting by their means, he contracts no taint from their impurity, nay, how, in a common operation, he is exempt from all guilt, and can justly condemn his own ministers.

— Institutes I.18.1, source.4 Having refused “bare permission,” Calvin must answer how God can positively order a wicked deed without sharing its wickedness — and his answer is the ancient one, that the same act is done for different ends. He borrows it from Augustine on the crucifixion: “Since the Father delivered up the Son, Christ his own body, and Judas his Master, how in such a case is God just, and man guilty, but just because in the one act which they did, the reasons for which they did it are different?”5 God, Satan, and the human agent may concur in one deed, but God wills it justly (for a good end) while the creature wills it wickedly; the guilt lodges in the creature's motive, never in God's. This is Calvin's version of the fathers' insistence — Basil's, Augustine's, Aquinas's — that God is nowhere the author of moral fault, now made to carry the extra weight of a providence that has renounced bare permission.

The origin of sin: Adam fell by God's ordination, yet “by his own fault”

Man therefore falls, divine providence so ordaining, but he falls by his own fault.

— Institutes III.23.8, source.6 The whole difficulty of a Calvinist theodicy concentrates in this one sentence, and Calvin states it without evasion. The Fall is not outside the decree — God “so ordaining” — and yet the whole guilt of it is the creature's. Calvin knows the objection this invites (why then is Adam blamed for what God ordained?) and answers by distinguishing the certainty of the event under God's decree from the cause of its sinfulness, which is the will of the creature alone. It is the same fault/authorship distinction the schoolmen drew — being from God, defect from the creature — but staked now to an ordaining decree rather than a permitting one.

Reprobation: “the decree, I admit, is dreadful”

The decree, I admit, is dreadful; and yet it is impossible to deny that God foreknew what the end of man was to be before he made him, and foreknew, because he had so ordained by his decree.

— Institutes III.23.7, source.7 This is Calvin's most-quoted line — the decretum horribile, “dreadful decree.” Notably he does not flinch from the word: the decree by which the reprobate are appointed to their end is, he grants, dreadful to contemplate. His defense is not to soften it but to ground it in God's foreknowing ordination and to refuse to arraign the divine justice for what it cannot fathom — the appeal, at the limit, to a wisdom the creature cannot survey, which is the modern skeptical-theist move in a sterner key. Calvin's willingness to call the doctrine dreadful and hold it anyway is itself a datum for the problem of evil: it marks the point past which the Reformed answer stops explaining and starts adoring.

The comfort of providence: “The Lord willed it, it must therefore be borne”

Calvin does not leave the doctrine at its hardest edge; a whole chapter (I.17) turns it to pastoral use. The believer who knows that nothing befalls him but from God's hand draws from it “gratitude in prosperity, patience in adversity, and… security for the future,” because every affliction, being willed by a good God, is bent to the sufferer's good — so that the settled mind can always come to this: “The Lord willed it, it must therefore be borne.”8 This is the greater-good and consolation thread of Lactantius, Chrysostom, and Boethius — the same conviction that no suffering is finally purposeless — now resting not on a permitted evil overruled for good but on an evil willed, from the first, within the good government of God.

The Reformed confessions carry the answer forward: “not by a bare permission”

The almighty power, unsearchable wisdom, and infinite goodness of God, so far manifest themselves in his providence, that it extendeth itself even to the first Fall, and all other sins of angels and men, and that not by a bare permission, but such as hath joined with it a most wise and powerful bounding, and otherwise ordering and governing of them, in a manifold dispensation, to his own holy ends; yet so, as the sinfulness thereof proceedeth only from the creature, and not from God; who being most holy and righteous, neither is nor can be the author or approver of sin.

— Westminster Confession of Faith V.4 (“Of Providence,” 1646), source.9 The Reformed confessions crystallize Calvin's two joined claims into a single sentence, and they keep his very word: providence orders even the Fall and all sins “not by a bare permission” — yet “the sinfulness thereof proceedeth only from the creature, and not from God.” The Second Helvetic Confession (1566) had already refused to make God “the author of… sin” while affirming His government of all things; Westminster (1646) gives the mature statement. The confessional tradition thus fixes as church doctrine exactly the balance Calvin struck: a sovereignty that reaches sin itself, held together with a holiness that cannot author it.

Notes

  • Calvin's contribution to this timeline is not a new metaphysics of evil — he keeps the fathers' privation-and-fault machinery — but a hardening of the providence side of the answer. Where John of Damascus and Aquinas insulated God with a real distinction between willing and permitting, Calvin collapses the insulation: there is no bare permission, only an ordaining will, and the defense of God's holiness must therefore be borne entirely by the fault/authorship distinction and, at the limit, by the confession of a wisdom past finding out.
  • The overlap with the timeline on predestination, grace and free will is direct: the “dreadful decree” and the ordained-yet-culpable Fall are the same doctrine viewed from the angle of election rather than of evil. The eternal-decree arguments there weigh whether unconditional election and reprobation cohere with God's universal salvific will and with human responsibility — the questions Calvin's answer to the problem of evil stakes everything on.
  • Whether Calvin's refusal of “bare permission” can be squared with God's not authoring sin — the sharpest form of the authorship objection — is a live dispute, not settled by the fact that Calvin held both. This page records what he taught; the adjudication belongs on this doctrine's argument pages and on the predestination timeline.
