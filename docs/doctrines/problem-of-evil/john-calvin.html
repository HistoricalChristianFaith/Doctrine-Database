John Calvin on the problem of evil

Dates: c. 1559 (John Calvin, 1509–1564; the doctrine cited — Institutes of the Christian Religion I.16–18 on providence and III.23 on reprobation — reached its full form in the final 1559 Latin edition, the text the quotations below follow) · Wikipedia
Summary: Calvin, the Reformer of Geneva, takes up the difference between what God wills and what He merely permits. His doctrine of universal providence denies that God ever bare-permits anything: God “holds the helms and overrules all events,” so that “nothing happens but what he has knowingly and willingly decreed,” and “fortune and chance are heathen terms.” Against the objection that this makes God the author of sin, Calvin holds God uses even the deeds of the wicked and of Satan for His own righteous ends while “he contracts no taint from their impurity.” On the origin of sin he writes that Adam fell “divine providence so ordaining, but he falls by his own fault,” and of the decree of reprobation, “the decree, I admit, is dreadful.” The Westminster Confession (1646) states the same pairing of providence and creaturely sinfulness. The subject overlaps with the timeline on predestination, grace and free will.

Claims & evidence

Providence as God's active government of all events

First, then, let the reader remember that the providence we mean is not one by which the Deity, sitting idly in heaven, looks on at what is taking place in the world, but one by which he, as it were, holds the helms and overrules all events.

— Institutes I.16.4, source (trans. Henry Beveridge, 1845).1 Calvin sets his doctrine against a providence of a God who foresees but does not govern. He holds the divine government to be actual and total: “there is no random power, or agency, or motion in the creatures, who are so governed by the secret counsel of God, that nothing happens but what he has knowingly and willingly decreed,” so that “fortune and chance are heathen terms; the meaning of which ought not to occupy pious minds.”2 On this account nothing whatever is exempt from God's government.

“Bare permission” rejected

…it is the merest trifling to substitute a bare permission for the providence of God, as if he sat in a watch-tower waiting for fortuitous events, his Judgments meanwhile depending on the will of man.

— Institutes I.18.1, source.3 Earlier witnesses on this timeline speak of permission: John of Damascus had God govern “partly according to good-will… and partly according to permission,” and Aquinas had God permit the evils He could prevent. Calvin holds that to say God only allows evil, as though standing by, is “the merest trifling”; on his account the divine will is active even here.

“He contracts no taint from their impurity”

For the carnal mind can scarcely comprehend how, when acting by their means, he contracts no taint from their impurity, nay, how, in a common operation, he is exempt from all guilt, and can justly condemn his own ministers.

— Institutes I.18.1, source.4 Having rejected “bare permission,” Calvin answers how God can positively order a wicked deed without sharing its wickedness: the same act is done for different ends. He borrows it from Augustine on the crucifixion: “Since the Father delivered up the Son, Christ his own body, and Judas his Master, how in such a case is God just, and man guilty, but just because in the one act which they did, the reasons for which they did it are different?”5 On this account God, Satan, and the human agent may concur in one deed, but God wills it justly (for a good end) while the creature wills it wickedly; the guilt lodges in the creature's motive, never in God's. Basil, Augustine and Aquinas also hold that God is nowhere the author of moral fault; Calvin holds it together with a providence that excludes bare permission.

The origin of sin: “divine providence so ordaining” yet “by his own fault”

Man therefore falls, divine providence so ordaining, but he falls by his own fault.

— Institutes III.23.8, source.6 In this sentence the Fall is not outside the decree — God “so ordaining” — and yet the whole guilt of it is the creature's. Calvin anticipates the objection (why then is Adam blamed for what God ordained?) and answers by distinguishing the certainty of the event under God's decree from the cause of its sinfulness, which is the will of the creature alone. The schoolmen had drawn a related distinction — being from God, defect from the creature; Calvin applies it to an ordaining decree rather than a permitting one.

Reprobation: “the decree, I admit, is dreadful”

The decree, I admit, is dreadful; and yet it is impossible to deny that God foreknew what the end of man was to be before he made him, and foreknew, because he had so ordained by his decree.

— Institutes III.23.7, source.7 This is the decretum horribile, “dreadful decree.” Calvin grants that the decree by which the reprobate are appointed to their end is dreadful to contemplate, grounds it in God's foreknowing ordination, and declines to arraign the divine justice for what the creature cannot fathom, appealing to a wisdom the creature cannot survey.

The comfort of providence: “The Lord willed it, it must therefore be borne”

A whole chapter (I.17) turns the doctrine to pastoral use. The believer who knows that nothing befalls him but from God's hand draws from it “gratitude in prosperity, patience in adversity, and… security for the future,” because every affliction, being willed by a good God, is bent to the sufferer's good — so that the settled mind can always come to this: “The Lord willed it, it must therefore be borne.”8 Lactantius, Chrysostom, and Boethius also hold that no suffering is finally purposeless; Calvin grounds it not in a permitted evil overruled for good but in an evil willed within the good government of God.

The Westminster Confession (1646): “not by a bare permission”

The almighty power, unsearchable wisdom, and infinite goodness of God, so far manifest themselves in his providence, that it extendeth itself even to the first Fall, and all other sins of angels and men, and that not by a bare permission, but such as hath joined with it a most wise and powerful bounding, and otherwise ordering and governing of them, in a manifold dispensation, to his own holy ends; yet so, as the sinfulness thereof proceedeth only from the creature, and not from God; who being most holy and righteous, neither is nor can be the author or approver of sin.

— Westminster Confession of Faith V.4 (“Of Providence,” 1646), source.9 The Westminster Confession states both claims in one sentence, using Calvin's phrase: providence orders even the Fall and all sins “not by a bare permission” — yet “the sinfulness thereof proceedeth only from the creature, and not from God.” The Second Helvetic Confession (1566) also refuses to make God “the author of… sin” while affirming His government of all things. Both confessions adopt as doctrine a sovereignty that reaches sin, held together with a holiness that does not author it.

Reception and context

  • Calvin does not offer a new metaphysics of evil; he keeps the fault/authorship distinction. His distinctive claim concerns providence: where John of Damascus and Aquinas distinguish willing from permitting, Calvin rejects bare permission in favour of an ordaining will, and rests God's holiness on the fault/authorship distinction and on a wisdom past finding out.
  • The overlap with the timeline on predestination, grace and free will is direct: the “dreadful decree” and the ordained-yet-culpable Fall are the same doctrine viewed from the angle of election rather than of evil. The questions whether unconditional election and reprobation cohere with God's universal salvific will and with human responsibility are traced on the predestination timeline.
