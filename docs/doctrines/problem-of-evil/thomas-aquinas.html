Thomas Aquinas on the problem of evil

Dates: c. 1274 (Thomas Aquinas, c. 1225–1274; the questions cited — Summa Theologiae I, qq. 2, 48–49 — belong to the Prima Pars, completed c. 1268) · Wikipedia
Summary: Aquinas gathers the whole inherited answer — privation, the deficient will, providence, the greater good — into a single, tightly-argued system, and it is his statement that Leibniz and the moderns argue with. Evil is no nature but the absence of a due good; it has no formal, final, or efficient cause of its own but only a deficient one, so that good is the cause of evil merely accidentally. God is therefore not the author of the evil of fault (malum culpae), which is traced no higher than the created will; He is the author only of the evil of penalty (malum poenae), which serves the order of justice. And He permits the evils He could prevent because His omnipotent goodness can draw a greater good out of them — “that He should allow evil to exist, and out of it produce good.”

Claims & evidence

Evil is no nature, but the absence of a due good

One opposite is known through the other, as darkness is known through light. Hence also what evil is must be known from the nature of good… it cannot be that evil signifies being, or any form or nature. Therefore it must be that by the name of evil is signified the absence of good. And this is what is meant by saying that “evil is neither a being nor a good.”

— Summa Theologiae I, q. 48, a. 1, source.1 This is the privatio boni of Basil, Gregory of Nyssa, Augustine, and above all Pseudo-Dionysius (whom Aquinas cites in the very article), now pinned down with a precision the fathers had not reached: not every absence of good is evil, but only the privation of a good that is due to a thing by its nature — blindness is an evil in an animal, not in a stone, “since it is against the nature of a stone to see.” Evil is thus real (it is found in things, as corruption is2) but parasitic: it has no subject but the good it corrupts, and it “never wholly consumes” that good. Whether this privatio boni holds — against the objection that pain and cruelty are positive realities, and with the caveat that it says what evil is rather than why God permits it — is weighed on the evil-as-privation argument page.

Evil has no cause of its own — only a deficient, accidental one

evil has no formal cause, rather is it a privation of form; likewise, neither has it a final cause, but rather is it a privation of order to the proper end… Evil, however, has a cause by way of an agent, not directly, but accidentally.

— Summa Theologiae I, q. 49, a. 1, source.3 Because evil is a privation, it cannot be produced as such: no agent aims at it. It arises only accidentally — either as the by-product of a good act (fire, perfecting its own form, incidentally corrupts the air) or from a defect in the agent. In the moral case that defect is a will that fails to hold to its rule: “in voluntary things the defect of the action comes from the will actually deficient, inasmuch as it does not actually subject itself to its proper rule.”4 This is the exact refinement of Augustine's “not an efficient but a deficient cause” — the will's deficiency, not any positive dark principle, is the root of moral evil.

God is not the author of the evil of fault — only of the evil of penalty

the evil which consists in defect of action, or which is caused by defect of the agent, is not reduced to God as to its cause… And so God is the author of the evil which is penalty, but not of the evil which is fault, by reason of what is said above.

— Summa Theologiae I, q. 49, a. 2, source.5 Here the whole apparatus does its work. Everything real in a sinful act — its being, its motion — traces back to God, the first cause of all being; only its defect does not, for a defect has no positive source in the first, non-deficient cause. Aquinas illustrates with the limp: “whatever there is of being and action in a bad action, is reduced to God as the cause; whereas whatever defect is in it is not caused by God, but by the deficient secondary cause” — as the power to walk comes from the leg's motive force, but the obliqueness of the limp from the crookedness of the leg.6 So God, willing the good order of the universe, permits the corruptions that order allows, and deals out penalty in justice, without being in any way the author of moral fault.

The two evils: fault (malum culpae) and penalty (malum poenae)

the evil which comes from the withdrawal of the form and integrity of the thing, has the nature of a pain… for it is of the very nature of a pain to be against the will. But the evil which consists in the subtraction of the due operation in voluntary things has the nature of a fault… Therefore every evil in voluntary things is to be looked upon as a pain or a fault.

— Summa Theologiae I, q. 48, a. 5 (“pain” = poena, penalty), source.7 The distinction sorts the whole problem. Malum poenae (translated “pain,” from poena) is the evil that befalls against the will — the loss of some good, up to the loss of the vision of God; malum culpae is the evil the will does. And fault is the worse of the two: “fault has the nature of evil more than pain has,” for “one becomes evil by the evil of fault, but not by the evil of pain” — quoting Dionysius, “To be punished is not an evil; but it is an evil to be made worthy of punishment.”8 This is why God can author penalty but never fault: penalty is the lesser evil the wise governor permits to prevent the greater.

God permits evil to draw a greater good from it

As Augustine says (Enchiridion xi): “Since God is the highest good, He would not allow any evil to exist in His works, unless His omnipotence and goodness were such as to bring good even out of evil.” This is part of the infinite goodness of God, that He should allow evil to exist, and out of it produce good.

— Summa Theologiae I, q. 2, a. 3, reply to objection 1, source.9 This is the famous one-liner — Aquinas's answer to the very objection that opens his proof of God: if one of two contraries were infinite it would destroy the other, so if God (infinite good) existed, there would be no evil; but evil exists; therefore God does not. Aquinas turns it: God's infinite goodness is precisely what permits evil, in order to bring a greater good out of it. He spells out the mechanism elsewhere in the same treatise: “many good things would be taken away if God permitted no evil to exist; for fire would not be generated if air was not corrupted, nor would the life of a lion be preserved unless the ass were killed. Neither would avenging justice nor the patience of a sufferer be praised if there were no injustice.”10 It is the greater-good answer of Lactantius and Augustine (“better to bring good out of evil than to permit no evil”), now set as the keystone of the system.

Notes

  • Aquinas is a synthesizer here, not an innovator: every move — privation, the deficient will, providence and secondary causes, the greater good, the fault/penalty division — he inherits from the fathers above (he cites Dionysius and Augustine on nearly every page of qq. 48–49). His contribution is the architecture: the pieces are locked into a single coherent account of how a good, omnipotent God relates to evil, which became the standard reference of Latin theology and the target of the modern debate.
  • The permission-and-providence thread — God as first cause of all being, the free creature as the deficient secondary cause of its own fault — runs straight on into the timeline on predestination, grace and free will, where the same distinction between what God wills and what He permits is worked out for grace and reprobation.
  • Whether the greater-good and free-will defenses actually succeed as theodicy — and whether “evil is a privation” answers or merely relabels the problem — are questions weighed on this doctrine's argument pages, not settled by the fact that Aquinas held them. This page records what he taught; the adjudication belongs there.
