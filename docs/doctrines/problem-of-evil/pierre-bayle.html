Pierre Bayle on the problem of evil

Dates: c. 1697 (Pierre Bayle, 1647–1706; the Dictionnaire historique et critique appeared at Rotterdam in 1697, with an expanded second edition in 1702) · Wikipedia
Summary: Bayle, a Huguenot refugee in Holland, wrote as a skeptic on the problem. In the articles “Manichéens,” “Pauliciens,” and “Rorarius” of his Dictionnaire (1697) he argued that reason cannot resolve the problem of evil: monotheism is the simpler and more elegant hypothesis a priori, but faced with the observed mixture of good and evil the dualist can account for the facts better, and every rational reply to the problem is less agreeable to reason than the objection it answers. Reason, he held, can raise the difficulty but not solve it, and the believer should submit to revelation by faith. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy states that Bayle motivated Leibniz to write the Théodicée, which sought mainly to refute him.

Claims & evidence

“Not only inexplicable, but even incomprehensible”

In the article “Pauliciens” (remark E) Bayle stages a debate between a fictionalized Zoroaster (the dualist, who traces all good to a benevolent god and all evil to a malevolent one) and Melissus (the monotheist). Melissus wins on a priori grounds — one infinite perfect being is a simpler, more elegant first principle than two warring gods. But when the debate turns to the world as observed, Bayle has the dualist press the monotheist hard:

the manner in which evil was introduced under the empire of a sovereign being, infinitely good, infinitely holy, and infinitely powerful … not only inexplicable, but even incomprehensible; and everything that is opposed to the reasons why this being permitted evil is more agreeable to the natural light and to the ideas of order than these reasons are.

— Dictionnaire, art. “Pauliciens,” remark E (trans. Richard Popkin).1 Bayle's claim is not merely that we cannot explain how a perfectly good, holy, and omnipotent God came to permit evil; it is that every rational defence offered for that permission is less in keeping with reason and our ideas of order than the objection it is meant to answer. On the observable evidence, Bayle held, the Manichaean has the advantage — not because dualism is true (on a priori grounds he rejects it), but because, he argued, monotheism cannot make rational sense of the mixture of good and evil that the world displays.

The a priori and the a posteriori, and the turn to faith

Bayle builds the impasse from a distinction he draws out in the companion article “Manichéens” (remark D). A priori — reasoning from the idea of a first principle — monotheism, he holds, has the advantage: an infinite, necessary, perfect being is simpler and more coherent than two independent gods. A posteriori — reasoning back from the phenomena, from the tangle of good and evil in human life — the dualist hypothesis fits the data better. On his account reason therefore does not resolve the problem of evil; it splits against itself. Bayle’s conclusion is fideist: since reason can only raise objections it cannot answer, the believer must not try to win the argument on reason’s ground but should captivate the understanding to the obedience of faith and hold the truth of God’s goodness on the authority of Scripture, not of demonstration. (Elisabeth Labrousse and Walter Rex read this fideism as sincere Calvinist Christianity; Gianluca Mori reads Bayle’s views as leading to atheism.)2

Reception and context

  • Bayle’s articles drew replies: Leibniz’s Essais de Théodicée (1710) sought mainly to refute Bayle, according to the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy.3
  • Bayle does not offer a theodicy; he presses the objection. Lactantius recorded the Epicurean dilemma in order to answer it; Bayle holds that reason cannot answer it.
  • Voltaire drew on Bayle’s articles and praises him in the Lisbon poem (1756);4 Hume’s Dialogues (1779) take up related reasoning.
