Pierre Bayle on the problem of evil

Dates: c. 1697 (Pierre Bayle, 1647–1706; the Dictionnaire historique et critique appeared at Rotterdam in 1697, with an expanded second edition in 1702) · Wikipedia
Summary: Bayle, a Huguenot refugee in Holland, is the skeptic who set the modern problem its terms. In the articles “Manichéens,” “Pauliciens,” and “Rorarius” of his Dictionnaire (1697) he argued that the problem of evil simply defeats reason: monotheism is the simpler and more elegant hypothesis a priori, but faced with the observed mixture of good and evil the dualist can account for the facts better, and every rational reply to the problem is weaker than the objection it answers. Reason can raise the difficulty; it cannot solve it — so one must fall silent and submit to revelation by faith. It was largely to answer Bayle by name that Leibniz wrote the Théodicée.

Claims & evidence

The problem of evil is not merely inexplicable but incomprehensible

In the article “Pauliciens” (remark E) Bayle stages a debate between a fictionalized Zoroaster (the dualist, who traces all good to a benevolent god and all evil to a malevolent one) and Melissus (the monotheist). Melissus wins on a priori grounds — one infinite perfect being is a simpler, more elegant first principle than two warring gods. But when the debate turns to the world as we actually find it, the monotheist is overwhelmed:

the manner in which evil was introduced under the empire of a sovereign being, infinitely good, infinitely holy, and infinitely powerful … not only inexplicable, but even incomprehensible; and everything that is opposed to the reasons why this being permitted evil is more agreeable to the natural light and to the ideas of order than these reasons are.

— Dictionnaire, art. “Pauliciens,” remark E (trans. Richard Popkin).1 The claim is deliberately sharp. It is not merely that we cannot explain how a perfectly good, holy, and omnipotent God came to permit evil; it is that every rational defence offered for that permission is less in keeping with reason and our ideas of order than the objection it is meant to answer. On the observable evidence, Bayle held, the Manichaean has the better of it — not because dualism is true (reason’s a priori verdict is against it), but because monotheism cannot make rational sense of the mixture of good and evil that the world plainly displays.

Reason wins a priori, loses a posteriori — and must yield to faith

The impasse is structural, and Bayle builds it from a distinction he draws out in the companion article “Manichéens” (remark D). A priori — reasoning from the idea of a first principle — monotheism plainly wins: an infinite, necessary, perfect being is simpler and more coherent than two independent gods. A posteriori — reasoning back from the phenomena, from the tangle of good and evil in human life — the dualist hypothesis fits the data better. Reason therefore does not resolve the problem of evil; it splits against itself, decisive one way and defeated the other. Bayle’s conclusion is fideist: since reason can only raise objections it cannot answer, the believer must not try to win the argument on reason’s ground but should captivate the understanding to the obedience of faith and hold the truth of God’s goodness on the authority of Scripture, not of demonstration. (Whether this fideism was sincere piety or covert unbelief has been argued ever since — part of why Bayle is claimed by devout and skeptic alike.)2

Notes

  • Bayle’s articles “did not invent” the problem, but they framed it so forcefully that they provoked the great constructive replies of the next generation: Leibniz’s Essais de Théodicée (1710) was written largely as an answer to Bayle by name, and William King’s De origine mali (1702) likewise. The constructive optimism this timeline traces through Leibniz is in real part a response to the skeptic recorded here.
  • Bayle stands to the constructive tradition roughly as the ancient statement of the Epicurean dilemma stood to the Fathers: he does not offer a theodicy, he sharpens the objection. Where Lactantius merely preserved the pagan trilemma to break it, Bayle presses it as unbreakable by reason — the first fully modern form of the claim that the problem of evil has no rational solution.
  • Through Bayle the skeptical strand runs on to Voltaire, who admired and mined his articles, and to Hume, whose Dialogues (1779) give the same reasoning its canonical modern statement. Bayle is the bridge from the confessional age to the Enlightenment problem of evil.
