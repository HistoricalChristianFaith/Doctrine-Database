The modern debate — Hume's challenge and the analytic reply

Dates: the early-modern to contemporary turn, from David Hume’s Dialogues concerning Natural Religion (1779) through the twentieth- and twenty-first-century analytic debate · representative works: J. L. Mackie (1955), John Hick (1966), Alvin Plantinga (1974), William Rowe (1979), Stephen Wykstra (1984) · Hume at Project Gutenberg
Summary: After Leibniz the initiative passes to the critics, and the problem of evil is reforged as the standing objection to belief in God. Hume, in the Dialogues (1779), revives the Epicurean trilemma in its sharpest modern form and makes it the central difficulty for natural theology. In the twentieth century the debate splits into two problems. The logical problem (J. L. Mackie, 1955) charges that God and evil are flatly contradictory; Alvin Plantinga’s free-will defense (1974) is widely judged to have answered it, showing the triad is at least consistent. Attention then shifts to the evidential problem (William Rowe, 1979), which grants consistency but argues that the amount and apparent pointlessness of suffering — Rowe’s fawn dying unseen in a forest fire — make God’s non-existence probable; the reply of skeptical theism (Wykstra, Bergmann) denies we are in a position to judge that such suffering is truly pointless. Alongside the defenses run the constructive theodicies — John Hick’s Irenaean “soul-making” (1966) chief among them. And a protest strand, sharpened by the Holocaust, refuses the whole project: some, with Dostoevsky’s Ivan Karamazov, would rather “return the ticket” than justify a world bought at the price of innocent suffering. This survey records who said what; the positions themselves are weighed on this doctrine’s argument pages.

Claims & evidence

Hume revives the Epicurean trilemma (1779)

Epicurus’s old questions are yet unanswered. Is he willing to prevent evil, but not able? then is he impotent. Is he able, but not willing? then is he malevolent. Is he both able and willing? whence then is evil?

— David Hume, Dialogues concerning Natural Religion, Part X (the sceptic Philo speaking).1 This is the ancient dilemma that Lactantius had transmitted (and answered) fifteen centuries earlier, now turned from a puzzle the theist resolves into the decisive objection against natural religion. Hume does not merely restate it: through Philo he presses that the mixed world we observe gives us no ground to infer a wholly good and omnipotent author — at best a cause “indifferent” to good and evil. The trilemma becomes the modern problem’s canonical opening.

The logical problem — Mackie (1955)

In its simplest form the problem is this: God is omnipotent; God is wholly good; and yet evil exists. There seems to be some contradiction between these three propositions, so that if any two of them were true the third would be false.

— J. L. Mackie, “Evil and Omnipotence” (1955).2 Mackie gives the objection its hardest twentieth-century form: not that evil makes God unlikely, but that the theist’s own beliefs are logically inconsistent — that no possible reason could reconcile an omnipotent, wholly good God with any evil at all. Answer this and the theist is not merely probable but rationally in the clear; fail and belief is incoherent. The gauntlet set the agenda for the decades that followed.

The free-will defense — Plantinga (1974)

A world containing creatures who are significantly free (and freely perform more good than evil actions) is more valuable, all else being equal, than a world containing no free creatures at all… To create creatures capable of moral good, therefore, He must create creatures capable of moral evil; and He can’t give these creatures the freedom to perform evil and at the same time prevent them from doing so.

— Alvin Plantinga, God, Freedom, and Evil (1974).3 Plantinga’s reply is a defense, not a theodicy: it need not give God’s actual reason, only show that a good, omnipotent God could have a sufficient one — which is all that is needed to break Mackie’s alleged contradiction. If genuine freedom is a great good and even omnipotence cannot make a creature freely choose only the good, then a world with free creatures who sometimes sin may be better than any sinless world of automata, and God is not impugned for permitting the evil freedom makes possible. This is the ancient free-will answer of Irenaeus and Basil given rigorous modal form; it is now widely held to have settled the logical problem — even Mackie’s successors largely conceded consistency — and to have shifted the argument onto evidential ground.

The soul-making theodicy — Hick (1966)

Rather, this world must be a place of soul-making. And its value is to be judged, not primarily by the quantity of pleasure and pain occurring in it at any particular moment, but by its fitness for its primary purpose, the purpose of soul-making.

— John Hick, Evil and the God of Love (1966).4 Where Plantinga only defends consistency, Hick offers a positive theodicy, and names it after the father who first gave it: the Irenaean, over against the Augustinian. Humanity is not a perfection that fell but an immature creature made to grow, through a world of real hardship, from innocence into the earned likeness of God. A frictionless paradise would produce no courage, compassion, or faith; the “vale of soul-making” (Hick borrows the phrase from a letter of the poet John Keats) is the environment such growth requires. It is the soul-making answer of Irenaeus and the greater-good answer of Lactantius — a painless world would be a virtueless one — carried into the modern debate as the leading rival to the free-will defense and often paired with it.

The evidential problem — Rowe and the fawn (1979)

Suppose in some distant forest lightning strikes a dead tree, resulting in a forest fire. In the fire a fawn is trapped, horribly burned, and lies in terrible agony for several days before death relieves its suffering. So far as we can see, the fawn’s intense suffering is pointless. For there does not appear to be any greater good such that the prevention of the fawn’s suffering would require either the loss of that good or the occurrence of an evil equally bad or worse.

— William Rowe, “The Problem of Evil and Some Varieties of Atheism” (1979).5 Rowe concedes what Plantinga proved — that God and evil are not contradictory — and mounts a different case: that the sheer quantity of apparently gratuitous suffering makes atheism the more reasonable belief. His formal premise is that “there exist instances of intense suffering which an omnipotent, omniscient being could have prevented without thereby losing some greater good or permitting some evil equally bad or worse.”6 The fawn — suffering unseen, benefiting no one, teaching no one — is chosen precisely to escape every soul-making or free-will answer: no human freedom is exercised, no soul is schooled. If even one such evil is truly pointless, Rowe argues, the traditional God does not exist. This is the problem’s living form in contemporary philosophy.

Skeptical theism — the reply of Wykstra and Bergmann

The standard rejoinder to Rowe grants his logic but denies his key premise the support it needs. Stephen Wykstra argued (1984) that the move from “we can see no justifying good” to “there is none” is licensed only if we could reasonably expect to see God’s reasons were there any — and, given the gulf between infinite and finite minds, we have no such expectation. His “CORNEA” principle holds that one may infer “it appears there is no X” to “there is no X” only where, if there were an X, it would likely be within our ken.7 On this skeptical theism — developed also by Michael Bergmann, William Alston, and others — our failure to spot a God-justifying reason for the fawn’s agony is exactly what we should expect whether or not one exists, so it is no evidence against God. It is the modern, epistemological form of the fathers’ appeal to providence we cannot survey: God’s reasons may be real and simply beyond us. The cost, critics note, is a scepticism that may cut wider than its users intend.

The protest strand — the problem left unanswered

A final voice refuses the whole enterprise of theodicy. Its classic literary form is Ivan Karamazov, who — heaping up the tortures of children — will not accept even a final harmony bought at that price: “It’s not God that I don’t accept, Alyosha, only I most respectfully return Him the ticket.”8 After Auschwitz the protest hardened into theology. Richard Rubenstein’s After Auschwitz (1966) held that the death camps had made the old God of history unbelievable; John K. Roth’s “theodicy of protest” (1981) accuses God rather than acquits Him, holding the complaint of Job open; and Elie Wiesel’s Night and his Trial of God dramatize a faith that arraigns heaven and receives silence.9 These are not answers but a standing refusal of answers — a witness, worth recording honestly on any timeline of the problem, that for some the problem of evil is not solved but survived.

How this survey feeds the timeline

This page closes the timeline the way modern surveys close others: it lays out the positions clearly and fairly and leaves the adjudicating to the rated argument pages. In brief, the modern debate supplies:

  • the logical problem (Mackie) and the free-will defense (Plantinga) that is widely held to answer it — the modern form of the ancient free-will answer of Irenaeus and Basil;
  • the evidential problem (Rowe’s fawn) and the skeptical-theist reply (Wykstra, Bergmann) — the live centre of the current dispute;
  • the constructive theodicies — Hick’s soul-making, and the greater-good and best-possible-world lines of Lactantius, Augustine, Aquinas, and Leibniz;
  • the protest tradition that declines theodicy altogether — the honest reminder that the timeline records a question some still hold open.
