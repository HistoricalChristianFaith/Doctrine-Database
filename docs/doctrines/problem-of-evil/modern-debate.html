The modern debate — Hume's challenge and the analytic reply

Dates: the early-modern to contemporary turn, from David Hume’s Dialogues concerning Natural Religion (1779) through the twentieth- and twenty-first-century analytic debate · representative works: J. L. Mackie (1955), John Hick (1966), Alvin Plantinga (1974), William Rowe (1979), Stephen Wykstra (1984), Marilyn McCord Adams (1989), Eleonore Stump (2010) · Hume at Project Gutenberg
Summary: After Leibniz the problem of evil is pressed as an objection to belief in God. Hume, in the Dialogues (1779), gives the Epicurean trilemma to the sceptic Philo as a difficulty for natural theology. In the twentieth century the debate splits into two problems. The logical problem (J. L. Mackie, 1955) charges that God and evil are flatly contradictory; Alvin Plantinga’s free-will defense (1974) argues that the triad is consistent, a conclusion the survey cited below reports as widely accepted, with named dissent. Attention then shifts to the evidential problem (William Rowe, 1979), which grants consistency but argues that the amount and apparent pointlessness of suffering — Rowe’s fawn dying unseen in a forest fire — make God’s non-existence probable; the reply of skeptical theism (Wykstra, Bergmann) holds that we are not in a position to judge that such suffering is truly pointless. Alongside the defenses run the constructive theodicies — John Hick’s “Irenaean” soul-making theodicy (1966) among them — and a later person-centred turn (Marilyn McCord Adams, 1989; Eleonore Stump, 2010) that faults the earlier answers for justifying a person’s suffering by goods enjoyed elsewhere and insists the good must accrue to the sufferer herself. And a protest strand, sharpened by the Holocaust, refuses the whole project: some, with Dostoevsky’s Ivan Karamazov, would rather “return the ticket” than justify a world bought at the price of innocent suffering. This survey records who said what.

Claims & evidence

Hume revives the Epicurean trilemma (1779)

Epicurus’s old questions are yet unanswered. Is he willing to prevent evil, but not able? then is he impotent. Is he able, but not willing? then is he malevolent. Is he both able and willing? whence then is evil?

— David Hume, Dialogues concerning Natural Religion, Part X (the sceptic Philo speaking).1 This is the dilemma that Lactantius had recorded (and answered) fifteen centuries earlier, here pressed as an objection to natural religion. Through Philo, Hume presses that the mixed world we observe gives us no ground to infer a wholly good and omnipotent author — at best a cause “indifferent” to good and evil.

The logical problem — Mackie (1955)

In its simplest form the problem is this: God is omnipotent; God is wholly good; and yet evil exists. There seems to be some contradiction between these three propositions, so that if any two of them were true the third would be false.

— J. L. Mackie, “Evil and Omnipotence” (1955).2 Mackie states the objection in logical form: not that evil makes God unlikely, but that the theist’s own beliefs are logically inconsistent — that no possible reason could reconcile an omnipotent, wholly good God with any evil at all. On Mackie’s framing, if the charge holds, theistic belief is incoherent.

The free-will defense — Plantinga (1974)

A world containing creatures who are significantly free (and freely perform more good than evil actions) is more valuable, all else being equal, than a world containing no free creatures at all… To create creatures capable of moral good, therefore, He must create creatures capable of moral evil; and He can’t give these creatures the freedom to perform evil and at the same time prevent them from doing so.

— Alvin Plantinga, God, Freedom, and Evil (1974).3 Plantinga presents his reply as a defense, not a theodicy: it need not give God’s actual reason, only show that a good, omnipotent God could have a sufficient one — which, he argues, is all that is needed to answer Mackie’s charge of contradiction. On his argument, if genuine freedom is a great good and even omnipotence cannot make a creature freely choose only the good, then a world with free creatures who sometimes sin may be better than any sinless world of automata, and God is not impugned for permitting the evil freedom makes possible. Irenaeus and Basil had also lodged moral evil in free will; Plantinga states the answer in modal terms. The Wikipedia survey cited reports wide support among philosophers for the view that the defense answers the logical problem (citing Howard-Snyder and O’Leary-Hawthorne, 1998, and Chad Meister, 2009), records dissent from Graham Oppy (2006) and A. M. Weisberger (1999), and reports Daniel Howard-Snyder’s (2013) view that Mackie conceded the logical problem though not because of Plantinga.

The soul-making theodicy — Hick (1966)

Rather, this world must be a place of soul-making. And its value is to be judged, not primarily by the quantity of pleasure and pain occurring in it at any particular moment, but by its fitness for its primary purpose, the purpose of soul-making.

— John Hick, Evil and the God of Love (1966).4 Where Plantinga defends consistency, Hick offers a positive theodicy, and names it after Irenaeus: the Irenaean, over against the Augustinian. Humanity is not a perfection that fell but an immature creature made to grow, through a world of real hardship, from innocence into the earned likeness of God. A frictionless paradise would produce no courage, compassion, or faith; the “vale of soul-making” (Hick borrows the phrase from a letter of the poet John Keats) is the environment such growth requires. Hick draws on Irenaeus; Lactantius had also argued that a painless world would be a virtueless one. Hick carries the theodicy to a universalist completion — soul-making must continue beyond death until all are perfected, or God’s purpose would be defeated.

The evidential problem — Rowe and the fawn (1979)

Suppose in some distant forest lightning strikes a dead tree, resulting in a forest fire. In the fire a fawn is trapped, horribly burned, and lies in terrible agony for several days before death relieves its suffering. So far as we can see, the fawn’s intense suffering is pointless. For there does not appear to be any greater good such that the prevention of the fawn’s suffering would require either the loss of that good or the occurrence of an evil equally bad or worse.

— William Rowe, “The Problem of Evil and Some Varieties of Atheism” (1979).5 Rowe grants that God and evil are not shown to be contradictory and mounts a different case: that the sheer quantity of apparently gratuitous suffering makes atheism the more reasonable belief. His formal premise is that “there exist instances of intense suffering which an omnipotent, omniscient being could have prevented without thereby losing some greater good or permitting some evil equally bad or worse.”6 Rowe chooses the fawn — suffering unseen, benefiting no one, teaching no one — as a case in which no human freedom is exercised and no soul is schooled. If even one such evil is truly pointless, Rowe argues, the traditional God does not exist.

Skeptical theism — the reply of Wykstra and Bergmann

Skeptical theists grant Rowe’s logic but deny his key premise the support it needs. Stephen Wykstra argued (1984) that the move from “we can see no justifying good” to “there is none” is licensed only if we could reasonably expect to see God’s reasons were there any — and, given the gulf between infinite and finite minds, we have no such expectation. His “CORNEA” principle holds that one may infer “it appears there is no X” to “there is no X” only where, if there were an X, it would likely be within our ken.7 On this skeptical theism — developed also by Michael Bergmann, William Alston, and others — our failure to spot a God-justifying reason for the fawn’s agony is exactly what we should expect whether or not one exists, so it is no evidence against God: God’s reasons may be real and beyond us. Boethius and Calvin had also appealed to a providence the creature cannot survey.

The person-centred turn — Adams (1989) and Stump (2010)

evils the participation in (the doing or suffering of) which gives one reason prima facie to doubt whether one’s life could (given their inclusion in it) be a great good to one on the whole.

— Marilyn McCord Adams, defining horrendous evils (1989).8 A distinct late-modern line faults the whole preceding debate for staying abstract and third-personal — trading God against “evil” in general, and justifying an evil by a good enjoyed by the world, by others, or by a completed cosmos. Adams insists the worst evils pose a concrete, first-personal problem: how can God be good to the very person who participates in a horror, so that her life is still a great good to her? Generic, “religion-neutral” goods, she argues, are the wrong size to defeat such evils (in Roderick Chisholm’s sense, as opposed to merely balancing them off); only an incommensurate good — beatific intimacy with God — can. Eleonore Stump, in Wandering in Darkness (2010), builds a positive theodicy to meet that demand, reconstructing Aquinas: God permits an adult’s suffering when it is the best available means to that same person’s own greatest good, union of love with God — a case she defends not by abstract proof but by close reading of the narratives of Job, Samson, Abraham, and Mary of Bethany.

God is justified in allowing human beings to endure suffering such as that experienced by Job, Samson, Abraham, and Mary in the stories, because, through their suffering and only by its means, God gives to each of the protagonists something that these sufferers are willing to trade their suffering to receive, once they understand the nature of what they are being given.

— Eleonore Stump, Wandering in Darkness.9

The protest strand

A further strand declines the enterprise of theodicy. One literary form of it is Ivan Karamazov, who — heaping up the tortures of children — will not accept even a final harmony bought at that price: “It’s not God that I don’t accept, Alyosha, only I most respectfully return Him the ticket.”10 After Auschwitz the protest was taken up in theology. Richard Rubenstein’s After Auschwitz (1966) held that the death camps had made the old God of history unbelievable; John K. Roth’s “theodicy of protest” (1981) accuses God rather than acquits Him, holding the complaint of Job open; and Elie Wiesel’s Night and his Trial of God dramatize a faith that arraigns heaven and receives silence.11 These writers offer a refusal of answers rather than answers. The philosophers of anti-theodicy — Kenneth Surin (1986), Terrence Tilley (1991), D. Z. Phillips (2004) — argue against the practice of theodicy itself; Tilley holds that “engaging in the discourse practice of theodicy creates evil.”12

Placement on the timeline

This page closes the timeline as a survey of modern positions, as modern surveys close others. In brief, the modern debate includes:

  • the logical problem (Mackie) and the free-will defense (Plantinga) offered in reply, with its reception as reported above (compare the free-will answer of Irenaeus and Basil);
  • the evidential problem (Rowe’s fawn) and the skeptical-theist reply (Wykstra, Bergmann);
  • the constructive theodicies — Hick’s soul-making, with the greater-good and best-possible-world answers given earlier by Lactantius, Augustine, Aquinas, and Leibniz;
  • the person-centred theodicies that answer the sufferer rather than the spectator — Adams’s horrendous evils and Stump’s narrative theodicy;
  • the protest and anti-theodicy writers who decline theodicy altogether.
