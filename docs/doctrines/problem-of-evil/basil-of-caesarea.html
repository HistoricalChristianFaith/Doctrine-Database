Basil of Caesarea on the problem of evil

Dates: c. 378 · Wikipedia
Summary: Basil the Great supplies the two moves that would organize the classical Christian answer: evil is not a substance — not a living essence God made, nor an eternal rival principle — but the condition of a soul that has fallen away from the good; and moral evil originates in the free turning of the creature's own will, so that each person is “the first author of his own vice.” God is therefore in no sense the author of evil. Basil makes the case at length in the homily That God is Not the Author of Evil; the same theodicy is stated verbatim in his Hexaemeron, quoted here.

Claims & evidence

Evil is not a substance or an essence, and does not come from God

It is equally impious to say that evil has its origin from God; because the contrary cannot proceed from its contrary.… Evil is not a living animated essence; it is the condition of the soul opposed to virtue, developed in the careless on account of their falling away from good.

— Hexaemeron, Homily 2.4, source. This is the privation answer in its Greek form: evil has no nature of its own but is a defect, a falling-away — so one need not posit a dark creator or a co-eternal principle of wickedness to explain it. Basil develops it against the dualist reading of the primordial “darkness” of Genesis 1:2.

Each is the first author of his own vice; evil has no origin but our voluntary falls

Do not then go beyond yourself to seek for evil, and imagine that there is an original nature of wickedness. Each of us, let us acknowledge it, is the first author of his own vice.… Do not look for the guiding cause beyond yourself, but recognise that evil, rightly so called, has no other origin than our voluntary falls.

— Hexaemeron, Homily 2.5, source. The free-will strand joined to the privation strand: because evil is a falling-away and not a thing, its cause is the free creature that falls, not God who made only good. This is exactly the pairing Augustine would systematize in the Latin West.

Notes

  • Basil distinguishes what later writers call “true evil” (moral evil, kakia — sin, the soul's turning) from the hardships and afflictions loosely called evils (illness, want, death), which he treats as God's disciplinary or corrective goods, not evils “rightly so called.” The distinction between moral and natural evil is already doing work here.
  • Sourcing note. Basil's dedicated homily That God is Not the Author of Evil (Quod Deus non est auctor malorum, Homily 9 in the standard numbering — conjecturally preached after a public calamity such as the earthquake that destroyed Nicaea) carries this argument at length: it takes up the hard verses that seem to make God evil's author (Isaiah 45:7, “I make peace and create evil”; Amos 3:6) and resolves them, concluding — in the summary of the standard edition — that “of the evils of hell the cause is not God, but ourselves… the origin and root of sin is what is in our own control and our free will” (NPNF2 8, Prolegomena § VI, on the homilies). The verbatim quotations above are from his Hexaemeron (Homily 2), where the identical theodicy — evil as no substance, moral evil from the free will — is stated in the standard NPNF translation; Homily 9 itself is available in English only in modern editions (e.g. the Popular Patristics On the Human Condition), so it is summarized rather than quoted here.
  • Basil appears on this wiki also as a witness on the astrology, prayer to the saints, and universal restoration (as a counter-witness) timelines.
