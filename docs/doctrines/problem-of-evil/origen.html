Origen of Alexandria on the problem of evil

Dates: c. 248 · Wikipedia
Summary: Origen sets out a theodicy with several joined parts. Moral evil is lodged wholly in the free rational creature, not in God or in matter; evil is no substance God made but an incidental residue of His good works; the manifest inequality of creatures — the objection that would make God unjust — is defended as God's justice, the outworking of the creatures' own prior free choices; and the whole is governed by a providence whose punishments are remedial, correcting rather than destroying, a theme he carries into his teaching on the cleansing fire and the final restoration of all.

Claims & evidence

Moral evil lodges in the free rational creature, not in outward things

Seeing, then, that these positions are thus established by a sort of natural evidence, is it not superfluous to throw back the causes of our actions on those things which happen to us from without, and thus transfer the blame from ourselves, on whom it wholly lies? For this is to say that we are like pieces of wood, or stones, which have no motion in themselves, but receive the causes of their motion from without.

— De Principiis III.1.5, source.1 Origen's whole chapter “On the Freedom of the Will” drives at this: a rational animal, unlike a stone, has a reason that assents to or refuses what presents itself, so the tempting object is “not a complete and absolute cause” of the sin. Because reason “has within itself the power of distinguishing between good and evil,” the creature “may justly be deemed worthy of praise in choosing what is good, and deserving of censure in following that which is base or wicked.”2 On Origen's account the blame for moral evil rests on the will, and nowhere higher.

The inequality of creatures traced to their own free choices

…this freedom of will incited each one either to progress by imitation of God, or reduced him to failure through negligence. And this, as we have already stated, is the cause of the diversity among rational creatures, deriving its origin not from the will or judgment of the Creator, but from the freedom of the individual will… On which account the Creator will neither appear to be unjust in distributing (for the causes already mentioned) to every one according to his merits; nor will the happiness or unhappiness of each one's birth… be deemed accidental.

— De Principiis II.9.6, source.3 This answers a form of the problem the Gnostics pressed — why are creatures so unequally placed, one born to ease and another to misery, if one just God made them all? Against the Marcionites and Valentinians, who inferred different creators or different natures of soul, Origen holds that God made all rational natures “equal and alike,” and that their present diversity is the just wage of choices they themselves made. The mechanism is freedom used or squandered: God gave “the power of free and voluntary action, by which the good that was in them might become their own… but slothfulness, and a dislike of labour in preserving what is good… furnished the beginning of a departure from goodness. But to depart from good is nothing else than to be made bad.”4 (This defence rests on his teaching of the soul's pre-existence and pre-natal fall, a teaching anathematized in the sixth century; see the condemnation of Origen ⚠ unverified.)

Evil traced to the created mind rather than to God or matter; its amount under providence

It is true, certainly, that evils do not proceed from God… But to maintain that matter, dwelling among mortal things, is the cause of evils, is in our opinion not true. For it is the mind of each individual which is the cause of the evil which arises in him…

— Against Celsus IV.66, source.5 Celsus had located evil in matter and declared the sum of evils fixed and unchangeable for all time. Origen places the source in the wicked mind rather than in matter, and denies the fixity: the “amount is not always the same, owing to the working of a Providence which either preserves earthly things, or purges them by means of floods and conflagrations,” so that on his account evil is finite and under God's remedial government.6

Evil as an incidental residue of God's good works

We, on the other hand, maintain that ‘evil,’ or ‘wickedness,’ and the actions which proceed from it, were not created by God… Evils, then, if those be meant which are properly so called, were not created by God; but some, although few in comparison with the order of the whole world, have resulted from His principal works, as there follow from the chief works of the carpenter such things as spiral shavings and sawdust…

— Against Celsus VI.55, source.7 Answering Celsus's taunt “How is it that God created evil?”, Origen distinguishes real (moral) evil, which God did not create, from the merely corporeal evils He uses to correct. In the image of the carpenter's shavings and the builder's rubble, evil is not a product God intended but an unwilled by-product at the edge of good works. Later witnesses on this timeline (Basil, Pseudo-Dionysius, Aquinas) describe evil as a privation rather than a substance.

Reception and context

  • Remedial punishment and universal restoration. In Origen's account providence does not merely permit evil; it corrects it. Punishment is medicinal, a purging fire that heals rather than destroys — the theme traced on the purgatory timeline — and he carries this remedial logic into his restoration of all things (apokatastasis), on which even the last enemy is finally subdued and God becomes “all in all.” In his writing the theodicy and the universal restoration form one continuous argument.
  • Overlap with his other timelines. The diversity-as-justice defence is built on the soul's pre-existence and pre-natal fall — a teaching the sixth-century proceedings traced on the condemnation of Origen anathematized ⚠ unverified. His account of evil's origin in a prior rebellion of rational spirits (he asks, at Against Celsus IV.65, “what he was before he became a devil, and how he became such”) bears on the divine council timeline.
  • Placement on the timeline. Origen (c. 248) stands between Irenaeus (c. 180), with whom he shares the free-will lodging of moral evil, and Lactantius (c. 313). His is the earliest extant sustained Christian answer to the inequality objection surveyed here, and in it providence, remedial punishment, and a final restoration form a single account.
