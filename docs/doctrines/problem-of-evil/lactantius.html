Lactantius on the problem of evil

Dates: c. 313 · Wikipedia
Summary: Lactantius, the Christian rhetorician known as the “Christian Cicero,” preserves the sharpest ancient statement of the problem — the fourfold dilemma he attributes to Epicurus (God is either unwilling, or unable, or both, or neither) — and then answers it. His resolution is not privation or free will but a contrast theodicy: God could remove evils but does not, because He gives wisdom along with them, and wisdom and virtue cannot exist without evils to know, endure, and overcome. It is the earliest form on this timeline of the “evil makes the good knowable” reply, kin to Irenaeus's knowledge-of-the-contrary.

Claims & evidence

The Epicurean dilemma, stated in full

God, he says, either wishes to take away evils, and is unable; or He is able, and is unwilling; or He is neither willing nor able, or He is both willing and able. If He is willing and is unable, He is feeble, which is not in accordance with the character of God; if He is able and unwilling, He is envious, which is equally at variance with God; if He is neither willing nor able, He is both envious and feeble, and therefore not God; if He is both willing and able, which alone is suitable to God, from what source then are evils? or why does He not remove them?

— A Treatise on the Anger of God (De Ira Dei), ch. 13, source. This is the locus classicus of the “inconsistent triad,” and Lactantius is the reason it survives in the form later philosophy calls the “Epicurean trilemma” (whether Epicurus himself framed it so is debated; Lactantius is quoting to refute).

His answer: God gives wisdom with evils, and wisdom cannot exist without them

For God is able to do whatever He wishes, and there is no weakness or envy in God. He is able, therefore, to take away evils; but He does not wish to do so, and yet He is not on that account envious. For on this account He does not take them away, because He at the same time gives wisdom, as I have shown; and there is more of goodness and pleasure in wisdom than of annoyance in evils.… Therefore, unless we first know evil, we shall be unable to know good. But Epicurus did not see this, nor did any other, that if evils are taken away, wisdom is in like manner taken away; and that no traces of virtue remain in man, the nature of which consists in enduring and overcoming the bitterness of evils.

— A Treatise on the Anger of God, ch. 13, source. The dilemma is broken at its last horn: God is willing to remove evils only if that were the greater good, but it is not — for “if there is no evil… all the material of wisdom is taken away.” Virtue is by definition the endurance and conquest of hardship; a painless world would be a virtueless one.

Notes

  • Lactantius's reply is a defense-plus-theodicy in one: it argues the triad is not contradictory (the last horn is answered) and offers a positive reason God permits evil (the making of wisdom and virtue). Its weak point, pressed by later critics, is that it addresses moral and pedagogical evil far better than gratuitous natural suffering.
  • The contrast principle — “no one can… know what is good, unless he at the same time knows to reject and avoid the things which are evil” — is the same reasoning Irenaeus used (knowledge requires its contrary) and anticipates the later “greater-good” theodicies.
  • Lactantius appears on this wiki also as a witness on the astrology, Old Testament canon, and origin of the soul timelines.
