John Chrysostom on the problem of evil

Dates: c. 400 · Wikipedia
Summary: Chrysostom (c. 347–407), the great preacher of Antioch and later archbishop of Constantinople, answers the problem of evil not with metaphysics but from the pulpit and out of his own sufferings. Where the Cappadocians ask what evil is, he asks what evil can do to a soul — and answers: nothing, unless the soul consents. His two most sustained treatments span his career: the homily That Demons Do Not Govern the World (the first of Concerning the Power of Demons), preached at Antioch, argues that God's very chastisements — famine, disease, calamity — are the “greatest form of His providence,” a physician's bitter medicine, and that the prosperity of the wicked and the suffering of the just are deferred to a coming requital that proves the Resurrection; and the treatise That No One Can Harm the Man Who Does Not Injure Himself, written from his final exile c. 407, presses the pastoral thesis to its limit — that no loss, slander, sickness, or death touches the one thing that is a person's true good, the virtue of the soul, so that “no one of those who are wronged is wronged by another, but experiences this injury at his own hands.”

Claims & evidence

God's chastisements are the highest form of His providence, not evidence against it

Hold fast this argument then with me, and let it ever be fixed and immoveable in your minds, that not only when he confers benefits but even when he chastises God is good and loving. For even his chastisements and his punishments are the greatest part of his beneficence, the greatest form of his providence. Whenever therefore thou seest that famines have taken place, and pestilences, and drought and immoderate rains, and irregularities in the atmosphere, or any other of the things which chasten human nature, be not distressed, nor be despondent, but worship Him who caused them, marvel at Him for His tender care.

— Concerning the Power of Demons, Homily 1 (“That Demons Do Not Govern the World”), §4, source.1 The homily's target is the fatalist who, seeing the wicked flourish and the just suffer, concludes that God has handed the world over to demons. Chrysostom's answer is the pastoral core of his theodicy: apparent evils are the work of a physician who “when he cuts, and when he cauterizes, and when he brings his bitter medicines… is equally a physician.” The suffering is not a sign that providence has failed but the very instrument through which it works — the same greater-good frame the timeline meets in Lactantius and Augustine, here voiced as consolation rather than argument.

Calamity is called “evil” only in name — real evil is sin alone

There is then evil, which is really evil; fornication, adultery, covetousness, and the countless dreadful things, which are worthy of the utmost reproach and punishment. Again there is evil, which rather is not evil, but is called so, famine, pestilence, death, disease, and others of a like kind. For these would not be evils… Why then? Because, were they evils, they would not have become the sources of good to us, chastening our pride, goading our sloth, and leading us on to zeal, making us more attentive.

— Concerning the Power of Demons, Homily 1, §5, source.2 Reconciling Amos 3:6 (“there is no evil in the city which the Lord hath not done”) and Isaiah 45:7 (“I… create evil”) with God's goodness, Chrysostom draws the distinction the whole tradition turns on: moral evil (sin), which God does not author, and natural evil (famine, disease, death), which He does send but which is “not evil” in truth because it is remedial. This is his nearest approach to the metaphysical question — not the privation answer of the Greeks so much as a moral re-description: what men call evil in their calamities is, rightly seen, medicine.

Why the wicked prosper and the just suffer — the deferral to a coming requital

On this account some of the wicked he chastens, and some he does not chasten and some of the good he honours and some he does not honour. He does not chasten all, in order that he may persuade thee, that there is a Resurrection. But he chastens some in order that he may make the more careless, through fear by means of the punishment of the others, more in earnest.… it is necessary to hope for some other requital awaiting us, in order that by each one receiving according to his desert, the justice of God may be made manifest.

— Concerning the Power of Demons, Homily 1, §7, source.3 Here Chrysostom meets the sharpest form of the problem — the unequal distribution of fortune, the same objection Origen answered from pre-natal merit — but his answer is eschatological, not protological: the mixed lot of the present life is deliberately mixed, precisely so that neither the sceptic (were all justly paid now, faith in the Resurrection would be needless) nor the careless (were none paid now, the wicked would grow bolder) could rest. The unevenness is itself a work of providence, and the balance is struck in the world to come. He adds that God does not punish every sin here because, on Psalm 130:3, “if Thou observedst iniquity O Lord, who shall stand?” — our race would be swept away.

No one is truly harmed except by himself — the soul's virtue is untouchable

What then is the virtue of man? not riches that thou shouldest fear poverty: nor health of body that thou shouldest dread sickness, nor the opinion of the public, that thou shouldest view an evil reputation with alarm, nor life simply for its own sake, that death should be terrible to thee: nor liberty that thou shouldest avoid servitude: but carefulness in holding true doctrine, and rectitude in life. Of these things not even the devil himself will be able to rob a man, if he who possesses them guards them with the needful carefulness.

— That No One Can Harm the Man Who Does Not Injure Himself, §3, source.4 The treatise's whole burden is stated at the outset: “no one of those who are wronged is wronged by another, but experiences this injury at his own hands.” It is not a denial that men do wrong — “I did not say that no one injures, but that no one is injured” — but a relocation of a person's true good. Since virtue alone is that good, and virtue lies in the will, no external loss can reach it: Job stripped of everything, Lazarus in his sores, Paul beaten and shipwrecked, the three children in the furnace, all emerge “more illustrious.” Suffering “unjustly inflicted… expiate[s] sins, and work[s] righteousness”; only a person's own consent to blasphemy or despair — “his own littleness of soul” — can truly injure him. Written from exile as his enemies hounded him toward death, it is theodicy lived rather than theorized.

Notes

  • Chrysostom's answer is deliberately pastoral rather than systematic. He does not resolve the triad by analysis of evil's being (as Basil and Gregory of Nyssa do) but disarms it practically: even granting the reality and the sting of suffering, it cannot harm the one good that matters, and it serves ends a good God intends. The Antiochene preference for the plain moral sense over allegory shows throughout.
  • Job is his master-case, cited in both works; Chrysostom returns to Job so often that he appears on this wiki as a witness on the Job timeline as well. The devil, permitted to strip Job of goods, children, health, and reputation, “not only did him no damage but rendered him more glorious” — the permitted-affliction reading Chrysostom generalizes into a theodicy.
  • His treatment of natural evil as “not evil” but remedial medicine is a distinctively Antiochene consolation; it sits beside, rather than inside, the Greek privation metaphysic, and answers the pastoral question (how should the sufferer regard his suffering?) more than the speculative one (whence evil?).
  • Chrysostom appears across this wiki as a witness on many timelines — from the Old Testament canon to the intermediate state — and as a principal of the Theophilus–Chrysostom conflict that drove him into the exile from which this treatise was written.
