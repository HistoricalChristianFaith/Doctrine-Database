Thomas Aquinas on the Immaculate Conception

Dates: c. 1270 (Summa Theologiae III) · 1225–1274 · Wikipedia
Summary: The Dominican theologian, in his Summa Theologiae, holds that Mary was sanctified in the womb after animation, her soul having first contracted original sin, and that an immaculate conception would be “derogatory to the dignity of Christ” as universal Saviour, since then Mary would not have needed his redemption.

Claims & evidence

Sanctification after animation, before birth

the soul of the Blessed Virgin did indeed contract original sin, but was cleansed therefrom before her birth from the womb.

— Summa Theologiae III, q. 27, a. 2, ad 3, source. The sanctification, Aquinas argues in the body of the article, “cannot be understood as having taken place before animation”: sanctification is a cleansing from original sin, and only a rational creature can be the subject of sin, so before the soul is infused there is nothing to cleanse. Had Mary been sanctified before animation, “she could never have incurred the stain of original sin: and thus she would not have needed redemption and salvation which is by Christ.” It “remains, therefore, that the Blessed Virgin was sanctified after animation” — and, he adds in the previous article, in the womb before her birth.1

The argument from Christ’s universal redemption

If the soul of the Blessed Virgin had never incurred the stain of original sin, this would be derogatory to the dignity of Christ, by reason of His being the universal Saviour of all.

— Summa Theologiae III, q. 27, a. 2, ad 2, source. Aquinas reasons that Christ is “the Saviour of all men” (1 Timothy 4:10), and that to save is to redeem from sin either present or contracted; if Mary had never contracted the stain, she would not be among the “all” whom Christ redeemed. Duns Scotus, lecturing at Oxford c. 1300, answered this argument by holding that to be preserved from contracting sin is the highest form of being redeemed.2

Reception and debate

  • In the Summa, like Bernard and Bonaventure, Aquinas affirms Mary’s sanctification in the womb and her freedom from actual sin while denying that she was preserved from original sin at her conception. Frederick Holweck (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1910) writes that “the Dominicans, however, were under special obligation to follow the doctrines of St. Thomas.”3 The later disputes between Dominicans and Franciscans at Paris are recorded under John of Montson (1387) and the University of Paris oath (1497).
  • Aquinas also appears as a witness on the perpetual virginity timeline, a distinct Marian question, which he affirmed.
  • His handling of the feasts (on his account the Nativity feast attests sanctification in the womb, while the tolerated Conception feast attests nothing about the conception) is set in its wider context on the lex orandi, lex credendi timeline: see Aquinas on the custom of the Church.
  • His On the Angelic Salutation says that from original sin Mary “was cleansed in the womb” (§7), and that “the Blessed Virgin was conceived in original (sin), but not born (in it)” (§8).4 Holweck writes that “much discussion has arisen as to whether St. Thomas did or did not deny that the Blessed Virgin was immaculate”; he notes that Aquinas “at first pronounced in favour of the doctrine in his treatise on the ‘Sentences’”.5 The readings of his position, including the dispute over the authentic text of the Angelic Salutation, are set out on the disputed-reading page Did Thomas Aquinas deny the Immaculate Conception?
