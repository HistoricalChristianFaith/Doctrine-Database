The Venerable Bede on “full of grace” (Luke 1:28) and the Immaculate Conception

Dates: c. 735 (the Northumbrian monk of Wearmouth–Jarrow, c. 672/673 – 735, called “the Venerable” and the father of English history — his Commentary on Luke (In Lucae evangelium expositio), dedicated to Bishop Acca of Hexham, was written c. 709–716, and his Homilies on the Gospels somewhat later) · Wikipedia
Summary: Bede expounds Gabriel’s gratia plena (“full of grace,” Luke 1:28) in his Commentary on Luke and his Annunciation homily. He writes that Mary is “rightly… called full of grace, because she obviously attained the grace that no other had merited, that she would conceive and bear the author of grace himself,” and in the homily he unfolds the phrase through her virginity, her converse with the angel, and her bearing of Christ. The passages quoted here do not take up the question of Mary’s own conception, which became a subject of dispute in the Latin West some four centuries after him.

Claims & evidence

“Full of grace”: to conceive and bear the Author of grace (Luke 1:28)

Commenting on the salutation, Bede explains gratia plena by what Mary is chosen for — to conceive and bear the incarnate Word:

And the angel entered to her and said, Hail, full of grace, the Lord is with you: blessed are you among women. It is rightly that she is called full of grace, because she obviously attained the grace that no other had merited, that she would conceive and bear the author of grace himself.

— Commentary on Luke (In Lucae evangelium expositio), Book I, on Luke 1:28.1 The grace “no other had merited” is defined by a purpose clause: that she would conceive and bear the author of grace himself. On the next verse Bede adds that the angel, having called her full of grace, “further establishes that grace and explains it more abundantly,” and goes on to the conception and bearing of the Lord.2

The fullness unfolded: virginity, maternity, and a sanctification “afterwards” (Homilies 1.3)

Bede expounds the same greeting at length in his Annunciation homily. Three times he repeats “truly she was full of grace,” each time naming a privilege — her primacy in vowed virginity, her worthiness to see and hear an angel, and her bearing of Christ:

Truly she was full of grace, to whom it was granted by divine gift, that she might be the first among women to offer to God the most glorious gift of virginity. Therefore, she rightly deserved to enjoy at once the sight and speech of an angel, who strove to imitate the angelic life. Truly she was full of grace, to whom it was granted to bear Jesus Christ Himself, through whom grace and truth came into being, and afterwards, through the human nature, consecrated all fullness of divinity. Truly also blessed among women was she who, without example of female condition, rejoiced with the honor of motherhood along with the beauty of virginity, and who, as befitted a virgin mother, bore God the Son.

— Homilies on the Gospels 1.3 (on the Annunciation, Luke 1:26–38).3 The Cistercian translation of Lawrence T. Martin and David Hurst renders the clause with Mary as its object: the Lord “first raised [her] up from earthly to heavenly desires… and afterwards sanctified [her], by means of his human nature, with all the fullness of his divinity.”4

Reception and debate

  • Bede and Luke 1:28. Gabriel’s kecharitōmenē / gratia plena is among the texts that later writers adduced for and against the Immaculate Conception. Bede’s exposition of it, written c. 709–716, ties the fullness of grace to the divine maternity and to Mary’s virginity. Bonaventure, who also expounded gratia plena, held in his Sentences commentary that Mary’s soul contracted original sin and was sanctified shortly after animation.
  • Text and transmission. Bede’s Luke commentary, woven largely from Ambrose, Augustine, Gregory the Great, and Jerome, became a staple of Carolingian and medieval exegesis and fed the Glossa Ordinaria; his Annunciation homily circulated as a fixed lection. Through both, his maternity-centred reading of “full of grace” reached the schoolmen who later disputed the Immaculate Conception.
