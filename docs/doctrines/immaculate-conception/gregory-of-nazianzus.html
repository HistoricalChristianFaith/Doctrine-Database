Gregory of Nazianzus on the Immaculate Conception

Dates: c. 380 (the Oration on the Theophany, Oration 38, preached at Constantinople c. Dec. 380–Jan. 381; the same passage recurs in Oration 45, On Easter) · c. 329–390 · Wikipedia
Summary: Gregory the Theologian, preaching on the Nativity c. 380, says that Christ was conceived “by the Virgin, who first in body and soul was purified by the Holy Ghost” — the participle prokathartheisa, “prepurified.” Later Greek writers on this timeline, among them Sophronius and John of Damascus, also speak of Mary as purified beforehand. Christiaan Kappes (Catholic, 2014) reads Gregory’s word as a Greek antecedent of the Immaculate Conception; Craig Truglia (Orthodox, 2020) reads it as a cleansing, at the conception of Christ, of a nature subject to the ancestral condition; Kallistos Ware (Orthodox, 2007) and Vladimir Lossky (Orthodox, 1974) read it as an added grace at the Annunciation. The readings are set out on the disputed-reading page Does Gregory Nazianzen’s prokathartheisa testify to the Immaculate Conception?

Claims & evidence

The prokathartheisa passage: the Virgin “first in body and soul… purified by the Holy Ghost”

…the Word of God Himself… came to His own Image, and took on Him flesh for the sake of our flesh, and mingled Himself with an intelligent soul for my soul’s sake, purifying like by like; and in all points except sin was made man. Conceived by the Virgin, who first in body and soul was purified by the Holy Ghost (for it was needful both that Childbearing should be honoured, and that Virginity should receive a higher honour), He came forth then as God with that which He had assumed, One Person in two Natures…

— Oration 38 (On the Theophany, or Birthday of Christ), §13, source. The Greek phrase behind “first… was purified” is tēs parthenou… prokathartheịsēs — prokathartheisa, an aorist passive participle of prokathairō, “to purify beforehand / prepurify.” Gregory repeats the sentence almost verbatim in his later Easter oration — “conceived by the Virgin, who first in body and soul was purified by the Holy Ghost” (Oration 45, On Easter, §9, source) — a formula he used more than once.1

Readings of the word

Catholic and Orthodox writers differ over what the prefix pro- (“before”) modifies and what the purifying removes.

[Kappes:] The Greek Fathers — in the line of the Nazianzen until the introduction of Byzantine Thomism in the 14th century — never vacillated about the all-immaculate status of the Blessed Virgin Mary.

Christiaan Kappes (Catholic) takes prokathartheisa as an already-pure nature raised to a still-higher purity — a “preparation” rather than a cleansing from defilement, on the analogy of Christ’s own baptism (purified though sinless). On his account Gregory is an Eastern antecedent of the Latin teaching on Mary’s conception, claim (b).2 Craig Truglia (Orthodox, 2020) takes purification to presuppose something to be purified, and reads Gregory as placing it at the conceiving of Christ: in his words, “Mary had a postlapsarian womb subject to corruption and death due to original sin,” which the Spirit cleansed when she conceived. He cites John of Damascus, who writes that after the Virgin’s assent the Holy Spirit descended on her, “purifying her,” as placing the prepurification at the Annunciation rather than at Mary’s own conception.3 Kallistos Ware (Orthodox, 2007), following Nicholas Cabasilas’s homilies on the Mother of God, takes the purification as “an addition of the gifts of grace” to a Virgin who, “while sinless in all her personal actions, nonetheless inherited fallen human nature”; Vladimir Lossky (Orthodox, 1974) places this preparation at the Annunciation, “when the Holy Spirit came down upon the Virgin to make her fit to receive the Word of the Father in her womb.” This reading is set out on the disputed-reading page.4

The translation-history datum: prokathartheisa → immaculata

Kappes notes that when Rufinus of Aquileia rendered Gregory’s Nativity oration into Latin near the end of the fourth century, he translated the Greek prokathartheisa with the Latin immaculata.5

Reception and debate

  • The point at issue. Gregory’s clause speaks of the Virgin at the moment Christ was “conceived by the Virgin.” Kappes reads its pro- as pointing back to a purity Mary already possessed; Truglia reads it as a purification worked at the Annunciation. Ware and Lossky read it as an added grace at the Annunciation in a Virgin who never sinned. These readings, with that of Nicholas Cabasilas, are set out on the disputed-reading page.
  • Placement on the timeline. Gregory appears on this wiki also as a witness on Theotokos, prayer to the saints, infant baptism, and the Old Testament canon. On the immaculate conception he stands c. 380, between Ephrem (c. 361) and Augustine (c. 415).
