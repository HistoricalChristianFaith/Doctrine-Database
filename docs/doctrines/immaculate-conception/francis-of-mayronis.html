Francis of Mayronis on the Immaculate Conception

Dates: c. 1288–c. 1328 · defended the doctrine at Paris c. 1320–21; master of theology 1323 · Catholic Encyclopedia
Summary: Francis of Mayronis (Franciscus de Mayronis, “princeps Scotistarum”), a Franciscan disciple of Scotus, is named with Peter Auriol by Frederick Holweck (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1910) as one of the doctrine’s “most fervent champions” in the generation after Scotus. In Christiaan Kappes’s account (2019), where Scotus advanced preservative redemption tentatively, Mayronis argued “forcefully for Mary’s divine ‘privilege’ or exemption from original sin.” Kappes and Juniper Carol credit him with two arguments of his own: he extended Scotus’s doctrine of Christ’s absolute predestination to Mary, and he read original sin through the categories of Roman law, as an inherited liability from which a singular exemption could be juridically granted.

Claims & evidence

Kappes on Mayronis and Scotus

Notably, Francis speculated beyond the bounds set by his doctor, throwing Duns’ caution into the wind on the question of the Immaculate Conception. Duns employed language that has been best classified as “restrained and prudent” regarding the controversial opinio theologica. Diversely, Francis gained notoriety for extolling this doctrine, arguing forcefully for Mary’s divine “privilege” or exemption from original sin.

— Christiaan Kappes, “Francis of Meyronnes and the Immaculate Conception,” in Medieval Franciscan Approaches to the Virgin Mary (Brill, 2019), pp. 196–197, source. Auriol had held that the preservation “can be held without danger to faith”; Mayronis, on Kappes’s account, asserted it. Holweck writes: “Following in the footsteps of their own Duns Scotus, the learned Petrus Aureolus and Franciscus de Mayronis became the most fervent champions of the doctrine, although their older teachers (St. Bonaventure included) had been opposed to it.”1

Predestination and Roman law

Juniper B. Carol (1980) writes that Scotus himself did not extend his doctrine of the absolute predestination of Christ to the Virgin, and that “the first to do so were his disciples John de Bassolis… and Francis de Mayronis”: Mary’s exemption is grounded in the same eternal decree by which the Incarnate Word was willed, so that she is predestined immaculate together with her Son.2 Kappes credits Mayronis as the first schoolman to deploy the categories of Roman civil law systematically in this question, treating original sin as an inherited legal liability or imputed debt — a patrimony rather than a personal fault — on which Mary’s exemption is construed as a juridically granted privilegium, a framing he distinguishes from Scotus’s argument from Christ as perfect mediator.3 ⚠ partly verified: Mayronis’s treatment of the Conception stands in his In quatuor libros Sententiarum (Bk. III, dist. 3), printed at Venice in 1520, and his questions and sermons on it were gathered in Pedro de Alva y Astorga’s Monumenta antiqua seraphica pro Immaculata Conceptione (Louvain, 1665), pp. 275–326.4 The Roman-law/privilegium reading rests on Kappes’s analysis, and the Latin passages have not been read verbatim here.

Reception and debate

  • Mayronis as a Scotist. Mayronis called himself Scotus’s follower (schola nostra) and was hailed by later Scotists as princeps Scotistarum. The Catholic Encyclopedia’s article on him gives his epithets as “Doctor acutus, or Doctor illuminatus, also Magister abstractionum” and calls him “a distinguished pupil of Duns Scotus, whose teaching he usually followed.”5
  • Dating. The dates c. 1288–c. 1328 used here follow recent scholarship ⚠ unverified; the Catholic Encyclopedia gives “Born about 1280” and has him die at Piacenza, “probably 26 July, 1327.” He heard Scotus lecture at Paris c. 1304–07 and defended Mary at Paris c. 1320–21; on 24 May 1323, at the request of King Robert of Naples, Pope John XXII ordered the chancellor of the University of Paris to grant him the degree of master of theology.6
