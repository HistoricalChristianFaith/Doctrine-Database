John of Montson and the Paris condemnation of 1387

Dates: 1387 (condemnation at Paris) · Wikipedia
Summary: John of Montson (Juan de Monzón / Johannes de Montesono), an Aragonese Dominican and Paris master of theology, taught that Mary was conceived in original sin like every other child of Adam and, on the account of Victor O’Daniel (Catholic Encyclopedia), that the immaculist opinion was heretical. The Paris theology faculty condemned fourteen of his propositions; the Bishop of Paris excommunicated defenders of them; Montson appealed to Avignon and then withdrew to Aragon. The Dominicans left the University of Paris and, by Philip Schaff’s account, did not return until 1403. Montson denied claim (b).

Claims & evidence

The thesis: Mary conceived in original sin, and the contrary view heretical

Montson lectured at the Dominican convent of St-Jacques and obtained his mastership in theology at Paris in 1387. Taking the Dominican side of the Franciscan–Dominican quarrel, he taught that the Blessed Virgin was conceived in original sin like all Adam’s descendants. O’Daniel’s article has him “maintaining the proposition that the Blessed Virgin was conceived without sin was heretical,” and reports Heinrich Denifle’s judgement that “Montesono would not have been condemned had he not declared the doctrine of the Immaculate Conception heretical”; the 1911 Britannica gives the ground of the condemnation as his teaching that Mary, “like other ordinary descendants of Adam, was born in original sin.”1,2 Defenders of the doctrine are reported as later observing that no Dominican writer is cited as having denied Mary’s privilege in these terms before 1387 ⚠ unverified.

The condemnation of the fourteen propositions

The faculty of theology condemned fourteen propositions drawn from his lectures, warned him privately and then publicly to retract, and when he refused referred the matter to Pierre d’Orgemont, Bishop of Paris, “who promulgated a decree of excommunication against all who should defend the forbidden theses.”

The appeal, the flight, and the delegates

Montson appealed to the Avignon pope Clement VII, who summoned both him and the university’s delegates. Philip Schaff reports that Montson “refused to recant on the plea that in being condemned Thomas Aquinas was condemned,” and that d’Ailly, before the papal consistory, “took the ground that Thomas, though a saint, was not infallible.”3 Foreseeing defeat, and despite an order to remain at Avignon under pain of excommunication, Montson secretly withdrew into Aragon and thence to Sicily, transferring his allegiance to Clement’s Roman rival Urban VI. Among the university delegates who pressed the case were Pierre d’Ailly (whom Denifle conjectured to have written the faculty’s condemnatory letters) and the young Jean Gerson. The 1911 Britannica describes the delegates’ ground:

John of Montson appealed to Pope Clement VII at Avignon, and Pierre d’Ailly, Gerson and the other university delegates, while they personally supported the doctrine of the Immaculate Conception, were content to rest their case upon the legal rights of the university to test in its own way its theological teachers.

— Encyclopædia Britannica (1911), “Gerson, John.”2

The Dominicans expelled from Paris

Following the papal decision the Order of Preachers withdrew from the University of Paris (c. 1389); Schaff writes that the Dominicans “refused to bow to the decision, left the university and did not return till 1403.” In 1497 the university adopted an oath requiring its members to defend the Immaculate Conception; see the University of Paris oath.

Notes

  • Dating. O’Daniel’s biographical article, the 1911 Britannica and Wikipedia date Montson’s mastership and the faculty’s censure to 1387; Frederick Holweck’s article “Immaculate Conception” in the same Catholic Encyclopedia gives “(John of Montesono, 1373).”4
  • The observation that no Dominican is cited denying the privilege before 1387 is reported as tracing to the eighteenth-century Dominican Thomist Charles-René Billuart; it is given here on secondary authority, not verified against a primary text.
  • The bishop’s name appears as both “Pierre d’Orgemont” and “Pierre Orgement” — the same man. O’Daniel’s article names the pope of the appeal “Clement VIII”; the 1911 Britannica, Schaff and Wikipedia name Clement VII, the Avignon pope. The interval to the 1403 return is about fourteen years from the Dominicans’ withdrawal c. 1389, sixteen from the 1387 condemnation.
