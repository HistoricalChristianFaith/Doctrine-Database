The University of Paris oath on the Immaculate Conception (1497)

Dates: 1497 (Cologne 1499, Vienna 1501, and others following) · Catholic Encyclopedia
Summary: In 1497 the University of Paris decreed that no one could be admitted a member of the university without swearing to defend and assert the Immaculate Conception of Mary. McClintock and Strong’s Cyclopædia traces the oath to the disputes between the Franciscans and Dominicans, in which the Paris faculty had condemned the Dominican John of Montson in 1387. Other universities across Europe and the Americas adopted similar oaths.

Claims & evidence

The oath itself

In 1497 the University of Paris decreed that henceforward no one should be admitted a member of the university, who did not swear that he would do the utmost to defend and assert the Immaculate Conception of Mary.

— The Catholic Encyclopedia, “Immaculate Conception.”1 McClintock and Strong’s Cyclopædia states that the oath was “introduced by the Sorbonne in consequence of the disputes… between the Franciscans and Dominicans”; the 1387 condemnation of John of Montson belongs to the same disputes.2

The oath spreads across Christendom

Other universities followed within a few years — Cologne in 1499, Vienna in 1501 — and eventually a long roll of studia across Europe and the New World:

Toulouse followed the example; in Italy, Bologna and Naples; in the German Empire, Cologne, Mainz, and Vienna; in Belgium, Louvain; in England, before the Reformation, Oxford and Cambridge; in Spain, Salamanca, Toledo, Seville, and Valencia; in Portugal, Coimbra and Evora; in America, Mexico and Lima.

— The Catholic Encyclopedia, “Immaculate Conception” (same article as note 1). The Protestant historian Philip Schaff records that in 1497 Paris “exacted from all candidates for degrees an oath accepting the dogma of the immaculate conception.”3

The oath and the papal acts of the period

The oath was a university statute, a condition of membership and of degrees. Fourteen years earlier, in Grave nimis (1483), Sixtus IV had forbidden either party to charge the other with heresy, the charge against those who held Mary conceived in original sin being forbidden because the question was “not yet decided by the Roman Church and the Apostolic See.”4 The definition of 1854 is recorded under Pius IX.

Dating and sources

  • The year is 1497 across the sources consulted (Catholic Encyclopedia, McClintock & Strong, Schaff); no source supports 1496.
  • The English “do the utmost to defend and assert” is the Catholic Encyclopedia’s own rendering of the oath’s substance, not a translation of the Latin formula, which the sources consulted do not reproduce. It is given here as a report of what was sworn, not as the ipsissima verba.
  • The Cologne 1499 / Vienna 1501 dates follow the sources at note 2; Vienna’s requirement was reaffirmed and formalized in the seventeenth century ⚠ unverified. “Tübingen” is sometimes added to the list but is not named in the sources cited here and is omitted.
