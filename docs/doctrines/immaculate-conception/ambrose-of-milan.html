Ambrose of Milan on the Immaculate Conception

Dates: c. 389 (Ambrose, bishop of Milan c. 374–397; the Marian purity texts are in his Exposition of the Gospel of Luke, completed c. 385–389, and the treatise On the Instruction of a Virgin of c. 391–392) · Wikipedia
Summary: Ambrose praises Mary as “a virgin through grace, free from every stain of sin,” and as uniquely “full of grace.” The same Exposition of Luke says that to be immaculate “from the beginning… is impossible for human nature” and speaks of the “stain of original sin” in the line from which Christ took flesh, and his On the Instruction of a Virgin says that Christ “alone is without sin.” Frederick Holweck (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1910) cites Ambrose among the patristic witnesses to Mary’s purity; Hilda Graef (1963) judged that he “never stretched his claim… as far as the Immaculate Conception.”

Claims & evidence

A virgin “free from every stain of sin” (Exposition of Luke II.56)

Take me up not from Sarah but from Mary, that she may be an incorrupt virgin — but a virgin through grace, free from every stain of sin (ut incorrupta sit virgo, sed virgo per gratiam ab omni integra labe peccati).

— Exposition of the Gospel of Luke, II.56. The sentence sets Mary against Sarah, who bore Isaac in the ordinary way, and qualifies Mary’s freedom from every stain as per gratiam, “through grace.” It is the line from Ambrose that Catholic writers on Mary’s sinlessness most often cite; Taylor Marshall (Catholic) gives the Latin and discusses it under the title “St. Ambrose on Mary’s Sinlessness.”1

“Full of grace… which no other deserved” (Luke 1:28)

This greeting was preserved for Mary alone. For she alone is said to be full of grace, who alone attained grace which no other deserved, so that she might be filled with grace by the author.

— Exposition of the Gospel of Luke, II.9, on Luke 1:28, source. Ambrose comments on Gabriel’s greeting (Greek kecharitōmenē, Latin gratia plena): the grace is one Mary alone “attained,” and with which she was “filled… by the author.”2

Ambrose on sin in all but Christ, and on the “stain of original sin”

Three further passages in Ambrose speak of sin and its inheritance:

Thus, it is not immaculate from the beginning; for this is impossible for human nature: but through the grace of God and its own quality, because it no longer sins, it becomes as if it were immaculate.

— Exposition of the Gospel of Luke, I.17, on the righteousness of Zechariah and Elizabeth (Luke 1:6).3

He is one who does not have a second; for He alone is without sin (solus enim sine peccato), alone without assistance, who says: I looked, and there was no one to help.

— On the Instruction of a Virgin, on Christ’s uniqueness, source.4

…he did not even think it necessary to assert that he himself was exempt from the stain of original sin… Finally, so that he would also initiate the benefit of redemption from his own ancestors; lest anyone think that the stain of his origin could be an impediment to virtue.

— Exposition of the Gospel of Luke, III.35, on the sinners in Christ’s genealogy, source. The Latin for “stain of original sin” is originalis peccati labem; Ambrose speaks of it in the line of Christ’s ancestors, from whom, he writes, Christ began “the benefit of redemption.”5

Reception and debate

  • Holweck’s Catholic Encyclopedia article also cites Ambrose’s Sermo 22 on Psalm 118, where “Ambrose says she is incorrupt, a virgin immune through grace from every stain of sin” — the same per gratiam formula as Exposition of Luke II.56.6 Hilda Graef (Mary: A History of Doctrine and Devotion, 1963) wrote that Ambrose “never stretched his claim… as far as the Immaculate Conception.”7 How the first-millennium texts on Mary’s purity bear on the 1854 definition is the subject of a disputed-reading page.
  • Not to be confused with “Ambrosiaster.” The anonymous fourth-century Latin commentator conventionally called “Ambrosiaster” (“would-be Ambrose”) is a different author. His importance to this family of doctrines is his Latin of Romans 5:12 — in quo omnes peccaverunt, “in whom all sinned” — which fed Augustine’s doctrine of inherited original sin. He is not among the witnesses on this timeline.
  • Ambrose appears on this wiki also on Mary as the New Eve, the perpetual virginity of Mary, and Theotokos; his pupil Augustine is the next Latin witness on this timeline.
