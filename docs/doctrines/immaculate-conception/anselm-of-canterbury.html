Anselm of Canterbury on the Immaculate Conception

Dates: c. 1098 (Cur Deus Homo) · c. 1033–1109 · Wikipedia
Summary: Anselm, archbishop of Canterbury, writes in Cur Deus Homo that Mary “was of the number of those who were cleansed from their sins before” Christ’s birth. In De conceptu virginali he argues that it was fitting (decens erat) that the Virgin shine with “a purity than which, under God, none greater can be conceived,” within a treatise on original sin and its transmission to Adam’s descendants. His disciple Eadmer drew on the argument from fittingness in a treatise arguing that Mary was preserved at her conception.

Claims & evidence

Mary among those “cleansed from their sins before his birth”

the virgin, from whom that man was taken of whom we are speaking, was of the number of those who were cleansed from their sins before his birth.

— Cur Deus Homo (Why God Became Man) II.16, trans. Sidney Norton Deane, source. The passage is a dialogue: Anselm’s interlocutor Boso raises the difficulty that “the virgin herself… was conceived in iniquity, and in sin did her mother bear her” (echoing Psalm 51:5), and Anselm answers by placing Mary among those cleansed from sin before the Lord’s nativity. The positions of Bernard, Bonaventure, and Aquinas, who held that Mary was sanctified after her conception, are recorded on their own pages.1

The argument from fittingness: a “purity than which none greater”

It was fitting that that Virgin should shine with a purity than which, under God, none greater can be conceived.

— De conceptu virginali et de originali peccato (On the Virgin Conception and Original Sin), ch. 18 (Latin: “Decens erat ut ea puritate, qua maior sub Deo nequit intelligi, virgo illa niteret”), trans. Joseph M. Colleran; edition. The phrasing mirrors Anselm’s formula for God in the Proslogion — “that than which nothing greater can be conceived” — here applied to Mary’s purity. The same treatise sets out Anselm’s account of original sin as transmitted to Adam’s descendants apart from the personal fault of their parents. Eadmer, his disciple, took up the argument from fittingness (decuit) in his treatise on Mary’s conception; Duns Scotus argued for Mary’s preservation c. 1300.2

Reception and debate

  • Two works on Mary’s conception. The “purity than which none greater” line is from Anselm’s De conceptu virginali (ch. 18), not from Cur Deus Homo. A second work, the Tractatus de conceptione sanctae Mariae, was long printed among Anselm’s works and is assigned to Eadmer in the edition of Thurston and Slater (1904); F. S. Schmitt’s critical edition of Anselm keeps De conceptu virginali as Anselm’s. Frederick Holweck (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1910) names Anselm as the author of “De Conceptu virginali et originali peccato,” and of a treatise he calls “De Conceptu virginali” “usually ascribed to him” writes that it “was composed by his friend and disciple, the Saxon monk Eadmer of Canterbury”; Holweck also writes of Anselm that “it is certain that he did not introduce the feast anywhere.”3
  • On the timeline Anselm stands between the Greek and Latin fathers and his disciple Eadmer, whose treatise argued that Mary was preserved, not only cleansed.
