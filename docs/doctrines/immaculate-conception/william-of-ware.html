William of Ware on the Immaculate Conception

Dates: fl. c. 1290–1305 (Oxford Sentences lectures, on Book III, dist. 3) · teacher of Duns Scotus · Catholic Encyclopedia
Summary: William of Ware (Guillelmus de Ware, “Doctor Fundatus”), an English Franciscan named in several sources as the teacher of John Duns Scotus, argues in a disputed question “whether the Blessed Virgin was conceived in original sin” that God could preserve Mary from ever contracting the stain, that it was fitting that the Son make his Mother as pure as he could, and that therefore he did so, preserving her rather than only cleansing her. Berard Vogt (Franciscan, 1955) writes that the words “often attributed to Scotus,” potuit, decuit, ergo fecit, “are already found with William of Ware”; the four-word Latin formula itself does not stand in Ware’s question as printed in 1904. Which of the two men first held the immaculist position is disputed.

Claims & evidence

The argument from possibility, fittingness and fact

Congruentia autem patet, quia, postquam Filius eius est ipsa munditia, congruum fuit, ut faceret Matrem suam ita mundam, sicut potuit, et sic non solum mundaret, sed ab omni immunditia praeservaret… et quod potuit, congruum fuit, quod fecerit; et ex hoc sequitur, quod ita fecerit, cum filius debeat matrem honorare.

— Quaestio: Utrum beata Virgo concepta fuerit in originali peccato, ed. Quaracchi (1904), pp. 6–7, source. In translation: “The congruity is clear: since her Son is purity itself, it was fitting that he should make his Mother as pure as he could — and so not merely cleanse her but preserve her from all impurity… and what he could do, it was fitting that he did, and from this it follows that he did so, since a son ought to honour his mother.” Ware sets praeservaret (“preserve”) against mundaret (“cleanse”): on his account Mary is not purged of a stain she contracted but kept from ever contracting it. He builds the argument on Anselm’s De conceptu virginali (which he quotes at length): it was fitting the Virgin shine with “a purity than which, under God, none greater can be conceived.”1

Ware’s reply on John of Damascus’s “purifying”

The opening objection of the question cites John of Damascus (De fide orthodoxa III.2): “Spiritus sanctus supervenit purgans ipsam” (the Holy Spirit came upon her, purifying her), so that she had something to be purified, which can only be original sin. Ware replies that “purified” can be understood in two ways, as the removal of something unclean or as the addition of a perfection, and that the Damascene means the second; or else:

…purgans non a peccato quod infuit, sed quod infuisset, nisi praeservata fuisset; sicut matrimonium excusat a peccato, non quod inest, sed quod inesset…

— same question, reply to the opening objection (Quaracchi ed., p. 11), source. In translation: “…purifying not from sin that was present, but from sin that would have been present had she not been preserved — as marriage excuses from a sin not present but that would be present [absent the good of marriage].” On this reply a preservation can still be called a purifying. The readings of the Greek “prepurified” language are set out on the disputed-reading page Does Gregory Nazianzen’s prokathartheisa testify to the Immaculate Conception?1

Reception and debate

  • Ware’s wording and Scotus’s. Ware’s question sets out the argument in the order possibility, congruity, fact, in his own words and grounded on Anselm. The four-word slogan potuit, decuit, ergo fecit does not appear in it; in the 1904 volume the nearest wording is an objection set out in Peter Auriol’s treatise, which states that the whole case for the preservation rests on the reasoning “quod Deus hoc facere potuit, et si potuit, decuit, et si decuit, factum fuerit” and calls it “frivola et inanis.”2 The conclusion that, where Scripture and the Church do not forbid it, “it seems probable that the more excellent should be attributed to Mary” stands in Scotus’s question. The Quaracchi editor notes that Scotus “follows the method indicated by Ware” (ducit modum a Guarra indicatum).1 Berard Vogt (Franciscan, Duns Scotus, Defender of the Immaculate Conception, 1955), citing L. Siekaniec (1941), writes: “The famous words often attributed to Scotus: ‘Potuit, decuit, ergo fecit — Christ could preserve His Mother from original sin; it was becoming thus to honor her; therefore, we may presume that He did so’ — are already found with William of Ware.”3
  • The three questions printed together. Ware’s question, Scotus’s, and Auriol’s were edited side by side in the 1904 Quaracchi critical edition, with Ware’s at pp. 1–11 and Scotus’s immediately following.
  • The direction of influence. Paschal Robinson (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1912) reports that “several authorities concur with Bartholomew of Pisa (1399) in calling him the teacher of Duns Scotus,” and that Wadding records an inscription on Scotus’s tomb at Cologne, “Magister Gulielmus Varro Præceptor Scoti.”4 Vogt sets out two views: “Some maintain that Scotus received his views on the Immaculate Conception from this teacher; while others claim that William of Ware, in the earlier editions of his Commentary on the Sentences, followed the received Franciscan tradition,” adopting Scotus’s position only after his pupil’s defense of it; Éphrem Longpré (Franciscan, La Vierge Immaculée, 1939), “relying on newly discovered testimony, inclines to this theory.”3 Francesco Fiorentino discusses the question in “The Immaculate Conception and Incarnation according to William of Ware” (2023).1
