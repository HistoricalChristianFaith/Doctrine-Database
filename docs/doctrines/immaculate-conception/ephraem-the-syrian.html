Ephrem the Syrian on the Immaculate Conception

Dates: c. 361 (Nisibene Hymns 27) · c. 306–373 · Wikipedia
Summary: Ephrem, the Syriac hymnographer, praises Christ and his mother together in a stanza of the Nisibene Hymns: “in Thee, O Lord, there is no spot, and in Thy Mother no stain.” Frederick Holweck (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1910) cites Ephrem among the Syrian Fathers who “never tire of extolling the sinlessness of Mary.” In his Commentary on the Diatessaron, as reported by Isho‘dad of Merv, Ephrem reads Christ’s words to Mary at Cana as a reproof.

Claims & evidence

“In Thy Mother no stain” (Nisibene Hymns 27)

Thou and Thy Mother are the only ones who are totally beautiful in every respect; for in Thee, O Lord, there is no spot, and in Thy Mother no stain.

— Nisibene Hymns (Carmina Nisibena) 27, as rendered by Ludwig Ott, Fundamentals of Catholic Dogma; the Syriac and Latin are in Gustav Bickell’s edition of 1866 (Hymn 27, pp. 122–123).1 There is no standard critical English translation of this stanza — it is not among the Nisibene Hymns Englished in the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, where hymns in this range are fragmentary — so every wording in circulation (Ott’s above; Michael O’Carroll’s “no blemish in you… no stain in your mother”) is a rendering from the Syriac, and they differ in detail. In context the hymn compares Christ’s indwelling of Mary’s womb to his baptism in the Jordan. The pairing “totally beautiful… no spot… no stain” echoes the language of Song of Songs 4:7 (tota pulchra es… macula non est in te). Holweck writes: “To St. Ephraem she was as innocent as Eve before her fall, a virgin most estranged from every stain of sin.”2

Ephrem on Cana

In his Commentary on the Diatessaron Ephrem takes Christ’s words at the wedding of Cana — “Woman, what have I to do with thee?” (John 2:4) — as a reproof of Mary for a moment’s doubt, a reading the later Syriac commentator Isho‘dad of Merv (9th c.) reports from him.3

Reception and debate

  • Michael O’Carroll (Catholic Mariologist) writes that the Nisibene stanza needs “subtle interpretation” to bear on the doctrine, and notes that Ephrem elsewhere speaks of “Mary’s baptism.”4 How such first-millennium texts bear on the 1854 definition is the subject of a disputed-reading page.
  • Many circulating “all-pure / all-immaculate” prayers attributed to Ephrem belong to the Greek Precationes ad Deiparam, whose ascription to him is doubted (Pseudo-Ephrem) ⚠ unverified. The Syriac Nisibene stanza is the text discussed here.
  • Ephrem appears on this wiki also on the Mary as the New Eve timeline, where his “Eve and Mary” typology is recorded.
