John Duns Scotus on the Immaculate Conception

Dates: c. 1300 (Ordinatio III, Oxford lectures) · c. 1266–1308 · Wikipedia
Summary: The Franciscan “Subtle Doctor” answered the objection Aquinas had raised, that an immaculate Mary would not have needed Christ’s redemption. Scotus argued that the most perfect Mediator redeems most perfectly by preserving from sin, not merely by rescuing after it; so Mary was redeemed more excellently, being preserved from ever contracting original sin. He offered the conclusion as probable, “not against the authority of the Church and of Holy Scripture.”

Claims & evidence

The argument from the perfect Mediator

he who prevents lest one offend, is a more perfect mediator than he who merely reconciles after the offense.

— Ordinatio (Opus Oxoniense) III, d. 3, q. 1, source. Scotus reasons that Christ is the most perfect Mediator, and a perfect Mediator performs the most perfect possible act of mediation toward at least one person. The most perfect mediation is not to cleanse a stain already contracted but to prevent it from ever being contracted at all. So it was fitting that Christ should exercise this supreme degree of mediation toward his mother.

Preservation as a mode of redemption

Mary had the greatest need of Christ as Redeemer, for she would have contracted original sin by reason of her descent unless this had been prevented by the grace of a Mediator.

— Ordinatio III, d. 3, q. 1, source. Aquinas had objected that an immaculate Mary would not have needed redemption, breaking Christ’s universal saviourhood. Scotus answers that Mary’s need of the Redeemer was the greatest of any creature’s: left to her descent from Adam she too would have contracted the stain, and it was only Christ’s grace, applied in anticipation of his merits, that preserved her from it. On his account preservation is a mode of redemption — the highest — not an exemption from it. He concludes that, so long as it be “not against the authority of the Church and of Holy Scripture, it would seem probable to attribute to Mary that which is more perfect.”1

Notes

  • Scotus frames his conclusion as the more probable and more fitting view, subject to the authority of the Church and of Scripture. Frederick Holweck (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1910) writes that Scotus’s followers “the learned Petrus Aureolus and Franciscus de Mayronis became the most fervent champions of the doctrine” (see Peter Auriol and Francis of Mayronis).2
  • Holweck writes that Scotus “brought forward, by way of illustration, the somewhat dangerous and doubtful argument of Eadmer (S. Anselm) ‘decuit, potuit, ergo fecit’” (“it was fitting, he could, therefore he did”), and that he “removed the great difficulty of St. Thomas” by showing that Mary “obtained of her Divine Son the greatest of redemptions through the mystery of her preservation from all sin.”3 The “could and willed” reasoning appears earlier on this timeline in Eadmer of Canterbury. How Aquinas’s own position is read is set out on the disputed-reading page Did Thomas Aquinas deny the Immaculate Conception?
