Durandus of Saint-Pourçain on the Immaculate Conception

Dates: c. 1275–1334 · Dominican; bishop of Le Puy, then Meaux · Wikipedia
Summary: Durandus of Saint-Pourçain (Durand de Saint-Pourçain), a Dominican called the “Doctor Resolutissimus” and censured within his order for departing from Aquinas, held, on Bogusław Kochaniewicz’s reading of his Sentences commentary, that Mary was conceived without original sin: the union of her soul with her body and the gift of sanctifying grace happened at one and the same instant, so that no interval remained in which she could have contracted the stain. Kochaniewicz notes that Durandus does not cite Scotus but relies on Augustine, Anselm, and Richard of St-Victor.

Claims & evidence

He held that the Virgin was conceived without original sin — ⚠ reported on secondary authority

Kochaniewicz reads Durandus’s Sentences commentary (Book III, dist. 3, q. 1) as arguing that Mary “was conceived without original sin, since the union of soul and body and the bestowal of sanctifying grace took place at one and the same moment,” and singles him out in his survey of the Dominican record:

…it is the first testimony of Dominican theological reflection, which is part of the current of supporters of the Immaculate Conception… it cannot be maintained that all Dominicans were against the Immaculate Conception of Mary.

— B. Kochaniewicz, “The Stance of the Dominicans Towards the Immaculate Conception…,” Scripta Theologica 56 (2024), p. 573, source.1 The wording of Durandus’s own text has not been checked here against the 1571 Venice printing on which the account rests, so the attribution is carried as an open lead rather than a verified quotation.

His reasons and authorities

The reasons Durandus adduces, on Kochaniewicz’s reading, are the incorruption of Mary’s body foreshadowed by her sinless origin, the fittingness of a “most perfect” mediation for the mother of the Redeemer, and above all the simultaneity of ensoulment and grace, which leaves no “instant of contracted guilt” such as those who held a sanctification after conception posited. Kochaniewicz writes that Durandus “did not quote Duns Scotus. Instead, he relied on the texts of Augustine, Anselm and Richard of St. Victor,” while his answer to the redemption objection “coincides with the teachings of Duns Scotus.”2

Reception and debate

  • Durandus and the Dominicans of his time. Kochaniewicz sets Durandus against the position of other Dominicans of his generation: Herveus Natalis (Hervé Nédellec), Master General of the Dominicans 1318–1323, “was not only against the Immaculate Conception, but also polemicized with the position of [its] supporters,” arguing from the universality of original sin and of Christ’s redemption; John of Naples (Giovanni Regina di Napoli, d. c. 1336), of the same Thomist position, helped draft the list of Durandus’s deviations from Aquinas that the order censured.3
  • The source. Kochaniewicz’s abstract describes the Dominican position as “negative and homogeneous”; his treatment of Durandus presents the fourteenth-century picture as not homogeneous, with Durandus as the exception. The underlying primary reference is Durandus, Super IV Sententiarum, Lib. III, d. 3, q. 1 (Venice, 1571), fol. 217r–v, not consulted directly here.
