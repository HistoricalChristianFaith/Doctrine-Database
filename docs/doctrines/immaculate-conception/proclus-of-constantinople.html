Proclus of Constantinople on the Immaculate Conception

Dates: c. 430 (Homily 1, On the Holy Virgin Theotokos, preached in the Great Church of Constantinople, by tradition before Nestorius; Proclus archbishop of Constantinople 434–446) · Wikipedia
Summary: Proclus’s homily on the Theotokos, preached at the start of the Nestorian controversy, praises Mary as “the spotless flesh of the Virgin,” “the awesome loom of the divine economy,” “the purest fleece” — the flesh from which the Word wove the garment of his humanity. Frederick Holweck (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1910) cites a further phrase among his patristic texts on Mary’s purity, rendering Proclus as saying she “was formed without any stain,” while cautioning that “the rhetorical character” of such passages “prevents us from laying too much stress on them.”

Claims & evidence

The purity imagery: “spotless flesh,” the “awesome loom,” the “purest fleece”

… the awesome loom of the divine economy … the spotless flesh of the Virgin … the purest fleece … the untarnished vessel of virginity.

— Homily 1, On the Holy Virgin Theotokos, trans. Nicholas Constas, source. The homily’s governing image is a loom: the Virgin’s flesh is the “wool” from which the Word wove the garment of his humanity, so the praise of her purity is a praise of the material of the Incarnation. Mary is “the pure one,” kept “without pollution” and “without defilement,” “the bars of virginity unbroken.” The homily’s praise is of Mary’s bodily integrity as the one who bore God.1

“Formed without any stain”

… she was formed without any stain.

— Laudatio in S. Dei Genitricis ortum I.3, as rendered in the Catholic Encyclopedia’s catalogue of witnesses to “the absolute purity of Mary.”2 Holweck lists the line, with passages from other Fathers, as evidence that “the belief in Mary’s immunity from sin in her conception was prevalent amongst the Fathers, especially those of the Greek Church,” and adds: “The rhetorical character, however, of many of these and similar passages prevents us from laying too much stress on them, and interpreting them in a strictly literal sense. The Greek Fathers never formally or explicitly discussed the question of the Immaculate Conception.” The phrase is given here as the encyclopedia renders it; the primary text was not located online.
