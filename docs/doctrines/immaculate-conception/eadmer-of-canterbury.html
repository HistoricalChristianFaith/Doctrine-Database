Eadmer of Canterbury on the Immaculate Conception

Dates: c. 1125 (Tractatus de conceptione sanctae Mariae) · c. 1060–c. 1126 · Wikipedia
Summary: Eadmer — monk of Canterbury, secretary and biographer of Anselm — wrote a treatise on Mary’s conception arguing that she was preserved from original sin at it. Taking up his master’s argument from fittingness, he reasons that God “clearly could and willed it; if therefore he willed it, he did it.” Frederick Holweck (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1910) gives the argument in the form decuit, potuit, ergo fecit and calls it “the axiom of Pseudo-Anselmus (Eadmer) developed by Duns Scotus.”

Claims & evidence

“He could and he willed it; therefore he did it”

Potuit plane et voluit; si igitur voluit, fecit.

— Tractatus de conceptione sanctae Mariae (“He clearly could and he willed it; if therefore he willed it, he did it”).1 Against the objection that God could not, or would not, keep Mary free of the sin every child of Adam contracts, Eadmer answers with divine omnipotence and fittingness together: it was possible, it was fitting, and God willed it — so it was done. He presses the point with an image: as a chestnut is “conceived and grown amid spines which do it no harm,” so God could form the body of his mother untouched amid the thorns of sin.

Preservation at conception

Where Anselm had placed Mary among those cleansed from sin before Christ’s birth, Eadmer argues that Mary was preserved at her conception itself, not purified afterwards. His treatise belongs to the revival in England of the feast of Mary’s conception after the Norman Conquest, and it circulated under Anselm’s name.2 David Knowles (The Monastic Order in England, 1941) is quoted as calling it “the first, and for two centuries the most valuable, Western exposition of the doctrine of the Immaculate Conception” ⚠ unverified.3

Reception and debate

  • The four-word tag potuit, decuit, ergo fecit (“he could, it was fitting, therefore he did”) is a later compression, associated with the Franciscans and Scotus; Eadmer’s own words are “Potuit plane et voluit; si igitur voluit, fecit.” Holweck writes that Scotus “also brought forward, by way of illustration, the somewhat dangerous and doubtful argument of Eadmer.”4
  • The Tractatus de conceptione sanctae Mariae was for centuries printed among Anselm’s works and attributed to him; Thurston and Slater’s edition (1904) and Holweck assign it to Eadmer. It is a distinct work from Anselm’s De conceptu virginali (see Anselm).
