Pope Pius IX and the definition of the Immaculate Conception

Dates: 2 February 1849 (Ubi primum, the consultation of the bishops) · 8 December 1854 (Ineffabilis Deus, the definition) · reigned 1846–1878 (1792–1878) · Wikipedia
Summary: Pius IX consulted the Catholic episcopate on the Immaculate Conception in the 1849 encyclical Ubi primum, and on 8 December 1854, in the bull Ineffabilis Deus, defined as a doctrine “revealed by God” that Mary “in the first instant of her conception… in view of the merits of Jesus Christ… was preserved free from all stain of original sin.”

The consultation of the episcopate — Ubi primum (1849)

Writing from exile at Gaeta — he had fled Rome in the revolution of 1848 — he issued the encyclical Ubi primum on 2 February 1849, putting two questions to every bishop: how great was the devotion of their clergy and people toward the Immaculate Conception and their desire for its definition, and what the bishops themselves, in their own judgment, thought and desired.

We eagerly desire, furthermore, that, as soon as possible, you apprise Us concerning the devotion which animates your clergy and your people regarding the Immaculate Conception of the Blessed Virgin and how ardently glows the desire that this doctrine be defined by the Apostolic See. And especially, Venerable Brethren, We wish to know what you yourselves, in your wise judgment, think and desire on this matter.

— Ubi primum, given at Gaeta, 2 February 1849.1 The occasion, the encyclical explains, was that already under Gregory XVI “there was in the entire Catholic world a most ardent and wondrous revival of the desire” that the Mother of God “be finally declared by a solemn definition of the Church to have been conceived without the stain of original sin.”

Of the roughly six hundred bishops who answered, some 536 favoured defining the doctrine; four or five, among them the Archbishop of Paris, were opposed, and about three dozen, mostly from Protestant countries, judged the moment inopportune.2 In Ineffabilis Deus Pius IX recalled the consultation: “We were certainly filled with the greatest consolation when the replies of our venerable brethren came to us.”3 How the consultation and the definition bear on the first-millennium record is set out on the disputed-reading page How does the first-millennium record bear on the 1854 definition of the Immaculate Conception?

Claims & evidence

The dogmatic definition — preserved from all stain of original sin from the first instant

We declare, pronounce, and define that the doctrine which holds that the most Blessed Virgin Mary, in the first instant of her conception, by a singular grace and privilege granted by Almighty God, in view of the merits of Jesus Christ, the Savior of the human race, was preserved free from all stain of original sin, is a doctrine revealed by God and therefore to be believed firmly and constantly by all the faithful.

— Ineffabilis Deus, 8 December 1854, source. The definition names the moment (“the first instant of her conception”), the mode (“preserved”), and the ground (“in view of the merits of Jesus Christ, the Savior of the human race”) — terms already used in Alexander VII’s bull Sollicitudo omnium ecclesiarum (1661), which speaks of Mary’s soul as “preserved immune… in view of the merits of her Son, Jesus Christ, the Redeemer of our human race.” This is claim (b).

Genesis 3:15 and Luke 1:28 in the bull

Among the grounds the bull adduces is the Fathers’ reading of Genesis 3:15 — “I will put enmities between you and the woman, between your seed and her seed” — applied to Mary, who, “united with him by a most intimate and indissoluble bond, was, with him and through him, eternally at enmity with the evil serpent, and most completely triumphed over him, and thus crushed his head with her immaculate foot.” Ineffabilis Deus gathers this, together with the Fathers’ praises of the Mother of God as “the Virgin undefiled, immaculate, ever blessed, and free from all contagion of sin,” into the case for the definition.3 The patristic reading of Mary as the New Eve has its own timeline, and the Vulgate’s feminine ipsa conteret (“she shall crush”) beside the Hebrew hû is the subject of the disputed-reading page “Who crushes the serpent’s head in Genesis 3:15?”

The bull’s other scriptural text is the angelic salutation — Mary “proclaimed full of grace” (Luke 1:28), which the Fathers, it says, took to show “that the Mother of God is the seat of all divine graces and is adorned with all gifts of the Holy Spirit.” Frederick Holweck (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1910) writes that the salutation “indicates a unique abundance of grace,” but that “the term kecharitomene (full of grace) serves only as an illustration, not as a proof of the dogma.”4
