Ramon Llull on the Immaculate Conception

Dates: c. 1232–1315/16 · the Marian argument in the Disputatio eremitae et Raimundi, Paris, autumn 1298 · Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy
Summary: Ramon Llull (Raymond Lull), the Majorcan layman and philosopher, argued for Mary’s freedom from original sin at Paris in the autumn of 1298, a few years before Duns Scotus’s Oxford lectures (c. 1300). Reasoning in the demonstrative style of his “Art,” he held that God and sin cannot be united in the one subject who was to bear the incarnate Son, and so Mary did not contract the stain; his stated account is a sanctification of the very matter from which she was formed. Luigi Gambero credits him with the earliest use of the phrase “Immaculate Conception”; the Catholic Encyclopedia does not name him in that connection.

Claims & evidence

The argument of the Disputatio eremitae et Raimundi, question 96

In the Disputatio eremitae et Raimundi super aliquibus dubiis quaestionibus Sententiarum (a hermit-and-Ramon dialogue on Peter Lombard’s Sentences, written at Paris in the autumn of 1298), question 96, Llull argues — in the rendering of the Marian historian Luigi Gambero — that “the Son of God could not become incarnate in a mother who was stained by sin in any way: God and sin cannot be united in the one and same object,” so that “the Blessed Virgin Mary did not contract original sin; rather she was sanctified in the instant in which the seed from which she was formed was detached from her parents.” He adds a parallel between Christ’s and Mary’s conceptions (they should share a “like nature”), and an argument that the second creation — the Redemption begun in Christ and Mary — had, like the first, to open under the sign of total innocence.1 Provenance: the wording follows Gambero’s English translation of question 96; the underlying Latin (in the Mainz Opera Omnia and the 2024 critical edition) is not reproduced here.

Reception and debate

  • Llull’s account and Scotus’s. Llull argues his conclusion, that Mary did not contract original sin, by “necessary reasons” rather than proof-texts. His stated account, a sanctification of the seminal matter at the moment it is “detached from her parents,” is a sanctification of the material. Scotus argued instead that Mary was preserved from original sin by the grace of Christ the Mediator.
  • The phrase “Immaculate Conception.” Gambero writes that Llull “is the first author to use the expression ‘Immaculate Conception’ to designate the Virgin’s exemption from original sin.”1 Frederick Holweck’s article in the Catholic Encyclopedia (1910) does not name Llull; it describes the term as growing out of the feast of Mary’s Conception, and notes that “before 1854 the term ‘Immaculata Conceptio’ is nowhere found in the liturgical books,” apart from the invitatory of a votive office.2
  • Biography. Accounts circulate that Llull met Duns Scotus in 1297, that this won him the name Doctor Illuminatus, and that he shaped Scotus’s position on Mary’s conception ⚠ unverified (no source for them was located). The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on Llull does not mention them; it dates his “stay in Paris between 1297 and 1299,” with residence there “from August 1298 until July 1299.” The same entry reports that Llull “decided to join the Franciscans,” and that Fernando Domínguez Reboiras (2016) “suggests that there is no historical evidence that Llull entered the order of Saint Francis.”3
