Bonaventure on the Immaculate Conception

Dates: c. 1255 (Commentary on the Sentences) · c. 1221–1274 · Wikipedia
Summary: Bonaventure — the “Seraphic Doctor,” Franciscan theologian and minister-general of his order — taught in his Sentences commentary what he called the “more common” opinion of his day: Mary’s soul contracted original sin and was sanctified shortly after animation, not preserved from it at the instant of conception.

Claims & evidence

Mary contracted original sin and was sanctified after animation

In his commentary on the Sentences, treating whether the Blessed Virgin’s soul was sanctified before it contracted original sin, Bonaventure answers in the negative: her flesh was conceived in the ordinary way, subject to the law of transmission; her soul, once infused (animation), contracted the original stain like every descendant of Adam; and God then sanctified her in the womb, soon after animation, cleansing what had briefly been contracted.1 He presents this as the “more common” opinion (communior) among the masters and judges it the safer and more reasonable, because it keeps intact Christ’s office as the universal Redeemer.

The ground of his position: Christ’s universal redemption

Bonaventure reasons that if Mary were conceived immaculate she would not have needed to be redeemed, and Christ would not be the Saviour of all. The debt of original sin, and therefore the need of redemption, he holds, must be common to Mary with the rest of the race; her privilege lies in how swiftly and fully grace cleansed her, not in an exemption at conception.2

Reception and debate

  • Bonaventure affirms Mary’s complete freedom from actual sin and her sanctification in the womb while denying that she was preserved from original sin at her conception. Frederick Holweck (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1910) writes that “St. Peter Damian, Peter the Lombard, Alexander of Hales, St. Bonaventure, and Albert the Great are quoted as opposing it.”3
  • Within the Franciscan order, William of Ware and John Duns Scotus (c. 1300) argued that Mary was preserved from original sin, Scotus framing her redemption as preservative; their arguments are recorded on their own pages.
