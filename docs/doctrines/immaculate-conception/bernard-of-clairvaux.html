Bernard of Clairvaux on the Immaculate Conception

Dates: c. 1140 (Letter 174) · 1090–1153 · Wikipedia
Summary: Bernard, abbot of Clairvaux, wrote to the canons of Lyons to rebuke them for adopting a new Feast of the Conception. He argues that Mary was sanctified in the womb after her conception, which made her birth holy but not her conception.

Claims & evidence

Sanctification in the womb, after conception

in order to be holy it is necessary to exist, and a person does not exist before being conceived… she received sanctification when existing in the womb after conception, which, by excluding sin, made her birth holy, but not her conception.

— Letter 174, to the Canons of Lyons, on the Conception of S. Mary, source (Samuel J. Eales trans., where the letter is numbered LXV). Bernard’s reasoning has two prongs. First, holiness presupposes a subject: before Mary was conceived she did not exist, so she could not then be made holy. Second, the conception itself — the act of ordinary generation — he takes to be bound up with the concupiscence through which original sin is transmitted, so it could not be the moment of her sanctification either. He concludes that God sanctified her in the womb, after conception, as Scripture says of Jeremiah, “sanctified before he came forth from the womb,” and of John the Baptist, who leapt in his mother’s womb at the presence of the Lord.

Bernard’s objections to the new feast

Bernard presses that the church of Lyons had introduced the feast on its own authority and without reason, and that Mary needs no honour built on error: to celebrate her conception as holy is, he holds, to sanctify what was not holy and to give the Queen of Heaven a “false honour” she does not desire. His objection, as he states it, is to the feast and to the holiness it ascribes to her conception, not to honouring Mary.1

Reception and debate

  • Bernard affirms Mary’s sanctification before birth while denying that her conception was holy. Frederick Holweck (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1910) describes the letter as “a vehement letter (Epist. 174)” in which Bernard “reproved them for taking the step upon their own authority” and “asserted that the feast was foreign to the old tradition of the Church,” and judges that “St. Bernard was perfectly justified when he demanded a careful inquiry into the reasons for observing the feast.”2
  • The same letter argues from the Church’s received feasts to doctrine while refusing the new one, and so appears on the lex orandi, lex credendi timeline: see Bernard on the law of prayer.
  • The scholastic positions that followed — Bonaventure and Aquinas for a sanctification after conception, William of Ware and Duns Scotus for a preservation at it — are recorded on their own pages.
