Pope Sixtus IV on the Immaculate Conception

Dates: 1476–1483 (Cum praeexcelsa; Grave nimis) · reigned 1471–1484 · Wikipedia
Summary: Sixtus IV, a Franciscan, approved the feast of Mary’s conception with its Mass and Office in Cum praeexcelsa (1476), speaking of “the miraculous conception of this immaculate Virgin” and defining nothing about original sin. In Grave nimis (1483) he forbade either party to charge the other with heresy or mortal sin, the charge against those who held Mary conceived with original sin being forbidden because the question was “not yet decided by the Roman Church and the Apostolic See.”

Claims & evidence

Cum praeexcelsa (1476): the feast approved

…we consider it just, even a duty, that all the faithful of Christ for the miraculous conception of this immaculate Virgin give praise and thanks to Almighty God…

— Cum praeexcelsa, 28 February 1476.1 Sixtus calls Mary herself “immaculate,” and attaches to the conception the adjective “miraculous” (mirifica). He approves the feast, its Mass and Office, and grants indulgences to those who attend; the constitution makes no pronouncement on original sin. Frederick Holweck (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1910) writes that by this decree “Sixtus IV at last adopted the feast for the entire Latin Church.”

Grave nimis (1483): charges of heresy forbidden to both sides

…subjecting to the same penalty and censure those who shall presume to assert, holding a contrary opinion, viz. that the glorious Virgin Mary was conceived with original sin, incur the guilt of heresy, or deadly sin, when it was not yet decided by the Roman Church and the Apostolic See…

— Grave nimis, 4 September 1483.2 The bull reproves preachers who said that those who hold Mary “conceived without the stain of original sin, sin mortally, or that they are heretical,” condemning such assertions “as false and erroneous and far removed from the truth”; it then subjects to the same penalty those who charge the contrary opinion with heresy or mortal sin. The second prohibition is grounded, in the bull’s words, on the question’s being “not yet decided by the Roman Church and the Apostolic See.”

Notes

  • Sixtus’s two constitutions were later renewed by the Council of Trent (1546) and by Alexander VII (1661); see their pages.
  • Holweck reports that the Office adopted by Sixtus IV was composed by Leonard de Nogarolis, and that he “published in 1483 a constitution in which he punished with excommunication all those of either opinion who charged the opposite opinion with heresy.”3
