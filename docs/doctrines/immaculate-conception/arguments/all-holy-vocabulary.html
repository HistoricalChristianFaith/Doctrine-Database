Does the Eastern “all-holy” vocabulary (Panagia) testify to the Immaculate Conception?

Disputed reading · on the Immaculate Conception of Mary · witnesses: the Byzantine liturgy and the feast of Mary’s conception (9 December); Andrew of Crete; John of Damascus

Related readings: Does Gregory Nazianzen’s prokathartheisa testify to the Immaculate Conception?

The passage

This Day, O Faithful, from saintly parents begins to take being the Spotless Lamb, the Most Pure Tabernacle, Mary… The Unique All-Immaculate [Mary] is today made manifest to the Just by the Angel.

— hymns of the Byzantine feast of the Conception of St Anne (9 December), in the English quoted by Nishant Xavier (2021).1

O spotless, undefiled, incorrupt, immaculate, pure Virgin, Lady Bride of Christ.

— prayer to the Theotokos in the Byzantine Small Compline, quoted by Aidan Kimel (2018), who lists the titles Panagia (“all-holy”), Achrantos (“undefiled”), Theotokos and Aeiparthenos as abounding “not only in the private prayers of Eastern Orthodox Christians but in the public liturgies and offices.”2

The question

Byzantine prayer calls Mary all-holy, spotless and immaculate, and from at least the seventh century, on Frederick Holweck’s account, the East kept a feast of her conception. The dispute is whether this vocabulary, read by the principle that the law of prayer is the law of belief, attests the doctrine Pius IX defined in 1854, that Mary was preserved from original sin from the first instant of her conception, or names a holiness whose origin it leaves to other questions. The question matters because the Eastern Orthodox Church, which prays these texts, does not hold the 1854 dogma.3

The prayer of the East confesses Mary free of every stain from her conception

On this reading the superlative titles, and above all the hymns of the conception feast, express in prayer what the West defined in 1854.

Held by: Pius IX (Catholic, Ineffabilis Deus, 1854); Martin Jugie (Catholic, L’Immaculée Conception dans l’Écriture sainte et dans la tradition orientale, 1952; grants that the epithets alone would not prove it); Lev Gillet (Orthodox, “A Monk of the Eastern Church,” Chrysostom, 1983; grants that the titles alone do not always show it); John Janaro (Catholic, Saint Anselm Journal, 2006).4

Pius IX gathered the Fathers’ titles — “immaculate, as immaculate in every respect; innocent, and verily most innocent; spotless, and entirely spotless; holy and removed from every stain of sin; all pure, all stainless” — and observed that “this style of speech has passed almost spontaneously into the books of the most holy liturgy and the Offices of the Church.” Of the Western Mass and Litany texts proclaiming the Immaculate Conception he wrote that “the rule of prayer might thus serve to illustrate the rule of belief.”5

Jugie rests the case on the whole liturgy of the feast rather than on single words. The feast’s object, on his account, includes “la conception passive, la venue à l’existence de la future mère de Dieu,” and its hymn-writers took it as the occasion to proclaim “la sainteté perpétuelle de la Toute-Sainte”: “C’est bien, en fait, la Vierge immaculée, exempte de toute souillure dès le premier instant de son existence… qu’ils ont chantée et louée.”6 Gillet points to Byzantine homilies: the patriarch Euthymios (d. 917) said that on “this very day” the Father fashioned a tabernacle for his Son that was “fully sanctified,” and the monk Theognostes, in a homily for the Dormition, spoke of Mary conceived by “a sanctifying action” ex archēs, “from the beginning,” which, Gillet writes, “exactly corresponds to the ‘in primo instanti’ of Roman theology.”7

Janaro calls for a “hermeneutic of doxology”: read as the praise they are, John of Damascus’s “Marian praises” “correspond essentially to the doctrine of the Immaculate Conception,” his cry “O blessed loins of Joachim, whence the all-pure seed was poured out” and his praise of Anne’s womb “in which was conceived the living heaven” “must indicate that Mary’s very creation was nothing other than a pure divine work.”8 On this reading a Church that daily calls the newly conceived Mary “the Spotless Lamb” and “All-Immaculate” confesses in its worship that no stain ever touched her.

The titles honour Mary’s all-holiness and freedom from personal sin, without exempting her from the common human condition

On this reading the titles praise a holiness that surpasses every creature’s and a life free of all sin, while Mary remains, by birth, one of Adam’s fallen race.

Held by: Vladimir Lossky (Orthodox, “Panagia,” in In the Image and Likeness of God, 1974); Kallistos Ware (Orthodox, The Orthodox Church, and “‘Beyond all holiness’,” 2007); Patrick (Craig) Truglia (Orthodox, Revista Teologică, 2021).9

Ware sets out the titles and what they affirm: “The Orthodox Church calls Mary All-Holy; it calls her immaculate or spotless (in Greek, achrantos); and all Orthodox are agreed in believing that Our Lady was free from actual sin. But was she also free from original sin?” On that second question, he writes, the Orthodox Church “has never in fact made any formal and definitive pronouncement on the matter,” and since 1854 most Orthodox have answered it in the negative.3 Lossky holds both at once: “She was holy and pure from all sin from her mother’s womb, but still this holiness does not place her outside the rest of humanity before Christ,” and “It is not by virtue of a privilege received at the moment of her conception by her parents that we venerate the Mother of God more than any other created being.”10

Ware reads Nicholas Cabasilas as calling Mary “first and uniquely detached from sin once for all” while writing that “She did not receive a nature unacquainted with all wickedness.”11 Truglia finds the same anthropology in the Byzantine narratives of Mary’s Dormition, which contrast her with Christ because “her experience of the Fall was involuntary and postlapsarian”: “the Byzantine narratives teach that the Theotokos had original sin.”12 On the feast, Frederick Holweck (Catholic, 1910) records that the older feast of Mary’s conception and the modern feast of the Immaculate Conception “are not identical in their object,” and that the Greeks “did not think it absurd to celebrate a conception which was not immaculate, as we see from the Feast of the Conception of St. John.”13 On this reading the prayer of the East honours the holiest of Adam’s children, who never sinned, as the summit of the old covenant’s holiness, born like them into the common condition of fallen humanity.
