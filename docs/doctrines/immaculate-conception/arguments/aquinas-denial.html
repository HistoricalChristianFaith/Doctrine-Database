Did Thomas Aquinas deny the Immaculate Conception?

Disputed reading · on the Immaculate Conception of Mary · witness: Thomas Aquinas

Related readings: How does the first-millennium record bear on the 1854 definition of the Immaculate Conception?

The passage

If the soul of the Blessed Virgin had never incurred the stain of original sin, this would be derogatory to the dignity of Christ, by reason of His being the universal Saviour of all. Consequently after Christ, who, as the universal Saviour of all, needed not to be saved, the purity of the Blessed Virgin holds the highest place. For Christ did not contract original sin in any way whatever, but was holy in His very Conception… But the Blessed Virgin did indeed contract original sin, but was cleansed therefrom before her birth from the womb.

— Thomas Aquinas, Summa Theologiae III, q. 27, a. 2, reply to objection 2 (c. 1270), trans. Fathers of the English Dominican Province.1

For sin is either original, and from this she was cleansed in the womb; or mortal or venial, and from these She was free.… But Christ excelled the Blessed Virgin in this, that He was conceived and born without original (sin). Moreover the Blessed Virgin was conceived in original (sin), but not born (in it).… For She Herself was the most pure even as much as regards fault, because the Virgin Herself incurred neither mortal nor venial sin.

— Thomas Aquinas, On the Angelic Salutation (Expositio salutationis angelicae), §§7, 8 and 12.2 In §12 the Turin edition of 1954 reads nec mortale nec veniale peccatum incurrit (“incurred neither mortal nor venial sin”) and records the variant of J. F. Rossi’s edition, nec originale nec mortale: “neither original nor mortal [nor venial] sin.”3

The question

Aquinas wrote before Pius IX defined in 1854 that Mary was preserved from original sin from the first instant of her conception, and before John Duns Scotus argued for preservative redemption (c. 1300). The dispute is over how his texts relate to that later definition: whether they teach against it, whether they deny something other than what was defined, and whether his last work on the subject affirmed it. The answer bore on the Dominican school, which followed him, and on later appeals to him by Catholic, Orthodox and Protestant writers.4

Aquinas taught that Mary contracted original sin and was cleansed in the womb

On this reading Aquinas held a sanctification of Mary after her soul existed, and so taught against what 1854 defined.

Held by: the Dominican school after Aquinas, among them John of Montson (Paris, 1387; dated 1373 by Holweck); St John Maximovitch (Russian Orthodox, The Orthodox Veneration of the Mother of God); Basil Cole and Francis Belanger (Dominican, Nova et Vetera, 2006; they hold his view of her sanctification close to the dogma); Steven Wedgeworth (Anglican, Ad Fontes, 2024).5

Holweck records that the Dominicans “were under special obligation to follow the doctrines of St. Thomas, and the common conclusion was that St. Thomas was opposed to the Immaculate Conception,” so that they kept the feast but called it the Sanctification of Mary.4 The texts give the order of events for that school: sanctification “cannot be understood as having taken place before animation,” it “remains, therefore, that the Blessed Virgin was sanctified after animation,” and she “did indeed contract original sin.” The reason given is Christ’s universal saviourhood.1

Cole and Belanger, reviewing his works in order, argue “that Aquinas’s basic argument is against the idea of the Immaculate Conception.” They note that in the late Expositio “he states very clearly that Mary was conceived in original sin, though not born in it,” and they judge the view that he did not deny the doctrine “an incorrect judgment, although it is true that Aquinas’s opposition to the doctrine is vaguer and less forceful as he matures than is often supposed.”6 Maximovitch writes that “Thomas Aquinas and Bernard of Clairvaux decisively censured it, while Duns Scotus defended it,” and that “the Latin Dominican monks, after their teacher Thomas Aquinas, preached against the teaching.” Wedgeworth: “Thomas Aquinas did not believe that Mary was conceived without sin. This is not merely a case of him being silent on the matter or not yet being in possession of the fullness of later development. Instead, Thomas rejects the claim made by the contemporary dogma.”7 On this reading Aquinas held, consistently and for a stated reason, that Mary was sanctified in the womb from an original sin she had contracted.

Aquinas denied a doctrine other than the one defined in 1854

On this reading his objection rested on senses of “conception” and of redemption that the later definition does not use, and his own principles support that definition.

Held by: John Henry Newman (Catholic, “Memorandum on the Immaculate Conception”); Frederick Holweck (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1910); Réginald Garrigou-Lagrange (Dominican, The Mother of the Saviour, English 1949).8

Newman writes that the doctrine “was implied in early times, and never denied. In the Middle Ages it was denied by St. Thomas and by St. Bernard, but they took the phrase in a different sense from that in which the Church now takes it. They understood it with reference to our Lady’s mother.”9 Holweck reports that Aquinas “at first pronounced in favour of the doctrine in his treatise on the ‘Sentences’,” and that “learned books have been written to vindicate him from having actually drawn the negative conclusion.” His difficulty, on Holweck’s account, was how Mary “could have been redeemed if she had not sinned,” yet “he himself laid down the principles which, after they had been drawn together and worked out, enabled other minds to furnish the true solution of this difficulty from his own premises.” Holweck concludes: “If St. Thomas, St. Bonaventure, and other theologians had known the doctrine in the sense of the definition of 1854, they would have been its strongest defenders instead of being its opponents.”4

Garrigou-Lagrange locates the gap in one distinction. Aquinas’s argument shows “that Mary incurred the debt of original sin since she descended from Adam by way of natural generation. Unfortunately he did not distinguish sufficiently the debt from actually incurring the stain.” His texts “do not therefore imply any contradiction of the dogma… They could even be retained if the idea of preservative redemption were introduced,” and “the reasons he invoked against the privilege are not conclusive, and… they even support it when considered in the light of the idea of preservative redemption.”10 On this reading Aquinas guarded Christ’s universal redemption, the principle the 1854 definition affirms when it says Mary was preserved “in view of the merits of Jesus Christ.”

At the end of his life Aquinas affirmed Mary free of original sin

On this reading the authentic text of his late Expositio says Mary incurred “neither original nor mortal nor venial sin,” a return to the position of his early commentary.

Held by: J. F. Rossi (C.M., critical edition in Divus Thomas, 1931) ⚠ unverified; Réginald Garrigou-Lagrange (Dominican, The Mother of the Saviour, English 1949).11

The Expositio salutationis angelicae, which Garrigou-Lagrange dates to 1272 or 1273 and calls “certainly authentic,” contains in his text the sentence: “For she (the Blessed Virgin) was most pure in the matter of fault (quantum ad culpam) and incurred neither original nor mortal nor venial sin.” He reports that in Rossi’s critical edition “the passage quoted just now is found in sixteen manuscripts out of nineteen consulted by the author, who concludes that it is authentic,” with photographs of the principal manuscripts.11

Garrigou-Lagrange holds that “In spite of the objection raised by Fr. P. Synave the text appears to be authentic. If it is, then St. Thomas returned towards the end of his life—moved, we may believe, by his love of the Mother of God—to the position he had adopted when he affirmed the Immaculate Conception in his Commentary on the Sentences.” He adds that “Such an evolution of doctrine is not rare among theologians,” and that “Nor is the text we are considering the only indication of such a return.”11 On this reading Aquinas’s thought on Mary’s conception moved in three stages, open in the Sentences, negative in the Summa, and affirmative in his last commentary on the Ave Maria.
