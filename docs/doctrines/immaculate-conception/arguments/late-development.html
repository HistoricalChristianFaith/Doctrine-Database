How does the first-millennium record bear on the 1854 definition of the Immaculate Conception?

Disputed reading · on the Immaculate Conception of Mary · witnesses: Pius IX; the Fathers on Mary’s holiness (Ephrem, Augustine, Andrew of Crete); Bernard of Clairvaux; Thomas Aquinas

Related readings: Did Thomas Aquinas deny the Immaculate Conception? · Does Trent’s exemption clause tacitly affirm the Immaculate Conception?

The passage

And indeed, illustrious documents of venerable antiquity, of both the Eastern and the Western Church, very forcibly testify that this doctrine of the Immaculate Conception of the most Blessed Virgin, which was daily more and more splendidly explained, stated and confirmed by the highest authority, teaching, zeal, knowledge, and wisdom of the Church, and which was disseminated among all peoples and nations of the Catholic world in a marvelous manner — this doctrine always existed in the Church as a doctrine that has been received from our ancestors, and that has been stamped with the character of revealed doctrine. For the Church of Christ, watchful guardian that she is, and defender of the dogmas deposited with her, never changes anything, never diminishes anything, never adds anything to them…

— Pius IX, Ineffabilis Deus (8 December 1854), “Testimonies of Tradition.”1

The question

In 1854 Pius IX defined that Mary was preserved from original sin from the first instant of her conception, and grounded the definition in Scripture and the tradition of the Eastern and Western Churches. The surviving first-millennium texts praise Mary’s holiness and freedom from sin, and the East kept a feast of her conception; in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries Bernard of Clairvaux, Bonaventure and Aquinas taught that she was sanctified after she was conceived. The dispute is over what this record shows: that the doctrine was held from the beginning and later defined, that it is a later addition, or that the ancient faith in Mary’s holiness was given a new form in the West.2

Held from the beginning, and defined in 1854

On this reading the doctrine belongs to the apostolic deposit, held at first implicitly in the Church’s faith about Mary and made explicit when controversy required it.

Held by: Pius IX (Catholic, Ineffabilis Deus, 1854); John Henry Newman (Catholic, Letter to Pusey, a reply to Pusey’s Eirenicon of 1865); Frederick Holweck (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1910); Pius XII (Catholic, Fulgens Corona, 1953).3

Pius IX found the Fathers’ teaching in their titles and types for Mary, holding that they “had nothing more at heart than to vie with one another in preaching and teaching in many wonderful ways the Virgin’s supreme sanctity, dignity, and immunity from all stain of sin.” Pius XII wrote that the definition was “merely carefully conserving and sanctioning with his authority the teaching of the Fathers and of the whole Church from its earliest days.”1

Newman, answering Pusey, located the doctrine in the earliest Marian teaching, the contrast of Mary with Eve found “in St. Justin, St. Irenæus, and Tertullian, three of the earliest Fathers.” If Eve had grace “from the first moment of her personal existence,” he asked, “is it possible to deny that Mary too had this gift from the very first moment of her personal existence? I do not know how to resist this inference:—well, this is simply and literally the doctrine of the Immaculate Conception.” He had drawn it “as an immediate inference, from the primitive doctrine that Mary is the second Eve,” and judged that “it was implied in early times, and never denied,” the medieval opponents having taken “the phrase in a different sense from that in which the Church now takes it.”4

Holweck grants that “The Greek Fathers never formally or explicitly discussed the question,” and that the rhetoric of their purity-passages “prevents us from laying too much stress on them”; he reads the Fathers’ doctrine of Mary’s sanctity as including “the implicit belief in the immaculateness of her conception,” holds that this belief “was prevalent amongst the Fathers, especially those of the Greek Church,” and attributes the thirteenth-century opposition to the word “conception” being used “in different senses, which had not been separated by careful definition.”5 The Catechism of the Catholic Church describes the original sin from which the dogma holds Mary preserved as “called ‘sin’ only in an analogical sense: it is a sin ‘contracted’ and not ‘committed’ - a state and not an act,” which “does not have the character of a personal fault in any of Adam’s descendants” (§§404–405).6 On this reading the definition of 1854 states as dogma the faith in Mary’s total purity that the Church had held since the second-century comparison of Mary with Eve.

A later doctrine without foundation in Scripture or the early Church

On this reading the doctrine is absent from Scripture and the first thousand years of Christian writing, and arose in the medieval West.

Held by: E. B. Pusey (Anglican, An Eirenicon, 1865); Philip Schaff (Protestant, The Creeds of Christendom, vol. 1, 1877, and, with D. S. Schaff, the Schaff–Herzog Encyclopedia); E. Jerome Van Kuiken (Wesleyan, Christian Scholar’s Review, 2017); Steven Wedgeworth (Anglican, Ad Fontes, 2024).7

Pusey wrote that “The doctrine of the Immaculate Conception has prevailed, although opposed at its first appearance by S. Bernard, with no foundation in antiquity,” and supported the claim with the sixteenth-century Dominican Melchior Cano: “All the saints, who speak of it, say with one voice, that the Blessed Virgin was conceived in original sin.”8 Schaff measured the definition against the Vincentian canon the bull invokes, “the semper, the ubique, and the ab omnibus,” and found “instead of a ‘unanimous consent’ of the Fathers in its favor… a unanimous silence, or even protest, of the Fathers against it. For more than ten centuries after the Apostles it was not dreamed of, and when first broached as a pious opinion, it was strenuously opposed, and continued to be opposed till 1854 by many of the greatest saints and divines of the Roman Church, including St. Bernard and St. Thomas Aquinas, and several Popes.” Only God could know such a fact, he argued, and “He did not reveal it to the inspired Apostles, nor to the Fathers.”9 The Schaff–Herzog article adds that the Fathers, “though many of them (even Augustine) exempted Mary from actual trans gression, know nothing of her freedom from original sin, but always imply, and often expressly teach, the contrary.”10 Wedgeworth argues from the medieval doctors: the dogma’s “claims to universality can be disproven by several of the Roman Catholic Church’s own greatest doctors, not the least of whom are Thomas Aquinas, Augustine of Hippo, and John Chrysostom.”11 Van Kuiken (Oklahoma Wesleyan University), answering Jack Mulder Jr.’s case for the doctrine in the same journal, writes that “For Protestants generally, this dogma fails the test of the Scripture principle (as interpreted in light of the sensus literalis)”; he dates its advocacy to the defence of the Western feast, when “the twelfth-century theologian Eadmer first advocated the Immaculate Conception,” and names among “Church fathers willing to fault Mary” Irenaeus, Tertullian, Origen, Basil of Caesarea and John Chrysostom.12 On this reading the record shows a Church that honoured Mary’s holiness for a millennium without teaching her exemption from original sin.

The ancient faith in Mary’s all-holiness given a new form tied to a Western doctrine of original sin

On this reading the ancient Church held Mary all-holy and free of personal sin, and the 1854 dogma is a new formulation that depends on an Augustinian account of inherited guilt.

Held by: Patriarch Anthimos of Constantinople and his synod (Eastern Orthodox, Patriarchal Encyclical, 1895); Vladimir Lossky (Orthodox, “Panagia,” in In the Image and Likeness of God, 1974); Kallistos Ware (Orthodox, The Orthodox Church).13

The Patriarchal Encyclical of 1895, replying to Leo XIII, set the teaching of “the seven Ecumenical Councils,” that the incarnation “of the Holy Ghost and the Virgin Mary, is alone pure and immaculate,” against “a novel dogma concerning the immaculate conception of the Mother of God and ever-Virgin Mary, which was unknown to the ancient Church (and strongly opposed at different times even by the more distinguished among the papal theologians).”14

Ware locates the difference in the doctrine of the Fall: “Most orthodox theologians reject the idea of original guilt, put forward by Augustine and still accepted (albeit in a mitigated form) by the Roman Catholic Church. Men (Orthodox usually teach) automatically inherit Adams corruption and mortality, but not his guilt.” Since 1854 most Orthodox, he writes, have rejected the doctrine because “They feel it to be unnecessary; they feel that, at any rate as defined by the Roman Catholic Church, it implies a false understanding of original sin; they suspect the doctrine because it seems to separate Mary from the rest of the descendants of Adam.” He adds that before 1854 “individual Orthodox have made statements which, if not definitely affirming the doctrine of the Immaculate Conception, at any rate approach close to it,” and that for Orthodoxy “the whole question belongs to the realm of theological opinion.”15 Lossky sees in the ancient veneration of Mary the summit of an “uninterrupted succession of Old Testament holiness,” which “The Roman Catholic dogma of the Immaculate Conception seems to break up.”16 On this reading the early record attests the faith the East still prays, Mary as Panagia, holy from her mother’s womb and sinless in her life, within the common human inheritance.
