Does Gregory Nazianzen’s prokathartheisa testify to the Immaculate Conception?

Disputed reading · on the Immaculate Conception of Mary · witness: Gregory of Nazianzus

Related readings: Does the Eastern “all-holy” vocabulary (Panagia) testify to the Immaculate Conception?

The passage

…came to His own Image, and took on Him flesh for the sake of our flesh, and mingled Himself with an intelligent soul for my soul’s sake, purifying like by like; and in all points except sin was made man. Conceived by the Virgin, who first in body and soul was purified by the Holy Ghost (for it was needful both that Childbearing should be honoured, and that Virginity should receive a higher honour), He came forth then as God with that which He had assumed…

— Gregory of Nazianzus, Oration 38 (On the Theophany), §13 (c. 380), repeated at Oration 45 (On Easter), §9, trans. Browne & Swallow.1 “First… was purified” renders the Greek participle προκαθαρθείσης (prokathartheisa), from προκαθαίρω, “to purify beforehand.” Three paragraphs later Gregory uses the same verb of Christ at the Jordan: “you will see Jesus submitting to be purified in the River Jordan for my Purification… (for indeed He had no need of purification Who taketh away the sin of the world).”2

The question

Gregory says the Virgin was “purified beforehand” by the Spirit, without saying from what or when. The dispute is over what the purification was (a cleansing, or an added grace) and what it implies about Mary’s condition before it, and so whether the passage bears on the doctrine Pius IX defined in 1854, that Mary was preserved from original sin from the first instant of her conception. Martin Jugie (Catholic, 1952) called it “un passage énigmatique, qui peut recevoir plusieurs interprétations.”3 The principal modern proponent of the first reading below, Christiaan Kappes, is cited here through reports of his 2014 monograph; the book itself was not opened for this page. For the third reading only an apologetic writer was located; its academic literature was not found.

A preparation in grace of one already all-pure

On this reading “purified beforehand” names a positive outpouring of grace that prepared an already pure Virgin to conceive, and the word stands at the head of a Greek tradition that held Mary all-immaculate from her first moment.

Held by: Rufinus of Aquileia (Latin translation of the oration, late fourth century); Christiaan Kappes (Catholic, The Immaculate Conception, 2014) ⚠ unverified; Luigi Gambero (Catholic, Mary and the Fathers of the Church) ⚠ unverified.4

Gregory chose the compound pro-kathaírō, a purification that comes before and prepares, and in the same oration he applies purification language to the sinless Christ at his baptism. Kappes reads the two together: since the one who “had no need of purification” is nonetheless “purified,” the word need not mean cleansing from pollution. In context, he argues, “only the following meanings of ‘purification’ are possible”: an exterior sign of an outpouring of grace, or a “special preparation” for personal merit.4 Andrew Cuff’s review summarizes the result: the East “consistently viewed the prepurification at the Annunciation as a positive purity—not a removal of concupiscence, but a superaddition of grace that prepared the Theotokos for the incarnation,” while “Her sinlessness from birth… is designated under expressions like ‘all-holy,’ ‘ever-blameless,’ and ‘all-immaculate.’” Kappes reads John of Damascus’s prepurification in the same positive sense.5

The earliest Latin reader points the same way: Rufinus, translating the Nativity oration, rendered prokathartheisa by immaculata. From Gregory the word passes into later Greek Marian writing, and Kappes traces it as a continuous line, concluding that “the Greek Fathers—in the line of the Nazianzen until the introduction of Byzantine Thomism in the 14th century—never vacillated about the all-immaculate status of the Blessed Virgin Mary, from the first moment of her existence until her glorious assumption into heaven.” Gambero finds in “this doctrine of Mary’s purification before the conception of Christ” “an intuition of that truth which, in 1854, the Church would define as the dogma of the Immaculate Conception.”4 On this reading Gregory’s word describes a gift added to purity, and the Greek tradition that took it up honoured a Virgin who was never stained.

An increase of grace at the Annunciation, in a sinless Virgin who shared fallen nature

On this reading the Spirit’s purification at the Annunciation added grace rather than removed sin, while Mary, though she never sinned, was born with the rest of Adam’s race and was not exempt from its condition.

Held by: Nicholas Cabasilas (Byzantine, c. 1350, homilies on the Mother of God, as read by Nellas and Ware; Jugie reads him otherwise); Vladimir Lossky (Orthodox, “Panagia,” in In the Image and Likeness of God, 1974); Kallistos Ware (Orthodox, “‘Beyond all holiness’: St Nicolas Cabasilas on the Mother of God,” 2007).6

Cabasilas took up the Fathers’ phrase in his homilies on the Mother of God. “Even though some of the Holy Teachers state that [at the Annunciation] the Virgin was purified beforehand by the Spirit,” he writes, this does not mean she was previously sinful; the purification “merely indicates ‘an addition of the gifts of grace.’” Yet the same homilies, in Ware’s reading, hold that Mary “inherited fallen human nature”: “She did not receive a nature unacquainted with all wickedness.” Ware concludes that “as a link between the two Covenants she was, prior to the Annunciation, in the same position as the other righteous men and women of the Chosen People,” and that this “renders her sinlessness all the more remarkable.”6

Lossky places the decisive moment at the Annunciation, the culmination of an “uninterrupted succession of Old Testament holiness, which reaches its fulfillment at the moment of the Annunciation, when the Holy Spirit came down upon the Virgin to make her fit to receive the Word of the Father in her womb.” Before it, “the Holy Virgin was born under the law of original sin, sharing with all the same common responsibility for the Fall. But sin never could become actual in her person.”7 Read in this frame, Gregory’s “purified beforehand” is the Spirit’s final preparation of the holiest member of the old covenant, one of Adam’s seed who never sinned, to bear the Word.

A cleansing at the Annunciation of a nature subject to the ancestral condition

On this reading the purification is a cleansing in the ordinary sense, worked on Mary just before the Incarnation, so that Christ took flesh from a womb that had been subject to corruption and death.

Held by: Craig (Patrick) Truglia (Orthodox, “Mary’s ‘Subjection to Impurity’ and the Prepurification Doctrine,” 2020).8

Truglia reads the word through the texts that apply it to the same event. The Menaion and Octoechos hymns that call the Theotokos prokathartheisa tie her purification to the moment of the Incarnation, and John of Damascus writes that at the Annunciation “The sanctifying power of the Spirit reposed on her, cleansed her and made her holy.” Truglia also reads Gregory’s own setting this way: the Word “mingled Himself with an intelligent soul for my soul’s sake, purifying like by like,” so that the Virgin, purified in the next sentence, “too was purified ‘like by like.’”8 Earlier Fathers whom Truglia gathers speak of Christ kept “undefiled” by the Virgin, language that on his account cannot mean sin in her, since she was “undefiled before, during, and after birth,” but refers to her mortal flesh: “Mary had a postlapsarian womb subject to corruption and death due to original sin.”8

Since the writers before and after Gregory used purification of Mary “in a decidedly ‘negative’ sense, in that it cleanses Mary so as to prevent Jesus’ being exposed to impurity,” Truglia holds that “The prepurification of Nazianzus cannot be interpreted anachronistically—he must have understood its application to the annunciation in a similarly negative sense.” His conclusion: “Mary’s flesh was fallen and subject to original sin. Jesus avoided ‘all taint’ of sin because He was conceived by the Spirit. Mary was preserved from harm, how awesome the incarnation was, by being ‘prepurified.’”8 On this reading Gregory teaches a real cleansing of Mary’s fallen flesh by the Spirit at the moment she conceived, which made her fit to bear the sinless Son.
