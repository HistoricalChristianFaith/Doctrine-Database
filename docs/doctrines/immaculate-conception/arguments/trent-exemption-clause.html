Does Trent’s exemption clause tacitly affirm the Immaculate Conception?

Disputed reading · on the Immaculate Conception of Mary · witnesses: the Council of Trent; Sixtus IV

Related readings: How does the first-millennium record bear on the 1854 definition of the Immaculate Conception?

The passage

This same holy Synod doth nevertheless declare, that it is not its intention to include in this decree, where original sin is treated of, the blessed and immaculate Virgin Mary, the mother of God; but that the constitutions of Pope Sixtus IV., of happy memory, are to be observed, under the pains contained in the said constitutions, which it renews.

— Council of Trent, Session 5 (17 June 1546), Decree Concerning Original Sin, trans. J. Waterworth (Denzinger 792 / DS 1516).1

The question

The decree to which this clause is attached declares that Adam’s sin passed to the whole human race. The constitutions of Sixtus IV that it renews had approved the feast and Office of Mary’s conception (1476) and, in Grave nimis (1483), excommunicated “all those of either opinion who charged the opposite opinion with heresy.”2 The dispute is whether the clause, by naming Mary “immaculate,” exempting her by name and renewing those constitutions, in effect affirmed or favoured the doctrine defined in 1854, or deliberately left the question undecided. No modern Catholic historian reading the clause as an affirmation was located for this page.

The clause exempts Mary from the universal decree, and so favours her freedom from original sin

On this reading the words of the clause, whatever the council’s reserve, set Mary apart from the original sin it had just declared universal.

Held by: Martin Chemnitz (Lutheran, Examen Concilii Tridentini, 1565–73; he rejects the doctrine); Pius IX (Catholic, Ineffabilis Deus, 1854).3

Chemnitz, examining the decree, granted that since the constitutions of Sixtus are mentioned it might seem that the council leaves the dispute open to free opinion: “videri sane posset, quia mentio fit constitutionum Sixti, quod disputationem illam relinquat sub libera opinandi licentia, sed verba diversum omnino ostendunt” (“but the words show something altogether different”). Having said that Adam passed sin to the whole human race, the fathers “at once add an appendix” excluding Mary. By renewing Sixtus’s constitutions, he held, the appendix took in the feast, its Office and the legends attached to it, wrapped “generalitate quadam,” in a certain generality. He then asked on what grounds Mary was excepted from Scripture’s general statements on original sin: “Num sacra Scriptura ita docet? Non docet… Num Patres ita docuerunt? Neque hoc ostendi potest.”3

Pius IX read the same features from the other side. Trent, “when it promulgated the dogmatic decree concerning original sin… decreed and defined that all men are born infected by original sin; nevertheless, it solemnly declared that it had no intention of including the blessed and immaculate Virgin Mary, the Mother of God, in this decree and in the general extension of its definition. Indeed, considering the times and circumstances, the Fathers of Trent sufficiently intimated by this declaration that the Blessed Virgin Mary was free from the original stain; and thus they clearly signified that nothing could be reasonably cited from the Sacred Scriptures, from Tradition, or from the authority of the Fathers, which would in any way be opposed to so great a prerogative of the Blessed Virgin.”4 Hubert Jedin, whose own reading follows below, reports that the doctrine “commanded the adherence of the majority of the Council.”5 On this reading a council that calls Mary “immaculate,” lifts her by name out of a decree on universal sin, and renews the Office of her conception has placed her case on the side of her exemption.

The clause deliberately left the question undecided

On this reading the clause limits the decree’s scope and keeps in force Sixtus IV’s ruling that either opinion may be held.

Held by: Philip Schaff (Protestant, The Creeds of Christendom, 1877; he sees “a leaning to the Franciscan side”); Frederick Holweck (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1910); Hubert Jedin (Catholic historian, A History of the Council of Trent, vol. II, English 1961).6

“It is not its intention to include” marks where the decree does not reach. The constitutions renewed are those of Sixtus IV, and the operative one, Grave nimis, punished those of either opinion who called the other heresy. Schaff writes that Sixtus acted “since the Roman Church had not yet decided the question,” and that Trent “confirmed this neutral position, but with a leaning to the Franciscan side.” The Schaff–Herzog article: “The Council of Trent left the doctrine unsettled and referred back to the decree of Sixtus IV.”6

Jedin’s account of the drafting describes the choice: Cardinal Pacheco “strove with might and main to strip the formula of its non-committal character and to give it a turn favouring the doctrine of the Immaculate Conception.” When the votes were counted, “twenty-four (nearly one half) agreed with Pacheco in rejecting the formula submitted to them but… the majority, which included a number of adherents to the doctrine, did not wish the Council to decide the controversy.” Cervini, “who personally opposed the doctrine, insisted on a formula which would not conflict too sharply with either opinion while it maintained the situation created by the Constitutions of Sixtus IV”: “I shall not suffer the Council to have wrung from it (extorqueatur) a decision which it is incapable of issuing at this time.”5

Holweck records the reception: “Since, however, this decree did not define the doctrine, the theological opponents of the mystery, though more and more reduced in numbers, did not yield.”2 On this reading Trent, with an Immaculist majority, chose to protect both opinions and to leave the question to a later decision.
