The Eastern Feast of Mary’s Conception — Andrew of Crete and Germanus of Constantinople

Dates: 7th–8th century (Andrew of Crete, c. 660–740; Germanus of Constantinople, c. 634–740) · Wikipedia
Summary: A feast of the conception of Mary by her mother Anne was kept in the Christian East from the seventh century. Its two earliest voices, Andrew of Crete and Germanus of Constantinople, hymn Mary as all-pure and undefiled. Frederick Holweck (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1910) writes that this older feast and the modern feast of the Immaculate Conception “are not identical in their object”; how the feast’s hymns and titles bear on the doctrine is read differently by Catholic and Orthodox writers.

Claims & evidence

The feast of the Conception of St Anne (the conception of Mary), 7th century East

The feast keeping the conception of Mary by Anne originated in the monasteries of Palestine no later than the end of the seventh century and spread from the cloister to the cathedral churches, fixed on 9 December in the Eastern calendars (8 December in the Western calendar). The oldest surviving witness to it is the liturgical Canon composed by Andrew of Crete while a monk at the monastery of St Sabas near Jerusalem, in the second half of the seventh century. John of Euboea (c. 750) says the feast “was not yet known to all the faithful.”1 Holweck writes: “The older feast of the Conception of Mary… and the modern feast of the Immaculate Conception are not identical in their object.”

Andrew and Germanus praise Mary as all-pure and undefiled

O undefiled, untainted, uncorrupted, most pure, chaste Virgin… who didst unite the Word of God to mankind through thy most glorious birth-giving.

— Andrew of Crete, Homily on the Nativity of the Theotokos.2 Germanus of Constantinople hymns her in the same key, calling her “wholly immaculate” and praising one “having no affinity with sin whatever.” This is the Eastern vocabulary of Mary the Panagia (“all-holy”) and Achrantos (“undefiled”). Whether that vocabulary, and the feast it adorns, attests Mary’s preservation from original sin is the subject of a disputed-reading page, which sets out the Catholic and Orthodox readings.

Reception and debate

  • The feast reached England early. It had been kept at Canterbury since around 1030, and, after the Norman Conquest, it was suppressed in Lanfranc’s reorganisation of the Church calendar.3 Holweck traces its English establishment to “the monks of Winchester,” notes an Exeter pontifical “assigned to 1046-1072” that contains it, and names as “the principal propagator of the feast after the Conquest” Anselm, “the nephew of St. Anselm,” who established it at Bury St Edmunds on becoming abbot in 1121.4 Eadmer’s treatise arguing that Mary was preserved at her conception belongs to this English revival.
  • The English lines above circulate through devotional anthologies and the scholarly works of Hilda Graef (Mary: A History of Doctrine and Devotion) and Michael O’Carroll (Theotokos); the Greek originals are in the Patrologia Graeca (Andrew, PG 97; Germanus, PG 98).
