Papal acts on the Immaculate Conception — Paul V to Clement XI (1616–1708)

Dates: 1616–1708 (Paul V 1616–1617, Gregory XV 1622, Innocent XII 1693, Clement XI 1708) · Catholic Encyclopedia
Summary: Four disciplinary and liturgical acts of the popes between Trent and 1854. Paul V (1617) and Gregory XV (1622) forbade teaching that Mary was conceived in original sin, publicly and then even privately, the latter “until the Holy See should define the question”; Innocent XII (1693) and Clement XI (1708) raised the rank of the feast of the Conception and made it a holy day of obligation throughout the Latin Church. Alexander VII’s bull of 1661, which falls within this period, has its own page.

Claims & evidence

Paul V — the ban on public denial (1616–1617)

James MacCaffrey (Catholic historian) relates the occasion: a Spanish Franciscan, Francis of Santiago, claimed a vision in favour of the doctrine, a sharp controversy broke out in Spain, and King Philip III asked the pope for a definitive decision. In 1616 Paul V renewed the decrees of Sixtus IV and Pius V forbidding either party to charge the other with heresy. The following year, in the Apostolic Constitution Sanctissimus of 12 September 1617 he forbade anyone to teach publicly that Mary was conceived in original sin:

Paul V (1617) decreed that no one should dare to teach publicly that Mary was conceived in original sin.

— The Catholic Encyclopedia, “Immaculate Conception.”1 Sixtus IV had barred each side from calling the other heretical; the 1617 constitution forbade public teaching that Mary was conceived in original sin. A Decreto of Paul V “in favor of the Immaculate Conception” was printed at Lima in 1618.2

Gregory XV — silence even in private (1622)

In 1622 Gregory XV extended the prohibition to writings and sermons “even private,” again reserving the question for the Holy See:

Gregory XV (1622) imposed absolute silence (in scriptis et sermonibus etiam privatis) upon the adversaries of the doctrine until the Holy See should define the question.

— The Catholic Encyclopedia, “Immaculate Conception” (same article as note 1). In the same year, by the Catholic Encyclopedia’s account, he abolished the name Sanctificatio B.M.V. (the “Sanctification” of the Blessed Virgin Mary), which the Dominicans had used for the feast in place of Conceptio. MacCaffrey reports that Gregory XV was “unwilling to yield to the request of the Spanish Court for a formal definition” (as note 2).

Innocent XII — raising the feast (1693)

The next two acts are liturgical. In 1693 Innocent XII raised the rank of the feast:

Innocent XII (1693) raised it to a double of the second class with an octave for the universal Church.

— The Catholic Encyclopedia, “Immaculate Conception” (same article as note 1). A “double of the second class with an octave” is a high grade in the old calendar, with its own eight-day observance.

Clement XI — a holy day of obligation (1708)

By the bull Commissi Nobis Divinitus of 6 December 1708, Clement XI ordered 8 December kept everywhere as a holy day of obligation:

Clement XI decreed on 6 Dec., 1708, that the feast should be a holiday of obligation throughout the entire Church.

— The Catholic Encyclopedia, “Immaculate Conception” (same article as note 1); the bull’s title, Commissi Nobis Divinitus, from the standard account of the feast.3

Later citation of these acts

None of these four acts declares the doctrine revealed; Gregory XV’s decree reserved the question “until the Holy See should define the question.” In 1661 Alexander VII renewed “the Constitutions and Decrees issued by the Roman Pontiffs, our predecessors, especially Sixtus IV, Paul V, and Gregory XV”; in 1854 Pius IX quoted those words of Alexander VII in Ineffabilis Deus, citing the 1617 and 1622 acts by the title Sanctissimus.4

Notes

  • The 1617 constitution is cited by title as Sanctissimus (12 September 1617), the name under which Ineffabilis Deus refers to Paul V’s act (it gives the same title to Gregory XV’s act of 4 June 1622). The English rendering of its substance (“no one should dare to teach publicly…”) is the Catholic Encyclopedia’s report of what was decreed, not a translation of the Latin text, which the sources consulted do not reproduce in full.
  • Paul V took two steps: 1616 renewed the no-heresy-charges decrees of Sixtus IV and Pius V; the prohibition of public denial belongs to the 1617 constitution.
  • MacCaffrey reports that both Paul V (1616–17) and Gregory XV (1622) acted on requests from the Spanish court — Philip III and Philip IV respectively.
