Cardinal Cajetan on the Immaculate Conception

Dates: c. 1469–1534 · treatise to Leo X, Rome, 1515 · Wikipedia
Summary: Thomas de Vio, called Cajetan (Gaetano) after his birthplace, was Master General of the Dominicans (1508), commentator on Aquinas’s Summa, cardinal (1517), and the legate who examined Luther at Augsburg (1518). In 1515, while the Fifth Lateran Council sat, he addressed a treatise on Mary’s conception to Pope Leo X, holding with Aquinas that Mary was cleansed from original sin after her conception rather than preserved from it at the first instant. He affirmed her sinless life and her sanctification in the womb while ranking the preservation view as no more than “tolerable.” The treatise drew replies from within his own order.

Claims & evidence

The question as he framed it: did God preserve Mary, or cleanse her?

It is proposed that you decide in the Lateran Council whether God preserved the most blessed Virgin Mary from original sin, or cleansed her from it.

— Cajetan, Treatise on the Conception of the Blessed Virgin Mary, to Leo X (Rome, 1515), ch. 1, modern English translation.1 The treatise turns on this fork — praeservata vs. mundata. Cajetan lines up “an innumerable multitude of holy doctors and saints” for the cleansing view and ranks it the “probable” one.

Two senses of “preserved” and the universality of Christ’s death

If Christ died for all, all died: but according to this position, Not all died, because the blessed Virgin did not die the death of sin: therefore Christ did not die for all: which is manifest heresy.

— Treatise, ch. 3.2 Cajetan argues from the universality of Christ’s redemption, as Bernard, Bonaventure, and Aquinas had done, and distinguishes two senses of “preserved.” The strong sense — that Mary was “totally preserved… exempt from all things pertaining to said sin,” her very flesh never infected and so under no debt (debitum) of contracting it — he calls “contrary to the Catholic faith… a manifest error in faith,” since it would place Mary outside those for whom Christ died. The weak sense — that she was preserved “from the stain of original sin alone” by infused grace, her flesh still infected and still under the debt — he allows is “not contrary to Sacred Scripture,” but holds it no more than tolerable, and not what he takes to be true.

The Council of Basel’s decree

…that congregation of Basel at that time was not a synod, nor a council, but was schismatic: as being cut off from the head of the Church Eugene IV… Hence from this nothing but the authority of the synagogue of Satan can be brought forward.

— Treatise, ch. 5.3 Defenders of the doctrine cited Basel’s session 36 (1439), which had declared it “pious, and consonant… with the Catholic faith.” Cajetan answers that Basel had already deposed Pope Eugene IV in session 34 and gone into schism, so that its later decree binds no one (see the Council of Basel page). In the same chapter he affirms the sanctification of Mary in her mother’s womb as the “probable and pious” belief of the Church.

His conclusion: the cleansing view “probable,” the preservation view “tolerable”

…neither part of the proposed question in the sense in which it is turned into doubt, is necessarily to be believed, and that one (namely saying, that the blessed Virgin was cleansed from original sin) is probable, but the other namely saying, that the blessed Virgin was preserved from the stain of original sin, is tolerable…

— Treatise, concluding chapter; subscribed “Rome, in the year of salvation 1515.”4 Cajetan submits both opinions to the Pope’s judgment, ranking the cleansing after conception as probable and the preservation as tolerable.

Reception and debate

  • Replies within his order. Cajetan’s treatise touched off a running controversy within the Dominican order. The Dominican Bartolomeo Spina answered it directly with a Tractatus contra opusculum Caietani de conceptione Beatae Virginis (Venice, 1535); the Dominican Ambrosius Catharinus (Lancelotto Politi) defended the Immaculate Conception against Cajetan across 1532–51 — the episode traced in Patrick Preston’s study of the dispute.5
  • Kochaniewicz’s reading. Bogusław Kochaniewicz (2024) reads the treatise as a “turning point” in the Franciscan–Dominican debate: in distinguishing two senses of “preserved,” Cajetan is, on his account, the first Dominican to admit the preservation from the stain alone, by a redemptio praeservativa, as tolerable rather than heretical, “although he remained faithful to the traditional position of the Dominicans.” Kochaniewicz renders Cajetan’s conclusion as the cleansing view “to be believed” and the preservation view “at best… tolerated.”6
  • Cajetan affirmed Mary’s sinlessness and her sanctification in the womb while denying that she was preserved from original sin at her conception. He appears on this wiki also as a witness on the Old Testament canon timeline, where his views on the deuterocanonical books, following Jerome, are recorded.
  • On the text. The Latin original is the Tractatus de conceptione Beatae Mariae Virginis ad Leonem X, printed among Cajetan’s Opuscula omnia (Lyons, 1575); the standard scholarly English is R. C. Jenkins’s 1858 rendering, The Judgement of Thomas de Vio Cajetan against the Immaculate Conception. The English quoted above is from a freely available modern translation; its exact wording has not been collated against the critical Latin, and is marked accordingly in the sources.
