Pope Alexander VII on the Immaculate Conception

Dates: 8 December 1661 (Sollicitudo omnium ecclesiarum) · Wikipedia
Summary: Alexander VII’s bull Sollicitudo omnium ecclesiarum (1661) describes the devotion of the faithful who believe that Mary’s soul was preserved from the stain of original sin “from the first instant of its creation and infusion into her body… in view of the merits of her Son” — claim (b) — and declares that this is the sense in which they celebrate the feast of her conception. It renews the constitutions of Sixtus IV, Paul V and Gregory XV in favour of that opinion and, in Frederick Holweck’s summary (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1910), forbids “all further discussion against the common and pious sentiment of the Church.” It does not declare the doctrine revealed by God.

Claims & evidence

The object of the feast: preservation at the first instant, in view of Christ’s merits

The devotion to the most blessed Virgin Mary is indeed of long standing among the faithful of Christ who believe that her soul, from the first instant of its creation and infusion into her body, was preserved immune by a special grace and privilege of God from the stain of original sin, in view of the merits of her Son, Jesus Christ, the Redeemer of our human race, and who, in this sense, esteem and solemnly celebrate the festivity of her conception.

— Alexander VII, Sollicitudo omnium ecclesiarum (8 December 1661), Denzinger 1100 (DS 2015–2017), source.1 The bull names the first instant of the soul’s creation and infusion (animation), preservation from the stain, a special grace and privilege, and the merits of Christ “the Redeemer” as the ground of the preservation — the terms in which Scotus had answered the objection from Christ’s universal redemption. The 1854 definition of Pius IX is reported on its own page.

Renewal of the earlier constitutions

We renew the Constitutions and decrees published by Roman Pontiffs in favor of the opinion that asserts that the soul of the blessed Virgin Mary at its creation, and at its infusion into her body, was blessed by the grace of the Holy Spirit and was preserved from original sin.

— Alexander VII, Sollicitudo omnium ecclesiarum (1661), source.2 Sixtus IV’s Grave nimis (1483) had forbidden either side to brand the other heretical; Paul V (1617) and Gregory XV (1622) had forbidden teaching against the doctrine (see the papal acts of 1616–1708). Holweck writes that Alexander, “to put an end to all further cavilling,” issued the bull “defining the true sense of the word conceptio, and forbidding all further discussion against the common and pious sentiment of the Church”: the object of the feast is Mary’s immunity from original sin “in the first moment of the creation of her soul and its infusion into the body.”3

Notes

  • Description and protection. Sollicitudo describes what “the faithful of Christ… believe” and forbids attack on that belief; it does not declare the doctrine “revealed by God,” the wording of Ineffabilis Deus (1854).
  • Occasion. In the words quoted from the bull in Ineffabilis Deus, Alexander acted “at the instance and request of the bishops mentioned above, with the chapters of the churches, and of King Philip and his kingdoms” — Philip IV of Spain.4 Earlier institutional acts on this timeline include the Council of Basel, Sixtus IV, the Paris oath, and Trent’s clause on Mary.
  • Denzinger numbering. The text is Denzinger 1100 in the older (Denzinger–Bannwart) numbering, DS 2015–2017 in the current Denzinger–Hünermann edition; it cross-refers to Sixtus IV (old DS 734–735 / new DS 1400, 1425–1426) and Trent (old DS 792 / DS 1516).
