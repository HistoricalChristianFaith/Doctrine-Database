The Swiss Reformers (Zwingli and Bullinger) on the Immaculate Conception of Mary

Dates: c. 1525–1566 (Huldrych Zwingli, 1484–1531, reformer of Zurich, and his successor Heinrich Bullinger, 1504–1575) · Zwingli · Bullinger
Summary: Huldrych Zwingli and his successor at Zurich, Heinrich Bullinger, praise Mary’s purity in strong terms. Zwingli calls her “the immaculate and perpetual Virgin,” says she bore not “the smallest trace of a stain,” and holds that she alone conceived her Son without transmitting to him the weakness inherited from Adam; Bullinger hymns her most holy body as “the most pure bed and temple of the Holy Spirit.” Zwingli described original sin as an inherited disease or defect, “not a sin, because sin implies guilt.” Gottfried Locher (Zwingli’s Thought, 1981) writes that there is “no trace in Zwingli of the doctrine” of the immaculate conception; the compilation “The Protestant Reformers on Mary” at catholicapologetics.info quotes Zwingli, from E. Stakemeier (1962), calling Mary “the ever chaste, immaculate Virgin Mary.”

Claims & evidence

Zwingli — Mary “the immaculate and perpetual Virgin” (1530)

In the confession of faith he laid before the Diet of Augsburg in 1530, Zwingli confesses the Incarnation in these terms:

I believe and understand that the Son assumed flesh, because he truly assumed of the immaculate and perpetual Virgin Mary the human nature, yea, the entire man, who consists of body and soul.

— Zwingli, Fidei ratio (A Reckoning of the Faith, 1530).1 He uses “immaculate” (immaculata) of Mary without further definition. Elsewhere he affirms her perpetual virginity, holding that she “brought forth for us the Son of God and in childbirth and after childbirth forever remained a pure, intact Virgin.”

Zwingli — “not the smallest trace of a stain” (1525)

In his systematic Commentary on True and False Religion (1525) and his preaching, Zwingli praises Mary’s freedom from sin: she alone of all women, “since the beginning of the world,” conceived her Son without transmitting to him “the human weakness that we all bring with us from Adam,” and she bore not “the smallest trace of a stain.”2 George Tavard (The Thousand Faces of Mary, 1996), surveying these texts, writes that Zwingli “does not relate her virginal conception of Jesus to her own immaculate conception,” and notes that the Assumption is the one Marian theme absent from his preaching.

Zwingli on original sin (1526)

In his Declaration Regarding Original Sin, Addressed to Urbanus Rhegius (15 August 1526) Zwingli describes the inherited condition of Adam’s line as a defect of nature, not a personal guilt:

I have said that the original contamination of man is a disease, not a sin, because sin implies guilt, and guilt comes from a transgression or trespass on the part of one who designedly perpetrates a deed.

— Zwingli, Declaration Regarding Original Sin (1526).3 He illustrated the point with hereditary ailments — original sin is a lasting defect “as when stammering, blindness, or gout is hereditary in a family” — and concluded that “on account of such a thing no one is thought the worse or the more vicious, for things which come from nature cannot be put down as crimes or guilt.”

Zwingli in modern scholarship

Gottfried Locher writes that “there is no trace in Zwingli of the doctrine of the ‘immaculate conception’”; a second study, quoted without its author’s name by James Swan (Beggars All Reformation, 2013), finds that “constantly as he insisted upon the certainty of the perpetual virginity of the mother of God, he accepted neither her immaculate conception nor her assumption.”4 Locher adds that for Zwingli “the holiness of Mary is derived from that of Christ, and not the other way round.” Locher’s footnote also states that “Mary was, at least at the moment of conception, wholly pure and free from original sin”; Swan asks which conception is meant, Mary’s own or Christ’s, and holds that Zwingli’s own texts need to be examined in context.4

Bullinger on Mary

Zwingli’s successor at Zurich, Heinrich Bullinger, taught that Mary is properly called Mother of God and that she remained ever-virgin, and, unlike Zwingli, he affirmed her bodily assumption, on the biblical model of Elijah taken up in the chariot of fire:

We believe that the Virgin Mary, Begetter of God, the most pure bed and temple of the Holy Spirit, that is, her most holy body, was carried to heaven by angels.

— Bullinger, sermon on the Virgin.5 The Second Helvetic Confession (1566), largely Bullinger’s own work, confesses that Christ was born of Mary “ever a virgin” who “most chastely conceived” him by the Holy Spirit, and the earlier First Helvetic (Basel) Confession (1536) that he took flesh “from the undefiled Virgin Mary.”

Reception and debate

  • The most-quoted lines. Two Zwingli sayings on Mary, that he esteemed “the ever chaste, immaculate Virgin Mary” and that “it was fitting that such a holy Son should have a holy Mother,” are quoted in the compilation “The Protestant Reformers on Mary” at catholicapologetics.info, which cites them to E. Stakemeier, De Mariologia et Oecumenismo (1962), p. 456.6 Swan wrote his 2013 post in reply to a claim on a discussion board that Luther, Calvin and Zwingli all believed in the Immaculate Conception.4
  • Placement on the timeline. The Swiss witness is dated between Cajetan’s treatise (1515) and Luther’s statements (1527–1540). The Council of Trent’s decree on original sin (1546) and its clause on Mary are recorded on their own page, and the Orthodox response to the 1854 definition on its own page.
