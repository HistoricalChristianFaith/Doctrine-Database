Martin Luther on the Immaculate Conception of Mary

Dates: c. 1527–1540 (the Festival Postil sermon On the Day of the Conception of Mary the Mother of God, 1527, and the Disputation on the Divinity and Humanity of Christ, 1540; Martin Luther, 1483–1546, Augustinian friar and Reformer) · Wikipedia
Summary: In a 1527 sermon Luther called it “a sweet and pious belief” that Mary’s soul was infused without original sin, purified “in the very infusion of her soul” — placing the exemption at ensoulment (his “second conception”) rather than at bodily conception, and offering it as a pious opinion, not an article of faith. The passage is absent from the reprints after 1527. In his 1540 Disputation on the Divinity and Humanity of Christ he wrote that “in conception the flesh and blood of Mary were entirely purged, so that nothing of sin remained,” a conception James Swan (Reformed) reads as Christ’s; and in sermons of 1539–40, as Julius Köstlin reports them, he taught that Mary, like every believer, was conceived and born in sin and made free from it by faith.

Claims & evidence

The 1527 sermon — purification “in the very infusion of her soul”

Preaching on the feast of Mary’s conception, Luther set out, as a “sweet and pious belief,” a soul-infusion account of her purity from the first instant she lived:

It is a sweet and pious belief that the infusion of Mary’s soul was effected without original sin; so that in the very infusion of her soul she was also purified from original sin and adorned with God’s gifts, receiving a pure soul infused by God; thus from the first moment she began to live she was free from all sin.

— On the Day of the Conception of Mary the Mother of God (Am tage der Empfengknus Marie der mutter Gottes), in the Festival Postil compiled by Stephan Roth, 1527.1 The mechanism is Luther’s distinction between two “conceptions”: a first conception of the body, drawn by ordinary generation from her parents, and a second conception — the infusion of the soul by God — at which the purifying grace falls. Luther locates the exemption at ensoulment, not at the body’s conception, within the medieval delayed-animation embryology the scholastics argued within. He declined to bind it, adding (in the Erlangen printing) that “everyone may think what he wants, as long as he makes no article of faith about it.”

The passage absent from the reprints after 1527

The 1527 statement appears in the 1527 Festival Postil (Roth’s compilation of Luther’s preaching) but was removed from the 1528 and all later editions printed in Luther’s lifetime; the Erlangen edition marks the end of the passage with a note that from that point “to the end” the words are “only found in the edition of the year 1527.” James Swan reports the removal as documented by the Catholic historians Hartmann Grisar and Nicholas Paulus, the Lutheran Eric Gritsch, and Ewald Plass (What Luther Says).2 The Festival Postil is Stephan Roth’s redaction of Luther’s sermons, not a treatise Luther wrote himself.

The 1540 Disputation on the Divinity and Humanity of Christ

In the Disputation on the Divinity and Humanity of Christ (27–28 February 1540), discussing why Christ has no concupiscence, Luther speaks of Mary’s flesh being “purged”:

Every man who is not a divine Person, as is Christ, has concupiscence, but the man Christ has none, because he is a divine Person, and in conception the flesh and blood of Mary were entirely purged, so that nothing of sin remained. Therefore Isaiah says rightly, “There was no guile found in his mouth”; otherwise, every seed except for Mary’s was corrupted.

— Disputation on the Divinity and Humanity of Christ, thesis X (response), trans. Christopher B. Brown.3 James Swan (Reformed, 2007) reads the “conception” here as Christ’s: “A close reading of this quote denies any notion that Mary was purified from sin at her conception. Rather, the focus is on Christ’s conception.” On his reading the Holy Spirit sanctifies Mary at the conceiving of the Son so that Christ’s humanity comes without concupiscence — a purification at the conception of Christ that Peter Lombard also teaches.

The sermons of 1539–1540

In sermons of 1539 and 1540 Luther taught that Mary was conceived and born in sin from sinful parents like the rest of Adam’s line, and became blessed and free from sin by faith — the position Julius Köstlin identified as Luther’s settled teaching.4

Notes

  • Three texts. The 1527 sermon places Mary’s purification at the infusion of her soul; the 1540 Disputation speaks of her flesh and blood purged “in conception,” read by Swan as Christ’s conception; the 1539–40 sermons, as Köstlin reports them, have her conceived in sin and made free from it by faith.
  • Transmission of the 1527 text. The 1527 passage stands in Roth’s postil, is absent from every reprint after 1527, and its ending is marked in the Erlangen edition as found only in that first printing.
  • Chronology on this timeline. Luther’s texts fall between Cajetan’s treatise (1515) and the Council of Trent’s decree on original sin (1546).
