The Eastern Orthodox response to the Immaculate Conception

Dates: modern (representative statements, 20th century) · Wikipedia
Summary: The Eastern Orthodox Church venerates Mary as Panagia (“all-holy”) and Achrantos (“undefiled”), free of every personal sin. Its modern theologians have declined the Catholic dogma of 1854. Vladimir Lossky (1974) writes that “the Orthodox Church does not admit the idea that the Holy Virgin was thus exempted from the lot of the rest of fallen humanity”; Kallistos Ware writes that most Orthodox theologians reject the idea of inherited “original guilt,” and that since 1854 “the great majority of Orthodox have rejected the doctrine,” while noting that the Orthodox Church “has never in fact made any formal and definitive pronouncement on the matter.”

Claims & evidence

Lossky: Mary’s holiness within fallen humanity

The Orthodox Church does not admit the idea that the Holy Virgin was thus exempted from the lot of the rest of fallen humanity—the idea of a ‘privilege’ which makes her into a being ransomed before the redemptive work, by virtue of the future merits of her Son… Like other human beings… the Holy Virgin was born under the law of original sin, sharing with all the same common responsibility for the Fall. But sin never could become actual in her person; the sinful heritage of the Fall had no mastery over her right will.

— Vladimir Lossky, “Panagia,” in In the Image and Likeness of God (St Vladimir’s Seminary Press, 1974), pp. 203–204.1 For Lossky Mary stands at the end of an “uninterrupted succession of Old Testament holiness”; she is holy within fallen humanity, and her sinlessness is personal — sin never became actual in her.

Ware and Bulgakov on original sin and the 1854 dogma

Most Orthodox theologians reject the idea of ‘original guilt,’ put forward by Augustine and still accepted (albeit in a mitigated form) by the Roman Catholic Church. Men (Orthodox usually teach) automatically inherit Adam’s corruption and mortality, but not his guilt: they are only guilty in so far as by their own free choice they imitate Adam.

— Kallistos (Timothy) Ware, The Orthodox Church.2 Ware gives the reasons for which, since 1854, most Orthodox have rejected the doctrine: “They feel it to be unnecessary; they feel that, at any rate as defined by the Roman Catholic Church, it implies a false understanding of original sin; they suspect the doctrine because it seems to separate Mary from the rest of the descendants of Adam, putting her in a completely different class from all the other righteous men and women of the Old Testament.” Sergius Bulgakov writes that “the Orthodox church does not accept the Catholic dogma of 1854 -- the dogma of the immaculate conception of the Virgin, in the sense that she was exempt at birth from original sin,” for “this would separate her from the human race, and she would then have been unable to transmit to her Son humanity.” He continues: “But Orthodoxy does not admit in the all-pure Virgin any individual sin, for that would be unworthy of the dignity of the Mother of God.”3

Reception and debate

  • Ware writes: “In the past individual Orthodox have made statements which, if not definitely affirming the doctrine of the Immaculate Conception, at any rate approach close to it; but since 1854 the great majority of Orthodox have rejected the doctrine, for several reasons.” He adds that “all Orthodox are agreed in believing that Our Lady was free from actual sin.”4
  • The Catholic Church gives its own account of original sin in the Catechism of the Catholic Church, which states that “original sin does not have the character of a personal fault in any of Adam’s descendants” (§405); this account is set out with the Catholic reading on the disputed-reading page on the first-millennium record.5
  • Frederick Holweck (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1910) records that the Greek Orthodox “continue to call it ‘Conception of St. Anne’” and that the Russian writer Muraview and other Orthodox authors “loudly declaimed against the dogma after its promulgation.”6
  • How the Eastern “all-holy” vocabulary and the first-millennium record bear on the doctrine is set out, reading by reading, on the disputed-reading pages on Panagia and on the first-millennium record.
