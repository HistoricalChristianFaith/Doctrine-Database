Sophronius of Jerusalem on the Immaculate Conception

Dates: c. 634 (Sophronius of Jerusalem, monk and then Patriarch of Jerusalem, c. 560–638; the key texts are his Oration II, On the Annunciation of the Theotokos, c. 635, and his Synodical Letter of 634, later read and approved at the Sixth Ecumenical Council, Constantinople III, 680–681) · Wikipedia
Summary: Sophronius, preaching the Annunciation, greets Mary as “the Immaculate,” on whom the Spirit descends “to make you purer,” filled with a grace granted to her alone and surpassing every saint and every rank of angels; his Synodical Letter of 634, read into the acts of the Sixth Ecumenical Council, confesses her “prepurified (prokathartheisēs) in body and soul,” the word Gregory Nazianzen had used. Martin Jugie (Catholic, 1952) reads the homily as presenting Mary “déjà immaculée” before the Annunciation; Craig Truglia (Orthodox, 2020) quotes the Synodical Letter in arguing that Mary’s flesh was purified at the Annunciation from the condition of fallen nature.

Claims & evidence

“All else is inferior when compared with the grace you have been given”

Hail, full of grace, the Lord is with you. What joy could surpass this, O Virgin Mother? What grace can excel that which God has granted to you alone?… Before the miracle we witness in you, all else pales; all else is inferior when compared with the grace you have been given.

— Oration II, On the Annunciation, §§21–22, 26 (PG 87.3, cols. 3242, 3250), in the Roman Office of Readings translation.1 Sophronius reads Gabriel’s kecharitōmenē as a grace unique to Mary, setting her above the whole of creation — “you have surpassed the hosts of angels; you have overshadowed the dazzling splendour of the archangels.”2

“No one has been purified in advance as you have been”

Many saints appeared before you, but none was filled with grace as you. No one has been purified in advance as you have been.

— Oration II, On the Annunciation, §25 (PG 87.3, cols. 3247–3248).3 The Greek is a long litany of parallel clauses — “none has been graced (kecharitōtai) as you… none magnified as you… none prepurified (prokekathartai) as you…” — of which the received English quotes two; Migne’s facing Latin renders the key clause “nemo, sicut tu, purificante gratia praeoccupatus est” (“none, like you, has been forestalled by purifying grace”). The word is prokekathartai, the perfect passive of prokathairō (“to purify beforehand”) — the prokathartheisa root, here in the perfect tense. Migne’s nineteenth-century editor glossed the clause as Mary’s “most singular preservation from original infection.” Jugie, discussing Gregory’s word, writes that “Saint Sophrone de Jérusalem paraît bien avoir compris la purification préalable dans ce sens” (Saint Sophronius of Jerusalem appears to have understood the prior purification in this sense), that is, of a preservation from all stain, citing this clause.4

“The Spirit… descends upon you, the Immaculate, to make you purer”

The Holy Spirit descends upon you, who are the Immaculate, to make you purer and give you the virtue of fertility.

— Homily on the Annunciation, in Georges Gharib, Testi mariani del primo millennio, vol. 2 (Rome, 1989), p. 139.5 The Greek of this sentence (§43, PG 87.3, 3273 D) calls Mary tēn amolynton (“the undefiled”).6 Jugie reads it: “Marie est déjà immaculée, à l’heure où l’archange vient la saluer. La descente du Saint-Esprit n’aura pour effet que de lui donner un surcroît de pureté et de sainteté” (Mary is already immaculate at the hour when the archangel comes to greet her; the descent of the Holy Spirit will have only the effect of giving her an increase of purity and holiness). He grants that the homily gives no earlier date for her holiness (“l’orateur ne donne pas d’autre date”), and adds that nothing in Sophronius’s teaching suggests a moment between her conception and the Incarnation when she did not merit the epithet.6

The “prepurified” virgin of the Synodical Letter

Sophronius’s Synodical Letter of 634 confesses that the Word made his dwelling “in the womb of the spotless (achrantou) virgin and Theotokos Mary, after she was prepurified (prokathartheisēs) in body and soul… and from her holy and blameless (amōmou) flesh.” That letter was read into the acts and approved at the Sixth Ecumenical Council (Constantinople III, 680–681).7 Craig Truglia (Orthodox, 2020) quotes this passage as making the prepurification teaching binding at Constantinople III, and asks what Mary’s body needed purifying from; his answer is her fallen, postlapsarian flesh.7

Reception and debate

  • Mary and John the Baptist. A reading attributed to Christiaan Kappes (Catholic, The Immaculate Conception, 2014) ⚠ unverified takes Sophronius’s exaltation of John the Baptist (sanctified in the womb) and his subordination of John to Mary as implying that Mary was holier than the in-utero-sanctified Baptist, and so already immaculate when “purified” at the Annunciation. Aidan Kimel (Eclectic Orthodoxy, 2015) puts the reasoning this way: “Jeremiah and John the Baptist both received the prophetic Spirit while in the womb. Is not the Theotokos greater than they? Such, I think, is the logic driving Kappes’s analysis.” This is a reconstruction of the sermon’s logic, not a verbatim sentence of Sophronius.8
  • The disputed reading. How the “prepurified” language of Gregory and Sophronius is read — as a preparation in grace of one already all-pure, as an increase of grace in a sinless Virgin who shared fallen nature, or as a cleansing of a nature subject to the ancestral condition — is set out with its proponents on the disputed-reading page Does Gregory Nazianzen’s prokathartheisa testify to the Immaculate Conception?
