The Life of the Virgin (attributed to Maximus the Confessor) on the Immaculate Conception

Dates: attributed to the early 7th century (to Maximus the Confessor, c. 580–662, by its editor Michel van Esbroeck and its translator Stephen Shoemaker); Phil Booth (2015) argues for a date no earlier than the 10th century. The text survives only in Old Georgian, and its earliest manuscript is 10th-century (see Notes) · Wikipedia
Summary: The Life of the Virgin, a full biography of Mary, calls her the “all-holy, immaculate, and most blessed Theotokos” and “ever immaculate: before birth, in birth, and after birth.” Its one passage reasoning about a conception free of “the original sin of Adam and Eve” concerns the conception of Christ; its account of Mary’s own conception is the miracle of the barren Anne, after the Protevangelium. Its translator, Stephen Shoemaker, cautions against reading later Latin Marian doctrine into it. Its authorship and date are disputed (see Notes).

Claims & evidence

The purity-titles — “all-holy, immaculate, and most blessed”

…let us hymn, praise, and glorify the all-holy, immaculate, and most blessed Theotokos and ever-virgin Mary.

— Life of the Virgin, §1, trans. Stephen J. Shoemaker (Yale, 2012), p. 35; the dedication likewise praises “the story of her immaculate and blessed life.”1 The titles belong to the Greek Panagia (“all-holy”) and Achrantos (“undefiled”) vocabulary. Where the text calls her “a virgin ever immaculate: before birth, in birth, and after birth” (§21), the phrase is set in the formula of her perpetual virginity.2

A conception “neither by the seed of a man nor by the corruption of sin”

For the fruits of other women were under the curse from the original sin of Adam and Eve, and by carnal marriage and the corruption of sin they entered into the world. But the fruit of your womb alone is blessed, for he was conceived neither by the seed of a man nor by the corruption of sin… And he committed no sin at all.

— Life of the Virgin, Annunciation section, trans. Shoemaker, p. 56.3 In this passage the conception free from “the original sin of Adam and Eve” is the virginal conception of Christ, contrasted with “the fruits of other women.” When the text turns to Mary’s own conception, it follows the Protevangelium: “from the barren Anna was born Mary the illuminator of all.”

Mary “consecrated immaculately to God”

…I have been consecrated immaculately to God… [she] was trained from the beginning in complete holiness and purity of soul and body… no desire of passion at all had come into her heart.

— Life of the Virgin, §23, trans. Shoemaker, p. 53.4 The passage describes Mary as “trained from the beginning in complete holiness and purity of soul and body,” with “no desire of passion” in her heart — the purity of her upbringing and disposition.

Notes

  • The translator’s caution. Shoemaker devotes an excursus to warning against reading this Greek text through later Western Marian categories: the Life’s “profoundly incarnational soteriology” predates and is “altogether foreign to” Anselm’s juridical account of the atonement, so its exalted Marian language should not, on his account, be recast in the mould of the medieval Latin doctrines. His named target is the Coredemptrix / Mediatrix reading.5
  • Authorship and date. Michel van Esbroeck, who edited the Georgian (1986), and Shoemaker, who translated it (2012), assign the work to Maximus the Confessor in the early 7th century. Phil Booth (Journal of Theological Studies, 2015) argues that “Maximus cannot be the author… and… it cannot date to the seventh century”: no Greek manuscript survives, no characteristically Maximian themes appear, and no witness attests the whole Life before the second half of the 10th century. Maximos Constas is reported as dating the Greek prototype to the 10th century ⚠ unverified; Mary Cunningham defends an earlier date while treating the attribution to Maximus as doubtful. The earliest surviving manuscript (Tbilisi A-40) is 10th-century.6
