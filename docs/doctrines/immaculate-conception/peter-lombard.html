Peter Lombard on the Immaculate Conception

Dates: c. 1155–58 (Sentences, Book III) · c. 1096–1160 · Wikipedia
Summary: Peter Lombard’s Sentences (c. 1155–58) became the standard theology textbook of the medieval schools, and later commentators, among them Bonaventure, Aquinas and Scotus, discussed Mary’s sanctification at its Book III, distinction 3. There Lombard teaches that Mary’s flesh was “subject to sin” (obnoxia peccato) like the rest of Adam’s line, and was purged and sanctified by the Holy Spirit when she conceived Christ; he also quotes Augustine’s exception of Mary from the question of sins. His frame is sanctification after conception (claim (a)), not preservation at the instant of conception (claim (b)).

Claims & evidence

Mary’s flesh was “subject to sin” before it was purified by the Holy Spirit

Quaeritur etiam de carne Verbi, an, priusquam conciperetur, obligata fuerit peccato, et an talis assumta fuerit a Verbo. Sane dici potest et credi oportet, iuxta Sanctorum attestationis convenientiam, ipsam prius peccato fuisse obnoxiam, sicut reliqua Virginis caro, sed Spiritus sancti operatione ita mundatam, ut ab omni peccati contagione immunis uniretur Verbo, poena tantum, non necessitate sed voluntate assumentis, remanente.

— Sentences, Book III, dist. 3, ch. 1 (“On the flesh assumed, what it was like before”), source (Quaracchi critical edition, 1916, pp. 557–558; = PL 192). In translation: “It is asked, too, concerning the flesh of the Word, whether, before it was conceived, it was bound by sin, and whether it was assumed by the Word in that condition. It can indeed be said, and ought to be believed, in agreement with the testimony of the Saints, that it was previously subject to sin (obnoxia), like the rest of the Virgin’s flesh, but was so cleansed by the operation of the Holy Spirit that, free from every contagion of sin, it was united to the Word.” The passage speaks of the flesh from which Christ’s body was formed as previously subject to sin and then cleansed.1

The Holy Spirit purged Mary and freed her from the “tinder of sin” — at Christ’s conception

Mariam quoque totam Spiritus sanctus, in eam praeveniens, a peccato prorsus purgavit et a fomite peccati etiam liberavit, vel fomitem ipsum penitus evacuando, ut quibusdam placet, vel sic debilitando et extenuando, ut ei postmodum peccandi occasio nullatenus exstiterit…

— Sentences, Book III, dist. 3, ch. 1, source (Quaracchi ed., 1916, p. 558). In translation: “Mary too the Holy Spirit, coming upon her, wholly purged from sin and even freed from the tinder of sin (fomes peccati) — either by wholly evacuating the tinder itself, as some hold, or by so weakening and diminishing it that afterward no occasion of sinning existed for her at all…” Lombard reads this as the moment of the Annunciation — the “coming upon” is the Spiritus sanctus superveniet in te of Luke 1:35, which he quotes next. In this passage the purgation is worked at the conceiving of Christ, and is described as a purging from sin and from its fomes. That the tinder was “wholly evacuated” he reports only as what “some hold” (ut quibusdam placet).

“Before it was united to the Word” the flesh was subject to sin in Mary and in her forebears

Cum autem illa caro, cuius excellentia singularis verbis explicari non valet, antequam esset Verbo unita, obnoxia fuerit peccato in Maria et in aliis, a quibus propagatione traducta est, non immerito videri potest in Abraham peccato subiacuisse, cuius universa caro peccato subiacebat.

— Sentences, Book III, dist. 3, ch. 3, source (Quaracchi ed., 1916, pp. 559–560). In translation: “But since that flesh, whose singular excellence words cannot explain, before it was united to the Word, was subject to sin in Mary and in the others from whom by propagation it was handed down, it can not without reason be thought to have lain under sin in Abraham, whose whole flesh lay under sin.” The passage places the flesh Christ took within the line that transmits sin by ordinary generation, back to Abraham.

Mary’s freedom from sin — Lombard quotes Augustine (claim (a))

Quod autem sacra Virgo ex tunc ab omni peccato immunis exstiterit, Augustinus evidenter ostendit… “Excepta sancta Virgine Maria, de qua propter honorem Domini nullam prorsus, cum de peccatis agitur, haberi volo quaestionem.”

— Sentences, Book III, dist. 3, ch. 2 (“That no one here is without sin, except the Virgin”), source (Quaracchi ed., 1916, p. 559), quoting Augustine, On Nature and Grace 36 (42). Lombard quotes Augustine’s text verbatim: Mary is set apart from the question of sins “out of honour to the Lord,” and “from then on” (ex tunc) was immune from all sin. The passage stands beside his teaching that her flesh was purged.

Notes

  • The Sentences in the schools. From the 1220s onward bachelors of theology in the Latin West lectured on the Sentences, and the sanctification of Mary was debated at Book III, distinction 3. Bonaventure, Aquinas, and Scotus each frame the question as a commentary on this distinction.
  • Reception. Frederick Holweck (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1910) names “Peter the Lombard” with Peter Damian, Alexander of Hales, Bonaventure and Albert the Great as those “quoted as opposing” the doctrine.2 Lombard raises no polemic on the point, unlike Bernard, who wrote against the new feast.
  • The schoolmen’s question. Lombard’s own words treat the flesh (caro) as obnoxia peccato and place the purgation at Christ’s conception; the commentators asked the further question of when Mary’s soul was sanctified relative to its animation, and their answer — contraction of original sin at animation, then sanctification in the womb — is not a verbatim quotation of his text.
