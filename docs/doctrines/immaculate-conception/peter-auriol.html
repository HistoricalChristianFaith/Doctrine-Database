Peter Auriol on the Immaculate Conception

Dates: c. 1280–1322 · Franciscan lector at Toulouse; the Tractatus de conceptione completed 20 December 1314 · Catholic Encyclopedia
Summary: Peter Auriol (Petrus Aureoli), a Franciscan lector at Toulouse, wrote a treatise devoted to claim (b). He defended the Immaculate Conception in a public disputation at Toulouse in 1314, completed his Tractatus de conceptione beatae Mariae Virginis on 20 December 1314, and answered his critics the next year in the Repercussorium. His case runs from possibility to fittingness to fact — God could preserve Mary, it was supremely fitting, and it may be held that he did — and he claims that the preservation “can be held without danger to faith,” pending the Church’s judgment. William Duba (2000) studies his treatment of the debitum peccati, the question whether Mary was ever under any debt or obligation to contract original sin.

Claims & evidence

The plan of the treatise

…in tertio quoque probabitur, quod Deus de potentia absoluta Virginem, ne originale contraheret, potuit praeservare; in quarto vero evidentissime ostenditur, quod de summa decentia Mariam potuit praeservare; in quinto vero, quod absque periculo fidei et erroris potest teneri, quod Deus eam praeservavit de facto; nec una pars nec alia est de necessitate fidei, donec per ecclesiam determinatum fuerit, quid sit tenendum.

— Tractatus de conceptione beatae Mariae Virginis (1314), ed. Quaracchi (1904), p. 23, source. In translation: “…in the third [part] it will be proved that God, by his absolute power, was able to preserve the Virgin from contracting original sin; in the fourth it is shown most evidently that with the highest fittingness he was able to preserve Mary; in the fifth, that it can be held without danger to faith or error that God did in fact preserve her — though neither side is of the necessity of faith until the Church shall have determined what is to be held.” The plan runs from possibility to fittingness to fact, and the fifth conclusion is framed as what may be held without danger to faith. The treatise takes its text from Proverbs 8:24 (“…et ego iam concepta eram,” “and I was already conceived”).1

The debitum peccati: whether Mary was ever under any obligation to contract the stain

William Duba (Vivarium, 2000) is cited for Auriol’s analysis of the debitum peccati, the “debt” of original sin, as distinct from the preservation-argument he shares with Ware and Scotus. Where thirteenth-century masters had held that Mary, as a daughter of Adam, contracted the stain because “in Adam all sinned” (Romans 5:12), Auriol asked whether she was ever under the necessity or obligation of contracting it — treating original sin as an inherited legal liability from which a singular exemption could be granted.2

Notes

  • The treatise and the Repercussorium. Where Ware and Scotus argued the point within a single disputed question, Auriol devoted a whole treatise to it (the Tractatus) and then a second polemic (the Repercussorium editum contra adversarium innocentiae Matris Dei, “a rebuttal against the adversary of the innocence of the Mother of God”) to answering a Dominican critic. His question was printed alongside Ware’s and Scotus’s in the 1904 Quaracchi critical edition.
  • The fifth conclusion. Auriol states that the preservation “can be held without danger to faith” and that neither side is of the necessity of faith “until the Church shall have determined” what is to be held.
  • The 20 December 1314 date. The completion date is reported from the treatise’s colophon by its editors; it, the 1314 Toulouse disputation, and the early-1315 Repercussorium are reported in the Catholic Encyclopedia and the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy articles cited in note 1.
