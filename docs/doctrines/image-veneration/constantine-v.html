Constantine V and the Council of Hieria on the Veneration of Images

Dates: 718–775 (reigned 741–775) · Wikipedia
Summary: Leo III's son and the most theologically committed of the iconoclast emperors. He gathered the iconoclast Council of Hieria (754), which for the first time gave imperial iconoclasm a conciliar definition — condemning the making and veneration of icons of Christ and the saints as idolatry and calling the Eucharist the only true image of Christ. Hieria is the high-water mark of iconoclast doctrine and the “false council” the Second Council of Nicaea was convened to overturn.

Claims & evidence

The Council of Hieria (754) condemned the making of images

Constantine convened some 338 bishops at the palace of Hieria across the Bosphorus (Feb–Aug 754). With no patriarch of Rome, Alexandria, Antioch, or Jerusalem present, the council nonetheless styled itself ecumenical and issued a horos (definition) against images. It anathematized image-making itself:1

“…there shall be rejected and removed and cursed out of the Christian Church every likeness which is made out of any material…”

The Eucharist is the only admissible image of Christ

Hieria's Christological argument was that a painted icon of Christ either divides his two natures or confuses them; the only legitimate “image” of the incarnate Lord is the consecrated bread and wine:2

“The only admissible figure of the humanity of Christ, however, is bread and wine in the holy Supper.”

This exact reasoning was quoted and refuted, clause by clause, in the acts of Nicaea II thirty-three years later.

Notes

  • Hieria's claim to be the seventh ecumenical council was rejected by the Second Council of Nicaea (787), which took that title for itself and denounced Hieria as a robber-synod — largely because it lacked the assent of the five patriarchates.
  • Constantine's own polemic (the Peuseis, or “Inquiries”) survives only in fragments quoted by his opponent the patriarch Nikephoros; like his father Leo III, the iconoclast case reaches us mostly through the writers who defeated it.
  • The distinction between venerating an image and worshipping God — which Hieria denied was possible — is weighed on its own terms in the summary's Arguments section.
