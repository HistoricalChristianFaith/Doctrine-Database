Constantine V and the Council of Hieria on the Veneration of Images

Dates: 718–775 (reigned 741–775) · Wikipedia
Summary: Leo III's son, an iconoclast emperor who wrote on the question himself. He gathered the iconoclast Council of Hieria (754), which for the first time gave imperial iconoclasm a conciliar definition — condemning the making and veneration of icons of Christ and the saints as idolatry and calling the Eucharist the only true image of Christ. The Second Council of Nicaea (787) rejected its definition.

Claims & evidence

The Council of Hieria (754) condemned the making of images

Constantine convened some 338 bishops at the palace of Hieria across the Bosphorus (Feb–Aug 754). The council issued a horos (definition) against images. It anathematized image-making itself:1

“…there shall be rejected and removed and cursed out of the Christian Church every likeness which is made out of any material…”

The Eucharist is the only admissible image of Christ

Hieria's Christological argument was that a painted icon of Christ either divides his two natures or confuses them; the only legitimate “image” of the incarnate Lord is the consecrated bread and wine:2

“The only admissible figure of the humanity of Christ, however, is bread and wine in the holy Supper.”

This reasoning was quoted and answered, clause by clause, in the acts of Nicaea II thirty-three years later.

Reception and debate

  • The council styled itself ecumenical; the Second Council of Nicaea (787), which took that title for itself, rejected that claim, noting that no patriarch of Rome, Alexandria, Antioch or Jerusalem had taken part.1
  • Constantine's own polemic (the Peuseis, or “Inquiries”) survives only in fragments quoted by his opponent the patriarch Nikephoros; as with his father Leo III, the iconoclast case reaches us mostly through its opponents' writings.
  • Hieria rejected the distinction between venerating an image and worshipping God that the image's defenders drew; Hieria's own account of the early church is one of the readings set out at “Was the church of the first centuries aniconic, and is the veneration of images apostolic or later?”
