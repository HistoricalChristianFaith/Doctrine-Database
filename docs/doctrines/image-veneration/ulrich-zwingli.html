Ulrich Zwingli on the Veneration of Images

Dates: 1484–1531 · Wikipedia
Summary: The reformer of Zurich, who in June 1524 had the city's churches emptied of images, relics, crucifixes, and altars by order of the magistrates — the model of an orderly, authority-directed stripping, as against the mob iconoclasm Luther feared. His fullest treatment is An Answer to Valentin Compar (1525). Zwingli's line is Reformed but discriminating: images misused for worship are idols and must go, but images that serve no worship are lawful, even “God's gifts.”

Claims & evidence

Images misused for worship are idols; images not worshipped are God's gifts

Zwingli's target is not art but cult. The condemnation falls on images to which divine honour is paid; painting and sculpture as such he explicitly refuses to condemn.1

“Images… which are misused for worship… [are] diametrically opposed to the Word of God. But those which do not serve for worship and in whose cases there exists no danger of future worship, I am so far from condemning that I acknowledge both painting and statuary as God's gifts.”

— An Answer to Valentin Compar (1525).

Trust and worship are due to God alone

The root objection is that a worshipper who turns to the image turns from the Creator to the created — the very inversion the First Commandment forbids.2

“The believers, or those who are trusting, go to God alone; but the unbelievers go to the created.”

— An Answer to Valentin Compar (1525).

The orderly stripping of Zurich's churches (1524)

After the Zurich disputations on images and the mass, in June 1524 a deputation of church and civic authorities, with masons and carpenters, removed the pictures, relics, crucifixes, altars, and ornaments from the city's churches; the frescoes were effaced and the walls whitewashed, leaving the bare building for the congregation. Zwingli insisted the work be done by the magistrate in order, not by riot.3

Notes

  • Where he stands on the Protestant fork. Zwingli is squarely on the Reformed, image-stripping side — the whitewashed church is his legacy — against Luther's permissive adiaphora stance. But he is not a pure iconoclast: he permits images kept out of worship (even, elsewhere, a private portrait of Christ's humanity), so his ban is on the cult, not on picturing as such. His covenantal successor Calvin systematized the case in the Institutes.
  • Zwingli also appears on this wiki as a witness on the perpetual virginity of Mary (his 1522 sermon) and infant baptism — the same reformer who stripped the images defended Mary's perpetual virginity and paedobaptism.
