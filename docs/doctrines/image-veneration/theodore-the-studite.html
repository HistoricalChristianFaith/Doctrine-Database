Theodore the Studite on the Veneration of Images

Dates: 759–826 · Wikipedia
Summary: Abbot of the Stoudios monastery in Constantinople and the leading defender of the icons during the second iconoclasm (renewed by Emperor Leo V the Armenian in 815). Where John of Damascus had written from safety under Muslim rule, Theodore resisted from inside the empire — suffering exile and scourging — and sharpened the defence into a tight Christological argument: because Christ became truly man, he became circumscribable and so depictable, and to deny his icon is to deny his Incarnation.

Claims & evidence

The honour paid the image passes to its archetype

Theodore takes up the formula John of Damascus had fixed and presses it against the second wave of iconoclasm: the veneration shown an icon is not stopped at the wood and paint but travels to the person depicted, so it is no idolatry.1

“The honor paid to the image ascends to the archetype.”

— from his letter to the monk Naukratios (c. 818), written during his exile.

Because Christ appeared in the flesh he can be circumscribed — and so depicted

Theodore's distinctive contribution is the argument from circumscription: the divine nature as such cannot be bounded or pictured, but the Word made flesh took on a real, bounded human body — and whatever is circumscribed can be depicted. To say Christ cannot be portrayed is therefore to say he did not truly become man.2

“If he ‘appeared in the flesh,’ then he must necessarily also be circumscribed, because each is a concomitant and corresponding feature of the other.”

— letter to Naukratios (c. 818). The fuller case runs through his three Refutations (Antirrhetici tres adversus iconomachos), where he treats the rejection of icons as a Christological heresy: if Christ cannot be painted, he was not truly circumscribed, and so not truly incarnate.

Notes

  • Theodore's incarnational defence is a substantive argument, not merely a dated position; the strength of the “the Incarnation warrants picturing Christ” case is weighed on its own page, “Does the Incarnation warrant images of Christ?” (assessed plausible). This page fixes him on the timeline as the defender of the second iconoclasm.
  • Where he overshoots: Theodore's boldest form — that to deny the icon of Christ is to deny the Incarnation — overstates the case. The circumscription argument shows an image of the incarnate Christ may lawfully be made; it does not follow that refusing to make one denies the enfleshment, since a Christian may affirm the Incarnation fully and still, on other grounds, decline to picture it. The argument warrants the possibility of the icon, not the charge of heresy against everyone who abstains; the more modest form is the one that holds (see the argument page).
  • He stands between John of Damascus (the defender of the first iconoclasm) and the Triumph of Orthodoxy (843), which vindicated the cause he had suffered for — though he did not live to see it.
  • Standard English edition: St. Theodore the Studite: On the Holy Icons, trans. Catharine P. Roth (St. Vladimir's Seminary Press, 1981).
