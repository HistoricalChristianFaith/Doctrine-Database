Theodore the Studite on the Veneration of Images

Dates: 759–826 · Wikipedia
Summary: Abbot of the Stoudios monastery in Constantinople and a leading defender of the icons during the second iconoclasm (renewed by Emperor Leo V the Armenian in 815). John of Damascus wrote outside the empire, under Muslim rule; Theodore wrote inside it, suffering exile and scourging. Theodore set out a Christological argument: because Christ became truly man, he became circumscribable and so depictable, and to deny his icon is to deny his Incarnation.

Claims & evidence

The honour paid the image passes to its archetype

Theodore takes up the formula Basil had stated and John of Damascus applied, and presses it against the second wave of iconoclasm: the veneration shown an icon is not stopped at the wood and paint but travels to the person depicted, so it is no idolatry.1

“The honor paid to the image ascends to the archetype.”

— from his letter to the monk Naukratios (c. 818), written during his exile.

Because Christ appeared in the flesh he can be circumscribed — and so depicted

Theodore argues from circumscription: the divine nature as such cannot be bounded or pictured, but the Word made flesh took on a real, bounded human body — and whatever is circumscribed can be depicted. To say Christ cannot be portrayed is therefore to say he did not truly become man.2

“If he ‘appeared in the flesh,’ then he must necessarily also be circumscribed, because each is a concomitant and corresponding feature of the other.”

— letter to Naukratios (c. 818). The fuller case runs through his three Refutations (Antirrhetici tres adversus iconomachos), where he treats the rejection of icons as a Christological heresy: if Christ cannot be painted, he was not truly circumscribed, and so not truly incarnate.

Reception and debate

  • This page places Theodore on the timeline as a defender of the icons in the second iconoclasm; his argument that the Incarnation warrants picturing Christ is recorded above in his own words.
  • On the timeline he stands between John of Damascus (who wrote during the first iconoclasm) and the Triumph of Orthodoxy (843), which restored the icons after his death (826).
  • Standard English edition: St. Theodore the Studite: On the Holy Icons, trans. Catharine P. Roth (St. Vladimir's Seminary Press, 1981).
