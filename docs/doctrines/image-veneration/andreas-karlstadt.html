Andreas Karlstadt on the Veneration of Images

Dates: c. 1486–1541 · Wikipedia
Summary: Luther's senior colleague at Wittenberg and the author of the first Protestant tract against religious images, On the Abolition of Images (Von Abtuung der Bilder, Wittenberg, 1522). While Luther was in hiding at the Wartburg, Karlstadt's preaching helped touch off the Wittenberg image-smashing of early 1522. His case is the radical Reformed one: to keep images in churches at all is to break the First Commandment, so they must be removed.

Claims & evidence

To have images in churches breaks the First Commandment

Karlstadt's opening thesis is not that images are misused but that their very presence in a place of worship is forbidden. Where the medieval catechisms had treated the ban on graven images (Ex 20:4–5) as an Old-Testament rule for the Jews, Karlstadt — like Zwingli and Calvin after him — held it binding on Christians still.1

“That we have images in churches and houses of God is wrong and contrary to the first commandment.”

— On the Abolition of Images (1522), opening argument.

God hates images and counts them an abomination

He presses the point to its sharpest edge: images are not neutral matter but objects God abhors, so that those who love them become abominable with them.2

“God hates and is jealous of pictures, as I will demonstrate, and considers them an abomination.”

— On the Abolition of Images (1522).

Therefore they must be removed

The conclusion is practical and urgent: not reform of a cult but the clearing-out of the images themselves. Karlstadt invoked the Old-Testament kings who tore down the high places, and looked to the magistrate to do the same.3

“Therefore it is good, necessary, praiseworthy, and pious that we remove them and give Scripture its due.”

— On the Abolition of Images (1522).

Notes

  • The intra-Protestant split starts here. Karlstadt's tract and the disorder it fed provoked Luther's return from the Wartburg and his Invocavit sermons (March 1522), and later his Against the Heavenly Prophets (1525). Luther held images to be adiaphora — a thing indifferent, to be cleansed from the heart by preaching, not torn from the walls by force — and treated Karlstadt's compulsion as a new legalism. So the Protestant side divided at the outset into a permissive Lutheran wing and the image-stripping Reformed wing that runs Karlstadt → Zwingli → Calvin.
  • Whether the Decalogue in fact forbids all religious images, or only idols of false gods, is weighed on its own page, “Does the Second Commandment forbid all religious images?” (assessed contested) — Karlstadt's is the anchoring form of that objection.
  • Standard English translation: A Reformation Debate: Karlstadt, Emser, and Eck on Sacred Images. Three Treatises in Translation, trans. Bryan D. Mangrum and Giuseppe Scavizzi (Toronto: CRRS, 1991).
