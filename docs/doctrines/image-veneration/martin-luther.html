Martin Luther on the Veneration of Images

Dates: 1483–1546 · Wikipedia
Summary: On images Luther broke sharply with the Reformed line. Returning from the Wartburg to a Wittenberg convulsed by Karlstadt's iconoclasm, he preached the Invocavit sermons (March 1522) and later wrote Against the Heavenly Prophets (1525) to argue that images are adiaphora — things neither commanded nor forbidden. Only an image set up as a god is banned; a crucifix or a saint's image may lawfully be kept, and the way to be rid of idolatry is to teach the heart, not to smash the walls. This is the fork that splits Protestantism into a permissive Lutheran wing and an image-stripping Reformed one.

Claims & evidence

Only an image that one worships is forbidden — not the crucifix as such

Against Karlstadt's blanket reading of the First Commandment, Luther distinguished the making of an image from the worshipping of it. The Mosaic ban, he held, falls only on an image treated as a god.1

“I will first discuss images according to the Law of Moses, and then according to the gospel. And I say at the outset that according to the Law of Moses no other images are forbidden than an image of God which one worships. A crucifix, on the other hand, or any other holy image is not forbidden.”

— Against the Heavenly Prophets in the Matter of Images and Sacraments (1525), AE 40:85–86.

Tear images out of the heart by the Word — not off the walls by force

Luther's quarrel with Karlstadt was as much about method as doctrine: physical smashing leaves the idol standing in the heart, whereas preaching that images are nothing before God removes the idolatry at its root and lets the images fall of themselves.2

“I approached the task of destroying images by first tearing them out of the heart through God's Word and making them worthless and despised… But Dr. Karlstadt, who pays no attention to matters of the heart, has reversed the order by removing them from sight and leaving them in the heart.”

— Against the Heavenly Prophets (1525), AE 40:85.

Images for memorial and witness are to be tolerated

He did not defend images as good or necessary — only as permitted, so that a free conscience is not bound by a “mischievous law” forbidding what God has not forbidden.3

“Now I say this to keep the conscience free from mischievous laws and fictitious sins, and not because I would defend images… But images for memorial and witness, such as crucifixes and images of saints, are to be tolerated.”

— Against the Heavenly Prophets (1525), AE 40:91.

Notes

  • The Protestant split. Luther's adiaphora stance kept crucifixes, altarpieces, and saints' images in Lutheran churches, where the Reformed — Karlstadt, Zwingli, and Calvin — stripped and whitewashed theirs. Luther's is not a veneration theology: he still condemned trusting or worshipping images, and his defence licenses having them, not the “honour passing to the prototype” of the Eastern icon-cult. It is a third position — images lawful but indifferent — nearer to Gregory the Great's didactic line than to Nicaea II.
  • Whether the Decalogue forbids all religious images (Karlstadt's premise) or only idols of false gods (Luther's reading) is weighed at “Does the Second Commandment forbid all religious images?” (assessed contested).
  • Texts: the Invocavit sermons of March 1522 (AE 51:69–100) and Against the Heavenly Prophets (AE 40), Luther's fullest treatment, written against Karlstadt.
