Leo III the Isaurian on the Veneration of Images

Dates: c. 685–741 (reigned 717–741) · Wikipedia
Summary: The emperor who opened the first Byzantine iconoclasm. Between c. 726 and 730 he moved against the public veneration of images — by tradition removing the image of Christ over the Chalkē (Bronze) Gate of the imperial palace and, c. 730, issuing an edict against images that forced the resignation of the iconophile patriarch Germanus I. He is the archetype of the imperial iconoclast the later councils condemned.

Claims & evidence

He removed the image of Christ from the Chalkē Gate (c. 726)

The classic account, told by the iconophile chronicler Theophanes the Confessor, dates the opening of iconoclasm to Leo's reign: after the eruption of the Aegean volcano at Thera (726), which Leo is said to have read as a divine judgement on image-worship, he ordered the celebrated icon of Christ over the palace's ceremonial Chalkē Gate taken down and replaced with a cross — and a band of image-defenders killed the officer sent to do it.1

The event is genuinely debated. Our narrative sources for it were written decades later by the icon party, and some modern scholars (notably Marie-France Auzépy) have argued that the Chalkē-Gate episode may be partly later iconophile construction rather than plain fact — a caution worth keeping, since the story became the founding legend of the whole struggle.2

He issued an edict against images (c. 730) and deposed the patriarch Germanus

Around 730 Leo moved from a personal to an imperial policy, promulgating an edict against the images. The patriarch Germanus I, who defended them, refused to comply and resigned (or was deposed), replaced by the compliant Anastasius. From the West, Pope Gregory II resisted the imperial policy. Leo left no theological treatise of his own; his iconoclasm is known through its acts and through the hostile record of the writers who outlived it.3

Notes

  • Leo's son Constantine V carried the policy to its dogmatic peak at the iconoclast Council of Hieria (754); both were condemned by the Second Council of Nicaea (787).
  • What Leo actually objected to — images as such, or the veneration paid them — is part of the substantive dispute; the theology is weighed in the summary's Arguments section, not here. This page fixes the who-and-when.
