The Council of Trent on the Veneration of Images

Dates: Session XXV, 3–4 December 1563 · Wikipedia
Summary: The Catholic reply to the Reformation's iconoclasm. In its last session the Council of Trent issued the decree “On the Invocation, Veneration, and Relics of Saints, and on Sacred Images,” which reaffirmed the veneration of images against the Reformers, grounded it expressly in the Second Council of Nicaea and the “prototype” principle, and at the same time ordered the removal of the abuses and superstitions the Protestants had denounced.

Claims & evidence

Images are to be kept in churches and given due honour

Trent restates the Latin practice as binding: images of Christ, the Virgin, and the saints are to be retained in churches and honoured — while denying that any power resides in the images themselves.1

“The images of Christ, of the Virgin Mother of God, and of the other saints, are to be had and retained particularly in temples, and… due honour and veneration are to be given them; not that any divinity, or virtue, is believed to be in them, on account of which they are to be worshipped.”

— Session XXV (1563), decree on sacred images.

The honour passes to the prototype — as Nicaea II defined

The decree adopts the classic veneration theology in almost the words of the East: the reverence shown the image travels to the person it depicts, so the kissing and prostration before an icon terminate not in the wood but in Christ and the saints. It names the Second Council of Nicaea (787) as its authority.2

“The honour which is shown them is referred to the prototypes which those images represent; in such wise that by the images which we kiss, and before which we uncover the head, and prostrate ourselves, we adore Christ; and we venerate the saints, whose similitude they bear: as, by the decrees of Councils, and especially of the second Synod of Nicaea, has been defined against the opponents of images.”

— Session XXV (1563).

Abuses and superstition are to be removed

Trent conceded ground to the reformers' complaint about the popular cult: bishops were charged to purge every superstition, mercenary abuse, and unseemly image from the practice of veneration.3

“All superstition shall be removed, all filthy lucre be abolished; finally, all lasciviousness be avoided…” — and no images “(suggestive) of false doctrine, and furnishing occasion of dangerous error to the uneducated, be set up.”

— Session XXV (1563).

Notes

  • The Catholic bookend of the 16th-century collision. Where Karlstadt, Zwingli, and Calvin read the Second Commandment as forbidding the practice outright, Trent re-canonized Nicaea II for the Latin West and drew the dulia/latria line the Reformers denied — while ceding that the abuses they attacked were real and must be reformed. The dogmatic gulf between the confessions on this point dates from here.
  • Whether the “honour passing to the prototype” is a real distinction or a cover for idolatry is weighed at “Is the veneration–worship (dulia/latria) distinction a real difference?” (assessed contested).
  • The same session's decrees also treat the invocation of the saints and the veneration of relics — see the invocation of the saints, for which this decree is likewise a charter.
