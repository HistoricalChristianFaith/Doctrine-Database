The Libri Carolini and the Synod of Frankfurt (794)

Dates: c. 790–794 · Wikipedia
Summary: The Latin West's formal rejection of the veneration decree of the Second Council of Nicaea (787). When a garbled Latin translation of Nicaea's acts reached Charlemagne's court, his theologians answered with the Opus Caroli regis contra synodum — the Libri Carolini (c. 790–793, drafted largely by Theodulf of Orléans) — rejecting both the iconoclast Council of Hieria (754) and Nicaea II, and holding images to be for memory and adornment, neither to be destroyed nor adored. The Synod of Frankfurt (794), under Charlemagne, then condemned the “Greek synod” outright. The rejection rested in part on a real mistranslation — the Greek distinction between proskynēsis (reverence) and latreia (worship) collapsed into a single Latin adoratio — but it also expressed a settled Frankish preference for Gregory the Great's moderate, didactic line over Byzantine veneration theology. This is a Western rejection of the Eastern settlement, not a minor footnote to it.

Claims & evidence

The Libri Carolini reject both Hieria and Nicaea II — the Frankish middle line

The four-book Opus Caroli regis contra synodum was composed on Charlemagne's command, in his voice, to refute the acts of Nicaea II; the scholarly consensus, established through the work of Ann Freeman, assigns its drafting chiefly to Theodulf of Orléans. It condemns both Byzantine extremes at once: against the iconoclasts of Hieria (754) it insists images need not be destroyed, and against Nicaea II it denies that they are to be venerated. Images belong in churches as ornament and as an aid to memory and instruction — but the honour the Greeks assigned them, the Franks held, verges on idolatry. This is essentially the position Gregory the Great had stated to Serenus of Marseilles two centuries earlier: images for teaching, adoration forbidden. Its impact in its own day was limited — the work circulated little and then sank into obscurity until the Reformation, when Protestant controversialists rediscovered it as evidence that the medieval West had not received Nicaea II's icon-theology.1

The mistranslation: proskynēsis and latreia collapsed into adoratio

The Latin copy of the Nicene acts that reached the Frankish court was badly made. The Roman cleric charged with the translation worked word by word from Greek–Latin glossaries, and rendered the two distinct Greek terms — proskynēsis (the relative reverence Nicaea assigned to images) and latreia (the worship due to God alone) — indiscriminately by the single Latin word adoratio. The distinction on which the whole Greek settlement turned thus vanished in translation, and the Franks read the council as decreeing that images were to be worshipped. The most notorious instance flipped a bishop's meaning into its opposite: where the Greek bishop Constantine of Constantia (Cyprus) had said images were not to be given the service due the Trinity, the Latin made him profess the reverse:2

“I receive and embrace honourably the holy and most venerable images according to the service of adoration which I pay to the consubstantial and life-giving Trinity.”

The same philological instability — that the Greek and Latin vocabularies of honour and worship do not map cleanly onto each other — is exactly what the veneration-vs-worship (dulia/latria) distinction has to carry, and here it broke down at the level of the translator's desk.

The Synod of Frankfurt (794) condemns the “Greek synod”

Charlemagne gathered a large Western synod at Frankfurt in 794 — chiefly to condemn the Spanish Adoptionist heresy, but its second canon took up the images question. On the strength of the faulty translation, the assembled bishops rejected what they took to be Nicaea's teaching, that images were to be given the service and adoration due to God:3

“The question was brought forward concerning the recent synod which the Greeks had held at Constantinople concerning the adoration of images, that all should be judged as worthy of anathema who did not pay to the images of the Saints service and adoration as to the Divine Trinity… Our most holy fathers rejected with scorn and in every way such adoration and service, and unanimously condemned it.”

The canon garbles its target — it locates the “recent synod” at Constantinople (where the iconoclast council of 754 had met) rather than at Nicæa, and it ascribes to Nicaea II a decree no council in fact issued: as Schaff notes, “neither of these synods, nor any other synod, decreed that the ‘service’ (latreia) and ‘adoration’ (proskynēsis) due to the holy Trinity was under pain of anathema to be given to ‘the images of the Saints.’” Frankfurt was thus condemning a position Nicaea II had not actually held — but the underlying Frankish conviction, that images are for use and not for worship, was sincerely and independently its own.4

Notes

  • Mistranslation, or pretext? The honest reading is that the bad translation was a genuine cause, not a mere excuse. The Franks were not fabricating an objection: the text they held really did make the Greeks appear to command the worship of images, and their alarm followed from what they read. But the mistranslation is not the whole story — the Carolingian court had a standing, Gregory-shaped view of images as didactic ornament, and would very likely have balked at the developed Eastern veneration theology even had the translation been faithful. Recent scholarship (Foden, 2024) shows that the meaning of adoratio was itself contested among Charlemagne's own advisers, so the dispute was as much about competing Latin theologies of honour as about a translator's slip.
  • This episode is why Gregory the Great's letters to Serenus, and the “books of the illiterate” rationale, matter for more than the sixth century: the Frankish West carried Gregory's third position — images for teaching, adoration barred — forward against the Byzantine settlement, so the gap between Rome's historic rationale and Nicaea II's veneration dogma is a real feature of the record.
  • Pope Hadrian I, who had approved Nicaea II, defended the council against the earlier Frankish tract (the Capitulare adversus synodum) and did not accept the Frankish reading; Rome and the Franks were not of one mind here. The Latin West received Byzantine icon-theology slowly, and never wholly on Byzantine terms.
