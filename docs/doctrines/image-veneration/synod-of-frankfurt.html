The Libri Carolini and the Synod of Frankfurt (794)

Dates: c. 790–794 · Wikipedia
Summary: The Frankish response to the veneration decree of the Second Council of Nicaea (787). When a Latin translation of Nicaea's acts reached Charlemagne's court, his theologians answered with the Opus Caroli regis contra synodum — the Libri Carolini (c. 790–793, drafted largely by Theodulf of Orléans) — rejecting both the iconoclast Council of Hieria (754) and Nicaea II, and holding images to be for memory and adornment, neither to be destroyed nor adored. The Synod of Frankfurt (794), under Charlemagne, then condemned the “Greek synod.” The translation rendered both proskynēsis (reverence) and latreia (worship) by the single Latin adoratio; how much of the Frankish rejection rested on that, and how much on the Franks' own view of images, is read differently by the historians cited below.

Claims & evidence

The Libri Carolini reject both Hieria and Nicaea II

The four-book Opus Caroli regis contra synodum was composed on Charlemagne's command, in his voice, to refute the acts of Nicaea II; its editor Ann Freeman assigns its drafting chiefly to Theodulf of Orléans, the “consensus opinion” according to Peter Brooke (2017), after a long ascription to Alcuin. It condemns both Byzantine councils at once: against the iconoclasts of Hieria (754) it insists images need not be destroyed, and against Nicaea II it denies that they are to be venerated. Images belong in churches as ornament and as an aid to memory and instruction — but the honour the Greeks assigned them, the Franks held, verges on idolatry. The work cites Gregory the Great's letter to Serenus of Marseilles, and Francesco Stella (2022) reads it as developing “the mediating and realistic solutions of Gregory the Great.” The work circulated little in its own day ⚠ unverified; it was first printed in 1549, edited by Jean du Tillet, whom Schaff describes as “suspected of Calvinism.”1

The translation: proskynēsis and latreia rendered by adoratio

The Latin copy of the Nicene acts that reached the Frankish court rendered the two Greek terms — proskynēsis (the relative reverence Nicaea assigned to images) and latreia (the worship due to God alone) — by the single Latin word adoratio; the translator is said to have worked word by word from Greek–Latin glossaries ⚠ unverified. Schaff records that the papal librarian Anastasius later “charged the translator with ignorance of both Greek and Latin,” and that Anastasius's own improved translation also rendered proskynēsis by adoratio. Peter Brooke (Orthodox Arts Journal, 2017) gives the instance of the bishop Constantine of Constantia (Cyprus): where the Greek had him say that images were not to be given the service due the Trinity, the Latin made him profess the reverse:2

“I receive and embrace honourably the holy and most venerable images according to the service of adoration which I pay to the consubstantial and life-giving Trinity.”

The Synod of Frankfurt (794) condemns the “Greek synod”

Charlemagne gathered a large Western synod at Frankfurt in 794, chiefly to condemn the Spanish Adoptionists; its second canon took up the images question. The assembled bishops rejected what they took to be Nicaea's teaching, that images were to be given the service and adoration due to God:3

“The question was brought forward concerning the recent synod which the Greeks had held at Constantinople concerning the adoration of images, that all should be judged as worthy of anathema who did not pay to the images of the Saints service and adoration as to the Divine Trinity… Our most holy fathers rejected with scorn and in every way such adoration and service, and unanimously condemned it.”

Schaff reads the canon’s “at Constantinople” as confusing the iconoclast council of 754 with Nicaea II. The acts of Nicaea II include a closing “eighth session” held at the Magnaura Palace in Constantinople (23 October 787), a session Erich Lamberz, as reported by the article “Second Council of Nicaea,” judges a late ninth-century forgery ⚠ unverified. Schaff also writes that “neither of these synods, nor any other synod, decreed that the ‘service’ (latreia) and ‘adoration’ (proskynēsis) due to the holy Trinity was under pain of anathema to be given to ‘the images of the Saints.’”4

Reception and debate

  • Mistranslation and the Frankish view of images. Baronius, Bellarmine and Hefele, as Schaff reports, charged the synod with misrepresenting Nicaea, which “sanctioned the worship (in a wider sense), but not the adoration, of images”; Thomas Shahan (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1908) writes that the bishops at Frankfurt “on the above-described erroneous supposition rejected (can. 2) the Second Nicene Council,” the supposition being a Latin version in which “the council was made to assert that the images were to be adored as the Trinity itself”; Schaff answers that the Latin versions gave “double sanction to the confusion.” Brooke writes that, taking Frankfurt alone, “we might say that the quarrel with the Council of Nicaea was based on a misunderstanding,” but that “there is much more to it than that,” pointing to the Frankish court's earlier Capitulare adversus synodum and the Opus Caroli. Foden (2024) argues that the meaning of adoratio was itself contested among Charlemagne's own advisers.2,4,1
  • The Libri Carolini cite Gregory the Great's letter to Serenus, and how far the Frankish position follows Gregory's is part of the dispute set out at “Do Gregory the Great’s letters to Serenus (599, 600) support the veneration of images?”
  • Pope Hadrian I, who had approved Nicaea II, answered the earlier Frankish tract (the Capitulare adversus synodum) with a Responsum defending the council and did not accept the Frankish reading; Hadrian I and Charlemagne’s court differed on the council.1
