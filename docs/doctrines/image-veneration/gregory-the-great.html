Gregory the Great on Images and the “Books of the Illiterate”

Dates: c. 540–604 (pope 590–604) · Wikipedia
Summary: A Western statement on Christian images, from two letters (599 and 600) to Serenus, bishop of Marseilles, who had smashed the images in his church because he found people adoring them. Gregory blames the destruction and praises the ban on adoration: images may be kept — indeed should be kept — because “what writing presents to readers, this a picture presents to the unlearned who behold”; but the people must be forbidden to adore them. Whether the letters support the veneration of images is disputed.

Claims & evidence

Images are “the books of the illiterate” — keep them for teaching (599)

Serenus had heard of people adoring the images in his church and responded by breaking and throwing them down. Gregory's first letter (Book 9, Epistle CV, dated 599) both commends and corrects him: the zeal against adoration is right, the destruction is wrong, because the images teach the unlettered.

“And we commend you indeed for your zeal against anything made with hands being an object of adoration; but we signify to you that you ought not to have broken these images. For pictorial representation is made use of in Churches for this reason; that such as are ignorant of letters may at least read by looking at the walls what they cannot read in books. Your Fraternity therefore should have both preserved the images and prohibited the people from adoration of them, to the end that both those who are ignorant of letters might have wherewith to gather a knowledge of the history, and that the people might by no means sin by adoration of a pictorial representation.”

The letter makes two points: images are useful as instruction for the illiterate, and they are not to be adored. Gregory blames Serenus for the smashing, not for the ban on adoration.1

“To adore a picture is one thing, to learn from it what is to be adored is another” (600)

Serenus, apparently doubting the letter was genuine, did not comply. Gregory's second, longer letter (Book 11, Epistle X, dated 600) repeats the ruling and gives its most-quoted formula. The picture is a substitute for reading, not itself an object of worship:

“For to adore a picture is one thing, but to learn through the story of a picture what is to be adored is another. For what writing presents to readers, this a picture presents to the unlearned who behold, since in it even the ignorant see what they ought to follow; in it the illiterate read. Hence, and chiefly to the nations, a picture is instead of reading.”

He tells Serenus how to reconcile his flock: gather them, and show “by testimonies of sacred Scripture that it is not lawful for anything made with hands to be adored, since it is written, Thou shalt adore the Lord thy God, and him only shalt thou serve” (Luke 4:8).2

Making images permitted, adoration forbidden; the image as a prompt to compunction

Gregory's rule for the future:

“And if any one should wish to make images, by no means prohibit him, but by all means forbid the adoration of images. But let thy Fraternity carefully admonish them that from the sight of the event portrayed they should catch the ardour of compunction, and bow themselves down in adoration of the One Almighty Holy Trinity.”

The picture is to move the viewer to compunction and to the adoration of the Trinity.3

Reception and debate

  • Whether Gregory's letters reach the veneration of images — the bowing, kissing, lights, and incense defined by the Second Council of Nicaea (787) — is disputed; the readings, with their proponents, are set out at “Do Gregory the Great’s letters to Serenus (599, 600) support the veneration of images?”
  • When a Latin translation of Nicaea II's acts reached Charlemagne's court, the Frankish Libri Carolini cited the letter to Serenus against the council, holding images to be for adornment and memory, neither to be adored nor to be destroyed, and the Synod of Frankfurt (794) rejected the council. Pope Hadrian I, defending Nicaea, asserted that his teaching on images was the same as Gregory's.4
