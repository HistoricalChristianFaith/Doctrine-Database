Gregory the Great on Images and the “Books of the Illiterate”

Dates: c. 540–604 (pope 590–604) · Wikipedia
Summary: The classic Western statement on Christian images, from two letters (599 and 600) to Serenus, bishop of Marseilles, who had smashed the images in his church because he found people adoring them. Gregory takes a careful middle line that would define the Latin position for centuries: images may be kept — indeed should be kept — because “what writing presents to readers, this a picture presents to the unlearned who behold”; but the people must be forbidden to adore them. It is a didactic, utilitarian defence of images, and it is notably narrower than the veneration theology the Greek East was forging at the same time.

Claims & evidence

Images are “the books of the illiterate” — keep them for teaching (599)

Serenus had heard of people adoring the images in his church and responded by breaking and throwing them down. Gregory's first letter (Book 9, Epistle CV, dated 599) both commends and corrects him: the zeal against adoration is right, the destruction is wrong, because the images teach the unlettered.

“And we commend you indeed for your zeal against anything made with hands being an object of adoration; but we signify to you that you ought not to have broken these images. For pictorial representation is made use of in Churches for this reason; that such as are ignorant of letters may at least read by looking at the walls what they cannot read in books. Your Fraternity therefore should have both preserved the images and prohibited the people from adoration of them, to the end that both those who are ignorant of letters might have wherewith to gather a knowledge of the history, and that the people might by no means sin by adoration of a pictorial representation.”

Two commitments are already fixed here and never move: images are useful (as instruction for the illiterate) and images are not to be adored. Gregory blames Serenus only for the smashing, not for the ban on adoration — that ban he shares.1

“To adore a picture is one thing, to learn from it what is to be adored is another” (600)

Serenus, apparently doubting the letter was genuine, did not comply. Gregory's second, longer and sharper letter (Book 11, Epistle X, dated 600) restates the whole position and gives it its most-quoted formula. The picture is a substitute for reading, not itself an object of worship:

“For to adore a picture is one thing, but to learn through the story of a picture what is to be adored is another. For what writing presents to readers, this a picture presents to the unlearned who behold, since in it even the ignorant see what they ought to follow; in it the illiterate read. Hence, and chiefly to the nations, a picture is instead of reading.”

He tells Serenus how to reconcile his flock: gather them, and show “by testimonies of sacred Scripture that it is not lawful for anything made with hands to be adored, since it is written, Thou shalt adore the Lord thy God, and him only shalt thou serve” (Luke 4:8). The picture stays; the adoration goes.2

Make them freely, adore them never — the image as prompt to worship God

Gregory's rule for the future is stated flatly, and it draws the line exactly at adoration:

“And if any one should wish to make images, by no means prohibit him, but by all means forbid the adoration of images. But let thy Fraternity carefully admonish them that from the sight of the event portrayed they should catch the ardour of compunction, and bow themselves down in adoration of the One Almighty Holy Trinity.”

This is as far as Gregory goes toward devotion: the picture may move the viewer to compunction and prompt the adoration of God — but the adoration terminates on the Trinity, never on the image. Nowhere does he say the honour paid an image “passes to the prototype,” and nowhere does he license bowing to, kissing, or censing the image itself. Making is free; adoring is forbidden.3

Notes

  • Gregory's position is didactic, not the Eastern veneration theology. He is often cited as a patristic defender of images, and for the lawful making and keeping of images that is right; but whether his rationale reaches the cult of the icon — the bowing, kissing, lights, and incense that the Second Council of Nicaea (787) would dogmatize — is weighed on its own page: “Does the ‘books of the illiterate’ defence support the veneration of images?”
  • The gap was felt at the time. When Charlemagne's court received a garbled Latin translation of Nicaea II, the Frankish Libri Carolini (c. 790–793) and the Synod of Frankfurt (794) rejected the Greek veneration decree and re-asserted essentially Gregory's line: images are for adornment and memory, are neither to be adored nor to be destroyed. The Latin West received Byzantine icon-theology slowly and never wholly on Byzantine terms.4
