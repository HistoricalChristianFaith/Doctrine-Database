Does the Incarnation warrant images of Christ?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: plausible — as a defeater of the iconoclast Christological dilemma the argument is strong and, within a Chalcedonian frame, essentially sound: because the Word truly took circumscribable flesh, an image of the incarnate Christ neither confounds nor divides His natures. As a positive license to depict Christ it is coherent and well-grounded — the invisible God has, uniquely, shown a visible form — but short of cross-traditional consensus, and it is narrower than the doctrine it is marshaled for: on its own logic it warrants images of Christ, not of the Father, and it does not by itself reach the veneration cult (that turns on the latria/dulia distinction weighed separately). · classic proponents: John of Damascus, Theodore the Studite.

The claim

“Of old, God the incorporeal and uncircumscribed was never depicted. Now, however, when God is seen clothed in flesh, and conversing with men, I make an image of the God whom I see. I do not worship matter, I worship the God of matter, who became matter for my sake, and deigned to inhabit matter, who worked out my salvation through matter.”

— John of Damascus, First Apology Against Those Who Attack the Divine Images, Fordham Medieval Sourcebook. The argument is that the Incarnation changes what may be pictured. The Old-Covenant ban on images of God stood because God had shown “no form” and was “invisible, incorporeal, uncircumscribed, formless”; but “after God… became in truth man… and dwelt among men,” He acquired a visible, circumscribable human existence, and so may now be depicted. To forbid the image of the incarnate Christ, on this reading, is to deny that the Incarnation was real.1

Who advances it

This is the central pro-image theological argument, distinct from the historical and Second-Commandment disputes. Its classic author is John of Damascus (c. 675–749), whose three Treatises on the Divine Images answered the first Byzantine iconoclasm, and who fixed the point again in his Exposition of the Orthodox Faith (IV.16, “Concerning Images”). In the second iconoclasm Theodore the Studite (759–826) sharpened it into a precise Christological form: because Christ truly became man He became circumscribable (able to be delimited, located, seen), and whatever is circumscribed can be depicted — so the icon of Christ is not merely permitted but is entailed by the reality of the Incarnation. The Second Council of Nicaea (787) made the reasoning conciliar, and its logic is why the Eastern church treats iconoclasm as a Christological error, a covert denial of the enfleshment.2

The evidence for

Scripture calls the incarnate Son the visible image of the invisible God. The argument is not a bare inference; it rests on the New Testament's own language. “The Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us, and we beheld his glory” (John 1:14). Christ “is the image (eikōn) of the invisible God” (Col 1:15). And to Philip's request to be shown the Father, Jesus answers, “he that hath seen me hath seen the Father” (John 14:9). The very God whom Exodus says no one can see and live (Ex 33:20) has, in the Son, made Himself seeable. If God has given Himself a visible human face, a likeness of that face is a likeness of something real, not a fiction.3

The Old-Covenant ban had a stated reason, and the Incarnation meets it. The prohibition's strongest form (Deut 4:15–16) is grounded expressly on the fact that at Horeb Israel “saw no manner of similitude” — there was no form to copy, so any image of God would be a falsehood. The Damascene grants this exactly: “who can make an imitation of the invisible, incorporeal, uncircumscribed, formless God?… in the Old Testament the use of images was not common.” His claim is that the ground of the ban has been removed for the one case of the incarnate Son: the reason not to depict God was that He had shown no form; He has now shown one. The argument therefore does not overturn Deut 4 — it accepts its logic and locates the exception the Incarnation creates.4

It answers the iconoclast dilemma without heresy. The strongest attack on images of Christ is a Christological trap, pressed by Constantine V and the Council of Hieria (754): an image of Christ either (a) tries to circumscribe His divine nature — impossible and blasphemous — or (b) depicts only His humanity apart from His divinity, which severs the natures and lapses into Nestorianism; either horn is heresy, so no true image of Christ is possible. The Damascene–Studite reply is that the icon circumscribes neither nature but the person (hypostasis): what is depicted is the one Christ in His visible human existence, and by the union that person is God the Word, without the picture claiming to bound the divinity. Circumscribing the humanity no more divides the natures than the Incarnation itself does; refusing it, by contrast, implies the flesh was not truly assumed. Turned back on the iconoclast, the dilemma becomes an argument for the icon.5

Adversarial assessment

What holds. As a reply to the iconoclast Christological objection the argument is very strong, and within the two-natures framework both sides share it is essentially decisive: the image terminates on the person, who is circumscribable in His assumed humanity, so it neither bounds the Godhead nor divides Christ. The scriptural anchor is real — the New Testament itself calls the incarnate Son the image of the invisible God and has Him say that to see Him is to see the Father — and the appeal to Deut 4 is handled honestly rather than evaded: the argument concedes the premise (God showed no form) and claims the Incarnation is precisely the event that supplies one. Where the Second-Commandment objection is at its sharpest — images of God, forbidden because He is formless — the incarnation argument meets it head-on rather than dodging it.

Where it is narrower than the doctrine it serves. Three limits keep the argument from proving as much as it is often made to carry. First, scope. On its own logic it licenses images of Christ, the one divine person who took visible flesh — not of the Father, who did not, and whom later Christian art nonetheless depicted (the aged “Ancient of Days,” the hand from the cloud). The Damascene himself does not rest images of the saints on the Incarnation but on a different footing (honour to “fellow-servants,” men made after God's image); so the incarnation argument, taken strictly, supports only a sub-set of the images the doctrine defends, and a depiction of God the Father remains exposed to the Deut-4 objection the incarnation reply cannot cover. Second, from licit to venerated is a further step. That an image of Christ may lawfully be made does not by itself establish that it may be bowed to, kissed, and censed; the Incarnation answers “may we depict Him?” but the cult of the icon turns on whether that homage is idolatry or a relative honour passing to the prototype — a question this argument leaves to the latria/dulia distinction. Third, permission versus obligation. Even granting that the Incarnation makes an image of Christ possible and fitting, it does not follow that refusing to make one denies the Incarnation; a Christian may affirm the enfleshment fully and still, on other grounds (Deut 4, prudential fear of idolatry), decline to picture it. The Studite's stronger claim — that to reject the icon is to reject the Incarnation — is rhetorically powerful but overshoots: it treats a fitting expression of a doctrine as if it were the doctrine itself.6

Where the real dispute lies. Once the caricatures fall away, the two sides agree on more than they admit. The defenders concede the invisible God cannot be depicted; the Reformed concede the humanity of Christ was real and visible. The live disagreement is whether the visibility of the assumed humanity licenses a cultic image at all, given that the same person is God and that the Decalogue's concern is precisely with images given religious homage. That is not settled by the fact of the Incarnation alone; it depends on the further judgement about veneration. So the incarnation argument is best read as doing one job well — clearing the Christological objection and grounding the possibility of an image of Christ — while the weight of the veneration controversy rests on the separate question it does not itself decide.

Assessment

Plausible. The incarnation defense is the strongest theological card the icon party holds, and it earns its place: as a defeater of the iconoclast dilemma it is, within the shared Chalcedonian framework, close to sound — the image circumscribes the person, not a nature, so it implies neither a bounded Godhead nor a divided Christ; and it rests on genuine New Testament data (Christ as the eikōn of the invisible God, the Father seen in the Son) rather than on inference alone. What keeps it at plausible rather than sound as a defense of the doctrine is that it proves less than the doctrine claims. It warrants images of Christ, not of the Father; it establishes that such an image may be made, not yet that it may be venerated; and it does not license the sweeping equation of iconoclasm with Christological heresy that its boldest proponents drew. The honest verdict is that the argument decisively wins the narrow contest it was framed for — may the incarnate Christ be depicted? — and hands the broader contest, the cult of the icon, on to the veneration question weighed separately. Theodore the Studite's overreach (that rejecting the icon is rejecting the Incarnation) is flagged on his detail page; the more modest form of the same argument is the one that survives.
