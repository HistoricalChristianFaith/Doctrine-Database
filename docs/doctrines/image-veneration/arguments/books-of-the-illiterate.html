Do Gregory the Great’s letters to Serenus (599, 600) support the veneration of images?

Disputed reading · on The Veneration of Images and Icons · witness: Gregory the Great

Related readings: Was the church of the first centuries aniconic, and is the veneration of images apostolic or later?

The passage

“For to adore a picture is one thing, but to learn through the story of a picture what is to be adored is another. For what writing presents to readers, this a picture presents to the unlearned who behold… a picture is instead of reading.”

“And if any one should wish to make images, by no means prohibit him, but by all means forbid the adoration of images. But let thy Fraternity carefully admonish them that from the sight of the event portrayed they should catch the ardour of compunction, and bow themselves down in adoration of the One Almighty Holy Trinity.”

— Gregory the Great, Register of Epistles XI.10 (600), to Serenus of Marseilles.1

The question

Serenus, bishop of Marseilles, broke the images in his church on finding people adoring them; Gregory, in two letters (599 and 600), blamed him for the breaking, praised his ban on adoration, and told him to keep the images for those who cannot read. In the 790s the Frankish court cited the letters against the Second Council of Nicaea (787), which had defined the veneration of images, while Pope Hadrian I, defending that council, claimed Gregory’s teaching as his own.2 What is disputed is whether the letters set out a didactic use of images that excludes their cult, belong with the Roman approval of honouring images, or are chiefly a ruling against iconoclasm rather than a statement of what images are for.

A didactic use of images, with their adoration barred

In brief: Gregory keeps images in churches to teach and to stir devotion, and forbids any adoration of them, a position distinct from the veneration defined at Nicaea in 787.

Held by: the Frankish Opus Caroli regis contra synodum (Libri Carolini, drafted by Theodulf of Orléans, c. 790–793);3 Philip Schaff (Protestant church historian, d. 1893), History of the Christian Church, vol. IV;4 Francesco Stella (medievalist, European Review, 2022);5 Aidan Hart (Orthodox iconographer), “The Theology of the Icon,” St Andrews Encyclopaedia of Theology (2024).6

Gregory’s letters praise the ban and blame the breaking: “in that thou forbadest them to be adored, we altogether praise thee; but we blame thee for having broken them.” He tells Serenus to teach his people from Scripture “that it is not lawful for anything made with hands to be adored, since it is written, Thou shalt adore the Lord thy God, and him only shalt thou serve”; the adoration he enjoins is of “the One Almighty Holy Trinity,” prompted by the sight of the picture.7 The images’ use rests on reading and memory: they teach those “ignorant of letters.”

The Frankish theologians drew on these letters against Nicaea. Stella reports that the Opus Caroli (II.21) “denies any legitimacy to the cult of images, while their use is accepted for ‘fear of forgetting’ past events or for ‘love of embellishment’,” citing Gregory’s letter to Serenus, “which was not considered in Nicaea,” and that the work set out to develop “the mediating and realistic solutions of Gregory the Great: the didactic and mnemotechnical use of images was accepted… but its cult was refused.”5 Schaff places Gregory in a “Moderate theory” that “sought a via media between image-worship and image-hatred, by distinguishing between the sign and the thing, the use and the abuse”: Gregory “commended Serenus… for his zeal against the adoration of pictures, but disapproved of his excess in that direction.”4 Hart writes that Gregory “defended icons primarily on the grounds of their pedagogic role for the illiterate” and “seemed reluctant to distinguish between worship and veneration”: he “made no attempt to explain to Serenus that veneration of images was acceptable and even laudable, as the Eastern iconodules took pains to assert.”6 On this reading the letters license making and keeping images to instruct and to move the viewer toward God, and locate the line at adoration of the image itself; the Libri Carolini drew on that line against Nicaea.

A defence of holy images, one with the Roman approval of their honour

In brief: Gregory defends the images against a bishop who destroyed them, and his teaching is the same as that of the popes who later approved the honour paid to images.

Held by: Pope Hadrian I, Responsum to Charlemagne (c. 792–794);8 Adrian Fortescue (Catholic), “Veneration of Images,” Catholic Encyclopedia (1910).9

Gregory twice rebukes a bishop for destroying images, orders them preserved, and rules that “if any one should wish to make images, by no means prohibit him”; the picture moves its viewers to “catch the ardour of compunction” and to bow down in adoration of the Trinity.1 Hadrian, answering the Frankish chapters against Nicaea, “asserted the identity of his teaching with that of the highly-respected Pope Gregory the Great concerning images,” and reminded the king that twelve of his bishops had taken part in a Roman synod that “approved the ‘cultus’ of images”; his Responsum “praised the Carolingian choice of quoting the text of Gregory the Great to Serenus and advised the king to stick to this position.”8

Fortescue counts Gregory among the defenders of images: “St. Gregory the Great (d. 604) was always a great defender of holy pictures.” He calls Serenus “an Iconoclast bishop” and, with Philostorgius, a forerunner of the iconoclasts, “to whom St. Gregory the Great wrote his defence of pictures.” He gives the letter in the translation “Not without reason has antiquity allowed the stories of saints to be painted in holy places,” and finds in it the Roman position: “in Rome especially, we find the position of holy images explained soberly and reasonably.” Fortescue glosses the vocabulary of the Second Council of Nicaea: the worship the council reserved to God alone is latreia; “In Latin, adoratio is generally (though not always…) used in this sense,” and the council distinguished from it the “honourable reverence” that “may be paid to any venerable or great person.”9 On this reading the letters protect the images, forbid the adoration due to God, and state the teaching Hadrian I claimed as his own when he defended the Second Council of Nicaea.

A ruling against iconoclasm rather than a theory of images

In brief: the letters condemn a bishop’s destruction of his people’s images and defend that community, and were not written to define what images are for.

Held by: Barbara Crostini (Byzantinist, Uppsala University), “Keeping Everyone on Board: Gregory the Great’s ‘Theory of Iconoclasm’,” European Review (2022).10

Crostini argues “that Gregory did not invent in these letters any ‘theory of art’, and that it was not his aim to articulate any such theory. Rather… the pope expounded a powerful ‘theory of iconoclasm’.” Gregory “condemned image destruction on legal grounds as infringement of property rights,” using legal language: “we judge (iudicamus) that you should not have broken these very images.” The letters defend a group in Serenus’s congregation “who saw themselves effectively persecuted by their bishop for their veneration of images,” and Gregory’s concern is “to impose on the bishop a principle of respect and tolerance for diversity of views and practices within his church.” She grants that the pope wanted images in the church “ideally only in a limited pedagogical function,” and holds that “Gregory’s pedagogical dictum did not crush the essence of images or limit other uses for them.” On this reading the letters set a legal and pastoral precedent against destroying images in the West.10
