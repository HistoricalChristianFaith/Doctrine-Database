Was the early church aniconic, and image-veneration a later innovation?

Type: historical-thesis argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — the thesis is weak in its strong form (that the early church was programmatically aniconic, image-free on principle), which the material record refutes: figural Christian art is attested by c. 240 at Dura-Europos and through the mid-third-century catacombs, and even the “anti-image” fathers attack pagan idolatry, not Christian picturing as such. It is strong in its narrower form (that the defined cultic veneration of icons — bowing, kissing, lights, incense, “honour passing to the prototype” — and the theology that licenses it are a later development, forged in the eighth- and ninth-century controversy). The honest verdict turns on the distinction the lead itself flags: having images is early; venerating them as a doctrine is late. · classic proponents: the Reformers and Protestant church-historians.

The claim

The earliest Christians made and used no religious images; the church of the apostles and the martyrs was aniconic, and the veneration of icons — bowing to them, kissing them, burning lights and incense before them — is a later corruption that crept in from the surrounding pagan world and was only dogmatized, against much resistance, in the eighth and ninth centuries. What the fathers say about images is uniformly hostile; image-veneration is therefore not apostolic tradition but a medieval innovation.

— the standard Protestant historical case, from the Reformers onward. It is a claim about history, not (like the Second-Commandment objection) about the meaning of a text: the contention is that whatever the Decalogue means, the record of the first three centuries shows a church without cultic images, so the practice cannot claim antiquity.

Who advances it

The thesis is a mainstay of Reformed and evangelical polemic and of a long line of church-historians. It rests on two bodies of evidence — a run of hostile patristic texts and the disciplinary Canon 36 of the Council of Elvira (c. 305) — read together as proof of an original aniconism. Its most careful modern form drops the crude “no art at all” version and argues instead that the cult of images (proper veneration, as against didactic or funerary decoration) is undatable before the later patristic period and was contested when it arrived — the position the two Byzantine iconoclast crises and the Reformation both express.

The evidence for

The fathers speak against images. A real strand of early Christian writing is wary or hostile. Tertullian, in On Idolatry, condemns not merely the worship but the very manufacture of images: “God prohibits an idol as much to be made as to be worshipped,” so that “every artificer of an idol is guilty of one and the same crime”; and he dismisses the maker's excuse — “‘I make,’ says one, ‘but I worship not’… Nay, you who make, that they may be able to be worshipped, do worship.”1 Clement of Alexandria holds that “Moses ages before enacted expressly, that neither a graven, nor molten, nor moulded, nor painted likeness should be made,” that the Hebrews “constructed the temple without an image,” and that “it were… ridiculous… for man… to make God… Works of art cannot then be sacred and divine.”2 Origen tells Celsus that among the people of God “no maker of images was permitted to enjoy the rights of citizenship… the law expelling all such,” that Christians “shrink from raising altars, statues, and temples,” and (in his homily on the commandment) reads it as forbidding “a likeness of all things that are in the earth, and in the waters, and in heaven.”3

Elvira Canon 36. The Spanish council of c. 305 decreed — in the disputed but well-attested Latin — placuit picturas in ecclesia esse non debere, ne quod colitur et adoratur in parietibus depingatur: on the traditional reading, “there shall be no pictures in churches, lest what is reverenced and adored be depicted on the walls.” This is the earliest conciliar text on the subject, and it appears to forbid church images — the sharpest single documentary datum for an early hostility to them.4

Eusebius, even where he records images, keeps his distance. He reports the bronze statue at Paneas and painted “likenesses of his apostles Paul and Peter, and of Christ himself” — but frames both as something done “according to a habit of the Gentiles,” i.e. a pagan-derived custom, not an apostolic one; and (if the letter is his) he declined the empress Constantia's request for an image of Christ, objecting that his glorified nature could not be caught in “dead colours.” Read for the proponent, this is a church still treating images as an alien import.5

The cult of images is undatable early. The strongest form of the case does not need to deny that Christians ever painted; it observes that the specific devotional acts the later doctrine defends — proskynēsis before an icon, kissing it, burning lights and incense to it, the belief that the honour “passes to the prototype” — are simply not attested as a practice in the first three centuries. The theology that licenses them (the latria/dulia distinction, the incarnational defence) is the work of John of Damascus and the Second Council of Nicaea (787), five centuries on; and it had to be imposed over two imperial iconoclasms. A practice that has to be invented, defended, condemned, and re-imposed does not look apostolic.6

Adversarial assessment

What holds — the veneration half. On the narrower claim the proponent is on firm ground, and it is the honest core of the whole case. Figural decoration is one thing; a defined cult of icons is another, and the second is genuinely late. Nothing in the first three centuries describes Christians bowing to, kissing, or burning lights before images, and the vocabulary that makes such acts defensible — relative veneration, honour terminating on the prototype, the sharp dulia/latria line — is forged in the heat of the eighth-century crisis, not received from the apostles. That the East itself twice condemned the practice at the imperial and conciliar level (Hieria, 754; Leo V's revival, 815) shows the veneration of icons was not the undisputed inheritance its defenders claim but a development that had to win. To that extent “image-veneration is a later innovation” is a fair reading of the record.

Where it overreaches — the aniconic half. The strong thesis, that the early church was image-free on principle, is refuted by the material record. The Dura-Europos house-church (converted c. 240) has a baptistery painted with the Good Shepherd, the healing of the paralytic, Christ walking on the water, the woman at the well, and the women at the tomb — figural Christian scenes, in a functioning place of worship, a full generation before the earliest fathers the proponent quotes had finished writing. The Roman catacombs (Callixtus, Priscilla) carry Good-Shepherd and orant frescoes from the mid-third century. Christian figural art plainly existed early; the claim that it did not is simply false to the evidence.7

The literary evidence is anti-idol, not anti-image. The patristic texts, read in context, are apologetic assaults on pagan cult statues and the idolatry of the surrounding world — not blanket bans on Christian picturing. Tertullian's target is the idol-maker's trade; Clement mocks Aphrodite and “the image of an image”; Origen ridicules “not lifeless images, but those only who worship them,” and answers the specific charge that Christians shun pagan altars and temples. Decisively, Clement — one of the sternest — in the same breath prescribes acceptable Christian imagery: “let our seals be either a dove, or a fish, or a ship… or a ship's anchor… or one fishing” — permitting the very devices found in the catacombs, while forbidding only “the faces of idols.” A father who tells Christians which images to engrave is not an iconoclast; he is drawing the line between pagan idol and Christian symbol. Modern scholarship (Sr. Mary Charles-Murray, Paul Corby Finney, Robin Jensen) has accordingly overturned the old “early church hostile to art” consensus: what inhibited images was chiefly the philosophical conviction of God's invisibility, not a rule against art, and when Christian art appears it serves didactic and funerary, not yet devotional, ends.8

The documentary props are weaker than they look. Elvira Canon 36 is real but ambiguous: its rationale — “lest what is reverenced and adored be depicted on the walls” — reads to many scholars (Jensen, Grigg) as aimed at the veneration of wall-portraits liable to attract homage, not at narrative or symbolic art as such; and it is a single local Spanish canon, disciplinary and not ecumenical, that never cites the Second Commandment. Eusebius's Letter to Constantia survives only inside the later iconoclast dossier and its authenticity is genuinely disputed; leaning on it is circular. And the five Epiphanius of Salamis fragments against images — often paraded as a fourth-century iconoclast witness — are of contested authenticity: Ostrogorsky argued them spurious against Holl, Florovsky and Meyendorff followed, and Bigham's monograph concludes the “iconophobe Epiphanius” is largely a myth. An honest case does not rest weight on them.9

Assessment

Contested. The thesis divides cleanly, and the two halves fare oppositely. As the claim that the early church was aniconic — that it made and used no religious images on principle — it is weak, refuted by Dura-Europos and the catacombs and by the recognition that the hostile fathers were attacking pagan idolatry (and even licensing Christian symbols) rather than banning Christian art; the invisibility of God, not a prohibition of images, is what held Christian picturing back, and it did not hold it back for long. As the claim that the defined cultic veneration of icons, and the theology that defends it, are a later development — forged in the eighth-century controversy, contested by two imperial iconoclasms, and undatable as a practice in the first three centuries — it is strong. The honest verdict is exactly the distinction the practice's own defenders must concede and its critics often blur: possessing images is early; venerating them as a doctrine is late. Because the popular argument advances the compound — aniconism and late veneration — and only its second limb survives, the total case rates contested rather than sound or weak. Whether the developed veneration is a faithful development of apostolic worship or a corruption of it is a further question, argued under the veneration-vs-worship distinction and settled by neither the archaeology nor the apologists.
