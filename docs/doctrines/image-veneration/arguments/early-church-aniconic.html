Was the church of the first centuries aniconic, and is the veneration of images apostolic or later?

Disputed reading · on The Veneration of Images and Icons · witnesses: the Council of Elvira (c. 305), Eusebius of Caesarea (c. 325), and the early fathers on idols

Related readings: Do Gregory the Great’s letters to Serenus (599, 600) support the veneration of images?

The passage

Placuit picturas in ecclesia esse non debere, ne quod colitur aut adoratur, in parietibus depingatur.

“It is ordained (Placuit) that Pictures are not to be in churches, so that that which is worshipped and adored shall not be painted on walls.”

— Council of Elvira (c. 305), canon 36; Latin as given by Philip Schaff, English as given by Adrian Fortescue.1

“…we have learned also that the likenesses of his apostles Paul and Peter, and of Christ himself, are preserved in paintings, the ancients being accustomed, as it is likely, according to a habit of the Gentiles, to pay this kind of honor indiscriminately to those regarded by them as deliverers.”

— Eusebius, Church History VII.18 (c. 325), on the statue at Paneas and the painted likenesses.2

The question

Christian figural art survives from the third century (the Dura-Europos house-church, c. 240, and the Roman catacombs), while several early writers attack the making of idols and a Spanish council of the early fourth century forbade pictures in churches; the veneration of images was defined at the Second Council of Nicaea (787). What is disputed is whether the church of the first centuries kept its worship free of images, so that the later cult departs from it; whether images and their honour belong to an apostolic tradition only partly recorded in writing; or whether the early polemic was aimed at pagan idols and the veneration of Christian images took shape by degrees. Two texts adduced in the debate, Eusebius’s Letter to Constantia and writings against images ascribed to Epiphanius of Salamis, are of disputed authenticity.3

Images and their honour belong to the Church’s tradition from the apostles

In brief: making and honouring images is part of what the apostles handed on, partly without writing, so the thinness of the early written record is not the measure of the practice’s origin.

Held by: John of Damascus, An Exact Exposition of the Orthodox Faith IV.16 (c. 730);4 the Second Council of Nicaea, Definition (787);5 Leonid Ouspensky (Orthodox iconographer and theologian), Theology of the Icon (English ed. 1978);6 the Catechism of the Catholic Church §1160 (Catholic; Libreria Editrice Vaticana, 1993).7

John of Damascus, having written that “the honour rendered to the image passes over to the prototype,” adds: “But this is an unwritten tradition, just as is also the worshipping towards the East and the worship of the Cross, and very many other similar things.” He relates the tale of the likeness of his face that Christ sent to Augarus of Edessa, and grounds unwritten tradition in Paul’s charge to “hold the traditions which ye have been taught of us, whether by word or by epistle.”4 The Second Council of Nicaea defined the practice as one such tradition: “we keep unchanged all the ecclesiastical traditions handed down to us, whether in writing or verbally, one of which is the making of pictorial representations,” with incense and lights offered to images “according to ancient pious custom.”5 The Catholic Catechism quotes that definition: “We declare that we preserve intact all the written and unwritten traditions of the Church which have been entrusted to us. One of these traditions consists in the production of representational artwork.”7

Ouspensky writes that “The Church believes that the first icons originated within and were propagated together with Christianity,” and that it supports this belief not “with some formal and obvious proofs, but rather with the belief that the image is inseparable from Christianity.” He reads the art of the catacombs as “above all an art which teaches the faith,” with Old and New Testament themes appearing in the Roman catacombs “already in the first and second centuries.”6 On this side the texts later cited against images were answered as not authentic: the bishops at Nicaea in 787 said of a writing ascribed to Epiphanius, “We reject the writing, while we acknowledge the holy father as a teacher of the catholic Church,” and Theodore the Studite held that such writings had to be false, for “otherwise they would agree with the inspired fathers.”3 On this reading the conciliar definition states what the Church had received, and the honour paid to images is of a piece with other customs, such as praying toward the East, that the apostles handed on by practice.

An early church that kept its worship free of images, whose cult came in later

In brief: the first Christians, bound by the commandment and opposed to pagan idols, did not use images in worship, and the cult of images entered the churches afterwards.

Held by: the Council of Hieria (754);8 John Calvin (Reformed), Institutes I.11 (c. 1559);9 Philip Schaff (Protestant church historian, d. 1893), History of the Christian Church, vols. II–III, on the ante-Nicene aversion to images of Christ;10 Hugo Koch (church historian, Munich), Die altchristliche Bilderfrage nach den literarischen Quellen (1917).11

The iconoclast council of 754 told the story as a decline: the apostles adorned the Church with Christ’s doctrines, which “the holy Fathers and the six Ecumenical Councils have preserved inviolate,” until the devil “gradually brought back idolatry under the appearance of Christianity”; it cited Scripture and the fathers, among them Eusebius’s Letter to Constantia.8 Calvin holds that “for five hundred years, during which religion was in a more prosperous condition, and a purer doctrine flourished, Christian churches were completely free from visible representations,” and that “their first admission as an ornament to churches took place after the purity of the ministry had somewhat degenerated”; he cites the thirty-sixth canon of Elvira (“the Libertine Council”) and Augustine, who he says “distinctly declares, that it is unlawful not only to worship images, but to dedicate them.”9

The patristic texts this reading draws on speak against the making of images as well as their worship. Tertullian: “God prohibits an idol as much to be made as to be worshipped.”12 Clement of Alexandria: “Moses ages before enacted expressly, that neither a graven, nor molten, nor moulded, nor painted likeness should be made,” and “Works of art cannot then be sacred and divine.”13 Origen reports Celsus’s charge that “we shrink from raising altars, statues, and temples,” and answers that “the statues and gifts which are fit offerings to God are the work of no common mechanics, but are wrought and fashioned in us by the Word of God.”14 Eusebius, recording painted likenesses of Christ, ascribes them to “a habit of the Gentiles.” Schaff writes that “The ante-Nicene church, inheriting the Mosaic decalogue, and engaged in deadly conflict with heathen idolatry, was at first averse to those arts”; that “Previous to the time of Constantine, we find no trace of an image of Christ, properly speaking, except among the Gnostic Carpocratians.”10 Koch (1917), reading the literary sources, describes a church that “for three centuries” had held back from depicting its holiest sign, the cross, while images entered “from below” as the word of imageless worship was still officially spoken; “the same powers that had opened the cemeteries and churches to images drove on further to the cult of images,” and that cult “and its theological justification” was “the Church’s final payment for its victory over the grossest polytheism and paganism.”11 On this reading the church of the martyrs worshipped without images, and the acts of the later cult enter the record only afterwards, against continuing protest.

No principled ban on Christian images; their veneration took shape over time

In brief: Christians made figural and symbolic art early, the fathers’ polemic was aimed at pagan idols, and the honour paid to Christian images developed gradually.

Held by: Adrian Fortescue (Catholic), “Veneration of Images,” Catholic Encyclopedia (1910);15 Paul Corby Finney, The Invisible God: The Earliest Christians on Art (1994);16 Robin M. Jensen, “Aniconism in the first centuries of Christianity,” Religion 47 (2017).17

Fortescue writes that “The catacombs are the cradle of all Christian art.” Of the early invectives against idols he says, “These texts all regard idols, that is, images made to be adored”; of Elvira’s canon, that “The meaning of the canon has been much discussed” and that it “can have produced but a slight effect even in Spain.”15 The baptistery of the Dura-Europos house-church (c. 240) is painted with the Good Shepherd, the healing of the paralytic, Christ walking on the water, the woman at the well and the women at the tomb, and the Roman catacombs carry Good Shepherd and orant frescoes from the mid-third century.18 Clement, who forbids Christians “to delineate the faces of idols,” names the devices their seals may bear: “a dove, or a fish, or a ship scudding before the wind… or a ship’s anchor”; and Origen writes, “we throw ridicule not upon lifeless images, but upon those only who worship them.”19,14

The book’s catalogue description summarises Finney’s study as finding that “it was primarily the Christian belief in the invisibility of God that inhibited the production of images, rather than opposition to images as such.”16 Jensen argues “that classical philosophical arguments were even more influential on Christian condemnation of divine images than biblical texts and that when it emerged, Christian art essentially served non-idolatrous didactic rather than devotional purposes,” and that later criticism turned to “whether saints’ portraits were proper foci for prayer and veneration.”17 On the honour paid to images, Fortescue writes: “For the first period we have no information”; the care early Christians gave their pictures argues “at least decent reverence,” and “It is from this reverence that the whole tradition of venerating holy images gradually and naturally developed.”15 On this reading the early church drew its line between pagan idol and Christian image, and the forms of veneration grew from that early reverence over the following centuries.
