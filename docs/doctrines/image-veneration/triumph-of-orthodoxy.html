The Triumph of Orthodoxy (843) on the Veneration of Images

Dates: 843 · Wikipedia
Summary: The final restoration of the icons in the East. After the second iconoclasm (815–843), a synod at Constantinople under the Empress Theodora (regent for the boy-emperor Michael III) and the patriarch Methodios I re-affirmed the Second Council of Nicaea and restored the images to the churches. It is commemorated every year on the first Sunday of Great Lent as the “Sunday of Orthodoxy,” and it closes the iconoclast era for good in the Byzantine world.

Claims & evidence

The synod of 843 restored the icons under Theodora and Methodios

The second iconoclasm, begun by Leo V the Armenian in 815 and continued under Michael II and Theophilus, ended when Theophilus died (842) and his widow Theodora, ruling for their young son, moved to reverse it. A synod met at Constantinople (early March 843), deposed the iconoclast patriarch John the Grammarian, installed the iconophile Methodios, and reinstated the Nicene definition on images. The restoration was celebrated with a procession of icons on the first Sunday of Lent, 11 March 843 — the origin of the annual feast.1

The Synodikon of Orthodoxy proclaims the faith of the icons

From the feast comes the Synodikon of Orthodoxy, a profession still chanted in the East on the Sunday of Orthodoxy, which sums up the settled doctrine — honouring Christ and the saints in icons, with the worship of God kept distinct from the veneration of the saints:2

“…Christ our true God, and His saints we honor in words, in writings, in thoughts, in sacrifices, in temples, in icons; on the one hand worshipping and reverencing Christ as God and Lord, and on the other hand honoring as true servants of the same Lord of all and offering them veneration.”

Notes

  • 843 restored images in the East; it did not touch the separate Western reception, where the Second Council of Nicaea (787) was itself received unevenly (the Frankish Libri Carolini had criticized it). The Reformed rejection of images (see Calvin) reopened the whole question in the sixteenth century in the West.
  • This page fixes the closing event of the Byzantine controversy on the timeline; the substantive question of whether the honour paid an icon is licit veneration or forbidden worship is weighed in the summary's Arguments section.
  • The defender Theodore the Studite (d. 826) did not live to see this vindication of the cause he had suffered exile for.
