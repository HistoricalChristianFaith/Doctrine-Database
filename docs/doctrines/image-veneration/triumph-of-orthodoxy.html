The Triumph of Orthodoxy (843) on the Veneration of Images

Dates: 843 · Wikipedia
Summary: The restoration of the icons in the East. After the second iconoclasm (815–843), a synod at Constantinople under the Empress Theodora (regent for the boy-emperor Michael III) and the patriarch Methodios I re-affirmed the Second Council of Nicaea and restored the images to the churches. It is commemorated every year on the first Sunday of Great Lent as the “Sunday of Orthodoxy,” and it ended the second iconoclasm.

Claims & evidence

The synod of 843 restored the icons under Theodora and Methodios

The second iconoclasm, begun by Leo V the Armenian in 815 and continued under Michael II and Theophilus, ended when Theophilus died (842) and his widow Theodora, ruling for their young son, moved to reverse it. A synod met at Constantinople (early March 843), deposed the iconoclast patriarch John the Grammarian, installed the iconophile Methodios, and reinstated the Nicene definition on images. The restoration was celebrated with a procession of icons on the first Sunday of Lent, 11 March 843 — the origin of the annual feast.1

The Synodikon of Orthodoxy proclaims the faith of the icons

From the feast comes the Synodikon of Orthodoxy, a profession still chanted in the East on the Sunday of Orthodoxy, which sums up the faith of the icons — honouring Christ and the saints in icons, with the worship of God kept distinct from the veneration of the saints:2

“…Christ our true God, and His saints we honor in words, in writings, in thoughts, in sacrifices, in temples, in icons; on the one hand worshipping and reverencing Christ as God and Lord, and on the other hand honoring as true servants of the same Lord of all and offering them veneration.”

Reception and debate

  • 843 restored images in the East; it did not touch the separate Western reception, where the Second Council of Nicaea (787) was rejected by the Synod of Frankfurt (794), after the Frankish Libri Carolini had criticized it, while Pope Hadrian I defended the council.3 In the sixteenth-century West, Karlstadt (1522) urged the removal of images from churches, Zurich’s churches were emptied of images under Zwingli (1524), and Calvin argued against images in churches in the Institutes (c. 1559); Luther (1522, 1525) held images to be things neither commanded nor forbidden.4

  • The icons' defender Theodore the Studite died in 826, before the restoration.
