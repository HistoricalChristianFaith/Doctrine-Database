Old-World Peoples in the Pre-Columbian Americas

The Reformed pastor Douglas Wilson — a pointed critic of the Book of Mormon — states, in passing and on his own Reformed grounds, a belief that would sound at home in the very scriptures he rejects: “that King Solomon was behind the settlement of Central America.” His argument is one of feasibility: Captain Cook found people already waving from the beaches of Hawaii, and there are the statues of Easter Island “way the heck out there,” so somebody in antiquity knew how to cross open water — and if somebody could, then the crossing that (he holds) peopled Mesoamerica is no obstacle. Wilson is the modern front door to this page; he is also the tail of a tradition far older than he is.

The idea that Old-World peoples — the Ten Lost Tribes of Israel, Phoenicians, Egyptians, Solomon's own fleet — crossed an ocean and founded the civilizations of the pre-Columbian Americas is a traceable, four-century tradition, and this page tells it as a reception-history timeline: not a survey of belief-among-many-witnesses but a chronology of the datable sources who advanced or answered the claim, earliest first. Its “witnesses” are therefore dated books and reports — a friar's history, a rabbi's messianic tract, a trader's ethnography, a folio of copied codices, a Smithsonian excavation report, a Semitist's monograph, a pastor's blog post — each footnoted to its source. Two threads run through it and are worth watching for: a counter-voice is present from the first generation (the Jesuit Acosta reasons his way to an overland migration from Asia in 1590, and the mounds' “lost race” is demolished by 1894), and the affirmative evidence repeatedly turns out to be a misreading or a forgery.

Genuine pre-Columbian trans-oceanic contact is real and attested — the Norse reached Newfoundland, and a pulse of Native-American ancestry reached eastern Polynesia around AD 1200 — so the bare question “could ancient people cross an ocean?” is not the fringe part. The fringe part is the leap from that feasibility to the claim that Old-World civilizations founded the Mesoamerican ones. That leap is weighed adversarially in the Arguments below and comes out weak; the same argument-shape, run for the Israelite migrants of the Book of Mormon, is tested against the same material record on the Book of Mormon and the material record.

Timeline

Bartolomé de las Casas weighs the idea — and doubts it (c. 1550)

The tradition is often traced to the great Dominican defender of the Indians, Bartolomé de las Casas, but the ascription is a later legend, first spread by Juan de Torquemada. In his Apologética historia sumaria Las Casas in fact judged that circumcision and a handful of Hebrew-sounding words were no sufficient ground to make the Indians “descend from the Jewish people” — so he belongs at the head of this timeline not as the thesis's founder but as its first skeptic.1

Fray Diego Durán: the natives as the Ten Lost Tribes (c. 1581)

The clean early proponent is the Dominican friar Diego Durán, raised among Nahuatl-speakers in Texcoco and Mexico City. He opened his Historia de las Indias de Nueva España with his own “opinion and suspicion” that the natives of New Spain descended from the Ten Lost Tribes of Israel, reasoning from a perceived likeness between the Aztec Aztlán-migration story and the Exodus, from the apocryphal exile of the tribes to a distant land in 2 Esdras, and from the Indians' own professed ignorance of their origin.2

José de Acosta answers by elimination: overland from Asia (1590)

The decisive early counter-voice is the Jesuit José de Acosta, whose Historia natural y moral de las Indias (1590) rejected both the Lost-Tribes idea and any deliberate ocean crossing. Working by elimination, he argued that the first peoples had come overland from Asia, because the two landmasses must be “joyned and continued one with another in some part, or at the least, are very neere,” the country peopled gradually as hunting bands changed “by little and little their lands and habitations.” He dismissed the scriptural-migration route — the apocryphal Euphrates of 2 Esdras — as giving “no better passage” to the New World than Plato's “fabulous Atlantis.” His inductive guess, a century and a half before the Bering Strait was charted, is in outline the modern consensus.3

The 1650 cluster: Menasseh ben Israel and Thomas Thorowgood (1644–1660)

The idea crossed from Catholic New Spain into Protestant and Jewish messianism on a single traveller's tale. On the sworn 1644 relation of Antonio de Montezinos — who claimed to have been led over the Andes to a hidden people reciting the Shema in Hebrew — the Amsterdam rabbi Menasseh ben Israel built The Hope of Israel (1650), which set out to prove “the place wherein the ten tribes at this present are … partly by the strange relation of one Anthony Montezinus,” making a worldwide dispersion (America included) a precondition of the messianic restoration — and a lever for readmitting Jews to England.4 In the same year the English Puritan Thomas Thorowgood published Iewes in America, or, Probabilities that the Americans are of that Race, tying the thesis to a program of missions; the antiquary Sir Hamon L'Estrange answered him the next year with Americans no Jewes (1651).5

James Adair's twenty-three arguments (1775)

The fullest early case in English came from a trader who had lived four decades among the Chickasaw, Cherokee, Choctaw, and Creek. James Adair devoted much of his History of the American Indians to some twenty-three “arguments” that the southeastern nations descended lineally from the Israelites — from their division into clans, their purity and mourning rites, their festivals, a supposed theocratic order, and a long list of claimed language parallels. The ethnography is still valued as among the best early records of those peoples; the Israelite thesis is not.6

Ethan Smith systematizes it for America: View of the Hebrews (1823)

The Congregationalist minister Ethan Smith of Poultney, Vermont, gathered the scriptural prophecies and the ethnographic reports — Adair among his sources — into View of the Hebrews; or the Tribes of Israel in America (1823; enlarged 1825), the popular American synthesis of the Hebraic-origin view. It is also the book whose possible influence on the Book of Mormon, published seven years later and a few Vermont-New York counties away, has been debated ever since.7

The Book of Mormon crystallizes the idea in scripture (1830)

In March 1830 the tradition took scriptural form. The Book of Mormon presents Israelite migrants — the Jaredites from the Tower of Babel and the Lehites who left Jerusalem about 600 BC — as the founders of literate, metal-using civilizations in the ancient Americas. Whether that record survives the archaeological, genetic, and linguistic evidence is a large question of its own, weighed on the Book of Mormon and the material record; on this timeline it marks the point at which the diffusionist idea became the charter of a new church.8

Lord Kingsborough spends a fortune to prove it (1830–1848)

Edward King, Viscount Kingsborough, poured his inheritance into the nine elephant-folio volumes of Antiquities of Mexico (1830–1848), commissioning Agostino Aglio to copy the Mesoamerican codices scattered through the libraries of Europe expressly to prove the Mexicans descended from the Lost Tribes. He died in a Dublin debtors' prison, ruined by the cost. The thesis failed; the facsimiles endured — Aglio's copy of the Dresden Codex preserves readings later lost to damage of the original.9

A cousin thesis: British Israelism (from 1840)

A related current placed the Lost Tribes not across the Atlantic but in Britain: John Wilson's Our Israelitish Origin (1840), later popularized by Edward Hine, held the Anglo-Saxons themselves to be Israel, traced by philological look-alikes. It is flagged here only to keep the two apart — British Israelism locates the tribes in Europe, not the Americas — though its fusion with the American strand shaped later movements.10

Cyrus Thomas demolishes the “lost race” (1894)

The nineteenth-century American form of the tradition was the “Mound-Builder” myth — that a vanished, superior race, Israelite or otherwise Old-World, and not the ancestors of the living Indians, had raised the great earthworks of the Ohio and Mississippi valleys. The Smithsonian's Bureau of Ethnology set Cyrus Thomas to test it; after eight years of excavation his massive Report on the Mound Explorations concluded that the mounds were built by the ancestors of the historic Native American nations, and the “lost race” collapsed. Thomas had begun the work a believer in the vanished builders and was turned by his own evidence — the mainstream verdict ever since.11

The epigraphic diffusionists: Cyrus Gordon and Barry Fell (1971–1976)

The twentieth-century revival ran on inscriptions. The Brandeis Semitist Cyrus H. Gordon defended the Bat Creek and Paraíba texts as genuine ancient Hebrew and Phoenician in Before Columbus (1971); the Harvard zoologist-turned-epigrapher Barry Fell read scattered New-World rock-markings as Celtic Ogham, Punic, and Iberian script in America B.C. (1976).12 Specialists reject the whole corpus: the Bat Creek stone — the one genuinely excavated piece, and so weighed on its own page — closely tracks a line from an 1870 Masonic handbook, and Fell's “inscriptions” repeatedly proved to be plough-scars, glacial striae, or Native petroglyphs. This is the affirmative evidence Wilson's Solomonic thesis leans on, and it is weighed in full below.13

Douglas Wilson revives it: Solomon in Central America (2026)

At the modern tail stands the Reformed pastor Douglas Wilson, who — though a hard critic of the Book of Mormon — makes the same species of claim on his own grounds: “I have written elsewhere about my belief that King Solomon was behind the settlement of Central America.” His stated case is feasibility: Polynesians reached islands “way the heck out there,” so somebody in antiquity could cross open water. The interpretation the aside serves is weighed on its own page — weak as a general diffusionist thesis, unsupported in its specific Solomonic form.14

Arguments

Related arguments — genuine trans-Pacific contact

Two arguments on the responsible core the hyper-diffusionist thesis overreaches from: that a real, limited, human-scale contact crossed the open Pacific between Polynesia and South America before Columbus. They share a single load-bearing datum (the AD-1200 Native-American–Polynesian genetic admixture) and are best read together — one weighs the human-genetic case as a whole and comes out sound, the other weighs the sweet-potato prop and comes out contested. Both run the opposite way from the rest of this timeline: the transfer ran from South America to Polynesia, and neither claims a founding migration.

Did genuine pre-Columbian Polynesia–South America contact occur? (assessment: sound)

The one crossing the genetics prove — set apart from the forged-inscription corpus and the weak founding thesis, this is the honestly-established case that some real contact happened. Two independent genome-wide studies — one on living eastern Polynesians (Ioannidis 2020), one on ancient Rapanui skeletons directly (Moreno-Mayar 2024) — converge by different methods on a shared Native-American ancestry component dated to the thirteenth–fifteenth centuries, well before any European. The softer props stay contested (the sweet potato below; the El Arenal chicken, pre-Columbian in date but of doubtful Polynesian origin) and the direction of the crossing is open, but the genetics carry the core claim on their own. It is the partial vindication of Thor Heyerdahl — right that the ocean was crossed, wrong about the direction and the moai — and it licenses only a brief meeting of seafaring peoples, not the founding of a civilization (L'Anse aux Meadows is the standing proof a real crossing can found nothing).15

Does the sweet potato evidence pre-Columbian human contact across the Pacific? (assessment: contested)

The crop that ran the other way — the botanical facet of that contact. The South-American sweet potato was under cultivation across eastern Polynesia before any European could have brought it, and much of Polynesia calls it kumara, echoing Andean k'umar. A 2018 whole-genome study argues the plant could have arrived by natural long-distance dispersal (no humans needed), so the crop alone does not prove contact; but a cultivated South-American cultigen bearing an Andean name is best explained by people — and the independent 2020 genome study fixing a real Native-American–Polynesian admixture to c. AD 1200 makes human transfer the probable reading. Note the direction: the crop ran from South America to Polynesia, and the case turns on mainstream botany and genetics, not on any forged inscription.16

Related arguments — the American epigraphic corpus

Two stones offered as proof of an ancient Hebrew or Phoenician presence in America, best read as a set. Each is weighed here on its own authenticity — and each comes out weak. They share a script (palaeo-Hebrew/Phoenician), a champion in Cyrus Gordon (and in the economist J. Huston McCulloch, who curates the technical case for both), and a common fate: the letters, not the enthusiasm, decide them. Both are kept distinct from the settlement thesis they are enlisted to prove — even a genuine stone would be a stray-contact datum, not a founding population.

The Bat Creek stone: ancient palaeo-Hebrew, or a 19th-century forgery? (assessment: weak; unsupported for settlement)

The one excavated exhibit — of the Semitic-inscription corpus the settlement thesis leans on, the Bat Creek stone is the only piece with a genuine provenance: not a surface find but a tablet dug from a Tennessee burial mound in 1889 by a Smithsonian field agent and published by Cyrus Thomas. Cyrus Gordon and, at length, the economist J. Huston McCulloch read it (inverted) as palaeo-Hebrew LYHWD, “for Judea,” and pointed to a radiocarbon date on associated wood (c. AD 32–769) and “Roman” brass bracelets. It gets a fair, adversarial hearing here — and comes out weak: Mainfort and Kwas showed the inscription tracks an 1870 Masonic drawing of the phrase “Holy to Yahweh,” the carbon date fixes the ancient mound a forger would exploit (not the carving), the brass fits a colonial trade-bracelet as well as a Roman one, and the lone excavator had motive and a shadowed record. And even were it genuine, one stone is a stray-contact datum, not a founded civilization — unsupported for the settlement thesis it is enlisted to prove.17

The Los Lunas Decalogue stone: ancient palaeo-Hebrew, or a modern carving? (assessment: weak; unsupported for settlement)

The boulder with no context — the corpus's surface find: a basalt boulder on Hidden Mountain near Los Lunas, New Mexico, carrying an abridged Ten Commandments, first reported by the archaeologist Frank Hibben in 1933. Barry Fell, Cyrus Gordon, and the geologist George Morehouse read it as ancient — Morehouse estimating 500–2000 years from the re-weathering of the grooves. It gets a fair hearing here and comes out weak: the hand mixes archaic palaeo-Hebrew, post-Alexandrian Greek, and later Samaritan letter-forms no ancient scribe combined; it follows the Masoretic wording in a Samaritan-looking script (sinking Gordon's mezuzah reading); its punctuation looks modern; and the one dating method (patina re-weathering) is unrecoverable after generations of visitor scrubbing. With no excavated context, a sole witness whose later career is shadowed by fraud allegations, and Feder's point that a real colony would leave far more than one inscription, the ancient reading does not hold — and even if it did, one boulder is a stray-contact datum, not a founded civilization (unsupported for settlement).18

Did Old-World peoples trans-oceanically settle pre-Columbian Central America? (assessment: weak; unsupported for Solomon)

Somebody knew how to do it — the flagship of this timeline, taking Wilson's aside in its strongest form. Kept as three distinct sub-claims it comes apart. That some pre-Columbian contact happened is attested (the Norse at Newfoundland; a Native-American genetic pulse into Polynesia c. AD 1200), so the bare feasibility premise is sound — but possibility is not actuality, the jump to “Old-World civilizations founded the Mesoamerican ones” is a non-sequitur, and the specific evidence for Semites-in-America (the Bat Creek, Los Lunas, and Paraíba inscriptions, Fell's America B.C.) is a corpus of forgeries and misreadings, with Solomon's Ophir and Tarshish lying in the Old World.19

Where did Solomon's fleet actually sail? Ophir and Tarshish located (assessment: sound; contested on the exact pin)

The destinations were in the Old World — the settlement thesis above leans partly on placing Solomon's gold-and-silver entrepôts Ophir and Tarshish across the Atlantic, so the location scholarship is adjudicated on its own. Two distinct operations emerge, and the distinction alone sinks a single trans-Atlantic reading: the Ophir fleet is built at Ezion-geber on the Gulf of Aqaba and sails south down the Red Sea (toward Arabia, the Horn of Africa, or India — the Septuagint and Josephus read it as India, and Bede's Commentary on Genesis already routes it “by the Red Sea”), while the “navy of Tarshish” is the Mediterranean silver-trade pointed west (Tarsus, Sardinia, or Iberian Tartessos, the lead-isotope source of Levantine silver). “Ships of Tarshish” had even become a vessel-class — Jehoshaphat's are built on the Red Sea and bound for Ophir. The exact pin for each name stays contested among these Old-World candidates, but every candidate is Old-World, so the interpretation at issue is sound and Wilson's “Ophir = Central America” fails.20

Coca and nicotine in Egyptian mummies: pre-Columbian trans-Atlantic contact? (assessment: weak)

The evidence running the other way — this timeline's trans-Atlantic mirror: not Old-World peoples reaching America but American plants reaching the Old World. Balabanova, Parsche, and Pirsig (1992) reported cocaine, nicotine, and hashish in Egyptian mummies of c. 1000 BC, taken by diffusionists as proof of a pre-Columbian crossing. Kept as two sub-claims it comes apart. Nicotine needs no ocean at all — the Old World has its own nicotine-bearing plants (Nicotiana africana; trace nicotine across the nightshades) — so it is unsupported as contact evidence whatever the chemistry. Cocaine is the real anomaly, since only the American Erythroxylum coca makes it; the finding is genuine, metabolite-level, and partly replicated, and gets a fair hearing — but a single lab's result, cleanly reproduced only in its non-diagnostic (nicotine) half, shadowed by museum-era contamination, and unaccompanied by any other American trace anywhere in the pre-Columbian Old World, is weak as proof of contact. The contrast with the genetically-secured trans-Pacific contact above measures how much this case is missing.21
