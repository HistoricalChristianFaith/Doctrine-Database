The Bronze Sea's Capacity: 2,000 or 3,000 Baths? (1 Kings 7:26 vs. 2 Chronicles 4:5)

(1 Kings 4:26 vs. 2 Chronicles 9:25) · Ahaziah's Age: 22 or 42? (2 Kings 8:26 vs. 2 Chronicles 22:2) · What Jesus Wrote on the Ground (John 8:6) · The Two Genealogies of Jesus (Matthew 1 vs. Luke 3) · The Death of Judas (Matthew 27 vs. Acts 1) · Naming the Wrong Prophet (Mark 1:2; Matthew 27:9) · Baptized for the Dead (1 Corinthians 15:29)

Two books of the Old Testament measure the capacity of Solomon's great cast-bronze basin — the "sea" in which the priests washed — and the numbers disagree by a thousand baths. The Masoretic Hebrew of 1 Kings 7:26 says the sea "contained two thousand baths."1 The parallel notice at 2 Chronicles 4:5 — the Chronicler retelling the same furnishing of the temple — says it "received and held three thousand baths."2 Every other detail matches: the same ten cubits from brim to brim, the same five cubits high, the same handbreadth-thick wall and lily-worked rim, the same twelve oxen beneath. Only the capacity differs, by exactly a third. This is a plain text-versus-text disagreement between two biblical books, of the same numeral-crux family as Solomon's forty-thousand-or-four-thousand stalls and Ahaziah's twenty-two-or-forty-two years.

The geometry gives the smaller figure a foothold. From the sea's own stated dimensions — ten cubits across, five deep, round in compass3 — the vessel's volume can be computed, and the computation caps it near two thousand baths: on the reckoning Keil & Delitzsch cite from Thenius, "the capacity of the vessel, from the dimensions given, could not exceed 2000 baths."4 If that holds, three thousand is not merely the larger reading but one the basin could not physically contain — which tells both against the "three thousand" of Chronicles as a figure and against the popular harmonization that reads it as a brim-full maximum. Two honest cautions keep this short of the certainty of its cousins, though: the computation depends on the disputed size of the cubit and the bath, and — unlike Ahaziah's impossible forty-two — three thousand baths is not impossible, only improbable, so no arithmetical contradiction forces the question shut.

Nor do the oldest witnesses converge the way they do for Ahaziah. Each book's number travels with it into the versions, and Josephus, retelling the reign at the end of the first century, sides with the larger figure — "this sea contained three thousand baths" — just as he sided with the larger number in the count of Solomon's stalls. So the case for "two thousand" rests not on an external line of witnesses but on the internal geometry and the direction in which numerals drift under copying. This page gathers the witnesses earliest first — the book of Kings that gives "two thousand," the Chronicler whose text reads "three thousand," Josephus siding with the larger, and the harmonizers and text-critics who reopened the count — and leaves the two sub-claims (is the "three thousand" a scribal slip? do the verses measure different fills?) and the separate geometry objection (does the sea make the Bible give π as 3?) to be weighed under Arguments.

Timeline

The book of Kings (c. 560 BC)

The earliest witness to "two thousand" is the book of Kings itself. Describing the last of Hiram's bronze works, 1 Kings 7:26 closes the account of the sea: "it was an hand breadth thick, and the brim thereof was wrought like the brim of a cup, with flowers of lilies: it contained two thousand baths."5 This is the older of the two accounts and the Chronicler's written source; its "two thousand" is the figure the vessel's own dimensions will bear, and the one most critics take as prior.

The Chronicler (c. 400 BC)

Retelling Solomon's temple from Kings, the Chronicler reaches the sea at 2 Chronicles 4:5 — and where Kings has one verb, he has two: the sea "received and held three thousand baths."6 That double verb (maḥăzîq, "holding," with yāḵîl, "it would contain") is the hinge of the whole dispute: read as Hebrew pleonasm it is simply an emphatic way of saying "held," and the "three thousand" is then a copyist's inflation of the "two"; read as two distinct measures it names a maximum capacity of three thousand against the two thousand of ordinary use, and both books stand. Either way the larger number enters the tradition here.

Josephus (c. 93)

Retelling the reign in the Antiquities, Josephus describes the sea as a bronze hemisphere on its twelve oxen and follows the larger figure: "Now this sea contained three thousand baths."7 Writing in Greek from a Hebrew source a thousand years before the Masoretic codices, he shows that "three thousand" was the received number for the sea in his day — the same pattern as his witness to Solomon's stalls, where he likewise carried the larger of the two biblical figures. His testimony makes the "three thousand" old, not a late scribal peculiarity, and denies the smaller-number case the united early witness that settled the Ahaziah crux.

Matthew Poole (1685)

With the early-modern annotators the harmonizing begins in earnest. Matthew Poole, in his Annotations upon the Holy Bible, keeps both numbers by making them count different fills: Kings "speaks of what it did actually and usually contain, to wit, two thousand baths, which was sufficient for use," while Chronicles "speaks of what it could contain if it were filled to the brim."8 He grounds the reading in the Chronicler's fuller Hebrew — the sea "strengthening itself" to receive and hold "as much as it could" — and John Gill, on the same verse, gives the identical solution: "in common, for the use of the priests … it had no more than 2,000 baths in it, but, if filled up to the brim, it would hold 3,000." It is the classic "different thing being measured" harmonization, weighed under Arguments.

Keil & Delitzsch (1872)

The nineteenth-century commentary that set the conservative-critical consensus states the verdict without hedging. Keil & Delitzsch, on 1 Kings 7:26, judge the "three thousand" a copyist's error and locate it in the numeral-letters: "The latter statement has arisen from the confusion of ג (3) with ב (2); since, according to the calculation of Thenius, the capacity of the vessel, from the dimensions given, could not exceed 2000 baths."9 On their reading Kings keeps the original number and Chronicles the slip — and the geometry, not any theory of how the letters were confused, is what does the work. On the parallel verse (2 Chronicles 4:5) they say the same, filing the "3000 baths instead of 2000" among "orthographical errors in the Chronicle"; Ellicott, Barnes, and the Cambridge Bible concur that a numeral has been corrupted, though the Cambridge editors add that "whether the textual corruption is to be attributed to 1 Kin. or to Chron. cannot be determined."

Arguments

The sub-claims that cut across the timeline — not "who reported which number, and when," but "why do the two books differ, and does the sea's geometry embarrass the text?" — are weighed adversarially on their own pages, each with an assessment of the reading it serves. The shared floor for the capacity crux is that the sea's stated dimensions cap its volume near two thousand baths; what is disputed is whether the fix is textual (the "three thousand" a corrupted numeral, the original being "two thousand") or harmonistic (the "three thousand" a real brim-full maximum against a working fill of two thousand, so both texts stand). The geometry objection about π is a separate matter and is rated on its own page.

Related arguments — reconciling the capacity

Everyone weighing the crux grants that the two figures cannot both be an ordinary count of the same water; the two advocated fixes divide over where the trouble lies. One is textual — the "three thousand" is a copyist's inflation of an original "two thousand," so only the numeral is wrong and the two books never disagreed about the basin. The other is harmonistic — the Chronicler's double verb "received and held" names a brim-full maximum of three thousand against a working fill of two thousand, so both texts stand as written. Each is weighed on its own page.

Is the "three thousand baths" of 2 Chronicles 4:5 a scribal inflation of an original "two thousand" (preserved by 1 Kings 7:26)? — plausible

The majority text-critical reading: the sea's own dimensions cap its capacity near two thousand baths, so "three thousand" is the improbable figure; a clean numeral mechanism (the letter-numerals ב = 2 and ג = 3, or a simple "2 → 3" slip) supplies the how; and numbers drift upward under copying. It is the more probable of the two fixes — but it runs weaker than its Solomon's-stalls and Ahaziah cousins: there is no arithmetical impossibility, the geometric ceiling depends on the disputed size of the cubit and bath, the received text spells the number in words (so the letter-confusion is a reconstruction), and the earliest external witnesses split — Josephus and Chronicles both carry "three thousand" (plausible).

Do the two verses measure different fills — two thousand baths in ordinary use, three thousand filled to the brim? — weak

The harmonizer's route (Poole, Gill, and the older annotators, after the Chronicler's double verb "received and held"): Kings gives the water usually kept in the sea and Chronicles what it could hold brim-full, so both numbers are true. But the geometry that helps the textual reading defeats this one — if the vessel maxes near two thousand baths, three thousand cannot be its brim-full capacity; the double verb reads more naturally as Hebrew pleonasm (Keil & Delitzsch call it "pleonastic") than as two calibrated measures; and the reading has to posit an exact working fill of two-thirds that the text never signposts (weak).

Does the bronze sea's geometry make the Bible give π as 3? (1 Kings 7:23) — weak

The separate objection often raised alongside the capacity crux: the sea is ten cubits "from the one brim to the other" and "a line of thirty cubits did compass it round about," and 30 ÷ 10 = 3, so Scripture supposedly fixes π at 3. The charge is weak: the dimensions are plainly round builders' figures (ten, thirty, five whole cubits), and rounding a circumference of about 31.4 cubits to thirty — or measuring a diameter across a flared, handbreadth-thick lily rim while the thirty-cubit line girdles the narrower body below it — is exactly what an ancient description of a cast vessel would do; three is an ordinary practical value for π in the ancient Near East, not a mathematical assertion. (The celebrated rabbinic gematria that reads a precise π out of the ketiv/qere of "line" — קוה = 111 against קו = 106, whose ratio nudges 3 to 3.1415 — is a numerical curiosity, popularly but doubtfully credited to the Vilna Gaon, and is treated on the page.)
