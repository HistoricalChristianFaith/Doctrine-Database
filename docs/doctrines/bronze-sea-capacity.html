The Bronze Sea's Capacity: 2,000 or 3,000 Baths? (1 Kings 7:26 vs. 2 Chronicles 4:5)

(1 Kings 4:26 vs. 2 Chronicles 9:25) · Ahaziah's Age: 22 or 42? (2 Kings 8:26 vs. 2 Chronicles 22:2) · What Jesus Wrote on the Ground (John 8:6) · The Two Genealogies of Jesus (Matthew 1 vs. Luke 3) · The Death of Judas (Matthew 27 vs. Acts 1) · Naming the Wrong Prophet (Mark 1:2; Matthew 27:9) · Baptized for the Dead (1 Corinthians 15:29)

Two books of the Old Testament measure the capacity of Solomon's great cast-bronze basin — the "sea" in which the priests washed — and the numbers disagree by a thousand baths. The Masoretic Hebrew of 1 Kings 7:26 says the sea "contained two thousand baths."1 The parallel notice at 2 Chronicles 4:5 — the Chronicler retelling the same furnishing of the temple — says it "received and held three thousand baths."2 Every other detail matches: the same ten cubits from brim to brim, the same five cubits high, the same handbreadth-thick wall and lily-worked rim, the same twelve oxen beneath. Only the capacity differs. Kings and Chronicles differ over a number in the same way in the count of Solomon's stalls and in Ahaziah's age.

Commentators have computed the vessel's volume from the sea's own stated dimensions — ten cubits across, five deep, round in compass3 — and reached different results. Keil & Delitzsch cite Thenius's reckoning that "the capacity of the vessel, from the dimensions given, could not exceed 2000 baths."4 Curtis and Madsen, computing with a bath of about 65 pints, judge that "Both estimates appear too large," and Montgomery and Gehman report estimates of the bath ranging from 22 to about 46 litres, and the view of Nowack and Šanda "that too little is known of the form of the vessel to estimate its capacity."5

Josephus, retelling the reign at the end of the first century, follows the larger figure — "this sea contained three thousand baths" — as he follows the larger number in the count of Solomon's stalls. Curtis and Madsen note that the Greek text of 1 Kings, at this verse, is "lacking, however, the statement of the capacity of the tank."6 This page gathers the witnesses earliest first — the book of Kings that gives "two thousand," the Chronicler whose text reads "three thousand," Josephus, and the commentators who explained the difference — and links the readings of why the two books differ under Disputed readings.

Timeline

The book of Kings (c. 560 BC)

The earliest witness to "two thousand" is the book of Kings itself. Describing the last of Hiram's bronze works, 1 Kings 7:26 closes the account of the sea: "it was an hand breadth thick, and the brim thereof was wrought like the brim of a cup, with flowers of lilies: it contained two thousand baths."7 This is the older of the two accounts and a source the Chronicler used. Keil & Delitzsch hold its "two thousand" to be the figure the vessel's dimensions allow, and Ellicott's commentary reports that "Most critics assume this to be correct," though it judges it more likely that "three" dropped out of Kings.8

The Chronicler (c. 400 BC)

Retelling Solomon's temple from Kings, the Chronicler reaches the sea at 2 Chronicles 4:5 — and where Kings has one verb, he has two: the sea "received and held three thousand baths."9 That double verb (maḥăzîq, "holding," with yāḵîl, "it would contain") is read in several ways. Keil & Delitzsch call it "pleonastic," an emphatic way of saying "held," and take the "three thousand" for a copyist's error; Matthew Poole reads it as naming a maximum capacity of three thousand against the two thousand of ordinary use; and Ellicott's commentary takes the second verb for "a gloss borrowed from Kings." Chronicles is the earliest extant attestation of the larger number.

Josephus (c. 93)

Retelling the reign in the Antiquities, Josephus describes the sea as a bronze hemisphere on its twelve oxen and follows the larger figure: "Now this sea contained three thousand baths."10 He attests "three thousand" as the figure for the sea in the late first century, as he attests the larger of the two biblical figures for Solomon's stalls.

Matthew Poole (1685)

Matthew Poole, in his Annotations upon the Holy Bible, keeps both numbers by making them count different fills: Kings "speaks of what it did actually and usually contain, to wit, two thousand baths, which was sufficient for use," while Chronicles "speaks of what it could contain if it were filled to the brim."11 He grounds the reading in the Chronicler's fuller Hebrew — the sea "strengthening itself" to receive and hold "as much as it could" — and John Gill, on the same verse, gives the same solution: "in common, for the use of the priests … it had no more than 2,000 baths in it, but, if filled up to the brim, it would hold 3,000." Whether the two figures measure different fills is one of the readings set out under Disputed readings.

Keil & Delitzsch (1872)

Keil & Delitzsch, in their Biblical Commentary on the Old Testament, on 1 Kings 7:26, judge the "three thousand" a copyist's error and locate it in the numeral-letters: "The latter statement has arisen from the confusion of ג (3) with ב (2); since, according to the calculation of Thenius, the capacity of the vessel, from the dimensions given, could not exceed 2000 baths."12 On their reading Kings keeps the original number and Chronicles the slip, and they give the capacity calculation as their reason. On the parallel verse (2 Chronicles 4:5) they say the same, filing the "3000 baths instead of 2000" among "orthographical errors in the Chronicle." Ellicott, Barnes, and the Cambridge Bible also hold that a numeral has been corrupted: Ellicott's commentary judges it "more likely" that "three" dropped out of Kings, and the Cambridge editors add that "whether the textual corruption is to be attributed to 1 Kin. or to Chron. cannot be determined."
