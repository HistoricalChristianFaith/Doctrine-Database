Judas Iscariot

Judas Iscariot is the member of the Twelve whom the New Testament names as the one who handed Jesus over, and the only one of the Twelve whose death it records twice. He stands last in every list, tagged “who also betrayed him”; John adds that he kept the disciples’ purse and took from it; Matthew fixes his price at thirty pieces of silver. Matthew has him throw the silver into the temple and hang himself, after which the priests buy a potter’s field with the money and it is called “the field of blood”; Acts has Judas acquire a field with “the reward of iniquity” and, “falling headlong,” burst open, the field coming to be called “Aceldama… the field of blood.” Papias, in the early second century, reports a third manner of death, a bodily swelling; and the Middle Ages told a full life-story of Judas. This page sets out, earliest extant source first, how Judas was described and re-described: the New Testament record, Papias’s account of his death, the second-century Gospel of Judas, the medieval tale in the Golden Legend and Dante’s placing of Judas in the Inferno, and modern scholarship on the two death-notices. It is a biographical tradition-timeline: its “witnesses” are the datable sources that report on Judas. For the sources common to all the apostles, see the apostles hub.

Timeline

The New Testament (c. 30; written c. 50–95)

The New Testament gives Judas a surname (“Iscariot,” for which the Hebrew ’îsh-Qêriyôth, “man of Kerioth” in Judah, is one of several proposed derivations), a father (Simon), a role (keeper of the common bag, and, John says, a thief), and an act (handing Jesus over for thirty pieces of silver). It also records his death twice. Matthew has Judas repent, throw the silver into the temple, and “hanged himself”; the priests then buy the potter’s field with the money, so the field is called “the field of blood,” and the whole is tied to a prophecy of “Jeremy.” Acts, in Peter’s speech, has Judas himself “purchase a field with the reward of iniquity; and falling headlong, he burst asunder in the midst, and all his bowels gushed out,” the field named “Aceldama… the field of blood.” Both notices report a violent death and a field called “the field of blood”; they are worded differently on how he died, who acquired the field, and how it came by its name. How the two notices relate is discussed under Modern scholarship below.1

Papias of Hierapolis (c. 100–130)

Papias, bishop of Hierapolis and a writer of the early second century, reports a third account of Judas’s end, surviving as a quotation in Apollinaris of Laodicea: an account of a bodily swelling — “his body having swollen to such an extent that he could not pass where a chariot could pass easily, he was crushed by the chariot, so that his bowels gushed out.” It shares the gushing bowels of Acts and gives a different cause. Theophylact, quoting the passage, adds further particulars (swollen eyes, worms, a stench) “as if they were derived from Papias.”2

The Gospel of Judas (2nd c.; Coptic copy c. 300)

A second-century Gnostic text gives Judas a different role. Irenaeus, about 180, names a “Gospel of Judas” and ascribes it to a group who held that Cain “derived his being from the Power above” (the ANF chapter heading labels them “Cainites”); a Coptic copy was published in 2006. In it Judas is the disciple who can say to Jesus, “you are from the immortal realm of Barbelo,” and to whom Jesus reveals teaching withheld from the others. On the reading published with the 2006 edition, the handing-over is a service Jesus himself commands, freeing his spirit from the flesh; April DeConick reads the Sethian author as condemning Judas instead. Thomas A. Wayment (BYU Studies) dates its composition to the mid-second century, holds it dependent on the canonical Gospels, and judges it “highly unlikely” to contain historically authentic material about Jesus or his disciples.3

Eusebius of Caesarea (early 4th c.)

Eusebius, bishop of Caesarea, in the Proof of the Gospel (Book 10), takes the oracle that Matthew ascribes to “Jeremy” to be Zechariah’s; he notes that the passage “is not found in the prophecy of Jeremiah” and asks whether it had been removed or miscopied.4

The Golden Legend and Dante (c. 1260–1320)

Jacobus de Voragine’s Golden Legend (c. 1260), as the preamble to the life of Matthias, tells a life of Judas before his discipleship: an infant set adrift on the sea after his mother’s dream that he would “destroy their people,” who grows up to kill his own father and unknowingly marry his mother before coming to Christ for mercy. Voragine introduces the story as read “in a history, though it be named apocrypha” and closes it as “the history which is not authentic.” Dante’s Inferno (c. 1320) places Judas in the innermost ring of Hell, named Giudecca, head-first in the central mouth of Satan.5

Modern scholarship

Modern discussion of the two death-notices includes the following views. Kirsopp Lake (1933) read each as shaped by an Old Testament pattern: Matthew’s hanging after the traitor Ahithophel, who “hanged himself” (2 Samuel 17:23), and Acts’ wording after Wisdom 4:19 on the ruin of the ungodly. C. K. Barrett (1994) holds that the two accounts cannot be harmonized, and Bart Ehrman (2015) that they are “at odds with one” another. Darrell Bock (2007) argues against Barrett that a combination is possible, and James Bejon (2021) reads Acts’ prēnēs genomenos as “to become headlong/prostrate,” a state of the body rather than a fall; Peter J. Williams holds that the Geneva Bible of 1560 “probably introduced” the English “headlong.”6
