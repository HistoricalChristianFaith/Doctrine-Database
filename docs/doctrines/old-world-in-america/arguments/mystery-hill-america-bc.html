Mystery Hill (“America’s Stonehenge”): an ancient Old-World megalithic site, or a colonial New England farm?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: weak — on both of its believing sub-claims. The construction claim (that the stone chambers and walls on this Salem, New Hampshire hill were raised by ancient Celts or Phoenicians) does not hold: the chambers match colonial New-England root cellars, the “Sacrificial Table” matches a farm lye-leaching or cider stone, the site sits on the documented Jonathan Pattee farmstead, and its first modern owner admittedly rearranged the stones to fit his theory — so there is no undisturbed ancient context left to read. The inscription claim (that Barry Fell’s Ogham, Punic, and Iberian readings are genuine ancient script) does not hold either: Fell read a vowelless Ogham that can be fitted to almost any set of scratches, and his “letters” resolve into natural granite veins, plough- and quarry-marks, and glacial striae, a method its critics judged not merely mistaken but fraudulent. It earns a real hearing rather than a wave-off, because the hill genuinely is old (radiocarbon puts human activity here about four thousand years ago) and genuinely puzzling in parts. But that antiquity is Native American, not Old-World, and a third sub-claim fails outright: even were the chambers ancient and the marks script, one hilltop complex would be a stray-contact datum, not evidence that Old-World peoples founded the American civilizations (unsupported for the settlement thesis it is enlisted to prove).

Related arguments: the American epigraphic corpus — see also the Bat Creek stone (assessment: weak), the corpus’s one genuinely excavated exhibit, the Los Lunas Decalogue stone (assessment: weak), a surface boulder with no excavated context, and the Paraíba inscription (assessment: weak), a lost stone known only from a copy.

The claim

The megaliths of Mystery Hill … are the ruins of an ancient temple … The inscriptions are written in Ogam, Punic, and Iberian scripts. … The site was dedicated to Bel, the Phoenician sun-god. … Celtic peoples, together with Phoenician traders, had established a colony in New England.

— the interpretation of the site advanced by Barry Fell in America B.C. (paraphrasing his running argument; the book reads the hill’s markings as Ogham and Punic, dedicates its central stone to the sun-god Bel, and reports one inscription as recording a Carthaginian prince’s annexation of part of Massachusetts).1 The interpretation assessed here is that Mystery Hill — a roughly thirty-acre spread of dry-stone chambers, walls, and standing stones on a hill in Salem, New Hampshire — is a pre-Columbian Old-World religious complex, built and inscribed by Bronze- or Iron-Age Celts and Phoenicians, and so, in the hands of the epigraphic diffusionists who cite it, the flagship proof that Old-World peoples reached and settled North America. Three claims are folded together in that reading and must be kept apart: that the structures are ancient Old-World construction; that the markings are genuine ancient script; and that either, if granted, would establish a settlement. Take the case at its strongest before weighing it.

Who advances it

The site entered the pseudo-archaeological literature not with Fell but with William B. Goodwin, a Hartford insurance executive who bought the hill in 1937 and became convinced it had been built by Irish Culdee monks who fled the Norse and reached New England “long before the time of Christopher Columbus.” Goodwin excavated the site to prove it — and, by the judgement of later archaeologists, rearranged many of the stones to fit his theory. The site opened to tourists as “Mystery Hill” in 1958; in 1982 its owner Robert Stone renamed it “America’s Stonehenge” to press its claim to antiquity and astronomy, the name and the claim maintained by the Stone family who still run it.2 The Harvard marine biologist turned epigrapher Barry Fell made the hill the centerpiece of America B.C. (1976), reading its markings as Celtic Ogham and Phoenician Punic and folding it into his larger thesis of a Celto-Iberian–Phoenician New England; it is Fell’s reading, not Goodwin’s Culdee one, that the modern settlement argument leans on.1

The evidence for — the steelman

1. The hill is genuinely old, and the antiquity is real. This is the part that must be granted its full weight, because it is true: sixteen radiocarbon determinations have been run at the site, and the oldest charcoal is more than four thousand years old, with a spread of dates running from about 2000 BC down into the last centuries BC. Something was happening on this hill in deep antiquity — it is not a modern fabrication from nothing.3

2. The site is real, complex, and in parts puzzling. Mystery Hill is not a roadside pile of rocks. It is a substantial dry-stone complex — chambers roofed with multi-ton lintels, a network of walls, a grooved table stone, and perimeter standing stones its promoters read as marking the solstices and equinoxes. A site of that scale and effort invites the question the diffusionists press: who built it, and why does no colonial deed mention raising it?4

3. A fair opponent’s concession. Even a sharp critic can grant that Fell raised a genuine puzzle. The Maya epigrapher David H. Kelley — who catalogued Fell’s “major academic sins… distortion of data, inadequate acknowledgment of predecessors, and lack of presentation of alternative views” — still concluded that “without Fell’s work there would be no [North American] ogham problem to perplex us.” That there is a problem worth adjudicating, rather than a claim beneath notice, is the honest starting point.5

Adversarial assessment

1. The construction is colonial, not ancient. The mainstream reading of the structures is mundane and well-evidenced: they are the works of a nineteenth-century New England farm. The hill sits on the documented homestead of the shoemaker-farmer Jonathan Pattee, whose family lived there in the first half of the 1800s and whose “cave” the local History of Salem already noted in 1907. The chambers match the root and cold cellars ordinary on old farms of the region; the grooved “Sacrificial Table” matches a lye-leaching stone (the first step in making soap) or a cider-press bed stone — the University of New Hampshire archaeologist Meghan Howey notes it “bears the same sort of drainage channels that would be found on a rock slab used to make soap.” Many stones carry the drill-marks of the quarrying that worked the site in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries.4

2. The context an ancient reading needs was destroyed by its own champion. Even were an ancient layer present, it could no longer be read cleanly, because Goodwin dug and rearranged the stones to suit his Culdee theory before any controlled excavation. The archaeologist David Starbuck records the standing judgement: “It is widely believed that Goodwin may have ‘created’ much of what is visible at the site today.” A megalithic complex that a modern enthusiast has rebuilt to his own hypothesis is not a sealed ancient context; it is a disturbed one, and its present arrangement testifies to Goodwin as much as to any builder.2

3. The radiocarbon dates the Native American hill, not the Old-World temple. The steelman’s strongest card — the four-thousand-year charcoal — proves less than it seems, because radiocarbon can only be run on organic material, and there is no organic material in a dry-stone wall. The dates fix human activity on the hilltop, not the building of the structures; and the activity they fix is best read as the Native American presence one expects on a New England hill in the Archaic and Woodland periods. The point is sharpened by the one securely-indigenous find on the site: when David Stewart-Smith excavated a quarried megalith to the north in 1982 under the state archaeologist’s supervision, his team recovered hundreds of stone chips showing the piece was worked by percussion with stone tools — indigenous lithic technique, not the metal tools of a European mason. The material record on the hill, where it speaks at all, speaks Native American.3

4. Fell’s Ogham is over-readable — and the method was judged a fraud. The inscription claim fails on its own terms. Fell read the American markings as an Ogham without vowels — and Ogham, a script of simple parallel strokes crossing a stem-line, becomes infinitely elastic once the vowels are dropped and the reader is free to place the stem-line where he likes. As the lawyers Monroe Oppenheimer and Willard Wirtz showed in the definitive 1989 critique, Fell would take “three perpendicular lines” and make them “an ‘f’ if he constructs his stem-line above them, into a ‘t’ if he places the stem-line below them, or into an ‘n’ if he runs the stem-line across them” — adding the stem-lines the stone lacked wherever a reading required one. A vowelless consonant-string is over-readable by design: WSTVRGN is “West Virginia” or “Was it Vera again?” at the decipherer’s pleasure. Oppenheimer and Wirtz, canvassing academic linguists and archaeologists, concluded the readings were not honest error but a hoax; and at Mystery Hill specifically, Cazeau and Scott (1979) showed that the marks Fell read as a Roman numeral on the “Beltane Stone” are natural granite veins. The Smithsonian’s Ives Goddard and William Fitzhugh gave the book’s verdict in a line: “the arguments of America B.C. are unconvincing.”6

5. No Old-World artifact has ever been found — here or anywhere. The reading asks us to posit a colony of Bronze- or Iron-Age Celts and Phoenicians in New England; a colony leaves things behind. The archaeologist Curtis Runnels states the empty result plainly: “No Bronze Age artifacts have been found there. … In fact, no one has found a single artifact of European origin from that period anywhere in the New World.” The absence is not local to this hill but total across the hemisphere — the same barren shelf that comes up empty for every other exhibit in this corpus.7

6. Even granting it all, the settlement thesis does not follow. Keep the sub-claims apart. Suppose, arguendo, that some ancient people did raise a chamber or scratch a mark on this hill: it would attest at most a visit, not a founding. A single site says nothing about who built the American civilizations, and (the point made against every item in this corpus) a real transplanted people would leave crops, domesticates, metallurgy, a script-tradition, and genes — not one ambiguous stone complex a nineteenth-century farmer could have raised. So the construction/inscription question and the diffusionist conclusion are logically independent, and the second does not follow from the first even at the argument’s most favorable — the same non-sequitur that sinks the settlement argument as a whole.8

Assessment

Weak (sub-claim a: the structures are ancient Old-World construction); weak (sub-claim b: the markings are genuine ancient Ogham/Punic script); unsupported (sub-claim c: either licenses the Old-World-settlement thesis). Mystery Hill gets the fair hearing its scale and its genuine antiquity earn — the hill really was in use four thousand years ago, and Fell really did leave a puzzle worth adjudicating. But adjudicated, both believing readings fail. The most economical account of a complex of New-England dry-stone chambers, a grooved farm table, quarry-marked stones, and a scatter of ambiguous scratches — sitting on a documented colonial homestead, rebuilt by a twentieth-century enthusiast, and “deciphered” by a vowelless method that reads anything into anything — is a Native-American-frequented hill overbuilt as the Pattee farm and mistaken, by wishful excavators and an amateur epigrapher, for a temple. The deep-time antiquity is real but indigenous; the Old-World temple is not there. And even were it, one hilltop is a lone datum, not a founding population — the same non-sequitur that sinks the settlement argument as a whole, and the same barren material shelf that comes up empty for the Book of Mormon’s own claimed civilizations on the Book of Mormon and the material record. The verdict is the mainstream one, reached evenhandedly: the hill is a real and interesting place, but the Old-World reading of it does not stand. It is the Celtic-Ogham companion to the Semitic stones of this corpus — Bat Creek, Los Lunas, and Paraíba — and shares their fate: the stones, not the enthusiasm, decide it.
