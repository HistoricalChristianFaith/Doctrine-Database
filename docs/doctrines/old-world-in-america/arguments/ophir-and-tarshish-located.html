Where did Solomon’s fleet actually sail? Ophir and Tarshish located

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: sound for the interpretation actually at issue — Solomon’s gold-and-silver entrepôts Ophir and Tarshish lay in the Old World, and his fleets sailed toward them the Red-Sea/Indian-Ocean way (east and south) and the Mediterranean way (west), never across the Atlantic. The finer question — which Old-World site each name pins — is genuinely contested (Ophir: Arabia, the Horn of Africa, or India; Tarshish: Tarsus, Sardinia, or Iberian Tartessos), but every serious candidate is in the Old World, so the trans-Atlantic reading fails whichever candidate wins. This page adjudicates the location scholarship in its own right; the wider diffusionist thesis it defeats is weighed on the Old-World settlement argument.

The question

Three short passages describe Solomon’s maritime trade, and a rival reading turns on where they point. He “made a navy of ships in Ezion-geber, which is beside Eloth, on the shore of the Red sea, in the land of Edom,” crewed it with Phoenician sailors lent by Hiram of Tyre, and “they came to Ophir, and fetched from thence gold” (1 Kings 9:26–28; 2 Chronicles 8:17–18). Separately he “had at sea a navy of Tharshish… once in three years came the navy of Tharshish, bringing gold, and silver, ivory, and apes, and peacocks” (1 Kings 10:22; 2 Chronicles 9:21).1

The mainstream reading places both destinations in the Old World. Against it stands the trans-Atlantic reading — that the fleet reached the Americas — whose fullest modern advocate is the Reformed pastor Douglas Wilson:

The phrase “ships of Tarshish” is probably best translated as “refinery ships,” ships equipped to carry smelted ore. In my view, the land of Ophir was most probably Central America.

— Douglas Wilson, “Solomon in All His Glory,” Blog & Mablog.2 The interpretation assessed here is the mainstream one his aside denies: that both entrepôts lay in the Old World. We take it in its strongest form, adjudicate the location scholarship, and rate it.

Two fleets, not one — and a ship-name that is not a place

The first thing the texts settle is that these are two distinct operations, and the distinction by itself sinks any single trans-Atlantic reading. The Ophir fleet is built and berthed at Ezion-geber on the Gulf of Aqaba — the northern tip of the Red Sea — and sails from there. A ship leaving Ezion-geber goes south down the Red Sea toward Arabia, the Horn of Africa, and the Indian Ocean; it cannot reach the Atlantic without a circumnavigation of Africa the text never describes. The “navy of Tarshish,” by contrast, is the Mediterranean trade, reached from a Levantine port and pointed west. One fleet faces the sunrise, the other the sunset; neither faces across the ocean toward America.1

The second thing they settle is that “ships of Tarshish” had already become a vessel-class — a type of large ocean-going merchantman, like a later “East Indiaman” — not necessarily a statement of destination. The proof is internal to the text: Jehoshaphat “made ships of Tharshish to go to Ophir for gold: but they went not; for the ships were broken at Ezion-geber” (1 Kings 22:48) — here “ships of Tarshish” are built on the Red Sea and bound for Ophir, the place-name Tarshish nowhere in view. The Chronicler, retelling the same wreck, reads the class-term as a destination and has them “go to Tarshish” (2 Chronicles 20:36–37) — the very slippage between ship-type and place that muddies the trail. W. F. Albright long ago argued the word denoted a smelter- or ore-ship (a “refinery fleet”), the reading Wilson adopts. But that reading, far from carrying the fleet to America, points it toward the Old-World smelting hubs the ore came from — and in any case it makes “Tarshish” a description of the hull, not a dot on a map across the Atlantic.3

Ophir located: Arabia, the Horn, or India — all reached down the Red Sea

That Ophir was a real gold entrepôt is not in doubt: an eighth-century-BC potsherd from Tell Qasile, near modern Tel Aviv, records a shipment of “gold of Ophir to Beth-horon — 30 shekels,” fixing Ophir as an actual source of imported gold in the Israelite economy.4 Its location has been disputed since antiquity, but the serious candidates reduce to three, and all three lie down the Red Sea and Indian Ocean, exactly where a fleet out of Ezion-geber would go:

  • Arabia. In the Table of Nations Ophir is a son of Joktan, listed between Sheba and Havilah (Genesis 10:28–29) — a south-Arabian genealogy — and classical writers (Pliny, Strabo) knew south-eastern Arabia as gold-bearing. This is the candidate with the fewest extra assumptions, since it need not suppose the Ophir of Kings differs from the Ophir of Genesis.
  • The Horn of Africa. The wares of Ophir parallel those Egyptian pharaohs fetched from Punt (Somaliland), and the three-year round trip fits an East-African run as well as an Arabian coasting one.
  • India. The Septuagint transliterates Ophir as Souphir/Sôphir — the Coptic name for India — and Josephus flatly identified it with “the Aurea Chersonesus, which belongs to India” (the “Golden Peninsula”); the almug-wood and ivory of the cargo suit an Indian-Ocean terminus.

The early church inherited exactly this eastward geography. Bede, digesting Jerome and Josephus in his Commentary on Genesis, notes that “Solomon’s servants went to Ophir by the Red Sea, and brought from there gold, and almug wood, and precious stones, which are believed to be from the region of India, and to have taken their name from Ophir, the son of Joktan.” The oldest Christian reading of the passage already routes the fleet down the Red Sea toward India — the opposite bearing from the Atlantic.56

Tarshish located: a western silver-source, wherever the pin falls

Tarshish is the Mediterranean pole of Solomon’s trade, and the archaeometry ties it to the silver that flowed into the Levant from the west. Lead-isotope analysis of Levantine silver hoards shows that in the tenth century BC the silver came from Anatolia and Sardinia, and that by the ninth and eighth centuries the main source had switched to Iberia — the very direction and commodity the “navy of Tarshish” carries in the text. Tzilla Eshel accordingly argues that “the Tarshish in the story of Solomon and Hiram refers either to Tarsus in Turkey or Tharros in Sardinia, while Jeremiah and Ezekiel are speaking of Tartessos in Spain” — the western Iberian silver kingdom the name most famously denotes. Bede, again following Josephus, had located Tarshish in the Old World from the other end, taking “Tharsis to be Cilicia… hence also their metropolis city is called Tarsus.” Every reckoning — Cilician Tarsus, Sardinian Tharros, Iberian Tartessos, or Albright’s ore-ship — keeps Tarshish in the eastern-to-central-to-western Mediterranean. None reaches the Atlantic.76

Adversarial assessment

What holds. Two things. First, the negative conclusion is secure on any reading: whichever candidate one adopts for either name, it is an Old-World place (or, for Tarshish, possibly a ship-type), and the fleets sail away from the Atlantic — the Ophir navy south down the Red Sea, the Tarshish navy west across the Mediterranean. Second, Wilson’s “refinery ships” etymology is not a blunder: it is a respectable reading (Albright’s) of an obscure phrase. It simply does not carry the weight he puts on it, because a smelter-ship points to the Old-World smelting hubs the ore came from — Iberian Tartessos above all — not to Mesoamerica.

What is overstated. The trans-Atlantic reading survives only by ignoring the plain geography of the launch-point. A fleet built at Ezion-geber on the Gulf of Aqaba is a Red-Sea fleet; to reach Central America it would have to round Africa (an eastward, Old-World coasting voyage attested only three centuries later, under Necho, and disputed even then) or cross an ocean the narrative never mentions. “Ophir = Central America” requires discarding the Table-of-Nations genealogy that seats Ophir among the sons of Joktan beside Sheba and Havilah, the Septuagint and Josephus who read it as India, and the eighth-century Israelite invoice that treats “gold of Ophir” as ordinary imported bullion — a great deal of concrete evidence traded for a resemblance between Mesoamerican and Near-Eastern monumental culture that convergent invention already explains.

The honest residue. The wiki does not pretend the location is settled: the exact site of Ophir is genuinely open between Arabia, the Horn, and India, and Tarshish between Tarsus, Sardinia, and Tartessos (with the ship-type reading live alongside). That internal dispute is real and is rated contested. But it is a dispute within the Old World, and it does not touch the question this page exists to answer. On the interpretation actually at issue — Old World, not the Americas — the evidence converges from every direction: the biblical launch-geography, the Table of Nations, the Septuagint and Josephus, the church’s oldest reading in Bede, the Tell Qasile ostracon, and the lead-isotope trail of the silver.

Assessment

Sound for the interpretation at issue: Solomon’s Ophir and Tarshish lay in the Old World, and his two fleets — the Red-Sea navy out of Ezion-geber bound south and east for Ophir’s gold, and the Mediterranean “navy of Tarshish” bound west for silver — sailed nowhere near the Atlantic. The precise pin for each name remains genuinely contested (Ophir: Arabia / the Horn / India; Tarshish: Tarsus / Sardinia / Tartessos, or a vessel-class), but that is a quarrel among Old-World candidates, and the trans-Atlantic reading loses under all of them. Wilson’s “Ophir = Central America” is not a factual impossibility so much as a reading that discards the launch-geography, the genealogy, the versions, and the archaeology at once — a far-voyage extension that outruns everything the texts and the material record actually say. This is one leg of the wider diffusionist thesis weighed on the Old-World settlement argument, which comes out weak; on this leg the mainstream location holds.
