Did genuine pre-Columbian Polynesia–South America contact occur?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: sound. Set apart from the debunked Semitic-inscription corpus and the weak “Old-World civilizations founded the Americas” thesis, there is a genuinely strong, honestly-established case that some real contact crossed the open Pacific between Polynesia and South America before Columbus. The load-bearing evidence is not a crop or a chicken bone but human genetics: two independent genome-wide studies — one on living eastern Polynesians (2020) and one on ancient Rapanui skeletons directly (2024) — find a shared Native-American ancestry component that both date, by different methods, to the thirteenth–fifteenth centuries, well before any European could have carried it. That two disciplines' worth of props once used to argue contact (the sweet potato, the El Arenal chicken) remain individually contested does not lower the core claim, because the genetics now carry it on their own. Keep the finding rigorously bounded: it shows a brief meeting of seafaring peoples, not that an Old-World or a South-American civilization founded the other — that larger claim is the one weighed on the flagship settlement page and rated weak. This is also the partial vindication of Thor Heyerdahl: right that a Pacific contact happened, wrong about its direction and about the moai.

Related arguments: genuine trans-Pacific contact — see also the sweet potato that ran the other way (assessment: contested), the botanical facet of the same contact, and the El Arenal chicken (assessment: contested), its faunal facet.

See also: the flagship Old-World-settlement argument (weak; unsupported for Solomon), whose sound feasibility premise this page establishes and whose non-sequitur founding claim it does not; and the Easter Island moai argument (weak), which folds this same contact datum as a “narrow contrary” point against Heyerdahl's lost-civilization reading.

The claim

The interpretation assessed here is deliberately modest and precisely scoped: a genuine, human-mediated contact event occurred between Polynesians and Native South Americans at some point before 1492. It is not the claim that South Americans peopled Polynesia (Heyerdahl's thesis, which the linguistic, archaeological, and genetic record refutes — Polynesia was settled west-to-east out of island Southeast Asia by Austronesian navigators), and it is not the claim that either side founded, seeded, or civilized the other. It is only that the two peoples met and mingled once, briefly, across the widest ocean on Earth — the responsible core that the hyper-diffusionist thesis overreaches from. For most of the twentieth century this modest claim rested on soft props (a shared crop, a contested chicken bone) and was “as hotly contested today as when first suggested.” The reason it is now rated sound is that the props have been overtaken by direct genetic evidence.1

The evidence for

1. The genetics of living eastern Polynesians (2020). Alexander Ioannidis and colleagues genotyped more than eight hundred living Indigenous people from Pacific islands and the Americas and found a common, low-level signal of Native-American (specifically Colombian-related) ancestry running across the peoples of eastern Polynesia — the Marquesas, Tuamotus, Mangareva, Palliser, and Rapa Nui. Because the same admixed segments recur across islands that were settled at different times, the signal traces to a single ancestral contact event, which their linkage-based dating places at about AD 1150–1230 — before Rapa Nui itself was settled, and centuries before any European reached the Pacific. A single shared source and an early date are exactly the fingerprints of one real prehistoric meeting, not of post-contact European-era mixing.2

2. Ancient Rapanui genomes confirm it directly (2024). The 2020 result inferred an ancient event from modern DNA; the obvious worry — that the Native-American ancestry entered after European contact and was only projected backward — was closed four years later. J. Víctor Moreno-Mayar and colleagues sequenced the whole genomes of fifteen ancient Rapanui individuals and detected about 10% Native-American admixture (and, tellingly, no European admixture) in all of them — a pattern impossible if the mixing had happened after Europeans arrived in 1722. Using a joint genetic-and-radiocarbon method they dated the admixture to about AD 1250–1430. Two independent studies, one on the living and one on the dead, converging by different methods on a pre-European thirteenth-to-fifteenth-century contact, is as firm as this kind of prehistory gets.3

3. The sweet potato: a South-American crop cultivated in pre-European Polynesia. The oldest independent prop remains real and pulls the same way. A South-American domesticate, Ipomoea batatas, was under cultivation across eastern Polynesia before any European could have carried it, and much of Polynesia calls it by an Andean-echoing name (kumara ↔ Quechua k'umar). Its full adversarial weighing — including the 2018 whole-genome counter that the plant could have arrived by natural long-distance dispersal — is on its own page (assessment: contested), so it is not re-argued here. What matters for the present claim is only that the crop no longer has to carry the burden of proving contact — the genetics do that — and that a real human meeting dated to almost exactly the window in which the cultivated crop appears makes human transfer of the tuber the parsimonious reading.4

4. The El Arenal chicken: a faunal prop, weighed on its own page. The most-cited faunal prop is a cache of chicken bones from the El Arenal-1 site on the Arauco Peninsula of south-central Chile, reported in 2007 as pre-Columbian in date and carrying (on an ancient-DNA reading) a Polynesian haplotype — on its face a Polynesian chicken in the Americas before Columbus. Its full adversarial weighing is on its own page (assessment: contested), so it is not re-argued here. What matters for the present claim is only the split it resolves into: the dating sub-claim (the bones are genuinely pre-Columbian) has largely held — a marine-reservoir objection was answered by sulphur-isotope data, and a 2023 re-excavation reconfirmed the chronology — while the Polynesian-origin sub-claim is contested (the haplotype was challenged as laboratory contamination and the re-excavation found South-American Neuquén rather than Pacific connections, though Storey and Matisoo-Smith still defend Polynesia). Since a chicken cannot cross an ocean unaided, a pre-Columbian bird still implies a human crossing; so the datum corroborates that a contact happened even as its Polynesian attribution stays disputed — and, either way, it is no longer load-bearing here, the genetics having carried the core claim.5

5. What a confirmed crossing does and does not license: L'Anse aux Meadows. The one Old-World contact with the Americas that is archaeologically confirmed is on the other ocean: the Norse turf-and-timber settlement at L'Anse aux Meadows, Newfoundland, a securely-dated eleventh-century site whose worked wood is fixed by dendrochronology to exactly AD 1021. It is worth setting beside the Pacific evidence precisely because it draws the boundary of the claim. A real crossing happened; a real camp was built; and it founded no civilization — it was abandoned, left a tiny footprint, and altered nothing in the Indigenous Americas. That is the exact limit the Pacific contact also respects and the settlement page insists on: contact of this kind is a brief meeting of seafaring peoples, not a founding migration. “Contact happened” and “a civilization was founded by contact” are different claims, and only the first is sound.6

Adversarial assessment

The direction of travel is genuinely open — and it does not weaken the claim. The genetics fix that Polynesians and South Americans mixed, not who reached whom. The leading reconstruction has Polynesian voyagers reaching the South-American coast (near modern Colombia) and carrying Native-American companions or ancestry back into the islands; a minority possibility is that a drifting South-American craft reached Polynesia. Ioannidis himself conceded the ambiguity: “whether the people were physically standing on an island in Polynesia… or… on the coast of South America, we can't say.” But this openness is about the mechanism, not the fact: a contact occurred on either reading. The one reading the evidence does exclude is Heyerdahl's — that South Americans peopled Polynesia — because the deep ancestry of every Polynesian population, Rapa Nui included, is overwhelmingly west-Pacific Austronesian, with the Native-American component a thin late admixture layered on top.7

The partial vindication of Heyerdahl. Thor Heyerdahl's 1947 Kon-Tiki raft voyage and his Easter Island work argued for a real trans-Pacific link between South America and Polynesia, and for that he was mocked for half a century as the archetype of the amateur diffusionist. The honest verdict is a split decision. He was right that a genuine pre-Columbian contact crossed that ocean — the very thing the 2020 and 2024 genomes now establish — and right that the sweet potato was a real clue to it. He was wrong on the two things he cared most about: the direction (Polynesia was settled from the west, not from South America) and the monuments (the Easter Island moai are the work of the historical Rapa Nui, carved from soft local tuff and “walked” into place, needing no South-American engineers — see the moai page). Crediting the one true instinct while holding the errors is the point: the contact is sound, the lost-civilization frame he hung on it is not.7

Symmetry of skepticism. Rating this sound while rating the sweet potato contested, the chicken's Polynesian attribution doubtful, and the founding thesis weak is not special pleading — it is the props sorting themselves by evidential quality. The soft botanical and faunal props are genuinely disputed and are labelled so; what changed is that a hard, independent, twice-replicated line of evidence arrived that does not depend on any of them. A claim can be sound on its best evidence even while several of the arguments historically made for it stay contested; conversely, granting this modest contact does nothing to rescue the maximal claim built on the same premise, which still needs an Old-World imprint in the American record that is simply absent. Possibility is not proof in both directions: the genetics prove a meeting, and prove only a meeting.8

Assessment

Sound. That a genuine, human-mediated pre-Columbian contact occurred between Polynesians and Native South Americans is now well-grounded and, after two independent genome-wide studies converging on a thirteenth-to-fifteenth-century date, close to consensus — the strongest single line being direct ancient-DNA evidence from Rapanui skeletons carrying Native-American but no European ancestry. Keep the sub-claims sorted: the human-genetic case is sound; the sweet potato is genuine but contested and weighed on its own page; the El Arenal chicken is pre-Columbian in date but its Polynesian attribution is doubtful; the direction of the crossing is honestly open. None of that lowers the core finding, because the genetics carry it without them. What the finding does not license is the leap the flagship page rates weak — that contact founded a civilization; L'Anse aux Meadows, the one confirmed Old-World landfall, is the standing proof that a real crossing can found nothing at all. Heyerdahl was right that the ocean was crossed and wrong about almost everything he inferred from it. This is the rare datum on this timeline where the trans-oceanic-contact claim, kept to its evidenced and modest form, simply holds.
