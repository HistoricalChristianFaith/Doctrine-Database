The El Arenal chicken: a Polynesian bird in pre-Columbian Chile?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested. A cache of chicken bones from the El Arenal-1 site on the Arauco Peninsula of south-central Chile was reported in 2007 as directly-dated, pre-Columbian, and — on an ancient-DNA reading — carrying a “Polynesian” haplotype: on its face a Polynesian chicken in the Americas before Columbus, a faunal line of contact evidence independent of the human genome. The claim must be split into two sub-claims that have fared very differently. The dating sub-claim (the bones are genuinely pre-Columbian) has largely held — a marine-reservoir objection was answered by sulphur-isotope data, and a 2023 re-excavation reconfirmed a pre-Columbian chronology. The Polynesian-origin sub-claim is contested: the diagnostic value of the mtDNA haplotype was challenged as laboratory contamination, and the 2023 dig found the site’s wider connections running to South-American Neuquén (Argentina), not to Polynesia — yet Storey and Matisoo-Smith continue to defend Polynesia as the likeliest source, and a chicken (an Old-World domesticate that cannot cross an ocean unaided) still requires some pre-Columbian trans-oceanic vector if the date holds. Hence contested: strong as evidence that a crossing happened, genuinely disputed as evidence that it was a Polynesian one.

Related arguments: genuine trans-Pacific contact — see also whether genuine pre-Columbian Polynesia–South America contact occurred (assessment: sound), the human-genetic case this bird would corroborate, and the sweet potato that ran the other way (assessment: contested), the botanical facet of the same contact.

The claim

The interpretation assessed here is that the El Arenal-1 chicken bones are archaeological evidence — faunal, not genetic-human — of a pre-Columbian Polynesian contact with the South-American coast. The chicken (Gallus gallus) is a Southeast-Asian domesticate that reached the Americas, on the standard account, only with the Spanish and Portuguese after 1500. A securely pre-Columbian chicken in Chile would therefore be a bird that crossed an ocean before Columbus — and because chickens were a core commensal that Polynesian voyagers carried across the Pacific, the natural reading is that Polynesians brought it. Taken at its strongest this is a second, independent line of trans-Pacific contact evidence alongside the sweet potato and the human genetics: a physical animal, directly radiocarbon-dated, with (as originally reported) a Pacific genetic signature to match.1 It is a datum the genuine-contact page steelmans in passing but does not rate; this page weighs it and rates it.

Who advances it

The claim was made by Alice A. Storey, José Miguel Ramírez, Daniel Quiroz, Elizabeth A. Matisoo-Smith, and colleagues in PNAS in 2007, working the “commensal” approach — tracing human movement through the animals people carried with them — that Matisoo-Smith had developed for the Pacific rat and other voyaging species. It was quickly contested (Jaime Gongora and colleagues in 2008; Vicki Thomson and colleagues in 2014), and just as quickly defended (Nancy Beavan; Storey and Matisoo-Smith’s replies), and it remains a live dispute: the same team continues to argue that no published objection has actually overturned the pre-Columbian, plausibly-Pacific reading, while critics hold the specifically-Polynesian identification unproven.2

The evidence for

1. A direct pre-Columbian radiocarbon date. El Arenal-1, three kilometres inland on the southern side of the Arauco Peninsula and first excavated in 2002, yielded chicken bones from a securely archaeological context. A bone was directly radiocarbon-dated, and at two standard deviations the calibrated range (about AD 1304–1424) falls entirely before the first documented European arrival on that coast — a directly-dated pre-Columbian chicken, not an inference from associated material.1

2. The date survives the contamination and diet-offset objections. The standard way to sink such a date is a marine-reservoir effect: if the birds ate seafood, old ocean carbon would make the bone read older than it is, potentially pulling a post-Columbian bird into the pre-Columbian range. Nancy Beavan showed this does not apply here. The δ13C and δ15N values indicate a low-protein terrestrial diet, and the δ34S value (2.16‰) falls in the terrestrial range — unequivocally excluding a marine dietary component, and so any marine-reservoir offset. On the isotopes, the pre-Columbian date is not an artefact of what the chickens ate.3

3. A 2023 re-excavation reconfirmed the pre-Columbian chronology. Because a single site resting on one contested date is fragile, a new archaeological campaign returned to El Arenal-1 expressly to refine the chronology and recover more material. Its central chronological result held: the site is genuinely pre-Columbian. The brute fact the whole argument needs — a chicken in South America before Columbus — was independently reconfirmed a second time.4

4. Chickens cannot self-disperse — so a pre-Columbian date forces a human crossing. This is the structural strength the chicken has that the sweet potato lacks. The sweet potato has a natural-dispersal escape hatch: seeds of Ipomoea can drift or blow across the Pacific unaided, so the plant’s bare presence in Polynesia need not mean people crossed. A chicken has no such hatch. It is a managed domesticate that cannot fly oceans, drift on a log, or ride a bird’s gut; if a chicken bone is genuinely pre-Columbian and genuinely in Chile, then a human being carried a live bird across water to get it there. On the dating sub-claim’s own terms the datum therefore points at contact by construction — the only open question is whose crossing.5

Adversarial assessment

What holds: the dating. After the isotope reply and the 2023 re-excavation, the pre-Columbian date is the strong end of this argument. The marine-reservoir objection — the one that could have collapsed the whole case — was met with direct sulphur-isotope evidence, and independent re-excavation reconfirmed the chronology. There is genuine, twice-tested reason to accept that a chicken was present at El Arenal-1 before Columbus.4

What is contested: the specifically-Polynesian origin. The 2007 case rested heavily on an ancient-DNA sequence read as a diagnostic Polynesian haplotype. That prop is the one that failed. Vicki Thomson and colleagues argued in 2014, from a large ancient-DNA study of Pacific chickens, that the sequence in question is not uniquely Polynesian at all: it is a globally-distributed haplogroup found in modern European and Asian birds, and its appearance in the El Arenal sample is most economically read as contamination by modern chicken DNA in laboratory reagents rather than as a genetic fingerprint of the Pacific. Jaime Gongora and colleagues had earlier (2008) placed Chilean and Pacific chicken mtDNA within broadly Indo-European and Asian lineages, undercutting a clean Polynesia-only signal. And the 2023 re-excavation, which reconfirmed the date, found no positive evidence of Polynesian contact; the site’s discernible interregional connections ran instead to Neuquén, in Argentina — South-American, not trans-Pacific.6

Why it stays contested rather than weak. Two things keep the Polynesian reading alive. First, it is genuinely, independently advocated: Storey and Matisoo-Smith answered the contamination critique point by point and maintain that linguistic, archaeological, and ethnohistoric considerations still make Polynesia the likeliest source of a pre-Columbian South-American chicken, even conceding that direct genetic proof of the link is now lacking. Second, and more structurally, the dating sub-claim does most of the Polynesian reading’s work for it. A pre-Columbian chicken in Chile had to be carried across an ocean by someone (evidence-for §4), and Polynesians — documented long-range Pacific voyagers who kept chickens as a voyaging commensal, and who demonstrably reached South-American ancestry into their own gene pool by about AD 1200 — are the only serious candidate vector on offer. The fall of the haplotype removes the positive genetic identification; it does not supply a rival crosser. So the honest verdict differentiates by sub-claim: the specific “this is a genetically Polynesian bird” identification is doubtful, but “a pre-Columbian chicken here implies a Polynesian introduction” remains the most parsimonious reading of the securely-dated fact, and it converges with, rather than carries, the genetically-established contact.7

Symmetry of skepticism. Rating this contested — not sound like the human genetics, not weak like the forged-inscription corpus elsewhere on this timeline — is the evidence sorting itself. The dating deserves acceptance because it was tested and survived; the Polynesian genetic identification deserves doubt because its one diagnostic prop was shown non-diagnostic and its re-excavation turned up an Argentine, not a Pacific, connection. It would be special pleading to wave the contamination worry away by authority, and equally so to treat the bird as decisively Polynesian on a haplotype that also sits in European coops. What the datum cannot do, on any reading, is what the settlement thesis needs: even a genuine Polynesian chicken would attest a brief meeting of seafaring peoples, not a founding migration — one crossing, not a colony.7

Assessment

Contested. Keep the two sub-claims sorted. The dating sub-claim — a genuinely pre-Columbian chicken at El Arenal-1 — is well-grounded: the marine-reservoir objection was answered by sulphur-isotope data and the chronology was reconfirmed by a 2023 re-excavation. The Polynesian-origin sub-claim is genuinely disputed: the ancient-DNA sequence once read as a diagnostic Polynesian haplotype is a cosmopolitan lineage open to a contamination reading (Thomson et al.), and the re-excavation found South-American (Neuquén) rather than Pacific connections — yet Storey and Matisoo-Smith still defend Polynesia as the likeliest source, and a chicken, unable to cross an ocean unaided, still needs a human carrier if the date holds. The interpretation the argument serves — a Polynesian chicken evidencing pre-Columbian contact — is therefore stronger as evidence that a trans-Pacific crossing occurred (where it converges with the genetically-secured contact) than as evidence of a specifically Polynesian one carried on the bird’s own genetics. That split — a sound date, a doubtful genetic identity, and a live, still-advocated dispute over the vector — is exactly what contested names.
