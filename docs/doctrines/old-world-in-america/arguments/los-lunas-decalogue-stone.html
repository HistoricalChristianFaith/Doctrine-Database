The Los Lunas Decalogue stone: ancient palaeo-Hebrew, or a modern carving?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: weak — the ancient-Semitic reading does not hold on its best available case: the inscription blends letter-forms from alphabets a thousand years and a sea apart (archaic palaeo-Hebrew, post-Alexandrian Greek, and later Samaritan) that no single ancient scribe ever used together, follows the Masoretic wording in a hand dressed up as Samaritan, and rests its one dating argument on a re-weathering estimate fatally compromised by decades of scrubbing. Its provenance is a single mid-twentieth-century sighting by an archaeologist later dogged by fraud allegations. It nonetheless earns an honest hearing rather than a wave-off, because a serious defender still presses it and a professional geologist did argue for antiquity. And a second sub-claim fails outright: even were it authentic, one inscription on one New Mexico boulder would be a stray-contact curiosity, not evidence that Old-World peoples founded the American civilizations (unsupported for the settlement thesis it is enlisted to prove).

Related arguments: the American epigraphic corpus — see also the Bat Creek stone (assessment: weak), the corpus's one genuinely excavated exhibit, and the Paraíba inscription (assessment: weak), a lost stone known only from a copy.

The claim

… the Decalogue inscription is clearly many times older than this [1930] graffito, and that 500 to 2000 years would not be an unreasonable estimate of its age … [its apparent freshness owing to its having been] chalked and scrubbed up [in modern times].

— the geologist George E. Morehouse, as reported by J. Huston McCulloch, arguing from the re-weathering of the incised grooves that the carving is ancient.1 The interpretation assessed here is that the Los Lunas stone — a basalt boulder on the slope of Hidden Mountain, some 35 miles south of Albuquerque, carrying an abridged Ten Commandments in nine lines — is a genuine ancient Semitic inscription, and so, in the hands of Barry Fell and the epigraphic diffusionists who cite it, evidence that Old-World Hebrews or Phoenicians reached pre-Columbian America. Unlike the Bat Creek stone, it was never excavated from a dated context: it is a surface-exposed boulder, first reported in the twentieth century, and that is the case to take at its strongest before weighing it.

Who advances it

The stone entered the record in 1933, when the University of New Mexico archaeologist Frank C. Hibben said he was led to it by a guide who claimed to have seen it as a boy in the 1880s; the earliest written notice is from 1936.2 Its defenders form a chain. Robert H. Pfeiffer of Harvard's Semitic Museum inspected it in 1949 and read it as an abridged Decalogue in a mixture of Moabite, Greek, and ancient Phoenician letters. The Brandeis Semitist Cyrus H. Gordon — the same scholar who championed Bat Creek — proposed it was a Samaritan mezuzah (a large inscribed gateway stone), dating it to around AD 390. The Harvard-zoologist-turned-epigrapher Barry Fell folded it into the Iberian-Punic diffusionism of America B.C., arguing its punctuation was consistent with antiquity. The professional geologist George E. Morehouse, Fell's colleague, supplied the weathering-age estimate above; and the economist J. Huston McCulloch — again, as at Bat Creek — has curated and defended the technical case.1

The evidence for — the steelman

1. A recognizably Semitic Decalogue. The base script is genuinely the Old-Hebrew (palaeo-Hebrew) alphabet, close kin to Phoenician, and the text is a real, if compressed, rendering of Exodus 20 — a four-fold Tetragrammaton included. This is not random scratching; whoever cut it knew the Ten Commandments and a West-Semitic script.1

2. The weathering argument for antiquity. This is the argument's real dating claim and should be granted its strongest form. Morehouse, a working geologist, compared the re-darkening (desert-varnish/patina) inside the Decalogue's grooves with that of a nearby graffito dated 1930 and judged the Decalogue “many times older,” offering 500–2000 years — and he pre-empted the obvious objection (that the grooves look fresh) by attributing their freshness to repeated modern chalking and scrubbing.1

3. Samaritan letter-forms and a coherent context. Gordon's case is more than an assertion of age: the yodh, qoph, and flat-bottomed shin carry distinctively Samaritan forms, which let him read the stone as a Samaritan diaspora artifact — and Samaritan shipowners are attested in the Greek-speaking Mediterranean of late antiquity, giving the reading a milieu rather than a miracle.1

Adversarial assessment

1. An alphabet no ancient scribe ever wrote — the decisive blow. Apply the honest dating test for an out-of-place inscription: name the letter-forms and see whether they cohere at any one date. They do not. The hand substitutes Greek tau, zeta, delta, eta, and (reversed) kappa for the Hebrew taw, zayin, daleth, heth, and caph — forms that cannot predate Alexander — while also using an archaic form of aleph and later Samaritan forms elsewhere.1 Character by character, Mark Perkins and Titus Kennedy find shapes spanning eleventh-century-BC Phoenician, seventh-century-AD Samaritan, and ancient Greek at once — an impossible combination for a single authentic artifact, but exactly what a modern copyist assembling letters from a reference table would produce; they judge the stone made after the nineteenth-century rediscovery of Phoenician script, with modern tools, by a hand with little or no working Hebrew, sometime c. 1880s–1930s.3 The palaeographer Frank Moore Cross — among the foremost readers of ancient Semitic scripts — likewise rejected its authenticity, and the British archaeologist Keith Fitzpatrick-Matthews calls it “a clear, but well constructed forgery (for its day).”4

2. The text is Masoretic in a Samaritan disguise — which sinks Gordon's own reading. The inscription reads “remember the Sabbath day” — the Masoretic wording — not the Samaritan “preserve the Sabbath day,” and it omits the Samaritan commandment mandating a temple on Mount Gerizim. As Reinhard Pummer observes, no genuine Samaritan mezuzah would follow the Masoretic text almost letter-for-letter while wearing partly Samaritan letter-forms; the mismatch is the fingerprint of a modern carver working from a printed Hebrew Bible and dressing the result up in old-looking script.1 Fell's appeal to the “punctuation” cuts the same way: the marks he read as ancient sentence-points look instead like a modern convention (ancient West-Semitic texts use word-dividers, as on the Mesha stele, not full-stops).2

3. The weathering date is unrecoverable — and was never a secure method. Do not unseat a date on bare possibility, but do weigh how the method actually behaves here. Patina/rock-varnish re-darkening is a notoriously variable clock even in expert hands, and Morehouse's expertise was mining geology, not rock-art dating. Decisively, the very scrubbing and chalking he invokes to explain the grooves' freshness — reportedly by generations of visitors, Boy Scouts among them — will itself have stripped and disturbed the patina, destroying the one surface a re-weathering estimate would need; Barry Fell himself conceded the cleaning kept the inscription “fresh.”5 A comparison to a 1930 graffito of unknown depth and exposure cannot bear the weight of a 500–2000-year verdict. The date is not proven modern by the varnish; it is simply unrecoverable from it — so the antiquity claim gets no support from the one physical method offered for it, and must stand on the letters, which condemn it.

4. No provenance, and a compromised sole witness. The stone has no excavated context at all: it is a surface boulder, and its documentary history begins with Hibben's 1933 sighting and a second-hand tale of an 1880s guide. That single-witness pedigree is weak on its own, and weaker still because palaeo-Hebrew and Phoenician script were already published and available to scholars by about 1870 — so even a genuine 1880s carving need not be ancient. It is weaker again because Hibben's later career is shadowed by well-documented fraud allegations: his Sandia Cave “discoveries” are widely thought to have been salted, and a separate Alaskan claim proved false. This does not convict him of carving the stone — he was not known to manufacture artifacts wholesale, and the stone supports none of the theories he was attached to — but it removes the reliability a single-witness provenance depends on.6 That proponents cannot even agree on the language (one UNM reader took it for Cypriot Greek, not Hebrew at all) tells the same story.2

5. The empty context around it. Kenneth Feder presses the archaeologist's decisive point: a people who reached New Mexico and paused to carve the Decalogue would have left everything else too — camps, hearths, broken pots, iron tools, Old-World livestock, and much more writing. “Nowhere in the history of the world do people leave one inscription and nothing else.” Around Los Lunas there is exactly one inscription and nothing else, which is the signature not of a lost colony but of a lone modern carving.7

6. Even a genuine stone would not reach the settlement thesis. Keep the two sub-claims apart. Grant, arguendo, that the reading is ancient Semitic: one inscribed boulder in New Mexico would attest at most a single improbable journey — a stray-contact curiosity — and would say nothing about who built the Puebloan or Mesoamerican cultures. A lone artifact is not a founding population; no crops, domesticates, metallurgy, script-tradition, or genes travel with it. So the inscription's authenticity and the diffusionist conclusion are logically independent, and the second does not follow from the first even at the argument's most favorable. This is why mainstream Latter-day Saint scholarship, too, treats the stone as fraudulent rather than as support for a Hebrew America.8

Assessment

Weak (sub-claim a: the inscription is genuinely ancient Semitic); unsupported (sub-claim b: it licenses the Old-World-settlement thesis). The Los Lunas stone gets the fair, adversarial hearing a real geologist's dating argument and a tenacious defender earn — but the interpretation does not hold on its best available case. The single most economical account of a boulder bearing the Masoretic Decalogue, cut in an alphabet that mixes archaic Hebrew, post-Alexandrian Greek, and later Samaritan letter-forms no ancient scribe combined, and dressed in modern-looking punctuation, is a modern hand working from a printed Bible and a script table; the one physical dating method offered (varnish re-weathering) is unrecoverable after generations of scrubbing and was never secure; and the provenance is a single sighting by a witness whose record cannot carry it. And even were the stone vindicated, it would remain a lone datum — the same non-sequitur that sinks the settlement argument as a whole, and the empty context around it (Feder's point) is the same barren material shelf that comes up empty for the Book of Mormon's own claimed script on the reformed-Egyptian argument. The verdict is the mainstream one, reached evenhandedly: a live dispute in the sense that a defender still presses it, but not an open one in the sense of standing an even chance. It sits a notch below its neighbor Bat Creek, which at least had a genuine excavated provenance; Los Lunas has none.
