Does the sweet potato evidence pre-Columbian human contact across the Pacific?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested. The South-American sweet potato (Ipomoea batatas) was under cultivation across eastern Polynesia before any European could have carried it there, and much of Polynesia calls it by a name — kumara — that echoes the Andean Quechua/Aymara k'umar(a). For a century this was the flagship botanical proof of genuine pre-Columbian human contact across the Pacific. It is a genuinely disputed scientific question, not a settled one: a 2018 whole-genome study argued the plant could have reached Polynesia by natural long-distance dispersal, with no human hand at all. Keep two sub-claims apart — the linguistic parallel (kumara ↔ k'umar) and the botanical/genetic transfer of the crop itself. On its own the sweet potato does not prove human contact (the natural-dispersal mechanism for the plant's bare presence is real and unrefuted); but it is strong evidence, and once an independent 2020 genome study fixed an actual Native-American–Polynesian admixture event to about AD 1200, human transfer of the crop became the more probable reading of the whole picture. Strong by archaeology's, linguistics', and population-genetics' lights; genuinely challenged by the botany — hence contested.

Related arguments: genuine trans-Pacific contact — see also whether genuine pre-Columbian Polynesia–South America contact occurred (assessment: sound), the human-genetic case this crop is one facet of.

The claim

The sweet potato is a New-World domesticate: it was bred from wild Ipomoea ancestors in tropical America, and at European contact it was grown nowhere in the Old World — except, unaccountably, across a broad sweep of Polynesia, from the Cook Islands to New Zealand to Hawaii to Rapa Nui. The interpretation assessed here is the long mainstream one, the “tripartite hypothesis”: the crop reached Oceania in three separate waves, the first of which — the “kumara line” — was carried by Polynesian voyagers directly from north-western South America some centuries before Columbus, later joined by two post-1500 European introductions (a Spanish “camote” line by way of Mexico and the Philippines, and a Portuguese “batata” line by way of the Caribbean). On this reading the pre-European sweet potato is the single hardest piece of biological evidence that human beings crossed the open Pacific between the Andes and Polynesia before 1492.1 It is the datum the flagship Old-World-settlement argument grants in passing but does not weigh; this page weighs it.

The evidence for

1. The crop was demonstrably in Polynesia before Europeans. This is not an inference but an excavation. Carbonised sweet-potato tissue was recovered from the Tangatatau rock-shelter on Mangaia, in the southern Cook Islands, and identified by John Hather and Patrick Kirch — the earliest direct archaeological find of the crop in central Polynesia. Long bracketed to about AD 1000–1100 on the original radiocarbon chronology, the context has since been re-dated by uranium-series and short-lived-sample modelling to roughly AD 1361–1466; either way it is firmly pre-European, and the later date in fact sits closer to the independently-dated contact window discussed below. Comparable pre-contact experimentation with the crop is now modelled at the cool southern margins of its range in New Zealand.2 A South-American cultigen was thus being grown, and named, in the eastern Pacific before any European ship reached it.

2. The genetics of the oldest lineage point to South America. Working from historical herbarium collections that predate the modern reshuffling of the crop's varieties, Caroline Roullier and colleagues recovered the signal of that earliest “kumara” line and tied its chloroplast and nuclear markers to the Peru–Ecuador region of South America, distinct from the later European-mediated introductions — the genetic form of the tripartite model, and consistent with a prehistoric human-carried transfer of a specific South-American stock into Oceania.1

3. The name travelled with the plant. Across much of Polynesia the sweet potato is kumara (Māori kūmara, Rapa Nui kumara, reconstructed to a Proto-Eastern-Polynesian *kumara; Hawaiian ‘uala is a regular reflex of the same root), and this closely matches the word for the plant in the Quechua and Aymara languages of the Andes, recorded as k'umar(a) or cumar. A wild seed drifting ashore does not arrive carrying an Andean-sounding name; a shared word is a fingerprint of contact between people. The obvious deflationary move — that kumara is really a Polynesian word carried into South America by later European-era voyagers — is blocked by chronology: the Andean cumar is attested in colonial Chincha sources in print by 1586, too early for such a back-transfer. The parallel is not decisive on its own — it rests essentially on a single word-pair, and a lone lexical look-alike across two unrelated language families can be coincidence (a fair statistical worry, since one may not then trawl for further matches to prop up the first) — but it is specific, and it is exactly the kind of trace natural dispersal cannot supply.3

The counter-case: natural long-distance dispersal

The strongest objection is not a quibble about any one strand but a rival mechanism for the whole thing. In 2018 Pablo Muñoz-Rodríguez, Robert Scotland, and colleagues published a whole-genome (nuclear and chloroplast) phylogeny of the sweet potato and its wild relatives. Two results bear on this question. First, the crop is an autopolyploid whose sole close wild relative is Ipomoea trifida — no second, Pacific-side progenitor is involved. Second, and more pointedly, the Polynesian lineage — sampled from a specimen collected in the Society Islands by Joseph Banks on Cook's first voyage in 1769, before the crop's varieties were globally scrambled — diverged from its South-American relatives on the order of 100,000 years ago, vastly earlier than any human presence in the remote Pacific. On that timescale the plant cannot have been carried by Polynesians, who did not exist as a seafaring people until the last two millennia; it must have arrived on its own. And the genus supplies a plausible vehicle: Ipomoea is a proven ocean-crosser, with several morning-glory species (e.g. I. littoralis, I. tuboides) naturally distributed across the Pacific by sea, wind, or birds. If the sweet potato reached Polynesia the same way, then it is no evidence of human contact at all — and since the parallel evidence once drawn from Polynesian chickens and from early human-DNA claims had also come under doubt, the plant was, on this telling, the last biological prop of the contact theory to fall.4

Adversarial assessment

What the natural-dispersal result does and does not settle. The 2018 phylogeny is real science and its central botanical point stands: the wild lineage ancestral to the Polynesian sweet potato split from its South-American kin long before humans, and Ipomoea can cross oceans unaided. But a divergence date is the age of a lineage split, not the arrival date of the cultivated tuber that Polynesians were digging and planting. Two facts the ancient-natural-arrival model does not, by itself, explain remain on the table: the pre-European crop was domesticated and under cultivation, not a wild strand-plant growing feral; and it travelled with an Andean name. A seed that floated in a hundred millennia ago accounts for neither the cultivation nor the word. So the honest split is this: the presence of the plant in Polynesia can, in principle, be natural; the presence of a named crop of South-American stock under Polynesian cultivation is much more economically explained by people. The 2018 authors themselves fielded a wave of published criticism on exactly this point and issued a detailed defence of their reasoning — a sign the dispute is live, not closed.4

The outside datum that broke the deadlock. The sweet-potato debate no longer runs on the sweet potato alone. In 2020 Alexander Ioannidis and colleagues found a common, low-level signal of Native-American ancestry across the peoples of eastern Polynesia and dated the single admixture event to about AD 1150–1230 — independent, human-genetic proof that Polynesians and South Americans actually met, and interbred, before Easter Island was even settled. That result does not prove the sweet potato rode that particular contact, but it dissolves the deadlock in two ways: it removes any need to make the plant carry the whole burden of proving contact happened (it demonstrably did), and it supplies the very human vector that makes the crop's transfer the parsimonious reading — a real meeting of peoples, dated to almost exactly the window (once the Mangaia context is re-dated to the fourteenth–fifteenth century) in which the cultivated crop appears in the archaeological record.5

Symmetry of skepticism. The pro-contact case has had to give ground too, and saying so keeps the verdict honest. The crop's oldest archaeological anchor was softer than a century of textbooks implied — the canonical “AD 1000” Mangaia date has been pushed three to four centuries later on better dating — and the linguistic argument really does hang on a single word-pair, where coincidence cannot be excluded by that pair alone. Neither concession touches the core: a South-American cultigen was being grown in pre-European Polynesia, and the population genetics now show the human contact that most simply accounts for it. Note the direction this argument runs, unlike most on this wiki's Old-World-in-America timeline: it does not claim Old-World civilizations founded New-World ones (the sweet potato ran from South America to Polynesia, west across the Pacific), and it turns on mainstream archaeology, botany, and genetics rather than on any forged inscription. It is the responsible core that the hyper-diffusionist thesis overreaches from — genuine, limited, human-scale contact, not a founding migration.

Assessment

Contested. That the sweet potato proves pre-Columbian human contact, taken as a solo knock-down, overreaches — the 2018 genome study shows a bare-presence natural-dispersal mechanism for the plant that cannot be ruled out, so the crop alone does not compel the conclusion. But the interpretation the argument serves — that the sweet potato is genuine evidence of human trans-Pacific contact — is strong and, on the full picture, probable. Keep the two sub-claims distinct: the botanical/genetic transfer of a cultivated South-American cultigen into pre-European Polynesia is well-grounded and best explained by people, especially now that an independent 2020 admixture study fixes a real Native-American–Polynesian contact to about AD 1200; the linguistic parallel (kumara ↔ k'umar) is specific and its early-1586 attestation blocks the easy reversal, but rests on a single word-pair and so is corroborative rather than decisive. The label is contested not because the case is weak but because two competent disciplines genuinely pull against each other: strong by archaeology's, linguistics', and population-genetics' lights, and honestly challenged by the botany of natural Ipomoea dispersal. On balance, and with the 2020 genetics in hand, human transfer of the crop is the more probable reading — a rare instance on this timeline where the trans-oceanic-contact claim, kept to its modest and evidenced form, largely holds.
