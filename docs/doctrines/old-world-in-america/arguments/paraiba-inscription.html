The Paraíba inscription: a genuine ancient Phoenician text, or an 1872 forgery?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: weak — the ancient-Phoenician reading does not hold on its best available case: no stone was ever seen by any scholar (only a transcription, from a correspondent who could never afterward be found), the letter-forms mix shapes drawn from a span of some eight to ten centuries that no single ancient scribe ever wrote together, and its narrative reads as a learned pastiche of Herodotus, the Hebrew Bible, and the Phoenician handbooks available by 1872. It earns an honest hearing rather than a wave-off, because a serious Semitist — Cyrus Gordon — pressed a real linguistic case for it. But the case fails, and a second sub-claim fails outright: even were the text genuine, a lost inscription known only from a copy would be a stray-contact curiosity, not evidence that Old-World peoples founded the American civilizations (unsupported for the settlement thesis it is enlisted to prove).

Related arguments: the American epigraphic corpus — see also the Bat Creek stone (assessment: weak), the corpus's one genuinely excavated exhibit, and the Los Lunas Decalogue stone (assessment: weak), a surface boulder with no excavated context.

The claim

We are Sidonian Canaanites from the city of the Mercantile King. We were cast up on this distant shore, a land of mountains. We sacrificed a youth to the celestial gods and goddesses in the nineteenth year of our mighty King Hiram and embarked from Ezion-geber into the Red Sea. We voyaged with ten ships and were at sea together for two years around Africa. Then we were separated by the hand of Baal and were no longer with our companions. So we have come here, twelve men and three women, into … New Shore. Am I, the Admiral, a man who would flee? Nay! May the celestial gods and goddesses favour us well!

— Cyrus H. Gordon's translation of the inscription.1 The interpretation assessed here is that this text — reported in 1872 from the Brazilian province of Paraíba as a copy of a broken stone, and purporting to record a shipwrecked crew of Sidonians who reached the New World — is a genuine ancient Phoenician inscription, and so, in the hands of the epigraphic diffusionists who cite it, evidence that Old-World Semites crossed to pre-Columbian America. Unlike the Bat Creek stone (dug from a dated mound) or the Los Lunas boulder (a surface find one can at least go and touch), no Paraíba stone was ever seen by anyone: the entire artifact is a transcription on paper. That is the case to take at its strongest before weighing it.

Who advances it

On 11 September 1872 the Instituto Histórico e Geográfico Brasileiro in Rio de Janeiro received a letter, signed by a planter named Joaquim Alves da Costa, reporting that a slave had found an inscribed, broken stone at his estate of Pouso-Alto “on the shores of the Parahyba,” and enclosing a copy his son had made of the strange characters. The letter was passed to Ladislau de Souza Mello Netto, a botanist then directing the National Museum, who had some Punic and Hebrew, recognized the script as Phoenician, published a reading, and championed the find as authentic.2 Netto later recanted (see below); the inscription lay largely forgotten until the Brandeis Semitist Cyrus H. Gordon — the same scholar who defended Bat Creek — revived it in the 1960s, arguing in a 1968 Orientalia article and in Before Columbus (1971) that the text was genuine because it contained Phoenician usages no nineteenth-century forger could have known.1

The evidence for — the steelman

1. A grammatically competent Phoenician text. Whatever its origin, the inscription is not gibberish: it is a coherent, idiomatic Phoenician commemorative narrative, of the kind a votive or dedicatory stele would carry. Whoever composed it commanded the language, not merely an alphabet table.1

2. Gordon's real card: usages supposedly unknown in 1872. This is the argument's strongest form and must be granted its full weight. Gordon pointed to features of the text that, he held, no forger of 1872 could have supplied — most famously the idiom he rendered “the hand of Baal,” which (he said) was not paralleled in a published Phoenician text until 1939, decades after the inscription surfaced. If a turn of phrase in the Paraíba text was genuinely unattested and unpublished in 1872, then a forger could not have copied it, and its presence would point to a real ancient author. Gordon built his authenticity case on exactly this kind of “a forger couldn't have known X” lever.1

3. A plausible historical frame. The narrative is not arbitrary. A Sidonian fleet setting out from Ezion-geber on the Red Sea and sailing “around Africa” matches Herodotus's report of Phoenicians who circumnavigated the continent for Pharaoh Necho; a King Hiram of Tyre is a real Phoenician royal name; and Ezion-geber is the biblical port of Solomon's and Jehoshaphat's fleets. The story hangs together as something a Phoenician crew might plausibly have recorded.3

Adversarial assessment

1. There is no artifact — the decisive collapse of provenance. Every other exhibit in this corpus is at least a physical object one can examine; the Paraíba “stone” is not. No scholar ever saw it, no fragment was ever produced, and when Netto tried in 1873–74 to locate either the stone or its finder, Joaquim Alves da Costa could not be found, and neither could Pouso-Alto. The whole artifact is a copy of a copy, of unknown accuracy, from an untraceable source. This is not one weak point among several; it is the removal of the very thing an authenticity claim must rest on. Where Bat Creek at least had an excavated context and Los Lunas a boulder to weather-test, Paraíba offers a sheet of paper.2

2. Letter-forms no single ancient scribe ever wrote together. This was the objection from the first and it has only hardened. Netto sent a copy to his former teacher, the great Semitist Ernest Renan, who pronounced it a forgery without hesitation; the epigrapher Konstantin Schlottmann and the orientalist Julius Euting concurred. A century later the palaeographer Frank Moore Cross — among the foremost readers of ancient Semitic scripts, and the same authority who rejected Los Lunas — reached the same verdict on the same ground: the writing is “too completely preserved,” and its characters mix forms drawn from across some eight to ten centuries of Phoenician script — a confluence “impossible for an authentic inscription,” but exactly what a nineteenth-century copyist assembling letters from a reference chart would produce.4 This is the identical fingerprint that condemns Los Lunas: a script anthology, not a script.

3. Gordon's “unknown in 1872” card, answered. The steelman deserves a direct reply, not a dismissal. Cross's rebuttal was that the lever does not actually engage: “Everything in the inscription was available to the forger in nineteenth century handbooks or from uninspired guesses based on these easily available sources.” The Paraíba forger was plainly not an ignoramus but a person with real Semitic learning — which is why the text scans as competent Phoenician — and such a person, working in the 1870s, had grammars, lexica, and the growing Phoenician epigraphic corpus to hand. A phrase's not being published in a convenient place until 1939 is not the same as its being unknowable in 1872: an idiom like “the hand of Baal” is a transparent Semitic construction a competent forger could coin without any parallel at all, and the historical furniture (Ezion-geber, King Hiram, the circumnavigation of Africa) is lifted straight from the Bible and Herodotus. The very learnedness Gordon read as a mark of antiquity is equally the signature of an educated hoaxer — and against the paleographic impossibility and the missing stone, it is decisively the weaker reading.4

4. The proponent recanted — and the forgery was traced home. Netto himself, having once staked his reputation on the stone, later withdrew the claim entirely, writing that he had been “carried away by enthusiasm” and concluding flatly that “the Phoenician inscription of Parahyba is an apocryphal inscription.” The trail of suspicion runs, if anywhere, toward the museum's own circle: in a 1972 study Geraldo Irenêo Joffily argued that Netto was himself the likely author of the fabrication, concocted to advance his career and flatter the emperor Dom Pedro II — the “Joaquim Alves da Costa” who could never be found being a convenient fiction. Whether or not Joffily fingered the right man, the point stands: the one scholar who authenticated the text is the one who unmasked it, and no independent witness to the stone ever existed.5

5. Even a genuine text would not reach the settlement thesis. Keep the two sub-claims apart. Grant, arguendo, that the copy faithfully records a real ancient stele: it would attest at most a single storm-driven landfall by fifteen castaways — a stray-contact curiosity, on the text's own telling — and would say nothing about who built the American civilizations. A lone shipwreck is not a founding population; no crops, domesticates, metallurgy, script-tradition, or genes travel ashore with it, and (the point made against every item in this corpus) a real colony would leave far more than one inscription. So the text's authenticity and the diffusionist conclusion are logically independent, and the second does not follow from the first even at the argument's most favorable — the same non-sequitur that sinks the settlement argument as a whole.6

Assessment

Weak (sub-claim a: the inscription is a genuine ancient Phoenician text); unsupported (sub-claim b: it licenses the Old-World-settlement thesis). The Paraíba text gets the fair, adversarial hearing that a real Semitist's defense earns — but the interpretation does not hold on its best available case, and it is arguably the weakest of the three stones in this corpus, because it is not a stone at all. The single most economical account of a competent Phoenician narrative, known only from an untraceable transcription, written in letter-forms that no ancient scribe combined and stocked with historical furniture lifted from Herodotus and the Bible, is an educated nineteenth-century hoax — a verdict reached by Renan, Schlottmann, and Euting when it first appeared, confirmed on paleographic grounds by Frank Moore Cross, and conceded in the end by the very man who first authenticated it. And even were the copy vindicated, it would remain a lone datum — the same non-sequitur that sinks the settlement argument as a whole, and the same barren material shelf that comes up empty for the Book of Mormon's own claimed script on the reformed-Egyptian argument. The verdict is the mainstream one, reached evenhandedly: a live dispute in the sense that a defender once pressed it, but not an open one in the sense of standing an even chance. It sits a notch below its neighbors Bat Creek and Los Lunas, which at least left something to examine; Paraíba left only a page.
