Did Old-World peoples trans-oceanically settle pre-Columbian Central America?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: weak as a general diffusionist thesis, and unsupported in its specific Solomonic form. Keep three sub-claims apart. That some pre-Columbian trans-oceanic contact happened is not merely possible but attested — the Norse reached Newfoundland, and a pulse of Native-American ancestry reached eastern Polynesia around AD 1200 — so a bare “could ancient people cross an ocean?” is contested-to-sound. But the leap from that feasibility to the claim that Old-World civilizations founded the Mesoamerican ones is a non-sequitur (different peoples, technologies, eras, and directions of travel), and the specific evidence marshaled for Semites-in-America — the Bat Creek, Los Lunas, and Paraíba inscriptions, Barry Fell's America B.C., Cyrus Gordon's defenses — is a corpus of hoaxes and misreadings, with Solomon's Ophir and Tarshish lying in the Old World, not across the Atlantic. Possibility is not actuality.

The claim

I have written elsewhere about my belief that King Solomon was behind the settlement of Central America. Now if a learned scholar were to deign to give me the time of day on this question, he would just sniff and say that the consensus in the journals is that ancient man did not have the navigational ability to transit the Pacific. … when Captain Cook, harbinger of civilized man, representing all those who did know how to do it, arrived at Hawaii, right out in the middle of said Pacific, what did he find there? Who was waving at him from the beach? You guessed right . . . people. My argument is fairly simple. Somebody knew how to do it. … And don't forget Easter Island, which is also way the heck out there.

— Douglas Wilson, “The Brilliance of Ancient Man,” Blog & Mablog (14 September 2026), link.1 The interpretation assessed here is the one his aside serves: that Old-World mariners — for Wilson, Solomonic-Israelite or Phoenician ones — crossed an ocean and seeded the pre-Columbian civilizations of the Americas. His stated move is a feasibility inference: because Polynesians reached islands “way the heck out there,” somebody in antiquity could cross open water, so the crossing that populated Central America is no obstacle. We take the interpretation in its strongest form, not merely Wilson's one-line version of it.

Who advances it

Wilson (a Reformed/CREC pastor, who appears on this wiki also as a witness on the intermediate-state timeline) states the thesis in passing and grounds it only on feasibility; he cites no evidence in the post and says his fuller case lives in an earlier piece he does not link. The substantive case he gestures at belongs to a long hyper-diffusionist tradition — the view that the world's civilizations radiate from a few Old-World sources rather than arising independently. Its twentieth-century popularizer for a North-American audience was Barry Fell, whose America B.C. (1976) read scattered New-World rock-markings as Iberian, Phoenician, and Celtic (Ogham) inscriptions; the Semitic strand was pressed by the Brandeis orientalist Cyrus H. Gordon, who defended the Bat Creek and Paraíba texts as genuine ancient Hebrew and Phoenician.2 The tradition reaches back through Thor Heyerdahl's voyage-experiments to the nineteenth-century “Mound Builder” and “lost race” speculations. It is worth noting at the outset the irony of the source: Wilson is a pointed critic of the Latter-day Saint scriptures, yet the evidential move he makes here — ancient seafarers of Israelite stock founded a New-World civilization — is structurally the same one Book-of-Mormon apologists make for the Lehite and Jaredite voyages, and it faces the same material record (see the Book of Mormon and the material record).

The evidence for

Genuine pre-Columbian trans-oceanic contact is real — grant it in full. The strongest honest case does not rest on Wilson's Pacific analogy but on hard data he does not cite. Norse sailors did reach the Americas centuries before Columbus: the excavated turf-and-timber settlement at L'Anse aux Meadows, Newfoundland, is a securely dated eleventh-century Norse site, and precise dendrochronology on its worked wood fixes Norse activity there to exactly AD 1021.3 More strikingly, a 2020 genome-wide study found a small but real signal of Native-American ancestry in the peoples of eastern Polynesia, dating a contact event to roughly AD 1200 — independent confirmation that open-ocean peoples and Americans did meet before 1492.4 The trans-Pacific spread of the South-American sweet potato (its Polynesian names echoing Quechua kumar) and of the bottle gourd points the same way, and diffusionist authors such as John L. Sorenson and Carl Johannessen have compiled long catalogues of claimed biological transfers across the oceans.5 The point to concede without flinching is that trans-oceanic voyaging in antiquity was possible and, in specific instances, actual. Wilson's “somebody knew how to do it” is, at that level of generality, simply true.

Adversarial assessment

1. The feasibility inference proves far less than the thesis needs. That some ancient people crossed open water does not establish that these people — tenth-century-BC Levantine Israelites under Solomon — crossed the Atlantic and built the Olmec, Maya, and later Mesoamerican civilizations. Every step of the analogy breaks: the Polynesians were first-millennium-AD Austronesian navigators sailing west-to-east across the Pacific in outrigger canoes refined over centuries of island-hopping, not Iron-Age Semites crossing the Atlantic; the Norse and Polynesian contacts that are attested left tiny genetic and material traces and founded no civilizations. From “a crossing was possible” one may infer “a crossing may have happened,” never “this civilization was founded by that crossing.” This is the recurring discipline of evidential reconstruction: possibility is not proof, and the burden is to show that a New-World civilization actually bears an Old-World founder's imprint. Wilson's move supplies the possibility and skips the imprint. (This is an overreach — a reading that runs well ahead of the evidence — not a falsehood; the feasibility premise itself is sound as far as it goes.)

2. The epigraphic and artifactual case for Semites-in-America does not survive scrutiny. The inscriptions marshaled for a Hebrew or Phoenician presence are, on specialist examination, modern productions or misreadings. The Bat Creek stone (Tennessee, excavated 1889 and long read as palaeo-Hebrew) was shown by Mainfort and Kwas to reproduce, letter for letter, a line from an 1870 Freemasonry handbook — a nineteenth-century forgery, not an ancient text.6 The Los Lunas Decalogue stone (New Mexico) carries a compressed Ten Commandments in a mix of letter-forms no ancient Semitic scribe used together, with weathering and script that point to a modern hand; the Paraíba inscription (Brazil, surfacing in 1872) was pronounced a fraud by the leading Semitist of the day, Ernest Renan, and is rejected as such by epigraphers despite Cyrus Gordon's lone defense; and Barry Fell's America B.C. “Ogham” and “Punic” readings founder on natural plough-scars and glacial striae read as script and on decipherments no working epigrapher accepts.7 Nor does the biblical geography help: Solomon's Ophir and Tarshish, the sources of his gold and silver, are placed by the mainstream in the Old World — Arabia, the Horn of Africa, or India for Ophir; a western Mediterranean or south-Iberian smelting hub (Tartessos) for Tarshish, the direction from which Near-Eastern silver demonstrably flowed — not across the Atlantic.8 The material record that would corroborate an Old-World founding — Old-World crops, domesticates, iron-and-steel metallurgy, a Semitic or Egyptian-derived script in the pre-Columbian Americas — is the very record that comes up empty on the Book of Mormon's own pages (see the crops-and-metallurgy argument and the reformed-Egyptian argument).

3. Diffusionism versus independent invention. The wider “brilliance of ancient man” framing — pyramids and mounds and monumental stonework the world over, taken to imply a single dispersed source — runs against the archaeological consensus that the great civilizations arose independently, by convergent solutions to shared human problems (piling earth or stone for height and burial is not a fingerprint of contact). This wiki weighs that generic “shared source” inference elsewhere and finds it wanting: the simultaneous-rise-of-civilizations argument and the Eastern-U.S. mounds-as-pyramids argument both come out weak, and the engineering feats Wilson lists (Easter Island's moai among them) are, on inspection, within documented native capacity rather than evidence of an outside founder (see the Easter Island moai argument, also weak).

The symmetry Wilson does not notice. Wilson is a hard critic of Mormonism, yet his Solomon thesis is the same species of argument as the Latter-day Saint claim he rejects: an ancient Israelite sea-voyage founding a literate New-World civilization, defended by feasibility and by contested epigraphy, and testable against the same material shelf. The consistent verdict cuts both ways. The Lehite/Jaredite version is weighed across the Book of Mormon's own timeline — its limited-geography model, its crops and metallurgy, its Lehite DNA — and comes out weak on the same grounds. A skeptic of one trans-oceanic founding thesis owes the other the same skepticism.

Assessment

Weak as the general diffusionist thesis (Old-World peoples founded the pre-Columbian Central-American civilizations); unsupported in the specific Solomonic form Wilson advocates. Distinguish the sub-claims, because they do not stand or fall together. That trans-oceanic contact was feasible, and in scattered instances actual, is well-grounded — the Norse landfall and the AD-1200 Polynesian admixture prove it, and to that extent Wilson's “somebody knew how to do it” is right. But feasibility is not actuality, and the specific claim needs an imprint: an Old-World founding population would have left Old-World crops, animals, metallurgy, script, or genes in the Mesoamerican record, and it did not. The affirmative evidence actually offered for a Semitic presence — Bat Creek, Los Lunas, Paraíba, Fell's corpus — is a catalogue of forgeries and misreadings, and Solomon's own trade-geography (Ophir, Tarshish) sits in the Old World. Wilson's inference from Pacific islands to a Solomonic Mesoamerica is a less-probable extension that outruns its premise, not a factual error about seafaring; rate the interpretation on its evidence, which does not reach the conclusion. That the verdict falls out the same as for the Book of Mormon's own trans-oceanic founding is not a coincidence — it is the same test applied evenhandedly.
