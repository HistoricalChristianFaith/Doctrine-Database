Cyrus Thomas on Old-World peoples in the pre-Columbian Americas

Dates: c. 1825–1910 · Wikipedia
Summary: The entomologist-turned-archaeologist whom the Smithsonian's Bureau of Ethnology put in charge of its Division of Mound Exploration (1882–1890). The nineteenth-century American form of the Old-World-founders tradition was the “Mound-Builder” myth — that a vanished, superior race, Israelite or otherwise, and not the ancestors of the living Indians, had raised the great earthworks of the Ohio and Mississippi valleys. Thomas was set to test it, and had himself entered the field inclined toward the vanished-race view; after most of a decade of excavation his massive Report on the Mound Explorations (1894) concluded that the mounds were built by the ancestors of the historic Native American nations. He is a rare “turned by his own evidence” witness on this timeline — the man who demolished the reading he began half-inclined to share.

Claims & evidence

Turned by his own evidence: from the vanished race to the living Indians

Thomas did not set out to debunk the lost-race theory; the Bureau of Ethnology's director, John Wesley Powell, appointed him to settle the question the theory raised, and Thomas began the work inclined to the very view he would overturn — that the mounds were the work of a more advanced people who had since vanished from the land. Nearly a decade of systematic excavation across the eastern United States reversed him, and he became the figure most responsible for retiring the “Mound-Builder” race from serious archaeology. The report frames the pivot plainly as the Bureau's whole purpose: “The most important question to be settled is, ‘Were the mounds built by the Indians?’” — and Thomas answered it in the affirmative.1

The Ohio mounds were built by Indians — the ancestors of the Cherokees

In his 1889 essay The Problem of the Ohio Mounds — the argument later folded into the great report — Thomas stated his thesis against the extinct-race consensus of his day:

The opinion advanced in this paper, in support of which evidence will be presented, is that the ancient works of the State are due to Indians of several different tribes, and that some at least of the typical works, were built by the ancestors of the modern Cherokees.

— The Problem of the Ohio Mounds (Washington: Bureau of Ethnology, 1889); source.2 The “people long since extinct or driven from the country, who had attained a culture status much in advance of that reached by the aborigines,” was, he noted, “the one which has been most generally accepted” — the reading he set out to dislodge.

An unbroken chain “which no sophistry or reasoning can break”

The load-bearing evidence, for Thomas, was the stone graves interlocked with the mounds — a continuity of burial practice binding the mound-builders to the historic tribes with no gap into which a lost race could be inserted:

…it is so interlocked with other facts relating to the works of the “veritable mound-builders” as to leave no hiatus into which the theory of a lost race or a “Toltec occupation” can possibly be thrust. It forms an unbroken chain connecting the mound-builders and historical Indians which no sophistry or reasoning can break.

— The Problem of the Ohio Mounds; source.2

The Bureau's verdict: mound-builders and Indians “one and the same people”

The full Report on the Mound Explorations of the Bureau of Ethnology (1894) — more than seven hundred pages summarizing the division's excavations — set out its findings in a numbered outline. The last of them delivered the conclusion that closed the controversy:

The links directly connecting the Indians and mound-builders are so numerous and well established that archeologists are justified in accepting the theory that they are one and the same people.

— Report on the Mound Explorations of the Bureau of Ethnology, Twelfth Annual Report of the Bureau of Ethnology, 1890–91 (Washington, 1894), “Outline of this paper”; source.3 Powell's administrative introduction to the same volume states the ultimate conclusion in almost identical words — “the links of evidence connecting the Indians and mound-builders are so numerous and well established as to justify archeologists in assuming that they were one and the same people.” This has been the mainstream verdict ever since, and the same body of eastern earthworks is weighed again — against a modern “lost 2,000-year civilization” framing — on the question whether the eastern mounds are the “pyramids” of a lost civilization revealed by LiDAR.

The Bat Creek stone: Thomas read it as Cherokee

The report also published the engraved stone dug from a burial mound at Bat Creek, Tennessee, in 1889 by the Bureau's field agent John Emmert. Thomas took its markings to be letters of Sequoyah's Cherokee syllabary — a reading consistent with the whole thrust of his work:

The engraved characters on it are beyond question letters of the Cherokee alphabet said to have been invented by George Guess (or Sequoyah), a half-breed Cherokee, about 1821.

— Report on the Mound Explorations, p. 393 (the Bat Creek mounds, Loudon County, Tennessee); source.4 The irony is that this same object was later inverted and re-read by Cyrus Gordon and J. Huston McCulloch as palaeo-Hebrew — the one excavated exhibit in the Semitic-inscription corpus enlisted for the Old-World-founders thesis Thomas's life work told against. That afterlife is weighed on its own page, The Bat Creek stone: ancient palaeo-Hebrew, or a 19th-century forgery? (assessment: weak; unsupported for settlement).

Notes

  • Thomas stands on this timeline as the counter-voice who demolished the tradition's nineteenth-century American form. Where Diego Durán and James Adair read the natives as displaced Israelites and the “Mound-Builder” writers read the earthworks as the relics of a vanished superior race, Thomas showed the builders were the ancestors of the living tribes — a known, continuous people, not a lost one. His 1894 report is often called the birth of scientific American archaeology.
  • The conversion is the point worth keeping: Thomas is not a skeptic who came to the evidence already decided, but a sympathizer of the vanished-race idea whom his own eight years of digging turned around. That is a stronger testimony against the “lost race” than an outsider's would have been.
  • Cross-link: the same eastern mounds Thomas identified are revisited — against the claim that publicly-available LiDAR has revealed the “pyramids” of a lost, sophisticated civilization mainstream archaeology has missed — on the primeval-evidence mounds argument (assessment: weak). The builders there, as here, are the named and dated Adena, Hopewell, and Mississippian traditions, not an anonymous vanished people.
  • Provenance note: the verbatim quotations follow Thomas's own printed texts — the 1889 Problem of the Ohio Mounds (Project Gutenberg transcription) and the 1894 Report on the Mound Explorations (Internet Archive scan of the Twelfth Annual Report). The dated term in the Bat Creek passage is Thomas's own 1894 wording, retained verbatim.
