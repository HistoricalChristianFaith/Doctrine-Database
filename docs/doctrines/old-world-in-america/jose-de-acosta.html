José de Acosta on Old-World peoples in the pre-Columbian Americas

Dates: c. 1539–1600 · Wikipedia
Summary: The Jesuit naturalist and missionary whose Historia natural y moral de las Indias (Seville, 1590) is the decisive early counter-voice on this timeline. Reasoning by elimination, Acosta rejected both the Lost-Tribes idea and any deliberate ocean crossing, and concluded that the first peoples had reached the Americas overland, by way of a stretch where the two landmasses are “ioyned and continued … or at the least are very neere” — in outline the modern Bering consensus, a century and a half before the strait was charted.

Claims & evidence

The Old and New Worlds are joined, or very near, in some undiscovered part

Acosta's starting problem is how animals — not just people — reached the Indies without a deliberate voyage carrying them. He answers by inference: the continents cannot be wholly cut off from one another, because no one has yet explored the far north where they would meet.

I coniecture then, by the discourse I have made, that the new world, which we call Indies, is not altogether severed and disioyned from the other world; and to speake my opinion, I have long beleeved that the one and the other world are ioyned and continued one with another in some part, or at the least are very neere. And yet to this day there is no certaine knowledge of the contrary. For towards the Articke or Northerne Pole all the longitude of the earth is not discovered…

— The Naturall and Morall Historie of the East and West Indies, trans. Edward Grimston (London, 1604), Bk. 1, ch. 20; source.1 His inductive guess — a land connection or near-connection toward the unexplored Arctic — is in outline what the charting of the Bering Strait would confirm a century and a half later.

The first peoplers were savage hunters who spread gradually, not a purposed fleet

From the land-connection Acosta draws the consequence: the Indies were peopled not by an organized expedition but by degrees, as wandering hunting bands drifted from land to neighbouring land.

…the true and principall cause to people the Indies, was, that the lands and limits thereof are ioyned and continued in some extremities of the world, or at the least were very neere. And I beleeve it is not many thousand yeeres past since men first inhabited this new world and West Indies, and that the first men that entred, were rather savage men and hunters, then bredde vp in civill and well governed Common-weales; and that they came to this new world, having lost their owne land, or being in too great numbers, they were forced of necessitie to seeke some other habitations; the which having found, they beganne by little and little to plant…

— Naturall and Morall Historie, Bk. 1, ch. 24 (“The peopling of the Indies was gradual… the first arrivals savage and hunters”); source.2 The same chapter states the mechanism plainly: the Indians “came by little and little to this newe world, and that by the helpe and meanes of the neerenesse of lands, or by some navigation… and not that they prepared any armie to goe thither of purpose; neyther that they have been caried thither by any ship-wracke or tempest.” Elsewhere in the peopling chapters he pictures the process as men “changing by little and little their lands and habitations” until, “some peopling the lands they found, and others seeking for newe,” the whole hemisphere was filled.1

Neither the apocryphal Esdras route nor Plato's Atlantis explains the crossing

Acosta gives the two rival origin-theories of his day a full chapter each and rejects both. Against the notion (later systematized as the Lost-Tribes thesis) that the Indians descend from the Jews, he turns the proof-text of 2 Esdras against its users — the ten tribes withdrew, that passage says, “to keepe their faith and ceremonies,” whereas the Indians are “given to all the Idolatries in the world” — and he denies that the apocryphal Euphrates crossing offers any real passage:

To conclude, I cannot see how that Euphrates in Esdras Apocrypha should be a more convenient passage to goe to the new world, then the inchanted and fabulous Atlantis Iland of Plato.

— Naturall and Morall Historie, Bk. 1, ch. 23 (“That the opinion of many which hold that the first race of the Indians comes from the Jews is not true”; the preceding ch. 22 disposes of “the Atlantis Island as some do imagine”); source.2 He had already judged Plato's island a fable in its own right (ch. 22, “Atlantis a fable”): the sea-swallowed continent is a poetic invention, not a bridge one can walk the ancestors of the Indians across.

Notes

  • Acosta stands on this timeline as the first-generation skeptic: he answers, by 1590, the very Lost-Tribes reading that the Dominican Diego Durán had set out a decade earlier, and he does so on grounds — overland migration from Asia — that the later charting of the Bering Strait would vindicate. His argument-by-elimination is the shape the modern consensus still takes.
  • Two guards on how much weight to give him. His “savage men and hunters” language is a sixteenth-century civilizational judgment, not a claim these pages endorse; the enduring insight is the route (overland, by a land connection or near-connection in the far north), not his estimate of the migrants' culture. And his method is inductive conjecture (“I coniecture… I have long beleeved”), offered against the still-unexplored Arctic — a well-reasoned guess that happened to be right, not a demonstration.
  • Provenance note: the verbatim quotations here follow Edward Grimston's 1604 English translation (original spelling retained; the Clements R. Markham Hakluyt Society reprint carries the same text with an analytical table of contents). The Spanish 1590 original is also online.
