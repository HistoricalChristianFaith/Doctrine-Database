Ibn Taymiyya on tahrif (the moderate middle: reduced, not rejected)

Dates: c. 1263–1328 (661–728 AH) · Wikipedia
Summary: The Damascene Hanbali jurist and theologian Taqi al-Din Ahmad ibn Taymiyya wrote the major systematic Muslim polemic against Christianity, al-Jawab al-Sahih li-man baddala din al-Masih (“The Correct Answer to Those Who Changed the Religion of Christ”), c. 1316–1321, as a reply to the Letter from the People of Cyprus — itself a reworking of the Melkite bishop Paul of Antioch’s Letter to a Muslim Friend. On the corruption of the earlier scriptures he takes a deliberately intermediate line: against Ibn Hazm’s verdict that the Bible is a wholesale forgery no longer worth engaging, and against the sanguine Ash‘arite view (al-Ghazali, Fakhr al-Din al-Razi) that the text is basically sound and only misread, Ibn Taymiyya holds that alteration of both the wording (tahrif al-lafz) and the meaning (tahrif al-ma‘na) did occur — but that the whole text was not rewritten, and that the graver charge is the corrupted meaning: Christians “changed the religion of Christ” by substituting the innovations of church councils for what Jesus taught. The Gospels are thereby demoted to a secondary, Sunnah-level testimony about Jesus rather than a Qur’an-level revelation; the Torah he rates higher still, though not immune. He sits on the tahrif timeline as the bridge witness between the hard-textual (Ibn Hazm) and meaning-only (al-Qasim, ‘Abduh) camps — and, as the teacher of Ibn Kathir, one generation before the tafsir that fixed the single-Injil doctrine.

Claims & evidence

The intermediate position: the Gospels reduced, not rejected

Ibn Taymiyya explicitly locates himself between two extremes. He rejects Ibn Hazm’s conclusion that the biblical text is so corrupt as to be an “accursed book” to be set aside entirely; he equally rejects the Ash‘arite readers (al-Ghazali, al-Razi) for whom the text is basically sound and only misinterpreted. As the scholar Sona Grigoryan summarises his conclusion, it is

neither as radical as was the case with Ibn Hazm (who eventually rejected the Gospels as a source of knowledge) nor is it similar to the positions of those who claimed that the previous Books could be used as historical sources without a critical approach towards their content or taken as sacred prophetic Books (as accepted by al-Ghazali).

Ibn Taymiyya, on her reading, “did not reject but reduced the status of the Gospels, presenting them as a secondary source of knowledge like the Sunnah in Islamic tradition and unlike the Qur’an as an absolute source.”1 The historian Younus Mirza puts the same point in Ibn Taymiyya’s own frame: he “contends that he supports an intermediate position between two extremes,” arguing “that distortion did occur through transmission and translation but that the Bible can still be engaged with after critical evaluation.”2

How the wording was corrupted: fallible transmission and alterable copies

Ibn Taymiyya grants a real tahrif al-lafz, but locates it in the ordinary fragility of a text that — unlike the Qur’an — was never mass-memorized (tawatur) by the whole community. Because the Gospels rest on manuscript copies held by a learned few, they are alterable:

The copies are available for intelligent people of ahl al-kitab, and their common people do not memorize the Gospels in a way common Muslims memorize the Qur’an. Therefore, if a group of them [intelligent people] contemplates to change a copy or copies it possesses, it is possible to do so. Then, if another group contemplates not to memorize it, it is possible to do so.

— al-Jawab al-Sahih, trans. Grigoryan.3 To this he adds the compounding problem of translation (Jesus’ words, uttered in Hebrew, passing through Greek, Latin, and Syriac into many tongues, carrying errors along the way). These, however, are for Ibn Taymiyya largely “technical” and often non-deliberate defects — the point is that they make total, coordinated preservation impossible, not that every word was consciously forged. Textual corruption, in his handling, becomes secondary to the corruption of meaning.

The graver charge: a corrupted meaning — “the religion of Christ” changed

The heart of Ibn Taymiyya’s case, announced in his very title, is not that the Gospels were rewritten but that their meaning was — that Christians read into the texts, or built onto them, doctrines the texts never taught. He insists that the defining Christian creed is simply absent from the sources:

There is nothing in their books that proves through the text or its obvious meaning the Creed that is the root of their religion [i.e. the Nicaean Creed] such as the Trinity, the Union and Incarnation, and also there is nothing that proves their rules like prayer to the East, permission of forbidden things such as pork or carrion and things like that explained somewhere else.

— al-Jawab al-Sahih, trans. Grigoryan.4 These doctrines, he holds, are innovations (bida‘) — Christians “abandoning the prophetic message and replacing (tabdil) it with legislations of Church councils and leaders.” He dates the decisive break to Constantine’s first council, whose “three hundred eighteen participants… established for themselves the creed which is now the belief of Christians,” ordinary men liable to error, not prophets. On this account the religion of the Christians “has a human and not divine origin” — precisely the supersession claim the title makes: the religion of Christ was changed, and stands in need of the final revelation that restores it.5

The Torah: “the second Book after the Qur’an,” yet not immune

Ibn Taymiyya rates the Torah higher than the Gospels — “textually… sounder,” the second Book after the Qur’an — but denies it absolute accuracy, on grounds of the same fallible transmission:

As for the Torah, its transmission was interrupted when Jerusalem was destroyed the first time, and Israelites were deported. Further it is said that he who dictated the words was one person whom they called Ezra, and they claim him a prophet. Some said that he was not a prophet and claimed that the books were checked against an old copy which they found. It was said that the copy was brought from Maghreb. All this does not necessitate the concomitance of all the words and does not exclude errors as happens to all books that are copied, checked and preserved only by a few people.

— al-Jawab al-Sahih, trans. Grigoryan.6 Notably, where Ibn Hazm recast Ezra as a deliberate forger who fabricated the Torah, Ibn Taymiyya is careful and non-hostile: whether Ezra was a prophet is for him a question to be proved or disproved, not an occasion for denunciation. He does not go beyond the observation that a text dictated and checked by a few is liable to error — the measured face of the same tahrif Ibn Hazm pressed to its extreme.

Notes

  • Where this sits on the timeline. Ibn Taymiyya is the hinge between the two classical camps: he grants the hard textualists (Ibn Hazm) their point that the wording was not perfectly preserved, while agreeing with the meaning-only tradition (al-Qasim b. Ibrahim, and later Muhammad ‘Abduh) that the deeper corruption is interpretive — a false religion read onto, and built around, a text that survives in usable if imperfect form. His own student Ibn Kathir would, a generation later, supply the exegetical spine of the single-Injil doctrine; Ibn Taymiyya’s al-Jawab (c. 1316–1321) actually predates that tafsir, and is placed after it on the summary only because the two single-Injil tafsirs (al-Qurtubi and Ibn Kathir) are read together there as one doctrinal block.
  • Both kinds of corruption, but not a wholly rewritten text. It is a common oversimplification to file Ibn Taymiyya under either “textual” or “interpretive” corruption. He affirms both, but ranks them: the alterable, un-memorized, multiply-translated copies make textual slippage real, yet the sheer circulation of copies makes total coordinated alteration impossible — so the load-bearing charge is the corrupted meaning, the doctrines “ascribed to the Gospels which they did not contain.” On the interpretive point he is, as Grigoryan notes, close to al-Ghazali; on the textual point he parts from him.7
  • The verbatim English above is Sona Grigoryan’s translation from the Arabic al-Jawab al-Sahih (ed. Muhammad Hasan Isma‘il, al-Maktabah al-‘Ilmiyah, 2003), quoted in her thesis at the pages cited; Thomas F. Michel’s A Muslim Theologian’s Response to Christianity (Caravan Books, 1984) translates roughly a third of the four-volume work into English and is the standard partial English edition. Where a claim rests on Grigoryan’s or Michel’s characterization rather than a rendered quotation, it is cited as such.
