al-Qasim b. Ibrahim al-Rassi on tahrif

Dates: c. 785–860 (169–246 AH) · Wikipedia
Summary: A Zaydi Shi‘i imam and one of the earliest Muslim authors of a sustained refutation of Christianity (the Radd ‘ala al-Nasara, written in Egypt c. 826). He is conventionally cited as an early exponent of tahrif al-ma‘na — the reading that Christians corrupted the interpretation of their scriptures rather than the words themselves. In fact his method is more radical and less tidy than that label implies, which is why he is a revealing rather than a clean witness.

Claims & evidence

The corruption is in the reading, not (primarily) the wording

al-Qasim’s Radd attacks Christian doctrine chiefly by re-reading the Gospels, not by charging that their text was rewritten. He faults the interpretation Christians place on their books — that they have read the divinity of Christ into texts that, rightly understood, teach only his humanity. Di Matteo, who edited and translated the Radd, drew exactly this conclusion, as David Thomas reports:

… al-Qasim does not accuse Christians of corrupting the actual text of scripture, but … his reworking presupposes that the text has been contaminated.

— David Thomas, The Bible in Early Muslim Anti-Christian Polemic, summarizing (and then qualifying) Ignazio Di Matteo’s judgment.1 Thomas characterizes al-Qasim’s own move the same way: the Christian texts, he holds, can be prised apart from the church’s doctrine, so that

The texts themselves can thus be dissociated from the doctrinal tradition which has distorted their true teachings.

— Thomas, paraphrasing the second of al-Qasim’s three justifications of method (Radd, p. 319).2 This is the ninth-century root of the “meaning-only” counter-current that later runs through to the reformer Muhammad ‘Abduh: the Gospel Christians hold is genuine, but they have misconstrued it.

— but he also suspects the transmission, and edits the text himself

The clean “text intact, meaning corrupted” label is a simplification, and the same scholarship that supplies it says so. Thomas notes two ways al-Qasim goes further than mere reinterpretation. First, al-Qasim’s opening argument is a transmission suspicion: Christians received their Gospels from Jews “whom they regard as untrustworthy,” so the books “do not meet the criterion of sound transmission, even by Christian standards” — a charge that shades toward corruption of the text, not just its reading. Second, and more strikingly, al-Qasim does not only reinterpret the Gospels; he rewrites them:

… it did not involve new interpretation but actual editing and rewriting of the transmitted text itself.

— Thomas, on al-Qasim’s translation of parts of Matthew.3 In rendering the Sermon on the Mount he silently excises Matthew 5:25–32 and 5:38–42 (the teaching on turning the other cheek), which Thomas says “one might even call … censorship,” and he recasts the temptation narrative — naming the tempter Iblis — so that it conforms to Islamic assumptions:

The Messiah left the villages and withdrew, and fasted in the wilderness for forty days, neither eating food there nor taking drink. Iblis came to him there …

— al-Qasim’s edited version of the temptation, in Thomas’s translation.4 So the man cited as the founder of the “meaning-only” reading in practice treats the transmitted text as contaminated and freely emends it — a tension the “interpretive-corruption” label papers over.

Notes

  • “First” is overstated. No source surveyed here flatly calls al-Qasim the first to frame tahrif as corruption-of-meaning. Thomas calls the Radd “the earliest surviving sustained anti-Christian polemic by a Muslim author,” and Thomas F. Michel lists al-Qasim alongside ‘Ali al-Tabari and al-Hasan b. Ayyub as early appliers of tahrif al-ma‘na, not ahead of them — so “one of the earliest” is the defensible claim, not “the first.”5
  • He belongs early on the tahrif timeline, just after the Qur’an itself, as the head of the internal Muslim counter-current that holds the earlier scriptures’ wording sound — the current the later textual-corruption polemicists (Ibn Hazm, Ibn Taymiyya) argue against. The reading he heads is weighed on the argument page Was only the meaning of the earlier scriptures corrupted (tahrif al-ma‘na)? (assessment: plausible).
  • The primary text is the Radd ‘ala al-Nasara, edited with an Italian translation by Ignazio Di Matteo in Rivista degli Studi Orientali 9 (1921–22); this page follows David Thomas’s English study and translations of it.
