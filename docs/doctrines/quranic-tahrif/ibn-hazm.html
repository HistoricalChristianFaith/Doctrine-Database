Ibn Hazm of Cordoba on tahrif

Dates: c. 994–1064 (384–456 AH) · Wikipedia
Summary: The Andalusian Zahiri jurist and polymath Abu Muhammad ‘Ali ibn Hazm al-Qurtubi, in the comparative-religion sections of his Kitab al-Fisal fi al-Milal wa’l-Ahwa’ wa’l-Nihal (mid-11th century), built the most systematic and thoroughgoing case in classical Islam for tahrif al-lafz — the charge that the words of the Torah and the Gospels were deliberately falsified, not merely misread. Across roughly 140 pages he catalogues alleged internal contradictions, chronological and theological impossibilities, and lapses of transmission to prove that the biblical text in Jewish and Christian hands is “other than that which Allah revealed.” He is the hard-textual pole of the doctrine and the foil to the meaning-only current headed by al-Qasim b. Ibrahim al-Rassi.

Claims & evidence

The thesis: contradictions prove the text was altered

Ibn Hazm frames his biblical criticism as a demonstration, from the text’s own incoherence, that it cannot be the revelation God gave. His literalist test — drawn from the Qur’an’s claim that a truly divine book would contain no discrepancy (Q 4:82) — is turned on the Bible: where he finds contradiction, he infers corruption. The chapter that opens his catalogue states the thesis in its title:

Concerning the manifest contradictions and clear falsifications in the book the Jews call the Torah, and the rest of their books, as well as in the four gospels, confirming thereby their alteration and corruption, such that it is other than that which Allah, The Sublime, revealed to them.

— al-Fisal, vol. 1, p. 116, in the translation of Abdul Rashied Omar.1 Lest he be misread as denying the original revelations, Ibn Hazm is careful to say he affirms the Torah and Gospel as God gave them — “We have never ever rejected any of these two books… God, the Sublime, truly revealed the Torah on Moses” — and holds that God preserved part of the true text as a testimony against those who tampered with the rest. The corruption is a historical event that befell the books, not a denial that they were once revealed.2

How the Torah was corrupted: a fragile single line of transmission

Where al-Qasim mostly re-read the Christians’ text, Ibn Hazm attacks its very custody. The Torah, he argues, was never mass-transmitted like the Qur’an but was the exclusive possession of the priestly Kahanim, handed father to son across many centuries — a defective single-line transmission that made tampering easy and undetectable. He compounds this with a history of Israel’s repeated total apostasy, so that (he claims) there were long stretches in which no faithful community held the book at all. His chapter “How was the Torah corrupted?” closes the argument rhetorically:

Reflect! … What scripture can remain (safely preserved) with the rebelliousness of infidelity and the rejection of belief, throughout lengthy periods, and in a small country which is the size of a three day journey, and no-one remained on their religion or followed their scripture besides them on the surface of the earth.

— al-Fisal, vol. 1, pp. 187–189 (the chapter Kayfa hurrifat al-Tawra?), tr. Omar.3 He reinforces the point by playing the divergent Hebrew, Samaritan, and Greek (Septuagint) Torahs off against one another: three mutually contradictory recensions cannot all be the untampered original, and their disagreement is itself evidence of alteration.4

The Gospels contradict each other — “the works of accursed liars”

Ibn Hazm applies the same method to the four Gospels, tabulating discrepancies among their parallel narratives. On the call of the first apostles he finds the four accounts (Matthew 4, Mark 1, Luke 5, John 1) irreconcilable as to time, place, and circumstance, and concludes that at least one must be false — which for him condemns the whole. The Gospels, he says, are

the works of accursed liars.

— al-Fisal, vol. 2, pp. 19–21, tr. Omar.5 His invective can be extravagant — of the human author he blames for the Pentateuch he writes that “the bull is more discreet than he, and the jackass wiser” — and he presses the Gospels’ own admission of composite, human authorship (“a composite of works by the four evangelists… and a number of other writings”) as further proof they are not a single revealed book.6

Ezra the scribe as deliberate forger

Ibn Hazm’s most consequential move is to name a culprit. Earlier Muslim writers, following a motif with Jewish and Christian roots (4 Ezra 14; the rabbinic tradition), had cast Ezra as the pious restorer who rewrote the Torah from memory after it was lost. Ibn Hazm inverts this: Ezra becomes the fraudulent corrupter who foisted a falsified text on the Jews. Camilla Adang, surveying nine Muslim writers on the Hebrew Bible, finds that of them all only al-Maqdisi and Ibn Hazm held that the text itself had been distorted, and that

Ibn Hazm in his Al-Fisal fi al-Milal wal-Ahwa wal-Nihal, carefully built a case for the verbal corruption of the biblical text… The person held responsible by Ibn Hazm for corruption of the Torah was Ezra the scribe, who was generally put in a very positive light by Hazm’s predecessors.

— Camilla Adang, Muslim Writers on Judaism and the Hebrew Bible, p. 251 (Adang’s words, not Ibn Hazm’s).7

Notes

  • “The first” is overstated; “the systematizer” is right. On the bare textual-corruption thesis Ibn Hazm had a predecessor — al-Maqdisi (d. after 966) — and the Ezra-rewrote-the-Torah motif is older than Islam. His near-contemporary al-Juwayni (d. 1085) argued deliberate falsification independently in the East. What is genuinely his is the scale and rigour: he is the most systematic and detailed classical exponent of hard tahrif al-lafz, whose position “marked a departure from the prevailing opinion before his time and was followed by subsequent writers only with careful qualifications” (Thomas Michel), and he is the first to recast Ezra from restorer into deliberate forger. The scholarly correction against overstating his originality is the point of Martin Whittingham’s reassessment.8
  • A caveat on his “contradictions.” Theodore Pulcini notes that Ibn Hazm worked from a Christian-produced Arabic Bible resting on a Latin Vorlage, so some of the discrepancies he catalogues partly reflect the peculiarities of his particular Arabic version rather than the Hebrew or Greek — a point that bears on how much weight his contradiction-lists can carry.9
  • He sits on the tahrif timeline as the hard-textual pole against the meaning-only current headed by al-Qasim b. Ibrahim al-Rassi. Whether his catalogued contradictions actually establish deliberate textual corruption — as against scribal variation, harmonizable difference, or corruption of interpretation — is weighed on the argument page Was the text of the earlier scriptures corrupted (tahrif al-lafz)? (assessment: weak).
  • The verbatim renderings above follow Abdul Rashied Omar’s translations of the Arabic al-Fisal (Beirut 1980 / ‘Ukaz 1982 editions); page numbers are to those Arabic editions. The published English study to cross-check is Pulcini’s Exegesis as Polemical Discourse.
