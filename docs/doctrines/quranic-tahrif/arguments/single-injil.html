Was the Injil a single revelation to Jesus, distinct from the four Gospels?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: weak — as a claim about Qur’anic theology (that the Qur’an conceives the Injil as one revelation given to Jesus, parallel to the Torah given to Moses) the reading is defensible; but the historical claim it is marshalled to carry — that a distinct single Injil text once existed and is now lost, the canonical Gospels being only its altered residue — has no evidential trace: no manuscript, no patristic citation, no report of any such book, and the only Gospel in circulation in Muhammad’s day was the canonical one. The genuine mismatch between the Qur’an’s revelation-model and the Gospels’ literary genre is a category difference, not evidence of a lost scripture · advanced by the classical exegetes (al-Qurtubi, Ibn Kathir) and the modern translator Yusuf Ali; contested even among Muslim writers.

The claim

The Injil… spoken of by the Qur’an is not the New Testament. It is not the four Gospels now received as canonical. It is the single Gospel which, Islam teaches, was revealed to Jesus, and which he taught. Fragments of it survive in the received canonical Gospels…

— Abdullah Yusuf Ali, note on Q 3:3 / 5:46.1 The interpretation this page weighs is the positive premise beneath the doctrine of tahrif: that the Injil the Qur’an honours is a single, divine revelation given to Jesus — parallel to the Torah given to Moses, the Zabur to David, and the Qur’an to Muhammad — and not the four canonical Gospels the church transmits, which are human biographies holding at most a partial, altered residue of it. On this reading the true Injil is lost: if the Christians’ four books are not the Injil, then the revelation Jesus received has not survived intact, and the Gospel in their hands can only be its corrupted remains. The question is not “was the surviving text altered?” (that is the textual-corruption page) but “is there reason to think a separate, single Injil scripture ever existed and was lost?”

Who advances it

The single-Injil reading is the mainstream classical position, articulated by two of the most influential Sunni commentators: the Andalusian Maliki jurist al-Qurtubi (d. 671/1273), who even derives Injil from the Arabic najl — a “broad source” of light and guidance — and the Damascene traditionist Ibn Kathir (d. 774/1373), whose tafsir treats the Injil throughout as the one book God “gave to” Jesus, sifted by the Qur’an’s muhaymin testimony. Its crispest modern statement is the note of the translator Abdullah Yusuf Ali quoted above. The scholar Martin Whittingham reconstructs the reasoning that produced it: because the four canonical Gospels do not fit the Muslim expectation of a Injil — a single revelation dictated to a prophet — exegetes inferred that there must once have been a pure original Injil, only partly preserved in the Christians’ books.2

The evidence for

The steelman is a genuine argument from the Qur’an’s own theology of revelation, not a bare assertion. (1) The Qur’an speaks of the Injil as a book given to Jesus. It says God “gave him the Injil, in which was guidance and light” (Q 5:46), “We… gave him the Injil” (Q 57:27), and lists it among the scriptures God “sent down” alongside the Torah of Moses (Q 3:3) — consistently a single, discrete revelation entrusted to one prophet, never a multi-authored collection.3 (2) The parallel with the other scriptures is exact. On the Qur’an’s own scheme every prophet brings a book: Moses the Torah, David the Zabur, Muhammad the Qur’an. Read by that pattern, the Injil must be the book of Jesus — a revelation he received and proclaimed — and the four Gospels, which narrate his life in the third person and are ascribed to followers (Matthew, Mark, Luke, John) rather than to Jesus himself, simply do not answer to that description. (3) The genre mismatch is real. Modern Gospel scholarship agrees the canonical Gospels are ancient biographies (Graeco-Roman bioi) — narrative accounts about Jesus composed by others — not a scripture dictated to him.4 So the thing the Qur’an calls “the Injil given to Jesus” and the thing Christians call the Gospel are, on their face, two different kinds of object — which is exactly what the classical exegetes seized on.

Adversarial assessment

What holds — and only that far. As a description of what the Qur’an envisions, the reading is fair. The Qur’an does speak of a Injil given to Jesus in the same idiom it uses for the Torah and itself; and there is a real category difference between that revelation-model and the literary form of the canonical Gospels, which are biographies by followers, not a book Jesus received. A Muslim reading the Qur’an on its own terms will indeed expect “the Injil” to be a single prophetic revelation, and will find that the four Gospels do not match that expectation. To that extent the single-Injil reading is a coherent account of Qur’anic self-understanding.

Where it fails. The argument is marshalled to carry a far heavier historical load — that a distinct single Injil text actually existed and was lost — and there the evidence collapses. (1) No trace of any such book. There is no manuscript, no fragment, no patristic citation, no heresiological notice, no report from any quarter — Christian, Jewish, or later Muslim — of a separate, single Injil scripture given to Jesus and distinct from the Gospels. The one text sometimes produced as the “true Injil,” the Gospel of Barnabas, is a late-medieval forgery (14th–16th century), not a first-century document, and does not answer the description either.5 (2) The only Injil in circulation in the 7th century was the canonical one. What “the People of the Gospel” possessed in Muhammad’s day — in Greek, and in the Syriac Peshitta and Diatessaron — was the four Gospels, textually continuous with the manuscripts we hold now; there was no rival “single Injil” alongside them for the Qur’an to be pointing at. The inference runs backwards: from “the Gospels do not look like a prophetic revelation-book” the exegetes concluded “therefore a pure Injil once existed and is lost” — a leap from a genre observation to a vanished text, with nothing in the record to fill the gap.6 (3) The Qur’an’s own stance cuts against a lost text. It tells the People of the Gospel to “judge by what Allah has revealed therein” (Q 5:47) and stands as muhaymin (“guardian”) over the prior scripture (Q 5:48) — and one cannot be told to judge by, or to guard, a book that no longer exists. As the Muslim writer V. A. Mohamad Ashrof puts it, arguing the lost-Injil idea is a myth, “to be a muhaymin over something requires that the ‘something’ exists.” The single-Injil reading thus sits in tension with the same verses the intact-Gospel argument turns on.7

Keep the two claims apart. The category difference the exegetes noticed is real, but it is a mismatch between the Qur’an’s expectation of what a scripture is and the Gospels’ actual literary form — it evidences a difference of concept, not the past existence of a different document. That the Qur’an may assume a Injil-as-dictated-revelation does not establish that such a text was ever written; possibility of the concept is not proof of the artifact. Reading the mismatch as a lost book converts a theological premise into a historical claim it cannot support.

Assessment

Weak. Split into its two component claims, the reading comes apart cleanly. As an account of Qur’anic theology — that the Qur’an conceives the Injil as a single revelation given to Jesus, on the model of the Torah given to Moses — it is defensible, and the genre gap between that model and the canonical Gospels (biographies by followers, not a dictated book) is genuine. But that is as far as it reaches. The claim it is made to serve — that a distinct single Injil text once existed and was lost, the Christians’ Gospels being its corrupted residue — has no evidential basis whatever: no manuscript, no citation, no historical notice of such a book, and the only Injil the People of the Gospel actually possessed in Muhammad’s day was the canonical Gospel we still read. The argument reasons from a category difference (the Qur’an’s revelation-model vs. the Gospels’ genre) to a vanished document, a step the evidence does not license — and it runs against the Qur’an’s own directive to judge by and guard the Gospel then in Christian hands. As the load-bearing premise it is meant to be — that the true Gospel is a lost book, so the Christians’ can only be its remains — it is weak.
