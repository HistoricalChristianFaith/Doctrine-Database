Does the Qur’an itself presuppose an intact Gospel in Muhammad’s day?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: plausible — on its strongest verses (Q 5:47, 10:94) and on the modern finding that the formative-period exegetes assumed intact scriptures, the Qur’an’s own stance does read as presupposing a Gospel still available and authoritative in Muhammad’s day, which sits in real tension with a strong textual corruption; but the case is uneven — its “none can change His words” proof-texts fail on the classical reading (and one backfires), the muhaymin verse cuts the other way, and the argument leaves the mainstream single-Injil and meaning-only readings standing rather than refuting tahrif as such · advanced by Christian apologists (the “Islamic dilemma”), and converging with the historical finding of Gordon Nickel.

The claim

So let the People of the Gospel judge by what Allah has revealed therein. And whoever does not judge by what Allah has revealed — then it is those who are the defiantly disobedient.

— Qur’an 5:47 (Sahih International).1 The interpretation this page weighs is an internal-consistency reading of the Qur’an: that a cluster of its verses treats the earlier scriptures — and the Gospel above all — as still available, guidance-bearing, and authoritative in Muhammad’s own generation, and treats the word of God as something no one can alter. If that is what the Qur’an assumes, then a thoroughgoing corruption of the Gospel’s text — a version of tahrif on which the book in Christian hands is a doctored forgery — sits in tension with the Qur’an’s own stance. The question is not “did the text change?” (that is the companion textual-corruption page) but “does the Qur’an, read on its own terms, presuppose an intact Gospel?”

Who advances it

The argument is a staple of modern Christian–Muslim apologetics, where it circulates as the “Islamic dilemma”: the Qur’an both accuses the People of the Book of distortion and commands them to judge by their own scriptures, so (the apologist urges) it cannot coherently hold that those scriptures were already corrupt. Nabeel Qureshi puts the popular form crisply — “the Quran tells Christians to ‘judge by the Injil.’ That means they still had it in Muhammad’s day. The Injil is not a lost scripture” — and the same case is pressed at length by David Wood and others.2 Its serious scholarly warrant is independent of the apologetic framing: Gordon Nickel’s study of the earliest commentaries finds that the formative-period exegetes did not read the Qur’an’s tampering-verses as charging textual corruption but assumed an intact Torah and Gospel in the People of the Book’s hands — which is precisely the presupposition this argument reads off the text.3

The evidence for

The steelman rests on four strands of the Qur’an’s own language. (1) The command to judge by the Gospel. Q 5:47 tells “the People of the Gospel” to “judge by what Allah has revealed therein” — a directive that presupposes a Gospel in their hands containing what Allah revealed and fit to be judged by; the parallel verses fault Jews and Christians for not upholding the Torah and the Gospel (Q 5:68), which only makes sense if those books were accessible and reliable.4 (2) The appeal to prior scripture as confirmation. Q 10:94 tells Muhammad himself, “if you are in doubt… about that which We have revealed to you, then ask those who have been reading the Scripture before you” — an appeal that would be self-defeating if that Scripture were already corrupt.5 (3) The affirmation of the books. The Qur’an says God gave Jesus the Injil, “in which was guidance and light” (Q 5:46), and stands over the prior scripture as muhaymin, a guardian confirming it (Q 5:48) — present-tense affirmations, not elegies for a lost text.6 (4) The inviolability of God’s word. Q 6:115 and 18:27 declare that “none can alter His words” and “there is no changer of His words”; if the Torah and Gospel are the words of God, the apologist infers, then no one could have corrupted them.7 Taken together these paint a Qur’an that treats the earlier revelation as living and usable — the reading the earliest Muslim exegetes in fact adopted.

Adversarial assessment

What holds. Against a strong textual tahrif — the claim that the Gospel in Christian hands is a wholesale forgery, its true content buried — the argument has real force, and it is the force of the Qur’an’s plainest affirmations, not merely of apologetic ingenuity. Q 5:47 does command judgment by the Gospel, and Q 10:94 does appeal to the prior scripture as a check; a book one is told to judge by and consult is not naturally a book one is told is falsified. And the historical warrant is genuine: the formative exegetes read the tampering-verses just this way, assuming an intact text and faulting the People of the Book for concealing and mis-handling it — so the argument converges with the mainstream critical finding rather than resting on a strained proof-text.8

What is overstated or imprecise. Three limits keep it from establishing the strong apologetic conclusion (“the Qur’an therefore validates the Bible / refutes tahrif”). (1) Half the proof-texts fail on the classical reading — and one backfires. “None can change His words” (Q 6:115, 18:27) is read by the classical commentators not of the transmitted Bible but of God’s decrees: Tafsir al-Jalalayn glosses it as “none can change His words either by contravening His rulings or evading His appointed terms,” and al-Tabari reads it of what God has decreed. Worse for the argument, the report from Qatada preserved in al-Qurtubi says that even “if one were to change and substitute the words just as the People of the Book did with the Torah and Gospel, God doesn’t consider this” — so on the very verse the apologist invokes, the classical tradition grants that the Torah and Gospel were altered.9 (2) The muhaymin verse cuts the other way. To stand as “guardian” over the prior scripture is, in the classical reading, for the Qur’an to certify and correct it: Ibn Kathir glosses Q 5:48 as the Qur’an being “trustworthy over the Books that preceded it,” so that “whatever… conforms to the Qur’an is true, and whatever disagrees… is false” — which presupposes that the prior text may contain falsehood needing the Qur’an to sort out.10 (3) “Intact in Muhammad’s day” does not refute tahrif; it refutes only its strongest form. The command to judge by the Gospel is fully compatible with the Qur’an’s own charge that the People of the Book conceal what is revealed and “twist [Scripture] with their tongues” (Q 3:78, 4:46) — i.e. that an available text was mis-read and mis-applied, which is exactly the meaning-only reading, not a refutation of corruption. And it leaves wholly untouched the mainstream single-Injil position, on which the true Injil was a lost revelation given to Jesus and the canonical Gospels are its partial residue: that view already denies the Christians possessed the whole Injil, without needing a post-Qur’anic rewrite. So the argument is a genuine constraint on a strong textual tahrif, but not the general disproof its apologetic packaging claims.11

Assessment

Plausible. Read on its own terms, the Qur’an’s stance toward the earlier scriptures does look like the presupposition the argument names: on its strongest verses — the command to judge by the Gospel (Q 5:47) and the appeal to consult those who read the prior Scripture (Q 10:94) — and on the historical finding that the earliest exegetes assumed an intact Torah and Gospel, a Gospel available and authoritative in Muhammad’s day is the natural reading, and that reading is in real tension with a strong textual tahrif. But the case is uneven and the apologetic form overreaches. Its “none can change His words” proof-texts (Q 6:115, 18:27) fall to the classical decree-reading and, in the Qatada report, positively concede that the Torah and Gospel were altered; the muhaymin verse (Q 5:48) is read by the same tradition as the Qur’an correcting the prior text, not endorsing it; and a Gospel intact in Muhammad’s day is compatible with the Qur’an’s own charge of concealment and mis-reading — the meaning-only reading — and leaves the single-Injil position (a true Injil lost long before) standing. So the internal argument tells honestly against the wholesale-forgery reading of the Gospel — the same reading the manuscript record independently defeats — but it does not disprove tahrif as such: plausible as a constraint on a strong textual corruption, short of the Bible-validating conclusion it is usually marshalled to prove.
