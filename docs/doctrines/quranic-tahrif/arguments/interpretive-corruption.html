Was only the meaning of the earlier scriptures corrupted (tahrif al-ma‘na)?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: plausible — as a reading of what the Qur’an’s yuharrifuna verses charge and of how the earliest Muslim exegetes understood them it is well-grounded and, unlike the textual charge, survives the manuscript record untouched; but it is a far more modest claim than a strong tahrif needs, and it relocates rather than settles the substantive question · advanced by al-Qasim b. Ibrahim al-Rassi and Muhammad ‘Abduh.

Related arguments: the nature of the corruption — see also Was the text corrupted (tahrif al-lafz)?

The claim

Contrary to the general Islamic view, the Qur’an does not accuse Jews and Christians of altering the text of their scriptures, but rather of altering the truth which those scriptures contain. The people do this by concealing some of the sacred texts, by misapplying their precepts, or by altering words from their right position.

— Mahmoud Ayoub, stating the reformist premise (a statement about what the Qur’an charges, not a quotation of any one proponent).1 The interpretation this page weighs is that the Qur’an’s charge of “distortion” is of the handling and reading of scripture — concealing passages, misapplying precepts, twisting words in the recitation — not a claim that Jews and Christians physically rewrote the Torah and Gospel, so that the extant text is substantially authentic and only its interpretation went astray.

Who advances it

The counter-current runs from al-Qasim b. Ibrahim al-Rassi (d. 860), whose ninth-century Radd ‘ala al-Nasara faults the church’s interpretation rather than charging that the words were rewritten — “the texts themselves can thus be dissociated from the doctrinal tradition which has distorted their true teachings” — through to the modern reformist reading headed by Muhammad ‘Abduh (d. 1905), whom the standard scholarly taxonomy (Gaudeul and Caspar) classes among those for whom tahrif is falsification of the interpretation, not of the text. Its strongest modern warrant is the critical study of the formative Islamic exegesis itself.2

The evidence for

The steelman is historical and textual, and it is strong on its own ground. First, the earliest Muslim exegesis reads this way: Gordon Nickel’s study of the two foundational commentaries — Muqatil ibn Sulayman (d. 767) and al-Tabari (d. 923) — concludes that “the exegetes in the formative period did not in the first instance understand the Qur’anic verses of tampering to mean the textual corruption of the earlier scriptures,” but assumed an intact Torah and Gospel and faulted the People of the Book for concealing and mis-reading a text still in their hands; Lazarus-Yafeh and Adang trace the systematic tahrif al-lafz polemic as a later, medieval hardening held early by only a minority.3 Second, the Qur’anic text fits the modest reading better than the hard one: it affirms the earlier books — God gave Jesus the Injil, “in which was guidance and light” (Q 5:46), and the Qur’an stands muhaymin (“guardian”) over the scripture before it (Q 5:48) — and its yuharrifuna verses describe twisting words “with their tongues,” shifting them “from their [right] places,” and concealing what was revealed: acts of reading and handling, not a report that the autographs were rewritten. Third, and decisively for its durability, the meaning-only reading does not need the coordinated textual rewrite that the manuscript record makes impossible, so it survives the objection that sinks the textual charge.4

Adversarial assessment

What holds. As an account of what the Qur’an’s distortion-verses charge, and of how the earliest Muslim community understood them, the reading is well-grounded — arguably the sounder one, and on the narrow historical question (what did the formative exegetes mean?) more likely than not. It is internally coherent and, because it concedes an intact text, it is immune to the manuscript refutation that defeats the hard-textual charge.

What is overstated or imprecise. Three limits keep it from doing the work a strong tahrif wants. (1) It is a much thinner claim, and it concedes the ground the restoration narrative needs. Meaning-only corruption leaves the text of the Bible standing; that is precisely why Rashid Rida rejected it as strategically costly — conceding the Torah’s textual integrity, he warned, “gave missionaries the chance” against Islam. If the text is sound, the polemical work of delegitimizing the Christian scriptures is undone, and the quarrel reduces to a theological disagreement over how to read them. (2) Its own celebrated proponents do not hold it cleanly. al-Qasim, the supposed founder, in practice treats the transmitted text as contaminated and freely emends it — silently excising the Sermon-on-the-Mount passages on turning the other cheek and recasting the temptation narrative (naming the tempter Iblis) — so that David Thomas can say his method “did not involve new interpretation but actual editing and rewriting of the transmitted text itself”; and the Tafsir al-Manar that carries ‘Abduh’s authority swung, in Rida’s hands, back to the textual charge. “Meaning-only” as a stable Muslim consensus is therefore illusory.5 (3) The substantive charge is relocated, not resolved — and on one point it strains. Whether the church actually misread the Gospel — whether the Trinity, the divinity of Christ and the atoning crucifixion are eisegesis rather than the text’s plain sense — is the very theological dispute between the two faiths, and this reading asserts the Muslim side of it without settling it. It strains hardest at the crucifixion: an intact Gospel narrates the crucifixion as an event, not a doctrine read into a verse, so the meaning-only reading sits in tension with the Qur’an’s own apparent denial that Jesus was crucified (Q 4:157) — a datum that pushes back toward some textual or substitutionary claim rather than a mere misinterpretation.6

Assessment

Plausible — and, on the narrow historical question of what the Qur’an’s distortion-verses charged and how the formative exegetes read them, probably right: the meaning-only reading is well-grounded, internally coherent, and (conceding an intact text) untouched by the manuscript record that defeats the textual charge. But it is a far more modest claim than a strong tahrif needs. It surrenders the textual integrity the restoration narrative wants to impugn; its own celebrated proponents (al-Qasim, the ‘Abduh–Rida commentary) do not actually hold it cleanly; and the substantive charge that the church misread the Gospel is a contested theological dispute this reading relocates rather than resolves — the more so where the meaning-only line runs against a hard Qur’anic datum such as the crucifixion-denial of Q 4:157. Plausible as an account of the charge; contested as the substantive claim it is marshalled to support.
