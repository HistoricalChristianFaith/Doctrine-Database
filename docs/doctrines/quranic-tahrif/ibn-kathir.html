Ibn Kathir on the Injil (the single revealed Gospel)

Dates: c. 1300–1373 (701–774 AH) · Wikipedia
Summary: The Damascene Shafi‘i traditionist and historian Abu al-Fida’ Isma‘il ibn ‘Umar ibn Kathir, a student of Ibn Taymiyya, wrote in his Tafsir al-Qur’an al-‘Azim (Tafsir Ibn Kathir) the most widely read of the classical Sunni commentaries. On the Qur’an’s “Injil verses” he articulates the mainstream reading that stands behind the doctrine of tahrif: the Injil is a single, divine revelation given directly to Jesus — parallel to the Torah given to Moses and the Qur’an given to Muhammad — and the Qur’an stands as the trustworthy guardian (muhaymin) over the earlier books, its testimony the measure of what in them is true. On this reading the Gospel Islam honours is not the four canonical Gospels but the one revelation Jesus received, of which the Christians’ books are at best a partial and altered residue. He sits on the tahrif timeline as the classical exegetical anchor of the single-Injil view, alongside his older Andalusian contemporary al-Qurtubi.

Claims & evidence

The Injil is one revelation given to Jesus — “guidance and light”

Commenting on Qur’an 5:46 — “And We gave him the Injil, in which was guidance and light and confirmation of the Tawrah that had come before it” — Ibn Kathir treats the Injil straightforwardly as the scripture God gave to Jesus, a single revealed book set beside the Torah and the Qur’an. He glosses “guidance and light” as

a guidance that directs to the truth and a light that removes the doubts and solves disputes.

— Tafsir Ibn Kathir on Q 5:46.1 He adds that Jesus, in receiving the Injil, confirmed and kept the Torah —

he adhered to the Tawrah, except for the few instances that clarified the truth where the Children of Israel differed.

— Tafsir Ibn Kathir on Q 5:46.2 The same framing runs through his notes on the other Injil verses: on Q 3:3 the Injil is listed among the books God “sent down” (Torah to Moses, Injil to Jesus, Qur’an to Muhammad), and on Q 57:27 “We… gave him the Injil” — in each case a discrete revelation entrusted to a single prophet, never the composite, multi-authored collection Christians carry.3

The Qur’an as muhaymin: the guardian that measures the earlier books

The mechanism by which the extant Gospels are subordinated is Ibn Kathir’s reading of Q 5:48, which calls the Qur’an muhaymin (“guardian,” “trustworthy witness”) over the scripture before it. He writes:

The Qur’an is trustworthy over the Books that preceded it. Therefore, whatever in these previous Books conforms to the Qur’an is true, and whatever disagrees with the Qur’an is false.

— Tafsir Ibn Kathir on Q 5:48.4 This is the classical instrument of tahrif in practice: the earlier scriptures are not thrown out wholesale, but their contents are sifted by the Qur’an’s testimony — what agrees is retained as the residue of the true revelation, what conflicts (the crucifixion, the divinity of Christ, the Trinity) is judged the mark of alteration. The Gospel that survives in Christian hands is thereby demoted from a self-authenticating revelation to a source whose truth must be certified from outside itself.

The command “judge by the Injil” belongs to an earlier age

Q 5:47 — “Let the people of the Injil judge by what Allah has revealed therein” — might seem to endorse the Gospel Christians actually possess. Ibn Kathir is among the exegetes who read it otherwise: the injunction, on his account, addresses the people of the Injil before the coming of Muhammad, after which they are bound to the Qur’an. As the scholar Martin Whittingham summarises his position, Ibn Kathir mentions the possibility that the verse “refers to a command given to the people of the Injil before the time of Muhammad, whereas after the time of Muhammad they should consult the Qur’an.” The verse, so read, is no warrant for the standing authority of the Christians’ text.5

Notes

  • What Ibn Kathir does and does not say. On these verses Ibn Kathir does not stop to name the four canonical Gospels and pronounce them counterfeit; his commentary treats the Injil as the single book given to Jesus and lets the Qur’an’s muhaymin testimony do the sorting. The explicit contrast — “the Injil is not the four Gospels” — is drawn most sharply by the later tradition working from exactly this material. The translator Abdullah Yusuf Ali gives its crispest modern statement: “The Injil… spoken of by the Qur’an is not the New Testament. It is not the four Gospels now received as canonical. It is the single Gospel which, Islam teaches, was revealed to Jesus, and which he taught. Fragments of it survive in the received canonical Gospels…” Ibn Kathir supplies the exegetical foundation; Yusuf Ali the slogan.6
  • Where this sits between the two poles. Ibn Kathir’s handling is closer to Ibn Hazm’s hard tahrif al-lafz than to the meaning-only current of al-Qasim b. Ibrahim al-Rassi: the very premise that the true Injil is lost and only fragments remain assumes the text did not survive intact. But his emphasis falls less on cataloguing the Christians’ textual errors than on the positive doctrine of the one revealed Injil and the Qur’an’s authority to adjudicate it — the frame within which the corruption charge does its work.
  • The verbatim English above is the Darussalam abridged Tafsir Ibn Kathir (ed. Safiur-Rahman al-Mubarakpuri), as reproduced per verse at the cited pages; the Arabic original is Tafsir al-Qur’an al-‘Azim. His older contemporary al-Qurtubi (al-Jami‘ li-Ahkam al-Qur’an) holds the same single-Injil reading, but his commentary on these verses is not available in a published English translation; he is carried on the summary timeline through the scholarly reporting of his position rather than a firsthand English quote.
