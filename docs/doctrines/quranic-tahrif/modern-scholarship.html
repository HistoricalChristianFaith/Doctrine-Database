Modern scholarship on tahrif: how the charge developed, and the record it is measured against

Dates: 1992–2011 (modern critical study of the tahrif tradition and of New-Testament transmission)
Summary: The closing witness is not a person but the modern scholarship that lets the timeline be read as a development. Two findings converge. First, the historical-critical study of the early tafsir (Gordon Nickel; Hava Lazarus-Yafeh; Camilla Adang) shows that the wholesale textual-corruption charge was not the Qur’an’s original meaning nor the earliest exegetes’ reading: the formative-period commentators assumed an intact Torah and Gospel and faulted the People of the Book for concealing and mis-reading a text still in their hands — the hard-textual polemic of Ibn Hazm and, later, Rashid Rida is a medieval and modern hardening of a charge that began far narrower. Second, the manuscript record of the New Testament — the terrain the textual charge stands or falls on — is among the best-attested of any ancient text, and physically predates Islam by centuries, so the specific claim that the Gospel was rewritten to bury the true Injil faces evidence it does not fit.

Claims & evidence

Nickel: the earliest exegetes did not read the tampering-verses as textual corruption

the exegetes in the formative period did not in the first instance understand the Qur’anic verses of tampering to mean the textual corruption of the earlier scriptures.

— Gordon Nickel, Narratives of Tampering in the Earliest Commentaries on the Qur’an (Brill, 2011), conclusion, pp. 223–224. Nickel works through the two foundational commentaries — Muqatil ibn Sulayman (d. 767) and al-Tabari (d. 923) — and finds that their treatment of the “distortion” verses builds a “narrative framework” that, far from presupposing a lost or rewritten scripture, “favors the scenario of a variety of tampering actions revolving around an intact Torah.” The verbs are read as concealing what the scripture said and twisting it in the telling — the sin of Muhammad’s Jewish contemporaries who would not acknowledge him — not the physical rewriting of the book. The wholesale tahrif al-lafz charge is thus a later development, not the Qur’an’s plain sense or its earliest exegesis.1

Lazarus-Yafeh and Adang: the textual charge is a medieval hardening held first by a minority

In early Islam, tahrif was bounded by the understanding that the holy books were misinterpreted for an ulterior motive. There was no belief that the texts of the Torah themselves, or the Gospel were rearranged.

— Ramazan Biçer, “The Alteration of the Sacred Books According to the Islamic Theologians,” RAIS Conference Proceedings (2020), 258. This is the same finding from a Muslim theologian’s side, and it matches the picture drawn by the two standard studies of the medieval polemic. Hava Lazarus-Yafeh’s Intertwined Worlds argues that systematic biblical textual criticism in the Islamic world — the catalogue-of-contradictions method — effectively begins with Ibn Hazm in the eleventh century, not with the Qur’an or the first exegetes. Camilla Adang’s survey of nine early writers from Ibn Rabban to Ibn Hazm finds that only two of them — al-Maqdisi and Ibn Hazm — held that the actual text (as against its meaning) had been corrupted; al-Tabari himself could speak of “the Torah that they possess today.” The hard-textual reading, in short, was a minority position that hardened into dominance only across the classical centuries.2

The New Testament manuscript record: abundant, and older than Islam

In contrast with these figures, the textual critic of the New Testament is embarrassed by the wealth of material.

— Bruce M. Metzger and Bart D. Ehrman, The Text of the New Testament: Its Transmission, Corruption, and Restoration (4th ed., Oxford, 2005), 51. The textual form of the tahrif charge — that the Gospel was rewritten so as to bury the true Injil, or to erase a prophecy of Muhammad — is a historical claim about the transmission of a text, and on that terrain the evidence runs the other way. The New Testament survives in thousands of Greek manuscripts, alongside early translations and a dense web of patristic citation; and the physical record long predates Islam. The Rylands fragment 𝔿52 of John, Metzger and Ehrman note, was dated by its script to “the first half of the second century” and is “the oldest copy of any portion of the New Testament known to be in existence,” while the great codices Vaticanus and Sinaiticus are of the fourth century — that is, roughly two to five centuries before Muhammad. A coordinated rewriting of the Christian scriptures in reaction to Islam is therefore chronologically impossible: the manuscripts that would have to have been altered were already ancient, dispersed, and in circulation before Islam existed.3

Even the skeptical end concedes the variants are overwhelmingly minor

of all the hundreds of thousands of textual changes found among our manuscripts, most of them are completely insignificant, immaterial, of no real importance for anything other than showing that scribes could not spell or keep focused any better than the rest of us.

— Bart D. Ehrman, Misquoting Jesus: The Story Behind Who Changed the Bible and Why (HarperSanFrancisco, 2005), 207–208 (cf. 10–11). Ehrman is the most prominent modern popularizer of New-Testament textual instability, and no apologist — which makes the concession weightier: the sheer number of variants (which he elsewhere presses hard) resolves, on his own accounting, into a mass of trivial slips against a recoverable text. The honest qualification cuts the other way and must be kept: Ehrman denies that the variants are all theologically inert — “It would be wrong… to say… that the changes in our text have no real bearing on… the theological conclusions that one draws,” he adds, holding that a meaningful minority do matter. The point for tahrif is narrower and survives that qualification: the manuscript tradition shows ordinary scribal drift within a stable, reconstructable text, not the wholesale doctrinally-motivated rewriting the textual-corruption charge requires.4

Notes

  • These findings do not settle every strand of the doctrine. They bear directly on the textual charge (tahrif al-lafz) and on the “lost single Injil” premise — both weakened by the transmission evidence and by the discovery that the earliest tradition did not read the verses that way. They leave untouched the distinct tahrif al-ma‘na claim (that Christians misread a genuine text), which is a question of interpretation, not of manuscripts, and is argued on its own terms elsewhere on this timeline.
  • The scholars cited are of varied conviction — Nickel a Christian specialist in Muslim–Christian polemic, Lazarus-Yafeh and Adang historians of the Islamic tradition, Biçer a Muslim theologian, Metzger a believing text-critic and Ehrman a skeptic — and across that spectrum none supports the wholesale textual-rewrite reading. That convergence is the counter-witness against the hard-textual verdict of Ibn Hazm and its revival in Rashid Rida.
