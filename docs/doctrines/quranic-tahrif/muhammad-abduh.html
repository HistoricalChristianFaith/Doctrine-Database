Muhammad ‘Abduh and the Tafsir al-Manar (the modern reformist reading of tahrif — and its internal split)

Dates: c. 1849–1905 · Wikipedia
Summary: Muhammad ‘Abduh — Grand Mufti of Egypt from 1899 and, with Jamal al-Din al-Afghani, the founder of Islamic modernism — is the figure most often named as the modern anchor of the “meaning-only” reading of tahrif: that the Qur’an charges the People of the Book with corrupting the sense of their scriptures (tahrif al-ma‘na), not with rewriting the text (tahrif al-lafz), so that the extant Bible is substantially authentic and only its interpretation went astray. The reading is real, and scholarship does class ‘Abduh with it. But the picture is less tidy than the popular attribution makes it: the Qur’an-commentary that carries his name, the Tafsir al-Manar, was largely written by his student Muhammad Rashid Rida (1865–1935), and in Rida’s own hands the movement swung back toward the classical textual charge — Rida explicitly rejecting the very “impossibility-of-coordination” argument the meaning-only case rests on, and reproving earlier Muslim scholars for conceding the Bible’s textual integrity. ‘Abduh thus sits at the modern end of the tahrif timeline not as a clean consensus but as the head of a reformist current that is itself split — a genuine counter-voice to the hard-textual tradition of Ibn Hazm, alongside a revival of that very tradition in his closest heir.

Claims & evidence

The reformist reading: a corruption of meaning, not of text

‘Abduh belongs to a strand of modern Muslim exegesis that reads the Qur’an’s yuharrifuna (“they distort”) verses as an accusation about the handling of scripture — concealing passages, misapplying precepts, twisting words in the reading — rather than a claim that Jews and Christians physically rewrote the Torah and Gospel. In the standard scholarly taxonomy of Muslim positions on tahrif, Jean-Marie Gaudeul and Robert Caspar place ‘Abduh among those (with Ibn Sina and, on the usual reading, Ibn Khaldun) for whom tahrif is falsification of the interpretation of the texts, not of the texts themselves.1 The Lebanese scholar Mahmoud Ayoub states the reformist premise in general terms:

Contrary to the general Islamic view, the Qur’an does not accuse Jews and Christians of altering the text of their scriptures, but rather of altering the truth which those scriptures contain. The people do this by concealing some of the sacred texts, by misapplying their precepts, or by altering words from their right position.

— a statement, note, about what the Qur’an says, not a quotation of ‘Abduh; Ayoub elsewhere judges the attitudes of both ‘Abduh and Rida toward Christianity “not intransigent” but “conciliatory.”2

The widely-quoted “true Gospel” statement — and its uncertain provenance

The line most often cited to make ‘Abduh the standard-bearer of the meaning-only camp is this, attributed to him in apologetic and reference literature:

The charge of corruption of the Biblical texts makes no sense at all. It would not have been possible for Jews and Christians everywhere to agree on changing the text. Even if those in Arabia had done it, the difference between their Book and those of their brothers, let us say, in Syria and Europe would have been obvious… We believe that these Gospel accounts are the true Gospel.3

This is the impossibility-of-coordination argument in its sharpest form: scattered communities across Arabia, Syria and Europe could not have converged on one identical alteration, so the surviving Gospels are essentially the Gospel Jesus’ followers received. It is a coherent argument and a fair statement of the meaning-only position — but the attribution is ⚠ unverified: the English sentence circulates in this exact wording across web apologetics and reference sites without any primary citation — no work, volume, or page — and its polished phrasing reads like a paraphrase rather than a rendered translation. No firsthand passage in ‘Abduh’s writings or in the Tafsir al-Manar has yet been located behind it. The defensible ground for placing ‘Abduh in the meaning-only camp is the scholarly classification above, not this floating quotation; it is reproduced here because a reader will meet it, and it should be met with its provenance flagged.

The twist: the Tafsir al-Manar takes the harder, textual line

The commentary that bears ‘Abduh’s authority cuts against the neat story. The Tafsir al-Manar grew out of ‘Abduh’s Qur’an lectures at al-Azhar (1899–1905), taken down and greatly expanded by Rida, who carried the work on alone after ‘Abduh’s death until his own in 1935; it reaches only into Surah 12 and is, as Jane McAuliffe notes, largely Rida’s composition. And Rida, in his 1901 al-Manar polemic against the Coptic apologist Niqula Ghabriyal, met the impossibility argument head-on — because in that exchange it was Ghabriyal, the Christian, who advanced it:

Ghabriyāl argued that it was impossible that both the Old and the New Testament were distorted, as both Judaism and Christianity became widespread throughout the East and the West. In his words, ‘the Scriptures, especially the New Testament, was translated from the original Greek and Hebrew languages into the languages of the peoples among whom they were spread, including Arabic, Aramaic, Abyssinian, Coptic, and Latin.’ It was not logical, therefore, that these thousands of Christians had collaborated on altering the Scriptures.

— Umar Ryad’s account of al-Manar vol. 4/19 (Dec. 1901).4 Far from adopting that argument, Rida rejected it and defended a real corruption of the biblical text:

As for the alteration of the Bible, Riḍā argued that Muslims do not acknowledge that all these Scriptures were accurately transmitted from the prophets. They believe that the Jews and Christians subsequently altered them after dispersing throughout the East and the West, and each people embracing Judaism and Christianity had translated them into their own languages.

— Ryad, on al-Manar vol. 4/18.5 Rida drew the distinction that keeps this from being a bare contradiction of the reformist premise: Muslims “believed in the genuineness of the origin of the Bible” — that God truly revealed a Torah and a Gospel — without believing the present text secure from later corruption. He pressed the standard textual arguments (the Torah cannot have been written by Moses, since it narrates his death and burial in the third person; its transmission was broken at the destruction of Jerusalem and its text reassembled by Ezra), aligning himself, as Ryad observes, with Ibn Kathir, al-Qurtubi, al-Tabari and al-Qarafi — the hard-textual classical tradition, not the meaning-only one.6

Rida even reproached the meaning-only reading by name, faulting earlier Muslim authorities for surrendering too much:

Riḍā criticised Muslim scholars, such as Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī and others, for their conclusion that the Torah was transmitted by uninterrupted chains of transmission (tawātur), and that its distortion (taḥrīf) according to the Qur’ānic verses was not related to the text… Riḍā maintained that such views gave missionaries the chance to convince common people that Muslim scholars admitted the invulnerability of the Torah against textual [corruption].

— Ryad, ch. 1.7 For Rida the meaning-only concession was not merely mistaken but strategically costly: it handed the Christian missionary his best card. That is the opposite of the position the popular quotation puts in ‘Abduh’s mouth.

Notes

  • Two men, one commentary — and a genuine difference of temper. The tahrif verses most in play (Q 2:75, 3:78, 4:46) fall within the stretch of the Tafsir al-Manar that ‘Abduh himself dictated (Surat al-Fatiha through Q 4:125), so a meaning-only reading there may well be his; the Surah-5 material and the anti-missionary polemics against Ghabriyal belong to Rida’s solo continuation. The honest upshot is that the “modern rejection of textual corruption” is not a single reformist voice but a spectrum: ‘Abduh (and the broader modernist reading Ayoub and Gaudeul–Caspar describe) leaning to tahrif al-ma‘na, and Rida — the pen behind most of the actual commentary — swinging back to tahrif al-lafz. Treating “‘Abduh and Rida in the Tafsir al-Manar” as a united meaning-only front, as popular summaries do, does not survive a reading of the text.
  • Why he still keeps the timeline honest. Even with the caveat, ‘Abduh earns his place as the internal counter-current: the modernist reading he heads is the live Muslim alternative to the hard-textual verdict of Ibn Hazm, and a partial return to the measured middle of Ibn Taymiyya, who likewise ranked the corrupted meaning above the corrupted wording. That a movement launched under ‘Abduh’s banner could also revive the textual charge is itself the point — the meaning-versus-text question is unsettled within Islam right down to the modern period, which is exactly why the timeline runs a counter-voice at its far end rather than a consensus.
  • Provenance of the quotations. The verbatim English on Rida is Umar Ryad’s translation and close reading of the Arabic al-Manar (the volume/issue references are Ryad’s own, given below); his study is the standard critical treatment of the al-Manar circle’s writing on Christianity and is open access. The “true Gospel” passage attributed to ‘Abduh is, by contrast, cited only to secondary web sources that supply no primary reference, and is flagged unverified above.
