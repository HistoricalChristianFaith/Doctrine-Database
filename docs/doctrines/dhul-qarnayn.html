Dhū’l-Qarnayn and the Syriac Alexander Legend

Sūrat al-Kahf — the same sura that carries the Seven Sleepers (the Companions of the Cave, 18:9–26) — tells, a few verses later, of Dhū’l-Qarnayn, “the two-horned one” (18:83–101): a God-favoured world-ruler who journeys to the setting and the rising of the sun and, at a pass between two mountains, builds a rampart of iron and molten copper to shut out Gog and Magog until the last day. The classical commentator al-Ṭabarī and the modern philologists from Theodor Nöldeke to Kevin van Bladel identify the figure with Alexander the Great — the Alexander of the Alexander Romance as reworked by Syriac Christians — while a South-Arabian tradition made him a king of Himyar. Nöldeke and van Bladel trace the episode from the Greek Romance through the Syriac Christian Legend concerning Alexander into the Qur’an, as the sleepers’ story in the same chapter is traced to a Christian source.

This page is not a life of Alexander; it is a history of a legend and of how it travelled, set out earliest extant source first: the Greek Alexander Romance of Pseudo-Callisthenes, its Syriac descendants — the Syriac Romance, the Christian Legend concerning Alexander (the Neṣḥānā, the earliest extant text to carry the wall against Gog and Magog and the end-times prophecy together), and the verse Alexander Poem — then the Qur’anic Dhū’l-Qarnayn, the Muslim commentators who differed over his identity, the Christian and Muslim apocalyptic that grew around the barrier, and the modern scholarship (Nöldeke, Reinink, van Bladel, Tesei) on the texts’ relations and dates. Its “witnesses” are the datable sources that carry the story, ordered by the age of the source — not a claimed chronology of the events they narrate.

Timeline

The Greek Alexander Romance of Pseudo-Callisthenes (c. 3rd c. AD)

The later Alexander traditions descend from a Greek novel. The Alexander Romance, a legendary biography ascribed to Callisthenes (Alexander’s court historian, who died before his king; hence the modern name “Pseudo-Callisthenes”), survives in its oldest Greek form, the α recension, from roughly the third century AD. Armenian, Ethiopic, Latin, Persian and Syriac versions descend from it. The eschatological iron gate penning Gog and Magog is absent from the earliest Greek form; van Donzel and Schmidt treat it as a later apocalyptic addition made in Christian hands.1

The Syriac Alexander tradition: the Romance, the Legend, and the Poem (6th–7th c.)

In the Christian East the story was reworked. A Syriac Alexander Romance rendered Pseudo-Callisthenes into Syriac (perhaps through a lost Persian intermediary); a short prose Christian Legend concerning Alexander — the Neṣḥānā d-Aleksandrōs — presents the conqueror as a monotheist who vows his kingdom to God, travels to the fetid sea where the sun sets and to the place of its rising, and builds a gate of iron and bronze against the Huns and Gog and Magog, sealing it with a prophecy of the end of days and of a Roman world-empire triumphant over Persia. Theodor Nöldeke placed the Legend in north Mesopotamia c. 629–630, G. J. Reinink read it as Heraclian propaganda composed just after the emperor’s victory over Khosrow II, and Kevin van Bladel adopted that dating; Tommaso Tesei (2023) dates its core a century earlier, to Justinian’s reign (see modern scholarship). A verse Alexander Poem, a mēmrā once ascribed to Jacob of Serugh (d. 521) and dated by Reinink, its editor, to the early 630s (hence the name “Pseudo-Jacob”), followed. Van Bladel finds in the Legend a counterpart to almost every element of the Qur’anic episode.2

The Qur’an: Dhū’l-Qarnayn, the two-horned one (early 7th c.)

In Sūrat al-Kahf 18:83–101 the story is told allusively, in answer to a question: Dhū’l-Qarnayn, “the possessor of the two horns,” whom God “established upon the earth,” follows a way to “the setting of the sun,” finds it setting “in a spring of dark mud” and a people to be judged, then journeys to “the rising of the sun” and a people with no shelter from it; at last, “between two mountains,” he meets a people who beg protection from Yaʾjūj wa-Maʾjūj (Gog and Magog) and builds them a barrier of iron sheets and poured molten copper that the corrupters cannot scale or breach — a barrier that will stand “until the promise of my Lord comes,” then be levelled at the end (18:98–99). Whether the Qur’an drew on the Syriac Legend, and whether the figure is Alexander, are among the disputed readings below.3

The Islamic elaboration and the identity debate (8th–10th c.)

Because the Qur’an names no name behind the epithet, the Muslim commentators (tafsīr) differed over who Dhū’l-Qarnayn was. The dominant line, al-Ṭabarī among them, took him for Alexander (Iskandar). A Yemeni tradition — traced to Wahb ibn Munabbih and reported through Ibn Hishām — made him instead a pre-Islamic South-Arabian Himyarite king, al-Ṣaʿb ibn Dhī Marāthid, on the ground that “Dhū” is a Himyarite name-element; the biography it gives him parallels the Alexander Romance. Ṭabarī read the “two horns” as the two extremities, east and west, that Dhū’l-Qarnayn reached.4

The apocalyptic afterlife: Gog and Magog and the Gate (late 7th c. onward)

The gate motif continued in later apocalyptic. Fused with the biblical Gog and Magog of Ezekiel 38–39 and Revelation 20, Alexander’s iron gate in the Caucasus — located by tradition at the Caspian Gates and the pass of Darband/Derbent, and connected by van Donzel and Schmidt with real Sasanian frontier walls against the northern nomads — became a fixture of end-times expectation. In Christian apocalyptic the Syriac Apocalypse of Pseudo-Methodius (c. 690–692) made the breaking of Alexander’s gate a station on the road to the end and is the earliest extant source for the Last Roman Emperor — the final ruler who defeats the Ishmaelites, sees Gog and Magog loosed and destroyed, then lays his crown on the Cross at Golgotha and surrenders the kingdom to God — a figure taken up in the medieval Latin West. In Islamic eschatology the release of Yaʾjūj wa-Maʾjūj became one of the great signs of the Hour, developed in the malāḥim/fitan literature and the hadith, which drew on the Qur’an’s own “when the promise of my Lord comes” (18:98); the caliph al-Wāthiq (r. 842–847) sent Sallām the Interpreter to find the gate. The motif ran on into medieval maps and the vernacular romances, and the penned nations were identified in turn with the Khazars and later the Mongols.5

Modern scholarship: the texts compared and dated (1890 → today)

The year after Budge’s 1889 edition put the Syriac texts in reach, Theodor Nöldeke (1890) argued that the Syriac Alexander Legend is the source of the Qur’anic Dhū’l-Qarnayn episode and placed the Legend in north Mesopotamia c. 629–630. G. J. Reinink elaborated the dating into a case for the Legend as Heraclian propaganda; Kevin van Bladel (2008) renewed Nöldeke’s thesis with a point-by-point structural comparison, concluding the Qur’anic passage “is a retelling” of the Syriac text. Tommaso Tesei (2023) dates the core Legend to the reign of Justinian, a century earlier, with a Heraclian-era prophecy interpolated; van Bladel, reviewing the book, notes that on this dating too the Legend predates the Qur’an.6

Disputed readings

Questions about how the Qur’anic episode, its sources, and the Gog-and-Magog barrier have been read.

The Alexander connection

Two questions about the Qur’an’s two-horned world-ruler and the Alexander legend: who Dhū’l-Qarnayn is, and whether his account derives from the Syriac Legend.

  • Is Dhū’l-Qarnayn (Qur’an 18:83–101) Alexander the Great? — Alexander (Iskandar) or the Himyarite king al-Ṣaʿb.
  • Did the Qur’an’s Dhū’l-Qarnayn account (18:83–101) draw on the Syriac Alexander Legend?

Further questions concern the Legend’s date, the barrier’s geography, and two other passages of the sura:

  • When was the Syriac Alexander Legend (the Neṣḥānā) composed — and did it originate as Heraclian propaganda? — c. 629–630, or a sixth-century core with a later prophecy.
  • Does Alexander’s Gog-and-Magog barrier reflect a real Caucasus/Sasanian frontier wall?
  • Does Qur’an 18:86 assert that the sun literally sets in a “spring of dark mud”? — turns on the verb wajada, “he found.”
  • Does the al-Kahf Moses–al-Khiḍr pericope (Qur’an 18:60–82) draw on the Alexander water-of-life legend?
