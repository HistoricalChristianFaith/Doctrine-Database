Dhū’l-Qarnayn and the Syriac Alexander Legend

Sūrat al-Kahf — the same sura that carries the Seven Sleepers (the Companions of the Cave, 18:9–26) — tells, a few verses later, of Dhū’l-Qarnayn, “the two-horned one” (18:83–101): a God-favoured world-ruler who journeys to the setting and the rising of the sun and, at a pass between two mountains, builds a rampart of iron and molten copper to shut out Gog and Magog until the last day. On the settled judgement of modern scholarship that figure is Alexander the Great — not the historian’s Alexander, but the fabulous Alexander of the Alexander Romance, reshaped by Syriac Christians into an apocalyptic prophet and carried from there into the Qur’an. It is a textbook case of a heroic legend crossing from Greek paganism through Syriac Christianity into Islamic scripture — exactly parallel to the sleepers’ own Christian-to-Qur’anic passage in the very same chapter.

This page is not a life of Alexander; it is a history of a legend and of how it travelled, set out earliest source first: the Greek Alexander Romance of Pseudo-Callisthenes, its Syriac descendants — the Syriac Romance, the pivotal Christian Legend concerning Alexander (the Neṣḥānā, where the wall against Gog and Magog and the end-times prophecy first appear), and the verse Alexander Poem — then the Qur’anic Dhū’l-Qarnayn, the Muslim commentators who argued over his identity, the vast Gog-and-Magog apocalyptic that grew around the barrier, and the modern scholarship (Nöldeke → Reinink → van Bladel → Tesei) that traced the chain and still argues out its dating. Its “witnesses” are the datable sources that carry the story, ordered by the age of the source — not a claimed chronology of the events they narrate.

Timeline

The fountainhead: the Greek Alexander Romance of Pseudo-Callisthenes (c. 3rd c. AD)

The whole tradition rises from a single Greek novel. The Alexander Romance, a legendary biography falsely ascribed to Callisthenes (Alexander’s real court historian, dead before his king and so no possible author — hence “Pseudo-Callisthenes”), took shape by roughly the third century AD in its oldest surviving Greek form, the α recension. From it descend every later branch — Armenian, Ethiopic, Latin, Persian, and above all the Syriac. What the Romance did not yet carry is the feature that would matter most downstream: the eschatological iron gate penning Gog and Magog is not present in the earliest Greek form — it is a later apocalyptic graft, added when the tale reached Christian hands.1

The Syriac Alexander tradition: the Romance, the Legend, and the Poem (6th–7th c.)

In the Christian East the story was made over. A Syriac Alexander Romance rendered Pseudo-Callisthenes into Syriac (perhaps through a lost Persian intermediary); then, crucially, a short prose Christian Legend concerning Alexander — the Neṣḥānā d-Aleksandrōs — recast the pagan conqueror as a monotheist who vows his kingdom to God, travels to the fetid sea where the sun sets and to the place of its rising, and builds a gate of iron and bronze against the Huns and Gog and Magog, sealing it with a prophecy of the end of days and of a Roman world-empire triumphant over Persia. Theodor Nöldeke placed this Legend in north Mesopotamia c. 629–630, and G. J. Reinink read it as Heraclian propaganda composed just after the emperor’s victory over Khosrow II — a dating renewed by Kevin van Bladel and now contested by Tommaso Tesei, who would push the core a century earlier. A verse Alexander Poem (a mēmrā long miscredited to Jacob of Serugh, in fact by a later “Pseudo-Jacob”) followed within a decade. Here, in the Syriac Legend, the elements the Qur’an would carry are all in place.2

The Qur’an: Dhū’l-Qarnayn, the two-horned one (early 7th c.)

In Sūrat al-Kahf 18:83–101 the same story appears, told allusively as a sign already known: Dhū’l-Qarnayn, “the possessor of the two horns,” whom God “established upon the earth,” follows a way to “the setting of the sun,” finds it setting “in a spring of dark mud” and a people to be judged, then journeys to “the rising of the sun” and a people with no shelter from it; at last, “between two mountains,” he meets a people who beg protection from Yaʾjūj wa-Maʾjūj (Gog and Magog) and builds them a barrier of iron sheets and poured molten copper that the corrupters cannot scale or breach — a barrier that will stand “until the promise of my Lord comes,” then be levelled at the end (18:98–99). Every one of these beats has its more explicit counterpart in the Syriac Legend; whether the Qur’an drew on it, and whether the figure is Alexander, are weighed below.3

The Islamic elaboration and the identity debate (8th–10th c.)

Because the Qur’an names no name behind the epithet, the Muslim commentators (tafsīr) argued over who Dhū’l-Qarnayn was. The mainstream, al-Ṭabarī among them, took him for Alexander (Iskandar) — the identification the surrounding legend everywhere implies. But others balked at seeing a Qur’anic near-prophet in a pagan Greek, the pupil of Aristotle, and a Yemeni counter-tradition — traced to Wahb ibn Munabbih and reported through Ibn Hishām — made him instead a pre-Islamic South-Arabian Himyarite king, al-Ṣaʿb ibn Dhī Marāthid, on the ground that “Dhū” is a Himyarite name-element; yet the biography they gave him is itself lifted from the Alexander Romance and reassigned. Ṭabarī also read the “two horns” as the two extremities, east and west, that Dhū’l-Qarnayn reached.4

The apocalyptic afterlife: Gog and Magog and the Gate (late 7th c. onward)

The wall outlived every text that carried it. Fused with the biblical Gog and Magog of Ezekiel 38–39 and Revelation 20, Alexander’s iron gate in the Caucasus — located by tradition at the Caspian Gates and the pass of Darband/Derbent, and reflecting distant knowledge of real Sasanian frontier walls against the northern nomads — became a fixture of end-times expectation. In Christian apocalyptic the Apocalypse of Pseudo-Methodius (late 7th c.) made the breaking of Alexander’s gate a herald of the Last Days and the Last Roman Emperor; in Islamic eschatology the release of Yaʾjūj wa-Maʾjūj is a sign of the Hour, exactly as the Qur’an’s own “when the promise of my Lord comes” foretells. The motif ran on for a millennium — into medieval maps, the vernacular romances, the Khazar and later the Mongol identifications of the penned nations.5

Modern scholarship: the chain traced, the dating argued (1890 → today)

The year after Budge’s 1889 edition put the Syriac texts in reach, Theodor Nöldeke (1890) first argued that the Syriac Alexander Legend is the source of the Qur’anic Dhū’l-Qarnayn episode and placed the Legend in north Mesopotamia c. 629–630. G. J. Reinink elaborated the dating into a full case for the Legend as Heraclian propaganda; Kevin van Bladel (2008) renewed Nöldeke’s thesis with a point-by-point structural comparison, concluding the Qur’anic passage “is a retelling” of the Syriac text. Tommaso Tesei (2023) has since re-dated the core Legend to the reign of Justinian, a century earlier, with a Heraclian-era prophecy interpolated — which, he and van Bladel both stress, leaves the Legend→Qur’an dependence intact even as it unsettles the “born-as-propaganda” reading.6

Arguments

Claims adduced about the legend, weighed adversarially and cutting across the timeline above.

Related arguments — the Alexander connection

Two questions cluster around the single claim that made this a transmission story at all: that the Qur’an’s two-horned world-ruler is the Alexander of the Syriac Legend. One asks who Dhū’l-Qarnayn is; the other asks how his tale reached the Qur’an. They are best read together — the source question largely settles the identity question — but each stands on its own evidence and is weighed on its own page.

Is Dhū’l-Qarnayn (Qur’an 18:83–101) Alexander the Great? — sound

The figure’s literary derivation from the Alexander tradition is a scholarly near-consensus: the “two horns” answer to Alexander’s Zeus-Ammon ram-horn iconography (and to the horns the Syriac Legend sets on his head), and the journeys, the world-rule, and the Gog-and-Magog wall are the Legend’s own. The classical Muslim mainstream (Ṭabarī) agreed. The South-Arabian alternative (al-Ṣaʿb the Himyarite) arose from later discomfort at a pagan identity, not from independent evidence — and rests on a biography itself borrowed from the Alexander Romance. The one honest caveat is between the figure’s literary source (secure) and any claim that the Qur’an endorses the historical Macedonian as a saint or prophet (which it does not assert) (sound).

Did the Qur’an’s Dhū’l-Qarnayn account (18:83–101) draw on the Syriac Alexander Legend? — sound

The Syriac Legend supplies a more explicit counterpart to almost every element of the short Qur’anic tale — the God-granted world-dominion, the fetid waters at the sun’s setting, the unsheltered people at its rising, the iron-and-brass wall against Gog and Magog, the end-times prophecy — in the same order; the Legend circulated in the Syriac-Christian milieu from which the Qur’an drew much of its narrative matter, and predates it on any current dating. Van Bladel’s verdict that the passage “is a retelling” of the Legend is the mainstream view; the live disputes (the Legend’s exact date; whether the Qur’an used the written text or a living oral form) touch the mechanism, not the fact of dependence (sound).

When was the Syriac Alexander Legend (the Neṣḥānā) composed — and did it originate as Heraclian propaganda? — contested

The classic thesis (Nöldeke → Reinink → van Bladel) dates the Legend to c. 629–630 and reads it as Heraclian war-propaganda: its internal “prophecy” names 940 Anno Graecorum (628/629 AD), the exact hinge of Heraclius’s victory over Persia, so the vaticinium ex eventu runs out just then. Tommaso Tesei (2023), seconded by Shoemaker and Debié, re-dates the core to Justinian’s reign (527–565) — the Alexander–Tubarlaq material reading as sixth-century, the end-times prophecy a later graft — which would unseat the “born-as-propaganda” reading. Each case is strong by its own lights: the Heraclian side has a textual anchor (the 940 AG date is in the manuscripts), the Justinianic side has no copy lacking that prophecy and argues from narrative fit. A genuine, undecided dispute — and one that leaves the Legend→Qur’an dependence intact either way, since every proposed date precedes the Qur’an (contested).
