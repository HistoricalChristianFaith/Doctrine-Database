Cyrus named a century and a half early — predictive prophecy or a sixth-century “Second Isaiah”?

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — strong by literary-historical lights (the exilic standpoint of Isaiah 40–55), answerable by those who grant predictive prophecy and lean on the book’s own trial-of-the-nations claim; no clear winner. The bare-name form of each side’s argument is the weak form.

The claim

Isaiah names Cyrus twice — “He is my shepherd” (Isaiah 44:28) and “his anointed, to Cyrus” (45:1). On the traditional dating, Isaiah ben Amoz prophesied c. 740–700 BC; Cyrus rose c. 559 and took Babylon in 539 BC. Naming him is thus a prediction some 150 years in advance — the classic linchpin of the critical case that Isaiah 40–55 is not the eighth-century prophet’s work but an exilic “Second Isaiah,” writing near the end of the Babylonian captivity when Cyrus was already on the march. Josephus already read it the other way: Cyrus “read this, and admired the divine power,” and set out to fulfil what Isaiah wrote “before the temple was demolished.”1

Who advances it

The critical (Deutero-Isaiah) reading. The doubt is old — the medieval exegetes Moses ibn Gikatilla and Abraham Ibn Ezra hinted that Isaiah 40ff. reflects the exile — and was systematized in the rationalist era (J. C. Döderlein, J. G. Eichhorn) before Bernhard Duhm fixed the standard three-part paradigm (Proto-, Deutero-, Trito-Isaiah) in Das Buch Jesaia (1892). It is the majority scholarly position today (S. R. Driver; Claus Westermann; Joseph Blenkinsopp’s Anchor Bible; and, on canonical grounds, Brevard Childs). The conservative (single-Isaiah, predictive) reading is defended by O. T. Allis (The Unity of Isaiah, 1950), E. J. Young, R. K. Harrison, John Oswalt, and J. Alec Motyer — and is the reading behind Josephus and Cosmas.2

The evidence for — both sides at their strongest

For an exilic Second Isaiah. The load-bearing evidence is not the name but the pervasive standpoint of chapters 40–55: Jerusalem is addressed as already fallen and the temple as destroyed (44:26–28; 51:3; 52:9); the audience is spoken to in Babylon with return imminent (43:14; 48:20); the enemy is Babylon, not the Assyria of chapters 1–39; Cyrus is treated as a present, rising figure whose conquests are being interpreted for exiles (41:2–3, 25; 45:1–3); and the tone, themes, and vocabulary shift markedly (from judgment to “Comfort, comfort,” with a sustained polemical monotheism). This standpoint is independent of the mere fact of the name, and it is why the exilic dating commands a majority even among believing scholars.3

For single authorship and genuine prediction. The strongest card is internal: chapters 40–48 make prediction itself the proof of God’s unique deity. In the trial-of-the-nations passages (41:21–23; 44:7–8; 46:9–10; 48:3–5) the LORD stakes his Godhead on announcing events before they happen — “declaring the end from the beginning… I have spoken, and I will bring it to pass” — and offers the Cyrus oracle as the demonstration. Alongside this: the New Testament cites both halves of the book as “Isaiah,” crossing the critical seam in a single breath (John 12:38–41 quotes Isaiah 53:1 and 6:10 together); and no manuscript or version ever transmitted Isaiah in parts — the Great Isaiah Scroll (1QIsaa, c. 125 BC) runs all 66 chapters continuously, with 40:1 beginning on the last line of the column that ends chapter 39.4

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The exilic-standpoint argument is genuinely strong and is not reducible to “no one can predict a name.” A book-length address that treats the fall of Jerusalem as past and speaks to captives in Babylon about a conqueror already rising is, on ordinary literary-historical grounds, most naturally read as written from that situation. And the conservatives’ internal point is equally real: a text that grounds monotheism in a predictive contest is awkward to read as itself entirely written after the events it “foretells.” Both sides hold a strong card; neither card is decisive against the other.

What is overstated or imprecise — on both sides. The popular critical form — “Isaiah could not have named Cyrus 150 years out, therefore Deutero-Isaiah” — is weak as stated, because taken as a logical necessity it smuggles in an anti-supernatural premise; per this wiki’s standing rule, “no one can predict that far” is not by itself a refutation of a prophecy claim. But the compositional case does not need that premise — it rests on the standpoint evidence, which conservatives must answer directly. The popular conservative form overstates too: 1QIsaa shows the book was a literary unity by c. 125 BC and was never transmitted in parts — it says nothing about who wrote chapters 40–55 three centuries earlier (a 2021 handwriting study even locates a scribal transition near columns 27–29, nowhere near chapter 40). And the “ideal standpoint” reply (the prophet projected forward to comfort a future generation) is coherent but is the weaker side of the ledger: it must posit a sustained, book-length projection with none of the future-tense signals prophecy normally carries.5

Assessment

Contested. The crux is genuinely disputed by discipline, with no clear winner: the exilic-standpoint evidence is strong and majority, the predictive-contest evidence is a real and under-weighed counter, and the wiki does not resolve it by ruling predictive prophecy out a priori. Two clarifications keep the verdict honest. First, the fault line is literary-historical, not faith-versus-unbelief: granting Deutero-Isaiah is a dating judgment, not a disproof of inspiration (a text can be exilic and inspired — the position of most believing critical scholars); conversely, granting that prediction is possible does not establish that this passage is prediction rather than exilic testimony (possibility ≠ proof). Second, each camp’s bare-name argument is its weak form — the critic’s if it presupposes naturalism, the conservative’s if it leans on the Qumran scroll as evidence of eighth-century composition. The strong arguments lie elsewhere, and there the question stays open.
