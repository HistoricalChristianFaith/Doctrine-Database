Bede on Cyrus — the faith and piety of a pagan king

Dates: c. AD 725 (the Commentary on Ezra and Nehemiah; Bede c. 672/3–735) · Wikipedia
Summary: Reading the edict of Ezra 1, Bede praises the “great faith” and “great piety” of Cyrus — that a Gentile king should confess Israel’s God as the Lord of heaven who is also present in Jerusalem, and should free the captives without exception — and turns the whole return into a figure of the soul’s passage from the Babylon of sin to the Jerusalem of freedom.

Claims & evidence

The king’s faith and piety in the edict

Great is the faith of the king in these words, great shines his piety. Faith indeed, that he understood the people of Israel to be the people of God above all other nations. Piety, that he allowed all who wished without exception to return to their country as free men. Faith, that he confessed that the same Lord God both dwells in heaven and is in Jerusalem… Is it not clearer than light that he believed this one to be not corporeal and confined to one place, but a Spirit present everywhere?

— Bede, Commentary on Ezra and Nehemiah, on Ezra 1:3, historicalchristian.faith. Bede reads the proclamation of Ezra 1:3 as a genuine confession: Cyrus grasps that the God of Israel is no local deity but omnipresent Spirit, yet truly has “a place in Jerusalem and in the temple.”1

The return as a figure of the soul’s freedom

For whom is it not easily evident that only those with whom God is, from the confusion of sins, to the works of virtues, as if from Babylonian captivity, can pass to Jerusalem to freedom?… those who desire to please God must necessarily lift their minds to higher things, yearn for heavenly things, surpass all the pomp and enticements of the world with the love of eternal things.

— Bede, Commentary on Ezra and Nehemiah, on Ezra 1:3, historicalchristian.faith. The literal decree carries a “spiritual sense”: the ascent to Jerusalem is the soul’s return from the captivity of sin to “free peace and light.”2

Notes

  • Bede lands where Cyril and Theodoret lead: Cyrus is a true instance of the Gentile who confesses Israel’s God, kin to Naaman and Nebuchadnezzar — though whether his confession is saving faith or the natural reverence of a just king is the question the tradition never quite closes.
