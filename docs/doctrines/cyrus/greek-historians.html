The Greek historians on Cyrus — Herodotus and Xenophon

Dates: c. 430–360 BC (Herodotus, Histories c. 430 BC; Xenophon, Cyropaedia c. 370 BC) · Wikipedia
Summary: The earliest datable sources about Cyrus outside the Bible are Greek, and they already split into the two poles the later tradition would inherit: Xenophon’s idealized model king, whose willing empire proves that men can be well ruled, and Herodotus’s more ambivalent conqueror, born under an ominous royal dream and killed at last in the deserts of the Massagetae.

Claims & evidence

Xenophon: the model king whose obedience was freely given

But when we came to realise the character of Cyrus the Persian, we were led to a change of mind: here is a man, we said, who won for himself obedience from thousands of his fellows, from cities and tribes innumerable: we must ask ourselves whether the government of men is after all an impossible or even a difficult task, provided one set about it in the right way.

— Xenophon, Cyropaedia 1.1 (Dakyns trans.), gutenberg.org. The Cyropaedia (“the education of Cyrus”) is a partly-idealized life that makes Cyrus the pattern of the wise ruler — the very work Jerome would send his readers to (“the history of Cyrus the Great, consisting of eight books by Xenophon”) to see Isaiah’s prophecy of the subduer of nations fulfilled.1

Herodotus: the rise under a dream, and the death by Tomyris

Herodotus tells the harder story. Cyrus is born to the Persian Cambyses and the Median princess Mandane, daughter of king Astyages — whom two ominous dreams warn that his grandson will supplant him; the child, ordered exposed, is spared, grows up, and overthrows Astyages to found the Persian empire (Histories 1.107–130). And Cyrus does not die in triumph: campaigning beyond the Araxes against the Massagetae and their queen Tomyris, he is defeated and slain in what Herodotus calls the fiercest battle fought among barbarians — Tomyris, avenging her son, dipping the dead king’s head in a skin of blood to give him at last his fill of it (1.201–214).2

Notes

  • The Greek portrait is itself divided — Xenophon’s paragon against Herodotus’s mortal, defeated conqueror — a split that runs oddly parallel to the rabbinic “upright king who soured.” Neither Greek source knows anything of the Isaiah prophecy or the Jewish return; they are the secular memory against which the biblical and patristic reading of Cyrus stands out.
