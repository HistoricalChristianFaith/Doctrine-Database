Cyril of Alexandria on Cyrus — anointed to the kingdom, anticipating Christ the King

Dates: c. AD 428 (the Commentary on Isaiah; Cyril c. 376–444) · Wikipedia
Summary: Cyril takes the middle path the tradition would settle on: Cyrus really is the “anointed” of Isaiah 45:1 — not because he is a saint, but because God anointed him to a kingdom, exactly as prophets anointed Israel’s kings — and in bearing that title he “anticipat[es] Christ as king.”

Claims & evidence

“Anointed” names a God-given kingship, not sanctity

For it calls Cyrus “Christ” and not as if he were one of the saints, who could bear this name, but it functions to mean that he bears the name as anointed by God, in that God was in the habit of anointing those called to the kingdom even if they were not saints or worshipers. For we find the holy prophets saying to those who have received this, “The Lord has anointed you king over Israel.” So Cyrus is the anointed one, anticipating Christ as king.

— Cyril of Alexandria, Commentary on Isaiah, on 45:1, historicalchristian.faith. Cyril resolves the puzzle a Greek reader felt at hearing a pagan called christos: the word marks divine appointment to rule, not holiness — and precisely so it becomes a shadow of the true Anointed King.1

Chosen for Israel’s sake, and crowned though he did not know God

I am the Lord God who calls your name, the God of Israel, for the sake of Jacob my servant/son and Jacob my chosen. I, God alone, have called you for this, and I have honored you with radiance… I have called you and have crowned you with glory, even though you do not know that I am the Lord and that there is no God other than me.

— Cyril of Alexandria, Commentary on Isaiah, on 45:1 (paraphrasing Isaiah 45:3–5), historicalchristian.faith. The stress falls where the Hebrew puts it (“though thou hast not known me,” Isaiah 45:4): the honour is wholly God’s gift and wholly for Israel’s sake, laid on a king who does not yet worship the God who crowns him.2

Notes

  • Cyril keeps the christological resonance that Tertullian pressed too hard, but grounds it in the plain sense Jerome defended: the word is about Cyrus, and for that very reason he is a figure of Christ. He stands with Theodoret on the “anointed = divinely commissioned” reading — see the Cyrus-as-type argument.
