Jerome on Cyrus — it is “to Cyrus,” not “to my Christ the Lord”

Dates: c. AD 408–410 (the Commentary on Isaiah; the Prologue to Ezra; Jerome c. 347–420) · Wikipedia
Summary: Jerome carries the whole figure into Latin and does the tradition’s hard philological work: Isaiah 45:1 is addressed to Cyrus the Persian king — not, as many Latins and Greeks wrongly read, “to my Christ the Lord” — and its fulfilment is written both in the pagan histories of Xenophon and in the opening of Ezra, where Cyrus’s edict frees the captives and orders the temple rebuilt.

Claims & evidence

The addressee is Cyrus (Hebrew Chores), not “Lord” — the common error corrected

I know that in this chapter not only many Latin but also Greek people greatly err, thinking that it is written: Thus says the Lord to my Christ, the Lord… For it is not κυρίὶ, which means Lord, but it is said to Cyrus, who is called Chores in Hebrew, the king of the Persians, who overcame Babylon and the Chaldeans… This one is called Christ, that is, the anointed of the Lord, which was a sign among the Hebrews of royal power… so among the Hebrews those who were to reign were anointed with oil. Hence Saul is also called the anointed of the Lord.

— Jerome, Commentary on Isaiah, on 45:1–2, historicalchristian.faith. Jerome pins the mistake on the Greek homophony of Kurōi (“to the Lord”) and Kurōi (“to Cyrus”): the word names the Persian, whose title “anointed” marks kingship, exactly as it did for Saul.1

The prophecy is fulfilled in the histories of Cyrus

Let us read the history of Cyrus the Great, consisting of eight books by Xenophon, and we will see the prophecy of Isaiah fulfilled. What city did not yield to him? What king did not submit to him? What walls, before impregnable, were not brought down by his siege?

— Jerome, Commentary on Isaiah, on 45:1–2, historicalchristian.faith. Jerome appeals to the pagan record itself — Xenophon’s Cyropaedia — to show the oracle answered in history: the subduing of nations and the opening of the “two leaved gates” of Isaiah 45:1.2

“My shepherd,” and the edict of Ezra

He also says to Cyrus, the king of the Persians, who first destroyed Babylon and the Chaldeans: you are my shepherd… It is written at the beginning of Ezra, that by the edict of Cyrus, the king of the Persians and Medes, the captivity of the people of Israel was released, and those who wanted to return were sent back to Jerusalem… For under Cyrus, it was commanded to build Jerusalem and the Temple, of which only the foundations were laid while he was alive. However, under Darius, in his second year, with the prophets Haggai and Zechariah, the building of the Temple was begun.

— Jerome, Commentary on Isaiah, on 44:28, historicalchristian.faith. Jerome ties Isaiah 44:28 (“He is my shepherd”) to the decree of Ezra 1, and is careful about the history: Cyrus authorized the rebuilding and the foundations were laid in his reign, but the temple itself rose under Darius — a distinction the summary oracle collapses.3

Notes

  • Jerome is the corrective to the christological over-reading of Tertullian: where Tertullian took Isaiah 45:1 straight to Christ, Jerome insists the literal addressee is the Persian king. He does not deny the deeper sense — “anointed” still points beyond Cyrus — but he fixes the plain meaning first. See the Cyrus-as-type argument.
  • In the Prologue to Ezra Jerome defends his fresh translation of the book from the Hebrew against critics who preferred the Septuagint and the apocryphal “third and fourth” books of Ezra — the setting in which he handled the Cyrus edict.
