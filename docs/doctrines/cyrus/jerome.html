Jerome on Cyrus — it is “to Cyrus,” not “to my Christ the Lord”

Dates: c. AD 408–410 (the Commentary on Isaiah; the Prologue to Ezra; Jerome c. 347–420) · Wikipedia
Summary: Jerome treats the figure in Latin with attention to the Hebrew: Isaiah 45:1, he holds, is addressed to Cyrus the Persian king — not, as he says many Latins and Greeks read, “to my anointed, the Lord” — and he finds its fulfilment both in the pagan histories of Xenophon and in the opening of Ezra, where Cyrus’s edict frees the captives and orders the temple rebuilt.

Claims & evidence

The addressee read as Cyrus (Hebrew Chores), not “Lord”

I know that in this passage very many, not only of the Latins but of the Greeks, err badly, thinking that it is written: Thus says the Lord to my anointed, the Lord… For it says not κυρίῳ, which means Lord, but Cyrus, who in Hebrew is called CHORES (כרש), the king of the Persians, who overcame Babylon and the Chaldeans… He was called Christ, that is, the anointed of the Lord, because this was the mark of royal power among the Hebrews; so that, as among us the diadem and the purple are given to emperors alone, so among the Hebrews those who were to reign were anointed with ointment. Hence Saul too is called the Lord's anointed (1 Sam 6).

— Jerome, Commentary on Isaiah, on 45:1–2, historicalchristian.faith. Jerome traces the reading he rejects to the Greek homophony of Kurōi (“to the Lord”) and Kurōi (“to Cyrus”): the word names the Persian, whose title “anointed” marks kingship, exactly as it did for Saul.1

The prophecy is fulfilled in the histories of Cyrus

Let us read Xenophon's history of the elder Cyrus in eight books, and we shall see the prophecy of Isaiah fulfilled. For what city was not opened to him? What city did not bow beneath him the backs of its kings? What walls, before impregnable, were not overthrown by his siege?

— Jerome, Commentary on Isaiah, on 45:1–2, historicalchristian.faith. Jerome cites Xenophon’s Cyropaedia as the oracle’s fulfilment in history: the subduing of nations and the opening of the “two leaved gates” of Isaiah 45:1.2

“My shepherd,” and the edict of Ezra

And he who restores Jerusalem and destroys Babylon also says to Cyrus, king of the Persians, who first destroyed Babylon and the Chaldeans: You are my shepherd… It is written at the beginning of Ezra that at the edict of Cyrus, king of the Persians and Medes, the captivity of the people of Israel was loosed, and those who wished to return were sent back to Jerusalem… For under Cyrus it was ordered that Jerusalem and the Temple be built, but while he lived only the foundations of the Temple were laid. For the rest, under Darius, in his second year, when Haggai and Zechariah prophesied, the Temple began to be built.

— Jerome, Commentary on Isaiah, on 44:28, historicalchristian.faith. Jerome ties Isaiah 44:28 (“He is my shepherd”) to the decree of Ezra 1, and distinguishes the stages: Cyrus authorized the rebuilding and the foundations were laid in his reign, but the temple itself rose under Darius.3

Notes

  • Jerome’s reading differs from that of Tertullian (c. 200), who applied Isaiah 45:1 directly to Christ; Jerome holds that the literal addressee is the Persian king.
  • In the Prologue to Ezra Jerome defends his fresh translation of the book from the Hebrew against critics who preferred the Septuagint and the apocryphal “third and fourth” books of Ezra — the setting in which he handled the Cyrus edict.
