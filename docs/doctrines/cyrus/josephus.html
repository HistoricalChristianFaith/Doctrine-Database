Josephus on Cyrus — the king who read his own name in Isaiah

Dates: c. AD 93 (Antiquities of the Jews 11.1; Josephus c. 37–100) · Wikipedia
Summary: Josephus gives the earliest surviving form of the predictive reading: Cyrus, reading in the book of Isaiah the prophecy that named him — written, Josephus says, long before the temple fell — was seized with desire to fulfil it, and so released the captives and decreed the temple rebuilt, confessing that the God of Israel “foretold my name by the prophets.”

Claims & evidence

Cyrus reads the prophecy of his own name and resolves to fulfil it

This was known to Cyrus by his reading the book which Isaiah left behind him of his prophecies… This was foretold by Isaiah one hundred and forty years before the temple was demolished. Accordingly, when Cyrus read this, and admired the divine power, an earnest desire and ambition seized upon him to fulfil what was so written.

— Josephus, Antiquities of the Jews 11.1.2 (Whiston trans.), penelope.uchicago.edu. Josephus sets Cyrus’s first year as “the seventieth from the day that our people were removed out of their own land into Babylon” — the terminus of Jeremiah’s seventy years (Jeremiah 25:11–12).1

The decree: “he foretold my name by the prophets”

Thus saith Cyrus the king: Since God Almighty hath appointed me to be king of the habitable earth, I believe that he is that God which the nation of the Israelites worship; for indeed he foretold my name by the prophets, and that I should build him a house at Jerusalem, in the country of Judea.

— Josephus, Antiquities of the Jews 11.1.2 (Whiston trans.), penelope.uchicago.edu. Josephus expands the terse edict of Ezra 1 into a confession that turns on the fulfilled prophecy, and reports that Cyrus wrote to the regional governors to furnish gold, silver, and beasts for the sacrifices.2

Notes

  • Josephus is a first-century witness to how the tradition was already read predictively — not independent evidence of Cyrus’s actual motives (the Cyrus Cylinder credits Marduk, not the God of Israel). His value here is the reception: the “Cyrus read his own name in Isaiah” move that Cosmas and the later apologetic inherit, and that the Deutero-Isaiah argument weighs.
  • The figure “one hundred and forty years” is Whiston’s rendering; some manuscripts and the Loeb text read “two hundred and ten years.” Either way Josephus places Isaiah’s prophecy well before Cyrus — the whole point of his story.
