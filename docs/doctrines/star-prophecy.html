The Star Prophecy (Numbers 24:17) and Its Claimed Fulfilments

The fourth oracle of Balaam, “there shall come a Star out of Jacob, and a Sceptre shall rise out of Israel” (Numbers 24:17), speaks of a ruler who “shall smite the corners of Moab, and destroy all the children of Sheth.” Between the second century BC and the second century AD it was read of a coming deliverer by several communities, and modern scholars call it the “Star Prophecy.” The sectarians of the Dead Sea Scrolls read it of two coming figures, a teacher and a prince; Josephus reports a Judean oracle of a world-ruler, which Martin Hengel identifies with it, and applies that oracle to the Roman emperor Vespasian; Christian writers tied it to the star of the wise men in Matthew’s Gospel; and Rabbi Akiva hailed the rebel leader Simon, known by the star-name “Son of the Star,” as its fulfilment.

This page is a reception-history timeline: not a life of Balaam (for that see Who Was Balaam son of Beor?), but a history of one verse and of the figures whom its readers held to have fulfilled it, set out earliest extant source first — the oracle itself, the Qumran scrolls, Josephus’s “ambiguous oracle” and Vespasian, the Christian star of Bethlehem, the rabbinic acclamation of Bar Kokhba, the synagogue Targums, and modern scholarship. Each source is placed at the moment its reading enters the record. Questions on which the sources are read differently — among them, which scripture Josephus meant — are set out under Disputed readings. The oracle’s closing clause, “destroy all the children of Sheth,” recurs across the record: Qumran quotes it of an eschatological warrior, and Cosmas Indicopleustes reads it of Christ.

Related doctrines: Revelation, prophecy & discernment — see also the discernment of dreams (how a true revelation is told from a false one).

Timeline

The oracle itself — Numbers 24:17 (the Torah baseline)

Hired by the king of Moab to curse Israel, the Mesopotamian seer Balaam speaks his fourth oracle of a figure “not now … not nigh”: “there shall come a Star out of Jacob, and a Sceptre shall rise out of Israel, and shall smite the corners of Moab, and destroy all the children of Sheth … Out of Jacob shall come he that shall have dominion.” John J. Collins reads the star and sceptre, in the text’s own horizon, as royal emblems of a conquering Israelite king, most naturally David, who subdued Moab and Edom (Numbers 24:17–19). The oracle is cast as future and distant, and its two symbols, star and sceptre, have been read both of one figure and of two.1 The date of the oracle, and whether it was read messianically before Christianity, are set out on the Balaam timeline’s page “How old is the ‘Star out of Jacob,’ and was it read messianically before Christianity?”

The Dead Sea Scrolls (2nd–1st c. BC) — the star and the sceptre as two figures

The Dead Sea Scrolls are the earliest extant witnesses on this timeline to a messianic reading of the oracle. Where the Hebrew has one image-pair, the Damascus Document (CD 7:18–21) reads two coming figures: “The star is the Interpreter of the Law who shall come to Damascus … The sceptre is the Prince of the whole congregation, and when he comes he shall smite all the children of Seth” — figures John J. Collins reads as the priestly and royal Messiahs of the sect’s twin expectation, “the Messiahs of Aaron and Israel.” The same verse is set among the community’s eschatological proof-texts in the Testimonia (4Q175), beside the “prophet like Moses” and the blessing of Levi, and is prayed as a war-oracle in the War Scroll (1QM 11:6–7): “There shall come forth a star out of Jacob … and shall crush the forehead of Moab.” Collins takes the title of his study of Qumran messianism, The Scepter and the Star, from this verse.2 Full detail »

Josephus’s “ambiguous oracle” and Vespasian (Jewish War, c. AD 75) — applied to a Roman emperor

Josephus reports that what “most elevated” the Jews to war was “an ambiguous oracle … found in their sacred writings, how, about that time, one from their country should become governor of the habitable earth.” The rebels read it of a Jewish deliverer; Josephus, who had surrendered to the Romans and prophesied to Vespasian that he would become emperor, writes that “this oracle certainly denoted the government of Vespasian, who was appointed emperor in Judea.” Tacitus and Suetonius report a Judean oracle of a world-ruler and its application to the Flavians. Josephus does not name the scripture: Martin Hengel identifies it as the Star Prophecy, Per Bilde and N. T. Wright as Daniel, and a composite reading (⚠ unverified — no named proponent located) takes it as a cluster of messianic texts; see which scripture Josephus meant.3 Full detail »

The star of Bethlehem — Matthew and the Fathers (Gospel c. AD 80–90; patristic reading to c. 450)

Christian writers read the prophecy as fulfilled in Jesus and its star as the star of Bethlehem. Matthew does not quote Numbers 24:17; his wise men are Eastern seers who read a star as the birth-sign of a Jewish king. The Fathers connect the two. Justin and Irenaeus tie the magi’s star to “a star out of Jacob”; Lactantius quotes the verse of the incarnate mediator; Origen and then Eusebius explain that “Balaam’s successors” possessed his prophecy and set out when they saw the star; Leo the Great preaches that the magi were “reminded through the ancient pronouncements of Balaam … ‘A star will appear out of Jacob’”; and Cosmas Indicopleustes reads the oracle’s “children of Seth” — the clause Qumran read of a warrior — of Christ, “and to Him alone.” How the magi’s star relates to Balaam’s oracle is set out on the magi timeline’s page “How is Balaam’s ‘star out of Jacob’ related to the magi’s star?”; the star and star-divination are treated on the astrology timeline.4

Bar Kokhba and Rabbi Akiva (the revolt of AD 132–135) — “Son of the Star”

Simon, leader of the second revolt against Rome, is the Jewish claimant recorded as proclaimed on this verse. His own letters, recovered from the Judean desert, name him Shim‘on bar Kosiba; Rabbi Akiva, reading “a Star (kokhav) steps forth out of Jacob,” declared, “This is the King Messiah” — to which Rabbi Yohanan ben Torta replied, “Akiva, grass will grow between your cheeks and still the son of David will not have come.” The star-name bar Kokhba, “Son of the Star,” is usually explained from Akiva’s reading. The revolt was crushed, Akiva died c. 135, and the fortress of Betar fell; the rabbinic sources, including the Talmud’s own text of Akiva’s derash, give the name as bar Koziba, which Lamentations Rabbah reads as “fraudulent” (kozav). The Christian writers Justin and Eusebius preserve the Greek form of his star-name: Eusebius writes that he “pretended that he was a star that had come down … from heaven,” and Justin that he ordered Christians punished who would not deny Christ. Whether the star-like emblem above the Temple on his coins alludes to the verse is set out on the star-coins page.5 Full detail »

The synagogue Targums (redacted c. 2nd–8th c. AD) — king and Messiah

The Aramaic Targums, the running paraphrases read aloud beside the Hebrew in synagogue, render the star and sceptre as a king and the Messiah by name: Onkelos, “when a king shall arise out of Jacob, and the Messiah be anointed from Israel”; Neofiti, “a king … a redeemer and ruler from the house of Israel”; and Pseudo-Jonathan, expanding it into the end-time war of “the Messiah … the Power-sceptre of Israel” against the armies of Gog. The three span the Babylonian (Onkelos) and Palestinian (Neofiti, Pseudo-Jonathan) Targum traditions. The same Targums, with their portrait of Balaam himself, are treated on the Balaam timeline’s Aramaic Targums page.6

Modern scholarship — “the Star Prophecy” as a category

Modern writers name the tradition and trace its course. Robert Eisenman and Robert Gnuse, as Wikipedia’s survey reports, speak of “the Star Prophecy” as a messianic tradition running from the Scrolls through the revolts. John J. Collins, in The Scepter and the Star (2nd ed. 2010), builds his account of Qumran’s Messiahs around Numbers 24:17. Martin Hengel, in The Zealots (Eng. trans. 1989), reads it as the revolutionary oracle of the Jewish freedom movement and as the oracle behind Josephus’s prophecy to Vespasian.7
