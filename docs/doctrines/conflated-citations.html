Naming the Wrong Prophet: Composite Old Testament Citations (Mark 1:2; Matthew 27:9)

Related doctrines: Hard passages — see also Who Killed Goliath? (2 Samuel 21:19) · How Tall Was Goliath? (six or four cubits) · Solomon's Stalls: 40,000 or 4,000? (1 Kings 4:26 vs. 2 Chronicles 9:25) · What Jesus Wrote on the Ground (John 8:6) · The Two Genealogies of Jesus (Matthew 1 vs. Luke 3) · The Death of Judas (Matthew 27 vs. Acts 1) · Baptized for the Dead (1 Corinthians 15:29)

Four times the Gospels name a prophet — or “the prophets” — and quote words that the named prophet did not write, or that no single passage contains. Mark 1:2–3 opens “as it is written in Isaiah the prophet,” then quotes Malachi 3:1 (fused with Exodus 23:20) before it reaches Isaiah 40:3. Matthew 27:9 credits “Jeremy the prophet” with an oracle whose words are Zechariah 11's. Matthew 2:23 reports “He shall be called a Nazarene,” “spoken by the prophets,” yet no verse reads so. And Matthew 13:35 cites “the prophet” for a line that belongs to Asaph's Psalm 78 — where a strong early line of manuscripts, harder still, names “Isaiah.” The charge is the same each time: the Evangelist has named the wrong prophet, a plain error in the text.1

This page gathers the readers who felt the difficulty and answered it, earliest first — not the sources for any one crux (Matthew's “Jeremy” is weighed in full with Judas's biographical timeline) but the chain of interpreters who recognized a single phenomenon and explained it. The arc runs one way. The “correction” begins in the manuscripts almost at once — Irenaeus already reads Mark's opening as “the prophets,” plural — and the fathers answer with a shared principle: the citation is a deliberate composite, and the Evangelist names the senior or earlier prophet, giving (in Jerome's phrase) “the sense, not the words.” Augustine adds a text-critical defence of the harder attribution; the medievals systematize the answer; and modern criticism vindicates the harder readings as original while recovering “composite citation” as a recognized ancient convention rather than a blunder. Whether any given case is deliberate design or a genuine slip is weighed adversarially under Arguments.

Timeline

Irenaeus of Lyons (c. 180)

The earliest datable trace of the difficulty is not an explanation but a “correction” already in place. Quoting Mark's prologue, Irenaeus reads it in the plural: “Mark, the interpreter and follower of Peter, does thus commence his Gospel narrative: The beginning of the Gospel of Jesus Christ… as it is written in the prophets, Behold, I send My messenger before Thy face.” His second-century text already carries the general “in the prophets” rather than the singular “in Isaiah” — the shape the later manuscript tradition would take, and the first sign that scribes felt the tension between the named prophet and the composite quotation.2

Ambrosiaster (c. 380)

The anonymous Roman commentator known as Ambrosiaster is the first to state the crux as a formal question and give the classic answer. His Questions on the Old and New Testaments asks outright why, though the words stand “in the prophet Malachi… the Evangelist Mark claims that this is written in the prophet Isaiah.” His reply: Mark “could not have been unaware of what he was writing… Knowing that all sayings must be referred back to their original author, he attributed these words to Isaiah, who had first conveyed this meaning… so that he might unite the words of both prophets… under the name of the earlier prophet.” Naming the senior prophet for a blended citation is thus a deliberate convention, not a mistake.3

John Chrysostom (c. 390)

Preaching on Matthew 2:23, Chrysostom meets the harder version of the charge — a citation found in no extant prophet at all — with a different resort: the loss of prophetic books. “And what manner of prophet said this? Be not curious, nor overbusy. For many of the prophetic writings have been lost; and this one may see from the history of the Chronicles.” Where Ambrosiaster harmonizes a known composite, Chrysostom appeals to a source no longer preserved — the weakest of the ancient answers, but a candid admission that the words could not be located.4

Jerome (c. 398)

Jerome is the pivot: he addresses all four loci and supplies the principle that governs the rest. On Mark 1:2 he is blunt — the quotation “is made up from two prophets, Malachi and Isaiah… on what ground then has Mark … set the words ‘As it is written in the prophet Isaiah’” when part is “not written in Isaiah at all, but in Malachi?” His answer is the method itself: the Evangelist “has not rendered his original word for word, but… has given the sense in different terms.” On Matthew 2:23 he notes that Matthew wrote “the prophets” in the plural precisely to show “that he did not take words from the Scriptures, but the meaning” (pointing to Isaiah 11:1's netzer, “branch”). On Matthew 27:9 he judges the Jeremiah oracle “more like a testimony taken from Zechariah.” And on Matthew 13:35 he diagnoses the “Isaiah” reading as an early scribal error for “Asaph the prophet”: the “first scribe did not understand Asaph, and thought it was a mistake… and corrected it to the more obvious name of Isaiah.”5

Augustine of Hippo (c. 400)

Augustine's Harmony of the Gospels gives the fullest single engagement with the charge — on Matthew's “Jeremiah” for Zechariah, the case the tradition treats as paradigmatic. He first weighs the text-critical escape and declines it: though “this ascription… is not contained in all the codices,” so that one might follow those that read simply “the prophet,” he is “not satisfied” with that, “because a majority of codices contain the name of Jeremiah, and… the more ancient Greek exemplars” have it — the harder reading is original, and there “was… an intelligible reason for erasing the name.” He then explains the name as providential: even if Matthew's memory first supplied “Jeremiah” for “Zechariah,” he would have corrected it “had he not reflected that… it was not without a purpose… directed by the Holy Spirit,” showing “the marvellous manner in which all the holy prophets, speaking in one spirit, continued in perfect unison.”6

Bede (c. 730)

Bede transfers Augustine's reasoning back to Mark and gives it its fullest harmonizing form. Of Mark's two testimonies, one “is only found in Isaiah, while the other is found in Malachi,” yet “the evangelist should not be thought to have erred or been deceitful… even if the words… from Malachi are not found in Isaiah, the sense of them is found in Isaiah.” He allows that “Isaiah occurred instead of Malachi” in Mark's mind “as happens,” but that Mark left it uncorrected because the name that surfaced was “governed by the Holy Spirit, not without reason” — Augustine's providential-memory argument, now settled Latin doctrine for the Mark passage.7

The Byzantine and scholastic readers (c. 1100–1270)

By the high Middle Ages the composite citation is stated as routine and the rival answers are catalogued. Theophylact of Ohrid notes without alarm that Mark's testimony “is taken from two prophets — from Malachi… and from Isaiah,” and for Matthew 2:23 offers both the lost-books resort and “an unwritten prophecy among the Jews.” Thomas Aquinas's Catena Aurea gathers the whole spectrum — including the Greek Pseudo-Chrysostom (Victor of Antioch), who states the two live options plainly: “it may be said that it is a mistake of the writer… Otherwise… he has compressed into one, two prophecies… by two prophets.” The deliberate-composite reading and the scribal-error reading are, by now, both on the table.8

Modern scholarship

Modern textual criticism vindicates the harder attributions as original: at Mark 1:2 the United Bible Societies' committee prints “in Isaiah the prophet” with an {A} (virtually certain) rating, reasoning that the composite Malachi–Isaiah quotation gave scribes the motive to soften the singular name to “in the prophets” — the lectio difficilior is therefore the source, not the “correction.” Modern exegesis, in turn, has recovered “composite citation” as a recognized convention of ancient authors (Jewish, Greco-Roman, and Christian alike), not a Gospel blunder — the two-volume survey of Sean Adams and Seth Ehorn distinguishing combined, conflated, and condensed citations across the corpus, with a dedicated study of Matthew. The one case still argued as a mislabelling (Matthew 13:35's “Isaiah” for Asaph) is the case the manuscripts and Jerome alike treat as a scribal slip — the exception that proves the fathers were reading the rest as design.9

Arguments

The contested cruxes — each cutting across the timeline rather than sitting at one date — are weighed adversarially on their own pages. The settled floor beneath them is that “composite citation” is a real ancient convention and that the harder attributions are the original text; what is genuinely disputed is whether a given case is deliberate design or a genuine slip.

Why does Matthew credit the thirty-pieces oracle to “Jeremy the prophet”? (assessment: sound)

Why Matthew names Jeremiah (Matthew 27:9–10) — the paradigm case, argued in full with Judas's biographical timeline. The quoted words come mainly from Zechariah 11, yet Matthew credits “Jeremy”; best read as a deliberate composite — Zechariah's words wrapped in Jeremiah's imagery of potter, purchased field, and a place renamed for innocent blood, and named for the governing prophet. This is sound: coherent, consonant with Matthew's demonstrable habit with fulfilment citations, and the mainstream explanation, so the bare charge of a blunder is not the reasonable verdict; what remains underdetermined is only why Jeremiah in particular.10
