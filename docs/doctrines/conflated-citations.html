Naming the Prophet: Composite Old Testament Citations (Mark 1:2; Matthew 27:9)

Related doctrines: Hard passages — see also Who Killed Goliath? (2 Samuel 21:19) · How Tall Was Goliath? (six or four cubits) · Solomon's Stalls: 40,000 or 4,000? (1 Kings 4:26 vs. 2 Chronicles 9:25) · Ahaziah's Age: 22 or 42? (2 Kings 8:26 vs. 2 Chronicles 22:2) · The Bronze Sea: 2,000 or 3,000 Baths? (1 Kings 7:26 vs. 2 Chronicles 4:5) · What Jesus Wrote on the Ground (John 8:6) · The Two Genealogies of Jesus (Matthew 1 vs. Luke 3) · The Death of Judas (Matthew 27 vs. Acts 1) · Baptized for the Dead (1 Corinthians 15:29)

Four times the Gospels name a prophet — or “the prophets” — and quote words that the named prophet did not write, or that no single passage contains. Mark 1:2–3 opens, in Codex Sinaiticus, Codex Vaticanus and modern critical editions, “as it is written in Isaiah the prophet” (the Byzantine text reads “in the prophets”), then quotes Malachi 3:1 (close to Exodus 23:20) before it reaches Isaiah 40:3. Matthew 27:9 credits “Jeremy the prophet” with an oracle whose words are drawn largely from Zechariah 11:12–13; Catherine Sider Hamilton (2018) reads the verse as “calling up both Zech 11 and Jeremiah,” with Jeremiah 19 chiefly in view. Matthew 2:23 reports “He shall be called a Nazarene,” “spoken by the prophets,” and no verse reads so. And Matthew 13:35 cites “the prophet” for a line from Asaph's Psalm 78, where a line of early manuscripts names “Isaiah.” Each has been read as naming the wrong prophet, and each has been read otherwise.1

This page gathers, earliest first, the readers who discussed these citations and how they explained them. Matthew's “Jeremy” is also treated on Judas's biographical timeline. The explanations recorded here include variation in the manuscripts (“Isaiah” or “the prophets” at Mark 1:2; “Jeremiah,” no name or “Zechariah” at Matthew 27:9); a copyist's error; a lost or secret prophetic book; a deliberate composite headed with one prophet's name; a name supplied by memory under the Spirit's guidance; and, in modern study, composite citation as an ancient technique and the hypothesis of collections of proof-texts. How particular cases have been read is set out under Disputed readings.

Timeline

Irenaeus of Lyons (c. 180)

The earliest datable witness to the wording is Irenaeus, who, in the Latin translation in which Against Heresies III survives, quotes Mark's prologue both ways. In one passage he reads the plural: “Mark, the interpreter and follower of Peter, does thus commence his Gospel narrative: The beginning of the Gospel of Jesus Christ… as it is written in the prophets, Behold, I send My messenger before Thy face.” In another, describing the four Gospels, he quotes Mark as beginning “as it is written in Esaias the prophet.”2

Origen of Alexandria (c. 246)

Origen is the earliest extant writer on this timeline to comment on the difficulty by name. Reaching Matthew 27:9 in his Commentary on Matthew, he says that the quotation credited to “Jeremiah” “is not found to have been prophesied by Jeremiah anywhere in his books, whether those which are read in the churches or those which are recited among the Jews,” and, inviting correction (“if anyone can know, let him show where it is written”), offers two conjectures: “either it is an error of the Scripture and Jeremiah is put for Zechariah, or… there is some secret writing of Jeremiah in which it is written.” He develops the second, defending the “secret writing” by analogy with Paul’s use of writings outside the public books.3

Eusebius of Caesarea (c. 315)

Expounding Zechariah 11 in his Proof of the Gospel, Eusebius reaches the thirty-pieces oracle Matthew credits to “Jeremiah” and finds it “not… in the prophecy of Jeremiah”; the reader must therefore “consider whether… they have been removed through any evil intention, or whether there has been an error in copying, through the mistake of some careless transcriber of the Holy Gospels, who wrote Jeremiah instead of Zechariah.” He extends the conjecture from the prophet’s name to the wording, supposing the same scribe replaced “they cast them into the house of the Lord, into the furnace” with “they bought… the field of the potter.”4

Ambrosiaster (c. 380)

The anonymous Roman commentator known as Ambrosiaster sets out the Mark crux as a formal question. His Questions on the Old and New Testaments asks why, though the words stand “in the prophet Malachi… the Evangelist Mark claims that this is written in the prophet Isaiah.” His reply: Mark “could not have been unaware of what he was writing… Knowing that all sayings must be referred back to their original author, he attributed these words to Isaiah, who had first conveyed this meaning… so that he might unite the words of both prophets… under the name of the earlier prophet.” For Ambrosiaster the heading is deliberate.5

John Chrysostom (c. 390)

Preaching on Matthew 2:23, where the saying is found in no extant prophet, Chrysostom appeals to the loss of prophetic books: “And what manner of prophet said this? Be not curious, nor overbusy. For many of the prophetic writings have been lost; and this one may see from the history of the Chronicles.”6

Jerome (c. 398)

Jerome addresses all four loci. In his letter on translation (Letter 57, 395) he states the Mark difficulty: the quotation “is made up from two prophets, Malachi… and Isaiah… On what grounds then has Mark… set the words: ‘As it is written in the prophet Isaiah’” when part is “not written in Isaiah at all, but in Malachi?” He gives it, with other cases, to show that the Evangelists and Apostles did not quote word for word, holding that “in dealing with the scriptures it is the sense we have to look to and not the words.” On Matthew 2:23 he reads the plural “prophets” as a sign that Matthew took the sense of the Scriptures rather than their words (pointing also to Isaiah 11:1's netzer, “branch”). On Matthew 27:9 he judges the Jeremiah oracle “more like a testimony taken from Zechariah.” And on Matthew 13:35 he holds that the original read “Asaph the prophet” and that the “first scribe did not understand Asaph, and thought it was a mistake… and corrected it to the more obvious name of Isaiah.”7

Augustine of Hippo (c. 400)

Augustine's Harmony of the Gospels discusses at length Matthew's “Jeremiah” for words drawn largely from Zechariah. He considers following the codices that read simply “the prophet” and declines: though “this ascription… is not contained in all the codices,” he is “not satisfied” with that defence, “because a majority of codices contain the name of Jeremiah, and… the more ancient Greek exemplars” have it, and there “was… an intelligible reason for erasing the name.” He then explains the name as providential: even if Matthew's memory first supplied “Jeremiah” for “Zechariah,” he would have corrected it “had he not reflected that… it was not without a purpose… directed by the Holy Spirit,” showing “the marvellous manner in which all the holy prophets, speaking in one spirit, continued in perfect unison.”8

Bede (c. 730)

Bede applies Augustine's reasoning to Mark. Of Mark's two testimonies, one “is only found in Isaiah, while the other is found in Malachi,” yet “the evangelist should not be thought to have erred or been deceitful… even if the words… from Malachi are not found in Isaiah, the sense of them is found in Isaiah.” He allows that “Isaiah occurred instead of Malachi” in Mark's mind “as happens,” and that Mark left it uncorrected because the name that surfaced was “governed by the Holy Spirit, not without reason.”9

The Byzantine and scholastic readers (c. 1100–1270)

Theophylact of Ohrid notes that Mark's testimony “is taken from two prophets — from Malachi… and from Isaiah,” and for Matthew 2:23 offers both the lost-books answer and “an unwritten prophecy among the Jews.” Thomas Aquinas's Catena Aurea gathers earlier comment on Mark 1:2, including the Greek Pseudo-Chrysostom (Victor of Antioch), who sets out two options: “it may be said that it is a mistake of the writer… Otherwise… he has compressed into one, two prophecies… by two prophets.”10

Modern scholarship

At Mark 1:2 the United Bible Societies' committee, in Bruce Metzger's account, prints “in Isaiah the prophet” with an {A} rating, reasoning that the composite Malachi–Isaiah quotation gave copyists the motive to change the singular name to “in the prophets”; James Snapp Jr. defends the plural as original. The two-volume survey edited by Sean Adams and Seth Ehorn studies “composite citation” across Jewish, Greco-Roman and Christian authors, distinguishing combined, conflated and condensed citations, with a study of Matthew. At Matthew 13:35 the committee prints “the prophet” and rates the decision {C}.11

Disputed readings

Questions on which readers of these citations disagree, each set out with its rival readings on its own page.

The named prophet

How particular Gospel citations that name a prophet, or “the prophets,” have been read.

  • How should “in Isaiah the prophet” at Mark 1:2, over a quotation that opens with Malachi, be read? (which wording Mark wrote, and what the name signifies)
  • Where does Matthew 2:23’s “He shall be called a Nazarene,” “spoken by the prophets,” come from?
  • Did Matthew 13:35 originally name “Isaiah,” “Asaph,” or simply “the prophet”?

Where composite citations came from

Whether combined citations were made by the New Testament writers or received from earlier collections.

  • Were the composite citations drawn from a pre-formed testimonia collection? (the Testimony Book hypothesis and its critics)
  • Did Paul combine the scriptural texts in Romans 3:10–18, 9:33 and 11:8 himself, or receive them already combined? (with the related chain of Hebrews 1; on the text-form of these citations, see Which text did the New Testament quote?)
