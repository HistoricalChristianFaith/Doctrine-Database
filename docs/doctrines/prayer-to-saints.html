Prayer to the Saints (invocation of the saints)

May the living address the saints in heaven — "Holy Mary, pray for us"; "Saint Peter and Saint Paul, intercede for us" — asking them to bring their needs before God? The Council of Trent teaches that it is "good and useful suppliantly to invoke them," and the Orthodox Church in America’s manual of instruction speaks of “our invocation of the saints’ prayers”;1 the Lutheran, Reformed and Anglican confessions of the sixteenth century reject the practice.2 This timeline records, earliest witness first, the extant texts on the belief that the departed saints pray for the living and on the practice of addressing them.

Writers on both sides distinguish two things: the belief that the saints in glory intercede for those still on earth, and invocation, the second-person address “O Saint N., pray for me.” The Apology of the Augsburg Confession (1531) grants that the saints in heaven “pray for the Church in general” while holding that “it does not follow that they are to be invoked”; Gavin Ortlund (Reformed Baptist, 2021) likewise trains his critique on the act of addressing them; and the Catechism of the Catholic Church (1992) says of the saints, “We can and should ask them to intercede for us.”2 Whether asking a saint to pray is a different kind of act from believing the saints pray, and which act a given early witness attests, is disputed; see Is invoking a saint the same act as believing the saints intercede?

On the date of the practice, the Apology (1531) holds that “this is a new custom in the Church,” and Ortlund that it “genuinely looks like its forming, coming to be, in the mid to late patristic era”; the Council of Trent (1563) calls it a usage “received from the primitive times of the Christian religion,” and the Orthodox Church in America’s manual (2023) says it has been “sought and invoked in the Church from almost the very beginning of its history”; John Henry Newman (Catholic, 1845) counts “their invocation and cultus” among the developments that “follow” from the doctrine of mediation.1 These readings, and the evidence each draws on, are set out under Disputed readings below.

Timeline

Each entry notes whether the witness addresses a saint in the second person or speaks of the saints’ intercession in the third. Two bodies of evidence whose reading is disputed are treated under Disputed readings rather than slotted here: the third-century graffiti to Peter and Paul at the Roman catacomb of San Sebastiano,3 and the closing benediction of the Council of Constantinople’s letter to Theodosius (381).4

Irenaeus of Lyons (c. 202)

Irenaeus of Lyons is the earliest witness on this timeline. In the passages surveyed here he does not address a departed saint. His account of how the church works her miracles says she does so "in His name" and "not … by means of angelic invocations," but "directing her prayers to the Lord, who made all things … and calling upon the name of our Lord Jesus Christ." His editors read the clause differently: Grabe took it as excluding any invocation of angels in the primitive church; Massuet restricted it to the conjuring of evil spirits.5

Hippolytus of Rome (c. 204)

In the oldest surviving Christian commentary on a book of Scripture, Hippolytus of Rome breaks off to address the three youths of the fiery furnace directly — "remember me, I entreat you, that I also may obtain the same lot of martyrdom with you" — within a homiletic apostrophe whose same breath asks them to "describe to us" the fourth man in the furnace. It is cited for an early date of invocation in the dispute over when the practice arose; see Is the invocation of saints a fourth-century development or an older practice?6

Origen of Alexandria (c. 233)

Origen holds that the saints and angels pray with and for the living, and allows "request to saints alone, should some Paul or Peter appear," where J. P. Kirsch (Catholic historian, Eng. tr. 1910) takes "saints" to mean "living believers"; he also writes that "if we accept prayer in its full meaning, we may not ever pray to any begotten being," but only to "the God and Father of All" through Christ, and in Against Celsus he declines to invoke the angels. His text is cited by holders of more than one reading; see Is invoking a saint the same act as believing the saints intercede?7

Cyprian of Carthage (c. 252)

Writing to the exiled Cornelius, Cyprian of Carthage asks that whichever of them dies first, "our love may continue in the presence of the Lord, and our prayers for our brethren and sisters not cease," and he pictures the dead in paradise as "still solicitous for our salvation." In the texts surveyed here he speaks of the departed praying for the living and does not address a prayer to a departed saint.8 In the exchange of 1530–31 the Roman Confutation cited the letter, “asking that ‘if any depart first, his prayer for our brethren and sisters may not cease,’” and reasoned that “if this holy man had not ascertained that after this life the saints pray for the living, he would have given exhortation to no purpose”; Melanchthon’s Apology (1531) replied that Cyprian “asked Cornelius while yet alive to pray for his brothers when departing.”9

The Sub Tuum Praesidium (papyrus dated c. 250 – c. 700)

The anonymous Greek prayer on Papyrus Rylands Greek 470 addresses Mary in the second person and calls her Theotokos: "Beneath thy compassion we take refuge, O Theotokos: do not despise our petitions in time of trouble, but rescue us from dangers." The papyrus is undated; palaeographers have placed the hand anywhere from the third century (Lobel) to the sixth–seventh or later (Förster). On the earliest of these dates it is the earliest extant petitionary prayer addressed to Mary and the earliest extant text to call her Theotokos; it cannot be fixed at one year on the timeline.10

Methodius of Olympus (d. c. 311; the invocation attributed to him)

Methodius of Olympus is listed among early witnesses to the invocation of the saints on the strength of the Oration on Simeon and Anna, which addresses the Virgin and Simeon in the second person. The editors of the Ante-Nicene Fathers note that its Theotokos language is "hardly appropriate to the period preceding Cyril of Alexandria," and the oration’s authorship is questioned; Methodius’s Symposium contains no second-person address to a saint.11

Cyril of Jerusalem (c. 350)

Walking the newly baptized through the Eucharistic prayer, Cyril of Jerusalem reports that the church commemorates "Patriarchs, Prophets, Apostles, Martyrs, that at their prayers and intercessions God would receive our petition." The prayer is addressed to God and names the saints’ intercession in the third person.12

Ephrem the Syrian (c. 363–373)

Ephrem the Syrian, writing in Syriac, praises Mary’s purity and new-Eve honour — "lo! in Mary their sister, greatly magnified was their honour" — in hymns whose petitions, in the texts surveyed here, are addressed to Christ. The second-person prayers to Mary transmitted under his name belong to the Greek corpus known as Ephraem Graecus, one of which Roger Pearse, following M. I. Manousakas, dates to around 1400.13

Basil of Caesarea (c. 373)

Basil of Caesarea's homily on the Forty Martyrs of Sebaste describes the martyrs’ shrine, feast and relics and hails them: "O common guardians of the human race! … helpers in prayer, most powerful intercessors," in whom the afflicted "take refuge." Its address to the martyrs is in the vocative, and its one imperative of request — "May your requests be with the martyrs" — is addressed to the living congregation.14

Epiphanius of Salamis (c. 377)

Epiphanius of Salamis distinguishes the honour paid to a saint from the worship owed to God alone. Writing against the Arabian Collyridians, women who "decorate a barber's chair … set out bread and offer it in Mary's name," he insists that the Virgin be honoured and states the rule: "Mary should be honored, but the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit should be worshiped; no one should worship Mary." He extends it to every saint — "Elijah is not to be worshiped … John is not to be worshiped … neither is Thecla worshiped, nor any of the saints."15

Ambrose of Milan (c. 377)

Expounding the healing of Peter's mother-in-law, Ambrose of Milan enjoins his flock that "The angels must be entreated for us … the martyrs must be entreated, whose patronage we seem to claim for ourselves by the pledge as it were of their bodily remains." The text directs the living to petition the martyrs; its grammatical addressees are the living congregation.16

Gregory of Nazianzus (c. 379–381)

Gregory of Nazianzus's funeral orations turn to address the holy dead and petition them: to Basil he prays, "mayest thou gaze upon us from above … either stay by thy entreaties our thorn in the flesh … or prevail upon us to bear it boldly." In Oration 24 he addresses the martyr Cyprian on his feast and asks his protection for the Church.17

Gregory of Nyssa (c. 380–381)

In his encomium on the martyr Theodore, Gregory of Nyssa turns and addresses the dead saint directly — "Come from that place to those who beseech you, invisible friend!" — and petitions him to intercede and "as a soldier, fight for us" against the barbarians on the frontier, and to enlist his fellow-martyrs and the apostles.18

John Chrysostom (c. 390)

Preaching on 2 Corinthians, John Chrysostom describes the emperor who lays aside his pride and "stands begging the saints to be his advocates with God … implores the tent-maker and the fisherman, though dead, to be his patrons," as an instance of the honour of the apostles after death. The petition is reported as the emperor’s act.19

Jerome (c. 406)

Answering Vigilantius, Jerome argues that if apostles and martyrs interceded in the body, "how much more must they do so when once they have won their crowns"; two years earlier, closing his eulogy of the dead Paula, he addresses her directly — "And now, Paula, farewell, and aid with your prayers the old age of your votary."20 The Roman Confutation (1530), rejecting the Augsburg article on the saints, wrote that “eleven hundred years ago St. Jerome vanquished in this area the heretic Vigilantius”; Melanchthon’s Apology (1531) replied that it concerns “honors for the saints, not … invocation.”21

Vigilantius (c. 406)

Vigilantius, a Gallic presbyter, denied that the dead saints can hear or help the living, asking whether the martyrs' souls "hover round" their tombs "lest haply if any one come to pray and they were absent, they could not hear," and stating that "once we die, the prayer of no person for another can be heard." His tract is lost; his words survive only as quoted by Jerome in his reply. On the neighbouring question of prayer for the dead, Aerius of Sebaste (c. 355) was answered by Epiphanius, who included him in his catalogue of heresies, as Vigilantius was answered by Jerome.22

Augustine of Hippo (c. 426)

Augustine affirms the martyrs' intercession and the church's seeking "the assistance of their prayers," and bars any temple, priesthood, or sacrifice directed to a saint — they "are not invoked by the sacrificing priest," "for they are not our gods, but their God is our God." He reserves worship to God alone, and he holds that for the departed "by their proper nature" to be interested in the affairs of the living is "impossible".23

Gregory the Great (c. 604)

Teaching as pope, Gregory the Great holds that the martyrs, though absent in body, "be there present to hear our prayers"; he recounts a paralytic maid who "long … had prayed to St. Peter" and was healed after the apostle appeared to her, and closes an official letter to Queen Brunichild "through the intercession of the blessed Peter."24 The Roman Confutation (1530) had claimed for invocation “the agreement of the holy fathers, Augustine, Bernard, Jerome, Cyprian, Chrysostom, Basil”; Melanchthon’s Apology (1531), replying, names Gregory as the earliest of the ancient writers to mention invocation: “Neither have the rest of the ancient writers before Gregory made mention of invocation.”25

The Second Council of Nicaea (c. 787)

The Second Council of Nicaea, whose central business was images, distinguishes in its Definition the honorific reverence (τιμητικὴ προσκύνησις) paid to images, relics, the Cross, and the saints from the adoration (λατρεία) "which pertains alone to the divine nature," and receives the confessions of returning bishops who "ask for the intercessions of our spotless Lady the Holy Mother of God … and those of all the Saints." Its acts also preserve the Epitome of the iconoclast council of 754, whose anathemas defend "the invocation of Saints."26

Thomas Aquinas (c. 1272)

Asking "whether we ought to pray to God alone?", Thomas Aquinas distinguishes two ways of offering prayer to a person — as to be fulfilled by him, or obtained through him — so that "On the first way we offer prayer to God alone," but "in the second way we pray to the saints … not that God may through them know our petitions, but that our prayers may be effective through their prayers and merits." In q.83 a.11 he defends the saints' prayers for the living against "the error of Vigilantius."27

John Calvin (c. 1559)

In the Institutes John Calvin rejects invocation: "since the Scripture calls us away from all others to Christ alone … it were the extreme of stupidity … to attempt to obtain access by means of others," and of the intercession ascribed to the saints as mediators he writes, "There is not a word on the subject in Scripture." He grants that the saints in heaven pray for the church in general ("though I grant that in this way they pray for us") while holding that "far less are they, therefore, to be invoked by us." The Augsburg Confession (1530) had stated that "the Scripture teaches not the invocation of saints."28

The Council of Trent (c. 1563)

In its final session the Council of Trent decrees that the saints "offer up their own prayers to God for men; that it is good and useful suppliantly to invoke them … for obtaining benefits from God, through His Son, Jesus Christ our Lord, who is our alone Redeemer and Saviour," and condemns as impious those who "deny that the saints … are to be invocated," or who call "the invocation of them … idolatry," "repugnant to the word of God," or "opposed to the honour of the one mediator of God and men, Christ Jesus."29
