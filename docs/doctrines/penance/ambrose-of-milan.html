Ambrose of Milan on penance

Dates: c. 384 (Concerning Repentance / De Paenitentia, two books against the Novatianists; the date is the Benedictine editors’ estimate, with a possible range of c. 384–394) · Wikipedia
Summary: Ambrose is the Latin fourth-century voice for the church’s power to reconcile even the gravest sinners. Written directly against the rigorist heirs of Novatian, his two books Concerning Repentance argue that Christ gave the church — through its priests — the power to remit all sins, resting the claim on the keys and on John 20:22–23, and reading the parable of the prodigal son as the pattern of a penitent restored to the feast.

Claims & evidence

Christ’s gift of the keys (John 20:22–23) gave the church the power to loose sins

For inasmuch as the Lord Jesus Himself said in the Gospel: “Receive ye the Holy Spirit whosesoever sins ye forgive they are forgiven unto them, and whosesoever sins ye retain, they are retained,” Who is it that honours Him most, he who obeys His bidding or he who rejects it?

For the Lord willed that the power of binding and of loosing should be alike… Each is allowed to the Church, neither to heresy, for this power has been entrusted to priests alone. Rightly, therefore, does the Church claim it, which has true priests; heresy, which has not the priests of God, cannot claim it.

— Concerning Repentance I.6–7, source (also at New Advent). For Ambrose the same word of Christ that grants the power to retain grants the power to remit; a church that could bind but not loose would contradict the very text it claims. The Novatianists, by denying they may loose, unwittingly disclaim the priesthood altogether.

God “made an exception of none” — the church may reconcile the gravest sinner

For when the Lord forgave all sins, He made an exception of none.

But God does not make a distinction, Who has promised His mercy to all, and granted to His priests the power of loosing without any exception. But he who has heaped up sin must also increase his penitence. For greater sins are washed away by greater weeping.

— Concerning Repentance I.5 and I.10, source. Against Novatian’s pole — that the lapsed have “no longer… a hope of salvation” — Ambrose insists no sin is beyond the church’s power to remit; the graver the sin, the heavier the penance, but the door is never shut. This is the same mediating course Cyprian set a century earlier, now argued from the keys rather than the discipline of the lapsed.

Penance and baptism are one and the same mystery of remission

If baptism is certainly the remission of all sins, what difference does it make whether priests claim that this power is given to them in penance or at the font? In each the mystery is one.

— Concerning Repentance I.36, source. Ambrose catches the Novatianists in an inconsistency: they baptize (remitting all sins) and lay on hands, yet deny that sins can be remitted in penance — but the remitting power is one, whether exercised at the font or in the penitent’s restoration.

The prodigal son is the pattern of the reconciled penitent

So quickly does he gain forgiveness, that, as he is coming, and is still a great way off, his father meets him, gives him a kiss, which is the sign of sacred peace; orders the robe to be brought forth, which is the marriage garment… places the ring on his hand, which is the pledge of faith and the seal of the Holy Spirit… bids the calf to be slain, for “Christ our Passover hath been sacrificed.”

— Concerning Repentance II.18 (expounding the parable, II.13–18), source. Ambrose turns the parable of the prodigal (Luke 15) into a proof that “reconciliation ought not to be refused to the greatest offender upon suitable proof of repentance”: the father runs to the returning wastrel and restores him to robe, ring, and feast — the very thing Novatian would deny.

Notes

  • Ambrose fills the Latin fourth-century gap on the penance timeline between Basil of Caesarea’s canonical code (c. 375) and the Irish penitentials (c. 525–650). His discipline is still the ancient public, once-only penance — he warns that penance repeated too often is “destroyed” (II.98) — but his defence of the church’s power to remit even grave sins is the doctrinal ground on which the later repeatable, private form would build.
  • The same treatise supplies Ambrose’s witness against the parity of sins: he charges the Novatianists with reasoning “after the fashion of the Stoics,” then turns the charge into a reductio.
  • Ambrose appears on this wiki also as a witness on the seven sacraments (his On the Mysteries), purgatory, and the nephilim.
