Basil of Caesarea on Penance

Dates: c. 375 · Wikipedia
Summary: Basil's three canonical letters to Amphilochius of Iconium turned the graded penance of Nicaea into a detailed penitential code: for each sin he assigns a fixed term of years, distributed across the four standing orders of penitents — weepers, hearers, kneelers, and standers. These letters became the backbone of Eastern canon law on penance.

Claims & evidence

The four grades and a fixed term, sin by sin

Basil's canon on the deliberate murderer lays out all four orders in sequence and assigns a twenty-year term distributed among them:

The intentional homicide, who has afterwards repented, will be excommunicated from the sacrament for twenty years… for four he ought to weep, standing outside the door of the house of prayer, beseeching the faithful as they enter in to offer prayer in his behalf, and confessing his own sin. After four years he will be admitted among the hearers, and during five years will go out with them. During seven years he will go out with the kneelers, praying. During four years he will only stand with the faithful, and will not take part in the oblation. On the completion of this period he will be admitted to participation of the sacrament.

— Basil, Letter CCXVII (the Third Canonical Letter), Canon LVI (trans. Blomfield Jackson, Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, 2nd ser., vol. 8), source.1 The four stations — weepers (outside the door), hearers, kneelers (or prostrators), and standers (who stand with the faithful but do not yet offer) — are here a fixed penitential ladder, each with its own count of years.

Notes

  • The system perfected. Basil supplies for the East what Nicaea had only sketched: a complete schedule matching sin to term to grade. The result is a public, graduated, and essentially once-only discipline — the mature form of the ancient "canonical penance" that the Irish penitentials would later displace with private, repeatable confession.
  • Confession within the order. Basil's murderer, standing outside the door, is "confessing his own sin" and begging the prayers of those entering — confession here is a public act embedded in the penitential progression, not yet the private auricular confession of the medieval West.
  • Basil across the wiki. Basil appears here for his canonical letters; he is also a witness on prayer to the saints and the perpetual virginity of Mary.
