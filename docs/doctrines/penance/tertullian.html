Tertullian on Penance

Dates: c. 203 and c. 217 · Wikipedia
Summary: Tertullian is a witness on both sides of himself. As a churchman (On Repentance, c. 203) he gives the classic account of the "second repentance" after baptism and of its public rite, exomologesis — a once-only plank for the shipwrecked sinner. As a Montanist (On Modesty, c. 217) he turned rigorist, attacking a bishop's edict that forgave adultery and fornication and dividing sins into the remissible and the irremissible, which the church has no power to pardon at all.

Claims & evidence

Early: a "second repentance" after baptism — the plank after shipwreck

In On Repentance Tertullian teaches that baptism washes away former sins, but for sins after baptism God has, reluctantly, left one further remedy — not to be presumed upon:

do you so hasten to, so embrace, as a shipwrecked man the protection of some plank. This will draw you forth when sunk in the waves of sins, and will bear you forward into the port of the divine clemency.

— Tertullian, On Repentance, ch. IV, source.1 He calls repentance and its outward act "these two planks (as it were) of human salvation," baptism being the first.2 But it is emphatically a single reserve: "We have escaped once: thus far and no farther let us commit ourselves to perils."3

Early: exomologesis — the public discipline of the penitent

Tertullian gives the earliest detailed description of the outward rite of public penance — confession, sackcloth and ashes, fasting, and prostration before the presbyters and the congregation:

This act, which is more usually expressed and commonly spoken of under a Greek name, is εξομολόγησις, whereby we confess our sins to the Lord, not indeed as if He were ignorant of them, but inasmuch as by confession satisfaction is settled… And thus exomologesis is a discipline for man's prostration and humiliation… it commands (the penitent) to lie in sackcloth and ashes, to cover his body in mourning… to bow before the feet of the presbyters, and kneel to God's dear ones; to enjoin on all the brethren to be ambassadors to bear his deprecatory supplication (before God).

— Tertullian, On Repentance, ch. IX, source.4

Late (Montanist): an edict forgiving adultery — and the church's power denied

In On Modesty the now-Montanist Tertullian rounds on a bishop's edict remitting sexual sin (probably Callistus of Rome, whom he mockingly styles "Pontifex Maximus… the bishop of bishops"):

I hear that there has even been an edict set forth, and a peremptory one too. The Pontifex Maximus—that is, the bishop of bishops—issues an edict: "I remit, to such as have discharged (the requirements of) repentance, the sins both of adultery and of fornication."

— Tertullian, On Modesty, ch. I, source.5 He now divides sins into two classes and denies the church any power over the graver kind:

We agree that the causes of repentance are sins. These we divide into two issues: some will be remissible, some irremissible… Every sin is dischargeable either by pardon or else by penalty: by pardon as the result of chastisement, by penalty as the result of condemnation.

— Tertullian, On Modesty, ch. II, source.6 Adultery and fornication, he argues, belong with "all other crimes equally capital" — either all are remissible or these too must be "retained inconcessible."7

Notes

  • The reversal. The two works bracket Tertullian's break with the catholic church for the New Prophecy. In On Repentance he defends the one post-baptismal reconciliation that Hermas had already granted; in On Modesty he attacks the church for extending it to the "capital" sins. He thus anticipates both poles of the later crisis — the mediating discipline of Cyprian and the rigorism of Novatian.
  • Whose edict? The "bishop of bishops" is most often identified as Callistus I of Rome (bishop c. 217–222), whose reported leniency toward sexual sin Hippolytus also attacked; a minority argue for Agrippinus of Carthage. Either way the edict shows a bishop already claiming, in the early third century, the authority to reconcile even grave sinners.
  • The "plank after shipwreck." Tertullian's image (naufragii tabula) passed permanently into the church's language for penance; the Council of Trent would still call penance "a second plank after shipwreck" thirteen centuries later.
  • An overstatement flagged. The Montanist Tertullian does not merely report a stricter discipline; he argues that the graver "capital" sins are irremissible — that the church has no power to pardon them at all, and that they must be "retained inconcessible." As a historical claim (that this was the church's original, universal rule) this overreaches: the catholic church of his own day already forgave such sins, as Callistus's edict, which he is attacking, shows, and reconciliation of grave sinners is attested earlier still (Hermas's "second repentance"; Dionysius of Corinth received back even apostates and heretics). Tertullian is a rigorist pole, not a witness to a lost consensus — a distinction weighed on the argument page Did the early church deny it could reconcile the gravest sins?
