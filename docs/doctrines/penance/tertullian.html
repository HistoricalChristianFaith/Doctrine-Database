Tertullian on Penance

Dates: c. 203 and c. 217 · Wikipedia
Summary: Tertullian wrote on both sides of the question. As a churchman (On Repentance, c. 203) he describes the "second repentance" after baptism and its public rite, exomologesis — a once-only plank for the shipwrecked sinner. As a Montanist (On Modesty, c. 217) he attacked a bishop's edict that forgave adultery and fornication, divided sins into the remissible and the irremissible, and denied that the bishop could remit the graver kind, whose pardon he reserved to God.

Claims & evidence

Early: a "second repentance" after baptism — the plank after shipwreck

In On Repentance Tertullian teaches that baptism washes away former sins, but for sins after baptism God has, reluctantly, left one further remedy — not to be presumed upon:

do you so hasten to, so embrace, as a shipwrecked man the protection of some plank. This will draw you forth when sunk in the waves of sins, and will bear you forward into the port of the divine clemency.

— Tertullian, On Repentance, ch. IV, source.1 He calls repentance and its outward act "these two planks (as it were) of human salvation," baptism being the first.2 But it is a single reserve: "We have escaped once: thus far and no farther let us commit ourselves to perils."3

Early: exomologesis — the public discipline of the penitent

Tertullian gives the earliest extant detailed description of the outward rite of public penance — confession, sackcloth and ashes, fasting, and prostration before the presbyters and the congregation:

This act, which is more usually expressed and commonly spoken of under a Greek name, is εξομολόγησις, whereby we confess our sins to the Lord, not indeed as if He were ignorant of them, but inasmuch as by confession satisfaction is settled… And thus exomologesis is a discipline for man's prostration and humiliation… it commands (the penitent) to lie in sackcloth and ashes, to cover his body in mourning… to bow before the feet of the presbyters, and kneel to God's dear ones; to enjoin on all the brethren to be ambassadors to bear his deprecatory supplication (before God).

— Tertullian, On Repentance, ch. IX, source.4

Late (Montanist): the edict on adultery, and remissible and irremissible sins

In On Modesty the now-Montanist Tertullian attacks a bishop's edict remitting sexual sin, styling its author "Pontifex Maximus… the bishop of bishops" (on who issued it, see the notes below):

I hear that there has even been an edict set forth, and a peremptory one too. The Pontifex Maximus—that is, the bishop of bishops—issues an edict: "I remit, to such as have discharged (the requirements of) repentance, the sins both of adultery and of fornication."

— Tertullian, On Modesty, ch. I, source.5 He now divides sins into two classes:

We agree that the causes of repentance are sins. These we divide into two issues: some will be remissible, some irremissible… Every sin is dischargeable either by pardon or else by penalty: by pardon as the result of chastisement, by penalty as the result of condemnation.

— Tertullian, On Modesty, ch. II, source.6 Adultery and fornication, he argues, belong with "all other crimes equally capital" — either all are remissible or these too must be "retained inconcessible."7 The pardon of mortal sins, he writes, is God's: "Who, moreover, was able to forgive sins? This is His alone prerogative"; to the claim that "the Church has the power of forgiving sins" he answers with the saying of the Montanist prophets, "The Church has the power to forgive sins; but I will not do it, lest they commit others withal."8

Notes

  • The two works. They fall on either side of Tertullian's turn to the New Prophecy. In On Repentance he defends the one post-baptismal reconciliation that Hermas had granted; in On Modesty he attacks its extension to the "capital" sins.
  • Whose edict? The "bishop of bishops" is identified as Callistus I of Rome (bishop c. 217–222), whose reported leniency toward sexual sin Hippolytus also attacked, by Harnack and by Hanna; F. X. Murphy (New Catholic Encyclopedia) holds that he "was probably Agrippinus, Bishop of Carthage." Harnack reads the edict as Callistus claiming the power of readmission "as a right appertaining to the bishops as successors of the Apostles."9
  • The "plank after shipwreck." The Council of Trent (1551) keeps the image, anathematizing anyone who says that "Penance is not rightly called a second plank after shipwreck."
  • Reception and debate. Tertullian holds that the churches grant "no restoration of peace" to idolatry or bloodshed and that the apostles allowed none to adultery. Harnack (1894) and O. D. Watkins (1920) read the second-century discipline in the same direction, as an older rule of exclusion later relaxed; Edward Hanna (1911) reads Tertullian's own answer, "The Church has the power to forgive sins; but I will not do it," as witness to "the existence of that power in the Church which he had abandoned," and cites Hermas and Dionysius of Corinth for its earlier exercise. The two readings are set out on Did the early church hold that the gravest sins lay outside its power to reconcile?10
