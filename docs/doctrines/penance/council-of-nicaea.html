The Council of Nicaea on Penance

Dates: 325 (the First Ecumenical Council, convoked by Constantine) · Wikipedia
Summary: Nicaea's disciplinary canons on the lapsed show the mediating discipline of Cyprian hardened into fixed, church-wide law. Penitents pass through graded orders — hearers, then prostrators, then those who stand with the faithful but may not yet offer — for set terms of years before full communion is restored; and no one, however grave the sin, may be denied the viaticum at the point of death.

Claims & evidence

The grades of penance: hearers, prostrators, and standers (Canon 11)

Canon 11 deals with those who lapsed in the persecution of Licinius without being compelled to. It grants them mercy, but on a fixed graduated term:

As many as were communicants, if they heartily repent, shall pass three years among the hearers; for seven years they shall be prostrators; and for two years they shall communicate with the people in prayers, but without oblation.

— Council of Nicaea (325), Canon 11 (trans. Henry R. Percival, Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, 2nd ser., vol. 14), source.1 The three named stages — the hearers (who may stay for the readings, then leave), the prostrators or kneelers (who lie prostrate and are dismissed), and those who "communicate… in prayers, but without oblation" (the consistentes or standers) — are the canonical orders of penitents, a public progression back toward the altar.

Longer terms for the graver fall (Canon 12)

Canon 12 concerns Christians who had served in the army, given up their military belts to profess the faith, then returned to the army — treated as a graver relapse and given a heavier term:

let these, after they have passed the space of three years as hearers, be for ten years prostrators.

— Council of Nicaea (325), Canon 12, source.2 The bishop may shorten the term for genuine conversion, tested "by fear, and tears, and perseverance, and good works."3

The dying may never be refused communion (Canon 13)

However far a penitent stands from the completion of his term, death overrides the discipline:

Concerning the departing, the ancient canonical law is still to be maintained, to wit, that, if any man be at the point of death, he must not be deprived of the last and most indispensable Viaticum.

— Council of Nicaea (325), Canon 13, source.4 This is the very concession Novatian's schism denied — peace for the dying penitent — now made "ancient canonical law."

Notes

  • From pastoral judgment to fixed canon. What Cyprian had exercised as a bishop's discretion — graded penance ending in restoration — Nicaea turned into standing law with named orders and terms of years. Basil of Caesarea would systematize the terms sin-by-sin a generation later.
  • The fourth grade. Alongside the hearers, prostrators, and standers, the Eastern discipline also counted a first order of "weepers" or mourners, who remained outside the church door; Nicaea's canons name the three inside the building, and Basil's canonical letters lay out all four in sequence.
  • Against rigorism, permanently. Canon 13's viaticum rule fixed the church's rejection of Novatian's position as ecumenical law: no penitent, however grave the sin or incomplete the penance, dies excommunicate.
