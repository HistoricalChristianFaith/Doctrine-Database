The Council of Trent on Penance

Dates: 1551 (Session 14, under Julius III) · Wikipedia
Summary: Against the Reformers — who kept confession but denied it was a sacrament of satisfaction and held priestly absolution to be a mere announcement of the gospel — the Council of Trent defined penance dogmatically. It is a true sacrament instituted by Christ for sins committed after baptism (the "second plank after shipwreck"); its parts are contrition, confession, and satisfaction; and the priest's absolution is a judicial act, not a bare declaration.

Claims & evidence

Penance is a sacrament for sins after baptism — instituted at John 20:22–23

Trent grounds the sacrament in Christ's post-resurrection breathing on the apostles, giving them power to forgive and retain sins:

the Lord then principally instituted the sacrament of penance, when, being raised from the dead, He breathed upon His disciples, saying Receive ye the Holy Ghost, whose sins you shall forgive, they are forgiven them, and whose sins you shall retain, they are retained… the power of forgiving and retaining sins was communicated to the apostles and their lawful successors, for the reconciling of the faithful who have fallen after baptism.

— Council of Trent, Session 14 (1551), Doctrine on the Sacrament of Penance, ch. 1 (trans. J. Waterworth), source.1 The council keeps Tertullian's ancient image: to confound penance with baptism, so that "Penance is not rightly called a second plank after shipwreck," is anathema.2

The parts: contrition, confession, and satisfaction

Against the Protestant reduction of penance to faith, Trent defines three required acts of the penitent:

the acts of the penitent himself, to wit, contrition, confession and satisfaction, are as it were the matter of this sacrament. Which acts, inasmuch as they are, by God's institution, required in the penitent for the integrity of the sacrament, and for the full and perfect remission of sins, are for this reason called the parts of penance.

— Council of Trent, Session 14, ch. 3, source.3

Contrition and attrition

Trent distinguishes perfect sorrow (contrition, moved by love of God) from the imperfect sorrow of fear (attrition), and — settling a long scholastic dispute — rules attrition a genuine gift of God that disposes the penitent toward grace:

as to that imperfect contrition, which is called attrition, because that it is commonly conceived either from the consideration of the turpitude of sin, or from the fear of hell and of punishment… if, with the hope of pardon, it exclude the wish to sin… it is even a gift of God, and an impulse of the Holy Ghost.

— Council of Trent, Session 14, ch. 4, source.4 The council settles that attrition is a true and dispositive sorrow (Canon 5), but stops short of defining whether it suffices without any beginning of love — the joint at which the later attrition-and-contrition dispute divides: the attritionist reading (contested) and the contritionist reading (plausible), weighed adversarially on their own pages.

Absolution is a judicial act, not a bare declaration

The council's central anti-Reformation definition: the priest does not merely announce that sins are forgiven; he pronounces sentence as a judge:

although the absolution of the priest is the dispensation of another's bounty, yet is it not a bare ministry only, whether of announcing the Gospel, or of declaring that sins are forgiven, but is after the manner of a judicial act, whereby sentence is pronounced by the priest as by a judge.

— Council of Trent, Session 14, ch. 6, source.5 Canon 9 anathematizes the contrary — that absolution "is not a judicial act, but a bare ministry of pronouncing and declaring sins to be forgiven to him who confesses."6

Notes

  • What Trent was answering. The Reformers had not abolished confession — Luther retained private confession and absolution — but they denied that it was a sacrament of satisfaction and read priestly absolution as the minister declaring the forgiveness that faith receives directly from God's promise. Trent fixes the opposite on every point: penance is a proper sacrament, satisfaction is a real part of it, and absolution is an effective judicial sentence. The two readings — the power of the keys — are weighed adversarially on the doctrine's argument pages: the effective / judicial reading (contested — strong on the patristic weight, but Trent's sharp “sentence as by a judge” form is a Latin development the Greek East never took) and the declarative reading (plausible). Trent's own “judicial act” specification is the sharpest form of the effective reading, not the whole of it.
  • The end of a long road. Trent is the terminus of the arc that began with Hermas's single second repentance: what was once a rare, public, once-only reconciliation is now a repeatable, private, sacramental court, obligatory yearly since Lateran IV and here defined dogma.
  • Trent across the wiki. The Council of Trent is also a witness on the Real Presence, justification, and the Old Testament canon.
