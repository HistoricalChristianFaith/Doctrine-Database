The Council of Trent on Penance

Dates: 1551 (Session 14, under Julius III) · Wikipedia
Summary: In Session 14 the Council of Trent defined penance dogmatically and anathematized the positions it rejected, among them the view that absolution is "a bare ministry of pronouncing and declaring sins to be forgiven" (Canon 9). Penance, it teaches, is a true sacrament instituted by Christ for sins committed after baptism (the "second plank after shipwreck"); its parts are contrition, confession, and satisfaction; and the priest's absolution is a judicial act, not a bare declaration.

Claims & evidence

Penance as a sacrament for sins after baptism, instituted at John 20:22–23

Trent grounds the sacrament in Christ's post-resurrection breathing on the apostles, giving them power to forgive and retain sins:

the Lord then principally instituted the sacrament of penance, when, being raised from the dead, He breathed upon His disciples, saying Receive ye the Holy Ghost, whose sins you shall forgive, they are forgiven them, and whose sins you shall retain, they are retained… the power of forgiving and retaining sins was communicated to the apostles and their lawful successors, for the reconciling of the faithful who have fallen after baptism.

— Council of Trent, Session 14 (1551), Doctrine on the Sacrament of Penance, ch. 1 (trans. J. Waterworth), source.1 The council keeps Tertullian's ancient image: to confound penance with baptism, so that "Penance is not rightly called a second plank after shipwreck," is anathema.2

The parts: contrition, confession, and satisfaction

Trent defines three required acts of the penitent:

the acts of the penitent himself, to wit, contrition, confession and satisfaction, are as it were the matter of this sacrament. Which acts, inasmuch as they are, by God's institution, required in the penitent for the integrity of the sacrament, and for the full and perfect remission of sins, are for this reason called the parts of penance.

— Council of Trent, Session 14, ch. 3, source.3

Contrition and attrition

Trent distinguishes perfect sorrow (contrition, moved by love of God) from the imperfect sorrow of fear (attrition), a question long discussed among the scholastics, and rules attrition a genuine gift of God that disposes the penitent toward grace:

as to that imperfect contrition, which is called attrition, because that it is commonly conceived either from the consideration of the turpitude of sin, or from the fear of hell and of punishment… if, with the hope of pardon, it exclude the wish to sin… it is even a gift of God, and an impulse of the Holy Ghost.

— Council of Trent, Session 14, ch. 4, source.4 The council treats attrition as a true and dispositive sorrow (Canon 5); whether it suffices without any beginning of love was disputed among scholastic theologians after the council: Edward Hanna (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1907) reports that Alexander VII's decree of 6 May 1667 forbade either side to brand the other "with any mark of theological censure" until the Holy See should define the question.5

Absolution as a judicial act (ch. 6 and Canon 9)

On absolution the council teaches that the priest does not merely announce that sins are forgiven; he pronounces sentence as a judge:

although the absolution of the priest is the dispensation of another's bounty, yet is it not a bare ministry only, whether of announcing the Gospel, or of declaring that sins are forgiven, but is after the manner of a judicial act, whereby sentence is pronounced by the priest as by a judge.

— Council of Trent, Session 14, ch. 6, source.6 Canon 9 anathematizes the contrary — that absolution "is not a judicial act, but a bare ministry of pronouncing and declaring sins to be forgiven to him who confesses."7

Notes

  • The Lutheran confession of 1530. The Augsburg Confession retains private absolution ("Private Absolution ought to be retained in the churches"), describes repentance as contrition and the faith "born of the Gospel, or of absolution," condemns the Novatians, and rejects those who "command us to merit grace through satisfactions of our own." Trent's Session 14 defines penance as a sacrament, satisfaction as one of its parts, and absolution as "after the manner of a judicial act."8 Archimandrite Job (Getcha), an Orthodox liturgist, notes that Byzantine service books contain both deprecative ("May God forgive…") and indicative ("I forgive and absolve you") absolution prayers, the indicative "as for instance in the Trebnyk of St. Peter Mohyla," and holds that the indicative entered the Orthodox books through Mohyla, who "inspired himself of the Latin Ritual of Pope Paul V for the prayer of absolution that he introduced into his Trebnyk published in Kiev in 1646"; "From there, this prayer of absolution was adopted in the XVII century during the correction of liturgical books undertaken by the Russian Patriarch Nikon," and it "replaced the former one." Comparing the older deprecative prayer, in which the priest "is an intercessor," with Mohyla's, he writes: "If the second one reflects the patristic theology on confession, the first one has inherited of the scholastic view on confession" (the "second" being the older prayer). He sets the older prayer in a "therapeutic" model of confession, in which "Christ appears as the physician, the healer," and the indicative in a "juridical" one, the model "of a law court."9
  • Placement on the timeline. Trent is the last witness on this timeline, which begins with Hermas's single second repentance; the confession it treats is repeatable and private, and obligatory yearly under Lateran IV's canon, which Trent cites.
  • Trent across the wiki. The Council of Trent is also a witness on the Real Presence, justification, and the Old Testament canon.
