The Irish Penitentials on Penance

Dates: c. 525–650 · Wikipedia
Summary: The Irish and Welsh monastic handbooks of penance — Finnian (c. 525–550), Columbanus (c. 600), Cummean (c. 650) — replaced the ancient public, once-only canonical penance with something new: a private confession to a priest, a fixed "tariff" of penance set to each named sin, and — decisively — a discipline that could be repeated. Carried to the Continent by Columbanus and to England by the Anglo-Saxon and Roman missions, this Celtic system became the ancestor of medieval and modern confession.

Claims & evidence

A fixed tariff for each sin — and it is to be absolved "in secret"

The Penitential of Finnian assigns set terms of bread-and-water fasting graded to the gravity and the frequency of the sin, and states plainly that the whole business is private:

But if one who is a cleric falls miserably through fornication he shall lose his place of honor, and if it happens once [only] and it is concealed from men but known before God, he shall do penance for an entire year with an allowance of bread and water… For, we say, sins are to be absolved in secret by penance and by very diligent devotion of heart and body.

— Penitential of Finnian (c. 525–550), canon 10, in John T. McNeill & Helena M. Gamer, Medieval Handbooks of Penance (Columbia UP, 1938), p. 89, source.1

The confessor as physician of souls

The Penitential of Columbanus opens its section for clergy and laity with the medical image that governs the whole genre — the priest matches remedy to wound, sin by sin:

Diversity of guilt occasions diversity of penalty; for even the physicians of bodies prepare their medicines in various sorts… So therefore the spiritual physicians ought also to heal with various sorts of treatment the wounds, fevers, transgressions, sorrows, sicknesses, and infirmities of souls.

— Penitential of Columbanus (c. 600), Part B, in McNeill & Gamer, pp. 251–252, source.2 Columbanus's handbook, like Cummean's "health-giving medicine of souls," scales the penance by the offender's rank as well as the deed — for the same sin, "if he is a cleric [he shall do penance for] three years; if a monk or deacon, five years; if a priest, seven; if a bishop, twelve years."3

The two breaks with the old discipline: private, and repeatable

The editors of the standard edition draw out what made this discipline new. First, it is private at every stage:

According to the penitentials penance is to be administered privately at every stage; confession is to be made in secret to a qualified person, who is regularly, of course, a priest.

— McNeill & Gamer, Medieval Handbooks of Penance, p. 28, source.4 Second — against the ancient rule that penance, like baptism, could be received only once — it may be done again:

In the penitentials, on the other hand, except where life sentences are assigned, there is no impediment to the iteration of penance.

— McNeill & Gamer, p. 15, source.5

How it displaced the public discipline

The old public penance had never taken root among the newly-converted Germanic and Insular peoples; the Celtic system filled the vacuum and could not be dislodged:

The penitentials could not be thus summarily suppressed. Their use made possible the general spread of the discipline, while the bishops had nothing practicable to offer in their place. The demand for canonical penance was idle: as a working system the public discipline could hardly have been imposed on the Germanic peoples.

— McNeill & Gamer, p. 27, source.6

Notes

  • The hinge of the whole story. Everything before this — Hermas, Tertullian, Cyprian, Nicaea, Basil — assumes penance is a grave, public, essentially once-in-a-lifetime event. The Irish penitentials quietly reverse all three features: private, tariffed, and repeatable. The confession that the Fourth Lateran Council would make annually mandatory, and that Trent would define as a sacrament, is this Celtic form, not the old canonical one.
  • Not a papal or conciliar act. The transformation came from monastic practice, not from Rome or a council — and Continental bishops at first resisted it (a ninth-century reform movement even tried to burn the penitentials). It spread because it worked pastorally where the public discipline could not.
  • A gap worth flagging. These handbooks are cited here from McNeill & Gamer's standard English edition; the Latin originals of Finnian, Columbanus, and Cummean survive in editions such as Ludwig Bieler's The Irish Penitentials (1963).
