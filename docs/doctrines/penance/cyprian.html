Cyprian of Carthage on Penance

Dates: c. 251 · Wikipedia
Summary: The Decian persecution (250–251) produced a mass of Christians who had sacrificed or bought certificates to escape death — the lapsi. Cyprian's On the Lapsed and his letters chart the mediating course the church took between laxity and rigorism: the lapsed may be reconciled, but only after full penance graded to the gravity of the fall, and by the bishop's authority — not on the strength of martyrs' letters of peace hastily produced.

Claims & evidence

Even the libellatici — who bought certificates without sacrificing — have sinned and must repent

Cyprian refuses to let those who obtained a certificate (libellus) of sacrifice, without actually sacrificing, think themselves innocent:

Nor let those persons flatter themselves that they need repent the less, who, although they have not polluted their hands with abominable sacrifices, yet have defiled their conscience with certificates… He says that he has done what another has actually committed… he has been more obedient to human authority than to God.

— Cyprian, On the Lapsed, §27, source.1 The gravest wound, he says, is "to have sinned, and not to make atonement—to have committed crimes, and not to bewail your crimes."2

Reconciliation is possible — but through the church's ministers, not by usurped peace

Against those who demanded immediate readmission on the authority of confessors' libelli pacis (letters of peace), Cyprian insists that pardon is God's to give through his appointed ministers, and is not to be seized:

Thus, not everything that is asked is in the pre-judgment of the asker, but in the free will of the giver; neither can human judgment claim to itself or usurp anything, unless the divine pleasure approve.

— Cyprian, On the Lapsed, §19, source.3

The lapsed are not abandoned: to the penitent, even the adulterer, peace is offered

Cyprian's mediating principle, stated against the rigorists in his letter to Antonianus, is that repentance keeps its "fruit" — a real hope of reconciliation — for those who do it:

For to adulterers even a time of repentance is granted by us, and peace is given. Yet virginity is not therefore deficient in the Church.

— Cyprian, Epistle LI (Oxford Ep. 55, to Antonianus, a.d. 252), §20, source.4 Denying repentance its fruit, he warns, would only drive the fallen to despair, so that "no one can be constrained by us to repentance, if the fruit of repentance be taken away."5

Notes

  • The middle position. Cyprian stands between the laxists (who reconciled the lapsed at once on confessors' certificates) and Novatian's rigorists (who denied them reconciliation altogether). His answer — graded, bishop-controlled penance ending in restoration — became the shape of the later Nicene canonical system.
  • Discipline in the bishop's hands. Cyprian's insistence that peace be granted by the bishop with his clergy, not by the martyrs' prerogative, is inseparable from his high doctrine of the episcopate (see the government of the church and Roman primacy, where Cyprian is also a witness).
  • The same Novatian, on the other side. Cyprian's letter records that Novatian himself, before his schism, had subscribed the Roman clergy's ruling that "peace ought to be granted to the lapsed who were sick and at the point of departure" — a position he would soon abandon.6
