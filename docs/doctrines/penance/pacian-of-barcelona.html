Pacian of Barcelona on penance

Dates: c. 380 (bishop of Barcelona c. 365–391; three Letters to Sympronian and the Paraenesis ad Paenitentiam — Exhortation to Penance — against the Novatianists then flourishing in Spain) · Wikipedia
Summary: Pacian's writings are an extended Latin reply to Novatianism, a few years before Ambrose's. Prompted by a Novatianist correspondent, Sympronian, he wrote three Letters and a treatise arguing that the church remits post-baptismal sin — including the grave sins. His argument is that Christ’s power to bind and loose (Matthew 18:18) was not the Apostles’ alone but passed to the bishops, so that priests baptize, impose penance, and absolve “by the authority of Christ,” who in granting the power to loose “excepted nothing whatever.” He is also the source of the famous tag “Christian is my name, but Catholic my surname.”

Claims & evidence

The Novatianist position as Pacian states it

The whole treatise of the Novatians, which you have addressed to me thronged with propositions on all sides, amounts to this, brother Sympronian: That there is no room for repentance after Baptism; that the Church cannot remit mortal sin; that by the receiving of sinners she herself perishes.

— Letter 3, Against the Treatise of the Novatians, source (trans. C. H. Collyns, Library of Fathers of the Holy Catholic Church, vol. 17, 1842). Pacian frames the whole controversy as a single thesis: that the church has no power over post-baptismal sin. At Rome a century and a quarter earlier, Novatian, whom Eusebius reports as treating the lapsed as having “no longer… a hope of salvation” (Socrates reports his letter to the churches as leaving their pardon to God), had refused the lapsed admission to the mysteries; here a party in fourth-century Spain holds the position Pacian answers.

The power to bind and loose, the Apostles, and the bishops

Why said He this, if it was not lawful for men to bind and loose? Is this allowed to Apostles only? Then to them also only is it allowed to baptize, and to them only to give the Holy Spirit, and to them only to cleanse the sins of the nations; for all this was enjoined on none others but Apostles. But if both the loosening of bonds and the power of the Sacrament are given in one place, either the whole has been derived to us from the Apostolic form and authority, or else not even this relaxation has been made from the decree.

— Letter 1, On the Catholic Name, source. Pacian argues by reductio: if the power to loose belonged to the Apostles alone, then so did baptism and the giving of the Spirit — which the Novatianists themselves still practise. Since they claim the one, he argues, they cannot consistently deny the other; the Apostolic functions descend together, or not at all. Ambrose makes a parallel argument (c. 384).

Priests baptize, impose penance, and absolve “by the authority of Christ”

Wherefore, whether we baptize, whether we constrain to penance, or grant pardon to the penitent, we do this by the authority of Christ.

— Letter 3, source. Against the Novatianist charge that reserving forgiveness to God alone honours Him, Pacian answers that the priest does not usurp the divine prerogative but exercises a delegated one: the absolution is Christ’s, worked through his minister.

Christ “excepted nothing” — the power to loose and every sin

Whatsoever ye shall loose, He says. He excepted nothing whatever. Whatsoever, He says, great or small.

— Letter 3, source. Pacian’s answer to the division of sins into the remissible and the irremissible: Christ’s grant of the loosing power is, he argues, unrestricted in its terms, so the church may not set apart a class of “mortal” sins it refuses to touch. Ambrose puts the same claim as “when the Lord forgave all sins, He made an exception of none.”

The rite of penance: confession, mourning, and absolution “by the tears of the Church”

He therefore, who hides not his sins from the brethren, assisted by the tears of the Church, is absolved by Christ.

— Paraenesis ad Paenitentiam (Exhortation to Penance), source. The Paraenesis is Pacian’s pastoral appeal to those under discipline not to shrink from the public penance — to weep, fast, and confess before the church — and a warning of the far heavier reckoning that awaits those who die with their penance unperformed. His discipline is the exomologesis: public, once-only, and severe — the same shape Tertullian and Cyprian describe — and its end is reconciliation, which Novatian's party refused.

“Christian is my name, but Catholic my surname”

Christian is my name, but Catholic my surname. The former gives me a name, the latter distinguishes me. By the one I am approved; by the other I am but marked.

— Letter 1, On the Catholic Name, source. Sympronian had faulted the church for taking the title “Catholic”; Pacian answers with the epigram he is best remembered for, arguing that the name marks out the one undivided church from the sects called after their founders (Novatian’s Cathari among them). The line is why Pacian is one of the Latin sources — with Cyprian — who give his name as “Novatian.”

Notes

  • Pacian sits on the penance timeline between Basil of Caesarea’s canonical code (c. 375) and Ambrose of Milan’s De Paenitentia (c. 384) — the two Latin anti-Novatianist defences of the church’s power to reconcile grave sinners are near-contemporary, with no sign in the texts that either read the other. Both rest the case on the keys and the loosing power rather than on the discipline of the lapsed.
  • Jerome (De viris illustribus 106) praises Pacian’s eloquence, learning, and holiness and lists his works, which is much of why they survived; the Latin texts are in Migne, Patrologia Latina 13. The English here follows the Library of Fathers translation (Oxford, 1842), where Pacian’s works were printed as an appendix to the epistles of Cyprian.
