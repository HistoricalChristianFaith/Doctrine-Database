Callistus of Rome on Penance

Dates: c. 217–222 (bishop of Rome) · Wikipedia
Summary: The earliest bishop we can name explicitly claiming the power to reconcile the "capital" sins. Callistus is the churchman on the other side of the edict that Tertullian, turned Montanist, mocks in On Modesty — an edict remitting adultery and fornication to penitents. He left no treatise of his own; his position survives only through two hostile witnesses, Tertullian and his Roman rival Hippolytus, who attacks the same laxity at length in the Refutation of All Heresies. He is the hinge on which the third-century timeline turns: the bishop actually exercising the power that Tertullian denies and Novatian would later make the ground of a schism.

Claims & evidence

An edict remitting adultery and fornication

Tertullian, now a Montanist rigorist, quotes and derides an episcopal edict pardoning sexual sin — styling its author, with heavy sarcasm, "the bishop of bishops":

I hear that there has even been an edict set forth, and a peremptory one too. The Pontifex Maximus—that is, the bishop of bishops—issues an edict: "I remit, to such as have discharged (the requirements of) repentance, the sins both of adultery and of fornication."

— Tertullian, On Modesty, ch. I, source.1 Whether the edict is Callistus's is a disputed point (see the note below), but the reading that identifies it with him is the majority one and fits the independent testimony of Hippolytus.

"All had their sins forgiven by himself" — the Callistian laxity

Hippolytus, Callistus's rival at Rome, gives an independent and equally hostile account of the same policy in his long chapter on Callistus's career and "school." He charges Callistus with being the first to reconcile the sexually sinful:

And he first invented the device of conniving with men in regard of their indulgence in sensual pleasures, saying that all had their sins forgiven by himself. For he who is in the habit of attending the congregation of any one else, and is called a Christian, should he commit any transgression; the sin, they say, is not reckoned unto him, provided only he hurries off and attaches himself to the school of Callistus.

— Hippolytus, Refutation of All Heresies, Book IX, ch. VII, source.2 Hippolytus's report is polemical — he writes as a rival claimant to the Roman see — but it independently confirms Tertullian: a Roman bishop was in fact reconciling sins the rigorists held irremissible.

A bishop who could not be deposed — even for a sin unto death

Hippolytus adds a second charge that widened the same laxity to the clergy: Callistus held that a sinning bishop kept his office, and admitted the twice- and thrice-married to holy orders:

This one propounded the opinion, that, if a bishop was guilty of any sin, if even a sin unto death, he ought not to be deposed.

— Hippolytus, Refutation of All Heresies, Book IX, ch. VII, source.3

A church of wheat and tares, clean and unclean — the ecclesiological ground

According to Hippolytus, Callistus defended his leniency with the parables and figures of a mixed church — the field of wheat and tares, and Noah's ark carrying clean and unclean alike — a church that holds sinners within it rather than expelling them:

But also he affirmed that the ark of Noe was made for a symbol of the Church, in which were both dogs, and wolves, and ravens, and all things clean and unclean; and so he alleges that the case should stand in like manner with the Church.

— Hippolytus, Refutation of All Heresies, Book IX, ch. VII, source.4 He read the parable of the tares (Matthew 13:30) the same way — "let the tares grow along with the wheat," that is, let those guilty of sin remain in the church.5

Notes

  • Whose edict? Tertullian's "bishop of bishops" in On Modesty is not named. Most scholars identify him with Callistus of Rome, whose reported reconciliation of sexual sin Hippolytus independently attacks; a minority (following G. Esser and others) argue for Agrippinus of Carthage, since Tertullian was a North African writing chiefly about African discipline. The wiki follows the majority reading: the parallel with Hippolytus's firsthand Roman testimony is close and specific, and Callistus's dates (bishop c. 217–222) fit the composition of On Modesty. Either way, the edict shows a bishop already claiming, in the early third century, the authority to reconcile even grave sinners — which is the point the timeline needs.
  • Known through hostile witnesses. Like Novatian at the opposite pole, Callistus wrote no surviving treatise on penance; his doctrine reaches us only through opponents — Tertullian, who mocks the edict, and Hippolytus, who was his rival for the Roman see and whose Refutation is a work of open hostility. The reader should weigh the sources accordingly: the two are independent of each other and agree on the substance (a Roman bishop reconciling the sexually sinful), but every characterization of Callistus's motives is an enemy's.
  • The hinge of the crisis. Callistus is the churchman actually exercising the power that Tertullian the Montanist denied and that Novatian would later reject as schism-founding. His parables of the mixed church — wheat and tares, the ark of clean and unclean — are the same arguments the mainstream church would make for the rest of the century, culminating in Cyprian's mediating discipline and, against the rigorists, the reconciliation reaffirmed at Nicaea.
