Callistus of Rome on Penance

Dates: c. 217–222 (bishop of Rome) · Wikipedia
Summary: Callistus, bishop of Rome, is identified by Harnack and by Hanna as the author of the edict that Tertullian, turned Montanist, mocks in On Modesty — an edict remitting adultery and fornication to penitents — while F. X. Murphy assigns that edict to Agrippinus of Carthage. His Roman rival Hippolytus attacks his leniency toward sexual sin at length in the Refutation of All Heresies. Callistus left no treatise of his own; his position survives only through these two opponents.

Claims & evidence

An edict remitting adultery and fornication

Tertullian, now a Montanist rigorist, quotes and derides an episcopal edict pardoning sexual sin — styling its author, with heavy sarcasm, "the bishop of bishops":

I hear that there has even been an edict set forth, and a peremptory one too. The Pontifex Maximus—that is, the bishop of bishops—issues an edict: "I remit, to such as have discharged (the requirements of) repentance, the sins both of adultery and of fornication."

— Tertullian, On Modesty, ch. I, source.1 Whether the edict is Callistus's is disputed (see the note below).

"All had their sins forgiven by himself" — Hippolytus's charge

Hippolytus, Callistus's rival at Rome, gives a hostile account of his policy in his long chapter on Callistus's career and "school." He charges that Callistus "first invented the device" of reconciling the sexually sinful:

And he first invented the device of conniving with men in regard of their indulgence in sensual pleasures, saying that all had their sins forgiven by himself. For he who is in the habit of attending the congregation of any one else, and is called a Christian, should he commit any transgression; the sin, they say, is not reckoned unto him, provided only he hurries off and attaches himself to the school of Callistus.

— Hippolytus, Refutation of All Heresies, Book IX, ch. VII, source.2 Hippolytus writes as a rival claimant to the Roman see. Harnack reads his account together with Tertullian's as evidence that Callistus "granted readmission to those who had fallen into sins of the flesh."6

Hippolytus's second charge: a bishop not to be deposed, even for a sin unto death

Hippolytus adds a second charge, concerning the clergy: Callistus held that a sinning bishop kept his office, and admitted the twice- and thrice-married to holy orders:

This one propounded the opinion, that, if a bishop was guilty of any sin, if even a sin unto death, he ought not to be deposed.

— Hippolytus, Refutation of All Heresies, Book IX, ch. VII, source.3

A church of wheat and tares, clean and unclean

According to Hippolytus, Callistus defended his leniency with the parables and figures of a mixed church — the field of wheat and tares, and Noah's ark carrying clean and unclean alike — a church that holds sinners within it rather than expelling them:

But also he affirmed that the ark of Noe was made for a symbol of the Church, in which were both dogs, and wolves, and ravens, and all things clean and unclean; and so he alleges that the case should stand in like manner with the Church.

— Hippolytus, Refutation of All Heresies, Book IX, ch. VII, source.4 He read the parable of the tares (Matthew 13:30) the same way — "let the tares grow along with the wheat," that is, let those guilty of sin remain in the church.5

Notes

  • Whose edict? Tertullian's "bishop of bishops" in On Modesty is not named. Harnack writes of "the peremptory edict of bishop Calixtus," and Hanna that "Pope Callistus (218-22) published his 'peremptory edict'"; on this identification Hippolytus's Roman charges run parallel, and Callistus's dates (bishop c. 217–222) fit the composition of On Modesty. F. X. Murphy holds that "the supreme pontiff to whom Tertullian referred was probably Agrippinus, Bishop of Carthage (218 – 22), and not the bishop of Rome," and others (G. Esser among them ⚠ unverified) have argued the same, since Tertullian was a North African writing chiefly about African discipline. Bryant treats Agrippinus and Callistus as separately issuing such rulings, and Rondet holds that the question of irremissible sins is independent of the identification.7
  • Known through hostile witnesses. Like Novatian, Callistus wrote no surviving treatise on penance; his doctrine reaches us only through opponents — Tertullian, who mocks the edict, and Hippolytus, who was his rival for the Roman see. Every characterization of Callistus's motives in the surviving record comes from one of the two.
  • Reception and debate. Harnack calls Callistus's mixed-church argument "quite a logical proceeding" within what he describes as the church's change from a communion of saints into an institution of salvation; whether the edict changed an older rule or regulated the use of a power already held is set out on Did the early church hold that the gravest sins lay outside its power to reconcile? For the later course of the question see Cyprian, Novatian, and Nicaea.6
