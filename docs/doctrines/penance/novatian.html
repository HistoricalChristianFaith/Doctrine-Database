Novatian on Penance

Dates: c. 251 · Wikipedia
Summary: The rigorist pole of the third-century crisis. The Roman presbyter Novatian, having failed to be elected bishop against Cornelius, led a schism holding that the church cannot reconcile those who denied Christ under persecution — the lapsed have no return, however deep their penance. His followers called themselves the Cathari, "the pure." He wrote no surviving treatise on penance; his doctrine is preserved by his contemporary opponents.

Claims & evidence

The lapsed have no hope of reconciliation — even after true repentance

Eusebius, quoting the synodical account, states Novatian's position and the schism it founded:

After this, Novatus, a presbyter of the church at Rome, being lifted up with arrogance against these persons, as if there was no longer for them a hope of salvation, not even if they should do all things pertaining to a genuine and pure conversion, became leader of the heresy of those who, in the pride of their imagination, call themselves Cathari.

— Eusebius, Church History VI.43 (trans. A. C. McGiffert, Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, 2nd ser., vol. I), source.1 Eusebius (following Cornelius's Greek) calls him "Novatus," conflating the Roman Novatian with Novatus of Carthage; the figure and the doctrine are Novatian's.2

The church's answer: heal the fallen with "the medicines of repentance"

Eusebius records that a synod of sixty bishops at Rome condemned the rigorist line and reaffirmed that the lapsed were to be received back:

those who adopted his brother-hating and inhuman opinion, should be considered by the church as strangers; but that they should heal such of the brethren as had fallen into misfortune, and should minister to them with the medicines of repentance.

— Eusebius, Church History VI.43, source.3

He had once held the opposite

Cyprian's letter to Antonianus records that Novatian, before his schism, had himself drafted and read aloud the Roman clergy's ruling that peace should be granted to the dying lapsed:

It was added also—Novatian then writing, and reciting with his own voice what he had written…—that peace ought to be granted to the lapsed who were sick and at the point of departure.

— Cyprian, Epistle LI (Oxford Ep. 55, to Antonianus), §5, source.4

Notes

  • Rigorism as schism. Where Tertullian had denied the church power over "capital" sins from within his Montanism, Novatian made rigorism the ground of a rival church with its own bishop. The mainstream church rejected both, and the Novatianist churches persisted for centuries before dying out.
  • Novatian on both sides of himself. Like Tertullian, Novatian is a witness against his earlier self: the very policy he once wrote up for the Roman clergy — peace for the dying penitent — is the one his schism came to deny.
  • No firsthand treatise. Novatian's surviving works (On the Trinity, On the Jewish Meats) do not treat penance; his position is known only through his contemporaries Cyprian and Cornelius (via Eusebius). Those are the earliest and best witnesses, but the reader should note the doctrine reaches us through its critics.
