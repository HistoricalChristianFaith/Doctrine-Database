Novatian on Penance

Dates: c. 251 · Wikipedia
Summary: The Roman presbyter Novatian was set up as a rival bishop of Rome to Cornelius and led a separate church. In his letter to the churches, as Socrates reports it, he asked that those who had sacrificed in the persecution not be admitted to the mysteries but be exhorted to repentance, the pardon of their offence left to God; Eusebius, quoting the synodical account of his opponents, reports him as treating the lapsed as having no hope of salvation however genuine their conversion. His followers called themselves the Cathari, "the pure." He wrote no surviving treatise on penance; a letter on the lapsed that he drafted for the Roman clergy before 251 survives among Cyprian's letters, and his later position is reported by his contemporary opponents and by the historian Socrates.

Claims & evidence

Novatian's position, as Eusebius reports it

Eusebius, quoting the synodical account, states Novatian's position and the separate church it founded:

After this, Novatus, a presbyter of the church at Rome, being lifted up with arrogance against these persons, as if there was no longer for them a hope of salvation, not even if they should do all things pertaining to a genuine and pure conversion, became leader of the heresy of those who, in the pride of their imagination, call themselves Cathari.

— Eusebius, Church History VI.43 (trans. A. C. McGiffert, Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, 2nd ser., vol. I), source.1 Eusebius (following Cornelius's Greek) calls him "Novatus," conflating the Roman Novatian with Novatus of Carthage; the figure and the doctrine are Novatian's.2

Novatian's letter to the churches, as Socrates reports it

Socrates, writing c. 439, reports the letter Novatian sent to the churches after his consecration as bishop:

Having seceded on this account, on being afterwards elevated to the episcopacy by such bishops as entertained similar sentiments, he wrote to all the churches that 'they should not admit to the sacred mysteries those who had sacrificed; but exhorting them to repentance, leave the pardoning of their offense to God, who has the power to forgive all sin.'

— Socrates Scholasticus, Ecclesiastical History IV.28 (trans. A. C. Zenos, Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, 2nd ser., vol. II), source.3 Socrates, who calls him "Novatus," adds that the rule was that they "should not receive to the sacraments those who after baptism had committed any deadly sin."

The Roman synod of 251: heal the fallen with "the medicines of repentance"

Eusebius records that a synod of sixty bishops at Rome condemned Novatian's position and ruled that the lapsed were to be received back:

those who adopted his brother-hating and inhuman opinion, should be considered by the church as strangers; but that they should heal such of the brethren as had fallen into misfortune, and should minister to them with the medicines of repentance.

— Eusebius, Church History VI.43, source.4

His earlier signature on the Roman clergy's ruling

Cyprian's letter to Antonianus records that Novatian, before he was set up as a rival bishop, had himself drafted and read aloud the Roman clergy's ruling that peace should be granted to the dying lapsed:

It was added also—Novatian then writing, and reciting with his own voice what he had written…—that peace ought to be granted to the lapsed who were sick and at the point of departure.

— Cyprian, Epistle LI (Oxford Ep. 55, to Antonianus), §5, source.5

The words Cyprian quotes from the Roman clergy's reply ("the peace of the Church must first be maintained") appear in the letter of the Roman clergy to Cyprian that survives as Epistle XXX (Oxford Ep. 30), and John Chapman (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1911) writes that Novatian "certainly wrote letters in the name of the Roman clergy, which were sent by them to St. Cyprian (Epp. xxx and xxxvi)."6 That letter treats the lapsed at length; on those near death it rules:

but of such as impending death does not suffer to bear the delay, having repented and professed a detestation of their deeds with frequency; if with tears, if with groans, if with weeping they have betrayed the signs of a grieving and truly penitent spirit, when there remains, as far as man can tell, no hope of living; to them, finally, such cautious and careful help should be ministered, God Himself knowing what He will do with such, and in what way He will examine the balance of His judgment

— The Roman clergy to Cyprian, Epistle XXX (Oxford Ep. 30), §8 (Ante-Nicene Fathers, vol. V), source.7

Notes

  • A separate church. Where Tertullian had denied the bishops power over "capital" sins from within his Montanism, Novatian's party formed a church with its own bishop. Novatianist churches were still active in Spain c. 380, when Pacian answered one of their members; Chapman (1911) writes that "The work of Eulogius shows that there were still Novatians in Alexandria about 600."6 Harnack (1894) describes the party as "demanding adherence to the old practice with regard to lapsed members"; whether there was such an older practice is set out on Did the early church hold that the gravest sins lay outside its power to reconcile?8
  • The Roman clergy's ruling and his later letter. Cyprian (Ep. 55.5) sets Novatian's own drafting of the Roman clergy's ruling — peace for the dying penitent — before Antonianus, who had been swayed by Novatian's later letters. Both texts are quoted above: the ruling of Ep. 30 and the letter Socrates reports.
  • No firsthand treatise. Novatian's surviving treatises (On the Trinity, On the Jewish Meats) do not treat penance. The letter he drafted for the Roman clergy before 251 (Cyprian, Ep. 30) survives; his position after 251 is reported by his contemporaries Cyprian and Cornelius (via Eusebius), who opposed him, and later by Socrates (c. 439), who quotes his letter to the churches.
