The Fourth Lateran Council on Penance

Dates: 1215 (convoked by Innocent III; counted in the Catholic Church as the twelfth ecumenical council) · Wikipedia
Summary: Lateran IV's Canon 21, Omnis utriusque sexus, requires every Christian who has reached the age of discretion to confess all their sins to their own priest at least once a year and to receive the Eucharist at Easter — and in the same canon binds the priest to secrecy, the seal of confession. For the private, repeatable confession of the earlier Irish penitentials, see their page.

Claims & evidence

Annual confession made obligatory for all

The decree makes yearly confession a binding precept, enforced by exclusion from the church and denial of Christian burial:

All the faithful of both sexes shall after they have reached the age of discretion faithfully confess all their sins at least once a year to their own (parish) priest and perform to the best of their ability the penance imposed, receiving reverently at least at Easter the sacrament of the Eucharist… otherwise they shall be cut off from the Church (excommunicated) during life and deprived of Christian burial in death.

— Fourth Lateran Council (1215), Canon 21 (Omnis utriusque sexus), trans. H. J. Schroeder, Fordham Medieval Sourcebook.1

The priest as discreet physician

The same canon instructs the confessor to inquire into the circumstances of sinner and sin and prescribe accordingly, using the physician-of-souls image also found in the penitentials:

Let the priest be discreet and cautious that he may pour wine and oil into the wounds of the one injured after the manner of a skilful physician, carefully inquiring into the circumstances of the sinner and the sin, from the nature of which he may understand what kind of advice to give and what remedy to apply.

— Fourth Lateran Council (1215), Canon 21, Fordham Medieval Sourcebook.2

The seal of confession

And it lays down, with grave penalties, the confessor's inviolable secrecy — the earliest general conciliar enactment of the seal ⚠ unverified:

let him exercise the greatest precaution that he does not in any degree by word, sign, or any other manner make known the sinner… He who dares to reveal a sin confided to him in the tribunal of penance, we decree that he be not only deposed from the sacerdotal office but also relegated to a monastery of strict observance to do penance for the remainder of his life.

— Fourth Lateran Council (1215), Canon 21, Fordham Medieval Sourcebook.3

Notes

  • "The tribunal of penance." The decree speaks of confession as a court (in poenitentiae iudicio) and the priest as judge — a juridical language the Council of Trent also uses in 1551, when it calls absolution "after the manner of a judicial act."
  • The scholastic anatomy. Around Lateran IV the schoolmen were fixing the parts of penance — contrition of heart, confession of mouth, satisfaction in deed — and debating whether sorrow from fear (attrition) suffices or sorrow from love (contrition) is required. Trent's Session 14 (1551) names contrition, confession, and satisfaction as the parts of penance and treats attrition in its ch. 4.
  • Trent's own backward glance. Trent (Session 14, ch. 5, and Canon 8) grounds the once-a-year obligation in "the constitution of the great Council of Lateran."
