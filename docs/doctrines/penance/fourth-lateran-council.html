The Fourth Lateran Council on Penance

Dates: 1215 (the Twelfth Ecumenical Council, convoked by Innocent III) · Wikipedia
Summary: By 1215 the private, repeatable confession of the Irish penitentials had become universal Western practice. Lateran IV made it universal law: Canon 21, Omnis utriusque sexus, requires every Christian who has reached the age of discretion to confess all their sins to their own priest at least once a year and to receive the Eucharist at Easter — and in the same breath binds the priest to absolute secrecy, the seal of confession.

Claims & evidence

Annual confession made obligatory for all

The decree turns yearly confession from custom into a binding precept, enforced by exclusion from the church and denial of Christian burial:

All the faithful of both sexes shall after they have reached the age of discretion faithfully confess all their sins at least once a year to their own (parish) priest and perform to the best of their ability the penance imposed, receiving reverently at least at Easter the sacrament of the Eucharist… otherwise they shall be cut off from the Church (excommunicated) during life and deprived of Christian burial in death.

— Fourth Lateran Council (1215), Canon 21 (Omnis utriusque sexus), trans. H. J. Schroeder, Fordham Medieval Sourcebook.1

The priest as discreet physician

The same canon instructs the confessor to weigh the circumstances of sinner and sin and prescribe accordingly — the physician-of-souls image inherited from the penitentials, now in conciliar law:

Let the priest be discreet and cautious that he may pour wine and oil into the wounds of the one injured after the manner of a skilful physician, carefully inquiring into the circumstances of the sinner and the sin, from the nature of which he may understand what kind of advice to give and what remedy to apply.

— Fourth Lateran Council (1215), Canon 21, Fordham Medieval Sourcebook.2

The seal of confession

And it lays down, with grave penalties, the confessor's inviolable secrecy — the earliest general conciliar enactment of the seal:

let him exercise the greatest precaution that he does not in any degree by word, sign, or any other manner make known the sinner… He who dares to reveal a sin confided to him in the tribunal of penance, we decree that he be not only deposed from the sacerdotal office but also relegated to a monastery of strict observance to do penance for the remainder of his life.

— Fourth Lateran Council (1215), Canon 21, Fordham Medieval Sourcebook.3

Notes

  • "The tribunal of penance." The decree already speaks of confession as a court (in poenitentiae iudicio) and the priest as judge — the juridical conception that the Council of Trent would define dogmatically three centuries later against the Reformers.
  • The scholastic anatomy. Around Lateran IV the schoolmen were fixing the parts of penance — contrition of heart, confession of mouth, satisfaction in deed — and debating whether mere fear-driven sorrow (attrition) suffices or full love-driven sorrow (contrition) is required. That framework, and the theology of the "keys" and priestly absolution, is what Trent later canonized.
  • Trent's own backward glance. Trent (Session 14, ch. 5, and Canon 8) explicitly grounds the once-a-year obligation in "the constitution of the great Council of Lateran," making these two the twin legal anchors of Western confession.
