Is heartfelt contrition (love of God) required for the sacrament to forgive? — the contritionist reading

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: plausible — the interpretation weighed here is that some act of the love of God — at least an incipient charity — is required for the penitent to be reconciled, so that sorrow arising from nothing but servile fear does not justify even with sacramental absolution: the attrite must, in the sacrament, become contrite (ex attrito fit contritus). It is coherent and genuinely grounded — justification is by charity (Trent, Session 6), and Aquinas taught that the sacrament converts imperfect sorrow into the perfect sorrow that alone remits guilt — and Rome expressly left it standing beside the rival in 1667. But it is short of majority: the attritionist reading was “more common among the scholastics,” and Trent’s Canon 5 cuts against the harder (Jansenist) form that would deny fear-born sorrow any value at all: coherent, not ruled out, short of majority support (plausible) · advanced by Thomas Aquinas and the Thomist school (in its measured form), and by the Jansenists (in its strong form)

Related arguments: attrition and contrition — see also Does attrition (fear-born sorrow) suffice with the sacrament?

The claim

Attrition and contrition do not denote a habit, but an act only… until grace be infused, there is no habit by which afterwards the act of contrition may be elicited; so that attrition can nowise become contrition… [yet] the origin of contrition [is] the process of thought, when a man thinks of his sin and is sorry for it… with that [sorrow] of attrition. The term of contrition is when that sorrow is already quickened by grace.

— Thomas Aquinas, Summa Theologiae, Supplement, q. 1, a. 3, and q. 2, a. 1, link.1 The reading this states is that mere attrition — sorrow “quickened” by no charity — is not itself the disposition that reconciles: it is only the origin of contrition, and forgiveness comes at the term, when grace has turned that fear-born sorrow into a sorrow informed by love of God. On the strong (Jansenist) form the point is pressed further: fear that has not yet become love is no true conversion at all, so absolution over a penitent who is merely attrite does not remit his sin.

Who advances it

The moderate contritionist line is the older, Thomist one. Aquinas and the thirteenth-century schools held that only contrition — sorrow formed by charity — is the proper disposition for justification; where a penitent comes attrite only, the sacrament must effect the change, ex attrito fit contritus (“from being attrite he becomes contrite”), before guilt is remitted.2 It was this consensus that Duns Scotus broke by making attrition as such a sufficient disposition. The dispute hardened into two named schools — the contritionists, requiring a motive of love beyond the motive of fear, and the attritionists, who did not — and boiled over in the seventeenth century, sharpest in Belgium, where the Jansenists pushed the strongest form: the Jansenist Pasquier Quesnel taught that “fear merely restrains the hand, while the heart is attached to sin so long as one is not motivated by love of justice.”3 A decree of the Holy Office under Alexander VII (5–6 May 1667) then forbade either party to censure the other “until something shall have been defined in this matter by the Holy See” — leaving the contritionist reading a permitted, if minority, opinion.4

The evidence for

The steelman rests on the logic of justification itself. First, justification is by charity. Trent’s decree on justification (Session 6) makes the infusion of faith, hope, and charity the form of the grace that justifies; if a man is not justified without charity, then a “sorrow” that contains no love of God looks like a disposition to something short of justification — which is exactly why Aquinas locates forgiveness at the term, when attrition has been “quickened by grace,” not at its fear-born origin.1 Second, the moral realism. Sorrow motivated by nothing but dread of hell can coexist with a will still bent on sin, held back only by fear of the penalty — the very state the contritionists (and, at the extreme, the Jansenists) say is not repentance: one may regret the consequence of sin while loving the sin. A disposition that turns the heart, however feebly, toward God is the honest floor for calling a change of heart real.3 Third, even Trent’s attrition is ordered to love. Session 14 blesses attrition precisely as an “impulse of the Holy Ghost” that “prepares a way… unto justice” and disposes toward grace — language of movement toward God, which a reading that requires at least the beginnings of love fits naturally, and which pure servile fear fits awkwardly.5

Adversarial assessment

What holds. In its measured Thomist form the reading is coherent and theologically weighty: it keeps the sacrament of penance in step with the doctrine of justification, refuses to call fear-restrained sin a change of heart, and has the authority of Aquinas and a whole school behind it. Rome’s refusal in 1667 to condemn it means it remains a fully permitted Catholic opinion.

What is overstated or imprecise. The strong (Jansenist) form — that attrition without perfect charity is worthless, that fear-born sorrow leaves a man a hypocrite — runs straight into Trent’s Canon 5, which anathematizes exactly the claim that this sorrow “is not a true and profitable sorrow… but… makes a man a hypocrite, yea even more a sinner.”6 So the maximal contritionism that Quesnel and the Jansenists pressed is not merely a minority view but one Trent had already ruled out; the defensible version is the softer one that asks for the beginnings of love, not for perfect charity. And even there the reading is short of majority: Alexander VII’s own decree records that the rival, attritionist opinion was “the more common among the scholastics,” and the letter of Trent’s Session 14 (attrition “disposes… to obtain the grace of God in the sacrament of penance”) reads more easily as blessing attrition as a sufficient proximate disposition than as demanding its conversion into charity first.5 The two readings divide at the joint Rome refused to close: is the “imperfect” sorrow Trent blesses already the seed of love, or not?

Assessment

Plausible. Rate the interpretation, not the polemic. In its measured form — that reconciliation requires at least an incipient love of God, so that the sacrament must move the merely attrite penitent toward contrition rather than reward fear alone — the reading is coherent, grounded in the necessity of charity for justification and in Aquinas’s ex attrito fit contritus, and permitted by Rome; it is not ruled out. But it is short of probable: the rival attritionist reading was the more common scholastic view and sits closer to the letter of Trent’s Session 14, and the harder Jansenist form of contritionism collides with Canon 5. Coherent and live, but the minority side of a question the Church deliberately left open (plausible). Where a proponent presses the strong form — that fear-born sorrow is worthless or hypocritical — the overreach is the very position Trent anathematized, and is flagged as such on the council’s page.
