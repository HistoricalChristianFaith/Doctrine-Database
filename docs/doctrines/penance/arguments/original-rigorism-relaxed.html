Did the early church deny it could reconcile the gravest sins? — an original rigorism, later relaxed

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — the trajectory of relaxation (once-only → repeatable, deathbed-only → in life, disputed → routine) is near-consensus and sound; but the strong form — that the primitive church uniformly denied it held the power at all, only later inventing it — is weak against the earliest evidence · advanced by Tertullian (as a Montanist)

The claim

We agree that the causes of repentance are sins. These we divide into two issues: some will be remissible, some irremissible… Every sin is dischargeable either by pardon or else by penalty: by pardon as the result of chastisement, by penalty as the result of condemnation.

— Tertullian, On Modesty (De Pudicitia), ch. II, link.1 The historical thesis this states is that the church originally held a rigorist discipline — the gravest sins (the three traditionally reckoned "capital": apostasy/idolatry, murder, and adultery/fornication) were irremissible, permanently excluding the sinner this side of death — and that the church's later claim to reconcile even these was a novelty, a laxity crept in against the ancient rule, not a power the church had always possessed.

Who advances it

In antiquity the case is made by the rigorists themselves, who argued precisely that the catholic practice of forgiving capital sins was an innovation. The Montanist Tertullian mocks the bishop's edict remitting adultery as the act of a "Pontifex Maximus… bishop of bishops" overstepping the church's real authority;2 a generation later Novatian makes the permanent exclusion of the lapsed the founding principle of a whole schism (the Cathari, "the pure"). Modern historiography took up the same reading: Adolf von Harnack, in his History of Dogma, treated the edict of Callistus and the Cyprianic settlement as the watershed at which "the old practice" of a holy communion that expelled its capital sinners was abolished, the church becoming instead a corpus permixtum and an institution of salvation that keeps the sinner within it.3 Against this the Catholic monographs of A. d'Alès (L'Édit de Calliste, 1914) and Bernhard Poschmann (Paenitentia secunda, 1940) argued the contrary thesis — that the power of the keys to reconcile even grave sinners was present from the start, the severity being one of discipline, not of denied authority.4

The evidence for

The steelman is strong on two fronts. First, the discipline demonstrably tightens as you go back. The earliest datable ruling on post-baptismal sin, the Shepherd of Hermas (c. 150), reports that "some teachers" held baptism to be the only repentance there is — and that this was "sound doctrine"; Hermas softens it only to a single, unrepeatable second chance: "he has opportunity to repent but once."5 That the concession had to be argued for against a stricter default is itself evidence of an original rigor. The warnings of Hebrews 6:4–6 and 10:26 — that it is "impossible… to renew them again unto repentance" — gave that rigor a scriptural spine. Second, the loosening is a real and traceable curve. When a bishop (most often identified as Callistus of Rome, c. 217) issued an edict forgiving "the sins both of adultery and of fornication," it provoked open revolt from Tertullian and from Hippolytus, who complained that under Callistus "all had their sins forgiven by himself" — the reaction of men who believed an ancient rule was being broken.2 The trajectory from there runs one direction only: Cyprian mediates peace for the lapsed after the Decian persecution; Nicaea (325) guarantees the dying penitent the "last and most indispensable Viaticum";6 and the Irish penitentials at last make the once-only public discipline private and repeatable. Rigor at the fountainhead, leniency downstream: the pattern is exactly what the thesis predicts.

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The direction of travel is not in dispute and is close to a scholarly consensus: across the second to seventh centuries penance moved from severe toward mild — from once-only to repeatable, from a discipline that in places postponed the gravest sinners' reconciliation to the deathbed toward one that granted it in life, and from a genuinely contested question ("can adultery, apostasy, or murder be forgiven at all?") toward a settled affirmative. That a real rigorist strand existed, appealed to an older practice, and lost, is well attested by the very existence of the Montanist and Novatianist protests.

What is overstated or imprecise. The strong form of the thesis — that the primitive church as a whole denied it possessed the power to reconcile capital sinners, so that the later power was a sheer invention — does not survive the earliest evidence. (1) Hermas's "second repentance" is itself the reconciliation of grave, post-baptismal sin; the door it opens is the very thing the strong thesis says did not yet exist. (2) Well before Callistus, Dionysius of Corinth (c. 170) is reported instructing the churches of Pontus "to receive those who come back again after any fall, whether it be delinquency or heresy" — reconciliation extended even to apostasy and heresy.7 (3) The rigorists appear on the record as schismatics breaking away from catholic practice (Tertullian into Montanism, Novatian into his own church), not as the guardians of a retained original discipline; their opponents, a Roman synod of sixty bishops, condemned Novatian's position and reaffirmed healing the fallen "with the medicines of repentance."8 The honest reading of Callistus's edict, then, is contested at exactly this joint: was it the abolition of an older rule (Harnack) or the authoritative settlement of a question the church had always in principle answered by its power of the keys (d'Alès, Poschmann)? A further imprecision dogs the popular version: the "three capital sins" were never uniformly treated. Idolatry/apostasy and adultery drew the fiercest rigorism; the discipline for other grave sins varied by region and decade, so "the church excluded the three capital sins" flattens a genuinely patchy record into a single rule that no one century actually kept.

Assessment

Contested. Rate the interpretation, not the polemic. In its moderate form — that the early discipline was markedly more severe, that reconciliation of the gravest sins was in places doubted, delayed, or refused, and that the church's practice relaxed decisively over the following centuries — the reading is well-grounded and near the historical consensus. In its strong form — that the church uniformly held it had no power to reconcile capital sinners, the later power being a novelty with no earlier root — it is weak: Hermas already grants the power, Dionysius of Corinth already exercises it toward apostates, and the rigorists surface as schismatic revolts against catholic practice rather than as its original custodians. The live scholarly dispute (Harnack's "abolition of the old practice" vs. the "always held the keys" reading of d'Alès and Poschmann) turns on how to weigh Callistus's edict — abolition or settlement — and no side has won cleanly. Strong by the historian's eye for a tightening-then-loosening curve, weak where it hardens that curve into a claimed denial of the church's authority at the source: genuinely disputed, no clear winner (contested). Where a proponent presses the strong form — Tertullian arguing the graver sins must be "retained inconcessible" — the overreach is flagged on his page as a Montanist rigorism that the catholic church of his own day already rejected.
