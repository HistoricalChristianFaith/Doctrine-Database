Did the early church hold that the gravest sins lay outside its power to reconcile?

Disputed reading · on Penance & Confession · witness: Tertullian

The passage

We agree that the causes of repentance are sins. These we divide into two issues: some will be remissible, some irremissible… Every sin is dischargeable either by pardon or else by penalty: by pardon as the result of chastisement, by penalty as the result of condemnation.

Hence it is that there is no restoration of peace granted by the Churches to "idolatry" or to "blood." From which final decision of theirs that the apostles should have departed, is (I think) not lawful to believe; or else, if some find it possible to believe so, they will be bound to prove it.

— Tertullian, On Modesty (De Pudicitia), chs. II and XII, source.1

The question

Tertullian wrote these lines as a Montanist, c. 217, against a bishop's edict remitting "the sins both of adultery and of fornication" to penitents; Hippolytus attacked the same leniency in Callistus of Rome.2 What is disputed is whether the church of the second century held that apostasy, murder and adultery lay outside its power to reconcile in this life, so that their reconciliation in the third century changed an older rule, or held that power from the start while restricting its use; the answer bears on how the edict, the Novatianist separation and the later canonical penance relate to what came before them.

An older rule of exclusion, relaxed in the third century

In brief: until about 220 the church did not readmit in this life those who fell into idolatry, adultery or murder after baptism; the edict of c. 217 and the Decian persecution relaxed that rule, first for sexual sins and then for apostasy.

Held by: Tertullian (Montanist, On Modesty, c. 217);1 Adolf von Harnack (Protestant church historian, History of Dogma, 3rd German ed. 1894);3 Oscar D. Watkins (Anglican, A History of Penance, 1920);4 Joseph M. Bryant (historical sociologist, Athens Journal of History, 2023).5,6

Tertullian rests the rule on the apostles. The decree of Acts 15 set adultery and fornication "between idolatry and murder" among the crimes the apostles put forward "as not remissible," and the author of Hebrews, "who learnt this from apostles," "never knew of any 'second repentance' promised by apostles to the adulterer and fornicator." For Tertullian the edict of the "Pontifex Maximus" broke with that apostolic rule.1 Harnack writes that down to the early third century "final exclusion from the Church was still the penalty of relapse into idolatry, adultery, whoredom, and murder; though at the same time the forgiveness of God in the next world was reserved for the fallen provided they remained penitent to the end." For him "the rule was first infringed by the peremptory edict of bishop Calixtus," and "up to the year 250 no concessions were allowed in the case of relapse into idolatry."3

Watkins reads the sources the same way: "The rigorism of the latter half of the second century appears to be one of the established facts of history"; the church receded from it "by gradual steps involving much difference and struggle" (impurity under Callistus, apostasy after Decius, homicide later, all sins by Nicaea).4 For Bryant, fornication and adultery "had long ranked alongside apostasy and murder in the peccata mortalia category," until "Agrippinus and Callistus now ruled that sacerdotal absolution could be extended to those carnal offenses."5 On this reading the protests of Tertullian, of Hippolytus (who wrote that under Callistus "all had their sins forgiven by himself"), and of Novatian were defences of a received rule; that is why, in Watkins's words, Callistus's step "can nevertheless be represented by Tertullian as altogether revolutionary," and why the change came by stages, one class of sin at a time.2,4

A discipline in the use of a power the church always held

In brief: the church held from the start a power received from Christ to reconcile grave sinners; where bishops refused peace for certain sins, that was a restriction in the use of the power, varying from church to church, not a denial of it.

Held by: Cyprian (bishop of Carthage, Ep. 55, 252);7 Edward Hanna (Catholic, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1911);8 Archimandrite Job (Getcha) (Orthodox, 2004).9,10

Hermas (c. 150), the earliest extant text on the question, qualifies the teaching that baptism is the only repentance with a further repentance the Lord "has set" for those already called; Hanna reads him as taking "a resolute stand" against that school, his "one repentance" referring to public penance, so that "they imply no limitation on the sacramental power itself."11,8 In the same Mandate Hermas rules that a husband must take back a wife who has committed adultery and repents: "If the husband do not take her back, he sins, and brings a great sin upon himself; for he ought to take back the sinner who has repented. But not frequently. For there is but one repentance to the servants of God."11 About 170, Dionysius of Corinth told the churches of Pontus "to receive those who come back again after any fall, whether it be delinquency or heresy," which Hanna takes as teaching "that no sin is so great as to be excluded from the exercise of that power."12 For Hanna, Tertullian, in reporting his opponents' claim that "the Church has the power of forgiving sins," "bears witness to the existence of that power in the Church which he had abandoned," the early severity being "done for disciplinary reasons, not because the Church lacked power to forgive."1,8

Cyprian reports that some of his predecessors "wholly closed the gate of repentance against adultery," yet did not break communion with bishops who granted peace: "every bishop disposes and directs his own acts."7 In 251 a Roman synod of sixty bishops ruled that the fallen be healed "with the medicines of repentance."13 Getcha describes the classical discipline as exclusion, a period of penance and reconciliation, in which, he writes, "The power received by Church from Christ to bind and loose (cf. Mt 16:19) was interpreted precisely as the power to excommunicate and to reconcile," and the penance "was not regarded as a punishment but as a therapy."9 The fourth-century answers to the Novatianists, Pacian's and Ambrose's, rest the power on Christ's grant itself; on this reading the severity of any period governed how the power was used, not whether the church held it.10
