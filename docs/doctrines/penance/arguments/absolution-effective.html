Does priestly absolution effect forgiveness? — the judicial (instrumental) reading

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — the interpretation weighed here is that the minister’s absolution does not merely announce a forgiveness already given but is the appointed instrument through which God forgives, so that Christ ratifies the priest’s sentence over a particular penitent. On the historical evidence this is strong — the near-universal reading of the pre-Reformation church, East and West, and the natural sense of a power to remit and retain conferred at John 20:23. But its sharpest form — Trent’s absolution as “a judicial act, whereby sentence is pronounced by the priest as by a judge” — is a Latin scholastic specification the fathers do not uniformly hold (several read the power as one of discernment), and the Greek of John 20:23 does not by itself force effect over declaration: genuinely disputed between the confessions, no clear winner (contested) · advanced by the Council of Trent (the judicial definition), Ambrose, and John Chrysostom

Related arguments: the power of the keys — see also Is priestly absolution a declaration of the gospel? (the declarative reading)

The claim

although the absolution of the priest is the dispensation of another’s bounty, yet is it not a bare ministry only, whether of announcing the Gospel, or of declaring that sins are forgiven, but is after the manner of a judicial act, whereby sentence is pronounced by the priest as by a judge.

— Council of Trent, Session 14 (1551), Doctrine on the Sacrament of Penance, ch. 6, link; Canon 9 anathematizes the contrary — that absolution “is not a judicial act, but a bare ministry of pronouncing and declaring sins to be forgiven.”1 The interpretation this page weighs is that the words “whose soever sins ye remit, they are remitted” (John 20:23) and “whatsoever thou shalt bind … loose” (Matthew 16:19; 18:18) confer on the minister a real, effective power over sins: his absolution is the instrument by which God forgives the penitent before him, not a report that God has already done so independently. Grace works through the priestly act — the reading the Reformers denied and Trent defined against them.

Who advances it

Trent is the sharp modern form, but the reading is far older and far wider than Trent: it is the ordinary sense of the pre-Reformation church, Greek and Latin alike. Ambrose of Milan, writing against the Novatianist rigorists, treats the power to forgive as a genuine office entrusted to priests, not a bare announcement anyone might make:

they are showing great reverence for God, to whom alone they reserve the power of forgiving sins. But in truth no one does him greater injury than those who … reject the office entrusted to them. … For this power has been entrusted to priests alone.

— Ambrose, Concerning Repentance I.2.6–7.2 John Chrysostom states the effective reading at its strongest — the priest’s sentence is ratified in heaven:

what priests do here below God ratifies above, and the Master confirms the sentence of his servants. For indeed what is it but all manner of heavenly authority which He has given them when He says, “Whose sins ye remit they are remitted, and whose sins ye retain they are retained?”

— Chrysostom, On the Priesthood III.3 Cyril of Alexandria gives the same power a pneumatological form — the deed is done through the minister, but it is the indwelling Spirit who remits:

they … should have power also to remit or retain the sins of whomsoever they would, the Holy Spirit That dwelt in them remitting or retaining them according to His Will, though the deed were done through human instrumentality.

— Cyril of Alexandria, Commentary on John, Book XII.4 The same “ratifying” note runs through the Latin exegesis of the binding-and-loosing texts: Hilary of Poitiers has heaven confirm “the judgment of the Apostles” on Matthew 18:18, and Jerome, on the same verse, that “the sentence of man is ratified by the sentence of God.”5

The evidence for

The words confer a power, not a message. John 20:23 does not read “proclaim that sins are forgiven” but “whose soever sins ye remit, they are remitted … whose soever sins ye retain, they are retained.” The two-sided grant — a discretion both to remit and to retain — is hard to reduce to a general announcement of the gospel: one does not “announce” retention over the world at large; retention is a discriminating judgment about a particular person, which is exactly what a declaration of the universal promise is not. The keys of Matthew 16:19 point the same way — a delegated authority “to bind and to loose,” language the rabbis used for authoritative rulings, not mere reportage.

The pre-Reformation consensus reads it this way. Across the fathers the power is treated as genuinely given and genuinely exercised: Chrysostom’s “God ratifies above” is not idiosyncratic but the mainstream, echoed by Ambrose’s “office entrusted to priests,” by Cyril’s Spirit “that dwelt in them,” and by the Latin “ratifying” gloss on the binding texts. Theophylact of Ohrid, in the Byzantine tradition, presses even the point that the minister’s unworthiness does not void the grace — “grace acts through them, just as it once spoke through Balaam’s donkey” — the very logic of an effective (rather than merely declarative) act.6 When Trent defines absolution as more than “a bare ministry … of declaring that sins are forgiven,” it is codifying, not inventing, this long reading.

Adversarial assessment

What holds. That the texts confer a real power over sins — not a bare announcement — is the natural sense of the “remit / retain” grant and the near-unanimous witness of the ancient and medieval church, East and West. On that broad claim the effective reading is very strong; the burden lies on the declarative side to show that a two-way discretionary power collapses into a general proclamation.

What is overstated or imprecise. Two joints keep the reading from being simply sound. (1) The specific Tridentine form — absolution as “a judicial act, whereby sentence is pronounced by the priest as by a judge” — is a Latin scholastic development, not the uniform patristic shape of the doctrine. The Christian East never adopted the juridical “I absolve thee”; its classical absolution is deprecative (“May God … forgive thee”), a form that sits more easily with the minister as the Spirit’s instrument than as a sentencing judge. And a real patristic strand reads the keys as a power of discernment rather than causation: Jerome, on Matthew 16:19, likens the priest to the Levitical examiner who declares a leper clean or unclean but does “not make” him so, adding that “with God, it is not the judgment of priests, but the life of the accused that is sought”; Gregory the Great holds the absolution valid only “when it follows the judgment of the internal Judge,” God vivifying Lazarus first and the disciples loosing him after.7 These are the same fathers the effective reading claims — they support a conferred and heaven-ratified power, but not always Trent’s juridical-sentence account of how it works. (2) The exegesis of John 20:23 does not by itself settle effect over declaration: the verbs are perfect passives (“they have been remitted”), and the passive voice makes God the agent — a datum the declarative side marshals to argue that the forgiveness is already God’s and the minister ministerial. The grammar is genuinely compatible with either reading and decides neither (see the declarative page for the tense debate).

Assessment

Contested. Rate the interpretation, not the polemic. In its broad form — that the keys and John 20:23 confer a real, God-ratified power to remit and retain the sins of a particular penitent, so that the minister is an instrument of forgiveness and not a bare herald — the reading is well-grounded, is the near-universal pre-Reformation consensus (East and West), and follows the natural sense of a two-way discretionary grant. In its sharp form — Trent’s absolution as a “judicial act … sentence pronounced by the priest as by a judge” — it is a Latin juridical development that the Greek East never took (its absolution stays deprecative) and that a discernment-reading strand in the fathers (Jerome on Matthew 16:19; Gregory) does not require; and the exegesis of John 20:23 does not compel effect over declaration. Strong by the historian’s and the exegete’s eye for a conferred power, weaker where it hardens into a single juridical mechanism the whole tradition never shared: genuinely disputed between the confessions, no clear winner (contested). The rival — that absolution only declares a forgiveness God gives directly to faith — is weighed on its own page.
