Is priestly absolution a declaration of the gospel? — the declarative reading

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: plausible — the interpretation weighed here is the Reformers’: that in the words of John 20:23 and the keys of Matthew 16:19 the minister does not himself confer forgiveness but is a herald who declares and applies the forgiveness God gives directly to faith through the gospel. It has a real exegetical footing (the perfect-passive verbs make God the agent; “who can forgive sins but God alone?”), a genuine patristic minority behind it (Jerome’s leprosy analogy on Matthew 16:19; Gregory the Great; Origen), and is a major living confessional position. But it is short of the patristic majority and strains against the retain clause — a discriminating, person-specific power that a general proclamation of the gospel does not obviously supply. Coherent and not ruled out, but not the stronger reading of the whole record (plausible) · advanced by Martin Luther and John Calvin

Related arguments: the power of the keys — see also Does priestly absolution effect forgiveness? (the judicial / instrumental reading)

The claim

we must not separate this power of forgiving sins from the office of teaching, with which it is closely connected in this passage. … we have ground of thanksgiving to God, who hath conferred on men so high an honor, as to make them the ambassadors and deputies of God, and of his Son, in declaring the forgiveness of sins.

— John Calvin, Commentary on John (on 20:23), link.1 The interpretation this page weighs is that the keys are bound to the word: the minister forgives sins only in the sense that he authoritatively proclaims and applies the pardon God grants to the believing penitent through the promise of the gospel. He is an ambassador announcing terms of peace already made, a witness testifying to a verdict God has rendered — not a judge whose sentence itself decides the case. Forgiveness is God’s act, received by faith; the absolution declares it.

Who advances it

Luther grounds the whole of penance in the promise received by faith, not in any power resident in the priest or the penitent’s own sorrow:

Whereas this, like the other two sacraments, consists of the word of the divine promise on one side and of our faith on the other … Beware then of trusting in thine own contrition, or attributing remission of sins to thy own sorrow. It is not because of these that God looks on thee with favour, but because of the faith with which thou hast believed His threatenings and promises.

— Luther, The Babylonian Captivity of the Church (1520), on the sacrament of penance.2 Calvin makes the keys inseparable from preaching: “when we treat of the keys, we must always beware of dreaming of any power apart from the preaching of the Gospel … the whole power of which consists in this, that the grace of the Gospel is publicly and privately sealed on the minds of believers … and the only method in which this can be done is by preaching.”3 The reading is not a Reformation invention: a real strand of the fathers reads the keys as a power of discernment rather than causation. Jerome, on Matthew 16:19, likens the priest to the Levitical examiner who declares a leper clean or unclean but does not make him so:

not that priests make lepers and unclean; but so that they have knowledge … So just as the priest there makes the leper clean or unclean, so here the bishop and priest bind or loose … according to their office, when they hear the varieties of sins, they know who should be bound and who should be loosed.

— Jerome, Commentary on Matthew, adding that “with God, it is not the judgment of priests, but the life of the accused that is sought.”4 Gregory the Great holds the absolution valid only “when it follows the judgment of the internal Judge” (God raises Lazarus first; the disciples loose him after), and Origen ties the whole power to the minister acting as one who “forgives whatever God forgives.”5

The evidence for

The grammar makes God the agent. In John 20:23 the verbs of forgiveness and retention are perfect passives — the sins “have been remitted” / “have been retained” — and the passive voice points past the minister to God as the one acting. On the strongest form of the case, J. R. Mantey argued that the perfects should be rendered as future-perfects (“they shall have been remitted”; “whatever thou shalt bind … shall have been bound in heaven”), so that the disciples’ act announces or ratifies what God has already determined.6 Only God forgives sins. The declarative reading is the natural way to honour the axiom the scribes state and Jesus does not deny — “who can forgive sins but God alone?” (Mark 2:7) — without emptying John 20:23: the minister genuinely exercises the keys, but as the mouth by which God’s own pardon is pronounced. The keys are the word. Where Matthew 16:19 gives the keys and Matthew 18:18 the binding-and-loosing, the immediate contexts are confession of the gospel (16:16) and the discipline of an unrepentant brother (18:15–17) — functions of proclamation and admonition, which is why fathers as early as Jerome read the keys as a discerning declaration of a state, on the model of the priest who pronounces (but does not cause) cleanness.

Adversarial assessment

What holds. The reading is coherent and genuinely grounded. The passive verbs do make God the acting agent; the “God alone forgives” axiom is real; and the Reformers can point to a true patristic minority (Jerome’s leprosy analogy, Gregory’s “follows the internal Judge,” Origen) that already read the keys as discernment rather than causation. As an account of how ministerial forgiveness relates to God’s, it is not ruled out, and it remains the confession of a large part of the church.

What is overstated or imprecise. (1) The grammar does not prove it. Mantey’s future-perfect rendering was answered in the same journal by H. J. Cadbury, who showed that the perfect “is not necessarily prior to” the action of the other clause — the tense is resultative (“they stand forgiven”) and settles neither timing nor mechanism. D. A. Carson, who accepts the substantive “heaven acts first” sense on other grounds, judges Mantey’s studies “marred” by one-sided citation of grammarians. So the tense is compatible with the declarative reading but does not establish it — a proponent’s overreach flagged rather than relied on.7 (2) The retain clause resists it. “Whose soever sins ye retain, they are retained” grants a discriminating, person-specific power to withhold as well as to grant — and one does not “announce” retention over the world; a bare declaration of the universal gospel promise has no obvious room for a minister’s retaining of a particular penitent’s sins, which reads more like a conferred discretion than a herald’s report. (3) It is a minority of the tradition. The mainstream of the fathers — Chrysostom’s “God ratifies above,” Ambrose’s “office entrusted to priests,” Cyril’s Spirit remitting “through human instrumentality” — treats the power as more than announcement, and the Reformers’ own patristic witnesses (Jerome, Gregory) affirm a heaven-ratified power, not a bare declaration. The stronger of these — the effective reading — is weighed on its own page.

Assessment

Plausible. Rate the interpretation, not the polemic. The declarative reading — that the minister proclaims and applies a forgiveness God gives directly to faith, rather than conferring it — is coherent, has a real exegetical case (the perfect-passive verbs with God as agent; “God alone forgives”; the keys bound to the word), draws a genuine patristic minority (Jerome on Matthew 16:19; Gregory; Origen), and is the living confession of much of the church. It is short of probable, however: the grammar its proponents lean on does not decide the question (Mantey’s future-perfect overstates it; Cadbury’s caution is the mainstream corrective), the retain clause fits a conferred discretion better than a general proclamation, and the weight of patristic testimony — including the fathers the reading claims — runs toward a heaven-ratified power that is more than announcement. Coherent and not ruled out, but not the stronger reading of the whole record (plausible). The rival — that absolution is the appointed instrument through which God forgives — is weighed on its own page.
