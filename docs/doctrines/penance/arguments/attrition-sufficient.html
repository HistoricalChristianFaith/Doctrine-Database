Does attrition (fear-born sorrow) suffice with the sacrament? — the attritionist reading

Type: supporting argument (off-timeline) · Assessment: contested — the interpretation weighed here is that imperfect sorrow — attrition, conceived from fear of hell or the shamefulness of sin rather than from love of God — suffices to reconcile the penitent when it is joined to sacramental absolution, no distinct act of charity being required. On the Tridentine letter this is strong: Trent’s Session 14 says attrition “disposes” the penitent “to obtain the grace of God in the sacrament of penance,” Canon 5 protects attrition as “a true and profitable sorrow,” and it was “more common among the schoolmen” (so Alexander VII’s own decree). But Trent deliberately declined to define that no beginning of love is needed, and the contritionist objection — that servile fear which does not yet turn the heart toward God is not real conversion — is coherent and was left standing by Rome in 1667: genuinely disputed, no clear winner (contested) · advanced by Duns Scotus and the later scholastics, and defined only ambiguously at the Council of Trent

Related arguments: attrition and contrition — see also Is heartfelt contrition (love of God) required for the sacrament to forgive?

The claim

…that imperfect contrition… which is called attrition, because that it is commonly conceived either from the consideration of the turpitude of sin, or from the fear of hell and of punishment… if it exclude the wish to sin, with the hope of pardon… is even a gift of God, and an impulse of the Holy Ghost… And though it cannot of itself, without the sacrament of penance, conduct the sinner to justification, yet does it dispose him to obtain the grace of God in the sacrament of penance.

— Council of Trent, Session 14 (1551), Doctrine on the Sacrament of Penance, ch. 4, link.1 The reading this states, pressed to its point, is that a penitent who comes to confession with attrition only — sorrow born of fear, not of love — is truly reconciled when the priest absolves him. The sacrament supplies what the disposition lacks: it need only find the penitent well-disposed and not resisting (non ponens obicem, “placing no obstacle”), and it confers grace ex opere operato, by the working of the rite itself. Attrition is therefore a sufficient proximate disposition inside the sacrament, even though, outside it, only contrition (with the desire of the sacrament) reconciles.

Who advances it

The attritionist line is a medieval development. Where the earlier consensus (Aquinas and the Thomist school) required that the attrite penitent become contrite — ex attrito fit contritus, the sacrament converting imperfect sorrow into the perfect sorrow that alone justifies — Duns Scotus (d. 1308) and the nominalists after him lowered the threshold: attrition as such, without transformation into charity, is enough, because the sacrament’s efficacy rests on the rite (and on the absolution as its “form”), not on the height of the recipient’s sorrow, provided he places no obstacle.2 This became the working view of the later schoolmen and of much post-medieval pastoral practice, and it is the position the Council of Trent comes closest to endorsing (Session 14, ch. 4), though the council carefully stops short of defining that attrition without any incipient love suffices. When the question flared into the 17th-century attritionism controversy — sharpest in Belgium, the homeland of Jansenism — a decree of the Holy Office under Alexander VII (5–6 May 1667) forbade either party to brand the other with “any note of theological censure… until something shall have been defined in this matter by the Holy See,” noting in passing that the attritionist opinion “appears to be the more common among the scholastics.”3

The evidence for

The steelman is strong within the Tridentine framework it presupposes. First, the conciliar letter tilts toward it. Trent Session 14, ch. 4 states flatly that attrition “does not… make a man a hypocrite… but… is even a gift of God, and an impulse of the Holy Ghost… whereby the penitent being assisted prepares a way for himself unto justice,” and that though it cannot justify by itself, “it disposes him to obtain the grace of God in the sacrament of penance.”1 Canon 5 then anathematizes anyone who says that this fear-conceived sorrow “is not a true and profitable sorrow, that it does not prepare for the reception of the grace of God, but that it makes a man a hypocrite, yea even more a sinner.”4 Second, the mechanism is coherent and general. If the sacraments give grace ex opere operato to one who places no obstacle — the same principle that lets an infant be baptized, or an imperfectly-fervent communicant be nourished — then it is arbitrary to demand of penance alone a disposition (perfect charity) which, if the penitent already had it, would have reconciled him before he reached the priest. Attrition is exactly the disposition proper to a sacrament that exists to raise the fallen who are not yet in charity. Third, it is the pastoral and majority reading. Alexander VII’s own decree records it as the more common scholastic view, and the church declined to condemn it — the closest a genuinely open question comes to an official lean.

Adversarial assessment

What holds. Within Catholic sacramental theology the attritionist reading is well-grounded and arguably the stronger by the letter of Trent and by the general logic of ex opere operato. The council took real pains to rescue attrition from the Reformers’ charge that fear-born sorrow is worthless or hypocritical, and Rome has never defined against it; it is the reading most later scholastics held and most confessors assumed.

What is overstated or imprecise. The strong form — that pure servile fear, containing no turning of the heart toward God whatever, reconciles — claims more than Trent granted. (1) The council’s drafts debated whether attrition must include “some beginning of love,” and the final text is deliberately silent on the point; it defines that attrition disposes and is not worthless, not that it suffices without any incipient charity.1 (2) Trent’s own decree on justification (Session 6) makes charity the form of the grace that justifies — which the contritionist reading presses into an objection: a “sorrow” wholly without love may restrain the hand while the heart still clings to sin (so the Jansenist Quesnel), and such a disposition looks less like conversion than like fear of consequences.5 (3) Even Scotus’s attrition is not mere calculation — on a careful reading the absolution avails only where the sinner truly repents — so the caricature of “forgiveness on servile fear alone” is not the strongest attritionist case, and it is the caricature the contritionists rightly resist. The honest situation is that the two readings divide at exactly the joint Rome refused to close: does the “imperfect” sorrow Trent blesses already contain the seed of love, or not?

Assessment

Contested. Rate the interpretation, not the polemic. In its measured form — that attrition is a genuine, grace-given sorrow which, joined to sacramental absolution, truly reconciles a penitent who is not resisting God — the reading is well-grounded, Trent-friendly, and the more common scholastic view; by the letter of Session 14 it has the better of the argument. In its strong form — that sorrow arising from nothing but fear, with no incipient turning toward God, suffices — it claims what Trent pointedly declined to define and what the contritionist reading coherently contests. The Church itself certified the standoff: in 1667 Alexander VII forbade either side to censure the other, which is the textbook shape of a contested question — strong by the letter of the council and by the sacramental economy, genuinely open where it presses past what was defined, and left undecided by the authority best placed to decide it (contested). Where a proponent over-reads Trent as having settled pure attritionism, the overstatement is flagged on the council’s page: Session 14 defined attrition a true and dispositive sorrow, not the sufficiency of servile fear without love.
