The Shepherd of Hermas on Penance

Dates: c. 150 · Wikipedia
Summary: The earliest full witness to the church's struggle over post-baptismal sin. Hermas records a view he has heard — that baptism is the only repentance — and then softens it: for those already baptized the Lord has, in his mercy, appointed one further repentance, but one only. This "second repentance," granted once and not to be repeated, is the seed from which the whole later system of canonical penance grew.

Claims & evidence

Some teachers say baptism is the only repentance there is

Hermas reports the rigorist position of his day as a thing he was taught — and the Shepherd (the angel of repentance) confirms it as "sound doctrine," before qualifying it:

I heard, sir, some teachers maintain that there is no other repentance than that which takes place, when we descended into the water and received remission of our former sins. He said to me, That was sound doctrine which you heard; for that is really the case. For he who has received remission of his sins ought not to sin any more, but to live in purity.

— The Pastor of Hermas, Commandment Fourth, ch. III (Ante-Nicene Fathers, vol. II), source.1

But the Lord has appointed one repentance after baptism — and one only

Against the bare rigorism, Hermas grants a single further chance, entrusted to the angel of repentance, for the sins of those already called. It is explicitly a once-only concession, not a standing remedy:

if any one is tempted by the devil, and sins after that great and holy calling. in which the Lord has called His people to everlasting life, he has opportunity to repent but once. But if he should sin frequently after this, and then repent, to such a man his repentance will be of no avail; for with difficulty will he live.

— The Pastor of Hermas, Commandment Fourth, ch. III, source.2 The same rule governs his discussion of a divorced-then-repentant wife: the husband must take her back — "But not frequently. For there is but one repentance to the servants of God."3

Notes

  • Why this counts as a relaxation, not a tightening. Hermas is often read as a rigorist because of the "once only" limit; the ANF editors read him the opposite way — as opening a door the strictest teachers had shut. His own framing is that God, "knowing the weakness of men," added a repentance for those already baptized that the "one baptism" school would have denied them. The later canonical system took over exactly this shape: a serious, public, once-in-a-lifetime second chance.
  • The texts behind the debate. The rigorist side rested on Hebrews 6:4–6 and 10:26 (it is "impossible… to renew them again unto repentance"); Hermas's concession is the first datable attempt to hold those warnings together with the pastoral fact of Christians who sin and grieve for it. The tension runs straight through Tertullian, Cyprian, and Novatian a century later.
