The Shepherd of Hermas on Penance

Dates: c. 150 · Wikipedia
Summary: The earliest extant text on this timeline to treat post-baptismal sin at length. Hermas records a view he has heard — that baptism is the only repentance — and then qualifies it: for those already baptized the Lord has, in his mercy, appointed one further repentance, but one only, granted once and not to be repeated.

Claims & evidence

Some teachers say baptism is the only repentance there is

Hermas reports this position as a thing he was taught — and the Shepherd (the angel of repentance) confirms it as "sound doctrine," before qualifying it:

I heard, sir, some teachers maintain that there is no other repentance than that which takes place, when we descended into the water and received remission of our former sins. He said to me, That was sound doctrine which you heard; for that is really the case. For he who has received remission of his sins ought not to sin any more, but to live in purity.

— The Pastor of Hermas, Commandment Fourth, ch. III (Ante-Nicene Fathers, vol. II), source.1

One repentance after baptism, and one only

Hermas then grants a single further repentance, entrusted to the angel of repentance, for those already called:

if any one is tempted by the devil, and sins after that great and holy calling. in which the Lord has called His people to everlasting life, he has opportunity to repent but once. But if he should sin frequently after this, and then repent, to such a man his repentance will be of no avail; for with difficulty will he live.

— The Pastor of Hermas, Commandment Fourth, ch. III, source.2 The same rule governs his discussion of a divorced-then-repentant wife: the husband must take her back — "But not frequently. For there is but one repentance to the servants of God."3

Notes

  • How the "once only" limit has been read. His own framing is that the Lord, who "knew the weakness of men," set a repentance for those already called. O. D. Watkins (1920) calls the book "a book of rigorist conviction, which indicates a wide measure of pardon as available for a single and exceptional occasion"; the Ante-Nicene Fathers editor, annotating the "once only" clause, treats the book as opposing "the very Montanism" whose strictness some of its words seem to echo; and Edward Hanna (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1911) holds that Hermas "takes a resolute stand" against a school of rigorists and that the limit "may well be understood as referring to public penance."4 How far the earliest church held the gravest sins to be reconcilable is a disputed reading.
  • Hebrews and the "Shepherd." Tertullian, writing as a Montanist (c. 217), sets Hebrews 6:4–6 ("For impossible it is that they who have once been illuminated… should be again recalled unto repentance") against "that apocryphal 'Shepherd' of adulterers," and says its author "never knew of any 'second repentance' promised by apostles to the adulterer and fornicator."5 Repentance after baptism is also treated by Cyprian and Novatian a century after Hermas.
